Polynomial McKean-Vlasov SDEs
Christa Cuchiero, Janka Möller

Abstract
We study a new class of McKean-Vlasov stochastic differential equations (SDEs), possibly with common noise, applying the theory of time-inhomogeneous polynomial processes. The drift and volatility coefficients of these SDEs depend on the state variables themselves as well as their conditional moments in a way that mimics the standard polynomial structure. Our approach leads to new results on the existence and uniqueness of solutions to such conditional McKean-Vlasov SDEs which are, to the best of our knowledge, not obtainable using standard methods. Moreover, we show in the case without common noise that the moments of these McKean-Vlasov SDEs can be computed by non-linear ODEs. As a by-product, this also yields new results on the existence and uniqueness of global solutions to certain ODEs.



Keywords:
McKean-Vlasov stochastic differential equations, polynomial processes,

time-inhomogeneous Markov processes, non-linear ODEs

60H10, 60J25, 34A34

Introduction

Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) of McKean-Vlasov type are a class of SDEs where the drift and diffusion coefficient depend not only on time and the current state but also on the marginal law of the solution. To make this precise, let us first consider SDEs (without common noise) of the form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX_{t}=B(t,X_{t},\bm{\mu}_{t})dt+\Sigma(t,X_{t},\bm{\mu}_{t})dW_{t},\\
\bm{\mu}_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(X_{t}),\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a one-dimensional Brownian motion. We refer to such equations as
McKean-Vlasov SDEs
in the following. Of course, McKean-Vlasov SDEs can be extended to multiple dimensions, in the sense that
X_{t}
is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued for all
t\geq 0
. For the purpose of this work, we however, restrict ourselves to the one-dimensional case.

McKean-Vlasov equations were introduced in
[
28
]
and have since then been widely studied. They gained importance in various fields such as finance
[
17
,
38
,
4
,
22
,
26
]
, biology
[
29
,
8
,
36
,
24
]
, physics
[
5
,
37
,
2
]
as well as optimal control
[
16
,
31
,
25
]
.

McKean-Vlasov SDEs often arise from a particle system, where the particles’ dynamics depend on the empirical measure of the system and the number of particles goes to infinity. In this limit, one can then describe a ”typical” particle by McKean-Vlasov dynamics, see e.g.
[
6
,
3
]
for an overview. In the context of this paper, we study the McKean-Vlasov SDEs directly, although, for applications, such SDEs supposedly arise by taking the limit of some particle system. Establishing the existence of the limit stemming from a particle system rigorously will involve specific techniques tailored to the current setting, compare e.g.,
[
9
,
34
]
, and is beyond the scope of this paper.

In our work, we are interested in building a connection between the theory of polynomial processes and McKean-Vlasov SDEs. This allows us to develop new techniques to establish existence and uniqueness results for certain classes of McKean-Vlasov SDEs which cannot be treated by standard methods.

Before we elaborate more on our approach, we would like to provide a brief - by no means complete - overview on existence and uniqueness results for solutions to McKean-Vlasov SDEs. The common approach consists in considering a space of probability measures
\mathscr{P}
(often the Wasserstein-2-space) and studying the maps
B,\Sigma
on the space
[0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\mathscr{P}
. A standard result in the literature is that existence and uniqueness of solutions to McKean-Vlasov SDEs can be established by requiring
B,\Sigma
to be Lipschitz-continuous in all arguments, and then applying a fixed-point argument, see
[
35
,
6
]
. Since such Lipschitz assumptions are often too restrictive, finding milder conditions is an active field of research, and we would like to briefly point out a few such approaches. For examples, the authors of
[
1
]
go beyond Lipschitz-continuity requiring instead an Osgood-type condition, however not considering any law-dependence of the diffusion term. In
[
11
]
, the authors use Euler-Maruyama approximations to show existence of a solution to a martingale problem, for which they establish in turn pathwise uniqueness leading to a strong solution. The approach taken in
[
18
]
is based on Lyapunov functions to show existence and uniqueness of solutions. Another route is taken in
[
30
]
where the authors study the existence of weak and strong solutions to McKean-Vlasov SDEs, however requiring non-degeneracy in the diffusion term for weak solutions and additionally Lipschitz-continuity in the state-variable of the diffusion term to construct a strong solution. Very recently,
[
12
]
have established existence of McKean-Vlasov SDEs under mild continuity assumptions, in particular, without requiring the dependence on the law to be Lipschitz continuous. However, in general they do not obtain uniqueness of the solution.

In contrast to those approaches we consider a more specific dependence of the SDEs’ coefficients on the law, that is, we let them primarily depend on the marginal moments. By this restriction, we do not only get rid of Lipschitz-requirements on the diffusion term, but we avoid having to work on the space of probability measures. Instead, we can fully treat the McKean-Vlasov SDEs under our specification using the theory of time-inhomogeneous Markov processes, without explicit dependence on the marginal laws. In particular, we apply the theory of (time-inhomogeneous) polynomial processes, which was first introduced in
[
10
]
. We also refer the reader to
[
27
]
for an introduction to the topic.

The appeal of this class of processes lies in the fact that one has an analytic expression for the (conditional) moments, which is computable via the solution of a linear ODE. For our purpose, we need to work in a time-inhomogeneous setting, which was first considered in
[
20
]
. The polynomial property of a Markov process is a requirement on infinitesimal generator. More specifically, they are assumed to map polynomials to (time-dependent) polynomials of the same or lower degree. In this context, it is therefore natural to establish also existence of solutions to SDEs via properties of their generators, that is via martingale problems. This is the approach we pursue in this work, as outlined in detail in Subsections
2.2
and
3
.

To be more specific, we treat, in the case without common noise, McKean-Vlasov SDEs of the following form and establish existence and uniqueness of solutions thereof:

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider a one-dimensional McKean-Vlasov SDE of the form

\displaystyle dZ_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(b({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])+\beta({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])Z_{t}\right)dt+\sqrt{c({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])+\gamma({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])Z_{t}+\Gamma({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])Z_{t}^{2}}dW_{t}

(1.2)

where
(\mathbb{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}:=(Z_{t},Z^{2}_{t},\dots,Z^{N}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for
N\geq 1
and with fixed
{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{0}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, some maps
b,\,\beta,\,c,\,\gamma,\,\Gamma:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and a Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. If
b,\beta,c,\gamma,\Gamma
are locally Lipschitz continuous on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, satisfy either the set of Assumptions
A1
or Assumptions
A2
(specified below), and the term under the square-root is non-negative, then there exists a unique strong solution
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Moreover,
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a time-inhomogeneous polynomial process whose moments can be computed by a non-linear ODE, see (
3.6
).

Note that in (
1.2
) the polynomial structure of one-dimensional diffusions, exhibiting an affine drift and a quadratic diffusion function, is mimicked. The respective coefficients of these functions are, however, no longer constants, but general maps of the moments. This structure is the very reason why we obtain an
autonomous non-linear ODE system for the moments.

The above result is actually obtained from two different viewpoints; a primal and a dual approach, which we will both outline in detail later. However, it is important to emphasize that even though both approaches lead to the same sufficient conditions, there are important differences between the two approaches, reflected in the fact that in some cases the existence of a solution can only be shown via one of the two approaches, see Remark
3.17
for more details.

Additionally, we would like to point out that studying McKean-Vlasov SDEs from a polynomial perspective has benefits beyond establishing merely existence and uniqueness. Indeed, the solutions are in particular time-inhomogeneous polynomial processes themselves, which means that we obtain analytic expressions for their (conditional) moments. We believe that this can be of importance for modeling purposes. Moreover, we can formulate sufficient conditions for the process to remain in certain state-spaces which are subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. In particular, we can construct McKean-Vlasov SDEs such that the solution stays in a compact set, which is another aspect we believe may be useful for modeling in areas like population genetics.

The second part of our main contributions lies in the study of polynomial McKean-Vlasov SDEs with a common noise term and coefficients depending on the moments conditional on the common noise. We call these processes
conditional polynomial McKean-Vlasov SDEs
and present the corresponding results in Subsection
3.3
. We establish general sufficient conditions for existence and uniqueness of solutions to this of class of McKean-Vlasov SDEs (see Theorem
3.24
, Proposition
3.28
) and show that for a subclass the solution is even a time-homogeneous polynomial process.

In summary, this paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we briefly introduce the theory of time-inhomogeneous polynomial processes with an emphasis on the aspects that are relevant for our main results. After that, in Subsection
2.2
, we first recall what it means to establish existence of a solution to an SDE via the solution to the associated martingale problem, following the work of
[
14
]
and then extend some results of
[
14
]
to milder growth-conditions on time-dependent coefficients of SDEs. We are then ready to present our main results on polynomial McKean-Vlasov SDEs in Section
3
. There, we present two approaches, called primal and dual, to establish existence of solutions. The primal approach is then extended to the setting with common noise.

Preliminaries

2.1
Time-inhomogeneous polynomial processes

In this subsection, we introduce the notion of time-inhomogeneous polynomial processes and state some of their properties, which will be crucial for the main results of this paper. We mainly follow
[
20
]
. However, while in
[
20
]
general time-inhomogeneous polynomial processes possibly with jumps are studied, we restrict ourselves to the case of continuous processes, which is the most relevant setting for us. On the other hand, the authors of
[

]
formulate everything on a finite time-horizon, while we consider a possibly infinite time-horizon.
This extension is straightforward, and we therefore only comment briefly on the required adjustments in some of the proofs.

Before moving to polynomial processes, we first briefly recall the definition of a Markov process as well as its associated family of transition functions and transition operators, where we mostly follow
[
13
]
. For further details on Markov processes, we refer to
[
13
,
14

.

Throughout, we fix a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)},\mathbb{P})
. We consider a state-space
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and denote its Borel
\sigma
-algebra by
\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 2.1

(Markov Process)
.

We call a stochastic process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
taking values in
S
a
Markov process
if it is
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
-adapted and

\mathbb{P}\left[X_{t}\in B\lvert\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[X_{t}\in B\lvert X_{s}\right]\quad{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s. for any }B\in\mathcal{S}\textrm{ and }0\leq s\leq t<\infty.

Definition 2.2

(Transition Function)
.

Let us define
\Delta=\{(s,t)\,\lvert\,0\leq s\leq t<\infty\}
. We associate with a Markov process
X
a family of functions, called
transition function
,
(p_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta}
such that
p_{s,t}:(S,\mathcal{S})\rightarrow[0,1]
for all
(s,t)\in\Delta
and

{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{t}\in B\lvert\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]=p_{s,t}(X_{s},B)\quad{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s. for any }(s,t)\in\Delta\textrm{ and }B\in\mathcal{S}.

Definition 2.3

(Transition Operator)
.

We can describe the Markov process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
via
transition operators

(P_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta}
with

P_{s,t}f(x)=\int_{S}f(\xi)p_{s,t}(x,d\xi)

for all
x\in S
and
\mathcal{S}
-measurable functions
f:S\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
. Moreover, these transition operators satisfy the
Chapman-Kolmogorov
equations

P_{s,u}f(x)=P_{s,t}P_{t,u}f(x)

for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq u<\infty
and
f
as above.

Following
[
20
]
, time-inhomogeneous polynomial processes are a particular type of Markov process, for which we shall need the subsequent notions.

We denote by
\mathbf{n}:=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})\in\{0,1,\dots\}^{d}
a multiindex, where
\lvert\mathbf{n}\rvert:=n_{1}+\dots+n_{d}
and for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
x^{\mathbf{n}}:=x_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{n_{d}}
. Let us introduce the following spaces of (time-dependent) polynomials up to degree
m\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{m}(S)

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\mapsto\sum_{\lvert\mathbf{n}\rvert=0}^{m}a_{\mathbf{n}}x^{\mathbf{n}}\,\lvert\,x\in S,a_{\mathbf{n}}\in{\mathbb{R}}\right\}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(S)

\displaystyle:=\left\{(s,t,x)\mapsto\sum_{\lvert\mathbf{n}\rvert=0^{m}}a_{\mathbf{n}}(s,t)x^{\mathbf{n}}\,\lvert\,(s,t,x)\in\Delta\times S,\,a_{\mathbf{n}}(\cdot)\in C^{1}(\Delta,{\mathbb{R}})\right\}.

Definition 2.4

(Polynomial Process)
.

We call a family of transition operators
(P_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta}

polynomial
if for all
m\geq 0
and
f\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(S)
it holds that

\left((s,t,x)\mapsto P_{s,t}f(x)\right)\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(S).

Moreover, we call a Markov process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
a
(
time-inhomogeneous
)
polynomial process
if its associated transition operators are polynomial.

Remark 2.5
.

Let us note that in the above definition, we have implicitly assumed that polynomials are
\mathcal{S}
-measurable and, more crucially, that all moments exist.

Definition 2.6

(Infinitesimal Generator)
.

For a Markov process with transition operators
(P_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta}
we define the family of
infinitesimal generators

(\mathcal{H}_{s})_{s\in[0,\infty)}
as the linear operators given by,

(\mathcal{H}_{s}f)(x):=\lim_{h\searrow 0}\frac{(P_{s,s+h}f-f)(x)}{h}\quad\textrm{for all }x\in S,

whenever this limit exists. We denote the set of
\mathcal{S}
-measurable functions
f:S\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
for which the limit exists by
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{s})
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
(\mathcal{H}_{s})_{s\in[0,\infty)}
be the infinitesimal generator associated with a polynomial transition operator, then it holds for all
m\geq 0
and
s\in[0,\infty)
that
\mathcal{P}_{m}(S)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{s})
and
\mathcal{H}_{s}(\mathcal{P}_{m}(S))\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{m}(S)
.

Proof.

The result follows from the fact that a polynomial transition operator maps polynomials to polynomials with continuously differentiable time-dependent coefficients. Hence, the limit translates to taking the derivative of these coefficients. Note that the coefficients of
\mathcal{H}_{s}f
for
f\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(S)
depend on
s\in[0,\infty)
. See
[
20
, Proof of Lemma 3.3]
for further details.
∎

\displaystyle A^{i}

To this end, let us consider polynomials up to an arbitrary but fixed degree

Av_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{N(m)}\mathbf{A}_{ji}v_{j}.

(2.1)

Indeed, for polynomial processes and for fixed time-points

(resp.

For a polynomial process

2.2
Existence and uniqueness of solutions to time-inhomogeneous diffusions

In this section, we present the approach to establish existence of weak solutions to SDEs via existence of solutions to the
martingale problem
. First, we briefly outline the concept behind this approach and refer the reader to
[
14
, Chapter 4 and Chapter 5.3]
for more details. In Proposition
2.17
and Theorem
2.18
we present sufficient conditions on the coefficients of the SDE for a solution to the martingale problem to exist in a time-inhomogeneous setting. While Proposition
2.17
follows the same reasoning as in the time-homogeneous setting outlined e.g., in
[
27
]
, Theorem
2.18
is an extension of
[
14
, Proposition 5.3.5 and Theorem 5.3.10]
allowing for milder growth-conditions of the time-dependencies. Let us start by the definition of a solution to the martingale problem (see
[
14
, Chapter 4.3]
for more details).

Definition 2.15

(Solution to the Martingale Problem)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic process with state-space
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
on some filtered probability space and let
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq C_{b}(S)\rightarrow C_{b}(S)

be a linear operator for
C_{b}(\dot{)}
denoting the set of bound continuous functions. We call
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
a
solution to the martingale problem for

A
if

f(X_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}(Af)(X_{s})ds

is a martingale for all
f\in\mathcal{D}(A)
.

For our purpose, we are interested in the solution to martingale problems for a given (possibly time-extended)
generator of an SDE

\mathcal{H}^{0}
with
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}^{0})=C^{\infty}_{c}(S)
i.e. the smooth functions of compact support on the state space
S
.

Let us from now on consider a one-dimensional SDE of the form

dX_{t}=B(t,X_{t})dt+\Sigma(t,X_{t})dW_{t}

(2.2)

for continuous functions
B,\sigma:[0,\infty)\times S\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
,
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
, some given initial distribution for
X_{0}
and
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denoting a one-dimensional Brownian motion. We can associate with this SDE a family of
time-dependent
generators
(\mathcal{H}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
acting on
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(S)
as

(\mathcal{H}_{s}f)(x)=B(s,x)f^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{2}(s,x)f^{\prime\prime}(x)

for all
s\geq 0
and
x\in S
. We refer the reader to
[
14
, Propsition 5.3.1 and Lemma 5.3.2]
on how to construct a weak solution of an SDE given a solution to the martingale problem for the generator of the SDE. For brevity, we merely note here that a weak solution to an SDE can be constructed, if a solution to the martingale problem of its generator exists.

Let us point out, that in the time-inhomogeneous case, we need to study the time-extended martingale problem to establish existence of a solution, see
[
14
, Theorem 4.7.1 and Proposition 5.3.5]
. For an SDE with time-dependent coefficients and associated generators
(\mathcal{H}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
we define the generator of the time-extended process
(t,X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{0}:\{\tau f\,\lvert\,(\tau,f)\in C_{c}^{1}([0,\infty))\times C_{c}^{\infty}(S)\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow C([0,\infty)\times S)

\displaystyle\tau f

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathcal{H}^{0}\tau f

such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{0}\tau f:[0,\infty)\times S

\displaystyle\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle(t,x)

\displaystyle\mapsto\tau(t)\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)+\tau^{\prime}(t)f(x).

As in
[
14
, Proposition 5.3.5]
we study the existence of the solution fully in the time-extended setting first and then project the solution down to the space-component.

Establishing the existence of a solution
(t,X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to the martingale problem for
\mathcal{H}^{0}
can be broken down to the following two essential steps (see
[
14
, Proposition 5.3.5 and Theorem 5.3.10]
):

(i)

showing that
\mathcal{H}^{0}
satisfies the
positive maximum principle
2
2

2

We will introduce the positive maximum principle in Definition
2.16
.
(see
[
14
, Theorem 4.5.4]
)

(ii)

verifying that the solution
(t,X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has continuous sample paths with values in
[0,\infty)\times S
(see
[
14
, Proposition 3.9]
)

Clearly, both of the above steps will impose some conditions on the generator
\mathcal{H}^{0}
which translate to conditions on the SDE’s coefficients
B
and
\sigma
. Let now us tackle step (i):

Definition 2.16

(Positive Maximum Principle, see
[
14
, p. 165]
)
.

We say a linear operator
A
satisfies the
positive maximum principle (PMP)
on
S
if for
x_{0}\in S
such that
f(x_{0})=\sup_{x\in S}f(x)
for
f\in\mathcal{D}(A)
it holds that
(Af)(x_{0})\leq 0
.

We now establish conditions on the coefficients
B,\Sigma
for the positive maximum principle to be fulfilled on a given state-space
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
in the next proposition. Although we consider the time-inhomogeneous setting here, the reasoning is very similar to the time-homogeneous case outlined in
[
27
, Examples 4.15, 4.16 and 4.18]
.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let

dX_{t}=B(t,X_{t})dt+\Sigma(t,X_{t})dW_{t}

and denote by
\mathcal{H}^{0}
the associated time-extended generator.

(i)

For
S={\mathbb{R}}
, the generator
\mathcal{H}^{0}
satisfies the PMP without further assumptions as
\Sigma^{2}(\cdot,\cdot)\geq 0
on all of
[0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}
.

(ii)

For
S={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
,
\mathcal{H}^{0}
satisfies the PMP, if
\Sigma^{2}(\cdot,0)\equiv 0
and
B(\cdot,0)\geq 0
for
x=0

(iii)

For
S=[0,1]
,
\mathcal{H}^{0}
satisfies the PMP, if
\Sigma^{2}(\cdot,0)\equiv\Sigma^{2}(\cdot,1)\equiv 0
and the drift term fulfills

B(\cdot,0)\geq 0
,
B(\cdot,1)\leq 0
.

Proof.

Let
(t_{0},x_{0})\in[0,\infty)\times S
such that
\tau(t_{0})f(x_{0})=\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,\infty)\times S}\tau(t)f(x)
. We note that
\tau(t_{0})f(x_{0})\geq 0
since the functions have compact support on
[0,\infty)\times S
. Denoting by
S^{\circ}
the interior of
S
it clearly holds that for
(t_{0},x_{0})\in(0,\infty)\times S^{\circ}

\tau^{\prime}(t_{0})f(x_{0})=0,\quad\tau(t_{0})f^{\prime}(x_{0})=0,\quad\tau(t_{0})f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})\leq 0.

Hence, since
\Sigma^{2}(\cdot,\cdot)\geq 0
it is straightforward to check that
\mathcal{H}^{0}
satisfies the PMP on
(0,\infty)\times S^{\circ}
. Moreover, if
t_{0}=0
, it holds that

\tau^{\prime}(t_{0})f(x_{0})\leq 0,\quad\tau(t_{0})f^{\prime}(x_{0})=0,\quad\tau(t_{0})f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})\leq 0

and it is straightforward to check that
\mathcal{H}^{0}
therefore fulfills the PMP on
[0,\infty)\times S^{\circ}
. We are now left to check the cases where
S
contains some boundary points, i.e.
\partial S\cap S\neq\emptyset
. This case is relevant for
S={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
and
S=[0,1]
and yields the additional conditions. If
x_{0}\in\partial S\cap S
and
t_{0}\in[0,\infty)
, it holds that

\begin{cases}\tau(t_{0})f^{\prime}(x_{0})\leq 0\quad&\textrm{if }x_{0}=\inf{x\in S}\\
\tau(t_{0})f^{\prime}(x_{0})\geq 0\quad&\textrm{if }x_{0}=\sup{x\in S}.\\
\end{cases}

Note that we can not say anything about the second order derivative of
f
at the boundary and therefore require
\Sigma^{2}
to vanish on boundary points of
S
. It is easy to check that the conditions on
B
at the boundary points are exactly what we need for the PMP to be satisfied.
∎

Having taken care of (i), we address step (ii) in the following theorem:

Theorem 2.18
.

Consider a time-inhomogeneous SDE of the form

dX_{t}=B(t,X_{t})dt+\Sigma(t,X_{t})dW_{t},

with state-space
S={\mathbb{R}}
,
S={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
or
S=[0,1]
and
B,\Sigma:[0,\infty)\times S\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
continuous such that the infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{H}^{0}
satisfies the positive maximum principle on the corresponding state space. Then there exists a solution to the corresponding martingale problem, if there is a map
h(t):[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
which is continuous and strictly increasing s.t.

\Sigma^{2}(t,x)\leq K^{\Sigma}(1+h^{2}(t)+h(t)x+x^{2})

(2.3)

and

x\cdot B(t,x)\leq K^{B}(1+h(t)x+x^{2})

(2.4)

for all
x\in S
and
t\in[0,\infty)
and some constants
K^{\Sigma},K^{B}\geq 0
. Moreover, this solution is path-wise unique if
\Sigma^{2}(t,x),B(t,x)
are locally Lipschitz in
x
uniformly in
t
on bounded intervals, that is: for any open subset
U\subset S
and
T>0
there is a constant
K
such that

\lvert\Sigma^{2}(t,x)-\Sigma^{2}(t,y)\rvert\vee\lvert B(t,x)-B(t,y)\rvert\leq K\lvert x-y\rvert

for all
x,y\in U
and
t\in[0,T]
.

3
Polynomial McKean-Vlasov equations

In this section, we study sufficient conditions for existence and uniqueness of solutions to one-dimensional McKean-Vlasov SDEs. In particular, we are concerned with studying McKean-Vlasov SDEs which are of the following form:

Definition 3.1

(Polynomial McKean-Vlasov SDEs)
.

Let
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
be a one-dimensional Brownian motion. We call an SDE a
polynomial McKean-Vlasov SDE
if it is given by

\displaystyle dZ_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(b({\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}],\dots,{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}^{N}])+\beta({\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}],\dots,{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}^{N}])Z_{t}\right)dt

(3.1)

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{c({\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}],\dots,{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}^{N}])+\gamma({\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}],\dots,{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}^{N}])Z_{t}+\Gamma({\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}],\dots,{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}^{N}])Z_{t}^{2}}dW_{t}

for some
N\geq 1
, continuous maps
b,\,\beta,\,c,\,\gamma,\,\Gamma:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and
Z_{0}
with
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{0}],\dots,{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{0}^{N}]<\infty
.

For now the SDE in (
3.1
) is only formal, since we have not established the existence of solutions, which is the main goal of this section. We shall distinguish several state-spaces for
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
which will lead to certain restrictions on the maps
b,\beta,c,\gamma,\Gamma
. One obvious condition is that the term under the square-root must be positive, as we want the solution to be real-valued. We discuss possible state spaces in more detail in Theorem
3.8
, Remark
3.9
and Corollary
3.10
. We would like to point out that certain SDEs that are contained in the class of
3.1
have already been studied in the 1980s, see
[
33
]
, in the context of diffusions with a period law. Note that the form of (
3.1
) strongly resembles the one of the SDE in Example
2.10
. This resemblance is by no means a coincidence. In fact, we want to solve SDEs of the form of (
3.1
) using the theory of time-inhomogeneous polynomial processes. To do so, we can follow two approaches, a
primal
and a
dual
one, which we explore in detail in Section
3.1
and
3.2
respectively.

The primal approach is rather straightforward: we apply Itô’s lemma to find a system of ODEs describing the moments
({\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}],\dots,{\mathbb{E}}[Z^{N}_{t}])_{t\geq 0}
. We then establish conditions under which we obtain a unique global solution to the corresponding system of ODEs and hence have a full description of the (deterministic) process
({\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}],\dots,{\mathbb{E}}[Z^{N}_{t}])_{t\geq 0}
. Plugging this process back into the original SDE (
3.1
), we establish existence and uniqueness of solutions to the SDE via a solution of the associated martingale problem and conclude that this solution is a time-inhomogeneous polynomial process.

The dual approach is slightly more involved: we start by considering a time-inhomogeneous polynomial process, depending on a deterministic auxiliary process. At first glance, this has nothing to do with the McKean-Vlasov SDEs. However, the auxiliary process is required to satisfy a system of ODEs, which are in fact associated with the Kolmogorov forward and backward equations of the process’ transition operator. Using the moment formula, we can then conclude that the auxiliary process coincides with
({\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}],\dots,{\mathbb{E}}[Z^{N}_{t}])_{t\geq 0}
.

As a third consideration of this section, we extend the primal approach to a common-noise setting, where the moments are replaced by the conditional moments (conditional on the common noise). We present these results in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Primal point of view

Recall the form of the polynomial McKean-Vlasov SDEs given by (
3.1
). To shorten the notation, we write
{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}:=(Z_{t},\dots,Z_{t}^{N})
and accordingly
{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}]:=({\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}],\dots,{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{t}^{N}])
. Using Itô’s formula, it is then straightforward to derive for
k\in\{2,\ldots,N\}

\displaystyle dZ_{t}^{k}

\displaystyle=\Big[k\cdot\big(b({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])+\beta({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])Z_{t}\big)Z_{t}^{k-1}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\big(c({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])+\gamma({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])Z_{t}+\Gamma({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])Z_{t}^{2}\big)Z_{t}^{k-2}\Big]dt

(3.3)

\displaystyle\quad+k\cdot\sqrt{c({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])+\gamma({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])Z_{t}+\Gamma({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}])Z_{t}^{2}}Z_{t}^{k-1}dW_{t}.

(3.4)

We would then like to take the expectation and obtain a non-linear ODE for the moments. However, at this stage we do not know whether

M^{(k)}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{c({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}])+\gamma({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}])Z_{s}+\Gamma({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}])Z_{s}^{2}}Z_{s}^{k-1}dW_{s}

is a true martingale or just a local martingale. Note that
(M^{(k)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a true martingale if

\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big\lvert\big(c({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}])+\gamma({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}])Z_{s}+\Gamma({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}])Z_{s}^{2}\big)Z_{s}^{2k-2}\Big\rvert\Big]ds<\infty

(3.5)

for
t\geq 0
.
To establish this for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we impose certain growth-conditions on the maps
b,\beta,c,\gamma,\Gamma
. We present two sets of assumptions on these maps under which we can establish the true martingale property and later the existence of a solution to (
3.1
).

Definition 3.2
.

Let us define
\|x\|_{N}:=(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|x_{i}|^{\frac{2}{i}})^{\frac{1}{2}}
for any

Proof.

Let us first note that by assumption

\displaystyle z_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=z_{1}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}b(z(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\beta\cdot z_{1}(s)ds

\displaystyle z_{k+1}(t)

\displaystyle=z_{k+1}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}kb(z(s))z_{k}(s)+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\big(c(z(s))z_{k-1}(s)+\gamma(z(s))z_{k}(s)\big)ds

Remark 3.17

\displaystyle=0\textrm{ with }(\mathbf{vec}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{0},{\textsc{c}}}(0))_{1}=z

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{vec}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{0},{\textsc{c}}}(t))_{2}(\mathbf{vec}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{0},{\textsc{c}}}(t))_{1}\textrm{ with }(\mathbf{vec}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{0},{\textsc{c}}}(0))_{2}=z^{2}

which admits a global unique solution

z^{2}e^{zt}\end{pmatrix}.

Using this it is straightforward to solve for

the PMP holds, and we can apply Theorem

to establish existence of path-wise unique solutions.
Ex post

is indeed a true martingale, due to the fact that we have an explicit expression for

and can therefore check square-integrability.

Having found an explicit expression for the moments by solving the above ODE, we can conclude

that the process is a true martingale, due to square integrability.

Theorem A.1

(Existence and Uniqueness of Solutions to non-linear ODEs, see

.

Let us consider the following autonomous non-linear ODE:

du_{t}=F(u_{t})dt\quad u_{0}\in A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

and

which is defined as

if

B.5
Proof of Lemma
3.19

Proof of Lemma
3.19
.

We recall the
L^{2}
-convergence of the Itô integral as formulated e.g. in
[
23
, Section 3.2, particulary Definition 2.9]
. That is, for any square-integrable
(H_{t})_{\geq 0}
there exists a sequence of simple processes
((H^{(n)}_{t})_{\geq 0})_{n\geq 0}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}H^{(n)}_{s}dW_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}H_{s}dW_{s}\right)^{2}\right]=0.

Let us also note the simple fact that for any sequence
X^{(n)}\xrightarrow{L^{2}}X
it also holds that
{\mathbb{E}}[X^{(n)}\lvert\mathcal{M}]\xrightarrow{L^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[X\lvert\mathcal{M}]
for any sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{M}
since

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[({\mathbb{E}}[X^{(n)}\lvert\mathcal{M}]-{\mathbb{E}}[X\lvert\mathcal{M}])^{2}]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[({\mathbb{E}}[X^{(n)}-X\lvert\mathcal{M}])^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{E}}[(X^{(n)}-X)^{2}\lvert\mathcal{M}]]=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[(X^{(n)}-X)^{2}]

by Jensen’s inequality and the tower property.

We now proceed to show the first statement. Clearly, for all
n>0
it holds that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}H^{(n)}_{s}dW_{s}\Big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\right]=0.

To see this, we use the tower property, i.e.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}H^{(n)}_{s}dW_{s}\Big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{\mathbb{E}}[H^{(n)}_{t_{i}}(W_{t\wedge t_{i+1}}-W_{t\wedge t_{i}})\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}[H^{(n)}_{t_{i}}(W_{t\wedge t_{i+1}}-W_{t\wedge t_{i}})\lvert\sigma(W_{t_{i}},W^{0}_{t})]\big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[H^{(n)}_{t_{i}}\underbrace{{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{t\wedge t_{i+1}}-W_{t\wedge t_{i}})\lvert\sigma(W_{t_{i}},W^{0}_{t})]}_{=0}\big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\Big]=0,

where
\sigma(W_{t_{i}},W^{0}_{t})
denotes the sigma algebra generated by
W_{t_{i}},W^{0}_{t}
. Hence,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}H^{(n)}_{s}dW_{s}\Big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\right]\xrightarrow{L^{2}}0

and on the other hand

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}H^{(n)}_{s}dW_{s}\Big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\right]\xrightarrow{L^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}H_{s}dW_{s}\Big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\right]

and it follows
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}H_{s}dW_{s}\big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\right]=0
almost surely.

Similarly, we show the second statement. Note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}H^{(n)}_{s}dW^{0}_{s}\Big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{\mathbb{E}}[H^{(n)}_{t_{i}}(W^{0}_{t\wedge t_{i+1}}-W^{0}_{t\wedge t_{i}})\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{\mathbb{E}}[H^{(n)}_{t_{i}}\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}](W^{0}_{t\wedge t_{i+1}}-W^{0}_{t\wedge t_{i}})=\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}[H^{(n)}_{s}\lvert{\mathcal{W}}^{0}_{t}]dW^{0}_{s}

Moreover, for all
s,t\geq 0
it obviously holds that
{\mathbb{E}}[H^{(n)}_{s}\lvert{\mathcal{W}}^{0}_{t}]\xrightarrow{L^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[H_{s}\lvert{\mathcal{W}}^{0}_{t}]
by assumption that
H^{(n)}\xrightarrow{L^{2}}H
. Hence, by the
L^{2}
-convergence of the Itô integral

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}H^{(n)}_{s}dW^{0}_{s}\Big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\right]=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[H^{(n)}_{s}\big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\right]dW^{0}_{s}\xrightarrow{L^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[H_{s}\big\lvert{\mathcal{W}}_{t}^{0}\right]dW^{0}_{s}.

holds almost surely.

∎
