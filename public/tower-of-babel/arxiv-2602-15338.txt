Discovering Implicit Large Language Model Alignment Objectives
Edward Chen, Sanmi Koyejo, Carlos Guestrin

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) alignment relies on complex reward signals that often obscure the specific behaviors being incentivized, creating critical risks of misalignment and reward hacking. Existing interpretation methods typically rely on pre-defined rubrics, risking the omission of "unknown unknowns", or fail to identify objectives that comprehensively cover and are causal to the model behavior. To address these limitations, we introduce Obj-Disco, a framework that automatically decomposes an alignment reward signal into a sparse, weighted combination of human-interpretable natural language objectives. Our approach utilizes an iterative greedy algorithm to analyze behavioral changes across training checkpoints, identifying and validating candidate objectives that best explain the residual reward signal. Extensive evaluations across diverse tasks, model sizes, and alignment algorithms demonstrate the framework’s robustness. Experiments with popular open-source reward models show that the framework consistently captures > 90% of reward behavior, a finding further corroborated by human evaluation. Additionally, a case study on alignment with an open-source reward model reveals that Obj-Disco can successfully identify latent misaligned incentives that emerge alongside intended behaviors. Our work provides a crucial tool for uncovering the implicit objectives in LLM alignment, paving the way for more transparent and safer AI development.

Introduction

Aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences is a prerequisite for deploying safe and useful artificial intelligence (AI) systems. To scale this process beyond expensive human supervision, developers typically optimize models against complex proxy reward functions, such as learned reward models or LLM-as-a-Judge systems
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Dann et al. 2023
;
Go et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Christian et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025a
;
Liu et al. 2025b
;
Gunjal et al. 2025
)
. While scalable, this abstraction creates a critical challenge: the complexity of these reward functions often renders the objectives embedded within them opaque, making it difficult to discern precisely what behaviors the model is being aligned towards. This ambiguity can create a divergence between the intended goals and the realized behaviors, leading models to adopt misaligned shortcuts such as sycophancy or verbosity
(
Tamkin et al. 2023
;
Singhal et al. 2024
;
Wen et al. 2024
;
Shen et al. 2023
)
. Consequently, developing methods to break down and interpret the implicit objectives driving LLM alignment is essential for ensuring robust and trustworthy deployment.

While developers can attempt to infer implicit alignment objectives by observing the model’s behavioral evolution, relying on manual inspection is insufficient for capturing the full breadth of changes. Standard practice typically involves monitoring behavioral trends based on a predefined set of objectives on a static validation dataset
(
Stiennon et al. 2022
;
Dubois et al. 2024
;
Singhal et al. 2024
)
. However, this paradigm is fundamentally constrained by two distinct coverage limitations. First, the static nature of the datasets inevitably biases the analysis, potentially missing unintended behaviors that only manifest on certain inputs. Second, even assuming comprehensive data oversight, humans face a significant cognitive bottleneck in enumerating objectives to attain coverage. Humans generally lack the capacity to track the subtle distributional shifts required to identify "unknown unknowns" across thousands of outputs
(
Ribeiro & Lundberg 2022
;
Gao et al. 2023
;
Dunlap et al. 2024
;
Dunlap et al. 2025
;
Zhong et al. 2025
;
Williams et al. 2025
)
. Even when a potential objective is identified, validating its semantic meaning requires grounding it in concrete examples; without a curated set of illustrative trajectories, developers may struggle to interpret how an abstract objective like "helpfulness" actually materializes across different contexts. Consequently, manually deriving a full set of valid objectives becomes practically infeasible to scale up
(
Zhong et al. 2022
)
.

To address these scalability challenges, recent research has introduced automated frameworks to help understand model behavior. However, these systems operate primarily as benchmarks constrained by predefined metrics. Unlike discovery-based approaches, the majority of these works are prescriptive and evaluate models using a fixed set of objectives
(
Pan et al. 2023
;
Murthy et al. 2025
;
Yao et al. 2025
;
Ren et al. 2024
;
Ryan et al. 2025
)
. This inherently limits their ability to uncover unanticipated "unknown unknowns." Conversely, descriptive “proposer-validator” frameworks attempt to explain differences between datasets or models from a blank slate
(
Dunlap et al. 2024
;
Zhong et al. 2022
;
Zhong et al. 2025
;
Dunlap et al. 2025
)
. However, they generally operate in static settings, comparing fixed snapshots rather than analyzing the continuous dynamics of the training process
(
Ryan et al. 2025
)
.
Consequently, prior works fail to leverage a rich source of signal regarding what LLMs actually learn during alignment: the trajectory of model weights checkpointed during training. Without this temporal information, these methods struggle to disentangle the model’s pre-existing inductive biases from the specific behaviors actively reinforced by the reward signal.
Our work bridges this gap by leveraging these checkpoints to facilitate higher-fidelity objective discovery without relying on predefined categories.

We introduce Obj-Disco, a framework that automatically decomposes an LLM’s implicit alignment objectives into a set of objectives expressed using natural language, which we refer to as the Discovered Interpretable Reward (DIR)
. Our approach rests on the core assumption that the reward signal driving alignment can be approximated as a function of separate basis functions, or objectives, as also pointed out by Silver et al
(
Silver et al. 2021
)

(
Jang et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Go et al. 2024
;
Sharma et al. 2025
)
. Accordingly, we frame this task as a sparse representation problem and employ an iterative, greedy algorithm inspired by matching pursuit to sequentially identify the objectives that best explain the residual reward signal
(
Mallat & Zhang 1993
)
. To ensure both coverage and validity, each iteration of Obj-Disco consists of a structured Objectives Discovery phase followed by an Objectives Verification phase. During discovery, we address the intractability of the exponential objective space by using a proposer LLM to generate candidates based on response trajectories observed across multiple training checkpoints. By dynamically sampling inputs where the residual error is largest, we explicitly guide the proposer to target unexplained behavioral shifts and select the candidate that minimizes this residual. Subsequently, the verification phase utilizes LLM-as-a-Judge evaluations to screen the selected candidate against formal criteria, ensuring it is both human-interpretable and follows a predictable trend throughout alignment fine-tuning. Finally, to aid developer understanding, Obj-Disco provides
Objective Explanations
, a curated set of sample trajectories selected to maximize both trend salience and semantic diversity, offering concrete evidence for each discovered objective.

We validate Obj-Disco through an extensive experimental suite spanning multiple alignment algorithms, model sizes, and tasks. In controlled settings with known ground-truth objectives, our framework recovers the target signals with high fidelity, consistently modeling over 90% of the true alignment behavior. Experiments on popular open-source reward models further demonstrate that Obj-Disco can approximate complex, implicit reward functions with similar accuracy. To verify the practical utility of these discoveries, we conduct human-subject studies which confirm that our DIRs are significantly more causal to the final policy behavior than those identified by baseline methods. Participants also rated our Objective Explanations as having higher fidelity to the underlying objective signals. Finally, a targeted case study on safety alignment reveals that Obj-Disco consistently successfully surfaces latent misaligned incentives, such as increased permissiveness in discussing illegal acts, which other baselines rarely detect.

Problem Definition

We consider the
a posteriori
setting, where an LLM has already been aligned. Specifically, we assume access to a full sequence of
\mathcal{T}
model checkpoints,
\pi_{\theta_{1}},\dots,\pi_{\theta_{\mathcal{T}}}
, saved chronologically during an alignment process such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Our goal is to analyze this alignment trajectory to discover the set of implicit objectives the model has learned. We make no assumptions about the characteristics of the checkpoint sequence.

We formally define an
objective
as a function
r_{n}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{O}
which maps prompts in space
\mathcal{X}
and responses in
\mathcal{Y}
to a scalar score in
\mathcal{O}
. For simplicity, we assume
\mathcal{O}=[0,1]
. We posit that for an objective to be useful for human understanding, it must meet two key desiderata:

Desiderata 1: An objective is human-interpretable
. An objective
r
is human-interpretable if its scoring function can be closely approximated by the scores a (human) evaluator would assign based on its natural language description. Let
s_{h}(x,y|n)\in\mathcal{O}
denote the score a (human) evaluator assigns to the input-output pair,
x
,
y
, when using the natural language description
n
. An objective
r
is then human-interpretable if:

\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X},y\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|x)}[|r_{n}(x,y)-s_{h}(x,y|n)|]\leq\epsilon_{interp}

(1)

Desiderata 2: An objective follows a predictable trend
. An objective
r
follows a trend if the model’s performance on that objective, measured across the sequence of models, is predictable. Let
V_{n}^{t}(r)
denote the expected score of objective
r
, with natural language description
n
, with respect to model
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
:

V_{n}^{t}(r)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim X,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|x)}[r_{n}(x,y)]

(2)

The objective
r
follows a predictable trend if the sequence of scores
V_{n}^{1}(r),...,V_{n}^{\mathcal{T}}(r)
can be fit with a function from a pre-defined class
\mathcal{F}_{trend}
(e.g. linear function). Formally,

f^{*}=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{trend}}\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{T}-1}\sum_{t=2}^{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{L}(f(t|V_{n}^{1:t-1}(r)),V_{n}^{t}(r))

(3)

where
\mathcal{L}
is a squared error loss function. We claim that the objective
r
follows a predictable trend if the average prediction error is bounded:

\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{T}-1}\sum_{t=2}^{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{L}(f^{*}(t|V_{n}^{1:t-1}(r)),V_{n}^{t}(r))\leq\epsilon_{trend}

(4)

In practice, we operationalize
r_{n}
with an LLM-as-a-Judge setup, detailed in Appendix
A.4

(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
. Overall, we assume there are
k
implicit objectives, where
k
is determined by the user. As a result, we aim to discover the underlying set of objectives
R^{*}=\{r_{n_{1}},...,r_{n_{k}}\}
.

2.1
Objectives Approximation

Given a sequence of models
\pi_{\theta_{1}},\dots,\pi_{\theta_{\mathcal{T}}}
from an alignment process on a target dataset
\mathcal{D}
, our main goal is to uncover the Discovered Interpretable Reward (DIR), a set of interpretable objectives
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
that explains the underlying reward signal
\mathcal{R}^{*}
. We assume this signal to be shaped by the interplay between the training data
\mathcal{D}
, the alignment algorithm, and the final operating point among objectives
(
Wei et al. 2023
;
Lambert & Calandra 2024
;
Guo et al. 2024
)
. We hypothesize that the total reward can be decomposed into a simple function of our discovered objectives, allowing us to quantitatively measure how well
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
approximates
\mathcal{R}^{*}
.

To formalize this, we use the standard RLHF setting with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) as a canonical example
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
. In this setup, the policy
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
is optimized to maximize a reward
r(x,y)
for prompts
x\in\mathcal{D}
:

r(x,y)=r_{\phi}(x,y)-\beta(\log\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)-\log\pi_{ref}(y|x))

(5)

r_{\phi}(x,y)
is associated with a reward model trained on human preferences derived from the domain
\mathcal{D}
, and it is this component that contains the implicit objectives we aim to discover.
Our central hypothesis is that this complex reward model score can be approximated by a simple, low-complexity function
\mathcal{C}
, which we refer to as the
composition function
1
1

1

See Sharma et al.
(
Sharma et al. 2025
)
, Hosking et al.
(
Hosking et al. 2024
)
, and Go et al.
(
Go et al. 2024
)
for evidence of human preferences being predicted with a function of interpretable features.
(e.g., linear regression or gradient boosting), over our discovered set of objectives
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
:

r_{\phi}(x,y)\approx\mathcal{C}(\hat{r}_{n_{1}}(x,y),\dots,\hat{r}_{n_{|\hat{R}|}}(x,y))

(6)

Here,
\mathcal{C}
primarily serves to learn the appropriate scaling and trade-offs (the operating point) between the discovered objectives in
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
. To quantitatively measure how well
\hat{R}
approximates
R^{*}
, we first define the pointwise squared residual as
\mathcal{E}(x,y;\hat{R})=(R^{*}(x,y)-\mathcal{C}(\hat{r}_{n_{1}}(x,y),...,\hat{r}_{n_{|}\hat{R}|}(x,y)))^{2}
, where the dependency on
R^{*}
is implicit. Following the PPO example above,
R^{*}=\{r\}
. We then define Obj-Error as the root-mean-square of these residuals averaged over the alignment trajectory:

\text{Obj-Error }(\hat{R},R^{*})=\left[\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x,y;\hat{R})\right]\right]^{1/2}

(7)

Intuitively, a lower Obj-Error indicates that our discovered objectives provide a better fit for the ground-truth reward signal. The squared error term
\mathcal{E}
penalizes outliers that may point to unexplained aspects of the reward.

As a result, our main goal is to obtain:

\hat{R}=\argmin_{\hat{R}\subset\mathcal{R},|\hat{R}|\leq k}\text{Obj-Error }(\hat{R},R^{*})

(8)

While we use PPO as our example here, Obj-Disco applies to any alignment algorithm with an explicit reward signal, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
. Additional details in Appendix
A.2
.

3
Proposed Method: Obj-Disco

Our method takes inspiration from the Matching Pursuit algorithm
(
Mallat & Zhang 1993
)
, which greedily builds up a sparse approximation of a signal. In our setting, the “signal” is the underlying reward function
R^{*}
that guides the model’s alignment, and our goal is to approximate it with the function
\mathcal{C}
over a small set of
k
interpretable objectives
\hat{R}
(Equation
8
). To solve this optimization problem, we propose an iterative greedy algorithm that, at each iteration
i
, selects the single objective that provides the maximum marginal decrease in our objective error:

\min_{r_{n}\in\mathcal{R}}[\text{Obj-Error }(\hat{R}^{i-1}\cup\{r_{n}\},R^{*})-\text{Obj-Error }(\hat{R}^{i-1},R^{*})]

(9)

where
\hat{R}^{i-1}
is the set of estimated objectives from the previous iteration. Minimizing Equation
9
is equivalent to finding the objective
r_{n}
that best explains the current residuals, which corresponds to solving the following problem:

r_{n}^{*}=\argmin_{r_{n}\in\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x,y;\hat{R}^{i-1}\cup\{r_{n}\})\right]

(10)

Intuitively, this seeks the objective
r_{n}^{*}
that best approximates the unexplained differences between the true and predicted objectives.

Solving the above directly presents two significant challenges. First, a direct search for
r_{n}^{*}
over the discrete text space
\mathcal{R}
is hard. Second, verifying that a candidate objective
r_{n}
is valid requires ensuring it meets our desiderata from Section
2
, namely being human-interpretable and following a predictable trend, which are non-trivial properties to assess. Our algorithm addresses these two challenges with a two-step process at each iteration
i
:

1. Objectives Discovery
.
Searching over the discrete text space
\mathcal{R}
is hard. Section
3.1
addresses this by using a powerful “proposer” LLM to analyze model response trajectories and propose a small, high-quality set of candidate objectives
R^{i}_{\text{cand}}\subset\mathcal{R}
. This process is guided by a principled, sampling-based strategy to select the most informative samples for the LLM to analyze.

2. Objectives Verification
. Verifying the properties of human-interpretability and trend-predictability typically require human input, which is expensive. Section
3.2
instead leverages LLM-as-a-Judge
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
to validate the best candidate from
\mathcal{R}^{i}_{\text{cand}}
against our desiderata. The algorithm concludes once
|\hat{R}^{i}|=k
.

3.1
Step 1: Objectives Discovery

To efficiently search the discrete space
\mathcal{R}
for the next objective, we employ a proposer LLM to hypothesize candidates based on informative response trajectories. We structure this discovery process into three phases: identifying the most informative samples to analyze, using them to generate candidate objectives, and then greedily selecting the optimal candidate.

Informative Samples Identification.

Equation
10
aims to identify the objective
r_{n}
that minimizes the residual error across the entire data distribution. To guide the discovery of such a generalizable objective, we direct the proposer’s attention toward regions of the input space where the current approximation is most deficient. Specifically, we seek a set of inputs that yields the highest
average
residual error. Maximizing the average ensures that the discovered objective addresses systematic behavioral trends unexplained by the current set
\hat{R}^{i-1}
, rather than overfitting to isolated outliers. Given the finite context window of the proposer LLM, we constrain this discovery set
\mathbb{X}_{\text{disc}}
to a fixed cardinality
\nu
. Formally, we seek to solve the following maximization:

\mathbb{X}_{\text{disc}}=\operatorname*{argmax}_{\mathbb{X}\subset\mathcal{X},|\mathbb{X}|=\nu}\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{X},y\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}}[\mathcal{E}(x,y;\hat{R}^{i-1})]

(11)

Finding the exact solution to Equation
11
over the full dataset is computationally prohibitive. We therefore approximate this via a scalable two-stage process. First, we draw a larger random candidate pool
\mathbb{X}_{\text{cand}}\subset\mathcal{X}
of size
N_{\text{cand}}
. This stochastic step avoids the cost of evaluating the entire input space while maintaining exploration coverage. Second, we compute the trajectory-averaged residuals for these candidates and select the top
\nu
instances to form
\mathbb{X}_{\text{disc}}
.

Candidate Objectives Discovery.

Using the identified set of informative samples
\mathbb{X}_{\text{disc}}
, we then prompt the proposer LLM to generate our candidate objectives
\mathcal{R}^{i}_{\text{cand}}
. To have control over the granularity of discovered objectives, we split the set of
\nu
inputs in
\mathbb{X}_{\text{disc}}
into batches of size
b
and provide
b
full response trajectories to the proposer LLM at a time. To encourage the discovery of novel and non-redundant axes, we provide the already-discovered objectives
\hat{R}^{i-1}
as part of the prompt’s context. The proposer LLM is then prompted to describe the primary behavioral trend observed in the samples. The prompt we use is shown in Figure
7
. The union of all unique natural language objectives proposed across the inputs forms our candidate objectives set
\mathcal{R}^{i}_{\text{cand}}
.

Objectives Selection.

Finally, we identify the optimal objective
r_{n}^{i,*}
from the candidate set
\mathcal{R}^{i}_{\text{cand}}
by solving Equation
9
restricted to these proposed candidates. The selected objective is then passed to the
Objectives Verification
stage.

3.2
Step 2: Objectives Verification

We now check the validity of the selected objective
r_{n}^{i,*}
by determining whether it is human-interpretable and follows a predictable trend, as described in Section
2
.

Human-Interpretability Verification.
To ensure a robust and representative validation of human-interpretability, we compute the human-aligned score
s_{h}(x,y|n)
in Equation
1
by aggregating judgments from an ensemble of LLM-as-a-Judge models. This approach mitigates the idiosyncratic biases of any single model and captures a more generalizable consensus
(
Sun et al. 2025
)
. Within the set of evaluator models,
\mathcal{M}_{eval}=\{m_{1},...,m_{\ell}\}
, each evaluator
m
is provided with a standardized scoring template, including the objective description
n
, input
x
, and response
y
, and is instructed to output a numerical score. The final human-aligned score is computed as:

s_{h}(x,y|n)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{eval}|}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{eval}}s_{m}(x,y|n)

The objective is then deemed human-interpretable if Equation
1
is satisfied. Additional details in Appendix
A.3.2
.

Trend-Predictability Verification.
To validate the criterion of trend-predictability, we analyze whether the objective’s expected scores across the model sequence follow a predictable pattern (Equation
4
). We iteratively fit the sequence of scores
\{V_{n}^{1}(r),...,V_{n}^{\mathcal{T}}(r)\}
to a predefined class of trend functions
\mathcal{F}_{\text{trend}}
. Following established literature on parametric learning curves
(
Viering & Loog 2022
)
, we populate
\mathcal{F}_{\text{trend}}
with functions that model common optimization behaviors, specifically: (1) linear growth, (2) logarithmic growth, (3) power law with an asymptote, and (4) exponential saturation. An objective is deemed valid if its trajectory can be fit by any function in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{trend}}
such that the error bound in Equation
4
is satisfied. While we employ these specific forms to capture standard learning behaviors, our framework is agnostic to the choice of
\mathcal{F}_{\text{trend}}
and can accommodate other function classes.

Finally, if both criteria are satisfied, we set
\hat{R}^{i}=\hat{R}^{i-1}\cup\{r_{n}^{i,*}\}
. If any of the criteria are not met, we proceed with
\hat{R}^{i}=\hat{R}^{i-1}
and go to the next iteration. This ensures that only objectives that exhibit human-interpretability and trend-predictability are included in our final set of discovered objectives. Additional details in Appendix
A.3
.

3.3
Exemplar-Based Objective Explanations

To help users understand the behavior of a discovered objective
r_{n}
, Obj-Disco presents explanations in the form of a small set of input-response trajectory samples illustrative of that objective, which we refer to as Objective Explanations (or OEs). As manual inspection of the entire dataset would be cognitively prohibitive, our goal is to select a sparse subset of points that maintains as much explanatory utility as possible. We achieve this by leveraging the notion of
diminishing returns
: the marginal utility of adding a sample decreases if similar samples are already represented in the set. This notion is formally encapsulated by the property of
submodularity
, which allows us to design optimization algorithms with strong theoretical guarantees
(
Nemhauser et al. 1978
)
(Appendix
A.3.3
).

We construct the OE set
E
based on two desiderata. First,
Trend Fidelity
: the selected samples must closely follow the global behavioral trend identified for
r_{n}
, ensuring the alignment effect is visually consistent. Second,
Diversity
: the samples must cover distinct semantic clusters of the input space (e.g., coding, creative writing) to demonstrate that the objective is a general behavioral principle rather than an artifact of a specific domain.

Formally, we define a sample trajectory
\xi\in\Xi
as the tuple containing an input prompt
x
and the sequence of model responses across the
\mathcal{T}
checkpoints:
\xi=(x,y_{\pi_{\theta_{1}}},\dots,y_{\pi_{\theta_{\mathcal{T}}}})
. Given the set of all available trajectories
\Xi
, we aim to select a subset
E\subset\Xi
subject to a cardinality constraint
|E|\leq\kappa
, where
\kappa
is a small integer (e.g., 5) to ensure the OE remains manageable for human inspection.

Trend Fidelity
: We quantify how well a specific sample trajectory
\xi
exemplifies the global trend
f^{*}(t)
(defined in Equation
3
) by adapting Equation
4
and calculating the squared L2-error between the sample’s objective scores
u_{t}=r_{n}(\xi_{x},\xi_{y_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}})
and the global trend values. We convert this error into a similarity score using the following:

\text{fid}(\xi)=\exp\left(-\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}(u_{t}-f^{*}(t))^{2}\right)

(12)

Following this, we define the total fidelity of the OE as a modular function:
f_{\text{fid}}(E)=\sum_{\xi\in E}\text{fid}(\xi)
.

Diversity
: To ensure semantic coverage, we first partition the input space
\Xi
into
m
semantic clusters
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\}
(e.g., via K-Means on prompt embeddings). We employ a diversity function explicitly designed to capture diminishing returns. Instead of simply counting samples, we sum the square roots of the counts of selected items per partition. Because the square root function is concave, the marginal gain of adding a sample from a specific cluster decreases as more samples from that cluster are added. This formulation is known to be monotone submodular
(
Prasad et al. 2014
;
Tschiatschek et al. 2014
)
:

f_{\text{div}}(E)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sqrt{|E\cap P_{j}|}

(13)

The final objective is a convex combination parameterized by
\lambda\in[0,1]
:
F(E)=(1-\lambda)f_{\text{fid}}(E)+\lambda f_{\text{div}}(E)
. Since the property of submodularity is closed under convex combinations, the total objective
F(E)
is monotone submodular. Consequently, maximizing
F(E)
under a cardinality constraint can be solved using the standard greedy algorithm (Algorithm
1
) with a theoretical guarantee of achieving at least
(1-1/e)
of the optimal solution
(
Nemhauser et al. 1978
)
. Additional details in Appendix
A.3.3
.

4
Experimental Setup

We comprehensively validate the efficacy of Obj-Disco by first assessing reconstruction fidelity with a controlled synthetic environment where ground-truth objectives are explicitly known. Second, we demonstrate real-world applicability by decomposing the implicit signals of popular open-source reward models, including DeBERTaV3 and the Skywork Reward family, across varied policy models and alignment algorithms. To rigorously verify our findings, we conduct two user studies evaluating the causal impact of our discovered rewards and the fidelity of the generated Objective Explanations. Finally, we perform extensive ablations to isolate the contributions of key components of our algorithm.

4.1
Setup Details

We evaluate on two open-source LLMs of different families: Llama-3.1-8B
(
meta llama
)
and Qwen3-4B
(
Team 2025
)
. We perform alignment using two different RLHF algorithms:
PPO

(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
and
GRPO

(
Shao et al. 2024
)
.

Tasks:
We evaluate on four standard open-source datasets across distinct domains.
Summarization:

Baseline Methods
.
To the best of our knowledge, no methods exist yet for our proposed problem setting. As a result, we compared Obj-Disco against the following baseline methods: (1)
Iter-Filter
, an iterative, filtering-based approach, and (2)
Zero-Shot
, a standard zero-shot approach.
Iter-Filter
is inspired by the algorithm from VibeCheck
(
Dunlap et al. 2025
)
and IterAlign
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
. We generate a set of objectives at once, filter out the ones which do not satisfy the two criteria, and then iteratively do that until we obtain
k
objectives.
Zero-Shot
serves to represent a simple discovery-based baseline; obtain a random sample and hypothesize a set of
k
objectives based on their trajectories. More details about the baselines in Appendix
A.4
.

4.2
Evaluation Of Discovered Objectives

We aim to evaluate the functional utility of the discovered objectives
\hat{R}
for the downstream task of alignment. Specifically, if
\hat{R}
accurately captures the implicit incentives of the ground-truth reward
R^{*}
, a policy optimized against
\hat{R}
should converge to a similar behavioral profile as one trained on
R^{*}
. We operationalize this with the
Model-Fit
metric:

\text{Model-Fit}(\hat{R},t)=\dfrac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X},y\sim\pi_{\hat{\theta}_{t}|\hat{R}}}[R^{*}(x,y)]}{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X},y\sim\pi_{\theta_{T}|R^{*}}}[R^{*}(x,y)]}

(14)

where
\pi_{\hat{\theta}_{t}|\hat{R}}
is the policy model aligned with our DIR
\hat{R}
and
\pi_{\theta_{T}|R^{*}}
is the policy model aligned with the true reward
R^{*}
. A Model-Fit score approaching 1.0 indicates that
\hat{R}
successfully replicates the alignment signal of
R^{*}
. We note that
Model-Fit
may sometimes exceed the value of 1 due to variance or the magnitude of the discovered objectives. We decided not to penalize this since it suggests that
\hat{R}
is a strong proxy for
R^{*}
, potentially more effective at exhibiting the ground-truth reward signal.

5.4

Related Works

LLM Evaluation.

More recent research is specifically auditing the
alignment
of LLMs. Some works audit for value alignment by analyzing model responses on interactive benchmarks and specialized datasets
(
Ye et al. 2025
;
Huang et al. 2025
;
Murthy et al. 2025
;
Hendrycks et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2025
;
Ren et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2025a
;
Pan et al. 2023
)
. In a study on the feasibility of auditing, Marks et al. first induced a hidden objective into a model and then tasked separate teams with discovering it using interpretability and prompting tools
(
Marks et al. 2025
)
. While insightful, these approaches typically rely on evaluating models against a pre-defined set of values or find a single, known hidden objective. Our work does not make such assumptions as developers often cannot anticipate the full range of behaviors that may emerge during alignment.

Understanding LLM Misalignment.

A significant body of research focuses on understanding misalignment
(
Betley et al. 2025
;
Entezami & Naseh 2025
;
Liang et al. 2023
)
. Many studies have cataloged such unintended consequences, such as producing overly long responses
(
Singhal et al. 2024
)
, reducing output diversity
(
Kirk et al. 2024
)
, degrading refusal behavior
(
Song et al. 2025
)
, and misleading humans or giving malicious advice
(
Wen et al. 2024
;
Betley et al. 2025
;
Ghandeharioun et al. 2024
;
Tamkin et al. 2023
;
MacDiarmid et al. 2025
)
. Other works have proposed to mitigate certain misalignment through techniques like causal reward modeling
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
, balanced preference datasets
(
Singhal et al. 2024
)
, or decoupling reward from confounding variables
(
Shen et al. 2023
)
. Our work instead focuses on discovery from scratch rather than mitigation or documentation of a bias.

Reward Model Interpretability.

Jiang et al. leverage controlled perturbations to interpret RMs and propose to generate contrastive explanations by modifying responses along a predefined set of attributes (e.g., helpfulness, verbosity) and observing the impact on the RM’s preference
(
Jiang et al. 2025b
)
. Christian et al. perform an exhaustive search over the RM’s vocabulary, identifying tokens that are considered most "optimal" and "pessimal" to reveal any inherent biases
(
Christian et al. 2025
)
. Others have focused on decomposing a scalar reward into a multi-objective vector, making the trade-offs more explicit
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025
)
. Finally, Marks et al. applied sparse autoencoders to interpret implicit RMs by analyzing features obtained from the activations between the base and fine-tuned model
(
Marks et al. 2023
)
. Instead of analyzing predefined, potentially correlated attributes, we discover objectives from scratch and aim to isolate out the directional nature of alignment and maximize coverage.

A.1
.

7
Conclusion

We introduced Obj-Disco, a framework which decomposes an opaque alignment reward signal into a set of interpretable natural language objectives. To do so, we employ an iterative greedy algorithm that analyzes the model’s behavioral trajectory, sequentially identifying and verifying the objectives that best explain the residual reward signal. Our extensive empirical evaluations confirm the method’s efficacy: in controlled settings with known objectives and with popular open-source reward models, Obj-Disco consistently recovers over 90% of the reward behavior, a finding strongly corroborated by human user studies and a case study identifying latent misalignment. Primary limitations include computational cost and reliance on LLM-based judges, which may introduce inherent biases
(
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Shi et al. 2025
)
(limitations in Appendix
A.7
). We believe Obj-Disco to be a step toward scalable, yet transparent, AI alignment. Future work may include extending this framework into an online and real-time setting.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work which may result from better understanding the behavior of the policy model after RL fine-tuning. We hope that our work may pave the path for safer and more transparent AI deployments by making more transparent any potential AI misalignment, biases, or other harmful behaviors.

A.1
Additional Related Works

LLM Evaluation.

Evaluating the capabilities and behaviors of large language models (LLMs) has been a major focus of the natural language processing (NLP) community. Early efforts sought to improve upon classic metrics like BLEU
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
and ROUGE
(
Lin 2004
)
by developing more nuanced criteria for open-domain generation, such as fluency and conciseness
(
Pang et al. 2020
;
Sellam et al. 2020
;
Banerjee & Lavie 2005
)
. This has evolved into comprehensive benchmarking suites that measure model performance across a diverse set of pre-defined tasks and metrics
(
Liang et al. 2023
;
Srivastava et al. 2023
;
Kiela et al. 2021
)
. To assess qualities like helpfulness and clarity without a ground-truth, others have leveraged LLMs themselves as scalable judges of model outputs
(
Mehri & Eskenazi 2020a
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Mehri & Eskenazi 2020b
)
.

Automated Data Analysis.

Automating the discovery of patterns in unstructured data is a long-standing goal, with classical methods like topic models aiming to extract topical variations from documents
(
Blei 2012
)
. More recently, a prominent line of work has leveraged LLMs to automatically propose and validate interpretable, natural language descriptions of differences between two static sets of data, such as texts or images
(
Zhong et al. 2022
;
Dunlap et al. 2024
;
Zhong et al. 2023
;
Zhong et al. 2025
;
Torii et al. 2024
;
Byun et al. 2023
;
Kahng et al. 2024
)
. This includes systems like VibeCheck, which discovers qualitative differences to explain pairwise model preferences
(
Dunlap et al. 2025
)
. More recently, StringSight is a tool using similar approaches to automate our understanding of agentic AI systems by analyzing their outputs
(
Dunlap 2025
)
. Report Cards is another work proposing to use LLMs to automatically generate human-interpretable, natural language summaries of language models, in the form of report cards
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
. Ryan et al. focuses on the related task of interpreting human feedback with a set of correlated metrics
(
Ryan et al. 2025
)
but assumes a predefined initial set of metrics and a static feedback dataset. Our work is inspired by this paradigm of automated, language-based data analysis but makes several crucial departures. First, rather than comparing two (or more) distinct models or static datasets, we analyze the
temporal evolution
of a single model across its alignment trajectory. Second, our method does not rely on pairwise preference data or an initial set of predefined metrics as required by VibeCheck and AutoMetrics
(
Dunlap et al. 2025
;
Ryan et al. 2025
)
; instead, it operates directly on the underlying reward signal. Finally, our framework generalizes beyond discovering binary predicates (e.g.
(
Zhong et al. 2025
)
) by aiming to identify any valid objective that exhibits a predictable trend, and we introduce a novel, principled sampling strategy based on alignment residuals to guide the discovery process.

Interpretable LLM Alignment Frameworks.

While many alignment methods treat an LLM’s values as an implicit byproduct of training data, a distinct paradigm focuses on codifying desired behaviors into explicit, interpretable documents
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
. These approaches, often called “Model Specifications” or “Constitutions”, provide a transparent foundation for alignment. Prominent examples include OpenAI’s Model Spec
(
OpenAI 2024
)
, Anthropic’s Constitutional AI (CAI)
(
Bai et al. 2022
)
, and DeepMind’s Sparrow, which used a set of 23 natural-language rules
(
Glaese et al. 2022
)
. This concept has been extended to democratically-sourced constitutions
(
Huang et al. 2024
)
and multi-objective alignment techniques that integrate a transparent set of goals into the model
(
Go et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. Some work, like IterAlign
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
, even aims to automatically discover a constitution from model behavior to improve alignment. Our work complements these prescriptive approaches; rather than defining a constitution for the model to follow, we aim to discover the implicit objectives a model has already learned from its alignment process.

Mechanistic Interpretability.
Mechanistic interpretability is a bottom-up approach to interpretability which aims to study the fundamental components of models through fine-grained analysis of layers, neurons, and connections. The goal is often to uncover causal relationships transforming inputs to outputs, essential for fostering trust and transparency. Elhage et al. developed a mathematical framework to analyze up to a two layer attention-only transformer
(
Elhage et al. 2021
)
. Foote et al. and Bills et al. proposed to leverage a larger language model to predict what a neuron in the smaller model represents
(
Foote et al. 2023
;
Bills et al. 2023
)
. Rather than study neurons as the fundamental unit, Black et al. proposed to study polytopes as the fundamental building block instead
(
Black et al. 2022
)
. Bereska & Gavves provide a comprehensive survey on mechanistic interpretability approaches for AI safety
(
Bereska & Gavves 2024
)
. Rather than study the internal mechanisms of the language models, our proposed method directly uses the model output behavioral changes to discover and quantify a set of interpretable objectives.

Exemplar-Based Explanations.

One paradigm for enhancing the transparency of complex machine learning models is the use of exemplar-based explanations, which ground model decisions in specific, representative data instances rather than abstract feature scores
(
Wei et al. 2025
;
Hoang-Xuan et al. 2024
)
. Concretely, prototype-based methods, originally developed for computer vision, aim to improve explainability by comparing inputs with representative examples rather than using abstract feature weights
(
Chen et al. 2019
)
. For LLMs, Wei et al. developed a prototype-based model to explain the predictions of an LLM in a post-hoc manner
(
Wei et al. 2025
)
. Purohit et al. investigated into exemplar selection methods for improving LLM reasoning
(
Purohit et al. 2024
)
. As users often desire for the sets of exemplars to be manageable in size, other works have applied submodular optimization to select such subsets, leveraging the property of diminishing returns to balance representativeness and semantic diversity
(
Tschiatschek et al. 2014
;
Sinha et al. 2011
)
. Our work extends this paradigm by applying it to the temporal dimension of LLM alignment, using trajectory-based exemplars to explain the directional behavioral shifts actively reinforced during the alignment process.

A.2
Problem Definition

If the output scores for the rewards are normalized to be in [0, 1], as detailed in Section
2
, then the square root in Equation
7
acts to push higher (or make worse) the Obj-Error scores for a worse set of
\hat{R}
.

In practice, to ensure a rigorous evaluation and to avoid overfitting, we calculate Obj-Error using a chronological train-test split across the model checkpoints. We designate the first set of models, from checkpoint 1 to
\mathcal{T}_{\text{split}}
, as the training set, and the subsequent models as the test set. The function
\mathcal{C}
is fit exclusively on the training set; we learn its parameters by regressing the scores of our discovered objectives against the ground-truth reward scores for the early-stage model responses. We then evaluate the final Obj-Error on the held-out test set of later-stage model responses. This chronological split ensures we are measuring how well the objective relationships learned early in alignment generalize to the model’s behavior trajectory. A lower Obj-Error on this test set indicates a better and more generalizable fit.

When linear regression is used as the composition function
\mathcal{C}
, we enforce positive coefficients as our discovery method assumes increasing trends across the checkpoints. Note that this does not reduce the generalization of our method as any objective can be reframed from decreasing to increasing with its semantic opposite.

A.3.2
Objectives Verification

Trend-Predictability Verification.

To operationalize the trend-predictability criterion, we define
\mathcal{F}_{\text{trend}}
as a library of parametric functions widely established in the learning curve literature for modeling performance trajectories
(
Viering & Loog 2022
)
. We specifically include models that capture distinct optimization dynamics, ranging from heavy-tailed improvement to rapid saturation:

1.

Linear Growth:

f(t)=at+b
. Represents strictly increasing, constant-rate improvement.

2.

Logarithmic Growth:

f(t)=a\ln(t)+b
. Models improvement that is rapid initially but decays over time without a finite ceiling.

3.

Power Law with Asymptote:

f(t)=c-at^{-b}
. Frequently observed in LLM scaling laws, this models "heavy tail" learning that approaches an asymptotic limit
c
as
t\to\infty
.

4.

Exponential Saturation:

f(t)=S+g(1-e^{-k(t-1)})
. Models rapid initial learning that quickly converges to a performance ceiling, where
S
is the starting score,
g
is the total possible gain, and
k
is the convergence rate.

We emphasize that while we employ these specific forms to capture standard learning behaviors, Obj-Disco is theoretically agnostic to the contents of
\mathcal{F}_{\text{trend}}
and can accommodate any user-defined class of trend functions.

A.3.3
Exemplar-Based Objective Explanations

The construction of Objective Explanations (OEs) for each discovered objective involves selecting a small, representative set of trajectory samples that maximize both trend fidelity and semantic diversity. To assist with computational tractability and financial cost, we first randomly sample 100 prompts from the dataset. Algorithm
1
then operates over those samples. We set the number of examples to
\kappa=8
to ensure the summary remains manageable for human inspection while providing sufficient coverage. To balance the two desiderata, we set the fidelity-diversity trade-off parameter
\lambda=0.5
in our objective function. For diversity calculations, we partition the input space into
m=8
semantic clusters using K-Means on embeddings generated by OpenAI
text-embedding-3-small
, where the input text for each embedding is formed by concatenating the prompt with its corresponding sequence of model responses.

Definition A.1

(Submodular)
.

F
is submodular if and only if for all
A\subseteq B\subseteq V
and
s\in V\text{\textbackslash}B
it holds that
F(A\cup\{s\})-F(A)\geq F(B\cup\{s\})-F(B)

Theorem A.2
.

(
Nemhauser et al. 1978
)
In the case of any normalized, monotonic submodular function
F
, the set
E_{G}
obtained by the greedy algorithm achieves at least a constant fraction
(1-\dfrac{1}{e})
of the objective value obtained by the optimal solution, that is,

F(E_{G})\geq(1-\dfrac{1}{e})\max_{|E|\leq\kappa}F(E)

Algorithm 1

Greedy Sample Selection for Objective Explanations

Input:
Trajectory set
\Xi
, partitions
\mathcal{P}
, size
\kappa
, weight
\lambda

Initialize:

E\leftarrow\emptyset

while

|E|<\kappa

do

For all
\xi\in\Xi\setminus E
, calculate marginal gain:

\Delta(\xi)=F(E\cup\{\xi\})-F(E)

Select best candidate:

\xi^{*}\leftarrow\arg\max_{\xi\in\Xi\setminus E}\Delta(\xi)

Update set:
E\leftarrow E\cup\{\xi^{*}\}

end

while

Return

E

Experimental Setup

Scoring and Evaluation

For all experiments, unless otherwise noted, we utilize
gpt-4o-mini
as the LLM-as-a-Judge for evaluating objective scores (
r_{n}
). To ensure robust and calibrated scoring, we employ a dynamic prompting strategy. For each objective to be scored, the system first retrieves a domain-specific scoring rubric (e.g., specific criteria for "Helpfulness" in coding tasks vs. general dialogue) and a set of few-shot calibration examples (Low, Medium, and High scoring responses). If a predefined rubric or example set does not exist for a newly discovered objective, an auxiliary LLM call is made to generate them dynamically before scoring proceeds. The prompt templates used for scoring, rubric generation, and calibration examples generation are provided in Figures
8
,
10
, and
9
, respectively. The prompt template used for objective discovery is provided in Figure
7
.

To compute the human-interpretability verification (Section
3.2
), we use
gpt-4o-mini
and
gpt-4.1-nano
for
\mathcal{M}_{eval}
and use 50 random samples. To compute trend-predictability, we used
gpt-4o-mini
for scoring and 50 random samples for Equation
2
.

We compute the Model-Fit metric (Section
4.2
) by randomly sampling two sets of 100 input samples from the test set of each of the tasks. Each model checkpoint is then passed through both sets of samples to compute the mean and standard error statistics.

Obj-Disco Parameters

Unless explicitly specified (e.g. for the ablations), we use the following parameters for Obj-Disco:

•

Candidate Generation
: We sample
N_{\text{cand}}=100
candidate prompts and select the top
\nu=25
most informative samples (based on residual error) for the discovery set
\mathbb{X}_{\text{disc}}
.

•

Proposer Configuration
: We use GPT-4o-mini as the proposer model. For each batch of 3 trajectories, we generate 2 candidate objectives.

•

Combination Function
: We use linear regression to model the composition function
\mathcal{C}
, fitting the weights of the discovered objectives to the ground-truth reward signal.

•

Verification Thresholds
: We set the threshold for interpretability
\epsilon_{\text{interp}}=0.15
and for trend predictability
\epsilon_{\text{trend}}=0.12

To calculate Obj-Error, we set
\mathcal{T}_{\text{split}}
to
\mathcal{T}-1
, meaning the final checkpoint is reserved as the held-out test set for evaluation. The specific checkpoint indices vary by experiment type to accommodate different convergence rates. For synthetic PPO experiments, we sample checkpoints at steps
[0,25,50,75,100]
. For PPO experiments using real reward models, we sample at
[0,100,200,300]

[0,75,150,225]
due to dataset constraints. For synthetic GRPO experiments, we use checkpoints
[0,100,200,300]
, while GRPO experiments with open-source reward models are sampled at
[0,125,250,375,500]

The weights for all discovered objectives from all compared methods are calculated from a final calculation of Obj-Error with 100 randomly sampled inputs at the end of the method.

Baseline Comparisons

Iter-Filter
Details.
Although VibeCheck
(
Dunlap et al. 2024
)
was designed for comparing two singular checkpoints of different model families, we took inspiration from it and used it as a baseline comparison. To initiate
Iter-Filter
, a random set of
\nu
inputs are sampled to form
\mathbb{X}_{\text{disc}}
. As in VibeCheck (and Obj-Disco), we split the set of
\nu
inputs in
\mathbb{X}_{\text{disc}}
into batches of size
b
and provide
b
full response trajectories to the proposer LLM at a time. The prompt we use for discovery is the same as the one used for Obj-Disco. We then randomly select objectives from the candidate set of objectives and check for human-interpretability and trend-predictability. The objectives which fail any of the criteria are filtered out of the set. If we end up with
k
objectives after the filtering, then
Iter-Filter
concludes. Otherwise, we move on to the next iteration of
Iter-Filter
but instead focus discovery in the input samples in
\mathbb{X}_{\text{disc}}
with the highest residuals (as also similarly done in VibeCheck).

Zero-Shot
Details.
The discovery prompt for
Zero-Shot
is instructed to output exactly
k
objectives. Otherwise, the discovery prompt used is the same one as in Obj-Disco.

We ran 3 trials for each of our experiments. To ensure fair comparisons with baseline methods (
Iter-Filter
and
Zero-Shot
), we maintained consistent compute budgets and model configurations:

•

Iter-Filter
: We sample 25 random prompts per iteration (matching the size of
\mathbb{X}_{\text{disc}}
) and use the same verification thresholds (
\epsilon=0.15/0.12
). 2 objectives are generated for each response trajectory in the set.

•

Zero-Shot
: This zero-shot approach uses a sample of 25 prompts (matching the size of
\mathbb{X}_{\text{disc}}
) to propose objectives.

\alpha

\alpha=3

\alpha=15

A.6
Additional Discussion

A.6.1
How To Decide The Number Of Objectives?

In practical settings, the optimal number of objectives
k
is often unknown a priori. We recommend treating
k
as a tunable parameter determined through an "elbow method" approach, similar to selecting the number of components in Principal Component Analysis. Specifically, developers should run Obj-Disco iteratively, monitoring the marginal decrease in Obj-Error on the validation set at each step. We suggest stopping the discovery process when the marginal improvement in the reconstruction error falls below a predefined threshold or when the newly discovered objective is semantically redundant with the existing set. This data-driven approach ensures that the discovered set
\hat{R}
is sparse yet sufficiently expressive to capture the core alignment dynamics.

What Happens If We Discover More Objectives?

We show qualitative results of this scenario in Figure
34
. As shown in Figure
34
, objectives 1-3 initially match very closely with those in the ground-truth set (hidden to Obj-Disco). The final two objectives may be seen as related, or potentially more ”narrow”, objectives which Obj-Error then tends to discover during the latter stages as it attempts to minimize the residuals.

A.7
Limitations

Our work presents a promising approach, but it has several practical limitations. First, Obj-Disco is computationally intensive, incurring some time and financial costs (due to the LLM-as-a-Judge setup) to discover the set of objectives
\hat{R}
. In order for Obj-Disco to be feasible, our proposed method requires relatively inexpensive models, such as GPT-4o-mini to serve as the proposer and scoring models. However, such judges are also subject to inaccuracies in judgment and potential biases in their outputs, potentially skewing the results
(
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Shi et al. 2025
)
. Furthermore, the generative nature of our objective proposer introduces stochasticity. Different runs may therefore produce varied sets of objectives, a challenge common to automated discovery methods that makes exact replication difficult
(
Dunlap et al. 2025
)
. Finally, the quality of the discovered objectives may be sensitive to hyperparameter selection. For instance, the thresholds for our human-interpretability and trend-predictability criteria,
\epsilon_{\text{interp}}
and
\epsilon_{\text{trend}}
, may require careful tuning to ensure stable and meaningful results.
