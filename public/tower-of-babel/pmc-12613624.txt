Limb strength asymmetries in elite academy volleyball players: impacts on performance outcomes and insights derived from principal component analysis
Authors: Hakkı Yeşilyurt, Salih Çabuk, İzzet İnce, Erkan Tortu, Süleyman Ulupınar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this study is twofold: (1) to investigate intra- and inter-limb strength imbalances and asymmetries in elite academy volleyball players and their relationships with sprint performance, kinetics, kinematics, countermovement jump (CMJ), squat jump (SJ), and agility performances and (2) to apply principle component analysis (PCA) to identify and better understand the underlying factors that contributing to volleyball performance.MethodsThirty-seven elite academy volleyball players completed four testing sessions, assessing pro-agility, sprint, isokinetic strength at 60°s-1 and 180°s-1, and jump performance. Intra-limb asymmetries were calculated using hamstring-to-quadriceps ratios, whereas inter-limb asymmetries were determined using the percentage difference method.ResultsAt 60°s-1, the intra-limbNDL ratio showed negative correlations with dominant and non-dominant leg ground contact time (GCTDL and GCTNDL, respectively) and a positive correlation with dominant leg flight time (FTDL) and difference between CMJ and SJ heights (CMJSJdiff). Hamstring asymmetry (Hasymmetry) at 60°s-1 correlated positively with 5 m (S5m) and 20 m (S20m) sprint performances and negatively with theoretical maximal velocity (V0) while at 180°s-1, it showed small negative correlations with V0. At 180°s-1, quadriceps asymmetry (Qasymmetry) correlated positively with S5m, relative theoretical maximum force (F0rel), and relative theoretical maximum power (Pmaxrel) but negatively with V0, CMJDL, and CMJ. The PCA results showed that the first four principal components, explaining 51.0% of the total variance and contributing to 88.2% across 12 dimensions, were mainly linked to jump performance, lower-limb strength, sprint performance, and intra-limb ratios.ConclusionsIntra-limb and inter-limb strength imbalances and asymmetries were found to be weakly to moderately associated with sprint performance, sprint kinetics, kinematics, and jump-related outcomes. PCA results further identified jump variables, lower-limb strength, sprint metrics, and intra-limb ratios as key, interconnected contributors to volleyball performance.Keywords: Strength imbalances and asymmetry, Sprint kinetics, Horizontal force-velocity profile, Volleyball, Principle component analysis

Introduction

Vertical jump, quick acceleration, and change of direction (CoD) maneuvers are fundamental performance components in volleyball, a sport characterized by high-intensity, dynamic movements such as jumping, sprinting, and changing direction [1–3]. As a player’s jump height increases, their potential for successful performance in offensive and defensive actions also improves proportionally [4]. The structural characteristics of the volleyball court emphasize the necessity of very short-distance sprints, as well as quick and agile movements, for successful defensive actions [2]. Therefore, the performance of a volleyball player is directly related to their ability to generate power during jumping and sprinting actions [2, 5–7]. Such movements can provide a basis for the development of inter and intra-limb asymmetries due to dominant limb (DL) usage (e.g., the preferred kicking or striking leg) [8, 9]. Inter-limb asymmetry is defined as an imbalance or deficit between limbs, such as between the left and right or between dominant and non-dominant limbs (NDLs), whereas intra-limb asymmetry is defined as an imbalance within the same limb, such as the quadriceps-hamstring ratio [10–12]. Between-limb differences in power and strength are purported as significant risk factors for sport injuries [13, 14] and, at times, associated with decrements in athletic performance [13, 15]. In any task, the weaker limb’s reduced capacity to both produce and absorb force can increase injury risk, as it may exceed its “tolerance capacity” sooner than the stronger limb during repeated high-intensity actions [13, 16, 17]. In this context, Maloney et al., (2019) highlighted that the weaker limb offers a broader “window of opportunity” for capacity improvement and emphasized that reducing asymmetries may simply be a byproduct of targeted training interventions aimed at addressing deficiencies in the weaker limb [18]. The relationship between intra and inter-limb asymmetries and sports performance in volleyball players has been investigated in recent studies. Schons et al. (2019) reported that the inter‑limb difference in knee‑extensor peak torque between the DL and NDL at 60°s-1 showed a moderate, but non‑significant negative correlation with countermovement jump (CMJ) (r= -0.53). The same study further noted that, at 300°·s-1, the within‑limb balance of knee‑flexor to knee‑extensor peak torque (the conventional hamstring‑to‑quadriceps ratio) showed a moderate inverse correlation with CMJ [6]. In the another study, no significant correlation between the hamstring/quadriceps (H:Q) ratio or bilateral muscle asymmetry at both higher (180°s-1) and lower (60°s-1) angular velocity and vertical jump height or power (p > 0.05) [19]. On the other hand, recent studies have demonstrated significant negative relationships between dominant and non-dominant H:Q ratios (60 and 180 °s-1) and CMJ height in volleyball players [20]. Similarly, significant associations have been identified between the H:Q ratio and performance variables in CMJ (30°s-1, 60°s-1, 90°s-1, and 120°s-1), 15 (60°s-1, 90°s-1, and 120°s-1) and 30 (120°s-1) cm drop jump at specific angular velocities in female football players [21]. Studies investigating asymmetries have generally focused on different tasks or muscle groups [22]. Specifically, in volleyball players, inter- and intra-limb asymmetries have typically been examined in the context of unilateral and/or bilateral strength asymmetries [23, 24]. However, in recent years, there has been an increase in the use of “percentage difference” method for calculating asymmetry through isokinetic strength imbalances [22]. Although the relationship between inter- and intra-limb asymmetries with sprint and CoD performance has been investigated in sports such as tennis [25], soccer [26, 27], and hockey [28], to the best of our knowledge, no study has examined this relationship in elite academy volleyball players. A previous systematic review and meta-analysis study concluded that inter-limb asymmetries have a negative impact on sprint and CoD performance [29]. However, studies examining sprint performance have typically focused solely on the performance itself, without evaluating the underlying kinetic and kinematic characteristics [30]. Therefore, the existing literature highlights the need for more research on strength imbalances and limb asymmetries in elite academy volleyball players. Unilateral movement patterns, such as sprint, jumping and changing direction, are key contributors to the development of inter- and intra-limb asymmetries, which may impact performance outcomes. However, there is limited understanding of how these asymmetries affect performance, particularly in younger players with less exposure to the sport’s demands. Investigating these imbalances could provide valuable insights into optimizing training programs, improving performance profiles, and reducing injury risks in elite volleyball players. In addition to that, performance indicator (PI) research is crucial for empirically measuring sports performance and has been widely conducted in sports like soccer, rugby union, and basketball [31]. However, no comprehensive PI research exists for volleyball, revealing a significant gap in the literature. Parmar et al. (2018) highlighted the usefulness of dimension reduction techniques, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), for analyzing large datasets with interrelated variables. PCA simplifies data by grouping related variables into components, enabling researchers to uncover key factors driving athletic success. For volleyball players, where performance depends on multiple interconnected variables, PCA can identify impactful contributors, such as CMJ, arm swing CMJ (CMJAS), and squat jump (SJ), to improve success rates. This method provides coaches with actionable information to optimize training and strategies, allowing for more informed management of load distribution and athlete monitoring processes. In this way, individual strengths and weaknesses can be identified, and performance-specific intervention strategies can be developed [31, 32]. Hence, the purpose of this study is twofold: [1] to examine intra- and inter-limb strength imbalances and asymmetries in elite academy volleyball players and their relationships with sprint performance, sprint kinetics, kinematics, jump, and agility performances and [2] to apply PCA to identify and better understand the underlying factors that contribute to volleyball performance success.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

During the data collection phase, participants, who were elite academy volleyball players, were randomly assigned to three groups to ensure balanced distribution. Each group randomly participated in four distinct testing sessions. 48-hour rest period was provided before and after each test session. Before the first test session, all participants’ body composition and anthropometric measurements were conducted using standardized equipment and protocols. The first session included pro-agility performance using standardized protocols. The second test session focused on sprint performance, kinetics, and kinematics. The third test session involved isokinetic strength assessment of the quadriceps and hamstring muscles at angular velocities of 60°s-1 and 180°s-1, performed using an isokinetic dynamometer under the supervision of a physiotherapist. The fourth test session was dedicated to jump performance assessments. Before each test, except the isokinetic strength assessment, participants completed a standardized warm-up session, which included traditional warm-up exercises and stretching routines targeting both the lower and upper extremities (Fig. 1). Although participants were experienced in such tests due to their background as elite volleyball players, all procedures were thoroughly explained by the lead investigator, who also provided detailed demonstrations before the tests. All players maintained their regular training routines throughout the testing period. To ensure consistency and account for circadian rhythm effects, all sessions were conducted between 14:00 and 16:00.

Research design

Participants

The G-Power analysis conducted to determine the required sample size for the study was based on the following parameters: correlation (p H1) = 0.50, 1-β = 0.90, α = 0.05, correlation (p H0) = 0, tail(s) = two. According to the analysis, it was determined that a total of 37 volleyball players (age = 17.35 ± 2.137 year; body height = 193.28 ± 6.222 cm; body weight = 84.22 ± 10.780 kg; maturity offset = 4.11 ± 1.507 year; body fat = 12.71% ± 3.315). The physical characteristics of these participants are presented in Table 1 (Table 1). All participants were healthy and met the following inclusion criteria: (a) aged between 16 and 22 years; (b) no lower limb injuries in the past six months and (c) no history of severe injuries that could impact lower limb function. The study protocols were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of Erzurum Technical University (Meeting Number: 08, Decision Number: 7, Date: 04.07.2024).

Procedures

Anthropometric characteristics and maturity offset

Body mass, body fat, lean body mass, fat mass and muscle mass were measured using a Tanita body fat analyzer (Model HD–318, Tanita Corporation, Japan), while body height measurements were obtained using a Holtain portable stadiometer (Model 603VR). These anthropometric parameters, along with the participants’ date of birth were meticulously recorded to calculate the maturity offset. The maturity offset provides an estimate of the time relative to the attainment of peak height velocity (PHV), serving as an indicator of biological maturation. Lower-limb muscle strength test, intra-limb ratio and inter-limb asymmetry

An isokinetic dynamometer (Biodex Corp, Shirley, NY, USA) was utilized to measure the torque of the knee extensor and flexor muscles of the limbs. The device was calibrated in accordance with the manufacturer’s specified procedures. Previously procedures in the literature were used for the test [34, 35]. Prior to the isokinetic assessment, each participant engaged in a 10–15 min warm-up session. This included pedalling on a Monark 828E Ergomedic stationary cycle ergometer (Monark, Vansbro, Sweden) at a moderate intensity (100 watts). Additionally, participants performed dynamic stretches targeting the major muscle groups of the lower limbs [36, 37]. Isokinetic torque of the quadriceps and hamstring muscles was assessed using continuous, bidirectional knee extension-flexion movements at angular velocities of 60°s-1 and 180°s-1, covering a range of motion from 0° (full extension) to 90° (flexion). These velocities have been previously employed in research examining muscle strength in elite competitive level female athletes [38]. Participants first completed three submaximal trials, incrementally increasing the load to 50%, 75%, and approximately 100% of their maximum capacity [36, 39]. This was followed by one set of five repetitions at maximal concentric contraction at 60°s-1 and 15 repetitions at 180°s-1. The same procedure was repeated for the opposite leg. Participants were given a 30-second rest after the submaximal trials, a one-minute break between different velocities, and a three-minute break for machine adjustments between legs. They were instructed to perform the movements through the full range of motion. Testing order for the dominant and non-dominant limbs was randomized based on the preferred kicking leg [37]. To ensure data consistency and reliability, each volleyball player performed two trial attempts, with a standardized 10-minute active rest period between them.

The intra-limb ratio was determined based on the conventional hamstring-to-quadriceps (H:Q) ratio, calculated using peak torque values obtained during the knee flexion and extension for the same limb at 60°s-1 and 180°s-1. These peak torque values were absolute (not normalized to body mass) and were used to compute the H:Q ratio for both the DL and NDL according to Eq. 2 [36].

The inter-limb asymmetries (%) from isokinetic tests were calculated using Eq. 3, which has been suggested to be accurate for the quantification of asymmetries from unilateral tests [22].

To determine the direction of asymmetry, which indicates limb dominance, an IF function was integrated into the formula in Microsoft Excel: “IF (non-dominant < dominant, 1, -1)”. This adjustment converted the asymmetry value to a negative value if the non-dominant limb was stronger [36, 40].

Sprint performance, kinetics, and kinematics

Sprint performance were evaluated with 5, 10, 15, 20 and 30-meter sprint tests (S5m, S10m, S15m, S20m and S30m, respectively) conducted on a parquet floor. Photocell gates (Microgate, Bolzano, Italy) and standard protocols were used for these tests [41]. Before the sprint tests, air pressure and temperature were measured using a barometer and temperature monitor (TFA 3006.42). The tests were conducted in a wind-free environment. A time correction is necessary when using timing gates due to the lag time between the first instance of force generation and when the timing gates are initially triggered [42, 43]. Sprint split time data were analyzed for each volleyball player, with model parameters and HF-V profiles components (sprint kinetics) estimated using established methods [44, 45]. These calculations utilized the open source “shorts” package [46] in R (R Development Core Team, 2020), which applies non-linear least squares regression via the “nls” function. Sprint kinematic variables, including DL ground contact time (GCTDL), NDL ground contact time (GCTNDL), DL flight time (FTDL), NDL flight time (FTNDL), DL step length (SLDL), NDL step length (SLNDL), DL step velocity (SVDL), and NDL step velocity (SVNDL), were meticulously recorded during the initial S30m of each sprint using the OptoJump Next system (Microgate, Bolzano, Italy). This advanced optical measurement system enables precise and reliable quantification of key kinematic parameters. To ensure consistency and reliability of the data, each volleyball player performed two trial sprints, with a standardized two-minute active rest period between attempts.

Jump performances

The methodologies for the unilateral CMJ (CMJDL and CMJNDL), bilateral CMJ, CMJAS and SJ were described in detail previously [47–51]. All jumps were performed on dual force platforms (Model 9286BA, Kistler, Winterthur, Switzerland) with a sampling frequency of 1.000 Hz. Estimated absolute peak power for each CMJ (CMJ PP), CMJAS (CMJAS PP), and SJ (SJ PP) trial was calculated using Sayers’ formula (Eq. 4) [52] to account for variations in body mass among the participants. The difference between CMJ and SJ heights (CMJSJdiff) was determined by subtracting the SJ height from the CMJ [48]. All data were analyzed using the best measurement from two trials for both CMJDL, CMJNDL, CMJ, CMJAS, and SJ heights for each player.

Agility performances

The methodologies for the pro-agility test were described in detail previously [53, 54]. The test was performed on a parquet surface with sports shoes. After a warm-up and familiarization with the test, players completed two attempts with a minimum rest period of 5 min between trials. Players were instructed and motivated to perform the test at their maximum capacity.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean (X̄) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) and standard deviation (SD). The associations between the intra- and inter-limb asymmetry with sprint performances, kinematics, horizontal force-velocity profile components (sprint kinetics), jump, and agility performances were calculated using Pearson product-moment correlation test. Pearson Correlation Test were conducted using the “psych” package in R (version 2023.12.0). The results of Pearson Correlation Test were presented using a heatmap, created with GraphPad version 8.0.2. Correlations was interpreted according to the following thresholds: <0.1, trivial; 0.1–0.3, small; 0.3–0.5, moderate; 0.5–0.7, large; 0.7–0.9, very large; >0.9, nearly perfect [55]. To better understand how isokinetics strength, intra- and inter-limb asymmetry, sprint performances, kinetics, kinematics, jump, and agility performances relate to each other and to simplify the dataset, we used a method called PCA with a varimax rotation. This method helps clarify the relationships by focusing on the most important patterns in the data. The suitability of the data for this analysis was controlled using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure, which confirmed the appropriateness of the dataset. For the PCA, the “factoextra” package in R was utilized, while the KMO analysis was conducted using the “psych” package in R. In the PCA, only principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 were retained, ensuring that the focus remained on the most significant factors explaining the variations in the data. All statistical tests were executed with a significance level of α = 0.05.

Table 4 presents a detailed summary of the PCA results, highlighting the explained variance, component loadings, and the contributions of key variables, including inter-limb ratios and intra-limb asymmetry at angular velocities of 60°s-1 and 180°s-1, as well as sprint performance, sprint kinetics, sprint kinematics, jump and agility performance variables. The PCA analysis revealed a total of 12 components, collectively accounting for 88.2% of the total variance. Among these, the first four principal components were particularly significant, as they were linked to jump performance metrics, lower-limb muscle strength, sprint performances, and intra-limb ratios between DL and NDLs at different angular velocities. Together, these four components explained 51.0% of the total variance. The first principal component, which contributed the highest proportion at 20.0%, was strongly correlated with jump performance. Key variables influencing this component included SJ, unilateral and bilateral CMJ, and CMJAS (Table 4).

Discussion

The purpose of this study is to examine intra- and inter-limb strength imbalances and asymmetries in elite academy volleyball players in relation to sprint performance, sprint kinetics and kinematics, jump, and agility performances, while also utilizing PCA to identify and understand the key factors contributing to volleyball performance success. To the best of our knowledge, no study has comprehensively examined the relationship between intra-limb and inter-limb asymmetries and sprint kinetics, kinematics, performance outcomes, jump performance, and agility in elite academy volleyball players. This gap highlights a significant research void in the field and underscores the unique contribution of our study. In this context, our research represents the first study to comprehensively investigate the effects of muscular imbalances on athletic performance in this population. The study observed that intra-limbNDL ratios were minimal at angular velocity of 60°s-1 (0.514) and maximal at angular velocity of 180°s-1 (0.653). Similarly, asymmetry values were minimal for Hasymmetry at angular velocity of 180°s-1 (5.42%) and maximal for Qasymmetry at an angular velocity of 180°s-1 (8.43%). Lockie et al. (2014) stated that asymmetries below 15% did not have a significant impact on performance and that a deficit greater than 15% is required to affect performance [56]. Similarly, Yoshioka et al.’s computer simulation study demonstrated that a 10% strength difference between muscles did not result in significant changes in jump performance [57, 58]. The inter- and intra-limb asymmetry values obtained in our study, although below the established thresholds (10–15%), showed significant associations with sprint and jump performance in elite academy-level volleyball players, indicating that even these levels of asymmetry may have negative effects on performance. The findings suggest that the widespread approach of disregarding isokinetic local strength asymmetries below 10% should be reconsidered, particularly in specific athletic populations. However, such comparisons should be interpreted with caution due to differences in testing protocols, age groups, and playing standards. The values reported in our study present up-to-date asymmetry profiles of elite academy volleyball players that have not previously been documented in the literature. In this context, the findings offer valuable reference data for volleyball coaches and practitioners, with the potential to guide talent identification, athlete monitoring processes, and return-to-play decisions following injury.

In our study, small positive correlations were observed between Hasymmetry at 60°s-1 and sprint performances (S5m and S20m), while a significant small negative correlation was found with V0. At 180°s-1, both Qasymmetry and Hasymmetry showed significant small negative correlations with V0. Additionally, Qasymmetry at 180°s-1 exhibited significant small positive correlations with S5m, F0rel, and Pmaxrel. Although the lack of comparable studies makes it challenging to directly compare our findings with the existing literature, Coratella et al. (2018) identified a moderate positive correlation between inter-limb hamstring high-velocity (300°s-1) concentric peak torque asymmetry and S10m and S30m in U21 elite soccer players [27]. İnce and Tortu (2025) found that Hasymmetry at 60°s-1 had a significant weak to moderate negative correlation with S30m (r= -0.27) in seventy-four youth athletes from football, volleyball, and handball. Also, they found Hasymmetry at 180°s-1 showed significant moderate negative correlations with F0rel and Pmaxrel [16]. A previous systematic review and meta-analysis study concluded that inter-limb asymmetries have a negative impact on sprint and CoD performance [29]. However, in our study, no significant relationship was found between inter- and intra-limb asymmetries and CoD performance. Ujaković and Šarabon (2020) highlighted that CoD performance cannot be explained by a single factor; it is a complex quality shaped by many physical and biomechanical variables [9]. Therefore, the inconsistent results reported in the literature on the link between inter‑ and intra‑limb asymmetries and CoD performance may reflect differences in participants’ physical traits, maturation levels, sport disciplines, and the angular velocities chosen for testing. Sprint requires athletes to overcome initial resistance in order to achieve high velocities from a stationary position, and during this process, the increasing running speed necessitates the production of substantial horizontal force and power [44]. During sprint, the horizontal force vector must be effectively oriented [59–61]. The role of the hamstring and quadriceps muscles in the development of horizontal force during sprint has been previously emphasized [62, 63]. In the context of these findings, the impact of hamstring and quadriceps asymmetry levels on sprint performance may be associated with changes in horizontal force production capacity during different phases of sprint. Specifically, the positive correlations observed over short distances suggest that force production during the initial acceleration phase could be influenced by asymmetry levels. Conversely, the correlations observed over longer distances and with maximal force and power components indicate that mechanical imbalances at higher speeds may reduce sprint efficiency.

The intra-limbNDL ratio at 60°s-1 showed small negative correlations with GCTDL and GCTNDL, and a positive correlation with FTDL. Similarly, Ince and Tortu (2025) found significant moderate negative correlations between inter-limb asymmetry at 180°s-1 and left and right GCT, emphasizing the role of muscular imbalances in shaping ground contact dynamics [16]. In this context, intra-limb asymmetry in the non-dominant limb may affect ground contact time, directly shaping sprint performance. Indeed, it has been emphasized that GCT is one of the most critical features of efficient sprint [64, 65]. On the other hand, the weaker limb’s attempt to compensate for its strength deficit may result in a longer flight time. This suggests that the athlete spends additional time maintaining balance during horizontal propulsion, thereby extending the movement cycle [66]. Furthermore, the prolonged flight time can reduce step efficiency, affecting the sprint performance kinetics and consequently having a direct impact on overall sprint performance.

No significant relationship was found between the intra-limb ratio at 60°s-1 and 180°s-1 for both the DL and NDL and jump performances. However, the intra-limbNDL ratio at angular velocities of 60°s-1 demonstrated a small positive correlation with CMJSJdiff, suggesting that strength imbalances within the NDL may contribute to the observed differences in jump heights between CMJ and SJ. This finding highlights the potential influence of NDL strength on the ability to utilize stored elastic energy during the stretch-shortening cycle, which is more prominent in the CMJ compared to the SJ. Such imbalances could reflect inefficiencies in force transmission or neuromuscular coordination, ultimately impacting performance. Atik et al. (2024) emphasized that the effects of H:Q ratios and bilateral muscle asymmetries on jump performance in volleyball players were not statistically significant [19], whereas Soylu et al. (2020) identified significant correlations between H:Q ratios and CMJ height at both 60°s-1 and 180°s-1 in volleyball players [20]. Despite the diverse findings, these variations highlight the complex and multifactorial nature of the relationship between intra-limb asymmetries and jump performance [9]. On the other hand, a significant small negative correlation was identified between Qasymmetry at angular velocities of 180°s-1 and both CMJDL and CMJ. Schons et al. (2019) reported a similar negative correlation (r= -0.53) at an angular velocity of 60°s-1; however, this relationship was not statistically significant [6]. Additionally, they observed a moderate correlation between knee muscle strength asymmetries and vertical jump performance at 300°s-1 in professional volleyball players, suggesting that the influence of asymmetries may become more pronounced at higher angular velocities. A computer simulation study demonstrated that a 10% strength difference between muscles did not result in significant changes in jump performance when compared to a symmetrical model, neither in SJ [58] nor in CMJ [57]. To investigate this, the authors introduced a 10% strength difference in various muscle groups, including the quadriceps, hamstrings, gluteal muscles, and gastrocnemii, in both studies. The asymmetrical model showed greater performance by the stronger lower limb and reduced performance by the weaker lower limb in both SJ [58] and CMJ [57]. Our findings suggest that Qasymmetry at 180°s-1 may reduce movement efficiency, particularly in CMJDL and CMJ jump height performances, where rapid contraction and force production are crucial. Strength asymmetry may negatively affect jump performance at higher angular velocities, and this effect is likely associated with the compensatory mechanism of the stronger limb. Specifically, the stronger limb may undertake greater loads to compensate for the deficiencies of the weaker limb, which could disrupt the balance of force distribution and movement efficiency, ultimately limiting overall jump performance.

To comprehensively examine the factors affecting the performance of volleyball players, PCA was applied in our study. As a result of the PCA analysis, a total of 12 components were identified, explaining 88.2% of the total variance. Notably, the first four principal components were found to be associated with jump performances, lower-limb muscle strengths, sprint performances over different distances, and intra-limb ratios between DL and NDLs at various angular velocities. These four principal components accounted for 51.0% of the total variance. The first principal component, which contributed the most, explained 20.0% of the variance and was determined to be strongly associated with jump performances. The most influential factors were identified as SJ, unilateral and bilateral CMJ, and CMJAS. The findings of our study provide more in-depth analyses compared to studies in the literature where PCA was not employed. The application of PCA in this study demonstrates a methodological distinction from previous research in the field. Previous studies investigating the effects of intra-limb and inter-limb asymmetries on sprint performance, sprint kinetics, kinematics, jump performance, and CoD performance [6, 16, 25–28] have typically been limited by univariate statistical approaches, often failing to comprehensively analyze the complex interactions among performance variables. Our study is the first to apply PCA in this context, providing a clearer identification of performance-related parameters and simplifying the complex interrelationships into key contributing factors in elite academy volleyball players. By leveraging PCA, our study offers a more precise assessment of individual performance deficiencies, enabling the identification of asymmetries and their specific impacts on performance metrics. This approach provides coaches and sports scientists with valuable data to design individualized and targeted training programs, ultimately supporting the development of optimized training strategies. The application of PCA in this study not only highlights the critical factors influencing athletic performance but also offers a methodological framework for future research seeking to address performance optimization and injury prevention in elite athletes.

A potential limitation of this study is that, although the force assessments provided by isokinetic dynamometers are accurate, they focus solely on single-joint movements, which may be insufficient for fully understanding the complexity and multi-joint nature of CoD, sprint, and jump abilities. Furthermore, the study did not evaluate upper-limb strength asymmetries, which could play a significant role in overall volleyball performance, particularly during actions such as spiking and blocking. Another limitation is the cross-sectional design, which captures relationships between asymmetries and performance at a single point in time but does not allow for insights into the longitudinal development or changes in these relationships over a competitive season or training period. Additionally, since small correlation coefficients are more susceptible to statistical noise, especially in relatively small sample sizes, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, the utility of asymmetry assessments should not rely solely on statistical associations but should be integrated into a broader athlete monitoring framework that considers sport-specific context, individual variability in response, and longitudinal performance trends.

Conclusion

As a result of this study, it was found that intra-limb and inter-limb strength asymmetries of the lower extremities showed statistically significant, but small to moderate correlations with various performance variables such as sprint performance (S5m, S20m), sprint kinetics (V0, F0rel, Pmaxrel), CMJ, sprint kinematics (GCTDL, GCTNDL, FTDL), and CMJSJdiff. By applying PCA, key performance contributors such as jump variables, lower-limb muscle strength, sprint metrics, and intra-limb ratios were identified, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the interconnected nature of performance outcomes in elite academy volleyball players. Given that this study is the first to comprehensively examine the effects of intra- and inter-limb asymmetries on sprint kinetics, kinematics, and jump performances in elite academy volleyball players, and represents the first application of performance indicator research specifically for volleyball players, there remain gaps in the literature that necessitate further research to validate the broader implications of addressing asymmetries and performance contributors in various sports contexts.

Future studies should consider longitudinal designs to monitor changes in asymmetry and their impact on performance over an entire training season or competitive period. Additionally, expanding the scope to include upper-limb asymmetries and their influence on volleyball-specific skills, such as spiking and blocking, would provide a more holistic understanding of performance. Future research could also explore the integration of sport-specific, multi-joint dynamic assessments, which would better replicate on-court demands. Advanced biomechanical tools, such as motion capture systems and wearable sensors, may further refine our understanding of the role of asymmetries in real-time gameplay, ultimately informing more effective injury prevention and performance optimization strategies.
