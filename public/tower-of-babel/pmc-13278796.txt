Introduction

Permanent pacemaker implantation is required in approximately 5% of patients following orthotopic heart transplantation (OHT), most commonly for sinus node dysfunction (SND). This complication occurs more frequently after biatrial anastomosis due to surgical injury or sympathetic denervation.1,2 Although often transient, persistent bradycardia or chronotropic incompetence can cause significant symptoms and necessitate permanent pacing. In this scenario, conventional transvenous pacing can be technically challenging because of complex post-surgical anatomy and a higher risk of infection.3 Leadless systems provide a less invasive alternative and reduce lead- and pocket-related complications. The Aveir AR device (Abbott), engineered for right atrial fixation, optimizes atrioventricular synchrony even in anatomically complex cases.4–6 Integration of three-dimensional computed tomography (CT) with fluoroscopy enhances spatial orientation even in post-transplant hearts and can be valuable in guiding device implantation.7 This case illustrates the feasibility and clinical benefit of CT–fluoroscopy–guided leadless atrial pacing for persistent SND after biatrial orthotopic heart transplantation.

Case report

A 36-year-old woman with end-stage restrictive cardiomyopathy underwent successful biatrial OHT in May 2024. The early postoperative course was uneventful, but she developed SND with junctional or low atrial rhythm (55–65 bpm) and a narrow QRS complex. After excluding acute rejection, theophylline was initiated, modestly increasing heart rate to 75–80 bpm. She was discharged on standard immunosuppression. Eleven months after transplantation, the patient reported exertional fatigue and pulsatile headaches. Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed persistent junctional rhythm (55–60 bpm), and exercise testing demonstrated reduced chronotropic response (52% of predicted maximal heart rate; 7.6 METs). Repeat ECG confirmed junctional rhythm with retrograde P waves and a ventriculoatrial interval of 60 ms, consistent with persistent SND. Because of symptomatic bradycardia, an electrophysiological study (EPS) was performed. Pre-procedural 3D cardiac CT was acquired and fused with fluoroscopy using the EPNavigator 5.6 system (Philips) with the bronchial tree and superior vena cava serving as landmarks. EPS confirmed electrical dissociation between donor and recipient atria (Figure 1). A quadripolar catheter positioned in the native right atrium showed complete atrioventricular dissociation. In the donor right atrium, junctional rhythm was recorded with an HV interval of 48 ms and an atrioventricular Wenckebach point of 430 ms, indicating preserved AV conduction but absent intrinsic sinus activity. Given persistent symptomatic SND and intact AV conduction, permanent atrial pacing was indicated. Because of the patient’s immunosuppressed status and complex atrial anatomy, a leadless atrial pacemaker (Aveir AR, Abbott) was selected to minimize procedural and infectious risks. Through the right femoral vein, the delivery catheter was advanced into the donor right atrium under CT–fluoroscopy fusion guidance (Figure 2). In a right anterior oblique 30° projection, the catheter was directed towards the base of the donor right atrial appendage, confirmed by contrast injection. Counterclockwise rotation in the left anterior oblique 30° projection delineated the lateral plane, allowing precise localization of the appendage base. Electrical mapping identified viable atrial tissue using current of injury as a marker. A sharp electrogram with minimal S-wave delay and transient pressure response defined the optimal fixation site. Gentle forward pressure enhanced contact, producing the expected electrogram widening and S-wave transition. The device was actively fixed, stability tested, and released successfully. Final parameters were atrial sensing 1.1 mV, threshold 1.5 V at 0.2 ms, and impedance 420 Ω. Total procedure time was 20 min with 5 min of fluoroscopy. The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged the next day (Figure 3). At one-month follow-up, she reported complete resolution of symptoms and improved exercise tolerance. Device interrogation showed 96% atrial pacing with stable parameters and appropriate rate histogram. Repeat cardiopulmonary exercise testing demonstrated enhanced chronotropic response, with heart rate increasing from 60 to 142 bpm (77% of predicted), achieving 10.2 METs and 9 min of exercise under continuous atrial pacing (Figure 4).

Discussion

Patients undergoing biatrial anastomosis are more likely to develop postoperative sinus node dysfunction and exhibit lower resting sinus heart rates.1,2 Incidence ranges from 8% to 20%, and while many cases are transient, persistent chronotropic incompetence can impair quality of life and may require pacing.1,2 Conventional transvenous pacemakers remain standard therapy but are often suboptimal in transplant recipients due to distorted atrial anatomy, limited venous access, and infection risk.8 Leadless pacemakers overcome these limitations by eliminating intravascular leads and subcutaneous pockets.3,9 The Aveir AR system, specifically designed for atrial pacing, provides active fixation, high-fidelity sensing, and modular compatibility with the ventricular Aveir VR system for future dual-chamber configuration.3 Reported implantation success rates are high, with low complication rates and reliable atrioventricular synchrony in most patients.3 However, in post-transplant patients, especially after biatrial anastomosis, the main procedural challenge lies in ensuring stable atrial fixation and reliable electrical performance. In this context, CT–fluoroscopy fusion represents an incremental procedural aid that improves spatial orientation and facilitates device deployment, optimizing catheter handling and target site positioning within viable donor atrial tissue. Previous studies have highlighted the value of CT–fluoroscopy fusion in complex device implantations, including leadless pacemakers.7 Although the leadless pacemaker (Abbott) system enables electrogram mapping and electrical measurements before fixation to guide site selection, CT integration provides additional spatial guidance during implantation, potentially reducing procedural time and enhancing overall safety. This guided navigation and optimal positioning of the Aveir AR device at the base of the donor right atrial appendage, achieving satisfactory measurement without complications. The procedure was completed efficiently, with a short procedural duration and minimal fluoroscopy exposure, consistent with recent reports.10 To our knowledge, this represents a rare example of leadless atrial pacemaker implantation in a heart transplant recipient using CT–fluoroscopy fusion. Compared with earlier reports, this experience highlights the synergy between advanced imaging and next-generation pacing systems in anatomically complex post-transplant hearts. The patient’s complete symptom resolution and improved exercise capacity support the clinical benefit of individualized atrial pacing. Long-term studies are warranted to evaluate device performance and impact on transplant outcomes.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the feasibility and safety of CT–fluoroscopy fusion–guided leadless atrial pacing in a heart transplant recipient with persistent sinus node dysfunction. Advanced imaging integration enhances spatial orientation and device deployment. The approach provided excellent electrical performance, rapid symptom resolution, and improved functional capacity while minimizing infection risk associated with transvenous systems. Imaging-guided leadless pacing thus represents a valuable alternative for pacing in anatomically complex post-transplant patients.
