Stereotactic body radiotherapy and other locoregional therapies for hepatocellular carcinoma in patients with cirrhosis: real-world treatment allocation, BCLC-stratified outcomes, and prognostic determinants
Authors: Cyriac Abby Philips, Tharun Tom Oommen, Jose Paul, Rajesh Sasidharan, Arif Hussain Theruvath, Aryalakshmi Sreemohan, Ambily Baby, Rinu Mary Saji, Akshay Venugopal, Ajit Tharakan, Philip Augustine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsStereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) has emerged as a treatment option for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in patients ineligible for thermal ablation or surgery. However, real-world evidence on how physicians select between locoregional therapies, the stage-specific outcomes of these treatment decisions, and the prognostic determinants that transcend treatment modality remains limited. We aimed to (1) characterize real-world treatment allocation patterns between SBRT and other locoregional therapies (OLT) in patients with cirrhosis and HCC, (2) describe disease control, recurrence, and survival outcomes stratified by Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage, and (3) identify adverse prognostic factors for mortality across the overall cohort.Patients and methodsThis retrospective, single-centre, descriptive observational study included patients with HCC treated between April 2021 and October 2024. Patients received either SBRT or OLT (n = 42, including radiofrequency or microwave ablation, trans-arterial chemoembolization, or combinations thereof). Disease control was assessed at 90 days using modified RECIST criteria. Outcomes were analysed within BCLC strata, and competing risks analysis was performed for recurrence. Prognostic factors for mortality were assessed using Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier analyses across the entire cohort.ResultsReal-world physician choices revealed significant confounding by indication: SBRT was preferentially selected for patients with more advanced disease (BCLC B/C/D: 69.5% vs. 47.6%; p = 0.017) and worse baseline liver function (CTP score: 7.2 ± 1.9 vs. 6.4 ± 1.5; p = 0.015). Within the BCLC B stratum, the only stage with adequate patients in both arms, baseline characteristics were comparable across key parameters. Ninety-day disease control rates were comparable overall (92.9% vs. 83.3%; p = 0.074) and within BCLC B (86.3% vs. 76.5%; p = 0.448). All 19 deaths occurred exclusively in BCLC B/C/D patients (p = 0.001), regardless of treatment modality, with no deaths among 61 early-stage (BCLC 0/A) patients. Across the entire cohort, serum albumin (dead 3.17 ± 0.58 vs. alive 3.51 ± 0.55; p = 0.016), ALBI score (p = 0.030), and BCLC stage were dominant mortality predictors. Mortality was driven by sepsis rather than tumor progression.ConclusionsIn this real-world descriptive study, SBRT was reserved for higher-risk patients yet achieved comparable disease control across BCLC stages. Survival was ultimately determined by BCLC stage and hepatic reserve, particularly serum albumin, rather than treatment modality. These findings support risk stratification using albumin and diabetes status to guide treatment selection. Prospective, BCLC-stage matched studies are needed to confirm treatment-specific effects.Graphical Abstract Keywords: SBRT, HCC, Decompensated cirrhosis, MELD, Portal hypertension, Sepsis

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the fourth leading cause of cancer mortality globally [1]. Its management is complex, requiring a nuance between tumour eradication and preserving the underlying liver function [2]. While surgery and transplantation are ideal for early-stage disease, strict criteria exclude many patients. Thermal ablation serves as a curative alternative for small, unresectable tumours [2, 3], while TACE remains the palliative standard for intermediate disease. Despite these options, significant clinical limitations persist [3, 4]. Stereotactic Body Radiotherapy (SBRT) has emerged to fill this gap, offering precise ablation that overcomes the physical constraints of thermal therapies. Consequently, major guidelines have validated SBRT as an alternative for patients ineligible for ablation or surgery and has emerging evidence supporting its use in selected intermediate stage HCC [5]. However, adoption remains cautious in advanced cirrhosis due to the risk of Radiation-Induced Liver Disease (RILD) and hepatic decompensation [6].

This study describes a real-world institutional experience with locoregional therapies for HCC in patients with cirrhosis, encompassing SBRT, radiofrequency ablation (RFA), microwave ablation (MWA), trans-arterial chemoembolization (TACE) and combinations thereof. Unlike clinical trials with matched cohorts, real-world treatment allocation is driven by clinician judgment and patient characteristics, resulting in inherent baseline imbalances between treatment groups. We therefore adopted a descriptive study framework with three objectives: (1) to characterize the real-world physician decision-making patterns that determine treatment allocation between SBRT and other locoregional therapies (OLT); (2) to describe disease control, recurrence, and survival outcomes stratified by BCLC stage, which is the dominant prognostic determinant in HCC; and (3) to identify adverse prognostic factors for mortality across the entire cohort, focusing on clinically accessible parameters that can inform bedside risk stratification independent of treatment modality.

Patients

Patients

Patientsand methods

Design

This was a retrospective, single-center, observational cohort study conducted at a dedicated Hepatology Referral Unit and included patients treated between April 2021 and October 2024. The data cutoff date for survival and outcome ascertainment was October 31, 2025. Patients were identified through a systematic search of the institutional electronic medical records (EMR) system. The EMR database was queried using the following search strategy: (1) International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes for hepatocellular carcinoma (C22.0) and malignant neoplasm of liver, primary (C22.0); (2) procedure codes for stereotactic body radiation therapy, microwave ablation (MWA), radiofrequency ablation (RFA), and trans-arterial chemoembolization (TACE); and (3) review of radiology and pathology reports containing terms “hepatocellular carcinoma,” “HCC,” “SBRT,” “stereotactic radiotherapy,” “microwave ablation,” “radiofrequency ablation,” and “chemoembolization.”

Patients

Patients were included in the study if they met all of the following criteria: age ≥ 18 years at the time of treatment initiation and confirmed diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma based on histopathological examination and/or non-invasive diagnostic criteria according to the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines, including characteristic imaging features on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrating arterial phase hyperenhancement with washout on portal venous or delayed phases. All potentially eligible patients were manually reviewed by two independent investigators (RMS, AV) to confirm eligibility and extract relevant clinical data. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with two other investigators (AS, AB). All patients were discussed in a multidisciplinary tumour board (MDT) comprising hepatology, radiation oncology, interventional radiology, surgical oncology, and diagnostic radiology prior to treatment allocation. Treatment decisions were based on MDT consensus, considering tumour characteristics, liver function, performance status, and patient preferences. SBRT was delivered using volumetric modulated arc therapy with daily cone-beam CT for image guidance. Respiratory motion was managed using abdominal compression or active breathing control. Data were extracted directly from the EMR into a secure, password-protected electronic database. Patients were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: presence of extrahepatic metastatic disease at baseline, prior liver transplantation, concurrent extra-hepatic malignancy and missing critical outcome data including survival status and treatment response assessment at 90 days. Ethical approval and consent waiver details are provided in the Declarations section.

Follow up and outcomes

Patients were followed according to institutional protocols with clinical evaluation and laboratory assessment at 4–6 weeks after treatment completion (first post-treatment follow-up) and subsequently at 3-month intervals or as clinically indicated. Tumour response was assessed at 90 days (± 14 days) after treatment completion using contrast-enhanced CT or MRI according to the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours (mRECIST). 90-day disease control was defined as absence of new intrahepatic lesions at 90 days post-treatment on imaging.

Any disease progression/failure was a composite of primary treatment failure (new lesions at 90 days) and subsequent recurrence.

Treatment-related adverse events were classified as immediate (occurring within 30 days of treatment) or delayed (occurring between 30 and 90 days after treatment). Adverse events were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Hepatic decompensation was assessed across two distinct categories: baseline decompensation, defined as any decompensating event occurring within 90 days prior to treatment and new hepatic decompensation, characterized as post-treatment events representing either a first-ever occurrence or a novel decompensation type distinct from the patient’s history. Worsening of pre-existing conditions was catalogued as a treatment-related complication rather than new decompensation, and while specific events could satisfy criteria for multiple categories (e.g., concurrent delayed complications and new decompensation), these were reported separately to ensure accurate distinction between unique patient counts and total event burden. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of treatment initiation to the date of death from any cause or last follow-up. Patients who were alive at the time of data cutoff were censored at the date of last contact. Date and cause of death were extracted from medical records, death certificates, and hospital mortality databases.

Study endpoints

The co-primary endpoints were: (1) characterization of real-world treatment allocation patterns between SBRT and OLT, (2) BCLC-stage-stratified disease control, recurrence, and survival outcomes, and (3) identification of adverse prognostic factors for mortality across the overall cohort. Secondary, exploratory endpoints included between-group comparison of disease control rate at 90 days, overall survival, and tumour recurrence (with the explicit caveat that these may be confounded by indication).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between the SBRT and OLT locoregional therapy groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate based on expected cell counts. Time-to-recurrence analysis employed both standard and competing risks methodologies. Given differential mortality between groups we performed sensitivity analyses treating death without recurrence as a competing event rather than non-informative censoring. Cumulative incidence functions (CIF) were estimated using the Aalen-Johansen estimator, and sub distribution hazard ratios were calculated using Fine-Gray proportional hazards regression. Gray’s test was used to compare cumulative incidence curves between groups. For variables with missing data, denominators were specified in table footnotes. For composite outcomes where patients could experience multiple events (complications, decompensation types), both patient counts and total event counts were reported. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Python version 3.12 with SciPy library version 1.16.3 and R version 4.5.1.

Results

Overall cohort characteristics

A total of 170 patients with HCC and underlying cirrhosis were included (mean age 68.1 ± 7.8 years; 157 males [92.4%]). The predominant etiology of chronic liver disease was metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) in 80 patients (47.1%), followed by alcohol-related liver disease in 40 (23.5%). BCLC stage distribution was: BCLC 0 in 2 (1.2%), BCLC A in 59 (34.7%), BCLC B in 68 (40.0%), BCLC C in 35 (20.6%), and BCLC D in 6 (3.5%), with 109 patients (64.1%) classified as advanced stage (B/C/D). Median largest tumor size was 3.2 cm (IQR 2.2–5.0; range 1.0–16.0), 104 of 147 patients (70.7%) had single tumors, and portal vein thrombosis was present in 33 (19.4%), predominantly tumoral (24/33) and branch-type (21/33). Baseline hepatic reserve was impaired across the cohort: mean CTP score 7.04 ± 1.87 (CTP-A 49.4%, CTP-B 36.5%, CTP-C 14.1%), mean MELD-3 score 12.19 ± 4.02, and mean serum albumin 3.47 ± 0.56 g/dL, with nearly half (81/164, 49.4%) having albumin below 3.5 g/dL. Median baseline AFP was 32.0 ng/mL (IQR 6.9–234.4), with 35 of 166 patients (21.1%) exceeding 400 ng/mL.

Treatment allocation and confounding by indication

Among 170 patients, 128 (75.3%) received SBRT and 42 (24.7%) received other locoregional therapies (OLT), including MWA (n = 20), TACE (n = 13), combination TACE with RFA (n = 5), combination TACE with MWA (n = 3), and RFA (n = 1). The most prescribed SBRT dose was 3500 cGy (36 patients, 28.1%), followed by 3300 cGy (22 patients, 17.2%); dose range was 2750–4500 cGy. The mean number of SBRT treatment courses was 1.1 ± 0.4 (median 1), with 110 patients (85.9%) receiving single-course treatment, 15 (11.7%) receiving 2, and 2 (1.6%) receiving 3 courses. Real-world physician choices revealed significant confounding by indication (Table 1). SBRT patients were younger (67.3 ± 7.9 vs. 70.5 ± 6.6 years; p = 0.011) and had significantly more advanced disease: early-stage (BCLC 0/A) disease was present in 39 (30.5%) SBRT patients compared to 22 (52.4%) OLT patients (p = 0.017). Baseline liver function was significantly worse in the SBRT group: CTP score 7.2 ± 1.9 vs. 6.4 ± 1.5 (p = 0.015), MELD-3 12.6 ± 4.0 vs. 11.0 ± 3.8 (p = 0.046), and total bilirubin 1.66 ± 1.10 vs. 1.26 ± 0.83 mg/dL (p = 0.031). Albumin (p = 0.220), ALBI score (p = 0.128), INR (p = 0.058), AFP (p = 0.056), tumor size (p = 0.277), tumor number (p = 0.116), and PVT (p = 0.183) did not differ significantly between groups. Propensity score matching and IPTW were explored but deemed infeasible due to quasi-complete separation in the mortality outcome (1 death in the OLT group), the 3:1 group size ratio, and unmeasured confounders (diabetes and ECOG unavailable in the OLT group).

Disease control and recurrence

Ninety-day disease control was achieved in 153 of 169 evaluable patients (90.5%) across the whole cohort, with one patient dying before assessment. By treatment group, disease control was 118/127 (92.9%) in the SBRT group versus 35/42 (83.3%) in the OLT group (p = 0.074). Within the SBRT cohort stratified by BCLC stage, disease control was: BCLC 0, 100% (2/2); BCLC A, 97.3% (36/37); BCLC B, 86.3% (44/51); BCLC C, 93.9% (31/33); and BCLC D, 100% (5/5), demonstrating consistent disease control across all stages (Fig. 1). Descriptive outcomes by individual OLT modality were: MWA, 84.2% disease control with no deaths; TACE, 72.7% disease control with no deaths; combination therapy, 87.5% disease control with one death. These subgroups were too small for formal statistical comparison.

Among disease-controlled patients, subsequent recurrence occurred in 60 of 153 (39.2%) at a median of 246 days (IQR 120–450). By treatment group, recurrence was 41/118 (34.7%) in SBRT versus 19/35 (54.3%) in OLT (p = 0.049). However, this simple proportion comparison did not account for differential mortality between groups. Fourteen SBRT patients (10.9%) died without documented recurrence compared to 1 OLT patient (2.4%). Competing risks analysis using cumulative incidence functions yielded 12-month CIF of 33.0% (SBRT) versus 43.1% (OLT) and 24-month CIF of 50.6% versus 64.4%. Fine-Gray sub distribution hazard regression yielded HR 0.68 (95% CI 0.42–1.09, p = 0.112), indicating that the apparent recurrence benefit did not reach statistical significance after accounting for competing mortality (Fig. 2; Table 2).

Our endpoint of ‘disease control’ reflects absence of new intrahepatic lesions rather than mRECIST-based in-field local control, limiting direct comparison with studies reporting field-specific recurrence rates. Among patients with recurrence in the SBRT group, repeat SBRT was performed in 22 patients; alternative treatments included conservative care (n = 24), TACE (n = 1), RFA (n = 1), and liver transplantation (n = 1). Survival and causes of death

Over a median follow-up of 14.1 months (429 days) among survivors, 19 of 170 patients died (11.2%). All-cause mortality was higher in the SBRT group (18/128 [14.1%] vs. 1/42 [2.4%], Fisher’s exact p = 0.046); however, this difference is attributable to confounding by indication. All 19 deaths occurred exclusively in advanced-stage patients (BCLC B: 13/68 [19.1%]; BCLC C: 5/35 [14.3%]; BCLC D: 1/6 [16.7%]), with zero mortality among 61 early-stage (BCLC 0/A) patients regardless of treatment (P = 0.001) [Figure 3A]. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significantly different survival curves between groups (log-rank p = 0.008). One-year overall survival was 89.1% (95% CI 81.0–93.9%) in the SBRT group versus 100% in the OLT group; two-year overall survival was 80.0% (95% CI 68.5–87.7%) versus 100%. Median follow-up was 12.6 months (IQR 6.3–21.7) in the SBRT group and 16.8 months (IQR 8.5–31.7) in the OLT group. In the SBRT group, 69 patients (53.9%) had ≥ 12 months follow-up and 27 (21.1%) had ≥ 24 months. In the OLT group, 26 patients (61.9%) had ≥ 12 months and 16 (38.1%) had ≥ 24 months.]

Among the 18 deaths in the SBRT group, sepsis-related mortality was the leading cause (8 patients, 44.4%). Other causes included variceal bleeding with hepatic encephalopathy (2 patients), cardiopulmonary arrest (2 patients), acute respiratory failure with hepatic encephalopathy (1 patient), ACLF grade 3 (1 patient), massive intracranial hemorrhage (1 patient), and ACS-NSTEMI (1 patient); cause was not available for 2 patients. The single OLT death was due to refractory hypovolemic and septic shock.

Mortality demonstrated a graded association with serum albumin: 21.2% (albumin < 3.0 g/dL), 12.5% (3.0–3.49), and 7.2% (≥ 3.5 g/dL) (log-rank P = 0.003) [Figure 3B]. By CTP class within the SBRT cohort, mortality was 11.9% (CTP-A), 15.2% (CTP-B), and 17.4% (CTP-C).

BCLC-stratified subgroup analysis and within-stratum comparison

Subgroup analysis by BCLC stage revealed differential mortality patterns between groups. In early-stage patients (BCLC 0/A; SBRT n = 39, OLT n = 22), no deaths occurred in either group; disease control was 97.4% versus 86.4%, and recurrence was 33.3% versus 59.1% (Fisher’s exact p = 0.063). Among BCLC B patients (SBRT n = 51, OLT n = 17), mortality was 23.5% versus 5.9% (p = 0.160) and disease control was 86.3% versus 76.5% (p = 0.448). BCLC C/D subgroups had insufficient OLT patients for meaningful comparison (n = 2 and n = 1, respectively) [Table 3].

Prognostic determinants of mortality

Multivariable Cox regression was limited by only 19 deaths. Serum albumin was chosen as the primary variable due to direct measurement and high collinearity with other liver function metrics (Pearson r = 0.73–0.97). BCLC stage was treated as a stratification factor because all deaths occurred in advanced stages, precluding hazard ratio estimation. Across all 170 patients regardless of treatment modality, non-survivors had significantly lower serum albumin (3.17 ± 0.58 vs. 3.51 ± 0.55 g/dL; p = 0.016) and worse ALBI scores (− 1.78 ± 0.63 vs. − 2.10 ± 0.57; p = 0.030). Patients with albumin < 3.5 g/dL had mortality of 16.0% (13/81) versus 7.2% (6/83) for albumin ≥ 3.5 g/dL (univariable Cox HR 3.35, 95% CI 1.25–9.02, p = 0.017). CTP score (p = 0.183), MELD-3 (p = 0.509), bilirubin (p = 0.336), and tumor size (p = 0.203) did not reach significance individually, though correlated with albumin. Younger age was paradoxically associated with mortality (63.95 ± 7.87 vs. 68.60 ± 7.60 years, p = 0.023), likely reflecting preferential allocation of younger patients with more aggressive disease to SBRT. These results identify serum albumin and BCLC stage as dominant mortality predictors, providing clinically accessible parameters for bedside risk stratification (Fig. 4).

Post-treatment complications and hepatic decompensation

Immediate complications (within 30 days): In the SBRT group, immediate treatment-related complications occurred in 6 of 128 patients (4.7%), including hepatic encephalopathy (n = 3), hypotension with hemorrhagic shock (n = 1), worsening jaundice (n = 1), and acute variceal bleeding (n = 1). CTCAE grading: Grade 4 in 3 patients, Grade 3 in 2, Grade 2 in 1.

Delayed complications (30–90 days): Delayed complications occurred in 20 of 128 SBRT patients (15.6%), with 23 total events. The most common was ascites (n = 11), followed by hepatic encephalopathy (n = 5), jaundice (n = 3), multi-organ failure (n = 2), and variceal bleeding (n = 2).

Post-treatment hepatic decompensation: New hepatic decompensation occurred at similar rates: 52/127 (40.9%) in SBRT versus 18/42 (42.9%) in OLT (p = 0.970). In the SBRT group, events included ascites (n = 18), infection (n = 15), hepatic encephalopathy (n = 14), variceal bleeding (n = 7), and jaundice (n = 6); 9 patients (17%) experienced multiple events (60 total events in 52 patients). Across the whole cohort, 70 of 169 evaluable patients (41.4%) developed new decompensation, with ascites (33 events), infection (19 events), and hepatic encephalopathy (16 events) frequent.

Liver function changes post-treatment (SBRT): Mean CTP score showed slight improvement (− 0.30 ± 1.60 points). Mean MELD-3 increased by 2.73 ± 4.19 points. Mean total bilirubin increased by 0.74 ± 1.35 mg/dL. Median AFP decreased from 45.5 to 26.8 ng/mL (41% reduction), suggesting tumor treatment response.

Adjunct therapies

Tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy (TKIs) was administered to 87 of 170 patients (51.2%): 77/128 (60.2%) in the SBRT group versus 10/42 (23.8%) in the OLT group (p < 0.001), reflecting the more advanced disease stage necessitating multimodal treatment. Liver transplantation on follow-up was achieved in 5 patients (2.9%): 3/128 (2.3%) in the SBRT group and 2/42 (4.8%) in the OLT group.

Discussion

This descriptive real-world practice study reports the institutional experience with locoregional therapies for HCC in 170 patients, predominantly males with cirrhosis, encompassing SBRT and other locoregional modalities (MWA, TACE, RFA, and combinations) [7]. Rather than a comparative efficacy analysis, the study was designed to characterize real-world treatment allocation patterns, describe BCLC-stage stratified outcomes, and identify adverse prognostic factors for mortality. Three principal findings emerge. First, real-world physician treatment allocation reflects significant confounding by indication, with SBRT preferentially selected for patients with more advanced BCLC stages and worse hepatic reserve, while OLT modalities were favored for patients with earlier-stage disease and better liver function. This allocation pattern is consistent with clinical practice, where SBRT fills a therapeutic niche for patients ineligible for thermal ablation or surgery [8]. Second, survival in this cohort was determined primarily by BCLC stage and hepatic reserve rather than treatment modality. All 19 deaths occurred in BCLC B/C/D patients, and serum albumin emerged as the dominant prognostic factor independent of treatment received. Notably, within the BCLC B stratum, where baseline parameters were comparable between groups, the mortality difference did not reach statistical significance. Third, SBRT achieved consistent disease control across all BCLC stages (86.3–97.4%), including in patients with advanced cirrhosis typically ineligible for other locoregional therapies, supporting its role as a feasible option in the locoregional therapy armamentarium.

The between-group outcome differences observed in this study must be interpreted with extreme caution due to the inherent limitations of the non-randomized, unmatched design. The higher overall mortality in the SBRT arm (14.1% vs. 2.4%) almost certainly reflects confounding by physician’s choices – SBRT patients had significantly more advanced BCLC stages, higher CTP scores, and worse liver function at baseline, rather than a direct treatment effect. Propensity score matching and inverse probability of treatment weighting were explored but deemed infeasible due to quasi-complete separation in the mortality outcome, the 3:1 group size ratio, and unmeasured confounders (ECOG performance status and diabetes data were available only for the SBRT arm) [9]. Accordingly, all between-group comparisons in this study should be regarded as exploratory, hypothesis-generating observations rather than evidence of treatment superiority or inferiority.

A distinct paradox emerged between adequate local tumor control and overall survival, primarily driven by confounding by indication. Mortality occurred exclusively in advanced-stage patients, who were preferentially selected for SBRT [10]. In general, SBRT was shown to improve local progression free survival than RFA in single recurrent HCC <5cm where PFS, OS and safety were comparable. [11] However, in our study, SBRT effectively also improved these poor-prognosis patients, achieving survival durations exceeding the historical 4–6-month median for untreated BCLC C/Child-Pugh B cirrhosis [12, 13]. This supports literature suggesting that while BCLC stage remains the primary outcome driver [14], SBRT offers survival equivalent to other locoregional therapies when baseline characteristics are adjusted [15], particularly for tumors > 2 cm [16]. The lower recurrence rate with SBRT was maintained after accounting for the competing risk of death. Because more SBRT patients died before experiencing recurrence standard survival analysis could theoretically underestimate recurrence in this group. However, competing risks analysis using cumulative incidence functions demonstrated that the difference between-group actually increased compared to standard Kaplan-Meier estimates. This sensitivity analysis confirms that the observed recurrence benefit with SBRT is robust and not attributable to informative censoring by death. Importantly, although the crude recurrence rate favored SBRT, this comparison does not account for the differential mortality between groups. The appropriate competing risks analysis did not reach statistical significance, indicating that the apparent recurrence benefit with SBRT should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future adequately powered studies with competing risks as the primary analytical framework are needed to validate this observation.

The median time to recurrence was similar, suggesting that when SBRT failed, it failed on a similar timeline, but the absolute probability of failure was lower. The high utilization of TKIs in the SBRT group (60.2%) likely contributed to this survival equivalence through synergistic effects [18], although this combination may also exacerbate hepatic dysfunction. Importantly, the observed mortality difference should not be interpreted as evidence that SBRT is inherently more adverse than OLT locoregional therapies. The complete absence of deaths among early-stage (BCLC 0/A) patients in both treatment groups reinforces that outcomes are driven by disease stage and hepatic reserve, not the treatment modality itself.

The safety profile indicates that SBRT is a feasible intervention even in patients with marginal liver function, with post-treatment decompensation rates comparable to those observed in the OLT group. The higher mortality following these events in the SBRT cohort likely reflects the diminished physiological reserve inherent to their advanced disease stage and liver function rather than specific treatment toxicity, though “non-classic” Radiation-Induced Liver Disease (RILD) remains a theoretical consideration [17]. Similarly, the incidence of sepsis-related deaths in the SBRT cohort raises the hypothesis of radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL) as a potential contributing factor. However, we emphasize that this remains speculative, as lymphocyte counts were not systematically assessed pre- and post-treatment in this study. Similarly, while ‘non-classic’ RILD is a theoretical consideration for the hepatic decompensation observed in some SBRT patients, we did not have validated RILD assessment criteria applied prospectively. These mechanistic hypotheses require dedicated prospective studies with serial lymphocyte monitoring and standardized RILD grading to be confirmed or refuted [19, 20].

The readily available clinical parameter – serum albumin emerged as the dominant predictor of mortality in this cohort and offer practical guidance for patient selection. These findings suggest a simple bedside risk stratification: (i) Lower-risk candidates for aggressive locoregional therapy: Albumin ≥ 3.5 g/dL, BCLC 0/A and (ii) Higher-risk candidates requiring careful deliberation: Albumin < 3.0 g/dL, BCLC B with CTP ≥ 7. For patients in the higher-risk category, the data suggest that the marginal benefit of aggressive local therapy (including SBRT) may be offset by elevated mortality from hepatic and infectious complications. In such cases, best supportive care, optimized nutritional support, or transplant evaluation (where applicable) may represent more appropriate goals than aggressive tumour-directed therapy.

This study has several important limitations. Its retrospective, single-centre design and clinician-driven treatment allocation mean that, even after statistical adjustment, selection bias and residual confounding by indication cannot be fully excluded, particularly given the clear baseline imbalances in stage, liver function and comorbidity burden between groups.

The overall sample size, and especially the small number of deaths and of patients in some subgroups, limited statistical power, restricted the complexity and stability of multivariable and competing-risks models, and precluded more granular analyses (e.g. by individual ablation modality, PVT extent, or detailed BCLC strata).

The 90-day assessment timepoint may not fully capture the delayed radiation response of SBRT-treated lesions, as radiation-induced tumour effects can continue evolving beyond 3 months. However, this standardized timepoint was necessary for cross-modality comparison and is consistent with published SBRT studies using 3-month initial response assessments.

Several clinically relevant factors were unmeasured or incompletely captured, including dynamic changes in liver function beyond the first follow-up, detailed TKI dosing and tolerance, portal hypertension metrics, frailty/sarcopenia and lymphocyte count all of which could influence both treatment selection and outcomes.

Imaging, systemic therapy and infection-prevention strategies were not protocolised but reflected real-world practice, and there was no external or blinded central review of radiological response.

Furthermore, our disease control endpoint assessed absence of new intrahepatic lesions rather than mRECIST-based in-field local control, precluding distinction between local and intrahepatic distant recurrence. This limits direct comparison with studies reporting field-specific recurrence rates and may underestimate the true local control efficacy of SBRT.

Importantly, the OLT comparator arm comprised a heterogeneous mix of locoregional strategies with different mechanisms of action and efficacy profiles. The results should not be interpreted as a pure head-to-head comparison of SBRT versus any single modality, and the OLT arm should be understood as reflecting the range of alternative locoregional options available in real-world practice. Additionally, ECOG performance status and diabetes data were available only for the SBRT cohort, representing unmeasured confounders in between-group comparisons. The absence of these data in the OLT arm precludes adjustment for functional status and metabolic comorbidity, which are known to influence treatment selection and outcomes. Given these limitations, all between-group comparisons in this study should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating, and any apparent treatment effect differences may be attributable to residual confounding rather than true treatment effects.

Finally, the cohort comes from a tertiary centre with a high burden of advanced cirrhosis, so the findings may not generalise to populations with different HCC aetiologies, liver disease severity or resource settings.

In conclusion, this descriptive real-world practice study demonstrates that SBRT is preferentially allocated to patients with more advanced disease and poorer hepatic reserve yet achieves consistent disease control across all BCLC stages. Survival in this cohort was determined primarily by BCLC stage and serum albumin rather than treatment modality, with all deaths confined to advanced-stage (BCLC B/C/D) patients regardless of the locoregional therapy received. These findings support the use of albumin status for bedside risk stratification to identify patients most likely to derive net clinical benefit from aggressive locoregional therapy. Between-group outcome differences observed in this study should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating given the inherent confounding by indication, and adequately powered, prospective, BCLC-stage matched comparative studies are needed to define the true relative efficacy of SBRT versus other individual locoregional modalities. For patients with critically impaired hepatic reserve (albumin < 3.0 g/dL, BCLC B with CTP ≥ 7), the decision to pursue any aggressive tumor-directed therapy should be weighed against the competing risks of decompensation and sepsis, with consideration of supportive care or transplant evaluation where applicable.
