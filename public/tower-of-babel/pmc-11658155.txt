Efficacy and safety of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors in advanced colorectal cancer: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Zhenzi Wang, Yuan Liu, Kedi Wang, Liyan Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors have emerged as promising therapies for advanced colorectal cancer (CRC), but their efficacy and safety profiles require further evaluation. This meta-analysis aims to assess the efficacy and safety of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in this patient population.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following PRISMA guidelines, with data sourced from PubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, Web of Science, and CNKI up to August 3, 2024. Nine randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 1680 patients were included. The primary outcomes were overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS) and objective response rate (ORR), while safety was assessed through adverse events (AEs) and grade ≥ 3 AEs. Effect sizes were calculated using mean differences (MD) and risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsOverall, the meta-analysis showed that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not significantly extend OS (MD = 0.86, 95% CI: -0.55, 2.27), but they significantly improved PFS (MD = 2.53, 95% CI: 0.92, 4.15). Additionally, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not significantly increase the ORR compared to controls (RR = 1.19, 95% CI: 0.99, 1.44). In terms of safety, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not significantly increase the incidence of overall AEs. Subgroup analysis further indicated that PD-1 inhibitors significantly improved OS (MD = 1.24, 95% CI: 0.20, 2.29) and PFS (MD = 6.27, 95% CI: 0.56, 11.97), while PD-L1 inhibitors did not have a significant impact on these outcomes. Additionally, PD-L1 inhibitors were associated with a higher risk of grade ≥ 3 AEs (RR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.07, 1.57), a risk not observed with PD-1 inhibitors.ConclusionPD-1 inhibitors significantly improve PFS and OS in advanced CRC, making them a preferable option over PD-L1 inhibitors, which show limited efficacy and a higher risk of severe AEs. These findings support prioritizing PD-1 inhibitors in clinical practice for this patient group, while caution is warranted with PD-L1 inhibitors due to their safety concerns.Trial registrationPROSPERO (CRD42024611696).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-024-03554-8.Keywords: PD-1, PD-L1, Colorectal cancer, Meta-analysis, Immunotherapy, Adverse events

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) stands as the third most common malignancy worldwide, presenting a significant challenge to global health with its persistently rising incidence and mortality rates [1]. By 2040, the number of new CRC cases is projected to escalate to 3.2 million, with a corresponding death toll of 1.6 million [2], underscoring the urgent need for effective treatment strategies to combat this looming health crisis. This heavy burden significantly impacts individuals and society, resulting in high healthcare costs and economic strain.

Traditional treatment modalities for advanced CRC, including surgery, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies [3, 4], have shown limited efficacy, particularly in the late stages of the disease, and are often accompanied by significant adverse effects [5]. Immunotherapy, particularly the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors, has emerged as a transformative approach in oncology. These therapies work by blocking the programmed death-1 (PD-1) receptor or its ligand (PD-L1), thereby enhancing the immune system's ability to recognize and destroy cancer cells [6–8]. Recent advancements have highlighted the complexity of immune-oncology (IO) in solid tumors, including CRC. Predictors of response and resistance, such as mismatch repair (MMR) status, tumor microenvironment (TME), and neoantigen presentation, play pivotal roles in shaping treatment outcomes [9, 10]. For instance, tumors with deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) or high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) exhibit higher mutational burdens and increased neoantigen presentation, rendering them more responsive to ICIs. Conversely, proficient MMR (pMMR) tumors, characterized by low immunogenicity, often demonstrate resistance, necessitating combination therapies to overcome these barriers [11, 12]. The ability of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors to modulate the immune response has positioned them at the forefront of CRC research, particularly in exploring novel therapeutic strategies such as early-stage application in neoadjuvant settings or combinations with other agents targeting the TME [13]. This evolving landscape underscores the critical need for tailored approaches to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects.

The potential of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors in the treatment of advanced CRC has garnered increasing attention. Clinical trials have demonstrated their efficacy in significantly extending overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with advanced disease [14]. However, the safety profile of these inhibitors, particularly when used in combination with chemotherapy, remains a critical area that requires thorough evaluation to ensure that the benefits outweigh the risks. The combination of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors with existing chemotherapy regimens may offer a synergistic effect, potentially enhancing therapeutic outcomes [15]. Despite the promising results, there is a notable gap in the literature, with a lack of comprehensive meta-analyses that quantitatively assess the efficacy and safety of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in this specific patient population.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the efficacy and safety of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in patients with advanced CRC by synthesizing data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Through a comprehensive analysis of existing evidence, this study seeks to contribute to the evolving IO paradigm, providing valuable insights for clinical practice and guiding the development of optimized treatment strategies that improve survival and quality of life for CRC patients.

Methods

Search strategy

This pre-registered systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [16]. The protocol was pre-registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42024611696). A comprehensive and systematic search was performed across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Web of Science, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) databases. The search spanned from the inception of each database up to August 3, 2024, without any language restrictions. The detailed search strategy, including specific terms, dates, and methodologies, is provided in Supplementary File 1. Manual review of reference lists of relevant articles and reviews was also conducted to identify additional studies. Title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening, was independently carried out in duplicate by two investigators (YL and KDW), with discrepancies resolved through discussion or by a third author (ZZW) if necessary.

Study selection

Two independent reviewers (YL and KDW) systematically assessed all identified abstracts and selected studies based on predetermined eligibility criteria. To be included, studies had to involve patients diagnosed with advanced CRC and investigate the use of PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors, comparing these treatments to placebo or chemotherapy alone. The primary outcomes of interest were efficacy and safety metrics. Only RCT was considered for inclusion.

Studies were excluded if participants had other types of cancer or comorbid conditions, if the interventions included treatments other than PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors, if data were incomplete, or if the publication type was a review, meta-analysis, abstract, letter, or communication. Full-text articles of potentially relevant studies were retrieved for further evaluation. Any discrepancies in the study selection process were resolved through discussion or by consultation with a third reviewer (ZZW) to ensure consensus.

Data extraction

Data from the selected studies were independently extracted by two authors (YL and KDW) using EndNote X9, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. Extracted data included study characteristics (authors, publication year, journal), participant demographics (sample size, age, sex), intervention details (e.g. type of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor, dosage), comparator details, and outcomes. For quantitative data, standard deviations (SD) were derived from standard errors (SE) using the formula: SD = SE × √n. When SE was unavailable, SD was estimated from confidence intervals, t-values, quartiles, ranges, or p-values as recommended in the Cochrane Handbook Sect. 7.7.3. Missing critical data prompted up to four contact attempts to the original study authors over six weeks to obtain necessary information.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes assessed in this study encompassed both efficacy and safety metrics. Efficacy outcomes included overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the start of treatment to death from any cause, and progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from the start of treatment until disease progression or death, whichever occurred first. Objective response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of patients achieving a predefined reduction in tumor size for a minimum duration, including both complete and partial responses. Safety outcomes focused on the incidence of treatment-related adverse events (TRAE), defined as any adverse event deemed causally related to the treatment by the investigator, and grade ≥ 3 TRAE, which referred to severe or life-threatening adverse events classified according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE). OS and PFS were treated as continuous variables, while ORR, any TRAE, and grade ≥ 3 TRAE were considered categorical variables. These outcomes were meticulously selected to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the benefits and risks associated with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors compared to chemotherapy for patients with advanced CRC.

Risk of bias

The quality of the cumulative evidence in this meta-analysis was assessed using the GRADE approach. This framework evaluates evidence across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The quality of evidence for each outcome was graded as high, moderate, low, or very low. Two reviewers (YL and KDW) independently conducted the assessments, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (ZZW).

Data synthesis

Heterogeneity among the included studies was quantified using the I2 statistic, categorized as follows: I2 < 25% indicated very low heterogeneity, 25% to < 50% low, 50% to < 75% moderate, and ≥ 75% high [17]. If I2 ≤ 0.1, a fixed-effects model was applied; if I2 > 0.1, a random-effects model was used to account for variability across studies. Effect sizes for continuous outcomes, specifically OS and PFS, were calculated as mean differences (MD) using reported means and standard deviations (SD). The choice of SD over hazard ratios (HR) was based on the availability of reported data across studies, as many studies provided summary statistics rather than time-to-event data. For categorical outcomes, such as ORR and AEs, risk ratios (RR) were used to determine effect sizes.

For outcomes with high heterogeneity, further sensitivity analyses were conducted. This involved sequentially removing individual studies to observe any significant changes in the overall effect size and heterogeneity. Consistent results centered around the line representing the average effect size indicated the absence of influential outlier studies. If substantial discrepancies were noted, the removed studies were further evaluated based on inclusion criteria and study quality to determine if exclusion was warranted. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA software version 13.0. A two-tailed p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To assess potential publication bias, adjusted funnel plots and Egger's test were employed [18], with a p-value < 0.05 indicating the presence of bias.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Through the initial electronic search, a total of 968 records were identified. After removing duplicates, 353 records underwent title and abstract screening, leading to a full-text assessment of 43 papers. Ultimately, nine studies involving 1680 participants were included in this review (Fig. 1) [19–27]. Among these, five studies focused on patients with dMMR CRC, including a total of 976 participants, while four studies focused on pMMR CRC, involving 704 participants. These studies primarily focused on patients with advanced CRC. The sample sizes ranged from 48 to 445, with a median of 180 participants. Participant ages varied from 55.7 to 65.5 years, with a median age of 62 years. The studies were published between 2020 and 2023, with a median publication year of 2021.

PRISMA Flow diagram of the search process for studies. RCT randomized controlled trials

Among the included studies, six studies assessed the use of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in the metastatic setting, while three studies evaluated their use in the adjuvant or neoadjuvant settings. Additionally, five studies investigated PD-L1 inhibitors combined with additional treatment regimens such as chemotherapy, targeted therapies (e.g., bevacizumab), and/or other immune checkpoint inhibitors [20, 21, 23, 24, 26]. For instance, the AtezoTRIBE trial combined atezolizumab with FOLFOXIRI plus bevacizumab, while another study assessed the addition of atezolizumab to capecitabine and bevacizumab in refractory settings. Radiotherapy was not included in these trials. The control groups comprised eight studies using chemotherapy and one study using Best Supportive Care [23]. Detailed information about the timing of immunotherapy and these additional regimens is provided in Supplementary File 2.

Risk of bias

Using the GRADE approach, the quality of evidence for key outcomes in this meta-analysis was evaluated. The OS and PFS outcomes were rated as moderate quality, with downgrades due to minor inconsistency among studies. The ORR was rated as low quality, primarily due to imprecision and inconsistency across the included trials. For safety outcomes, TRAE of any grade were assessed as low quality, reflecting imprecision in reporting. However, TRAE of grade ≥ 3 was rated as moderate quality, with downgrades for imprecision but consistent reporting across studies. Detailed GRADE assessments for all outcomes are summarized in Supplementary File 3.

Assessment of heterogeneity and inconsistency

High levels of heterogeneity were observed for both OS and PFS. Sensitivity analyses were conducted for OS and PFS (Supplementary File 4), revealing consistent results centered around the line representing the average effect size for OS, indicating no presence of outlier studies. For PFS, one study deviated significantly from the central line [19]; however, given the small number of included studies and the low risk of bias in this particular study, it was not excluded from the analysis.

Regarding publication bias, funnel plots (Supplementary File 5) were used to identify potential biases. The plots demonstrated symmetric distribution of scatter points along both sides of the vertical axis, indicating varying degrees of publication bias. Despite these individual instances, Egger's test results (P > 0.05) suggested no presence of small study effects across the analyzed data (Supplementary File 5), reinforcing the robustness of these findings.

The results of meta-analysis

Efficacy

Five studies involving 850 patients evaluated the impact of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors on OS. The meta-analysis indicated that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not significantly extend OS compared to the control group (MD = 0.86, 95% CI: −0.55, 2.27) (Fig. 2a). However, subgroup analysis based on the type of inhibitor showed differential effects: PD-1 inhibitors significantly improved OS (MD = 1.24, 95% CI: 0.20, 2.29), whereas PD-L1 inhibitors did not show significant improvement (MD = 0.61, 95% CI: −1.56, 2.79).

Forest plot of the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in advanced CRC (a, OS; b, PFS; c, ORR). OS, Overall Survival; PFS, Progression-Free Survival; ORR, Objective Response Rate

Seven studies involving 1289 patients assessed the effect of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors on PFS. The meta-analysis demonstrated that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors significantly prolonged PFS compared to the control group (MD = 2.53, 95% CI: 0.92, 4.15) (Fig. 2b). Subgroup analysis revealed that PD-1 inhibitors significantly extended PFS (MD = 6.27, 95% CI: 0.56, 11.97), whereas PD-L1 inhibitors did not show significant improvement (MD = −0.07, 95% CI: −0.36, 0.23).

Eight studies involving 1426 patients evaluated the impact of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors on ORR. The meta-analysis indicated that overall, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not significantly increase ORR compared to the control group (RR = 1.19, 95% CI: 0.99, 1.44) (Fig. 2c). Subgroup analysis showed no significant improvement in ORR for both PD-1 inhibitors (RR = 1.15, 95% CI: 0.90, 1.47) and PD-L1 inhibitors (RR = 1.25, 95% CI: 0.93, 1.67).

Safety

Six studies involving 1153 patients evaluated the occurrence of any AEs with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. The meta-analysis indicated that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not significantly increase the risk of any AEs compared to the control group (RR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.95, 1.14) (Fig. 3a). Further subgroup analysis showed no significant increase in any AEs for both PD-1 inhibitors (RR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.86, 1.14) and PD-L1 inhibitors (RR = 1.07, 95% CI: 0.95, 1.20).

Forest plot of the safety of Colorectal cancer (a, AEs; b, grade ≥ 3 AEs). AEs, adverse events

Nine studies involving 1667 patients assessed the occurrence of grade ≥ 3 AEs with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. The meta-analysis revealed that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not significantly increase the risk of grade ≥ 3 AEs compared to the control group (RR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.95, 1.22) (Fig. 3b). However, subgroup analysis indicated differential effects: PD-1 inhibitors did not significantly increase the risk of grade ≥ 3 AEs (RR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.76, 1.07), whereas PD-L1 inhibitors significantly increased the risk (RR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.07, 1.57).

Notably, the higher incidence of grade ≥ 3 AEs in the PD-L1 inhibitor group may be partially explained by the inclusion of combination treatment regimens in these trials. For instance, the AtezoTRIBE trial combined atezolizumab with FOLFOXIRI plus bevacizumab, which are known to contribute to severe toxicities. Such combination regimens could exacerbate chemotherapy-induced adverse effects or amplify immune-related toxicity when used alongside PD-L1 inhibitors. These findings underscore the importance of accounting for combination treatment regimens when evaluating safety outcomes, as they may significantly influence the overall adverse event profile of PD-L1 inhibitors.

Discussion

This meta-analysis, which included nine RCTs involving a total of 1680 patients with advanced CRC, systematically evaluated the efficacy and safety of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. The key findings of this study are as follows: First, the overall meta-analysis results indicated that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not significantly extend OS in patients with advanced CRC, although a significant improvement in PFS was observed. However, subgroup analyses revealed that PD-1 inhibitors were associated with significant improvements in both OS and PFS, while PD-L1 inhibitors did not show a statistically significant effect. Second, the analysis showed that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not provide a significant advantage in terms of ORR. Third, in terms of safety, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not significantly increase the risk of overall AEs. However, PD-L1 inhibitors were found to significantly increase the risk of grade 3 or higher adverse events, highlighting the importance of closely monitoring patients receiving these treatments.

This study contributes significantly to the field of CRC research by consolidating evidence on the differential efficacy and safety profiles of PD-1 versus PD-L1 inhibitors. The superior efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors, particularly in patients with dMMR tumors, reinforces their role as a cornerstone of treatment for these patients. Beyond immediate clinical applications, this work lays the foundation for advancing our understanding of immune checkpoint blockade mechanisms in CRC and beyond.

The timing of immunotherapy administration appears to play a critical role in determining treatment outcomes. Most included studies focused on the metastatic setting, where PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors demonstrated efficacy in delaying disease progression. However, limited data from neoadjuvant and adjuvant settings showed variability in outcomes, likely due to differences in patient selection and trial designs. Trials such as NICHE-1 and NICHE-2 have provided compelling evidence supporting the use of PD-1 inhibitors in neoadjuvant settings. NICHE-1 reported 100% major pathological response (MPR) and 63% complete pathological response (pCR) rates in dMMR patients. Similarly, NICHE-2 reinforced these findings, highlighting the potential of early immunotherapy to remodel the tumor immune microenvironment prior to systemic therapy [28, 29]. These results underscore the potential benefits of early intervention with PD-1 inhibitors, particularly in dMMR patients, to improve outcomes and potentially reduce reliance on more invasive interventions.

TME plays a pivotal role in modulating the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Tumors with a "hot" TME, characterized by high infiltration of effector T cells and pro-inflammatory cytokines, are more likely to respond to immune checkpoint blockade. Conversely, "cold" TMEs, lacking immune infiltration, often exhibit resistance [30]. In addition to TME characteristics, the role of organ preservation in CRC has gained increasing attention. Immunotherapy, particularly neoadjuvant PD-1 inhibitors, has demonstrated the potential to achieve significant tumor regression, thereby enabling less invasive surgical interventions or even non-operative management in select cases. For example, the NICHE trials showed that neoadjuvant immunotherapy can produce complete pathological responses, laying the groundwork for minimally invasive and organ-sparing approaches. Conversely, in the metastatic setting, the immune landscape is often more heterogeneous, potentially attenuating treatment efficacy.

Additionally, the presence of neoantigens, often associated with high tumor mutational burden (TMB), enhances the likelihood of a robust immune response. This explains the observed superior efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors in dMMR/ MSI-H CRC, where neoantigen load is high, compared to pMMR/ MSS tumors, which have lower TMB and reduced immunogenicity. Nevertheless, accurately identifying which patients will benefit most from immune checkpoint inhibitors remains a significant challenge. Tumor heterogeneity, including variability in neoantigen expression and TME composition, often complicates patient stratification. For instance, while high TMB is generally predictive of responsiveness, the interplay between TMB and other factors, such as immune cell infiltration and suppressive cytokines in the TME, may attenuate response rates in certain cases [31]. Furthermore, integrating these biomarkers into clinical practice requires robust validation and standardization to ensure their reproducibility and predictive value. However, while this immunogenicity presents opportunities, challenges remain in optimizing patient stratification and therapeutic sequencing to maximize response rates. For instance, in adjuvant settings, the immune response may be compromised by postoperative immune suppression, leading to residual micrometastatic disease and suboptimal treatment outcomes [32]. Developing comprehensive biomarker panels that incorporate TMB, neoantigen diversity, and immune cell phenotypes could improve patient selection and treatment efficacy in this phase.

In adjuvant settings, the immune response may face additional challenges due to postoperative immune suppression or residual micrometastatic disease. These complexities underscore the need for further research into biomarkers and optimal timing strategies for immunotherapy in this phase [33, 34]. Advances in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and spatial transcriptomics hold promise for monitoring minimal residual disease and tailoring adjuvant therapies. The density and subtypes of TILs are also pivotal in determining treatment response [35]. High densities of CD8 + cytotoxic T cells within the TME are strong predictors of favorable outcomes, whereas increased infiltration of immunosuppressive cells, such as FOXP3 + regulatory T cells, is associated with resistance. Emerging data suggest that the spatial distribution and activation states of TILs further refine response predictions, emphasizing the need for immune profiling in clinical decision-making [36].

PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors share a common mechanism of action by targeting immune checkpoint pathways, enhancing the immune system's ability to recognize and attack tumor cells [37]. However, the distinct clinical and biological behaviors of these inhibitors suggest differing therapeutic potentials, especially in organ preservation strategies. The role of organ preservation has gained increasing attention, particularly in CRC, where preserving rectal function significantly impacts quality of life. Immunotherapy, particularly neoadjuvant PD-1 inhibitors, has demonstrated the potential to achieve significant tumor regression, as evidenced by complete or near-complete pathological responses in dMMR/MSI-H tumors from trials like NICHE-1 and NICHE-2. These findings highlight the possibility of less invasive surgical interventions or even non-operative management in select cases. Integrating immunotherapy with radiotherapy or other localized therapies could further amplify tumor control while preserving organ integrity. Radiotherapy's immune-modulating properties may synergize with PD-1 inhibitors, particularly in sphincter-preserving strategies for rectal cancer, where functional outcomes are paramount [38].

Despite these promising advances, several challenges persist. Identifying suitable patient populations for organ preservation requires robust predictive biomarkers and improved diagnostic imaging. Additionally, longitudinal studies are needed to assess the durability of responses achieved through organ-sparing approaches, ensuring oncological safety without compromising quality of life. Future research should also explore the integration of advanced imaging techniques and ctDNA-based monitoring into clinical practice to refine treatment protocols and enable adaptive therapeutic strategies [39, 40].

Although this meta-analysis revealed that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not significantly extend OS in advanced CRC, the observed improvements in PFS suggest that these therapies may be more effective in delaying disease progression rather than achieving long-term survival benefits. This observation highlights the need to balance quality-of-life considerations with treatment efficacy, particularly in advanced-stage disease where progression control is often the primary therapeutic goal. Subgroup analyses further demonstrated notable differences between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors: PD-1 inhibitors significantly extended both OS and PFS, while PD-L1 inhibitors did not show comparable efficacy. These findings align with existing literature, which reports stronger anti-tumor activity of PD-1 inhibitors, such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab, particularly in patients with dMMR/MSI-H tumors [19, 22]. Conversely, the inconsistent efficacy of PD-L1 inhibitors underscores the need for further research to optimize their application and identify patient populations most likely to benefit. Such efforts may include novel combinations targeting complementary pathways, such as VEGF or TGF-beta inhibitors, to enhance responses in pMMR/MSS tumors and overcome intrinsic resistance mechanisms.

The differential efficacy between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors may be attributed to several factors. PD-1 inhibitors directly block the interaction between PD-1 on T cells and its ligands, PD-L1 and PD-L2, on tumor cells and other cells in the TME [41, 42]. This direct blockade may lead to a more robust and sustained activation of T cells, enhancing their ability to eliminate cancer cells [43]. In contrast, PD-L1 inhibitors only block the interaction between PD-L1 and PD-1, potentially leaving PD-L2-mediated immune suppression unchecked, which might explain the reduced efficacy observed with PD-L1 inhibitors [44, 45]. Additionally, the expression levels of PD-L1 on tumor cells can be variable, and in some cases, targeting PD-L1 alone may not be sufficient to elicit a strong anti-tumor response [46]. The variability in TIL density and immune infiltration further contributes to these differences, underscoring the importance of personalized treatment approaches. These insights reinforce the importance of patient stratification and tailored therapeutic strategies to maximize the clinical utility of immune checkpoint blockade.

ORR is a critical metric in oncology, reflecting the proportion of patients whose tumors significantly shrink or disappear following treatment. Previous studies have shown that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors can improve ORR to varying degrees across different cancer types. For instance, in melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer, these inhibitors have significantly increased ORR, often resulting in durable responses [47]. However, in CRC, the lack of a significant improvement in ORR observed in this study suggests unique challenges inherent to this disease. This discrepancy may be attributed to the unique dynamics of immune response with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, which differ from traditional therapies. For example, some patients may experience delayed immune activation or pseudoprogression, where tumors initially appear larger due to immune infiltration before shrinking [48]. These atypical response patterns highlight the limitations of standard ORR assessments in capturing the full therapeutic potential of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Advanced response evaluation methods, including immune-specific response criteria, are needed to better characterize these outcomes [49].

Additionally, intrinsic resistance mechanisms in CRC may also contribute to the lack of significant ORR improvement. Factors such as a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, low mutational burden in pMMR/MSS tumors, and limited infiltration of effector T cells collectively hinder the efficacy of checkpoint blockade [50]. Strategies aimed at overcoming these barriers—such as combining immune checkpoint inhibitors with agents targeting the TME (e.g., VEGF inhibitors) or therapies enhancing T-cell activation—could hold promise for increasing ORR in resistant CRC subtypes. These factors suggest that while PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors can delay disease progression (as reflected in improved PFS), they may not lead to immediate, measurable tumor shrinkage in all patients, as indicated by ORR.

Particularly for patients with advanced CRC, safety is a critical consideration. Ensuring that treatments not only extend survival but also maintain a manageable safety profile is essential for improving patient quality of life and overall clinical outcomes. This study found that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors did not significantly increase the incidence of overall AEs or grade ≥ 3 AEs compared to the control group. This result is encouraging, as it underscores the feasibility of incorporating these therapies into clinical practice without introducing undue toxicity. However, subgroup analysis revealed a notable distinction: PD-L1 inhibitors were associated with a significantly higher risk of grade ≥ 3 AEs (RR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.07–1.57), whereas PD-1 inhibitors did not show this increased risk (RR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.76–1.07).These findings are consistent with some previous studies but contrast with others that have reported varying safety profiles for PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors across different cancers. For example, some studies have noted higher incidences of immune-related AEs with PD-1 inhibitors [51, 52], whereas others have found that PD-L1 inhibitors are more likely to induce severe toxicities, especially when combined with chemotherapy [53].

The increased risk of grade ≥ 3 AEs associated with PD-L1 inhibitors may be attributed to differences in their mechanisms of action and interaction with the tumor microenvironment. PD-L1 inhibitors might engage broader immune pathways or lead to less selective immune blockade, potentially triggering off-target immune activation and increasing toxicity [54]. Moreover, PD-L1 inhibitors are often used in combination with cytotoxic agents, such as chemotherapy or VEGF inhibitors, which may amplify the immune system's activation, leading to overlapping toxicities and a heightened risk of severe adverse events. This insight underscores the importance of patient selection and monitoring in clinical practice, particularly for PD-L1 inhibitors. Clinicians should carefully evaluate patients' comorbidities, autoimmune predispositions, and prior treatment history before incorporating PD-L1 inhibitors into their treatment regimen. For instance, patients with autoimmune diseases or those receiving concurrent cytotoxic agents may benefit more from PD-1 inhibitors, which exhibit a lower risk of severe AEs in this analysis. Future research should prioritize the identification of predictive biomarkers, such as ctDNA or immune-related signatures, to stratify patients likely to benefit from PD-L1 inhibitors while minimizing toxicity risks. Moreover, further studies are needed to optimize combination regimens involving PD-L1 inhibitors. Strategies such as dose adjustments, scheduling modifications, or the incorporation of agents targeting complementary immune pathways, such as CTLA-4 or TIGIT, may enhance efficacy while mitigating adverse effects. Prophylactic interventions, including immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with immunomodulatory agents, could also help manage immune-related adverse events in high-risk patients [55]. Understanding these factors is critical for improving the safety profile of PD-L1 inhibitors and maximizing their clinical utility in advanced CRC. In addition to safety considerations, it is crucial to evaluate how treatment regimens can be tailored to specific TME. For instance, understanding how immunosuppressive TMEs in pMMR/MSS tumors can be reprogrammed using combination therapies may expand the utility of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. This area remains underexplored but represents a promising avenue for enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy in resistant subtypes.

Furthermore, another emerging area is the potential of utilizing PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors earlier in the disease course, such as in neoadjuvant settings or for organ preservation strategies. Current evidence, including data from trials like NICHE-1, highlights the benefits of early immunotherapy in reshaping the TME, potentially allowing for less invasive treatments [56]. Exploring the timing and sequencing of these therapies across disease stages will likely be pivotal in optimizing outcomes. Expanding on these approaches could reduce the reliance on cytotoxic therapies, mitigate long-term toxicities, and improve quality of life for patients.

This study has several strengths and limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, it represents the most comprehensive meta-analysis to date on the use of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in the treatment of advanced CRC, incorporating data from nine high-quality RCTs involving 1680 patients. The rigorous statistical methods employed, including subgroup and sensitivity analyses, enhance the robustness of the findings and provide a nuanced understanding of the differential efficacy and safety profiles of these inhibitors. However, the relatively small number of included studies represents a key limitation, potentially affecting the generalizability of the results and reducing the statistical power to detect smaller effect sizes or subgroup differences. While efforts were made to include all relevant high-quality RCTs, the limited number of studies also increases the likelihood of publication bias, as trials with positive findings are more likely to be published, potentially skewing the overall conclusions. Secondly, significant heterogeneity among the included studies—such as differences in patient populations, treatment regimens, follow-up durations, and reported outcomes—may introduce variability that affects the comparability of the results. For example, variations in patient selection criteria, including differences in MMR status (dMMR vs. pMMR), may influence treatment efficacy and safety outcomes, contributing to the observed differences across trials. Thirdly, the relatively short follow-up periods in some trials limit the ability to fully assess long-term outcomes such as overall survival (OS) and durable response rates. This limitation is particularly relevant for CRC subtypes like pMMR/MSS tumors, where delayed treatment effects might not be captured within the study timeframe. Future studies with extended follow-up periods will be critical for validating these findings. Finally, the absence of standardized reporting for certain outcomes, such as immune-related adverse events (irAEs) and detailed biomarker analyses, poses challenges for evaluating the broader applicability of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Variations in adverse event reporting across studies also make it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about safety profiles.

To address these limitations, future research should prioritize the following: (1) conducting large-scale RCTs with standardized protocols and extended follow-up durations; (2) exploring the integration of advanced biomarker technologies, such as ctDNA and transcriptomics, to refine patient stratification; (3) evaluating long-term survival benefits and durable response rates in diverse CRC populations; and (4) minimizing publication bias by encouraging the dissemination of neutral or negative findings. These efforts will be crucial for advancing the clinical application of immune checkpoint inhibitors in CRC and ensuring that findings are generalizable across varied patient populations.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis, including nine RCTs with 1680 patients with advanced CRC, reveals that PD-1 inhibitors significantly improve OS and PFS, while PD-L1 inhibitors do not demonstrate similar efficacy. PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors do not significantly increase overall AEs; however, PD-L1 inhibitors are associated with a higher risk of grade ≥ 3 AEs. These findings suggest that PD-1 inhibitors may offer a more favorable risk–benefit profile for advanced CRC, while PD-L1 inhibitors require careful consideration due to safety concerns. Future studies should aim to further clarify these findings and optimize treatment protocols for these therapies.
