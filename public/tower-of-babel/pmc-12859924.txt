Integrating microbiome and machine learning for precision diagnosis of rice bakanae disease
Authors: Sishi Chen, Fahui Yuan, Hongda Fang, Mostafa Gouda, Wenyuan Wu, Haixiang Zhang, Zhonghua Ma, Lei Feng, Mengcen Wang, Yufei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bakanae is a fungal rice disease that is threatening global rice production, causing severe yield losses. The plant microbiome plays a significant role in plant stress resistance, but its high-dimensional characteristics have not been fully exploited. Therefore, we integrated the microbiome and machine learning (ML) to diagnose bakanae disease in this study. We found significant correlations between Gammaproteobacteria and Bacteroidia and the severity of bakanae disease. We constructed different diagnosis models based on random forests (RF), support vector machines (SVM), and convolutional neural networks (CNN) on 88 biological replicates with an independent test set. We found that the RF model demonstrated strong performance across four taxonomic levels, with an accuracy of 88.9% and an F1 score of 94.1%. Notably, a Bray-Curtis dissimilarity-based extraction method was proposed to rapidly screen practical information from the original microbial community, which can enhance the model performance to a certain extent. According to phenotypic data, the disease severity of infected samples was classified into two levels (high and low infected levels) using the K-means clustering method. In the diagnosis of infection severity based on the family level, the model’s prediction accuracy reached 77.8%. Collectively, these findings highlight that the combination of microbiome with ML can advance diagnostic strategies for bakanae disease, providing new avenues for precision agriculture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13007-025-01486-2.

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a significant crop contributing to global food security. It holds strategic importance for countries highly dependent on its consumption, including Asian countries such as China, India, and Thailand [1]. However, rice productivity is affected by both biotic and abiotic stresses. Fusarium species are the most devastating biotic stresses, causing significant crop losses [2, 3]. Among these fungal pathogens, Fusarium fujikuroi (F. fujikuroi) is one of the most destructive pathogens causing bakanae disease in rice [4]. The bakanae disease may cause abnormal plant elongation, leaf chlorosis, and poor root development, resulting in a 10% to 50% reduction in rice yield [5].

Currently, the most prevalent method for eliminating rice bakanae disease is treating infected seeds with a variety of chemical fungicides [6]. Fungicide is for fungi, so not a direct effect on bacteria is expected. However, the consistent use of these chemical fungicides in rice has led to the development of resistance in pathogenic fungi over time, even affecting bacteria. Studies have widely documented that the excessive use of fungicides develops resistance in fungal pathogens [7, 8]. It is reported that the Fusarium species have developed resistance to a variety of fungicide classes, including triazoles, strobilurins, and benzimidazoles [9]. This resistance diminishes the fungicidal efficacy of the chemical fungicides or renders them completely ineffective in inhibiting the pathogen.

As shown in Fig. 1, the plant microbiome encompasses multiple plant-related components, including the aboveground (phyllosphere), underground (rhizosphere), and internal (endosphere) [10]. The infection of fungal pathogens can lead to significant disorder in the plant’s overall microbial pattern, including beneficial microbes such as Bacillus, Sphingomonas, Chitinophagaceae, and Flavobacteriaceae [11–13]. Within these changing communities, beneficial microbes recruited by plants combat diseases, while potentially harmful microbes manipulated by pathogens facilitate infections [14, 15]. Duran et al. [16] mentioned that the adverse effect could be controlled by bacterial classes such as Gammaproteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Betaproteobacteria, which inhibit the secretion of some fungal proteins. Beyond understanding their biological or ecological functions, there are also significant correlations between these community changes and infections, which play an essential role in disease detection and early warning [17].

In recent years, many scholars have begun to explore the potential applications of the microbiome in detecting plant diseases. By utilizing high-throughput sequencing technology and multi-omics methods, researchers can conduct a comprehensive exploration of the interactions between plants and their microbial communities [18, 19]. In cassava studies, Zhang et al. [20] conducted 16 S rRNA and internal transcribed spacer amplicon sequencing on 10 varieties of cassava. The results showed that Lactococcus sp. was significantly correlated with the disease resistance of cassava bacterial blight. In citrus systems, Blaustein et al. [21] found that the microbial communities in the leaf and root tissues, as well as their diversity, were closely related to the degree of Huanglongbing severity. In rice microbiome diagnostics, Edward et al. [22] successfully utilized random forests (RF) to predict the plant age based on the microbial communities in the rhizosphere of rice. These studies have demonstrated that microbial signatures can reflect plant disease status, but they predominantly rely on simple univariate correlations or low-resolution taxonomic comparisons. These approaches fail to fully utilize the high-dimensional and nonlinear patterns contained in the microbiome data.

Machine learning (ML), as a powerful tool for data analysis and prediction, has advantages in crop disease detection [23, 24]. Jena et al. [25] employed several ML-based methods to classify diseased and healthy rice leaves, and achieved high detection accuracy. Alyas and Mohanmed [26] used support vector machine (SVM) and K-means clustering techniques to process the images of crop disease samples in order to extract color and texture information. The results showed that the SVM classifier could effectively detect and identify crop plant diseases. Rathore et al. [27] proposed a lightweight convolutional neural network (CNN) model, which can detect rice plant diseases more accurately compared with some classic CNN models. However, these studies focused on the phenotypic characteristics of diseased plants, neglecting the role of inter-leaf microorganisms in the pathogen resistance.

Consequently, we integrated microbiome profiling and the powerful predictive capacity of ML to diagnose bakanae disease in rice seedlings. In this study, we analyzed the correlation between rice bakanae disease and microbial community changes based on the microbiome of rice seedlings. Further, using different ML algorithms (including RF, SVM, and CNN), we successfully diagnosed rice bakanae disease based on the microbiome data. Therefore, this study aims to develop and validate a microbiome-based ML diagnostic framework for rice bakanae disease.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

Three rice varieties were used as target samples, including Zhenuoyou 1 (v1) and Huazhongyou 9326 (v2) cultivars as subspecies Japonica, and Zhongzheyou 8 (v3) as a subspecies Indica. A total of 100 seeds were used, comprising 50 of v1, 25 of v2, and 25 of v3. We randomly assigned 80% of the seeds from each rice cultivar to the infection group, and the remaining 20% served as the control (CK) group. For surface sterilization, the method of Liu et al. [28] was followed with modifications. These seeds were washed with salt water (2.6%) and then soaked in hypochlorous acid (2.6%) for 45 min. We soaked these seeds in sterile water for 2 days at 28 ℃ in the dark to induce germination.

Fungus inoculation

Sterile vermiculite, autoclaved at 121 °C for 15 min, served as the growth substrate. After that, seedling pots were sprayed with 70% ethanol for disinfection and filled with sterile vermiculite. Mycelium blocks of 7-day-old F. fujikuroi strain JS16 excised by a sterile cork borer (d = 5 mm) were used as inoculum. The F. fujikuroi used in this study was originally isolated from a bakanae-diseased rice plant in Jiangsu Province, China, and its identity was confirmed by TEF-1 gene sequencing (provided in Supplementary Material 1) [29]. We placed the mycelium blocks into the seedling pot, covered with 5 mm thick vermiculite, and then sowed the germinated seeds of the infection group. Seeds assigned to the CK group were sown directly onto vermiculite without fungal inoculation. We covered the germinated seeds with a thin layer of vermiculite and then put the CK and the infected group in the different incubators for cultivation, with the following conditions set: temperature of 28 ℃, humidity of 80% RH, and light exposure for 12 h; temperature 20 ℃, humidity 80% RH, dark for 12 h.

Phenotypic traits acquisition

On the 9th, 15th, and 21st days after the rice seedlings were infected by F. fujikuroi, we obtained the data on plant height, leaf length, leaf angle, and chlorophyll content of rice seedlings. We measured the plant height and leaf length using a ruler (with an accuracy of 1 mm). Meanwhile, we took photos of the rice seedlings and used the software ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Germany) to measure the leaf angle. Continuous chlorophyll content measurement was obtained by SPAD chlorophyll content analyzer (SPAD-502, Konica Minolta, Japan) for each new leaf of rice seedlings. Additionally, the upper and lower parts of the leaves were measured each time, and the average value was used for the data analysis. The collection was repeated three times, and the average value was taken as the final data.

Classification of disease severity

In this study, the K-means clustering algorithm was employed to classify the severity of rice bakanae disease based on phenotype data, including plant height, leaf length, and chlorophyll content. K-means is an unsupervised clustering algorithm that divides data into K distinct clusters, such that the points within each cluster are similar to one another [30]. The optimal number of clusters (K) was determined by evaluating the within-cluster sum of squared errors (inertia) with different K values (1 to 10). Lower inertia indicates tighter clusters, indicating that the classification effect for the severity of bakanae disease is better. We aimed to achieve unsupervised classification of the severity of bakanae disease based on rice phenotypic data using the K-means clustering algorithm.

Microbiome sequencing

For microbial diversity identification, we applied 16 S rRNA sequencing to 100 leaf samples from different rice seedlings 21 days after being infected by F. fujikuroi. In the 16 S rRNA sequencing technology, the sequence variation degree of the nine short fragments, which are significantly higher than that of adjacent segments and have a faster evolutionary rate, is referred to as V1-V9. They are located between highly conserved segments and are commonly used to distinguish different species or strains. In this study, the V3-V4 fragment was used, which offers high taxon identification resolution and moderate sequence length, allowing for the compelling depiction of microbial community composition in the sample. We conducted sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, USA), with a read length of 2 × 250 base pairs.

Microbiome data processing

DADA2 (v2020.2.0) was used to quality-filter, denoise, and generate amplicon sequence variations (ASVs) and a feature table after the primer sequences were removed [31]. The SILVA (v138) was used to classify ASVs. Mitochondrial and chloroplast reads were removed from the bacterial datasets. Each rice seedling sample yielded 891 ASVs, and the most minor taxonomic units were resolved to the species level. Microorganisms with an abundance ratio of less than 0.01% after rarefaction are excluded to obtain representative data [32]. In addition, samples with a total abundance of less than 200 in the detection results would be removed to ensure the universality of the data. To reduce the dimensionality of the microbiome data and prevent overfitting of ML model, we clustered ASVs with a similarity of 97% to generate operational taxonomic units (OTUs). Based on OTUs, we analyzed the microbial community changes before and after infection. In addition, the Shannon Index (SHDI) was used to analyze taxa abundance for each seedling sample, where a higher SHDI indicates greater community diversity.

Key taxa extraction

An excessive number of taxa annotated by OTUs contains redundant information, which can affect the analytical ability of the model. To rapidly extract practical information, we proposed an extraction method for key taxa based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. As shown in Fig. 2, we removed one taxon from the original microbial community (containing x taxa) of the infected group at a time, and then calculated the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity between the new community and that of the healthy sample. This process was repeated x times to select n microbial communities with the smallest dissimilarity results. The smaller the dissimilarity, the more similar the microbial composition of the removed taxa is to that of the healthy sample. The presence of these taxa has a significant impact on changes in microbial composition; therefore, the corresponding removed taxa are considered key taxa.

Algorithm selection and model construction

To explore suitable prediction algorithms, three ML algorithms were applied, including RF, SVM, and CNN. The data were randomly divided into a training set, validation set, and prediction set in a 3:1:1 ratio. To obtain optimal model performance, the number of decision trees of the RF model was determined through a grid search process, with the search range between 25 and 50. For SVM, we determined the regularization parameter and kernel function parameter through a grid search process, with the search range from 10− 8 to 108. In addition, we designed a lightweight CNN optimized with the backpropagation algorithm for bakanae disease diagnosis. The detailed layer parameters of the CNN are provided in Supplementary Material 2. Using the RF, SVM, and CNN algorithms, we constructed diagnostic models of rice bakanae disease based on the phenotypic characteristics and the microbial community, respectively. In this study, all models use the same dataset split and random seed, and the random seed was uniformly set to 24. The performance of the models was evaluated using the accuracy and F1 score.

Results and analysis

Microbiome disparity in diseased and healthy rice seedlings

The study analyzed the interaction of F. fujikuroi with its rice host microbiota. Taxonomic classification of ASVs was performed against the SILVA (v138) database. The results of ASVs clustering produced 6, 20, 35, and 50 taxa in class, order, family, and genus, respectively. After data processing, a total of 88 biological replicates were used in this study. The relative abundance of microorganisms in the 5 classes of the infected group showed differences compared to the CK group (Fig. 4a), especially in Bacteroidia, Gammaproteobacteria, and Bacilli classes. It indicates that the invasion of the F. fujikuroi caused significant changes in the microbial community. As shown in Fig. 4b–d, we found significant differences in the relative composition of microbial communities between the infected and CK groups of different varieties. At the class level, the changes in Gammaproteobacteria and Bacteroidia were more pronounced. Overall, taxa with higher relative abundance tend to exhibit more significant changes after infection.

We normalized the abundance and visually presented the taxonomic composition and diversity of each sample through a heatmap (Fig. S1). Based on the performance of the three varieties, we found that at the class level, the relative abundance of Gammaproteobacteria decreased while that of Bacteroidia increased. At the order level, the relative abundance of Clostridiales decreased, while Burkholderiales and Xanthomonadales increased. The Bray-Curtis dissimilarity of microbial communities based on different taxonomic levels in the samples of the three varieties is shown in Fig. 5a. As the taxonomic level was refined, the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity increased, and the differences in microbial communities between healthy and infected samples became more pronounced. The Bray-Curtis dissimilarity of v2 is the smallest among the three varieties. Furthermore, we calculated the SHDI of the samples from the CK and infected groups at the genus level. The results showed significant differences between the two groups, with a P value of 0.011 (P < 0.05).

Microbial changes correlate with disease severity

As the taxonomy level was subdivided, the changes in microbial composition became more complex and challenging to capture. Thus, we conducted a correlation analysis between changes in microbial taxa and disease severity based on class level. Bakanae disease can directly cause abnormal plant height and chlorophyll content of rice seedlings. Samples with higher plant height and lower chlorophyll content were considered to be more highly infected with F. fujikuroi. Therefore, we used the plant height and chlorophyll content as quantitative standards for the infection severity.

Based on the plant height and chlorophyll content, we analyzed the Pearson correlation coefficient in 88 biological replicates, revealing a significant correlation between the taxa’s relative abundance and the severity (Fig. 6a–d). The results showed that the disease outbreak led to a relative decrease in the content of the Gammaproteobacteria and a relative increase in the content of the Bacteroidia. Bruto et al. [33] studied the plant microbiome and found that some taxa in the Gammaproteobacteria have characteristics that promote plant growth. In addition, Bacteroidia, a type of Gram-negative bacterium, also has a significant impact on the ability of plants to resist external stress [34]. Compared with the CK group, the relative abundance of Gammaproteobacteria taxa in the infected group decreased, while the relative abundance of Bacteroidia increased. The decrease in the content of Gammaproteobacteria is associated with the inhibition of plant growth, while the increase in the content of Bacteroidia is related to plant defense. These findings suggest that the change in microbial community has the potential to serve as an indicator of rice bakanae disease infection.

Further, we used a K-means clustering algorithm based on the phenotype data of infected samples to classify the infection severity. K-means partitioned infected samples according to plant height, leaf length, and chlorophyll content, grouping phenotypically similar individuals. The elbow rule diagram after K-means clustering model training is shown in Fig. S2a. The level of distortion was significantly improved when K = 2, which was considered the optimal number of clusters (Fig. S2b). The sample data were classified into two levels (infected level 1 and infected level 2). The 3D scatter plot shows that the plant height of samples in infected level 1 was lower and the chlorophyll content was higher, while the samples of infected level 2 were the opposite (Fig. 6e). Therefore, it is believed that the infected level 1 represents a lower severity, while the infected level 2 represents a higher severity.

In addition, we compared the SHDI of the two infected and CK group samples at the class level (Fig. 6f). The SHDI of the two infected groups reflects the imbalance of microbial composition. It shows significant differences from healthy samples with P-values of 0.002 and 0.009, respectively (P < 0.01). In addition, the SHDI of low infection severity is lower, which may be caused by the small number of samples belonging to high infection severity in the test sample, because the ratio of the samples with low and high infection severity was approximately 2:1.

ML-driven diagnostic model based on microbial changes

In terms of diagnostic models based on phenotypic data, CNN has demonstrated outstanding performance among the three models with 93.8% accuracy and 96.3% F1 score based on four phenotypic traits (Table S1). Both SVM and RF reached an accuracy of 81.3% and an F1 score of 87.0% which were lower than that of CNN. Notably, the microbial communities were closely related to the plant growth status. By mining microbial information, the plant health status was predictably modeled (Fig. 7a–d). The results show that the accuracy and F1 score of the models gradually increased from the class level, but did not further improve at the genus level. It indicates that the complex microbial community may pose difficulties for model analysis.

At the family and genus levels, SVM models had good prediction performance, with an accuracy of 88.9% on both the validation prediction sets. However, the SVM model performed poorly at the other two taxonomy levels. The CNN model reached a diagnosis accuracy of 90.4% at the family level but performed poorly on the validation set. In contrast, the RF model has stable prediction performance and consistent results across the four taxonomy levels. In the training, validation and prediction sets, the diagnosis accuracy was 100.0%, 83.3%, and 88.9%, respectively. The F1 score was maintained at 94.1% at all taxonomy levels. The results indicate that the RF model was more suitable for information mining of microbial communities, with high accuracy and substantial stability advantages. Meanwhile, these results show that diagnosis accuracy based on the original microbiome data can be comparable to that based on phenotype data.

Diagnostic model of key taxa-based and infection severity

In this study, we proposed an extraction method for key taxa based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity to extract practical information from the original microbial community. Key taxa were extracted based on different taxonomy levels (Table S2). As only six effective taxa at the class level, we did not extract key taxa at this level. For the order level, 10 taxa were extracted; 20 taxa were extracted for the family level, and 30 taxa were extracted for the genus level. Additionally, we attempted to extract key taxa for the original data (species level), resulting in 30 taxa.

Using the RF model for prediction, we found that the extracted key taxa can improve the model’s predictive ability to a certain extent. Specifically, based on order-level data, the RF model improved accuracy by 5.6% on the validation set, while the prediction set results remained unchanged. Based on family-level data, the RF model reached an accuracy of 100.0% for all three datasets (training set, validation set, and prediction set), demonstrating extremely high diagnosis accuracy. However, the perfect performance was obtained without additional diagnostic experiments. The reported accuracy may reflect over-optimistic data splits rather than generalizability. We emphasize that these results should be regarded as proof-of-concept and require strict external validation before any practical application. In addition, based on species-level data, the RF model reached 100.0%, 94.4%, and 88.9% diagnosis accuracy on three datasets. Compared with the original microbiome data at the four taxonomy levels (class, order, family, and genus), the accuracy of the validation set improved by 11.1%. The results show that the extraction method can effectively extract practical information from raw datasets.

Further, we also attempted to construct a prediction model of infection severity based on the microbial community at the family level. The sample grouping was based on the analysis results of K-means clustering and 3D scatter visualization of phenotypic data of cluster samples, which divided the samples into the CK group, low-infected group, and high-infected group. The prediction results are shown in Fig. 8, with accuracies of 100.0%, 94.4%, and 77.8% on the three datasets, respectively. These results indicate that combining ML with microbiome-based diagnostic methods for plant disease severity has excellent potential.

In the extracted key taxa, the Gammaproteobacteria have the highest number of microbial taxa (Fig. 9), consistent with the correlation analysis in Fig. 6a, c. As shown in Fig. 9, the results showed that the top five most predictive microbiota are Enterobacteriaceae, Comamonadaceae, Rhizobiaceae, Erwiniaceae, and Clostridiaceae. Discussion

Biological implications

The present study has demonstrated the feasibility of assessing rice bakanae disease severity using changes in microbial community composition. Infected rice plants typically have a low difference in reflectance in the visible spectral region because of strong absorption by chlorophylls. The results indicate that the diagnosis accuracy based on microbiome data was higher than that based on phenotype data. In contrast, acquiring microbial community data is faster and more accurate, enabling more precise disease detection. Infection with F. fujikuroi resulted in a significant decrease in pigments, primarily chlorophylls, which are closely linked to photosynthetic efficiency and overall plant health conditions (Fig. 6a). This trend is consistent with the findings of Xiang et al. [35], which were attributed to their implications for chlorophylls, carotenoids, anthocyanins, and xanthophylls in response to the infection, causing the pigments to deteriorate and absorb less efficiently in the visible region.

We found that there were significant differences in the microbial communities of diseased and healthy rice seedlings. The changes in the Gammaproteobacteria and Bacteroidia are highly correlated with the severity of bakanae disease, which has the potential to serve as an indicator of disease infection. Both of the two taxa play a significant role in the growth and stress resistance of plants. We observed that after rice was infected with bakanae disease, the abundance of Gammaproteobacteria decreased, while the abundance of Bacteroidia increased. It indicates that the decrease in Gammaproteobacteria content is associated with the inhibition of plant growth and development, while the increase in Bacteroidia content is related to the antagonism of pathogen–microbiome and the plant defense mechanism. We extracted the key taxa that were more predictive for the ML model based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. The majority of them belonged to the Gammaproteobacteria, which indicated a strong correlation and predictability between the Gammaproteobacteria and the severity of bakanae disease.

Model performance

In this study, we used RF, SVM, and CNN models to diagnose rice bakanae disease. The results showed that the models based on microbiome data could be comparable to the models based on phenotypic characteristics. Compared with the other two models, the RF model demonstrated superiority in the microbial-based model, with stable prediction performance across the four taxonomy levels. Additionally, we proposed a key taxa extraction method based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. By extracting key taxa, the predictive performance of the RF model was further improved, indicating that the proposed extraction method can extract practical information from high-dimensional microbiome data. The model based on family-level data had a diagnostic accuracy of 100%, which is an encouraging but potentially misleading initial indicator. This is the result obtained from a relatively small sample size and multiple rounds of training-validation-testing. Specifically, this study lacks nested cross-validation for estimating generalization variance. However, we emphasize that these results should be regarded as a proof-of-concept and require strict external validation in the future. In the future, the large-scale verified microbiome-ML integration can be extended for early disease surveillance using portable sequencing devices.

Conclusion

In this study, we found a significant correlation between the severity of the rice bakanae disease and the changes in the microbial community, especially Gammaproteobacteria and Bacteroidia. Due to this strong correlation, the performance of models based on microbiome data can be comparable to that of models based on phenotypic data. Specifically, the RF model reached a prediction accuracy of 88.9% across four taxonomy levels with 88 biological replicates. Further, the key taxa extracted based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity enhanced the performance of the model. In the diagnosis of infection severity based on the family level, the model’s prediction accuracy reached 77.8%. It is noted that due to the limited sample size and the lack of temporal validation, our results should be regarded as a proof-of-concept. In the future, we will conduct a large-scale and rigorous cross-validation to enhance the generalizability of the model. Considering that the current analysis primarily focuses on the family level and may overlook some species-specific associations relevant to plant disease. In further research, a more detailed analysis at the genus or species level will be conducted. Collectively, it is highlighted that ML-facilitated microbiome analysis has significant applications in crop disease detection, offering important implications for securing sustainable agricultural production.
