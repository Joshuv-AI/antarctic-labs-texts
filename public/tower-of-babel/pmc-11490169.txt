Expanding families: a pilot study on preconception expanded carrier screening in Bahrain
Authors: Cristina Skrypnyk, Rawan AlHarmi, Aanchal Mathur, Hussein Hifnawi AlHafnawi, Sri Hari Chandan Appikonda, Lova Satyanarayana Matsa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreconception expanded carrier screening (ECS) is a genetic test that enables the identification of at-risk carriers of recessive disorders by screening for up to hundreds of genes. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) development has paved the way for its integration into ECS. This study aims to identify the carrier genetic status of couples experiencing or anticipating conception challenges through NGS-based ECS and to gain an overview of the rare genetic disorders in a population with increased consanguinity.MethodsThirty couples who presented to the Genetic Disease Clinic between 2015 and 2024 with failed reproductive outcomes or with a positive personal or family history of genetic disorders and underwent ECS were included and retrospectively analyzed.ResultsFifty-four individuals (90.00%) were found to carry at least one variant of 95 identified genes, totaling 174 variants. Six individuals (10.00%) tested negative for any variant. Seven individuals had one variant (11.67%), 13 had two variants (21.67%), and 34 had 3 or more variants (56.67%). The most common variants identified were of HBA, HBB, CYP21A2, and G6PD genes. Most of the detected variants were unknown or unexpected (n = 143, 82.18%). Eight couples carried two or more variants in common. Consanguinity was reported in 14 couples (46.67%).ConclusionsPreconception ECS is crucial for reproductive planning, permitting couples to evaluate their combined genetic risks and make informed decisions, reducing the chance of having children with genetic disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-024-06878-1.

Background

Preconception expanded carrier screening (ECS) is a genetic test that enables the identification of couples at high risk of having a child with recessive disorders through screening for up to several hundred genetic disorders. ECS represents an extension to conventional carrier screening tests that investigate a limited range of disorders. Most importantly, it allows room for couples to make an informed reproductive decision based on calculated estimates [1–4]. For instance, in autosomal recessive (AR) disorders, if both parents are carriers of the disorder, offspring have a 25% risk of inheriting the disorder. This risk is increased to 50% in a male child of a mother carrying an X-linked (XL) condition [5].

Tracing its roots back to 1971, carrier screening was first conducted on a community level targeting Tay-Sachs disease (TSD) in Ashkenazi Jews [6–8]. Since this pioneering experience, there has been a dramatic decrease in the incidence of TSD among Jewish populations [9]. In general, carrier screening utilizes technologies such as next-generation sequencing (NGS), polymerase chain reaction (PCR), Sanger sequencing, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA), and microarray, among others [10]. The evolution of NGS has set the stage for its integration into ECS approaches, rendering ECS nothing but dynamic [1]. NGS offers greater clinical sensitivity, unparalleled throughput, and reduced cost [11, 12]. From the past to the present, ECS has transcended ethnicity, transitioning from targeting a single ethnic-specific disorder to encompassing a panel of disorders [1]. Moreover, exome sequencing (ES), an NGS method, has demonstrated its feasibility with a high diagnostic yield for AR disorders in consanguineous couples [13].

Efforts are being made to implement ECS in clinical practice. As a case in point, the National Society of Genetic Counselors (NSGC) recommends ECS to all individuals considering reproduction and all pregnant reproductive pairs [14]. Furthermore, a tiered approach is recommended based on risk assessment, with specifics determined by two prominent organizations: the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) [10]. A key aspect addressed by these recommendations and relevant to the populations of Middle Eastern countries is consanguinity. Genetic counseling is recommended for consanguineous couples (second cousins or closer), followed by Tier 4 screening, the most comprehensive panel that screens for conditions with a carrier frequency below 1/200 [10, 15]. The consensus is that the optimal timing for ECS should be preconception, allowing ample time for reproductive decisions and subsequent interventions. For couples, it can be conducted either successively or simultaneously, with the latter being favored in the event of an ongoing pregnancy [16]. From an ethical and social standpoint, it is imperative to provide appropriate pre- and post-testing genetic counseling and psychosocial support to aid the couples in weighing the pros and cons of being tested and handling the consequences of the results [17, 18].

Consanguineous marriages are prevalent in the Middle East region including the Gulf countries and are known to contribute to genetic disorders, particularly AR disorders [19]. In Bahrain, spanning a decade from 1990 to 2009, a decline in the prevalence of first-cousin marriages was witnessed, plummeting from 24% to nearly 7% [20]. The most prevailing genetic disorders among Arabs include hemoglobinopathies, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, and some metabolic disorders [21]. Concerning carrier genetic profiling, our research uncovered no recent studies specifically addressing this subject in Bahrain. Premarital screening was introduced in Bahrain in 1985. By 2004, the legislation mandated premarital genetic counseling through primary health centers, with particular emphasis on identifying hemoglobinopathy carriers. Despite the mandatory testing, couples retain the autonomy to decide whether to follow the genetic advice provided, resulting in some proceeding with the marriage despite genetic risks [22, 23]. It is worth noting that premarital screening is achieved by biochemical testing through hemoglobin electrophoresis, followed by genetic counseling referral and genetic testing, if indicated.

This study aims to identify the carrier genetic status of couples facing or anticipating conception challenges through NGS-based ECS and gain an overview of the rare genetic disorder carrier frequency in a population with increased consanguinity.

Methods

Study population, setting, and design

Thirty couples who presented to the Genetic Disease Clinic at the University Medical Center, King Abdullah Medical City, Arabian Gulf University, Bahrain, between 2015 and 2024, with failed reproductive outcomes or with a positive personal or family history for genetic disorders, and underwent ECS, were included and retrospectively analyzed. Personal and family history, in addition to laboratory data, were collected from the medical records.

Pre-testing genetic counseling

During the initial genetic counseling visit, after collecting the patient’s data and family history and reviewing previous medical investigations, the need for ECS was conveyed. The test suggested, purpose, process of the testing, result interpretation, accuracy, and limitations were discussed with each couple. Special emphasis was made on the emotional impact of a possible positive result and the right of autonomy in accepting or declining the test. As the test used is NGS-based, the option to know secondary unexpected/incidental findings was communicated. Confidentiality, protection of private information, and implications for the patient and the relatives were emphasized.

NGS Panel and genetic analysis

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from each participant and DNA isolation was performed. Genetic testing via NGS-based ES was done in collaboration with external genetic diagnostic laboratories. Approximately 45 Mb of the genome, corresponding to 99% of the Consensus Coding Sequence (CCDS) obtained from Reference Sequence (RefSeq), GENCODE, and ENSEMBL, were enriched from fragmented DNA with probes designed against the human genome (Nextera DNA Flex Pre-Enrichment Library Prep and Illumina Exome Panel). The library generated was sequenced with S2/S4 Reagents Kits (Illumina, San Diego, CA, United States) on the NovaSeq 6000 Sequencing System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, United States). Raw sequencing data were processed by in-house bioinformatics pipelines. The bioinformatics procedure includes the detection of germline single nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions or deletions. Only the variants that could give rise to biallelic variants in offspring were selected for the couple and subsequently filtered against a gene panel consisting of 2,380 genes associated with known AR or XL disorders (Table S1) based on Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database [24]. The remaining variants were classified according to the ACMG/Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) guidelines, after which only likely pathogenic (LP) and pathogenic (class IV/V) variants were reported individually in the partners. Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) and ClinVar reported pathogenic and LP intronic and intergenic variants were included in the study along with exonic variants.

ES testing run specifically included SMN1 and FMR1 gene testing. PCR amplification of the CGG trinucleotide repeat region in the FMR1 gene was performed. Triplet-primed PCR was conducted to differentiate normal homozygous females from heterozygotes with expansions. Fragment analysis was done using capillary electrophoresis (CE). Moreover, deletions of exon 7 of SMN1 gene were detected using duplex quantitative PCR (qPCR) for the region of interest (SMN1 exon 7). If needed, the study was extended to MLPA to analyze exons 7 and 8 of the SMN1/SMN2 genes using SALSA® MLPA® Probemix P060-B2 SMA Carrier Kit (MRC Holland, Amsterdam, Netherlands) followed by CE and analysis using Coffalyser.net software. The turnaround time was 4–5 weeks.

We included all variants reported by ECS and reassessed the variant significance based on recent database search including ClinVar, VarSome, InterVar, gnomAD, and UniProt [25–29]. In 7 out of 30 couples (11, 13, 14, 16, 21, 26 and 30), one partner was affected by a genetic disorder or there was a positive history of an affected undiagnosed child. Therefore, in addition to ECS, we also performed diagnostic ES for these individuals and reported variants of uncertain clinical significance (VOUS) that correlated with their phenotype. Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) score was calculated for these VOUS [30]. Moreover, 8 out of 30 couples (5, 10, 11, 18, 19, 22, 25 and 27) reported a history of one or more children affected by an AR disorder, previously confirmed by diagnostic ES. Accordingly, parental ECS focused specifically on the gene variants identified in their affected children. Family history and ES data were incorporated after the analysis was done.

Post-testing genetic counseling

The post-testing counseling session helped patients understand the significance of their genetic results and make informed health and family decisions. The test results were explained, including their medical implications, health risks, and further management options. Inheritance patterns and risks to relatives were elucidated. The emotional impact of the results, such as anxiety or relief, was addressed, and support was offered. Patients were reassured again about the confidentiality of their results. Reproductive planning options, such as preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders (PGT-M), prenatal testing, and natural conception, were presented in detail. Additional reliable resources were provided for further reading.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS®) software for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, Unites States). A significance level of probability (P-value) of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Population demographics

The screened population consisted of 30 couples, each comprising one female and one male. The ages of the females ranged from 21 to 38 years, while the males were between 24 and 48 years old. Consanguinity was observed in 14 couples, accounting for 46.67% of the sample. The couples were referred for screening by obstetricians and gynecologists or were self-referred to the clinic. Screening indications included premarital or preconception screening, recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL), miscarriage, stillbirth and neonatal death, ectopic pregnancy, affected pregnancy, infertility, failed in vitro fertilization (IVF), and a personal or family history of genetic disorders (Table 1).

Distribution of identified genes and carrier frequencies

In the screened couples, 6 individuals (10.00%) tested negative for gene variants. The remaining participants (n = 54, 90.00%) were found to carry at least one variant among the 95 identified genes, totaling 174 gene variants (Table 1). Four of these variants were in the homozygous state. The most common disorders identified were alpha-thalassemia, sickle cell disease (SCD), congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency, and G6PD deficiency, each with different gene variants (Fig. 1). Additionally, genes associated with deafness were identified in 8 individuals, and genes associated with eye disorders were also prevalent (Table 1; Fig. 1). We identified 31 variants in known or expected genes (17.82%) while most of the detected variants were unknown or unexpected (n = 143, 82.18%). Eighteen couples (60%) were known or expected to be carriers while unknown or unexpected variants were identified in all of the couples (100.00%) (Table S2). In non-consanguineous couples, 7 out of 16 couples (43.75%) shared at least one identical variant (11 variants), while 8 out of 14 consanguineous couples (57.14%) shared at least one variant (14 variants). Eight couples in total were identified to be carriers of two or more variants in common (Table S3). On average, there were 2.90 variants per individual (3.03 in females and 2.77 in males) and 5.80 variants per couple (Table S4). Moreover, 7 individuals had one variant (11.67%), 13 individuals had two variants (21.67%), and 34 individuals had 3 or more variants (56.67%). After excluding disorders that can be detected on premarital screening (i.e. thalassemias, SCD, and G6PD deficiency), the frequencies were 12 (20%), 12 (20%), 15 (25%), and 21 (35%) respectively.

Degree of consanguinity

The results demonstrated no association between the presence of variants and consanguinity in marriage (χ2 = 0.54, P-value 0.71). The Kruskal-Wallis H test revealed that the distribution of the number of variants is the same across the 4 categories of inbreeding coefficient, including non-consanguineous (P-value 0.60). Despite the difference in the means presented in Table S3, the Mann–Whitney U test revealed that the distribution of the number of variants is the same across consanguineous and non-consanguineous couples (P-value 0.50).

Discussion

In this study, NGS-based ECS was employed to determine the genetic carrier status of couples who were considering pregnancy, currently pregnant or had experienced adverse pregnancy outcomes, in a population with high consanguinity rates. Our findings indicate that ECS identified both shared and previously unknown variants in at-risk individuals and those from the general population. The study highlights how ECS has benefited families by preventing genetic disorders and enabling informed reproductive choices.

Carrier screening can be performed using ethnicity-based or pan-ethnic approaches. Ethnicity-based screening relies on accurate ancestry reporting to select the appropriate test. In contrast, ECS utilizes a pan-ethnic approach [31]. The decision to proceed with testing involves shared decision-making [14]. Pre-testing counseling should highlight and offer these approaches and elucidate that ECS screens for AR and XL disorders but can also reveal an autosomal dominant (AD) disorder in the screened individuals [31]. The inheritance patterns and frequencies of AR and XL disorders should be explained, emphasizing that shared carrier status in parents is crucial for the occurrence of AR disorders in offspring, with a 25% recurrence risk. Counseling should also address that a positive result in one partner typically does not impact their health and should highlight the possibility of secondary unexpected findings [14]. It is imperative that the consultands thoroughly comprehend the scope, benefits, risks, and limitations of ECS prior to providing informed consent.

In our study, 8 out of 14 consanguineous couples (57.14%) had at least one shared identical variant and two of them had 3 variants in common. In contrast, 7 out of 16 non-consanguineous couples (43.75%) shared at least one variant. This suggests the presence of consanguinity in their ancestral lineage and a common gene pool, as some of them are indeed far-related, i.e. beyond the second generation. Additionally, the degree of consanguinity did not correlate with the number of shared variants among couples (Table S3). We believe that this should be interpreted with caution due to the small size of our cohort and the sampling method, given the same geographic area of the consultands. Nonetheless, this could also be a true reflection of our population. Contrary to our findings, Sallevelt et al. [13] reported that an increasing degree of consanguinity did correlate with the number of identical shared variants among 56 consanguineous couples out of 100 studied, including far-related ones. Moreover, the most frequently identified disorders in our cohort were alpha-thalassemia (Frequency reported on Orphanet: 1:10,000–1:1,000,000), SCD (1–5:10,000), CAH due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency (1–9:100,000), and G6PD deficiency (< 1:1,000,000) [32]. Similar findings were reported in a study that included more than 1,314 individuals from Saudi Arabia, a population with a high consanguinity rate. CAH, SCD, and G6PD deficiency were among the most commonly identified disorders in ES-based carrier screening [33]. Similarly, in another study from Saudi Arabia that involved more than 1,000 individuals, the most commonly detected carrier statuses were for SCD and G6PD deficiency [34]. Moreover, in a large-scale study in Saudi Arabia on more than 2,200 families who underwent ES, 961 variants were identified, of which 742 were AR and 37 were XL. The most frequently identified gene variants were that of ADAT3, SLC25A42, and FBXL4, none of which were identified in our cohort. Yet, variants of HBB and G6PD were frequent in this study population [35].

Having a previous child affected was one of the major reasons to seek genetic consultation and to identify other potential unknown risks. Eight out of 30 couples had a history of having one or more children affected by an AR disorder, confirmed previously by diagnostic ES. Consequently, parental ECS specifically targeted the gene variants previously identified in their affected children. In couple 11, in addition to the known COL11A2 gene variant previously reported in their affected children, ECS combined with diagnostic ES identified a common carrier status for two additional variants in NEB and LARGE1 genes, which are associated with a risk for rare muscular disorders in their offspring. Both variants had a CADD score above 20 and were therefore considered deleterious. In couples 18 and 25, besides the previously known carrier status for the disorders reported in their children, a common carrier status for alpha thalassemia was identified. In couple 27, apart from the common carrier status for lethal congenital contracture syndrome 11 diagnosed in their previously affected child, the ECS revealed a common carrier status for CAH due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency.

Reproductive challenges were another reason to seek genetic evaluation. The female partner in couple 10 presented for counseling with an 8-month ongoing pregnancy, which later ended in stillbirth. Prenatal fetal ES identified a homozygous state for an MKS1 gene variant, correlating with the phenotype of a previous miscarriage, and a CYP21A2 gene variant. ECS identified the shared carrier state of the two genes in addition to CFTR, highlighting the impact of genetic pool retention with high consanguinity. Moreover, in couple 23, ECS identified the female partner to be a carrier of an FMR1 variant, highlighting its importance in addressing primary ovarian insufficiency (POI) and identifying disorders that affect the parent and pose a risk to the offspring. Furthermore, ECS was performed for couple 26 prior to PGT-M, considering the high-risk nature of pregnancy since the female partner is a known case of Marfan syndrome.

ES has proven to be efficient for ECS in both consanguineous and non-consanguineous couples. Xu et al. [36] conducted ES-based preconception screening in China and identified 21 metabolic disorders in a population of unrelated individuals. This emphasizes the importance of ES in detecting significant variants. Another study conducted in China involving couples seeking assisted reproductive technology identified more than one disease-causing variant in 94.5% of the screened individuals [37]. In our study, 90.00% of the individuals were found to carry at least one variant. Even after excluding disorders identifiable through premarital screening, genetic variants were still found in the individuals being evaluated.

Consultands should be informed that variant interpretations can be updated, either upgraded or downgraded. Currently, guidelines do not recommend reporting VOUS, but counseling and informed consent should address these once they become standard in ECS panels [14]. In the context of ECS that has ruled out a shared carrier status of a serious disorder where a child is homozygous for a VOUS, targeted parental testing for the variant is necessary and this should be subsequently considered for PGT-M if it matches the phenotype. However, the lack of guidelines on whether PGT-M should be offered for VOUS and low penetrance variants can be viewed as an impediment [38]. The interpretation of ECS results can be complex, especially when VOUS are identified. Importantly, to maintain high sensitivity, it is essential to continuously update genomic variant databases [39].

In some instances, diagnostic ES complements ECS. In our cohort, the consanguineous couple 10 with a miscarriage and an ongoing affected pregnancy exhibited shared carrier status of an MKS1 gene variant reported as a VOUS. Given the resemblance of the lost children phenotypes to Meckel syndrome, type 1, we believe that this variant bears clinical significance. Similarly, ECS combined with diagnostic ES in several couples confirmed the carrier status for VOUS previously reported in their affected children, in the genes PIGA, SNIP1, FOLR1, and GLDN. These findings were considered clinically significant for future pregnancies as they matched the phenotype observed in the affected children.

A personal history of a genetic disorder was another reason to seek genetic evaluation out of concern for offspring recurrence risk. Accordingly, ECS supplemented by diagnostic ES was conducted in 6 couples. In the male partner of couple 13, two VOUS in the AD genes C1QTNF5 and DNMT1 were identified. While the C1QTNF5 variant explains his night blindness and the DNMT1 variant explains deafness, familial segregation analysis of the gene variants was requested, considering incomplete penetrance and the variable expressivity reported with both genes. He was also referred to ophthalmology and neurology clinics for reassessment. This case illustrates the need to address late-onset disorders and mild phenotypes in ECS panels, particularly where treatment is available [1, 31]. In couple 14, the male proband is affected by retinitis pigmentosa and was found to harbor a homozygous pathogenic variant in the RDH12 gene. Segregation analysis was highly recommended in his affected brother and paternal cousin along with the unaffected siblings and first-degree relatives to further assess the clinical significance of the variant.

Post-testing counseling should address the implications for the individuals tested and their current or future pregnancies, focusing on reproductive options and delivery planning. Reproductive recommendations provided to our cohort included IVF with PGT-M, preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A), prenatal testing, and natural conception. Special emphasis was placed on the 8 couples who share multiple genetic variants. For these couples, the anticipated reproductive outcomes are particularly challenging, given the 25% risk of each disorder being passed on to their offspring. Ethics are often intertwined with reproductive decisions. If a prenatal diagnosis of a disorder is made and the patient chooses to continue the pregnancy, delivery at a tertiary center can be planned ahead [14]. When a pregnancy termination is considered, this should be discussed in the cultural and legal context. Also, the psychological implications of the results should be dealt with delicately and conscientiously. Ultimately, the importance of multidisciplinary management cannot be overstated. For example, in couple 11, the healthy female partner was referred for cardiology assessment after identifying an MYH7 c.632C>T variant with a CADD score of 18.66, which is associated with a potentially high risk for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

ECS results should be interpreted with caution, particularly when unexpected findings arise. In couple 24, the female partner carried a variant in the LRTOMT gene, while the male partner carried a variant in the SLC26A4 gene, both associated with hearing loss. Given that digenic hearing loss is documented in the literature, it was challenging to determine whether these variants predispose their offspring to deafness [40]. Additionally, in couple 9, the female partner reported that her father is affected by a vision disorder. Segregation analysis was recommended to determine the phase (cis/trans) of the ABCA4 variants detected in her and to assess her risk for Stargardt disease 1.

ECS has its limitations. As an illustration, 6 individuals tested negative for any variants, having undergone ECS for RPL, infertility and failed IVF attempts, and preconception. Also, the asymptomatic male partner in couple 6 who tested negative by ECS had a chromosomal rearrangement (46,XY,inv(2)(q31q32.1)). Additionally, couple-based ECS failed to explain the cause of death in a newborn with a suspected kidney disease (couple 12) and the loss of a child suspected of having an inborn error of metabolism (couple 21). Furthermore, no XL variants were identified in the female partners in couples 17 and 24 to explain POI. Consultands must be aware that ECS has technological limitations and may not detect all gene variants associated with a condition, potentially leading to false-negative results.

The acceptability of preconception ES-based ECS by couples stems from the desire to prevent childhood conditions. Personal values, social situations, testing issues, and concerns are crucial in the decision-making process [41]. Experiences of relatives and friends can be an influencing factor. In our cohort, two couples are related. The first couple influenced the choice of the second to seek genetic advice, extending their knowledge to expand families. However, barriers to wide implementation include a lack of public interest due to limited knowledge and access to genetic services [16]. Furthermore, the cost of ECS can be prohibitive for some individuals, particularly if it is not covered by insurance. Efforts to secure insurance coverage and minimize out-of-pocket expenses are of paramount importance. Lastly, some healthcare providers lack sufficient knowledge about ECS, hindering their ability to recommend and interpret screening appropriately.

Study limitations

The number of subjects is considered limited due to the private nature of the facility, which serves a select patient base consisting of couples seeking clinical genetics services. Additionally, the cost of genetic testing is incurred by the couples themselves. Furthermore, NGS-based technologies are not reliable in detecting specific types of variants such as trinucleotide repeat expansions and structural variations. ES testing run for these patients specifically included SMN1 and FMR1 gene testing.

Conclusions

Preconception ECS is pivotal in reproductive planning and periconceptional care, enabling couples to assess their combined genetic risk and make informed reproductive decisions, thereby reducing the likelihood of having a child with a serious genetic disorder. In the present study, 54 individuals (90.00%) were found to carry at least one variant of 95 identified genes, totaling 174 variants. ECS holds significant importance in consanguineous populations due to the heightened risk of inherited genetic disorders. Implementing ECS in consanguineous populations can significantly reduce the prevalence of genetic disorders, leading to improved public health outcomes and reduced healthcare costs associated with managing these conditions. Although consanguineous couples have a higher risk due to shared ancestry, ECS is recommended for all couples, as unrelated partners may unknowingly carry variants for the same rare genetic disorder, especially in populations who share the same genetic pool. ECS supports ethical decision-making and can reduce the social and emotional burden on families by preventing the birth of children with serious genetic conditions, thereby improving overall family health and well-being. Given this is the first study to address this question in the population of Bahrain, we believe our results should be interpreted with caution prior to generalization.
