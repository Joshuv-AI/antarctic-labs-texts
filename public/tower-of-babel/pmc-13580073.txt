Fermentation-driven valorization of cereal by-products: Mechanisms, functional applications, and emerging AI-assisted approaches
Authors: Yibo Chen, Chuang Liu, Tianyin Chen, Tianxiang Xue, Mengwei Chang, Yufeng He, Kunlun Liu, Guangsu Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cereal processing generates abundant by-products, which remain underutilized despite being rich in bioactive compounds. Recent studies highlight the crucial role of fermentation in releasing bound compounds through microorganism-mediated enzymatic hydrolysis. Herein, we summarize the nutritional and functional components present in common cereal by-products, specific fermentation mechanisms for enhancing their bioactive components, and applications of fermented cereal by-products in food and other industrial sectors. Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a promising tool for predicting fermentation performance, optimizing fermentation parameters, and improving bioactive compound extraction in cereal by-product valorization. Although large-scale application is challenging, integrating fermentation with AI is expected to develop the circular bioeconomy and sustainable development.

Introduction

Cereals, the fundamental components of the global food system, provide essential nutrients and support global food security. However, the increasing cereal processing demand has generated a substantial amount of by-products including bran, germ, and other residual fractions. Although these by-products are regarded as low-value residues (Bianco et al., 2020; Xue et al., 2024), they have considerable potential for conversion into valuable food ingredients and industrial resources (Yaashikaa et al., 2022). The low use of cereal by-products is an economic challenge and barrier to achieving a sustainable and circular bioeconomy.

Microbial fermentation has emerged as an important strategy for improving the utilization potential of cereal by-products. Fermentation can transform substrates into products with functional properties by exploiting microbial metabolism and enzymatic activities. Common microbial fermentation methods include lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and yeast fermentation. Complex interactions among substrates, microorganisms, and processing conditions influence fermentation outcomes, making it difficult to achieve precise control and stable quality. These limitations demonstrate the necessity for a highly efficient approach to understand and regulate fermentation processes.

AI offers new possibilities for addressing these challenges by enabling data-driven analysis, prediction, and optimization of complex biological processes. Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), two critical AI components, can facilitate real-time monitoring and dynamic regulation of fermentation parameters. ML models such as random forest (RF) have been used to optimize submerged fermentation (SMF) conditions, significantly increasing the production of commercially valuable enzymes such as L-asparaginase (Baskar et al., 2025). However, the application of AI in cereal by-product fermentation remains at an early stage, and its integration with microbial transformation processes has not yet been systematically established.

An extensive literature search was performed up to April 2026 based on PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. Keywords and subject terms included (cereal by-product AND fermentation) OR (cereal by-product AND bioactive compound) OR (cereal by-product AND fermentation AND AI) OR (cereal by-product AND fermentation AND machine learning). We manually searched the reference lists of the retrieved studies for relevant articles. Publications focus on new articles (since 2021) and exclude those without full manuscripts. Consistent with the expanding interest, several reviews on the efficacy of valorization for cereal by-products have been published. Studies were selected based on their relevance to microbial fermentation, valorization mechanisms, and AI-assisted process optimization of cereal by-products. Duplicate records were manually removed during the screening process. Older studies were included only when they represented foundational research or were widely recognized as significant contributions to the field. In addition, non-cereal studies were selectively included when they provided transferable methodologies or conceptual frameworks relevant to cereal by-product valorization, and their relevance was explicitly indicated in the corresponding sections. Despite reviewing the current knowledge reported in the early literature in this field, these reviews have several limitations (Adil et al., 2026; Arya et al., 2025; Dimoso et al., 2025; He et al., 2026; Mpalanzi et al., 2025). Among fermentation-based reviews, one comprehensive review summarized microbial fermentation strategies for cereal wastes and by-products (Adil et al., 2026). Another review focused substantially specifically on the biochemical mechanisms underlying fermentation-driven bioactive enhancement of cereal grains (Arya et al., 2025). Dimoso et al. narrowed the scope to coculture fermentation of cereal by-products (Dimoso et al., 2025). Among AI-oriented reviews, Mpalanzi et al. examined AI-assisted optimization of bioactive compound extraction from diverse food by-products, without treating fermentation as the central conversion process (Mpalanzi et al., 2025). Another recently published review focused on ML-guided synthetic microbial community design for fermented foods, rather than on the complete valorization chain of cereal by-products (He et al., 2026). Notably, the integration of substrate characteristics, microbial conversion, and intelligent regulation has received limited attention.

To address these limitations, we present the mentioned activities herein. (i) We provide a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying fermentation-driven valorization of cereal by-products, summarize the major functional components present in cereal by-products, and discuss the mechanism of microbial fermentation promoting the release, transformation, and enhancement of bioactive compounds. (ii) We clarify the role of AI in advancing intelligent fermentation systems for cereal by-product utilization. Specifically, we discuss AI-based approaches, facilitating fermentation process optimization, bioactive compound extraction, and the development of functional food and industrial products. (iii) We establish an integrated framework linking substrate characteristics, microbial conversion, AI-assisted optimization, and applications. We highlight the major barriers limiting practical implementation including process scalability, technoeconomic feasibility, food safety, regulatory requirements, and insufficient experimental validation. Therefore, this review aims to provide a comprehensive perspective on fermentation-based valorization of cereal by-products by integrating current knowledge of transformation mechanisms, food applications, and emerging AI-assisted approaches. Beyond summarizing existing fermentation strategies, this review further discusses how AI-based prediction and optimization methods may contribute to more efficient process regulation and resource utilization. We also highlight the major barriers limiting practical implementation, including process scalability, technoeconomic feasibility, food safety, regulatory requirements, and insufficient experimental validation. This review provides future perspectives for the sustainable and intelligent valorization of cereal by-products.

Proteins

The cereal processing by-products are abundant in plant protein (Tian et al., 2022), making them excellent raw materials for nutritional enhancement and the development of functional foods. Key proteins primarily include albumin, globulin, zein, and gluten. Prolamins, the predominant storage proteins, are represented by zein in maize, hordein in barley, glutenin in wheat, and kafirin in sorghum (Ma et al., 2024). Although lysine and tryptophan contents are relatively low, their amino acid compositions are complementary to those of other plant-based proteins. Physical technologies assisted protein extraction, including ultrasound, microwave and high pressure can effectively increase the extraction rate of protein, and the release and digestibility of amino acids, improving the protein digestibility corrected amino acid score and hydrolysis degree. The protein content varies significantly depending on grain types and processing methods (Li, Wang, et al., 2023).

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates constitute the richest component of cereal by-products. Cereal bran and husk, abundant in dietary fiber, account for >50% of their dry weights (Li, Wang, et al., 2023). Insoluble fibers primarily comprise three substances: cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. These fibers account for >90% of the total dietary fiber found in cereal bran (Tian et al., 2024). Among these, cellulose is a linear polymer formed by glucose polymerization. Hemicellulose mainly exists as arabinoxylans (AXs), with branched, irregular structures. The substantial dietary fiber content in cereal by-products exhibits significant physiological functions including regulating intestinal flora balance, and reducing cholesterol absorption and persistent diseases such as obesity and Type 2 diabetes (Liao et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2022).

Micronutrients

The by-products of cereal processing are abundant in micronutrients including vitamins and minerals. Cereal by-products comprise the highest calcium content, followed by iron and zinc (Saroj, Malik and Kaur, 2025a, Saroj, Malik and Kaur, 2025b). As micronutrients are present in the outer structure of grains such as bran and malt, cereal by-products serve as excellent sources of micronutrients. However, nutrient-rich outer layers are removed during the grain refining process. Rice bran is abundant in bioactive compounds such as tocopherols, tocotrienols, and phytosterols (Tan et al., 2023). Studies have reported that fermentation of wheat bran with Propionibacterium freudenreichii and Lactobacillus acidophilus can effectively enhance the vitamin B12 content (Ibrahim et al., 2021).

Bioactive components

Cereal by-products contain a wide range of bioactive components. Although their concentrations are relatively low, these bioactive components exhibit significant physiological functions. Among them, the bran and hull of grains are particularly rich in bioactive phenolic compounds including phenolic acids and flavonoids (Mikhailova et al., 2023; Wanyo et al., 2024). Additionally, the bran and hull contain phytosterols, γ-oryzanol, bioactive peptides, and dietary fiber derivatives. The types and concentrations of these components vary depending on the cereal variety, processing method, and type of by-product.

Overall, cereal by-products are abundant in bioactive substances, which are essential for cereal valorization. However, many functional components remain embedded within the rigid plant cell wall matrix or exist in bound forms, thereby limiting their bioavailability and potential applications. The cereal cell wall matrix is primarily composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, and AXs, with phenolic acids frequently esterified to polysaccharide chains (Lin et al., 2021). The utilization rate of these bioactive components is low owing to issues with texture characteristics and processing compatibility. Currently, microbial fermentation is an important biotechnological process for exploring the biological activity of cereal by-products (Bianco et al., 2020).

Bioactive enhancement through microbial fermentation

The valorization of cereal by-products through microbial fermentation has emerged as a promising strategy for addressing cereal by-product waste and promoting the development of functional ingredient. Microbial fermentation, an exothermic biochemical process, involves microorganisms such as LAB, yeast and fungi (Yousfi et al., 2025). These microorganisms metabolize organic substrates through enzymatic reactions, producing simpler molecular products. Fermentation process is characterized by mild operating conditions, high conversion rates, and its metabolic nature. Different microorganisms exhibit distinct functional capabilities due to their unique enzymatic systems and metabolic pathways. LAB play a crucial role in the fermentation of cereal by-products owing to produce organic acids, particularly lactic and acetic acids, as well as antimicrobial compounds such as bacteriocins, which contribute to food preservation and regulate fermentation dynamics (González-González et al., 2023). Moreover, LAB possess diverse enzymatic systems capable of hydrolyzing complex macromolecules into simpler and more bioavailable compounds, including phenolic compounds, volatile compounds, oligosaccharides, and bioactive peptides (Zhang et al., 2023). In addition, molds have strong enzymatic capabilities and mycelial growth patterns that facilitate efficient substrate colonization and biotransformation (Schmidt et al., 2014). Molds are important producers of lignocellulose-degrading enzymes, including phytase, esterase, xylanase, and protease, thereby improving the nutritional bioavailability, flavor characteristics, and antioxidant properties of cereal by-products (Wu et al., 2022). Meanwhile, yeasts are recognized for their capacity to produce microbial proteins and ethanol, and other metabolites such as esters, aldehydes, and ketones, which contribute to the unique textural, sensory, and nutritional characteristics of fermented products (Ranjan et al., 2019).

Based on oxygen requirements, fermentation can be classified as aerobic or anaerobic fermentation. Based on the fermentation of substrates, it can be categorized as solid-state or liquid-state fermentation. These different fermentation processes exhibit different characteristics (Fig. 1) (Chen, Zhang, et al., 2025; Escobar et al., 2025; Sosa-Martínez et al., 2023). Solid-state fermentation (SSF) is a biotechnological process in which microorganisms grow on solid substrates containing little or no free water, with the substrate serving as both a physical support and a source of nutrients. In this system, the solid substrate provides a nutrient source and physical support for microbial attachment and growth. Compared with SmF, SSF provides higher yields of target products, reduces water and energy consumption, and promotes the release of bound phenolic compounds and other bioactive compounds, making it an environmentally sustainable approach for cereal by-products valorization. Phenolic liberation and biotransformation of structures

Phenolic compounds in cereal processing by-products primarily exist in a bound form. These bound phenolic substances are covalently linked to cell wall components including cellulose and hemicellulose through ester or ether bonds. Microbial fermentation effectively secretes carbohydrate-active enzymes and esterases. Microbial fermentation promotes the enzymatic degradation of cell wall polysaccharides, thereby disrupting the ester and ether linkages that bind phenolic acids to AXs and other cell wall polysaccharides. This process can release glycosidic phenolic compounds and convert them into highly bioavailable aglycones (Zhang et al., 2024). These aglycones often exhibit higher solubility, increased antioxidant activity, and stronger anti-inflammatory potential. However, enzyme expression levels and cereal types can affect the release efficiency of phenolic compounds.

Beyond promoting the release of phenolic compounds, microbial fermentation also facilitates their structural transformation through enzymatic activity, resulting in derivatives with enhanced bioactivity and bioavailability. Streptomyces tunisiensis DSM 42037 can convert ferulic acid in wheat bran into 4-vinylguaiacol, which has potential applications in the food industry and other fields (Lu et al., 2025). This process improves bioactivity and enhances sensory attributes. However, strain, fermentation conditions, and substrate composition affect the direction and efficiency of phenolic biotransformation. Fermentation conditions require precise regulation, yet significant challenges remain in large-scale applications.

Bioactive peptide release

Microbial fermentation can effectively promote the release of bioactive peptides. Cereal by-products contain various proteins, including albumin, globulin, and glutenin, which serve as direct precursors of bioactive peptides. LABs, through their membrane-bound proteases, can hydrolyze food proteins and release peptides with antimicrobial, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory activities (Huang et al., 2024). A study used LAB to coferment wheat bran, significantly improving protein degradation efficiency (Song et al., 2025). Currently, fermentation significantly enhances bioactive peptide production, with variations in strains and substrates influencing their yields (Ayala-Niño et al., 2019). However, peptide yield and bioactivity remain inconsistent across strains and substrates.

Remodeling of dietary fiber and cell wall polysaccharides

Microbial fermentation can remodel the cell-wall polysaccharides structures of cereal by-products through the combined action of carbohydrate-active enzymes and microbial metabolites. Enzymes such as cellulases and xylanases hydrolyze glycosidic linkages within cellulose- and hemicellulose-rich structures, whereas fermentation-associated acidification further facilitates the disruption of compact cell-wall matrix. These processes reduce the degree of polymerization and structural integrity of insoluble polysaccharides, thereby promoting their solubilization into lower-molecular-weight and more soluble carbohydrate fractions (Lin et al., 2021). For example, one study used Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Monascus purpureus for the co-fermentation of wheat bran, which significantly increased the soluble AXs fraction (Chen, Zhang, et al., 2025). This transformation was accompanied by increases in total phenolic content and antioxidant activity, suggesting that disruption and solubilization of the cell-wall matrix may also enhance the accessibility of associated bioactive compounds. Thus, fermentation-induced remodeling of dietary fiber involves not only changes in polysaccharide solubility but also improved accessibility of compounds entrapped within or associated with cereal cell walls.

Applications of fermented cereal by-products

Fermentation can convert cereal by-products into various functional active ingredients. These ingredients have broad applications in food, health products, and industrial materials. By integrating fermentation-based value-added utilization strategies into the agricultural sector, the generated cereal waste can be converted into valuable biological products. In this section, we discuss the applications of cereal fermentation products in food and industry. Finally, we address the technoeconomic, food-safety, and regulatory considerations that shape industrial implementation and commercialization.

Cereal by-products based fermented foods

Fermented foods based on cereal by-products represent an important component of the food industry. Fermented cereal by-products can be directly developed into functional foods or used as nutritional ingredients in bakery products, beverages, and other fermented foods, thereby contributing to the sustainable utilization of cereal resources (Verni et al., 2019).

Rice bran has been investigated as a substrate for the development of fermented food product. One study developed a fermented rice bran product using Lactiplantibacillus plantarum EM as a starter culture and evaluated its potential as a functional food (Moon & Chang, 2021). This study demonstrated the feasibility of transforming rice bran into a value-added fermented food product rather than considering it merely as an industrial residue. In addition, wheat bran has been incorporated into fermented bakery products through fermentation with lactic acid bacteria. A study investigated the application of fermented wheat bran in bread production and demonstrated the potential of fermented bran as a functional ingredient in bakery products (Prückler et al., 2015). Similarly, maize milling by-products have been converted into fermented functional ingredients through lactic acid bacteria fermentation and subsequently incorporated into bread formulations, providing another example of cereal by-product upgrading through fermentation (Pontonio et al., 2019). Thus, fermented food based on cereal by-products exhibits substantial potential for product development and scientific research.

Value-added bioproducts for industrial use

Fermented cereal by-products have significant potential for industrial valorization. Microbial fermentation enhances the bioavailability of essential nutrients, including minerals, phenolic compounds, and vitamins. This microbial treatment converts cereal processing waste into biofuels, industrial enzymes, organic acids, and biofertilizers (Hassan et al., 2021). Solid-state fermentation (SSF) of wheat bran and rice husk can produce enzyme preparations, flavor additives, and bioplastics. Additionally, Aspergillus niger pretreatment improves rice husk fermentation, and ethanol and antioxidant yields.

Commercialization of fermented cereal by-products

The commercial feasibility of fermented cereal by-products depends on the cost, collection, transportation, storage, pretreatment, fermentation efficiency, downstream recycling, and product standardization of raw materials (Aluthge et al., 2025). Although SSF requires less water and may produce high-concentration products, its industrial-scale application is limited by heterogeneous heat and mass transfer, limited process monitoring, and difficulties in maintaining uniform humidity and oxygen conditions (Khaswal et al., 2024). SMF provides highly precise process control, with advanced reactor infrastructures but may incur high costs based on water use, stirring, separation, and drying. When high-purity phenols, peptides, enzymes, or biopolymers require extensive solvent extraction, membrane separation, purification, or dehydration processes, the economic advantages of using low-value cereal by-products may be reduced. Therefore, adopting integrated biorefining technologies to recover multiple products from the same raw material may increase profitability and reduce dependence on the market price of a single product. However, costs associated with sensors, data infrastructure, model development and maintenance, and specialized personnel may affect the economic viability of AI-assisted industrial-scale fermentation (Cano-González et al., 2026; Cheng et al., 2023).

Food applications require systematic safety and regulatory evaluation because cereal by-products may accumulate mycotoxins (Zhen et al., 2024), pesticide residues, heavy metals, allergens, pathogenic microorganisms, and contaminants originating from cultivation, storage, and processing. Fermentation can reduce certain mycotoxins through microbial adsorption or biotransformation. A Bacillus subtilis strain was employed to ferment maize processing by-products, improving protein digestibility and mycotoxin degradation, specifically reducing zearalenone by 89.75% and aflatoxin B1 by 43.36% (Sun et al., 2025). However, modified, masked, or degraded products may retain toxicity or be reconverted during digestion. Therefore, validated multimycotoxin analyses and, where necessary, toxicological assessments of transformation products are required (Eskola et al., 2020; Stoev, 2024). Regulatory requirements vary depending on whether the fermented material is marketed as a conventional food ingredient, processing aid, enzyme preparation, food additive, dietary supplement, or novel food. Consequently, the history of safe use of the original cereal does not automatically establish the regulatory acceptability of a concentrated or biotransformed derivative. Accordingly, food commercialization should integrate compositional characterization, exposure assessment, allergenicity evaluation, shelf-life stability testing, labeling, and substantiation of any proposed health claims from the early stages of product development (EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens et al., 2024).

Overall, cereals and their by-products possess substantial value for food and industrial applications. However, product yield and quality remain highly sensitive to the substrate composition, microbial activity, and fermentation conditions. AI-assisted approaches can mitigate this variability by predicting process responses and identifying favorable operating conditions. The application of AI technology aligns with the principle of the circular bioeconomy. Successful industrial implementation requires consideration of technoeconomic feasibility, process scalability, food safety, and regulatory compliance.

Leveraging AI for valorization of fermented cereal by-products

AI is emerging as a valuable tool for data analysis and process optimization in microbiology (He et al., 2026). Traditional production approaches, which rely heavily on empirical knowledge, often suffer from low efficiency and poor process stability (Sun & Wang, 2026). AI technologies enable data-driven modeling, trend prediction, and real-time dynamic regulation. By leveraging these capabilities, AI models can learn from experimental data and provide valuable support for process prediction and optimization (Shi et al., 2026). Emerging AI approaches encompass ML models, such as artificial neural networks (ANNs) and support vector machines (SVMs); evolutionary optimization algorithms, such as genetic algorithms (GAs); and rule-based intelligent systems, such as fuzzy logic (FL) systems. These methods perform complementary roles in prediction, optimization, and decision-making. ML is a subset of AI that develops algorithms capable of learning patterns from data and improving predictions over time. It includes algorithms such as random forests and SVMs, which are widely used for pattern recognition, regression analysis, and prediction tasks in process optimization. For example, ANNs are computational models inspired by the structure and information-processing mechanisms of biological neural networks. They consist of interconnected neurons or nodes and are capable of modeling complex systems by learning nonlinear relationships between input variables and outputs. ANNs have been widely applied across various fields, including food processing (Ding et al., 2023). Compared with conventional statistical methods, ANNs achieve higher predictive accuracy because it can capture complex nonlinear relationships and interactions among multiple process variables without requiring predefined mathematical models (Huang et al., 2025). DL, an advanced form of ML, employs multi-layer neural networks to analyze large-scale datasets, thereby enhancing feature extraction, pattern recognition, and image-based quality assessment capabilities in complex food systems. In addition, FL enables reasoning under conditions of uncertainty and partial truth, making it suitable for food process control, where parameters such as temperature and moisture content continuously fluctuate. In contrast, GAs are evolutionary optimization algorithms that iteratively search for optimal process parameters and are frequently integrated with predictive models such as ANNs, to enhance optimization performance (Nguyen et al., 2024; Pournader et al., 2021).

Hybrid AI frameworks are continuously being developed by integrating complementary computational strategies with process monitoring technologies. Compared with individual AI approaches, hybrid framework combines the strengths of multiple approaches to solve complex optimization problems in food and biological process systems. For example, a GA and ANN-based hybrid model improved the fermentation performance of Lacticaseibacillus casei, which significantly increased the viable bacterial count (Chen, Guo, et al., 2025). Furthermore, integrating AI with process analytical technologies (PATs) and Internet of Things (IoT) platforms enables real-time monitoring and adaptive process control, thereby enhancing automation, repeatability, and scalability in intelligent fermentation systems (Adil et al., 2026). Although ANNs have received widespread attention, the application of other AI approaches, such as SVM-based prediction models and hybrid prediction-optimization frameworks, remains relatively limited in the valorization of cereal by-product. In this section, we focus on fermentation process optimization, bioactive compound extraction, and functional food innovation to illustrate AI applications in cereal by-product valorization, as well as transferable AI strategies reported in broader fermentation and food systems.

Fermentation process optimization

Microbial growth and product accumulation undergo dynamic changes. These changes interact with environmental parameters and exhibit significant nonlinear characteristics. However, conventional statistical methods fail to capture these inherent patterns adequately, while AI has been addressing this issue. AI application provides essential technical support for the real-time monitoring and adaptive control of the fermentation process. However, in cereal by-product fermentation, current applications remain largely focused on prediction and parameter optimization, whereas real-time monitoring and closed-loop control remain emerging research directions. Intelligent systems rely on fermentation data to optimize fermentation processes, transforming traditional empirical judgments into algorithm-based intelligent models.

Among the limited studies directly involving cereal by-products, one example is the production of poly-β-hydroxybutyrate (PHB) from BSGs. BSGs comprise carbohydrates, proteins, vitamins, and minerals, which support microbial growth and make it a low-cost fermentation substrate. Imandi et al. used Bacillus sphaericus NCIM 2478 for SMF (Imandi et al., 2022). The BSGs concentration, yeast extract, and salt solution were used as input variables, while the PHB concentration was used as the output variable. The ANNs was trained using 35 fermentation runs, and a GAs was used to identify the optimal medium composition. The model predicted 916.31-mg L−1 PHB at 50.12 g L−1-BSGs, 0.22-g L−1 yeast extract, and 24.06% salt solution. Experimental production was ∼916 mg L−1. This case demonstrates the integration of the substrate composition, microbial fermentation, AI-assisted optimization, experimental validation, and the production of a biodegradable biopolymer.

Another example is the ML-assisted modification of dietary fiber in highland barley bran. This by-product comprises a high proportion of insoluble dietary fiber, which limits its direct use in functional foods. Lin et al. used Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Kluyveromyces marxianus for cofermentation (Lin et al., 2024). Temperature, time, pH, inoculum levels, and material-to-liquid ratios were used as input variables. Soluble dietary fiber (SDF), insoluble dietary fiber (IDF), total dietary fiber (TDF), and the SDF:IDF ratio were used as output variables. Support vector machine, RF, and extreme gradient lift decision tree (XGBoost) models were compared, with XGBoost showing the best overall performance. Under the optimized conditions, the SDF:IDF ratio reached ∼0.6911. IDFs decreased by 27.65%, while SDFs increased by 19.11%. Furthermore, water-holding, oil-holding, and swelling capacities were enhanced. This case links the substrate composition, cofermentation, ML optimization, experimental validation, and the production of functional dietary fibers.

The hybrid modeling strategy has demonstrated significant advantages in practical applications. In a study on ethanol fermentation, a biomass concentration XGBoost model was integrated with a kinetic parameter ANNs model to compensate for unknown nonlinear relationships, resulting in improved prediction performance under dynamic operating conditions (Li, Dong, et al., 2023). The integration of computational fluid dynamics and ML can design a digital twin system for fermentation reactors, which can accurately simulate the transfer of nutrients and dissolved oxygen in the reactor.

Bioactive compound extraction

Cereal processing is gradually transitioning from traditional experimental design toward a model-driven approach based on intelligent algorithms, which has changed the method of extracting functional components from cereal processing residues (Adil et al., 2026). Classic techniques such as Soxhlet extraction and impregnation have been used in laboratory and industrial settings for a long time. However, these techniques exhibit several drawbacks, including high solvent consumption, prolonged operation cycles, and a destructive impact on heat-sensitive substances (Mpalanzi et al., 2025). New extraction technologies such as ultrasound- and microwave-assisted extraction offer significant advantages by reducing solvent consumption and improving extraction yields. However, these methods face optimization challenges owing to high equipment costs, process complexity, and complex nonlinear relationships among process parameters.

AI provides a technical foundation for addressing this issue. Using ML models for pattern recognition and relationship modeling on extensive experimental data, researchers can identify optimal operational ranges within the multidimensional parameter space and pinpoint key variables that significantly influence extraction efficiency. Although AI-assisted extraction studies specifically targeting cereal by-products remain limited, research from other food-processing systems provides valuable methodological insights. For example, ANNs have accurately modeled phenolic extraction from mango peels, whereas random forests models have optimized flavonoid recovery from tomato seeds, achieving higher yields with lower solvent and energy consumption (Mpalanzi et al., 2025). Notably, the combination of ANNs with GAs achieves higher prediction accuracy and extraction efficiency than traditional response surface methodology (RSM) (Kumar & Tripathy, 2025). Hybrid systems integrating AI with GAs have been used to optimize extraction parameters for pectin recovery from sour orange peels, reducing experimental workloads while maintaining high extraction efficiency. Moreover, one study investigated the bioconversion of cashew apple bagasse using ANNs coupled with GAs and compared the results with RSM (Patra et al., 2022). The results demonstrate the superior predictive and optimization capability of ANN-GA approach for enhancing bioactive compound recovery. Although conducted using a non-cereal substrate, this study demonstrates an AI-based optimization strategy that can be adapted to cereal by-product fermentation processes involving similar multivariable optimization challenges.These transferable strategies provide valuable insights for future development of AI-assisted extraction for functional compounds from cereal by-products.

In cereal by-product systems, AI-assisted extraction strategies are gradually emerging to improve the recovery and characterization of functional compounds. Combining near-infrared spectroscopy with DL models can accurately detect grinding intensity in real time, effectively reducing the loss of active ingredients and preserving the nutritional and functional substances (Yang et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2025). Furthermore, intelligent sensors, including electronic noses and tongues, can be integrated with ML to provide rapid and nondestructive analytical capabilities for assessing flavor quality and detecting freshness in cereal products (Wang et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2025). This technology can be applied to the sensory characteristic analysis and quality evaluation of extracts containing active ingredients. Thus, incorporating AI throughout the extraction process can yield significant benefits including enhanced recovery efficiency, target product purity, and reduced experimental duration, solvent use, and energy consumption (Kouamé et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025). Generally, by using the predictive power of AI, researchers can maximize recovery of bioactive compounds from diverse food by-products, reduce resource consumption, and design adaptable, industrially scalable processes. However, most studies are still in the laboratory stage and require further validation before industrial implementation.

Functional food innovation

Formulation design

AI may support the design and optimization of functional food formulations. Traditional formula design primarily relies on microbial fermentation technology. However, there are differences in the efficiency of substrate degradation among different bacterial strains. Microbial communities have complex relationships involving synergy and antagonism (Adil et al., 2026). The traditional single-factor screening method is inefficient and time-consuming. This method is difficult to use to screen for the optimal formula within a reasonable period. It should be noted that AI applications in cereal by-product valorization remain at an early stage.

The combination of large language models and nutritional databases offers new research potential, supporting the formulation of personalized rice bran–based meal products for diabetic groups. DL has been applied in broader food systems to predict GIs and glycemic response curves. These approaches may provide valuable insights for the future development of functional foods based on cereal by-product. Analyzing the release kinetics of functional active ingredients in rice bran, a low-sugar food formula with a GI of ≤55 can be developed (Zeng et al., 2025). AI integrating sensory evaluation and the experimental data of in vitro digestion can optimize the taste of the product and ensure that it meets the nutritional standards. Adding rice bran before yogurt fermentation can cause significant changes, reducing syneresis of yogurt to 6.86% (Wu et al., 2023). The development of fermentation formulas should be integrated with product functions to enable the precise selection of bacterial strains. AI models can efficiently identify such adaptive associations by parsing multidimensional datasets (Jo et al., 2025).

Direct applications of AI in the formulation of cereal by-product remain limited. Therefore, AI-based formulation strategies developed in other food systems may provide valuable insights for the future cereal by-product valorization. For example, Zhang et al. developed a hybrid machine learning framework for food product design, in which sensory properties were predicted from formulation variables, enabling more efficient exploration of new formulations (Zhang et al., 2019).

Intelligent packaging

Fermented cereal by-products can serve as basic materials for developing intelligent packaging materials. Following microbial fermentation, the properties of the primary components of the cell wall undergo significant changes. The original structures of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin are reconstructed, which significantly enhances the water absorption, film-forming properties, and biological activity of the material. Such transformation offers a new approach for developing edible packaging films and active packaging materials.

Cereal by-products can be modified and used to produce functional, active, intelligent packaging materials. AI haves the potential to support the optimization and design of such packaging materials (Murugesan & Li, 2026). The yield of bacterial cellulose fermentation extracted from rice bran hydrolysate could reach 20.69 g L−1 (Adil et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025). It exhibited an excellent nanofiber structure and thermal stability, with an ideal choice for sustainable packaging (Taokaew, 2024). Another study indicated that cereal waste could be converted into polyhydroxyalkanoates, which play a positive role in extending the shelf life of food packaging (Koller, 2018). Cereal by-products can serve as fermentation substrates for the production of biodegradable packaging materials. Nevertheless, the direct application of AI in the development of intelligent packaging from cereal by-products remains limited. Currently, AI-assisted optimization has been successfully demonstrated in related biodegradable packaging systems. For example, ANNs have been applied to optimize starch-based active packaging films by predicting mechanical and barrier properties, thereby outperforming conventional RSM approaches (Gautam et al., 2025). In addition, ML models have been used to predict the mechanical performance of sustainable corrugated paperboard, thereby reducing experimental workload and supporting packaging design optimization (Shirzad & Joodaky, 2024). These studies provide transferable methodologies that could facilitate the future development of AI-assisted biodegradable packaging derived from fermented cereal by-products.

AI has been increasingly essential in the valorization of cereal fermentation by-products. We have primarily introduced process optimization, bioactive extraction, and functional food innovation. In the future, based on fermented cereal by-products, we will develop integration methods toward multifunctionality.

Challenges and prospects

Despite the growing interest in AI-assisted bioprocessing, direct applications to cereal by-product fermentation remain limited. Therefore, the current value of AI lies mainly in prediction, parameter screening, and process optimization, while fully automated fermentation remains an emerging goal. This section focuses on the sustainability of agroindustrial cereal processing, advancing emerging and hybrid fermentation technologies, and the synthesis of microbial communities.

Advancing agroindustrial cereal processing sustainability

The extensive use of cereal by-products in fermentation faces numerous sustainability challenges. Annually, approximately 1.1–1.3 billion tons of lignocellulosic residues are generated globally. Among these, 30%–35% are open-air incinerated (Mehdizadeh et al., 2025), resulting in equivalent carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions of 45–52 million tons. Integrating fermentation-based valorization strategies into the agricultural industry can transform cereal waste into valuable bioproducts, advancing multiple sustainable development goals (SDGs) of the United Nations.

Fermentation technology plays a crucial role in advancing the implementation of SDGs. In terms of environmental protection, it is directly relevant to SDG 7 and SDG 13. It effectively converts raw materials such as husks, rice husks, and straws into enzymes and organic acids. Such resource use robustly supports achieving SDG 9 (Castro & Sato, 2015; Khaswal et al., 2024). Furthermore, mixed culture fermentation can convert cereal by-products into nutritious food, probiotics, and fortified animal feed. This resource utilization pathway has a direct driving effect on achieving SDG 2 and SDG 3 (Dimoso et al., 2025). Cereal residues can be used in anaerobic fermentation systems to generate clean energy. At the same time, anaerobic fermentation can reduce methane emissions from landfills and balance the rural energy supply with sustainable development of the ecological environment (Dinh & Fujiwara, 2023). Anaerobic fermentation can convert cereal by-products into clean energy sources, such as methane and hydrogen, which can replace fossil fuels and achieve SDG 7 and SDG 13 (Xu et al., 2021).

Emerging and hybrid fermentation technologies

Traditional fermentation processes face issues such as long processing cycles and susceptibility to pollution. Heat inactivation of microorganisms can degrade thermosensitive compounds (Souza et al., 2025). Emerging technologies that leverage physical fields enhance microbial metabolic activity through precise physical regulation. Currently, the primary processing methods include pulsed elctric field (PEF), ultrasonic treatment, and magnetic field–assisted fermentation (MFF).

PEF technology can effectively reduce energy consumption and processing time. Additionally, this technology can preserve the natural quality of food (Aluthge et al., 2025). A low-dose PEF can promote microbial growth and reproduction. Under PEF conditions, Saccharomyces exhibits accelerated growth kinetics and cell-cycle synchrony. Ultrasonic treatment can destroy the cell structure and release phenolic compounds (Milicevic et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023). Microstructural analysis demonstrates that ultrasonic pretreatment can significantly alter the gluten network structure. The main change is the relaxed cell wall, making the precipitation of intracellular components easier (Wang et al., 2023). Furthermore, MFF, a novel biological treatment technology, relies on nonthermal static magnetic fields to regulate microbial metabolic pathways. One study confirmed that static magnetic field treatment can significantly enhance enzyme activity in germinating rice, enriching gamma-aminobutyric acid and optimizing the sensory quality of rice cakes (Luo et al., 2023). Microbial fermentation can be combined with biophysical stimuli to design a composite processing technology. Additionally, combining it with AI has become a cutting-edge method of functional food research and development. One study systematically summarized AI applications in green extraction technology (Mpalanzi et al., 2025).

Microbial community synthesis

Microbial community synthesis refers to the artificial design and assembly of specific bacterial strains. This can create a microecosystem with unique functions, stable structure, and controllable characteristics (He et al., 2026). The utilization efficiency of single-strain fermentation is relatively low. However, microbial community synthesis can solve this issue. The synergistic effects among microorganisms degrade and use complex polysaccharides and bound phenolic substances found in cereal by-products. Furthermore, ML can analyze the complex interactions of microbial communities and predict dynamic changes (Pérez-Pérez et al., 2024). ML can effectively support the optimized design of community synthesis (Asnicar et al., 2024; Gong et al., 2026).

Microbial community synthesis can analyze various complex components of cereal by-products. Compared with natural fermentation, these communities can regulate substrate reaction kinetics and enhance nutritional values (Bianco et al., 2024). Barley bran serves as a highly representative and unique fermentation substrate. ML models can be used to design microbial communities in a targeted manner. Based on this design strategy, the production process and physicochemical properties of dietary fibers can be precisely optimized (Lin et al., 2024). However, the promotion of this program faces numerous challenges, including data fragmentation, high-order interactions in quantitative microbiology, and the scalability of synthetic communities. Future studies should combine standardized fermentation data with interpretable models and independent validation. This may enhance the reliability of community design across different cereal substrates and processing conditions.

Conclusions

Fermentation-based valorization represents an effective and viable approach for the sustainable utilization of cereal by-products. When integrated with AI technologies, fermentation-based valorization can further reduce global cereal by-products waste and contribute to the advancement of a circular bioeconomy. AI facilitates the transition of fermentation processes from traditional empirical approaches to data-driven precision regulation. ML models have demonstrated potential for predicting changes in biomass accumulation and metabolite production throughout the fermentation process. In addition, AI may reduce experimental workload and improve research efficiency by optimizing microbial strain combinations and fermentation parameters. The fermentation potential of cereal by-products is enormous. However, the available evidence remains limited, particularly for studies that directly integrate AI with cereal by-product fermentation. Most applications remain limited to the laboratory scale. Further studies is required to validate model performance across different raw material batches, fermentation systems, and production scales. Therefore, AI should be considered a promising supporting technology rather than an advanced solution for industrial deployment.
