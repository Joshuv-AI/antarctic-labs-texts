Large time analysis of the rate function associated to the Boltzmann equation: dynamical phase transitions
Giada Basile, Dario Benedetto, Lorenzo Bertini, Daniel Heydecker

Abstract
. We analyse the large time behaviour of the rate function that describes the probability of large fluctuations of an underlying microscopic model associated to the homogeneous Boltzmann equation, such as the Kac walk. We consider in particular the asymptotic of the number of collisions, per particle and per unit of time, and show it exhibits a phase transition in the joint limit in which the number of particles N N and the time interval [ 0 , T ] [0,T] diverge. More precisely, due to the existence of Lu-Wennberg solutions, the corresponding limiting rate function vanishes for subtypical values of the number of collisions. We also analyse the second order large deviations showing that the probability of subtypical fluctuations is exponentially small in N N , independently on T T . As a key point, we establish the controllability of the homogeneous Boltzmann equation.

1.
Introduction

The limiting behaviour of many body systems in the low density regime
is described by kinetic equations. We focus on the spatially homogeneous case,
for which the typical behaviour is described by
the homogeneous Boltzmann equation for the
one-particle velocity distribution. Recently, some progress has been achieved in the analysis of atypical
behaviour, both in the contest of deterministic and stochastic microscopic
dynamics. In particular, after
[
16
,
21
]
,
large deviations results over a finite time window
in the limit of infinite many particles have been proven in

[
1
,
2
,
3
,
7
,
13
]
.

We focus on the behaviour of the number of collisions
in the joint limit in which the number of particles
N
and the time
window
[0,T]
diverge. More precisely, let

q_{N,T}
be the number of collisions per particle and per unit of time.
As follows from the Boltzmann-Grad limit
and the large time behaviour of the energy conserving solutions
to the homogeneous Boltzmann equation,
if we let first
N
and then
T
diverge,
then
q_{N,T}
converges to the typical value

\bar{q}_{e}=\frac{1}{2}\int B(v-v_{*},\omega)M_{e}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)M_{e}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega
,
where
M_{e}
is the Maxwellian of energy
e
, prescribed
by the initial conditions,

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega
is the Haar measure on the sphere
and
B
is the collision kernel.
By ergodicity of the microscopic dynamics, the same limit is obtained
when we let first
T
and then
N
diverge.
Note that if
B
does not depend on the velocities,
as in the case of Maxwellian molecules, then

\bar{q}_{e}
does not depend on
e
, and in fact
the statistic of
q_{N,T}
is described
by a Poisson random variable.

By considering first the limit in
N
and then in
T
,
the probability of fluctuations of
q_{N,T}

can be obtained by analysing the large time behaviour
of the rate function associated to the microscopic dynamics.
Here we perform this analysis for the microcanonical ensemble,
in the case of hard sphere interaction,
for which a candidate rate function has been identified in
[
2
,
13
]
.
A special role is played by the so-called Lu-Wennberg solutions
to the homogeneous Boltzmann equation
[
17
]
,
which are
weak solutions with increasing
energy. A class of these solutions can be obtained form the underlying microscopic dynamics by considering
only fluctuations of the initial distribution and therefore
their cost does not depend on the time interval.
Hence, the probability of a subtypical fluctuations of
q_{N,T}

is exponentially small in
N
but independent on
T
.
In contrast, to construct supertypical fluctuations,
it is necessary to change the dynamics and therefore the corresponding
probability is exponentially small in
NT
. Denoting by
{\mathbb{P}}_{e}^{N}
the distribution induced
by the microscopical dynamic with energy per particle given by
e
, we show that

{\mathbb{P}}_{e}^{N}(q_{N,T}\approx q)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-NTi_{e}(q)},

where the rate function
i_{e}
vanishes in
[0,\bar{q}_{e}]
.
We do not obtain a explicit expression for
i_{e}
, but we provide
explicit
upper and lower bounds.
The upper bound is obtained by choosing a time independent path,
where the single time velocity distribution is characterised by a
static variational problem, whose solution is not Maxwellian.
The lower bound is instead obtained by a comparison with a suitable
static strategy.
We expect that neither the upper nor the lower bound is optimal, and in fact
the optimal path should exhibit a non-trivial time dependence.

The second order asymptotic can be formalised
as

{\mathbb{P}}_{e}^{N}(q_{N,T}\approx q)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-Nj_{e}(q)}.

Clearly,
j_{e}(q)=+\infty
for
q>\bar{q}_{e}
.
In the case in which the initial microscopic distribution is the uniform
measure on the energy surface, we obtain a explicit expression for
j_{e}
,
related to the relative entropy between two Maxwellian.

The present analysis requires two auxiliary results of independent interest
which we next briefly describe.
Given a path with finite rate function,
we prove the chain rule for its entropy.
While for energy conserving path this statement has been proven in

[
11
]
, we here show
that it holds
also for path for which the energy is not constant, as in the case
of Lu-Wennberg solutions.
The second result regards the controllability of
the homogeneous Boltzmann equation.
More precisely, given two distributions with bounded
energy and entropy,
we show that they can be connected by a path with finite cost.

In the context of hydrodynamic scaling limits, an analogous problem
to the one we here consider is the fluctuation of the total current,
which exhibits interesting behaviour
[
5
,
4
,
6
]
.
The presence of two different scaling regimes for subtypical and
supertypical fluctuation of the total number of jumps has been proven for the so-called
east model, see
[
8
,
9
]
.
We finally refer to
[
12
]
for the asymptotic of the total number of
jumps in the context of non-linear Markov processes.

2.
Background and results

We first recall the analysis in
[
2
,
13
]
which describes the
large deviations asymptotics for the empirical measure and flow of the
Kac walk over a fixed time interval
[0,T]
in the limit of infinitely
many particles,
N\to\infty
.
This result yields, by projection, the large deviations
principle of the total number of collisions per particle in this
limit. The corresponding rate functional is expressed by a time
dependent variational problem. By analysing this variational problem
in the limit
T\to\infty
, we then show the total number of collisions
per particle and per unit of time exhibits a dynamical phase
transition in the joint limit
N,T\to\infty
.

Microscopic model and empirical observables

Fix
d\geq 2
and set
\Sigma^{N}=\big({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\big)^{N}
. We consider
the Kac walk given by the Markov process on the configuration space

\Sigma^{N}
whose generator acts on bounded continuous functions

f\colon\Sigma^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as

\mathcal{L}_{N}f({\boldsymbol{v}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\{i,j\}}L_{i,j}f({\boldsymbol{v}}),

where the sum is carried over the unordered pairs

\{i,j\}\subset\{1,..,N\}
,
i\neq j
, and

L_{i,j}f({\boldsymbol{v}})=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}}\!\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,B(v_{i}-v_{j},\omega)\big[f\big(T^{\omega}_{i,j}{\boldsymbol{v}}\big)-f({\boldsymbol{v}})\big].

Here
{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}
is the sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the post-collisional vector of velocities is given by

\big(T^{\omega}_{i,j}{\boldsymbol{v}}\big)_{k}=\begin{cases}v_{i}+(\omega\cdot(v_{j}-v_{i}))\omega&\textrm{if }k=i\\
v_{j}-(\omega\cdot(v_{j}-v_{i}))\omega&\textrm{if }k=j\\
v_{k}&\textrm{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2.1)

and the collision kernel
B
is given by

B(v-v_{*},\omega)=\frac{1}{2}|(v-v_{*})\cdot\omega|.

(2.2)

The collisional dynamics preserves the total particle number, momentum and energy, given by the integrals of

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mapsto[0,+\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\qquad{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}=(\zeta_{0},\zeta)(v)=(|v|^{2}/2,v).

In the sequel, we fix
e\in(0,\infty)
and consider the restriction of the Kac
walk to the set

\Sigma^{N}_{e}\coloneqq\Big\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\Sigma^{N}\colon\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(v_{i})=(e,0)\Big\}.

(2.3)

By Gallilean invariance, the choice of vanishing total
momentum is not special, and any other choice can achieved by selecting a suitable frame of
reference. In constrast, the parameter
e
, which represent the energy per particle,
will play an important role in our analysis. We denote by

({\boldsymbol{v}}(t))_{t\geq 0}
the Markov process generated by
\mathcal{L}_{N}

which is, by direct computation, ergodic and reversible with respect
to the uniform probability on
\Sigma^{N}_{e}
.
Given
T>0
and a probability
\nu
on
\Sigma^{N}_{e}
, the dynamics carries the underlying probability measure to the law of the Kac process
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}^{N}
on the Skorokhod
space
D([0,T];\Sigma^{N}_{e})
.

Let
{\mathscr{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be the set of probability measures
\pi
on

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
equipped with the weak topology. We denote by

the subset of
{\mathscr{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

;

\pi\in{\mathscr{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that

\pi(\zeta_{0})\leq e
and
\pi(\zeta)=0
.
{\mathscr{P}}_{e}

is a compact convex subset of
{\mathscr{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and we equip it with
the relative topology and the corresponding Borel
\sigma
-algebra.
For
T>0
, let
D\big([0,T];{\mathscr{P}}_{e}\big)
be the space of

{\mathscr{P}}_{e}
-valued càdlàg paths endowed with the
Skorokhod topology and the corresponding Borel
\sigma
-algebra.
The
empirical measure
is the map
\pi^{N}\colon\Sigma^{N}_{e}\to{\mathscr{P}}_{e}
defined by

\pi^{N}({\boldsymbol{v}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{v_{i}}.

(2.4)

We denote by
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{N}
the map from

D\big([0,T];\Sigma^{N}_{e}\big)
to
D\big([0,T];{\mathscr{P}}_{e}\big)

defined by
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{N}_{t}({\boldsymbol{v}})\coloneqq\pi^{N}({\boldsymbol{v}}(t))
,

.
Under suitable assumptions on the initial conditions, as
N
tends to infinity, by propagation of chaos
[
22
]

the family

converges to the unique energy-conserving solution to the spatially homogeneous Boltzmann equation

\partial_{t}f_{t}(v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\!\int_{S_{d-1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,B(v-v_{*},\omega)\big(f_{t}(v^{\prime})f_{t}(v_{*}^{\prime})-f_{t}(v)f_{t}(v_{*})\big).

(2.5)

For a fixed time horizon
T>0
, we denote by
{\mathscr{M}}_{T}
the subset of the finite measures
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
on

, which we endow with the weak topology

i.e. the topology generated by the maps
Q\mapsto Q(F),F\in C_{\mathrm{b}}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d})

where

In view of the conservation of the energy and momentum, the

The rate function in a fixed time window

For
T>0
, let
{\mathscr{S}}_{e,T}
be the subset of

D\big([0,T];{\mathscr{P}}_{e}\big)\times{\mathscr{M}}_{T}
given by elements

({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
that satisfies,
for each
\phi\in C^{1}_{\rm{b}}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
the balance equation

\pi_{T}(\phi_{T})-\pi_{0}(\phi_{0})-\int_{0}^{T}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,\pi_{t}(\partial_{t}\phi_{t})-\int{\boldsymbol{Q}}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t;\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v^{\prime},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}^{\prime})\bar{\nabla}\phi_{t}(v,v_{*},v^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{*})

(2.7)

where

\bar{\nabla}\phi(v,v_{*},v^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{*})=\phi(v^{\prime})+\phi(v_{*}^{\prime})-\phi(v)-\phi(v_{*}).

By conservation of
the number of particles, for each
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\Sigma^{N}_{e}

{\mathbb{P}}^{N}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}

(\pi^{N},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{N}_{[0,T]})

{\mathscr{S}}_{e,T}

The analysis in
[
2

\big(\pi^{N},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{N}_{[0,T]}\big)\in{\mathscr{S}}_{e,T}

N\to\infty

T

function, which takes into account both the fluctuations due to the
initial distribution of the velocities and ones due to the stochastic
dynamics, we must first specify the initial
distribution for the Kac walk. To this end, fix a probability

m\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
satisfying the following conditions.

Assumption 2.1
.

There exists
\gamma_{0}^{*}\in(0,+\infty]
such that

(i)

m
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue
measure and
m
is strictly positive on open sets;

(ii)

m(\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{0}\zeta_{0}})<+\infty
for any

\gamma_{0}\in(-\infty,\gamma_{0}^{*})
, and

\lim_{\gamma_{0}\uparrow\gamma_{0}^{*}}m(\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{0}\zeta_{0}})=+\infty
;

(iii)

for each

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\gamma_{0},\gamma)\in(-\infty,\gamma_{0}^{*})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
the Fourier transform of

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v}\mathrm{e}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}}
belongs to

L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
;

(iv)

there exists
c>0
such that

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v}\geq\frac{1}{c}\exp\{-c|v|^{2}\}
.

These conditions hold for the most important case when
m=M_{e}
, the Maxwellian with
vanishing average velocity and average energy
e
, with
\gamma_{0}^{*}=d/(2e)
.

Following
[
2
]
, we then consider the Kac
walk with initial distribution of the velocities given by
\nu^{N}_{e}
,
the product measure
m^{\otimes N}
conditioned on vanishing total
momentum and energy per particle given by
e
. Such a conditional measure is well defined in view of the existence of
a regular version of conditional probabilities and
\nu^{N}_{e}
is
supported on
\Sigma^{N}_{e}
. Furthermore, by standard properties of
Gaussian measures, if
m=M_{e}
then
\nu^{N}_{e}
is the uniform
probability on
\Sigma^{N}_{e}
, which is a reversible invariant measure for the Kac process.

For notation convenience we will hereafter also assume that
m
has
vanishing average momentum and average energy

; namely

m({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})=(e,0)
. This can be achieved by a suitable exponential
tilt which does not affect the conditional probability
\nu^{N}_{e}
.

For the choice
\nu^{N}_{e}
of the initial distribution of the
velocities, the static rate function
H_{e}(\cdot|m)\colon{\mathscr{P}}_{e}\to[0,+\infty]

is the convex functional given by

H_{e}(\pi|m)\coloneqq\mathop{\rm Ent}\nolimits(\pi|m)+\gamma_{0}^{*}\big[e-\pi\big(\zeta_{0}\big)\big]

(2.8)

where
\mathop{\rm Ent}\nolimits(\cdot|\cdot)
is the relative entropy between

This rate functions describes the static large
deviations of the empirical measure
\big\{\pi^{N}({\boldsymbol{v}})\big\}_{N\geq 1}

when
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\Sigma^{N}_{e}
is sampled according to
\nu^{N}_{e}
.
Note that

can be finite also when the energy of
\pi
is
strictly smaller than

; that is to say that with probability

N
– but not super-exponentially small – some
of the energy may ‘escape to infinity’. In the case in which
m=M_{e}

so that

is the uniform probability on
\Sigma^{N}_{e}
, this

It reads

H_{e}(\pi|M_{e})=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi\log\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v}+\frac{d}{2}\Big(\log\frac{4\pi e}{d}+1\Big).

(2.9)

In order to describe the dynamical contribution to the rate function,
let
r(v,v^{*};\cdot)
be the measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
supported on

\{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(v)+{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(v_{*})={\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(v^{\prime})+{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(v_{*}^{\prime})\}
such

,
where

and

are related to
\omega
by the collision rules,
as in (
2.1
). For
\pi\in D\big([0,T];{\mathscr{P}}_{e}\big)

and observe that

\\

Main results

The present purpose is to investigate the large time behaviour of the
rate function in (
2.15
) which has a non-trivial structure,
exhibiting in particular two different scaling regimes at large, respectively small, empirical collision numbers. As customary
in large deviations theory
[
18
]
, the relevant notion to describe
the converge of the functions (
2.15

\Gamma

To describe the first scaling regime, we first introduce the limiting
rate function.
Fix
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{e}
with
H_{e}(\mu|M_{e})<+\infty
,
let
i_{e}(q|\mu)\colon(0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty]
be the
function defined by

i_{e}(q|\mu)\coloneqq\inf_{T>0}\frac{1}{T}\inf_{({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\in{\mathscr{A}}_{e,T}(q|\mu)}J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})

(2.16)

where

{\mathscr{A}}_{e,T}(q|\mu)=\Big\{({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\in{\mathscr{S}}_{e,T}:\,\pi_{T}=\pi_{0}=\mu,{\boldsymbol{Q}}(1)=Tq\Big\}.

We extend
i_{e}(\cdot|\mu)
to a function on
[0,+\infty)

3.
Reversibility

The next statement is the counterpart at the level of the rate
functional of the reversibility of the microscopic dynamics.
Let
\Upsilon\colon\big({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\big)^{4}\to\big({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\big)^{4}
be the
involution that exchanges the incoming and outgoing velocities, that is

\Upsilon(v,v_{*},v^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{*})=(v^{\prime},v_{*}^{\prime},v,v_{*}).

(3.1)

Recalling the definitions of the functionals
H_{e}
and
J_{e,T}
in (
2.9
), (
2.11
), we have the following identity.

Proposition 3.1
.

Fix
T>0
. For each
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\in{\mathscr{S}}_{e,T}

H_{e}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{0}|M_{e})+J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})=H_{e}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{T}|M_{e})+J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon).

(3.2)

Moreover, if either side in (
3.2
) is finite, then for all
t\in[0,T]
,
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{t}
admits a density
f_{t}
enjoying the integrability

{\boldsymbol{Q}}\left(\left|\log\frac{f^{\prime}f_{\star}^{\prime}}{ff_{\star}}\right|\right)<\infty.

(3.3)

Finally, for any
[r,s]\subset[0,T]
, (
3.2
) the entropy satisfies the chain rule

H_{e}(f_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v|M_{e})-H_{e}(f_{r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v|M_{e})={\boldsymbol{Q}}\left(1_{[r,s]}\log\frac{f^{\prime}f_{\star}^{\prime}}{ff_{\star}}\right).

(3.4)

In particular,
t\mapsto H_{e}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{t}|M_{e})
is finite and continuous in time.

We understand that identity (
3.2
) holds in the sense that
if either side is finite, then also the other one is finite and
equality holds.
The strategy of the proof will be to show that any
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
for which the left-hand side
\mathcal{I}_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
of (
3.2
) is finite can be approximated by regular trajectories, for which we can compute a version of (
3.4
)

H_{e}(f_{s}dv|M_{e})-H_{e}(f_{r}dv|M_{e})=2\int_{r}^{s}(Q_{u}^{(1)}(\log f)-Q_{u}^{(3)}(\log f))du

where

{\boldsymbol{Q}}=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,Q_{t}
,
for any
[r,s]\subset[0,T]
, and where the superscript on the right hand side denotes the marginals,
namely

Q_{t}^{(1)}(\cdot)=\int Q_{t}(\,\cdot\,,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v^{\prime},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}^{\prime}),\qquad Q_{t}^{(3)}(\cdot)=\int Q_{t}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v^{\prime},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}^{\prime},\,\cdot\,,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}).

On such regular paths, the chain rule can be rearranged to find the identity (
3.2
), and the approximations are constructed so as to be able to pass to the limit. The finiteness of the right-hand side of (
3.2
) will then imply the claimed integrability (
3.3
), which allows us to pass to the limit to find (
3.4
).

Proof.

We fix, for the duration of the proof, a pair
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
for which the left hand side of (
3.2
) is finite; in the following steps, we will show that the right hand side is finite, and

H_{e}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{T}|M_{e})+J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)\leq H_{e}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{0}|M_{e})+J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}).

(3.5)

The converse inequality is then proven applying the previous case to the time-reversed path

\hat{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}_{t}:={\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{T-t};\qquad\hat{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}=(\Upsilon\circ Q_{T-t})dt.

(3.6)

Step 1. Bounds

We begin with some estimates. In
[
2
,
13
]
, it is shown that
J_{e,T}
admits the variational representation

J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})=\sup_{F}({\boldsymbol{Q}}(F)-{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}(\mathrm{e}^{F}-1))=:E({\boldsymbol{Q}}|{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi})

where the supremum
is carried out over all continuous
and bounded
F\colon[0,T]\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2}\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}

such that
F(t;v,v_{*},v^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{*})=F(t;v_{*},v,v^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{*})=F(t;v,v_{*},v^{\prime}_{*},v^{\prime})
.
From this, it immediately follows that
E(\cdot|\cdot)
is convex and
lower semicontinuous in both arguments.

Since
J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
is finite, by choosing
F=\log(1+|v|+|v_{*}|)

and using a standard truncation argument,
we obtain that

{\boldsymbol{Q}}(\log(1+|v|+|v_{*}|))<+\infty
,
and similarly
{\boldsymbol{Q}}(\log(1+|v^{\prime}|+|v_{*}^{\prime}|))<+\infty
.
Moreover, by choosing
F=-\log(B)
,
we obtain
{\boldsymbol{Q}}(\log 1/B)<+\infty
.
Since
B=|\omega\cdot(v-v_{*})|/2
we conclude that

{\boldsymbol{Q}}(|\log B|)
is finite.

Finally, under the additional assumption that
\pi_{t}
admits a strictly positive density
f_{t}>0
for all

, taking

F=\log\left(\frac{1}{(1+|v|)^{\alpha}(1+|v_{*}|)^{\alpha}f(v)f(v_{*})}\right)

with
\alpha>d+1
, and recalling that

{\boldsymbol{Q}}(\log(1+|v|+|v_{*}|)
is bounded,
we conclude that
-{\boldsymbol{Q}}(\log f)<+\infty
.
Moreover, if
f
is bounded, then

{\boldsymbol{Q}}(|\log f(v)|)<+\infty
.

Step 2. Velocity regularisation

We first perform a regularisation in the velocity variables.

Given a pair
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
,
we denote by
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{t}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)=f_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{Q}}=Q_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega

Given

be the Gaussian kernel on

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with variance
\varepsilon
, and let
\alpha_{\varepsilon}\to 1
be given by

\alpha_{\varepsilon}:=\sqrt{\frac{d}{2e}\varepsilon+1}

For each

({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\varepsilon})
by taking the density of
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}
to be

f^{\varepsilon}(v)=\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{d}(g_{\varepsilon}*f)(\alpha_{\varepsilon}v)

and setting

{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\varepsilon}=\alpha^{2d}_{\varepsilon}((g_{\varepsilon}\otimes g_{\varepsilon}\otimes\mathop{\rm id}\nolimits)*{\boldsymbol{Q}})(\alpha_{\varepsilon}v,\alpha_{\varepsilon}v^{*},\omega).

As an immediate result of the definition, it holds that

{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\zeta_{0})=\frac{1}{\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\Big(\frac{d}{2}\varepsilon+{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{t}(\zeta_{0})\Big)\leq e

and that
({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\varepsilon})
satisfies the balance equation. Using the bounds in Step 1, we can rewrite

J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})=E({\boldsymbol{Q}}|{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\pi})-{\boldsymbol{Q}}(\log B)+{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}(1)-{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\pi}(1)

(3.7)

where
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{{\boldsymbol{P}}}^{\pi}=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\pi_{t}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\pi_{t}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*})
.
Since
\big({\boldsymbol{P}}^{\pi}\big)^{\varepsilon}={\boldsymbol{P}}^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}
, by convexity and
lower semicontinuity,

E({\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\varepsilon}|{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\pi^{\varepsilon}})\to E({\boldsymbol{Q}}|{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\pi})
. Moreover, one can prove
that

{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\varepsilon}(|\log B|)
is finite
and

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\varepsilon}(\log B)={\boldsymbol{Q}}(\log B),\qquad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(1)={\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}(1)

(3.8)

Splitting
\log B
into its positive and negative parts, the convergence of the negative part is guaranteed by the argument of
[
2
, Appendix A]
. In the positive part, the argument is different, because we no longer (in contrast to the cited paper) assume that
{\boldsymbol{Q}}(\zeta_{0})<\infty

Moreover,
{{\boldsymbol{P}}}^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(1)={{\boldsymbol{P}}}(1)
.
Therefore
J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\varepsilon})
is finite and converges to

\varepsilon\to 0

Step 3. Time regularisation

In order to complete the approximation by smooth trajectories, we must also regularise in the time variable; it will be convenient to keep the parameters independent. Writing

(f^{\varepsilon}_{t},Q^{\varepsilon}_{t})
for the velocity-regularised pair constructed in the
previous step, let
\imath_{\eta}
be the a smooth approximation of the
Dirac measure in
{\mathbb{R}}
, with support in
(0,\eta)

(\tilde{f}^{\varepsilon}_{t},\tilde{Q}^{\varepsilon}_{t})=\begin{cases}(f^{\varepsilon}_{r},0)&t\leq 0\\
(f^{\varepsilon}_{t},Q^{\varepsilon}_{t})&t>0.\end{cases}

We now define
({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\eta,\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\eta,\varepsilon})
to be the path
with densities

and

Q_{t}^{\eta,\varepsilon}=(\imath_{\eta}*\tilde{Q}^{\varepsilon})_{t}
, where
\imath_{\eta}*

is the convolution in time.
Observe that
({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\eta,\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\eta,\varepsilon})
satisfies the balance equation.

Let
J_{[0,s]}
be the functional
J_{e,T}

when the interval
[0,T]
is replaced by
[0,s]
and we have dropped the
dependence on
e
. From now on we can follow
[

obtaining that for each

\lim_{\eta\to 0}J_{[0,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\eta,\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\eta,\varepsilon})=J_{[0,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\varepsilon}).

Step 4. Entropy chain rule for regular paths.

By construction,
f^{\eta,\varepsilon}
is regular in
(t,v)
and strictly positive.
The same holds for all the marginal densities

,

,

\displaystyle Q_{t}^{\eta,\varepsilon,(1)}(v)=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,Q_{t}(v,v_{*},\omega)

\displaystyle Q_{t}^{\eta,\varepsilon,(3)}(v)=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,Q_{t}(v^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{*},\omega)

where we recall the notation
Q_{t}^{\eta,\varepsilon,(2)}=Q_{t}^{\eta,\varepsilon,(1)}

and

As a consequence, we can write the

Since

admits bounded densities

{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\eta,\varepsilon}(|\log f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v)|+|\log f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v^{\prime})|)={\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\eta,\varepsilon,(1)}\big(|\log f^{\eta,\varepsilon}|\big)+{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\eta,\varepsilon,(3)}\big(|\log f^{\eta,\varepsilon}|\big)<+\infty.

(3.9)

Therefore, using that

is finite. For any

\partial_{t}(f_{t}^{\eta,\varepsilon}\log f_{t}^{\eta,\varepsilon})=2Q_{t}^{\eta,\varepsilon,(3)}(1+\log f_{t}^{\eta,\varepsilon})-2Q_{t}^{\eta,\varepsilon,(1)}(1+\log f_{t}^{\eta,\varepsilon}).

(3.10)

Since the reference measure is the Maxwellian

, we may use the representation (

) of

, so that the problem reduces to studying the evolution of

Integrating in

we conclude that

H_{e}(f^{\eta,\varepsilon}_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v|M_{e})-H_{e}(f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v|M_{e})=2{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{[0,s]}^{\eta,\varepsilon}(\log{f^{\eta,\varepsilon}}(v^{\prime}))-2{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{[0,s]}^{\eta,\varepsilon}(\log{f^{\eta,\varepsilon}}(v))

Step 5. Computation on regularised paths

We now perform a computation, still at the level of the regularised paths, in order to link the integrals appearing on the right-hand side of (

appearing in the desired conclusion (
3.2

[

for a similar argument. Since

is everywhere positive, one can check that
\mathbf{Q}^{\pi^{\eta,\varepsilon}}\circ\Upsilon
is absolutely continuous with respect to

with a density given by

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{Q}^{\pi^{\eta,\varepsilon}}\circ\Upsilon)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}^{\pi^{\eta,\varepsilon}}}=\frac{f^{\eta,\varepsilon}_{t}(v^{\prime})f^{\eta,\varepsilon}_{t}(v_{\star}^{\prime})}{f^{\eta,\varepsilon}_{t}(v)f^{\eta,\varepsilon}_{t}(v_{\star})}.

Meanwhile, since
\Upsilon
is an involution and the finiteness of
J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\eta,\varepsilon},Q^{\eta,\varepsilon})
implies that
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\eta,\varepsilon}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi^{\eta,\varepsilon}}
, it follows that
\mathbf{Q}^{\eta,\varepsilon}\circ\Upsilon
has a density with respect to
\mathbf{Q}^{\pi^{\eta,\varepsilon}}\circ\Upsilon
given by
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}^{\eta,\varepsilon}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}^{\pi^{\eta,\varepsilon}}}\circ\Upsilon
. By the chain rule for Radon-Nidokym derivatives, we finally see that
\mathbf{Q}^{\eta,\varepsilon}\circ\Upsilon
is absolutely continuous with respect to
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi^{\eta,\varepsilon}}
and

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{Q}^{\eta,\varepsilon}\circ\Upsilon)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}^{\pi^{\eta,\varepsilon}}}=\frac{f^{\eta,\varepsilon}_{t}(v^{\prime})f^{\eta,\varepsilon}_{t}(v_{\star}^{\prime})}{f^{\eta,\varepsilon}_{t}(v)f^{\eta,\varepsilon}_{t}(v_{\star})}\left(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}^{\eta,\varepsilon}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}^{\pi^{\eta,\varepsilon}}}\circ\Upsilon\right).

Substituting into the definition (
2.11
) of

and using the additivity of the logarithm, it follows that

J_{[0,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\eta,\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\eta,\varepsilon}\circ\Upsilon)=J_{[0,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\eta,\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\eta,\varepsilon})+(\mathbf{Q}_{[0,s]}^{\eta,\varepsilon}\circ\Upsilon)\left(\log\frac{f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v^{\prime})f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v_{\star}^{\prime})}{f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v)f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v_{\star})}\right).

(3.12)

Thanks to (

\begin{split}(\mathbf{Q}_{[0,s]}^{\eta,\varepsilon}\circ\Upsilon)\left(\log\frac{f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v^{\prime})f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v_{\star}^{\prime})}{f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v)f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v_{\star})}\right)&=2\mathbf{Q}_{[0,s]}^{\eta,\varepsilon}(\log f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v))-2\mathbf{Q}_{[0,s]}^{\eta,\varepsilon}(\log f^{\eta,\varepsilon}(v^{\prime})).\end{split}

(3.13)

Together with (

H_{e}(f^{\eta,\varepsilon}_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v|M_{e})+J_{[0,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\eta,\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\eta,\varepsilon}\circ\Upsilon)=H_{e}(f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v|M_{e})+J_{[0,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\eta,\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\eta,\varepsilon}).

(3.14)

Step 6. Proof of (

)

We now pass to the limit
\eta\to 0
in (

).
The right hand side converges
by Step 3.
By lower semicontinuity

H_{e}(f^{\varepsilon}_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v|M_{e})+J_{[0,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\varepsilon}\circ\Upsilon)\leq H_{e}(f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v|M_{e})+J_{[0,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\varepsilon}).

Now we pass to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.
By (
2.9
),
H_{e}(\cdot|M_{e})
is convex.
Then, by Jensen’s inequality, Step 2, and the lower semicontinuity,
we deduce that the more general version of (
3.15
) for any time interval
[0,s]
:

H_{e}(\pi_{s}|M_{e})+J_{[0,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)\leq H_{e}(\pi_{0}|M_{e})+J_{[0,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}).

(3.15)

This extends to any interval
[r,s]\subset[0,T]
by taking differences, and the special case
s=T
yields (
3.15
). The same argument applied to the time-reversed path (
3.6
)
shows that the previous inequality is actually an equality, and so is the version applied to a sub-interval
[r,s]
:

H_{e}(\pi_{s}|M_{e})+J_{[r,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)=H_{e}(\pi_{r}|M_{e})+J_{[r,s]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}).

(3.16)

generalising the the claim (
3.2
).

Step 7. Density, integrability and chain rule.

Let us now specialise to the case where the left-hand side, and
hence both sides, of (
3.2
) are finite. As a result, the
right-hand side of (
3.15
) is bounded in
s\in[0,T]
, and
since
J
is nonnegative, it follows that

\sup_{s\leq T}H_{e}(\pi_{s}|M_{e})<\infty
, from which it follows that
every
\pi_{s}
admits a density
\pi_{s}=f_{s}dv
. Using the finiteness
of
J_{[0,T]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)
, the same argument as
in Step 5 identifies

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}({\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}}=\frac{f_{t}(v^{\prime})f_{t}(v_{\star}^{\prime})}{f_{t}(v)f_{t}(v_{\star})}\left(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{Q}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}}\circ\Upsilon\right)

(3.17)

and, in particular, the first factor is finite
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}
-almost everywhere. As a result, it follows that

\left(\log\frac{f^{\prime}f_{\star}^{\prime}}{ff_{\star}}\right)_{+}\leq\left(\log\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}({\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}}\right)_{+}+\left(\log\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{Q}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}}\circ\Upsilon\right)_{-}

with
\pm
denoting the positive, respectively negative, parts of a function, and
{}_{\star},^{\prime}
indicating the arguments at which
f_{t}
is evaluated. We now integrate both sides with respect to
{\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon
and use the finiteness of

J_{[0,T]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)=({\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)\left(\log\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}({\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}}\right)-({\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)(1)+{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}(1)

to get

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{Q}}\left(\left(\log\frac{ff_{\star}}{f^{\prime}f_{\star}^{\prime}}\right)_{+}\right)&\leq J_{[0,T]}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)+({\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)(1)\\
&+({\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)\left(\left(\log\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}({\boldsymbol{Q}}\circ\Upsilon)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}}\right)_{-}\right)+{\boldsymbol{Q}}\left(\left(\log\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{Q}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}}\right)_{-}\right).\end{split}

The last two terms are readily seen to be finite, using the
boundedness of
k(\log k)_{-}
and the finiteness of
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\pi}(1)
,
and we ultimately conclude that

{\boldsymbol{Q}}\left(\left(\log\frac{ff_{\star}}{f^{\prime}f^{\prime}_{\star}}\right)_{+}\right)<\infty.

The argument for the negative part is similar, and we conclude the claimed
integrability (
3.3
).

The deduction of (
3.4
) from (
3.16
) proceeds as in Step 5, and the continuity of
t\mapsto H_{e}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{t}|M_{e})
follows by dominated convergence.
∎

4.
Controllability of the Boltzmann equation

The following result, which will be used in deriving Proposition
2.2
and Theorem
2.3
, shows that any two probability measures may be joined by a path of finite cost.

Theorem 4.1
.

Fix
T>0
and
e>0
. There exists
C_{1}=C_{1}(e)
and a function
F_{1}:[0,\infty)^{3}\to[0,\infty)
for which the following holds.
Given
\pi_{i}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{e}
with bounded entropy,
i=1,2
and
\kappa\geq C_{1}(e)
, there exists a path
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\in{\mathcal{S}}_{e,T}
such
that
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{0}=\pi_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{T}=\pi_{2}
, and

J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\leq F_{1}(e,T,\kappa)+H_{e}(\pi_{2}|M_{e}),\qquad{\boldsymbol{Q}}(1)=\kappa.

The function
F
may be chosen such that, for fixed
e,\kappa
, for all
0<T_{0}<T_{1}<\infty
, it holds that

\sup_{T_{0}\leq T\leq T_{1}}F_{1}(e,T,\kappa)<\infty

(4.1)

In order to contextualise this result, which may have
independent interest, we first discuss an alternative
formulation of the dynamical rate function
J_{e,T}
in (
2.11
)
in terms of a control problem for the homogeneous Boltzmann equation
(
2.5
), see also
[
16
,
13
]
.

On a fixed time interval
[0,T]
, we define a cost functional

\widehat{J}_{e,T}({{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}):=\frac{1}{2}\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{t}(dv){\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{t}(dv_{*})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,B(v-v_{*},\omega)\Psi({{\boldsymbol{F}}})\right\}.

(4.2)

where
\Psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is defined by

\Psi(x):=xe^{x}-e^{x}+1
, and the infimum runs over all controls
{\boldsymbol{F}}\colon[0,T]\times\big({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\big)^{4}\to{\mathbb{R}}
for which the density
f_{t}
of
\pi_{t}
is a weak solution to the
controlled Boltzmann equation

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\partial_{t}f_{t}(v)=\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,B(v-v_{*},\omega)e^{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{t}(v^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{*},v,v_{*})}f_{t}(v^{\prime})f_{t}(v_{*}^{\prime})}\\
\displaystyle{\phantom{\partial_{t}f_{t}(v)=}-\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,B(v-v_{*},\omega)e^{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{t}(v,v_{*},v^{\prime},v_{*}^{\prime})}f_{t}(v)f_{t}(v_{*})},\quad(t,v)\in(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\\
f_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v=\pi_{0}\end{cases}

(4.3)

with the energy never exceeding
e
.
Note that
\Psi
is positive with a unique
quadratic minimum achieved at
x=0
. Observe also that the case

{{\boldsymbol{F}}}=-\infty
, for which
\widehat{J}_{e,T}({{\boldsymbol{F}}})<+\infty
,
corresponds to a vanishing perturbed collision kernel.
With this definition, it holds for all
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
that

\widehat{J}_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}})=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})

(4.4)

In this way, Theorem
4.1
may be understood as asserting that, given
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{e}
with bounded entropy,
there exists a control
{\boldsymbol{F}}
and a solution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
to (
4.3
) such that
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{0}=\pi_{1}
,

{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{T}=\pi_{2}
, and such that the integral appearing in (
4.2
) is finite.

Let us remark that arguments from (
4.2
) run into issues of nonuniqueness, even for the (uncontrolled) Boltzmann equation (
2.5
). Given an initial datum
f_{0}
, taking
F=0
reduces (
4.3
) to the uncontrolled Boltzmann equation (
2.5
), for which there are multiple solutions
[
17
]
.
For this reason, we will not make precise the notion of
admissible controls, and have rather formulated Theorem
4.1
in terms of
J_{e,T}
. We emphasise that we do not need the initial and target measures to have the same energy.

Strategy of the Proof

Before proving the full statement of Theorem
4.1
, we first prove a ‘one-sided’ version in Lemma
4.2
, which is the special case where
\pi_{2}
is a Maxwellian

M_{e}
. In this case, we can construct the path
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
by taking a solution to the homogeneous Boltzmann equation, starting at
\pi_{1}
, and which has energy
e
for all times
t>0
, and then reparametrising time. Such a solution always exists, either as the unique energy-conserving solution if
\pi_{1}
already has energy
e
, or as a Lu-Wennberg solution otherwise. The total collision rate will be tuned to a desired value
\kappa
by using two different flux measures
Q^{1}_{\tau},Q^{2}_{\tau}
for the non-reparametrised solution:
Q^{1}_{\tau}
will be the ‘natural’ measure associated to the solution, while
Q^{2}_{\tau}
will remove certain collisions. By switching over after a suitably chosen time, we can guarantee that the total number of collisions reaches a desired, finite value
\kappa
, which will also ensure that the rate remains finite after the change of time-scale. The ‘two-sided’ case is argued separately, and is given by concatenating the path
\pi_{1}\to M_{e}
produced by Lemma
4.2
with the time-reversal of the path
\pi_{2}\to M_{e}
, using Proposition
3.1
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For every
e,T>0
, there exist
C_{2}=C_{2}(e)
and a function
F_{2}:[0,\infty)^{3}\to[0,\infty)
satisfying (
4.1
) such that, for every
\pi\in{\mathscr{P}}_{e}
and
\kappa\geq C_{1}(e)
, there exists
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\in{\mathcal{S}}_{e,T}
such that

{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{0}=\pi
,
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{T}=M_{e}
, and

J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\leq F_{2}(e,T,\kappa),\qquad{\boldsymbol{Q}}(1)=\kappa.

Proof.

We divide into steps; fix, everywhere,
e,T
and
\pi
as in the lemma. A non-reparametrised solution is defined in step 1, and the reparametrisation is given in step 2, yielding the final
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
. The asserted bounds are proven in steps 3-5.

Step 1. Infinite-time path.

Let us take
\big(f_{\tau}\big)_{\tau\geq 0}
to be
any
solution to the homogeneous Boltzmann equation whose initial datum
f_{0}
is the density of
\pi
and whose associated measures
\pi_{\tau}=f_{\tau}dv
satisfy
\pi_{\tau}(\zeta_{0})=e
for all
\tau>0
. In the case when
\pi
already has energy
e
, then
f_{\tau}
is the unique energy conserving solution, while if
\pi(\zeta_{0})<e
, then it is a Lu-Wennberg solution with a single jump of the energy at
\tau=0
. In view of
[
2
, Lemma 7.6]
, such a solution exists and setting

{Q}^{1}_{\tau}\coloneqq Q^{f_{\tau}}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}f_{\tau}(v)f_{\tau}(v_{*})B(v-v_{*},\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega

produces paths in
\mathcal{S}_{e,T}
with vanishing dynamical cost for any finite time horizon
T
. Moreover, since
\pi_{t}
is energy conserving away from
t=0
, it also satisfies any bounds valid for the energy conserving equation (e.g.
[
20
]
) which only require finite energy, replacing the initial energy by
e
if necessary.

In the sequel, we will use a different flow measure associated to the solution
(f_{\tau})_{\tau\geq 0}
in addition to
Q^{1}_{t}
given above.

Q^{2}_{\tau}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega)=\big[f_{\tau}(v)f_{\tau}(v_{*})-f_{\tau}(v^{\prime})f_{\tau}(v_{*}^{\prime})\big]_{+}B(v-v_{*},\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega,

(4.5)

in which
[\,\cdot\,]_{+}
denotes the positive part.

Step 2. Time reparametrisation

We now construct a path with a specified number of collisions, on a finite time interval
[0,T]
. First, we observe that, for any
\tau>0
,
Q^{1}_{\tau}(1)\geq Q^{2}_{\tau}(1)
. Moreover, a straightforward argument shows that
Q^{1}_{\tau}(1)
is bounded below, uniformly in
\tau,\pi
, so
\int_{0}^{\infty}Q^{1}_{\tau}(1)d\tau=\infty
. Let us define
\kappa_{\star}(\pi):=\int_{0}^{\infty}Q^{2}_{\tau}(1)d\tau
; we will see in Step 3 below that there exists
C_{2}(e)<\infty
such that
\kappa_{\star}(\pi)\leq C_{2}(e)
for any
\pi\in{\mathscr{P}}_{e}.
For any
\kappa\in[C_{2}(e),\infty)
, there is thus a unique
\tau_{\star}\in[0,\infty)
such that

\int_{0}^{\tau_{\star}}(Q^{1}_{\sigma}-Q^{2}_{\sigma})(1)d\sigma=\kappa-\kappa_{\star}(\pi)

and set

Q_{\tau}:=\begin{cases}Q^{1}_{\tau},&\text{if }0\leq\tau<\tau_{\star};\\
Q^{2}_{\tau},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(4.6)

to find that
\int_{0}^{\infty}Q_{\tau}(1)d\tau=\kappa
. We note, for future reference, that there exists
c(e)<\infty
such that
Q^{1}_{\tau}(1)\geq c(e)^{-1}
for all
\tau
, from which it follows that

\tau_{\star}\leq c(e)\kappa.

(4.7)

Setting
\phi\colon[0,T)\to[0,+\infty)
to be

\phi(t)=(T-t)^{-1}-T^{-1}
, we define a time-reparametrised path
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
by

{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{t}=f_{\phi(t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,\qquad{\boldsymbol{Q}}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t)=Q_{\phi(t)}\phi^{\prime}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

(4.8)

where we understand that
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{T}=M_{e}
.
In the remaining steps, we establish an upper bound
\kappa_{\star}(\pi)\leq C_{1}(e)
, which allows the previous construction for any
\kappa\geq C_{1}(e)
, that
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\in\mathcal{S}_{e,T}
, and the claimed bound on
J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
.

Step 3. Bound on
\kappa_{\star}(\pi)

In this step, we prove that there exists
C_{2}=C_{2}(e)
, depending only on the energy
e
, such that
\kappa_{\star}(\pi)\leq C_{2}(e)
for all
\pi\in{\mathscr{P}}_{e}
. We denote by
\big\|\cdot\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
the total
variation norm on the space of signed measures.
By
[
20
]
, there exist
\gamma=\gamma(e)>0
and
C=C(e)<+\infty
such
that for any
f_{0}
with energy
e

\big\|f_{\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v-M_{e}\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq Ce^{-\gamma\tau},\qquad\tau\geq 0.

(4.9)

Writing
g
for the density of
M_{e}
, recalling (
4.5
),
and using that
g(v)g(v_{*})B(v-v_{*},\omega)
is symmetric with
respect to
\Upsilon
, we have

\begin{split}&\big\|Q^{f_{\tau}}-Q^{M_{e}}\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,\big|f_{\tau}(v)f_{\tau}(v_{*})-g(v)g(v_{*})\big|B(v-v_{*},\omega)\\
&\quad\leq 2\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\,|v|\,\big|f_{\tau}(v)f_{\tau}(v_{*})-g(v)g(v_{*})\big|\\
&\quad\leq 2\big\|f_{\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v-M_{e}\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\,\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\,|v|f_{\tau}(v)+2\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\,|v|\big|f_{\tau}(v)-g(v)\big|.\end{split}

Since
f_{\tau}
has energy
e
for any
\tau>0
, the first term can be directly
bounded by using (
4.9
). In order to bound the second, given

\ell>0
we have

\begin{split}&\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\,|v|\big|f_{\tau}(v)-g(v)\big|\\
&\quad\leq\ell\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\,\big|f_{\tau}(v)-g(v)\big|+\int_{|v|\geq\ell}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\,|v|\,\big|f_{\tau}(v)-g(v)\big|\leq\ell Ce^{-\gamma\tau}+4\,\frac{e}{\ell}\end{split}

where we used (
4.9
) and Chebyshev inequality noticing that both

f_{\tau}
and
g
have energy
e
.
Optimising at
\ell=e^{\gamma\tau/2}
, we find that, for some
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime}(e)>0
and

C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(e)<+\infty
,

\big\|Q^{f_{\tau}}-Q^{M_{e}}\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C^{\prime}e^{-\gamma^{\prime}\tau},\qquad\tau\geq 0.

(4.10)

Recalling the definition (
4.5
) of
Q^{2}_{\tau}
, the bound
(
4.10
) implies

Q^{2}_{\tau}(1)\leq 2C^{\prime}e^{-\gamma^{\prime}\tau}.

(4.11)

Recalling that
C^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}
depend only on
e
and not on
\pi\in{\mathscr{P}}_{e}
, the claim thus holds when we define
C_{2}(e):=2C^{\prime}(e)/\gamma^{\prime}(e)
, since
\kappa_{\star}(\pi)
is defined to be the integral of the left-hand side over
t\in[0,\infty)
.

Step 4. Balance equation.

We now check that (
2.7
) holds, first on the interval
[0,T)
, and then extending to the endpoint by continuity. First, by definition of
(f_{\tau})_{\tau>0}
and
Q_{\tau}
,
\big(\big((f_{\tau})_{\tau\geq 0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,(Q_{\tau})_{\tau\geq 0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\big)

satisfies the balance equation (
2.7

, and because

satisfies the Boltzmann equation (

Q^{f_{\tau}}=Q_{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}\big(Q^{f_{\tau}}+Q^{f_{\tau}}\circ\Upsilon\big)-\frac{1}{2}\big|Q^{f_{\tau}}-Q^{f_{\tau}}\circ\Upsilon\big|

(4.12)

Since the last two terms on the right hand side above are
symmetric with respect to

\frac{\partial f_{\tau}}{\partial\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v=2\big[\big(Q^{f_{\tau}}\big)^{(3)}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)-\big(Q^{f_{\tau}}\big)^{(1)}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\big]=2\big[\big(Q_{\tau}\big)^{(3)}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)-\big(Q_{\tau}\big)^{(1)}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\big]

where the superscripts denote the marginals on the first and third

, and hence globally.
Therefore, by change of the time variable, the pair
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})

defined in (

, in that (

\int_{t}^{T}{\boldsymbol{Q}}(ds,1)\leq 2C^{\prime}\int_{t}^{T}(T-s)^{-2}\exp\left(-\gamma^{\prime}((T-s)^{-1}-T^{-1})\right)ds\to 0

as

. In particular, if we may pass to the limit of both sides when we evaluate the balance equation at

and send

. It follows that
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})
satisfies the balance equation on

({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\in{\mathscr{S}}_{e,T}
.

Step 5. Estimate of dynamic cost.

To estimate

, we notice that (

)
implies

, independently of whether or not

. We thus have

\begin{split}J_{e,T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})&=\int_{0}^{T}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,\phi^{\prime}(t)\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q_{\phi(t)}\log\Big(\phi^{\prime}(t)\frac{dQ_{\phi(t)}}{dQ^{f_{\phi(t)}}}\Big)-{\boldsymbol{Q}}(1)+\int_{0}^{T}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,Q^{f_{\phi(t)}}(1)\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,Q_{\tau}(1)\,\log\big(T^{-1}+\tau\big)^{2}+T\sup_{\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{e}}\frac{1}{2}\int\!\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,B.\end{split}

The final term may be estimated by

c(2+4e)T^{-2}
using a simple upper bound

, which is of the form required for (

). In the first integral, the contribution from

is bounded by recalling that

and that

, yielding a bound

and have the property (

Proof of Proposition

.

Step 1. The identity (

)

Since all paths
({\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{Q}})\in{\mathscr{A}}_{e,T}(q|\mu)
