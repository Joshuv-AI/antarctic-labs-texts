Medical problems in military personnel associated with burn pit exposure: a narrative review
Authors: Vivie Tran, Tarek Shihab, Anuhya Alapati, Connie Nugent, Rebecca Nugent, Kenneth Nugent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveMilitary personnel serving in Southwest Asia have had frequent exposure to fumes from burn pits. These pits provide a crude way to eliminate waste, including unused food, chemicals, plastic, etc. These exposures can have toxic effects on the respiratory system and other organ systems. This review will provide an overview of the toxic effects of burn pits on the respiratory system and the health of military personnel.MethodsThe PubMed database was searched using the terms military medical, military burn pit AND dyspnea OR deployment OR respirator OR toxicity. All articles used in this review were published in English and after 1980.Key Content and FindingsThousands of military personnel have been exposed to burn pits during their service in Afghanistan and Iraq. Some of these exposures have resulted in adverse health effects, including new or increased respiratory symptoms. Some service members underwent comprehensive evaluations that included lung biopsies. Fumes from burn pits can damage the epithelial surfaces. The most important factors in this damage include the type of exposure, the temperature of the burn pit, and the duration of exposure.ConclusionsExposure to burn pit fumes can have toxic effects on the respiratory system and other organ systems, such as the kidney. This review outlines these concerns and provides resources the reader can use to develop more information or to design studies related to these ideas, or to help manage patients in their military clinics. The military needs to perform longitudinal studies to learn the natural history of these respiratory symptoms.

Introduction

Exposure to potentially toxic chemicals during military deployment has changed significantly over time, with evolving sources of risk and injury. Chemicals used as a tool of war notably led to harmful exposures in various American military campaigns. The use of Agent Orange for herbicidal warfare during the Vietnam War, titled Operation Ranch Hand, resulted in numerous exposure-related illnesses. Chemical exposures in the Gulf War included chlorine gas, sarin, and cyclosarin, among others. The systematic burning of oil fields during the Iraqi retreat from Kuwait led to large-scale environmental contamination, with widespread exposure to fumes during the months’ long attempt to extinguish the hundreds of fires, the effects of which persist to date. Apart from direct biowarfare, multiple occupational exposures have occurred with American operations. Asbestos, fumes/exhaust, sandstorms, air pollution, noise pollution, and psychosocial stressors are all some of the exposures considered in health outcomes in service members.

A relatively new exposure has emerged as a concern among service members in 21st century American military campaigns in Iraq and Afghanistan. Burn pits are open air pits adjacent to military bases with the purpose of disposing waste in the absence of advanced waste management. A study of over 475,000 veterans deployed to 109 separate U.S. bases during 2001–2014 found that only 14.5% of veterans had no burn pit exposure. Among the 85.5% of veterans who did have exposure, the median number of days of deployment to areas with burn pits was more than one year (1). The U.S. military has made significant efforts to understand the frequency of symptoms related to burn pit exposure and the pathophysiology. This work will be discussed in the following paragraphs.

Burn pit exposure among military personnel deployed to areas such as Iraq and Afghanistan is linked to a variety of adverse health effects due to inhalation of toxic emissions from open-air waste burning, which contain mixtures of particulate matter, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), dioxins, and other hazardous chemicals (2). The risk and severity of health effects are strongly influenced by the type of waste burned and combustion conditions, with flaming plastics or mixed materials producing the highest levels of toxic compounds (2). Toxicity from burn pit emissions causes airway epithelial injury, increased pro-inflammatory cytokines [such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and IL-8], and persistent molecular alterations that contribute to cytotoxicity, mutagenicity, and the dysregulation of gene expression in the lung (3). Epidemiological and laboratory studies have identified increased risks of chronic respiratory disease such as asthma, chronic bronchitis, and bronchiolitis, systemic health conditions, and possible cancer in those exposed (2). Biomarker research, including microRNA and metabolite profiles, further highlights the potential for burn pit toxicity to cause long-term and system-wide biological disruption (4). These findings emphasize the need for ongoing biomonitoring, mechanistic research, and healthcare vigilance for people exposed to burn pit emissions (3). In addition, the time course for these changes in the presence of toxic compounds or respiratory symptoms needs to be investigated. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1454/rc).

Methods

For this narrative review, we searched the PubMed database using the terms Military medical, military burn pit AND dyspnea OR deployment OR respirator OR toxicity. The initial search was done by T.S. in November 2025; co-authors did additional searches as needed during the project. All articles used in this review were published in English and after 1980. Articles which focused on single case reports or short case series less than 5 or focused on organ systems other than the respiratory system were excluded. Pathophysiology

Analyses of byproducts of burning common warzone wastes have identified a variety of compounds, mostly under 2.5 microns (PM2.5) in size that could lead to adverse health outcomes. The production of these compounds varies due to factors such as waste type and burn temperature. A study analyzing the effects of these compounds on animal models identified burning temperature as a major contributor to toxicity (2,4).

Using ex vivo cultured human airway epithelial cells (HAECs), Ghosh et al. studied the effect of burn pit smoke condensates on pro-inflammatory cytokines and gene expression (3). A “Toxicity Score” was developed to quantify the cytotoxic effects of various materials and burning conditions. Results showed alterations in cytokine release, gene expression, and pro-inflammatory response within HAECs. Among the various materials and conditions tested, flaming mixed waste was found to be the most highly cytotoxic, with flaming military-grade cardboard that contains inks and glue leading to the highest cytokine release. Epidemiology

Following a 2013 act of Congress, the U.S. Veterans Administration (VA) established the “Airborne Hazards and Open Burn Pit Registry”, which began collecting data in 2014 (5). The stated goal of the registry was to “fuel research to advance treatments, enable more precise predictive medicine, and ensure proactive care and benefits for Veterans and service members exposed to hazardous toxins while serving”. The registry was further updated to ease inclusion criteria to those who served in specific Middle East military campaigns or operations, including Iraq, Afghanistan, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Djibouti, Gulf of Aden, Gulf of Oman, Oman, Qatar, United Arab Emirates, waters of the Persian Gulf, Arabian Sea, Red Sea, Syria, Uzbekistan, and Egypt between August 2, 1990, and August 31, 2021. A 2016 multi-phase Department of Defense study assessed airborne exposure, biomarkers, and health outcomes related to service members who worked adjacent to burn pits in Iraq and Afghanistan. A total of 200 pre-deployment service members were age and sex matched with 200 healthy controls; serum samples were obtained pre- and post-deployment in the study group, as well as in the control group whose samples were collected and analyzed at the same time as the study group. The second phase evaluated for an association between chemicals present in the deployed environment, and changes in serum biomarkers and inflammatory markers. The goal of the third phase of the study tested the hypothesis that there is an association between environmental exposures to chemicals detected in the deployed environment and changes in biomarkers that may be linked to increased risk of exposure-related disease (6). Multiple studies were conducted using data from Phase II. Xia et al. reported a statistically significant differences between pre- and post-deployment serum levels of PAHs, polychlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxins (PCDDs), and dibenzofurans (PCDFs), and concluded that PAH/PCDD/PCDFs in serum can serve as markers of exposure (7). Two subsequent studies using the Phase II data by Dalgard et al. and Woeller et al. further demonstrated statistically significant changes in microRNA expression between pre- and post-deployment serum samples (8,9).

STAMPEDE—“Study of Active-Duty Military for Pulmonary Disease Related to Environmental Deployment Exposures” Studies

STAMPEDE I

In an effort to characterize the increased respiratory symptoms reported by military personnel deployed to Iraq and Afghanistan, the 2014 STAMPEDE study prospectively evaluated personnel with new-onset respiratory symptoms following deployment (10). In total, fifty individuals were evaluated within 6 months of returning from deployment. The standardized evaluation included pulmonary function tests (PFTs), high-resolution computed tomography (HRCTs), methacholine challenge, and bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL). Forty percent of the participants had evidence of airway hyperreactivity, including 16% who met criteria for asthma (11,12). A reduced diffusion capacity (DLCO) with normal spirometry was found in 8% (11,12). Some patients had lung biopsies if needed. These individuals had normal spirometry, normal total lung capacity, and a normal DLCO. BAL fluid had 14.2% lymphocytes, 5.0% neutrophils, and 1.6% eosinophils. These individuals had normal cardiopulmonary exercise tests, indicating normal cardiorespiratory system responses to exercise. Overall, the most frequent finding was airway hyperreactivity. The authors of this study suggest that military personnel with post deployment respiratory symptoms should be evaluated for airway hyperreactivity. They did not find evidence for interstitial or bronchiolar disease.

STAMPEDE II

The second prospective STAMPEDE study was completed in 2019 (11). This study included 1,693 personnel who completed baseline evaluation and 843 personnel who completed the post-deployment testing. There was no significant change in spirometry; the baseline forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) was 94.2%±12.6% of predicted. Statistical improvement was seen in impulse oscillometry values with reduction in resistance and reactance. Subgroup analysis of subjects with pre-deployment obstruction, smoking history, elevated body mass index (BMI), or self-reported asthma did not reveal a significant change in spirometry values. These authors suggest that the routine use of spirometry is unnecessary and would be most useful in deployed personnel with persistent respiratory symptoms, especially wheezing (11). This study demonstrates that relatively common medical disorders such as asthma likely contribute to symptoms post deployment.

STAMPEDE III

The third prospective STAMPEDE study was published in 2020 (12). This study included 380 participants. The most frequent diagnosis was asthma in 87 patients; 57 patients had increased airway reactivity, 25 subjects had a laryngeal disorder, and 16 had excessive dynamic airway collapse. Interstitial lung disease was identified in six individuals, and 11 had obstructive lung disorders. This study group had significant comorbidities including an elevated BMI in 34.2%, regular smoking in 36.4%, positive allergy testing in 43.7%, sleep apnea in 38.5% and esophageal reflux in 13.6%. The authors suggest that clinicians should focus on frequent disorders, such as asthma and airway hyperactivity, and consider upper airway disorders in military service personnel with post exposure respiratory symptoms (12,13).

These studies are difficult to manage with complete data collection and analysis, especially if strict criteria are used for diagnosis. Military personnel deployed in Southwest Asia often have multiple additional environmental exposures. These include dust storms, diesel exhaust, burn pit fumes, and unusual localized chemical exposures. Post-deployment, these individuals may have increased respiratory symptoms or disorders. The military has completed 3 standardized prospective studies (STAMPEDE studies) of military personnel to determine whether there is new onset of a respiratory disorder. In summary, all patients were symptomatic, and asthma and airway hypersensitivity were frequent diagnoses.

These three prospective studies included 1,273 military service members deployed in Southwest Asia in Afghanistan, Iraq, Kuwait, or Qatar. The mean deployment ranged from 9.5 to 11.7 months. They underwent comprehensive cardiac and respiratory testing pre and post deployment. Their baseline FEV1 was within normal limits. They had chronic dyspnea. There is no significant change in FEV1 following deployment. The most frequent diagnoses included airway disease and laryngeal disease. In general, these three studies provide similar results and indicate that military service personnel post deployment have frequent respiratory symptoms, likely explained by chronic airway disease even though PFT was essentially normal. These studies were prospective and collected important information about the respiratory status of these military personnel.

Respiratory diseases

The data linking burn pit exposure to specific respiratory illnesses have remained relatively limited and are at times conflicting. Various studies suggest a direct association between burn pit exposure and respiratory diseases, while others suggest no association between exposure and respiratory syndrome. Despite the high prevalence of burn pit exposure in deployed service members, a potential confounding risk factor for respiratory illnesses in this population is environmental exposures associated with the Middle Eastern climate, particularly dust storms.

In response to a growing concern for the health effects of deployment during the Vietnam and Gulf Wars, the Department of Defense developed the Millennium Cohort Study (14). This large-scale (>250,000 subjects) longitudinal study assesses a wide range of health outcomes, with repeat surveys every 3–5 years. A 2012 study using data from the Millennium Cohort analyzing the relation between deployment to bases with known burn pits and self-reported respiratory illnesses showed no strong association between the two, with the only significant association found in the subgroup containing Air Force members stationed within two miles of a specific base (15). A major limitation in this study was the reliance on service members self-reporting the diagnoses of chronic bronchitis or emphysema, asthma, or persistent cough/shortness of breath. No objective diagnostic data were included in this analysis, and few disorders within a wide spectrum of potential respiratory disorders were investigated. Using data from the same Millennium Cohort, Powell et al. investigated the relationship between chronic multisystem illnesses (CMIs) (defined as general fatigue, mood and cognition problems, and musculoskeletal discomfort), and found no increase in CMI symptoms in service members deployed to bases with documented burn pits compared to those deployed to bases without burn pits (16).

In summary, many symptoms in these studies are self-reported and may have uncertain significance and may not reflect the effect of environmental exposures. They could reflect chronic airway disease or psychological disturbances creating respiratory symptoms.

Dyspnea

The presence of respiratory complaints, particularly dyspnea, has been well documented in service members exposed to burn pits. A cross-sectional study of 1,920 veterans used a questionnaire to characterize exposures and respiratory symptoms and found an increased frequency of dyspnea in veterans who reported burn pit exposure [odds ratio (OR) 1.22]. These veterans were deployed in Afghanistan, Kyrgyzstan, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, United Arab Emirates, or Djibouti (17). The majority of patients in the STAMPEDE studies had dyspnea explained by a diagnosis, though approximately one-third had “unexplained” dyspnea. The major limitation in categorizing unexplained dyspnea is the difficulty differentiating between patients without an organic cause and patients with an organic cause that workup failed to identify. In the STAMPEDE studies the final diagnosis was often not established. The patients were symptomatic, but no definitive diagnosis was made.

Constrictive bronchiolitis (bronchiolitis obliterans)

In 2004, a group of service members who had been deployed to Iraq was referred to Vanderbilt University Medical Center for further evaluation of exertional dyspnea despite being otherwise physically fit. They were unable to meet standards on a 2-mile run that all met pre-deployment. Of the 80 soldiers who were referred, non-invasive testing did not reveal an explanation in 49. The soldiers with unexplained symptoms then underwent thoracoscopic lung biopsy for further evaluation. All biopsy samples were reportedly abnormal, with 38 of the 49 having changes diagnostic of diffuse constrictive bronchiolitis (18). In summary, some veterans have undergone complete evaluation including thorascopic lung biopsies that revealed diffuse constrictive bronchiolitis. This diagnosis is often associated with very subtle interstitial/bronchiolar infiltrates which may not be apparent on routine evaluation. The development of airway disease seems quite plausible given the chronic inhalation of toxic compounds.

Reactive airway disease (RAD), including asthma

RAD related to burn pit exposure is increasingly recognized among post-9/11 veterans who inhaled smoke from the open-air combustion of mixed waste (plastics, fuels, metals, medical, and human waste), which generates fine particulate matter, PAHs, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), dioxins and metals capable of causing epithelial injury, oxidative stress and chronic airway inflammation leading to bronchial hyperresponsiveness, asthma-like disease and, in a minority, constrictive bronchiolitis (Figure 1). Large cohort and registry studies (Millennium Cohort, VA Airborne Hazards and Open Burn Pit Registry) show that deployment—especially prolonged service at bases with unsegregated burn pits—is associated with increased risk of new-onset asthma, chronic bronchitis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and respiratory symptoms compared with non-deployed or less-exposed personnel, with evidence of modest dose-response relationships for asthma and COPD. Therefore, patients typically present with exertional dyspnea, wheeze, cough, and chest tightness beginning during or after deployment; spirometry may show reversible airflow obstruction or be normal despite marked exercise limitation, while some highly symptomatic individuals have biopsy-proven constrictive bronchiolitis with largely normal computed tomography (CT) scans and near-normal spirometry, underscoring the importance of detailed history, full PFT (with bronchoprovocation when needed), high-resolution CT with expiratory imaging, and selective use of cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) and lung biopsy. Management is generally based on asthma/COPD guidelines—centered on removal from further exposure, inhaled corticosteroids with bronchodilators, treatment of comorbid upper-airway disease and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), pulmonary rehabilitation, smoking cessation and, in severe phenotypes, consideration of biologics—while no disease-modifying therapy yet exists for fixed small-airway fibrosis.

Service members with combat experience are 24–30% more likely to develop asthma. However, comparing deployed service members with non-deployed, no increased risk of new-onset asthma was found. The Liu study showed no dose-response association between burn pit exposure duration and asthma (19,20). For example, deployment to Iraq was associated with a significantly higher risk of asthma compared with stateside soldiers [6.6% vs. 4.3%; with a crude OR, 1.58; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.18–2.11]. These associations persist when stratified by gender and age. In summary, deployment to Iraq and Afghanistan is associated with new-onset asthma. Etiologic studies, surveillance, incidence, epidemiology, and assessing response to therapy are needed.

Upper airway diseases

The frequency of chronic respiratory symptoms (CRS) with nasal polyposis was 27.8% in patients with exposure compared to 8.5% in those were deployed without exposure, which also correlated with higher sinonasal outcome test scores (SNOT-22) scores in service personnel with nasal polyps. Lung-Kennedy endoscopy scores were also significantly higher in service personnel who had a burn pit exposure compared to those who did not have exposure and there was significantly higher rate of allergic rhinitis symptoms in deployed patients with exposure (61.1%) compared to those without exposure (23.9%). Interestingly, there was no significant difference in the rates of CRS without nasal polyposis. However, self-reporting can lead to recall bias, and there was no information to quantify duration of deployment between the groups being compared or duration of burn pit exposure (21).

Emphysema/chronic bronchitis/COPD

Schneiderman et al. found that of the nearly 1 million veterans of Operations Enduring Freedom, Iraqi Freedom, and New Dawn, 3% [32,076] had the coded diagnosis of COPD, of whom 69% [22,156] were newly diagnosed. However, only 31% of these diagnoses had spirometry concordant with VA Clinical Practice Guidelines, leading to questions regarding the accuracy of diagnosis in those without supportive spirometry (22).

Smoking is a potential confounding factor in the diagnosis of COPD in these veterans. This was addressed by Liu, who studied the association between self-reported burn pit exposure duration and respiratory/cardiovascular outcomes among participants of the Airborne Hazards and Open Burn Pit Registry. Veterans were categorized into four quartiles based on duration of burn pit exposure, and a strong dose-response association was found among the 527 subjects with the combined diagnoses of emphysema, chronic bronchitis, and COPD (OR 1.81 in 4th Quartile, P-trend 0.0005). There was no difference in rate of smoking in the four quartiles (20).

Mortality

In a large national cohort study of 474,634 U.S. veterans deployed between 2001 and 2011, Hoover et al. examined the relationship between deployment duration to bases operating open burn pits and mortality outcomes (23,24). The findings revealed a modest but statistically significant exposure–response gradient in all-cause mortality, with adjusted ORs of 1.07 (95% CI: 0.99–1.15), 1.08 (95% CI: 1.00–1.16), and 1.16 (95% CI: 1.06–1.27) across increasing tertiles of deployment duration, suggesting an incremental rise in mortality risk with longer exposure periods. While burn pit exposure was not significantly associated with deaths from cancer, heart disease, or chronic liver disease, positive associations were observed for deaths due to stroke, suicide, and unintentional injuries (24). This agrees with earlier observations pointing to behavioral and neurobiological sequelae among exposed veterans, indicating that prolonged exposure to toxicants from burn pits may contribute to neurological and psychological disorders (23).

Additional analyses by Hoover et al. extended these findings by linking deployment to burn pit-exposed bases to elevated risk of mental health conditions, including depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and injury-related mortality among 439,919 Army and Air Force veterans (23). Veterans exposed to burn pits for 130 days or longer were about twice as likely to have anxiety or brain injury and had higher rates of suicide-related deaths compared to those deployed to bases without burn pits. These results emphasize a broader systemic effect, suggesting that burn pit exposure may operate through toxic, behavioral, and psychosocial pathways that increase both accidental and intentional mortality risks among military populations.

In the Hoover study, mortality from all causes was estimated at approximately 2.4 deaths per 1,000 person-years of follow-up—consistent with prior veteran cohorts of similar age distributions. Follow-up in this study had a medium duration of 10.9 years (23,24). This analysis shows an urgent need for long-term monitoring and mechanistic studies exploring how chronic exposure to burn pit emissions influences mortality outcomes, particularly through pathways involving neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and psychosocial disorders. Together, these findings highlight the potential cumulative burden of environmental and combat-related exposures on the longevity and well-being of post-2001 U.S. veterans (23,24). In summary, this longitudinal study revealed a significant increase in neuropsychiatric symptoms, including self-harm, in veterans exposed to burn pits.

While the above studies all showed associations between burn pit exposure during combat missions and mortality and adverse mental health outcomes, the underlying relationships are likely complex interactions across several factors including environmental exposures, behavioral health factors and social determinants.

Invasive diagnostics studies

Routine evaluation should include PFTs, radiographs, exercise tests, and conventional laboratory tests. In all 308 patients who underwent bronchoscopy with BAL cytology in the STAMPEDE III trial, addition of cytology (cell count and flow cytometry) did not help differentiate patients with unexplained dyspnea from patients with established etiologies (25).

Impact

In 2022, the Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics (PACT) Act was passed as an expansion of VA health care benefits for veterans exposed to burn pits, Agent Orange, and other toxic substances. Prior to the PACT Act, multiple disorders were thought to be associated with burn pit exposure; however, confirming a link between a service-related exposure and a disease process has been difficult. As a matter of legal policy rather than establishing a causal link, the PACT Act expanded coverage to “presumptive conditions”, or conditions that are presumed to have been associated with exposure to respiratory diseases. Within this legislation, “presumption of service connection for certain diseases associated with exposure to burn pits and other toxins” likely included asthma (diagnosed after service), chronic bronchitis, COPD, chronic rhinitis/sinusitis, constrictive bronchiolitis or obliterative bronchiolitis, emphysema, granulomatous disease, interstitial lung disease, pleuritis, pulmonary fibrosis, sarcoidosis, and various types of cancer. These conditions no longer require the burden of proof that service directly caused the condition; rather, they are presumed to have been caused by military service. Studies of burn pit exposure have provided important information to military leaders, epidemiologists, and basic scientists. Clearly, the military takes on other important responsibilities when it assigns personnel to any region in the world; it must monitor their health for development of new problems and manage treatment. Though the correlation between burn pit exposure and respiratory complaints is supported by evidence, data supporting a direct link between exposure and specific disease processes are somewhat limited. Available data suggest that common diseases such as asthma, airway hyperreactivity, chronic bronchitis, and COPD make up most diagnoses seen in the symptomatic population. Particular attention should be paid to data suggesting that constrictive bronchiolitis may be a common explanation for patients left undiagnosed after conventional diagnostic workup.

Military personnel are assigned to military bases with and without burn pits; For example, in one study, 2,663 participants were deployed for at least 30 days within 2 miles from burn pits in Iraq and 1,680 participants were deployed for at least 30 days in Kuwait. The latter base had no documented burn pits. Information was collected by self-report and by review of medical records. There was a significant dose response associated between a longer time near burn pits and a higher risk of emphysema, chronic bronchitis and COPD. However, the associations were not present when the medical records were reviewed. There was also a significant association between hours per day of self-reported burn pit smoke exposure and hypertension. These studies are quite difficult to accomplish. The exact exposure is often unclear (26).

Garshick et al. published an update on CRSs following deployment related occupational and environmental exposures in U.S. veterans. This was a cross-sectional survey that was completed after 120 months post deployment. The average age of the veteran was 40.7 years, and the median deployment duration was 11.7 months. Some had heavy exposure to burn pit smoke and vapors gas, dust, and fumes. They reported dyspnea, chronic bronchitis and wheezing in the past 12 months; 15.6% reported wheezing. Burn pit smoke exposure was associated with dyspnea within OR of 1.22 (27).

Bell et al. studied quantitative CT images in 3 cohorts of military personnel post deployment to the Southwest Asia theater. They were able to demonstrate abnormal small airways in individuals with self-reported health effects following exposures during deployment (28).

Conclusions

Studies on the consequences of burn pit exposure have provided important information to military leadership, epidemiologists, and basic scientists. Clearly, the military takes on other important responsibilities when it assigns personnel to any region in the world; it must monitor their health for development of new problems and manage treatment. Although the correlation between burn pit exposure and respiratory complaints is supported by evidence, data supporting a direct and definite link between exposure and specific disease processes are somewhat limited. Available data suggest that common diseases such as asthma, airway hyperreactivity, chronic bronchitis, and COPD make up the majority of diagnoses seen in the symptomatic population. Particular attention should be paid to data suggesting that constrictive bronchiolitis may be a common explanation for patients left undiagnosed after conventional diagnostic workup. In addition, these exposures may occur in other occupations, such as firefighters and are not just relevant to military service members. Prospective studies need to be organized to collect information needed to establish a reasonable estimate of the cause of symptoms associated with burn pit exposure. In fact, the STAMPEDE studies provide a prototype for monitoring health or identifying new problems.
