Homocysteine levels alter the TyG index-CKD link in Chinese hypertensive patients
Authors: Wei Zhou, Chao Yu, Huihui Bao, Xiaoshu Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsHomocysteine (Hcy) is an established non-traditional risk factor for chronic kidney disease (CKD). However, the association between the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and CKD, and how this association may be modified by Hcy levels, remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate whether the association between the TyG index and CKD in hypertensive patients is modified by Hcy levels and to examine this potential interaction.Methods and results26679 subjects were included in this multicenter observational study. The TyG index was calculated as ln [TG (mg/dL) × fasting triglycerides (mg/dL)/2]. CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2. The association between the TyG index and CKD was analyzed using fitted smoothing curves and multivariable logistic regression. The overall prevalence of CKD was 7.0%. The association between the TyG index and CKD was modified by Hcy levels. A significant positive association was observed in participants with Hcy > 15 μmol/L (ORs per 1-unit increase: 1.37; 95% CI: 1.22, 1.54), but not in those with Hcy ≤15 μmol/L (ORs: 1.33; 95% CI: 0.99, 1.78). In quartile analyses, the association was stronger in the Hcy > 15 μmol/L group, with ORs (95% CI) of 1.38 (1.17, 1.63), 1.46 (1.22, 1.75), and 1.67 (1.37, 2.05) for Q2–Q4, respectively. In the Hcy ≤15 μmol/L group, the corresponding ORs were 1.38 (0.88, 2.15), 1.35 (0.84, 2.15), and 1.80 (1.09, 2.97). Moreover, after adjusting for confounding factors, a significant synergistic effect of high TyG index and Hcy on CKD risk was observed (OR = 11.47; 95% CI: 7.91, 16.65).ConclusionOur study findings suggest that Hcy and the TyG index are independently associated with an increased risk of CKD among Chinese patients with hypertension. The TyG index was strongly and consistently associated with increased CKD risk in individuals with elevated Hcy (>15 μmol/L), but this association was attenuated in those with lower Hcy levels.Trial registrationThe China H-type Hypertension Registry Study (CHHRS) was registered in ChiCTR1800017274 on. Introduction

The progressive nature of chronic kidney disease (CKD), coupled with its frequently asymptomatic early course, presents a significant challenge for timely detection. CKD affects nearly 10% of the world population, and its prevalence is increasing [1]. Besides, hypertension has been strongly associated with non-diabetic CKD; the two conditions participate in a vicious circle, mutually promoting one another and ultimately leading to kidney failure [2–4]. Moreover, hypertension combined with CKD significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular adverse events, which represent the leading cause of death in CKD [3]. However, despite standard measures to intervene in conventional risk factors for CKD, the number of patients who develop kidney failure continues to increase [5, 6]. Therefore, the identification of other modifiable risk factors and timely interventions is particularly important for the early prevention of CKD. In recent years, research on the impact of insulin resistance (IR) and high levels of homocysteine (Hcy) on CKD has gained significant momentum. Besides, growing evidence suggests that IR and Hcy could be used to predict the risk of CKD [7–10].

IR is defined as a diminished physiological response to insulin, characterized by impaired glucose uptake in surrounding tissues and dysregulated hepatic glucose output [11, 12]. Moreover, IR constitutes a fundamental disruption in glycolipid metabolism. While the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp is the gold standard for the diagnosis of IR, its application in clinical and large-scale epidemiological studies is limited by its high cost and technical demands [13]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has recently gained prominence as a simple and reliable surrogate marker for insulin resistance (IR), with its strong correlation to the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique well-established [14–16]. Several previous studies have revealed a relationship between the TyG index and the risk of CKD [17–19]. However, the potential modifying effect of Hcy levels on the TyG–CKD relationship remains unexamined. Furthermore, given that pharmacological management of IR is not universally tolerable or accessible, identifying adjunctive, non-pharmacological strategies for CKD prevention is imperative. The optimization of Hcy levels represents one such promising and complementary therapeutic target.

Hcy is now understood to play a major role in the folate cycle and methionine cycle [20]. Hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy) has long been established as a non-traditional risk factor for CKD and cardiovascular complications; however, the relationship between HHcy and CKD has received much attention recently [21, 22]. Multiple studies have reported a significant association between homocysteine and the risk of CKD [23–25]. Moreover, in the CKD sub-study of the Chinese Stroke Primary Prevention Trial (CSPPT-CKD), it was demonstrated that enalapril-folic acid significantly delayed the progression of CKD compared with enalapril in hypertensive patients with mild to moderate CKD [26]. Current evidence suggests that folic acid can significantly decrease the level of Hcy. Notably, the rate of folate deficiency and the frequency of the TT genotype in the MTHFR gene, a key enzyme for Hcy metabolism, are both higher in the Chinese population [27, 28]. Given that China has not implemented mandatory folic acid fortification, unlike many European and American countries, patients with HHcy are more common in China than in Europe and America, accounting for about 75% of the hypertensive population [29]. This suggests that the benefit of Hcy-lowering therapy may be more pronounced in the Chinese hypertensive population. Beyond its independent effects, a plausible biological rationale exists for a synergistic interaction between HHcy and insulin resistance (IR) in promoting renal damage. Experimental evidence indicates that elevated Hcy may inhibit insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity, thereby exacerbating IR [30], while Hcy-induced endothelial dysfunction could concurrently potentiate the renal injury driven by IR [31]. Given the high prevalence of HHcy in this population and the established association of the TyG index (a surrogate marker for IR) with CKD, we hypothesized that the strength of the association between the TyG index and CKD might be modified by serum Hcy levels.

Therefore, the study aimed to investigate whether the association between the TyG index and the risk of CKD in hypertensive patients is modified by serum Hcy levels, and to identify potential effect modifiers.

Methods

Study population

Data from the China H-type Hypertension Registry Study (CHHRS)(Registration number: ChiCTR1800017274, Date of Registration: July 20, 2018; URL: https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=28262) was used for this analysis. This real-world observational study, conducted in Wuyuan County and Yuexi County, China, aimed to establish a population-based registry cohort of H-type hypertension and to investigate its prevalence, treatment patterns, and prognostic determinants in China. Eligible participants included individuals aged 18 years or older who were diagnosed with hypertension according to the following criteria: systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication within the preceding two weeks, along with the provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria comprised patients with documented psychiatric conditions or communication impairments, short-term residents, and those deemed unable to adhere to the follow-up requirements. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Biology at Anhui Medical University (No. CH1059) and the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (No. 2018019) [32]. The research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. A total of 26,679 subjects were analyzed. Figure S1 illustrates the specific exclusion process used in this analysis.

The sample size was estimated using the Kish-Leslie formula for cross-sectional studies [33]. To ensure a robust sample, the calculation was based on the upper bound of the reported CKD prevalence in hypertensive cohorts (26.3%) [34, 35]. Assuming a conservative p value of 0.095–0.263, a 95% confidence level, and 99.3% power, and accounting for a design effect of 1.5 and a 10% non-response rate, the required sample size was calculated to be 11,122~25,074. The final cohort of 26,679 participants exceeded this upper threshold, providing a statistical power of 99.3%.

Data collection

Data were collected through clinical examinations and detailed questionnaires by trained investigators, in accordance with standard operating procedures. Current smoking was defined as smoking > l cigarette per day for l year or more or a cumulative smoking amount > 360 cigarettes per year [36]. Current drinking was defined as drinking alcohol an average of at least two or more times a week over a year [36]. There were three categories of educational levels, including illiterate, primary school, secondary school and above. A person’s degree of physical activity was described as light, moderate, or heavy. The medication status was determined according to the current bottle information. Diabetes was defined as current use of hypoglycemic agents, and/or fasting blood glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, and/or a history of diabetes. After 10 minutes of rest in a seated position, participants’ pulse and blood pressure were measured three times using electronic sphygmomanometers (Omron; Dalian, China), with the mean of the measurements used for analysis. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2).

Laboratory assays

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from subjects after an 8-hour fast and sent to the central laboratory (National Clinical Research Center for Kidney Disease in Guangzhou, China) for processing and analysis within 24 hours. Beckman Coulter’s automatic clinical analyzer was used to measure the following biomarkers: total cholesterol (TC), Hcy, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides (TG), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and γ-glutamyltransferase (GGT). The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula was used for calculating the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [37].

Definition of variables

The TyG index was calculated using the following equation: ln [TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)/2] [38]. CKD was defined in accordance with the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative guidelines as an eGFR of less than 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 of body surface [39]. Based on established clinical significance [26], serum Hcy levels were classified into two groups: ≤15 μmol/L and > 15 μmol/L.

Statistical analysis

Data for categorical and continuous variables were presented as frequencies (%) and means ± standard deviations (SD), respectively. Baseline characteristics of different quartiles of the TyG index were assessed using chi-square tests/Fisher’s exact test (for categorical variables) and one-factor ANOVA tests (for continuous variables). Multivariate logistic regression models {odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI)} were used to evaluate the independent association of TyG index with risk of CKD after adjusting for the main covariates. Variables for adjustment in the regression analysis model were selected based on clinical importance, and the potential confounders’ effect estimates were individually changed by at least 10%; meanwhile, variables with p < 0.1 in the univariate analysis or clinical relevance were also taken into consideration. Moreover, the TyG index was converted into quartiles and the P-trend value was calculated to ensure the stability of the analysis. The dose-response relationship between TyG index levels and risk of CKD was assessed based on a generalized additive model (GAM, estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood method) and a fitted smooth curve (penalized spline method). Besides, stratified analyses and interaction testing were used to investigate potential effect modifiers of the association between the TyG index and the risk of CKD. The multiplicative interaction term in the logistic regression model was used to assess the interaction between TyG and Hcy, with the corresponding P-value for the interaction reported. An initial screening for interactions between the TyG index and other key clinical variables was also performed (Figure S2). In addition, the joint effect of TyG quartiles and Hcy levels (categorized as ≤ 15 μmol/L and > 15 μmol/L) on CKD risk was evaluated, using the TyG Q1 group combined with the low Hcy group (≤15 μmol/L) as the reference.

All analyses and data visualizations were conducted using R (The R Foundation, https://www.R-project.org) and EmpowerStats (X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA; http://www.empowerstats.com). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Baseline characteristics of study participants

In the Hcy ≤15 μmol/L group, Patients in the higher TyG index group were more frequently female, younger, and more highly educated. They also exhibited a higher prevalence of diabetes and antihypertensive medication use, but a lower likelihood of a history of smoking or drinking and of engaging in heavy physical labor. Biochemically, this group was characterized by elevated levels of FPG, LDL-C, DBP, SBP, BMI, AST, GGT, pulse rate, TG, and TC, alongside significantly lower HDL-C concentrations (all p < 0.05).

In the Hcy > 15 μmol/L group, a higher TyG index demonstrated significant associations with younger age, female sex, higher education, diabetes, lower rates of drinking and smoking, coupled with lower levels of HDL-C and higher levels of FPG, DBP, BMI, TG, LDL-C, and TC (all p < 0.05).

Association between TyG index and CKD

As shown in Table 2, after adjustment for potential confounders, the positive associations between TyG index and CKD were found in all models (models l-4). Stratified analysis

Stratified analysis was conducted to evaluate the modifying effect of potential confounders on the association between the TyG index and CKD (Fig. 2). An initial screening of interaction effects (Supplementary Figure S2) indicated that, among various clinical factors, Hcy level was a prominent modifier of the TyG-CKD association. The positive association was significantly stronger in the high Hcy group ( > 15 μmol/L) than in the low Hcy group (≤15 μmol/L) (P for interaction = 0.027). Sensitivity analysis

To further evaluate the association between the TyG index and eGFR, a sensitivity analysis was conducted. This analysis covered the overall population (Table S4) and subgroups stratified by Hcy levels (Table S5). Furthermore, to more accurately explore the combined effect of the TyG index and Hcy on CKD risk, a multivariable logistic regression model was reconstructed after excluding outliers (top and bottom 1%) for TyG and Hcy to assess their independent and interactive associations with CKD (Table S6–S7). In addressing the potential for over-adjustment bias arising from the incorporation of fasting plasma glucose in the TyG formula, a multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to investigate the relationship between the TyG and CKD, independent of the contribution of FPG (Table S8–S9). The findings suggest that the positive correlation between the TyG index and CKD remains significant even when controlling for FPG.In evaluating the robustness of the association between TyG and CKD, a sensitivity analysis was conducted after excluding individuals who had taken folic acid or vitamin B preparations. The results demonstrated that within this subgroup, the positive association between TyG and CKD remained stable and statistically significant (Table S10–S11).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify serum Hcy as an effect modifier in the association between the TyG index and CKD in Chinese adults with hypertension. The results showed a significant and positive association between the TyG index and CKD across all quartiles and as a continuous variable in the Hcy > 15 μmol/L group. In contrast, in the Hcy ≤15 μmol/L group, a significant association was observed only in the highest TyG quartile (Q4), while the overall dose-response curve largely overlapped with the null line, and the association per 1-unit increase in the continuous TyG index was not statistically significant. However, within the low Hcy group, the wider confidence intervals, particularly for the quartile analyses, likely reflect the limited number of CKD events in this subgroup, resulting in lower statistical precision. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting these results.

Previous studies have assessed the association between the TyG index and CKD. In a cohort study involving more than 10,000 individuals, Takuro et al. [19] reported that the TyG index has potential predictive value for CKD. Using data from the Northern Shanghai study, researchers found that after adjusting for multiple covariates, an elevated TyG index was significantly associated with the risk of renal microvascular injury [17]. In addition, our research team found a significant association between the TyG index and CKD in Chinese individuals with hypertension, consistent with the literature [18]. This could be explained by the detrimental effect of insulin resistance on the kidney. Although the mechanism of this phenomenon was not clear, it might be related to multiple mechanisms such as adipocytokines, podocyte-specific insulin resistance, abnormal salt sensitivity, oxidative stress and inflammation [25, 40–44].

In the present study, we found that the TyG index was significantly associated with the risk of CKD in hypertensive patients with Hcy > 15 μmol/L. While the precise mechanism through which Hcy modifies the TyG-CKD relationship remains unclear, the observed effect modification is consistent with a synergistic interaction. This plausibility is supported by established evidence indicating a direct, causal role for elevated Hcy in the pathogenesis of CKD. In a large prospective cohort study, elevated Hcy was found to be an independent predictor of decreased renal function and CKD after adjustment for multiple confounding factors [24]. Furthermore, Cohen et al. [10] found that subjects with Hcy > 15 μmol/L had higher odds ratios for CKD compared with subjects with Hcy ≤15 μmol/L in a cross-sectional study, which may be attributed to the following mechanisms. First, Hcy has been identified to damage glomerular endothelial cells, which might lead to an imbalance between endothelial-derived NO and inducible NO synthase [45–47]. Moreover, Hcy leads to proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells by reducing plasma adenosine, which accelerates glomerular sclerosis [48]. Besides, Hcy could oxidize low-density lipoprotein and promote kidney damage [49]. Last but not least, Hcy might increase the risk of hypertension in a dose-dependent manner, and thereby promote the occurrence of renal microvascular disease [50]. As previously noted, elevated Hcy levels may inhibit insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity, thereby impairing insulin receptor autophosphorylation and exacerbating insulin resistance [30]. Moreover, Hcy-induced endothelial dysfunction may potentiate renal injury driven by insulin resistance [31].

In the subgroup analysis of patients with elevated homocysteine (Hcy > 15 μmol/L), the relationship between TyG index and risk of CKD was more pronounced in those with a history of alcohol consumption compared to those without a drinking history. This might be due to the fact that chronic alcohol consumption induced IR, which in turn synergistically promoted the development of CKD [51, 52].

No significant relationship was found between TyG index and risk of CKD in hypertensive individuals with Hcy≤15 μmol/L, a finding not previously documented. While the precise mechanism remains unknown, it may relate to lower Hcy levels exerting a minimal inhibitory effect on insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity, thereby preserving insulin receptor autophosphorylation. As a result, insulin signaling remains largely intact, which may attenuate the development of IR-mediated renal injury [53–55].

HCY has been identified as a standalone risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, with documented evidence of its adverse impact on vital organs, including the heart, brain, and kidneys [56, 57]. The present study further demonstrates that elevated HCY levels may synergistically exacerbate renal impairment alongside the fasting TyG index, thereby accelerating the progression of CKD. This underscores the significant clinical importance of reducing HCY levels. The most effective approach to reducing HCY at present involves supplementing with folic acid, vitamin B12, and vitamin B6. However, given the considerable variations in dietary patterns across different regions, it is essential that HCY intervention strategies are adapted accordingly. In Western countries, the focus should be on restricting alcohol consumption to enhance the absorption and metabolism of folate and B vitamins [58]. Conversely, in regions such as China, where vegetables are typically cooked before consumption, high temperatures can destroy folate and B vitamins in food [59]. Consequently, the recommendation is for supplementary intake of folate and B vitamin preparations.

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the causal relationship between the TyG index and the risk of CKD at different levels of Hcy could not be clarified due to its cross-sectional study design. Second, although this study has adjusted for several important confounders, it cannot exclude other potential confounders. Third, the subjects of this study were the Chinese H-type hypertension population, which excluded individuals with Hcy < 10 μmol/L and other populations; therefore, the generalizability of these populations remained to be verified. Finally, the definition of chronic kidney disease is dependent on a solitary measurement of eGFR, with no consideration given to proteinuria. This relatively narrow diagnostic parameter may significantly impact the accuracy of the results.

Conclusion

Overall, serum Hcy level acts as a significant modifier in the relationship between the TyG index and CKD among Chinese adults with hypertension. The positive association between the TyG index and CKD was strong and consistent in individuals with Hcy > 15 μmol/L but was attenuated and largely non-significant in those with Hcy ≤15 μmol/L. Our findings suggest that in hypertensive patients with elevated Hcy levels, the TyG index may serve as a simple and effective indicator for CKD risk assessment. Future prospective studies are needed to confirm these interactions and explore whether Hcy-lowering therapies can mitigate the risk of CKD associated with insulin resistance.
