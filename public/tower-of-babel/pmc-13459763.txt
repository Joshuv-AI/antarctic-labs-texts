Flavor characteristics of Shitou goose meat cooked using different methods based on sensory analysis and GC-IMS technology
Authors: Guoliang Wang, Yinglin Su, Xuehua Wu, Haixiang Nan, Guoyang Zhang, Peiyan Cen, Yingmei Jia, Fa Liu, Zhihui Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Shitou goose is one of the world's largest economic goose breeds, yet research on its derived meat products remains relatively scarce. To facilitate the development of Shitou goose meat products, this study optimized the relevant processing parameters for Shitou goose meat prepared via different cooking methods and identified the characteristic profiles of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in goose meat prepared by three methods (conventional boiling, plain boiling, and sauce stewing) using gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS). Sensory evaluation was conducted by 10 professionally trained panelists, and the data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple range test. Results showed that plain-boiled and sauce-stewed Shitou goose meat exhibited optimal sensory texture after a cooking time of 30 min. The highest sensory score for plain-boiled goose meat was obtained with the optimized dipping sauce formula (per 100 g of goose meat: 10 g of light soy sauce, 0.75 g of Kaempferia galanga, 0.2 g of green onion powder, and 0.15 g of garlic powder). For the sauce-stewed goose meat, the maximum sensory score was obtained with the addition of 0.75 g of Chinese prickly ash powder, 0.2 g of chili powder, and 0.15 g of white sugar per 100 g of goose meat. A total of 76 VOCs were identified in all the Shitou goose meat samples, among which aldehydes, accounting for 42% of the total identified compounds by quantity, were the predominant volatile flavor compounds. Alcohols and terpenes were the characteristic flavor compounds of the plain-boiled and sauce-stewed goose products, respectively. The conventionally boiled goose sample showed a flavor profile highly similar to that of the plain-boiled goose meat, while a significant difference in flavor was observed between these two samples and the sauce-stewed goose meat. This study provides basic data for the processing and development of Shitou goose meat products.

Introduction

As a typical type of waterfowl meat, goose meat is rich in high-quality protein, essential amino acids and trace mineral elements, serving as a healthy food with high nutritional value and dietary therapeutic effects.1,2 The composition ratio of essential amino acids in goose meat is consistent with the nutritional requirements of the human body, rendering it easily digestible and absorbable, and its fat content and melting point are both lower than those of other poultry meats.3,4 According to traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), goose meat exerts distinct dietary therapeutic effects, including dispelling wind-dampness and delaying senescence. The consumption of goose meat and goose soup by elderly patients with diabetes mellitus may slow down the progression of their illness.5 In addition, goose meat exhibits preventive and curative effects on cough, and particularly, it exhibits favorable efficacy in the prevention and treatment of common cold, acute and chronic tracheitis, chronic nephritis, senile edema and other disorders.6,7 In 2002, the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) listed goose meat as one of the key green foods for priority development in the 21st century. As the world's largest producer and consumer of meat goose, China accounts for more than 90% of the global total goose meat output in terms of processed goose meat production.8 Therefore, processed goose meat products hold broad development prospects.

In China, the commercially farmed meat goose breeds mainly include the Shitou goose, Sichuan white goose, and Yangzhou goose.9 Indigenous goose breeds from different regions exhibit certain variations in fatty acid, amino acid, and volatile flavor compound profiles,10 which have further given rise to a variety of traditional local delicacies and cultural practices, such as Cantonese-style plain-boiled goose, Yangzhou air-dried goose, Jiangsu brine-boiled goose, Rongchang sauce-stewed goose, and Cantonese-style roast goose.8 However, in comparison with other poultry (e.g., chickens and ducks), goose meat lags far behind in research and processing, and the industrial development of processed goose meat products remains relatively sluggish. This is attributed to the lower convenience of goose meat processing and preparation compared with chicken and duck meat products.11 Meanwhile, China has intensified its support for the development of ready-to-eat food and deeply processed ready-to-eat goose meat products not only to meet consumer demand but also to align with national policy initiatives.12 Plain boiling and sauce stewing are popular traditional cooking methods in China and are widely favored by the public.13,14 Plain boiling is a relatively simple and efficient processing method for meat products; meat products prepared by this method typically feature a tender texture, an alluring inherent meat aroma, and a rich but non-greasy taste, which well preserves the original flavor of the meat products. In contrast, meat products processed via the sauce stewing possess a bright luster, a savory taste and tender meat texture, and the method imparts a rich sauce aroma to the meat products.15 These two cooking methods have their own unique characteristics, making them typical processing approaches for poultry meat products.

Gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) has emerged as a powerful technique for meat flavor analysis, combining the high separation efficiency of GC with the high sensitivity of IMS. Compared with traditional SPME-GC-MS, it requires no sample preconcentration and can detect trace volatile compounds, while also distinguishing between monomer and dimer forms of compounds, which is superior to electronic nose in qualitative analysis.16,17

The Shitou goose originated in Raoping County, Guangdong Province, and is recognized as one of the world's largest goose breeds, as well as the only large-sized goose breed certified as a geographical indication product by the Ministry of Agriculture of China.18 Guangxi and Guangdong Province are the top two administrative regions in China for meat goose production and consumption, with an annual consumption of nearly 100 million meat geese; among the various breeds, the Shitou goose is one of the most widely raised and consumed. Plain-boiled and sauce-stewed Shitou goose represent the primary consumption forms of this breed. However, research on Shitou goose products remains relatively scarce to date, which has impeded the commercial development of the Shitou goose industry. Specifically, this study aimed to address three core questions: (1) what are the optimal processing parameters for plain-boiled and sauce-stewed Shitou goose meat? (2) how do different cooking methods affect the volatile flavor profiles of Shitou goose meat? and (3) what are the key characteristic compounds and formation mechanisms underlying the flavor differences among products? Therefore, in this study, we attempted to process Shitou goose meat using plain boiling and sauce stewing techniques with optimization of the corresponding processing parameters. Meanwhile, we analyzed the flavor characteristics of the Shitou goose meat products prepared using these two cooking methods and investigated the effects of different cooking approaches on the flavor profiles of the goose meat products, aiming to provide methodological and theoretical references for the processing and research of Shitou goose meat products.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

120-day-old healthy male Shitou geese with similar body weight were purchased from Guangxi Yijiaxing Ecological Agriculture Co., Ltd. The geese were slaughtered, defeathered, eviscerated, thoroughly rinsed under running water, air-dried at room temperature, and stored at 4 °C under refrigeration. The breast muscles were dissected for subsequent experimental use within 6 hours after slaughter. Spices including green prickly ash, red prickly ash, Lysimachia sikokiana, cassia bark, Alpinia katsumadai, star anise, Lysimachia foenum-graecum, cumin, mint, clove, fennel, Angelica dahurica, licorice, Amomum kravanh, bay leaf, Amomum villosum, Alpinia officinarum and Kaempferia galanga were purchased from the Chinese Medicine Port in Yulin City, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. Kaempferia galanga powder, chive powder and garlic powder were the commercial products of Jiangsu Weitaimei Food Co., Ltd. Light soy sauce, peanut oil, Chinese liquor, table salt and green Chinese onion were obtained from Shengshi Baihui Supermarket in Yulin City, Guangxi Province.

Effect of boiling time on the texture of Shitou goose meat

The texture profile of Shitou goose breast muscles as a function of boiling time was investigated using a texture analyzer. Goose breast muscles were immersed in boiling water with the addition of 5 g each of green Chinese onion, ginger slices and Chinese liquor, and the power of the induction cooker was set at 1000 W. The samples were boiled for 20 min, 25 min, 30 min, 35 min and 40 min, respectively, then cut uniformly into cubic pieces with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm. 6 Biological replicates were set for each boiling time treatment, and 3 technical replicate measurements were performed for each sample. The springiness, chewiness, cohesiveness and hardness of the samples were determined using a texture analyzer. The test parameters were set as follows: a cylindrical P/50 probe was adopted with a 25 N load cell; the trigger force was 0.3 N, the compression ratio was 20%, the test speed was 60 mm min−1, and the dwell time was 1 s. The rationale for selecting a 20% compression ratio is as follows: the breast muscle of Shitou goose has dense fibers and high hardness. In pre-experiments, a compression ratio of 30% or higher easily led to sample tissue fragmentation and unstable texture parameters. A 20% compression ratio can accurately determine the texture characteristics of goose meat without destroying the integrity of the sample tissue structure, which is consistent with previous studies on large waterfowl meat quality.19

Preparation of plain-boiled Shitou goose and the optimization of processing parameters

Pretreatment of goose breast muscles: 3–5 g each of green Chinese onion, fresh ginger and Chinese cooking liquor was added per 200 g of goose breast muscles; the mixture was thoroughly homogenized and marinated for 30 min. The marinated goose breast muscles were blanched in boiling water for 3 min, then retrieved and rinsed with cold water to remove blood foam on the surface, followed by air-drying to drain excess moisture.

Sensory scores were adopted as the evaluation index to optimize the boiling process and sauce formulation of plain-boiled Shitou goose, namely, to investigate the effects of boiling time and addition levels of light soy sauce, Kaempferia galanga powder, garlic powder and chive powder on the sensory quality of plain-boiled Shitou goose. First, the effect of different boiling times on the sensory quality of plain-boiled Shitou goose was investigated. Subsequently, with the boiling time fixed at 30 min, and the addition levels of Kaempferia galanga powder, garlic powder and chive powder set at 0.5 g/100 g of goose meat, 0.2 g/100 g of goose meat and 0.1 g/100 g of goose meat, respectively, the effect of varying addition levels of light soy sauce (0, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5 and 15 g/100 g of goose meat) on the sensory quality was explored to screen out the optimal addition level of light soy sauce. Furthermore, based on the screened optimal levels of the above-mentioned single factors, the effects of different addition levels of Kaempferia galanga powder (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 g/100 g of goose meat), garlic powder (0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 0.4 g/100 g of goose meat) and chive powder (0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15 and 0.2 g/100 g of goose meat) on the sensory quality of plain-boiled Shitou goose were investigated in sequence.

The optimized plain-boiled goose dipping sauce was prepared as follows: per 100 g of cooked goose meat, 10 g of light soy sauce, 0.75 g of Kaempferia galanga powder, 0.2 g of green onion powder, and 0.15 g of garlic powder were mixed with 5 g of boiled cold water and 3 g of peanut oil thoroughly. The sauce was mixed with the cooked and cut goose meat and left to stand for 5 min before sensory evaluation and flavor detection.

Preparation of sauce-stewed Shitou goose and the optimization of processing parameters

Pretreatment of goose breast muscles: 200 g of goose breast muscle was cut into pieces with dimensions of 5 cm × 2 cm × 0.5 cm, an appropriate amount of ginger shreds, green Chinese onion and 1% Chinese cooking liquor were added, and the mixture was kneaded thoroughly and marinated in a refrigerator at 4 °C for 30 min. The marinated goose breast muscles were first blanched in boiling water for 3 min, then rinsed thoroughly with cold water.

In the preparation of sauce-stewed goose meat, the addition levels of chili powder, Chinese prickly ash powder and white sugar, as well as the sauce-stewing time, exert a pivotal influence on its sensory quality; thus, these processing parameters require optimization. Sauce stewing of goose meat: lard and blended oil were added to the pot at a ratio of 1 : 1 and heated until smoking, followed by the addition of 4 g each of garlic slices, ginger slices, coriander and broad bean paste, along with 200 mL of boiling water. A sauce-stewing spice bag was then added, and the mixture was simmered over medium-low heat for 10–15 min to develop a rich aromatic flavor in the broth. Subsequently, the pretreated goose meat was placed into the pot, and tap water was added to completely submerge the meat. First, with the addition level of Chinese prickly ash powder fixed at 2.5 g/100 g of goose meat, white sugar at 15 g/100 g of goose meat, and sauce-stewing time at 30 min, the effect of different addition levels of chili powder (0, 0.75, 1.25, 1.75 and 2.5 g/100 g of goose meat) on the sensory quality of sauce-stewed goose meat was investigated. Then, based on the screened optimal levels of the single factor, the effects of different addition levels of Chinese prickly ash powder (0, 1.25, 2.5, 3.75 and 5 g/100 g of goose meat), white sugar (0, 7.5, 15, 22.5 and 30 g/100 g of goose meat) and varying sauce-stewing times (10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 min) on the sensory quality of sauce-stewed goose meat were investigated in sequence.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation of the Shitou goose meat products was performed with reference to the method reported by Deng et al.16 The sensory evaluation panel consisted of teachers and students majoring in Food Science and Engineering at Yulin Normal University, including 5 males and 5 females aged 20–40 years old. Panel training, the operation of the sensory evaluation laboratory, and the sensory evaluation procedures were all conducted in accordance with the Chinese national standard GB/T 22210-2008 Protocol for Sensory Evaluation of Meat and Meat Products.

Rationale for panel size

The number of samples in each sensory evaluation batch in this study did not exceed 6. According to GB/T 22210-2008 and published meat sensory evaluation studies, 8–10 professionally trained panelists can obtain stable and reliable sensory data for experiments with fewer than 8 samples; thus, the panel size of 10 was sufficient for this study.

Experimental procedure and replicate design

Sensory evaluations for the same series of single-factor experiments were conducted on the same day to avoid systematic errors caused by environmental and panelist status differences. All samples were coded with three-digit random numbers, and panelists were completely blinded to sample treatment information and experimental purposes. Any form of communication was prohibited during the evaluation. Three parallel samples were set for each treatment group, and each panelist performed two replicate scores for each parallel sample; the final average value was used for statistical analysis. Between evaluations of every two samples, panelists were required to rinse their mouths thoroughly with deionized water and rest for 1 min to avoid cross-flavor interference.

Several key evaluation criteria are summarized as follows

(1) Well-trained panelists were required to identify and score the appearance, texture, odor and taste of the samples using a 9-point scale, as specified in SI Tables 1 and 2. (2) All evaluators conducted the assessment in individual sensory booths, and any form of communication was prohibited during the evaluation process. (3) Smoking, alcohol consumption and food intake were forbidden for at least 1 h prior to the sensory analysis. (4) 5 g Aliquots of goose breast meat samples were placed on white ceramic plates at room temperature, and each sample was labeled with a unique three-digit random code. (5) Deionized water was provided for panelists to rinse their mouths between sample evaluations to avoid cross-flavor interference. All sensory evaluation data were collected for subsequent statistical analysis.

GC-IMS analysis

Sample preparation: control group (Shitou goose meat): pretreated goose breast muscles were immersed in boiling water with 4 g each of green Chinese onion, ginger slices and Chinese liquor added; the power of the induction cooker was set at 1000 W for a 30-min boiling process, followed by sample retrieval. Plain-boiled Shitou goose meat: pretreated goose breast muscles were boiled in water with 4 g each of green Chinese onion, ginger slices and Chinese liquor at an induction cooker power of 1000 W for 30 min; the cooked goose breast muscles were cut into small uniform pieces and thoroughly mixed with the prepared plain-boiled sauce. Sauce-stewed Shitou goose meat: lard and blended oil were added to the pot at a 1 : 1 ratio and heated until steaming, then 4 g each of garlic granules, ginger slices, coriander and broad bean paste were added, along with 200 mL of boiling water. A sauce-stewing spice bag was subsequently added, and the mixture was simmered over medium-low heat for 10–15 min to develop a rich aromatic flavor in the broth. The pretreated goose meat was then placed into the pot, with tap water added to completely submerge the meat. After adding 2.5 g of chili powder, 5 g of Chinese prickly ash powder and 30 g of white sugar, the mixture was brought to a boil over high heat and then switched to low heat for a 30-min stewing process, followed by sample retrieval.

The sample pretreatment for instrumental analysis was performed with minor modifications based on the method described by Zhao et al.17 For each of the three groups of the goose meat products, triplicate samples (2 g per aliquot) were prepared and individually placed into 20 mL headspace vials. The vials were incubated at 80 °C for 15 min prior to injection. Detection conditions: a gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) unit coupled with an automatic headspace sampling unit was employed, with nitrogen (N2) serving as both the carrier and drift gas. The total analysis time was 40 min; the chromatographic column was MXT-WAX (30 m, 0.53 mm inner diameter, and 1.0 µm film thickness) purchased from RESTEK Corporation (USA); column temperature was maintained at 60 °C; IMS temperature was 45 °C; injection volume was 500 µL; incubation time was 15 min; incubation temperature was 80 °C; injection syringe temperature was 85 °C; and incubation agitation speed was 500 rpm. GC conditions (carrier gas flow rate): 2 mL min−1 for 0–2 min, 10 mL min−1 for 2–10 min, 100 mL min−1 for 10–20 min, and 100 mL min−1 for 20–40 min. IMS conditions (drift gas flow rate): 150 mL min−1 (constant throughout the analysis).

Data acquisition and analysis were performed using the instrument-specific analytical software (VOCal, GC-IMS Library Search) and three plug-ins (Reporter, Gallery Plot, and Dynamic PCA), provided by the G. A. S. Division of Shandong Hanon Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd. Qualitative analysis of volatile organic compounds was performed via the built-in National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) database and IMS database integrated in the Library Search software. The Reporter and Gallery Plot plug-ins were used to compare the spectral differences among samples, while the Dynamic PCA plug-in was applied to conduct cluster analysis on all samples.

Statistical analysis

Data and image processing was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. This study adopted a single-factor completely randomized experimental design. One-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was used to analyze the significance of differences between different treatment groups, with treatment as a fixed effect and biological replicate as a random effect. Post-hoc test was performed using Duncan's multiple range test (DMRT). All results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with a significance level set at p < 0.05 (values with the same lowercase letters indicate no significant differences among groups, while those with different lowercase letters indicate significant differences among groups). Meanwhile, Tukey's HSD test was used to verify all data herein, and the significance grouping results of the two post-hoc methods were completely consistent. Principal component analysis (PCA) of meat quality attributes and volatile compounds was conducted using SIMCA-P software (Version 14.1).

Results and analysis

Effect of boiling time on the texture of Shitou goose meat

As shown in Fig. 1A–E, the springiness, gumminess, chewiness, cohesiveness and hardness, respectively, of Shitou goose meat all decreased significantly with the extension of boiling time (p < 0.05). These texture parameters exhibited the most significant changes when the boiling time was extended from 20 min to 30 min. The core reason for this change is that during boiling, actin and myosin in goose meat undergo progressive coagulation and contraction at 60–80 °C, leading to water loss between muscle bundles and gradual reduction in hardness. Meanwhile, collagen in the endomysium and perimysium is degraded and gelatinized under hydrothermal conditions, and the 20–30 min period is the key window for rapid collagen degradation, which directly causes the significant changes in texture parameters during this period.20 In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the springiness, gumminess, chewiness, cohesiveness and hardness of Shitou goose meat between the 30 min and 35 min boiling treatments (p > 0.05). When the boiling time was further extended to 40 min, the springiness, cohesiveness and hardness of the samples showed no significant difference compared with those boiled for 35 min (p > 0.05), while the gumminess and chewiness decreased markedly (p < 0.05). Although the values of springiness, gumminess, chewiness, cohesiveness and hardness of the goose meat boiled for less than 30 min were higher than those under other boiling durations, an insufficient boiling time resulted in high meat toughness, which impaired mastication. Additionally, whole geese were prone to under-cooked internal tissue when boiled for a short time, posing potential food safety hazards. Notably, the boiling time for Cantonese-style plain-boiled goose is typically controlled at 30–35 min, which was in good agreement with the optimal sensory values of goose meat treated for 30–35 min in this study (Fig. 1).

Effect of dipping sauce on the sensory quality of plain-boiled Shitou goose meat

Light soy sauce, Kaempferia galanga, green Chinese onion (chive powder) and garlic are the most commonly used ingredients in Cantonese-style plain-boiled dipping sauce. Among these, light soy sauce establishes the savory umami base, Kaempferia galanga primarily functions to eliminate fishiness and enhance aroma, and green Chinese onion combined with garlic synergistically elevates the flavor layering. Together, these four ingredients form a flavor network that maximizes the characteristic attributes of Shitou goose meat, namely crisp skin with rich fat aroma, as well as tender and juicy muscle texture. As shown in Fig. 2A, the sensory score exhibited a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with an increase in the addition level of light soy sauce. The dipping sauce achieved an optimal savory umami taste with the highest sensory score when the addition level of light soy sauce was set at 10 g/100 g of goose meat. However, an excessive addition level would result in an overly salty taste and a darker color of the dipping sauce, which impaired the taste and color of plain-boiled Shitou goose meat. The effect of Kaempferia galanga powder addition on the sensory quality of plain-boiled Shitou goose meat is presented in Fig. 2B. The dipping sauce had a faint Kaempferia galanga flavor when the addition level was ≤0.5 g/100 g of goose meat; the highest sensory score was obtained at an addition level of 0.75 g/100 g of goose meat. In contrast, an addition level of ≥1 g/100 g goose meat led to an overpowering Kaempferia galanga flavor that masked the aroma of the other ingredients, accompanied by a subsequent decline in the sensory score. The chemical mechanism underlying this masking effect is that the main flavor-active components of Kaempferia galanga powder are terpenoids such as 1,8-cineole and α-pinene, which have a strong pungent aroma and extremely low olfactory thresholds. When added in excess, high concentrations of these terpenoids occupy the main binding sites of olfactory receptors, inhibiting human perception of other low-concentration flavor substances and thus masking the background aroma of goose meat and the flavor of other ingredients. This mechanism is analogous to that by which anethole masks the intrinsic earthy and off-flavors naturally present in river snails.21 The effects of garlic powder and chive powder addition on the sensory quality of plain-boiled Shitou goose meat also followed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with an increase in their addition levels. Specifically, the highest sensory score was achieved at a garlic powder addition level of 0.2 g/100 g of goose meat (Fig. 2C) and a chive powder addition level of 0.15 g/100 g of goose meat (Fig. 2D).

Effect of boiling time and dipping sauce formulation on the sensory quality of plain-boiled Shitou goose. (A) Cooking time; (B) raw soy sauce addition; (C) galangal powder addition; (D) garlic powder addition; (E) chive powder addition.

Effect of sauce-stewing time and key ingredients on the sensory quality of sauce-stewed Shitou goose

In the preparation of sauce-stewed Shitou goose, sauce-stewing time and the addition levels of Chinese prickly ash powder, chili powder and white sugar are key factors influencing the sensory quality of the final product. As shown in Fig. 3A, when the sauce-stewing time was increased from 10 min to 30 min, the color, aroma, numbing-spicy taste and savory umami of the goose meat were enhanced, with the sensory score increasing progressively. At a sauce-stewing time of 30 min, the goose meat presented a characteristic reddish brown sauce color, with a compact texture and intact shape; it exhibited a tender yet non-mushy mouthfeel with favorable chewiness, along with a well-balanced numbing-spicy and savory flavor and a rich aftertaste. In contrast, when the sauce-stewing time exceeded 30 min, the color of the goose meat product changed from reddish brown to dark red, the meat texture became loose with a deteriorated shape and reduced chewiness, and the saltiness and numbing-spicy taste were overpowering. Notably, in the study conducted by Huang et al.,22 which prepared sauce-stewed goose using whole slaughtered geese, the optimal sauce-stewing time was 60 min, representing a significant difference from the 30 min optimal time obtained in this study where sliced goose breast muscles were used as the raw material. It should be noted that the use of 0.5 cm-thick goose breast slices in this study is a morphological simplification compared to commercial processing, which typically uses whole portions or intact pieces. The larger specific surface area not only accelerates heat transfer, but also increases the penetration rate of flavor compounds and the magnitude of water retention changes during stewing, all of which affect sensory outcomes. Therefore, the optimal 30 min stewing time obtained in this study is only applicable to thin-sliced goose breast, and further optimization is required for large pieces or whole geese in commercial production. The effects of Chinese prickly ash powder, chili powder and white sugar addition on the sensory quality of sauce-stewed goose meat all exhibited a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with an increase in their addition levels. Specifically, the optimal numbing intensity and the highest sensory score were achieved at a Chinese prickly ash powder addition level of 2.5 g/100 g of goose meat (Fig. 3B); moderate spiciness and the highest sensory score were obtained when the chili powder addition level was 1.25 g/100 g of goose meat (Fig. 3C). White sugar functions to soften the numbing-spicy mouthfeel and enhance the umami taste of the product. A well-balanced combination of sweet, numbing-spicy and savory umami flavors was achieved at an addition level of 7.5–15 g/100 g of goose meat, corresponding to the highest sensory score (Fig. 3D). When the addition level of white sugar exceeded 30 g/100 g of goose meat, the sweet taste became prominent, which disrupted the balance of numbing-spicy, salty and umami flavors (Fig. 3D).

Effect of stewing time and three key ingredients on the sensory quality of sauce-stewed Shitou goose. (A) Stewing time; (B) addition amount of Chinese prickly ash powder; (C) addition amount of chili powder; (D) addition amount of sugar.

Differential analysis of volatile flavor compounds in Shitou goose meat products under different cooking methods

Three-dimensional GC-IMS spectra of the volatile flavor compounds in the goose meat prepared by different cooking methods were generated via the Reporter plug-in (Fig. 4A). In the spectra, the X-axis represents drift time, the Y-axis denotes retention time, and the Z-axis indicates peak intensity. The control group contained the fewest types of volatile flavor compounds, while the plain-boiled and sauce-stewed goose meat samples had a significantly higher variety of volatile flavor compounds than the control group. However, considerable differences in the volatile flavor compounds were observed among the goose meat samples prepared by these three distinct cooking methods.

Three-dimensional GC-IMS spectrogram (A), direct comparison plot (B) and difference comparison plot (C) of Shitou goose meat prepared via different cooking methods.

Fig. 4B shows the two-dimensional GC-IMS spectra of the volatile flavor compounds in the goose meat prepared by different cooking methods. The main differences in volatile flavor compounds among the goose meat samples prepared using different cooking methods are reflected in the intensity, quantity, position and drift time of the ionic peaks. The thick red vertical line at the abscissa of 1.0 represents the reaction ion peak (RIP). Each dot on both sides of the RIP peak corresponds to one type of flavor compound, and the color depth indicates the signal intensity of the corresponding volatile flavor compound. White indicates a weak signal of the flavor compound, whereas red indicates a strong signal; namely, the deeper the color, the stronger the signal.

To facilitate the visualization of differences among the goose meat samples, a differential comparison mode was adopted for analysis, with the results presented in Fig. 4C. The differential maps of volatile flavor compounds in the different goose meat samples were obtained by removing the blue background of the two-dimensional top view (with the control group as the reference) and filling the area with white. In the maps, red indicates that the concentration of the corresponding flavor compound is higher than that of the reference, while blue indicates a lower concentration relative to the ref. 23. The majority of response signals in Fig. 4C (the red boxed area) were observed in the range of 100–1000 s for retention time and 1.0–2.0 ms for drift time. Within the red box, the number of red-signal spots in the plain-boiled goose meat products was significantly lower than that in the control group and the sauce-stewed goose meat products. This phenomenon could be attributed to the limited seasonings used in the plain-boiled goose meat and the diverse spices added in the sauce-stewed goose meat, where the volatile flavor compounds derived from seasonings or spices masked some endogenous flavor compounds of the goose meat. The red-signal spots of the control group were mainly concentrated in the range of 1.0–1.5 ms for drift time, whereas those of the sauce-stewed and the plain-boiled goose meat were predominantly distributed near 1.0 ms. From a chemical perspective, the drift time of a compound in IMS is negatively correlated with its molecular mass, molecular cross-sectional area, polarity and proton affinity: compounds with a smaller molecular size, lower polarity and higher proton affinity migrate faster in the drift tube and therefore have shorter drift times. The control group is dominated by long-chain unsaturated aldehydes, which have large molecular weights and strong polarity, resulting in longer drift times. In contrast, the plain-boiled and sauce-stewed groups contain higher proportions of small-molecule terpenes and alcohols from seasonings, which have smaller molecular weights and higher proton affinity, leading to shorter drift times.24 This finding indicated that the volatile flavor compounds of goose meat exhibited distinct characteristics with different cooking treatments. In summary, compared with the control group, the differential flavor compounds observed in the plain-boiled and sauce-stewed goose meat products were primarily the volatile flavor compounds originating from added seasonings or spices.

Qualitative analysis of the volatile flavor compounds in the goose meat prepared by different cooking methods was conducted using the built-in NIST and IMS databases of the FlavourSpec® flavor analyzer, in combination with the relative retention index, retention time, relative drift time and external standards.25 The results of the qualitative analysis are presented in Table 1 (some of the identified compounds existed as monomers, dimers or trimers). A total of 76 signal peaks were identified in the goose meat across all cooking methods, corresponding to 76 individual volatile flavor compounds, respectively. These volatile organic compounds were mainly categorized into aldehydes, alkenals, alcohols, esters, ketones, alkenes and terpenes, among other classes. Among them, 32 aldehyde compounds were identified, accounting for 42% of the total 76 identified compounds by quantity; in terms of relative peak area, aldehydes accounted for more than 55% of the total volatile compounds, making them the predominant volatile flavor compounds in the Shitou goose meat.

Qualitative identification results of volatile organic compounds in Shitou goose meat prepared via different cooking methods

The 76 identified compounds can be divided into two categories: (1) exogenous compounds, mainly including terpenes (γ-terpinene, α-terpinene, and β-myrcene) and sulfur-containing compounds, which were only detected in the plain-boiled and sauce-stewed groups and originated entirely from added seasonings and spices; and (2) endogenous compounds, including most aldehydes, alcohols and ketones, which were detected in all three groups and mainly generated from lipid oxidation and Maillard reaction during heating, constituting the background flavor of goose meat.

Among them, the aldehydes mainly included benzaldehyde, nonanal, (E)-2-heptenal, octanal, (E)-2-hexenal, 3-methyl-2-butenal, heptanal, hexanal, pentanal, 3-methylbutanal, butanal, propanal, isobutanal, 3-(methylthio)propanal, (E)-2-decenal, 2-undecenal and (E)-2-nonenal. Aldehydes are typically generated from lipid oxidation and exhibit low odor thresholds and high volatility, thus playing a pivotal role in the flavor formation of goose meat. For instance, octanal and nonanal impart a scorched aroma, pentanal contributes a fruity note, and 3-(methylthio)propanal exhibits a strong onion- and meat-like odor.26

Ketones are predominantly derived from the Maillard reaction and lipid oxidation, and they exert an important effect on flavor formation. The ketones identified in goose meat included 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one, 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, 2-butanone, acetone, hydroxyacetone, 2-heptanone and 2-methyltetrahydrofuran-3-one. Alcohols possess relatively high odor thresholds, resulting in a less pronounced effect on flavor formation compared with aldehydes, but their contribution is non-negligible. For example, 1-pentanol imparts an oily odor. The alcohols identified in the goose meat products comprised 1-octen-3-ol, 1-pentanol, 1-penten-3-ol, 1-butanol, (E)-2-penten-1-ol, 1-propanol, ethanol, 1-hexanol, isoamyl alcohol, (Z)-2-penten-1-ol, eucalyptol, methanol, isobutanol, 3-(methylthio)propanol, α-terpineol and 2-ethylhexanol.

Alkenes are mainly produced from the hydrolysis, oxidation and decomposition of unsaturated fatty acids in goose meat and added spices. Although present in low concentrations, they are critical for the formation of the overall flavor profile. For example, myrcene and terpinene exhibit a citrus- and lemon-like aroma. The alkenes identified in the goose meat products included terpinolene, terpinene, α-terpinene, β-myrcene and dipentene. The esters identified included ethyl 2-hydroxypropionate, ethyl hexanoate, ethyl acetate and ethyl isovalerate, which also exert a certain effect on flavor formation. Esters are typically formed by the esterification of free fatty acids with alcohols generated from goose meat during heating or alcohols derived from added seasonings. In addition, 2-n-pentylfuran imparts a bean-like, fruity, earthy and vegetable-like aroma, while 2-ethylfuran exhibits a strong scorched odor; both compounds contribute to the flavor formation of the goose meat products to a certain extent.

The fingerprint spectra clearly exhibit the differences in volatile components among the various samples. Each row in the spectra contains all the signal peaks of a single goose meat sample, while each column represents the differences in the content of the same volatile flavor compound in the goose meat prepared by different cooking methods. As shown in the red-boxed areas of Fig. 5, significant differences were observed in the volatile organic compounds of the goose meat across different cooking methods, which could be attributed to the addition of distinct spices during the cooking process. Meanwhile, the discrepancies could also be largely ascribed to the variations in lipid oxidation and Maillard reaction occurring in the goose meat under different cooking conditions.

The characteristic volatile compounds in the control goose meat samples mainly included (E)-2-undecenal, (E)-2-decenal, (E)-2-nonenal, (E)-2-octenal, (E)-2-heptenal, (E)-2-hexenal, (E)-2-pentenal, nonanal, octanal, heptanal, hexanal, pentanal, butanal, propanal, 2-ethylhexanol, 1-octen-3-ol, 1-penten-3-ol, 1-pentanol, 2-heptanone, 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one, ethyl hexanoate and limonene. Aldehydes dominated these volatile components, and most aldehydes have low odor thresholds, imparting pleasant aromas such as grassy, fruity, nutty and cheesy notes. The aroma synergism among different aldehydes ultimately constitutes the primary flavor profile of goose meat.26,27

In the plain-boiled goose meat samples (Fig. 5), the characteristic flavor compounds included 1-hexanol, 1-propanol, 3-methyl-2-butenal, 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, 3-(methylthio)propanal, 1-butanol, ethyl acetate, 3-methyl-1-butanol and isobutanol.

In the sauce-stewed goose meat samples (Fig. 5), the major characteristic flavor compounds were γ-terpinene, terpinolene, 1,8-cineole, α-terpinene, β-myrcene, 2-ethylfuran, ethyl 3-methylbutanoate, dimethyl sulfide, 2-methyltetrahydrofuran-3-one, 3-(methylthio)propanol, α-terpineol, (Z)-2-penten-1-ol, hydroxyacetone, isobutanal, 3-methylbutanal and methanol. γ-Terpinene, terpinolene, 1,8-cineole, α-terpinene, β-myrcene and α-terpineol originated from the spices and seasonings such as cinnamon and Chinese prickly ash.28

The green-boxed area in Fig. 5 shows the differences in the content of various flavor compounds in the goose meat prepared by different cooking methods. Among these, ethyl lactate, benzaldehyde and 2-butanone presented higher contents in the control and plain-boiled goose meat samples; 2-n-pentylfuran was more abundant in the control and the sauce-stewed goose meat samples. In addition, several flavor compounds exhibited similar contents across all goose meat samples with different cooking methods, including (E)-2-heptenal (dimer), 1-propanol (dimer), acetic acid (monomer), acetic acid (dimer), acetone and ethanol.

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a straightforward analytical method for the transformation and dimensionality reduction of complex datasets. Through PCA, complex data can be converted into visual coordinate plots that retain representative information. A total contribution rate of PCA exceeding 85% is considered to reflect the primary information among samples. As shown in Fig. 6, the contribution rates of the first principal component (PC-1) and the second principal component (PC-2) were 72% and 22%, respectively, with a total contribution rate of 94%. This indicated that the vast majority of sample information was captured, and the PCA results could thus be used to characterize the flavor profiles of the goose meat prepared by different cooking methods. In the PCA score plots, the Euclidean distance between individual scatter points represents the dissimilarity or similarity among different samples;10 a shorter projection distance between samples in the plot indicates higher similarity, whereas a longer distance denotes a greater degree of difference. As shown in Fig. 6, the sauce-stewed goose meat samples were the farthest from the other two groups, with a separation degree of 89.7%. This value was calculated as the proportion of the between-group sum of squares of the sauce-stewed group versus the control and plain-boiled groups to the total sum of squares in the PCA space, indicating that sauce-stewing processing explained 89.7% of the variation in the goose meat flavor profiles. In contrast, the control group and the plain-boiled group were closely clustered, suggesting a high similarity in their flavor compositions. This phenomenon could be attributed to the fact that the control and the plain-boiled goose meat underwent the same boiling treatment, and the only additional step for the plain-boiled goose meat was the application of a dipping sauce consisting of peanut oil, light soy sauce, water, Kaempferia galanga powder, garlic powder and chive powder, which was likely the primary cause of the flavor difference between the two groups. In comparison, the sauce-stewed goose meat was prepared with a markedly different boiling process and a more diverse range of spices (e.g., Chinese prickly ash, cinnamon and star anise), all of which exerted a substantial influence on its flavor profile.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that textural parameters of Shitou goose meat, including springiness and gumminess, decreased significantly with the extension of boiling time, with the optimal boiling duration identified as 30–35 min. This result is highly consistent with the mechanisms of protein denaturation and collagen solubilization during muscle thermal processing. Specifically, actin and myosin in poultry muscle undergo gradual coagulation and contraction at 60–80 °C, leading to moisture loss and subsequent textural changes.29 The most pronounced textural changes were observed during the 20–30 min boiling period, possibly due to the rapid degradation of intramuscular collagen, which impairs the integrity of muscle tissue structure. This finding is fully consistent with the observation by Xu et al. in chicken boiling studies that the first 30 min represents the critical period for collagen degradation.30 During the 30–35 min boiling period, the meat texture remained stable, which also prevented myofiber breakage caused by overheating. In contrast, when the boiling time exceeded 40 min, the gumminess of the goose meat decreased, which is associated with perimysium rupture and myofiber adhesion. This phenomenon is consistent with the result reported by M. Wereńska et al. in the low-temperature sous vide processing of goose meat that the integrity of the endomysium declines rapidly when the heating time exceeds 40 min.31 Collectively, this study fills the research gap in the processing of plain-boiled goose, particularly regarding the texture-specific characteristics of large goose breeds such as Shitou goose.

The optimal addition levels of the dipping sauce system composed of light soy sauce, Kaempferia galanga, green Chinese onion and garlic powder were determined as 10 g/100 g, 0.75 g/100 g, 0.15 g/100 g and 0.2 g/100 g, respectively, which conforms to the flavoring logic of “umami foundation-fishiness elimination-flavor layering enhancement”.32 Amino acids such as glutamic acid (with a content of approximately 2.3 mg g−1) in light soy sauce could form taste synergism with salt ions; the excessive saltiness caused by over addition is essentially attributed to the saturation of Na+ perception, which is consistent with the conclusion reported by Fang et al. that “basic amino acids can significantly enhance the salty taste perception of NaCl”.33 The optimal dosage of Kaempferia galanga powder is correlated with its content of 1,8-cineole, a key component in the volatile oil of Kaempferia galanga, which can inhibit the olfactory perception of fishy substances in meat, while an excessive dosage would mask the original meat flavor due to the excessively high concentration of terpenoids.34 The optimized low-dosage results of green Chinese onion and garlic powder are associated with the strong flavor characteristics of alliin decomposition products (3-(methylthio)propanal) contained therein, where a small amount can effectively improve the flavor complexity.35 This study, for the first time, clarifies the quantitative formula of the dipping sauce for Shitou goose meat, supplementing the research data on special dipping sauces for waterfowl meat products.

Thirty minutes of sauce-stewing was determined as the optimal processing time for the sliced Shitou goose meat, which was significantly shorter than the 60 min sauce-stewing time for duck legs reported by Wang et al.36 This difference stemmed from the effect of raw material morphology on heat transfer efficiency. The sliced goose breast (0.5 cm in thickness) used in this study had a large specific surface area, which accelerated the permeation rate of soy sauce pigments (caramel color) and hydroxy-α-sanshool (the numbing component) from the sauce into the meat. In contrast, duck legs with a muscle layer thickness of 3–5 cm required a longer time for flavor infiltration. Exceeding 30 min of sauce-stewing resulted in a loose meat texture, which was attributed to the decreased water-holding capacity caused by the excessive denaturation of muscle proteins. Meanwhile, excessive Maillard reaction led to the formation of melanoidins, resulting in a darker color of the meat product.37 This observation was consistent with the findings reported by Xu et al.38, who noted that the increased browning intensity of poultry meat after sterilization at 121 °C induced the accumulation of melanoidins. The optimal addition levels of Chinese prickly ash powder (2.5 g/100 g of goose meat) and chili powder (1.25 g/100 g of goose meat) were correlated with the gustatory perception of their respective active components, namely hydroxy-α-sanshool (in Chinese prickly ash) and capsaicin (in chili pepper). However, an excessive dosage of both components would trigger taste fatigue,39 which was highly consistent with the perceptual concentration range of numbing and spicy substances reported by Puengsurin et al.40

A total of 76 flavor compounds were identified in this study via GC-IMS, with aldehydes accounting for 42% of the total identified compounds by quantity. The differences in flavor composition were derived from both exogenous seasonings and endogenous reactions during cooking. The control goose meat group was dominated by (E)-2-nonenal and octanal, which are generated from the oxidation of linoleic acid in intramuscular fat and feature low odor thresholds (0.01–0.1 mg kg−1).41,42 Specifically, autoxidation of oleic acid mainly produces octanal and nonanal, while autoxidation of linoleic acid yields hexanal, (E)-2-nonenal and (E)-2-octenal, and oxidation of linolenic acid generates (E)-2-hexenal.43 These compounds constitute the core of the original flavor of goose meat, which is fully consistent with the conclusion reported by Cheng et al. that aldehydes are the predominant volatile flavor compounds in goose meat products.44 3-(Methylthio)propanal in the plain-boiled goose meat originates from two sources: endogenous formation via Strecker degradation of methionine during heating, and exogenous introduction from chive and garlic powder in the dipping sauce,42 while the newly detected γ-terpinene in the sauce-stewed goose meat is sourced from Chinese prickly ash, serving as a characteristic flavor compound of the added seasonings.28 PCA analysis revealed that the separation degree of the sauce-stewed goose meat from the other groups reached 89.7%, which was attributed to the significantly higher content of spice-derived terpenoids in the sauce-stewed goose meat compared with the plain-boiled group. This finding is consistent with the result reported by Yuan et al. that a greater variety of spices leads to a higher degree of separation in flavor profiles.45 The GC-IMS flavor analysis method adopted in this study is identical to the technical approach used by Yan et al. for analyzing goose meat flavor, which further verifies the reliability of the results obtained in this study.41

Additionally, the close clustering of the control and the plain-boiled groups may also be partially attributed to the limitations of the experimental design: the 10-person sensory panel, while meeting national standards, has limited ability to detect subtle flavor differences between the two groups. Furthermore, the high-abundance endogenous aldehydes in the goose meat may mask the signals of low-abundance compounds introduced by the dipping sauce in GC-IMS detection.46

Conclusion

This study identified the optimal processing parameters for Shitou goose meat prepared by different cooking methods and elucidated the underlying mechanism of flavor differences, thereby providing critical data support for the optimization and industrial standardization of waterfowl meat product processing. Texture analysis demonstrated that the optimal boiling time for Shitou goose meat ranged from 30 to 35 min, under which the texture parameters remained stable, being highly consistent with the traditional processing of Cantonese-style plain-boiled goose meat. The optimal quantitative formulation of the dipping sauce for the plain-boiled goose meat was determined as 10 g/100 g of light soy sauce, 0.75 g/100 g of Kaempferia galanga powder, 0.15 g/100 g of chive powder and 0.2 g/100 g of garlic powder, which conforms to the traditional Chinese flavoring logic. For the sauce-stewed Shitou goose, the optimal stewing time was 30 min, with the optimal addition levels of Chinese prickly ash powder, chili powder and white sugar being 2.5 g/100 g, 1.25 g/100 g and 7.5–15 g/100 g, respectively. A total of 76 volatile flavor compounds were identified via GC-IMS, among which aldehydes accounted for 42% and served as the core components constituting the intrinsic flavor of the goose meat. The formation of these aldehydes was closely associated with lipid oxidation and the Maillard reaction. The control group was predominantly composed of aldehydes such as (E)-2-nonenal and octanal. The characteristic flavor of the plain-boiled goose meat originated from 3-(methylthio)propanal derived from the dipping sauce, while the sauce-stewed goose meat developed a distinct flavor profile due to the enrichment of terpenoids from added spices. PCA, with a total contribution rate of 94%, revealed that the degree of separation in flavor profiles between the sauce-stewed goose meat and the control/plain-boiled goose meat reached 89.7%, which was attributed to the spice-derived terpenoids. In contrast, the plain-boiled goose meat shared a similar flavor profile with the control group, with only minor differences arising from the dipping sauce.

This study, for the first time, defined the quantitative formulation of the dipping sauce for Shitou goose meat, filling the research gaps in the processing of large goose breeds and special dipping sauces for waterfowl. The optimal process parameters and flavor characteristic data obtained provide an important technical basis for the industrial production, process standardization and precise flavor regulation of ready-to-eat Shitou goose products. However, this study has certain limitations: first, process optimization was conducted using a one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) design, and the interactions between factors were not explored; second, the sensory panel size was relatively small, limiting the detection of subtle flavor differences; finally, GC-IMS only provided qualitative and semi-quantitative data for VOCs, and absolute quantification and odor activity value (OAV) calculation of key flavor-active compounds were not performed. Subsequent studies can optimize process parameter interactions using response surface methodology and further clarify the key flavor-active compounds and regulatory mechanisms of Shitou goose products combined with sensomics and absolute quantification techniques.
