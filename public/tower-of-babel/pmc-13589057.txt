Patient Preferences for Tracker Pin–Related Incision Configurations in Robotic-Assisted Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Varah Yuenyongviwat, Chirathit Anusitviwat, Theerawit Hongnaparak, Pakjai Tuntarattanapong, Khanin Iamthanaporn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty (TKA) requires tracker pin placement, which may result in different incision configurations. Although clinical outcomes across pin placement strategies appear comparable, patient preferences regarding incision design remain unclear.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational study included 101 patients with knee osteoarthritis who were recruited consecutively at a tertiary university hospital. Three tracker pin–related incision configurations were presented using standardized lower-limb models: (A) all-inside, consisting of the longest single midline incision; (B) femoral pin in–tibial pin out, consisting of a medium-length midline incision plus two tibial stab incisions; and (C) pin outside, consisting of the shortest midline incision plus four stab incisions. Participants rated each design using a 5-point Likert scale and selected a single preferred option. Cosmetic and recovery concerns were assessed using five Likert-scale items.ResultsPostoperative scarring received the highest concern ratings, followed by recovery time, whereas concern about incision length and the number of incisions was lower. Preference scores showed a numerical ranking: all-inside 4.0 [interquartile range (IQR), 3.0–4.0]; femoral pin in–tibial pin out 3.0 [IQR, 2.0–4.0]; and pin outside 3.0 [IQR, 2.0–3.0], without a statistically significant difference (Friedman χ² = 5.52, P = .063). When selecting a single preferred option, 46.5% chose all-inside, 25.7% femoral pin in–tibial pin out, and 27.7% pin outside.ConclusionsNo clear differences in overall preference were observed among the three incision configurations. Preoperative counseling should address expectations regarding scar appearance, wound care, and recovery trajectory rather than emphasizing incision configuration alone in robotic-assisted TKA.Keywords: Robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty, Patient preference, Incision design, Tracker pin placement, Postoperative scarring, Cosmetic concern

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is an established treatment for advanced knee osteoarthritis. With the increasing adoption of robotic-assisted TKA, surgical workflows have evolved, including the use of tracking pins that may require additional skin incisions beyond the conventional midline approach [1,2]. As a result, several incision designs are currently used in robotic-assisted TKA, differing in incision length, number, and location [3,4]. These variations largely reflect different strategies for tracker pin placement.

These include an all-inside approach using a single midline incision, an all-outside approach requiring additional small stab incisions for tracker placement, and a hybrid approach with 1 tracker placed inside and the other outside the main incision. Existing studies suggest that functional outcomes following these approaches are generally comparable, although differences in pin-related complications have been reported [5,6]. Consequently, selection of pin placement strategy is often guided by surgeon preference and technical considerations rather than clear differences in overall functional recovery.

Although these incision variations are generally considered minor technical details from a surgical perspective, incision design may influence patient perceptions of surgery. The number and size of incisions may affect cosmetic concerns, perceived invasiveness, and overall acceptance of the procedure. For many patients, particularly those undergoing elective joint replacement, the appearance of the surgical wound is an important component of postoperative satisfaction [7,8].

Despite the growing emphasis on patient-centered care, the selection of incision design in robotic-assisted TKA remains largely surgeon-driven. There is limited evidence comparing different incision patterns from the patient's perspective, and patient preferences regarding these designs have not been well characterized. Consequently, surgeons have little data to support preoperative counseling when discussing incision-related concerns with patients. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to explore patient preferences for different surgical incision designs used in robotic-assisted TKA. By comparing patient-rated preferences among three commonly used incision patterns, this study aims to provide evidence to inform incision planning and improve patient-centered decision-making in robotic-assisted knee arthroplasty.

Material and methods

This study was designed as a cross-sectional observational study. It was conducted at a tertiary university hospital in Thailand. Patient recruitment and data collection were performed between 1 September 2025 and 30 November 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Patients with a diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis who had not yet undergone robotic-assisted TKA at the time of survey completion were eligible for inclusion. Osteoarthritis was diagnosed based on clinical evaluation and radiographic findings consistent with standard diagnostic criteria. Patients were excluded if they had communication difficulties that could interfere with questionnaire completion. Eligible patients were invited consecutively during outpatient clinic visits and participation was voluntary.

All eligible patients who consented to participate were included in the analysis. As this was a single-visit, questionnaire-based study, there was no loss to follow-up. A total of 101 participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis.

Three surgical incision designs commonly used in robotic-assisted TKA were evaluated:

2.Femoral pin in, tibial pin out: a midline incision measuring approximately 14 cm combined with two additional tibial stab incisions (0.5 cm each).

Femoral pin in, tibial pin out: a midline incision measuring approximately 14 cm combined with two additional tibial stab incisions (0.5 cm each).

3.Pin outside: a midline incision measuring approximately 12 cm combined with two tibial and two femoral stab incisions (0.5 cm each).

Pin outside: a midline incision measuring approximately 12 cm combined with two tibial and two femoral stab incisions (0.5 cm each).

These incision designs reflect variations related to tracker pin placement in robotic-assisted TKA. All designs were presented using lower-limb models to ensure consistent visualization for all participants (Fig. 1).

Variables, outcome measures, data sources, and bias

The primary outcome was patient preference for surgical incision design, measured using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = least preferred, 5 = most preferred) for each incision pattern. Participants were also asked to rank the three incision designs in order of preference.

Secondary data included age, sex, body mass index, history of previous knee surgery, education level, and occupation. Participants were encouraged to provide open-ended responses explaining their reasons for incision preference. These responses were reviewed descriptively and grouped into recurring themes. Noninformative or missing responses were excluded from the descriptive qualitative summary.

To minimize information bias, all participants received the same standardized explanation and visual presentation of incision designs. Questionnaires were self-administered, with assistance provided only for clarification without influencing responses.

Data analysis and study size

Sample size calculation was performed using G∗Power software (version 3.1; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). For repeated-measures comparison among three incision configurations, assuming a small effect size (f = 0.20), a significance level of 0.05, 80% power, and a correlation among repeated measures of 0.60, a minimum sample size of 34 participants was required. A total of 101 participants were recruited and completed the study, exceeding the minimum requirement and improving the precision of the estimates.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.0.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and preference scores. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate, and categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages.

Given the ordinal nature of Likert-scale data and the repeated-measures design, preference scores among the three incision designs were compared using the Friedman test. When the overall test was statistically significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were planned using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with appropriate adjustment for multiple comparisons. Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed according to sex, history of previous knee surgery, and age. A two-sided P value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 101 patients with knee osteoarthritis were included in the analysis. The mean age was 63.8 ± 5.8 years, and the majority of participants were women (n = 87, 86.1%). The mean body mass index was 26.7 ± 4.2 kg/m2. Regarding sociodemographic characteristics, most participants had an educational level below a bachelor's degree, and occupations varied across categories. Patients reported low-to-moderate levels of concern regarding surgical incision characteristics and postoperative recovery. The mean composite cosmetic and recovery concern score, calculated as the average of five Likert-scale items (1 = least concerned, 5 = most concerned), was 1.9 ± 1. The five items demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = 0.81). Among individual concern items, concern regarding postoperative scarring had the highest mean score (2.3 ± 1.3), followed by the importance of recovery time when choosing an incision (2.2 ± 1.3). Concern related to incision length (1.8 ± 1.2) and the number of incisions (1.7 ± 1.2) showed lower scores. The perceived impact of incision size on postoperative confidence demonstrated intermediate scores (1.9 ± 1.3). Individual concern scores are presented in Table 2.

Preference scores differed numerically among the three incision designs. The all-inside incision received the highest median preference score (4.0 [inter quatile range (IQR), 3.0–4.0]), followed by the femoral pin-in tibia-out design (3.0 [IQR, 2.0–4.0]) and the pin-outside design (3.0 [IQR, 2.0–3.0]). However, the Friedman test demonstrated no statistically significant difference among the three designs (χ2 = 5.52, df = 2, P = .063).

When participants were asked to select a single preferred incision design, 47 patients (46.5%) chose the all-inside incision, 26 patients (25.7%) selected the femoral pin-in tibia-out incision, and 28 patients (27.7%) selected the pin-outside incision. This single-choice preference reflected a numerical tendency toward the all-inside incision despite the absence of statistically significant differences in preference scores.

Open-ended responses were available for 70 participants. The remaining responses were either missing or noninformative and were excluded from the descriptive qualitative summary. The most common reasons for incision preference were related to having fewer or a single incision (n = 31), followed by preference for a smaller or shorter incision (n = 25) and easier wound care (n = 12). Less commonly, participants mentioned expected recovery or wound healing (n = 1) and cosmetic appearance (n = 1).

Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed according to sex, previous knee surgery, and age. Single-choice preference distribution did not differ significantly by sex. Among male patients, 6 of 14 patients (42.9%) selected the all-inside configuration, compared with 41 of 87 female patients (47.1%) (P = .764). Patients with previous knee surgery showed a numerical tendency to select the all-inside configuration more frequently than those without previous knee surgery (59.5% vs 39.1%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .085). When stratified by median age, patients aged ≥64 years selected the all-inside configuration more frequently than younger patients (56.9% vs 32.6%), but this difference was also not statistically significant (P = .053).

Discussion

Robotic-assisted TKA has expanded rapidly and introduced variations in tracker pin placement that can alter surgical incision configurations [9,10]. While these differences may not substantially affect overall functional recovery, incision design may influence patient perceptions of cosmetic appearance, perceived invasiveness, and satisfaction with an elective procedure. In this context, we conducted this study to characterize patient preferences for commonly used incision designs in robotic-assisted TKA and to explore incision-related concerns that may inform preoperative counseling. Because participants completed the survey before surgery, these findings should be interpreted as preoperative preference data rather than as evidence of postoperative satisfaction or actual scar perception. Overall, cosmetic and recovery-related concerns were low to moderate, and although preference scores did not differ significantly among the three incision designs, the all-inside incision was most frequently selected when patients were required to choose a single preferred option.

This study had a number of limitations. First, this was a cross-sectional, survey-based study evaluating anticipated preferences rather than postoperative satisfaction or clinical outcomes; therefore, patient perceptions may differ after actual surgery and recovery. Second, incision designs were evaluated using lower-limb models with drawn markings rather than actual postoperative scars. Because postoperative scarring was the highest-rated concern, preferences may have differed if participants had viewed standardized photographs of healed wounds. Third, although the sample size met the a priori calculation, the study may have been underpowered to detect small differences in preference scores across incision designs. Fourth, most participants were female. As cosmetic concerns and scar perception may differ between male and female patients, our findings should be interpreted cautiously when applied to male patients. Fifth, this study was conducted in Thailand and represents a Thai knee osteoarthritis population. Because cosmetic and scar-related perceptions may be influenced by cultural context, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other populations. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future studies should evaluate patient preferences in postoperative settings, include more balanced sex distribution, and assess whether incision design influences patient-reported satisfaction and early recovery outcomes across more diverse patient populations.

In our study, patients reported overall low-to-moderate concern regarding incision-related cosmetic and recovery issues, as reflected by a relatively low composite concern score (mean 1.93 on a 5-point scale) with good internal consistency across items (Cronbach's alpha = 0.81). Notably, postoperative scarring emerged as the most prominent concern, aligning with prior evidence in TKA suggesting that patients' perceptions of wound appearance can meaningfully influence satisfaction [11,12]. Sundaram et al [13].conducted a prospective randomized trial comparing skin closure using 2-octyl cyanoacrylate with polyester mesh vs staples and demonstrated better wound cosmesis scores and greater patient satisfaction with wound appearance at early follow-up. Importantly, patients' own perception of scar appearance may have a substantial impact on quality of life, regardless of the objective physical characteristics of the scar. In addition, patient self-assessment of scar features such as length, width, and color has been shown to correlate well with clinician assessment, supporting the relevance of incorporating patient-reported scar perception into postoperative evaluation [14].

The next most prominent concern in our study was the perceived importance of recovery time, whereas concerns regarding incision length and the number of incisions were rated lower. This pattern suggests that patients may prioritize practical, function-related expectations over characteristics of the incision itself. This is consistent with large registry–based evidence from the National Joint Registry for England and Wales, in which Baker et al [15].surveyed patients more than 1 year after TKA and demonstrated that pain- and function-related components were strongly associated with patient satisfaction. However, this study assessed anticipated preference rather than actual postoperative recovery. We did not evaluate early ambulation, wound morbidity, postoperative pain, or pin-site complications. Therefore, whether these incision configurations differ in recovery remains uncertain. Clinically, these results indicate that preoperative counseling in robotic-assisted TKA may be most effective when focused on setting realistic recovery expectations and addressing postoperative wound-care concerns, rather than emphasizing incision geometry alone.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore patient preferences regarding incision configurations related to tracker pin placement in robotic-assisted TKA. In our study, preference scores showed only a numerical ranking without a statistically significant difference (P = .063), suggesting that patients rated the three configurations similarly overall. Nevertheless, when participants were required to choose a single preferred option, the all-inside configuration was selected most frequently. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence supporting the superiority of any incision configuration. Rather, they suggest that patients may evaluate incision configurations through multiple considerations, including the length of the main incision, the number of additional stab incisions, scar appearance, recovery expectations, and postoperative wound management.

Conclusions

This study found no clear differences in overall patient preference among the three incision configurations related to tracker pin placement in robotic-assisted TKA. Postoperative scarring and recovery time were the highest-rated concerns. Clinically, preoperative counseling should focus on setting realistic expectations regarding scar appearance, wound care, and recovery trajectory rather than emphasizing incision configuration alone.
