Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists in movement disorders: From Parkinson's disease to the broader spectrum — Mechanisms, evidence, and future directions
Authors: Ahmed Dahshan, Marwa Ibrahim Mahfouz Khalil, Shaimaa I El-Jaafary, Ahmed Al Yamani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), originally developed for type 2 diabetes and obesity, have emerged as potential neuroprotective agents in neurodegenerative disorders. GLP-1 signaling modulates key pathogenic pathways relevant to movement disorders, including neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and impaired proteostasis, with receptors widely expressed in motor-related brain regions. This narrative reviewaimed to summarize current mechanistic, preclinical, and clinical evidence regarding the role of GLP-1RAs across the spectrum of movement disorders.MethodsA narrative review of the literature was conducted, including preclinical studies, randomized clinical trials, observational data, and pharmacovigilance analyses evaluating GLP-1RAs in movement disorders.ResultsPreclinical studies consistently demonstrate neuroprotective effects of GLP-1RAs, including preservation of dopaminergic neurons, reduction of α-synuclein aggregation, and improvement in motor function. Clinical evidence is most advanced in Parkinson's disease, where early randomized trials of exenatide and lixisenatide suggested modest motor benefits and possible disease-modifying effects. However, subsequent larger studies, including the phase III Exenatide-PD3 trial, failed to demonstrate significant clinical benefit. Preliminary signals have been reported in multiple system atrophy, whereas evidence in Huntington's disease, essential tremor, and other movement disorders remains largely preclinical.ConclusionsGLP-1RAs represents a biologically plausible but as yet unproven disease-modifying strategy in movement disorders. Translational challenges, including pharmacologic heterogeneity, variability in central nervous system exposure, and clinical trial design limitations—must be addressed. Future research should focus on biomarker-guided patient selection, optimized CNS-penetrant agents, and rigorously designed clinical trials.Keywords: Movement disorders, GLP-1 RAs, Parkinson's disease, Current evidence, Trials

Introduction

Movement disorders constitute a heterogeneous group of neurological conditions characterized by abnormalities in speed, fluency, quality, or ease of movement [1]. They include Parkinson's disease (PD) which is the most prevalent, affecting an estimated 8.5 million individuals globally, but the spectrum extends to multiple system atrophy (MSA), Huntington's disease (HD), essential tremor, Lewy body dementia, and drug-induced syndromes. Despite decades of research, current pharmacological treatments remain largely symptomatic, with no agent convincingly proven to halt or reverse neurodegeneration at the population level [2].

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) originally approved for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and subsequently for obesity — have emerged as one of the most consequential drug classes in modern medicine. Semaglutide, liraglutide, exenatide, lixisenatide, and the dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide collectively represent a multi-billion-dollar market with expanding indications. As of early 2026, they have become one of the fastest expanding therapeutic classes in metabolic medicine [3]. The potential neuroprotective properties of GLP-1 signaling were first described in 2002 — predating even the first FDA approval of a GLP-1 agent — when receptors were identified in the central nervous system. Since then, a cascade of preclinical findings has been validated by phase II clinical trials, and two phase III studies (evoke and evoke+) are currently evaluating semaglutide in Alzheimer's disease [4]. Critically, GLP-1 receptors are expressed not only in metabolic tissues but also in the substantia nigra, striatum, hippocampus, cerebellum, and frontal cortex — regions central to motor control and the pathophysiology of movement disorders [5]. GLP-1 receptor agonists have therefore emerged as promising candidates for disease modification in neurodegenerative disorders, including movement disorders.

This narrative review synthesizes the current mechanistic and clinical evidence for GLP-1 RA use across the full spectrum of movement disorders. We place particular emphasis on the translational gap between preclinical models and human disease, identify opportunities for future investigation, and discuss practical considerations for movement disorder specialists.

Methods

This narrative review was conducted following the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) framework. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library using the terms “GLP-1 receptor agonist,” “glucagon-like peptide-1,” “semaglutide,” “liraglutide,” “exenatide,” “lixisenatide,” “tirzepatide,” “NLY01” in combination with “movement disorder,” “Parkinson,” “multiple system atrophy,” “Huntington,” “essential tremor,” “dystonia,” “Lewy body,” “drug-induced parkinsonism,” “ataxia” and “neurodegeneration”. We included preclinical studies, clinical trials, observational studies, pharmacovigilance analyses, and systematic reviews published from inception through February 2026. Conference abstracts presented at the American Academy of Neurology Annual Meeting (2025) and MDS Congress (2025, Hawaii) were also reviewed. Only articles published in English were included. Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts for relevance. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. No formal quality assessment was performed, consistent with narrative review methodology; instead, evidence is categorized descriptively by study design and consistency of findings.

Molecular mechanisms relevant to movement disorders

GLP-1 receptors (GLP-1Rs) are G-protein-coupled receptors linked to cyclic AMP/protein kinase A (cAMP/PKA) signaling pathways. Within the central nervous system, GLP-1R expression has been documented in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), striatum, locus coeruleus, hypothalamus, hippocampus, cerebral cortex, and cerebellum [3]. This anatomical distribution is particularly relevant to movement disorders, as these regions are critically involved in motor control, neurodegeneration, and neuroinflammatory signaling [4].

Parkinson's disease and synucleinopathy-related mechanisms

A central component of the GLP-1 hypothesis in Parkinson's disease (PD) is its interaction with impaired brain insulin signaling pathways. Increasing evidence suggests that insulin resistance and disrupted insulin receptor substrate signaling contribute to dopaminergic neurodegeneration in PD. In a secondary analysis of the exenatide PD trial using neuronal-derived extracellular vesicles, exenatide-treated participants demonstrated increased activation of insulin signaling proteins and downstream effectors including Akt and mTOR, providing in vivo evidence of biological target engagement rather than merely symptomatic benefit [6].

Neuroinflammation is another major pathogenic mechanism in PD and related synucleinopathies. GLP-1RAs suppress activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, reduce interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) secretion, and promote microglial polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [7]. In PD animal models, NLY01 reduced reactive astrocytosis and microgliosis while preserving dopaminergic neuron survival. GLP-1RAs have additionally been shown to stabilize the blood-brain barrier in a dose-dependent astrocyte-mediated fashion, a mechanism potentially relevant to both neuroinflammation and vascular contributions to motor dysfunction [8].

Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress also represent core features of PD pathophysiology, particularly through pathways involving PINK1, Parkin, and DJ-1. GLP-1 signaling activates the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways, thereby enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, improving neuronal survival, and upregulating antioxidant mechanisms including Nrf2/ARE signaling [9]. In MPTP-lesioned mice and 6-OHDA rat models, GLP-1RAs consistently improved mitochondrial membrane potential and reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [10].

Autophagy–lysosomal dysfunction constitutes another key pathological mechanism in synucleinopathies. Exendin-4 has been shown to reduce pathological α-synuclein aggregation in rat α-synucleinopathy models through enhanced autophagy signaling via PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway modulation [11]. These effects were accompanied by lower Lewy body burden and improved dopaminergic neuron preservation in preclinical PD models [11].

Huntington's disease and related mechanisms

The mechanistic rationale for GLP-1RA therapy in Huntington's disease (HD) centers primarily on mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, impaired autophagy, and mutant huntingtin aggregation. In HD, mutant huntingtin protein disrupts mitochondrial respiratory chain function, particularly through complex II inhibition, resulting in bioenergetic failure and oxidative neuronal injury [12]. GLP-1 signaling appears capable of counteracting several of these abnormalities through activation of PI3K/Akt and AMPK-mediated pathways, enhancement of mitochondrial function, and reduction of oxidative stress [9].

Preclinical studies in HD transgenic mouse models have demonstrated that GLP-1RA treatment improves motor and cognitive performance, reduces oxidative injury, and prolongs survival [10], [12]. In addition, GLP-1 signaling may facilitate autophagic clearance of mutant huntingtin aggregates, thereby targeting one of the core pathological drivers of HD progression [12].

Neurotrophic and dopaminergic effects

Beyond disease-specific mechanisms, GLP-1 signaling also interacts with broader neurotrophic and dopaminergic pathways relevant across multiple movement disorders. GLP-1Rs couple to brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) signaling cascades, both of which are critical for dopaminergic neuron maintenance and synaptic plasticity [13]. Additionally, GLP-1 signaling modulates dopamine transporter (DAT) activity and dopamine release within mesolimbic and nigrostriatal pathways, effects that may influence both motor and non-motor manifestations of movement disorders [13]. Preclinical evidence across movement disorder models, primarily Parkinson's disease

Across rodent PD paradigms, GLP-1RAs have frequently demonstrated improvements in both behavioral outcomes (motor assays) and histological/biochemical correlates (tyrosine hydroxylase preservation, inflammatory markers, oxidative injury indices), motivating clinical translation; a systematic review/meta-analysis focused on rodent PD models concluded that GLP-1RA therapy is generally neuroprotective across multiple paradigms, while also highlighting typical preclinical limitations (model heterogeneity, short follow-up, variable rigor in blinding/randomization) [14]. However, preclinical literature also includes cautionary signals and complexity. For example, an extended-release GLP-1 analogue has been reported to increase α-synuclein accumulation in a mouse model of prodromal PD, underscoring that “class effect” assumptions may fail and that formulation/pharmacokinetics could shift biology in unanticipated directions [15]. However, despite consistent neuroprotective findings across toxin-based models, translation to human disease remains uncertain because many models reproduce acute dopaminergic injury rather than progressive synucleinopathy. Clinical evidence across movement disorder models

Parkinson's Disease: The vanguard of clinical evidence

Seven published trials have evaluated GLP-1 therapies in PD with four agents. The evidence landscape remains heterogeneous, with early positive signals contrasted by more recent negative phase III results. Lixisenatide (once-daily subcutaneous injection) was tested against placebo in patients with early PD (diagnosed within 3 years) over 12 months. The treatment arm demonstrated significantly less progression of motor disability (MDS-UPDRS Part III), with superiority over placebo maintained at the primary endpoint. Gastrointestinal adverse events (nausea, vomiting) were more frequent in the lixisenatide arm but manageable [16]. In 2017, Athauda and colleagues demonstrated a significant positive effect of exenatide once-weekly on motor scores in the off-medication state at 48 weeks, with a possible disease-modifying signal that persisted one-year post-treatment [17]. However, subsequent larger trials have tempered early optimism. The multicenter phase III Exenatide-PD3 trial, which evaluated extended-release exenatide over 96 weeks in 194 participants with Parkinson's disease, failed to demonstrate a significant improvement in the primary motor endpoint (MDS-UPDRS Part III in the OFF state) compared with placebo. These findings suggest that the apparent benefits observed in earlier phase II studies may not generalize to larger populations and highlight the complexity of translating promising preclinical mechanisms into clinically meaningful disease-modifying effects [18]. Real-world cohort studies using target trial emulation methodology have shown associations between semaglutide use and a lower first-time diagnosis of PD in patients with T2DM. Phase II interventional data are emerging and highly anticipated by the field [19]. Trials of NLY01 (pegylated exendin-4) and liraglutide demonstrated promising preclinical results but were inconclusive in phase II — likely attributable to study design limitations including short follow-up, small sample sizes, and heterogeneous patient populations rather than true lack of efficacy [20], [21]. Quantitative evidence demonstrated that GLP-1RAs produce a statistically significant improvement in MDS-UPDRS Part III scores in the on-medication state (−2.52 points; 95% CI −4.02 to −1.01; P = 0.001) and in global MDS-UPDRS total score (−3.43 points; 95% CI −6.48 to −0.48; P = 0.02). Cognitive performance assessed via the Mattis Dementia Rating Scale improved by 1.32 points (95% CI 0.16 to 2.52; P = 0.03). Non-motor symptom scales and quality of life measures did not show statistically significant differences, likely reflecting insufficient statistical power in available trials [22]. It should be noted that this pooled estimate falls below the published minimally clinically important difference for MDS-UPDRS Part III (approximately 3.25 points), underscoring the need for larger, longer trials to determine whether statistically significant improvements translate into clinically meaningful functional benefit. But, taken together, the clinical evidence suggests that GLP-1 receptor agonists remain promising but unproven disease-modifying therapies in Parkinson's disease. Multiple system atrophy

The rationale for GLP-1 RA use in MSA mirrors that for PD: shared alpha-synuclein pathology, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. A landmark phase II randomized controlled trial published in Annals of Neurology in 2025 evaluated exenatide once weekly in MSA patients. Results showed slowing of progression on the Unified MSA Rating Scale (UMSARS) and improvements in biomarkers of neurodegeneration, including neurofilament light chain (NfL). While the trial was not powered for definitive disease-modification claims, the biological plausibility and consistency of findings with PD data represents an encouraging preliminary signal and provides the basis for a phase III program [23]. This represents the first positive interventional signal in MSA and underscores the potential of GLP-1RAs to address shared mechanisms across synucleinopathies — a conceptual shift that may transform how we approach clinical trial design in atypical parkinsonism.

Other atypical parkinsonian syndromes

In the publicly surfaced literature reviewed here, robust randomized trial evidence for GLP-1RAs specifically in PSP/CBS is not established; this remains a clear evidence gap for the “broader movement disorder spectrum” framing, despite mechanistic interest in neuroinflammation and bioenergetic support [24].

Huntington Disease (HD)

These shared mechanisms with PD and MSA provide a compelling rationale for GLP-1 RA investigation in HD. Preclinical studies in transgenic HD mouse models have demonstrated that GLP-1 RA treatment — through activation of AMPK, reduction of mitochondrial oxidative stress, and upregulation of autophagy — results in better motor and cognitive performance on rotarod and open-field testing, as well as significantly prolonged survival compared to vehicle-treated controls. The relevant pathways (GLP-1R → cAMP/PKA → CREB → BDNF; and GLP-1R → PI3K/Akt → mTOR inhibition → autophagy) are mechanistically coherent with the known pathophysiology of HD. Preclinical work has examined GLP-1RAs (particularly exendin-4) in models with metabolic and neurodegenerative phenotypes; such studies are supportive biologically but have not yet translated into a robust, replicated clinical movement-disorder trial signal comparable to PD [25], [26], [27].

Essential tremor (ET)

A 2025 study from UT Southwestern Medical Center examining 452 postmortem brains found that ET patients had approximately 15% lower Purkinje cell density compared to controls and patients with spinocerebellar ataxia, further cementing the cerebellar pathological substrate [28]. GLP-1 receptors are also expressed in the cerebellum, and GLP-1 signaling has neuroprotective effects on cerebellar circuits in preclinical models. However, no clinical trials have evaluated GLP-1RAs specifically for ET, and the pharmacovigilance literature introduces a cautionary note: a FAERS database analysis of over 11,700 movement disorder cases found that GLP-1 analogues were associated with a notably higher adjusted reporting odds ratio (aROR) for tremor (aROR: 1.78; 95% CI 1.65–1.91; P < 0.001) compared to controls. While this pharmacovigilance signal requires careful interpretation — it may reflect unmasking of subclinical ET in predisposed individuals or medication-related metabolic effects rather than direct pharmacotoxicity — it represents an important caution against uncritical extrapolation of GLP-1 RA benefit to all tremor syndromes [29]. The evidence base for GLP-1RAs in ET thus remains purely mechanistic and hypothetical. Dedicated epidemiological studies and a carefully designed phase II trial are warranted before any clinical recommendations can be made.

Cerebellar ataxia and related disorders

A notable exception to the “no clinical translation” rule is Friedreich ataxia: a translational study reported that exenatide (GLP-1RA) could induce frataxin expression and described data spanning cellular/in vivo models and patients with Friedreich ataxia, providing a concrete example of incretin-based biology intersecting with a movement disorder outside PD [30]. Additionally, semaglutide has been studied in a PLA2G6-associated neurodegeneration model (PLAN), reporting improvements in mobility and lifespan—important because PLAN includes dystonia-parkinsonism phenotypes and bridges “parkinsonism–ataxia–dystonia” boundaries at a mechanistic level [31].

Safety considerations in movement disorder populations

GLP-1RAs are generally well tolerated; the most common adverse events across indications are gastrointestinal (nausea 15–44%, vomiting 5–16%, diarrhea 8–20%), typically transient and dose-dependent. In movement disorder populations, however, several specific concerns merit discussion [32]. A clinically crucial issue is that GLP-1RAs differ in molecular size, albumin binding, formulation (short-acting vs extended-release), and CNS exposure patterns, undermining the assumption that one trial “proves or disproves” the full class. Animal data demonstrates that lixisenatide and liraglutide can cross the BBB under certain dosing conditions, with measurable kinetics and brain cAMP effects; lixisenatide appears more consistently detectable across dose/time conditions in that work [33]. For liraglutide, brain-region binding/uptake studies show engagement in hypothalamic circuits and GLP-1R-dependent uptake patterns [36]. For semaglutide, high-impact mechanistic work suggests it does not broadly permeate the BBB and instead acts via circumventricular organs and select ventricular-adjacent regions—important when interpreting why semaglutide may show robust metabolic/central appetite effects yet uncertain PD disease-modification signals [37]. Human CNS exposure data exists for exenatide: in the exenatide PD trial, exenatide at licensed diabetes doses was detectable in CSF, supporting BBB passage (even if at low fractions of serum), and similarly low CSF/serum ratios were described in the MSA exenatide study [24].

Certain side effects could be of note with movement disorders especially PD. These include gastrointestinal adverse events (nausea, vomiting, reflux/constipation) which are prominent and can be consequential in movement disorders where dysautonomia, weight loss, and frailty are common. In LIXIPARK, nausea occurred in 46% and vomiting in 13%, and over one-third required dose reduction due to side effects—features that matter both clinically and methodologically (unblinding risk) [16]. Also, delayed gastric emptying, a known pharmacodynamic effect described in FDA labeling for GLP-1RAs (including exenatide and liraglutide), because PD already features gastric dysmotility that can impair oral levodopa absorption and amplify motor fluctuations. This raises the possibility that GLP-1RAs may exacerbate motor fluctuations in some patients, even if they are neuroprotective in principle—an example of where mechanism and symptomatic management can conflict [38]. Concerns about suicidality associated with GLP-1 RA use have been raised, though a 2024 meta-analysis of RCTs did not demonstrate a statistically significant increase in suicide or suicidal behavior. Prospective psychiatric monitoring is nonetheless warranted, particularly in HD patients who carry inherent psychiatric vulnerability [39].

Translational Challenges in GLP-1 Neurotherapeutics

Despite compelling mechanistic rationale and encouraging signals from early clinical trials, several translational challenges complicate the development of GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) as disease-modifying therapies in movement disorders. First, heterogeneity among GLP-1RA compounds complicates interpretation of clinical trial results. These agents differ substantially in molecular size, albumin binding, pharmacokinetics, and central nervous system exposure. Some compounds such as exenatide and lixisenatide demonstrate measurable cerebrospinal fluid penetration, whereas others—particularly larger molecules such as semaglutide—appear to exert central effects primarily through circumventricular organs and ventricular-adjacent regions rather than widespread blood–brain barrier permeation. These pharmacologic differences challenge the assumption of a uniform class effect and suggest that efficacy observed with one compound cannot be directly extrapolated to others [3], [34], [37]. Second, limitations of preclinical models may contribute to discrepancies between experimental and clinical findings. Many studies demonstrating robust neuroprotection employ toxin-based models such as MPTP or 6-hydroxydopamine, which reproduce acute dopaminergic injury rather than the progressive multisystem neurodegeneration characteristic of human Parkinson's disease and related disorders. While such models provide valuable mechanistic insight, they may overestimate therapeutic efficacy when translated to human disease [14]. Third, clinical trial design challenges complicate interpretation of therapeutic effects. Gastrointestinal adverse events—including nausea and vomiting—are common with GLP-1RAs and may lead to dose reductions or functional unblinding in randomized trials, as seen in the LIXIPARK study. In addition, the effects of GLP-1RAs on gastric emptying may alter levodopa absorption in Parkinson's disease, potentially confounding motor outcomes independent of neuroprotective mechanisms [16], [38]. Fourth, metabolic heterogeneity among patients may influence treatment response. Emerging evidence suggests that insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction may contribute to neurodegeneration in Parkinson's disease. It is therefore plausible that GLP-1RAs may exert greater therapeutic effects in metabolically vulnerable subgroups, highlighting the need for biomarker-guided patient stratification in future trials [12]. Finally, disease heterogeneity across movement disorders further complicates clinical translation. While synucleinopathies such as Parkinson's disease and multiple system atrophy share overlapping inflammatory and mitochondrial mechanisms, other disorders—including Huntington's disease and essential tremor—have distinct molecular substrates that may respond differently to incretin-based therapies. Consequently, extrapolation of therapeutic effects across the broader spectrum of movement disorders must be approached cautiously.

These considerations highlight the importance of carefully designed translational programs to determine whether GLP-1 receptor agonists can achieve meaningful disease modification in movement disorders.

Future directions and proposed research agenda

Although GLP-1RA have demonstrated compelling mechanistic and preclinical neuroprotective properties, current clinical evidence remains insufficient to establish a definitive disease-modifying effect in movement disorders. The negative findings of the Exenatide-PD3 trial and the high frequency of gastrointestinal adverse events observed in several studies underscore the importance of cautious interpretation and rigorous future trial design. Nevertheless, the biological plausibility of GLP-1 signaling and encouraging early-phase clinical findings justify continued investigation in carefully selected patient populations. Based on the current evidence, several research priorities may help clarify the therapeutic role of GLP-1RA in movement disorders.

Phase III trials in PD.

The positive signal observed in the LIXIPARK trial supports consideration of a well-powered, multicenter phase III study designed to evaluate whether GLP-1RA can meaningfully influence disease progression in Parkinson's disease [16], [40]. Trial design must address levodopa pharmacokinetics, stratification by diabetes status, and biomarker co-primary endpoints (NfL, DAT-SPECT). Future studies should also carefully address gastrointestinal tolerability, dose optimization, and patient frailty, particularly given the high rates of nausea, vomiting, and dose reduction observed in prior GLP-1RA trials.

MSA-specific program

The encouraging findings from the 2025 exenatide MSA study support further investigation in larger controlled trials, particularly given the absence of approved disease-modifying therapies for MSA and the potential regulatory advantages associated with orphan diseases [23].

HD trials

Given the mechanistic convergence and availability of validated biomarkers, an investigator-initiated phase II trial of semaglutide in prodromal or early HD represents a tractable and high-impact research opportunity.

CNS penetration optimization

Next-generation agents including NLY01 (CNS-targeted), tirzepatide (dual GLP-1/GIP agonist), and intranasally delivered GLP-1 formulations are under development specifically to maximize CNS exposure. Their evaluation in movement disorders is a priority [41].

Biomarker development

Validated biomarkers of target engagement (e.g., brain GLP-1R PET tracers) are needed to confirm that therapeutic brain concentrations are achieved and to stratify trial populations by metabolic phenotype and GLP-1R expression [41].

Real-world pharmacoepidemiology

Large national registries (e.g., Danish healthcare registries, UK Biobank, US TriNetX) can provide prospective data on GLP-1 RA exposure and movement disorder outcomes in diabetic and non-diabetic populations, informing trial design and regulatory strategy.

Importantly, future investigations should prioritize not only efficacy, but also confirmation of central target engagement, long-term tolerability, and identification of patient subgroups most likely to benefit from GLP-1-based therapies.

Conclusion

GLP-1 receptor agonists are among the most promising candidate classes currently under investigation in movement disorders since the discovery of alpha-synuclein pathology. Their appeal rests on a coherent and multi-targeted mechanistic rationale — encompassing anti-neuroinflammatory, antioxidant, proteostatic, and trophic effects — that are relevant across the full nosological spectrum of movement disorders rather than being confined to a single pathological entity.

Clinical evidence is most mature for Parkinson's disease, where phase II data collectively support a meaningful, potentially disease-modifying motor benefit, with the LIXIPARK trial standing as the clearest current demonstration of clinical efficacy. Emerging data in MSA are paradigm-shifting, offering the first credible disease-modification signal in this devastating condition. In HD, and essential tremor, the evidence remains preclinical or epidemiological, but the mechanistic groundwork is sufficiently robust to justify dedicated clinical investigation.

Movement disorder specialists should play a central role in shaping this emerging therapeutic frontier: engaging in trial design, raising awareness of nuanced safety signals specific to their patient populations, and ensuring that the enormous translational opportunity offered by GLP-1RAs is not squandered by methodologically inadequate studies.
