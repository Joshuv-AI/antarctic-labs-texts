A cross-sectional study of household disposal of unused and expired pharmaceuticals in western China
Authors: Weiting Liu, Wei Wei, Zhiye Zhang, Hui Du, Shengying Che, Rui Ding, YanBiao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe disposal of expired household pharmaceuticals constitutes a significant public health challenge in numerous countries, including China, posing toxicological risks to both human health and the environment particularly through contamination of water resources and soil.ObjectiveThis paper examined the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of residents in Lanzhou, China regarding household expired pharmaceuticals, providing scientific evidence to facilitate the development of sustainable pharmaceutical take-back systems.MethodsAn observational cross-sectional study was conducted using a structured questionnaire. Participants were recruited through an online survey link distributed via social media platforms, and statistical analyses were performed using both univariate and multivariate methods. The survey period spanned from January to December 2023.ResultsTotal of 1180 valid questionnaires were returned with a response rate of 90.9% in which 34.5% men and 65.5% women participated. Among the respondents, 76.4% resided in urban areas and 23.6% lived in rural areas. 970 respondents (82.2%) had the habit of storing drugs and 950 respondents (80.5%) store drugs in designated locations, 20.4% had the habit of drug expiration due to blind purchase. 562 respondents (47.6%) did not know the relevant policies or recycling activities of expired drugs. Upon the expiration of medications, 8.7% of respondents reported disposing of them in domestic waste bins without separation. In contrast, 22.8% of participants indicated that they segregated and recycled such pharmaceutical waste. A majority of respondents (44.3%) returned expired drugs to pharmacies or hospitals for appropriate disposal, while 24.2% engaged in financial incentive-based take-back schemes organized by social organizations. The demographic characteristics such as age, place of residence, education level, occupation and income of the survey subjects were significantly associated with the storage habits and disposal methods of drugs, as well as the attitude towards unused and expired drugs.ConclusionA considerable proportion of residents tend to store medicines at home but may not prioritize regular disposal, which likely contributes to a high rate of medication expiration. Residents have a certain understanding of the harm of expired drugs, and the recycling points of unused and expired drugs are few, and the recycling subject is not clear, which makes the recycling subject less active.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27866-4.

Introduction

With the rapid development of the global economy and healthcare industry, the usage of medications is continuously increasing [1, 2]. However, patients may not complete the full course of medication due to a variety of factors, such as resolution of symptoms, adjustments in treatment plans, lack of therapeutic efficacy, adverse effects, or drug expiration. Consequently, a considerable proportion of dispensed medications ultimately expire without being used [3–7]. Household stockpiling of medicines, often driven by emergency preparedness, has further contributed to the accumulation of expired pharmaceutical waste [8]. Expired drugs not only have safety risks, but also may produce toxic and harmful substances that harm the health of patients [9, 10]. For instance, degraded tetracycline products such as epianhydrotetracycline are nephrotoxic and can cause renal tubular dysfunction [11].

According to statistics, China produces more than 15,000 tons of expired medicines every year, with the majority of households discarding them as general waste without recycling [12]. The indiscriminate disposal of expired pharmaceuticals, whether in household trash or via drainage systems, can contaminate air, soil, and water, disrupt ecological chains, [13, 14] and contribute to antimicrobial resistance through the release of antibiotics into aquatic environments [15]. In addition, unscrupulous traders may acquire expired medicines at low prices, repackage them, and reintroduce them to the market, thereby endangering public health and causing financial losses to consumers [16, 17]. Therefore, the disposal of expired medicines at home is a pressing public health and environmental issue requiring urgent attention. While several countries have established formal systems for the collection and disposal of expired medications, [18–21] medication management systems remain relatively underdeveloped in many developing countries [22–24]. In 2018, China's Ministry of Ecology and Environment issued the Citizen Ecological Environment Behavior Norms, encouraging proper disposal of discarded medications and promoting access to collection facilities. However, these initiatives remain limited in scope and have not yet developed into a comprehensive national program. A nationwide cross-sectional study conducted by Yang Z et al. highlighted the urgent need for standardized management of unused medications in China [25]. Recent policy changes, including the “dual-channel” drug management reform—which allows patients to purchase prescription drugs at designated pharmacies with insurance coverage—and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, have further influenced public practices regarding medication disposal.

Against this backdrop, the present study examines the issue of household expired drug recycling, conducting an investigation and analysis of the current state of drug recycling in the nine districts (and counties) of Lanzhou City, located in western China, from January to December 2023. This study investigate the storage and disposal of unused household medications, as well as public practices and attitudes toward the recycling and disposal of expired medications, thereby providing a scientific basis for promoting the development of a sustainable pharmaceutical recycling system.

Methods

This descriptive cross-sectional study was carried out in Lanzhou City, the provincial capital of Gansu Province located in western China, which is also recognized as the geographical center of the nation's landmass. For administrative purposes, the city comprises nine districts, which are delineated according to their level of economic development. A cross-sectional survey was conducted from January to December 2023 using non-probability convenience sampling method to select participants, aiming to understand the current status of medication waste disposal among residents in Lanzhou City. The total permanent resident population of Lanzhou in 2023 was 4.4251 million. The study was conducted using a structured questionnaire administered online. Participants were recruited through an online survey link distributed via the WeChat social media platform. Upon accessing the link, prospective participants were directed to a landing page containing detailed study information and an electronic informed consent form. After providing consent, participants completed the questionnaire independently online. Trained investigators were available to provide technical assistance or clarify question intent if needed, but they were instructed not to interpret questions or suggest answers. This support was strictly limited to: (1) explaining ambiguous technical or medical terms in lay language (e.g., defining ‘pharmaceutical waste’ or ‘take-back scheme’), (2) clarifying the intent of a question if a participant expressed confusion about its meaning, and (3) assisting with platform navigation if needed. Investigators were expressly instructed not to interpret questions, suggest answers, or observe the participant’s selections. This procedure was designed to assist elderly participants or those less familiar with online surveys without compromising the self-administered and anonymous nature of the questionnaire.

Questionnaire design

A comprehensive review of the literature was conducted to develop an initial Chinese version of the questionnaire [26–28]. The questionnaire comprises 20 items. The first section collects participants’ demographic information, including gender, age, residence area, educational level, and occupation. The second section consists of 13 items covering three dimensions: the knowledge dimension (4 items assessing factual awareness, e.g., “Do you know how to store medicines at home?”), the attitude dimension (4 items, e.g., “Where do you expect the expired drug take-back station to be?”), and the behavior dimension (5 items evaluating self-reported behaviors, e.g., “How do you dispose of expired medicines?”). The complete questionnaire is provided in the Supplementary Materials. The internal consistency (α = 0.832) was established through Cronbach’s alpha test [29]. To ensure the face and content validity of the questionnaire, it was evaluated by experts and pre-tested with 30 respondents [30]. Feedback was collected regarding the clarity of wording, comprehensibility, relevance, response format, and logical sequence of the questionnaire items. Based on this feedback, several revisions were made, including simplifying ambiguous terminology in knowledge items, rewording the lead-in statement for the attitudes scale to reduce social desirability bias, and adjusting the order of practice questions to improve logical flow. The final version incorporated all these refinements. Data collected during the pilot phase were not included in the subsequent analysis. The key independent variables in this study included residential location and co-residence duration. Urban/rural status was classified based on the official 2018 Chinese statistical standard. Urban areas were defined as municipal districts, sub-districts of county-level cities, and adjacent zones with interconnected built-up infrastructure (e.g., resident committees). Rural areas referred to townships, towns, and their administered villager committees. Furthermore, the descriptive term “lived together for a long time” was operationalized as a co-residence period of five years or longer for the purpose of this analysis. The term “medications” in this survey refers to both prescription and over-the-counter (OTC) medications.

Data collection and analysis

The Data collectors received comprehensive training, during which they were instructed to distribute the questionnaire link to potential respondents and specifically directed to explain the nature and purpose of the study to participants prior to questionnaire completion. When contacting a household, data collectors used the “last birthday” method to select the survey respondent from all eligible adult members by choosing the individual who had most recently celebrated a birthday. A non-probability convenience sampling method was employed, with recruitment conducted by disseminating an online survey link via social media platforms. Before accessing the questionnaire, all participants were required to review an electronic informed consent form on the landing page and indicate their agreement by clicking “I agree to participate” before proceeding; only those who provided consent were able to continue to the survey questions. Following a rigorous screening process, 1,180 out of 1,298 users who accessed the recruitment landing page completed the survey in its entirety, yielding a response rate of 90.9%. Data analysis was conducted using both univariate and multivariate analyses. Descriptive statistics were first computed to summarize the data, with categorical variables presented as numbers and percentages. Subsequently, univariate analysis utilizing the Chi-square test was performed to identify factors associated with knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding the disposal of household expired medications. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to identify factors independently associated with various knowledge, attitude, and practice outcomes related to medication disposal (e.g., storing medicines at home, reading instructions carefully, disposal methods). All categorical independent variables were effect-coded, with their reference categories explicitly stated in the footnotes of the respective results tables. Key assumptions of logistic regression were assessed prior to interpreting the final models. Multicollinearity among predictors was examined using Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs); all VIF values were below 5, indicating no severe multicollinearity. The potential influence of outliers was evaluated using leverage values and Cook’s distance, with no observations found to exert undue influence on the model estimates. To assess the assumptions of the logistic regression model, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test was performed to evaluate model fit. The results indicated no significant lack of fit (χ2 (8) = 14.48, *p* = 0.07), suggesting that the model was well-calibrated. The results are expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0), and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association between storage and disposal of unused medicines and demographics

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the relationships between demographic characteristics and medication storage and disposal practices (Tables 4–10).

Factors associated with medication storage

Older age (> 50 years) and higher monthly income (> 5,000 yuan) were significantly associated with increased likelihood of storing medications at home (OR = 1.908, 95% CI: 1.033 to 5.180, p < 0.05; and OR = 6.016, 95% CI: 3.765 to 12.339, p < 0.05, respectively). No significant demographic predictors were identified for medication purchasing tendencies.

Factors associated with medication instruction reading

Higher education level (university degree or above), professional/technical occupation, government employment, and having a healthcare professional in the household were significantly associated with careful reading of medication instructions. Detailed odds ratios and confidence intervals for these associations are presented in Table 6.

Factors associated with medication storage knowledge and practices

Professional/technical occupation and having a healthcare professional in the household were significant predictors of better awareness of proper medication storage conditions (OR = 5.084, 95% CI: 1.950 to 13.255, p < 0.05; and OR = 3.492, 95% CI: 1.913 to 6.376, p < 0.05, respectively). Having a healthcare professional in the household was also significantly associated with regular disposal of expired medications (OR = 2.705, 95% CI: 1.057 to 6.925, p < 0.05), proper medication storage practices (OR = 3.354, 95% CI: 1.443 to 7.793, p < 0.01), and avoiding medication use when the expiration date was unknown (OR = 2.151, 95% CI: 1.274 to 6.719, p < 0.05). Complete results for these analyses are shown in Tables 7–10.

Association between practices and attitudes toward expired medicines and demographics

The relationships between practices and attitudes regarding expired medications and demographic characteristics are presented in Tables 11 to 16. A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed, incorporating gender, age, educational level, region, occupation, income, and family factors (specifically, the presence of a healthcare professional in the household). The results indicated that regarding the level of awareness about the hazards of expired medications, a university education (OR = 1.942, 95% CI: 1.028 to 4.320, P < 0.05), professional technical occupation (OR = 8.722, 95% CI: 5.875 to 39.665, P < 0.05), and having a healthcare professional in the household (OR = 2.138, 95% CI: 1.400 to 3.267, P < 0.01) were statistically significant. In the comparison between being “fully aware” and “completely unaware”, professional/technical occupation (OR = 3.502, 95% CI: 1.732 to 7.079, P < 0.05) and having a healthcare professional in the household (OR = 1.533, 95% CI: 1.062 to 2.213, P < 0.01) demonstrated significant. The analysis of disposal methods for expired medications, using indiscriminate disposal in the trash as the reference, revealed several significant preferences. When comparing disposal in segregated recycling bins to indiscriminate trash disposal, a university-level education (OR = 2.041, 95% CI: 1.441 to 5.334, P < 0.05), and having a healthcare professional in the household (OR = 2.991, 95% CI: 1.633 to 5.539, P < 0.001) were significant predictors of choosing recycling. In contrast, the preference for targeted free take-back programs over indiscriminate disposal was significantly associated with urban residence (OR = 1.989, 95% CI: 1.014 to 3.632, P < 0.05) and having a healthcare professional in the household (OR = 1.711, 95% CI: 1.004 to 3.175, P < 0.05). Regarding the choice of targeted paid recall programs, co-residence with a healthcare professional was the sole significant factor (OR = 2.463, 95% CI: 1.112 to 6.392, P < 0.05). Urban residence (OR = 2.342, 95% CI: 1.081 to 5.071, P < 0.01), and student status (OR = 2.605, 95% CI: 2.151 to 3.155, P < 0.05) were significantly associated with a perceived need for standardized recycling programs for expired household medications. However, no significant association was observed between the factors analyzed and the patterns of household medication use (p > 0.05). Significant factors associated with the level of awareness of government regulations on expired medication recall included male gender (OR = 1.969, 95% CI: 1.247 to 3.109, P < 0.05), technical occupation (OR = 3.649, 95% CI: 1.206 to 7.916, P < 0.05), government employment (OR = 1.951, 95% CI: 1.005 to 4.218, P < 0.01), and having a healthcare professional in the household (OR = 2.107, 95% CI: 1.297 to 3.424, P < 0.05). In a separate model assessing overall awareness (knowledge of the regulations), significant predictors were age 21–30 years (OR = 2.372, 95% CI: 1.115 to 3.046, P < 0.05), government employment (OR = 1.936, 95% CI: 1.112 to 3.979, P < 0.001), and having a healthcare professional in the household (OR = 1.746, 95% CI: 1.293 to 2.356, P < 0.05). Urban residence was identified as a statistically significant predictor influencing respondents' preference for designated medication drop-off points.

Discussion

Currently unused and expired medicines management and disposal is a topical issue of great concern because it has been realized that improper disposal can contaminate the environment and pose the risk to water, air, agricultural products, and food chain and even harm animals. While household stockpiling of medicines provides convenience to the public in self-medication, it also gives rise to a series of problems such as medication safety and wastage of medicines. Therefore, development and implementation of a well-run recovery and disposal system for the proper collection and disposal of unused and expired medications is of great significance to the public safety of residents and the protection of the natural environment [24, 31].

In China, the pharmaceutical environment is shaped by policies that influence how medications are accessed and accumulated in households. Prescription drugs require a formal prescription from a licensed practitioner, while non-prescription drugs bear an “OTC” label and are freely available. Recent healthcare financing reforms, particularly the “dual-channel” policy, allow patients to purchase prescription drugs covered by insurance at designated retail pharmacies, increasing medication accessibility. Additionally, integrated e-prescription systems enable seamless prescription transfers from hospitals to pharmacies and support home delivery services. These policies, while improving patient access and convenience, may inadvertently contribute to medication overstocking and subsequent expiration. Greater accessibility and convenience can lead to patients acquiring larger quantities of medications than needed, thereby potentially contributing to the accumulation of unused and expired pharmaceuticals in households.

This study revealed several important patterns in how residents of Lanzhou manage household medications. A considerable proportion of respondents reported stockpiling medicines at home and purchasing more than needed, while a substantial minority did not read medication instructions carefully. Encouragingly, most respondents demonstrated awareness of proper storage practices, such as keeping medicines in designated locations and avoiding use when the expiry date was unknown. Furthermore, over 90% of participants reported engaging in some form of conscious separation of expired medications from general household waste, either by returning them to pharmacies or hospitals or by participating in take-back programs. However, awareness of the environmental and health hazards associated with improper disposal was relatively low compared to findings from other regions, and nearly half of the respondents were unaware of relevant government policies. Despite this lack of awareness, an overwhelming majority expressed support for government-led standardized collection programs, with many favoring multiple collection points including communities, pharmacies, and hospitals. Multivariable analysis identified key demographic factors associated with medication storage and disposal behaviors. Notably, the presence of a healthcare professional in the household emerged as the most consistent predictor of positive practices, including careful reading of instructions, regular disposal of expired medications, and appropriate storage. Higher socioeconomic status—reflected in education level, income, and occupation—was also associated with better knowledge and practices, whereas older age and higher income were linked to increased medication stockpiling. These findings underscore the importance of targeted educational interventions and the potential role of healthcare professionals in promoting safe medication management within families.

In the current study, we found that the majority of residents had unused and expired medications stored in their homes. Similar findings were reported in studies conducted in Ireland, New Zealand and Nigeria [32–34] and another similar study conducted by Zhao et al. in 2022 in Lanzhou City reported that majority (93.2%) of the respondents had unused and expired medicines stored in their homes. Unfortunately, the survey showed that 75.1% of the respondents were aware of the environmental and health hazards of unused and improperly disposed of expired medicines, but this figure is lower than the results in Xi'an (93.3%) and Zhengzhou (91.1%), as well as in Afghanistan (98.0%) and Ethiopia (95.8%) [24, 35]. It has been reported that more than 85.0% of the respondents dispose of discarded household medicines with their household waste, a result that is very similar to studies conducted in Saudi Arabia (87.0%) [22] and Serbia (82.8%) [36], and it is important to point out that the disposal of expired medicines, which constitutes such a large proportion of the population of each country, poses a serious threat to the individual, to society and to the ecosystem. An essential and the significant debatable issue regarding who is responsible for collecting and recycling of expired drugs. A study conducted in Guangzhou, China, showed that about 60% of the respondents believed that the government should be responsible [27]. Our survey results show that nearly half (47.6%) of the public are unaware of the national policy on expired drug management, yet 86.5% of the residents hope that the government will formulate and implement a comprehensive drug take-back program and policy. Similarly, based on their 2018 survey findings, Yang Z et al. underscored the urgent need for China to formulate standardized drug recycling management policies to ensure the proper handling of unnecessary medications [25]. It is well known that Francisco's Safe Medicine Disposal program in California, [31] the Medications Return Program or Take-back programs in Canada [37] provide implementation plans for the recall of expired medicines. However, so far, this facility is not well-practiced in China.

In discussing these associations, we found that factors such as age, area of residence, and occupation of the surveyed population were significantly associated with home drug storage, disposal of unused and expired drugs. Several factors may contribute to these associations. For instance, younger urban residents with stable occupations may have greater access to information and resources, which could potentially lead to a more comprehensive understanding of household medication management. However, these motivations were not directly measured in our study. Particularly discussed are the effects of educational attainment and population income, although the explanations are also relatively straightforward. Public education plays a key role in improving drug medication, rationalizing the use of health resources, and understanding the need for pharmacy services, [38] as educated people tend to know more about their disease conditions, the medications they are using, and ways to store or dispose of unused or expired medications. Previous studies have concluded that more educated people are healthier and live longer compared to less educated people [39]. It is interesting to note that the income of the population was also significantly associated with the reasons for storing medicines and disposing of expired medicines. According to OECD Health Statistics 2021, China's healthcare expenditure accounts for only 5% of GDP, so patients have to pay a lot of their own expenses for diagnosis and treatment of diseases. A possible reason for the link between lower income and medication stockpiling may be financial pressure. For instance, people with limited funds might keep medications for future use to save on costs. It is also possible that economic difficulty could affect decisions about reusing expired medications, though our study did not directly ask about this behavior or its reasons.

Globally, the management of medicines has been a highly controversial topic as the frequency of diseases has increased, healthcare systems have evolved, and the use of medicines has greatly increased globally. The management of medicines (stored, unused and expired) is also of concern to health system professionals and social scientists, among others. According to the results of the current study, although the potential differences in managing medicines are closely related to socio-demographic characteristics and public healthcare-seeking behaviors, at the same time, this study also found that the lack of policies and programs related to medicines management, the weak publicity of medicines recycling and the awareness of recycling, the small number of recycling sites and the high cost of recycling have led to the lack of motivation and participation of residents in the recycling of medicines, while the important reason for the above discussed results is the lack of information related to the rational disposal of medicines by the public. The lack of reasonable awareness is associated with bias in the practice of disposing of medicines by residents. There is therefore an urgent need to raise public awareness on the proper disposal of unused and expired medicines. In order to solve these problems, it is necessary for the Government to take the lead and for medical institutions, pharmaceutical companies and the health-care industry to cooperate fully in order to design and formulate a reasonable policy on the recovery of medicines, to step up publicity on drug management, to raise public awareness of the disposal of medicines and to set up appropriate collection points for expired medicines in the community, hospitals and so on.

This study provides the information necessary for next steps in planning and health education improvements in medication management. However, the study still has some limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional design precludes the determination of causality. The associations observed between demographic factors and medication disposal practices cannot be interpreted as causal relationships. Longitudinal or interventional studies are needed to establish causality and understand the directions of these associations. Secondly, the study potentially involves selection bias, as participants were primarily recruited through online survey links distributed via social media platforms, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations (e.g., remote rural areas or groups affected by the digital divide). Thirdly, response bias may have been introduced during the research process; for instance, lower response rates to certain sensitive questions in the survey could lead to biased estimates of the results. Fourthly, the survey questionnaire did not include open-ended response options, thereby limiting the exploration of participants' more comprehensive feedback through free-text comments. Fifthly, due to limited precision, indicated by wide confidence intervals, the strength of these specific associations requires cautious interpretation. Furthermore, it is necessary to investigate the reasons for the presence of substantial quantities of unused or expired medications in respondents' households, such as poor medication adherence, excessive medication purchases, and alterations in treatment regimens. These factors warrant further comprehensive investigation. Finally, this study was conducted in a region of northwestern China, so its generalizations about China are limited.

Conclusions

The study showed that residents of Lanzhou City have relatively low awareness of the disposal of unused and expired medicines at home. Most of the respondents had unused and expired medicines in their households, and because they did not know the collection/recycling points for the medicines, the most common method of disposal was to throw them in the garbage bins as household garbage. On the other hand, the majority of the respondents believed that unused and expired medicines posed a serious threat to human health and the ecological environment, and that there was an urgent need for the Government to put in place a strong, safe and cost-effective management program for discarded medicines, as well as a comprehensive mass media campaign to improve the knowledge, attitudes and practices of the population regarding the disposal of expired medicines.
