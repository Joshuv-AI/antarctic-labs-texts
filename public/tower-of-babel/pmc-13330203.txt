Median effective dose (ED50) of ciprofol combined with alfentanil for suppressing cervical dilation responses during hysteroscopy: a prospective Dixon up-and-down study
Authors: Conghui Zhang, Yunyan Zhu, Linghua Zeng, Junjie Zhu, Jing Chen, Ting Zhang, YuanMing Tao, Shihong Li, Maolin Zhong, Fuzhou Hua, Weidong Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical dilation during hysteroscopy induces significant nociceptive stimulation that may provoke somatic response and compromise procedural conditions. The optimal induction dose of ciprofol combined with alfentanil for suppressing cervical dilation responses remains unclear. This study aimed to determine the median effective dose (ED50) of ciprofol using the Dixon up-and-down method and to estimate ED95 using logistic regression.MethodsIn this prospective dose-finding study, adult patients undergoing elective hysteroscopy received alfentanil 7.4 μg/kg followed by ciprofol at sequentially adjusted doses (initial dose 0.40 mg/kg; step size 0.025 mg/kg). A positive response (failure) was defined as the occurrence of any somatic movement (body movement, perioral twitching, frowning, or restlessness) during cervical dilation. A negative response (success) was defined as complete absence of movement. Enrollment continued until seven crossover pairs were obtained. ED50 was calculated using the Dixon–Mood method. ED95 and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using logistic regression.ResultsThirty-two patients were included. The ED50 determined by the Dixon–Mood method was 0.397 mg/kg. Model-based regression analysis yielded an ED50 of 0.397 mg/kg (95% CI: 0.366–0.428 mg/kg) and an ED95 of 0.521 mg/kg (95% CI: 0.389–0.652 mg/kg). Two patients (6.3%) required transient jaw-thrust intervention; no assisted ventilation or serious adverse events occurred.ConclusionWhen combined with alfentanil 7.4 µg/kg, ciprofol provided dose-dependent suppression of cervical dilation responses during hysteroscopy, with an ED50 of 0.397 mg/kg and a model-based ED95 of 0.521 mg/kg.Keywords: Ciprofol, Alfentanil, Hysteroscopy, Procedural sedation, Dixon up-and-down method, Dose-finding study

Introduction

Hysteroscopy is a widely performed minimally invasive gynecological procedure that enables direct visualization and treatment of intrauterine lesions through the natural vaginal orifice [1]. Owing to its minimal invasiveness and diagnostic precision, hysteroscopy has gradually replaced certain traditional surgical approaches and has become one of the core techniques for the diagnosis and management of intrauterine diseases [2]. With the increasing adoption of ambulatory surgical care, the demand for safe and effective anesthetic management during outpatient hysteroscopy continues to grow.

In current practice, intravenous anesthesia with preservation of spontaneous ventilation is commonly used for ambulatory hysteroscopy. However, evidence suggests that anesthesia administered in non-operating room settings may be associated with a higher incidence of adverse events compared with procedures performed in traditional operating room [3, 4]. In these settings, careful titration of anesthetic agents is essential to maintain adequate sedation and analgesia while minimizing respiratory and hemodynamic compromise.

Propofol in combination with short-acting opioids remains the most frequently employed regimen for procedural sedation [5]. Although propofol provides rapid onset and prompt recovery, its use is associated with dose-dependent respiratory depression and cardiovascular instability. Opioids, while reducing the required dose of propofol, may further increase the risk of respiratory suppression and hemodynamic fluctuations [6]. During hysteroscopy, cervical dilation represents the most intense nociceptive stimulus of the procedure and is often responsible for patient movement and sympathetic activation when anesthetic depth is inadequate, potentially compromising procedural conditions and patient safety.

Ciprofol is a novel intravenous anesthetic that enhances γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABA_A) receptor activity. Compared with propofol, ciprofol has demonstrated rapid onset, reduced injection pain, and relatively stable respiratory and hemodynamic profiles in previous studies [7]. Although its dosing has been investigated in general anesthesia induction and other short procedures, the optimal dose required to suppress cervical dilation–induced responses during hysteroscopy when combined with alfentanil has not been clearly defined.

The Dixon up-and-down sequential method is a well-established approach for estimating the median effective dose (ED50) in dose-finding studies with limited sample sizes [8]. Accordingly, the present study applied the Dixon method to determine the ED50 of ciprofol combined with alfentanil for suppressing cervical dilation responses during outpatient hysteroscopy. logistic regression analysis was subsequently performed to estimate ED95, thereby providing additional quantitative guidance for clinical dosing.

Methods

Study design, setting and ethics

This was a prospective, single-blind, single-center, dose-finding study designed to investigate the effective doses of ciprofol combined with alfentanil for suppressing cervical dilation response during hysteroscopy. The study employed the Dixon up-and-down sequential allocation method to estimate the median effective dose (ED50) [8]. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University in 2024 (Approval No.: LLSC-2024 No. 358). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Participants

Eligible participants were female patients aged 18–60 years with a body mass index (BMI) < 28 kg/m2, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I-II, and Mallampati classification I-II, who were scheduled to undergo hysteroscopy. Exclusion criteria included known allergy to ciprofol or alfentanil, significant cardiovascular, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal disease, obstructive sleep apnea, cognitive impairment, and refusal to participate.

Anesthesia protocol

All procedures were performed in an outpatient hysteroscopy unit under standard monitoring, including ECG, non-invasive blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, pulse oximetry, and bispectral index (BIS).

After establishment of intravenous access, supplemental oxygen (2 L/min via face mask) was administered. Alfentanil 7.4 μg/kg was injected intravenously over 10 s, followed by ciprofol administered over 30 s at the allocated dose. The initial ciprofol dose was 0.40 mg/kg, and the step size was 0.025 mg/kg. Sedation depth was assessed every 30 s using the Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) scale. Cervical dilation was initiated once MOAA/S ≤ 3 was achieved.

If a failure response occurred, a rescue bolus of ciprofol (0.05 mg/kg) was administered to ensure procedural completion; this rescue dose did not influence subsequent dose allocation. Maintenance infusion (1 mg/kg/h) was initiated after completion of cervical dilation and did not affect dose determination. All procedures were performed by the same experienced gynecologist to minimize procedural variability.

Definition of response and primary endpoint

The primary endpoint of this study was the median effective dose (ED50) of ciprofol for suppressing cervical dilation responses. Binary responses during the first cervical dilation attempt were used for sequential dose allocation. Patient responsiveness was assessed by a single experienced anesthesiologist using predefined criteria. The assessor was blinded to the administered dose of ciprofol. Failure (movement response) was defined as any observable purposeful or reflex somatic movement, including body movement, perioral twitching, frowning, or restlessness, occurring during cervical dilation; Success (no movement) was defined as complete absence of movement. To ensure consistency of stimulus intensity, only the response to the first cervical dilation attempt was considered for dose allocation. Bispectral index (BIS) values were recorded for physiological monitoring but were not included in the binary response definition to avoid measurement bias.

Sequential allocation and stopping rule

Prior to the formal study, a small number of preliminary pilot observations were conducted to explore appropriate starting dose and step size for the Dixon sequential design. An initial step size of 0.05 mg·kg⁻1 was tested but resulted in frequent dose reversals between consecutive patients, suggesting insufficient stability for ED50 estimation. Therefore, a smaller step size of 0.025 mg·kg⁻1 was selected to improve dose resolution and stabilize the up-and-down sequence. These pilot observations were exploratory and not part of a formally powered pilot trial.

The initial ciprofol dose was 0.40 mg/kg, which was selected based on the recommended induction dose in the drug prescribing information and and supported by clinical practice and guideline recommendations. A fixed step size of 0.025 mg/kg was used. This increment was determined based on preliminary testing, in which a larger step size (0.05 mg/kg) resulted in excessive oscillation in the sequential allocation, with frequent alternation between success and failure responses, leading to an unstable dose–response pattern. Therefore, a smaller step size was adopted to improve the precision and stability of ED50 estimation while maintaining clinical feasibility.

Dose allocation followed the standard Dixon up-and-down rule: after a failure response, the subsequent dose was increased by 0.025 mg/kg; after a success response, the subsequent dose was decreased by 0.025 mg/kg. A crossover was defined as a change in response between two consecutive patients (success to failure or failure to success). Crossovers were counted from the first observed reversal. In accordance with established recommendations for Dixon studies [8, 9], enrollment continued until seven independent crossover pairs (i.e., seven failure trough–success peak pairs) were obtained, which typically requires approximately 20—40 participants.

Safety assessment and peri-procedural monitoring

Hemodynamic and respiratory parameters, including heart rate (HR), mean arterial pressure (MAP), respiratory rate (RR), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), and bispectral index (BIS), were recorded at two predefined time points: T0, defined as baseline after hemodynamic stabilization upon arrival in the procedure room, and T1, defined as the time of cervical dilation.

Adverse events were recorded descriptively and included hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 80 mmHg or a decrease > 20% from baseline), bradycardia (heart rate < 50 beats per minute), oxygen desaturation (SpO2 < 90%), requirement for airway intervention, postoperative nausea or vomiting, and delayed emergence. These safety and physiological variables were not used for sequential dose allocation and were analyzed descriptively.

Statistical analysis

Formal sample size calculation was not performed, as the Dixon up-and-down method is a sequential design primarily intended for ED50 estimation. Based on previous methodological recommendations, approximately 20—40 patients and at least six to eight independent crossover pairs are considered sufficient to obtain stable estimates [8, 9]. The primary endpoint was the median effective dose (ED50) of ciprofol for suppressing cervical dilation responses. ED50 was calculated using the Dixon–Mood method as the mean of the midpoints of the seven independent crossover pairs. To estimate ED95, logistic regression analysis with a logit link function was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0, GraphPad Software, USA). Binary success/failure responses were summarized by dose level and analyzed using weighted logistic regression. ED50 and ED95 values with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were derived from the fitted model. Because the up-and-down design concentrates observations around the ED50 region, estimation of ED95 was considered model based.

Continuous variables were summarized descriptively as mean ± standard deviation. Because group assignment resulted from the sequential allocation design rather than randomization, no formal hypothesis testing was performed between success and failure observations. For exploratory analysis of physiological changes, paired comparisons between T0 and T1 were performed using paired t-tests for normally distributed variables. Sequential allocation plots and dose–response curves were generated using GraphPad Prism.

Discussion

In this study, the Dixon up-and-down sequential method was applied to determine the median effective dose (ED50) of ciprofol required to suppress cervical dilation–induced movement during hysteroscopy under a fixed background dose of alfentanil. The ED50 was 0.397 mg·kg⁻1. Logistic regression further estimated the ED95 to be 0.521 mg·kg⁻1. These findings provide quantitative data to inform dose selection of ciprofol in ambulatory hysteroscopic procedures, particularly for ED50 estimation, while the model-derived ED95 should be interpreted with caution. The applicability of these findings should be interpreted in the context of the selected study population.

Cervical dilation during hysteroscopy represents the most intense nociceptive stimulus of the procedure and may provoke somatic movement and stress responses if anesthetic depth is inadequate. Clinically, intravenous anesthesia with preservation of spontaneous ventilation is commonly adopted for short gynecologic procedures and typically consists of a sedative–opioid combination [10]. This strategy aims to achieve sufficient hypnosis and analgesia while minimizing respiratory and hemodynamic compromise, thereby facilitating rapid recovery in ambulatory settings.

Propofol remains the most widely used intravenous sedative but is associated with dose-dependent respiratory and circulatory depression, as well as injection pain [11]. Ciprofol, a novel intravenous anesthetic developed in China, enhances γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABA-A) receptor activity and has demonstrated rapid onset, reduced injection pain, and relatively stable hemodynamics [12]. Previous studies have shown that its anesthetic efficacy is non-inferior to propofol [13]. In a study by Zhang et al. [14], ciprofol provided comparable anesthetic effectiveness with improved hemodynamic stability and a lower incidence of injection pain during day surgery.

For opioid selection, alfentanil is frequently used in hysteroscopy because of its rapid onset and short elimination half-life compared with fentanyl and sufentanil [15].

The fixed alfentanil dose (7.4 μg·kg⁻1) was selected based on previously reported ED50 values of alfentanil in combination with propofol for cervical dilation–related procedures, as well as supporting evidence from clinical studies in similar settings [16, 17]. This intermediate dose was chosen to provide a clinically relevant and stable background analgesic effect while allowing evaluation of the hypnotic dose–response relationship of ciprofol. Using this standardized opioid dose enabled isolation of the hypnotic contribution of ciprofol to movement suppression. The Dixon sequential design was chosen because it efficiently estimates ED50 with a limited sample size through adaptive dose adjustment [8].

The ED50 of 0.397 mg·kg⁻1 observed in the present study is consistent with previously reported dose ranges of ciprofol in similar procedural settings. Li et al. [18] reported a high procedural success rate with 0.4 mg·kg⁻1 ciprofol, closely approximating the ED50 identified here. Jin et al. [19] reported an ED50 of 0.444 mg·kg⁻1 for ciprofol combined with low dose sufentanil in hysteroscopy. The slightly lower ED50 observed in the present study may reflect differences in opioid selection and synergistic interactions. Deng et al. [20] reported an ED50 of 0.366 mg·kg⁻1 (95% CI 0.337–0.396 mg·kg⁻1) for ciprofol induction in middle-aged adults. The ED50 identified in our study falls within a similar dose range, suggesting relative consistency of hypnotic requirements across short procedural contexts in comparable age populations. Min Pan et al. [21] reported a higher ED50 (0.433 mg·kg⁻1) during fiberoptic bronchoscopy in non-elderly female patients with pulmonary tuberculosis. Differences in study populations, procedural stimulation, and potential pharmacokinetic influences such as concomitant rifampicin therapy may partially explain the increased dose requirement. These findings underscore that dose requirements may vary across clinical contexts and patient characteristics.

A recent study by Gao et al. [22] investigated the ED50 and ED95 of alfentanil for suppressing cervical dilation responses when combined with ciprofol during hysteroscopy. In that study, alfentanil doses ranging from 8 to 14 μg·kg⁻1 were evaluated while the ciprofol dose was fixed, and an optimal alfentanil dose range of 10–12 μg·kg⁻1 was suggested.

In contrast, the present study adopted a complementary approach by fixing the opioid component and investigating the hypnotic dose requirement of ciprofol. This design allowed isolation of the sedative contribution of ciprofol to movement suppression, thereby providing additional information on dose optimization of the hypnotic component in this drug combination. Together, these studies offer complementary insights into the dose–response relationships of both opioid and hypnotic agents in hysteroscopic sedation.

From a safety perspective, no injection pain was observed. Two patients required transient jaw-thrust intervention for mild oxygen desaturation, and no assisted ventilation or serious adverse events were observed. Hemodynamic and respiratory variables decreased at cervical dilation compared with baseline; however, all parameters remained within clinically acceptable ranges without requiring additional intervention. Recovery profiles were consistent with the requirements of ambulatory hysteroscopic procedures [23]. These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that ciprofol may be associated with a lower incidence of injection pain and respiratory depression compared with conventional sedatives [24].

From a clinical perspective, the ED50 identified in this study represents the dose at which 50% of patients achieve adequate suppression of cervical dilation–induced movement and may serve as a useful reference for titration. The model-derived ED95 provides an estimate of the dose required to achieve a high probability of movement suppression and may offer approximate upper reference range for dosing consideration in clinical settings where a higher success rate is desired. However, given the ED50-focused nature of the Dixon design and the limited data in the upper dose range, the ED95 should be interpreted as an approximate, model-based estimate rather than a definitive clinical target, and individualized dose adjustment remains essential.

This study has several limitations. First, the Dixon up-and-down design concentrates observations around the ED50 region; therefore, while ED50 estimation is robust, ED95 estimation relies on regression modeling and is based on limited data in the upper dose range. As such, the ED95 value should be considered an exploratory, model-derived estimate and interpreted with caution. Second, the sample size was limited to 32 patients, as is typical for sequential dose-finding studies, which may affect precision and external validity. Third, this study included a relatively homogeneous and highly selected patient population (female patients aged 18–60 years, ASA physical status I-II, BMI < 28 kg/m2), which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In routine clinical practice, patients undergoing hysteroscopy often present with a broader range of characteristics, including advanced age, obesity, and higher comorbidity burden. Therefore, the applicability of the identified dose range to these populations remains uncertain and warrants further investigation in more diverse cohorts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, under a fixed background dose of alfentanil 7.4 μg·kg⁻1, the ED50 of ciprofol for suppressing cervical dilation–induced movement during hysteroscopy was 0.397 mg·kg⁻1. The ED95 was estimated to be 0.521 mg·kg⁻1 based on logistic regression modeling. However, given the ED50-focused nature of the Dixon design, this value should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. These findings provide a quantitative reference to dose selection in similar ambulatory hysteroscopic settings, particularly for ED50 estimation.
