Body roundness index outperforms body mass index in predicting 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk: Evidence from a health screening cohort in Taiwan
Authors: Chun-Li Wang, Chiann-Yi Hsu, Wei-Min Chu, Chia-Yen Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the association between body roundness index (BRI) and 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk and to compare its predictive performance with body mass index (BMI).MethodsWe included 13,086 adults aged 40–79 years undergoing health examinations in Taiwan between 2012 and 2019. ASCVD risk was estimated using the ACC/AHA Pooled Cohort Equations. Ordinal logistic regression assessed associations, and receiver operating characteristic curves compared the predictive performance of BRI and BMI.ResultsHigher BRI was significantly associated with increased ASCVD risk. In the multivariable ordinal logistic regression model, each one-unit increase in BRI was associated with an adjusted odds ratio of 1.58 (95 % CI: 1.54, 1.63) for higher ASCVD risk. Participants in the highest BRI quartile had 4.07-fold higher odds (95 % CI: 3.69, 4.49, p for trend <0.01) of being in a higher ASCVD risk category than those in the lowest quartile. BRI demonstrated superior predictive performance to BMI, with AUCs of 0.72 vs. 0.69 for intermediate risk and 0.70 vs. 0.68 for high risk.ConclusionsBRI is significantly associated with 10-year ASCVD risk and shows superior discriminative ability, supporting its use in cardiovascular risk stratification in East Asian populations.Keywords: Body roundness index, Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, Cardiovascular risk prediction, Anthropometric index, Preventive screening, Public health

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death globally, despite a substantial decline in age-standardized mortality rates over the past three decades (Mensah et al., 2023). According to the Ministry of Health and Welfare in Taiwan, heart and cerebrovascular disease have consistently remained among the top three causes of death in recent years (Ministry of Health and Welfare, 2023).

Given the substantial burden of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), early identification of individuals at elevated risk is critical for effective prevention. To facilitate clinical decision-making, the American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) developed the Pooled Cohort Equations, a widely used tool to estimate 10-year ASCVD risk based on factors including age, sex, blood pressure, cholesterol levels, diabetes, and smoking status (Goff et al., 2014). Accurate risk estimation guides individualized preventive strategies, including statins, antihypertensive therapy, and lifestyle modification (Goff et al., 2014; Arnett et al., 2019; Lloyd-Jones et al., 2019).

Among various risk factors, obesity, particularly central obesity, is recognized as a key modifiable contributor to ASCVD, primarily through its strong associations with hypertension, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation (Grundy et al., 2019). Body mass index (BMI), defined as weight divided by height squared (kg/m2), has long served as the standard tool for classifying obesity and has been extensively used in both clinical and epidemiological settings to estimate disease risk and mortality. Numerous studies have reported a positive association between BMI and ASCVD risk (Bhaskaran et al., 2018; Khan et al., 2018; Caleyachetty et al., 2017). Meta-analyses have shown a dose-dependent increase in coronary heart disease and stroke with higher BMI and abdominal fatness (Aune et al., 2018), while pooled Asian cohort data revealed a J-shaped association in East Asians and a linear relationship in South Asians (Chen et al., 2013).

Nevertheless, due to the limitations of BMI in capturing body fat distribution and distinguishing adipose from lean mass, recent studies have increasingly focused on waist circumference-based measures. For example, waist-to-height ratio has been proposed as a simpler and more effective predictor of cardiometabolic risk than BMI or waist circumference (Ashwell and Gibson, 2016), while waist-to-hip ratio has been recommended as a practical proxy for visceral fat (Snijder et al., 2006). To address the limitations of BMI, Thomas et al. developed the body roundness index (BRI), a novel geometry-based measure that incorporates waist circumference and height to estimate body shape and visceral adiposity (Thomas et al., 2013).

Recent studies have increasingly validated the predictive value of BRI for cardiovascular risk across diverse populations. Evidence from Japan and China suggests that BRI is significantly associated with metabolic dysfunction, stroke, and all-cause mortality, even in high-risk groups such as individuals with diabetes or steatotic liver disease (Huang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). A recent analysis of NHANES data by Gan et al. demonstrated that higher BRI values were independently associated with increased risk of stroke, reinforcing the potential role of BRI in predicting major cardiovascular events (Gan et al., 2025). Building on these findings, Tang and Zeng found that BRI outperformed BMI and waist circumference in predicting 10-year ASCVD risk in a multiethnic U.S. population using NHANES participants (Tang and Zeng, 2024). In contrast, Islam et al. reported that BRI was a consistent predictor of cardiovascular risk across 12 South and Southeast Asian countries, using the World Health Organization CVD risk charts based on non-laboratory variables, reinforcing its relevance in Asian populations (Islam et al., 2024).

While emerging evidence has highlighted the potential of the BRI as a superior anthropometric predictor of cardiovascular risk compared to traditional measures like BMI, direct comparisons between BRI and BMI within established ASCVD risk prediction models, such as the Pooled Cohort Equations, remain limited, particularly in large Asian populations. East Asian adults, including Taiwanese populations, often exhibit distinct cardiometabolic risk profiles, with higher rates of metabolic syndrome at lower BMI thresholds, underscoring the need for population-specific validation. By leveraging a government-subsidized health examination dataset of over 13,000 individuals, our study extends previous evidence by integrating BRI into a clinically recognized ASCVD risk estimation framework, enabling population-specific evaluation within a standardized predictive model. It thereby provides both statistical power and real-world relevance to inform cardiovascular risk screening and public health strategies in East Asian settings. To address this gap, we investigated the relationship between BRI and estimated 10-year ASCVD risk in a large Taiwanese cohort, directly compared the predictive performance of BRI with BMI, and evaluated the consistency of these associations across BMI-defined categories, highlighting the potential utility of BRI as a simple, scalable tool for cardiovascular risk stratification in community and primary care settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study used health examination data from a major tertiary medical center in central Taiwan. The dataset was derived from the Adult Preventive Medical Services Database between 2012 and 2019, which includes standardized health screening records from individuals participating in government-subsidized preventive health services.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (IRB No. CE21445B, approval date: November 4, 2021). All data were anonymized prior to analysis, and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of its retrospective design.

Study population

We included individuals who underwent preventive health examinations at a tertiary medical center in Taiwan between 2012 and 2019. Per national screening policy, this service targets adults aged 40 years and older. To calculate the 10-year ASCVD risk, we restricted the study population to those aged 40 to 79 years. For participants with multiple records, only the first was included.

Exclusion criteria included (1) self-reported history of major diseases (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, or cancer); (2) age outside the eligible 40–79 years range; (3) missing data required for calculating either the BRI or the ASCVD risk score; and (4) pregnancy. Among the 22,944 individuals initially assessed, 6683 were excluded due to prior major diseases, 1432 due to ineligible age, and 1743 due to incomplete data, leaving 13,086 participants in the final analysis. A flow diagram of participant selection is presented in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Data collection

Data were extracted from standardized forms used in the adult preventive health examination program. The collected variables included demographic information (age, sex, and residence), lifestyle factors (cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, betel nut chewing, and physical activity), medical history (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia), and medication use (antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antidiabetic drugs). Information on residence, lifestyle factors, medical history, and medication use was obtained from a standardized self-administered questionnaire.

Anthropometric and clinical features, including BMI, waist circumference, and systolic blood pressure, were measured by trained nurses following standardized protocols. The BRI was calculated as 364.2–365.5 × (1 − [waist circumference in centimeters / 2π]2 / [0.5 × height in centimeters]2), based on the method proposed by Thomas et al. (Thomas et al., 2013). BMI was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m2) and classified as <18.5, 18.5–23.9, 24.0–26.9, and ≥ 27.0 kg/m2, based on the Taiwan Medical Association for the Study of Obesity guideline (Obesity, 2023). Laboratory parameters, including fasting glucose, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL—C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and serum creatinine were analyzed at the central laboratory of Taichung Veterans General Hospital using automated biochemical analyzers. Estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation.

As this nationwide program was designed for chronic diseases prevention, it collects demographic, lifestyle, and laboratory variables, which we included in the descriptive analyses to characterize the study population.

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk estimation

The primary outcome was the estimated 10-year risk of ASCVD, defined as the composite risk of nonfatal myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease death, and stroke. Risk scores were calculated using the Pooled Cohort Equations developed by the ACC/ AHA, based on the 2013 guidelines. The variables included in the calculation were age, sex, total cholesterol, HDL—C, systolic blood pressure, hypertension treatment, diabetes, and smoking status.

ASCVD risk was estimated using the ASCVD Risk Estimator Plus tool (https://tools.acc.org/ascvd-risk-estimator-plus) and categorized according to the 2019 ACC/AHA guideline for the primary prevention of cardiovascular disease(Arnett et al., 2019) as low (<5 %), borderline (5 %–<7.5 %), intermediate (7.5 %–<20 %), and high (≥20 %). Because the borderline group had a small sample size, it was combined with the low-risk group in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics across quartiles of the BRI. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

To evaluate the association between BRI and estimated 10-year ASCVD risk, ordinal logistic regression analyses were performed, treating ASCVD risk categories (low, intermediate, and high) as ordered outcomes. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). To avoid over-adjustment, variables already included in the ASCVD risk calculation (age, sex, total cholesterol, HDL—C, systolic blood pressure, antihypertensive drugs, cigarette smoking, and diabetes) were not entered into the regression models. Instead, models were adjusted for alcohol consumption, betel nut chewing, physical activity, hyperlipidemia, triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL, and ALT >40 mg/dL. To confirm the robustness of the findings, multinomial logistic regression models were additionally performed to compare intermediate (≥7.5 %) and high (≥20 %) ASCVD risk versus the low-risk reference group.

Model discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses. To compare the predictive performance of anthropometric indices, separate multivariable logistic regression models were constructed for BRI (Model 1) and BMI (Model 2), each adjusted for the same covariates. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated for both models, and differences between AUCs were evaluated using DeLong's test for correlated ROC curves.

To further examine whether the association between BRI and ASCVD risk was independent of overall adiposity, a subgroup analysis stratified by BMI-defined categories (underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obesity) was conducted. Within each BMI group, ordinal logistic regression models were applied to evaluate the association between BRI (continuous and quartile categories) and ASCVD risk, adjusting for the same covariates as in the main analysis.

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Baseline characteristics

A total of 13,086 participants were included. Higher BRI quartiles were associated with older age (mean: 54.2 years in Q1 vs. 62.2 years in Q4, p < 0.01), a higher proportion of males (28.2 % vs. 44.4 %, p < 0.01), less urban residence, and greater likelihood of smoking, alcohol consumption, and betel nut use (all p < 0.01). Individuals in higher BRI quartiles had significantly greater prevalence of hypertension (8.5 % in Q1 vs. 31.3 % in Q4), diabetes (21.2 % vs. 25.0 %), and hyperlipidemia (2.5 % vs. 6.7 %) (all p < 0.01). The use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antidiabetic medications did not differ significantly across quartiles. Clinically, BMI, waist circumference, systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg, fasting glucose ≥100 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL, and ALT >40 mg/dL all increased with BRI while HDL-C decreased (all p < 0.01). Association between body roundness index and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk

Higher BRI was significantly associated with increased odds of elevated ASCVD risk. In the multivariable ordinal logistic regression model, each one-unit increase in BRI was associated with an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 1.58 (95 % CI: 1.54, 1.63) for higher ASCVD risk. When analyzed by quartiles, a clear dose–response relationship was observed, with participants in the highest BRI quartile having 4.07 times higher odds of being in a higher ASCVD risk category compared with those in the lowest quartile (95 % CI: 3.69, 4.49, p for trend <0.01). These results are summarized in Table 2. In addition to BRI, several covariates were significantly associated with higher ASCVD risk. Alcohol consumption and betel nut chewing showed strong positive associations with ASCVD risk (adjusted ORs: 2.33 and 3.32, respectively; both p < 0.01). Regular exercise, triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL, ALT >40 mg/dL, and a history of hyperlipidemia were also independently associated with increased odds of higher ASCVD risk. Full regression results are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Predictive performance of body roundness index and body mass index for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk

The predictive performance of BRI and BMI for identifying intermediate ASCVD risk was first assessed using ROC analysis in the overall population. As illustrated in Fig. 1, for intermediate ASCVD risk, BRI demonstrated higher AUC than BMI (0.67 vs. 0.63, p < 0.01) and remained superior after multivariable adjustment (0.72 vs. 0.69, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Table 3). For high ASCVD risk (≥20 %), the adjusted AUCs were 0.70 vs. 0.677 (DeLong test, p < 0.01; Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

In this large Taiwanese cohort, BRI was significantly associated with the estimated 10-year ASCVD risk and demonstrated superior discriminative ability compared with BMI. The graded relationship observed across BRI quartiles underscores a clear dose-dependent association between central adiposity and cardiovascular risk, which remained robust after multivariable adjustment. These findings support the concept that visceral adiposity-captured more accurately by BRI than by BMI-plays a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of atherosclerotic disease. Accordingly, BRI may represent a practical anthropometric marker for both population-level risk stratification and individualized risk assessment. However, clinically applicable cutoff values for BRI have not yet been standardized.

Subgroup analyses stratified by BMI-defined categories demonstrated that BRI remained significantly associated with ASCVD risk across both non-obese and obese groups. This finding reinforces that BRI captures cardiovascular risk independent of overall adiposity, reflecting central fat distribution rather than body size alone. Our results are consistent with longitudinal evidence from large Asian cohorts. Ding et al. reported that both baseline BRI and its trajectories over time were independently associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among middle-aged and older adults in China (Ding et al., 2023). Similarly, Wu et al. found that persistent or increasing BRI trajectories conferred significantly higher risks of cardiovascular outcomes and mortality (Wu et al., 2022).

From a mechanistic perspective, these findings are further supported by prior evidence. A consensus statement by the Italian Society of Cardiovascular Prevention, emphasized that visceral adiposity exerts a disproportionately negative impact on cardiometabolic health, through mechanisms including chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and endothelial dysfunction, thereby accelerating atherosclerotic processes even in individuals with non-obese BMI profiles (Volpe and Gallo, 2023). Similarly, the American Heart Association has identified abdominal adiposity, particularly visceral fat, as a distinct and high-risk phenotype contributing to residual cardiovascular risk beyond that explained by overall adiposity (Powell-Wiley et al., 2021). Population-based studies also show that visceral adiposity is independently associated with cardiometabolic risk (Ruiz-Castell et al., 2021), further justifying emphasis on central obesity indices like BRI (Chait and den Hartigh, 2020). These considerations help contextualize our finding that BRI demonstrated superior discriminative ability over BMI in ROC analysis for identifying individuals at elevated 10-year ASCVD risk. This is further supported by findings from large-scale studies in Eastern China, where BRI was more strongly associated with multiple cardiometabolic risk factors than BMI, reinforcing its clinical relevance (Xu et al., 2021). Moreover, as concerns about BMI's limitations continue to emerge, recent expert commentary has suggested that BRI may one day serve as a more physiologically meaningful and clinically actionable alternative in assessing cardiometabolic risk (Schweitzer, 2024).

From a clinical and public health standpoint, the BRI offers several practical advantages. Derived solely from waist circumference and height, it is low-cost, simple to implement, and can be readily integrated into existing health examination systems without additional resources. This integration could enhance early risk identification and support proactive intervention planning. This aligns with prior findings by Ashwell et al., who demonstrated that waist-to-height ratio, a similarly simple waist-based index, outperformed both waist circumference and BMI in identifying adults at risk for cardiometabolic conditions across diverse populations (Ashwell et al., 2012). Building on this evidence, our findings suggest that BRI provides greater clinical value than BMI by more accurately capturing central adiposity, a key determinant of cardiometabolic risk, particularly in Asian populations, thereby improving identification of high-risk individuals for preventive interventions.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes inference of causal relationships between BRI and ASCVD risk. Second, ASCVD risk was estimated using the Pooled Cohort Equations, which were developed based on U.S. populations and may not fully capture risk profiles, or genetic and behavioral patterns specific to East Asian populations. Third, unmeasured confounding factors, such as dietary patterns, education level, or socioeconomic status, were not available in our dataset and may have influenced the observed associations. Moreover, while the improvement in AUC for BRI over BMI was statistically significant, its impact on clinical decision-making remains uncertain. Lastly, our study population consisted of individuals participating in a voluntary health screening program, which may introduce healthy screened bias and limit generalizability to less health-conscious or underserved populations. Although the data were collected between 2012 and 2019, the dataset represents one of the most comprehensive hospital-based screening cohorts in Taiwan, and the ASCVD risk estimation framework has not changed substantially, supporting the continued relevance of our findings to current clinical practice. Future research should aim to validate these findings in prospective, population-based cohorts with longitudinal follow-up, and to examine whether incorporating BRI into risk prediction models leads to measurable improvements in clinical decision-making and patient outcomes.

Conclusion

In this large population-based cohort, BRI was positively associated with estimated 10-year ASCVD risk and demonstrated superior discrimination compared to BMI. This association risk remained robust after multivariable adjustment. Given its simplicity, low cost, and predictive value, BRI may serve as a practical tool for cardiovascular risk stratification in both clinical and community settings. Its compatibility with existing screening systems supports its potential role in population-level prevention. Future studies should validate these findings in prospective cohorts and explore the feasibility of integrating BRI into routine cardiovascular risk assessment.
