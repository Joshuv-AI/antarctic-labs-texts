Impact of time from diagnosis to endoscopic resection on clinical outcomes in early gastric cancer
Authors: Meiqian Wang, Feifei Zhou, Yiru Chen, Hongwei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhether a preoperative waiting period before endoscopic resection (ER) adversely affects early gastric cancer (EGC) outcomes remains unclear. This retrospective cohort evaluated whether delays up to 180 days impair oncological and pathological outcomes.MethodsWe enrolled 1646 consecutive EGC patients who underwent curative ER between 2010 and 2023. Primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS); secondary endpoints included positive resection margins, pathological upstaging, curative resection rate, and additional gastrectomy requirement. Analyses comprised Kaplan–Meier curves, multivariable Cox/logistic regression with time modeled categorically (<30 vs. ≥30 days) and continuously (per 10-day increment), plus restricted cubic spline (RCS) modeling. Sensitivity analysis was conducted after excluding high risk deep submucosal (SM2) lesions.ResultsMedian waiting time was 15 days (IQR 11–23); only 2.3% waited >90 days. Linear and RCS analyses showed no independent adverse associations between delays within 180 days and any primary or secondary endpoint (all p > 0.05). Baseline characteristics were well balanced apart from marginal discrepancies in age and family history, which were adjusted in multivariate models. Sensitivity analysis excluding SM2 lesions reproduced consistent neutral survival findings.ConclusionPreoperative waiting up to 180 days was not linked to worse EGC outcomes in this cohort. These exploratory results support reasonable 6-month deferral for preoperative assessment, but marked sample‑size imbalance among long-wait patients prevents definitive validation, requiring large prospective multicenter trials.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) brings a heavy global socioeconomic and public health burden. According to 2022 global cancer statistics, approximately 968,000 new GC cases and 660,000 cancer-related deaths are recorded annually, ranking GC fifth in both global incidence and mortality [1]. Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), the core modality of endoscopic resection (ER) has become the well-established standard curative approach for eligible early gastric cancer (EGC) per international guidelines [2]. The time-to-endoscopic resection (time-to-ER) interval between EGC diagnosis and ER varies widely in clinical practice, affected by patient comorbidities, multidisciplinary team (MDT) schedules, limited endoscopic resources and cross-hospital referral procedures. Clinicians worry that prolonged preoperative delays may trigger tumor progression, deeper submucosal invasion or lymph node metastasis, thus reducing curative resection rates and worsening long-term survival. On the other hand, excessively hasty ER may compromise comprehensive preoperative risk stratification and personalized treatment planning. Defining a clinically acceptable waiting window is essential to balance treatment timeliness and procedural safety.

Two landmark East Asian retrospective cohorts have investigated the prognostic impact of time-to-ER interval, yet obvious methodological defects limit their generalizability. Lee et al. [3] only followed Korean patients for a maximum of 90 days and merely took non-curative resection as the research endpoint, without long-term overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) analysis. Hoki et al. [4] extended the observation period to one year but mixed benign adenomas and malignant gastric lesions into a single cohort, causing severe population heterogeneity. Although their exploratory model implied elevated risks beyond 180 days, the sample size of delayed patients was insufficient, and complete multivariable survival adjustment was missing. In addition, median waiting times differed sharply across regions: 36.8 days in Korea and 72.5 days in Japan, versus merely 15 days in real-world Chinese tertiary hospitals.

Three major research gaps remain unresolved by existing literature: First, no prior study has simultaneously combined long-term survival and four intraoperative pathological indicators for comprehensive risk evaluation; second, no cohort has incorporated smoking as a modifiable lifestyle confounder together with waiting delay; third, large-scale Chinese real-world data are absent to verify waiting time safety under local medical workflows. Treatment delay findings for colorectal, breast, lung, prostate and cervical cancers are tumor-specific and cannot be extrapolated to superficial EGC treated with ER. While separate analyses have evaluated waiting delays in advanced gastric radical gastrectomy, colorectal, breast, lung, prostate and cervical malignancies, results are highly tumor-specific and cannot be extrapolated to superficial EGC treated via ER [5–9].

Against this background, we conducted a large single-center retrospective cohort study of pathologically confirmed EGC patients with a unified 0–180-day waiting limit. Six clinical endpoints were systematically assessed, including two primary survival indicators and four secondary pathological outcomes. We adopted a dual analytical framework combining categorical grouping for clinical interpretation and continuous/RCS regression for statistical rigor to eliminate bias from artificial time cutoffs. A sensitivity analysis excluding SM2 lesions was performed to rule out nodal metastasis confounding. We hypothesized that waiting intervals ≥30 days (within 180 days) would correlate with unfavorable survival and higher rates of adverse pathological outcomes. This study aims to provide Chinese real-world evidence and practical timing suggestions for gastroenterologists managing EGC patients.

Methods

Patient selection

EGC was histologically defined as gastric carcinoma confined to the mucosa or submucosa regardless of tumor size and lymph node status. We retrospectively enrolled patients who received curative ER at Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University from October 2010 to December 2023. Eligibility complied with absolute and expanded ER criteria from the 7th 2025 JGCA guidelines, and eCuraA curative resection standards were followed strictly [10]: (i) Absolute ER indication: Differentiated intramucosal cancer ≤2 cm without ulceration; (ii) Expanded ER indication 1: Differentiated intramucosal cancer >2 cm without ulceration or ≤3 cm with ulceration; (iii) Expanded ER indication 2: Undifferentiated intramucosal cancer ≤2 cm without ulceration; (iv) eCuraA standard: En bloc resection, negative horizontal (HM0) and vertical (VM0) margins, no lymphovascular invasion (Ly0/V0), and qualified lesion invasion ranges.

Patients were dichotomized into <30-day early ER and ≥30-day delayed ER groups based on a clinically intuitive 1-month cutoff (median waiting time 15 days, IQR 11–23). All intergroup comparisons and regression models adjusted for two unbalanced baseline covariates: age and family tumor history. To avoid statistical bias from arbitrary categorical division, waiting time was treated as a continuous variable (per 10-day increment) in multivariable Cox regression, and RCS Cox models with knots at the 10th, 50th and 90th waiting-time percentiles were constructed to test linear and nonlinear risk trends (visualized in Supplementary Figure 1). All patients with SM2 invasion (>500μm below the muscularis mucosae received institutional MDT recommendations for additional radical gastrectomy with lymphadenectomy; detailed SM2 patient management data are listed in Supplementary Table 1. A pre-specified sensitivity analysis excluding all 95 SM2 patients generated a restricted cohort of 1551 individuals.

This retrospective study obtained ethical exemption from the institutional ethics committee. A total of 2044 patients with biopsy-suspected EGC were initially screened. Following exclusion of ineligible cases, 1646 patients constituted the final analytical sample. The detailed inclusion criteria were as follows: (1)definitive histological diagnosis of EGC via preoperative gastroscopic biopsy;(2)availability of complete clinical, pathological, and postoperative follow-up data. Patients were excluded according to the following criteria: (1) missing key data on survival outcomes or time-to-ER intervals; (2)ER performed more than 180 days after initial diagnosis, as such prolonged waiting intervals were generally attributed to unmeasured special confounding factors;(3)pathological confirmation of chronic gastric mucosal inflammation, low to intermediate grade intraepithelial neoplasia, advanced gastric cancer, or recurrent residual gastric cancer on postoperative pathology;(4) diagnosis of benign gastric lesions on postoperative pathology (Figure 1).

Flowchart of patient selection for the present study on early gastric cancer patients undergoing endoscopic resection.

Observed indicators

Collected clinical and pathological variables included age, sex, smoking history, family tumor history, tumor size, tumor localization, histological differentiation, lymphovascular invasion, resection margin status, gross appearance and Helicobacter pylori infection, as well as submucosal invasion depth.

Standardized classification criteria were applied for behavioral variable grouping: occasional smokers were classified as non-smokers. Tumor locations were divided into three anatomical regions: the upper stomach (cardia and fundus), mid-stomach (gastric body), and lower stomach (antrum and pylorus). Tumor size was measured based on postoperative pathological specimens. Gross appearances were categorized into the elevated type and flat/depressed type. Tumor invasion depth was stratified as mucosal carcinoma, SM1 (invasion ≤500μm beneath the muscularis mucosae), and SM2 (invasion >500 μm).

Follow-up

All patients received standardized outpatient surveillance after ER: follow-up visits every 3 months in the first postoperative year, then semi-annual or annual examinations thereafter. Data were extracted from electronic medical records and standardized telephone follow-up; the final follow-up cutoff date was December 31, 2025.

The primary study endpoints were OS and DFS. OS was defined as the time interval from the initial endoscopic diagnosis of EGC to all-cause death or the last follow-up. DFS was defined as the time from ER to the detection of tumor recurrence, metastasis, or the end of follow-up. Resection margin positivity, pathological upstaging, curative resection rate, and requirement for additional surgery were designated as secondary endpoints. Pathological upstaging was defined as postoperative pathological T stage (pT) being higher than the preoperative clinical T stage (cT) based on endoscopic diagnosis. Additional surgery referred to radical gastrectomy with lymphadenectomy performed after ER.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages; continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. Age was analyzed as a categorical variable. Intergroup comparisons adopted χ2or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data, independent t tests for continuous data, and Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests for survival comparison. Univariate Cox regression screened OS/DFS prognostic factors; univariate logistic regression analyzed predictors of binary pathological outcomes. Variables with univariate p < 0.05 entered multivariable models. Time- to-ER (per 10-day increment) was forced into all multivariable Cox and logistic models regardless of univariate p value. Multivariable Cox regression was used for survival endpoints, and multivariable logistic regression for pathological outcomes.

Two continuous-variable analytical approaches were supplemented to avoid grouping bias: (1) Linear continuous regression: Waiting time modeled as a 10-day increment term to assess linear exposure–outcome associations. (2) RCS Cox regression: Three evenly distributed knots were set. OS models adjusted for age, smoking and invasion depth; DFS models adjusted for age and tumor size. We reported p for nonlinearity and p for overall effect, with adjusted hazard ratio curves (95% CI shaded bands) shown in Supplementary Figure 1. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 represented statistical significance. Analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0 and R Studio software.

Kaplan–Meier survival comparison

Kaplan–Meier survival curves for OS are displayed in Figure 3. Unadjusted survival analysis revealed no significant intergroup difference in OS between the early resection group (< 30 days) and the delayed resection group (≥30 days) (log-rank p = 0.11). Similarly, no statistically significant difference in DFS was observed between the two groups (log-rank p = 0.97), as illustrated in Figure 3. The restricted mean survival time for OS was 154.7 months (95% CI: 147.8–161.5) in the overall cohort, with median OS not reached in either group due to the low overall mortality rate.

This Kaplan–Meier overall survival and disease-free survival curve illustrates the long-term prognostic outcomes of patients with EGC, stratified according to the time interval between initial diagnosis and ER.

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression for overall survival

Univariate Cox regression showed age 60–69 years, age ≥70 years, smoking and SM2 invasion were independent adverse risk factors for OS (all p < 0.001 except smoking p = 0.002). RCS modeling revealed no nonlinear association between waiting time and OS (p for nonlinearity = 0.852, p for overall = 0.715, Supplementary Figure 1A). Summary of secondary endpoint analyses

Across the four above pathological endpoints analyzed via logistic regression, preoperative ER waiting time (per 10-day increment) showed no independent adverse correlation with any pathological adverse outcome (all p > 0.05). These consistent neutral results further validate that time to ER within 180 days does not impair pathological curability for EGC.

Sensitivity analysis excluding SM2 patients

We excluded 95 SM2 patients to eliminate high nodal-risk confounding, forming a restricted cohort of n = 1551. Supplementary Table 1 summarized SM2 patient treatment data: 48 (50.53%) received additional gastrectomy, among whom only 6 patients had lymph node metastasis; 47 patients refused surgery due to severe comorbidities or personal choice. Baseline patient distribution was balanced between two waiting subgroups after SM2 exclusion (p = 0.818). Log-rank OS comparison still yielded non-significant results (p = 0.311), consistent with primary cohort findings (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

Core findings and comparison with previous literature

This single-center cohort of 1646 pure EGC patients proved preoperative waiting up to 180 days exerted no independent adverse influence on OS, DFS or four secondary pathological endpoints. Identical neutral results were validated via binary grouping, continuous linear regression, RCS nonlinear modeling and SM2 sensitivity analysis.

Our overall conclusion is consistent with the general trend of previous East Asian studies, yet our research has eight unique innovations filling existing research gaps: (1) EGC population without mixed premalignant/benign lesions;(2)larger sample size with Chinese real-world short waiting characteristics; (3) comprehensive dual survival + multi-pathway endpoint system;(4) linear + nonlinear dual statistical framework to eliminate grouping bias;(5) first exploration of smoking as an independent OS prognostic factor for EGC waiting research;(6) pre-specified SM2 sensitivity analysis; (7)full multivariable adjustment for all baseline confounders; (8)balanced baseline pathological risk verification to rule out confounding by indication.

Notably, the prognostic impact of the time-to-ER interval varies substantially across different malignancies. Multiple clinical studies have validated that preoperative waiting duration does not adversely affect survival outcomes in several cancer types, including lung, prostate, and cervical cancers [7–9]. For colon cancer, despite a relatively prolonged median interval from diagnosis to surgical intervention, delayed treatment has not been shown to compromise patient survival [11]. In contrast, emerging evidence indicates that prolonged waiting intervals are detrimental in certain early-stage malignancies. For early-stage lung cancer, extended time intervals from diagnosis to surgery are significantly associated with inferior survival, with a waiting period exceeding 90 days identified as a critical threshold for reduced oncological prognosis [12].

Rationale for 30-day stratification and analytical framework

We adopted the 30-day cutoff as the primary grouping standard for two clinical and statistical reasons: First, a 1-month interval is universally accepted in routine gastroenterology consultation, convenient for clinical communication with patients. Second, the cohort median waiting time was only 15 days, and the 30-day boundary naturally separated the vast majority of short-wait patients from a small delayed subgroup.

We initially considered three-tier grouping (<1 month/1–3 months/3–6 months) but abandoned this design due to severe sample imbalance: merely 38 patients waited more than 90 days, leading to unstable regression estimates and insufficient statistical power. We supplemented continuous 10-day variable regression and RCS spline analysis to avoid artificial cutoff bias. RCS curves showed flat hazard ratios without elevated risk inflection points within 0–180 days, confirming no progressive risk increase with prolonged waiting. Binary grouping is only used for intuitive clinical explanation; continuous and spline models serve as core statistical evidence.

Cautious interpretation of the 180-day waiting threshold

We emphasize that the 180-day safe window conclusion is exploratory rather than definitive, with three major limitations: First, only 2.3% patients waited over 90 days, insufficient sample size weakens statistical power to detect subtle adverse risks in long-delay subgroups. Second, patients with ER delay >180 days were excluded, as ultra-long waiting was caused by non-tumor factors rather than planned clinical deferral. Third, Hoki’s research hinted potential risk growth beyond 180 days, which our dataset cannot rule out.

Clinically, our results only support reasonable controlled deferral within six months to complete comorbidity assessment, MDT discussion and cross-hospital referral. Large balanced prospective multicenter trials are required to formally verify this threshold before guideline formulation.

Intrinsic limitations of OS/DFS as primary endpoints

For EGC patients with SM2 invasion or positive margins, standardized salvage gastrectomy can be performed after ER. Even mild tumor progression during waiting can be remedied by additional radical surgery, making OS and DFS less sensitive to subtle pathological changes caused by short-to-medium delays. To offset this defect, we comprehensively analyzed four intraoperative pathological indicators closely linked to curability, and all showed neutral associations with waiting time, independently supporting our core result.

Explanation for non-significant LVI and positive margins in OS models

Lymphovascular invasion and positive horizontal/vertical resection margins are universally accepted poor prognostic markers for gastric cancer. For patients with noncurative ESD characterized solely by positive lateral margins, supplementary endoscopic treatment administered within three months has been recommended to optimize DFS [13]. In the neoadjuvant setting, previous studies have further demonstrated that extending the interval from the completion of neoadjuvant chemotherapy to radical gastrectomy beyond 30 days could achieve a higher major pathological response rate, without compromising oncological safety or increasing perioperative morbidity and mortality [14]. Yet these variables failed to retain statistical significance in our OS multivariable models for three cohort-specific reasons:(1) Extremely low all-cause mortality, median OS unreachable, insufficient events to detect moderate hazard differences; (2)Institutional standardized salvage gastrectomy offsets long-term survival risks of high-risk lesions; (3) Very low incidence of LVI (1.7%) and positive margins (2.9%) leads to unstable regression HR values.

Divergent smoking prognosis and elimination of confounding by indication

Smoking is well recognized as a critical independent modifiable risk factor for GC. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that smokers have a 50% higher risk of developing gastric cancer than non-smokers, with the risk rising in a dose-and time-dependent manner with prolonged smoking exposure [15]. Accordingly, targeted cancer prevention strategies focusing on the intervention of major modifiable risk factors, including smoking, overweight, obesity, and chronic infection, are essential to reduce the global burden of gastric malignancy, prevent numerous new cancer cases, and improve long-term patient survival worldwide [16].

Smoking independently worsened OS but had no impact on DFS, which can be explained by endpoint definitions: OS counts all-cause death, and smoking increases cardiovascular, pulmonary and other extra-gastric cancer mortality (competing risks); DFS only records gastric cancer-specific recurrence, which is weakly affected by tobacco carcinogenesis. Smoking cessation should be prioritized for all EGC patients to improve long-term OS.

Existing literature has identified distinct baseline tumor characteristics linked to short preoperative waiting intervals, which may mediate prognostic differences. Patients with shorter time-to-ER delays tend to exhibit more advanced tumor stages, higher tumor marker levels, lower R0 curative resection rates, and decreased body mass index, all of which are established adverse prognostic factors for long-term survival [17,18]. Confounding by indication (clinicians arranging early ER for more aggressive tumors) is a major bias in delay research. However, Table 1 confirmed all core pathological risk factors (differentiation, LVI, SM2 proportion) were balanced between two waiting groups, and minor age/family history differences were fully adjusted in all regression models. No evidence shows high-risk lesions received priority expedited ER.

Study strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include a large sample of 1646 homogeneous patients with EGC, which is considerably larger than comparable published Korean and Japanese cohorts and enhances statistical power for subgroup comparisons; comprehensive dual-layer endpoint evaluation combining long-term survival and intraoperative pathological curability outcomes; a multi-modal analytical strategy covering categorical grouping, and sensitivity analysis excluding SM2-positive cases to reduce methodological bias; and it represents the first Chinese real-world dataset to assess ER, supplementing existing East-Asian evidence from South Korea and Japan with uniquely short baseline waiting intervals. Additionally, this study newly identifies smoking as a modifiable independent prognostic factor for OS, offering practical lifestyle modification guidance for clinical EGC management.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged: the single-center retrospective design constrains generalizability to other healthcare systems with different endoscopic resource allocation and referral workflows; substantial sample imbalance among patients in waiting-time strata exceeding 90 days prevents definitive conclusions regarding the 180-day upper threshold, which calls for prospective multicenter validation; individuals with time-to-ER latency longer than 180 days were excluded, so our findings cannot be extrapolated to treatment delays beyond six months; furthermore, low mortality and recurrence event rates within this favorable-prognosis EGC cohort yielded inadequate statistical power to capture mild pathological or survival-related hazards induced by prolonged waiting periods.

Clinical implications

Furthermore, hospital surgical volume serves as a critical confounding modifier of the association between treatment delay and oncological outcomes. Prior research has revealed that prolonged treatment delays correlate with poor survival across all malignancies in low-and medium-volume hospitals. In high-volume medical centers, however, the prognostic impact of delayed treatment is tumor-specific: prolonged waiting intervals only worsen clinical outcomes in rectal and breast cancers, while exerting no adverse effects on gastric, colon, pancreatic, and lung cancers [19].

Within the safe 180-day waiting window validated in this cohort, controlled deferral of ER carries no measurable oncological harm for EGC patients. This permissible time interval creates clinical flexibility to complete full preoperative comorbidity evaluation, multidisciplinary tumor board discussion, and cross-hospital referral to high-volume endoscopic centers, which improves procedural curative resection rates without compromising long-term prognosis. Clinicians may reasonably reassure patients requiring short-to-medium term ER delays (within 6 months) of minimal oncological risk, while close monitoring and expedited intervention are recommended for patients with anticipated waiting periods exceeding 180 days pending future prospective validation.

Conclusion

Within the predefined 0–180-day observation window, time-to-ER interval exerted no independent adverse effects on either long-term survival (OS, DFS) or pathological curative outcomes for patients with superficial EGC receiving ER. Consistent neutral associations were validated via binary clinical grouping, continuous 10-day exposure regression, and restricted cubic spline nonlinear modeling; sensitivity analysis excluding high-risk SM2 deep submucosal lesions further confirmed the stability of these findings.

These exploratory real-world results support controlled clinical deferral of endoscopic resection to complete standardized preoperative risk assessment and multidisciplinary consultation within six months. Nevertheless, severe sample imbalance among patients with prolonged waiting periods prevents definitive validation of the 180-day safety threshold. Large-scale, balanced prospective multicenter trials are required before this 6-month deferral window can be incorporated into global clinical practice guidelines for EGC.
