MIMO Detection with Spatial Sigma-Delta ADCs: A Variational Bayesian Approach
Toan-Van Nguyen, Sajjad Nassirpour, Italo Atzeni, Antti Tölli, A. Lee Swindlehurst, Duy H. N. Nguyen

Abstract
The spatial Sigma-Delta ( Σ ​ Δ \Sigma\Delta ) architecture can be leveraged to reduce the quantization noise and enhance the effective resolution of few-bit analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) at certain spatial frequencies of interest. Utilizing the variational Bayesian (VB) inference framework, this paper develops novel data detection algorithms tailored for massive multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) systems with few-bit Σ ​ Δ \Sigma\Delta ADCs and angular channel models, where uplink signals are confined to a specific angular sector. We start by modeling the corresponding Bayesian networks for the 1 st 1^{\rm st} - and 2 nd 2^{\rm nd} -order Σ ​ Δ \Sigma\Delta receivers. Next, we propose an iterative algorithm, referred to as Sigma-Delta variational Bayes (SD-VB), for MIMO detection, offering low-complexity updates through closed-form expressions of the variational densities of the latent variables. Simulation results show that the proposed 2 nd 2^{\rm nd} -order SD-VB algorithm delivers the best symbol error rate (SER) performance while maintaining the same computational complexity as in unquantized systems, matched-filtering VB with conventional quantization, and linear minimum mean-squared error (LMMSE) methods. Moreover, the 1 st 1^{\rm st} - and 2 nd 2^{\rm nd} -order SD-VB algorithms achieve their lowest SER at an antenna separation of one-fourth wavelength for a fixed number of antenna elements. The effects of the steering angle of the Σ ​ Δ \Sigma\Delta architecture, the number of ADC resolution bits, and the number of antennas and users are also extensively analyzed.



I
Introduction

Beyond-5G wireless systems will require substantial bandwidth in both the millimeter (mmWave) and terahertz (THz) bands to deliver high data throughput
[
2
]
. Signals at these frequencies, however, are hindered by low penetration capabilities and high propagation loss, which restrict their practical communication range
[
3
]
.
Massive multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) arrays have been used to compensate for the propagation loss while simultaneously achieving high capacity through spatial multiplexing
[
4
]
. However, exploiting the full benefits of beamforming and multiplexing in massive MIMO can be challenging due to the need for dedicated high-resolution analog-to-digital converters (ADCs)/digital-to-analog converters (DACs) for each antenna element
[
5
,
6
]
. This results in high hardware complexity and increased power consumption, especially with larger bandwidths and sampling rates
[
7
]
.
To address these concerns, the use of low-resolution ADCs with, e.g.,
1
–
3
bits of precision, has emerged as an energy-efficient and low-complexity solution for massive MIMO systems
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

Low-resolution ADCs provide low-complexity and low-power designs for massive MIMO systems, but present challenges due to their non-linear nature
[
11
]
. The impact of low-resolution ADCs on MIMO systems has been explored, including analyses of the resulting capacity limits and achievable rates
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
11
,
15

. These studies demonstrate that low-resolution ADCs degrade system performance metrics such as achievable rate and symbol error rate (SER), especially at medium-to-high signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs), but this negative impact can be mitigated by increasing the number of antennas.
This observation indicates that massive MIMO can be effectively operated with few-bit ADCs. However, attaining a good tradeoff between the system performance and the number of quantization bits requires advanced signal processing algorithms and architectural innovations tailored for the specific characteristics of the quantized signals
[
16
,
10
]
.

Sigma-Delta (
\Sigma\Delta
) modulation is a well-established method for time-domain signal processing that encodes analog signals into low-bit-depth digital signals at a very high sampling rate. By combining oversampling and noise shaping,
\Sigma\Delta
modulation can achieve high resolution despite using low-bit quantizers
[
17
,
18

. In temporal
\Sigma\Delta
, oversampling involves sampling a signal at a rate higher than the Nyquist rate. A negative feedback loop is employed to quantize the difference between the input signal and the quantized output to effectively shape the quantization noise to higher frequencies
[

]
. A low-pass filter is then used to remove the high-frequency quantization noise while preserving the desired low-frequency signals
[
19
]
. The
1^{\rm st}
-order temporal
\Sigma\Delta

[
17
]

In massive MIMO, spatial
\Sigma\Delta
quantization has been used to enhance channel estimation and spectral efficiency
[
18
,
20
]
.
In spatial
\Sigma\Delta
, the difference between the input and the quantized output represents the quantization noise at each antenna, which is fed back and compared with the input to an adjacent antenna. This feedback enables more aggressive noise shaping, where the spatial spectrum of the quantization noise is pushed away from the spatial frequencies of interest
[
21
]
.

Motivations and Contributions

A common approach in the aforementioned works on

The VB method has been proposed as an alternative to effectively address the non-linear quantization operation. System Model

II-A

Channel Model

We consider an uplink MIMO system consisting of
K
single-antenna users and a BS equipped with a uniform linear array (ULA) of
N
antennas. The received signal
\mathbf{x}
at the BS before quantization can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{Hs}+\mathbf{n},

(1)

where
\mathbf{H}=[\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{N}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times K}
is the uplink channel matrix, with
\mathbf{h}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times 1}
denoting the channel vector from the
K
users to the
i
-th antenna at the BS,

\mathbf{s}=[s_{1},\ldots,s_{K}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times 1}
is the uplink data symbol vector, and
\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},N_{0}\mathbf{I}_{N})
represents additive Gaussian noise at the BS with variance
N_{0}
. We assume that the channel
\mathbf{H}
is known at the BS. The symbol
s_{k}
transmitted from user-
k
is drawn from a complex-valued discrete constellation
\mathcal{S}
, e.g., quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM) or phase-shift keying (PSK), with
\mathbb{E}[s_{k}]=0
and normalized such that
\mathbb{E}[|s_{k}|^{2}]=1
. The prior distribution of

can be expressed as

\displaystyle p(s_{k})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{S}}p_{a}\delta(s_{k}-a),

(2)

where
p_{a}
is the known prior probability of the constellation point
a
and
\delta(s_{k}-a)
indicates the point mass function at
a
.

We assume a geometric channel model typical of mmWave communications systems in which the channel for each user is composed of a linear combination of
L
propagation paths
[
37
,
3
]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{k}}{L}}\mathbf{A}_{k}\mathbf{g}_{k},

(3)

where
\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{k}
is the
k
th column of
\mathbf{H}
and the uplink channel from user-
k
, the columns of
\mathbf{A}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times L}
represent the array response for
L
propagation paths,
\mathbf{g}_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{L})
represents the small-scale fading, and
\beta_{k}
models the geometric attenuation and slow fading. We assume that
\mathbf{A}_{k}
is a full rank matrix whose
\ell
-th column is the array steering vector corresponding to the angle of arrival (AoA)
\theta_{k\ell}
of the
\ell
-th path, as given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}(\theta_{k\ell})=\big[1,\mathrm{e}^{-j2\pi\frac{d}{\lambda}\sin\theta_{k\ell}},\ldots,\mathrm{e}^{-j(N-1)2\pi\frac{d}{\lambda}\sin\theta_{k\ell}}\big]^{T},

(4)

where

is the inter-antenna spacing of the ULA and
\lambda
denotes the wavelength. For simplicity, we denote
\omega_{{k\ell}}=2\pi\frac{d}{\lambda}\sin\theta_{k\ell}
as the spatial frequency of the
\ell
-th path from user-

. We assume that the AoAs for all users are situated within a specific angular sector

This constraint frequently arises in practice because cell sectoring limits service to users within a specific angular sector of the BS coverage. Additionally, the high frequency and directional nature of mmWave signals produce narrow beam patterns and substantial attenuation of distant reflections, making nearby reflections of users more dominant and resulting in narrower angular spreads for users.

The received analog signals are then quantized using few-bit ADCs.
The mathematical models for both conventional and
\Sigma\Delta
quantizers in these few-bit ADCs will be detailed in the following subsection.

IV-A

Background on VB Inference

We denote the set of all observed variables as
\mathbf{y}
and the set of
V
latent variables as
\mathbf{x}
. To find a Bayes estimate of
\mathbf{x}
, one would need to determine the posterior distribution
p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
, which is often computationally intractable. To overcome this challenge, the VB method aims to find a distribution
q(\mathbf{x})
characterized by variational parameters within a predefined family
\mathcal{Q}
of densities, such that
q(\mathbf{x})
closely approximates
p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
. To this end, VB defines an optimization problem leveraging the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence from
q(\mathbf{x})
to
p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
:

\displaystyle q^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{q(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{Q}}\text{KL}\big(q(\mathbf{x})\|p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\big),

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{q(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{Q}}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x})}\big[\ln q(\mathbf{x})\big]-\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x})}\big[\ln p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\big]\big\},

where
q^{\star}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the optimal variational distribution.
The VB method assumes that
q(\mathbf{x})
is drawn from the mean-field variational family
[
39
]
, such that

q(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{V}q_{i}(x_{i}),

(15)

where the latent variables are mutually independent, with each being governed by a distinct factor in the variational density. The optimal value of
q_{i}(x_{i})
is obtained as
[
39
, Chapter 10]

\displaystyle q_{i}^{\star}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\propto\mathrm{exp}\left\{\big\langle{\ln p(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\big\rangle}\right\},

(16)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
is the expectation w.r.t. all latent variables except
x_{i}
, utilizing the currently fixed variational density
q_{-i}(\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\prod_{j=1,j\neq i}^{V}q_{j}(x_{j})
. To optimize (
14
), we use the Coordinate Ascent Variational Inference algorithm, which is an iterative method that ensures convergence to at least a locally optimal solution
[
40
]
.

\Sigma\Delta

Considering the input-output relationship of the

-order
\Sigma\Delta

and
p(y_{i}|r_{i})=\mathbbm{1}(r_{i}\in[y_{i}^{\mathrm{low}},y_{i}^{\mathrm{up}}])
, where

is the indicator function which equals one if the argument holds, or zero otherwise.
The optimal MAP detector
\hat{\bf s}_{\tt MAP}=\arg\max_{{\bf s}\in\mathcal{S}^{K}}p(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s};\mathbf{H},N_{0})
with spatial

\displaystyle\hat{\bf s}_{\tt MAP}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{{\bf s}\in\mathcal{S}^{K}}\int p(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s};\mathbf{H},N_{0})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}

(17)

\displaystyle=\!\arg\!\max_{{\bf s}\in\mathcal{S}^{K}}\,\!p(\mathbf{s})\!\int\!\prod_{i=1}^{N}\!p(y_{i}|r_{i})p(r_{i}|r_{i-1},y_{i-1},\mathbf{s};\mathbf{H},N_{0})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.

This integral is intractable to evaluate in closed-form due to the high dimensional integration and the intricate correlation between
\mathbf{r}
and

denotes the precision that is floated as an unknown random variable and the arrows indicate the conditional probability between variables.
The main goal is to infer the distribution of data
\mathbf{s}
given the observation
\mathbf{y}
. To accomplish this,
we employ the mean-field variational distribution
q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},\gamma)
, such that

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},\gamma|\mathbf{y};\mathbf{H})\approx q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},\gamma)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}q(s_{k})\prod_{i=1}^{N}q(r_{i})q(\gamma).

(18)

According to (
16
), to obtain the optimal solution of the variational densities in (
18
), we need the joint distribution
p(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H})
which can be factorized as

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle=p(\mathbf{s})p(\gamma)

\displaystyle\;\;\times\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(y_{i}|r_{i})p(r_{i}|r_{i-1},y_{i-1},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H}),

(19)

where

\displaystyle p(r_{i}|r_{i-1},y_{i-1},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{CN}\big(r_{i};\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{s}+\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(r_{i-1}-y_{i-1}),\gamma^{-1}\big).

(20)

To this end, we present the update process for each latent variable through the derived closed-form expression of each variational density.

1) Updating
r_{i}
:
For
i=1,\ldots,N-1
, the variational distribution
q(r_{i})
can be obtained by taking the expectation of the conditional in (
IV-B
) w.r.t.
q(\mathbf{s},\gamma)
as

\displaystyle q(r_{i})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\Big\{\big\langle\ln p(y_{i}|r_{i})+\ln p(r_{i+1}|r_{i},y_{i},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle\qquad+\ln p(r_{i}|r_{i-1},y_{i-1},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H})\big\rangle\Big\}

\displaystyle\propto p(y_{i}|r_{i})\exp\!\Big\{\!-\!\big\langle\gamma|r_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{s}-\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(r_{i-1}-y_{i-1})|^{2}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0001pt\!\!-\!\big\langle\gamma|r_{i+1}-\mathbf{h}_{i+1}^{T}\mathbf{s}-\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(r_{i}-y_{i})|^{2}\big\rangle\Big\}

\displaystyle\propto p(y_{i}|r_{i})\exp\big\{-\langle\gamma\rangle\big(|r_{i}-a_{i}|^{2}+|r_{i}-b_{i}|^{2}\big)\big\}

\displaystyle\propto\mathbbm{1}(r_{i}\in[y_{i}^{\mathrm{low}},y_{i}^{\mathrm{up}}])\,\mathcal{CN}\big(r_{i};(a_{i}+b_{i})/2,1/(2\langle\gamma\rangle)\big),

(21)

where
a_{i}\triangleq\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\langle\mathbf{s}\rangle+\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(\langle r_{i-1}\rangle-y_{i-1})
and
b_{i}\triangleq y_{i}+\mathrm{e}^{j\phi}\langle r_{i+1}\rangle-\mathrm{e}^{j\phi}\mathbf{h}_{i+1}^{T}\langle\mathbf{s}\rangle
.

Defining
v_{i}\triangleq(a_{i}+b_{i})/2
, we see that the variational distribution
q(r_{i})
in (
IV-B
)
is a truncated complex Gaussian distribution obtained by pruning the distribution
\mathcal{CN}\big(v_{i},1/(2\langle\gamma\rangle)\big)
onto the interval
[y_{i}^{\mathrm{low}},y_{i}^{\mathrm{up}}]
.
In Appendix
A
we show that the mean and variance of the resulting distribution are given by

{\langle r_{i}\rangle=\mathsf{F}_{r}(v_{i},2\langle\gamma\rangle,y_{i}^{\mathrm{low}},y_{i}^{\mathrm{up}})}
and
{\tau_{r_{i}}={\sf G}_{r}(v_{i},2\langle\gamma\rangle,y_{i}^{\mathrm{low}},y_{i}^{\mathrm{up}})}
, respectively.

For
i=N
, while the factor
p(r_{N+1}|r_{N},y_{N},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H})
does not exist, the variational distribution
q(r_{N})
can be computed similarly to (
IV-B
)
and is given by

\displaystyle q(r_{N})

\displaystyle\propto\mathbbm{1}(r_{N}\in[y_{N}^{\mathrm{low}},y_{N}^{\mathrm{up}}])\,\mathcal{CN}\big(r_{N};a_{N},1/\langle\gamma\rangle\big).

(22)

2) Updating
s_{k}
:
The variational distribution
q(s_{k})
is obtained by taking the expectation of the conditional in (
IV-B
) w.r.t.
q(\mathbf{r},\gamma)
:

\displaystyle q(s_{k})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg\{\!\bigg\langle\ln p(s_{k})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln p(r_{i}|r_{i-1},y_{i-1},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H})\bigg\rangle\!\bigg\}

\displaystyle\propto p(s_{k})\exp\bigg\{\!-\langle\gamma\rangle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big\langle|{r_{i}}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle\hskip 90.00014pt-\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}({r_{i-1}}-y_{i-1})|^{2}\big\rangle\bigg\}.\!

(23)

Expanding
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{s}={h}_{i,k}s_{k}+\sum_{j\neq k}^{K}{h}_{i,j}s_{j}
leads to

\displaystyle q(s_{k})

\displaystyle\propto p(s_{k})\exp\bigg\{-\langle\gamma\rangle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg|\langle r_{i}\rangle-\sum_{j\neq k}^{K}{h}_{i,j}\langle s_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0001pt-\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(\langle r_{i-1}\rangle-y_{i-1})-h_{i,k}s_{k}\bigg|^{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\propto p(s_{k})\exp\bigg\{-\langle\gamma\rangle\sum_{i=1}^{N}|z_{i,k}-{h}_{i,k}s_{k}|^{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\propto p(s_{k})\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{CN}\big(z_{i,k};{h}_{i,k}s_{k},\langle\gamma\rangle^{-1}\big),

(24)

where

\displaystyle z_{i,k}

\displaystyle=\!\langle r_{i}\rangle-\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(\langle r_{i-1}\rangle-y_{i-1})-\sum_{j\neq i}^{K}{h}_{i,j}\langle s_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\!\langle r_{i}\rangle-\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(\langle r_{i-1}\rangle-y_{i-1})-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\langle\mathbf{s}\rangle+h_{i,k}\langle s_{k}\rangle,

(25)

using the current estimate
\langle s_{k}\rangle
for all
k=1,\ldots,K
.
Interestingly, the variational distribution
q(s_{k})
is equivalent to the posterior distribution
p(s_{k}|\mathbf{z}_{k},\langle\gamma\rangle;\mathbf{h}_{k})
of
s_{k}
in a single-input multiple-output (SIMO) system:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{k}=\mathbf{h}_{k}s_{k}+\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\langle\gamma\rangle^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{N})

(26)

where
\mathbf{z}_{k}=[z_{1,k},\ldots,z_{N,k}]^{T}
. The variational mean and variance of
s_{k}
can be expressed as
\langle s_{k}\rangle={\sf F}_{s}(\mathbf{z}_{k},\mathbf{h}_{k},\langle\gamma\rangle)
and
\tau_{s_{k}}={\sf G}_{s}(\mathbf{z}_{k},\mathbf{h}_{k},\langle\gamma\rangle)
, respectively, as shown in Appendix
B
.

3) Updating
\gamma
:

We assume a conjugate prior Gamma distribution
\rm Gamma(\alpha,\beta)
for the precision
\gamma
, for given shape and rate parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
, respectively.
The variational distribution
q(\gamma)
is obtained by taking the expectation of the conditional distribution in (
IV-B
) w.r.t.
q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r})
:

\displaystyle q(\gamma)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg\{\bigg\langle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln p(r_{i}|r_{i-1},y_{i-1},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H})+\ln p(\gamma)\bigg\rangle\bigg\}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg\{-\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big\langle|r_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{s}-\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(r_{i-1}-y_{i-1})|^{2}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt+N\ln\gamma+(\alpha-1)\ln\gamma-\beta\gamma\bigg\}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg\{-\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\big\langle|r_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{s}|^{2}\big\rangle+\big\langle|r_{i-1}-y_{i-1}|^{2}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt-2\,\Re\big\{\big\langle(r_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{s})^{*}(r_{i-1}-y_{i-1})\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}\big\rangle\big\}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+(N+\alpha-1)\ln\gamma-\beta\gamma\bigg\}.

(27)

Using the expansion

\big\langle|r_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{s}|^{2}\big\rangle=|\langle r_{i}\rangle-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\langle\mathbf{s}\rangle|^{2}+\tau_{r_{i}}+\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathbf{h}_{i}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle q(\gamma)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg\{\!\!-\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\Big[|\langle r_{i}\rangle-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\langle\mathbf{s}\rangle-(\langle r_{i-1}\rangle-y_{i-1})\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\tau_{r_{i}}\!+\mathbf{h}_{i}^{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathbf{h}_{i}+\tau_{r_{i-1}}\Big]\!+(N+\alpha-1)\ln\gamma-\beta\gamma\bigg\}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg\{-\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[|u_{i}|^{2}+\tau_{r_{i}}+\mathbf{h}_{i}^{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathbf{h}_{i}+\tau_{r_{i-1}}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+(N+\alpha-1)\ln\gamma-\beta\gamma\bigg\}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\Big\{-\gamma\Big[\beta+\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}+2\mathrm{Tr}\{\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{r}}\}-\tau_{r_{N}}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\mathbf{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathbf{H}^{H}\big\}\Big]+(N+\alpha-1)\ln\gamma\Big\},

(28)

where
u_{i}\triangleq\langle r_{i}\rangle-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\langle\mathbf{s}\rangle-\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(\langle r_{i-1}\rangle-y_{i-1})
,
\mathbf{u}=[u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}]^{T}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{r}}={\rm diag}(\tau_{r_{1}},\ldots,\tau_{r_{N}})
is the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{r}
.
Here,
u_{i}
denotes the residual term at receive antenna
i
, which would equals the noise term
n_{i}
if
r_{i}
,
r_{i-1}
, and
\mathbf{s}
were perfectly known.

The variational distribution
q(\gamma)
is Gamma with mean

\displaystyle\langle\gamma\rangle=\frac{N+\alpha}{\beta+\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}+2\mathrm{Tr}\{\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{r}}\}-\tau_{r_{N}}+\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\mathbf{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathbf{H}^{H}\big\}}.

(29)

1^{\rm st}

\Sigma\Delta

\hat{\gamma}
in the variational distributions of (

In Algorithm

, we use
\hat{r}_{i}^{t}
,
\hat{s}^{t}_{k}
, and
\hat{\gamma}^{t}
to replace

a_{N}=\hat{r}_{N}^{t}-u_{N},&\textrm{ for }i=N,\end{array}\right.

in step 7 of Algorithm

.

Remark 2:
The residual noise term
\mathbf{u}
, initialized as
\mathbf{u}=\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{1}-\mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{1}

q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},\gamma)

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},\gamma|\mathbf{y};\mathbf{H})\approx q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},\gamma)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}q(s_{k})\prod_{i=1}^{N}q(r_{i})q(\gamma).

1) Updating
r_{i}
:

) w.r.t.

as

\displaystyle\!q(r_{i})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\big\{\big\langle\ln p(r_{i}|r_{i-1},y_{i-1},r_{i-2},y_{i-2},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle\qquad+\ln p(y_{i}|r_{i})+\ln p(r_{i+1}|r_{i},y_{i},r_{i-1},y_{i-1},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle\qquad+\ln p(r_{i+2}|r_{i+1},y_{i+1},r_{i},y_{i},\mathbf{s},\gamma;\mathbf{H})\big\rangle\big\},

(35)

which is expanded as in (
V-A
) on the next page,

\displaystyle q(r_{i})

\displaystyle\propto p(y_{i}|r_{i})\exp\big\{-\big\langle\gamma|r_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{s}-2\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(r_{i-1}-y_{i-1})+\mathrm{e}^{-j2\phi}(r_{i-2}-y_{i-2})|^{2}\big\rangle-\big\langle\gamma|r_{i+1}-\mathbf{h}_{i+1}^{T}\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle\quad-2\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(r_{i}-y_{i})+\mathrm{e}^{-j2\phi}(r_{i-1}-y_{i-1})|^{2}\big\rangle-\big\langle\gamma|r_{i+2}-\mathbf{h}_{i+2}^{T}\mathbf{s}-2\mathrm{e}^{-j\phi}(r_{i+1}-y_{i+1})+\mathrm{e}^{-j2\phi}(r_{i}-y_{i})|^{2}\big\rangle\big\}

\displaystyle f_{i}\triangleq y_{i}-\mathrm{e}^{j2\phi}\langle r_{i+2}\rangle+\mathrm{e}^{j2\phi}\mathbf{h}_{i+2}^{T}\langle\mathbf{s}\rangle+2\mathrm{e}^{j\phi}(\langle r_{i+1}\rangle-y_{i+1}).

we have

. We then denote variable

VII
Conclusions

In this paper, we have developed two MIMO detection algorithms based on the VB inference framework in massive MIMO systems with few-bit
\Sigma\Delta
ADCs. We first modeled the Bayesian networks for the
1^{\rm st}
- and
2^{\rm nd}
-order
\Sigma\Delta
receivers, based on which the variational distributions of the latent variables were obtained in closed form. We then proposed two iterative algorithms for MIMO detection with efficient updates and low-complexity implementation.
Simulation results showed that the proposed
1^{\rm st}
- and
2^{\rm nd}
-order SD-VB algorithms achieve their best data detection performance when the users are confined to angular sectors less than
80^{\circ}
wide and with antenna spacings on the order of
1/4
to
1/6
a wavelength. Under these conditions, a
2^{\rm nd}
-order SD-VB approach with
3
-bit ADCs can outperform the MF-VB algorithm implemented with infinite resolution quantization, since SD-VB directly exploits the users’ spatial correlation.
The simulation results also suggest using
1^{\rm st}
-order SD-VB rather than the
2^{\rm nd}
-order SD-VB implementation with
1
-bit quantization due to potential ADC overloading and unstable performance.

Define

\beta=\sqrt{2\gamma}(b-\mu)
. For an arbitrary complex random variable

[
10

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{r}(\mu,\gamma,a,b)=\mu-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}\frac{f(\beta)-f(\alpha)}{F(\beta)-F(\alpha)},

(49)

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}_{r}(\mu,\gamma,a,b)\!=\!\frac{1}{2{\gamma}}\bigg[1\!-\!\frac{\beta f(\beta)\!-\!\alpha f(\alpha)}{F(\beta)\!-F(\alpha)}\!-\!\bigg(\!\frac{f(\beta)-f(\alpha)}{F(\beta)-F(\alpha)}\!\bigg)^{2}\bigg],

and
\mathsf{G}_{r}(\mu,\gamma,a,b)

Denoting

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{r}(\mu,\gamma,a,b)=\mu+\frac{\mathrm{sign}(y)}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}\frac{f(\zeta)}{F(\zeta)},

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}_{r}(\mu,\gamma,a,b)=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\Bigg[1-\frac{\zeta f(\zeta)}{F(\zeta)}-\bigg(\frac{f(\zeta)}{F(\zeta)}\bigg)^{2}\Bigg].

(54)
