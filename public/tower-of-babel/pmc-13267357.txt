Psychometric properties of the Italian version of the State Fear of Missing Out Inventory in a sample of Italian young adults
Authors: Rocco Servidio, Paolo Soraci, Alex J Holte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the prevalence of social media use among Italian students, research on the state “Fear of Missing Out” (FoMO) remains limited due to the lack of reliable measures. Therefore, this study addresses the need for a reliable, culturally adapted instrument to assess this construct in the Italian context. Thus, the current study aimed to adapt the 8-item State Fear of Missing Out Inventory (SFoMOI) for use in the Italian context. Two studies were conducted to achieve this. In Study 1, an Exploratory Factor Analysis was performed on the scale items using a sample of 195 young adults (Mage = 21.5 years, SD = 3.34), who completed an online survey. The results supported one single factor solution, which was retained for further validation. In Study 2, a Confirmatory Factor Analysis was run to verify whether the results of Study 1 could be replicated with a different sample of 377 Italian young adults (Mage = 21.7 years, SD = 3.55). The participants in Study 2 completed a self-report questionnaire that included the FoMO scale, the Boredom Proneness Scale-Short Form, the Smartphone Application-Based Addiction Scale, and the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale to assess convergent validity. Age - and gender-invariance (configural, metric, and scalar) were examined. The Italian version of the SFoMOI scale was found psychometrically equivalent to the original version. It demonstrated good psychometric properties, χ2 (18, N = 377) = 49.32, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.981, TLI = 0.971, RMSEA = 0.068, 90% CI [0.049, 0.088], SRMR = 0.031, as well as discriminant (α = 0.92; ω = 0.92) and convergent validity. Ultimately, this work offered a measure which can be used as a diagnostic tool in clinical and educational settings to help detect increases in FoMO, which could make individuals more at risk for problematic online use and/or distress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04054-y.Keywords: Boredom proneness, Social media, Smartphone, State-FoMO, Trait-FoMO, Self-determination

Introduction

Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) is described by Przybylski as “a pervasive apprehension that others might be having rewarding experiences from which one is absent” ([1], p. 1841). Although FoMO has often been studied as a single construct, recent evidence suggests it may consist of multiple latent dimensions. For example, FoMO has been measured both as a behavioural trait [1] and as a state [2], with the key distinction that the trait reflects how an individual generally feels, and the state reflects how an individual feels at the current moment. Based on this understanding, FoMO exists as both a trait and a state; numerous studies have found novel findings. For example, Balta et al. [3] found that state FoMO is both directly and indirectly associated with phubbing and the severity of problematic social media use (PSMU). Li et al. [4] reported that both trait and state FoMO are positively linked to problematic smartphone use (PSU). Moreover, a recent study by Montag et al. [5] found that state FoMO is more strongly related to PSMU than trait FoMO. A recent study conducted by Hussain et al. [6] underlines that trait and state FoMO were significantly associated with PSMU severity. Additionally, state (but not trait) FoMO mediated the association between PSMU and PSU severity. Collectively, these findings suggest that FoMO significantly influences smartphone and social networking site use, potentially leading to problematic use in some individuals [5, 7].

Although these findings have been identified, two important considerations need to be acknowledged. First, the face validity of the most commonly used state measure of FoMO [8] has been critiqued for reflecting trait online-FoMO rather than the extent to which an individual experiences FoMO at the current moment [2, 9]. Second, it is crucial to note that the currently validated version of the State Fear of Missing Out Inventory [2] is available only in English. Therefore, current research on state FoMO is limited to English-speaking populations, thereby constraining cross-cultural exploration.

Moreover, this differentiation between trait and state FoMO is critical for several reasons. Firstly, it highlights the need for targeted interventions. Individuals with high trait FoMO might benefit more from cognitive-behavioural strategies aimed at altering their underlying fear of missing out. In contrast, those experiencing high state FoMO might require immediate coping mechanisms to manage their anxiety in specific situations, such as turning off notifications during certain hours or practising mindfulness [10, 11]. Secondly, understanding whether FoMO is a persistent personality trait or a transient state can influence how researchers design studies and interpret results. For example, interventions aimed at reducing state FoMO might focus on situational factors, whereas those targeting trait FoMO might address deeper psychological patterns. Furthermore, this distinction has significant implications for educational and public health policies. Educators and policymakers could design and develop programs that help young people build resilience against both forms of FoMO, promoting healthier digital habits and reducing the risk of long-term adverse outcomes associated with excessive technology use. Additionally, distinguishing between these two dimensions of FoMO enables mental health professionals to design more personalised and effective interventions tailored to individuals’ specific needs [2, 12].

Despite increasing scholarly attention to FoMO, most existing measures assess it as a stable personality trait, offering limited insight into its situational or fluctuating nature. Furthermore, in Italy, we do not have a specific measure to examine state FoMO. As a result, studies examining the effects of state FoMO remain largely unexplored. To address this limitation, Holte [2] developed the State Fear of Missing Out Inventory (SFoMOI), a novel instrument designed to capture the momentary experience of FoMO in specific contexts. However, no study has yet validated the SFoMOI in languages other than English, and its psychometric properties remain unexplored in cross-cultural settings. To fill these gaps, the present study aims to assess the psychometric validity of the SFoMOI in the Italian context. First, we outline the theoretical framework of FoMO. After presenting the theoretical framework, we examine age- and gender-related differences in FoMO experiences, and finally, we present the main objectives of the current study.

Theoretical background

Przybylski et al. [1] is one of the earliest and most cited research articles on FoMO worldwide. In their paper, they used the theoretical perspective of Self-Determination Theory [13] to describe why individuals experience FoMO. Namely, they argue that FoMO occurs when individuals have deficits across the psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Events provide individuals the opportunity to feel like they belong and gain mastery of these concepts. Thus, it is reasonable that an individual who scores high in these fundamental needs is likely not concerned about events they are absent from when engaged in an enjoyable experience and has a sense of mastery over these psychological needs, and thus reports having low FoMO severity.

On a similar note, according to Przybylski et al. [1], FoMO is a type of social anxiety that alerts individuals that they have low levels of these psychological needs. In turn, an individual with FoMO will want to alleviate the concern that they may be missing out on an opportunity to fulfil these psychological needs. One common way individuals alleviate their FoMO concerns is by using social networking sites (SNSs), such as Facebook and Instagram, among others. For example, research has revealed that FoMO is linked with problematic social media use [3, 5, 6, 12] and problematic smartphone use [14–16]. One theoretical account that explains how individuals cope with their negative feelings about FoMO is the Compensatory Internet Use Theory [17]. This theory suggests that individuals overuse technology as a way to manage various forms of negative affect, ranging from anxiety, depression, and boredom. In other words, technology use serves as a temporary distraction from one’s negative affect [18]. Since FoMO is a specific type of anxiety, it is reasonable that it may drive SNS and smartphone use. Namely, the question of whether an individual is truly missing out on a rewarding experience can be answered by using one’s SNS platforms or smartphone [19], as individuals frequently post their enjoyable experiences online.

Influence of age and gender on FoMO

Provided FoMO is linked with both problematic social media [12] and smartphone use [15], and young adults are considered to be the most active users of both entities [20, 21]; therefore, it is viable that one potential individual difference of FoMO could be age. When evaluated using bivariate correlations, many scholars have found that FoMO is inversely related to age [2, 22, 23]. However, when comparing age cohorts, Barry and Wong [24] found no statistical difference in FoMO. Thus, the current results are slightly inconclusive, and further research is needed to determine if age plays a significant role in FoMO.

Similarly, FoMO is experienced differently because gender differences are also inconclusive. While many have found no gender differences in FoMO [2, 12, 22, 24, 25], others have found that men score higher on FoMO than women [23, 26]. Furthermore, research has found that different psychological constructs predict FoMO, depending on the sample’s gender. For example, among women participants in Davis et al. [27], perceived social support was found to predict FoMO, although this relationship was not observed among men participants. This suggests that when studying FoMO, it is crucial to consider the influence of gender on the results.

Aim of the study

The present study aims to validate the Italian version of the State Fear of Missing Out Inventory (SFoMOI) by examining its factorial structure, internal consistency, convergent validity, and measurement invariance across gender and age. Given the inconclusive findings from previous studies, we also examined age- and gender-related differences in FoMO. Based on the existing literature, we hypothesised that (H1) state FoMO would be positively associated with FoMO, boredom proneness, problematic smartphone use, and PSMU, and (H2) would be negatively associated with age. Moreover, given inconsistent findings regarding gender differences in FoMO, particularly in state FoMO, these differences were examined exploratorily, without formulating a directional hypothesis.

Study 1

The objective of Study 1 was to examine the factorial structure and the reliability of the State Fear of Missing Out Inventory (SFoMOI).

Method

Participants and sampling

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Italy from February 2024 to June 2024 at the University of Calabria. In the first wave, 195 young adults (54% women; M = 21.53, SD = 3.34; age range: 18–35 years) participated in the study. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling. Inclusion criteria were: (a) aged between 18 and 35 years, (b) active use of at least one social media application, and (c) fluency in Italian. No exclusion criteria were applied. The study did not include any subgrouping or clinical screening (e.g., ADHD diagnosis). The sample predominantly comprised students from scientific (53.60%), social (29.20%), economic (9.90%), humanistic (6.80%), and medical (0.50%) programs. Regarding social media engagement, 57.4% of the participants spent less than one hour per day, 21.53% spent about one hour, 15.89% spent two hours per day, and 5.12% spent between three and six hours per day.

Procedure

Participants were recruited in person on the university campus. Data collection sessions were held before scheduled lectures to minimise academic disruption. At the start of each session, a researcher explained the purpose of the study, emphasising anonymity, voluntariness, and the absence of financial or academic incentives. Participants were sent a QR code and a short link to the online questionnaire hosted on the LimeSurvey platform. After providing informed consent digitally, participants completed the survey via their own mobile devices. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Calabria (Protocol No. 0288248/2024) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measures

Demographic information

Participants provided demographic data, including age, gender, degree program, and estimated daily time spent on social media. Additionally, participants were instructed to retrieve their device-reported average time spent on social media apps using the built-in screen time features on iOS and Android devices.

State Fear of Missing Out Inventory (SFoMOI)

The State FoMO Inventory was used to assess situational fear of missing out. The original scale, developed by Holte [2], comprises eight items rated on a six-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (6). The Italian version was generated in accordance with the International Test Commission guidelines [28] and using a forward–backwards translation procedure. A bilingual researcher not involved in the study translated the scale into Italian, and a second researcher independently back-translated it into English. The research team compared the back-translated version with the original to ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence. No subscales were specified. Higher scores reflect greater state-level fear of missing out. Prior to the final dissemination of the Italian version of the SFoMOI-I scale, the translation was pilot tested with a small sample of university students (n = 10). with participants reporting no difficulties in understanding or responding to the items. Accordingly, the pilot study was used exclusively to evaluate the clarity and understanding of the translated items, while reliability and other psychometric properties were examined in the main study with a larger sample.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (v. 2024.04.2) with the lavaan [29] and psych [30] packages. Before conducting the statistical analyses, the dataset was screened for missing or inconsistent values; no missing data were detected. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables. Normality assumptions were checked using Mardia’s multivariate normality test, which indicated significant deviations (p < 0.001). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated among the eight SFoMOI-I items. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed using principal axis factoring with Promax rotation. Factor retention and item loadings were interpreted in accordance with established guidelines [31]. Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (α) with values ≥ 0.70 considered indicative of acceptable internal consistency [32] and McDonald’s omega (ω) coefficient.

Table 3 presents the EFA results, which suggest that the single-factorial structure identified in the Italian context aligns with the original SFoMOI scale. Theoretical considerations and empirical evidence can justify this result. Theoretically, FoMO has been conceptualised as a unitary construct [1, 12]. Empirically, the parallel analysis indicated that only one factor had an eigenvalue exceeding the criterion derived from random data (e.g., the 95th percentile of simulated eigenvalues). This finding supports the appropriateness of a single-factor model. Additionally, theoretical considerations and only a prior study that used this scale consistently point to a unidimensional structure, which aligns with our findings. Moreover, no previous studies have tried to adapt this scale to another cultural context. This limitation underscores the need to verify its factorial structure, particularly given its adaptation into a different cultural context. Therefore, given the growing interest in cross-cultural research, future studies could investigate whether the SFoMOI-I maintains its factorial structure across various populations, increasing its global applicability. This would further establish the generalizability of the SFoMOI-I scale.

Study 2

The objective of Study 2 was to conduct a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to validate the results of the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) carried out in Study 1. Additionally, an exploration of convergent validity was performed by examining relationships with other relevant variables.

Method

Participants and sampling

The sample of the second study included 377 young adult Italian university students (55.5% were women) aged 18–35 years (M = 21.84, SD = 3.74). Participants were recruited during the second wave of the data collection using the same procedure described in Study 1. Similarly, we used the identical ethical standards as described in Study 1. Regarding participants’ educational profiles, 54.6% attended scientific degree courses, 34% attended economic and social degree courses, 10% attended humanistic degree courses, and 1.4% pursued other medical degrees. Moreover, 95% of the participants had at least three or four social media accounts. Regarding social media engagement, 57.5% of the participants spent less than one hour per day, 14.6% spent about one hour per day, 11.1% spent two hours per day, and 3.7% spent between three and seven hours per day.

Measures

State Fear of Missing Out Inventory-Italian (SFoMOI-I)

The SFoMOI-I is an 8-item self-report instrument assessing the degree of fear of missing out on a rewarding experience [2]. An example of an item is “I fear I am absent from a special moment”. Participants are asked to respond on a six-point Likert-type scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (6). The total score ranges from 8 to 48, with higher scores indicating a greater risk of experiencing state fear of missing out. In this study, the measure showed excellent internal consistency (𝛼 = 0.93; McDonald’s omega (ω) = 0.95).

Fear of Missing Out Scale (FoMOS)

The FoMOS is a self-report measure comprising 10 items that assesses the fear of missing out on opportunities to participate in online social media engagement (1; Italian adaptation: [35]). An example of an item was “I worry when I find out that my friends are having fun without me”. Participants had to indicate how true each statement was on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from not at all true for me (1) to extremely true for me (5). The total score ranges from 5 to 50, with higher scores indicating a greater risk of experiencing a fear of missing out. In this study, the reliability was excellent (α = 0.89; McDonald’s omega (ω) = 0.91).

Boredom Proneness Scale-Short Form (BPS-SF)

The BPS-SF scale was used to assess the individual’s disposition to experience boredom [36]. This questionnaire consists of 8 items and utilises a seven-point Likert-type scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). An example of an item is “It takes me more stimulation to get started than most people”. The total score ranges from 8 to 56, with higher scores indicating a greater risk of experiencing boredom proneness. The BPS-SF exhibits good internal consistency and construct validity, similar to the original BPS score [36]. Reliability analyses of the Italian BPS indicated acceptable internal consistency [37, 38]. In this study, the internal consistence of the scale was perfect (𝛼 = 0.89; McDonald’s omega (ω) = 0.92), and a one-factor solution was confirmed, robust χ2 (17, N = 377) = 36.67, p = 0.004, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.05, 90% CI [0.03, 0.08], SRMR = 0.03.

Smartphone Application-Based Addiction Scale (SABAS)

The SABAS is a brief six-item instrument designed to evaluate the risk of addiction to smartphone applications and was used in the present study as an indicator of problematic smartphone use (PSU; [39]; Italian adaptation: [40]). An example of an item is “If I cannot use or access my smartphone when I feel like it, I feel sad, moody, or irritable”. Respondents rate their agreement with these statements on a six-point Likert-type scale, ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (6). The total score ranges from 6 to 36, with higher scores indicating a higher risk of problematic smartphone use. For this study, the reliability of the scale was good (𝛼 = 0.82; McDonald’s omega (ω) = 0.89).

Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS)

Problematic social media use (PSMU) was measured using six items from the BSMAS scale ([41]; Italian adaptation: [42]). An example of an item is “You use social media in order to forget about personal problems”. The items were rated on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from never (1) to very often (5). The total score ranges from 6 to 30, and higher scores indicate a higher risk of problematic social media use. The reliability of the scale in this study was good (α = 0.79; McDonald’s omega (ω) = 0.87).

Statistical analyses

The data analysis process included the following steps. We first computed descriptive statistics of the main variables of the study. Missing data were limited, with frequencies ranging from 0 to 3.18%, which is below the advised threshold (< 5%). Based on Little’s MCAR test (χ2 = 53.34, df = 38, p = 0.050), suggesting data were missing completely at random. The Mardia’s skewness test for the collected data was 395.14, p < 0.001, and the Mardia’s kurtosis was 12.04, p < 0.001, indicating that the data were not multivariate normally distributed [43]. Then, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) with maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR) was performed. According to Kline [33], standard indexes were used to ascertain the goodness of fit of the tested models, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI ≥ 0.95), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI ≥ 0.95), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA ≤ 0.08), and the Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR ≤ 0.08).

Convergent validity was assessed through two steps. First, a bivariate Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the associations between the SFoMOI-I and related constructs. In addition, convergent validity was assessed using average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) estimates from the CFA. We also used independent-samples t-tests to examine differences in the SFoMOI-I scale by gender (coded as 1 = women and 2 = men) and a one-way ANOVA to test differences among age groups. Based on a previous study, we divided the participants into three age groups: 18–23, 24–29, and 30–35 [44].

Finally, to examine measurement invariance across gender (women vs. men) and the three age groups, multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA) was performed using the maximum likelihood estimator (ML). Following Kline’s [33] guidelines, a series of three hierarchically nested models with progressively stricter constraints was tested: (1) configural invariance, the baseline model, where the factor structure is identical across groups but all parameters are freely estimated; (2) metric invariance, which builds on the configural model by constraining unstandardized factor loadings to be equal across groups; and (3) scalar invariance, which further constrains item intercepts to equality, thereby testing whether participants from different groups interpret and use the scale’s response categories similarly. Model comparisons were conducted using the chi-square (χ2) difference test, with degrees of freedom equal to the difference between nested models. In addition, changes in fit indices were evaluated to support invariance testing: ΔCFI and ΔTLI values below 0.01 [45] and ΔRMSEA below 0.015 [46] were interpreted as evidence of invariance. All analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 2024.04.2), with the support of the lavaan [29] and psych [30] packages.

Results

Confirmatory factor analysis

To confirm the factor structure of the SFoMOI-I identified through EFA in Study 1, a CFA was conducted. The results of the initial CFA showed a poor fit to the data, robust χ2 (20, N = 377) = 133.20, p = 0.00, CFI = 0.92, TLI = 0.89, RMSEA = 0.14, 90% CI [0.12, 0.16], SRMR = 0.054. Since the model fit was poor, modification indices suggested adding the error covariance between item 1 and item 3 (i.e., concern about losing a rewarding experience) and item 2 and item 4 (i.e., concern/anxiety about being left out). This decision was made based on the items’ similar semantic contents, indicating the potential for residual variances to be correlated. The suggested modification more accurately reflected the underlying structure of the data. It reduced the impact of item-specific factors on measurement, thereby reinforcing the value of considering item content similarity in modelling (a similar approach was also employed in the original research, [2]). After adding these constraints, a good fit for the one-factor solution was obtained, robust χ2 (18, N = 377) = 49.32, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.06, 90% CI [0.04, 0.08], SRMR = 0.031. This modification more accurately reflected the underlying structure of the data. Although two error covariances were added, the one-factor model was preferred based on results from the EFA, which revealed a clear unidimensional structure: the first eigenvalue (4.77) was substantially higher than 1, while all subsequent eigenvalues were well below 1. The scree plot (Figure S1) showed a sharp decline after the first factor. In addition, the EFA supported the retention of only one factor. Given this consistent evidence, alternative factor solutions were not tested. Moreover, while error covariances improved the fit of the initial model, future studies might benefit from a review of item wording and content to enhance clarity and reduce redundancy. This would improve both the theoretical and practical aspects of the scale without unnecessary overlapping. Unstandardised factor loadings are shown in Fig. 1. Moreover, standardised factor loadings are included in the supplementary material (Table S2), whose values ranged from 0.55 to 0.90. Although all factor loadings are significant, some lower loadings (e.g., item 1 with a factor loading of 0.55) might suggest that some items contribute less strongly to the overall factor. Future research could investigate the reliability of individual items and their sensitivity to changes in FoMO experiences, which could be crucial for refining the scale. Convergent validity

To be consistent with the initial SFoMOI-I scale development [2], we followed the same construct validation approach. We assessed convergent validity by performing bivariate correlations between the one-factor SFoMOI-I scores and the other measures used in Study 2. Additionally, the values of Average Variance Extracted (AVE) and Composite Reliability (CR) were calculated, respectively. The AVE was 0.61, and the CR was 0.96. AVE scores for all variables were greater than 0.50 and lower than the CR, hence establishing convergent validity.

Discussion

Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) is a recognised risk factor for behavioural addictions, including problematic smartphone and social media use, and is associated with anxiety and depression. It is conceptualised as a dynamic, state-like construct influenced by social and interpersonal factors. According to the Compensatory Internet Use Theory [17], technology use may fluctuate as individuals cope with momentary FoMO-related concerns. Although FoMO has been studied in Italy, no instrument currently measures its state-like dimension, highlighting the need for an adapted tool. Therefore, this study adapted and validated the State of FoMO Inventory [2] for the Italian context and examined its associations with problematic social media use, problematic smartphone use, and boredom proneness. The results of Study 1 (EFA) confirmed the original one dimension of the scale. Furthermore, the findings of Study 2 (CFA) provided evidence to support the unidimensional structure of the SFoMOI-I, thereby aligning with the conclusions of the original study [2]. Factor loading values of scale items ranged between 0.55 and 0.90. Adjusted item-total correlation values, on the other hand, ranged between 0.59 and 0.85. The values obtained in the Italian version of the SFoMOI-I scale were similar to those reported in the original validation study [2]. Item factor loads of the SFoMOI-I scale ≥ 0.40 and above are considered appropriate [33]. Based on these results, it can be stated that the Italian version of the inventory confirmed the factor structure of the original scale.

Based on a correlational analysis, convergent validity was supported by positive correlations among SFoMOI-I, boredom proneness, FoMO, problematic smartphone use, and problematic social media use. These findings, confirming H1, suggest that as the state of FoMO increases, trait FoMO, social media use, boredom proneness, and smartphone use also increase. Moreover, the positive correlation between the state of FoMO and the propensity to boredom indicates that people who tend to experience boredom are more likely to feel a high “current” state of fear of missing out. This could imply that boredom drives individuals to seek immediate external stimuli to avoid a sense of exclusion [2, 12]. Positive correlations between the state of FoMO and problematic smartphone and social media use suggest that individuals with a high level of fear of missing out tend to use these tools in problematic ways [2, 12]. This behaviour can be interpreted as an attempt to stay constantly updated on what is happening in their social environment so as not to miss anything, but it can lead to excessive and potentially harmful use of technology [2, 12].

The theoretical distinction between state and trait FoMO should be discussed. Namely, it is theoretically reasonable that these variables are strongly correlated. While the correlation coefficient for this relationship was high (r = 0.78), it was not perfect, suggesting that although both variables captured similar concerns about being absent from an enjoyable experience, they were not “too alike” and thus reflect distinct constructs. This finding is consistent with prior conceptualisations of state FoMO as a situational manifestation of a more stable FoMO disposition [2]. Nevertheless, the present study did not explicitly test discriminant validity between state and trait FoMO using formal criteria (e.g., nested model comparisons or heterotrait-monotrait ratios). As such, caution is warranted in interpreting the distinctiveness of the two constructs, and future research should further examine their discriminant validity using longitudinal or experimental designs that can more clearly disentangle dispositional and situational components of FoMO. However, the current findings preserve the theoretical distinction between trait and state, and future iterations of trait and state FoMO measures should continue to keep this in mind as they are developed.

Gender invariance supports the validity of the Italian SFoMOI-I by showing that the measurement model is equivalent across sexes. This indicates that the absence of score differences between women and men reflects true similarity in SFoMOI-I levels rather than measurement bias, in line with previous evidence that social media use is comparable across genders [2, 12, 25]. This indicates that gender does not significantly influence social media use patterns, supporting the notion that the motivations and behaviours associated with social media are comparable across genders. Given the inconsistent findings in the literature regarding gender differences in FoMO, particularly in relation to state FoMO, gender differences were examined in an exploratory manner, without formulating a directional hypothesis. Exploratory mean comparisons indicated that women reported significantly higher levels of state FoMO than men, although the magnitude of this difference was small (d = 0.31). This finding should be interpreted cautiously and does not suggest a stable dispositional gender difference. Rather, it may reflect context-dependent processes that make situational FoMO experiences more readily activated among women, potentially due to greater sensitivity to relational cues and greater social connectedness. Importantly, because scalar invariance was established, the observed gender difference can be interpreted as substantive rather than as an artefact of measurement non-equivalence.

Similarly, and consistent with previous studies, age invariance further supports the validity of the SFoMOI-I state scale [12]. Consistent with H2, age was negatively associated with state FoMO: younger participants reported higher levels of state FoMO than older participants. Post hoc analyses indicated that individuals aged 18–23 scored significantly higher than those aged 30–35, suggesting that younger adults may be more susceptible to situational concerns about missing out on socially meaningful experiences. This pattern aligns with previous research [12, 44], indicating that FoMO tends to decrease with age, potentially reflecting greater emotional regulation, reduced reliance on social validation, and changes in social priorities over the lifespan. The one-way ANOVA analysis indicated that young adults are more susceptible to social anxiety when they are deprived of social experiences that they consider important. This, in turn, increases the likelihood of developing addictive symptoms [44].

Limitations and future studies

The findings of the present study should be considered in light of some limitations. First, the predominance of women participants may have influenced the observed associations, as prior studies suggest gender differences in social media use and experiences of FoMO [47]. This raises the possibility that the findings reflect patterns more typical of women users, potentially leading to distorted outcomes that could limit the generalizability of the results. Future research should recruit more balanced and diverse samples to examine whether these associations are stable across genders and other demographic factors. Second, although we acknowledged the possible role of social desirability bias, it is important to consider how it may have specifically influenced responses to socially sensitive items, such as those assessing PSMU and/or smartphone use. Participants might have underreported behaviours perceived as excessive or problematic. Future studies could address this issue by incorporating strategies to reduce social desirability, such as ensuring complete anonymity, using indirect questioning techniques, or integrating implicit or behavioural measures alongside self-reports or physiological indicators. Third, while the cross-sectional design limits causal inferences, future longitudinal studies could provide greater insight into the temporal and potentially reciprocal relationships between FoMO, social media use, and personality factors. For instance, tracking the online behaviour of individuals over time when using their smartphones, through real-time assessment, would help clarify whether FoMO leads to increased problematic engagement with social media or whether excessive use reinforces FoMO through constant exposure to social comparison strategies. Moreover, the observed association between boredom proneness and state FoMO deserves further attention. Indeed, individuals prone to boredom may experience a heightened sensitivity to under-stimulation and a persistent need for novelty, which could make them more susceptible to momentary feelings of missing out. From a motivational perspective, FoMO may serve as a compensatory coping mechanism, prompting these individuals to seek engagement or distraction via social media. Future research should examine this link, potentially exploring mediating variables such as self-regulation or attentional control, to clarify how boredom contributes to the emergence of state FoMO.

While we briefly noted the importance of conducting international comparison research, future studies should more systematically investigate how FoMO manifests across cultural contexts. Cultural norms surrounding social connectedness, individualism versus collectivistic society, and digital engagement likely shape the intensity and expression of FoMO. For instance, in more collectivistic cultures, social exclusion may carry greater emotional weight, potentially amplifying FoMO experiences [48]. Comparing Italian data with findings from English-speaking or non-Western populations could enhance the generalizability of the scale and inform culturally sensitive prevention and intervention efforts. Lastly, it is worth noting that the SFoMOI-I could be shortened to enhance its applicability in EMA designs. In addition, future research could expand the examination of the convergent validity of the scale by including other relevant psychological constructs, such as anxiety, depression [18] or loneliness, thereby strengthening the scale’s utility in both research and applied contexts. It would also be valuable to explore how FoMO interacts with individual differences, including personality traits (e.g., neuroticism, extraversion) and emotion regulation strategies, to better understand the mechanisms underlying momentary experiences of FoMO.

Conclusion

The SFoMOI-I was successfully adapted to the Italian context, demonstrating its applicability and psychometric soundness among young adults. The results of the present study confirmed the one-dimensional factorial structure of the original version, thereby supporting the theoretical coherence of the construct and its representation as a transient situational state. This outcome highlights the capacity of the scale to capture the core characteristics of the State Fear of Missing Out (SFoMO) phenomenon within a different cultural context. Another significant contribution of this study is the confirmation of measurement invariance across gender and age groups, indicating that the scale is equally applicable across comparative studies and is relevant to diverse demographic groups. In conclusion, this study not only corroborates the validity and reliability of the SFoMOI-I in the Italian context but also provides a foundation for broader theoretical, clinical, and educational applications. It encourages further multidisciplinary research into the dynamics of state-based FoMO, contributing to the understanding of digital behaviour and its implications for mental health and digital well-being in an increasingly connected social world.
