Weak solutions of a viscous model for fluid-bubbles interaction
Cosmin Burtea, David Gérard-Varet

Abstract
We present a system of Navier-Stokes type that describes the dynamics of several spherical bubbles of gas in a liquid. It is derived from a more complete model, where the bubbles are seen as inclusions of gas of homogeneous barotropic pressure with free surfaces. The usual condition of continuity of the stress is relaxed in order to preserve the sphericity of the bubbles through time. We construct weak solutions à la Leray for this relaxed system, up to collision between the bubbles. Although these solutions are reminiscent of weak solutions for fluid-solid interaction systems, accounting for the compression/dilation of the bubbles creates new and significant mathematical difficulties.



1
A new model for fluid-bubbles interaction

The evolution of one or several bubbles of gas in a liquid is a classical topic of fluid mechanics. One main domain of interest is bubble acoustics, as compression and dilation of the bubbles create sound waves in the liquid. Such waves are met in natural phenomena, like the roar of a waterfall, but also in many medical, industrial or military processes. They may be sometimes undesirable, when they scatter the signal emitted or received by sonars, for instance in military or geophysical surveying. On the contrary, they may facilitate medical diagnosis, amplifying the signal in the regions where they are injected.
Another phenomenon of current interest is
sonoluminescence
, which is the emission of photons through the collapse of bubbles. It has potential applications like in dental ultrasonics, but also potential negative impact, such as increasing erosion processes. Let us stress that in many contexts, the population of bubbles in a liquid has to be monitored and controlled, like in the oceans due to their greenhouse effect, or in many industrial processes such as production of paints. We refer to the remarkable review paper
[
29
]
, see also
[
37
]
for more.

From the modeling point of view, it is natural to describe the liquid and the bubbles as immiscible domains with interfaces. The liquid part is typically described by the incompressible Navier-Stokes equation, while the bubbles are governed by a compressible Navier-Stokes or Euler equation. Nevertheless, it is often argued that the pressure in the bubble can be considered as spatially homogeneous, see
[
37
]
, or
[
2
, Appendix A]
. One main justification is that the Mach number is so low that the pressure gradient at leading order is zero. Under a barotropic law, the pressure at time
t
in a bubble is then given for some
c_{0}>0
by

p(t)=c_{0}\rho(t)^{\gamma}=c|B(t)|^{-\gamma},\quad c:=c_{0}M_{0}^{\gamma}

where
c
is a positive constant,
\gamma>1
is the adiabatic exponent,
M_{0}
is the mass of the bubble (constant in time), and
|B(t)|
is the volume of the bubble at time
t
. This leads to a system of the following form, where the
N
bubbles occupy the domains
B_{1}(t),\dots,B_{N}(t)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
at time
t
, and the fluid domain is

\Omega(t):={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{N}B_{i}(t)
:

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}u+u\cdot{\nabla}u+{\nabla}p-\nu\Delta u=0,

\displaystyle t>0,\quad x\in\Omega(t)

(1.1)

\displaystyle\hbox{div \!}u=0,

\displaystyle t>0,\quad x\in\Omega(t)

\displaystyle p_{i}(t)=c_{i}|B_{i}(t)|^{-\gamma},

\displaystyle t>0,\quad i\in\overline{1,N}

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}(t)}=-p_{i}\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}(t)},

\displaystyle t>0,\quad i\in\overline{1,N}

\displaystyle{\partial}B_{i}(t)=\Theta_{t,0}({\partial}B_{i}(0)),

\displaystyle t>0,\quad i\in\overline{1,N}.

The first two lines are the standard incompressible Navier-Stokes equations for the liquid (Euler equations when
\nu=0
). In the third line, the pressure in the bubble
i
, denoted
p_{i}(t)
, obeys the relation that we have just described. The fourth line expresses the continuity of the stress at the boundary of each bubble: the stress
\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}
exerted by the fluid, with
\mathrm{T}(u,p):=2\nu D(u)-p\mathrm{Id}
the Newtonian stress tensor, is equal to the stress
-p_{i}\mathfrak{n}
exerted by the bubble
i
. Note that for any domain
O
,
\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}O}
will always refer to the unit normal vector pointing outward
O
. We could also have included surface tension, by replacing in the fourth line
p_{i}
by
p_{i}-2\alpha\kappa_{i}
with
\alpha>0
and
\kappa_{i}
the mean curvature of the surface
{\partial}B_{i}(t)
. As this additional surface tension would not play any significant role in the mathematical analysis of our relaxed model later, we neglect it. Finally, the last line of the model expresses that the boundary of each bubble is preserved by the flow
\Theta_{t,0}
of the fluid. This is a way to express that the boundary of each bubble is a material surface: the normal velocity of the bubble and the normal velocity of the fluid coincide, so that no exchange of mass across this boundary occurs.

The system (
1.1
), although natural, is very complex, especially as it involves multiple free surfaces. It relates to the free surface Navier-Stokes equations (or Euler when
\nu=0
), which is a classical model for water waves: there, the fluid domain is typically of the form
\Omega(t)=\{z<\varphi(t,x,y)\}
, and the stress tensor
\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}
at the boundary of the free-surface is given by the atmospheric pressure
p_{atm}\mathfrak{n}
. We refer to
[
1
,
42
,
17
]
in the Navier-Stokes case, and to the book
[
28
]
for the inviscid equations. Generically, one obtains local existence of smooth solutions for smooth data. In the context of fluid-bubbles interaction, the local strong well-posedness of system (
1.1
) was recently established in
[
45
]
. As far as we know, the only setting in which more was done on (
1.1
) is the case of a single bubble
N=1
, when this bubble is initially spherical. Under radial symmetry of the initial data, the sphericity of the bubble is preserved, as well as the symmetry of the fields, providing a simpler set of equations. Denoting
B(t)=B(x(t),r(t))
the spherical bubble at time
t
, an important subclass of solutions is then given by
x(t)=0
,

u(t,x)=-\frac{\dot{r}r}{|x|^{3}}x
, and
r(t)
satisfies the famous
Rayleigh-Plesset
equation

r\ddot{r}+\frac{3}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+4\nu\frac{\dot{r}}{r}=p(t)-p_{\infty}

where
p_{\infty}
is the uniform pressure at infinity. Substituting
p(t)=c|B(t)|^{-\gamma}=c\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\right)^{-\gamma}r(t)^{-3\gamma}
into this relation, we end up with a closed differential equation on
r
.
Still in the radial setting, several variations are possible, for instance replacing the barotropic law in the single bubble by the law of perfect gas and adding an evolution equation on temperature. We refer to
[
2
]
for an in-depth analysis of the well-posedness properties of such system. Recently, a nice stability analysis of this radially symmetric setting was conducted in
[
25
]
. See also
[
26
]
with an additional periodic forcing, and
[
27
]
where the introduction of asymmetric deformations of the bubbles is shown to trigger some strong instability.

Clearly, the model (
1.1
) is too complex to give rise to tractable numerical schemes, or even to get some qualitative insight into the dynamics of the bubbles. This is especially true when the number of bubbles
N
is very large and some simpler macroscopic effective model is needed. It is not even guaranteed that the time of existence of (
1.1
) does not shrink to zero when
N
goes to infinity.
In order to simplify the analysis, many physics papers assume that the bubbles remain spherical, notably when performing heuristically the asymptotics
N\rightarrow+\infty
: see for instance
[
6
]
.
However, the preservation of sphericity of the bubbles through time is generically wrong under the evolution of (
1.1
). This can be understood as follows. Assuming we have a solution with spherical bubbles, we can consider the pressures
p_{i}(t)
and the bubble trajectories
B_{i}(t)=B(x_{i}(t),r_{i}(t))
as given, and focus on the subproblem made of the Navier-Stokes equations (
1.1
a)-(
1.1
b) together with the stress condition (
1.1
d). This problem has already enough boundary conditions, so that no further constraint can be put on
u\cdot\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}}
. But for general configurations of bubbles, there is no reason for this normal velocity to be compatible with maintaining their sphericity.

Therefore, if we wish to describe the evolution of spherical bubbles, system (
1.1
) needs to be relaxed. Such relaxation is not discussed in
[
6
]
, where the manipulations are mostly formal, and it does not seem to be much discussed in the literature. The only references we know in which a relaxed model is explicitly written are
[
43
,
44
,
21
,
22
]
. The recent papers
[
21
,
22
]
will be discussed specifically at the end of this section. Articles
[
43
,
44
]
deal with the inviscid and irrotational case:
\nu=0,\hbox{curl \!}u=0
. In particular,
u={\nabla}\varphi
for some potential
\varphi
(say vanishing at infinity). The common idea in both papers is to assume that the bubbles
B_{i}=B(x_{i},r_{i})
are spherical and to write the equation satisfied by
\varphi

\Delta\varphi=0\quad\text{ in }\>\Omega,\quad{\partial}_{\mathfrak{n}}\phi|_{{\partial}B_{i}}=\dot{r}_{i}+\dot{x}_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}.

(the time dependence is omitted to lighten notations). The potential
\varphi
can be seen as a functional of

X=(x_{i})_{i\in\overline{1,N}}
,
R=(r_{i})_{i\in\overline{1,N}}

and of their time derivatives. The same is then true for the velocity:
u=\mathcal{U}\left[\dot{X},\dot{R},X,R\right]
. The keypoint is then to introduce a Lagrangian

\displaystyle L\left(\dot{X},\dot{R},X,R\right):=E_{k}-E_{p}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{c_{i}}{3\gamma-3}r_{i}^{3-3\gamma}

where
E_{k}
is the kinetic energy of the fluid, and
E_{p}
the sum of the potential energy of the bubbles. Writing the Lagrange equations associated with this Lagrangian, the authors derive a set of ordinary differential equations on
X,R
. Under the assumption that the bubbles are far enough from one another, this set of equations can be approximated by a more explicit one using an approximation of the field
u=\mathcal{U}[X,R]
given by the so-called method of reflections
[
24
]
. Although efficient, the approach of
[
43
,
44
]
is limited to the inviscid and curl-free setting, and does not connect directly to the PDE system (
1.1
).

Our ambition is to present and study a relaxed model for spherical bubbles, derived from (
1.1
) and valid in both the inviscid and the viscous case. In the present paper, we will introduce this model, explain briefly its origin, and build weak solutions
à la Leray
in the viscous case (
\nu>0)
. A much more complete discussion of the derivation of the model will be given in the companion paper
[
5
]
.

Let us introduce the relaxation of (
1.1
) that we have in mind. The bubbles are described at time
t
by balls

B_{i}(t):=B(x_{i}(t),r_{i}(t)),\quad i\in\overline{1,N}.

(1.2)

The fluid domain is

\Omega(t):={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{n}\overline{B_{i}(t)}.

(1.3)

The unknowns of the model are the centers and radii of the bubbles:

X:=(x_{i})_{i\in\overline{1,N}},\quad R:=(r_{i})_{i\in\overline{1,N}}

(1.4)

as well as the fluid velocity and pressure
u,p
in the fluid domain. Given
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}>0
, we claim that a natural set of governing equations is:

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}u+u\cdot{\nabla}u+{\nabla}p-\nu\Delta u=0,

\displaystyle t>0,\quad x\in\Omega(t)

(1.5)

\displaystyle\hbox{div \!}u=0,

\displaystyle t>0,\quad x\in\Omega(t)

\displaystyle p_{i}=\frac{c_{i}}{4\pi}r_{i}^{-3\gamma},

\displaystyle t>0,\quad i\in\overline{1,N}

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}\times\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}}=0,

\displaystyle t>0,\quad i\in\overline{1,N},

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}}\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}=-p_{i},

\displaystyle t>0,\quad i\in\overline{1,N},

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}}\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}=0,

\displaystyle t>0,\quad i\in\overline{1,N},

\displaystyle u\cdot\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}(t)}=\dot{r}_{i}+\dot{x}_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n},

\displaystyle t>0,\quad i\in\overline{1,N}.

It is completed with initial conditions:

X(0)=X_{0},\quad R(0)=R_{0},\quad u|_{t=0}=u_{0}\>\text{ in }\>\Omega_{0}:={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{N}B(x_{0,i},r_{0,i}).

Let us compare (
1.5
) to the full system (
1.1
). We notice that the Navier-Stokes equations, and the formula giving the pressure
p_{i}
in
B_{i}
stay the same,
after proper redefinition of the constant
c_{i}
(the normalization by
\frac{1}{4\pi}
will allow to have lighter formulas later). The last condition expresses again that
{\partial}B_{i}
is a material interface between the fluid and bubble
i
, as the normal component of the fluid velocity
u\cdot\mathfrak{n}
equals the normal component of the bubble velocity
u_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}:=\dot{x}_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}+\dot{r}_{i}
. But of course, the novelty is that the sphericity of the bubbles puts a restriction on the structure of
u\cdot\mathfrak{n}
at
{\partial}B_{i}
. Contrary to the condition
\Theta_{t,0}({\partial}B_{i}(0))={\partial}B_{i}(t)
of (
1.1
), which can be interpreted as an evolution equation on the surface of the bubble, this evolution has now only limited degrees of freedom, namely the velocity of the center
\dot{x}_{i}
and the compression/dilation speed
\dot{r}_{i}
. This imposes to relax the original relation of continuity of the stress in (
1.1
). The continuity of the tangential stress

\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}\times\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}}=0

is kept, but the continuity of the normal stress
\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}=-p_{i}\mathfrak{n}
is now replaced by two of its consequences, namely

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}}\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}=-p_{i},\quad\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}}\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}=0.

The unknowns
\dot{r}_{i}
and
\dot{x}_{i}
are somehow Lagrange multipliers associated with these two relations.

The derivation of system (
1.5
) will be discussed in depth in
[
5
]
. We give here only a short explanation of the relaxed conditions on the stress tensor. To understand where they come from, it is easier to start from the toy model

\displaystyle u-\nu\Delta u+{\nabla}p=f,

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\hbox{div \!}u=0,

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}}=-p_{i}\mathfrak{n},

\displaystyle i\in\overline{1,N}.

This toy model is inspired by (
1.1
). Vaguely, it corresponds to a crude time discretization of the Navier-Stokes equation: given the domains
B_{i}
,
\Omega
and pressure
p_{i}
at time step
t_{k}
, we wish to compute the velocity field
u
at time step
t_{k+1}
, in order then to update the domains of the bubbles. The boundary condition is the same as in (
1.1
). Under appropriate regularity and decay assumptions on the data, one can show that this Stokes problem with a stress boundary condition is well-posed: see
[
3
, Chapter 7]
for a detailed discussion (the discussion is carried in a bounded domain
\Omega
, but the addition of a zero order term in our toy model allows to adapt everything). Importanty, the solution
u
has a variational characterization: it is the minimizer in the space

V:=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega),\quad\hbox{div \!}v=0\}

of the functional

\mathcal{F}(v):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(|v|^{2}+2\nu|D(v)|^{2})-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{{\partial}B_{i}}p_{i}\mathfrak{n}\cdot v-\int_{\Omega}f\cdot v.

The problem with this toy model is the same as with (
1.1
):
u\cdot\mathfrak{n}
is then fully determined, and there is no reason that it has the right structure to preserve sphericity. Namely, there is no reason that it belongs to the space

W:=\left\{v\in V\>\text{ s. t. for all
$i\in\overline{1,N},\>\exists s_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}},\>v_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3};\>$ }v\cdot\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}}=s_{i}+v_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\right\}.

In order to enforce this constraint, a natural idea is to minimise
\mathcal{F}
on
W
instead of
V
. But it can be shown by standard arguments that such minimizer is the solution
u\in W
of

\displaystyle u-\nu\Delta u+{\nabla}p=f,

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\hbox{div \!}u=0,

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}\times\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}}=0,

\displaystyle i\in\overline{1,N},

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}}\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}=-p_{i},

\displaystyle i\in\overline{1,N},

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}}\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}=0,

\displaystyle i\in\overline{1,N}.

We recover in this way the integral constraints on the stress tensor. Note that in (
1.5
), in the case
\nu=0
, the constraint
\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}}\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}=-p_{i}
resumes to
\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}}p=p_{i}
. Such constraint on the average pressure is briefly mentioned in
[

, p472]
. For more on the derivation of (

), we refer again to our upcoming paper

where it will be shown that system (

Although related to the studies just mentioned, the analysis of (
1.5
) raises new and serious difficulties. First, and prior to any specific construction scheme, both
a priori
estimates and proof of sequential continuity require some new arguments, to be developped in Section
4
. Roughly, due to the new kind of boundary condition (
1.5
g), one relies on a new decomposition of the solution
u
(resp. of a sequence of solutions
(u^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
) as

u=\tilde{u}+w,\quad\text{ resp. }u^{n}=\tilde{u}^{n}+w^{n}

where
\tilde{u}
is tangent at
{\partial}\Omega
, resp.
\tilde{u}^{n}
tangent at
{\partial}\Omega^{n}
and where
w
, resp.
w^{n}
is a harmonic vector field. See Section
2.1
for more on this decomposition. The convergence of
\tilde{u}^{n}
to
\tilde{u}
(including strong convergence in
L^{2}
) relies on a generalization of Aubin-Lions lemma to moving domains taken from
[
33
]
. The convergence of
w^{n}
to

relies on domain continuity results for solutions of elliptic equations inspired by
[
19
]
.

Beyond sequential continuity, to design a good construction scheme also requires new ideas. Indeed, standard approximation schemes for fluid-solid problems rely on
global approximations
, that is on approximate equations set on the union of the fluid and the solid domains (which is
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
in our setting). Such equations typically include penalization terms supported in the solid part. For instance, if
\chi^{n}_{S}
is the indicator function of the (approximate) solid domain, these terms may take the form
-n\hbox{div \!}(\chi^{n}_{S}D(u^{n}))
in the case of no-slip conditions
[
39
]
, or
n\chi^{n}_{S}P_{S}^{n}(u^{n})
with
P^{n}_{S}
some projector on the space of rigid velocity fields in the case of Navier conditions
[
13
]
. In the case of bubbles, how to find such global approximation is unclear. On one hand, a global approximate solution
u^{n}
should be divergence-free inside the approximate fluid domain
\Omega^{n}
to avoid dealing with the pressure. On the other hand, its divergence should be non-zero in the approximate bubbly part
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\Omega^{n}
in order to accomodate the condition on
u\cdot\mathfrak{n}
. But then, there is no natural functional space independent of the domain in which finding
u^{n}
: choosing
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
is not appropriate to the fluid part, choosing
L^{2}_{\sigma}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
is not appropriate to the bubbly part.
Several other technical difficulties add up to this one, that will be described in due course. All the aspects related to the construction and compactness of approximate solutions will be developed in Sections
5
and
6
.

We conclude this introduction with the discussion of another interesting model for spherical bubbles, that was introduced recently in
[
21
]
and completed by a one-dimensional study in
[
22
]
. The starting point of
[
21
]
is a system of immiscible compressible Navier-Stokes flows, one with velocity
u
for the surrounding fluid, the others with velocities
u_{i}
for the bubbles. The authors impose the continuity of the stress but also
the continuity of the velocities
at each interface. To simplify the dynamics of the bubbles, the limit of infinite shear viscosity is considered: the Navier-Stokes evolution inside the bubbles is replaced by the constraint

D(u_{i})-\frac{1}{3}(\hbox{div \!}u_{i})\,\mathrm{Id}=0,\quad i\in\overline{1,N}.

Under the assumptions that the bubbles remain spherical and under an additional symmetry assumption on
u_{i}
, the previous relation implies:

u_{i}|_{B_{i}}=\dot{x}_{i}+\omega_{i}\times(x-x_{i})+\frac{\dot{r}_{i}}{r_{i}}(x-x_{i}).

Continuity of the velocity at the interfaces yields a Dirichlet condition on
u
: for all
i\in\overline{1,N}
,

u|_{{\partial}B_{i}}=\dot{x}_{i}+\omega_{i}\times(x-x_{i})+\frac{\dot{r}_{i}}{r_{i}}(x-x_{i}).

The final step to close the system is to derive an evolution equation for
\dot{x}_{i}
,
\dot{\omega}_{i}
and
\dot{r}_{i}
. This is done by coming back to the system of compressible fluid flows (with large but finite shear viscosity in the spherical bubbles) and considering test fields
\psi
that satisfy also the constraint

D(\psi)-\frac{1}{3}\hbox{div \!}\psi\mathrm{Id}=0
.

The system derived in
[
21
]
, for which the construction of weak solutions remains open, is very different from (
1.5
). It makes a crucial use of the viscosity of the bubbles: a no-slip condition is imposed at the boundary of the bubbles, and the limit of infinite shear viscosity is considered. On the contrary, in (
1.5
), the viscosity of the bubble is fully neglected, and tangential discontinuity of the velocity at the interface is allowed, like in the original free surface system (
1.1
). This makes possible to consider (
1.5
) in the purely inviscid case, where diffusion is omitted in the fluid part as well. This is common practice in physics studies related to bubbles. Another distinct feature is that the inertia of the bubbles does not show up in (
1.5
) (and already in (
1.1
)), while it appears in
[
21
]
. This is again a common approximation, arguing that the mass of the bubbles is very small. Finally, the angular velocity
\omega_{i}
of the bubble
i
does not show up in (
1.5
), as replacing
u_{i}
by
u_{i}+\omega_{i}\times(x-x_{i})
does not affect the normal component at
{\partial}B_{i}
.

2
Statement of the main result

2.1
Functional spaces

Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{N}
smooth bounded domains of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
with disjoint closures, and let
U:={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{N}\overline{U_{i}}
. We denote by
L^{2}\left(U\right)
the space of square-integrable functions over
U
(scalar or vector valued depending on the context),
and we distinguish two of its
subspaces: the classical

L_{\sigma}^{2}\left(U\right):=\left\{w\in L^{2}\left(U\right):\operatorname{div}w=0\text{ and }w\cdot\mathfrak{n}=0\text{
on }\partial U\right\}

and the less classical space

L_{\mathrm{dil}}^{2}\left(U\right):=\left\{w\in L^{2}\left(U\right):\begin{array}[c]{l}\operatorname{div}w=0\text{ and}\\
\forall i\in\overline{1,N}:w\cdot\mathfrak{n}=s_{i}+v_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\text{ on }\partial U_{i},\text{where }\\
s_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.\end{array}\right\}

which allows for dilation (or contraction) of the boundaries of the bubbles. The space
L_{\mathrm{dil}}^{2}\left(U\right)
will be crucial to the analysis of (
1.5
). Obviously,
L_{\sigma}^{2}\left(U\right)
is a subspace of

L_{\mathrm{dil}}^{2}\left(U\right)
which is a subspace of

L^{2}\left(U\right).
Moreover,

L_{\sigma}^{2}\left(U\right)=\overline{\left\{v\in C_{0}^{\infty}\left(U\right):\operatorname{div}v=0\right\}}^{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}}},

see
[
12
, Theorem III.2.3]
. We consider

\mathbb{G}\left(U\right):=\left\{w\in L_{\mathrm{dil}}^{2}\left(U\right),\left\langle w,u\right\rangle=0:\forall u\in L_{\sigma}^{2}\left(U\right)\right\}

so that

L_{\mathrm{dil}}^{2}\left(U\right)=L_{\sigma}^{2}\left(U\right)\oplus\mathbb{G}\left(U\right).

Moreover, we claim that
\mathbb{G}\left(U\right)
is finite
dimensional (which implies that
L_{\mathrm{dil}}^{2}\left(U\right)
is closed in
L^{2}(U)
). Indeed, consider
u\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}\left(U\right)
and
recall the Liouville decomposition

u=\mathbb{P}u+\nabla q

where
\mathbb{P}u\in L_{\sigma}^{2}\left(U\right)
and

q\in\dot{H}^{1}\left(U\right)\cap L^{2}_{loc}(U)
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{l}-\Delta q=0,\text{ in }U\\
\frac{\partial q}{\partial\mathfrak{n}}=u\cdot\mathfrak{n}\text{ on }\partial U.\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

Thus, elements from
\mathbb{G}\left(U\right)
are gradient fields
verifying

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{l}-\Delta q=0,\\
\frac{\partial q}{\partial\mathfrak{n}}=s_{i}+v_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\text{ on }\partial U_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\overline{1,N}.\end{array}\right.

Any element from
\mathbb{G}\left(U\right)
can be written as a
linear combination of gradients of solutions of the equations

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{l}-\Delta q_{i}=0\text{ on }U,\\
\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial\mathfrak{n}}=\delta_{ij}\text{ on }\partial U_{j},\end{array}\right.

(2.2)

for all
i\in\overline{1,N}
, and

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{l}-\Delta q_{i}^{k}=0\text{ on }U,\\
\frac{\partial q_{i}^{k}}{\partial\mathfrak{n}}=\delta_{ij}\mathfrak{n}^{k}\text{
on }\partial U_{j},\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

for all
i\in\overline{1,N},

k\in\overline{1,3}.
Thus these fields
generate
\mathbb{G}\left(U\right)
. Let us suppose that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\nabla q_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\alpha_{i}^{k}\nabla q_{i}^{k}=0

for some
\left(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i}^{k}\right)_{i\in\overline{1,N},\text{ }k\in\overline{1,3}}.
Then, for any fixed
j\in\overline{1,N}
and any
\ell\in\overline{1,3}

we have that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\int_{{\partial}B_{j}}\alpha_{i}{\partial}_{\mathfrak{n}}q_{i}+\sum_{i,k}\int_{{\partial}B_{j}}\alpha_{i}^{k}{\partial}_{\mathfrak{n}}q_{i}^{k}=\int_{{\partial}B_{j}}\alpha_{j}+\sum_{k}\int_{{\partial}B_{j}}\alpha_{j}^{k}\mathfrak{n}^{k}\mathrm{d}S=\int_{{\partial}B_{j}}\alpha_{j},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\int_{{\partial}B_{j}}\alpha_{i}{\partial}_{\mathfrak{n}}q_{i}\,\mathfrak{n}^{\ell}+\sum_{i,k}\int_{{\partial}B_{j}}\alpha_{i}^{k}{\partial}_{\mathfrak{n}}q_{i}^{k}\,\mathfrak{n}^{\ell}=\int_{\partial U}\alpha_{j}^{\ell}(\mathfrak{n}^{\ell})^{2}\mathrm{d}S,

relations that show the linear independence of
\left\{\nabla q_{i},\nabla q_{i}^{k}\right\}_{i\in\overline{1,N},k\in\overline{1,3}}
. Thus the space

\mathbb{G}\left(U\right)
is indeed
4N
dimensional. We can
orthonormalize it to produce, with a slight abuse of notations

\left\{\nabla q_{i}\right\}_{i\in\overline{1,4N}}

an orthonormal basis.

Remark 2.1
.

The solution
q
of system (
2.1
) is defined up to an additive constant, which of course does not affect its gradient. However, it is known that there is a unique choice of this constant such that
q\in L^{6}(U)
, with inequality
\|q\|_{L^{6}}\leq C\|{\nabla}q\|_{L^{2}}
. The same holds for
q_{i},q_{i}^{k}
solutions of (
4.8
) and (
4.9
). See
[
12
, Theorem II.6.1]
for details. We make implicitly this choice in all what follows.

In our context of time dependent domains,
U_{i}
will be replaced by
B_{i}=B_{i}(t)
and
U
by
\Omega=\Omega(t)
. Given an interval
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, we introduce the notation

Q(I):=\cup_{t\in I}\{t\}\times\Omega(t)

(2.4)

For
1\leq p<\infty
,
1\leq q\leq\infty
, we define the space

L^{p}(I;L^{q}(\Omega)):=\left\{u:Q(I)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\>\text{ measurable},\quad\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega(t))}^{p}dt<\infty\right\}

One can define similarly
L^{\infty}(I;L^{q}(\Omega))
,
\>L^{p}(I;H^{s}(\Omega))
for
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We further define

C^{\infty}_{c}(I;L^{q}(\Omega)):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &u:Q(I)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\>\text{ measurable},\>{\partial}_{t}^{k}u\in L^{\infty}(I;L^{q}(\Omega))\>\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}\\
&u(t,\cdot)=0\text{ for $t$ outside a compact set of $I$},\end{aligned}\right\},

One can define similarly
C^{\infty}_{c}(I;H^{s}(\Omega))
for
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Eventually, we define for any
1\leq p\leq\infty

L^{p}(I;L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(\Omega)):=\left\{u\in L^{p}(I;L^{2}(\Omega)),\>u(t)\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(\Omega(t))\>\text{ for a.e. }t\in I\right\}.

2.2
Definition of weak solutions

In this paragraph, we introduce a notion of weak Leray type solution for system (
1.5
). We restrict to situations where the bubbles do not shrink to points, and do not collide or merge. This means that the families
X
and
R
in (
1.4
) will always be considered on time intervals
[0,T)
such that: for all
t\in(0,T)
,
\displaystyle(X(t),R(t))
belongs to the admissible set

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{(X_{0},R_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3N}\times({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{N},\>\forall i\neq j,\>|x_{0,i}-x_{0,j}|>r_{0,i}+r_{0,j}\right\}.

(2.5)

As usual, the notion of weak solution is based on a variational formulation, deduced from formal multiplication of the momentum equation in (
1.5
) by a suitable test field
\psi
. Namely, we take
\psi
such that

\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}\left([0,T);H^{s}(\Omega)\right)\>\forall s\in{\mathbb{N}},\quad\psi(t)\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(\Omega(t))\>\forall t\in[0,T)

and integrate over
\Omega(t)
. We get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}(\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u)\cdot\psi

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}(\partial_{t}(u\cdot\psi)+u\cdot{\nabla}(u\cdot\psi))-\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}u\cdot(\partial_{t}\psi+u\cdot{\nabla}\psi)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}(u\cdot\psi)-\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}u\cdot(\partial_{t}\psi+u\cdot{\nabla}\psi).

The last equality comes from the following general formula about integrals on moving domains, to be used several times in the paper: for any function
f(t,\cdot):\Omega(t)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, and for
V_{\mathfrak{n}}
the normal velocity of
{\partial}\Omega
:

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega(t)}f(t,x)dx=\int_{\Omega(t)}{\partial}_{t}f(t,x)dx+\int_{{\partial}\Omega(t)}f(t,x)V_{\mathfrak{n}}(t,x)d\sigma(x).

We recall that the normal velocity is defined as follows. Let
t\geq 0
,
x\in{\partial}\Omega(t)
. Given a neighborhood
I_{t}
of
t
and a
C^{1}
field
\gamma:I_{t}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
such that
\gamma(s)\in{\partial}\Omega(s)
for all
s\in I_{t}
with
\gamma(t)=x
, we define

V_{\mathfrak{n}}(t,x):=\gamma^{\prime}(t)\cdot\mathfrak{n}(t,x).

This formula can be shown to be independent of the choice of
\gamma
. We refer to
[
38
, Chapter 2.5]
for all necessary details. In particular, if
\Theta_{t}
is a diffeomorphism that sends
\Omega(0)
to
\Omega(t)
, and
v
is the associated velocity field:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega(t)}f(t,x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(t)}{\partial}_{t}f(t,x)dx+\int_{{\partial}\Omega(t)}f(t,x)V_{\mathfrak{n}}(t,x)d\sigma(x)

(2.6)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(t)}{\partial}_{t}f(t,x)dx+\int_{{\partial}\Omega(t)}f(t,x)v(t,x)\cdot\mathfrak{n}(t,x)d\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(t)}{\partial}_{t}f(t,x)dx+\int_{\Omega(t)}\hbox{div \!}(vf)dx.

This is the identity that we used with
v=u
and
f=u\cdot\psi
, taking into account that
\hbox{div \!}(u(u\cdot\psi))=u\cdot{\nabla}(u\cdot\psi)
as
\hbox{div \!}u=0
.

Now, note that for all
i\in\overline{1,N}
, there exists
s_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
such that

\psi\cdot\mathfrak{n}=s_{i}+v_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\text{ on }{\partial}B_{i}\left(t\right).

We compute, with
\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{T}(u,p)
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}({\nabla}p-\nu\Delta u)\cdot\psi=-\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}\hbox{div \!}\mathrm{T}\cdot\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}\mathrm{T}:\mathbb{D}\left(\psi\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}(\mathrm{T}\mathfrak{n})\cdot\psi\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}\mathrm{T}:\mathbb{D}\left(\psi\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}(\mathrm{T}\mathfrak{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n})(\psi\cdot\mathfrak{n})\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}(\mathrm{T}\mathfrak{n}\times\mathfrak{n})\cdot(\psi\times\mathfrak{n})\mathrm{d}\sigma\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}\mathrm{T}:\mathbb{D}\left(\psi\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}(\mathrm{T}\mathfrak{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n})\mathrm{d}\sigma\,s_{i}+\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}(\mathrm{T}\mathfrak{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n})(v_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n})\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}(\mathrm{T}\mathfrak{n}\times\mathfrak{n})\cdot(\psi\times\mathfrak{n})\mathrm{d}\sigma\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}\mathrm{T}:\mathbb{D}\left(\psi\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}(\mathrm{T}\mathfrak{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n})\mathrm{d}\sigma\,s_{i}+\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}\mathrm{T}\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}\sigma\cdot v_{i}+\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}(\mathrm{T}\mathfrak{n}\times\mathfrak{n})\cdot(\psi\times\mathfrak{n}-v_{i}\times\mathfrak{n})\mathrm{d}\sigma\right].

Taking into account the conditions on
\mathrm{T}
in (
1.5
), we find

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}({\nabla}p-\nu\Delta u)\cdot\psi

\displaystyle=2\nu\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}\mathbb{D}(u):\mathbb{D}\left(\psi\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}r_{i}^{2-3\gamma}s_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\nu\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}\mathbb{D}(u):\mathbb{D}\left(\psi\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}r_{i}^{2-3\gamma}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}(t)}(\psi\cdot\mathfrak{n})\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Integrating from
0
to
t\leq T
, we deduce the following variational formulation:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}u\left(t\right)\cdot\psi\left(t\right)+2\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega\left(s\right)}\mathbb{D}\left(u\right)\cdot\mathbb{D}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}s-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}c_{i}r_{i}^{2-3\gamma}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(s\right)}\psi\cdot\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}s

(2.7)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(0)}u_{0}\cdot\psi\left(0\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega\left(s\right)}u\cdot(\partial_{t}\psi+u\cdot{\nabla}\psi)\mathrm{d}s.

This formal calculation leads to the following

Definition 2.2
.

(
weak solutions
)

Let
T>0
,
(X_{0},R_{0})\in\mathcal{A}
, cf. (
2.5
). Let
\displaystyle\Omega(0):={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{N}\overline{B(x_{0,i},r_{0,i})}
and

u_{0}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(\Omega(0))
.

A weak solution of (
1.5
) on
[0,T)
with data
(X_{0},R_{0},u_{0})
is a triple
(X,R,u)
such that

i)

(X,R)\in W^{1,\infty}_{loc}([0,T);{\mathbb{R}}^{3N}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N})

ii)

(X(0),R(0))=(X_{0},R_{0})
and for all
t\in[0,T)
,
(X(t),R(t))\in\mathcal{A}
.

iii)

u\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(\Omega)\right)
,
\>{\nabla}u\in L^{2}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
, where
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{N}\overline{B(x_{i},r_{i})}
.

iv)

For all
\psi\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;L_{\mathrm{dil}}^{2}(\Omega)\right)
with
\partial_{t}\psi,\nabla\psi\in L^{2}\left(0,T);L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
, the variational formulation (
2.7
) holds for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
.

v)

For a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, for all
i\in\overline{1,N}
,
\>u\cdot\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}}=\dot{x}_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}+\dot{r}_{i}
.

vi)

The inequality

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega(t)}|u(t)|^{2}+2\nu\int_{\Omega(t)}|\mathbb{D}(u)(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{c_{i}}{3\gamma-3}r_{i}(t)^{3-3\gamma}\leq E_{0}

(2.8)

where

E_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{0}}|u_{0}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{c_{i}}{3\gamma-3}r_{0,i}^{3-3\gamma}

(2.9)

holds for almost every
t\in[0,T)
.

Remark 2.3
.

We have chosen to consider settings in which the fluid domain
\Omega(0)
, and more generally
\Omega(t)
for all
t
, is an exterior domain. As a consequence, given any initial family of dilation speeds
S_{0}=(s_{0,i})_{i\in\overline{1,N}}
and of translation velocities
V_{0}=(v_{0,i})_{i\in\overline{1,N}}
, there exists
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega(0))
such that

\hbox{div \!}u_{0}=0,\quad u_{0}\cdot\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}}=s_{0,i}+v_{0,i}\mathfrak{n},\quad\forall i\in\overline{1,N}.

Namely, one can take

u_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{0,i}{\nabla}q_{i}(0)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{3}(v_{0,i}\cdot e_{k}){\nabla}q_{i}^{k}(0)

where the fields
{\nabla}q_{i}(0)
and
{\nabla}q_{i}^{k}(0)
are the harmonic fields introduced in Section
2.1
, in the special case
U=\Omega(0)
. This means that the space
L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(\Omega(0))
is reasonably large. The situation would have been different in the case of a fluid in a bounded cavity, that is
\Omega(t):=U\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{N}B_{i}(t)
with
U
a bounded open domain. In such a case, under a natural non-penetration condition
u_{0}\cdot\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}U}=0
at the boundary of the cavity, one has the necessary compatibility condition:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\partial}B_{i}(0)}u_{0}\cdot\mathfrak{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\partial}B_{i}(0)}u_{0}\cdot\mathfrak{n}-\int_{{\partial}U}u_{0}\cdot\mathfrak{n}=-\int_{\Omega}\hbox{div \!}u_{0}=0.

This condition, which can be shown to be also sufficient, reads
\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{0,i}^{2}s_{0,i}=0
. It is very stringent: it can in particular not be satisfied in the case of a single bubble unless the bubble does not dilate or contract: incompressiblity of the liquid prevents any variation of volume. Considering an exterior domain allows to overcome this difficulty: the fluid somehow dilates or compresses at infinity. Another way out of this difficulty is to let the boundary of the cavity dilate or compress at the rate given by the bubbles.

2.3
Existence of weak solutions up to collision

Our main result is

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(X_{0},R_{0})\in\mathcal{A}
. Let
\displaystyle\Omega(0):={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{N}\overline{B(x_{0,i},r_{0,i})}
and
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(\Omega(0))
. There exists
T>0
and a weak solution of (
1.5
) on
[0,T)
with data
(X_{0},R_{0},u_{0})
. Moreover, either one can take
T
arbitrary, or one can choose
T
such that

\liminf_{t\rightarrow T_{-}}\>\inf_{i\neq j}\left(|x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)|-r_{i}(t)-r_{j}(t)\right)=0.

(2.10)

Remark 2.5
.

From the energy inequality (
2.8
), one can deduce a lower bound on
r_{i}(t)
for all
i
: this rules out the collapse of a bubble on a single point. This helps to understand why the limitation on the time of existence of weak solutions may only occur from (
2.10
), that is from the collision between bubbles.

Remark 2.6
.

It will be shown in Section
4.1
that the positions and radii of the particles
(X,R)
are more than Lipschiz in time: they obey
H^{2}
bounds. This feature will be crucial to the derivation of strong compactness of the velocity field.

3
Strategy of proof

We explain here the main steps of the proof of Theorem
2.4
. First, we will tackle in Section
4
the derivation of
a priori
estimates and the proof of sequential continuity for weak solutions of system (
1.5
).The decomposition

L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(U)=L^{2}_{\sigma}(U)\oplus\mathbb{G}(U)

introduced in Section
2.1
will be crucial here. It will be applied with
U=\Omega(t)
and
U=\Omega^{n}(t)
, leading to a decomposition
u=\tilde{u}+w
,
u^{n}=\tilde{u}^{n}+w^{n}
, with
\tilde{u}
, resp.
\tilde{u}^{n}
tangent at the boundary of
{\partial}\Omega
, resp.
{\partial}\Omega^{n}
, and
w,w^{n}
gradient fields. While the compactness of
\tilde{u}^{n}
will rely on a version of Aubin-Lions compactness result in moving domains from
[
33
]
, the compactness of
w^{n}
will notably rely on
H^{2}
uniform estimates on
(X^{n},R^{n})
, see Remark
2.6
, and on the analysis of the basis
{\nabla}q_{i}^{n}
,
i\in\overline{1,4N}
seen in Section
2.1
.

The results of Section
4
will not be used as such in the subsequent sections, but will be applied with appropriate modifications to approximate solutions. As emphasized in Section
1
, standard approximation schemes, based on variational formulations on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
with penalization terms, seem hard to adapt. Therefore, we rather follow a time-stepping scheme. The broad idea is to divide the interval
(0,T)
in small subintervals of length
h\ll 1
. Then, iteratively on each time interval
[kh,(k+1)h]
, one considers a system analogue to (
1.5
), where:

i) the trajectory of the bubbles is prescribed, ii) this trajectory is based on the velocity field computed at the previous step on
[(k-1)h,kh]
. We will in this way obtain an approximate solution
u^{h}
, which will converge to a weak solution of (
1.5
) as
h\rightarrow 0
. This iteration and convergence process will be described briefly in the next three paragraphs, and developped rigorously in Sections
5
and
6
.

3.1
Simplified model with prescribed bubble dynamics.

As alluded to above, our construction of weak solutions relies on the study of a subproblem where the dynamics of the bubbles is prescribed. Namely, we give ourselves the two fields

X=(x_{i})_{i\in\overline{1,N}}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{3N},\quad R=(r_{i})_{i\in\overline{1,N}}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\rightarrow({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{N}.

More on
(X,R)
will be specified just below. We define

B_{i}[X,R]:=B(x_{i},r_{i}),\quad i\in\overline{1,N},\quad\Omega[X,R]:={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{N}\overline{B_{i}[X,R]}.

Let
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(\Omega[X,R](0))
and
\tilde{R}_{0}=(\tilde{r}_{0,i})_{i\in\overline{1,N}}\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{N}
. We consider the following variational formulation: find
T>0
and
u
such that for all
\psi\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;L_{\mathrm{dil}}^{2}(\Omega[X,R])\right)
with
\partial_{t}\psi,\nabla\psi\in L^{2}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega[X,R])\right)
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega[X,R]\left(t\right)}u\left(t\right)\cdot\psi\left(t\right)+2\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega[X,R]\left(s\right)}\mathbb{D}\left(u\right)\cdot\mathbb{D}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}s

(3.1)

Several remarks are in order.

Remark 3.2
.

The variational formulation (

) is similar to (

), but with a major difference: there is a mismatch between the normal velocity of

is also related to this mismatch. These modifications ensure (at least formally) that taking

in (

) and applying the formula (

), one recovers the energy inequality.

Remark 3.3
.

As explained in the previous remark, due to the mismatch between the normal components of the fluid and the bubbles at their interfaces, one had to add an extra boundary term and modify the pressure contribution so as to preserve the energy inequality.

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}\times\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}[X,R]}-\frac{1}{2}(\left(u\cdot\mathfrak{n}-\dot{r}_{i}-\dot{x}_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\right)u\times\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B_{i}[X,R]}=0,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}[X,R]}\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}-\frac{1}{2}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}[X,R]}\left(u\cdot\mathfrak{n}-\dot{r}_{i}-\dot{x}_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\right)u\cdot\mathfrak{n}=-\frac{r_{i}[u]^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}p_{i},

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}[X,R]}\mathrm{T}(u,p)\mathfrak{n}-\frac{1}{2}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{{\partial}B_{i}[X,R]}\left(u\cdot\mathfrak{n}-\dot{r}_{i}-\dot{x}_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\right)u=0.

Let us note that similar nonlinear boundary conditions are sometimes used as outflow conditions in the simulation of channel flows, see

Remark 3.4
.

Theorem

is reminiscent of article
[

, where the Navier-Stokes equation is studied outside a set of rigid obstacles with

and conclusion.

The last main step of the proof is to show that any accumulation point of

4

We collect in this part properties of weak solutions of system (

4.1
A priori estimates

Let
T>0
,
\delta>0
,
(X_{0},R_{0},u_{0})
as in Theorem
2.4
. We assume that there exists
(X,R,u)
a weak solution of (
1.5
) on
[0,T)
with data
(X_{0},R_{0},u_{0})
, such that

\forall i\neq j,\quad|x_{i}-x_{j}|\geq r_{i}+r_{j}+4\delta.

We have the energy inequality: for a.e.

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega(t)}|u(t)|^{2}+2\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega(t)}|\mathbb{D}(u)(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{c_{i}}{3\gamma-3}r_{i}(t)^{3-3\gamma}\leq E_{0}

(4.1)

where

E_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{0,F}}|u_{0}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{c_{i}}{3\gamma-3}r_{0,i}^{3-3\gamma}

which provides uniform bounds on
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}
and
\|{\nabla}u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}
, as well as the uniform lower bound

\forall i\in\overline{1,N},\>\forall t\leq T,\quad r_{i}(t)\geq c(E_{0})>0.

(4.2)

Lipschitz bounds on
X,R
.

We introduce for all
t\in\left(0,T\right)
,
i\in 1,N
the solution

\varphi_{i}^{\delta}
of

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)&=0\text{ on }C_{i}(t):=\left\{r_{i}\left(t\right)<\left|x-x_{i}\left(t\right)\right|<r_{i}\left(t\right)+\delta\right\},\\
\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)&=0\text{ on }\left|x-x_{i}\left(t\right)\right|=r_{i}\left(t\right)+\delta,\\
\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)&=1\text{ on }\left|x-x_{i}\left(t\right)\right|=r_{i}\left(t\right).\end{aligned}\right.

(4.3)

which is given explicitly by

\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t,x\right)=\frac{(\delta+r_{i}\left(t\right))r_{i}\left(t\right)}{\delta}\frac{1}{\left|x-x_{i}\left(t\right)\right|}-\frac{r_{i}\left(t\right)}{\delta}.

Note that

. Furthermore,

\nabla\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t,x\right)=-\frac{(\delta+r_{i}\left(t\right))r_{i}\left(t\right)}{\delta}\frac{x-x_{i}\left(t\right)}{\left|x-x_{i}\left(t\right)\right|^{3}}

from which we deduce that

\left\|\nabla\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(C_{i}(t))}\leq\left(\frac{1}{r_{i}\left(t\right)}+\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\text{ and }\left\|\nabla\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(C_{i}(t))}\leq(r_{i}\left(t\right))^{\frac{3}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{r_{i}\left(t\right)}+\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

From the first bound and the inequality

\left(\frac{c_{i}}{(3\gamma-3)E_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3\gamma-3}}\leq r_{i}(t)

deduced from (
4.1
), we get

\displaystyle 4\pi\left(\frac{c_{i}}{(3\gamma-3)E_{0}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3\gamma-3}}\left|\dot{r}_{i}\left(t\right)\right|\leq 4\pi r_{i}^{2}\left(t\right)\left|\dot{r}_{i}\left(t\right)\right|=\left|\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}u\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}\sigma\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\operatorname{div}(\varphi_{i}^{\delta}u)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(C_{i}\left(t\right)\right)}\|\leq\left\|\nabla\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(C_{i}(t))}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega(t))}\leq\sqrt{2E_{0}}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{1}{r_{i}}\right).

Combining this last inequality with (

|\dot{R}(t)|\leq C(E_{0},\delta).

(4.4)

It follows that

|R(t)|\leq C(R_{0},E_{0},\delta,T).

(4.5)

We proceed similarly for the estimate of

\frac{4\pi}{3}(r_{i}(t))^{2}\dot{x}_{i}\left(t\right)=\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}(u\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{n})\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}\sigma.

We obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\frac{c_{i}}{(3\gamma-3)E_{0}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3\gamma-3}}\left|\dot{x}_{i}\left(t\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}\left(u\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{n}\right)\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}\sigma\right|\leq\left\|(\varphi_{i}^{\delta}u)\cdot\mathfrak{n}\right\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)\right)}\left\|\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)\mathfrak{n}\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\|\operatorname{div}(\varphi_{i}^{\delta}u)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(C_{i}\left(t\right)\right)}+\left\|(\varphi_{i}^{\delta}u)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(C_{i}\left(t\right)\right)}\right)\left\|\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)\mathfrak{n}\right\|_{H^{1}\left(C_{i}\left(t\right)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)\mathfrak{n}\right\|_{H^{1}\left(C_{i}\left(t\right)\right)}.

The last term is estimated as follows. First, as

\left\|\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)\mathfrak{n}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(C_{i}^{n}\left(t\right)\right)}^{2}=\int_{C_{i}^{n}\left(t\right)}\left|\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\right|^{2}\leq Cr_{i}^{n}(t)^{2}\leq C(R_{0},E_{0},\delta,T)

thanks to (

\left\|\nabla(\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)\mathfrak{n})\right\|_{L^{2}\left(C_{i}\left(t\right)\right)}^{2}=\left\|(\nabla\varphi_{i}^{\delta}(t))\mathfrak{n}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(C_{i}\left(t\right)\right)}^{2}+\left\|\varphi_{i}^{\delta}\left(t\right)\nabla\mathfrak{n}\ \right\|_{L^{2}\left(C_{i}\left(t\right)\right)}^{2}.

Using the

estimate of
\varphi_{i}^{\delta}
just above, the previous

together with (

), we find

and eventually

|\dot{X}(t)|\leq C(R_{0},E_{0},\delta,T)

so that in turn

, and denote

,

, for all

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla q_{i}\left(t\right)\right\|_{H^{1}\left(\Omega\left(t\right)\right)}+\left\|\partial_{t}\nabla q_{i}\left(t\right)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\left(t\right)\right)}

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla q_{i}^{k}\left(t\right)\right\|_{H^{1}\left(\Omega\left(t\right)\right)}+\left\|\partial_{t}\nabla q_{i}^{k}\left(t\right)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\left(t\right)\right)}

are related to the more general question of differentiating with respect to the domain. In the case of Neumann problems like (

The dependence on

of these estimates. Both estimates involve upper and lower bounds on the radii of the balls and the distance between the balls, while the time derivative estimate involves additionaly an upper bound on

By the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization process we construct an orthonormal basis in

\mathbb{G}(\Omega(t))
(for the

scalar product) which by slightly abusing the

\left\{\nabla q_{i}(t)\right\}_{i\in\overline{1,4N}}
. These functions still satisfy (

) (this time for all

). Let
s\left(t\right)\in C_{0}^{\infty}\left(0,T\right)
. We observe that the above estimates allow us to use

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}s\left(t\right)\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}\mathbb{D}\left(u\right):\mathbb{D}\left({\nabla}q_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}t-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{c_{j}}{4\pi r_{j}\left(t\right)^{3\gamma}}s\left(t\right)\int_{\partial B_{j}\left(t\right)}\nabla q_{i}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}\left[\dot{s}\left(t\right)u\left(t\right)\cdot\nabla q_{i}\left(t\right)+s\left(t\right)u\left(t\right)\cdot\partial_{t}\nabla q_{i}\left(t\right)+s\left(t\right)u\left(t\right)\otimes u\left(t\right):\nabla^{2}q_{i}\left(t\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t.

We get

\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}\dot{s}\left(t\right)u\left(t\right)\cdot\nabla q_{i}\left(t\right)\right|\leq C\left(X_{0},R_{0},E_{0},\delta,T\right)\left\|s\right\|_{L^{2}}.

(4.10)

Thanks to the orthogonal decomposition

u\left(t\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(t\right)u\left(t\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{4N}\alpha_{j}\left(t\right)\nabla q_{j}\left(t\right)

where
\mathbb{P}\left(t\right)
is the Leray projector on

, we deduce from

that
for all

\left|\int_{0}^{T}\dot{s}\left(t\right)\alpha_{i}\left(t\right)\right|\leq C\left(X_{0},R_{0},E_{0},T,\delta\right)\left\|s\right\|_{L^{2}}.

(4.11)

Now, we know that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\dot{s}\left(t\right)\dot{r}_{i}\left(t\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\dot{s}\left(t\right)\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}u\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\dot{s}\left(t\right)\sum_{j=1}^{4N}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(t\right)}\alpha_{j}\left(t\right)\frac{\partial q_{j}\left(t\right)}{\partial\mathfrak{n}}\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathrm{d}t.

Using the estimates
\left(\text{\ref{lower_bound_R}}\right)
,
\left(\text{\ref{q1}}\right)
and a similar argument for

leads to the
estimate

\left\|\dot{R}\right\|_{H^{1}\left(0,T\right)}+\left\|\dot{X}\right\|_{H^{1}\left(0,T\right)}\leq C\left(X_{0},R_{0},E_{0},T,\delta\right).

(4.12)

4.2
Sequential continuity

We show in this section that any sequence
(X^{n},R^{n},u^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of weak solutions has a subsequence converging to a weak solution
(X,R,u)
.
Let again
T>0
,
\delta>0
,
(X_{0},R_{0},u_{0})
as in Theorem
2.4
. We assume that there exists
(X^{n},R^{n},u^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
a sequence of weak solutions of (
1.5
) on
[0,T)
with data
(X_{0},R_{0},u_{0})
, such that for all
n
, over
(0,T)

\forall i\neq j,\quad|x_{i}^{n}-x_{j}^{n}|\geq r^{n}_{i}+r_{j}^{n}+4\delta.

The point is to show that an accumulation point of this sequence is a weak solution.
By estimates of Section
4.1
, we get the uniform bounds

\displaystyle\|u^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{n}))}+\|{\nabla}u^{n}\|^{2}_{{}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{n}))}}\leq CE_{0},

(4.13)

\displaystyle\|(X^{n},R^{n})\|_{H^{2}(0,T)}\leq C\left(X_{0},R_{0},E_{0},\delta,T\right).

We can extend
u^{n}
inside the balls setting

\overline{u}^{n}=u^{n}\text{ in }\Omega^{n},\quad\overline{u}^{n}(t,x)=\dot{x}_{i}^{n}(t)+\dot{r}_{i}^{n}(t)\frac{x-x_{i}^{n}(t)}{r_{i}^{n}(t)},\quad x\in B_{i}^{n}(t).

The extended field
\overline{u}^{n}
belongs to
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}))
, but not to
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}))
due to discontinuity of the tangential components of the velocity across the spheres
{\partial}B_{i}^{n}
. By standard compactness arguments, we find that, after extraction of subsequence,

\displaystyle\overline{u}^{n}\rightarrow\overline{u}\quad\text{ weakly* in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})),

\displaystyle(X^{n},R^{n})\rightarrow(X,R)\quad\text{ weakly* in }H^{2}(0,T)\text{ and strongly in }W^{1,\infty}([0,T]).

We can then define, for all
i\in\overline{1,N}
,

B_{i}:=B(x_{i},r_{i}),\quad\Omega:={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{N}\overline{B_{i}},\quad u=\overline{u}|_{\Omega}.

By the uniform convergence of
(X^{n},R^{n})
to
(X,R)
, one has for all
i

\sup_{t\in[0,T)}d_{Haus}(B_{i}^{n}(t),B_{i}(t))\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}0

where
d_{Haus}
is the Hausdorff distance. Let
\eta\in(0,\delta)
. From the Hausdorff convergence, we get that for
n
large enough, for all
i
, for all
t
,

B_{i}^{n}(t)\subset B_{i}^{\eta}(t),\quad\text{ where }\>B_{i}^{\eta}(t):=B(x_{i}(t),r_{i}(t)+\eta),\quad\Omega^{\eta}:={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{N}\overline{B_{i}^{\eta}}.

Domains
B_{i}^{\eta}
and
\Omega^{\eta}
will be used later on.

Remark 4.1
.

Let
Q
an arbitrary compact subset of the time-space domain
\cup_{t\in(0,T)}\{t\}\times\Omega(t)
. By the Hausdorff convergence above, we find that for
n
large enough

\|\mathbb{D}(u^{n})\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\|\mathbb{D}(u^{n})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\Omega^{n})}\leq C_{0}

where
C_{0}
only depends on
E_{0}
.
As
n\rightarrow+\infty
, we recover that
\mathbb{D}(u)
belongs to
L^{2}(Q)
with
\|\mathbb{D}(u)\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq C_{0}
. As
Q
is an arbitrary compact subset, we find that

\mathbb{D}(u)\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

We wish to show that
(X,R,u)
is a weak solution on
(0,T)
starting from
(X_{0},R_{0},u_{0})
. The keypoint is to derive (
2.7
) from the variational formulation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{n}\left(t\right)}u^{n}\left(t\right)\cdot\psi^{n}\left(t\right)+2\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{n}\left(s\right)}\mathbb{D}\left(u^{n}\right)\cdot\mathbb{D}\left(\psi^{n}\right)\mathrm{d}s-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{c_{i}}{4\pi}(r^{n}_{i})^{-3\gamma}\int_{\partial B^{n}_{i}\left(s\right)}\psi^{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}s

(4.14)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{0,F}}u_{0}\cdot\psi^{n}\left(0\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{n}\left(s\right)}u^{n}\cdot(\partial_{t}\psi^{n}+u^{n}\cdot{\nabla}\psi^{n})\mathrm{d}s,

valid for any
\psi^{n}\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;L_{\mathrm{dil}}^{2}(\Omega^{n})\right)
with
\partial_{t}\psi^{n},\nabla\psi^{n}\in L^{2}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{n})\right)
.

We fix a test field

\psi\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;L_{\mathrm{dil}}^{2}(\Omega)\right)\cap W^{1,\infty}\left(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)\right)\>\forall s\in{\mathbb{N}}.

It is enough to establish (
2.7
) for this kind of
\psi
, more general test fields being handled by a density argument, see Corollary
C.3
.
Note however that we can not restrict to fields with compact support in
\overline{\Omega}
. Otherwise, the divergence-free condition would impose the additional constraint that
\int_{{\partial}\Omega(t)}\psi\cdot\mathfrak{n}=0
, a constraint that would forbid to reach any test field by density. We remind for instance that in the simple case
U={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus B(0,1)
,
the field

\psi(x)=\frac{x}{|x|^{3}}

belongs to
L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(U)
but does not satisfy condition
\int_{{\partial}U}\psi\cdot\mathfrak{n}=0
.

One can not use directly
\psi
as a test function in (
4.14
). We need to transform
\psi
with the help of diffeomorphisms that map
B_{i}(t)
to
B_{i}^{n}(t)
for all
i
. The existence of such diffeomorphisms is guaranteed by the following lemma, whose proof is in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
(X_{0},R_{0}),(\tilde{X}_{0},\tilde{R}_{0})\in\mathcal{A}
, see (
2.5
), there exists a smooth diffeomorphism

\Theta=\Theta[X_{0},R_{0},\tilde{X}_{0},\tilde{R}_{0}]:{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
depending smoothly on its parameters
X_{0},R_{0},\tilde{X}_{0},\tilde{R}_{0}
, with the following properties:

•

If
(X_{0},R_{0})=(\tilde{X}_{0},\tilde{R}_{0})
,
\Theta=\mathrm{Id}
.

•

\Theta(x)=x
for
|x|
large enough.

•

\Theta(x)=x_{i}+\frac{r_{i}}{\tilde{r}_{i}}(x-\tilde{x}_{i})
in the vicinity of
B(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{r}_{i})
, for all
1\leq i\leq N
. In particular, it send
B(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{r}_{i})
to
B(x_{i},r_{i})
for all
i\in\overline{1,N}
.

We set
\Theta^{n}(t,x):=\Theta[X(t),R(t),X^{n}(t),R^{n}(t)](x)
, with
\Theta
given by Lemma
4.2
, and set

\psi^{n}(t,x):=\det\left(D\Theta^{n}(t,x)\right)\left(D\Theta^{n}(t,x)\right)^{-1}\psi(t,\Theta^{n}(t,x)).

(4.15)

One can check that
\hbox{div \!}\psi=0\Rightarrow\hbox{div \!}\psi^{n}=0
, see Appendix
B
. Moreover, from the explicit formula for
\Theta^{n}
in the vicinity of
B_{i}^{n}
, one has easily
\psi(t)\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(\Omega(t))\Rightarrow\psi^{n}(t)\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{dil}}(\Omega^{n}(t))
for all
t
. Finally, the regularity properties of
\psi
and the Lipschitz regularity in time of
(X,R,X^{n},R^{n})
allow to conclude that

\psi\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;L_{\mathrm{dil}}^{2}(\Omega^{n})\right)\cap W^{1,\infty}\left(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega^{n})\right)\>\forall s\in{\mathbb{N}}.

We can therefore take
\psi^{n}
as a test function in the variational formulation (
4.14
). Proving directly the convergence of each term in this variational formulation is hard, as integrals are taken on a domain that depends on
n
. Our treatment will involve the auxiliary domain
\Omega^{\eta}
, notably the property that

\psi^{n}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}\psi\quad\text{in }W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega^{\eta}))

(4.16)

which follows from the strong convergence of
(X^{n},R^{n})
to
(X,R)
in
W^{1,\infty}(0,T)
.

Linear terms.

The convergence in the Hausdorff distance of
B_{i}^{n}
to
B_{i}
implies

1_{\Omega^{n}}\rightarrow 1_{\Omega}\quad\text{in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})),\quad\forall 1\leq p<\infty

It follows that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left|\int_{\Omega^{n}(t)\setminus\Omega^{\eta}(t)}u^{n}(t)\cdot\psi^{n}(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\|u^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{n}))}\,\|\psi^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega^{n}))}\,\|1_{\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{n}))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|1_{\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\eta}}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{n}))}=C^{\prime}\eta^{1/2}.

Moreover, from the weak convergence of
\overline{u}^{n}
and the strong convergence of
\psi^{n}|_{\Omega^{\eta}}
, we deduce easily

\int_{\Omega^{\eta}\left(t\right)}u^{n}\left(t\right)\cdot\psi^{n}\left(t\right)\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}\left(t\right)}u\left(t\right)\cdot\psi\left(t\right)

weakly* in
L^{\infty}(0,T)
, while

\left|\int_{\Omega(t)\setminus\Omega^{\eta}(t)}u\left(t\right)\cdot\psi\left(t\right)\right|\leq C\eta^{1/2}.

We conclude that

\int_{\Omega^{n}\left(t\right)}u^{n}\left(t\right)\cdot\psi^{n}\left(t\right)\rightarrow\int_{\Omega\left(t\right)}u\left(t\right)\cdot\psi\left(t\right)

(4.17)

weakly * in
L^{\infty}(0,T)
. Similar arguments yield

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{n}\left(s\right)}u^{n}\cdot\partial_{t}\psi^{n}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega\left(s\right)}u\cdot\partial_{t}\psi

(4.18)

for all
t\in[0,T)
and in
L^{1}(0,T)
,
the convergence in
L^{1}
coming from the pointwise convergence and the dominated convergence theorem. Also, from the uniform bound on
\|\mathbb{D}(u^{n})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{n}))}
and Remark
4.1
, we deduce similarly that

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{n}(s)}\mathbb{D}(u^{n}):\mathbb{D}(\psi^{n})\mathrm{d}s\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega(s)}\mathbb{D}(u):\mathbb{D}(\psi)\mathrm{d}s

(4.19)

for all
t\in[0,T)
and in
L^{1}(0,T)
. Finally, using the explicit formula for
\psi^{n}
in the vicinity of
B_{i}^{n}
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B^{n}_{i}}\psi^{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}=\int_{\partial B_{i}}\psi\cdot\mathfrak{n}.

Combining this with the uniform convergence of
R^{n}
to
R
, we conclude that

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}c_{i}(r^{n}_{i})^{2-3\gamma}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\partial B^{n}_{i}\left(s\right)}\psi^{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}c_{i}(r_{i})^{2-3\gamma}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(s\right)}\psi\cdot\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}s

(4.20)

uniformly in time.

Nonlinear terms.

As usual, the main difficulty is the limit in
n
of the nonlinear term. We have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{n}\left(s\right)\setminus\Omega^{\eta}(s)}u^{n}\cdot(u^{n}\cdot{\nabla}\psi^{n})\mathrm{d}s\right|

(4.21)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\|{\nabla}\psi^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega^{n}))}\|u^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{4}(\Omega^{n}))}\|1_{\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}))}

\displaystyle\leq C\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\|{\nabla}\psi^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega^{n}))}\|u^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega^{n}))}\|1_{\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}))}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\|1_{\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}))}=C^{\prime}\|1_{\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\eta}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}))}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\eta^{1/2}.

Here,
C^{\prime\prime}
is a constant that depends on
\psi
,
X_{0},R_{0},E_{0}
,
\delta
,
T
.
Similarly, taking into account Remark
4.1
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega\left(s\right)\setminus\Omega^{\eta}(s)}u\cdot(u\cdot{\nabla}\psi)\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq C\eta^{1/2}.

(4.22)

It remains to control

J^{n,\eta}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}\left(s\right)}u^{n}\cdot(u^{n}\cdot{\nabla}\psi^{n})\mathrm{d}s.

We have the Liouville decomposition

u^{n}(t)=\tilde{u}^{n,\eta}(t)+w^{n,\eta}(t)\quad\text{ in }\Omega^{\eta}(t)

where
\tilde{u}^{n,\eta}(t):=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}(t)\left(u^{n}(t)|_{\Omega^{\eta}(t)}\right)
is the orthogonal projection of
u^{n}(t)|_{\Omega^{\eta}(t)}
over
L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega^{\eta}(t))
and
w^{n,\eta}=\nabla q^{n,\eta}(t)
is a gradient field: as
u^{n}
is divergence-free, it satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta q^{n,\eta}&=0\text{ in }\Omega^{\eta},\\
{\partial}_{\mathfrak{n}}q^{n,\eta}&=-u^{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\text{ at }{\partial}\Omega^{\eta}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.23)

Study of
\tilde{u}^{n,\eta}
.
The terms involving
\tilde{u}^{n,\eta}
will be handled thanks to

Lemma 4.3
.

Denote
\tilde{u}^{\eta}(t):=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}(t)(u|_{\Omega^{\eta}(t)})
. There exists a subsequence of
(\tilde{u}^{n,\eta})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, still denoted
(\tilde{u}^{n,\eta})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
for simplicity, such that for any
M>0
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}(t)\cap B(0,M)}|\tilde{u}^{n,\eta}-\tilde{u}^{\eta}|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}0.

Proof.

The proof relies on a nice generalization of the Aubin-Lions lemma for non-cylindrical domains given in
[
33
, Theorem 2]
. Strictly speaking, the theorem in
[
33
]
is stated for space-time domains of the form

\hat{\Omega}=\cup_{t\in(0,T)}\{t\}\times\Omega^{t}

(4.24)

with
\Omega^{t}
bounded, but a look at the proof shows that the boundedness of
\Omega^{t}
can be relaxed if the final compactness statement is replaced by local compactness. Namely, we shall use

Theorem 4.4
.

(slight modification of
[
33
, Theorem 2]
)

Let
\hat{\Omega}
a domain of the form (
4.24
), where for all
t\in(0,T)
,
\Omega^{t}=\mathcal{A}_{t}(\Omega)
for a fixed Lipschitz domain
\Omega
and a family
(\mathcal{A}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of
C^{1}
-diffeomorphisms, with

(t,x)\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{t}(x)\in C([0,T];C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})).

Let
(\tilde{u}^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
a sequence of divergence-free fields in
L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})
s.t.
1_{\hat{\Omega}}\tilde{u}^{n}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}))
for all
n
. We assume

i)

\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\|\tilde{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})}+\|{\nabla}\tilde{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})}\right)\leq C.

ii)

\tilde{u}^{n}(t)\cdot\mathfrak{n}=0
at
{\partial}\Omega^{t}
for all
t\in(0,T)
.

iii)

There exists
C>0
and
N_{0}>0
such that for all
\Psi
smooth, divergence free and compactly supported in
\hat{\Omega}
:

\left|\langle{\partial}_{t}\tilde{u}^{n},\Psi\rangle\right|\leq C\sum_{|\alpha|\leq N_{0}}\|{\partial}^{\alpha}\Psi\|_{L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})}.

Then,
(\tilde{u}^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is compact in
L^{2}\left(\hat{\Omega}\cap((0,T)\times B)\right)
for any ball
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

We apply this theorem with
\Omega^{t}=\Omega^{\eta}(t)
, and
\tilde{u}^{n}=\tilde{u}^{n,\eta}
. Assumption i) comes from the bound

\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\|u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{\eta})}+\|{\nabla}u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{\eta})}\right)\leq C

inherited from (
4.13

\mathbb{P}^{\eta}(t)
over
L^{2}(\Omega^{\eta}(t))
and
H^{1}(\Omega^{\eta}(t))
. Assumption ii) is obvious by definition of the Leray projector. The only point that deserves attention is iii). Let
\Psi
as above. We can take it as a test function in (
4.14
). We insist that
\Psi=0
at
t=0
and
t=T
, and that
\Psi(t)
is compactly supported in
\Omega^{\eta}(t)
. We find:

\displaystyle\langle{\partial}_{t}\tilde{u}^{n,\eta},\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}}\tilde{u}^{n,\eta}\cdot{\partial}_{t}\Psi=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}}u^{n}\cdot{\partial}_{t}\Psi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}\left(s\right)}u^{n}\cdot(u^{n}\cdot{\nabla}\Psi)\mathrm{d}s-2\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}\left(s\right)}\mathbb{D}\left(u^{n}\right)\cdot\mathbb{D}\left(\Psi\right)\mathrm{d}s.

From the uniform bounds in (
4.13
) and standard manipulations, we end up with

\displaystyle|\langle{\partial}_{t}\tilde{u}^{n,\eta},\Psi\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|{\nabla}\Psi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\eta}))}+\|\Psi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega^{\eta}))}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\|\Psi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega^{\eta}))}

which proves that iii) is satisfied with
N_{0}=3
. Therefore, Theorem
4.4
provides a
\tilde{v}^{\eta}
such that, after extraction of a subsequence: for any
M>0

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}(t)\cap B(0,M)}|\tilde{u}^{n,\eta}-\tilde{v}^{\eta}|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}0.

On the other hand, from the weak convergence of
u^{n}
to
u
in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{\eta}))
and the weak continuity of the Leray projector
\mathbb{P}^{\eta}(t)
, we find that
\tilde{u}^{n,\eta}
converges weakly to
\tilde{u}^{\eta}
, and so
\tilde{v}^{\eta}=\tilde{u}^{\eta}
which concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Study of

. We first introduce two analogues of

. We define for all

These are the gradient parts in the Liouville decomposition of

in

and

respectively. We shall prove

Lemma 4.5
.

The following properties hold, for some

depending on

:

i)

\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\|w^{n,\eta}-w^{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{\eta}))}\leq C\eta^{\frac{1}{3}}
.

ii)

\|w^{\eta}-w\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{\eta}))}\leq C\eta^{\frac{1}{3}}
.

iii)

For all
M>0
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}(t)\cap B(0,M)}|w^{n}-w|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}0
.

Proof.

For i), we write for any scalar
\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}))
s.t.
{\nabla}\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}))
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}}(w^{n,\eta}-w^{n})\cdot{\nabla}\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}}{\nabla}q^{n,\eta}\cdot{\nabla}\varphi-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}}{\nabla}q^{n}\cdot{\nabla}\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}}{\nabla}q^{n,\eta}\cdot{\nabla}\varphi-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{n}}{\nabla}q^{n}\cdot{\nabla}\varphi+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}}{\nabla}q^{n}\cdot{\nabla}\varphi

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\partial}\Omega^{\eta}}u^{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\,\varphi+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\partial}\Omega^{n}}u^{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\,\varphi+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}}{\nabla}q^{n}\cdot{\nabla}\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}}u^{n}\cdot{\nabla}\varphi+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}}{\nabla}q^{n}\cdot{\nabla}\varphi.

We have

\displaystyle\|u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}))}

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega^{n})}|\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}|^{1/3}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega^{n})}|\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}|^{1/3}\leq C^{\prime}\eta^{1/3}.

and similarly

\|{\nabla}q^{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\eta}))}\leq C\eta^{1/3}

We end up with

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}}(w^{n,\eta}-w^{n})\cdot{\nabla}\varphi\leq C\eta^{1/3}\|{\nabla}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{\eta}))}.

The bound i) follows. The bound ii) can be proved exactly in the same way. As regards iii), we remind that
q^{n}(t)
solves

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta q^{n}(t)&=0\text{ in }\Omega^{n}(t)\\
{\partial}_{\mathfrak{n}}q^{n}(t)&=\dot{r}_{i}^{n}+\dot{x}_{i}^{n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\text{ at }{\partial}B^{n}_{i}(t),\>1\leq i\leq N\end{aligned}\right.

(4.25)

so that it can be decomposed over the harmonic fields
({\nabla}q^{n}_{i}(t))_{i\in\overline{1,N}}
,
({\nabla}q_{i}^{k,n}(t))_{i\in\overline{1,N},k\in\overline{1,3},}
analogue to the fields

a priori

and

is

. This concludes the proof of iii) and therefore of the lemma.
∎

Conclusion
.
We come back to the analysis of

Note that the right-hand side goes to zero when

Similarly,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}(t)\cap B(0,M)^{c}}u\otimes u:{\nabla}\psi\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|{\nabla}\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega\cap B(0,M)^{c})}

with the same right-hand side going to zero as

uniformly on

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left|J^{n,\eta}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{\eta}}u\cdot(u\cdot{\nabla}\psi)\right|\leq C\eta^{\frac{1}{3}}

(4.26)

uniformly on

, where

depends on
\psi
,
R_{0},X_{0},E_{0},T,\delta
.

Proof.

5.2
Galerkin approximation scheme and uniform in
n
estimates

Having introduced the mobile Riesz basis
(e^{m}_{\ell}(t))_{\ell\geq 1}
in the previous section,
the goal will be to construct some field

u^{m,n}\left(t\right)=\tilde{u}^{m,n}(t)+u^{m,n,\nabla}(t):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}U_{\ell+4N}^{m,n}\left(t\right)e_{\ell}^{m}\left(t\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{4N}U_{i}^{m,n}\left(t\right)\nabla q_{i}\left(t\right)\text{,}

(5.20)

solution of a variational formulation similar to (
3.1
), with test functions
\psi
of the form

\psi\left(t\right)=\tilde{\psi}(t)+\psi^{\nabla}(t):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\Psi_{\ell+4N}^{m,n}\left(t\right)e_{\ell}^{m}\left(t\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{4N}\Psi_{i}^{m,n}\left(t\right)\nabla q_{i}\left(t\right)

(5.21)

where the coefficients
\Psi_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,4N+n
are smooth over
[0,T]
. The variational formulation takes the following form:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(t\right)}u^{m,n}\left(t\right)\cdot\psi\left(t\right)+\int_{B(0,m)^{c}}u^{m,n,\nabla}\left(t\right)\cdot\psi^{\nabla}\left(t\right)+2\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(s\right)}\mathbb{D}\left(u^{m,n}\right)\cdot\mathbb{D}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}s

(5.22)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}c_{i}\left(r_{i}[u^{m,n}](s)\right)^{2-3\gamma}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(s\right)}\psi\cdot\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(s\right)}\left(u^{m,n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}-\dot{r}_{i}-\dot{x}_{i}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\right)(\psi\cdot u^{m,n})\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial B(0,m)}(u^{m,n}\cdot\mathfrak{n})(\psi\cdot u^{m,n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{m}(0)}u^{m,n}_{0}\cdot\psi\left(0\right)+\int_{B(0,m)^{c}}u^{\nabla}_{0}\cdot\psi^{\nabla}\left(0\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(s\right)}u^{m,n}\cdot(\partial_{t}\psi+u^{m,n}\cdot{\nabla}\psi)\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B(0,m)^{c}}u^{m,n,\nabla}\cdot\partial_{t}\psi^{\nabla}\mathrm{d}s,

where

r_{i}[u^{m,n}](t):=\tilde{r}_{0,i}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\partial B_{i}\left(s\right)}u^{m,n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(5.23)

We also define

x_{i}\left[u^{m,n}\right]\left(t\right):=x_{i}\left(0\right)+3\int_{0}^{t}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\partial B_{i}(s)}(u^{m,n}\cdot\mathfrak{n})\mathfrak{n}\mathrm{d}s.

(5.24)

Note that compared to the original variational formulation (
3.1
), most space integrals are on
\Omega^{m}
instead of
\Omega
, except for a couple of integrals that are on
B(0,m)^{c}
and involve only
u^{m,n,{\nabla}}
and
\psi^{m,n,{\nabla}}
. This is to accomodate the fact that the fields
{\nabla}q_{i}(t)
are defined over the whole of
\Omega(t)
, and to benefit from their orthogonality in
L^{2}(\Omega(t))
. The extra boundary term
\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial B(0,m)}(u^{m,n}\cdot\mathfrak{n})(\psi\cdot u^{m,n})
is added because
u^{m,n}\cdot\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B(0,m)}=u^{m,n,\nabla}\cdot\mathfrak{n}|_{{\partial}B(0,m)}\neq 0
. Let us take a moment to discuss the initial data
u^{m,n}_{0}
. We first notice that
u_{0}
is of the form

u_{0}=\tilde{u}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{4N}U_{0,i}\nabla q_{i}\left(0\right)

where
\tilde{u}_{0}
is the Leray projection of
u_{0}
on
L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega(0))
, so that

\tilde{u}_{0}\cdot\mathfrak{n}=0\text{ on }\cup_{j=1}^{N}\partial B_{j}\left(0\right).

For all
\ell\geq 1
, we define

U_{0,\ell+4N}^{m}:=\left\langle\left(\left.\tilde{u}_{0}\right|_{\Omega^{m}\left(0\right)}\right),e_{\ell}^{m}\left(0\right)\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega^{m}\left(0\right)\right)}.

Finally, we set

u_{0}^{m,n}:=\tilde{u}_{0}^{m,n}+u_{0}^{\nabla}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}U_{0,\ell+4N}^{m}e_{\ell}^{m}(0)+\sum_{i=1}^{4N}U_{0,i}{\nabla}q_{i}(0).

(5.25)

From the orthogonality relation (
5.9
) (applied at
t=0)
, and from the Bessel inequality applied with the orthonormal family
(e_{\ell}^{m}(0))_{\ell\geq 1}
, we get

\displaystyle\|u_{0}^{m,n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{m}(0))}

\displaystyle=\|\tilde{u}_{0}^{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{m}(0))}+\|u_{0}^{\nabla}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{m}(0))}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{P}u_{0}|_{\Omega^{m}(0)}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{m}(0))}+\|u_{0}^{\nabla}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{m}(0))}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{P}u_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega(0))}+\|u_{0}^{\nabla}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega(0))}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{2}.

Let

\displaystyle\tilde{U}^{m,n}

\displaystyle:=\left(U^{m,n}_{\ell+4N}\right)_{\ell\in\overline{1,n}},\quad U^{m,n,\nabla}:=\left(U^{m,n}_{i}\right)_{i\in\overline{1,4N}},

\displaystyle\tilde{U}^{m}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\left(U^{m}_{0,\ell+4N}\right)_{\ell\in\overline{1,n}},\qquad U^{\nabla}_{0}:=\left(U_{0,i}\right)_{i\in\overline{1,4N}}.

Taking into account the orthogonality relation (
5.9
) in
L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega^{m}(t))
, one can check that the variational formulation (
5.22
) is equivalent to a system
of ODEs of the form

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{l}\frac{d}{dt}(\mathcal{G}^{m,n}\left(t\right)\tilde{U}^{m,n})=\tilde{F}^{m,n}\left(t,\tilde{U}^{m,n},U^{m,n,\nabla}\right),\\
\frac{d}{dt}U^{m,n,\nabla}=F^{n,\nabla}\left(t,\tilde{U}^{m,n},U^{m,n,\nabla}\right),\end{array}\right.

(5.26)

for
C^{1}
-functions
\tilde{F}^{m,n},F^{m,n,\nabla}
, with the Gram matrix
\mathcal{G}^{m,n}
was defined in Proposition
5.11
. Relation (
5.9
) explains the decoupling between the time derivative of
\tilde{U}^{m,n}
and

: the Gram matrix of the basis

is block diagonal. Moreover, the orthogonality of
({\nabla}q_{i}(t))_{i\in\overline{1,4N}}
in

explains that the time derivative
\frac{d}{dt}U^{m,n,\nabla}
does not contain any additional matrix.

) on some local time interval
[0,T^{m,n}]
with initial data
\left(\tilde{U}_{0}^{m},U_{0}^{\nabla}\right)
. This gives a local in time solution

5.22
). We can take
\psi=u^{m,n}
as a test function in this variational formulation and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(t\right)}\left|\tilde{u}^{m,n}\left(t\right)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega(t)}\left|u^{m,n,\nabla}\left(t\right)\right|^{2}+\mu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(\tau\right)}\left|\mathbb{D}(u^{m,n})\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau

where

\tilde{E}_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{0}}|u_{0}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{c_{i}}{3\gamma-3}\tilde{r}_{0,i}^{3-3\gamma}.

(5.28)

In particular,

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(t\right)}\left|\tilde{u}^{m,n}\left(t\right)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega(t)}\left|u^{m,n,\nabla}\left(t\right)\right|^{2}\leq\tilde{E}_{0}

which together with Proposition

yields for all

\left|\tilde{U}^{m,n}(t)\right|+\left|U^{m,n,\nabla}(t)\right|\leq C\tilde{E}_{0}.

By the standard blow-up criterion for ODE’s, this implies that we can take
T^{m,n}=T
.

By the uniform control of
U^{m,n,\nabla}
and the control of
{\nabla}q_{i}(t)
in
H^{1}(\Omega(t))
,
cf.
(
4.8
)-(

), we get a uniform bound on

), this implies

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(\tau\right)}\left|\mathbb{D}(\tilde{u}^{m,n})\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau,+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(\tau\right)}\left|\mathbb{D}(u^{m,n,\nabla})\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C\tilde{E}_{0}.

(5.29)

Adapting the arguments of the last paragraph of Section

, one gets analogues of (

) and (

|\dot{U}^{m,n,\nabla}|\leq C(\tilde{E}_{0}),\quad\left\|\dot{R}\left[u^{m,n}\right]\right\|_{H^{1}\left(0,T\right)}+\left\|\dot{X}\left[u^{m,n}\right]\right\|_{H^{1}\left(0,T\right)}\leq C(\tilde{E}_{0})

where

and

.
In the following Proposition we establish an estimate for the time

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
T^{\ast}
given by Proposition
5.11
. For all

Proof.

From (
2.6
) and from the weak formulation (
5.22
) we deduce

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T^{\ast}}\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(s\right)}\partial_{t}u^{m,n}\cdot\tilde{\psi}\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{T^{\ast}}\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(s\right)}u^{m,n}\cdot\partial_{t}\tilde{\psi}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{T^{\ast}}\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(s\right)}\operatorname{div}(u^{m,n}\cdot\tilde{\psi}v^{ALE})\mathrm{d}s

Then standard manipulations and the uniform bound (

) imply

\left|\int_{0}^{T^{\ast}}\int_{\Omega^{m}\left(s\right)}\partial_{t}u^{m,n}\cdot\tilde{\psi}\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq C(\tilde{E}_{0})\left\|\tilde{\psi}\right\|_{L^{4}(0,T^{\ast};H_{\sigma}^{1}(\Omega^{m}))}.

(5.32)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega[X^{h},R^{h}](t)}|u^{h}(t)|^{2}+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega[X^{h},R^{h}](s)}|\mathbb{D}(u^{h})(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{c_{i}}{3\gamma-3}r_{i}[u^{h}](t)^{3-3\gamma}\leq E_{0}

as well as a uniform lower bound

Note that for all

),

\forall 1\leq i\leq N,\forall t\in[0,T],\quad r^{h}_{i}(t)\geq c(E_{0})>0.

and in turn

|\dot{X}^{h}(t)|\leq C(R_{0},E_{0},\delta,T),\quad\text{hence }\>|X^{h}(t)|\leq C(X_{0},R_{0},E_{0},\delta,T).

After these uniform Lipschitz estimates, one can derive

6.3
Extension of the solution up to collision

It remains to prove the last part of the main Theorem

, which is standard. We assume that we can not construct a weak solution on an arbitrary time interval
[0,T)
, and introduce the supremum

of all times

such that there exists a weak solution on
[0,T)
. This supremum is actually a maximum: if we select a sequence of times

with

a weak solution on
[0,T^{n})
, we can apply the sequential continuity result of Section
4.2
and obtain a solution

on
[0,T^{*})
. We now wish to prove that (

) holds for

. We argue by contradiction and suppose that there exists
\delta>0
such that

\forall t<T^{\ast},\quad\inf_{i\neq j}\left(|x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)|-r_{i}(t)-r_{j}(t)\right)\geq\delta.

(6.6)

Then, we can select a time

arbitrary close to
T^{\ast}
such that

is well-defined, with

. We can then build a weak solution

on

, with initial condition

. Due to the separation condition (

), the time

is lower bounded by a constant depending only on

, the initial data and

. In particular, it can be taken independent of

. Gluing the two solutions, that is settting

we obtain a weak solution
\overline{u}
on

, which contradicts the definition of

.

This concludes the proof of Theorem

.

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{t}^{(k)}\omega_{R}\left(t\right)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(B_{R,2R}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}\left\|\partial_{t}^{(k)}\tilde{\psi}\left(t\right)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(B_{R,2R}\right)}.

By slightly abusing notations, we observe that
\tilde{\psi}_{R}:=\chi_{R}\tilde{\psi}+\omega_{R}

Observe that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\tilde{\psi}-\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{R}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega\left(0\right)))}

(C.2)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{t}\tilde{\psi}-\partial_{t}\tilde{\psi}_{R}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega\left(0\right)))}

(C.3)

(C.4)

Corollary C.3
.

For all

with
{\partial}_{t}\psi,{\nabla}\psi\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))

\sup_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}\left\|\psi(t)-\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Omega(t)\right)}+\left\|\psi-\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{1}(Q([0,T]))}\leq\varepsilon.

is given by the previous proposition, while
