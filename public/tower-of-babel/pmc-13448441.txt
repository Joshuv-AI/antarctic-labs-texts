Amplicon-based DNA and RNA unified NGS for enhanced fusion variant detection in suboptimal real-world NSCLC FFPE specimens✰
Authors: Qin Feng, Yue Wang, Mengli Huang, Xin Yang, Shenyi Lian, Ping Wang, Dongbo Qiao, Bei Zhang, Ding Zhang, Ning Gao, Jing Ma, Yincong Gu, Lei Xiong, Dongmei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Unified DNA+RNA amplicon NGS delivered guideline-relevant biomarkers from 759 real-world NSCLC FFPE samples, including small biopsies and cytology.•High success rates (>95%) across all specimen types suggest that a single low-input assay can often replace sequential DNA→RNA testing and reduce repeat biopsies.•Robust performance in 5–8-year archived FFPE samples (≥96% success) enables treatment decisions even when only old tissue blocks are available.•D+R NGS confirmed 99.3% of known fusions/exon-skipping events and rescued additional actionable alterations that would otherwise be missed by standard workflows.•Detecting 18.2% more targetable fusions in DNA-NGS–negative cases has the potential to change first-line treatment selection from empirical chemo/chemo-immunotherapy to matched TKIs.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, and represents about 85% of all lung cancer cases in China [1]. Over the past decade, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have revolutionized the therapeutic landscape for NSCLC ([2,3]). Targeted therapies for anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), ROS proto-oncogene 1, receptor tyrosine kinase (ROS1), ret proto-oncogene (RET), neurotrophic receptor tyrosine kinase 1/2/3 (NTRK1/2/3) fusions, and MET proto-oncogene, receptor tyrosine kinase (MET) exon 14 skipping have shown high response rates and lasting effectiveness, significantly improving survival and symptom control, resulting in their approval for first-line treatment in advanced NSCLC [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Precisely identifying these molecular alterations is essential to identify patients who may benefit from these targeted treatments, and delayed or incomplete detection of these alterations may deprive patients of highly effective TKIs and result in suboptimal first-line treatment choices.

RNA-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) directly detects gene fusion and skipping events at the transcription level, avoiding potential missed detections arising from DNA-based approaches [10]. Previous studies have shown that RNA-based NGS can detect 10%−15.3% more fusion and skipping events with negative DNA-NGS results [[11], [12], [13]]. A study showed 8.7% (4/46) of MET exon 14 skipping mutations detected by RNA-based NGS were missed by DNA-based NGS [14]. Accordingly, National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend RNA-based NGS as a complementary assay following negative DNA-based testing. However, the clinical implementation of this sequential testing strategy is impeded by limited sample availability, extended turnaround times, and elevated costs [15]. In routine practice, these constraints mean that some patients either initiate non-targeted systemic therapy before their full molecular profile is available or never receive confirmatory RNA testing at all, limiting the real-world impact of guideline recommendations.

>70% of clinical NSCLC samples originate from small biopsies or cytological specimens in routine practice, which frequently provide inadequate quantities of nucleic acids for analysis [16]. Archival formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples serve as an important alternative for patients with advanced NSCLC who are unable to undergo repeat biopsies. Nevertheless, RNA in FFPE deteriorates over time, with one study showing that 20.4% of samples stored for two years were not amenable to sequencing due to poor RNA quality [17]. Another report showed that only 68.9% of archived FFPE samples produced usable RNA, with success rates dropping to under 30% for samples stored for more than five years [14]. As a result, many patients with advanced NSCLC are unable to receive guideline-recommended targeted therapies simply because their available specimens do not meet the technical requirements of conventional RNA-based assays. These collective challenges highlight the need for NGS methods capable of simultaneously detecting single-nucleotide variants/insertions and deletions (SNV/Indel) at the DNA level and fusions/skipping events at the RNA level, while maintaining high performance across diverse and suboptimal clinical sample types, so that clinically actionable alterations can still be captured in specimens that are actually used for real-world treatment decision-making.

We have developed an amplicon-based DNA and RNA unified next-generation sequencing (D+R NGS) assay with enhanced compatibility for various FFPE samples. Initially, we validated the concordance between D+R NGS and droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) in fresh formalin-fixed samples. Subsequently, we tested its performance in fusion/skipping detection using surgical specimens, needle biopsy samples and cytological samples. Finally, we verified its efficacy in archived FFPE specimens that had undergone reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) or DNA-based NGS.

Methods

Patients and samples

A total of 759 NSCLC tissue samples diagnosed at Peking University Cancer Hospital were collected and tested by D+R NGS. Concordance was evaluated in 46 freshly fixed FFPE samples with ddPCR results. Performance across specimen types was assessed in 172 surgical, 168 biopsy and 142 cytology FFPE samples processed within one year. Additionally, archival FFPE samples stored for 1–8 years were tested, including 95 previously analyzed by RT-PCR and 148 by DNA-based NGS for fusions and MET exon 14 skipping. 12 samples were subjected to both RT-PCR and DNA-based NGS tests. Sample details are provided in Fig. 1. Amplicon -based D+R NGS

DNA and RNA were extracted from NSCLC tissue samples prepared as FFPE sections on slides. Either DNA (10 ng) or RNA (10 ng) from the tissue samples was required for each target amplification reaction. The RNA was enzymatically converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) and the target regions in the cDNA or DNA were specifically amplified with PCR. Libraries were generated with 3DMed OncoTM Core Tissue Kit and Automated NGS Prep System ANDiS 500. The workflow of amplicon library preparation involves two rounds of PCR amplification. After template preparation, sequencing by synthesis was performed to determine the nucleic acid sequences of the amplified libraries. The DNA and RNA reads were mapped to the human reference genome to detect single-nucleotide variants or deletions in 35 genes at the DNA level and fusions/skipping events involving 15 genes at the RNA level (Supplementary Table 1). 3DMed’s proprietary TiNAiLab software analyzes the sequencing data and generates reports, and the total turnaround time was 3 days (Supplementary Figure 1). For RNA-expression quality control, the internal control genes coiled-coil domain containing 6 (CCDC6) and Bardet-Biedl syndrome 7 (BBS7) were each required to exceed 40 reads. The limit of detection (LOD) for SNV/Indel analysis was 2%, and the LOD for fusion detection was 100 copies per reaction.

DNA-based NGS

Eligible tumor tissue specimens with ≥20% tumor cells were retained for subsequent analyses. The sample processing and sequencing methods were performed as previously described in detail [16], with small modifications. In brief, genomic DNA was extracted using the ReliaPrepTM FFPE gDNA Miniprep System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and quantified using the QubitTM dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). The next-generation sequencing (NGS) library was constructed using the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA) and target capture was subjected to probe-based hybridization with a customized NGS panel. Captured libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with 100-base pair (bp) paired-end reads and a mean coverage depth of 500×. Raw data of samples were aligned with the reference genome hg19 via the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (v0.7.12). PCR duplicate reads were removed and sequence metrics were collected using Picard (v1.130) and SAMtools (v1.1.19), respectively. Variant calling was applied only in the targeted regions. For fusion detection, the requirement is that the support reads should be ≥3. For splice skipping detection, a variant qualifies if it is in a known splice-related hotspot with a variant allele frequencie (VAF) ≥ 1%.

RT-PCR

Detection of NSCLC-related fusion genes was performed using a real-time based PCR.

Droplet digital PCR

Droplet digital PCR was performed using a QX200 ddPCR system (Bio-Rad).

Sanger

Sanger sequencing was completed by Sangon Biotech® company (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd.).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on FFPE sections to assess MET (clone: SP44, Roche Tissue Diagnostics) and ROS1 (clone: OTI1A, ZSbio Diagnostics) protein expression. MET overexpression was defined as ≥50% of tumor cells showed moderate to strong staining. ROS1 staining intensity was evaluated as follows: IHC 3+ (≥10% strong staining), 2+ (≥10% strong or moderate staining), 1+ (≥10% weak staining), or 0 (no staining/≤10% weak staining).

Tumor area assessment

Tumor area assessment was performed using the APTIME analysis software (3D Medicines) on hematoxylin and eosin (HE)-stained sections.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software v 4.0.3. Differences between groups were analyzed using either a t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, and a Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. All statistical tests were two-sided and p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Analytical validation of D+R NGS with ddPCR in fresh FFPE samples

To evaluate the performance of the D+R NGS assay in fresh FFPE samples, 46 NSCLC samples with known ddPCR results were tested. The group included 28 epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations (19del=13, L858R=6, T790M=5, exon 20 insertions=4) and 18 gene fusions (ALK=5,ROS1=5,RET=4), as well as 4 MET exon 14 skipping events. D+R NGS accurately detected all alterations in these samples, achieving 100% concordance with ddPCR (Table 1). Further analysis showed a significant positive correlation between the VAFs of SNV/Indels detected by D+R NGS and ddPCR (R = 0.997, p < 0.001, Fig. 2A). Performance of D+R NGS across surgical, cytological, and biopsy specimens

To evaluate the performance of D+R NGS across different sampling types, this study included 172 surgical specimens, 142 cytological samples and 168 needle biopsy specimens processed within one year. Success rates were 99.4% (171/172) for surgical specimens, 98.6% (140/142) for cytological samples, and 95.2% (160/168) for biopsy specimens. Overall driver gene detection rates across sample types were comparable at 76.6%, 65.7%, and 71.9% for surgical specimens, cytological samples, and needle biopsy specimens, respectively, with SNV/indel positive rates of 67.8%, 56.4%, and 60.0% fusion/skipping positive rates of 9.9%, 9.3%, and 11.9% (Table 2). Among samples with recorded tumor areas, the mean tumor areas of surgical specimens, cytological samples, and biopsy specimens were 21.9 mm², 12.3 mm², and 3.53 mm², respectively. Additionally, the proportions of samples with tumor areas <3 mm² were 18.7%, 64.5%, and 70.4% for these three sample types (p < 0.001, Supplementary Figure 2). Despite the higher proportion of small tumors in biopsy samples, success rates for this subset remained robust at 96%, 98%, and 92% for surgical, cytological, and biopsy specimens, respectively. Performance of D+R NGS with low RNA input

An exploratory analysis on the RNA input limit was conducted. Eight freshly prepared FFPE samples were selected, including four fusion-negative samples as negative controls and four fusion-positive samples with VAF of 17.9%, 9.2%, 7.3% and 3.2%, respectively. When RNA input was reduced to 10 ng and 5 ng, all positive results were detected. At a dilution of 1 ng, only the sample with the lowest VAF (7.3% ALK) failed internal control gene read count criteria and showed no fusion detection (Supplementary Table 3).

Building on these findings, risk testing was conducted on 11 clinical samples with RNA extraction <10 ng that failed routine QC, mimicking real-world scenarios where nucleic acid quantity is insufficient for standard workflows. As detailed in Supplementary Table 2, the assay successfully identified actionable alterations in 4 biopsy samples and 1 cytology sample, including four EGFR mutations and one ALK fusion, even in one biopsy sample that failed QC.

Performance of D+R NGS in archived samples

To systematically evaluate the performance of D+R NGS in archived samples, two clinically collected groups of FFPE samples were analyzed, containing specimens stored for 1–2 years, 3 years, 4 years, and 5–8 years. One group had RT-PCR results at diagnosis for fusions and MET exon14 skipping, while the other had DNA-based NGS results for these alterations. D+R NGS was applied to retest all samples, and discordant results were validated using a third method (Table 3). It was observed that the number of detected support reads of fusion decreased significantly along with increasing sample storage duration (1–2 years vs 3 years, p = 0.048; 1–2 years vs 4 years, p = 0.004; 1–2 years vs 5–8 years, p < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Three fusion negative samples with 10 ng of extracted RNA were excluded. One from the RT-PCR group, resulting in an overall success rate of 98.9% (94/95) with a 96% (94/95) success rate in the 5–8-year subgroup (Fig. 3B). In the RT-PCR group, which included known fusion statuses for ALK, ROS1, RET, or NTRK, there were 60 positive samples (ALK=13, ROS1=18, RET=14, MET ex14 skipping=15) and 34 fusion-negative cases. D+R NGS successfully detected all fusions (100%, 60/60), and an additional 3 fusion-positive cases (8.8%, 3/34), including 2 ROS1 fusions and 1 RET fusion. All three were validated by Sanger sequencing (Table 3).

In the DNA-based NGS group, fusion statuses for ALK, ROS1, RET, NTRK, neuregulin 1 (NRG1), EGFR, B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (BRAF), fibroblast growth factor receptor 1/2/3 (FGFR1/2/3), and MET exon 14 skipping were known. There were 80 positive samples (ALK=47, ROS1=14, RET=14, MET=3, NTRK=1, NRG1=1) and 66 negative cases. D+R NGS successfully detected 79 fusions. The single discordant case was an ALK(20)::LRRTM4(4) reverse fusion, which likely lacked transcriptional activity at the RNA level, while insufficient sample availability precluded further validation. D+R NGS identified an additional 12 fusion-positive cases (18.2%, 12/66), including 6 ROS1, 3 MET, 1 ALK, 1 RET, and 1 BRAF fusions (Table 3). These additional detections were distributed across storage time as follows: 2 cases from the 1–2-year subgroup, 1 from the 3-year subgroup, 3 from the 4-year subgroup, and 6 from the 5–8-year subgroup. In the combined 231 unique samples, 16 MET exon 14 skipping and 126 fusions were identified (Fig. 3C). ALK fusions were the most frequent (n = 60), with V1 (EML4 [13]::ALK [20]) comprising the largest proportion at 43.3% (n = 26), followed by V3a/b (EML4 [6]::ALK [20]) at 31.7% (n = 19), together accounting for over 75% of all ALK fusions. The V2 (EML4 [20]::ALK [20]) represented 13.3% (n = 8), while other types including V5 (EML4 [18]::ALK [20], 5.0%, n = 3), V8a/b (EML4 [17]::ALK [20], n = 1), and V5a/b (EML4 [2]::ALK [20], n = 1) were observed (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Figure 4A). A total of 34 ROS1 fusions were detected, characterized by diverse partners. The most common was CD74 [6]::ROS1(32/34) (n = 11), followed by EZR [10]::ROS1(32/34/35) fusion (n = 8). Other fusion partners included SDC4 (n = 6), SLC34A2 (n = 5), MYO5C (n = 1), TFG (n = 1), and TPM3 (n = 2), highlighting the heterogeneity of ROS1 rearrangements (Fig. 3E). Among 29 RET fusions, the predominant type was KIF5B::RET (n = 23). Other RET fusion partners included CCDC6 (n = 5) and ELKS/RAB6-interacting/CAST family member 1 (ERC1) (n = 1) (Supplementary Figure 4B). Additionally, rare fusions of NTRK (n = 1), NRG1 (n = 1), and BRAF (n = 1) were also detected.

Discussion

This study systematically validated the performance of an amplicon-based D+R NGS technology in 759 NSCLC FFPE samples of diverse origins and storage time. In fresh surgical specimens (n = 46) validated by ddPCR, D+R NGS achieved 100% concordance for EGFR mutations (28/28) and fusion/skipping (18/18). For recent FFPE samples with <1 year storage time, success rates were 99.4% (171/172) in surgical specimens, 95.2% (160/168) in biopsy specimens, and 98.6% (140/142) in cytology specimens, with comparable SNV/Indel and fusion detection rates across sample types (67.8%vs. 60.0% vs. 56.4% for SNV/Indel; 9.9% vs. 11.9% vs. 9.3% for fusions). In archived FFPE specimens (1–8 years storage), D+R NGS confirmed 100% concordance (60/60) with known fusions in PCR-tested samples and identified 3 additional PCR negative ROS1 and RET fusions. Compared with DNA-based NGS, D+R NGS detected 18.2% (12/66) more fusion variants in DNA-based NGS negative samples, primarily ROS1 fusion (6/12) and MET skipping (3/12). Overall, D+R NGS demonstrates excellent robustness, high sensitivity, and broad applicability in FFPE samples of varying quality, overcoming key bottlenecks in NSCLC molecular detection by enabling reliable fusion and skipping analysis in small biopsies and long-term archived specimens.

According to enrichment methods, NGS can be divided into hybrid capture-based NGS and amplicon-based NGS. Hybrid capture-based NGS remains the mainstream for NSCLC molecular profiling because of its broad coverage and flexible probe design ([17,18]). Its nonspecific binding between DNA probes and RNA exons hinders simultaneous DNA and RNA co-detection, necessitating parallel or sequential DNA- and RNA-based sequencing [20]. In parallel detection scenarios, challenges include complex workflows, increased sample consumption and cost, while sequential detection faces prolonged turnaround times, as it requires waiting for DNA results before initiating RNA testing. In this study, we developed a D+R NGS method, which uses specific primers targeting exons and introns ([15,22]) to enable simultaneous analysis of multiple variant types at the dual nucleic acid level. D+R NGS leverages the advantage of initial PCR amplification of target sequences ([23,24]), reducing the RNA input requirement to as low as 10 ng ([14,25]). In this study, even when the RNA input is as low as 1 ng or RNA extraction fails QC, certain driver gene fusions can still be detected. This highlights the technology's unique capability to handle extreme sample limitations. In scenarios with low starting RNA input, D+R NGS demonstrates unique advantages.

D+R NGS achieved success rates exceeding 95% across all sample types, in contrast to previously reported RNA sequencing whose success rates are highly dependent on sample type. For example, a previous amplicon-based sequencing study using samples with RNA input ≥10 ng and concentration ≥2.5 ng/μL reported RNA detection success rates of 100% in surgical specimens, 80.4%−84.4% in cytological samples, and 81.1% in core needle biopsies [19]. Another capture-based NGS study using an RNA input of 10 ng and DV200 ≥50% achieved success rates of 95% in surgical samples, 73%−82% in cytological samples, and 100% in biopsy specimens [20]. Notably, biopsy specimens in our study had significantly smaller tumor areas (1.59 vs. 12.31 mm² in surgical samples, p < 0.001) but still maintained a 95.2% success rate. This superior performance positions D+R NGS as a leading solution for molecular profiling in clinical small biopsy scenarios. In contrast to a prior amplicon-based study requiring ≥30% tumor cellularity and ≥1 mm² surface area for 100% success, D+R NGS achieved a 100% success rate in samples with tumor surface areas >3 mm² regardless of cellularity [21]. This suggests that tumor surface area may be a more critical determinant of assay success than cellularity alone. However, the specific cutoff values for optimal tumor surface area require further validation.

Although RNA in FFPE samples is prone to fragmentation due to long storage, studies have reported successful RNA extraction and gene detection from FFPE samples stored for >3 years or even over 10 years [[22], [23], [24]]. RNA quality assessment should be prioritized over relying solely on storage duration [25]. Hybrid capture-based methods, which use metrics such as DV200 (the proportion of RNA fragments > 200 nt) to assess RNA quality in FFPE samples, indicate that ∼20% of FFPE RNA within 4 years may be unsuitable for analysis based on RNA integrity number equivalent (RIN) and DV200 criteria ([23,26]). D+R NGS addresses fragmentation by designing 60–80 bp RNA target amplicons, eliminating the need for DV200 metrics. Instead, RNA quality is validated via read counts of internal control genes CCDC6 and BBS7. While fusion support reads decline with storage duration (p < 0.001), D+R NGS maintains ≥96% success rates even in 5–8-year archived samples, detecting fusions missed by RT-PCR and DNA-based NGS. In Song et al.'s amplicon-based study, archived samples had an overall success rate of 68.9%, which dropped to 42.8% for samples stored for >5 years; this lower performance was attributed to template competition from co-extracted DNA and RNA [26]. In contrast, D+R NGS uses sequential extraction and pre-amplification to optimize nucleic acid ratios, achieving superior success rates. These findings highlight D+R NGS’s ability to expand the practical utility of long-term archived FFPE samples, offering critical clinical value for molecular profiling in advanced NSCLC.

RT-PCR is a commonly used method for fusion detection, known for its high sensitivity and short turnaround time [[27], [28], [29]]. However, its dependence on predefined probes with known breakpoints poses challenges in identifying noncanonical fusions. Additionally, the complexity of breakpoint locations may lead to occasional missed detections. This limitation is exemplified by the three undetected ROS1 and RET fusions in the current study. Regarding DNA-based NGS, as fusion breakpoints are mostly located in introns, it is prone to missed detection due to incomplete intron coverage and difficulties in breakpoint localization. Consistent with a previous study, D+R NGS identified 18.2% additional driver gene alterations in DNA-based NGS-negative samples, primarily ROS1 (6/12) and MET exon 14 skipping (3/12) [11]. ROS1’s intronic repeat sequences and MET exon 14 skipping’s reliance on RNA splicing events are inherently challenging for DNA-based approaches to detect accurately ([13,30]). Notably, an ALK [20]::LRRTM4 [4] reverse fusion detected by DNA-based NGS was not confirmed by D+R NGS, underscoring RNA-based NGS’s specificity for actively transcribed fusions. As demonstrated by Li et al., only ALK fusions detected by DNA-based NGS and validated at the RNA and protein levels were functionally active and predictive of targeted therapy efficacy [12]. Additionally, D+R NGS precisely discriminates ALK fusion subtypes, which exhibit distinct sensitivities to ALK-TKIs [31], potentially guiding treatment selection. These observations are also consistent with recent evidence emphasizing the clinical utility of RNA-based NGS for actionable fusion detection in NSCLC [32], the evolving role of different fusion-detection methods [33], the importance of reference standards for fusion assays [34], and the feasibility of ALK and ROS1 testing in cytology specimens [35].

This study highlights the robustness of amplicon-based D+R NGS across diverse FFPE samples. However, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, the retrospective nature of the study and the absence of multicenter validation may impact the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, while the assessment of archived samples primarily examines the temporal impact of RNA degradation, it does not assess other parameters such as fixation time and storage temperature on nucleic acid integrity, potentially leading to variable outcomes. Lastly, the clinical relevance of newly identified fusions in previously negative cohorts has not been prospectively linked to the efficacy of targeted therapies, necessitating further validation to ascertain the therapeutic potential of these variants.

Overall, D+R NGS demonstrates robustness, high sensitivity, and broad applicability in FFPE samples of varying quality. It effectively addresses challenges in detecting fusion and skipping events in real-world settings, particularly small biopsies and long-term archived specimens. This technology serves as a pivotal tool for precise diagnosis and treatment of NSCLC, facilitating the extension of targeted therapy benefits to a wider patient population. Future research should prioritize multicenter prospective trials incorporating clinical response data, while also expanding sample sizes and encompassing diverse cancer types to validate the technique's generalizability.
