DNA, RNA, and histone methylation regulation enzymes and their crosstalk in colorectal carcinogenesis and progression: a review of molecular mechanisms, clinical implications, and future perspectives
Authors: Sangni Qian, Hao Song, Lu Huang, Hui Hua, Xi’nan Zhang, Zixuan Li, Maomao Pu, Haijun Huang, Jianbin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the most common malignant cancers of the digestive system, and its initiation and progression are closely related to both genetic and epigenetic mechanisms. Three major forms of modifications, viz. DNA methylation, RNA m6A methylation, and histone methylation, play important roles in regulating gene expression at various stages of transcription and translation. These methylation processes are dynamic and reversible, relying on the functions of methyltransferases, demethylases, and methylation-binding proteins. Extensive studies have shown that DNA, RNA m6A, and histone methylation significantly impact multiple pathological and physiological processes in CRC, including carcinogenesis, recurrence, metastasis, resistance to both radiotherapy and chemotherapy, as well as immune regulation. Advances in high-throughput sequencing and laboratory techniques have facilitated the identification of methylation regulation enzymes with aberrant expression at the DNA, RNA, and protein levels, revealing their clinical potential for early diagnosis and treatment of CRC. The upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling these methylation regulation enzymes are crucial for understanding alterations in methylation patterns. Current evidence identifies several key mechanisms, including posttranslational modifications, epigenetic regulation, and genetic alterations, which collectively influence the expression, activity, and stability of methyltransferases, demethylases, and binding proteins. These mechanisms thereby modulate the dynamic methylation landscape across various biological contexts. Furthermore, the complex crosstalk among DNA, RNA m6A, and histone methylation is increasingly being elucidated, highlighting a need for further investigation in CRC. In this review, we systematically summarize the molecular mechanisms, clinical applications, and crosstalk involving DNA methylation, RNA m6A methylation, and histone methylation, along with their related enzymes in the development of CRC. This review aims to provide new insights and directions that underscore the significant role of epigenetic methylation modifications and their associated enzymes in CRC.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Epigenetic, Methylation, Crosstalk, Expression

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent digestive system malignancies worldwide. Global statistics indicates that the incidence of CRC remains high, ranking third among malignant tumors in terms of incidence and second in mortality rates [1]. CRC has a heavy social impact and disease burden, making it a serious public health concern [2]. There is an urgent need for novel predictive and diagnostic methods and therapeutic targets for CRC. According to the “adenoma–cancer” sequential genetic model proposed by Fearon and Vogelstein, the development of CRC is recognized as a multistage, multistep process involving a variety of environmental, genetic, and epigenetic factors, whose dynamic interactions promote the progression from normal mucosa to precancerous lesions and ultimately to invasive cancers [3, 4]. However, its underlying mechanisms are not yet fully elucidated. Thus, understanding the additional mechanisms of CRC carcinogenesis, progression, and metastasis remains a focus of current research to provide evidence for early prevention, diagnosis, and treatment target.

Epigenetics refers to heritable changes in the genome without altering the genome sequence, encompassing DNA methylation, histone modification, and noncoding RNA regulation [5]. Recently, RNA methylation modification at the posttranscriptional level, also categorized under epigenetics, has emerged as a new research hotspot in life science and medicine. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification is the most common and abundant type of RNA methylation in eukaryotes [6]. It plays a role in various RNA metabolic processes, including translation, nuclear export, and degradation [6–8]. Methylation modifications are specific to particular cells and tissues, influencing chromatin status and gene expression [9, 10].

Genomic studies have underscored that epigenetic alterations coincide with genetic alterations, and the complex interplay among them leads to carcinogenesis, malignant progression, and drug resistance in CRC. At present, three main molecular pathogenic mechanisms of CRC have been discovered: the chromosomal instability (CIN) pathway, the microsatellite instability (MSI) pathway, and the CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP) pathway [11]. The gradual accumulation of key mutations in genes such as APC, KRAS, TP53, and BRAF underlies activation of critical signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) pathways [12]. CRC can be distinguished into three methylation subgroups. CIMP-high is characterized by proximal tumors, BRAF mutations, MLH1 methylation, and MSI. CIMP-low is also associated with proximal tumors and frequently harbors KRAS mutations, but rarely exhibits MSI or BRAF mutations. In contrast, CIMP-negative is linked to distal CRC and shows a high prevalence of TP53 mutations [13]. Numerous studies have highlighted the bidirectional crosstalk between epigenetic and genetic alterations in regulating gene transcription and signaling pathways in CRC [14]. Genetic alterations can directly or indirectly modulate the expression and activity of epigenetic enzymes, thereby regulating the methylation patterns and contributing to differences in CRC phenotypes [15]. Conversely, epigenetic modifications can alter the expression of genes affected by genetic mutations and influence the accessibility of genetic alterations, thereby modulating their functional impact [14]. Consequently, epigenetic mechanisms play critical roles in CRC pathogenesis. Further understanding of the molecular mechanisms of epigenetic regulation and its regulatory factors will provide new insights into CRC progression and support the development of combined therapeutic strategies incorporating epigenetic drugs.

A large accumulation of evidence has shown that changes in DNA methylation, RNA m6A methylation, and histone methylation are crucial in the occurrence and development of CRC (Fig. 1). Aberrant methylation patterns are molecular hallmarks in CRC development, and they can affect the expression of various RNAs, including messenger RNA (mRNA), long noncoding RNA (lncRNA), microRNA (miRNA), and circular RNA (circRNA) [16, 17]. For instance, global DNA hypomethylation and hypermethylation at specific gene promoters has been observed in CRC tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues [17, 18]. Moreover, elevated total m6A levels have been detected in CRC tissues, particularly within the 3′ untranslated terminal region (UTR) of certain genes, and poorly differentiated carcinomas exhibit higher m6A levels than highly differentiated ones [19, 20]. Different histone methylation marks also exhibit distinct characteristics and functions in CRC, with active marks such as H3K4me1 and H3K79me3 promoting gene expression, whereas repressive marks such as H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 inhibit gene expression [21, 22].

The biological processes of DNA, RNA m6A, and histone methylation in the development of colorectal cancer

Under normal physiological conditions, the dynamic balance of genomic methylation patterns are maintained by methyltransferases and demethylases, ensuring chromatin accessibility and providing a stable environment for cellular activities [5, 23]. Dysregulation and inactivation of regulatory enzymes are primary causes of methylation changes, leading to abnormal gene expression and cell phenotypes, thereby contributing to disease development [24–26]. Additionally, methylation levels and related regulation enzymes hold significant clinical potential for diagnosis and treatment of CRC [27, 28]. However, during the occurrence and development of CRC, the upstream molecular mechanisms of methylation regulation enzymes and crosstalk among DNA methylation, RNA m6A methylation, and histone methylation still need to be investigated to unravel the epigenetic regulatory network and provide new clues for prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of CRC.

In this review, we systematically summarize the mechanisms and relationships among methylation-related enzymes and their upstream regulatory factors in the occurrence and development of CRC, addressing DNA, RNA, and histone levels. We also explore the role of crosstalk among these methylation modifications in colorectal carcinogenesis and progression. Furthermore, we discuss the clinical applications of aberrant methylation patterns in CRC. This review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the significance of epigenetic methylation changes in CRC and to propose new ideas and strategies for etiological research, predictive biomarkers selection, and exploration of novel therapeutic targets and approaches.

DNA methylation in colorectal cancer

DNA methylation involves the transfer of methyl groups to specific bases, including the cytosine 5-carbon, adenine 6-nitrogen, and guanine 7-nitrogen, through covalent binding with S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) as a methyl donor, a process catalyzed by methyltransferases [29]. In mammals, 5-methylcytosine (5mC) is the only form of DNA methylation, occurring at cytosine 5-carbon in cytosine–guanine dinucleotide (CpG) [30]. CpG islands, which are clusters of CpG sites in the human genome, typically exhibit low methylation under normal conditions, with most located in the promoter regions of functional genes [31].

DNA methylation is a crucial regulatory mechanism for genomic imprinting, chromosome structure, and gene expression, closely associated to the carcinogenesis and development of CRC. Studies on methylation profiling in CRC have revealed a pattern of site-specific hypermethylation and genome-wide hypomethylation [17, 18, 32]. Generally, promoter hypermethylation inhibits gene transcription, especially tumor suppressor genes (TSGs), while hypermethylation in the gene body might promote transcription [33, 34]. Global hypomethylation has been commonly observed in regions containing repetitive sequences such as transposon elements, centromeric repeats, and ribosomal DNA, causing transposon movement, genomic instability, chromatin structure abnormality, and gene mutation [35]. Gene-specific hypomethylation of oncogene promoters activates oncogenes and induces cell carcinogenesis [36]. Aberrant methylation has been documented in virtually all categories of genes in CRC, encompassing protein coding genes including key driver genes such as APC and MLH1 [37–39], as well as those encoding noncoding RNAs such as lncRNA, miRNAs, and circRNA [40–43]. Notably, changes in DNA methylation are already observed in precancerous lesions of CRC, demonstrating a gradual increase or decrease from normal colonic mucosa to adenomas and ultimately to carcinoma, indicating their roles as early molecular events in colorectal carcinogenesis [32, 33].

DNA methylation changes typically occur during the early stages of carcinogenesis, further supporting their status as early molecular events in cancer development. Significant correlations between DNA methylation changes and genetic alterations have been identified in CRC progression. For instance, in KRAS-mutant CRCs, promoter hypermethylation leads to silencing of the CDKN2A gene [44]. BRAF-mutant CRCs are frequently microsatellite unstable and characterized by high levels of DNA methylation, which are associated with poor prognosis [45]. Specifically, the BRAF V600E mutation promotes continuous activation of the Wnt pathway by inducing hypermethylation of regulators such as secreted frizzled-related protein 1 (SFRP1), facilitating the accumulation of downstream genetic mutations, ultimately creating a positive feedback loop that enhances Wnt signaling and drives malignant proliferation [46]. Additionally, somatic isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutations are associated with BRAF mutations but not with KRAS mutations or MSI, and all IDH-mutant CRCs were CIMP-positive [47]. Conversely, aberrant DNA methylation can silence TSGs that have acquired genetic alterations, further compromising their tumor suppressive function and accelerating tumor progression [48].

DNA methylation can regulate gene expression through dynamic interactions with histone modifications. This can inhibit transcription by directly obstructing transcription factor binding to promoter regions or by recruiting methyl-CpG-binding domain (MBD) proteins to form transcriptional repressive complexes [49]. Histone acetylation, a key histone modification, plays a critical role in modulating chromatin structure and transcriptional activity [50]. Under conditions of low-density methylation, methyl-binding proteins recruited histone deacetylases (HDACs), which remove the acetyl groups added by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) [51]. This results in reduced histone acetylation, chromatin compaction, and transcriptional repression. Furthermore, extensive crosstalk between DNA methylation and histone methylation has also been established in the regulation of gene transcription [52], and this will be described in detail in the corresponding sections.

As a relatively stable and tissue-specific modification in the human genome, DNA methylation shows great potential as a biomarker for CRC diagnosis, primary tumor traceability, and treatment response prediction, offering promising avenues for early screening and clinical application [53, 54]. Genome-wide methylome analyses and candidate gene studies have identified differentially methylated CpG sites and their associated genes as independent indicators for CRC. For example, Yu et al. developed a tissue-based prognostic classifier (MePEC) for early-stage CRC using four methylation markers, which was independently associated with time to recurrence [53]. The methylation status of SEPT9 in blood and of BMP3 and SDC2 in stool are currently key biomarkers used in approved auxiliary diagnostic and early screening tools for CRC and precancerous lesions, with approximately sensitivity of 70% and specificity of 95% [54–56]. Moreover, serum methylation of GALNT9/UPF3A discriminated advanced neoplasia with 78.8% sensitivity and 100% specificity, outperforming the methylated SEPT9 blood test. Nonetheless, only a limited number of methylation markers have been successfully translated into clinical practice, highlighting a major challenge for future research.

DNA methylation enzymes in colorectal cancer

DNA methylation is a reversible process mediated by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and demethylases. Key DNMTs include DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B, whereas demethylation is primarily carried out by ten–eleven translocation (TET) family enzymes such as TET1, TET2, and TET3 [57–59]. Additionally, methylated DNA binding proteins (MBPs) specifically recognize and bind methylated DNA sequences, recruiting repressor complexes to mediate epigenetic transcription repression [23, 60]. The dysfunction of DNMTs and TETs enzymes in CRC has been investigated, elucidating their critical roles and mechanisms in CRC development and progression.

The mechanisms and functions of DNA methyltransferases

DNMT1 is an essential enzyme that maintains methylation homeostasis during DNA duplication. Its expression is upregulated in CRC tissues and cells, where it contributes to multiple biological processes involved in tumor initiation and development [42]. Mechanistically, DNMT1-mediated suppression of CNN1 expression facilitates angiogenesis [40, 41] and modulates key signaling pathways such as MAPK and Wnt/β-catenin pathways in CRC [61]. DNMT1 also interacts with histone modifications including HDAC1-mediated histone deacetylation [62] and Enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2)-mediated H3K27me3 [63], resulting in synergistic transcription repression. Furthermore, DNMT1 collaborates with SET and MYND domain-containing protein 2 (SMYD2) to epigenetically silence adenomatous polyposis coli 2 (APC2), activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling and promoting epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in CRC metastasis [64]. More recently, DNMT1 has been implicated in immunity regulation: its inhibition upregulates neoantigen-bearing genes and enhances neoantigen-specific T-cell activation, supporting combined efficacy with radiotherapy in microsatellite stability (MSS) CRC [65].

DNMT3A and DNMT3B mediate de novo methylation of CpG sites independently of DNA replication and are significantly overexpressed in CRC [66]. DNMT3A directly inhibits disabled homolog 2 interacting protein (DAB2IP) expression to activate the mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathway [67], and suppresses miR-34a expression epigenetically to promote self-renewal of colon cancer stem cells (CSCs) and tumor growth [68]. Similarly, DNMT3A recruits EZH2 to the myeloid ecotropic viral integration site 1 (MEIS1) promoter to epigenetically suppress its transcription [52], and interacts with histone deacetylase HDAC1 in CRC [69]. Furthermore, DNMT3B downregulates MutL homolog 1 (MLH1) expression in both BRAF-mutant human CRC cells and tumors [70].

The mechanisms and functions of DNA demethylases

The TET family proteins, including TET1, TET2, and TET3, play important roles in regulating DNA methylation. Their transcription and protein levels are significantly lower in CRC and adenoma tissues compared with normal tissues [71–73]. Reduced level of TET1 mRNA is associated with advance stages and may serve as a prognostic marker for overall survival in CRC patients [74, 75]. TET1/thymine DNA glycosylase (TDG)-mediated DNA demethylation attenuates inflammatory and immune responses in CRC [76]. Conversely, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) treatment upregulates TET1, which mediates nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) activation and leads to 5-FU resistance in CRC cells [77]. The TET2–B-cell lymphoma 2-associated transcription factor 1 (BCLAF1) complex bounds to the ASCL2 promoter to restore its expression and modulate CRC stemness phenotypes [78]. Moreover, loss of nuclear localization of TET2 is associated with CRC metastasis [79], and its mRNA level serves as an independent prognostic factor for CRC recurrence and patient survival [71].

The mechanisms and functions of DNA methylation binding proteins

Methylated DNA binding proteins, including MBD proteins, ubiquitin-like containing PHD and RING finger domains (UHRF) proteins, and zinc finger proteins, specifically recognize and bind methylated DNA, recruiting repressor complexes to mediate transcription repression. MeCP2 has been reported to act as an oncogene in CRC [60], where it mediates epigenetic regulation of miR-137 expression throughout the adenoma–carcinoma sequence [80]. Furthermore, MeCP2 interacts with switch-insensitive 3a (SIN3A) and HDACs to form a repressive complex to silence H19 in a methylation-dependent manner [51]. UHRF1 is a key regulator of DNA methylation maintenance in CRC cells [81]. Its deletion, combined with HDAC inhibition, is shown to activate silenced genes and strongly inhibit CRC cell proliferation [82].

Upstream regulatory mechanisms of dysfunction of DNA methylation enzymes in colorectal cancer

CRC colorectal cancer, PTM posttranslational modification

Competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs). Many noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) are found to regulate the RNA degradation by constructing ceRNA networks. For instance, hsa_circ_0040809 targets miR-515-5p to regulate the mRNA expression of DNMT1, which in turn modulates A disintegrin and metalloprotease domain 23 (ADAM23) via aberrant promoter methylation [43]. Besides, mRNA expression levels of DNMT3A, DNMT3B, TET1, and TET2 are negatively regulated by specific ncRNAs during CRC development.

Posttranslational modifications (PTMs). Small ubiquitin-related modifier 1 (SUMO1)-mediated SUMOylation of DNMT1 strengthens its protein stability and inhibits von Willebrand factor C domain containing 2 (VWC2) expression through promoter hypermethylation [83]. Ubiquitination, a common protein modification, contributes to the degradation of DNMT1 [84] and DNMT3A [85]. Phosphorylation of TET2 enhances its protein stability, promoting cell proliferation in obesity-related CRC [73].

Signaling pathways. Various signaling pathways upregulate DNMT1 expression. Inflammatory factors interleukin (IL)−23 and IL-6 activate signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling pathways to increase DNMT1 expression, which induce SOCS3 promoter hypermethylation to promote CRC metastasis [25, 86]. Moreover, the Wnt signaling pathway upregulates DNMT1 to trigger NHERF1 promoter hypermethylation in colon cancer [87]. Mutant KRAS [88] and p53 [89] transcriptionally activate DNMT1 expression in CRC cells, promoting CRC growth and metastasis.

Epigenetic mechanisms. Aberrant DNA methylation plays a important role in regulating DNMT3B [90] and TET1 expression [71]. As CRC progresses, the expression of DNMT3B gradually decreases owing to the promoter hypermethylation.

Genetic alterations. Nucleotide variations may influence gene expression and protein structure, resulting in abnormal functions. Variants such as DNMT3B rs2424913 and C46359T are associated with hMLH1, ECAD, and MYOD methylation in CRC patients [91, 92].

Other regulatory mechanisms. DNMT3B can be regulated by abnormally expressed transcription factors such as Sp1 and through alternative splicing, leading to aberrant methylation of the PHIP and FABP5 promoters [93, 107].

Interestingly, Fusobacterium nucleatum and Hungatella hathewayi induce DNMT1 to mediate the epigenetic regulation of CDX2 and MLH1 promoters in CRC cells [94]. Certain active substances, such as kaempferol [95] and ginkgo biloba extract [96], exhibit epigenetic effects in CRC cells. Additionally, cadmium reduces TET2 expression, promoting CRC malignant progression [97]. Moreover, chemotherapy and radiotherapy reduce DNMT1 expression and enzymatic activity, thereby upregulating the downstream gene expression and inhibition of CRC [98]. However, these potential mechanisms require further exploration to clarify the regulatory axis.

DNA methylation inhibitors

DNA methylation inhibitors that target related regulatory enzymes are primarily classified into nucleoside analogs and non-nucleoside analog compounds. Nucleoside analogues, such as decitabine, azacitidine, and zebularine, are incorporated into DNA and act as competitive substrates for DNMTs [109–111]. Several DNMT inhibitors, including decitabine, azacitidine, and zebularine, are currently utilized in clinical practice for CRC treatment. Nonnucleoside compounds, such as RG108 and DC-05, can also inhibit DNMT activity [112, 113]. Additionally, methylglyoxal has been identified as a novel upstream regulatory of DNA methylation [114]. Thus, the development of new drugs targeting DNMTs, TETs, and MBPs holds great promise as potential therapeutic strategies.

RNA m6A methylation in colorectal cancer

RNA m6A methylation, the most abundant internal modification in eukaryotic RNAs, plays a critical role in posttranscriptional regulation. This process is mediated by methyltransferases (“writers”), demethylases (“erasers”), and RNA binding proteins (“readers”) [6]. m6A methylation is enriched primarily near stop codons and in 3′ UTRs, regulating multiple aspects of RNA metabolism, including stability, degradation, and translation [7, 8, 115].

Altered RNA m6A methylation results in malignant phenotypes in CRC, affecting cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis through dysregulation of gene expression. A targeted liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) metabolomics study revealed increased serum m6A concentrations in CRC patients compared with healthy controls and those with colorectal adenomas [19]. Transcriptome-wide m6A profiling using methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) identified distinct methylation patterns in mRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs between CRC tissues and adjacent normal tissues [116–118]. Numerous genes critical for CRC growth, metastasis, and invasion are found to operate in an m6A-dependent manner. For example, Rho/Rac guanine nucleotide exchange factor 2 (ARHGEF2), an activator of RhoA signaling, enhances carcinogenesis and metastasis in CRC by promoting translation through YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 1 (YTHDF1) binding [119]. Furthermore, m6A methylation is closely linked to key signaling pathways such as MAPK, Wnt, and PI3K/AKT in CRC development [16, 120, 121].

Previous studies have indicated that m6A modification influences various physiological and biological processes during CRC development. For instance, m6A modification regulates glycolysis, a hallmark of cancer, by modifying key genes such as GLUT1, HK2, and SOGA1, thereby modulating CRC progression [122–126]. Drug resistance, a major cause of recurrence and poor prognosis in CRC, has been associated with m6A-mediated mechanisms underlying resistance to oxaliplatin, 5-FU, cisplatin, and immune checkpoint inhibitors [8, 127–131]. Furthermore, key biological processes in CRC, such as ferroptosis [132] and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)-mediated autophagy [133, 134], have also been shown to depend on m6A modification. Emerging evidence reveals the crosstalk between m6A methylation and tumor immune microenvironment, affecting both immune cells and target genes [135]. Liu et al. demonstrated that methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3)/insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 2 (IGF2BP2)-mediated upregulation of circQSOX1-promoted CRC carcinogenesis and immune escape via the miR-326/miR-330-5p/phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1) axis [131]. Similarly, YTHDF1 is found to stabilize p65 transcripts in an m6A manner, enhancing MDSC migration through the C–X–C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1)/C–X–C chemokine receptor type 2 (CXCR2) axis, which suppresses CD8+T cells and promotes CRC progression [136, 137]. Additionally, crosstalk between m6A methylation and genetic alterations affect signal transduction pathways in CRC. The KRAS G12D mutation promotes CRC progression through suppressing ferroptosis via the METTL14/LINC02159/FOXC2 axis [138]. Likewise, aberrant m6A methylation contributes to drug resistance in both KRAS-mutant and BRAF V600E-mutant CRC [139, 140]. However, more effort is still needed to elucidate these complex molecular mechanisms, which may provide new insight for prediction, diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of CRC.

The clinical significance of m6A modification continues to grow, with implications for diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment response in CRC. Elevated m6A levels in peripheral blood immune cells correlate with CRC progression and metastasis, and decrease following treatment, revealing their potential as noninvasive diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets [141]. To date, several predictive models for CRC prognosis based on m6A-marked gene expression signatures have been established and verified [142–144], offering valuable tools for outcome prediction and guiding individual treatment strategies in precision medicine.

RNA m6A methylation enzymes in colorectal cancer

The homeostatic balance and functions of m6A methylation depend on methyltransferases, demethylases, and m6A binding proteins, which collectively regulate the posttranscriptional expression of m6A-marked genes [6]. Typical m6A methylation writers, such as METTL3, METTL14, and Wilms tumor 1-associating protein (WTAP), usually form a complex that binds target sequences to transfer the methyl group donated by SAM in a cotranscription manner [145]. The methylated genes are recognized by m6A readers, including IGF2BPs, YTHDCs, and YTHDFs [6]. The primary demethylases fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) and AlkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5) can remove m6A modification. Further research is needed to elucidate the upstream molecular mechanisms driving aberrant m6A methylation during colorectal carcinogenesis and development, which may yield novel predictive biomarkers for CRC diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment.

The mechanisms and functions of RNA m6A writers

METTL3, the first identified m6A methyltransferase, is the core catalytic subunit of the m6A methyltransferase complex. Previous studies have shown that METTL3 is frequently upregulated in CRC tissues and cells, facilitating CRC progression through the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mTOR signaling pathways [146, 147]. METTL3 also epigenetically represses Yippee-like 5 (YPEL5) and upregulates POU6F2-AS1 to enhance lipogenesis, tumor growth, and metastasis in CRC [7], and promotes cellular senescence in CRC by modulating CDKN2B mRNA stability [148]. WTAP is elevated in CRC cells and promotes malignant behavior through epigenetic regulation of PDK4 expression [149], while also activating vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) and MAPK signaling pathways to enhance angiogenesis in CRC [126]. Conversely, METTL14 is frequently downregulated in CRC and negatively correlates with poor prognosis [60], inhibiting CSC characteristic by regulation of β-catenin [150].

METTL4 mediates m6A methylation of TFRC mRNA, which is recognized by IGF2BP2 to dysregulate iron metabolism and promote CRC growth [151]. METTL16 has been shown to enhance SOGA1 expression via IGF2BP1, upregulating PDK4 to promote glycolysis and progression in CRC [125]. Similarly, KIAA1429 increases HK2 and POU6F2-AS1 expression via m6A modification, promoting aerobic glycolysis and malignant phenotypes in CRC carcinogenesis [152]. RBM15 is highly expressed in CRC tissues and negatively correlates with overall and disease-free survival rates, downregulating MyD88 to enhance CRC growth and metastasis [153]. ZCCHC4, associated with adverse outcome in CRC, promotes carcinogenesis by downregulating LncGHRLOS [154]. More recently, METTL17 is observed to be upregulated in CRC and associated with ferroptosis through mitochondrial RNA methylation [155]. The oncogenic role of METTL5 is evidenced by its promotion of CRC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration through downregulation of TLR8 [156].

The mechanisms and functions of RNA m6A erasers

Demethylases FTO and ALKBH5 catalyze the removal of m6A methylation modifications from RNAs. FTO exacerbates tumor growth, metastasis, and angiogenesis in CRC by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling through upregulation of ZNF687 in an m6A-dependent manner [157]. FTO is upregulated in both primary and 5-FU-resistant CRC, where it mediates m6A demethylation of SIVA1 mRNA, inhibits its degradation via YTHDF2, and significantly reduces sensitivity to 5-FU in chemoresistant CRC cells [129]. In hypoxic tumor microenvironments, decreased FTO expression enhances MTA1 stability in an IGF2BP2-dependent manner, promoting CRC metastasis [158]. Moreover, both FTO and ALKBH5 are significantly downregulated in high-fat environments, jointly regulating HK2 and FOXO to influence CRC progression [122].

ALKBH5 exhibits dual roles in CRC development, depending on the downstream target genes and associated m6A readers. Generally, ALKBH5 is downregulated at both transcription and protein levels in CRC tissues, enhancing proliferation, migration, and invasion in LOVO and RKO cells by stabilizing PHF20 mRNA [159]. Furthermore, ALKBH5 positively regulates FABP5 expression via an m6A-independent mechanism, leading to decreased FASN expression and lipid accumulation, thereby suppressing mTOR-mediated autophagy in CRC [133]. Conversely, recent studies report elevated ALKBH5 expression in some CRC tissues and cells, promoting CRC progression by posttranscriptionally activating RAB5A in an m6A-YTHDF2-dependent manner [160].

The mechanisms and functions of RNA m6A readers

m6A-marked RNAs interact with specific reader proteins, notably including IGF2BPs, YTHDCs, and YTHDFs, regulating processes such as RNA stability, translation, degradation, and intracellular transport [7, 8, 115]. The molecular mechanisms and clinical significance of these readers in the development of CRC are summarized below.

The expression level of IGF2BP1 is higher in CRC tissues compared to normal tissues, enhancing PDK4 mRNA stability to promote glycolysis and tumor progression [125]. IGF2BP2 supports ErbB2 expression by recognizing m6A-modified YAP transcript, thereby inhibiting apoptosis and promoting CRC cell proliferation [115], and also regulates TFRC expression in iron metabolism [151]. Similarly, IGF2BP3 is upregulated in CRC tissues and boosts epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) translation, contributing to cetuximab resistance [161]. Additionally, IGF2BPs cooperate with METTL3 to regulate several CRC related genes such as HMGA1 and VEGFA [162, 163].

YTHDF1 is significantly overexpressed in CRC [164] and mediates cisplatin resistance by enhancing glutaminase 1 (GLS1) protein synthesis [130]. The m6A induced six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate 3 (STEAP3) degradation is restrained by competitive interaction of YTHDF1 with STEAP3-AS1, thereby inhibiting CRC progression via suppression of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [165]. YTHDF2 overexpression is associated with poor overall survival in colon adenocarcinoma [28]. YTHDC1 activates the MAPK pathway in angiogenesis of CRC by enhancing VEGFA expression, and facilitates cytoplasmic translocation of circFNDC3B to inhibit CRC stemness and metastasis [120, 126]. Decreased YTHDC2 enhances LIMK1 expression in CRC cells and leads to 5-FU resistance through endoplasmic reticulum stress [8].

Overexpressed HNRNPA2B1 activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway by inducing TCF7L2 expression, leading to cetuximab resistance [166]. The recently identified m6A reader fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein 1 (FMR1) is upregulated in CRC cells, maintaining EGFR expression by recognizing METTL3-mediated m6A modification, thereby promoting carcinogenesis and metastasis [167]. RALY, an important hnRNP family member, promotes exosome biogenesis and CRC metastasis by enhancing PLD2 expression in an m6A-dependent manner [168], and also reprograms mitochondrial metabolism during tumor progression [169].

CRC colorectal cancer, PTM posttranslational modification

Competing endogenous RNAs. ceRNA networks involving mRNAs, miRNAs, and other ncRNAs play crucial roles. Specifically, tumor cell-derived exosome circ_0001739/AC159540.1 upregulates FTO by miR-218-5p inhibition [170]. In addition, miR-6125 downregulates YTHDF2 protein expression, leading to increased GSK3β and inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin/cyclin D1 pathway, suppressing CRC growth [171]. The expression and function of IGF2BP2 are modulated through circEZH2/miR-133b and other ncRNAs [172].

Posttranslational modifications. Multiple m6A regulators undergo ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation, including FTO [158], YTHDF1 [173], YTHDF2 [174], and IGF2BP2 [175, 176]. SUMOylation and lactylation enhance METTL3 expression [26, 177], which in turn facilitates circ_0000677 and Jak1 levels, inhibiting tumor growth and drug resistance in CRC. O-GlcNAcylation of YTHDF1 promotes CRC carcinogenesis via c-Myc upregulation [178], while palmitoylation of IGF2BP1 promotes CRC cell growth via upregulating endothelial lipase (LIPG) expression [179].

Signaling pathways. Fusobacterium nucleatum reduces METTL3 transcription through inhibiting FOXD3 via YAP signaling pathway, promoting KIF26B expression via an m6A-YTHDF2-dependent mechanism [180]. METTL3 is upregulated by β-catenin signaling pathway, promoting drug resistance in CRC [181]. FTO is upregulated by the adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway [182], while YTHDF2 is regulated by nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling pathway [183]. Additionally, p53 mutation reduces ALKBH5 expression [184].

Epigenetic mechanisms. METTL3 expression is positively regulated by ALKBH1-mediated m1A modification, affecting the m6A level of SMAD7 mRNA and promoting aggressive CRC phenotypes [185]. METTL14 transcription is inhibited by KDM5C-mediated H3K4me3 demethylation, promoting CRC cell migration, invasion, and metastasis [186]. WTAP is upregulated by m6A methylation to promote lung metastasis of colon cancer cells [187]. In addition, HDAC2-mediated H3K27 deacetylation suppresses ALKBH5 transcription and function, promoting carcinogenesis in CRC and colitis-associated tumor development [188].

Other regulatory mechanisms. TCF4 and HuR oppositely regulate METTL14 expression and its impact on ARRDC4 [189], while TTC22 upregulates WTAP expression to promote lung metastasis of CRC [187]. IGF2BP1 is upregulated by peptide RBRP, promoting CRC carcinogenesis via c-Myc upregulation [190].

Glutaminolysis inhibition significantly downregulates FTO expression, disrupting m6A modification and YTHDF2-mediated ATF4 decay, inducing prosurvival autophagy in CRC via mTOR suppression [134]. Furthermore, aberrant expression and functions of m6A enzymes are significantly associated with genetic mutations, copy number variations, chromatin accessibility, and gut microbiota [164, 191]. However, further experimental validation is required to confirm these regulatory relationships.

RNA m6A methylation inhibitors

Currently, numerous inhibitors specifically targeting m6A modification enzymes have been identified, demonstrating the potential to impede m6A functions in human diseases. For example, cycloleucine significantly reduces global m6A levels and disrupts the splicing and maturation of SLC7A11 mRNA by blocking the interaction between nuclear factor-κB activating protein (NKAP) and m6A sites, thereby regulating ferroptosis [195]. In CRC, the methyltransferase inhibitor STM2457 decreases m6A levels of OPA1, significantly suppressing its mRNA stability and mitochondria fusion [196]. Additionally, CS1 suppresses FTO demethylase activity and cell proliferation by downregulating CDC25C, thereby promoting apoptosis in HCT116 cells [197]. Other FTO inhibitors, such as meclofenamic acid [198], Dac51 [198], FB23 [199], and FB23-2 [200], have also shown considerable therapeutic potential.

Histone methylation in colorectal cancer

Histone methylation is a posttranslational modification that involves the addition of varying numbers of methyl groups to side chain amino acids of histone proteins, using SAM as the methyl donor. This process is catalyzed by a variety of specific enzymes [5]. It occurs predominantly on lysine and arginine residues of histones H3 and H4 [201]. Lysine residues can undergo single, double, or trimethylation, serving as major regulators of chromatin structure that influence the formation of active and repressed genomic domains. Arginine residues can be monomethylated, symmetrically dimethylated, or asymmetrically dimethylated [201].

Accumulating studies on histone methylation indicate that the transcriptional outcome whether activation or repression of target genes depends on the specific locations of histone residue methylated and the number of methyl groups deposited. Generally, H3K9me2/3, H3K27me1/2/3, and H4K20me3 are associated with transcriptionally repressed chromatin [21, 202, 203], whereas H3K4me1/2/3, H3K36me2, and H3K79me3 are found in transcriptionally active regions [22, 204, 205]. Symmetrical H3R8me2s and H4R3me2s are typically associated with transcriptional inhibition [21], whereas H3R8me2a and H4R3me2a are associated with transcriptional activation [206, 207].

Histone methylation often functions cooperatively with other modifications to regulate gene transcription. For instance, H3K4me3 frequently coexists with H3K27ac at promoters and enhancers, synergistically promoting gene activation [208, 209]. Histone methylation can also interact with DNA methylation to influence other histone methylation marks and regulate gene expression. H3K4 methylation helps maintain DNA in an unmethylated state through interactions with DNMTs, whereas H3K9 methylation promotes DNA methylation and gene silencing [210, 211]. Consistently, treatment with DNA demethylating agents reduces both DNA methylation and H3K9 methylation levels, while increases H3K4 methylation levels accordingly [212].

Histone methylation marks epigenetically regulate gene expression and affect various signaling pathways involved in cell growth, apoptosis, the EMT process, and chemoresistance, all of which play important roles in the occurrence and progression of CRC. Epigenetic dysregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a hallmark of CRC. For instance, reduced levels of H3K9me2 and H3K27me3 are observed at the promoters of Wnt target genes [22, 213, 214], while H3K4me3, H3K9me3, and H3K36me3 are significantly enriched at the promoters of both Wnt and β-catenin target genes [22, 215]. Additionally, upregulation of MALAT1 marked by H3K9me3 and H3K36me3 activates the β-catenin pathway in CRC [216]. Moreover, dysfunction of EMT in CRC is frequently associated with histone methylation changes, including H3K27me3 [217, 218], H3K9me3 [24, 202], and H3K4me2 [219], at relevant genes. CSCs are capable of tumor initiation, self-renewal, and evolution through self-protective mechanisms, leading to tumor progression, metastasis, and drug resistance [22]. Epigenetically, stem cell marker genes can be activated through elevated H3K4me3 or reduced H3K27me3, promoting the expansion of CRC stem cells [22, 220, 221]. Furthermore, histone methylation closely interacts with genetic alterations. In colon cancer cells, mutant p53 induces H3K4me1 and H3K27ac to mediate aberrant enhancer activity and tumor-promoting gene expression under chronic immune signaling [222]. Genetic alterations in histone methylation related enzymes may also regulate the histone methylation landscape in CRC, as detailed in the following sections.

Histone methylation enzymes in colorectal cancer

It is well known that histone methylation is a dynamic process regulated by histone methylesterases (HMTs) and histone demethylases (HDMs), which modulate chromatin structure and gene transcription through specific binding proteins [223]. Based on the modified amino acid residues, histone methylation can be categorized into lysine methylation and arginine methylation, each governed by distinct enzymatic systems. Lysine methylation is controlled by lysine methyltransferases (KMTs) and demethylases (KDMs) [224], while arginine methylation is primarily catalyzed by protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs), a family of nine members [225]. The main known protein arginine demethylases (PRDMs) include JMJD6 and JMJD1B [226, 227].

During colorectal carcinogenesis, dysfunction of HMTs and HDMs lead to changes in gene transcription, affecting cell proliferation, migration, invasion, colony formation, tumor immunity, EMT, and other vital physiological processes [21, 215]. Population studies indicate that histone methylation marks associated with CRC susceptibility genes may serve as promising therapeutic targets owing to their significant correlation with patient prognosis [21, 27]. This section discusses the biological functions, molecular mechanisms, and clinical significance of histone methylation enzymes in the occurrence and development of CRC.

The mechanisms and functions of histone methyltransferases

Lysine methyltransferases

EZH2, a subunit of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), is a critical KMT for forming the H3K27me3 mark. It is frequently upregulated in CRC tissues and cells, acting as a negative regulator of gene expression [228]. EZH2 directly binds the CDKN2B promoter to increase H3K27me3 levels and suppress CDKN2B expression in CRC [21]. Moreover, EZH2 interacts with the pseudogene DUXAP8 to inhibit E-cadherin expression and promote EMT process [217]. It also regulates macrophage polarization by suppressing STAT3 [229], and inhibits FXR transcription to promote CRC growth [230]. JARID2, a noncatalytic PRC2 subunit, is also upregulated in CRC [194]. Another methyltransferase for H3K27me3 mark, SUZ12, promotes cell proliferation, invasion, and the EMT process through interaction with DEPDC1 in CRC [218].

SETDB1, a KMT for the H3K9me3 mark, is upregulated in CRC cells [231]. Elevated H3K9me3 levels mediated by SETDB1 reduce p21 expression, regulating EMT and CRC carcinogenesis [202]. In hypoxia environments, SETDB1 represses APAK expression via H3K9me3 enrichment, activating p53 and inducing apoptosis in CRC cells [232]. SUV39H1-induced H3K9me3 is increased in invasive CRC regions and correlates positively with lymph node metastasis in CRC patients [233], while SUV39H1-mediated FAS downexpression leads to drug resistance against FasL-induced apoptosis [234].

Loss of SUV420H2-mediated H4K20me3 drives right-sided colorectal carcinogenesis through impairing heterochromatin compaction and inducing R-loop formation [235]. PRDM2 encodes RIZ1 protein, methylates histone H3 on lysine 9 [236]. EHMT2 (G9A), an euchromatic histone methyltransferase for H3K9me1 and H3K9me2, directly inhibits galectin-7 via H3K9me2 to enhance immune activity in MSS-CRC [237]. KMT2A (MLL1) is recruited by polycomb group RING finger protein 1 (PCGF1) to epigenetically activate stemness markers and enrich CRC stem cells [22], and also interacts with β-catenin [238]. It is a methyltransferase for H3K4me3, and significantly associated with PIK3CA mutation in CRC [239, 240]. KMT2C (MLL3) elevates H3K4me1 levels at genome-wide enhancers and negatively affects CRC cell growth [241]. KMT2D (MLL4) induces the H3K4 methylation, enhancing superenhancer formation and function by the positive regulator DBC1 in CRC cells [242]. SMYD3, upregulated in CRC at both the mRNA and protein levels, deposits H3K4me2/3 and H4K5me [243]. SMYD2 activates MEX3A expression by increasing H3K36me2 at the promoter, enhancing CRC growth [204]. WHSC1 catalyzes H3K36me2 on MHC-1 genes to enhance antitumor immunity in CRC [244]. NSD2, another H3K36me2 methyltransferase, induces mitochondrial depolarization, DNA damage, and apoptosis in CRC cells via activation of multiple oncogenes [245]. In CRC cells, SETD2 deletion reduces H3K36me3 at DVL2, aggravating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and facilitating intestinal carcinogenesis [246]. The DOT1L complex promotes cancer stemness and tumorigenic potential by inducing H3K79me2 of core stem cell genes, with its expression in CRC being a predictor of poor survival [247]. SET1A mediates histone H3 methylation, increasing chromatin accessibility to promote YAP-induced c-Met transcription, thereby promoting CRC cell stemness [248]. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the roles of other newly histone lysine methyltransferases in CRC.

Arginine methyltransferases

Histone arginine methyltransferases are categorized into three classes according to their catalytic activity. Class I enzymes include PRMT1, PRMT2, PRMT3, PRMT4 (CARM1), PRMT6, and PRMT8, which catalyze the formation of asymmetric dimethylarginine. Class II consists of PRMT5 and PRMT9, catalyzing symmetric dimethylarginine, while class III is represented by PRMT7, responsible for monomethylarginine production [225]. PRMT1 is significantly upregulated in CRC and mediates H4R3me2a at the promoters of EGFR and TNS4, promoting CRC progression by enhancing EGFR signaling [206, 249]. Furthermore, PRMT6 mediates H3R2me2a at the CDKN2B and CCNG1 promoters, together with PRMT5-mediated H4R3me2s and H3R8me2s, leading to transcriptional repression and CRC progression [250]. PRMT5, one of the well-studied members in CRC pathogenesis [251], increases H4R3me2s and H3R8me2s at the CDKN2B promoter and cooperates with EZH2 to enhance H3K27me3 deposition, synergistically repressing CDKN2B expression and accelerating CRC progression [21]. PRMT7 catalyzes arginine methylation at H4R3 and H3R2, and its isoform PRMT7-V2 is associated with the YAP, AKT, and KRAS pathways in CRC [252].

The mechanisms and functions of histone demethylases

Lysine demethylases

Histone lysine demethylases are classified into two major families on the basis of their structural characteristics. The first family, amino acid oxidase, encompasses KDM1 members, which specifically remove monomethylated and dimethylated marks. KDM1A (LSD1) targets H3K4me1/2 and H3K9me1/2 and is upregulated in CRC tissues, correlating with poor prognosis [27]. It is recruited by LINC00586 to the ASXL1 promoter, decreasing H3K4me2 levels to inhibit ASXL1 expression, inducing EMT and promoting CRC carcinogenesis [219]. KDM1A-mediated H3K4me2 demethylation also promotes adipocytic glutamine synthetase, resulting in 5-FU resistance in peritoneal carcinomatosis of CRC [253]. KDM1B (LSD2) acts as an oncogene by demethylating H3K4me2 at the p53 promoter, suppressing the p53–p21–Rb pathway and enhancing CRC cell proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis [254].

The second family, Jumonji domain-containing demethylases (JMJD), encompasses enzymes with the Jumonji C (JMJC) domain that can remove trimethylated marks, KDM2-8. Previous studies have identified that KDM3 plays a more essential role than KDM4 (JMJD2) in regulating oncogenic Wnt signaling in CRC through H3K9me2 demethylation at Wnt target gene promoters, and it recruits KDM1A to synergistically activate transcription [213, 214]. The JMJD2 subfamily is related to CRC metastasis. For example, JMJD2C is recruited by circPABPC1 to the HMGA2 promoter, reducing H3K9me3 levels and promoting liver metastasis of CRC [255]. Furthermore, JMJD2C also removes H3K9me3 and H3K36me3 at the MALAT1 promoter, enhancing β-catenin signaling in CRC cells [216]. JMJD2D (KDM4D) inhibits H3K9 methylation to active gene transcription and is associated with immunotherapy in CRC [256]. Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα)-induced JMJD2D promotes Hedgehog expression by interacting with Gli2 to reduce H3K9me3 levels at its promoter, promoting colitis recovery and colon carcinogenesis [257]. JMJD2D is increased in human colorectal tumors and activates β-catenin target genes by H3K9me3 demethylation [215]. In serrated neoplasia, activation of the Notch signaling pathway promotes JMJD3 cytonuclear translocation with NICD, reducing H3K27me3 levels at the EPHB4 enhancer [258]. KDM5D inhibits transcription factor E2F1 expression by H3K4me3 demethylation, thus suppressing FKBP4 expression and inhibiting CRC growth and metastasis [259]. Recent studies indicate that KDM5D impairs tight junction integrity by reducing H3K4me1 of AMOT, substantially contributing to sex differences in metastasis in KRAS G12D CRC [260]. KDM6 has effects on global enhancer reprogramming, particularly at super-enhancers associated stemness genes such as ID1 [221]. KDM6A is recruited by PCGF1 to the promoters of stemness genes, eliminating H3K27me3 to active CRC stem cells [22]. Moreover, oxaliplatin treatment activates KDM6A/6B expression, which elevate NOTCH2 expression levels through removing H3K27me3, thus promoting stemness gene expression and conferring oxaliplatin resistance in CRC [220]. UTX, a histone demethylase encoded by KDM6A, acts as a TSG in CRC by significantly reducing H3K27me3 at promoters of AUTS2 and EMP1 [261].

Arginine demethylases

Currently, the primary known PRDMs include JMJD6 and JMJD1B, both of which contain a JMJC domain and catalyze the removal of methyl groups from H3R2me2, H4R3me2, and H4R3me2s, thereby regulating the expression of specific genes in various cancers [226, 262]. However, the significant epigenetic functions and molecular mechanisms of JMJD6 and JMJD1B in CRC development and progression remain poorly understood, necessitating further investigation using both in vitro and in vivo models to confirm their regulatory effects.

The mechanisms and functions of histone methylation binding proteins

Histone methylation plays a vital role in cancers through recruiting proteins or complexes containing methyl-binding domains, ultimately regulating gene transcription. Lysine methylation is recognized by multiple structural domains, including ankyrin repeat domains, chromodomains, MBT repeats (L3MBTL1), PHD finger proteins (BPTF, RAG2, PYGO, ING2), PWWP domains (ZCWPW1), double/tandem Tudor domains, Tudor domains (Spindlin1), WD40 domains (WDR5), and zf-CW domains. Arginine methylation is primarily recognized by the ADD domain and members of the bromo-adjacent homology (BAH) domain family [263–267]. Identifying readers of histone methylation in CRC may enhance our understanding of this posttranslational modification and its functional links to tumor-related biological processes.

Histone methylation inhibitors

Given the pivotal role of histone methylation enzymes in cancer progression, a variety of inhibitors have been developed that show significant potential for CRC therapy. The primary class includes SAM-competitive inhibitors, such as EPZ005687, EI1, GSK343, EPZ6438, GSK126, DZNep, lidamycin, and UNC1999, that target EZH2, a key regulatory enzyme [21, 229, 230, 276, 277]. GSK-J4 inhibits KDM6, enhancing H3K27me3 deposition and suppressing oncogene expression [221]. Inhibitors such as UNC0638, BIX01294, and BRD4770 act on G9A, reversing H3K9me2 to reactivate gene expression [237, 270, 278]. Chaetocin and F5446 inhibit SUV39H1-mediated H3K9me3 [233, 234], whereas IOX1 restores KDM3-mediated H3K9 methylation [213]. Several other inhibitors also demonstrate therapeutic potential in CRC, including methylstat for SUV420H2 [235], OICR-9429 for KMT2A [239], BCI-121 for SMYD3 [243], GSK591 for PRMT5 [21], SGC3027 for PRMT7 [252], EPZ004777 for DOT1L [247], and JIB-04 for Jumonji HDMs [279].

Crosstalk among DNA, RNA m6A, and histone methylation

Crosstalk among DNA methylation, RNA m6A methylation, and histone methylation plays an essential role in regulating gene expression in CRC. These interactions occur through two main mechanisms. First, multiple enzymes collaboratively regulate target gene expression without direct mutual influence, yet exhibit spatial correlation [280–282]. Second, methylation modifications can directly affect the expression and functions of regulatory enzymes, thereby impacting downstream biological processes [283]. The epigenetic interplay between DNA methylation, RNA m6A methylation, and histone methylation is increasingly recognized as an important factor in CRC pathogenesis and development.

Spatial interaction among DNA, RNA m6A, and histone methylation enzymes

The most characterized synergistic mechanism occurs between DNA methylation and histone methylation at the transcriptional level. For instance, DNMT1 and EZH2 are recruited by LINC00114 to the miR-133b promoter, resulting in its suppression and subsequent regulation of NUP214 protein levels [63]. DNMT1 also collaborates with SMYD2 to epigenetically suppress APC2, activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and promoting CRC metastasis [64]. In addition, EZH2 and DNMT3A, assisted by lncRNA ELFN1-AS1, cooperatively suppress MEIS1 transcription through epigenetic silencing [52]. Methylation modifications on different genes can form integrated regulatory axes. For instance, LINC01605, modulated by SMYD2-EP300 via H3K4me3 and H3K27ac, binds to METTL3 and promotes m6A modification of SPTBN2 mRNA, thereby facilitating cancer cell proliferation and metastasis in CRC [284]. Furthermore, m6A modification represents a novel pathway through which H3K36me3 regulates gene expression. However, it remains unclear whether associations exist between m6A modification and H3K36me3 as well as other histone modifications in CRC.

Functional crosstalk of DNA, RNA m6A, and histone methylation enzymes

The complex regulatory network involving DNA methylation, RNA m6A methylation, and histone methylation is summarized in Fig. 2. Methyltransferases and demethylases for DNA and histone might be primarily influenced by their respective methylation states, whereas the effects of RNA m6A methylation on these enzymes remain less thoroughly explored in CRC. Aberrant DNA methylation significantly contributes to the regulation of enzymes such as DNMT3B [90] and TET1 [71]. KDM5C-mediated demethylation of H3K4me3 inhibits METTL14 transcription, thereby facilitating migration and invasion of CRC cells [186]. Moreover, m6A methylation upregulates WTAP, which promotes lung metastasis in colon cancer [187]. Notably, METTL14 and ALKBH5 regulated JMJD8 stability through IGF2BPs, increasing glycolysis and accelerating CRC progression by enhancing PKM2 enzymatic activity [188]. These intricate interactions among DNA, RNA m6A, and histone methylation present compelling avenues for research and offer important insights into the complex regulatory mechanisms underlying CRC progression.

Development of clinical therapy

Differing from genetic mutations, epigenetic alterations are reversible, making them promising targets for novel cancer therapies. Given the essential roles of epigenetic changes and regulation enzymes in the carcinogenesis, progression, metastasis, and drug resistance of CRC, anti-epigenetic therapies have been developed to reverse abnormal epigenetic states and restore normal gene expression patterns [285]. Nowadays, numerous epigenetic drugs have entered preclinical studies, and some have been approved for clinical use in CRC, demonstrating considerable efficacy. DNMT inhibitors (DNMTis) and HDAC inhibitors (HDACis) are the most widely used epigenetic drugs in CRC treatment [286]. As noted earlier, DNMTis include nucleoside analogs and nonnucleoside analogs, which restore the expression and anticancer effects of TSGs by demethylating their promoter regions. Notably, combination therapies incorporating epigenetic agents such as 5-azacitidine (5-AC) and 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine (DAC) with other treatment modalities have shown synergistic effects in CRC [287, 288].

Chemotherapy and radiotherapy resistance remains a major cause of treatment failure in clinical practice. Extensive studies indicate that methylation changes mediate occurrence of primary and acquired drug resistance to conventional anticancer drugs such as 5-FU, oxaliplatin, cetuximab, and irinotecan, by modulating the expression of key genes, altering CSCs differentiation, or reshaping the CRC microenvironment [289]. For example, the demethylating agent 5-AC has been demonstrated to improve sensitivity and overcome resistance to 5-FU or Irinotecan in BRAF-mutant CRCs, significantly enhancing the efficacy of subsequent BRAF inhibitor treatment [290]. However, 5-AC treatment may also lead to maintained repression of certain TSGs owing to loss of histone acetylation and increase of histone methylation around hypomethylated regions. Combination of EZH2 inhibition and 5-AC treatment significantly improves therapeutic efficacy in BRAF V600E CRC cells [291]. Thus, targeting multiple epigenetic modifications simultaneously can markedly enhance treatment outcomes in CRC, underscoring the need for accelerated research into the epigenetic mechanisms in the occurrence and development of CRC.

The majority of CRC patients exhibit the MSS subtype, which is known as an immunological “cold” tumor and typically unresponsive to the therapy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Recent studies indicate that anti-epigenetic therapy holds great potential for reprogramming the CRC microenvironment to improve immunotherapy efficacy [292]. For example, combing DNMTi with radiotherapy induces expression of neoantigen-bearing genes in MSS-CRC, promotes activation of neoantigen-reactive T cells, and improves treatment response [65]. Additionally, epigenetic drugs such as PRMT5 inhibitors play critical roles in regulating immune cell differentiation, infiltration, activation, and reversal of immune cell exhaustion [293]. Further elucidation of various methylation mechanisms in the occurrence and development of CRC may provide new reference for epigenetic therapy application [109].

However, epigenetic therapies still face challenges, including side effects, therapeutic target selection, drug specificity, efficacy limitations, and optimization of combination strategies [294]. Natural pharmaceutical ingredients with minimal toxicity are being explored for their ability to regulate epigenetic marks, offering considerable clinical application value [295]. Moreover, combining anti-epigenetic drugs with agents targeting other pathways such as ferroptosis, can also produce synergistic antitumor effect in CRC [296]. Novel strategies including dual-target or multitarget anti-epigenetic drugs and epigenetic-based chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapies may also offer new opportunities for CRC treatment [297, 298]. Further efforts should focus on elucidating epigenetic mechanisms in greater depth to improve the development of more specific and effective anti-epigenetic drugs and rationally designed combination therapies.

Conclusions and perspectives

This review underscores the significance of DNA, RNA m6A, and histone methylation in CRC, with a particular focus on aberrant methylation patterns, dysfunctional regulatory enzymes, and their upstream regulatory mechanisms in carcinogenesis, metastasis, resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and immune regulation.

Advances in high-throughput sequencing have provided a deeper understanding of genome-wide methylation patterns in CRC, highlighting novel targets for further research. Although many genes with oncogenic or tumor-suppressive functions in CRC have been identified, further continued exploration of epigenetic factors remains crucial for disease etiological research. The dynamic changes of methylation patterns depend on the relevant regulation enzymes. While the mechanisms and biological functions of many methylation regulatory enzymes have been elucidated, and inhibitors against them are increasingly being incorporated into clinical practice for CRC, the widespread application of anti-epigenetic drugs has also revealed their limitations. Therefore, there is an urgent need to adopt more advanced technologies to develop specific and effective anti-epigenetic drugs, or to combine them with conventional therapies to improve treatment efficacy. Investigating upstream regulatory mechanisms and the crosstalk among DNA, RNA, and histone methylation will be essential for developing targeted therapies. The roles of lesser-known methylation-related enzymes also warrant further attention. Further large-scale cohort studies and clinical trials are still necessary to translate novel findings from basic research into clinical application.

Looking ahead, advancements in single-cell sequencing, microbiome research, and multi-omics integration will open new avenues for understanding epigenetic mechanisms in CRC. Methylation serves as a crucial molecular event from the early stages of colorectal adenoma to carcinoma. Focusing on DNA, RNA, and histone methylation in precancerous lesions may provide critical insights for early detection and intervention. Moreover, other forms of methylation, such as mitochondria DNA methylation, are gaining recognition in CRC research. Although some aspects of methylation in CRC remain unexplored, emerging technologies and integrated approaches promise to unravel the complex regulatory networks involved. Ultimately, elucidating the crosstalk among DNA, RNA, and histone methylation may yield novel predictive, diagnostic, and prognostic biomarkers, as well as therapeutic targets, ushering in new strategies for personalized CRC treatment.
