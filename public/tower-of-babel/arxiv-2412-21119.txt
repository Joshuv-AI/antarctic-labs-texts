Eigenstructure Analysis of Bloch Wave and Multislice Formulations for Dynamical Scattering in Transmission Electron Microscopy
Arya Bangun, Oleh Melnyk, Benjamin März

Abstract
We investigate the eigenstructure of matrix formulations used for modeling scattering processes within materials in transmission electron microscopy. Dynamical scattering is crucial for describing the interaction between an electron wave and the material under investigation. Unlike the Bloch wave formulation, which defines the transmission function via the scattering matrix, the traditional multislice method is lacking a pure transmission function due to the entanglement of electron waves with the propagation function. To address this, we reformulate the multislice method into a matrix framework, which we refer to as the transmission matrix. This allows a direct comparison to the scattering matrix derived from Bloch waves in terms of their eigenstructures. Through theory, we demonstrate their equivalence with eigenvectors related by a two-dimensional Fourier matrix, given that the eigenvalue angles differ by modulo 2 ​ π ​ n 2\pi n (integer n n ). We numerically verify our findings as well as demonstrate the application of the eigenstructure for the estimation of the mean inner potential.



I
Introduction

Modeling the three-dimensional atomic structure of a material is essential for studying its physical characteristics, like atomic electrostatic potential
[
34
,
13
,
35
]
. Information about the structure can significantly aid in the process of structure retrieval from experimental data such as diffraction patterns, when analyzing the material in transmission electron microscopes
[
12
]
. Determining the crystalline structure of materials from diffraction experiments is inseparable from the theory of multiple scattering of electrons, developed by Bethe
[
4
]
. Since then, numerous formulations have been developed to model multiple scattering phenomena in crystals, including the multislice method
[
9
,
24
,
16
,
20
]
and the scattering matrix obtained from Bloch waves
[
45
,
18
]
. Both models are frequently used for model-based reconstruction in
TEM
(
TEM
)
[
1
,
44
,
33
,
6
,
14
,
17
,
8
,
3
,
41
]
.

Formulating the scattering matrix from the Bloch wave method incorporates the superposition of many periodic waves within a crystal to solve the Schrödinger equation. Therefore, the relation between electrostatic potential and Bloch waves within the crystal can be represented by an eigenvalue problem. Consequently, the scattering matrix is described by its eigenvalue decomposition, with the eigenvalues encoding parameters such as the specimen’s thickness, and the eigenvectors providing the Fourier coefficients of Bloch waves. On the other hand, the multislice method approximates the total electrostatic potential of the crystal by subdividing it into many thin slices of projected potentials. Then, the incident electron wave interacts with all slices consecutively, with the exit wave of one slice being used as the input wave for the following. A visualization of the Bloch wave and multislice approaches is given in Fig.
1
.
An important benefit of the multislice method is that the eigenvalue decomposition can be avoided, facilitating a more efficient computation of the propagation through the crystal.

The relationship between Bloch waves and the multislice method is discussed, for instance, in
[
11
]
. In several publications
[
51
,
43
,
29
,
15
]
, both models are numerically compared. Apart from the computation time, these analyses are mainly focused on the error in the intensity of the specimen exit wave functions for both methods, as this is the quantity that is finally obtained on a
TEM
detector.

While such comparisons are valid to capture the performance, accuracy and consistency of outputs produced by both approaches, the underlying model is treated rather as a black box system and similarity is measured only implicitly.
More importantly, measuring similarity solely based on intensity captures the projection information, which does not provide insight into the spatial structure of the electrostatic potentials, neither by the Bloch wave nor the multislice approach. In our study, we therefore go a step further and, rather than relying on the projected intensity alone, directly evaluate the 3D electrostatic potential.

To compare the models, we study how both methods transform the incidence wave. Using Bloch waves, we have a direct expression in terms of the Fourier coefficients; such an expression can not be directly achieved for the multislice method.
In the original formulation of the multislice method, the possibility of defining a pure transmission function, akin to the scattering matrix in the Bloch wave approach, is precluded due to the inherent entanglement between the exit wave across slices and Fresnel propagation. As a consequence, a direct comparison of the eigenstructure of the scattering matrix with the multislice method is made impossible. To circumvent this limitation, we reformulate the multislice model using matrix products and achieve a crucial separation between the input wave and the matrix that represents the transmission function of the crystal structure, which we call
transmission matrix
. This reformulation enables us to model the transmission function in a manner directly comparable to the scattering matrix by using eigenstructure analysis. Rather than the conventional approach of comparing resulting intensities, we present a direct mathematical comparison of the transmission matrix with the scattering matrix.
Furthermore, we investigate whether the similarity between both matrices implies that also the underlying atomic structure is the same by evaluating diffraction patterns simulated by both methods. In addition to that, we leverage the determinant (the product of eigenvalues) of the transmission matrix to estimate the
MIP
(
MIP
), an important physical property of the inner structure of a crystal often analyzed in
TEM
and particularly electron holography experiments
[
48
,
32
]
.

At first, we briefly introduce the scattering and transmission matrices:

I.1
Transmission Matrix.

Our formulation of the transmission matrix is derived from the multislice method. Instead of calculating an analytical solution, such as in the Bloch wave approach, the transmission function is built step-by-step using infinitely thin ‘sub-specimens’ (slices) represented by
N\times N
matrices. The (real-space) transmission matrix of a crystal with
M
slices is given by

\mathbf{A}=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{O}_{m},

(1)

where
\mathbf{O}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}\times N^{2}}
is the diagonal matrix representing the potential of each slice, and
\mathbf{G}_{m}
is the Fresnel propagator in free space. The elements of both potential and Fresnel propagator matrices depend on interaction constants, spatial frequencies, and tilt angles of the incoming wave. As scattering is typically considered in terms of the Fourier coefficients, so-called reciprocal space, we transform equation (
1
) as

\mathbf{\hat{S}}=\mathbf{F}_{2D}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}_{2D}^{-1},

(2)

where
\mathbf{F}_{2D}
and
\mathbf{F}_{2D}^{-1}
denote the two-dimensional Fourier and inverse Fourier transforms, respectively. The outlined transformation models the diffraction pattern in the back focal plane of a TEM.

I.2
Scattering Matrix.

The scattering matrix
\mathbf{S}
describes the interaction of an input wave function with a crystal, and it can be used to determine a crystal’s transmission properties. The derivation of the scattering matrix in
TEM
is based on the theory of electron scattering in crystals, namely,
by incorporating Bloch waves to solve the Schrödinger equation. Expanding Bloch waves into Fourier series
leads to
the scattering matrix of the form

\mathbf{S}:=\mathbf{C}\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}\times N^{2}},

(3)

where
\mathbf{C}
is a unitary matrix whose columns are the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{S}
, representing the Bloch wave Fourier coefficients, and
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix containing the corresponding eigenvalues.
These eigenvalues of modulus one encode phase shifts introduced by scattering in the specimen.

II
Results

In this section, we discuss the structural similarity between transmission matrix
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
and the scattering matrix
\mathbf{S}
, which we investigated by leveraging their eigenstructures, i.e., their eigenvalues and eigenvectors.
We discuss how this equivalency provides insights into the underlying physical processes of dynamical scattering and the accuracy of the approximations resulting from each method.

We conduct numerical simulations to generate the atomic potentials as well as scattering and transmission matrices for three different ideal crystals (GaAs, SrTiO
3
, Au), directly from the multislice and Bloch wave models. Both matrices are then compared by analyzing the distribution of the eigenvalues in terms of plane-wave diffraction patterns, which we generate using
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
, the estimated projected potentials as well as
MIP
s.

We propose a novel approach for the estimation of the
MIP
facilitating the product of the eigenvalues (i.e. determinant) of the transmission matrix, which we then validate by computing the
MIP
of a MoS
2
crystal from an experimental dataset obtained by
4D-STEM
(
4D-STEM
).

II.1
Characterizing Similarity.

Comparing the amplitudes of both exit waves after interaction with either the scattering or the transmission matrix is straightforward. However, it does not provide the means to investigate the (projected) potentials of a crystal solely from diffraction patterns.
We, instead, propose to directly investigate the structural similarity between scattering and transmission matrix in terms of their eigenstructures, as presented in Fig.
1
c, d.

Since the scattering matrix
\mathbf{S}
is directly defined by its eigendecomposition in equation (
3
), we perform the eigendecomposition of the transmission matrix
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
, yielding the matrix of eigenvectors
\mathbf{W}
and the diagonal matrix
\Sigma
containing eigenvalues.
The equivalence between the scattering and the transmission matrix holds as long as the following conditions are satisfied:

•

Eigenvectors
\mathbf{C}
of scattering matrix
\mathbf{S}
and eigenvectors
\mathbf{W}
of matrix
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
are equal to the relation
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{F}_{2D}\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{V}
is eigenvector of real space transmission matrix
\mathbf{A}
;

•

As the transmission matrix is unitary, its eigenvalues also have unit modulus. Thus, the eigenvalues
\Lambda
and
\Sigma
coincide if the respective angles differ by up to modulo
2\pi n
for integers
n
.

II.4
Determinant and Mean Inner Potential.

The
MIP
reflects the interaction between the beam electrons and the material. It is closely linked to the inner structure of a crystal and is calculated by the average of the Coulomb potential over a unit cell,

V_{0}=\frac{1}{\Omega}V_{total}.

(4)

Here,
\Omega
is the volume of the crystal unit cell
[
4
]
and
V_{total}
is the total potential.

The
MIP
has been studied both theoretically and experimentally and was estimated for different materials in numerous works
[
30
,
31
,
19
,
50
,

5

\mathbf{A}

1

\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{\hat{S}}

\det(\mathbf{\hat{S}})\approx\det(\mathbf{A})=\exp\left(-i\left(\pi\lambda QT-\sigma V_{total}\right)\right),

where,

is the electron wavelength,
Q
is the total integral over reciprocal space,
T
is the specimen thickness, and
\sigma
is the interaction constant. From this equation, we can extract the total potential that, in combination with the discrete grid of pixels of known distance (step size), leads to the

V_{0}^{\mathrm{Det}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=\frac{1}{\sigma N^{2}c}\left(\pi\lambda QT-i\log\det\left(\mathbf{\hat{S}}\right)-2\pi n\right),}

(5)

with
2\pi n
for an integer
n

N
is the total number of pixels, and the unit cell parameter

, which is synonymous with the specimen thickness.

\mathbf{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{Unwrap}}

II.5
Computational Complexity.

The apparent strength of the multislice over the Bloch wave method is the computational complexity. To compute the
N^{2}\times N^{2}
scattering matrix via equation (
3
), eigenvalue decomposition
has to be performed, requiring
\mathcal{O}\left(N^{6}\right)
operations. For the transmission matrix, we perform the sequential matrix product of Fresnel matrices
\mathbf{G}_{m}
and diagonal potential matrices
\mathbf{O}_{m}
. The products with
\mathbf{O}_{m}
and
\mathbf{G}_{m}
require
\mathcal{O}\left(N^{4}\right)
and, respectively,
\mathcal{O}\left(N^{4}\log N\right)
operations. In the first case, we use that
\mathbf{O}_{m}
is diagonal, and in the second, we employ the fast Fourier transform. Repeating these steps for
M
slices leads to a total complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(MN^{4}\log N\right)
operations.

III
Discussion

In this study, we discussed a new perspective for analyzing and understanding the relationship between the multislice and Bloch wave methods.
Our presented reformulation of the multislice method into a matrix framework, which we call transmission matrix, not only enables leveraging eigenstructure analysis to measure the similarity to the scattering matrix but also allows the estimation of the
MIP
from diffraction data.

By expressing the multislice method as the transmission matrix, we have demonstrated that its structure parallels that of the scattering matrix. Firstly, we demonstrate mathematically that the eigenvalues of both matrices are equal up to an additive multiple of
2\pi
. Secondly, we prove that the eigenvectors are interconnected through the two-dimensional Fourier matrix, with the eigenvector of the transmission matrix representing the Bloch wave itself in real space.

Our matrix-based approach allows a consistent approximation of the projected potential. Moreover, our numerical evaluations of plane-wave diffraction, eigenvalues, as well as projected and
MIP
support these theoretical results, confirming the robustness and accuracy of the theoretical derivation of eigenstructures for both the transmission and scattering matrix.

Furthermore, the structure of the transmission matrix enables the utilization of the determinant, i.e., the product of all eigenvalues, to estimate the
MIP
of crystalline materials, making it a convenient tool for the evaluation of practical experiments, particularly due to its lower computational demands relative to the scattering matrix of the Bloch wave approach.

To put our results in Table
1
into perspective, our multislice simulations were conducted using ideal crystals without considering surface relaxations of the terminating layer at the specimen/vacuum interface, that in reality would be expected. Our estimated
MIP
s therefore do not account for any specific surface contributions. It is thus noteworthy that, nevertheless, our results compare well to experimentally determined values of real-world samples (
\mathbf{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{Exp}}
), and to results of DFT calculations of relaxed structures (
\mathbf{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{DFT}}
) previously reported. Only the estimation of the
MIP
of SrTiO
3
remains a challenge which we attribute to its strong ionic character.

We estimate that our presented method for determining the
MIP
to perform equally well or better with any experimental
4D-STEM
data as well as with simulated
4D-STEM
data from relaxed structures.

IV.1
Notation.

Vectors are written in bold small-cap letters
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{L}
and matrices are written in bold big-cap letters
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times L}
for a complex field
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{R}
for a real field. Matrices can also be written as its element

\mathbf{A}=\left(a_{k\ell}\right)
, where
k\in[K],\ell\in[L]
. The set of integers is written as
[N]:=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
.
For both matrices and vectors the notation
\circ
is used to represent the element-wise or Hadamard product. The
\mathbf{A}^{H}
is used to represent conjugate transpose. A matrix is called unitary if
\mathbf{A}^{H}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{H}=\mathbf{I}
, where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix. For a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times L}
, the Frobenius norm is given by

\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{F}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left|a_{kl}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=\left(\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{A}^{H}\mathbf{A}\right)\right)^{1/2}.

IV.2
Scattering Matrix.

The derivation of the scattering matrix in
TEM
is tied to the theory of dynamical electron scattering in crystals.
We start with Klein-Gordon equation to describe the relativistic scattering of electrons in the crystal

\Delta\Psi\left(\mathbf{r}\right)+4\pi^{2}k_{0}^{2}\Psi\left(\mathbf{r}\right)=-\frac{8\pi^{2}me}{h^{2}}V\left(\mathbf{r}\right)\Psi\left(\mathbf{r}\right),

(6)

where
V
is the electrostatic potential at three-dimensional real space coordinates
\mathbf{r}=(x,y,z)^{T}
,
m
is the relativistic electron mass,
h
is the Planck constant,
e
is the elementary charge, and
k_{0}
is the relativistic wave number.
Intuitively, the goal is to find proper wave functions
\Psi
that fulfill the Klein-Gordon equation.

As discussed in
[
11
, eq. 5.43]
, the strategy to solve the partial differential equation (
6
) is by involving the expansion in the Fourier space, also called reciprocal space. Firstly, the potential is expanded into its Fourier series,

V\left(\mathbf{r}\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{h}}V_{\mathbf{h}}\exp\left(i2\pi\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{r}\right),

where
V_{\mathbf{h}}
is the lattice potential in reciprocal space. Secondly, the Bloch wave is incorporated, given as

\Psi\left(\mathbf{r}\right)=c\left(\mathbf{r}\right)\exp\left(i2\pi{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{g}}c_{\mathbf{g}}\exp\left(i2\pi{\left(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{g}\right)}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\right),

where
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are wave and reciprocal space vectors, respectively. Variable
c_{\mathbf{g}}
is the Bloch wave coefficient in reciprocal space. By rearranging this equation and writing the wave functions and potential in reciprocal space coordinates, we obtain the eigenvalue equation, the solution of which is the scattering matrix

\mathbf{S}=\exp\left(i2\pi T\mathbf{B}\right).

For a complete derivation, we refer the interested readers to the literature, for instance
[
36
,
23
,
11
]
.
Here
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}\times N^{2}}
is the structure matrix representing the electrostatic potential of the underlying crystal given by

\begin{pmatrix}&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\\
\ldots&s_{\mathbf{h}}&V_{\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{g}}&V_{\mathbf{h}}&V_{\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{g}}&V_{\mathbf{2}h}&\ldots\\
\ldots&V_{\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{h}}&s_{\mathbf{g}}&V_{\mathbf{g}}&V_{2\mathbf{g}}&V_{\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{g}}&\ldots\\
\ldots&V_{-\mathbf{h}}&V_{-\mathbf{g}}&s_{\bm{0}}&V_{\mathbf{g}}&V_{\mathbf{h}}&{\ldots}\\
\ldots&V_{-\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{h}}&V_{-2\mathbf{g}}&V_{-\mathbf{g}}&s_{-\mathbf{g}}&V_{-\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{h}}&{\ldots}\\
\ldots&V_{-2\mathbf{h}}&V_{-\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{g}}&V_{-\mathbf{h}}&V_{-\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{g}}&s_{-\mathbf{h}}&{\ldots}\\
&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&{\vdots}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where the diagonal and off-diagonal elements represent the excitation error and, respectively, potential in reciprocal space, and
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the reciprocal lattice vectors. This relation between the crystal and the scattering matrix is illustrated in Fig.
1
a, c.
Due to the nature of matrix exponentiation, both
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{B}
share eigenvectors
\mathbf{C}
,

\mathbf{C}=\begin{pmatrix}\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\\
c_{\mathbf{h}}^{1}&c_{\mathbf{h}}^{2}&\ldots&c_{\mathbf{h}}^{i}&\ldots\\
c_{\mathbf{g}}^{1}&c_{\mathbf{g}}^{2}&\ldots&c_{\mathbf{g}}^{i}&\ldots\\
c_{\mathbf{0}}^{1}&c_{\mathbf{0}}^{2}&\ldots&c_{\mathbf{0}}^{i}&\ldots\\
c_{-\mathbf{g}}^{1}&c_{-\mathbf{g}}^{2}&\ldots&c_{-\mathbf{g}}^{i}&\ldots\\
c_{-\mathbf{h}}^{1}&c_{-\mathbf{h}}^{2}&\ldots&c_{-\mathbf{h}}^{i}&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}\times N^{2}}.

Furthermore, the eigenvalues
\Lambda
of
\mathbf{S}
are given as

\left(\exp{\left(2\pi i\gamma_{1}T\right)},\exp{\left(2\pi i\gamma_{2}T\right)},\ldots,\exp{\left(2\pi i\gamma_{N^{2}}T\right)}\right).

where
\gamma_{j}
,
j\in[N^{2}]
are the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{B}
. Combined, this yields equation (
3
).

IV.3
Transmission Matrix.

Instead of directly deriving an analytical solution as is done with the Bloch wave approach, the transmission function can be constructed using multiple infinitely thin (sub-)specimens, i.e., slices, separated by free space. This is the approach we take using the multislice method.

More precisely, let
V
denote the potential energy of the three-dimensional crystal. The projection of
V
onto the
x
-
y
plane at
z_{m}
is expressed by
V_{m}(x,y)=\int_{z_{m}}^{z_{m+1}}V(x,y,z)\mathrm{d}z
. Then, the projected potentials
V_{m}
are arranged as diagonal matrices
\mathbf{O}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}\times N^{2}}
of equation (
1
), each representing the
m
-th slice. Its entries are given by

\left(\mathbf{O}_{m}\right)_{x+N(y-1),x+N(y-1)}=\exp\left(i\sigma V_{m}(x,y)\right)\,\text{for}\,x,y\in[N],

(7)

with the interaction constant
\sigma
for relativistic correction. For modeling the scattering process, we also account for the separation of the slices
\mathbf{O}_{m}
and
\mathbf{O}_{m+1}
by distance
\Delta_{m}=z_{m+1}-z_{m}
as depicted in Fig.
1
b. This results in a free space propagation described by the Fresnel matrix
\mathbf{G}_{m}
,

\mathbf{G}_{m}:=\mathbf{F}^{-1}_{2D}\mathbf{D}_{m}\mathbf{F}_{2D}\in\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}\times N^{2}},\ m\in[M],

(8)

where
\mathbf{D}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}\times N^{2}}
is a diagonal matrix. Its elements are

\left(\mathbf{D}_{m}\right)_{x+N(y-1),x+N(y-1)}:=\exp\left(-\pi i\Delta_{m}\lambda Q_{xy}\right)\,\text{for}\,x,y\in[N],

(9)

with
Q_{xy}=\left(q_{y}^{2}+q_{x}^{2}\right)+2\left(q_{x}\frac{\sin\theta_{x}}{\lambda}+q_{y}\frac{\sin\theta_{y}}{\lambda}\right)
defined by the spatial frequencies
q_{x},q_{y}
in reciprocal space and two-dimensional tilt angles
\theta_{x},\theta_{y}
describing the direction of the incoming wave.

Then, the scattering of the incident beam within the crystal in the multislice model is obtained by consecutive multiplication with slice
\mathbf{O}_{m}
and Fresnel propagators
\mathbf{G}_{m}
. Combining all multiplications together yields the real space transmission matrix
\mathbf{A}
and its reciprocal space counterpart
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
as in equations (
1
) and (
2
). For the detailed derivation, see
[
3
]
.

As highlighted in
[
9
,
11
]
, the multislice method serves as an alternative to the Bloch wave formulation, making the transmission matrix
\mathbf{\hat{S}}

an approximate solution to the Schrödinger equation (
6
). The main advantage that this reformulation offers is that the atomic structure of a crystalline object can now be represented independently by the transmission matrix
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
, without being influenced by the electron probe, which is not the case in the classical multislice model.

IV.4
Characterizing Similarity.

In the following, we compare the transmission matrix
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
in equation (
2
) and scattering matrix
\mathbf{S}
in equation (
3
) in terms of their eigenstructures.

As
\mathbf{S}
is defined via eigenvalue decomposition in equation (
3
), we first establish the eigendecomposition for
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
.
By construction, we have

\mathbf{\hat{S}}=\mathbf{F}_{2D}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{O}_{m}\mathbf{F}_{2D}^{-1}=\mathbf{F}_{2D}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{F}_{2D}^{-1}\mathbf{D}_{m}\mathbf{F}_{2D}\mathbf{O}_{m}\mathbf{F}_{2D}^{-1},

where matrices
\mathbf{D}_{m}
and
\mathbf{O}_{m}
are both diagonal with unit modulus entries, and are therefore unitary. Fourier transforms are also unitary and, consequently,
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
is unitary being the product of unitary matrices. Hence, this also implies that
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
is normal matrix, i.e., it satisfies
\mathbf{\hat{S}}^{H}\mathbf{\hat{S}}=\mathbf{\hat{S}}\mathbf{\hat{S}}^{H}=\mathbf{I}
. As a result,
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
admits eigenvalue decomposition
\mathbf{\hat{S}}=\mathbf{W}\Sigma\mathbf{W}^{-1}
with a unitary matrix
\mathbf{W}
, which columns are the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
, and with a diagonal matrix
\Sigma
with eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
on its diagonal. Since
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
is unitary, the eigenvalues are of unit modulus and can be written in terms of a complex exponential,
\lambda_{k}\left(\mathbf{\hat{S}}\right)=\exp\left(i2\pi\theta_{k}\right)
, where
\theta_{k}\in[0,1]
for
k\in[N^{2}]
.

To quantify any differences, the scattering and the transmission matrix are compared in terms of the Frobenius norm. This norm acts as a distance metric, enabling the assessment of the accuracy and consistency between both matrices.
For simplicity, we assume that the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
are sorted in the order of increasing angles
\gamma_{k}
and
\theta_{k}
.
Then, using triangle inequality, we split the error into the eigenvector and eigenvalue errors as

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{\hat{S}}\right\|_{F}=\left\|\mathbf{C}\Lambda\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{W}\Sigma\mathbf{W}^{-1}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|\mathbf{C}\Lambda\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{W}\Lambda\mathbf{W}^{-1}\right\|_{F}+\left\|\mathbf{W}\Lambda\mathbf{W}^{-1}-\mathbf{W}\Sigma\mathbf{W}^{-1}\right\|_{F}.

We further transform the first term as

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{C}\Lambda\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{W}\Lambda\mathbf{W}^{-1}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|\mathbf{C}\Lambda\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{W}\Lambda\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{F}+\left\|\mathbf{W}\Lambda\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{W}\Lambda\mathbf{W}^{-1}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\quad=\left\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{W})\Lambda\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{F}+\left\|\mathbf{W}\Lambda(\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{W}^{-1})\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{W}\right\|_{F}\left\|\Lambda\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{W}^{-1}\right\|_{F}\left\|\mathbf{W}\Lambda\right\|_{2},

where in the second line we used the triangle inequality again and in the last line we used that Frobenius norm of the product is bounded by the product of Frobenius and spectral norms. The spectral norm is the magnitude of the largest eigenvalue and for unitary
\Lambda\mathbf{C}^{-1}
and
\mathbf{W}\Lambda
it is equal to one. Moreover, since both
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{W}
are unitary, we get
\mathbf{C}^{-1}=\mathbf{C}^{H}
,
\mathbf{W}^{-1}=\mathbf{W}^{H}
and

\left\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{W}^{-1}\right\|_{F}=\left\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{W})^{H}\right\|_{F}=\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{W}\right\|_{F}.

Consequently, the upper bound for the first term simplifies to

\left\|\mathbf{C}\Lambda\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{W}\Lambda\mathbf{W}^{-1}\right\|_{F}\leq 2\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{W}\right\|_{F},

which is the error between the eigenvector matrices. Similarly, we bound the second term as the error on the eigenvalues,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{W}\Lambda\mathbf{W}^{-1}-\mathbf{W}\Sigma\mathbf{W}^{-1}\right\|_{F}=\left\|\mathbf{W}(\Lambda-\Sigma)\mathbf{W}^{-1}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|\mathbf{W}\right\|_{2}\left\|\Lambda-\Sigma\right\|_{F}\left\|\mathbf{W}^{-1}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\Lambda-\Sigma\right\|_{F}.

Combined, it yields

\left\|\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{\hat{S}}\right\|_{F}\leq 2\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{W}\right\|_{F}+\left\|\Lambda-\Sigma\right\|_{F}.

We can further expand the eigenvalue error in terms of angles,

\displaystyle\left\|\Lambda-\Sigma\right\|_{F}^{2}=2N^{2}-\text{trace}\left(\Lambda^{H}\Sigma\right)-\text{trace}\left(\Lambda\Sigma^{H}\right),

\displaystyle\quad\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(c)}}}{{=}}2N^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{N^{2}}[e^{\left(i2\pi\left(\gamma_{k}T-\theta_{k}\right)\right)}+e^{\left(-i2\pi\left(\gamma_{k}T-\theta_{k}\right)\right)}]

\displaystyle\quad\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(d)}}}{{=}}2N^{2}-2\sum_{k=1}^{N^{2}}\cos\left(2\pi\left(\gamma_{k}T-\theta_{k}\right)\right){.}

(10)

The equality
(c)
is derived from the fact that the eigenvalue of matrix
\mathbf{S}
is
\exp(i2\pi\gamma_{k}T)
for
k\in[N^{2}]
and that matrix
\mathbf{A}
is a unitary matrix with modulus eigenvalues of the form
\exp(i2\pi\theta_{k})
for
k\in[N^{2}]
.

IV.5
Determinant and Mean Inner Potential.

Apart from characterizing similarity, we can also estimate the
MIP
(
MIP
) using the transmission and scattering matrices. As defined in equation (
4
), it requires the total potential
V_{total}
that is estimated using the determinant of
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
. More precisely, we compute
\det(\mathbf{\hat{S}})
as

\displaystyle\det\left(\mathbf{\hat{S}}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(\mathbf{F}_{2D}\right)\det\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\det\left(\mathbf{F}_{2D}^{-1}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle=\det\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{O}_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(\mathbf{G}_{1}\mathbf{O}_{1}\right)\det\left(\mathbf{G}_{2}\mathbf{O}_{2}\right)\dots\det\left(\mathbf{G}_{M}\mathbf{O}_{M}\right).

The equality holds because
\det(\mathbf{F}_{2D}^{-1})=1/\det(\mathbf{F}_{2D})
and the determinant of a matrix product is equal to the product of the determinants of the individual factors. Usign that the determinant of diagonal matrix is the product of the elements, the determinant of products
\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{O}_{m}

\displaystyle\det\left(\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{O}_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:fresnel_matrix}}}{{=}}\det\left(\mathbf{F}_{2D}^{-1}\mathbf{D}_{m}\mathbf{F}_{2D}\right)\det\left(\mathbf{O}_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(\mathbf{G}_{m}\right)\det\left(\mathbf{O}_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:potential},\eqref{eq:fresnel_element}}}{{=}}\prod_{x,y=1}^{N}\exp\left(-\pi i\Delta_{m}\lambda Q_{xy}\right)\exp\left(i\sigma V_{m}(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-i\left(\pi\Delta_{m}\lambda Q-\sigma V_{m}\right)\right)

where we used notation

Q=\sum_{x=1}^{N}\sum_{y=1}^{N}Q_{xy}\quad\text{and}\quad V_{m}=\sum_{x=1}^{N}\sum_{y=1}^{N}V_{m}(x,y).

Returning to equation (
11
), the logarithm of the determinant of
\mathbf{\hat{S}}
is

\displaystyle\log\det\left(\mathbf{\hat{S}}\right)

\displaystyle=-i\pi\lambda Q\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Delta_{m}+i\sigma\sum_{m=1}^{M}V_{m}+2\pi in

(12)

\displaystyle=-i\pi\lambda QT+i\sigma\sum_{m=1}^{M}V_{m}+2\pi in.

where
T=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Delta_{m}
is the total thickness of the crystal and
2\pi in
with integer
n
results from periodicity of complex logarithm.

The sum of potentials is linked to the total potential

\displaystyle V_{total}

\displaystyle=\int_{x}\int_{y}\int_{z}V(x,y,z)\mathrm{d}_{z}\mathrm{d}_{y}\mathrm{d}_{x}

by the Riemman sum approximation. Following equation C.20 in Kirkland, 1998
[
28
]
, the inner integral is precisely
\int_{z}V(x,y,z)\mathrm{d}_{z}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}V_{m}(x,y)
. Hence, we can write

\displaystyle V_{total}\approx\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}V_{m}\left(j\frac{a}{N},k\frac{b}{N}\right)\frac{ab}{N^{2}}=\frac{ab}{N^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}V_{m},

(13)

where
a,b
and
c
are the height, width and thickness of the unit cell in Ångstrom.

With the unit cell volume
\Omega=abc
in cubic Ångstrom, the MIP is

V_{0}=\frac{1}{\Omega}V_{\text{total}}=\frac{1}{N^{2}c}\sum_{m=1}^{M}V_{m}.

(14)

By combining equations (
12
) and (
14
), we obtain

\log\det\left(\mathbf{\hat{S}}\right)=-i\pi\lambda QT+i\sigma{N^{2}c}V_{0}+2\pi in

and rearranging yields the estimate in equation (
5
).

IV.6
Mean Inner Potential and Unwrapping.

An alternative way to compute the
MIP
is by directly using the slices
\mathbf{O}_{m}
. From equation (
7
), we observe that the phases of
\mathbf{O}_{m}
are
\sigma V_{m}(x,y)
wrapped periodically due to the complex exponent. Thus,
\sigma V_{m}(x,y)
can be recovered from
\mathbf{O}_{m}
by phase unwrapping
[
22
]
up to an additive constant
c_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\text{{Unwrapp}}(\mathbf{O}_{m})=\sigma V_{m}(x,y)+c_{m}.

In crystallographic applications, the constant can be further estimated by assuming that the most prevalent part of the observed object is vacuum, which does not cause scattering. Hence, the minimal value of the potential is almost zero,
leading to

c_{m}=\min_{x,y}\text{{Unwrapp}}(\mathbf{O}_{m})_{x,y}.

and

V_{m}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\text{{Unwrapp}}(\mathbf{O}_{m})-c_{m}\right).

We can then use the above values to compute the total inner potential via equation (
13
).

A.1
Multislice Method.

We present the classical formulation of the multislice method and briefly present the derivation of an alternative formulation based on matrices. The complete derivation is given in Bangun et. al., 2022
[
3
]
.

Suppose we have a matrix that represents the
m-
th slice of the electrostatic potential of the specimen for a thin slice, similar to equation (
7
).

\exp\left(i\sigma\int_{z_{m}}^{z_{m+1}}V(x,y,z)dz\right)

In fact, for each combination axis
x,y
we collect the potential and construct it into matrix
\mathbf{X}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
for a total slice number of
m\in[M]
. The interaction between the first slice and the electron wave for focused beam illumination at a specific scanning point
s
is given by

\mathbf{E}_{1}=\mathbf{X}_{1}\circ\mathbf{P}_{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}

The exit wave is used as the source for the next slice by propagation using the Fresnel propagator
\mathcal{V}
,

\mathbf{E}_{m}=\mathcal{V}\left(\mathbf{E}_{m-1}\right)\circ\mathbf{X}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\quad\text{for}\quad m\in\{2,3,\dots,M\}.

The Fresnel propagator is given in Fourier space for a specific distance between slices. We define the Fourier space of the Fresnel propagator matrix
\mathbf{H}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
. Its elements are given in equation (
9
). Hence, the Fresnel propagation can be written as

\mathcal{V}\left(\mathbf{E}_{m}\right):=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{H}_{m}\circ\mathcal{F}\left(\mathbf{E}_{m}\right)\right).

As a result, the intensity recorded at a specific scanning point
s
in the microscope’s detector plane is given as

\mathbf{I}_{s}=\left|\mathcal{F}\left(\mathbf{E}_{M}\right)\right|^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},

where the absolute value is applied to each element of the matrix.

It can be seen that, a separation of the entanglement between the electron wave and the object is difficult. Hence, an exact representation of the transmission function of the object, independent of the propagated exit wave starting from the probe illumination, is not available. As discussed in Bangun et. al., 2022
[
3
]
, the reformulation by leveraging the property of element-wise or Hadamard matrix product, namely

\displaystyle\text{vec}\left(\mathbf{A}\circ\mathbf{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\left(\text{vec}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\right)\text{vec}\left(\mathbf{B}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\left(\text{vec}\left(\mathbf{B}\right)\right)\text{vec}\left(\mathbf{A}\right),

where the function
\text{vec}:\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}}
is used to convert a matrix into a vector and
\text{diag}:\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}\times N^{2}}
is the function to construct a diagonal matrix from a vector. The vectorized intensity at scanning point
s
can be written as

\mathbf{i}_{s}=\left|\mathbf{F}_{2D}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{O_{m}}\right)\mathbf{p}_{s}\right|^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}}\,\text{for}\,s\in[S]

Using this formulation, we can disentangle the illuminating electron wave and the object in order to get a pure transmission function

\mathbf{A}=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{O_{m}},

where the formulation of Fresnel matrix
\mathbf{G}_{m}
is derived in equation (
8
).

A.2
Matrix Representation of Fourier Transforms.

We present a matrix representation of Fourier transforms. The one-dimensional Fourier transform can be written as a matrix-vector product.
Here, the term one-dimensional Fourier matrix represents the discrete implementation of the Fourier basis, i.e., when we sample the Fourier basis and store it as a matrix
\mathbf{F}_{1D}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
. Hence, the one-dimensional Fourier transform of vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
can be written as

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{F}_{1D}\mathbf{x},

where the one-dimensional Fourier transform is given by
[
25
, eq. 5.44]
,

\mathbf{F}_{1D}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{-i2\pi f_{1}x_{1}}{N}}\quad e^{\frac{-i2\pi f_{1}x_{2}}{N}}&\dots&e^{\frac{-i2\pi f_{1}x_{N}}{N}}\\
e^{\frac{-i2\pi f_{2}x_{1}}{N}}\quad e^{\frac{-i2\pi f_{2}x_{2}}{N}}&\dots&e^{\frac{-i2\pi f_{2}x_{N}}{N}}\\
\vdots&\dots&\vdots\\
e^{\frac{-i2\pi f_{N}x_{1}}{N}}\quad e^{\frac{-i2\pi f_{N}x_{2}}{N}}&\dots&e^{\frac{-i2\pi f_{N}x_{N}}{N}},\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}.

Here,
x_{j}
for
j\in[N]
represents the sampling points at evenly spaced real space coordinates, and
f_{j}
for
j\in[N]
is the sample in the Fourier space. Along the same line, the two-dimensional Fourier matrix can be constructed using the Kronecker product between two of the one-dimensional Fourier matrices
\mathbf{F}_{2D}=\mathbf{F}_{1D}\otimes\mathbf{F}_{1D}\in\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}\times N^{2}}
. The two-dimensional Fourier transform of matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
can be written as
\mathbf{F}_{2D}\text{vec}\left(\mathbf{X}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}}
.
