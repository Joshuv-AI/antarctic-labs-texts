What is the prevalence of depression and anxiety in externally and internally displaced Ukrainian refugees? A European mixed-methods study
Authors: Nataliia Ponzel, Ileana Gefaell-Larrondo, Oksana Ilkov, Jānis Blumfelds, Limor Adler, Alejandra Aguilar Latorre, Avivit Golan, Sherihane Bensemmane, Macarena Chacón-Docampo, Philippe Domeyer, Robert D Hoffman, Hüsna Sarıca Çevik, Shlomo Vinker, Marija Zafirovska, Aleksandar Zafirovski, Kamer Billur Yücel Özden, Michael Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRefugees are more likely to experience higher rates of depression and anxiety. Since the start of the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, large numbers of Ukrainians have been forced to leave their country or become internally displaced. This study investigated the levels of depression and anxiety symptoms in Ukrainian refugees, both outside and inside Ukraine.MethodsA descriptive, mixed-methods study of Ukrainians aged > 18 who became refugees during or following February 2022, currently living in Belgium, Greece, Israel, Latvia, North Macedonia, Slovenia, Spain, Türkiye and Ukraine. We asked about previous episodes of depression and/or anxiety and used the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 questionnaires to assess current prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms (score ≥ 15, categorised as moderate-severe symptoms of depression, and ≥ 10 categorised as moderate-severe symptoms of anxiety, respectively). Participants’ current place of residence was categorised as ‘Outside Ukraine’ or ‘Inside Ukraine’. An open-ended question asked: “What life events/circumstances have affected your psychological state now?” Participant recruitment was through convenience sampling during 2023–2024. We used descriptive statistics, with bivariate, logistic regression of quantitative variables, and thematic analysis of the free-text answers.ResultsFour hundred sixty two respondents met the inclusion criteria, mean age 41 (SD:12.5); 422 (91.7%) were women. Before 2022, 64 (13.9%) had been diagnosed with depression and/or anxiety, and 36 (7.8%) had received treatment for these conditions. The current prevalence of moderate-severe symptoms of depression was 37.0% (95% CI: 32.6–41.6) and of moderate-severe symptoms of anxiety was 51.5% (95% CI: 46.9–56.2). After adjusting for age, the odds ratio for refugees outside Ukraine compared with those remaining in Ukraine was 1.86 (95% CI:1.18–2.93) for moderate-severe symptoms of depression, and 1.70 (1.11–2.58) for moderate-severe symptoms of anxiety. Thematic analysis revealed six themes: impact of war, disrupted networks, economic burdens, health-related problems, barriers to local integration, using tools, and resources for a better adaptation.ConclusionsThe prevalence of moderate-severe symptoms of depression and anxiety in Ukrainian war refugees was high, with those in Europe being more likely to have symptoms than those internally displaced. We found a wide range of circumstances that had affected respondents’ psychological states.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27853-9.Keywords: Refugees, War, Depression and anxiety, Ukraine, Europe

Introduction

One in every eight people worldwide lives with a mental health disorder (MHD) [1, 2]. In crisis situations, individuals are at higher risk of developing MHDs, particularly in the case of war-related violence [3–8].

The full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, with its civilian casualties and destruction of infrastructure, forced civilians to flee their homes in search of safety, protection and assistance [9, 10]. The forcibly displaced population living outside Ukraine are called refugees, while those that moved to another region within Ukraine are known as Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs). According to the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), over 17.6 million individuals fled Ukraine and became refugees (90% women), and 4.6 million IDPs resettled in Ukraine (60% women) in October 2024 [9–12]. The displaced population was exposed to many stressful factors, both before and after leaving their homes, including watching news feeds about the war and communicating with relatives who remained in Ukraine [13, 14].

While refugees and IDPs have higher levels of MHDs than non-migrants, the reported prevalence varies considerably between studies. A systematic review found that major depression and anxiety affected 5% and 4% of refugees resettled in western countries respectively [15]. Another systematic review reported higher rates, ranging from 30 to 40% for depression and 13–42% for anxiety [16], and a meta-analysis reported a prevalence of 13.8% for depression and 15.8% for anxiety [17]. Most of the recent research has taken place in high-income countries [15–18], while most refugees live in low and middle-income countries [18]. Variations in anxiety and depression levels can be attributed to factors such as country of origin and resettlement location [4], poor social integration, family separation, unemployment, and difficulties in accessing health care or the lack of appropriate social services interventions [15].

However, previous research shows that after five years of resettlement the rates of depression and anxiety decrease [4]. Being close to families or being able to keep in touch with them is a protective factor that enhances resilience [19].

The Ukrainian population experienced profound trauma, as shown in a cross-sectional survey of adolescents which revealed higher rates of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) among participants from a war-torn region damaged in 2014 compared to a less-damaged region: 60.2% had witnessed armed attacks, 13.9% had been victims of violence, and 27.9% had been displaced from their homes [20]. In Germany, Ukrainian refugees tended to show high scores for depression and anxiety in the PHQ-4 and GAD-2, with women being the most vulnerable group [21]. Some studies have compared the mental health of refugees in other countries with those of IDPs: preliminary data of an ongoing study showed that Ukrainian refugees in Poland had lower rates of anxiety compared to IDPs in Lviv, Ukraine [19], and another study comparing Ukrainian refugees in Poland and Taiwan with their native populations found higher rates of depression and anxiety in the refugees [22] Another study found that 95.3% of Ukrainian refugees in Europe and IDPs meet the threshold for diagnosis of acute stress syndrome [23]. However, one challenge when comparing the results of different studies is the wide variety of methods to assess the mental health status of refugees and IDPs: some studies have used clinical assessment and diagnostic criteria, with fewer studies using self-reported scales with a qualitative perspective [16–18, 24].

Up-to-date data on levels of MHDs in Ukrainian refugees is limited, and there are few studies that include IDPs [17]. Most of the reported studies describe post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with fewer studies examining depression and anxiety in early refugees and IDPs [18]. Our study investigated the prevalence of symptoms of depression and anxiety in both Ukrainian refugees and IDPs, whether there was a difference between the levels of severity in these two groups, and their views on what affected their psychological state.

Materials and methods

Study design

Descriptive, mixed-methods survey-based study, using convenience sampling.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome: prevalence of moderate-severe symptom severity of depression, was defined by a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 15, and of moderate-severe symptom severity of anxiety, defined by a GAD-7 score of ≥ 10, in both IDPs and refugees.

Secondary outcomes: To compare the prevalence of moderate-severe symptoms of depression and anxiety between IDPs and refugees.

Population and setting

Ukrainians aged 18 years or over living as refugees in: Belgium, Greece, Israel, Latvia, North Macedonia, Slovenia, Spain, and Türkiye; or as IDPs in Ukraine.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: Displaced Ukrainians aged 18 or over, who had confirmed refugee or IDP status.

Exclusion criteria: people who had been displaced before February 2022; individuals who were illiterate or unable to complete the questionnaire by themselves.

Variables

Sociodemographic: sex (male/female), age (years), region of origin (the 25 Ukrainian ‘Oblasts’), country of current residence in Europe.

Background clinical history: previous diagnosis of depression and/or anxiety (yes/no), previous treatment for depression and/or anxiety (yes/no).

Questionnaires PHQ9 and GAD-7:

1) Depressive symptom severity was measured with the PHQ-9 validated version in Ukrainian [25]. It is a 4-point Likert scale self-administered questionnaire with the nine DSM-IV criteria, scoring “0” (not at all) to “3” (nearly every day). A total score between 0 and 4 indicates no depressive symptoms, 5–9 mild depressive symptoms, 10–14 moderate depressive symptoms, 15–19 moderately-severe depressive symptoms, and 20–27 severe depressive symptoms [26].

2) The GAD-7 questionnaire is a self-administered tool validated in Ukrainian [25] for assessing generalised anxiety disorder and anxiety-related symptoms. It screens and stratifies the severity of generalised anxiety disorder symptoms, through seven questions with a 4-point Likert scale scoring 0 ‘not at all’ to 3 ‘nearly every day’. Total scores of 5, 10, and 15 are the cut-off points for mild, moderate and severe anxiety symptoms, respectively [27, 28]. While the GAD-7 is primarily designed to screen for generalised anxiety disorder symptoms, it has also demonstrated good accuracy in detecting other anxiety disorders, such as panic disorder and social anxiety disorder. However, we do not distinguish between these in this study and we use the phrase ‘anxiety symptoms’ to refer to the wide range of anxiety disorders encompassed by GAD-7 [29].

Open-ended question

We added an open-ended question to elicit the participants’ insights. Three possible versions of a question were designed by the core team, translated from English into Ukrainian, and tested by 8 doctors and 10 IDPs who visited a family medicine clinic in Uzhhorod, Ukraine. The question “What life events/circumstances have affected your psychological state now?” was chosen as the easiest to understand by both groups in the Ukrainian language. This question was added to the questionnaire.

The original of the questionnaire was written in English. Translation into Ukrainian and then linguistic validation by back translation of the sociodemographic sections were done by NP and OI at Uzhgorod University, Ukraine (https://www.uzhnu.edu.ua/en/). For GAD-7 and PHQ-9, we used the translated versions validated by Kogut A., et al. [25]. The whole survey was piloted with a group of 15 IDPs who had not taken part in the validation of the open-ended question. No changes were needed as a result of this pilot. The questionnaire is available in Supplement 1.

Data collection and recruitment

We recruited a ‘National Representative’ for each participating country. These were members of the European General Practice Research Network (EGPRN), each of whom signed a commitment agreement with the core team. They could choose whether to distribute the questionnaire in paper format or online (Google Forms). Participants were selected through convenience sampling, in that we recruited individuals that we could easily access and were able to reach following the full-scale invasion. We also used a non-probabilistic snowball sampling technique, where participants were encouraged to invite others to take part in the research [30]. To sample as broad a range of refugees and IDPs as possible, we recruited the study population through medical and refugee centres, invitations from general practitioners, a variety of social media, the Red Cross and other relevant Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs). Details of the different approaches are given in Supplement 2.

Both the paper and online questionnaires included a participant information sheet followed by an informed consent statement. In the online questionnaire, participants were told that clicking on the “Next” button would confirm their consent, whereas in the paper version consent was given via a handwritten signature.

Statistical analysis

Considering data from the literature [31, 32], we powered the study to detect a minimum clinically important difference in PHQ-9 scores of 3, and in GAD-7 scores of 4, with standard deviations (SD) of 6 and 7 respectively. To detect this difference, with a power of 80% and a significance level of 5%, 126 participants were needed, with a minimum of 63 in each of the refugee and IDP groups.

Descriptive analysis used numbers and percentages for categorical data and means and SD for normally distributed quantitative data.

We categorised depression symptom levels as moderate-severe if the PHQ-9 score was ≥ 15, and as none/mild if the score was below that. Anxiety symptom levels were categorised as moderate-severe if the GAD-7 score was ≥ 10, and as none/mild if the score was below that. Current country of residence was categorised into a binary variable, either outside Ukraine (‘Refugee’) or inside Ukraine (‘Internally Displaced Person’). We grouped participant’s ages into 18–30, 31–40, 41–50, 51–60 and > 61 age-bands for the regression model.

We performed bivariate analysis of baseline characteristics by refugee or IDP status, performing the t-test for quantitative variables that followed a normal distribution, Wilcoxon-Mann–Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis test for quantitative and ordinal variables that followed a non-normal distribution, and Pearson’s Chi-square test for categorical variables.

We fitted two logistic regression models. The first model assessed the association between refugee or IDP status and moderate-severe levels of depression symptoms. After adjusting the model for possible confounders, the best fit for this regression analysis included the age-group categories. We performed the second model to assess the association between refugee or IDP status and moderate-severe levels of anxiety symptoms. After adjusting the model for possible confounders, the best fit for this regression included age-group and history of previous MHD. For all the statistical analyses, aP value of ≤ 0.05 was considered significant. The quantitative analyses were performed using STATA 18 (StataCorp LLC, USA).

Thematic analysis

The researchers conducting the thematic analysis were NP, OI, JB, IGL and MH. The team comprised clinicians and researchers with experience in primary care, mental health and refugee health. Their backgrounds informed the study design, data collection and interpretation.

The participants’ responses were assigned an ID code: a number followed by the country of residence at the time they completed the questionnaire. While during the thematic analysis these ID codes and refugee/IDP status were concealed from the researchers, so that qualitative responses were analysed as a whole, the free-text data meant that some sub-themes were clearly related to either refugees or IDPs.

An inductive semantic thematic analysis was conducted following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework [33]. For data familiarisation, NP and OI read and re-read all responses to the open-ended question as a whole, without considering individual characteristics such as country of residence or mental health status. Initial coding was conducted independently and manually by NP and OI in Ukrainian. Codes were generated inductively by identifying recurring words and phrases in participants’ responses. NP and OI then translated both the responses and the codes into English. The codes were subsequently discussed reflexively with MH, JB and IGL to identify potential themes and subthemes. These themes and subthemes were then reviewed and refined by the researchers; some were collapsed where appropriate. A final thematic map was developed by NP, and themes were subsequently defined and named collaboratively by the research team. Finally, NP wrote the first draft of the report, adding the verbatim quotations with their corresponding ID codes and refugee/IDP status; this report was subsequently incorporated into the manuscript.

NP is a family physician and PhD student. OI, also a family physician and PhD student, is a trainer in the mhGAP programme. The Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) is a global WHO programme aimed at increasing access to mental health services. It trains family doctors, internists and paediatricians to diagnose and manage common mental, neurological and substance use disorders (SUDs) [34]. OI and NP are not IDPs but have had direct experience working with IDPs since the beginning of the full-scale invasion as members of volunteer organizations that provided medical assistance to victims. They are both native Ukrainian speakers. JB is a family physician and lecturer in Latvia with experience treating Ukrainian refugees, including those with common mental disorders. IGL is a clinical researcher and PhD student based in Spain, with training in qualitative methods and experience working with diverse refugee populations, but no regular contact with Ukrainian refugees. MH, an experienced qualitative researcher, has worked for many years as a general practitioner in the UK and he visits Ukraine to teach about primary care research skills. Reflexivity was maintained through ongoing team discussions to consider how the researchers’ backgrounds may have influenced data interpretation. Neither JB, IGL nor MH speak Ukrainian. None of the researchers had previously known any of the participants.

In the overall sample, the prevalence of current moderate-severe symptoms of depression was 37.0% (95% CI: 32.6–41.6) and the prevalence of current moderate-severe symptoms of anxiety was 51.5% (95% CI: 46.9–56.2). There was a statistically significant difference in the levels of moderate-severe symptoms of depression, present in 40.4% of refugees and 28.1% of IDPs, (P = 0.014), and in levels of moderate-severe symptoms of anxiety, 54.8% in refugees and 43.0% in IDPs (P = 0.023) (Table 2).

Refugees had significantly higher levels of moderate-severe symptoms of depression than IDPs, with an OR of 1.73 (95% CI:1.11–2.70); after adjusting for age-group, this increased to 1.86 (95% CI: 1.18–2.93) (Table 3).

Thematic analysis

We generated six main themes in our analysis of the free-text answers from the 458 respondents who wrote a free-text answer. These were: impact of war; disrupted social networks; socioeconomic insecurities; health-related issues affecting ability to cope; barriers to local integration; using tools and resources for a better adaptation. The themes and subthemes are described below, with each PCP’s quotation identified by a participant (PTP) code and their country of residence and represented visually in Fig. 4.

Visual representation of the themes and subthemes

Impact of war

The word “War” emerged frequently in participants’ responses and reflects the deep psychological toll associated with the ongoing military conflict. Many participants explicitly referenced the words “war” or “war in Ukraine” as a main reason for their mental health status. This theme underpinned all the other themes. Participants described war as a deep feeling or an unreal situation.

“War, the state when a person has no clear plan for the future, while memories of their previous life remain. It is hard to live here and now, to enjoy life when your country is being destroyed, and people are being killed. It’s hard to watch the paving stones, parks, and roads being built while in every corner of Ukraine, from east to west, there are fields filled with the dead.” (PTP 100, Slovenia).

Respondents living abroad often highlighted the overwhelming impact of daily news about the war, shelling and news of deaths. This is how the conflict came to dominate their concerns and daily lives.

“News from my hometown, where my family is now; the need to plan for the future without having the opportunity to do so”. (PTP 116, Other European countries).

“Together with my children, I fled Ukraine because my neighbors were killed due to shelling. My younger child screamed at night for 1.5 months. We’ve been abroad for over a year. We are calm. Only the news from the homeland scares us and brings sadness. We are waiting for our victory to return home.” (PTP 73, Spain).

Within Ukraine, the constant fear induced by air raid sirens, shelling, as well as the devastation of the homeland and the loss of former lives, contributed to anxiety, stress, and insomnia. Internally displaced persons (IDPs) described the challenge of finding joy or living in the present while their country faces destruction, explosions, and death. Accounts of ruined infrastructure and disrupted lives underscored the pervasive and enduring trauma caused by the conflict.

“The constant shelling, which happens every day and night, affects me the most. It disrupts my sleep and causes ongoing stress.” (PTP 182, Ukraine).

Ukrainians expressed feelings of hopelessness, emphasising their inability to plan for the future due to ongoing uncertainty. Some participants also reported concerns about the war ending, as they feared there would be little or nothing left in their hometowns upon return.

“Uncertainty about future plans or constant instability, (…). Recently, I’ve started feeling some stability and determination, but I catch myself fearing the phrase “the war is over” because it will require making important decisions and life changes again. It feels like I’ve adapted to living in this state and am afraid of any changes—both good and bad.” (PTP 437, Belgium).

“The very fact that there is a war in my country is terrifying! I don’t see a future, I can’t make plans, there is so much uncertainty.” (PTP 117, Ukraine).

Others were living the war as volunteers of the Ukrainian Armed Forces. Consequently, these individuals reported experiencing the war continuously, which could also take a significant toll on their mental health, particularly among those with no prior experience of similar work:

“I am a volunteer [supporting] the Armed Forces of Ukraine, and I live the war 24/7.” (PTP 241, Latvia).

“I volunteered actively for the first year, but now I am burned out, leading to emptiness and a feeling of guilt. I justified my decision to leave by thinking I would be able to do much more abroad.” (PTP 97, Slovenia).

Disrupted social networks

Participants highlighted social connections as being important for their mental well-being. Respondents noted that the disruption or loss of these ties adversely affected their mental health. At the same time, family members and friends were described as playing a crucial role in maintaining emotional well-being by providing stability, support, and a sense of belonging.

Refugees expressed deep concern for those left behind, including spouses, children, parents, and friends who remained in Ukraine or were directly involved in the war. The responses revealed diverse yet interconnected experiences of isolation, worry, and longing for loved ones affected by the conflict. Some participants described caring for children while their spouses served in the Ukrainian Armed Forces; the burden of ensuring survival in a foreign country added immense pressure and made it difficult to experience any semblance of peace:

“My husband is serving in the Ukrainian Armed Forces. How can I find joy when I worry about him every day? I have three children, and I need to survive in Latvia. How can I feel calm when I constantly think about how to feed them and keep them safe?” (PTP 152, Latvia).

The sudden loss or death of loved ones due to the war, such as a father stepping on a mine, or dying in the battlefield, added another layer of trauma. Those losses can not only affect well-being but change respondents’ personalities due to desperation. The indiscriminate nature of war, where civilians were vulnerable to unforeseen dangers, and the recognition of how deeply war disrupted their lives left scars:

“ (…), how we buried our neighbors, how corpses lay around… How people became looters. During the war, people showed their true faces, without masks. All this and much more often comes back to me, giving me no peace.” (PTP 277, Latvia).

“The sudden death of my father (he stepped on a mine) (civilian).” (PTP 221, Ukraine).

Even when individuals were able to live with their loved ones, the constraints imposed by the war intensified interpersonal strain in families, increasing relational tensions and, in some cases, resulting in separation:

“My husband refuses to pay child support for my nephew. He has money, he’s doing side jobs.” (PTP162, Türkiye).

“I moved from Sloviansk to my mother’s during the war. It’s hell. (….) She doesn’t care that I’m in pain and that there’s a war; she kicks me out, saying, “Go back to your Sloviansk.” But they’re bombing there, I can’t, it’s my second war already. She said she wants to see me die.” (PTP 5, Ukraine).

“I worry about my future - what if I argue with my husband, where will I go, where will I work, and how will I live?” (PTP 400, Türkiye).

Socioeconomic insecurities

Both refugees and IDPs experienced financial difficulties. The lack of work, the inability to bring economic stability to their homes, and the consequences related to the financial instability, were common explanations for their current mental health status.

“ (…) Problems with work, all my friends are in Ukraine… I don’t have a financial cushion…” (PTP 26, North Macedonia).

“I want a peaceful life and stability. Extreme fatigue and a lack of money prevent me from living fully. And the constant worries do not allow my nerves to rest.” (PTP 372, Ukraine).

Participants also emphasised the struggle to afford essential items such as food and clothing. They described the need to send money back to their family, who lacked resources to eat and provide for a growing child.

“There’s not enough money. I need to send money home to the family; they have nothing to eat. My nephew is growing up and needs clothes, needs money for school. I don’t know where to get that money.” (PTP 162, Türkiye).

Others highlighted the challenge of raising children in a foreign country, without sufficient financial means.

“Raising children in a foreign country. The inability to provide for them on my own.” (PTP 169, Slovenia).

Many respondents noted unemployment as a key issue in their mental health status. Even if they found a job, sometimes it was not related to their background expertise or did not cover the financial needs of their families.

“I fear for my financial situation. I want to find a job.” (PTP 377, Greece)

“Being in a foreign country, missing home, low-paying work, low social status in this country, not knowing the language, disliking the place where I live, not being able to afford my needs - clothing, haircuts, beauty treatments, going to the cinema more often than we do, hard work.” (PPT332, Latvia).

“Toxic work environment. Looking for a new job that matches my qualifications, experience, and skills (without fluent knowledge of the local language, it’s hard, even with good English …).” (PTP451, Belgium).

“I’m struggling to find a job, while troubling news from the front only adds to the difficulty.” (PTP 320, Ukraine).

The destruction of homes and displacement were recurring concerns. These were the commonest reasons for their refugee or IDP status. This resulted in temporary housing arrangements and uncertainty regarding access to permanent accommodation in their current country of residence, alongside a profound sense of grief following the loss of their homes due to shelling:

“(…) We saw houses burning, how our house shook during the shelling, and us with it, how the shells flew and whistled, how the windows were blown out by the blast wave, and we survived. How shells hit our home more than once, how a shell hit our apartment, (…). What we went through - no water, food, gas, light, internet, under constant shelling - trying to sleep without windows in -10°C, with the daily fear that today might be the day…” (PTP 277, Latvia).

“Thoughts about not having enough money and the possibility of war breaking out in Latvia. Where will I take my children? Where can I hide them from the war?” (PTP 264, Latvia).

“I constantly worry about housing conditions. The housing is temporary. I don’t know where to get clothes or food.” (PTP 203, Ukraine).

Health-related issues affecting ability to cope

This theme captures the additional burden faced by participants due to health-related challenges, including managing pregnancies, accessing medications, and dealing with chronic conditions or ongoing treatments amidst the chaos of war and displacement. The responses reflected the intersection of healthcare needs with mental health status.

Pregnant women faced considerable difficulties in navigating the healthcare system in their country of residence, while also dealing with housing insecurity and coping with the effects of the war.

“Pregnancy, difficulties in finding housing, and the war in Ukraine.” (PTP 39, Spain).

“War. Premature childbirth. Intensive care, rehabilitation, surgery. We were left without a home and work.” (PTP 336, Ukraine).

Individuals with chronic conditions faced difficulties in accessing and maintaining treatment, while concerns about caregiving added a further layer of stress. Participants also described the health of family members:

“War, loss of everything material, realising I can no longer give my children what I planned in life, having a child with a disability and speech delay and not being able to dedicate enough time to his development.” (PTP 361, Latvia).

“(…), fear, malnutrition, illness, lack of medication.” (PTP229, Ukraine).

Some participants described their current mental health status. Some had already been diagnosed with depression, and/or anxiety, and were being treated with medication or psychological therapy.

“If I didn’t take antidepressants, everything would be much worse. (…) It’s such a deep pain…, (…) and it seems to help, but there are moments when I lose my balance. (…) I went to a psychologist, but it didn’t help. (…). My soul hurts, and my heart cries. (…) You suggest psychological groups, but they’re not suitable for me. I went to art therapy, and many people shared their stories, and it turned my world upside down when they said they could return home after the war, but I can’t.” (PTT 277, Latvia).

In answers from Ukrainians in Israel we found descriptions of tense situations in both countries, with physical and mental health challenges compounded by dependency on others, reflecting how healthcare processes intersected with broader societal pressures.

“physical health, mental health, tense situation in both countries, dependence on others.” (PTP 420, Israel).

“War in both countries, health issues.” (PTP 421, Israel).

Barriers to local integration

Beyond family separation, participants reported challenges in their living abroad, such as disrespectful behaviour and a lack of psychological support, which compounded feelings of loneliness, worry and lack of integration.

“(…) On top of this, the psychological conditions where I currently live are difficult - both the residents and coordinators often violate personal boundaries. Most of the environment is rude, vulgar, and unpleasant.” (PTP 123, The Netherlands).

“Every day, the Red Cross organisation does everything to make my stay in Spain as uncomfortable and horrible as possible.” (PTP 71, Spain).

Some participants wrote about feelings of alienation and not belonging in their host countries. They described the experience of living abroad, learning the culture and still feeling like a stranger.

“(…) I am learning Slovene, studying the culture and history of this country, yet I still feel like a stranger here. I guess it will always feel this way because I no longer have a home.” (PTP 93, Slovenia).

Language was a key factor for integration for individuals displaced by the war, especially when they were looking for a job or simply trying to communicate with others:

“At the university where I study, the main language of communication and teaching is Dutch, which I started learning only two years ago, so I still find it hard to communicate with classmates or do written assignments.” (PTP 447, Belgium).

Education, whether at school or university, presents challenges that can significantly affect mental well-being. Refugee children, with difficulties like being unsettled at school, could create stress not only for the children but also for their parents. Even in the case of relocation inside Ukraine, people faced problems with integration in schools and online education.

“(…) Also, my child’s completely ineffective and difficult online learning at a Ukrainian school (12 years old). Not knowing Latvian for in-person schooling in local schools, bullying!!!!!!!!!” (PTP 270, Latvia).

“(…), online schooling with my younger child, uncertainty about the future…” (PTP 251, Ukraine).

However, while education could be demanding, it also contributed to personal growth and resilience. Ukrainians managed to continue their studies at university even when abroad, and they wanted to finish those properly.

“I’m studying at a university, so I’m always busy. Life goes on, and I no longer worry about things that don’t concern me.” (PTP 452, Belgium).

Practical obstacles, such as difficulties accessing essential services (getting a passport, for example) or getting other documents, could exacerbate feelings of frustration:

“The birth of my third child, financial instability, a lot of paperwork to fill out and submit with a small child, and the need to do this in Riga, not in the city where I live.” (PTP 324, Latvia).

Some participants expressed their disappointment with national governments and wondered why they were not being better supported.

“(…), Ukraine’s ineffective policy toward its citizens abroad, weak military support for Ukraine, and foreign management of Ukraine’s military defence, corruption in Ukraine, violations of human rights in Ukraine, and the threat of handing over occupied Ukrainian territories to Russia permanently.” (PTP 303, Latvia).

“All documents gone and impossible to recover, corruption, and impunity in the government (which is benefiting from the situation and will continue for a long time, while the deaths of ordinary Ukrainians have become statistics).” (PTP206, Ukraine).

Using tools and resources for a better adaptation

This theme is about how participants coped with the challenges that they faced. Ukrainians emphasised the value of stability, noting that living in a peaceful country without air raid sirens and with a predictable political situation allowed them to plan for the future.

“What affects my condition now is the peaceful life without air raid sirens, the stable political situation in the country where I currently live, and the ability to plan my future.” (PTP 356, Latvia).

Stability in their families and the joy of children were a positive support for dealing with the circumstances.

“Like every woman (loving…) my psychological state depends on my family’s well-being. Calm, calm, and the joy of my children are exactly what supports me and gives me the strength to overcome obstacles. “ (PTP 404, Türkiye).

Access to physical activities and mental health services helped some to remain optimistic.

“A psychologist, sports help as a distraction.” (PTP 8, North Macedonia).

Some participants emphasised personal efforts to focus on positiveness and adapt to their circumstances. They shared their strategy of using positive moments and emotions to cope with the challenges.

“I try to surround myself with positive moments and emotions” (PTPs 422, Israel).

“If I wake up in the morning and the house is still standing, that’s already good. It’s hard, as it is for everyone right now, but gardening and household chores help distract me, because if I do nothing, I could fall into depression. Since February 24, I’ve lost the desire to read or watch movies.” (PTPs 262, Ukraine).

All these answers underscore how the war disrupted every aspect of life, creating a sense of uncertainty, fear and grief in the participants. The added stress from health problems further reduced feelings of well-being. The need to balance health concerns with financial and social challenges was evident in responses who described financial stress while trying to integrate into a new country, adding to the pressure to secure medical and economic stability.

Discussion

Our study found high levels of moderate-severe symptoms of depression and anxiety among Ukrainian refugees and IDPs, with those who were outside Ukraine being more likely to suffer these. The analysis of the participants’ responses on the factors affecting their current psychological state generated the following themes: impact of war, disrupted social networks, socioeconomic insecurities, health-related issues affecting ability to cope, and the use of tools and resources for a better adaptation.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings are consistent with a growing body of research demonstrating elevated levels of depression and/or anxiety among Ukrainians affected by forced migration, although reported prevalence rates vary [16–19, 21, 22, 35–37]. A survey published 2023, six months after the start of the full-scale Russian invasion of Ukraine, showed lower levels of depression and anxiety in the general Ukrainian population compared to those who had left conflict-affected areas or the country (IDPs), and they found that participants who had directly experienced or witnessed military actions had higher levels of anxiety [36]. Armed conflict impacts mental health directly, through exposure to war-related violence and loss, and indirectly, through the harsh conditions of everyday life caused or exacerbated by armed conflict [38].

In our study, refugees were suffering more depression and anxiety symptoms than IDPs. Previous research shows that both refugees and internally displaced persons are affected by ongoing war, although their experiences differ, and refugees more often suffer from these conditions than IDPs [19]. Refugees face challenges related to displacement and integration in a new country, while IDPs may continue to live in close proximity to the conflict [19, 39–41]. There is evidence that refugees suffer more uncertainty of the future and the war than IDPs [19, 40]. Although we did not directly compare thematic answers between the two populations in our study, we found similar tendencies, with respondents outside Ukraine commenting on language barriers, as well as problems with social integration and acceptance by the local population. We argue that these differences might be because refugees have struggled more with adaptation to their new lives and may have felt “othered” and subjected to the denial of fundamental rights, including access to healthcare and employment, thus suffering a form of structural violence that has been shown in previous studies [42]. This might have played a role in the differences in levels of anxiety and depression symptoms that we found in our study population.

While other wars share many characteristics with this conflict, the Ukrainian Russian war is unique in its characteristics, motifs and political issues. However, as human beings, participants in this study were experiencing how life under war was confusing and full of anguish. It is important to consider that, when writing the word “war”, participants were aiming to construct an explanation of their mental health status based on their life experiences [43].

People give a subjective meaning to war, where the facts surrounding the war itself do not sufficiently explain the dimension that suddenly appears in every individual’s existence. This “new” dimension is extremely disorientated by the need for immediate and unexpected reactions that either paralyse or awaken the creativity of the individuals, and it is embedded in the disruption of routines that are suddenly converted into a survival path [39, 43].

In our study, the odds for both anxiety and depressive symptoms were higher in those aged ≤ 60, a finding echoed by previous work [44]. Our qualitative data showed that participants struggled to find employment and provide financial security to the rest of the family, which is usually the role of people under 65 years, and this may have had an impact on the mental health of the younger participants. We also found, consistent with previous research, that a previous episode of anxiety or depression increased the risk of anxiety symptoms in refugees [45].

However, a challenge in comparing other researchers’ findings with our study is that those other studies have been carried out in different settings, for example comparing IDPs and refugees within only one or two countries [18, 19, 22, 35, 46] or comparing displaced Ukrainians with those who remained at their home residence [47]. Some studies have used different measurement scales [47, 48].

Our findings on the importance of social connections is further supported by evidence that psychological distress and depressive symptoms have been associated with higher levels of loneliness, while stronger social networks had a protective effect [49]. Qualitative evidence from other studies has also identified trauma, cultural adaptation and separation from family as key stressors, while emphasising the protective role of social support [40, 50].

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

Strengths

We conducted the survey across multiple countries and designed the recruitment to use multiple methods to gain as much access as possible to both refugees and IDPs. For example, in some countries, such as Türkiye and Israel, recruitment was easier with paper forms, while in Latvia and Spain, most participants were recruited online. Other countries employed a combination of approaches. Collecting data across multiple European countries gave us a broad view of depression and anxiety symptoms among refugees, minimising regional bias. We used validated tools to measure levels of anxiety and depression symptoms, giving a reliable measure of the outcomes of interest. We adjusted for age-group and self-reported previous mental health status. Piloting the open-ended question with IDPs assessed its effectiveness. Ensuring confidentiality and privacy allowed participants to reflect on their mental health safely [51].

Weaknesses

Our study used a convenience sample, and this may limit external validity [52]. For example, individuals’ experiences in other European countries or Ukrainian Oblasts might have been different, leading to different effects on their mental health. The online survey risked excluding individuals without internet access and sufficient digital literacy, but making paper forms available mitigated this. Even though there were participants from all 25 Oblasts and in 10 different countries, we were unable to estimate how representative our samples from those populations were. The majority of respondents were women (91.7%), which we expected since most Ukrainian refugees and IDPs are female: by law, men between 21 and 60 who are not caregivers or who have health problems had to remain in Ukraine [53]. Participants with severe depression or anxiety may have faced challenges completing the survey. Conducting the survey in refugee and IDP centres could have introduced bias, as individuals in these settings may have been more likely to experience anxiety or depression than those staying in private accommodation [54]. Language barriers between the researchers and the Ukrainian population outside Ukraine may have influenced the number of participants recruited in each country. Access to Ukrainian refugees was hampered by the data confidentiality policies applied by some organisations. In Belgium, many organisations had reduced their contacts with Ukrainian refugees over time or had limited their role due to the immigration procedures in place for Ukrainians (e.g. immediate temporary protection instead of asylum-seeking procedure, limited follow-up) [55].

Although both the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 have been widely used in the scientific field, and have a good discriminatory effect compared to the clinical interview, they may have overestimated the prevalence of anxiety and depression symptoms in this population [56, 57]. While the GAD-7 is primarily designed to screen for generalised anxiety disorder, it has also demonstrated good accuracy in detecting other anxiety disorders, such as panic disorder and social anxiety disorder. Though some participants may have had these conditions, we could not distinguish between them in this study [29]. We did not assess for PTSD, so we could not determine whether the symptoms reflected generalised anxiety alone, or in combination with PTSD.

Importantly, we used these screening tools to identify people with higher levels of symptoms, not to make a clinical diagnosis. Studies show that rates based on self-reported questionnaires are often higher than those based on clinical interviews: one meta-analysis found that depression rates measured with PHQ-9 cut-offs were about twice as high as those based on diagnostic interviews [56].

The qualitative data resulted from answers to a single written question, and this may have limited the richness of data: qualitative data from a survey-based question may be rigid and inflexible and lack the opportunities for probing participant´s accounts for asking follow-up questions [51]. Themes were generated using a semantic approach, where themes were identified within the explicit or surface meanings without adding deeper interpretation. However, we believe that our qualitative data offered a diversity of perspectives, experiences and sense-making that allowed the research team to analyse it properly. Our research was undertaken during a specific period (November 2023- December 2024), and the geopolitical and frontline situation at that time could have had a specific effect on participants’ mental health.

Implications for research and practice

Our research adds to the existing European literature on the mental health of refugees from non-European regions and IDPs, as the experiences of Ukrainians differ in several important ways, largely due to geography, politics, and public perception [14]. We believe that this study brings a unique context to our collected quantitative data by presenting the real-life experiences and struggles of Ukrainian people within an ongoing conflict and underscoring a potential mental health crisis among Ukrainian refugees and IDPs. Our findings support the need for early identification and proactive screening in host countries. Similar proposals have been formulated by Miller and Rasmussen within their Ecological model, suggesting that mental health and psychosocial support services should work towards more holistic programming, in which the clinical treatment of psychological distress is provided alongside a range of interventions that target the social determinants such as housing, employment, and social connectedness [38].

Despite the implementation of the World Health Organization’s Mental Health Gap Action Programme in Ukraine, which seeks to strengthen primary care capacity, we found a persisting substantial mental health burden, indicating the need to improve public awareness and access to care as part of both prevention and treatment strategies [34, 58].

Host countries should strengthen their support systems by reducing structural barriers, including those relating to language difficulties, social isolation and healthcare access. Strategies should include mental health screening upon arrival, accessible psychological services, peer-support groups, employment and mentorship programmes, and culturally adapted interventions.

Finally, given that longer-term displacement may be associated with an improvement in mental health outcomes, future research should focus on longitudinal trajectories and evaluate the effectiveness of integrated, mixed-methods interventions.

Conclusions

Our study found substantial levels of moderate and severe symptoms of depression and anxiety in Ukrainian refugees and IDPs. Those outside Ukraine had significantly increased likelihood of suffering from depression and anxiety symptoms compared to IDPs. The impact of war disrupting social networks, giving an economic burden, the difficulties due to other current health-related problems, and the resources needed for a better adaptation were repeatedly recognised in our analysis, revealing specific and personal factors affecting individual participants’ mental well-being. The results of this study highlight the necessity for host countries, and to an extent IDP centres, to recognise existing mental health problems within these populations and act on them. The findings of our research are important, accessible and relevant to stakeholders who are responsible for refugee accommodation, treatment, organisation of work and general support.
