Serum soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR) in HFpEF: associations with cardiac function and exercise tolerance
Authors: Jianfei Ma, Jinghua Zhao, Guowei Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the potential links between soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR), the monocyte/HDL cholesterol ratio (MHR), and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), in order to provide new biomarkers for clinical evaluation of HFpEF and new ideas for disease treatment.Method66 patients diagnosed with HFpEF who visited the cardiology department of Cangzhou Central Hospital from January 2023 to October 2023 were selected as the study group, and 70 healthy participants from concurrent physical examinations at the hospital’s examination center were designated as controls. Record demographic data, hematological/biochemical parameters (including sTfR, MHR), and echocardiographic measures of cardiac structure and function. Compare these indices between groups to assess for statistically significant differences. Conduct a multi-factor analysis of the risk factors obtained from the single factor analysis above to explore the independent risk factors of HFpEF. Conduct subgroup analysis on the research group to explore the correlation between sTfR and cardiac structure, function, and activity tolerance in HFpEF patients. Follow up with the patients in the research group for 1 year and analyze their prognosis.ResultFemale representation (69.7% vs 51.4%), left atrial diameter (37.52 ± 3.57 mm vs 35.04 ± 2.83 mm), and sTfR [3.29 (2.76, 3.57) mg/L vs 2.43 (2.08, 2.78) mg/L] were significantly greater in the study group compared to the control group across both cohorts (P < 0.05); There were no significant intergroup differences in terms of age, demographic and clinical histories (smoking, alcohol use, hypertension, diabetes), blood lipid profile, hepatic and renal function, other biochemical parameters, or MHR (P > 0.05 for all). Multivariable analysis identified sTfR (OR 1.293, P = 0.012) and LAD (OR 15.229, P < 0.01) as independent risk factors for HFpEF. Their predictive performance, assessed by ROC curve analysis, yielded AUC values of 0.835 for sTfR and 0.609 for LAD. The corresponding optimal diagnostic thresholds for predicting HFpEF were 3.05 mg/L and 37.5 mm, respectively. Subgroup analysis revealed significantly higher BNP and LAD, but lower 6MWT, in patients with high versus low sTfR expression (all P < 0.05). Over the 1-year follow-up, cumulative event-free survival did not differ significantly between patients with high versus low sTfR expression (median 11.17 vs. 11.65 months; Log-Rank χ2 = 0.174, P = 0.676).ConclusionSerum sTfR correlates with HFpEF severity and prognosis, offering a potential biomarker for disease assessment and outcome prediction.

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) has a high incidence. It is characterized by normal left ventricular systolic function but diastolic dysfunction and elevated filling pressures [1]. Epidemiological evidence shows that the prevalence of HFpEF and the proportion of HFpEF in the population with heart failure are increasing year by year, but the timely clinical diagnosis of HFpEF is still challenging [2]. Soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR) is a major regulator of iron homeostasis and can be used to monitor inflammation in the body. Current studies have found that sTfR is associated with a variety of cardiovascular diseases (hypertension, coronary heart disease, chronic heart failure) [3]. High serum sTfR accurately reflects depleted iron stores in bone marrow in patients with HF[4]. The New Zealand PEOPLE Study has clarified that high serum sTfR is independently associated with all-cause mortality in patients with HFpEF [5]. Currently, there is limited research on the utility of sTfR as a biomarker for the diagnosis and prognostic assessment of HFpEF. Furthermore, whether sTfR can reflect cardiac function and exercise tolerance in patients with HFpEF remains an unresolved question. Therefore, based on previous studies, we further explore the diagnostic value of sTfR for HFpEF and its correlation with cardiac function. The monocyte to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (MHR) represents a valuable marker in evaluating chronic inflammation in recent research. This ratio reflects the inflammatory-oxidative stress balance between monocytes and high-density lipoprotein, known to be strongly linked to cardiovascular events like stroke and coronary heart disease [6]. However, its association with HFpEF remains less explored. This study investigates the correlation of sTfR and MHR with HFpEF, assessing their diagnostic, evaluative, and prognostic value in this condition.

Materials and methods

1.1 Between January and October 2023, 66 HFpEF patients diagnosed at Cangzhou Central Hospital's Department of Cardiology were designated the study group, while 70 healthy individuals undergoing physical examination at the same hospital's center served as the control group. The individuals who participated in this research were informed about the purposes, procedure, and the Ethics Committee of Cangzhou Central Hospital authorized this study. The approval opinion No. 2021–245-02 (Z). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Inclusion criteria

Patients were diagnosed with HFpEF according to the 2021 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guidelines for acute and chronic heart failure [7]. Diagnostic criteria: 1. symptoms and signs of HF.2. An LVEF ≥ 50%0.3. Objective evidence of cardiac structural and/or functional abnormalities consistent with the presence of LV diastolic dysfunction/raised LV filling pressures, including raised NPs(LV mass index Relative wall thickness > 95 g/m2 (female), 115 g/m2 (male) > 0.42; LA volume index > 34 mL/m2 (SR); E/e’ > 9; NT-proBNP BNP > 125 (SR) or > 365 (AF) pg/mL; PA systolic pressure TR velocity at rest > 35 mmHg > 2.8 m/s). Our study fully evaluated NT-proBNP BNP and E/e ' > 9, but did not evaluate LV mass index relative wall thickness, which is also the limitation of this study[8]. Exclusion criteria: patients with moderate-to-severe valvular heart disease, hypertrophic or infiltrative cardiomyopathy, and pericardial disease were excluded. Patients with LVEF < 50% were excluded. Patients whose dyspnea was primarily due to non-cardiac causes (e.g., severe pulmonary disease, anemia, hyperthyroidism) were also excluded.

Methods

Data collection comprised

(1) general subject characteristics: gender, age, and histories of hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and drinking; (2) blood-based indicators included routine venous parameters and biochemical measurements (AST, ALT, serum creatinine, fasting blood glucose, low-density lipoprotein, and sTfR). sTfR levels were measured using the Beckman IMMAGE 800 specific protein analyzer with its supporting reagents, and the kits were purchased from Shanghai Weiao Biotechnology Co., LTD.

6-min walk test (6MWT)

Patients were asked to walk back and forth as fast as possible in a 30-m corridor to test the maximum distance they could reach in 6 min. During the experiment, if the test participants had fatigue or dyspnea, they could slow down their walking speed by themselves, stop walking if necessary, rest against a wall or sit down, and continue walking after the symptoms were relieved.

Definition of MACCEs

The main adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in this study include: death, heart failure rehospitalization, arrhythmia, myocardial infarction, and stroke. The study featured a complete one-year follow-up for all researchers, with no losses.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0

Normally distributed quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) and compared using t-tests. Non-normally distributed quantitative data are expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (Q1, Q3)] and analyzed with the rank sum test. Qualitative data are reported as counts and percentages (%), with comparisons made using χ2 tests. Variable correlations were assessed via Spearman correlation analysis. Factors showing significant intergroup differences were entered into a binary multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for HFpEF. The predictive performance of these independent risk factors was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Survival outcomes were depicted using Kaplan–Meier curves, and the log-rank test was applied for survival analysis. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Comparison of clinical data between the two groups

Compared to the control group, the study group demonstrated significantly higher proportions of females, larger left atrial diameters (LAD), and elevated soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR) levels (all P < 0.05). Although the monocyte-to-HDL ratio (MHR) was also elevated in the study group, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.053). Conversely, the two groups showed comparable profiles (P > 0.05) for age, history of smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, or diabetes mellitus, as well as for LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, white blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelet count, and parameters of liver and kidney function (see Table 1). Discussion

HFpEF is a clinically heterogeneous syndrome characterized by heart failure signs and symptoms, preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF ≥ 50%), elevated natriuretic peptide levels, and evidence of diastolic dysfunction or related structural heart disease. Increasing age, obesity, sedentary behavior, and increased premorbid states such as metabolic disorders have led to an annual increase in its incidence. The incidence of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction is increasing year by year, but there are still some challenges in its diagnosis and treatment [9, 10]. Pathophysiological changes of HFpEF mainly include systemic inflammatory state, obesity-cardiometabolic stress, microvascular dysfunction, right ventricular dysfunction, and pulmonary vascular dysfunction [11, 12]. Multiple cardiovascular and metabolic comorbidities, including hypertension, obesity, type 2 diabetes, and coronary artery disease, promote a systemic chronic proinflammatory state and induce myocardial structural and functional alterations, culminating in HFpEF [13]. In recent years, studies have found that preanemic dysfunction, such as iron deficiency and iron metabolism disorders may be associated with the occurrence of HFpEF and affect the prognosis of the disease to a certain extent [14]. This study establishes sTfR as an independent risk factor for HFpEF. Furthermore, we observed an inverse association between sTfR levels and exercise tolerance. The following section elaborates on these findings.

Iron plays a crucial role in cardiomyocyte homeostasis, and adverse effects of iron deficiency on cardiomyocyte function have been reported [15, 16]. sTfR reflects the expression of transferrin receptor 1, which is mainly present on mature red blood cells, suggesting iron deficiency. When hemoglobin synthesis is reduced as a result of iron deficiency, sTfR can be shed from the surface of red cells and enter the bloodstream, resulting in an increase in serum sTfR levels. In clinical practice, serum ferritin is usually used to assess iron deficiency status, and the advantage of sTfR compared with ferritin is that it is not affected by long-term chronic inflammatory states [17]. Studies have reported that the pathophysiology of functional iron deficiency is mainly related to iron limitation related to chronic inflammation, and the availability of iron is reduced. The presence of chronic inflammation is also involved in the pathophysiological process of HFpEF. Immune activation with elevated serum cytokines has been found in HFpEF patients and is associated with the occurrence of adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events. Consistent with our findings. Sierpinski and R also demonstrated that serum sTfR, which has the highest diagnostic accuracy for detecting bone marrow iron deficiency, was significantly elevated in patients with heart failure [15]. In patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) or chronic heart failure (CHF), soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR) demonstrates the strongest association with iron deficiency as diagnosed by bone marrow aspiration, the gold standard. Furthermore, research by P. Leszek et al. highlights that sTfR is the sole serum marker capable of indicating myocardial iron levels [18]. Although hemoglobin levels in the study group were marginally reduced compared to the control group, this difference lacked statistical significance. The larger, landmark studies by Fitzsimons et al. (the New Zealand PEOPLE study) and Sierpinski et al. demonstrated a strong association between elevated sTfR levels and increased all-cause mortality in patients with heart failure, including those with HFpEF [4, 5]. Our study aligns with the foundational premise of these works, confirming that sTfR is a significant biomarker reflecting disease severity in HFpEF. sTfR levels were significantly increased in the study group compared to controls. Importantly, this elevation was independently associated with HFpEF risk, consistent with earlier research [19]. Regarding prognostic assessment, patients with high sTfR expression exhibited a trend toward shorter 1-year event-free survival compared to those with low expression; however, statistical significance was not reached. This lack of significance may be attributable to the current study's limited sample size and relatively brief follow-up period, necessitating further investigation with larger cohorts and extended observation to clarify this relationship.

As a new inflammatory marker reflecting the balance between inflammation and oxidative stress, MHR has been widely used in a variety of disease-related studies. Elevated MHR in patients suffering from acute myocardial infarction is associated with a higher incidence of poor cardiovascular outcomes [20]. Tumor cell necrosis, TNF-α, and IL-6 are key inflammatory factors in the pathophysiological process of HFpEF, and activated monocytes can produce the above inflammatory factors. In addition, IL-6 can also stimulate liver cells to produce C-reactive protein, which in turn stimulates the production of more inflammatory cytokines [21, 22]. Prior research has established that chronic inflammation is present in HFpEF patients, contributing to disease pathogenesis by inducing cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and apoptosis, elevating myocardial stiffness, and participating in ventricular remodeling [23]. Supporting the potential link between MHR and cardiac changes, Zhong J et al. reported an association of elevated MHR levels with cardiac remodeling [24]. This study indicated a larger left atrial diameter in the study group compared to the control group, findings aligning with prior research. While the present study observed a trend toward a higher MHR in the study group compared to controls, statistical significance was not attained for this difference (P = 0.053). The relatively small sample size may be a primary factor contributing to the observed results and represents a key limitation of the current study. Increasing the sample size in future research could enhance the precision and generalizability of the findings. We recommend that future studies investigate the role of MHR in HFpEF using larger, multi-center, prospective cohorts. Additionally, we also recommend that subgroup analyses (e.g., by sex, or the presence of comorbidities like diabetes or obesity) could be conducted to explore its potential utility in specific patient phenotypes.

Furthermore, sTfR levels were found to inversely associate with 6MWT distance in the HFpEF cohort studied, that is, the higher the sTfR level, the lower the 6MWT in HFpEF patients, the worse the exercise tolerance and heart function of the patients, which is also the innovation of this study. Our results align with those of Alcaide-Aldeano A et al. [25], who observed that elevated sTfR levels correlated with worse cardiac function in HFpEF patients. Ramos-Polo found that a higher sTfR level was strongly correlated with poorer exercise tolerance, and our study is consistent with Ramos-Polo's report.[26]. Simrat Dhaliwal's research found that elevated soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR) levels were associated with worse functional capacity and QoL. It is consistent with our research[27]. sTfR, as an easily measurable blood biomarker, could be integrated with BNP and echocardiographic parameters to form a comprehensive diagnostic and risk-stratification model for HFpEF. In clinically ambiguous cases, an elevated sTfR could provide additional objective evidence for diagnosis. Elevated sTfR may indicate functional iron deficiency that could alert clinicians to further investigate iron status in these HFpEF patients and consider whether they might be candidates for iron supplementation therapy in the future, although this requires validation in prospective trials. We emphasize that the sTfR assay is relatively inexpensive and rapid, making it suitable for widespread adoption in routine clinical settings. It holds promise as a cost-effective tool for the long-term management and prognostic monitoring of HFpEF patients.

In conclusion, serum sTfR level has a certain value in the clinical diagnosis and evaluation of the severity of HFpEF, and may have guiding significance in the prognosis of HFpEF.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the statistical power and the generalizability of our results. The follow-up period of one year was also short for a comprehensive assessment of long-term prognostic outcomes. Second, as noted in the response to reviewers, we lacked a full panel of echocardiographic diastolic parameters (such as E/A ratio and TR-PG) for all patients, which might have provided a more holistic view. Third, a crucial and widely recognized challenge for the clinical translation of novel biomarkers is the lack of standardization for the sTfR assay. Our sTfR measurements were performed using a specific immunoassay system (Beckman IMMAGE 800) and reagents from a single manufacturer. Different commercial assays may use various antibodies and calibrators, leading to significant inter-laboratory variations in absolute sTfR values. This lack of standardization impedes the establishment of universal diagnostic cut-off values (like the 3.05 mg/L identified here) and makes direct comparisons between studies from different institutions difficult. Future multi-center studies employing harmonized assays are essential to validate our findings and define standardized reference ranges for HFpEF. Fourth, a complete panel of echocardiographic diastolic parameters recommended by the ESC guidelines (such as left ventricular mass index (LVMI), left atrial volume index (LAVI), and TR jet velocity) was not systematically available for all patients. Finally, minor discrepancies in sample processing, reagent lot variations, and instrument calibration, despite our best efforts, might have introduced some degree of measurement bias.
