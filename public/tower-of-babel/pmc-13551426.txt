Surfactant incorporation enhancing the two-electron transfer reaction of MnO2 cathode materials in aqueous Zn batteries
Authors: Guoli Zhang, Haoren Zheng, Wenchao Fu, Jiaqi Zhu, Ping Lu, Fengzhi Liu, Xiao-Xia Liu, Xiaoqi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MnO2 cathodes would undergo one-electron or two-electron transfer reactions in aqueous zinc batteries, with the latter providing doubled theoretical capacity. However, with the proton as one of the reactants for the two-electron reduction, its contribution is limited in typical mildly acidic zinc batteries. The long-term stability is further hindered by irreversible material loss. Herein, we incorporate a surfactant of sodium dodecyl benzene sulfonate (SDBS) into MnO2 to regulate its reaction preference. SDBS not only prevents MnO2 nucleus aggregation but also enhances electrostatic attractions for cations in the system. The resulting proton adsorption facilitates the two-electron dissolution reaction during discharge, and this is further promoted by the weaker Mn binding in the lattice after SDBS interaction. Meanwhile, the attraction of Mn2+ together with homogenized flux enables its reversible back-deposition following the three-dimensional instantaneous nucleation model during charge. As a result, the MnO2/SDBS cathode delivers 446 mAh g−1 high capacity at 0.1 A g−1 and realizes 84% capacity retention over 10 000 cycles at 2 A g−1 in the ZnSO4 electrolyte without pre-added Mn2+. It also achieves over 2.5 mAh cm−2 capacity with 10 mg cm−2 high loading. The strategy is further validated with another surfactant containing the featured negatively charged head and alkyl tail.

Introduction

Aqueous zinc batteries hold great promise for large-scale electrochemical energy storage applications owing to their intrinsic high safety and low cost. Besides, the Zn metal anode provides high theoretical gravimetric/volumetric capacity (820 mAh g−1, 5855 mAh cm−3) and low redox potential (−0.76 V vs. SHE).1–5 Desired cathode materials are required to achieve high capacity together with long-term cycling stability. The proposed candidates include manganese/vanadium-based compounds and organic molecules.6–10 Among them, MnO2 shows considerable promise owing to its superiority in capacity and discharge voltage.11–15 The energy storage mechanism is relatively complicated in mildly acidic Zn batteries, involving the mixed reaction paths of one-electron redox with cation de/insertion (MnIV/MnIII) and two-electron redox with the dissolution/deposition process (MnIV/MnII). They deliver the theoretical capacity of 308 mAh g−1 and 616 mAh g−1, respectively.16–19

An apparent way to construct high-energy density Zn–MnO2 batteries is to promote the two-electron transfer reaction of the cathode considering its doubled capacity, and a few strategies have been proposed accordingly. With the proton as one of the reactants for the two-electron dissolution of MnO2, Qiao's group increased the electrolyte acidity to enhance this reaction.20 Our group introduced zinc hydrophosphate as a proton reservoir at the cathode to promote dissolution.21 Alternatively, Guo et al. identified that acetate anions help with Mn release and thus enhance the two-electron reaction in mildly acidic electrolyte.22 Together with the above proton or acetate strategy, redox mediators are shown to further accelerate the dissolution of MnO2.23–26 Another key limitation of MnO2 cathodes is that they experience irreversible active material loss in electrolytes and dead material accumulation over cycling, which hinders the cycling stability. The corresponding capacity loss is usually compensated for by the pre-addition of Mn2+ salts in electrolytes – a strategy applied in the majority of Zn–MnO2 studies based on either a one-electron or two-electron mechanism. However, these Mn2+ salts add extra active materials (not accounted for capacity calculation) and complicate the system. Therefore, it is essential to promote both the two-electron dissolution and reversible back-deposition from the cathode itself, in order to realize high capacity and stable cycling without relying on Mn2+ pre-addition.

Herein, we propose a surfactant incorporation strategy for MnO2 cathodes to increase the capacity contribution and reaction reversibility of the MnO2/Mn2+ two-electron transfer process in the conventional ZnSO4 electrolyte without pre-added Mn2+ salts in aqueous zinc batteries. The sodium dodecyl benzene sulfonate (SDBS) surfactant presents effective interactions with MnO2 in the composite. It not only leaves negatively charged surfaces to prevent the aggregation of crystal nuclei and realize smaller grain sizes with increased specific surface area, but also presents electrostatic attractions for the cations (H+ and Mn2+) in the electrolyte. In particular, with the proton as the other reactant for MnO2 two-electron reduction, its adsorption promotes this high capacity reaction path. This is further facilitated by the weaker binding of Mn in the lattice upon Mn–O bond elongation after SDBS interaction. The attraction of Mn2+ generated during discharge, on the other hand, ensures its reversible back-deposition and retrieval of the active material to the cathode during charge. In addition, the hydrophobic alkyl tail on SDBS regulates the local distribution of Mn2+, enabling uniform and compact deposition with the three-dimensional instantaneous (3DI) nucleation model. Thanks to these merits of the SDBS composite, the two-electron/one-electron capacity contribution ratio of the MnO2 cathode significantly increases from 0.84 to more than 3.4, together with a reduction of inactive material mass from 27.1% to 13.5%. Therefore, the MnO2/SDBS cathode delivers a high capacity of 446 mAh g−1 at 0.1 A g−1 in the 3 mol kg−1 (m) ZnSO4 electrolyte, superior to 313 mAh g−1 of the bare MnO2 cathode. MnO2/SDBS also retains 154 mAh g−1 capacity (84%) over 10 000 cycles at 2 A g−1, which again far exceeds only 97 mAh g−1 capacity/2486 cycles for the bare MnO2 cathode. Moreover, a 10 mg cm−2 high loading MnO2/SDBS cathode achieves over 2.5 mAh cm−2 capacity together with stable cycling.

Results and discussion

The standard MnO2 material was obtained by a hydrothermal reaction. For the synthesis of the MnO2/SDBS composite, sodium dodecyl benzene sulfonate (C18H29SO3Na, Fig. S1) was also introduced into the hydrothermal system. Fig. 1a shows their X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns, both of which are well assigned to the σ-phase MnO2 (PDF#80-1098). Lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.71 nm are identified in the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) image of MnO2/SDBS (Fig. 1b), which is again indexed to the (001) crystal plane of σ-MnO2. A detailed comparison of the XRD patterns suggests the broadening of peaks after SDBS incorporation, indicating a lower crystallinity and/or smaller particle sizes. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images also demonstrate the smaller particle sizes of MnO2/SDBS, and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping confirms the uniform element distribution with the S : Mn molar ratio of 1 : 19 (Fig. 1c and S2). Detailed particle size distribution analysis reveals a reduction of average grain size from 627 nm for bare MnO2 to 367 nm for MnO2/SDBS (Fig. 1d). Meanwhile, Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis demonstrates a significantly increased total surface area from 24.8 m2 g−1 to 156.0 m2 g−1 after introducing SDBS (Fig. 1e and f). The above analysis suggests that SDBS is uniformly incorporated with MnO2, which does not affect the lattice structure but reduces the particle size of MnO2.

The effects of SDBS on the properties of MnO2 are further studied. Fig. 1g compares the Raman spectra. The Mn–O vibrations show up at 641 cm−1 and 561 cm−1 in bare MnO2, and they experience redshifts to 629 cm−1 and 555 cm−1 in MnO2/SDBS. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra also demonstrate the redshift of Mn–O vibration peaks from 758 cm−1 and 565 cm−1 to 740 cm−1 and 490 cm−1 after SDBS introduction (Fig. 1h). These changes correspond to the elongation of the bonds. Meanwhile, FT-IR reveals the vibrations of the –SO3− group at 1150 cm−1, 1117 cm−1 and 1080 cm−1 in MnO2/SDBS, which are again shifted in comparison to bare SDBS. This suggests the strong interactions between MnO2 and SDBS in the composite material. It is worth noting that these signals of SDBS are maintained after soaking the composite in the 3 m ZnSO4 electrolyte, demonstrating its good stability (Fig. S3). The presence of SDBS in the composite material is further identified by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Fig. S4). The above analysis verifies that the SDBS species experiences effective interactions with the parent MnO2 in the composite. These structure regulations contribute to the increased electrical conductivity from 0.026 mS cm−1 of bare MnO2 to 0.104 mS cm−1 of MnO2/SDBS (Fig. 1i). Together with the smaller particle size, these factors would facilitate ion and electron transport in the MnO2/SDBS material, thereby enhancing the electrochemical activity. More importantly, the weaker Mn–O bond in the MnO2/SDBS material corresponds to weaker binding of Mn in the lattice, which would allow its easier release via the two-electron reaction path during electrochemical reduction.

The above modifications in the MnO2/SDBS material are investigated by the interactions between SDBS and MnO2. According to density functional theory (DFT) calculations, SDBS presents a strong interaction energy of −1.69 eV with MnO2 (Fig. S5). It modifies the bonding environment in the MnO2 lattice, resulting in the elongation of the Mn–O bond from 0.191 nm to 0.203 nm upon the introduction of SDBS (Fig. 2a). Crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) calculations are carried out to study the detailed chemical bonding properties between Mn and O (Fig. 2b). The negative and positive COHP correspond to the bonding and antibonding properties, respectively, and bonding strengths are evaluated using the integrated crystal orbital Hamilton population (ICOHP) up to the Fermi level. This results in a ICOHP of −1.01 eV for bare MnO2 and −0.98 eV for MnO2/SDBS, confirming the weaker Mn–O bond in the latter. These calculation results are in good agreement with Raman and FT-IR spectroscopy, confirming the weaker binding of Mn in the lattice of MnO2/SDBS. This would facilitate its release to undergo a two-electron transfer path during discharge.

Besides the above effects, the interactions of MnO2 with SDBS that contained a sulfonate head would leave a negatively charged surface. According to zeta potential measurements, a significantly reduced value is revealed from −3.3 mV for MnO2 to −31.6 mV for MnO2/SDBS (Fig. 2c). This stabilizes crystal nuclei and prevents their rapid aggregation during synthesis, thereby resulting in smaller grain sizes and increased specific surface area in MnO2/SDBS. More importantly, the negatively charged surface also provides stronger electrostatic attractions to the cations in the electrolyte. This first includes the proton, which is the other reactant for the two-electron reduction of MnO2 but the activity is limited in typical mildly acidic zinc electrolytes. With its preferential attraction toward MnO2/SDBS, the local activity at the electrode would be enhanced. This is evaluated by carrying out the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) on the MnO2 and MnO2/SDBS electrodes in sulfuric acid solution with a pH of 4.03. The Tafel plots from linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) present a smaller slope at a higher potential with MnO2/SDBS (Fig. 2d). It demonstrates an earlier HER with enhanced reaction kinetics, confirming the higher proton activity on the MnO2/SDBS electrode. This provides another factor, besides the weaker binding of Mn in the lattice discussed above, to enhance the two-electron reduction path of MnO2.

In addition to the discharge path, the resulting dissolved Mn2+ should be able to deposit back to the cathode during charge to ensure the reaction reversibility of the two-electron mechanism. It requires the attraction ability of the Mn2+ cation by the cathode, which would be benefited from the negatively charged surface of MnO2/SDBS. This is examined by soaking 10 mg MnO2 or MnO2/SDBS powder in 1 mL of 0.5 M MnSO4 solution for 2 days, and the Mn concentration changes in top liquid are measured by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis (Fig. 2e). Thanks to the negatively charged site on SDBS, soaking the MnO2/SDBS powder leads to a 2.6 times Mn concentration decrease in the solution in comparison to bare MnO2, demonstrating the much higher adsorption capability of the former. The effect of SDBS on the electrochemical Mn2+ deposition behavior is further investigated. Electrolytic cells are assembled with a carbon cloth electrode and the electrolytes of ZnSO4/MnSO4 without or with SDBS as the additive. The electrodeposition of MnO2 from Mn2+ oxidation on the carbon substrate is investigated using the chronoamperometry (CA) test at 1.9 V (Fig. S6). The resulting current–time transients are quantitatively analyzed using the established electrodeposition models (Fig. 2f).27 This reveals that MnO2 deposition in the presence of SDBS predominantly follows the Scharifker–Hills (SH) model for three-dimensional instantaneous (3DI) nucleation, whereas the deposition approaches the three-dimensional progressive (3DP) model without SDBS. This shift indicates that SDBS accelerates the nucleation rate, thereby transforming the nucleation mechanism from progressive to instantaneous and promoting more uniform deposits. SEM analysis of the deposited morphology provides further support for these kinetic interpretations (Fig. 2g and h). MnO2 deposited in the absence of SDBS appears as loose and uneven coverage on the substrate. Conversely, SDBS enables a dense and uniform structure for the MnO2 deposits. This modified nucleation mechanism is attributed to the regulated interfacial environment by the SDBS surfactant, with the negatively charged hydrophilic head to attract Mn2+ cations and the hydrophobic alkyl tail to homogenize interfacial Mn2+ flux. Overall, the above analysis confirms that the structural engineering with the SDBS surfactant would not only facilitate the two-electron reduction of MnO2, but also enhance the reversible back deposition of dissolved Mn2+ with uniform growth. This would guarantee the high capacity and cycling stability for the MnO2/SDBS cathodes in aqueous Zn batteries, without relying on the pre-added Mn2+ salts in the electrolyte.

The electrochemical performance of MnO2 and MnO2/SDBS cathodes is studied in zinc batteries. The 3 m ZnSO4 aqueous solution free of Mn2+ pre-addition is applied as the electrolyte. Fig. 3a compares the charge/discharge curves at a current density of 0.1 A g−1. The bare MnO2 cathode delivers a capacity of 313 mAh g−1. In comparison, the capacity of the MnO2/SDBS composite cathode largely elevates to 446 mAh g−1. The MnO2/SDBS cathode also presents much improved rate performance compared to MnO2 (Fig. 3b–d). With the increase in current density to 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 1 and 2 A g−1, the capacity of MnO2/SDBS remains at 395, 358, 304, 242 and 169 mAh g−1, respectively, superior to 283, 251, 201, 145, and 100 mAh g−1 of the MnO2 cathode. When the current density returns to 0.1 A g−1, the MnO2/SDBS cathode still exhibits a high capacity of 420 mAh g−1. Long-term cycling performance is investigated at different current densities. As shown in Fig. 3e, the MnO2/SDBS cathode maintains 428 mAh g−1 capacity after 100 cycles at 0.1 A g−1, which corresponds to a high capacity retention of 95%. At a higher current density of 2 A g−1, MnO2/SDBS still presents stable cycling behavior, with an excellent 154 mAh g−1/84% capacity retention after 10 000 long cycles (Fig. 3f). This cycling performance is also better than that of bare MnO2 (97 mAh g−1 capacity/2486 cycles) as well as recently reported Mn-based cathode materials in zinc cells using electrolytes containing pre-added Mn2+ salts (Fig. 3g).28–37 To investigate the practical application potentials of the cathodes, a higher mass loading of 10 mg cm−2 is applied. As compared in Fig. 3h and S7, the highly loaded MnO2/SDBS cathode achieves a good areal capacity of 2.76 mAh cm−2 at 3 mA cm−2 and remains stable upon cycling, whereas the MnO2 cathode experiences rapid capacity decay. The areal capacity of the high mass loading MnO2/SDBS cathode also exceeds that of recently reported MnO2 cathodes (Fig. 3i).33,38–44 The above analysis demonstrates the excellent electrochemical performance of the MnO2 cathode material in aqueous zinc cells with the help of SDBS incorporation. Furthermore, considering its key effects from the negatively charged head and alkyl tail, the strategy is examined by another surfactant of sodium N-lauroylsarcosinate (SNLS, Fig. S8) with these structure features. The MnO2/SNLS cathode also realizes a stable capacity of 445 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles at 0.1 A g−1 (Fig. 3j), verifying the broad applicability of this surfactant strategy for MnO2 cathode materials.

The reaction kinetics of the MnO2 and MnO2/SDBS cathodes are studied. Fig. 4a and b compares the Nyquist plots by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements, which are fitted by using the equivalent circuit shown in the inset. This reveals half reduced charge transfer resistance (Rct) from 23.1 Ω to 11.5 Ω upon SDBS introduction. Besides, the steeper slope in the low-frequency region of MnO2/SDBS demonstrates a lower Warburg diffusion resistance. The Warburg diffusion coefficient (σ) is further calculated by the linear fits of the Z′ versus ω−1/2 plot. This results in more than a half reduced σ value of 26.0 Ω s−1/2 for MnO2/SDBS compared to 55.7 Ω s−1/2 for MnO2, demonstrating the faster ion diffusion in the former. This is attributed to the smaller particle size and increased specific surface area with the SDBS composite. EIS is then performed at various temperatures. As shown in Fig. S9, the MnO2/SDBS electrodes all exhibit smaller semicircles and lower Warburg diffusion resistance than the ones of MnO2, suggesting the smaller charge transfer resistance and faster ion diffusion of the former. The activation energies (Ea) are further calculated from the linear fits of ln(Rct−1) versus 1/T plots according to the Arrhenius equation (Fig. 4c). This reveals an activation energy of 20.6 kJ mol−1 for the MnO2/SDBS cathode, which is apparently lower than 31.8 kJ mol−1 for MnO2. It suggests that the reaction kinetics of the MnO2 material is effectively enhanced with the help of SDBS. To unveil the diffusion-controlled and non-diffusion-controlled charge storage contributions, the cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests are carried out for the two cathodes (Fig. S10). According to the i = avb relationship (i: peak current, v: scan rate), the b values of MnO2/SDBS and MnO2 are calculated to be 0.57/0.60/0.80 and 0.50/0.54/0.65, respectively, for the three redox peaks (Fig. 4d and e). The closer to 1 values of the former suggest a larger portion of non-diffusion-controlled processes. The detailed contributions are further calculated by using the equation i = k1v (non-diffusion-controlled) + k2v1/2 (diffusion-controlled), which confirms a higher contribution of the non-diffusion-controlled process in MnO2/SDBS (Fig. 4f). These analyses demonstrate that SDBS helps to promote the reaction kinetics of the MnO2 active material in aqueous zinc cells.

The energy storage processes of the two cathodes in aqueous zinc batteries are further studied. The capacity and stability of MnO2 cathodes are directly determined by the reaction properties, i.e. whether the active material undergoes one-electron transfer or two-electron transfer reactions via the following equations: MnO2 + xZn2+ + (1 − 2x)H+ + e− → ZnxH1−2xMnO2 (1e); MnO2 + 4H+ + 2e− → Mn2+ + 2H2O (2e). The inactive materials in the systems also matter. Since the above two-electron reaction path involves the dissolution/deposition reaction between MnO2 and Mn2+, its contribution can be evaluated by the Mn concentration change in the electrolyte. Accordingly, we measure the Mn concentrations in the electrolytes at different states using ICP, with the corresponding Zn concentrations as the reference (Fig. 5a).45 A general trend is that the discharged state presents higher Mn concentration in the electrolyte than the charged state, attributed to Mn2+ dissolution from the two-electron reduction during discharge and its deposition to the cathode during charge. A detailed comparison reveals a much higher Mn concentration of 102.5 mM with the MnO2/SDBS cathode than 59.7 mM with MnO2 at the discharged state, as well as a much lower concentration of 2.9 mM vs. 11.4 mM at the charged state. This suggests that SDBS not only accelerates the dissolution of Mn2+via the two-electron reaction path during discharge, but also ensures its reversible back-deposition during charge. This agrees well with our studies on the two materials discussed in the early section. The ICP results further allow us to calculate capacity contributions in the MnO2 and MnO2/SDBS cathodes (Table S1). For MnO2, the capacity contribution from one-electron reaction is a little higher than two-electron reaction, i.e. 54.2% vs. 45.8%. In comparison, the two-electron capacity increases to more than 3.4 times that of the one-electron capacity in MnO2/SDBS. Besides, the mass proportion of the inactive material (including irreversible dissolution and dead solid) is 13.5% with MnO2/SDBS, which is significantly lower than 27.1% of MnO2 (Fig. 5b). Therefore, the SDBS composite not only ensures a higher contribution and reversibility from the high-capacity reaction path, but also elevates the electrochemical activity of MnO2 cathode materials.

In situ Raman spectroscopy is carried out to study the evolution of cathode materials during electrochemical cycling. In the MnO2 cathode, the Mn–O bond shows a redshift during discharge and blueshift upon charge (Fig. 5c). These bond length changes are attributed to the oxidation state change of the MnIV/MnIII center. With MnO2/SDBS, in contrast, apparent peak diminishment and re-appearance is observed with the Mn–O bond (Fig. 5d). This suggests the dissolution of the active material from the cathode during discharge and re-deposition during charge. Fig. S11 compares the Mn 3s XPS of the two cathodes at different states. In comparison to bare MnO2, SDBS introduction results in weakened peak intensities upon discharge and recovery upon recharge. The above trends agree well with the ICP evolutions in the electrolyte, supporting the occurrence of reversible dissolution–deposition reactions in the MnO2/SDBS cathode.

The morphologies of the cathodes at different states are studied by SEM. For the MnO2 cathode, the morphologies do not show apparent differences between the charged and discharged states, suggesting moderate active material dissolution/re-deposition processes (Fig. 5e). With MnO2/SDBS, in contrast, the particles from carbon and the binder are well exposed at the discharged state, while the typical nano-flower morphology of MnO2 is recovered upon charge (morphology varied from Fig. 2h due to the change in deposition conditions and substrates). EDS mappings also confirm the changes in Mn intensity as well as the uniform distributions of all elements (Fig. 5f). These changes suggest the reversible dissolution and uniform re-deposition of the MnO2 active material. The above morphology evolutions are also identified on the cathode after long-term cycling (Fig. S12), confirming the stability of the reaction process. Overall, the above analysis demonstrates that the SDBS incorporation directs the MnO2 active material to a high-capacity energy storage path, i.e., the two-electron transfer reaction, together with excellent reversibility in aqueous zinc batteries. This enables stable cycling with high capacity.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we present a surfactant incorporation strategy for MnO2 cathode materials to increase the capacity contribution and reaction reversibility of the two-electron dissolution/deposition processes in aqueous zinc batteries with the conventional ZnSO4 electrolyte. The introduction of the SDBS surfactant first helps with the formation of smaller grain sizes and increased conductivity, thereby facilitating ion and electron transport in the electrode. More importantly, it generates a negatively charged surface and weakens the Mn–O bond, which attracts H+, weakens Mn binding and thereby facilitates the two-electron reduction pathway during discharge. Meanwhile, the hydrophilic sulfonate anion head and hydrophobic alkyl tail of SDBS provide electrostatic attractions and homogenize the flux of Mn2+ cations. This enables its reversible back-deposition and uniform growth with a 3DI nucleation model during charge. As a result, SDBS introduction largely elevates the ratio of the two-electron/one-electron capacity contribution from 0.84 to more than 3.4 in the MnO2 cathode, together with the decreased inactive material mass from 27.1% to 13.5%. In the 3 m ZnSO4 electrolyte without pre-added Mn2+ salts, the MnO2/SDBS cathode delivers a high capacity of 446 mAh g−1 at 0.1 A g−1 and preserves 169 mAh g−1 at 2 A g−1, superior to 313 mAh g−1/100 mAh g−1 of the bare MnO2 cathode. MnO2/SDBS also retains 154 mAh g−1 capacity over 10 000 cycles at 2 A g−1, superior to only 97 mAh g−1 capacity/2486 cycles for the bare MnO2 cathode. Moreover, a 10 mg cm−2 high loading MnO2/SDBS cathode achieves over 2.5 mAh cm−2 capacity together with stable cycling, demonstrating its capability for practical Zn–MnO2 batteries. The broad applicability of the surfactant strategy is further confirmed by another candidate of SNLS. Our results not only provide a promising approach for MnO2 cathode materials, but would also inspire structural designs of other electrode materials to achieve high capacity and long-term cycling stability in rechargeable batteries.
