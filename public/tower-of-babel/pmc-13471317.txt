No Difference Between Intramedullary Kirschner-Wires and Titanium Elastic Nails in Treating Pediatric Femur Fractures: Results From a Randomized Clinical Trial in Dar Es Salaam, Tanzania
Authors: Msami Evarist Ngowi, Billy Haonga, Revocatus Benard Luziba, Bryson Mcharo Ikoshi, Emmanuel Roagthe Lema, Edmund Ndalama Eliezer, Patricia Rodarte, Mayur Urva, Jamieson O'Marr, John Ibrahim, Coleen Sabatini, David Shearer, Saam Morshed, Patrick F Curran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFemur fractures are a common cause of disability of children in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs). The introduction of flexible intramedullary nails improved the care of children with diaphyseal femoral fractures. Application of intramedullary nails is challenging in resource-limited settings due to implant cost and availability. Intramedullary Kirschner-wire (K-wire) fixation has been proposed as a low-cost alternative option for pediatric femur fractures. This study examines the non-inferiority of intramedullary K-wire to titanium elastic nail (TEN) for diaphyseal pediatric femur fractures.MethodsA randomized clinical trial was performed at a tertiary orthopaedic center in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. Children (5–14 years) with acute diaphyseal femur fractures were randomly assigned to intramedullary K-wire or TEN fixation. The primary outcome was malunion. Secondary outcomes included modified RUST score (mRUST), PedsQL questionnaire, squat depth, and complications.ResultsOf the 61 patients (33 K-wire, 28 TEN), 83% completed 1-year follow-up. This trial enrolled 61 of 160 planned patients (38% of target enrollment) and was therefore underpowered to establish non-inferiority. There were 5 cases of malunion (3 K-wire, 2 TEN; Risk difference 0.034, 95% CI -0.122-0.191, P = .52). No statistically significant difference was detected in mRUST score at 1-year follow-up (K-wire 16.00 vs. TEN 16.00, P-value = .38). Reoperation was required in 3 K-wire patients (9.1%) versus 0 TEN patients (P = .145). There were no statistically significant differences in PedsQL, squat depth, or clinical complications between groups.ConclusionsThis study is the first prospective RCT assessing intramedullary K-wire versus TEN for the treatment of pediatric femoral shaft fractures in Africa. No statistically significant difference was detected in malunion, radiographic union, functional outcomes, or patient/parent reported outcome measures between groups. Although this study did not achieve its target sample size and therefore cannot formally claim non-inferiority, non-inferiority was not established. A trend toward higher reoperation rates in the K-wire group (9% vs. 0%) warrants evaluation in adequately powered future trials. Introduction

Trauma is a leading cause of death and disability in children [1,2], particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [[3], [4], [5]]. Among injuries sustained by children, femoral shaft fractures are one of the most common causes of disability [1,6,7]. Children in LMICs are twice as likely to sustain femur fractures compared to children in high-income countries (HICs), in part due to population demographics and less road safety infrastructure [8].

Management of femoral shaft fractures is largely dictated by patient age, fracture pattern, associated injuries, and the socioeconomic status of the child and their family [[9], [10], [11]]. In children ages 5–12, these fractures were historically treated with traction followed by cast immobilization. This technique has been associated with delayed union, malunion, and knee stiffness as well as placing a high burden on patients and their families due to prolonged bed rest and immobilization [10,12,13]. The introduction of flexible intramedullary nails significantly improved the care of children with diaphyseal femoral fractures with quicker achievement of recovery milestones and decreased complication rates [12,[14], [15], [16], [17]]. However, application of intramedullary nails broadly is challenging in resource-limited settings due to high implant cost and low availability, leading many surgeons to opt for non-operative management [[18], [19], [20]].

Recently, use of intramedullary Kirschner-wire (K-wire) fixation has emerged as a low-cost alternative for fixation of pediatric femoral shaft fractures. K-wire fixation applies similar biomechanical principles to elastic nail fixation—namely, the use of two pre-bent implants inserted from opposing cortices to achieve three-point fixation and bidirectional stability within the medullary canal—while using commonly available and affordable implants. However, unlike titanium elastic nails (TENs), stainless steel K-wires have a smaller diameter and less inherent elasticity, which may affect load-sharing properties and insertion technique [21,22]. Several retrospective case series have demonstrated good outcomes with this technique in a variety of contexts, including comminuted fractures, open fractures, and polytraumatized patients [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. However, before expanding this technique more broadly, it is important to assess whether locally available K-wires are not inferior to elastic nails in treating femoral shaft fractures in resource-limited settings [26]. The purpose of this randomized controlled trial was to compare TENs to K-wires for intramedullary fixation of diaphyseal femur fractures in pediatric patients at a single tertiary referral center in urban Tanzania. We hypothesized that K-wire fixation was not inferior to intramedullary elastic nail fixation for pediatric diaphyseal femur fractures with respect to the rate of malunion.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This randomized clinical trial was designed as part of a long-standing partnership between Muhimbili Orthopaedic Institute (MOI) – a tertiary referral hospital in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania— and the Institute for Global Orthopaedics and Traumatology (IGOT) at the University of California, San Francisco. Ethical approval for this trial was obtained from the Tanzanian National Institute for Medical Research and the University of California, San Francisco Institutional Review Board (Institutional Review Board # 15-18166).

Trial objectives and endpoints

The primary outcome was malunion at each patient's final follow-up, regardless of the follow-up time point. Malunion was determined according to criteria described by Kasser and Beaty [27]. Using these criteria, malunion is defined as radiographic varus-valgus angulation >10°, anterior-posterior angulation >15°, and shortening >15 mm for patients 6–10 year old. For patients 11 year old to maturity, malunion is defined as varus-valgus angulation >5°, anterior-posterior angulation >10°, and shortening >10 mm. Additionally, patients between 5 and 6 year old were included in the analysis for the age 6–10 years group.

Secondary outcomes were the modified radiographic union scale for tibia fractures (mRUST) score, Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) Measurement Model score, and the Squat depth score, and complications. The mRUST score, while originally developed for tibial fractures (Whelan et al., J Orthop Trauma, 2010), has been validated for use in diaphyseal femur fractures in a resource-limited setting at our institution (Urva et al., JAAOS Glob Res Rev, 2022; https://doi.org/10.5435/JAAOSGlobal-D-21-00211), supporting its use here. Formal validation in a pediatric femoral fracture population has not been published and is noted as a limitation [28,29]. Complications were classified as minor (e.g., nail irritation, infection treated nonoperatively) or major (e.g., conditions leading to unscheduled implant removal or operative treatment, including deep infection, refracture, malunion, delayed union, or nonunion).

Study participants

All children aged 5–14 years admitted to MOI with acute traumatic diaphyseal femoral fractures were considered for inclusion in this study. The study excluded patients with subacute injuries (older than 14 days), skeletally mature patients, taking medication that could interfere with bone healing (e.g., steroids), non-diaphyseal fractures, pathologic fractures, fractures with significant bone loss, polytraumatic injuries, or severe traumatic brain injuries (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

A total of 247 patients were assessed for eligibility, of whom 186 were excluded prior to randomization. The most common reasons for exclusion were age not meeting eligibility criteria (n = 133) and non-diaphyseal fracture location (n = 49). Sixty-one patients were randomized to receive either titanium elastic nail (TEN) fixation (n = 28) or intramedullary K-wire fixation (n = 33). One patient in both groups did not receive their allocated intervention. At one-year follow-up, 26 patients in the TEN and 25 patients in the k-wire group completed in-person assessment; 2 patients in the TEN group and 7 patients in the K-wire group were lost to follow-up. All randomized patients were retained in their allocated groups for analysis under an intention-to-treat approach (TEN n = 28; K-wire n = 33). Study inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Recruitment took place between June 1, 2018, and April 1, 2020. All patients presenting to MOI were screened for inclusion by a research coordinator and the staff orthopaedic surgeon on call. Informed consent was provided by the parent or guardian of eligible patients prior to enrollment and randomization. There were no changes to the protocol after trial commencement.

Randomization

After informed consent was provided, participants were individually randomized into 1 of 2 treatment groups: TEN or K-wire fixation. Randomization was conducted via a computer-generated randomization module with a 1:1 allocation ratio using REDcap immediately before surgery by research coordinators [30,31]. Because surgical implants are an out-of-pocket cost for the majority of patients in Tanzania, implants were provided free of charge for all enrolled participants. Patients and their families were not blinded to treatment assignment.

Treatment groups and protocol

A standardized treatment protocol was employed for all study participants. Upon presentation, patients underwent standard trauma evaluation with radiographic imaging and physical exam. After enrollment, patients were randomized via the REDcap randomization module. With medical clearance, the patient underwent fixation using the allocated implant method in a standard fashion under general anesthesia. The operative technique for both the K-wire and flexible nail was similar to the technique as described by Metaizeau [32]. The fracture was reduced under fluoroscopic visualization or using an open reduction at the discretion of the treating surgeon. The diameter of the implant was selected using Flynn's formula (implant diameter = 40% of the narrowest intramedullary canal diameter on AP and lateral radiographs, as described by Flynn and Schwend) [10], and two implants were used for each fracture. For K-wire fixation, stainless steel K-wires with diameters ranging from 2.5 to 4.0 mm were used. Per conventional diameter-based terminology, wires 2.5 mm or smaller are classified as K-wires, while those exceeding 2.5 mm in diameter may be classified as Steinmann pins; however, all implants in this study are referred to as intramedullary K-wires consistent with regional institutional practice. Wires had a trocar (beveled) tip that was removed and were pre-bent to a 30–40° apex at approximately 30% of total wire length from the leading end, adapted from the standard TEN pre-bending technique, to produce a C-shaped contour providing three-point fixation. For TEN fixation, TENs with diameters ranging from 2.5 to 4.0 mm were used. At MOI, the cost of K-wires was $40 USD per wire ($80 USD total per case), compared to approximately $400 USD per TEN set. No routine external immobilization, cast, brace, or splint was applied postoperatively in either group. Postoperatively, patients were allowed to bear weight on the operative extremity based upon the primary treating surgeon's discretion. In many countries, routine implant removal is generally recommended for both K-wire and TEN fixation at 6-12 months postoperatively to prevent long-term complications including implant breakage, growth disturbance, and pain. In our East African institutional setting, implant removal is not uniformly performed due to a combination of resource constraints, patient travel burden, and cultural factors regarding elective re-operation.

All data were collected by research coordinators using the online REDCap system [30,31]. During their hospitalization, data were collected regarding the injury, mechanism, trauma severity score, and perioperative data. After discharge, participants returned at 2 weeks for suture removal and clinical examination. Additional follow-up visits were completed at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year, with clinical examination, radiographs, Squat & Smile test, and PedsQL completed at each visit. Clinical assessments were performed by physicians blinded to treatment assignment. If patients could not return for an in-person follow-up visit, a subset of questions relating to the PedsQL survey and complications were assessed by phone. Follow-up occurred between June 2018 and March 2021.

Radiographic assessment

Radiographic assessment was performed by two surgeons (one fellowship-trained orthopaedic trauma surgeon and one fellowship-trained pediatric orthopaedic surgeon), both of whom were blinded to treatment method. Deidentified radiographs were used, and the digital images were edited to obscure the ends of the implant to prevent the reviewers from identifying the implant as a K-wire or TEN (Fig. 3).

Example of blinded anterior/posterior and lateral radiographs for evaluation of fracture healing. The implant ends were digitally obscured prior to review to prevent identification of implant type.

Each surgeon independently rated all radiographs using the mRUST score. Radiographic alignment was assessed by measuring the coronal and sagittal angulation using a digital angle-measurement tool. Angular deformity in the coronal and sagittal planes was recorded and reported as the mean absolute value of deviation from neutral alignment. For all radiographs with a discrepancy between the two reviewers of 2 points for the mRUST or 5° for the angular measurement, a third reviewer (fellowship-trained orthopaedic trauma surgeon) was used to achieve consensus. For discrepancies below these thresholds, the average of the two reviewers was used.

Kasser and Beaty criteria were used to define radiographic malunion based on age group. For participants 5–10 year old, malunion was defined as varus-valgus angulation >10° and anterior-posterior angulation >15°. For patients 11 year old to maturity, malunion was defined as varus-valgus angulation >5° and anterior-posterior angulation >10°. Because clinical rather than radiographic shortening was assessed, the methods for malunion include only these two of three Kasser and Beaty criteria available via radiographic assessment (varus-valgus angulation, anterior-posterior angulation). This represents a deviation from the complete Kasser and Beaty criteria, which also include shortening >2 cm. Kasser and Beaty criteria (varus-valgus angulation, anterior-posterior angulation) based on radiographic data.

Functional assessment

Functional assessment and patient/parent reported outcome measure (PedsQL) were assessed at each follow-up visit. Squat depth was rated based on the criteria established by Wu et al., where de-identified photos of children performing the “Squat and Smile” test were rated by a single rater [28]. Scoring was based on squat depth using the following system: 1 = Unable to flex at hips, 2 = Hips above level of knee, 3 = Hips at level of knee, 4 = Hips below level of knee. The PedsQL was used to asses health-related quality of life [29]. The PedsQL has several domains, including Physical Functioning (8 items), Emotional Functioning (5 items), Social Functioning (5 items), and School Functioning (5 items). Answer choices range from 0 = “Never” to 4 “Almost Always”. The parents of patients aged <6 years were required to answer the PedsQL questions (Proxy Report), while patients ≥6 years could answer the questions themselves (Self-Report) in addition to parent answers. For the ≥ 6-year age group, a composite score was used, where the average of the parent and child answers was taken and included in analysis.

Sample size

This study was powered to demonstrate non-inferiority of the intramedullary k-wire relative to the TEN using the rate of malunion as the primary outcome. The non-inferiority limit was 15%, and the power and alpha were set to 80% and 5%, respectively. The calculation yielded a sample size of 144 participants. To allow for 10% loss to follow-up, the enrollment target was 160 participants. During the trial, the enrollment rate was lower than anticipated, and the target sample size was deemed unfeasible in a reasonable time frame. Therefore, the trial was terminated prior to reaching the target sample size. The trial was stopped early following an administrative decision by the study team, driven by two compounding factors: loss of grant funding that supported the study infrastructure, and the significant disruption to clinical research operations caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, which substantially curtailed patient enrollment and follow-up capacity at our institution. There was no pre-specified statistical stopping rule or interim analysis boundary in the original protocol; the decision to stop was purely administrative and was not based on any interim assessment of efficacy or safety.

Statistical analysis

All data analysis was carried out using Stata 16.0 (Statacorp, College Station, TX, USA). The analysis was performed using the intent-to-treat principle. Fischer's exact test and Student t-test were used for demographic data. Cohort study odds ratio, absolute risk difference, and Fischer's exact test were used to assess the primary outcome of malunion. Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare mRUST scores between treatment groups. Student t-test was used to compare angulation radiographic data between the two treatment groups. Student t-test was used to compare PedsQL composite score. Fischer's exact test was used to compare squat depth and incidence of complications (e.g., unscheduled operation). Significance was set at P < .05. Missing data were handled using complete case analysis based on the completion of 1-year follow-up.

Results

Study population

There were 247 patients screened, with 61 enrolled and randomized equally between the two groups (Fig. 1). One participant in each group underwent ORIF with a plate and screws after randomization based on the judgment of the treating surgeons. One participant did not have data available on the method of treatment. All other participants received the assigned treatment. At 1 year, 51 participants (83%) were evaluated for the final follow-up in-person. Similarly, 53 patients had radiographic data for at least one follow-up point.

Injury demographics

On average, the K-wire group was 1.3 years younger than the TEN group (7.9 years vs 9.2 years, P < .05). Of patients who sustained a fall as a mechanism of injury, fewer patients in the K-wire group sustained a fall from standing height. (Table 1). Otherwise, treatment groups were similar with respect to demographics, injury characteristics, and peri-operative details. There were two open fractures in the TEN group that were treated with surgical irrigation and debridement followed by primary closure at the time of surgery. Of the 51 patients who completed 1-year follow-up, 27 were in the K-wire group, and 24 were in the TEN group.

Radiographic healing and alignment

There were 53 patients with radiographic data, including 27 in the K-wire group and 26 in the TEN group, across follow-up visits. There was no significant difference between K-Wire and TEN in radiographic malunion at each patient's final follow-up according to Kasser and Beaty criteria (Table 3). No statistically significant absolute angular deformity was detected in the mean coronal or sagittal alignment at all time points (Table 2). Mean absolute coronal angulation ranged from 3.40° to 3.68° in the K-wire group and 2.39°–3.32° in the TEN group across all time points, with no statistically significant difference at any time point (P = .16-.93). Mean absolute sagittal angulation ranged from 3.47° to 5.81° in the K-wire group and 5.77°–8.07° in the TEN group, also with no significant difference at any time point (P = .07-.54). The sagittal angulation difference at 3 months trended toward significance (K-wire 3.47° vs. TEN 6.00°, P = .07) but did not reach the pre-specified threshold of P < .05. (Table 2). There were similar rates of union as determined by mRUST scores between the two study groups at each measured follow-up, with most fractures completely united with evidence of remodeling by 6 months (Fig. 4). Median mRUST scores were similar at 6 weeks (TEN 9.0, IQR 7.5-10.0 vs. K-wire 9.0, IQR 8.0-10.0; P = .98), 3 months (TEN 12.5, IQR 12.0-13.2 vs. K-wire 12.0, IQR 10.5-13.0; P = .20), 6 months (TEN 16.0, IQR 15.0-16.0 vs. K-wire 16.0, IQR 16.0-16.0; P = .64), and 12 months (TEN 16.0, IQR 16.0-16.0 vs. K-wire 16.0, IQR 16.0-16.0; P = .40). A ceiling effect was observed in the TEN group at 6 months, with all patients achieving the maximum mRUST score of 16, and in the K-wire group by 12 months. This limits the discriminative ability of mRUST at later time points; comparisons are most informative between 6 weeks and 6 months when score distributions were more variable.

To examine whether the statistically significant age imbalance between groups (K-wire mean 7.9 years vs TEN 9.2 years, P = .027) influenced radiographic alignment outcomes, two analyses were performed across two timepoints: an early timepoint combining 2-week and 6-week data (using 6-week data preferentially, with 2-week data substituted when unavailable) and the 12-month primary endpoint (Supplemental Table 1). This approach was used to maximize available sample size. At the early timepoint, mean absolute coronal angulation was 3.74 ± 3.70° for K-wire vs 3.43 ± 2.84° for TEN (P = .75), and sagittal angulation was 4.48 ± 4.52° vs 7.63 ± 5.78° (P = .06). At 12 months, coronal angulation was 3.68 ± 3.14° vs 2.53 ± 1.95° (P = .17) and sagittal angulation was 6.10 ± 6.79° vs 8.07 ± 6.09° (P = .38). No comparison reached statistical significance at either timepoint. Patients were stratified by age using a cutoff of 8 years, based on established clinical criteria for remodeling potential. In younger children (age <8 years), no significant difference in coronal or sagittal angulation was detected at either timepoint, and sagittal angulation consistently trended toward greater values in the TEN group. In older children (age ≥8 years), no significant differences were detected, though the K-wire sample in this stratum was small. In a pooled linear regression model adjusting for treatment group, age, and timepoint, the treatment effect remained non-significant for both coronal (P = .23) and sagittal (P = .15) angulation. Age was a significant predictor of sagittal angulation (coefficient 0.94° per year, P = .01), with older children demonstrating greater sagittal angulation regardless of implant type.

There were 52 patients with Squat & Smile images. There were increasing squat depth scores from 6 weeks to 1 year without any significant difference between K-wire and TEN (Fig. 5). There was no significant difference in PedsQL Scores between treatment groups at all time points. There were no significant differences in the PedsQL psychosocial domain or physical domain (Fig. 6).

Complications

At the time of final follow-up, there were 3 complications requiring reoperation in the K-wire group (9.1%) and none in the TEN group (0%) (1-sided Fisher's exact P-value = .145). The complications included one nonunion, one deep infection, and one case of hardware irritation. The hardware irritation required reoperation for wire trimming and was attributable to the wire being cut at an excessive length at the time of index surgery in a length-stable transverse femur fracture. This prompted a modification of the operative technique with no further occurrences. The nonunion occurred in a patient with a segmental femur fracture, a pattern that may have been better suited to a more rigid construct such as plate fixation and represents a relative contraindication to flexible intramedullary fixation. The relative rigidity of stainless steel compared to titanium may have also contributed by reducing the load-sharing properties that facilitate fracture healing with flexible implants. The nonunion was treated with open reduction and internal fixation using plate and screw osteosynthesis. The deep infection was treated with irrigation, debridement, and intravenous antibiotics and resolved uneventfully. Implant removal was performed in 4 of 33 K-wire patients (12.1%), the three patients who required reoperation for complications plus one elective removal at the patient family's request. Implant removal was performed in 2 of 28 TEN patients (7.1%), both electively. No retained-implant complications, including growth arrest, implant fracture in situ, or late refracture, were identified over the 1-year follow-up period.

Discussion

This study sought to compare the efficacy of k-wires and TENs for treatment of pediatric femur fractures. This study found no statistically significant difference in rates of union and alignment, patient-reported outcomes, and time to union between treatment cohorts. The intramedullary K-wires had a low rate of adverse events, suggesting the lower-cost implant may be an acceptable alternative to increase access to surgical care for pediatric femur fractures. However, due to slower-than-anticipated enrollment, our study failed to achieve the sample size to state that K-wires are statistically non-inferior to TENs.

To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial comparing K-wire to TEN in Africa. The only other published RCT comparing K-wire and TEN was performed in India and found no statistically significant difference between the two treatments with respect to time to union or complications [33]. Importantly, that study enrolled n = 102 patients, nearly double our sample, which afforded greater statistical power. The overall evidence base remains limited: to date, only two prospective randomized trials have been published on this topic, with the remainder of the literature consisting predominantly of retrospective case series. In their study, the K-wire group had 2 patients with entry point infections, 2 cases of leg length discrepancy, and 4 patients with varus malalignment. The TEN group had 2 cases of nail tip irritation, 3 patients with leg length discrepancy, 2 cases of varus malalignment. Similarly, in Türkiye, Isik [34] et al. retrospectively compared 42 patients treated with K-wire or retrograde TEN and found no significant differences in time to union or complications requiring reoperation. The largest study on K-wires was a retrospective case series of 178 children treated in Saudi Arabia that reported no major complications (as defined by limb length discrepancy >15 mm, non-union, avascular necrosis, knee joint stiffness) [21]. Two smaller case series in Japan and Saudi Arabia found similarly low rates of complications with k-wires [24, 35]. These studies, taken together with our findings, suggest that K-wires may represent a viable low-cost alternative implant for pediatric femur fractures in resource-limited settings, though formal non-inferiority has not been established. All three reoperations in our study occurred in the K-wire group, and while the difference did not reach statistical significance (9% vs. 0%, P = .145), important context surrounds each complication. The hardware irritation was attributable to a technical error, specifically the wire being cut at an excessive length, rather than an inherent property of the implant, and was eliminated following a modification of the operative technique. The nonunion occurred in a patient with a segmental femur fracture, a pattern that represents a relative contraindication to flexible intramedullary fixation regardless of implant type. The biomechanical properties of stainless-steel K-wires, including smaller diameter, reduced elasticity, and reduced load-sharing relative to titanium, may nonetheless contribute to complication risk in certain scenarios, and the relative rigidity of stainless steel may have been a contributing factor in this nonunion case. The deep infection resolved uneventfully following standard surgical management. Taken together, we do not believe these complications represent a systemic safety signal for K-wire fixation, but rather highlight the importance of meticulous surgical technique and appropriate patient selection, particularly the avoidance of K-wire fixation in segmental or comminuted fracture patterns. Future adequately powered trials should prospectively examine complication profiles and further define the indications and contraindications for K-wire fixation in this population.

Stainless-steel elastic stable intramedullary nails (ESIN) represent an additional comparator that warrants consideration in resource-limited settings. Several manufacturers continue to produce stainless-steel ESIN at substantially reduced cost relative to titanium equivalents, and they may offer biomechanical advantages over K-wires given their greater diameter and inherent elasticity. However, even stainless-steel ESIN remain more expensive than standard stainless-steel K-wires by an order of magnitude, and their availability at district-level hospitals throughout sub-Saharan Africa and other low-resource settings cannot be assumed. In our setting, stainless-steel ESIN were not routinely available, which motivated the use of standard K-wires as the low-cost alternative. Future studies comparing stainless-steel ESIN to both titanium ESIN and K-wires in low- and middle-income settings would further clarify the cost-effectiveness landscape for pediatric femoral fracture fixation.

The cost difference between implants is particularly relevant in low-resource settings: at MOI, stainless steel K-wires cost approximately $40 USD per wire ($80 per case), compared to approximately $200 per wire ($400 USD per TEN set), a cost reduction of 5-fold. This cost advantage may meaningfully expand access to operative fixation for pediatric femur fractures in low- and middle-income countries where implant costs are borne out-of-pocket by families. In this study, implant costs were covered for all enrolled participants; however, in routine clinical practice, cost remains a major barrier to surgical care. K-wires used in this study were standard stainless steel implants with diameters of 2.5–3.5 mm, which are widely available at district-level hospitals throughout sub-Saharan Africa and other low-resource settings.

Unlike prior literature, our study evaluated a number of secondary outcome instruments including the PedsQL, modified RUST score, and squat depth, all of which showed no statistically significant difference between groups. Our study also utilized dual screening of x-rays by fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons blinded to treatment assignment and found no statistically significant difference in healing or alignment.

We attribute our inability to claim non-inferiority to the fact that we did not meet our target sample size of 144 patients. Enrollment was slower than anticipated, and led us to terminate study enrollment prematurely. Enrollment of 61 patients over approximately 22 months represented a rate of less than 3 patients per month, well below our projected target. The most common reasons for exclusion were delayed presentation beyond 14 days of injury and fracture location in the metaphysis not amenable to flexible intramedullary fixation, reflecting the referral patterns and injury profiles at a busy urban tertiary center in a low-income country. The decision to terminate enrollment was made after it became clear that achieving the target of 160 patients within a feasible timeframe was not possible given the available resources and study infrastructure. Sample size was further exacerbated by loss to follow-up; however, we did achieve greater than 80% in-person follow-up at 1 year. There were several protocol deviations, including one patient who was randomized to TEN and received a plate, another who was randomized to K-Wire but received a plate, and one patient randomized to K-wire with a missing operative note. Due to our intention-to-treat approach, these three patients were still included in the data analysis to their randomized groups. Additionally, despite computer-generated randomization with a 1:1 allocation ratio, the K-wire group was on average 1.3 years younger than the TEN group (P = .027), which represents a statistically significant imbalance. This age difference likely arose by chance given the modest sample size, but it may have influenced outcomes given that younger children have a greater capacity for bony remodeling. This is an important limitation when interpreting results. Lastly, neither parents nor treating surgeons were blinded to the treatment, which could potentially introduce bias. Nonetheless, our analysis of radiographic data and squat depth by blinded assessors was aimed to reduce this bias.

This study has several important limitations. We did not achieve our target sample size of 144 patients due to slower-than-anticipated enrollment and exhaustion of study funding, and therefore lacked statistical power to formally establish non-inferiority. The upper bound of our confidence interval for malunion risk difference (20%) exceeded our pre-specified non-inferiority margin of 15%. Enrollment challenges reflected the referral patterns of a busy urban tertiary center in a low-income country, where delayed presentation and metaphyseal fracture location were the most common reasons for exclusion. Greater than 80% in-person follow-up was achieved at one year despite some loss to follow-up, and three protocol deviations were managed using an intention-to-treat approach. Although randomization was computer-generated, the K-wire group was on average 1.3 years younger than the TEN group (P = .027), an imbalance that may have influenced radiographic outcomes given the greater remodeling capacity of younger children. No covariate-adjusted analyses were performed given the small sample size. The 1-year follow-up is insufficient to exclude late complications from retained implants, which are not routinely removed in our setting due to resource constraints, patient travel burden, and cultural factors regarding elective re-operation. Longer follow-up of 2–5 years would be needed to confirm remodeling equivalence and exclude late implant-related complications. The asymmetric loss to follow-up between groups (K-wire n = 8, TEN n = 2) represents an additional limitation, as non-completers in the K-wire group may have had systematically different outcomes than completers; however, the small numbers preclude a formal sensitivity analysis comparing baseline characteristics between completers and non-completers. Finally, blinding of patients and surgeons was not feasible given the surgical nature of the trial. Blinded radiographic review and functional assessment were employed to mitigate this limitation, though its effect on patient-reported outcomes cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

Our study shows no statistically significant difference in outcomes between intramedullary K-wires and TENs for pediatric femur fractures in a resource-limited setting. We acknowledge that non-inferiority was not formally established given our underpowered sample, and that a trend toward higher reoperation rates in the K-wire group (9% vs. 0%) should be interpreted as a clinically meaningful signal warranting further investigation. When considering fracture union, alignment, quality of life, and functional outcomes, no statistically significant differences were detected between groups. Given these results were obtained at a fraction of the implant cost, K-wire implants represent a promising low-cost alternative to increase access to surgical care, and ultimately reduce the impact of femoral shaft fractures on children and their families around the world.
