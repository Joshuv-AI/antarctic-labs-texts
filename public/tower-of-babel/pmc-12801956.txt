Responses of Puccinellia distans to salinity stress: stress tolerance mechanisms and ecosystem restoration potential
Authors: Ahmad Rajabi Dehnavi, Sandra Lubińska‑Mielińska, Wiktoria Lewandowska, Agnieszka Piernik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSalinity is a major abiotic stressor threatening global agricultural productivity and ecological balance. Understanding the salt tolerance mechanisms in halophytic species can help develop strategies for restoring saline soils and improving crop resilience. Puccinellia distans, a halophytic grass species, serves as a model for exploring adaptive responses to salinity. This study aimed to evaluate the physiological, morphological, and biochemical responses of P. distans to varying salinity levels, assessing its tolerance capacity and potential for use in saline habitat restoration.ResultsSeeds and seedlings of P. distans were exposed to six NaCl concentrations (0, 100, 150, 200, 300, and 400 mM). Increasing salinity significantly reduced germination percentage, germination index, seedling biomass, and shoot and root lengths. At later growth stages, traits such as leaf area, specific leaf area, leaf dry weight, and total biomass exhibited a biphasic response, increasing at 200 mM NaCl and declining thereafter. Photosynthetic pigments, including chlorophylls and carotenoids, increased at 200 mM NaCl, but decreased at 400 mM, reflecting salt stress. Oxidative stress markers, such as malondialdehyde and hydrogen peroxide, peaked at 400 mM NaCl, indicating membrane damage and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species. Osmolytes, such as proline and soluble carbohydrates, accumulated significantly at 200 mM NaCl but declined under extreme salinity, suggesting that stress tolerance mechanisms were overwhelmed. Antioxidant enzyme activities, particularly catalase and ascorbate peroxidase, peaked at moderate salinity, indicating a well-coordinated oxidative defense. Non-Metric Multidimensional Scaling revealed optimal physiological and biochemical adjustments at 150–200 mM NaCl.ConclusionsP. distans demonstrates strong adaptability to salinity at levels of 150–200 mM NaCl through enhanced photosynthetic pigments content, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant activity. However, extreme salinity imposes significant physiological and biochemical constraints. These findings highlight the species’ potential for ecological restoration in saline environments and offer valuable insights into halophyte-based strategies for improving plant performance under salt stress. Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular pathways governing ion transport, osmolyte biosynthesis, and oxidative stress regulation to enhance the species’ resilience. Integrating Puccinellia distans into land management practices provides a sustainable approach to enhancing soil stability, productivity, and biodiversity in saline environments.

Introduction

Salinity is a significant environmental stressor that profoundly affects plant growth and productivity, particularly in coastal and arid regions [1]. The increasing salinization of soils substantially threatens agricultural sustainability and biodiversity [2]. Elevated salinity levels, primarily caused by osmotic effects, reduce plant water availability, leading to osmotic stress and decreased growth rates [3, 4]. In addition, ion toxicity resulting from the accumulation of excessive sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) ions inside the tissue can disrupt nutrient uptake, leading to nutrient deficiencies and oxidative stress caused by the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which damage cellular components [5, 6]. Excessive Na⁺ accumulation due to high salinity, especially from NaCl, disrupts the absorption and transport of essential nutrients like potassium (K⁺), calcium (Ca²⁺), and magnesium (Mg²⁺) [7, 8]. The competitive inhibition of K⁺ uptake by Na⁺ is a key factor in nutrient imbalance in salt-stressed plants [7, 8]. Additionally, salinity affects membrane transporters and ion channels, increasing the Na⁺/K⁺ ratio and impairing K⁺ transport, disrupting cellular enzyme activities and membrane stability [7, 8]. High salinity can also reduce the availability of micronutrients such as zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), and manganese (Mn) due to ionic antagonism or changes in soil and root zone pH and redox conditions [7, 8]. Consequently, these physiological and biochemical alterations result in lower crop yields as plants struggle to thrive in saline conditions [9, 10].

Understanding how halophytes respond to salt stress enhances their resilience and maintains ecosystem health [11]. By uncovering the mechanisms behind their tolerance, we can better support their role in stabilizing saline environments, promoting biodiversity, and improving land reclamation efforts [11–13]. These plants possess unique physiological and biochemical mechanisms that effectively manage osmotic stress and ion toxicity, which are critical challenges in saline environments [14, 15]. These plants activate advanced defense systems that include non-enzymatic antioxidants and antioxidant enzymes, which work in concert to neutralize ROS and prevent oxidative damage [13, 16, 17]. In addition, salt-tolerant plants accumulate compatible solutes such as carbohydrates, amino acids, proline, and proteins as part of their adaptive response to salinity stress [13, 16, 18]. These solutes play a crucial role in osmotic adjustment by facilitating water uptake and maintaining cell turgor under osmotic stress [13, 16, 19]. Together, these mechanisms enable plants to overcome the dual challenges of osmotic stress and ion toxicity while safeguarding their cellular structure and function.

Puccinellia distans, commonly known as salt marsh grass or weeping alkali-grass, is a halophytic species typical of natural saline areas [20–22]. P. distans plays a pivotal role as a pioneer species in the reclamation of salt-affected and eroded sites, contributing significantly to soil stabilization, the enhancement of microbial populations, and habitat restoration [23, 24]. Additionally, P. distans serves as an important component of coastal ecosystems by providing habitat for diverse wildlife species and contributing to the overall biodiversity of salt marshes [25]. Puccinellia distans serves as a key species in understanding salt tolerance mechanisms, making it a valuable model for ecological restoration efforts in saline environments. Despite its importance, significant research gaps remain regarding the effects of varying salinity levels on P. distans, particularly concerning its physiological response and adaptive mechanisms. Thus, this study aims to investigate how P. distans responds to varying NaCl concentrations, elucidating the physiological and biochemical adaptations that enable this species to cope with salt stress. We hypothesize that (1) increasing salinity will reduce germination, growth, and biomass of P. distans; (2) optimal salinity for growth will boost photosynthetic pigments, osmolyte accumulation, and antioxidant enzymes. Ultimately, this investigation aims to deepen our comprehension of the adaptive strategies employed by P. distans under salt stress conditions while highlighting its potential role in ecological restoration and sustainable land management in saline environments.

Materials and methods

Seed collection

In November 2023, seeds were collected from a long-term saline habitat influenced by both natural processes and anthropogenic activities, located adjacent to the Soda factory in Inowrocław, Poland (52°48′ N, 18°15′ E). This site is characterized by extreme soil salinity, with electrical conductivity values reaching up to 36 dS·m⁻¹ (approximately 360 mM NaCl), which results from a combination of natural halite deposits and decades of industrial brine waste discharge [21]. The site was specifically selected as a model system representing a unique, chronically salt-affected environment in Central Europe, where prolonged selective pressure is likely to have driven the evolution of salt-tolerant genotypes. Additionally, the area is a stable habitat for a diverse assemblage of halophytic species, contributing significantly to local biodiversity [20]. Notably, it is recognized as a suitable habitat for established populations of Puccinellia distans [22]. Plant material collection and research were conducted under permission from the Regional Director of Environmental Protection in Bydgoszcz, Poland (Permit No. WOP.6400.9.2023.MWK), following the same procedures as described in [21]. A voucher specimen has been deposited in the publicly accessible herbarium of Nicolaus Copernicus University in Toruń (Index Herbariorum code: TRN). Species identification was performed by Dr. Dariusz Kamiński (Department of Geobotany and Landscape Planning, Nicolaus Copernicus University in Toruń, Poland). All procedures complied with institutional, national, and international regulations and were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and legislation, as previously detailed [21].

Germination experiment

The germination experiment was conducted using a completely randomized design with four replications. The experiment was conducted in a controlled growth chamber maintained at 25 °C with a 16-hour light cycle to simulate optimal conditions for seed germination. Six treatments were applied, corresponding to 0 (control), 100, 150, 200, 300, and 400 mM NaCl, which were selected based on preliminary tests conducted prior to the main experiment and suggested by previous studies [20, 26]. For the experiment, 25 seeds of P. distans were placed in each Petri dish lined with Whatman No. 2 filter paper. A total of 24 dishes were prepared, with four replicates per salinity level. Each Petri dish received 5 mL of the assigned NaCl solution, which was refreshed every 48 h along with the filter paper to maintain consistent salinity and reduce microbial contamination. Germination and early growth parameters were monitored over 21 days.

Pot experiment

A pot experiment was conducted using a completely randomized design with six NaCl treatments (0, 100, 150, 200, 300, and 400 mM), selected based on preliminary trials and recommendations from prior research [20, 26]. For each salinity level, 24 seeds of Puccinellia distans were sown in plastic pots (82 cm³ volume), each filled with a 1:1 mixture of sand and vermiculite. This substrate was chosen for its ability to create a homogeneous, inert, and well-drained growth medium that minimizes nutrient interference and simulates the low-organic soils typically found in saline environments. It also allowed for better control of salinity levels throughout the experiment. In total, 144 pots were prepared and divided into six groups (24 pots per salt concentration). The pots were placed on individual trays, filled with 600 mL of NaCl solutions to ensure uniform salt distribution and establish stable initial salinity conditions in the root zone. This pre-treatment mimicked the saline conditions that seeds would naturally encounter in salt-affected soils. After the pots were saturated with the respective NaCl concentrations, the seeds were directly sown into the treated substrate. As the pots were initially saturated to full capacity with NaCl solution, the NaCl remained in the soil solution throughout the experiment, ensuring that the concentration of NaCl in the growth medium remained constant. However, salinity levels in each treatment were monitored throughout the experiment to ensure they remained stable and to prevent any fluctuations. During the eight-week growth period, the pots were irrigated twice a week with a Hoagland nutrient solution, which was added to each tray to provide balanced and consistent nutrition without altering the salinity levels. Distilled water was applied between nutrient irrigations, as needed, to maintain adequate moisture levels while preventing salt leaching. At the end of the experiment, 10 uniform plantlets per treatment were randomly selected for morphological and growth assessments. For biochemical analyses, three replicates per treatment were used, with each replicate consisting of pooled leaf tissue from several plants to ensure sufficient sample size and representativeness.

Growth assessments

To evaluate growth after eight weeks, ten plantlets from each treatment were carefully extracted from the growth medium. The following parameters were assessed using standard methodologies for evaluating plant functional traits [32]. The number of leaves (NOL), shoot length (SL), and root length (RL) were measured in centimeters, with SL and RL based on the longest shoot and root (Fig. 1).

Biochemical growth indicators

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) determination

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) concentration was determined according to the method described by Velikova et al. [34]. Briefly, 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of ice-cold 0.1% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) in a chilled mortar and pestle. The homogenate was transferred to microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and 0.5 mL of the supernatant extract was mixed with 0.5 mL of 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 mL of 1 M potassium iodide (KI). Zinc sulfate (1% w/v) was added to aid in protein precipitation and to clarify the solution. The mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for one hour. The absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured at 390 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Hydrogen peroxide concentration was calculated using the extinction coefficient of 0.28 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹, and the results were expressed as micromoles of H₂O₂ per gram of fresh weight (µmol/g FW) according to the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_2O_2=\frac{A\;390}{\varepsilon\times l}$$\end{document}

Where A₃₉₀ represents the absorbance at 390 nm, ε is the molar extinction coefficient (0.28 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹), and l is the path length of the cuvette (1 cm).

Malondialdehyde (MDA) determination

Lipid peroxidation in cell membranes induced by salinity was evaluated by measuring the concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) using the method outlined by Davey et al. [35]. A 0.1 g fresh plant tissue sample was homogenized in 0.5 mL of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA), followed by centrifugation to separate the supernatant. The supernatant was mixed with 20% TCA and 0.5% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) to form the MDA-TBA complex. The mixture was heated and subsequently cooled, after which the absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a spectrophotometer. The MDA content was calculated based on an extinction coefficient of 155 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and expressed as nanomoles of MDA per gram of fresh weight (nM MDA/g FW). The concentration of MDA was calculated using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MDA=\frac{A\;532-A\;600}{\varepsilon\times l}$$\end{document}

Where A₅₃₂ is the absorbance at 532 nm, A₆₀₀ is the absorbance at 600 nm (used to correct for non-specific turbidity), ε is the molar extinction coefficient (155 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹), and l is the path length of the cuvette (1 cm).

Proline content (P)

Proline content (P) was determined following the method of Bates et al. (1973) [37]. Fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 10 mL of 3% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid using a chilled mortar and pestle. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the clear supernatant was collected. An aliquot of 2 mL of the extract was mixed with 2 mL of acid ninhydrin reagent (containing 1.25 g of ninhydrin dissolved in 30 mL of glacial acetic acid and 20 mL of 6 M phosphoric acid) and 2 mL of glacial acetic acid in a screw-cap test tube. The reaction mixture was heated in a boiling water bath at 100 °C for 1 h, then immediately transferred to an ice bath. After cooling to room temperature, 4 mL of toluene was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 30 s. After phase separation, the upper toluene phase was carefully collected, and its absorbance was read at 520 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, with toluene used as blank. A standard curve was prepared using L-proline concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 µg/mL, yielding the calibration equation y = 0.0471x − 0.0742 with a coefficient of determination R² = 0.983. Soluble carbohydrate content (SC)

Soluble carbohydrate content (SC) was determined according to the method described by Irigoyen et al. [38], which involves ethanol extraction and anthrone reagent assay. A 0.5 g fresh leaf sample was homogenized in 5 mL of 95% ethanol and incubated in a water bath at 80 °C for 30 min to enhance the extraction of soluble sugars. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min, and the clear supernatant was collected. The residue was re-extracted twice with 70% ethanol (5 mL each time), and all supernatants were pooled to ensure complete extraction. The combined alcoholic extract was centrifuged again to remove any remaining particulates. A 1 mL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a clean test tube and mixed with 5 mL of freshly prepared anthrone reagent (150 mg of anthrone dissolved in 100 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid). The tubes were placed in a boiling water bath (100 °C) for 10 min to facilitate the reaction. After heating, samples were immediately cooled on ice to stop the reaction. The development of a blue-green color, indicative of soluble carbohydrate presence, was measured by reading the absorbance at 625 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, with distilled water as the blank. A standard curve was constructed using glucose solutions ranging from 0 to 100 µg mL⁻¹, yielding the calibration equation y = 0.0126x + 0.0304 with a coefficient of determination R² = 0.996. To determine the carbohydrate content in the sample, the absorbance value was substituted into the standard curve equation to calculate the glucose concentration (x). Then, the final soluble carbohydrate content was expressed as mg glucose equivalent per 100 mg fresh weight (mg/100 mg FW) using the formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varvec{S}\varvec{C}\:(\varvec{m}\varvec{g}/100\:\varvec{m}\varvec{g}\:\varvec{F}\varvec{W})=\frac{\varvec{C}\times\:\varvec{V}}{100\:\times\:\varvec{W}}$$\end{document}

Where: C is carbohydrate concentration from the standard curve (µg mL⁻¹), V is the total volume of extract used in the assay (mL), W is the fresh weight of the sample (mg), and 100 is the normalization factor to express results per 100 mg FW.

The activity of antioxidant enzymes

Catalase (CAT) specific activity was determined using a modified spectrophotometric method based on the procedure described by Alici and Arabaci (2010) [39]. Fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in the ice-cold extraction buffer (containing 50 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.0, with 1 mM EDTA and 1% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone [PVP]) using a chilled mortar and pestle. The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected as the crude enzyme extract.

The reaction mixture for the catalase activity assay contained 2.9 mL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 mL of freshly prepared 30 mM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) as the substrate. The reaction was initiated by adding 0.1 mL of the enzyme extract to the cuvette, and the decrease in absorbance was immediately recorded at 240 nm for 2 min at room temperature using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. The decomposition of H₂O₂ was monitored as a linear decrease in absorbance due to its breakdown, with readings taken every 15 s. Catalase activity was calculated based on the molar extinction coefficient of H₂O₂ at 240 nm, which is 39.4 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Protein concentration in the enzyme extract was determined using the Bradford method [40], with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. A standard curve for protein quantification using BSA (0–120 µg/mL) was generated with the equation y = 0.0088x + 0.0387 and a coefficient of determination R² = 0.990. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm, and the protein content was calculated using the standard curve generated with known BSA concentrations.

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was measured using the method described by Nakano and Asada [41], which involves monitoring the decrease in absorbance at 290 nm. The reaction mixture (total volume: 3 mL) consisted of 2.7 mL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM ascorbic acid, 0.1 mL of 1 mM H₂O₂, and 0.1 mL of the enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by adding H₂O₂, and the decrease in absorbance at 290 nm—corresponding to the oxidation of ascorbic acid—was recorded every 15 s over 2 min at 25 °C using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. APX activity was calculated using the molar extinction coefficient of ε = 2.8 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ for ascorbate at 290 nm. The specific activity of APX was calculated similarly to the method used for the CAT enzyme assay by dividing the volumetric activity by the protein concentration in the extract.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using PAST software version 4.13 [42]. One-way ANOVA was used to evaluate treatment differences and assess statistical significance at a threshold of p ≤ 0.05. Post-hoc comparisons among treatment groups were conducted using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test. Additionally, Non-Metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) based on the Bray–Curtis similarity index was performed to visualize and summarize the overall effects of salinity across all measured traits. The analysis was conducted using the CANOCO 5.0 software package [43].

Results

Germination and early growth stage

To evaluate the effect of salinity on germination and seedling performance in P. distans, various traits were measured, including GP, GI, MGT, SVI, seSL, seRL, seFW, and seDW. The germination test revealed significant effects of salinity on the germination and seedling performance characteristics of P. distans. GP showed a progressive decline with increasing NaCl levels, starting at 74% in the control (0 mM NaCl) and decreasing to just 4% at 400 mM NaCl, representing about a 95% reduction (Table 1). GI showed a similar trend, dropping by 95% at 400 mM NaCl compared to the control.

MGT increased with salinity, indicating delayed germination under higher NaCl concentrations (Table 1). MGT rose from 5.9 days in control to 13.5 days at 400 mM NaCl, reflecting a 129% increase, corresponding to a 2.3-fold longer germination time at the highest salinity level (Table 1). SVI was strongly affected, with a 99% reduction at the highest salinity level (Table 1). seSL and seRL also declined significantly with increasing NaCl concentration. The control group exhibited the highest seSL and seRL, while these values dropped at 400 mM NaCl, representing reductions of 97% (Table 1).

Overall responses to salinity

The NMDS analysis effectively captured the variation in physiological and biochemical traits of P. distans under different salinity levels, with the first and second axes explaining 81.4% and 19.6% of the variation, respectively (Fig. 6). Treatments were clearly separated along these axes, indicating distinct responses at varying NaCl concentrations. At 0–100 mM NaCl, P. distans had a close association with seedling growth traits such as seedling vigor index (SVI), germination percentage (GP), seedling fresh and dry weights (seFW, seDW), seedling root and shoot lengths (seRL, seSL), and leaf dry weight (LDW), reflecting optimal early growth performance under low salinity (Fig. 6). At 150–200 mM NaCl, traits such as antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT, APX), osmoprotectants (proline, soluble carbohydrates), pigment content (chlorophyll a, b, total chlorophyll, carotenoids), root length (RL), and specific leaf area (SLA) were most prominent, indicating enhanced stress tolerance mechanisms (Fig. 6). The close clustering of 150 and 200 mM NaCl treatments with these traits suggest that these salinity ranges reflects an adaptation zone where both growth and defense mechanisms are balanced (Fig. 6). Higher salinity levels (300–400 mM NaCl) were distinctly separated and associated with elevated levels of oxidative stress markers, including malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), as well as mean germination time (MGT), indicating increased stress and delayed germination under severe salinity conditions (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Effect of salinity on germination and the early growth stage

Germination and early growth stages are pivotal phases in the plant life cycle, directly influencing subsequent growth and reproductive success [44, 45]. These stages are essential for plant establishment and success in saline environments [44]. The results indicate a pronounced decline in GP, GI, SVI, and key seedling morphological traits, including seSL, seRL, seFW, and seDW, as NaCl concentrations increased (Table 1). In contrast, MGT increased significantly with higher salinity levels, indicating a delay in the germination process. In line with our findings, numerous studies on halophytes have reported similar trends, showing that higher salinity levels adversely affect germination rates and early growth parameters [46–49].

It is well-documented that the adverse effects of salinity on germination and early growth stages are primarily attributed to osmotic stress and ion toxicity. These factors hinder water uptake and disrupt metabolic processes. They also interfere with critical growth mechanisms necessary for successful germination and seedling establishment [50]. In addition, this decline in growth may be related to the impaired mobility of stored reserves in seeds. Under saline conditions, osmotic stress and ion toxicity hinder the mobilization of carbohydrates and proteins necessary for seedling growth [51]. This limitation affects the hydrolysis of starch and other reserves, which are crucial for energy and structural components during germination and early development [52].

Moreover, we observed that salinity negatively affected SVI (Table 1). As a result, seeds with lower vigor struggle to utilize their reserves effectively, leading to reduced seedling growth and establishment [51]. Conversely, seeds with higher vigor have a greater capacity for reserve mobilization, allowing them to perform better under salt stress conditions [51, 52]. This relationship highlights the importance of seed reserve mobilization in overcoming abiotic stresses like salinity, as efficient utilization of stored resources is essential for sustaining growth and development in challenging environments.

The significant increase in MGT suggests that osmotic stress affects the physiological processes involved in seed germination, such as enzyme activation and cellular expansion [46, 53]. An extended MGT under elevated salinity can lead to increased susceptibility to environmental stresses, reduced competitive ability against other species, and lower survival rates in natural habitats [54]. Overall, it seems that while P. distans demonstrates some salt tolerance, but it is vulnerable to salinity during the germination and early seedling growth stages.

Effect of salinity on morphological parameters

The results from the pot experiment demonstrate that increasing NaCl concentrations significantly affect several key morphological parameters, specifically, SL, RL, SFW, SDW, RFW, RDW, NOL, LFW, LDW, LDMC, LA, and SLA. All morphological parameters in our study exhibited biphasic responses to salinity, reflecting adaptive mechanisms and stress thresholds. This biphasic response is well recognized in salt-tolerant species and has been documented in halophytes [26, 55]. We observed a significant increase in SL at salinity levels up to 200 mM NaCl (Fig. 2A), likely due to osmotic adjustment and better nutrient uptake. Additionally, the increased SL allows P. distans to intercept light more effectively, thereby enhancing reproductive success [56]. Similarly, RL significantly increased at 200 mM NaCl (Fig. 2B), indicating enhanced root elongation to access water and nutrients. We also observed significant increases in shoot and root biomass, represented by SFW, SDW, RFW, and RDW, under 100–200 mM NaCl (Fig. 2). This response suggests that P. distans allocates resources effectively under such conditions to enhance growth.

Leaf traits, including NOL, LFW, and LA, reached their maximum values at 200 mM NaCl (Fig. 3A, B, and E). These responses suggest that P. distans optimizes its photosynthetic surface area at this salinity level. Higher LFW reflects effective water balance, supporting photosynthesis and growth [57]. Moreover, maintaining LDW highlights the plant’s role as a primary producer in saline ecosystems [58]. We also observed that SLA peaked at 200 mM NaCl (Fig. 3F), reflecting an adaptive strategy to optimize resource allocation. Increased LA also boosts photosynthetic capacity and energy transfer within the food web [57]. Higher SLA also enables thinner, more resource-efficient leaves, enhancing growth [56].

However, above 200 mM NaCl, all morphological parameters declined sharply (Fig. 3). This suggests a salinity threshold beyond which osmotic stress and ion toxicity impair cellular expansion and division, inhibiting metabolic activities such as carbohydrate synthesis, ion homeostasis, and final growth. From an ecological perspective, extreme salinity above 200 mM NaCl imposes significant constraints on P. distans, as shown by the decline in SL, which reflects the high energetic demands of stress tolerance and a reduction in competitive advantage [59]. Reducing LDW under extreme conditions diminishes its ecological contribution and overall biomass productivity [23]. Notably, producing smaller leaves under high salinity indicates a strategic trade-off, prioritizing resource conservation over maximal productivity [60]. The decline in SLA also highlights a shift towards resource efficiency and conservation, which, while adaptive, compromises ecological competitiveness in more saline environments [59, 61]. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have documented similar responses in other halophytes, where moderate salinity can stimulate growth, but excessive salinity leads to detrimental effects on various growth parameters [62–64]. While the P. distans can adapt to salinity levels up to 200 mM NaCl, its growth is severely constrained under higher salinity conditions. These biphasic responses to salinity can be traced through their physiological and biochemical adaptations at varying levels of salt stress.

Effect of salinity on photosynthetic pigments content

Photosynthetic pigments, especially chlorophylls and carotenoids, are essential for maintaining photochemical efficiency under salinity stress by enhancing light absorption and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced damage [65]. Maintaining pigment integrity under saline conditions ensures the functionality of the photosynthetic apparatus, supporting energy metabolism and stress resilience in plants [65]. The impact of NaCl salinity on photosynthetic pigment content in P. distans reveals distinct adaptive responses to varying salinity levels.

The results indicate that at moderate salinity levels, particularly at 200 mM NaCl, P. distans exhibited remarkable increases in pigment concentrations (Fig. 4). This enhancement suggests that P. distans can effectively utilize salinity to boost chlorophyll and carotenoid synthesis, which may be an adaptive strategy to potentially support photosynthetic performance under conditions where osmotic stress is manageable. This response can be attributed to effective osmotic adjustment, maintaining turgor pressure, and cellular functions vital for chlorophyll synthesis [15, 66]. Additionally, balanced sodium levels at lower salinity prevent disruption of nutrient uptake, ensuring adequate magnesium and nitrogen absorption, essential for chlorophyll production [26]. While pigment content can influence photosynthetic activity, no direct measurement of photosynthetic efficiency was performed in our study. Accordingly, further experimental evidence will be needed to better understand how P. distans regulates photosynthesis in response to salinity stress. Enhanced pigment content could improve light capture and may contribute to photosynthetic capacity, supporting energy production and growth [67]. However, salinity levels above 200 mM NaCl resulted in a marked decline in pigment content (Fig. 4), highlighting the detrimental effects of excessive salt stress. This negative response can be driven by stress-induced factors, including osmotic pressure restricting water availability, impairing chloroplast function, and causing chlorophyll degradation [13–15]. In addition, elevated sodium levels disrupt the uptake of nutrients essential for chlorophyll synthesis, leading to deficiencies [13, 14]. Moreover, high salinity triggers ROS production, damaging cellular components and accelerating pigment degradation through oxidative stress [10, 56, 68].

We also observed that under severe salinity stress (300 mM NaCl), plants prioritize the maintenance of chlorophyll a, leading to relatively stable levels of chlorophyll a and total chlorophyll content. In contrast, there were significant reductions in chlorophyll b and carotenoids, which serve as secondary light-harvesting and photoprotective pigments, respectively [36, 69]. These observations provide indirect insights into potential functional impacts on photosynthesis but cannot be interpreted as direct measurements of photosynthetic efficiency. Chlorophyll b is more prone to degradation under ionic and oxidative stress due to its peripheral role in the photosynthetic apparatus [70]. Similarly, although carotenoids play a role in ROS scavenging during the early stages of stress, their biosynthesis is disrupted when oxidative damage exceeds the plant’s protective capacity [69, 71]. These results suggest that plants adopt a protective strategy under high salinity to conserve essential components of the photosynthetic machinery, while secondary pigments become more vulnerable to degradation.

Overall, the significant increase in pigment content at 200 mM NaCl underscores this concentration as optimal for enhancing photosynthetic pigments in P. distans compared to other treatments. These changes may be associated with potential improvements in photosynthetic capacity, but direct functional measurements are required to confirm this. The enhancement and maintenance of photosynthetic pigments in P. distans, crucial for sustaining plant growth under moderate saline stress, can be explained by evaluating oxidative stress markers and plant defense mechanisms, including osmotic regulation and antioxidant enzyme activity.

Effect of salinity on ROS stress indicators

The effects of NaCl salinity on MDA and H₂O₂ content in P. distans offer valuable insights into the oxidative stress experienced by this halophyte under different salinity levels. MDA is a well-established marker of lipid peroxidation, formed when ROS damages cell membranes [72]. H₂O₂ is a key ROS that, if not controlled by the plant’s antioxidant defenses, can damage proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [73].

The study shows a progressive increase in MDA and H₂O₂ concentrations with rising salinity, indicating increased oxidative stress as P. distans encounters higher salt concentrations (Fig. 5A and B). As observed in our study, previous research has shown that increasing salinity induces H₂O₂ and MDA production in plants, including halophytes [74–76]. It is well-documented that genes such as Respiratory Burst Oxidase Homolog (RBOH), Lipoxygenase (LOX), NADPH Oxidase (NOX), and Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK), along with transcription factors like WRKY33, play pivotal roles in upregulating the production of H₂O₂ and MDA. These genes enhance ROS generation, lipid peroxidation, and activation of stress signaling pathways, particularly under salinity and oxidative stress conditions, mediating plant responses to environmental challenges [77–81].

MDA and H₂O₂ content peaked sharply at 400 mM NaCl, indicating a significant rise in oxidative stress (Fig. 5A and B). This significant rise in MDA and H₂O₂ at high salinity supports the idea that P. distans undergoes severe oxidative stress. It suggests extensive lipid peroxidation and membrane damage, which can impair cellular integrity and function [73, 74]. Nevertheless, at lower salinity levels (0 to 150 mM NaCl), MDA and H₂O₂ showed moderate increases, indicating manageable oxidative stress (Fig. 5A and B). These findings suggest that P. distans can tolerate moderate salinity levels. However, above 200 mM NaCl, MDA and H₂O₂ levels increase significantly, marking a threshold beyond which oxidative stress becomes pronounced (Fig. 5A and B). The sharp increases in MDA and H₂O₂ at higher salinities indicate that excessive salt concentrations disrupt the balance between ROS production and scavenging mechanisms in P. distans.

Generally, at 200 mM NaCl, the reduction in oxidative stress markers, likely through mitigation of ROS effects on photosynthetic pigments, enhances photosynthetic efficiency and improves plant growth. However, this positive effect is not sustained at higher salinity levels, where excessive ROS accumulation disrupts photosynthetic activity and impairs plant growth. To better understand the underlying mechanisms, the plant’s defense strategies, including osmotic regulation and antioxidant enzyme activity, were further examined.

Effect of salinity on osmolytes

Osmotic regulation plays a key role in alleviating oxidative stress, stabilizing photosynthetic pigments, and enhancing photosynthesis and overall growth under saline conditions [82]. The investigation into the effects of NaCl salinity on osmolyte content, notably P and SC, reveals critical insights into the adaptive mechanisms employed by P. distans in response to salinity stress.

Proline is a well-known compatible solute essential for osmotic adjustment, cellular protection, and stress mitigation [83]. Proline synthesis stabilizes proteins and membranes, scavenges ROS, and facilitates nutrient uptake under stress conditions [83, 84]. SC serves multiple functions in plants, including energy storage, osmoregulation, and providing structural support [85, 86].

The results show that P and SC levels significantly increase up to 200 mM NaCl, peaking at this concentration before declining at higher salinity levels (Fig. 5C and D). This accumulation suggests that P. distans effectively utilizes proline and SC to counteract osmotic stress and maintain turgor under moderate salinity. As observed in our study, several studies have shown that proline and soluble carbohydrate accumulation increase with elevated salinity, acting as key osmoprotectants [85–88]. In addition, the significant increases in P and SC levels at lower salinities (150 mM and 100 mM) further highlight the plant’s capacity to adapt to saline environments (Fig. 5C and D).

Under moderate salt levels, genes such as Δ1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase 1 (P5CS1), ornithine aminotransferase (OAT), proline transporter (KvProT), sucrose phosphate synthase (SPS), and sucrose synthase (SUS), along with transcription factors like WRKY and ethylene response factor (ERF), are upregulated to enhance the accumulation of P and SC, thereby aiding plant adaptation to salinity stress [89–91].

However, P and SC contents declined significantly at higher salinities (300 mM and 400 mM) (Fig. 5C and D). Excessive salt stress generally reduces proline accumulation by downregulating biosynthetic genes like Kalanchoe vacuolar Δ¹-Pyrroline-5-Carboxylate Synthase 1 (KvP5CS1) and increasing catabolism through Proline dehydrogenase (PDH) activity [91–93]. Similarly, under high salinity, genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism, including those encoding sucrose transporters, ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase), and beta-glucosidase, are downregulated [86, 94, 95]. Our findings indicate that excessive salinity overwhelms P. distans, impairing its ability to synthesize or accumulate osmolytes efficiently. This osmotic disruption compromises cellular protection, leaving the plant more vulnerable to stress-induced damage.

In summary, it seems that at salinity levels above 200 mM NaCl, P. distans struggles to accumulate osmolytes like proline and soluble carbohydrates. The energy required for osmotic adjustment forces the plant to prioritize survival mechanisms, such as synthesizing protective proteins and activating antioxidant defenses, limiting resources for osmolyte accumulation. Additionally, high salinity disrupts the metabolic pathways responsible for osmolyte synthesis and promotes their degradation. It seems that prolonged stress makes these compounds more vulnerable to breakdown, while the toxic effects of Na+ and Cl− further impair their stability. As a result, the biosynthesis of proline and soluble carbohydrates becomes unsustainable under extreme salinity conditions.

Effect of salinity on antioxidant enzyme activities

The effects of NaCl salinity on the activities of antioxidant enzymes, particularly CAT and APX, provide valuable insights into the oxidative stress response mechanisms of P. distans. CAT plays a crucial role in decomposing H₂O₂, a harmful ROS, into water and oxygen, thereby protecting cellular components from oxidative damage [96, 97]. APX is critical for detoxifying H₂O₂ in plant cells and plays a vital role in the ascorbate-glutathione cycle, which is essential for maintaining redox balance during oxidative stress [96, 98].

The results show a significant increase in CAT and APX activities up to 200 mM NaCl, reflecting the plant’s adaptive response to salt stress (Fig. 5E and F). Our results align with studies in other halophytes, where antioxidant enzyme activities increase under similar salt concentrations to mitigate oxidative stress and support survival under saline conditions [99–101]. The study shows that CAT activity peaked at 200 mM NaCl, showing a remarkable increase compared to the control (Fig. 5E and F). This substantial increase suggests that P. distans enhances CAT activity to mitigate oxidative damage. The elevated ROS levels associated with salt stress drive this response. Increases in CAT activity were also observed at lower salinities (150 mM and 100 mM), indicating that even moderate salinity triggers an adaptive response to enhance antioxidant capacity (Fig. 5E).

However, as salinity levels exceeded 200 mM NaCl, CAT activity declined (Fig. 5E). It seems that salinity stress, by increasing the production of ROS, overwhelms the antioxidant defense systems. As a result, enzyme activities decreased under prolonged high salt conditions. The plant struggled to cope with oxidative stress. Similar responses have been observed in other plants under high salinity [99, 100, 102]. It was observed that high levels of Na⁺ can interfere with enzyme activity by altering the ionic balance necessary for optimal function, resulting in decreased CAT and APX activities [99]. Moreover, salt stress can lead to reduced energy availability in cells, which is crucial for maintaining the activity of antioxidant enzymes. This energy depletion can further impair CAT and APX functions [102]. Despite these declines, CAT activity remained higher than in the control (Fig. 5E).

Similarly, APX activity peaked at 200 mM NaCl, showing a significant increase compared to the control (Fig. 5F). Increases in APX activity at 100–150 mM NaCl further emphasizes P. distans’ ability to enhance antioxidant capacity in response to osmotic stress. However, similar to CAT activity, APX levels decreased at higher salinities. Though higher than the control, these values indicate a decline in antioxidant efficiency under severe salt stress. This outcome suggests that while the plant can still activate its antioxidant defenses under high salinity conditions, the efficiency of these mechanisms may be compromised.

Overall responses to salinity

The NMDS analysis revealed key insights into the physiological and biochemical responses of P. distans to varying salinity levels. The clear separation of treatments along the first and second axes highlights the complexity of plant responses to salt stress, with distinct trait groupings along the salinity gradient.

At lower salinity levels (0–100 mM NaCl), P. distans showed robust germination and seedling growth, reflected in its strong association with germination indices, seedling vigor, and early biomass accumulation. These findings suggest that mild salinity does not significantly impair early development. In fact, it may enhance initial establishment, aligning with previous reports that halophytes often exhibit optimal germination under slightly saline conditions due to improved water uptake or osmotic balance [48, 103]. The central positioning of the 150–200 mM NaCl treatments reflects a shift in the plant’s strategy from growth promotion to integrated stress tolerance. Here, antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT, APX), osmoprotectant accumulation (proline, soluble carbohydrates), and maintenance of chlorophyll and carotenoid content were maximized. These responses may indirectly support photosynthetic performance, but it is important to note that direct measurements of photosynthesis were not conducted. This suite of responses indicates a well-coordinated defense mechanism that enables P. distans to mitigate oxidative stress, maintain photosynthetic efficiency, and sustain metabolic functions under moderate salinity. These findings align with studies showing that antioxidant enzyme activity is crucial for detoxifying reactive oxygen species (ROS) under salt stress [104]. Moreover, the significant P and SC contents rise at 150–200 mM NaCl, suggesting their crucial role as osmoprotectants, stabilizing proteins and cellular structures [85]. At higher salinity levels (300–400 mM NaCl), the plant’s physiological and biochemical status deteriorated, as indicated by oxidative stress markers (MDA, H₂O₂) and delayed germination (MGT). This response suggests that the tolerance threshold, the accumulation of toxic ions, and excessive ROS overwhelm defense systems. This leads to cellular damage, impaired metabolic functions, and reduced growth. The separation of these treatments from beneficial traits highlights the detrimental impact of extreme salinity on plant fitness. These mechanisms are summarized in Fig. 7.

Interestingly, the NMDS results emphasize the importance of interpreting antioxidant enzyme activity within the broader physiological context. While CAT and APX were elevated at 200 mM NaCl, their effectiveness depends on concurrent osmoprotectant accumulation and pigment stabilization. This supports the idea that antioxidant activity alone is not a sufficient indicator of stress tolerance [105]; it must be considered alongside other adaptive responses, such as osmotic adjustment, pigment maintenance, and growth.

In summary, P. distans exhibits a biphasic response to salinity, with enhanced adaptation at moderate salinity (150–200 mM NaCl) and physiological decline under extreme stress. These findings reflect the plant’s facultative halophytic nature and highlight the complex interplay between growth and stress tolerance mechanisms. Understanding this balance is essential for evaluating the species’ potential in saline agriculture and ecological restoration of salt-affected landscapes. Element concentrations were not measured due to limited biomass, and detailed information on P. distans is lacking. However, other Puccinellia species maintain ion homeostasis under salinity stress by preferentially absorbing potassium (K⁺) over sodium (Na⁺) and sequestering excess Na⁺ in vacuoles via Na⁺/H⁺ antiporters [106, 107]. Na⁺ is also stored in older tissues to protect younger, metabolically active cells [106, 107]. These plants maintain calcium (Ca²⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺) homeostasis to ensure membrane stability and likely reduce Na⁺ uptake at the root level [106, 107]. Therefore, further research on ion homeostasis in P. distans is crucial for clarifying the mechanisms that support its adaptation to salinity.

These findings highlight the role of salinity thresholds in shaping the resilience of Puccinellia distans, with moderate salt levels (150–200 mM NaCl) triggering beneficial physiological and biochemical responses. Future research should address the underlying molecular and physiological mechanisms, including ion regulation, nutrient balance, antioxidant activity, epigenetic regulation, gene expression, and root system architecture. Understanding these processes will improve our insight into the P. distans adaptive potential and facilitate its application in saline agriculture and ecological restoration.

Conclusion

Puccinellia distans exhibits explicit physiological and biochemical adaptations to varying salinity levels, with distinct thresholds for optimal and suboptimal performance. Germination and early growth are favored under low salinity conditions (0–100 mM NaCl), while maximum growth and stress tolerance occur at moderate salinities (150–200 mM NaCl). Within this range, the plant optimizes pigment synthesis, antioxidant activity, osmolyte accumulation, and biomass production, thereby mitigating oxidative stress. In contrast, salinities exceeding 200 mM NaCl disrupt these adaptive processes, reducing pigment content, lowering biomass accumulation, and diminishing stress resilience.

This study provides novel insights by identifying a specific “adaptation window” of salinity for P. distans, where moderate salinity exposure triggers the coordinated activation of key physiological and biochemical defense mechanisms. Unlike previous general assessments of halophyte behavior, our findings demonstrate how P. distans balances growth and stress tolerance under defined salinity stress conditions. These results advance the understanding of halophyte responses to salt stress and offer practical implications for ecological restoration and saline agriculture, defining the optimum salinity for P. distans growth and salinity limits, as this species can be utilized for pasture and forage [20]. This knowledge can help in managing salt-affected agricultural lands, which are increasing in area worldwide [44].
