Efficacy of artificial intelligence-based digital therapeutics versus traditional Schroth exercises for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis: protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Ruisi Ma, Ziqi Huang, Xiaowen Zhu, Xiaoyu Ma, Wanru Cheng, Di Tang, Jinde Liu, Lili Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) requires long-term conservative management to prevent curve progression. While physiotherapeutic scoliosis-specific exercises, specifically the Schroth method, are considered the gold standard for conservative treatment, their clinical efficacy is often limited by accessibility barriers, high costs and suboptimal treatment adherence. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of a novel artificial intelligence (AI)-based digital therapeutic system, which uses computer vision for remote, personalised posture analysis and adaptive exercise prescription compared with traditional outpatient Schroth therapy.Methods and analysisThis parallel-group randomised controlled trial will be conducted at Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (Guangzhou, China). 300 adolescents aged 10–18 years with AIS who present with a Cobb angle between 10° and 30° and a Risser sign of 0–2 will be recruited and randomised in a 1:1 ratio into an intervention group and a control group. The intervention group will use a smartphone application to capture standardised bi-weekly images. These images will be processed by an AI algorithm to classify curve patterns and assign personalised exercise modules with adaptive dosing ranging from maintenance to high-intensity levels. The control group will receive standard outpatient Schroth care. The primary outcome is the absolute change in the major curve Cobb angle from baseline to 6 months. Secondary outcomes include the angle of trunk rotation, trunk appearance perception, Scoliosis Research Society-22 Revised (SRS-22r) quality-of-life scores and adherence rates. Statistical analysis will follow the intention-to-treat principle using linear mixed models to account for repeated measures.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (Guangzhou, China) (approval no. [2025]497A01). Written informed assent and consent will be obtained from participants and their legal guardians respectively. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals and international conferences.Trial registration numberClinicalTrials.gov, NCT07341633.

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) remains the most common structural spinal deformity in adolescence, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 0.5% to 5% depending on screening protocols and diagnostic thresholds.1 2 While the aetiology of AIS is multifactorial, the management of spinal health faces evolving challenges in the context of modern lifestyle changes. Recent large-scale school screening programmes and observational analyses, particularly those conducted in the post-COVID-19 era, have reported high rates of abnormal postural markers and increased referral for suspected scoliosis. These studies have identified prolonged screen time, sedentary behaviour and low physical activity as factors associated with adverse postural profiles.35 This confluence of developmental susceptibility and lifestyle-related postural stress presents a growing public health challenge, potentially exacerbating the burden of spinal deformities and increasing the demand for effective, accessible conservative interventions.

The clinical imperative for effective management is underscored by the risk of progression. Longitudinal data indicate that progressive curves, particularly during the pubertal growth spurt, can lead to long-term sequelae ranging from chronic back pain and functional limitations to cosmetic deformity and, in severe cases, impaired cardiopulmonary function.68 To mitigate these outcomes, the International Society on Scoliosis Orthopaedic and Rehabilitation Treatment (SOSORT) guidelines recommend conservative management during growth, including physiotherapeutic scoliosis-specific exercises (PSSE) as part of a comprehensive non-operative strategy aimed at limiting progression and improving function.9 10

Despite its proven efficacy, the clinical utility of PSSE is often constrained by accessibility and adherence barriers. Standard Schroth therapy necessitates intensive supervision and frequent manual adjustments by specialised therapists to ensure exercise fidelity.11 12 This resource-intensive model creates substantial disparities in accessibility, particularly for patients in underserved regions.12 Furthermore, a critical limitation of current clinical practice is the temporal disconnect between static exercise prescriptions and dynamic skeletal maturation. An exercise regimen prescribed during a clinic visit may fail to adapt to rapid changes in spinal curvature and compensatory patterns that occur between follow-ups, potentially diminishing therapeutic efficacy.10 12

The integration of digital therapeutics and computer-vision-enabled assessment offers a scalable mechanism to augment standard care. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of radiation-free monitoring methods indicate that while radiography remains the reference standard, non-ionising surface topography demonstrates robust criterion validity against radiographic measures.13 Concurrently, prospective validation studies have established the diagnostic accuracy of smartphone-based photogrammetry, reporting sensitivity rates exceeding 96% for clinically significant curves compared with scoliometer screening.14 Beyond assessment, the therapeutic potential of remote delivery models has been explored. A randomised clinical trial published in 2025 demonstrated that a PSSE programme delivered via a digital platform, which relied on remote human supervision, could yield superior outcomes compared with conventional models.12 While this validates the concept of home-based digital care, such interventions primarily digitise the communication channel while retaining the resource-intensive requirement for manual therapist oversight to tailor prescriptions.

Moreover, the implementation of these technologies remains fragmented and lacks standardisation. A recent scoping review identified substantial heterogeneity in the technological frameworks and behaviour-change components of existing digital AIS interventions, underscoring a paucity of evidence regarding strategies that sustain long-term engagement.15 Furthermore, the reliability of surface topography metrics may be confounded by patient-specific variables, such as elevated body mass index or severe curvature, necessitating rigorous clinical oversight rather than full automation.13 Consequently, there is a critical need to evaluate artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled systems that rigorously combine validated non-ionising measurement with structured adaptive dosing. This involves algorithms that transparently define inputs and outputs to adjust exercise parameters based on real-time feedback while maintaining a hybrid human-in-the-loop safety protocol.

To address this knowledge gap, this randomised controlled trial (RCT) aims to evaluate the efficacy of a novel AI-based digital therapeutic system compared with traditional outpatient Schroth exercises. Unlike standard telerehabilitation, the proposed intervention uses a validated computer vision algorithm to provide dynamic triage and adaptive dosing based on standardised postural indices. We hypothesise that this AI-driven intervention will be superior to traditional outpatient care in controlling Cobb angle progression, while also demonstrating superior outcomes in treatment adherence and accessibility over a 6-month period.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This study is designed as a prospective RCT with a parallel-group structure. The investigation aims to compare the efficacy of an AI-based digital therapeutic system against traditional outpatient PSSE. The trial will be conducted in the Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center, Guangzhou, China. Eligible participants will be recruited from the department of paediatric orthopaedics during routine outpatient visits. The protocol strictly adheres to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines and incorporates the SPIRIT-AI extension for clinical trials involving AI16 17 (online supplemental appendix A, etable S1). Participants

Adolescents aged 10–18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of AIS will be assessed for eligibility. Inclusion criteria encompass a coronal Cobb angle between 10° and 30° and a Risser sign of 0–2, indicating significant remaining skeletal growth potential. Participants must be physically capable of performing exercise interventions and willing to comply with a 6-month follow-up schedule. Patients currently prescribed a rigid thoracolumbosacral orthosis are eligible, provided the brace does not involve body casts that prevent the requisite photographic assessment. Exclusion criteria are strictly defined to rule out non-idiopathic aetiologies, including congenital, neuromuscular or syndromic scoliosis. Additionally, candidates with severe cardiopulmonary, psychiatric or cognitive impairments that would hinder adherence to a self-directed exercise regimen or concurrent participation in other orthopaedic clinical trials will be excluded.

Randomisation and blinding

Following the acquisition of written informed consent, participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the digital intervention group or the control group. The randomisation sequence will be generated by an independent statistician using a computer-based block randomisation method with variable block sizes to ensure balanced allocation. Stratification will be employed based on the Risser sign and baseline curve magnitude to ensure balanced prognostic factors. Allocation concealment is guaranteed through a centralised web-based system that releases the group assignment only after baseline data are locked. Due to the visible nature of the exercise interventions, blinding of participants and treating therapists is not feasible. However, to minimise detection bias, the primary outcome will be assessed by independent radiologists who remain strictly blinded to group allocation and the timing of the radiographs.

Interventions

Traditional Schroth therapy (control group)

Participants in the control group will receive standard outpatient care based on the Schroth Best Practice guidelines.18 19 This involves an initial 60 min comprehensive evaluation and education session led by a certified Schroth therapist. Patients will be prescribed a home exercise programme tailored to their specific curve pattern, typically consisting of 3–4 core exercises to be performed daily for 30 min. Follow-up visits will be scheduled monthly to monitor adherence, review exercise quality and manually adjust the prescription based on clinical progression. Adherence will be monitored using paper-based daily exercise logs reviewed at each clinical visit, in accordance with the Schroth Best Practice guidelines (online supplemental appendix B); participants in both groups will also upload standardised standing photographs every 2 weeks for longitudinal monitoring, but these are used to adjust the exercise prescription only in the intervention group.

AI-based digital therapeutics (intervention group)

Participants in the intervention group will use a hybrid intelligence rehabilitation model facilitated via a secure instant messaging platform. Unlike fully automated applications, this study employs a workflow where a clinical research assistant acts as a human-in-the-loop intermediary to ensure data quality and patient safety (figure 2). The intervention integrates computer vision with a standardised biomechanical decision matrix to deliver personalised and adaptive exercise prescriptions. To ensure reproducibility and transparency, the intervention details for both the AI-based digital therapeutics group and the control group are reported in accordance with the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist and the Consensus on Exercise Reporting Template (CERT) (online supplemental appendix A, etables S2 and S3).20 21

Data acquisition and algorithmic triage logic

Participants are required to upload standardised standing photographs every 2 weeks via the secure messaging platform. On validation by the research assistant, the photographs are processed using the MediaPipe framework with a BlazePose-based pose estimator, which outputs 33 body landmarks from 2D images. All pixel-based measurements are normalised against the calculated trunk length to correct for camera distance variations, enabling the derivation of standardised posture indices for module assignment.22 To ensure reproducibility, the pose-estimation software version and preprocessing pipeline will be locked prior to trial initiation, and any updates during the trial will be documented as protocol amendments. The system employs a hierarchical triage logic grounded in clinical biomechanics to assign therapeutic modules, details of which are provided in online supplemental appendix C. The classification process begins by screening for coronal decompensation, termed Priority 1, which necessitates immediate global realignment (Module C). A Normalised Spine Index threshold of greater than 4.0% was selected to align with the clinical ‘2 cm rule’, where a lateral deviation exceeding 2 cm relative to a standard 45 cm adolescent trunk indicates significant decompensation.23 24 Furthermore, a trunk tilt exceeding 3.0° serves as a secondary threshold to identify cases where the gravity moment is exacerbating the curve.25 In the absence of decompensation, the algorithm proceeds to differentiate between thoracic major (Module A) and lumbar or thoracolumbar major (Module B) patterns by comparing the magnitude of the shoulder tilt angle versus the pelvic tilt angle relative to the horizontal axis.

Adaptive dosing and safety protocols

To ensure progressive adaptation, exercise intensity is stratified into three distinct tiers designated as Maintenance (Level 1), Intensification (Level 2) and High-Intensity (Level 3) based on the severity of baseline topographic metrics. The system employs a bi-weekly assessment and adjustment decision matrix to dynamically modulate the prescription. When metrics demonstrate improvement or stabilisation within the physiological range, the system triggers a progression protocol that increases the mechanical load, such as elevating the height of corrective props or extending the duration of isometric holds. Conversely, the protocol incorporates a strict safety mechanism where a rapid regression of metrics exceeding 20% triggers an immediate safety suspension. In such scenarios, the automated prescription is suspended, and the human research assistant is alerted to intervene via telephone or video call to assess for potential adverse events, improper exercise execution or technical errors in data capture. The complete repository of exercise modules and the detailed quantitative decision matrix for progression and regression are provided in online supplemental appendix D.

Safety review and adherence monitoring

Before the prescription is released to the patient, the assistant reviews the algorithmic output as a safety checkpoint. This step validates that the prescribed exercises are clinically appropriate and confirms that no rapid progression exceeding a 20% regression in metrics has occurred. Once validated, the assistant sends the personalised exercise plan back to the participant via the messaging platform. This plan includes specific instructional videos and parameter adjustments, such as the required thickness of props. To monitor adherence, participants are required to submit a clock-in message or short practice video after completing their daily session.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure is the major curve Cobb angle. To ensure measurement reliability, all radiographs will be assessed on a digital workstation by two independent, blinded radiologists. The mean of the two measurements will be used for analysis. Inter-rater reliability will be assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, and discrepancies exceeding 5° will be adjudicated by a third senior radiologist. Secondary outcomes include changes in the angle of trunk rotation assessed via a scoliometer, trunk appearance perception assessed by the Trunk Appearance Perception Scale (TAPS) questionnaire26 27 and health-related quality of life measured by the SRS-22r (Scoliosis Research Society-22 Revised) questionnaire.28 29 Assessments will be conducted at baseline, 3 months and 6 months. Adherence will be calculated as the percentage of prescribed sessions completed, verified by logbooks in the control group and digital timestamps and clock-in videos in the intervention group.

Sample size calculation

This trial is designed as a two-arm, parallel-group superiority study. The sample size was calculated based on the primary endpoint, defined as the between-group difference in the mean change in Cobb angle from baseline to the prespecified follow-up. Assuming a common standard deviation (σ) of 8.0° derived from prior studies and targeting a clinically meaningful between-group difference (δ) of 3.5°,12 30 we calculated the required sample size for a two-sample comparison of independent means with a two-sided type I error rate of 0.05 and 90% power.31 Using the standard formula as follow:

The estimated sample size was 110 participants per group (total n=220). To account for an anticipated 20% attrition or non-evaluable radiographs, such as withdrawal, loss to follow-up or inadequate imaging quality, the sample size was inflated to a minimum of 138 participants per group. To ensure adequate power under these pragmatic conditions, we will recruit 150 participants per group, yielding a total sample size of 300 adolescents.

Data management and statistical analysis

All digital data, including photographs and radiographic measurements, will be de-identified and stored on a secure, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant server with role-based access control.32 Facial features in photographs will be automatically cropped or masked to preserve anonymity. Statistical analysis will be performed using the intention-to-treat principle as the primary analysis set, with per-protocol analysis conducted as a sensitivity analysis.33 Continuous variables will be described as means with SD. The primary outcome will be analysed using linear mixed models to account for the repeated measure design and missing data under the missing-at-random assumption.34 The model will include fixed effects for treatment group, time and the group-by-time interaction, as well as random effects for individual subjects and covariates for baseline Cobb angle and Risser sign. The treatment effect will be reported as the least squares mean difference with 95% CIs. Secondary outcomes will be analysed using similar mixed models. All statistical tests will be two-sided with a significance level of 0.05, and adjustments for multiplicity will be applied to secondary outcomes using the Holm-Bonferroni method.

Trial status

This protocol describes trial V.1.0, dated 10 January 2026. Recruitment commenced on 1 February 2026 and is expected to be completed by January 2028. The trial is currently at the recruitment stage. The final participant is anticipated to complete the 6-month follow-up by January 2028, which is also the planned date of study completion. These timelines are consistent with the record held at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07341633).

None.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval for this study has been obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (Guangzhou, China) (approval no. [2025]497A01). The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT07341633) and will be conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent will be obtained from the legal guardians of all participating adolescents, and written assent will be sought from the adolescents themselves prior to enrolment (online supplemental appendix E). Participants retain the right to withdraw from the study at any time without strictly affecting their routine medical care. To ensure confidentiality, all participant data will be de-identified and stored on secure, encrypted servers. The findings of this trial will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and presentations at international orthopaedic conferences. Authorship will be determined based on the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) guidelines.

Discussion

AIS necessitates long-term management to mitigate curve progression and minimise the need for surgical intervention.35 Traditional conservative treatments have demonstrated efficacy in stabilising spinal curvature and improving quality of life.36 However, the success of these interventions is intrinsically linked to patient adherence and the precise execution of therapeutic exercises. Adolescents frequently struggle with compliance due to the repetitive nature of the regimens and the absence of professional supervision in the home environment.11 This RCT protocol introduces an AI-based digital therapeutic system designed to bridge this gap in care. By integrating computer vision technology with standard rehabilitation principles, this study aims to provide a novel solution for home-based scoliosis management.

A primary strength of this investigation lies in its rigorous methodological design intended to ensure the reliability of the findings. The statistical analysis plan adheres to the intention-to-treat principle to reflect the pragmatic effectiveness of the intervention in real-world settings. We recognise that long-term rehabilitation trials often face challenges regarding participant retention and protocol adherence. Consequently, we have incorporated comprehensive sensitivity analyses into the study design. These additional analyses will test the robustness of the primary results by employing per-protocol datasets and various missing data imputation strategies. Furthermore, the use of blinded outcome assessors for radiological measurements will minimise detection bias and enhance the internal validity of the trial.

The technical innovation of this study centres on the application of the MediaPipe framework for pose estimation.22 This technology enables the system to capture key body landmarks and analyse spinal posture in real time without the need for wearable sensors. The immediate feedback mechanism provided by this system is hypothesised to improve exercise accuracy and patient engagement compared with conventional paper-based or video-based instructions. This seamless integration of advanced posture analysis into a user-friendly mobile interface represents a significant step forward in digital orthopaedics.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations inherent in this study design. The nature of the exercise intervention precludes the blinding of participants and care providers, which may introduce some degree of performance bias. We attempt to mitigate this by standardising the care protocol across both groups. Additionally, the efficacy of the digital therapeutic relies on the stability of the user’s internet connection and the hardware specifications of their mobile devices. Variations in these technical factors could potentially influence the user experience and intervention consistency. The study is also limited to a single centre which may affect the generalisability of the results to broader populations with different demographic characteristics.

The successful validation of this AI-based digital therapeutic could offer a scalable and accessible alternative to traditional scoliosis rehabilitation. This approach has the potential to empower patients to manage their condition more independently while reducing the monitoring burden on healthcare professionals and families.
