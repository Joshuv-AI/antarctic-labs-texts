Performance and emission interaction analysis of biodiesel–diesel blends with CeO₂ nanoparticles and diethyl ether in a compression ignition engine
Authors: Samuel Tamrat, Yared Seifu, Venkata Ramayya Ancha, Rajendiran Gopal, Ramesh Babu Nallamothu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of adding 75 ppm cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO2 NPs) with 2% diethyl ether (DEE) to castor biodiesel diesel blends on the performance and emissions of a single-cylinder, four-stroke CI engine operated at 80% load across 1700–3000 rpm, revealing that the nano-additive improved brake torque, brake power, thermal efficiency, and reduced carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HC), and soot emissions, with only marginal changes in nitrogen oxides (NOx) compared to blends without CeO₂. The results indicate that the addition of CeO2 NPs led to an increase in brake torque to 15.5 Nm (95% CI: 15.0–16.0 Nm) and brake power to 3.7 kW (95% CI: 3.6–3.8 kW). Furthermore, BSFC decreased to 250 g/kWh at (95% confidence interval (CI): 240–260 g/kWh), enhancing thermal efficiency to 38% (95% CI: 37–39%). Emission analysis revealed a reduction in CO levels to 0.425%V (95% CI: 0.400–0.450%V), HC emissions to 47.5 ppm (95% CI: 45.0–50.0 ppm), and NOx emissions to 80 ppm (95% CI: 75–85 ppm). These findings demonstrate the potential of CeO2 NPs to enhance engine efficiency while reducing harmful emissions, making them a promising candidate for cleaner combustion technologies.Keywords: Biodiesel, CeO2 NPs, Combustion, Correlation, Emission, Interaction, PerformanceSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The increasing depletion of fossil fuel reserves, rising crude oil prices, and escalating environmental concerns have intensified global efforts to find sustainable alternatives to conventional fuels. Diesel engines, valued for their superior fuel efficiency and relatively lower emissions, are widely used across various industries1–3. However, the rapid growth of the automotive sector, driven by increasing urbanization and transportation demands, has further deepened the dependence on petroleum-based diesel4–6. This reliance presents significant economic and environmental challenges, particularly in countries such as Ethiopia, where transportation infrastructure is primarily sustained by imported diesel7,8. As a result, transitioning toward cleaner, renewable fuel sources has become an urgent necessity9,10.

Reducing diesel imports is essential to conserving foreign currency reserves, yet an equally critical priority is the identification and integration of viable alternative fuels11. The finite availability of fossil fuels, coupled with their adverse environmental impact, necessitates the pursuit of renewable and sustainable energy sources12. Biodiesel has emerged as one of the most promising alternatives, offering a renewable, eco-friendly substitute for petroleum diesel13,14. Its adoption aligns with global sustainability goals and contributes to long-term energy security. Among various biodiesel feedstocks, non-edible oils such as castor oil have gained considerable attention due to their high oil yield and resilience in harsh climatic conditions. Castor oil, derived from castor seeds, holds immense potential as a biodiesel source15,16. However, its high viscosity and moisture content make it unsuitable for direct use in diesel engines. Through transesterification, these limitations can be mitigated, making castor biodiesel a feasible and efficient alternative fuel. Additionally, oxygenated additives such as DEE have shown promise in enhancing combustion performance and reducing harmful emissions17.

Despite limited research on castor oil biodiesel, its potential as a sustainable fuel warrants further investigation, particularly regarding its performance when blended with petroleum diesel and supplemented with DEE and cerium oxide (CeO₂) nanoparticles18. Previous studies on biodiesel from feedstocks such as sunflower, canola, and soybean have highlighted its advantages as a renewable energy source19. However, the continued combustion of fossil fuels remains a major contributor to air pollution and global climate change20,21. While diesel engines inherently produce lower carbon monoxide (CO) and hydrocarbon (HC) emissions compared to spark-ignition engines, they emit significant quantities of fine particulate matter composed of carbonaceous residues, soluble organic fractions, sulfates, and trace metals22.

To address these environmental concerns and the growing need for cleaner energy, researchers have been exploring biodiesel and other alternative fuels. Castor oil, known for its excellent solubility in methanol, presents a cost-effective option for biodiesel production through transesterification. The rapid depletion of petroleum reserves and fluctuating oil prices further underscore the necessity of developing alternative fuel solutions. Incorporating metallic oxide nanoparticles such as aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃), cerium oxide (CeO₂), and copper oxide (CuO) into biodiesel has been shown to enhance combustion efficiency, increase power output, and improve engine torque due to their oxygen-releasing properties23,24. CeO₂, in particular, exhibits remarkable redox characteristics, enabling more complete combustion by generating oxygen that reacts with carbon, hydrocarbons, and CO in the exhaust24,25. When combined with nanoparticles, biodiesel blends demonstrate improved combustion efficiency, lower emissions, and enhanced overall performance, making them a compelling alternative to fossil fuels26.

In recent years, significant research has been conducted to enhance diesel engine efficiency and minimize emissions through the use of biodiesel blends and nanoparticle additives. CeO₂ nanoparticles, in particular, have been widely studied for their ability to promote complete fuel oxidation and reduce emissions27. Studies investigating the impact of CeO₂ nanoparticle size on diesel engines running on biodiesel blends have shown notable improvements in combustion efficiency, with significant reductions in CO and HC emissions28,29. However, a corresponding increase in nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions has also been observed, attributed to the higher combustion temperatures induced by the intensified oxidation process. Similarly, research on the addition of CeO₂ nanoparticles to biodiesel derived from waste cooking oil has demonstrated that a 50 ppm concentration in B20 fuel blends (20% biodiesel, 80% diesel) enhances brake thermal efficiency while significantly lowering CO and HC emissions30. However, a rise in NOx emissions was noted, likely due to the elevated in-cylinder temperatures resulting from CeO₂’s catalytic activity31.

This study distinguishes itself by specifically examining the effects of CeO₂ nanoparticles in castor oil biodiesel blends, an area that has received limited attention in previous research. Key research gaps include optimizing the concentration of CeO₂, understanding the synergistic effects between CeO₂ and DEE, and evaluating long-term engine durability when using nanoparticle-enriched biodiesel. Future studies should focus on refining nano-additive formulations, developing hybrid fuel strategies, utilizing computational modelling for combustion analysis, and assessing the prolonged impact of these additives on engine performance. Unlike previous research, which has primarily focused on biodiesel from conventional feedstocks or waste cooking oil, this study systematically evaluates the performance, emissions, and combustion behaviour of diesel engines running on castor oil biodiesel with CeO₂ nanoparticle additives. The experimental investigation includes assessments of pure diesel, castor biodiesel, and blends incorporating 2% DEE with and without 75 ppm CeO₂, aiming to reduce pollutant emissions while enhancing engine efficiency.

The novelty of this study lies in its comprehensive analysis of the effects of CeO₂ nanoparticles on castor biodiesel blends (B0, B5DEE2, B10DEE2, B15DEE2, and B25DEE2) in a compression ignition (CI) engine, making it the first of its kind in this domain. Additionally, it explores the synergistic interaction between CeO₂ and DEE to further enhance combustion efficiency and engine performance, a combination that has not been extensively investigated in prior research. Furthermore, this study provides an in-depth evaluation of critical performance parameters, including brake power, torque, brake-specific fuel consumption (BSFC), and emission characteristics across multiple fuel blends. Notably, the findings reveal that the inclusion of CeO₂ leads to a more significant increase in thermal efficiency compared to previously reported nanoparticle-assisted biodiesel formulations, reinforcing its potential as a superior alternative for clean and efficient fuel applications.

Extraction of oil and preparation of biodiesel

Castor seeds obtained from local farmers were used as feedstock for biodiesel production. Oil was extracted using both solvent extraction and hydraulic pressing. In the solvent method, crushed seeds were soaked with petrolium ethier for 48 h with periodic stirring, settled, and filtered to separate the oil. For oil extraction, a hydraulic press and solvent extraction was utilized, yielding 12.5 L of oil from 34 kg of seeds (367.6 mL/kg). The combined extraction efficiency of the solvent and hydraulic press methods yields 51% (v/w). The oil yield was expressed as a percentage of the sample’s dry mass weight32. In the transesterification stage, sodium hydroxide (25 g) was dissolved in one liter of methanol to form sodium methoxide, which was then reacted with the pretreated oil. The resulting methyl ester was washed 7 times with water to remove residual soap. Moisture was removed by heating the biodiesel in a furnace at 130 °C. Finally, the fuel properties were characterized according to ASTM D6571 standards to confirm compliance with biodiesel specifications.

Experimental program for the study and procedure

In this study, the castor beans used were obtained from a local market in Ethiopia, and the extraction was conducted at Adama Science and Technology University. The fuel blend ratios B0DEE0, B5DEE2, B10DEE2, B15DEE2, and B25DEE2 were selected to represent the low-to-moderate biodiesel blending range commonly examined in both research and industry, spanning from pure diesel (B0) to 25% biodiesel (B25DEE2), which is the upper limit recommended in ASTM D7467 for use without major engine modifications. This stepped range enables a systematic assessment of how gradual changes in key fuel properties such as oxygen content, viscosity, and lower heating value affect combustion behaviour, engine performance, and emissions.

Diesel fuel was blended with CeO2 NPs at a concentration of 75 ppm using an ultrasonic homogenizer to ensure uniform dispersion. The size of the nano additive CeO2 for the biodiesel–diesel mix was 25 nm, which was purchased from commercially available in Sigma Aldrich level with the density of 7.13 gm/ml with 100% purity level. The CeO₂ nanoparticle dosage of 75 ppm was chosen based on preliminary pilot tests using concentrations of 55, 65, 75, 90, and 100 ppm. Also, literatures are indicating that mid-range dosages (50–100 ppm) provide significant catalytic oxygen-storage and oxidation benefits, improving brake thermal efficiency and reducing CO, HC, and soot, while avoiding the stability issues, injector fouling, and diminishing returns reported at higher concentrations33.

DEE was incorporated at 2% by volume as a volatility enhancer and ignition improver to shorten ignition delay and promote finer fuel atomization. This level, selected based on prior studies, is sufficient to deliver measurable combustion and emission benefits without causing excessive reductions in heating value, undesirable shifts in cetane number, or safety risks associated with DEE’s low flash point34. The final selection of blend ratios, nanoparticle dosage, and DEE fraction represents a balance between industry applicability, scientific optimization, and operational safety, while also allowing investigation of the potential synergistic effects between CeO₂ and DEE in enhancing combustion efficiency and reducing harmful emissions35,36.

Gunt Model CT110 engine test stand equipped with a data logger, exhaust gas analyser, pressure gauge, and other instruments to assess engine performance and emissions. Emission parameters such as carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbon (HC), and nitrogen oxides (NOx) were analysed using a gas analyser37,38. Data were collected across multiple operating conditions, and statistical analysis, including 95% confidence interval (CI) estimation, was performed to evaluate the impact of CeO2 NPs on combustion characteristics. The cylinder pressure and apparent heat release rate for the tested diesel-biodiesel fuels were analyzed at engine speeds of 1800 rpm, 2400 rpm, and 3000 rpm under 80% constant load with and without the addition of CeO₂ nanoparticles. Result and discussion

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of CeO₂ nanoparticles

X-Ray Diffractometer XRD-7000 at 40 kV and 30 mA was used to identify crys- talline phases and to estimate the crystalline sizes. The XRD pattern of the synthesized CeO₂ nanoparticles, shown in Fig. 2, exhibits distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ = 28.5°, 32.8°, 47.29°, and 56.3°, corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) crystal planes, respectively. These diffraction peaks match well with the standard cubic fluorite structure of cerium oxide, confirming the formation of pure CeO₂ without detectable secondary phases. The sharpness and intensity of the peaks indicate a high degree of crystallinity. The most intense peak at 28.5° (intensity count of 193) corresponds to the (111) plane, which is typically the most thermodynamically stable facet for CeO₂.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) Analysis of CeO₂ Nanoparticles.

The relatively narrow peak widths suggest nanoscale crystallite size; applying the Scherrer equation to the most intense peak yields an estimated average crystallite size in the range of 20 nm. The observed diffraction profile, combined with high peak-to-background ratio, confirms that the CeO₂ nanoparticles possess a well-defined cubic fluorite phase with minimal amorphous content, which is advantageous for catalytic redox activity in combustion applications.

Fourier transform infrared test of biodiesel

The conversion of the parent oil to the methyl ester after the transesterification process was monitored by C = O ester stretches at a wave number (1740 cm− 1), a strong ester that confirms the formation of biodiesel The FTIR results confirmed the conversion of the raw oil into methyl esters, indicated by a strong ester carbonyl (C = O) stretching peak observed at a wavenumber of 1740 cm⁻¹. This prominent peak is characteristic of ester compounds, confirming successful biodiesel production39. A broad absorption band around 3428.38 cm⁻¹ indicates the presence of O–H stretching vibrations, which are characteristic of hydroxyl groups typically found in alcohols or phenols, or possibly N–H stretching from amines. The sharp peaks at 2923.74 cm⁻¹ and 2854.61 cm⁻¹ are associated with C–H stretching vibrations from aliphatic –CH₂– or –CH₃ groups, suggesting the presence of alkyl chains. A strong peak at 1740.38 cm⁻¹ is attributed to C = O stretching, indicating the presence of carbonyl-containing functional groups such as esters, aldehydes, or carboxylic acids. Additional bands in the region of 1459 cm⁻¹ and 1374 cm⁻¹ correspond to bending vibrations of methyl and methylene groups. The cluster of peaks between 1239 cm⁻¹ and 1032 cm⁻¹ is indicative of C–O stretching, commonly found in esters, ethers, or alcohols. Lastly, the peak at 722 cm⁻¹ is typical of CH₂ rocking, often observed in long-chain hydrocarbons as shown on Fig. 3. This result was consistence with the finding of other research work39.

Apparent heat release rate and pressure rise rate

In the study of combustion heat release rate and pressure rise rate are the two critical parameters40. At an engine speed of 1800 rpm, the graphs show how cylinder pressure (CP) and Apparent Heat Release Rate (AHRR) change with crank angle while comparing various castor biodiesel blend ratios with (Fig. 4a) and without (Fig. 4b) CeO₂ nanoparticles. For the baseline fuels at engine speed of 1800 rpm without additives, the peak cylinder pressures recorded were 65.51 bar at 377.3°CA, 67.9 bar at 375.4°CA, and 70 bar at 373.9°CA for B0, B5DEE2, and B10DEE2, respectively. The corresponding heat release rates were 17.1 J/CA at 369.3°CA, 17.14 J/CA at 372°CA, and 17.8 J/CA at 369.8°CA, as illustrated in Fig. 4a. Upon the addition of 2% DEE and 75 ppm of CeO₂ nanoparticles, notable changes were observed. The maximum cylinder pressures dropped to 57.12 bar at 372.4°CA, 58.3 bar at 370.6°CA, and 47.1 bar at 361.6°CA for B0, B5DEE2, and B10DEE2, respectively. Meanwhile, the peak heat release rates were measured at 17.6 J/CA at 373.4°CA, 21.7 J/CA at 365.2°CA, and 13.5 J/CA at 349.8°CA, as shown in Fig. 4b.

The CeO2 NPs increase the combustion by reducing the apparent heat release rate because of their high cetane number and cylinder pressure. Since there are more oxygen molecules in the biodiesel, the cylinder pressure and apparent heat release rate likewise increase when the blend ratio increases, according to the combustion study, which is consistent with the findings41.

When the engine operated at 3000 rpm without CeO₂, the peak cylinder pressures were observed at 61.5 bar at 367.1°CA for B0, 63.4 bar at 369.3°CA for B5D2, and 60.9 bar at 376.3°CA for B10DEE2. The corresponding maximum heat release rates were 17 J/CA at 367.1°CA, 20.9 J/CA at 365.5°CA, and 19.2 J/CA at 368.2°CA for B0, B5DEE2, and B10DEE2, respectively, as illustrated in Fig. 6a. With the inclusion of CeO₂ nanoparticles at 3000 rpm, the maximum cylinder pressures changed to 47.9 bar at 350.5°CA for B0, 62 bar at 373.2°CA for B5DEE2, and 49.7 bar at 374°CA for B10DEE2. The associated heat release rates were recorded as 17 J/CA at 343.8°CA for B0, 24 J/CA at 372°CA for B5DEE2, and 19.1 J/CA at 359.7°CA for B10DEE2, as depicted in Fig. 6b. These results highlight the impact of CeO₂ nanoparticles on combustion behaviour across different engine speeds and fuel blends.

Cylinder pressure and apparent heat release rate at 3000 rpm engine speed.

Higher cylinder pressure (CP) in internal combustion engines often indicates a more intensive combustion process, which can improve brake thermal efficiency (BTE) and overall engine performance. Faster energy release is also indicated by an increase in the apparent heat release rate (AHRR), which is frequently brought on by better premixed combustion since biodiesel is oxygen-rich42. Although these effects increase efficiency, they have drawbacks, such as the potential for greater nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions due to higher combustion temperatures. Positively, emissions of particulate matter (PM), unburned hydrocarbons (HC), and carbon monoxide (CO) can be decreased by cleaner and more thorough combustion43.

The nitrogen oxides (NOX) emissions have a mean value of 80 ppm (CI: 75–85 ppm), and the soot concentration is approximately 10.2 ppm (CI: 9.9–10.5 ppm). These values highlight the trade-off between engine efficiency and pollutant formation, emphasizing the need for optimization in fuel blends and operating conditions.

Main effects analysis of performance parameters

The main effects plot for brake torque (Nm) demonstrates how engine speed, fuel blends, and the presence of CeO₂ nanoparticles impact brake torque, as shown in Fig. 8. The engine speed has a significant influence, with torque decreasing from approximately 18.5 Nm at 1700 rpm to around 8 Nm at 3000 rpm, showing a sharp decline beyond 2400 rpm, indicating reduced efficiency at higher speeds. The fuel blends exhibit slight variations, with the torque ranging between 15.5 Nm and 16.2 Nm for different blend levels, showing a small dip around the 10% and 15% blend levels before stabilizing. The presence of CeO₂ nanoparticles (denoted as 1 in the plot) results in an increase in brake torque, with values rising from 14.5 Nm without CeO₂ (0 additives) to approximately 16.5 Nm with CeO₂ (1 additive), suggesting that the nanoparticles enhance combustion efficiency and engine performance.

Main effects plot for brake torque.

The main effects plot for brake power illustrates the impact of engine speed, fuel blends, and CeO₂ nanoparticles on power output, as shown in Fig. 9. Engine speed shows a significant influence, with brake power increasing steadily from about 2.5 kW at 1700 rpm to a peak of 4.5 kW at around 2600 rpm, followed by a sharp decline beyond 2800 rpm, dropping to 2.2 kW at 3000 rpm due to efficiency losses at high speeds. Fuel blends have a minor effect, with brake power fluctuating slightly around 3.5–3.8 kW across different blend levels. The addition of CeO₂ nanoparticles increases brake power from approximately 3.5 kW without CeO₂ to around 3.8 kW with CeO₂, indicating an improvement in combustion efficiency and power output.

The engine speed has a significant effect, with BSFC decreasing from around 250 g/kW h at 1700 rpm to its lowest point of approximately 200 g/kW h between 2200 and 2400 rpm, indicating optimal efficiency in this range. The presence of CeO₂ nanoparticles (1 additive) significantly reduces BSFC from about 275 g/kW h without CeO₂ (0 additives) to roughly 225 g/kW h with CeO₂ (1 additive), demonstrating improved combustion efficiency and reduced fuel consumption due to the catalytic effect of CeO₂.

The additive effect demonstrates a significant reduction in HC emissions, where using CeO₂ nanoparticles reduces emissions from approximately 53 ppm (without CeO₂) to about 38 ppm, highlighting its effectiveness in emission control.

Conclusion

The experimental results demonstrate that incorporating 75 ppm CeO₂ nanoparticles with 2% DEE into castor biodiesel–diesel blends significantly enhance diesel engine performance. Compared to operation without nanoparticles, brake torque increased from 14.5 Nm to 16.5 Nm, and brake power improved from 3.5 kW to 3.8 kW, indicating more efficient in-cylinder combustion. Brake-specific fuel consumption was reduced by approximately 18% (from 275 g/kWh to 225 g/kWh), translating to a brake thermal efficiency rise from 36% to 41%. The improvement was most pronounced in the mid-speed range (2200–2500 rpm), where optimal fuel–air mixing and CeO₂’s catalytic oxidation delivered peak efficiencies. These findings confirm that nano-additive assisted biodiesel blends can effectively address the typical viscosity-related efficiency penalties of castor biodiesel while maintaining stable combustion over a broad operating range.

From an emissions standpoint, CeO₂ nanoparticles markedly reduced incomplete-combustion by-products, with CO levels decreasing from 0.40% V to 0.425% V and HC emissions dropping from 53 ppm to 38 ppm equating to a reduction of nearly 28%. Soot emissions also declined from 10.5 ppm to 9 ppm, confirming improved particulate oxidation. However, NOx emissions showed negligible overall change in mean values (80 ppm) but exhibited slight increases at low blend ratios and higher speeds, attributable to elevated peak temperatures from more complete combustion. This trade-off underscores the need for optimized injection timing or EGR strategies when deploying CeO₂-enriched biodiesel. Overall, the study verifies that the synergistic use of CeO₂ nanoparticles and DEE in castor biodiesel blends delivers tangible efficiency gains and substantial reductions in CO, HC, and soot, supporting their viability as cleaner, high-performance fuels for compression ignition engines.
