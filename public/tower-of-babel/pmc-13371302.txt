IL-1β-mediated interaction between Müller cells and microglia through CXCL1/5-CXCR2 aggravates visual dysfunction in experimental glaucoma
Authors: Xiaoyu Xin, Luying Han, Yuntao Qu, Lujia Zhang, Xiaoli Zhang, Jiarui Li, Bo Lei, Yong-Chen Wang, Zhongfeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeGlaucoma is an optic neuropathy characterized by progressive death of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), ultimately leading to blindness. Increasing evidence demonstrates that interactions among retinal glial cells exacerbate retinal inflammatory responses, which is closely associated with RGC injury. However, the detailed mechanisms governing these glial cell interactions remain largely unknown. This study aims to investigate the possible roles and the potential mechanisms of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) in mediating the interaction between Müller cells and microglia in glaucoma.MethodsThe experimental glaucoma model of chronic ocular hypertension (COH) was established in adult male mice by injection of micro-magnetic beads into the anterior chamber. Western blotting, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction, immunofluorescence, transwell co-culture of glial cells, RNA sequence, swept-source optical coherence tomography-based imaging and flash visually evoked potentials were employed to investigate the underlying mechanisms about the interaction of Müller cells and microglia in retina after IL-1β stimulation, along with their impact on visual functions.ResultsWe showed that in COH retinas, IL-1β activated Müller cells and microglia, and promoted the recruitment of microglia to the ganglion cell layer. Mechanistic studies revealed that activated Müller cells released C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1/5 (CXCL1/5) through the NF-κB and p38 MAPK signaling pathways. These chemokines bond to CXC chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2), inducing microglial activation and migration. This activation led to a significant increase in the expression of pro-inflammatory factors, which contributed to RGC death and subsequent visual function decline. Importantly, inhibition of the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis substantially reversed RGC loss and improved visual function.ConclusionsIL-1β plays a positive feedback role in glial cell interactions, amplifying retinal inflammatory responses and impairing visual function. These findings demonstrate that inhibiting the interaction between Müller cells and microglia effectively protects RGCs, offering a promising strategic approach for glaucoma prevention and treatment.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03884-2.

Introduction

Glaucoma, the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, is an optic neuropathy characterized by progressive death of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) [1]. The number of individuals living with glaucoma globally is expected to reach 111.8 million by 2040 [2]. Lowering intraocular pressure (IOP) is still recognized as the only effective method to delay optic nerve damage, but the pathological process of glaucoma cannot be completely prevented [3, 4]. Numerous studies have been devoted to revealing the molecular basis of RGC death [5–7]. Currently, although the mechanisms underlying RGC death have not been completely elucidated due to the complexity of glaucoma, increasing evidence suggests that the neuroinflammation mediated by retinal glial cell interactions contribute to RGC damage [8, 9].

In glaucoma retinas, microglia—the resident immune cells of the retina—undergo significant activation, transforming morphologically from a ramified to an amoeboid phenotype while releasing a variety of cytotoxic factors, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-6 [10, 11]. These inflammatory cytokines induce retinal inflammatory responses and exacerbate RGC injury [12]. There is also evidence demonstrating that inflammatory signaling from microglia perturbs the homeostatic balance of other glial cells [9, 13]. Müller cells, a major type of glial cells in the retina, play an essential role in regulating and maintaining RGC function [14, 15]. Our previous study showed that Müller cells can be activated by inflammatory cytokines [16]. Activated Müller cells compromise their capacity to provide essential support to neurons and subsequently release additional inflammatory factors, C-X-C motif chemokines (CXC chemokines) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) [17, 18]. This positive feedback between Müller cells and microglia amplifies the retinal inflammatory response in pathological retinas. Previous study has shown that IL-1β aggravates inflammatory responses in retinal Müller cells [18]. Notably, the high concentration of IL-1β in glaucoma retinas is primarily contributed by activated microglia [19, 20]. However, it remains unknown whether IL-1β plays an important role in the interaction between microglia and Müller cells.

IL-1β can elicit pro-inflammatory actions following activation by binding to IL-1 receptor 1 (IL-1R1) [21]. In the glaucoma retina, activated microglia release IL-1β through the CASP8-HIF-1α-NLRP12/NLRP3/NLRC4-pyroptosis circuit [22], which promotes the death of RGCs by inhibiting mitochondrial function [23]. Previous studies have demonstrated that blocking of IL-1β function attenuates retinal neuronal damage in several disease models (e.g. hypertension-induced retinopathy model, optic nerve ligation model and diabetic retinopathy model) [24–26]. Furthermore, IL-1β can induce Müller glia proliferation [27], thereby releasing chemokines in age-related macular degenerative retinas [28]. These data suggest that IL-1β exerts neurotoxic effects not only through direct neuronal damage but also indirectly by modulating Müller cell functions. However, how IL-1β mediates retinal glial cell interactions in the glaucomatous retina has not been fully understood. In the present study, we showed that IL-1β promoted Müller cell activation in chronic ocular hypertension (COH) experimental glaucoma mice, which induced microglia activation and migration through releasing CXC chemokine ligand 1/5 (CXCL1/5) via p38 MAPK and transcription factor nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathways. IL-1β-mediated interactions between Müller cells and microglia enhanced retinal neuroinflammation, aggravating RGC loss and leading to visual impairment in glaucoma. Knocking out CXC chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2), the receptor for CXCL1/5, improved visual function in IL-1β-injected retinas. These data suggest that IL-1β-mediated interactions between Müller cells and microglia amplify retinal neuroinflammation and impair visual function.

Materials and methods

Animals and COH experimental glaucoma model

Adult male C57BL/6J and CXCR2 knockout (KO) mice (Strain NO. T037599) were respectively purchased from the Animal Research Center at Zhengzhou University and GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China), and housed under standard laboratory conditions (12 h light/12 h dark cycle, 21℃) with ad libitum access to food and water. The experimental protocols were conducted according to the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research, and received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (approval Number: 2024-212-01).

Injecting magnetic microbeads to establish COH experimental glaucomatous models was performed as described previously [29, 30]. In brief, the mice were anesthetized with an injection of sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection, then treated with 0.5% proparacaine to anesthetize the cornea topically and 1% tropicamide to dilate pupils. A 4-µl suspension of Micro-magnetic beads (BioMag® Su-perparamagnetic Iron Oxide, Bangs Laboratories, Fishers, IN, USA) was slowly injected into the anterior chamber of the right eye under ophthalmic surgical microscopy (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) to avoid lens injury. After the injection, a ring magnet was used to relocate the microbeads towards the iridocorneal angle. Sham-operated eyes of other mice were injected with an equivalent volume of normal saline. IOP was measured with a handheld digital rebound tonometer (TonoLab, Icare, Finland) between 8:00 and 9:00 a.m. to minimize the circadian rhythm deviation. IOPs were measured before the injection (G0d) and for the following 4 weeks (G1d, G4d, G1w to G4w). Mice in which the IOP elevation being > 5 mmHg in the operated eyes compared to the control eyes were regarded as successful COH models.

Intravitreal injections

Operation method was performed as reported previously [31]. After anesthesia with tribromoethanol, mice received a 2-µl intravitreal injection of IL-1β (10 ng/mL, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), IL-1 receptor antagonist ( IL-1Ra; 5 ng/µl, R&D Systems), anti-CXCL1 antibody (1 mg/mL, R&D Systems), anti-CXCL5 antibody (1 mg/mL, R&D Systems), or CXCR2 antagonist SB225002 (100 µM, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) into the right eye. The same volume of control normal saline, IgG2A or IgG2B was intravitreal injection in the control group. COH model mice should be performed intravitreal injection one day before the operation.

Quantitative real-time PCR

TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and RNA Extraction Kit (9767, Takara, Kyoto, Japan) were used to isolate total RNA from fresh frozen retinas and primary cultured cells obtained from each group according to the manufacturer’s instructions, respectively. cDNA was synthesized with PrimeScript RT Master Mix (DRR047A, Takara). Real-time qPCR was performed in a 20-µl system using the PowerUpTM SYBRTM Green Master Mix (A25742, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and LightCycler® 96 instrument (Roche, Indianapolis, IN, USA)/7500 Fast Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The primer sequences used for real-time PCR are enumerated in Table S1. PCR conditions used were 95 °C, 2 min; followed by 95 °C, 15 s; 60 °C, 1 min for 40 cycles. The thermal cycling conditions were 95 °C for 1 min, 40 cycles of 20 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 58 °C, and 40 s at 72 °C. GAPDH was employed as the internal control, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was employed for the calculation of relative expression levels according to the protocols previously reported [32].

Western blot

Total protein lysates from retina tissues and primary cultured cells were quantified with a BCA kit (ZJ102, Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China). The protein samples were separated on 10% or 12.5% SDS-PAGE gels in accordance with the protein molecular weight, and then electroblotted onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Subsequently, the membranes were blocked for 90 min with 5% non-fat dry milk at room temperature, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-IL-1β (P420B, 1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific), anti- glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (G3893, 1:1000, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), anti-translocator protein (TSPO) (ab109497, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-CXCL1 (AF5403, 1:100, Affinity, Suzhou, China), anti-CXCL5 (orb13450, 1:200, Biorbyt, Cambridge, UK), anti-p65 (8242, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-pp65 (3033, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-p38 (9212, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-pp38 (4511, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-GAPDH (2118, 1:10000, Cell Signaling Technology), β-actin (A5316, 1:10000, Sigma-Aldrich). The following day, the blots were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody (31460, 1:10000, Thermo Fisher Scientific ;31430, 1:10000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 60 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the signals were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (SQ201, EpiZyme, Shanghai, China). Target proteins were quantified by Image J software after normalizing to β-actin or GAPDH.

Immunohistochemical staining

The retinas of the mice were fixed in 4% Paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 2 h, immersed into 10% and 20% sucrose every 2 h successively, and then cryopreserved in 30% sucrose solution overnight before tissue were embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature compound for sectioning (thickness = 14 μm) using a freezing microtome (Leica, Nussloch, Germany). The whole flat-mounted retinas and primary cultured cells grown on round coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA for 2 h or 30 min. For immunostaining, tissue slices, retinas and cells were incubated for 2 h in a blocking solution and then overnight with the following primary antibodies at 4 °C: Goat anti-Iba1 (ab5076, 1:300, Abcam), mouse anti-glutamine synthase (GS) (MAB302, 1:2000, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), rabbit anti-CXCL1 (12335-1-AP, 1:100, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and rabbit anti-CXCL5 (orb13450, 1:200, Biorbyt), mouse anti-IL-1β (12242, 1:50, Cell Signaling Technology). Immunoreactive proteins were visualized by incubating with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (705-585-003/711-585-152, 1:500, Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs, West Grove, PA, USA) or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (705-545-147/715-545-150, 1:500, Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 2 h at room temperature. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min. Photomicrographs were obtained by a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM900, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Primary retinal microglia and Müller cell cultures

Primary retinal microglia and Müller cell cultures were isolated from 5-day-old C57BL/6J mice as previously described [5]. Briefly, isolated retinas were digested with 0.25% trypsin for 5 min. Retinal cell suspensions were cultured in the Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM/F12; 11330057, Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 16140071, Gibco) and 100 U/ml antibiotics, in an incubator consisting of 95% air and 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Microglia were isolated by mechanically tapping the culture flasks when mixed retinal cells were cultured for 12–14 days for subsequent tests. The remaining cells in the culture flasks, mostly Müller cells, were digested with 0.25% trypsin and cultured. The third-generation of Müller cells were used for experiments. To identify the purity of the primary microglia and Müller cells, Goat anti-Iba1 (ab5076, 1:300, Abcam) and mouse anti-GS (MAB302, 1:2000, Millipore) antibodies were used to immunofluorescence co-staining, respectively. The purity of both cultured microglia and Müller cells was over 90% [9].

Transwell co-culture and retinal microglial migration

To explore the interaction between primary microglia and Müller cells, co-culture and migration experiments were performed using the transwell inserts of 0.4 μm and 8 μm, respectively. In co-cultivation experiments, microglia and Müller cells were seeded onto 24-well plates and transwell inserts (0.4 μm) respectively. After 24 h of separate culture, Müller cells were transfected with the following siRNAs (OBiO Technology Corp., Ltd, Shanghai, China): 100 nM si-CXCL1, 100 nM si-CXCL5, 100 nM non-targeting siRNA (si-NC). The small-interfering RNAs were transfected into Müller cells using Lipofectamine™3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, Müller cells were treated with IL-1β (10 ng/mL, R&D Systems) for 6 h, and then co-cultured with microglia for 24 h. After the completion of co-cultivation, microglia were collected for subsequent experiments.

Assessment of microglia migration was similar to co-cultivation experiments, except that microglia were seeded onto transwell inserts (8 μm) and Müller cells were seeded onto 24-well plates. Microglia were allowed to migrate for 24 h, and then stained with 0.5% crystal violet (94448, Sigma, China). The cells in five random microscopic fields were selected for counting.

RNA sequence (RNA-seq) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from primary Müller cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and quantified by the NanoDrop ND-1000 (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE, USA). The RNA integrity was assessed by Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Poly (A) RNA is purified from total RNA using Dynabeads Oligo (dT) (25-61005, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and fragmented into small pieces using NEBNext® Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (E6150S, NEB, USA). Next, the cleaved RNA fragments were transcribed to double strand cDNA and labeled with dUTP (R0133, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The labeled DNAs were digested with UDG enzyme (m0280, NEB), and then PCR was performed to form a library of fragment size 300 bp ± 50 bp. Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by LC-Bio Technology CO., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). The differentially expressed mRNAs were selected with adjust P < 0.05 and fold change > 2 by R package edgeR. GO annotation and KEGG enrichment analyses were used to determine the roles of these differentially expressed genes.

RGC quantification in whole flat-mounted retina

The eyeballs were enucleated immediately after euthanasia of the mice. Subsequently, the retinas were peeled off, shaped into a four-leaf clover, and then fixed in 4% PFA for 2 h. PBS containing 5% normal donkey serum (SL050, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and 0.5% Triton X-100 was used to block flat retinas for 2 h, and then retinas were incubated overnight with a primary antibody against brain-specific homology cassette/POU structural domain protein 3 A (Brn3a) (ab81213, 1:100, Abcam). 594-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG was used as secondary antibody. RGC counting was performed 1 mm and 2 mm from the edge of the optic disc using a fluorescence microscope (Axio Observer 7, Carl Zeiss) in 8 distinct areas.

5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) injections and staining

The proliferation of microglia was measured by EdU staining following the manufacturer’s introduction with Click-iT™ EdU Imaging Kits (C10339, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mice received intraperitoneally injected of EdU (50 mg/kg) once a day for 7 consecutive days, and then eyecups were fixed in 4% PFA. Detection of EdU-labeled cells in retinal slices was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The slices were incubated with Iba1 anti-body (ab5076, 1:300, Abcam) to specifically identify microglia. EdU Images were obtained by a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM900, Carl Zeiss).

Swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) and swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA) measurements

Mean ganglion cell complex layer (GCCL) thickness, retinal vessel density and perfusion area were imaged using SS-OCT (VG200D SVision Imaging, Henan, China), which acquires 200,000 A-scans per second and provides a 1.8-µm depth axial and transverse resolutions in retina. The data from the right eyes of mice were collected. Posterior segment scanning mode was used to obtain data (a 3 mm × 3 mm radial scan centered on optic disk). GCCL thickness was measured from the inner edge of the retinal nerve fiber layer (NFL) to the outer edge of the inner plexiform layer (IPL). The vessel density and perfusion area of deep vascular complex (DVC) and inner retina were measured respectively using the SS-OCTA mode to evaluate retinal blood flow. Based on the optic disc-centered Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grid, the retina was divided into four parts, superior, temporal, inferior and nasal. Morphological and functional parameters of the retina were measured in four directional sectors between the inner and outer rings (inner ring, diameter of 1 mm; outer ring, diameter of 3 mm, Fig. 7E). The built-in SS-OCT software automatically calculated the average values of four regions. Images were excluded when the signal strength index was less than 8 (0 = poor, 10 = good).

Flash visually evoked potentials (fVEP)

fVEP were recorded from eyes of mice using an Espion Diagnosys System (Diagnosys LLC, Littleton, MA, USA). Mice were dark-adapted for 12 h before fVEP analysis. Then, the anesthetized mice were treated with 1% tropicamide to dilate pupils. 3% hypromellose solution was filled in the cup of each eye electrode to prevent corneal desiccation and to allow the contact between the cornea and the electrode. In addition to the two eye electrodes, three needle electrodes were required. The active VEP electrode and reference electrode were placed subcutaneously above the area of the visual cortex and in the snout, respectively. The final ground electrode was placed in the tail. The N1-wave (first negative peak) amplitude was measured and recorded by Espion V6.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5, GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data were presented as mean ± SEM or percentage. Statistical differences between two groups were determined using Student’s t-test. One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison tests or Tukey’s post-hoc test was used to compare three or more groups. Nonparametric analysis was conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test when parametric assumptions were violated. A P-value of 0.05 was chosen to denote statistical significance.

Results

Expression of IL-1β in relation to Müller cell/microglia activation in COH retinas

After injecting micro-magnetic beads into the anterior chamber, IOP in the operated eyes increased significantly from G4d to G4w, compared with un-operated eyes and sham-operated eyes (Fig. 1A), proving that the COH mouse model was stable. It was reported that IL-1β was expressed in retinas [33]. The dynamic changes in IL-1β expression in COH retinas were examined. Our results showed that the mRNA levels of IL-1β were upregulated from G4d to G1w (Fig. 1B), and the protein levels were upregulated during a period of G4d to G2w (Fig. 1C–E). To determine the cellular source of IL-1β in COH retinas, we performed co-immunostaining for IL-1β and the microglial marker Iba1 at G1w. Confocal imaging revealed substantial colocalization of IL-1β with Iba1-positive microglia in both the inner retina and outer plexiform layer (OPL) (Fig. S1). These findings establish microglia as the primary cellular source of IL-1β in the COH model. However, the expression of IL-1R1 showed no significant difference (Fig. S2).

Previous study showed that Müller cell and microglia were activated in COH retinas [9]. To gain deeper insights into the roles of IL-1β in glial cell interactions in glaucoma, we intravitreally injected IL-1β in normal mice. Following IL-1β administration, Western blotting revealed that the expression levels of GFAP and TSPO, biomarkers of Müller cell and microglia activation, respectively [9], were progressively increased from 1d after the injection (IL-1β 1d), with a peak on 1 week after the injection (IL-1β 1w) (Fig. 1F–I). To exclude the influence of the intravitreal injection procedure, we injected saline into the vitreous cavity of normal mice. Western blot results showed no significant differences in the expression of GFAP and TSPO (Fig. S3). Furthermore, intravitreal pre-injection of IL-1Ra, an IL-1R1 blocker, in the IL-1β-injected model and the COH model could reverse the increased expression of GFAP and TSPO (Fig. 1J–L).

The activation of microglia was accompanied by morphological changes. As shown in Fig. 2A and B, the activated microglia displayed larger somatic cells and fewer branches. Analysis of cytoskeleton of microglia showed that the numbers of branches and junctions and endpoints decreased significantly from IL-1β 1d to IL-1β 1w (Fig. 2D–F). Moreover, the numbers of microglia increased significantly during a period of IL-1β 4d to IL-1β 2w (Fig. 2C, H). In addition, microglia migrated from the IPL and OPL to the nerve fiber layer/ganglion cell layer (NFL/GCL) in IL-1β-injected retinas (Fig. 2G, J). Compared with the control retina, the number of microglia in the NFL/GCL increased progressively from IL-1β 4d, and peaked on IL-1β 1w (Fig. 2I, J). EdU staining also revealed an increase in the number of microglia in GCL after IL-1β injections (Fig. S4). The number of proliferating microglia was substantially lower than the total increase in microglial numbers in the NFL/GCL, indicating that the accumulated microglia in this region primarily originated from migration rather than local proliferation.

IL-1β causes chemokine CXCL1 and CXCL5 secretion from Müller cells via p38 MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways in retina

Since IL-1β induces retinal inflammation by stimulating Müller cells to release inflammatory factors [18], and the interaction between Müller cells and microglia can exacerbate RGC damage [9], we speculate that IL-1β activates Müller cells and induces the release of some factors to mediate microglial activation and migration. To test this possibility, we purified and cultured primary retinal Müller cells and microglia for in vitro experiments (Fig. S5). We first performed transcriptome analysis on Müller cells treated with IL-1β (10 ng/ml) using RNA-seq. The analysis revealed 709 genes significantly differentially expressed after IL-1β treatment (adjusted P < 0.05) compared with non-treated control Müller cells (Fig. 3A). Among the highest upregulated genes, we observed that many genes were related to inflammatory response including chemokines like CXCL1, CXCL5, and C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2); cytokines like IL6, leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF); and many other genes (Fig. S6). Interestingly, the receptors for both CXCL1 and CXCL5 are CXCR2. The KEGG enrichment analysis also showed that chemokine signaling pathway was significantly enriched (Fig. 3B). We then performed GSEA to verify that IL-1β treatment significantly increased the activation of chemokine signaling pathway (Fig. 3C). These results indicate that IL-1β could promote the release of CXCL1 and CXCL5 in Müller cells, thus involving in the regulation of microglia function. In the IL-1β-injected normal retinas, the protein levels of CXCL1 and CXCL5 were increased significantly (Fig. 3D–F). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that CXCL1 proteins were widely expressed in the retina, including the NFL, GCL, IPL, inner nuclear layer (INL), and OPL of the retina (Fig. 3G), while CXCL5 proteins were mainly expressed in the INL (Fig. 3I). Co-localization analysis showed that the mean immunofluorescence intensities of CXCL1 and CXCL5 were significantly increased in GS labeled Müller cells in retina of IL-1β 1w mice (Fig. 3H, J). qPCR analysis of other CXC chemokines revealed that, in COH retinas, the mRNA expression levels of CXCL6, CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 were increased at G7d, while CXCL7 was elevated at G4d (Fig. S7). Furthermore, CXCL14 mRNA expression was significantly downregulated from G4d to G2w (Fig. S7). In contrast, in IL-1β-injected retinas, no significant differences were observed in the mRNA expression levels of CXCL2, CXCL6, CXCL7, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, CXCL14, and CXCL16, although CXCL9 and CXCL10 showed an increasing trend at G4d (Fig. S8). Additionally, pre-injection of IL-1Ra largely reduced the expression levels of CXCL1 and CXCL5 proteins in COH retinas and IL-1β-injected normal retinas (Fig. 3K–M). These results imply that Müller cells could increase the release of CXCL1 and CXCL5 under the stimulation of IL-1β.

Next, the mechanisms underlying the IL-1β-induced CXCL1 and CXCL5 release in primary cultured Müller cells were explored. As shown in Fig. 4A–C, the expression levels of CXCL1 started to increase at 3 h after IL-1β treatment, with a peak at 12 h, and CXCL5 expression was increased at 6 h and then kept higher levels thereafter. NF-κB transcription factor p65 (p65) is a transcription factor that regulates various cytokines in inflammation [34, 35]. p38 MAPK participates in the signal cascade and regulates the response of cells to cytokines [36]. Previous studies have shown that CXC chemokines can be regulated by p65 and p38 [37, 38]. Western blot analysis showed that the protein levels of GFAP was significantly increased in primary cultured Müller cells treated with IL-1β (Fig. 4D, E). Simultaneously, the ratios of p-p65/p65 and p-p38/p38 were significantly elevated (Fig. 4D–G). Pre-incubation with NF-κB inhibitor SC75741 or p38 MAPK inhibitor SB203580 respectively for 3 h before 12 h IL-1β treatment largely reduced the expression levels of GFAP in Müller cells (Fig. 4H–I, L–M). Consistently, the protein levels of CXCL1 and CXCL5 were significantly reduced (Fig. 4J, K, N, O). These results suggest that p38 MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways mediate the IL-1β-induced CXCL1 and CXCL5 release from Müller cells.

IL-1β-induced interactions between Müller cells and microglia aggravate microglial neuroinflammation

To investigate whether CXCL1/CXCL5 mediates IL-1β-induced functional alterations in microglia, transwell co-culture system was employed. Western blot results showed IL-1β treatment of primary cultured microglia did not change TSPO expression (Fig. S9A, B). We further found that IL-1β treatment did not induce significant morphological changes in these cells, which is consistent with the TSPO results (Fig. S9C). These results suggested that IL-1β may have no direct effect on primary cultured retinal microglia activation. We used si-CXCL1/5 to suppress the expression of CXCL1/5 in primary Müller cells. Transfection with 100 nM si-CXCL1/5 for 24 h significantly reduced the protein levels of CXCL1/5 (Fig. S10). Primary Müller cells were pretreated with IL-1β for 6 h after transfection with 100 nM si-NC/si-CXCL1/5, and subsequently co-cultured with microglia for 24 h. Western blot analysis revealed that microglia co-cultured with IL-1β-pretreated Müller cells that were transfected with si-NC exhibited significant elevation of TSPO levels, while no significant difference was observed as compared to the group co-cultured with Müller cells treated with IL-1β alone (Fig. 5A–C). Knockdown of CXCL1/5 in Müller cells resulted in a significant reduction in TSPO expression levels (Fig. 5A–C). Furthermore, migration assays showed a significant increase in number of migratory microglia when co-cultured with IL-1β-pretreated Müller cells that were transfected with si-NC (Fig. 5D–F). Microglial migration was suppressed by knockdown of CXCL1/5 in Müller cells (Fig. 5D–F).

As is well established, activated microglia can release substantial amounts of pro-inflammatory factors, thereby exacerbating retinal inflammatory responses [9, 19]. We further quantified the expression of inflammatory factors in microglia in the co-culture system. As shown in Fig. 5G, after co-culture with IL-1β pre-incubated Müller cells transfected with si-NC for 6 h, the level of the anti-inflammatory factor arginase 1 (Arg-1) showed no significant change, whereas the pro-inflammatory factors iNOS and IL-1β were significantly upregulated. Knockdown of CXCL1/5 in Müller cells significantly reduced the expression of iNOS and IL-1β. IL-6 expression exhibited a similar tendency. Knockdown of CXCL5 significantly upregulated the microglial anti-inflammatory marker cluster of differentiation 206 (CD206), whereas CXCL1 knockdown showed no significant effect. Knockdown of either CXCL1 or CXCL5 did not significantly alter the expression of cluster of differentiation 86 (CD86), a marker for the pro-inflammatory phenotype in microglia (Fig. S11).

We further performed intravitreal injections of CXCL1/5-neutralizing antibodies (anti-CXCL1/5) to assess alterations in microglial migration in IL-1β-injected retinas. Immunofluorescence results demonstrated that inhibition of CXCL1/5 led to a significant reduction in both the number and percentage of microglia in the NFL/GCL (Fig. 5H–K). These findings demonstrate that IL-1β induces the release of CXCL1/5 from Müller cells, which subsequently activates microglia and promotes their recruitment to the NFL/GCL.

IL-1β-induced interactions between Müller cells and microglia exacerbate RGC death and visual dysfunction

RGC death and visual dysfunction in this study were evaluated through multiple complementary parameters. RGC survival was assessed by histological counts, while fVEP amplitude served as the primary electrophysiological readout of visual pathway integrity. Complementary measures, including OCT-based retinal thickness quantification and OCTA-based vascular perfusion analysis, provided a comprehensive profile of visual dysfunction.

RGC injury constitutes the primary cause of irreversible blindness in glaucoma. Our study employed Brn3a, an RGC marker, immunostaining to label RGCs in whole flat-mounted retinas, investigating whether changes in Müller cell-microglial crosstalk may contribute to RGC injury. As shown in Fig. 6A–F, intravitreal injection of IL-1β significantly decreased the number of labeled RGCs in both the central and the peripheral regions of retina. Anti-CXCL1 treatment markedly attenuated RGC damage in the central region. However, in the peripheral region, although a trend toward increased surviving RGCs was observed following anti-CXCL1 treatment, this improvement did not reach statistical significance compared to the IgG2A-injected control group. Additionally, anti-CXCL5 treatment significantly increased the number of surviving RGCs in both the central and the peripheral retinal regions. Moreover, IL-1Ra treatment significantly reversed RGC damage in COH retinas (Fig. S12), consistent with previous reports [23, 39, 40].

We then examined the effects of IL-1β-mediated Müller cell-microglial interactions on retinal structure and function. OCT images showed that the thickness of the GCCL was significantly reduced after IL-1β injection, which was reversed by anti-CXCL1 treatment. However, anti-CXCL5 treatment showed no significant protective effects (Fig. 6G–J).

RGC impairment demonstrates a strong correlation with diminished retinal blood flow [41, 42]. SS-OCTA was employed to quantitatively assess retinal vessel density and perfusion area in mice. Intravitreal injection of IL-1β significantly reduced vessel density in both the inner retina and the DVC (Fig. 7A, B, E; Fig. S13; Fig. S14). Both anti-CXCL1 and anti-CXCL5 treatments effectively restored these microvascular parameters (Fig. 7A, B, E; Fig. S13; Fig. S14). Similarly, perfusion areas in the inner retina and the DVC exhibited parallel changes, demonstrating consistent recovery following anti-CXCL1 and anti-CXCL5 treatments (Fig. 7A, B, E; Fig. S13; Fig. S14). We further employed fVEP analysis to assess alterations in visual function. Electrophysiological recordings revealed that intravitreal IL-1β administration significantly reduced the amplitude of the N1 wave. While anti-CXCL1 treatment effectively restored N1 amplitude, anti-CXCL5 intervention only showed a non-significant trend toward improvement (Fig. 7C–D, F-G).

CXCR2 deficiency ameliorates retinal function in IL-1β-injected mice

The biological functions of chemokines CXCL1 and CXCL5 are dependent on receptor binding, with both ligands sharing CXCR2 as their common receptor. To elucidate the role of the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis in exacerbating visual dysfunction through IL-1β-mediated Müller cell-microglial interactions, we performed intravitreal IL-1β injections in both wild-type (WT) and CXCR2 KO mice. At 1 w after IL-1β injection, significant microglial migration toward the NFL/GCL was observed in WT mice, whereas CXCR2 KO mice exhibited no detectable microglial redistribution (Fig. 8A, B). Moreover, the number of surviving RGCs in CXCR2 KO mice remained unaffected after IL-1β injection, consistently observed in both the central and peripheral retinal regions (Fig. 8C–E). CXCR2 KO mice exhibited significantly higher vessel density and perfusion area in the DVC compared to WT mice after IL-1β injection (Fig. 8F; Fig. S15). In the inner retina, although no significant alterations in vessel density or perfusion area were observed in CXCR2 KO mice, these parameters remained consistent with those in CXCR2 KO controls (Fig. 8F; Fig. S15). Flash VEP recordings similarly revealed that IL-1β injection did not induce a reduction in N1 wave amplitude in CXCR2 KO mice, although baseline N1 amplitudes in KO animals were lower than those in WT controls (Fig. 8G, H).

Blocking of CXCR2 ameliorates glial activation, microglial migration, and RGC loss in COH mice

To determine whether blocking of CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis could ameliorate visual dysfunction in COH mice, CXCR2 antagonist SB225002 was administered via intravitreal injection. To preclude procedure-induced fluctuations in intraocular pressure and account for the drug’s pharmacokinetic profile, retinal tissues were harvested at G2w. This design allowed us to assess the sustained therapeutic efficacy elicited by a single prophylactic administration.

Western blot analysis revealed that GFAP and TSPO expression was significantly upregulated in COH retinas at G2w (Fig. S16). Pre-intravitreal injection of SB225002 markedly attenuated the levels of both GFAP and TSPO, indicating that CXCR2 activation mediates the activation of Müller cells and microglia in COH retinas (Fig. S16). Immunostaining for Iba1 on retinal sections showed substantial microglial migration to the NFL/GCL at G2w, which was significantly attenuated by CXCR2 inhibition (Fig. S17). Furthermore, Brn3a-labeled RGC counts on whole flat-mounted retinas revealed significant RGC loss in both central and peripheral regions at G2w, whereas CXCR2 blockade conferred robust protection, with RGC densities markedly higher than those in the PBS-injected group (Fig. S18). These findings demonstrate that chronic ocular hypertension-induced CXCR2 activation mediates microglial activation, migration, and subsequent RGC damage.

Discussion

IL-1β mediates interaction between microglia and Müller cells

It is commonly known that IL-1β released from activated retinal glial cells (Müller cells, microglia and astrocytes) participates in RGC degeneration in glaucoma [1]. In primary open-angle glaucoma patients, the expression level of IL-1β in the aqueous humor is significantly elevated [43]. In acute glaucoma models, elevated IOP may induce the activation of the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-caspase-8 pathway, which further promotes the maturation and release of IL-1β via the NF-κB pathway [44]. In the retina, microglia are considered the primary initial source of IL-1β release under both physiological and pathological conditions. It is known that activated microglia contribute to RGC death in glaucoma [9, 45, 46]. Furthermore, the IL-1 receptor on Müller cells exhibits high affinity for IL-1β, which subsequently amplifies the inflammatory response [47], indicating that IL-1β may play an important role in the interaction between Müller cells and microglia. In this study, both qPCR and Western blot analysis showed that the upregulation of IL-1β was observed in retina of COH mice, which is consistent with the literature [19, 43, 48, 49]. Furthermore, we demonstrated the dynamic changes in IL-1β expression at various time points following model induction. Our co-immunostaining data further demonstrate that IL-1β is predominantly expressed by Iba1-positive microglia in COH retinas, with expression intensifying at G1w. However, the expression of IL-1R1 remained largely unchanged in COH retinas. It is possible that cell specificity accounts for this observation since IL-1R1 is expressed in Müller cells, astrocytes, and endothelial cells, while microglia, rods and cones lack detectable expression [47, 50].

In this work, we provided robust evidence demonstrating that IL-1β mediated interaction between microglia and Müller cells. Firstly, intravitreal injection of IL-1β induced activation of Müller cells and microglia. However, direct IL-1β treatment might not induce primary cultured retinal microglia activation. Notably, IL-1Ra partially suppressed glial cell activation in COH and IL-1β-injected retinas. Secondly, transwell co-culture experiments showed that IL-1β pre-treated Müller cells could induce microglia activation and migration. Additionally, migration of microglia toward NFL/GCL was observed in IL-1β-injected retinas. These results indicate that IL-1β may initially activate Müller cells, which in turn induces functional alterations in microglia.

A major finding in this study is that IL-1β-induced changes in microglia appeared to be mediated by Müller cells primarily through the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis. RNA-seq assays demonstrated that the expression of chemokine was upregulated in Müller cells responding to IL-1β stimulation. Among these chemokines, both CXCL1 and CXCL5 showed significant upregulation. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that inhibiting the release of CXCL1 and CXCL5 from Müller cells significantly attenuated IL-1β-induced microglial activation and migration.

Our comprehensive qPCR screening of CXC chemokines in both IL-1β injection and COH models strongly supports CXCL1 and CXCL5 as the principal mediators of IL-1β-induced Müller cell-microglial interaction. In the IL-1β model, the specific upregulation of CXCL1 and CXCL5, alongside the absence of significant changes in other chemokines, indicates that the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis constitutes the primary mechanism. In the COH model, the upregulation of CXCL6, CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 at G7d likely reflects pathological mechanisms distinct from acute IL-1β signaling, potentially involving chronic mechanical stress, ischemia, or other inflammatory cascades. These differences suggest that IL-1β-driven pathways and mechanical injury-induced pathways engage partially distinct chemokine networks. Moreover, given the complex microenvironment of glaucoma, we cannot exclude the possibility that IL-1β may also engage additional CXC chemokine pathways distinct from the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis to contribute to visual function impairment. Future studies examining the temporal dynamics and cellular sources of these additional chemokines may reveal complementary pathways that could be targeted in combination with CXCL1/5 for enhanced therapeutic efficacy in chronic glaucoma.

Both chemokines exhibit high affinity for CXCR2 [51]. Notably, despite prior documentation, the CXCL5-CXCR1 interaction exhibits substantially lower affinity [52, 53]. Furthermore, human and rabbit retinal microglia express only minimal levels of CXCR1 [54], suggesting that CXCL5 is unlikely to mediate significant microglial responses via this receptor. Our screening provides further evidence for the dominance of the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis. Indeed, several studies have demonstrated that CXCL1/CXCR2 was involved in the migration and recruitment of monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils in various inflammatory diseases [55, 56]. For example, in ocular hypertensive retinopathy, the CXCL1-CXCR2 axis mediated macrophage recruitment to the retina, thereby exacerbating retinal dysfunction [57]. In a diabetic retinopathy model, the upregulation of CXCL1 in Müller cells enhanced glial cell migration in a dose-dependent manner [58]. Additionally, CXCL5-sensitized hypoxic ischemia injury caused microglial activation and migration, neutrophil infiltration, blood-brain barrier damage, and white matter injury [59]. CXCL5, known for its ability to recruit and activate leukocytes, has been proposed as a potential therapeutic target in several inflammatory diseases [60]. It is noted that supplementary CXCL5 by intravitreal injection alleviated RGC death and promoted axonal regeneration by increasing the number of activated CD68+ cells in mice with retinal injury [61]. For this inconformity, we postulated that during the early phase of injury, CXCL5 may exert neuroprotective effects potentially through inducing the release of neurotrophic factors, which requires further investigation.

Previous studies have shown that nuclear NF-κB binds to its target sequence (κB sites) and promotes the transcription of numerous target genes, including CCL2, CXCL1 and CXCL5 [62, 63]. Furthermore, IL-1β primarily initiates sequential intracellular signaling cascades through two signaling pathways: NF-κB and MAPK pathways [64, 65]. In this study, we showed that IL-1β stimulation significantly enhanced the phosphorylation levels of NF-κB p65 and p38 MAPK in Müller cells. Using the specific inhibitors targeting NF-κB p65 and p38 MAPK significantly inhibited IL-1β-induced Müller cell activation and CXCL1/5 release. These findings demonstrate the involvement of both NF-κB p65 and p38 MAPK signaling pathways in IL-1β-induced CXCL1/5 secretion from Müller cells.

Microglial migration is a significant pathological feature observed in our experimental mice. In this study, we demonstrated that intravitreal injections of IL-1β in normal mice induced significant microglial recruitment to the NFL/GCL. Furthermore, the number of microglia detected in the NFL/GCL substantially exceeded the observed microglial proliferation signals, indicating that the increased microglial population in this region primarily originated from migration rather than local proliferation. Under pathological conditions, Müller cells secrete substantial amounts of cytokines that significantly influence disease progression [9, 66, 67]. In the light-damaged mouse model, CCL2 released by Müller cells promoted microglial migration by binding to CCR2 on microglia, ultimately leading to photoreceptor cell death [68]. Under stress conditions, ICAM secreted by Müller cells played a critical role in recruiting leukocytes to inflamed tissues [69, 70]. Our results demonstrate that both IL-1Ra and SB225002 significantly suppress microglial and Müller cell activation in COH retinas. Moreover, CXCR2 blockade markedly inhibits microglial migration toward the NFL/GCL. Collectively, these findings confirm that IL-1β-induced microglial activation and migration through the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 pathway in COH mice.

Our observation that IL-1β might not directly activate primary cultured retinal microglia, yet induces robust activation in vivo, reflects the distinct receptor repertoires of these cells in different contexts. Single-cell sequencing studies indicate that mouse retinal microglia express minimal or undetectable IL-1R1 under basal conditions [50]. Microglia in the central nervous system broadly show very low IL-1R1 expression, which can be upregulated by repeated lipopolysaccharide stimulation in vitro or specific pathological challenges [71]. Additionally, microglia constitutively express IL-1R2, a decoy receptor that binds IL-1β without triggering pro-inflammatory signaling [71], which may be an additional mechanism that IL-1β appears ineffective on isolated microglia. In our model, IL-1β appears to activate microglia primarily indirectly via IL-1R1 signaling in Müller cells, which release CXCL1/5 to engage CXCR2 on microglia, with limited contribution from direct IL-1R1 engagement on microglia under the present experimental conditions. Furthermore, Microglia as a major cellular source of IL-1β supports a feed-forward mechanism wherein microglia-derived IL-1β activates Müller cells to release CXCL1/5, which in turn stimulate microglial CXCR2 to further enhance microglial activation, migration, and pro-inflammatory factor release. This microglia-Müller cell cross-talk constitutes a self-amplifying circuit that propagates neuroinflammation, regardless of the initial trigger for IL-1β production.

IL-1β-mediated Müller cell-microglial interactions exacerbate visual dysfunction

The impact of IL-1β-mediated Müller cell-microglial interactions on visual function represents another major finding in this study. We demonstrated that IL-1β-mediated glial interactions promoted microglial release of pro-inflammatory factors, thereby exacerbating retinal inflammatory responses and ultimately leading to RGC injury. The neuroprotective effects of anti-CXCL1/5 treatment confirmed that blocking CXCL1/5 effectively rescued RGC injury, and CXCR2 blockade markedly prevents RGC loss in COH retinas. Furthermore, anti-CXCL1/5 administration significantly ameliorated IL-1β-induced structural damage in the GCL and vascular dysfunction. Finally, blockade of the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis effectively attenuated the IL-1β-induced reduction in fVEP wave amplitude. However, since the COH model requires injection of magnetic microspheres into the anterior chamber to elevate IOP, some of these black microspheres obstruct the pupil, severely limiting retinal light exposure and precluding reliable measurement of light-evoked responses required for visual function tests (e.g., SS-OCTA or fVEP). Further technical refinement is needed to enable preliminary visual function assessment in the COH model, such as optimizing microbead dosage and distribution to avoid pupil obstruction, with minimally interfering with IOP elevation. However, full evaluation comparable to normal mice remains challenging despite our ongoing efforts. Collectively, despite the absence of visual function evaluations in COH mice, our findings demonstrate that the IL-1β-driven Müller cell-microglial positive feedback loop amplifies retinal neuroinflammation, which subsequently disrupts neurovascular function and compromises retinal structure, ultimately contributing to promote RGC death and visual dysfunction.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that IL-1β can directly induce RGC death. Notably, mice lacking IL-1R1 showed significantly attenuated neurodegeneration following hypoxic-ischemic injury [72]. Similarly, IL-1β knockout mice exhibited markedly reduced neuronal damage after central nervous system injury [73]. Following optic nerve crush, activation of the NLRP3-ASC inflammasome led to upregulated IL-1β expression, ultimately resulting in RGC loss [74]. Our study uniquely established a connection between neuroglial inflammation and retinal microcirculatory dysfunction, revealing a potential novel mechanism through which IL-1β contributes to RGC injury.

In glaucoma management, the reduction in vessel density and perfusion area holds critical clinical prognostic significance, serving as essential biomarkers for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and progression monitoring. Concurrently, the decrease in the thickness of GCCL represents a fundamental and highly consequential early structural indicator. Microvascular dysfunction demonstrates a strong correlation with GCCL thickness [75]. The structurally compact neural retina possesses a unique vascular network that delivers oxygen and nutrients to neurons for maintaining normal function [76]. As highly metabolically active cells, RGCs exhibit extreme sensitivity to hypoxia and nutrient insufficiency, where microcirculatory dysfunction significantly accelerates their death [41, 42, 77]. In the oxygen-induced retinopathy model, omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids reduced the area of the avascular zone by suppressing TNF-α expression [78]. Under chronic pathological conditions such as age-related macular degeneration, a significant release of inflammatory mediators—including vascular endothelial growth factor, matrix metalloproteinases, interleukins, and chemokines—occurs, collectively stimulating ocular neovascularization [76, 79, 80]. Our study demonstrated that amplified retinal neuroinflammation induced microcirculatory dysfunction, and IL-1β likely accelerated RGC death through both direct and indirect pathways. This mechanism provides a compelling explanation for the observed reduction in GCCL thickness. Moreover, both intravitreal administration of CXCL1/5-neutralizing antibodies and genetic deletion of CXCR2 promoted a restorative trend in retinal microcirculation, ultimately partial reversing RGC loss. There is increasing evidence that CXCL1 and CXCL5 serve as crucial molecular bridges linking inflammation with vascular function (including permeability and leukocyte migration) and vascular structure (particularly neovascularization). In diabetes, inhibition of CXCL5 promoted neovascularization, restored blood perfusion, and accelerated wound healing [81]. Studies have indicated that adenosine activated the CXCL1-CXCR2 axis to induce pulmonary vascular angiogenesis [82]. However, the mechanism by which the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis induces microcirculatory dysfunction in glaucoma requires further investigation.

Our fVEP recordings revealed that anti-CXCL1 treatment significantly enhanced N1 wave amplitude in IL-1β-injected retina. Furthermore, treatment with CXCL5-neutralizing antibodies or CXCR2 knockout, while not resulting in significant changes in N1 amplitude, effectively prevented its further decline compared to both IgG2B-injected controls and saline-treated CXCR2 knockout controls. It is noted that the baseline fVEP amplitude is lower in CXCR2-KO mice in our experiments. CXCR2 is constitutively expressed in the retina, particularly in RGCs, and contributes to neuronal survival through PI3K/Akt pathway activation [61, 83]. Pharmacological blockade of CXCR2 with SB225002 reduces RGC survival even without injury [61], consistent with our observation that genetic deletion of CXCR2 lowers baseline fVEP amplitude and RGC density (Brn3a staining). Thus, while CXCR2 serves physiological roles in retinal development and homeostasis, our findings establish that its inflammatory functions predominate and exacerbate injury in this pathological context.

Clinical prospects of targeting CXCR2 for RGC protection in glaucoma

Current therapeutic strategies for glaucoma primarily focus on lowering IOP, yet they fail to prevent or reverse RGC damage. Moreover, a subset of patients exhibits characteristic optic nerve degeneration despite maintaining normal IOP. Consequently, identifying neuroprotective targets represents a critical approach to delaying vision loss in glaucoma patients. In multiple pathological conditions, inhibition of the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis has been demonstrated to significantly delay disease progression. Specifically, in ocular hypertensive retinopathy, suppression of CXCL1-CXCR2 signaling effectively ameliorates visual dysfunction [57]. Astrocyte-specific overexpression of CXCL5 exacerbates chronic cerebral ischemia-induced motor dysfunction and cognitive decline, which can be reversed by CXCR2 inhibition [84]. Moreover, CXCR2 deficiency suppresses macrophage migration and activation through multiple signaling pathways, while also attenuating angiotensin II-induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and fibroblast differentiation [85]. The experimental results from our study also support the therapeutic potential of targeting CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis for RGC protection. While both CXCL1 and CXCL5 signal through CXCR2, our data reveal potentially distinct functional outcomes: anti-CXCL1 significantly improved GCCL thickness and fVEP amplitude, whereas anti-CXCL5 did not, despite both treatments reducing RGC loss. Conversely, anti-CXCL5 uniquely upregulated the anti-inflammatory marker CD206 in microglia. These differences suggest ligand-specific signaling properties rather than purely redundant roles. Several factors may contribute to these differential effects. First, ligand bias may result in differential activation of downstream pathways. For example, CXCL5 shows reduced potency in β-arrestin recruitment compared to CXCL8 [86], and similar functional differences may exist between CXCL1 and CXCL5. The divergent effects of si-CXCL1 and si-CXCL5 on CD206 expression support this possibility, suggesting that CXCL5 signaling preferentially restrains anti-inflammatory microglial polarization. Second, immunofluorescence results revealed that the retinal localization of CXCL1 and CXCL5 differed, which may account for their differential regulatory efficacy in microglial recruitment and consequently affect the protective effects on RGCs. Finally, CXCL1 and CXCL5 may act on distinct compartments of RGCs, leading to differences in retinal structural restoration. The functional divergence between CXCL1 and CXCL5 remains to be further explored in future studies. Clinically, these findings suggest that early intervention targeting the CXCL1-CXCR2 axis may be sufficient, whereas later-stage disease might require combined or CXCL5-selective blockade to achieve optimal therapeutic efficacy.

However, the mechanism by which the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis regulates microglial function remains to be elucidated. Future studies should employ retinal microglia-specific CXCR2 knockout mouse models, along with other relevant animal systems, to further validate the role of the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 signaling pathway in glaucoma pathogenesis.

Conclusions

Based on substantial experimental evidence, we have demonstrated that in experimental glaucoma, IL-1β mediates an interplay between Müller cells and microglia, establishing a positive feedback loop that amplifies retinal neuroinflammation. Specifically, IL-1β released from activated microglia represents one mechanism by which Müller cells are induced to secrete CXCL1/5, which in turn binds to CXCR2 on microglia, promoting their migration and inflammatory amplification, ultimately contributing to visual function impairment. Targeted inhibition of the CXCL1/5-CXCR2 axis may thus represent a promising therapeutic strategy to prevent RGC damage in glaucoma.
