Contraception, stigma and misinformation: insights from University students in Türki̇ye
Authors: Yaşar Suveren, Büşra Küçüktürkmen, Hamide Arslan Tarus, Zekiye Turan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aims to explore university students’ perceptions, attitudes, and knowledge regarding contraceptive methods within the sociocultural context of Türkiye. It specifically investigates how cultural norms, religious values, and social stigma influence young people’s access to, and understanding of, contraception.MethodsA qualitative design was employed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis as developed by Braun and Clarke. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 38 university students aged 18–25 from diverse academic backgrounds using purposive sampling. The interdisciplinary research team, comprising sociologists and health professionals, engaged in iterative coding, reflexive memoing, and theme development, emphasizing contextual interpretation and reflexivity.ResultsFour main and nine subthemes emerged: (1) individual attitudes, showing ambivalence between personal autonomy and moral taboos; (2) sociocultural barriers, where religious and cultural norms stigmatize contraceptive use, especially among unmarried individuals; (3) knowledge gaps, with participants demonstrating limited or inaccurate understanding of methods beyond condoms and emergency contraception; and (4) educational needs, highlighting strong support for early, comprehensive sex education and accessible counseling services. A notable contradiction was observed between participants’ relatively liberal views and the conservative societal climate in which they navigated.ConclusionsThis study underscores the persistence of stigma and misinformation surrounding contraception among Turkish youth, despite signs of generational value shifts. These findings emphasize the urgent need for culturally sensitive, inclusive, and scientifically grounded sexual health education. The results are relevant for public health policymakers and practitioners in Türkiye and other conservative societies, where young people’s reproductive autonomy is constrained by moral and cultural norms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25487-x.Keywords: Contraceptive methods, Reproductive health, Sexual health, University student

Background

Adolescence, a transitional phase from childhood to adulthood, encompasses the ages of 10–19 years; youth refer to individuals aged 15–24, and young people to those aged 10–24. During this period, individuals establish their identities, form more profound social connections, and navigate with increased autonomy [1]. This developmental stage frequently coincides with the exploration of sexual attitudes and behaviors, including an elevated propensity toward high-risk sexual practices such as unprotected intercourse and multiple sexual partners [2]. These behaviors increase the likelihood of unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) [3].

Contraceptive use among young individuals is critical for reducing these risks; however, this remains a challenge owing to numerous barriers. These include societal disapproval, lack of access, stigmatization, and insufficient knowledge of contraceptive methods [4]. In societies where conservative tendencies are dominant, such as Türkiye, these barriers are compounded by cultural norms and religious beliefs that can perpetuate myths and misconceptions about contraception, particularly among unmarried individuals [5]. According to the literature, the use of contraception is seen as taboo, especially for unmarried young people [6]; half of the students between the ages of 13–21 experienced stigma regarding the use of contraceptive methods, and male students were more exposed to this stigma [7]. Emergency contraception is often preferred because of its easy access among students who have difficulty accessing and using contraceptive methods due to perceived social stigma [8] and university students do not have sufficient knowledge about family planning, its methods, and where to obtain information on this subject [9].

In Türkiye, societal attitudes often frame contraception as morally or religiously objectionable, leading to stigmatization and limited access to reproductive health services. These factors disproportionately affect young women by restricting their sexual health rights and perpetuating health inequities [10]. Despite efforts to promote modern contraceptive methods, misconceptions and knowledge gaps persist, highlighting the need for targeted education programs.

Previous studies have largely focused on quantitative analyses of contraceptive use, overlooking the nuanced sociocultural factors influencing individual perceptions and behaviors. This qualitative study investigated young people’s perceptions of contraceptive use and the social norms that influence these views. Understanding these perceptions will address an important gap in the literature and provide data to increase young people’s awareness of contraception, improve sexual and reproductive health, inform health education programs, and shape public health policies targeting young people. This study examined university students’ perceptions, attitudes, and knowledge about contraceptive methods from a socio-cultural perspective, addressing their lack of knowledge and social stigma. This study also suggests ways to increase sexual health awareness in young people.

This study sought to answer the following questions:

Methods

Design and setting

This study investigated university students’ perceptions and attitudes towards contraceptive methods, aiming to understand their experiences within a social context. Qualitative research is suitable for examining individuals’ lives and the meaning of their experiences. This allows researchers to capture the complexity and context of human behavior [11, 12], enabling the evaluation of participants’ emotions, thoughts, and attitudes in a broader context.

This qualitative study examined university students’ attitudes and perceptions of birth control methods within a sociocultural context. Braun and Clarke’s Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) method was used for data analysis [13, 14]. This method involves an interpretive and reflective process in which the researcher engages dynamically with data. Participant statements were analyzed not only as individual opinions, but also as reflections of the sociocultural context in which they were produced.

In this study, it was assumed that the participants’ statements were shaped by social context, cultural values, and personal experiences. Therefore, narratives were treated as contextual constructions of meaning rather than absolute truths.

Research team and reflexivity

The research team was an interdisciplinary team comprising experts in sociology, nursing, and midwifery. This diversity allowed for multidimensional analysis of the data: sociologists focused on social norms and cultural contexts, whereas healthcare professionals (nursing and midwifery specialists) focused on clinical practice, educational opportunities, and access to methods.

The research team held regular meetings throughout the study to systematically assess the influence of bias and cultural positioning on the data analysis. Given that sexuality and contraception are shaped by religious and cultural norms in Türkiye, the role of these factors in both researchers’ interpretations and participants’ statements was considered. Therefore, both the raw data and the researchers’ interpretations of these data were considered critical elements in the analysis process.

The data coding process in this study was conducted through the joint participation of team members, and thematic structures were developed using the richness provided by these interdisciplinary perspectives. This interdisciplinary reflexivity aligns with Braun and Clarke’s [14] emphasis on researcher subjectivity as a resource rather than a bias, ensuring that the sociocultural contexts of both participants and researchers were critically engaged throughout the analysis [13, 14]. The Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) approach emphasizes interpretive dialogue and analytical rigor among researchers, rather than quantitative measures of reliability. This process incorporated perspectives from different disciplines, enabling multidimensional conceptualization of the data, and systematic discussion sessions were held to build a shared understanding.

This study was conducted at a state university in the Türkiye’s Marmara Region, encompassing diverse students from multiple faculties and disciplines. The university has 35,400 students across 13 faculties and four schools, with 5,866 students in six institutes.

Sample and participants

The sample included 38 volunteer students from diverse socio-demographic backgrounds and academic disciplines, aged 18–25, from a state university in Türkiye, selected through purposive sampling. University students were chosen because they were beginning to experience the influence of social norms on sexual health behaviors and their awareness of contraceptive methods. Moreover, young adults are a significant group facing social pressure to make individual health decisions. The research team approached students from various faculties, student clubs, and shared university spaces to inform them about the study and invite participation. No social media announcements, notice boards, or electronic advertisements were used for recruitment.

Purposive sampling was employed to include students with varied perspectives on contraceptive use to gather insightful data relevant to the research question [15]. Qualitative researchers often use nonprobability sampling [16]. The sample size was based on data saturation, achieved after 38 interviews when no new themes emerged, ensuring comprehensive and detailed data [17]. Participants varied in age, sex, and academic discipline, providing diverse perspectives on contraceptive use [15]. Data saturation was confirmed when no new themes appeared [17]. This was discussed after interview 32; interviews 33–38 introduced no new codes. Sociodemographic details are shown in Table 1, which includes 22 female (57.9%) and 16 male (42.1%) students, mostly from the Marmara Region, with family incomes matching their expenses. Data on the number of siblings were collected as part of general profiling (higher sibling count often correlates with lower income in Türkiye), but this variable, along with relationship status, number of children, and personal contraceptive use, was not analyzed, as it was not directly relevant to the research questions.

Data collection

The semi-structured interview form consisted of 11 open-ended questions focusing on the participants’ knowledge levels, individual attitudes, social and cultural perceptions, and educational expectations regarding contraceptive methods (Table 2). The questions covered key topics such as (1) general knowledge and awareness of contraceptive methods; (2) personal and societal views on contraceptive use; (3) experiences of social stigma associated with contraceptive use; (4) family and close circle perspectives on the topic; and (5) recommendations regarding the need for education and information in this area. The semi-structured interview form was developed by the research team, with contributions from three experts in obstetrics and gynecology, midwifery, and sociology. Suggestions were sought, and necessary adjustments were made to ensure the form’s content validity. During the development process, the clarity of language and sensitivity of the topics were considered. The semi-structured interview guide was reviewed and finalized with contributions from members of the research team: obstetrics and gynecology nursing (ZT and HAT), midwifery (BK), and sociologist (YS). All interviews were conducted by a midwifery expert (BK), a member of the research team with experience in qualitative interviews on sensitive topics related to women’s health and reproductive issues. A pilot test with three university students assessed the clarity and feasibility of the questions, leading to final adjustments for standardization. The pilot interviews were excluded from the final analysis. The interviews were conducted between October and November 2021. Each interview lasted for approximately 30–45 min.

Semi-structured interview questions

Each session began with an explanation of the study’s purpose, confidentiality, and procedures, followed by obtaining verbal consent for audiovisual recording. Conducted in private, the interviews encouraged participants to respond freely to in-depth perspectives. To protect participants’ identities, each individual was assigned a unique code (e.g., P-1, P-2) in place of their names. Anonymisation was extended to all transcriptions and interviews. Audio recordings were securely stored and access was restricted to the principal researcher.

Ethical considerations

Before obtaining any data, ethical approval was obtained from the relevant Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (Date: 05.11.2021, No: E-61923333-050.99.99-77669). The participants were informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, and confidentiality measures before providing verbal consent. They were assured that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without consequences.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-step Reflexive Thematic Analysis process [13, 14]. The process began with researchers immersing themselves in the transcripts through repeated reading, engaging reflexively with the data from the outset. Initial codes were generated to capture meaning-based patterns, informed by the researchers’ theoretical perspectives and disciplinary backgrounds. Themes were developed iteratively, not as fixed categories but as interpretative patterns co-constructed through ongoing dialogue among team members. Throughout, researcher subjectivity was treated as an analytic resource, with reflexive discussions and interdisciplinary perspectives enriching the conceptualisation of themes. The final themes, clearly defined and meaningfully named, are presented with illustrative participant quotes to convey both shared and divergent understandings within the dataset.

The team analyzed the data by repeatedly reading and discussing them. When developing themes, they sought common meanings across all responses, not just frequently mentioned ones. Throughout the study, they regularly reviewed how each researcher’s expertise and cultural perspective influenced their results.

The coding and theme generation process in the study was designed as a dynamic process involving researchers’ interpretations and creativity, rather than a mechanical one. Themes were assumed to emerge from the researcher’s interactive dialogue with data. This approach transformed the data analysis from a simple classification exercise to a more focused approach that focused on the researchers’ active participation and interpretive perspectives. All coding and theme development were conducted manually, without the use of qualitative data analysis software, consistent with RTA’s emphasis on close, reflexive engagement with the data. A summary table of main themes/subthemes, representative quotes, and numbers of contributing participants is presented (Supplementary Table S1). In line with reflexive thematic analysis, themes were developed for their patterned meanings rather than frequency. Counts in Supplementary Table S1 are provided solely as descriptive indicators of data contribution, not as measures of importance.

Results

Overview of themes

Using Braun and Clarke’s [13, 14] Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) within a contextualist framework, we identified four key themes and nine subthemes that illuminate university students’ perspectives on contraceptive use. These themes capture (1) individual views (personal beliefs, family influences, and social sharing), (2) sociocultural pressures (religious and cultural norms and broader social attitudes), (3) knowledge and awareness levels (understanding of methods and reasons for preference), and (4) education and information needs (recommendations for training and consultancy expectations) (Fig. 1). Through iterative coding, reflexive memoing, and team discussions, we examined how personal agency interacts with external sociocultural factors to shape contraceptive behavior. While the thematic map resonates with the interview domains (individual views, sociocultural context, knowledge, and education), themes were constructed as interpretative patterns that cut across questions, rather than as topic summaries.

Main themes and sub-themes

Main theme 1: individual views on the use of birth control methods

This theme captures participants’ collective expressions of ambivalence between perceiving contraceptive use as a practical, personal choice and the conflicting social pressures and moral codes that hinder open discussion.

Sub-theme: general views

The participants viewed contraception as a personal, practical choice, rather than a moral issue. For instance, one participant stated, “I believe contraception is essential, but our society views it differently. It is still a taboo topic” (P-22, male).

Similarly, another participant emphasized respecting individual choices, saying:

“It’s really up to each person. I wouldn’t judge anyone for it - on the contrary, if someone knows about these things, it shows they’re aware and informed.” (P-13, female).

Echoing these views, one participant described how contraception should simply be considered a normal part of life:

“If it’s necessary, there’s no need to overthink it. Honestly, I think it should even be required. There’s nothing to be ashamed of. In our society, people can be a bit impulsive or harsh, so many are hesitant to talk about it. But really, it’s a completely normal thing.” (P-7, male).

Sub-theme: views on family members

Many participants expressed mixed reactions regarding contraceptive use within their families. One noted,

“It’s almost impossible to talk about birth control with my family. My mother always changes the subject, and my father finds it inappropriate to even talk about these topics before marriage. They act as if it’s wrong to even bring up the subject.” (P-21, female).

Many participants described feeling uncomfortable discussing contraception within their families due to strong social and cultural expectations. One male participant shared:

“In my family, I’ve never seen anyone talk about these things - it’s just not discussed. But when a couple gets married, everyone expects them to have kids right away. If they don’t, people start saying things to push them.” (P-12, male).

This social pressure extends beyond the family as well. As another female participant explained:

“In our society, doing something like this before marriage isn’t really accepted. If a single friend of mine says she’s using protection, people immediately assume all sorts of things. I don’t think they’d be understanding about it.” (P-18, female).

Sub-theme: personal ideas and social sharing

Participants were generally comfortable discussing contraception with peers but hesitant with family members. “I talk about these issues with friends but not with my family because they wouldn’t understand” shared one participant (P-10, male).

As one-woman student put it:

“Talking about these things doesn’t bother me. I might avoid it around relatives because they could feel uncomfortable, but with friends, it’s something we talk about - we share ideas and discuss them openly.” (P-24, female).

Echoing this sense of hesitation within family circles, another participant mentioned turning to other sources for information:

“For example, if I want to learn something, I don’t usually ask someone close to me. I just look it up online instead.” (P-12, male).

Main theme 2: social perceptions and the impact of religious-cultural norms

Participants cited societal and religious influences as significant barriers to discussing and accepting contraception, noting issues like judgment and stigmatization. They elaborated on the religious, cultural, and social attitudes towards extramarital sexual relations and contraceptive use.

Sub-theme: views on religious and cultural attitudes

Many participants believed that religious norms significantly influenced societal attitudes, often portraying contraceptive use as shameful or taboo. One representative statement reflecting participants’ views on religious and cultural attitudes is: “…talking about sexuality is seen as shameful in society. Therefore, people do not address this issue. If it is not discussed, there is no flow of information and this continues in a vicious circle. It causes wrong and illogical ideas to be strengthened and undesirable consequences to emerge” (P-25, female).

Many participants felt that religious and cultural beliefs strongly influenced negative attitudes toward contraception in society. One woman explained:

“I think religious values have a negative effect on how people view birth control, and because of that, we see more unplanned pregnancies. A lot of people say, ‘We’re Muslim, we can’t use protection,’ and end up facing unwanted pregnancies.” (P-23, female).

Another participant pointed out how these beliefs are especially strong in certain regions:

“Middle Eastern societies really struggle because of things like this. There’s this idea that using contraception means interfering with God’s will - like you’re going against what’s meant to happen. But our biology doesn’t work that way.” (P-21, female).

Building on this, another woman added that cultural habits make it harder for people to challenge these ideas:

“Our society is a little weak when it comes to education and social awareness, so people are more likely to believe things like that. They don’t really question or research; sometimes it’s just easier for them to ignore facts and hold on to old beliefs.” (P-26, female).

Sub-theme 2: views on social attitudes

Most participants viewed society as judgmental toward unmarried contraceptive users, particularly women, leading them to avoid public purchases. Nearly all believed that Turkish society is generally oppressive, exclusionary, prejudiced, and intolerant regarding premarital sexual activity or contraceptive use, fear of stigmatization, judgment, and exclusion.

An example of such an expression is: “[Unmarried] men and women [in a physical relationship] are highly disapproved of. This may bring about direct stigmatization, thus making them the subject of gossip. However, it’s the opposite case in my circle of friends. My immediate family also reacts negatively to it” (P-31, male).

Many participants described strong social and cultural pressures in Turkish society that make using or even talking about contraception difficult, especially for unmarried individuals. One woman participant explained:

“In our society, doing something like this before marriage isn’t seen as acceptable. If a single friend of mine says she’s using protection, people immediately assume the worst. I really don’t think society would be understanding about it.” (P-18, female).

This sense of judgment and stigma was echoed by another participant, who shared how it affects even the simplest actions:

“The social and cultural pressure is really bad. For example, I feel too embarrassed to go buy condoms at the store. I hesitate, wondering what people will think, how they’ll judge me. I don’t think women can just go into a pharmacy and buy condoms or pills either. When we did it, we went all the way to a market or pharmacy in a neighborhood where no one would recognize us. But it shouldn’t be like that - it’s a normal thing, yet I still feel nervous because people label you, and no one wants that.” (P-22, male).

A similar feeling was expressed by another woman, who noted how religious and family expectations shape these attitudes:

“They wouldn’t see it in a positive light, especially because of religious beliefs. My family and the people around me, I don’t think they would ever be okay with anything related to contraception or sexuality before marriage.” (P-13, female).

Main theme 3: level of knowledge and awareness

Overall, participants demonstrated limited and sometimes inaccurate knowledge of contraceptive methods, alongside varying personal attitudes. Participants commonly recognized condoms and emergency contraception, yet overall knowledge and understanding of less common methods were limited, with frequent misnomers.

Sub-theme: information and opinions on different methods

While condoms were widely recognized, many participants displayed limited knowledge of other methods or misconceptions. Nearly all the participants were aware of the use of condoms or other forms of contraception. Participants frequently misunderstood or misnamed methods, such as using terms like “ovary tying” or “thermal bands,” which indicated significant gaps in their understanding. Nevertheless, while participants could list the names of the contraceptive methods, they were aware of most methods within a limited framework or in an incorrect form. For instance, almost all participants had heard of the “morning-after pill,” yet it was obvious that they viewed it as a regular contraceptive method. Moreover, some methods were described with inaccurate terminology, such as “cord tying,” “ovary tying,” “testicle tying,” “chip insertion,” and “thermal bands.” These misnomers have been preserved to reflect participants’ authentic expressions and knowledge gaps.

One participant’s narrative exemplifies this situation: “I know about chip implantation under the skin, the morning-after pill, intravaginal devices, and birth control pills” (P-30, male).

While most participants were familiar with basic contraceptive methods like condoms and pills, many showed gaps or confusion in their knowledge, especially about less common methods. One participant explained:

“I know about condoms, pills, morning-after pills, and surgical methods. I also heard something called ‘cord tying,’ but I’m probably not saying the name right.” (P-25, female).

Another participant described a method he’d only vaguely heard about:

“I know about condoms and there’s this finger-thick thing… women put it inside the vagina… I’ve heard of that.” (P-17, male).

Similarly, one male participant listed methods with some unclear terms:

“Condoms are everywhere. There are more methods for women, like female condoms, something called ‘ovary tying,’ and hormonal methods.” (P-12, male).

Another participant provided a longer list but still included some inaccurate names:

“The first thing that comes to mind is condoms. Then there are pills, what people call the morning-after pill, and there were also thermal bands used with hormones. For women, I know about diaphragms, female condoms, and IUDs - that’s about it.” (P-1, male).

Sub-theme: opinions on reasons for use and preference

Most participants logically concluded who used contraceptive methods and provided reasons such as avoiding early parenthood, financial instability, lack of readiness for child-rearing, a desire to delay or avoid having children, being young or single, having an active sex life, avoiding children out of wedlock, having temporary sexual partners, and protection from STDs. Examples of such statements are as follows:

“Contraceptive methods are used by those who want to be protected from contagious diseases, or have a disease but are [still sexually active], both married and unmarried couples who don’t want to have children, sex workers, and anyone who needs it” (P-25, female).

Another participant emphasized financial concerns, adding:

“You need to earn a lot of money. If you don’t make enough income for your children, you can’t take care of them. I think those who don’t want children can use [contraception]” (P-13, female).

Significant gender-based differences emerged in the study. Women more commonly cited facing stigma and societal criticism regarding contraception, particularly in public settings like buying contraceptive items. Men, though also conscious of social judgment, generally reported somewhat wider knowledge of specific contraceptive methods-often acquired through friends or media-yet both genders showed considerable misunderstandings.

Main theme 4: education and information needs

Participants unanimously advocated comprehensive, early, and inclusive educational programs on contraception to correct misconceptions, especially among young people. They noted widespread misinformation and irrational beliefs about contraceptive methods in society, particularly among the youth. Some studies have suggested targeting less-educated populations, where misinformation is more prevalent. This theme encompasses participants’ expectations and recommendations for more comprehensive education and information activities about contraceptive methods, starting at an early age. In the context of Reflexive Thematic Analysis, this need is linked not only to increasing individual knowledge but also to transforming social norms.

Sub-theme: recommendations for the scope and timing of education

Participants highlighted the need for systematic and comprehensive education programs, particularly for younger age groups. They emphasized that education should begin much earlier, not just before marriage.

“In my opinion, those who are thinking of getting married must definitely receive a course. Educational courses should be provided not only on this matter but also on marriage in general. We see too many child brides and thoughtless people in our society. Nobody should get married without receiving family planning education” (P-23, female).

“I think there should be seminars on this topic in schools, without shame. Especially for young people, particularly at the high school level. We’ve seen 14-year-old girls coming into hospitals pregnant. That’s why I think there should be programs to raise awareness throughout society.” (P-6, female).

Sub-theme: information and consultancy expectations

Participants voiced the need not only for school-based education but also for accessible counseling services and widespread community information centers.

“This isn’t something that should be forced on people, but it’s something everyone needs to know. It shouldn’t be seen as shameful or forbidden. It would be good to have counseling centers, maybe in hospitals.” (P-7, male).

“I think it should begin before high school, even in elementary school, because sexuality is starting at younger ages. I’ve seen this happening in Turkey too. It could be taught from around 5th, 7th, or 8th grade, depending on when children start puberty. It should definitely be explained along with puberty education.” (P-15, female).

The narratives reveal significant gender differences. Women reported more frequent encounters with stigma and social disapproval in public settings (for example, when purchasing birth control pills), while men, while cautious in their judgments, exhibited a somewhat broader awareness of methods. This awareness appears to have been acquired primarily through peers or the media. Despite this, both groups held significant misconceptions.

Discussion

Findings and interpretation

This study highlights the complex interplay between personal beliefs, societal norms, and knowledge of contraceptive use among university students in Türkiye. Participants’ perceptions often deviated from traditional societal norms, reflecting a generational shift toward modern and secular values. According to the analyses of the participants’ general assessments in this study, it has been found that they are in absolute contradiction with the current traditional, religious, and cultural values ​​and norms in society on contraceptive methods. This finding corresponds with the findings of Idoko et al., who found that religion has no effect on the use of contraceptive methods [18]. Although participants predominantly perceived religious–cultural norms as shaping contraceptive attitudes, some studies with different samples (e.g., medical students in Nigeria) report limited associations with actual use; this suggests context- and sample-dependent dynamics. However, barriers such as stigma and misinformation remain significant challenges. In the study, the majority of participants indicated that they could freely talk about contraception methods with their close friends and peers, yet they hesitated and got shy to discuss this issue with their family and relatives. A qualitative study conducted in rural Sindh, Pakistan revealed that in the absence of spousal or in-law approval, young women tend to use contraceptive methods secretly and experience stigma as a significant barrier. This finding directly aligns with our participants’ hesitation to discuss contraception with family members and relatives [19]. White et al. pointed out that most participants defined it as “bizarre” to talk to their parents about sexuality and contraception, and many said that their parents remained uninformed that their children were sexually active before they became pregnant [20]. Therefore, our findings suggest that participants’ attitudes and behaviors regarding sexuality and reproductive health are shaped by generational prejudices and societal taboos, rooted in diverse cultural and social norms. These findings align with those of previous studies, suggesting that cultural and religious attitudes heavily influence contraceptive behaviors [10, 21]. Notably, the participants emphasized the importance of addressing myths and misconceptions, which aligns with global trends in sexual health education [22]. In our study, the participants stressed that society takes an oppressive, exclusionary, prejudiced, and intolerant stance towards sexual intercourse before marriage and the use of contraceptive methods by people with such affiliations. In the study by Rukundo et al., young people expressed that they were concerned about being stigmatized when accessing contraceptive methods, being verbally abused by adults, and reacting with negative attitudes from healthcare professionals [23]. Relatedly, qualitative work with pregnant adolescents in Uganda documents stigma and gatekeeping in reproductive health encounters, underscoring how moral judgments can spill over to youth SRH more broadly. In this study, the participants highlighted the urgent need to educate the public and provide counseling and awareness services about contraception. In a similar study conducted in Pakistan, both female and male adolescents emphasized the need for adolescent-friendly health facilities where they could openly and confidently discuss sexual and reproductive health, as well as the lack of such supportive spaces within the broader community [19]. The negative attitudes of society in terms of accessing and using contraceptive methods by youth will eventually adversely affect their sexual and reproductive health. Such attitudes may arise from traditionalism, long-established norms, and societal values.

Results in the context of what is known

The findings of this study corroborate existing literature on sociocultural barriers to contraceptive use. For instance, the observed stigma associated with unmarried individuals’ use of contraception mirrors findings from other conservative contexts, such as Kenya and Nigeria [7, 24]. A study conducted in a rural region of the United States highlighted that the prevailing conservative culture negatively influences sexual health education in schools, reinforces stigma related to sexuality, and consequently hinders access to necessary contraceptive methods [25]. A study conducted in Lebanon emphasized the significant role of gender in these matters, noting that women were less likely to undergo screening for sexually transmitted infections. It also highlighted that, due to the prevailing conservative culture, sexually active women often face societal stigma [26]. This study also highlights the relationship between lack of knowledge or understanding of contraceptive methods and lack of sexual experience. The negative correlation between the participants’ positive and open attitudes toward contraceptive methods and their knowledge and awareness levels is noteworthy. In a similar study conducted among university students in Lebanon, it was found that female students used oral contraceptives to prevent unintended pregnancies, but were less likely than male students to undergo screening for sexually transmitted infections [26]. Additionally, the limited awareness of contraceptive methods among participants highlights the persistent gaps in reproductive health education, consistent with studies from both Türkiye and other developing nations [27].

Clinical implications

The findings of this study significantly impact nursing practice and public health policies, especially sexual and reproductive health education. The participants disagreed with common beliefs about contraceptive use, and found that premarital sex and contraceptive use by their children were acceptable under certain conditions. They felt comfortable discussing contraception with friends and peers and believed that premarital sex led to societal stigmatization. Most students made logical assessments regarding contraceptive use and recognized condoms as a contraceptive method.

Accordingly, further qualitative and quantitative studies in different countries or with different sample groups will be useful for analyzing the knowledge and opinions of young people regarding contraceptive use. In addition, the participants of the present study suggested that educational programs, seminars, and conferences should be organized to increase the knowledge levels of young people and society in general regarding contraceptive methods, and rectify incorrect thoughts, beliefs, traditions, and perceptions. These findings highlight the importance of starting sexual health education at an early age and implementing antistigma campaigns for young people. Education programs should not only teach the correct use of contraceptive methods but also encourage questioning of social norms. This would contribute to the protection, maintenance, and improvement of sexual and reproductive health at both global and local levels. From a policy-making perspective, there is a need for multilevel strategies to enhance young people’s access to reliable information. These strategies may include social media campaigns, youth counseling centers, community-based educational seminars, and stigma-reduction events held on university campuses. Additionally, healthcare professionals should be encouraged to use open, non-judgmental, and inclusive languages when discussing contraception among young individuals. Such comprehensive approaches will not only increase awareness of contraceptive methods at the individual level, but also promote the development of healthier and more equitable sexual health policies within society as a whole.

Research implications

This study highlights several avenues for future research in this field. Mixed-methods studies can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing contraceptive use. In addition, longitudinal studies examining the long-term impact of educational interventions on contraceptive behaviors are valuable. Comparative studies across different regions in Türkiye have also shed light on regional variations in attitudes and behaviors.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its qualitative approach, which provides in-depth insights into participants’ perspectives. However, the findings are limited by sample size and regional focus, as the study was conducted at a single state university in Turkey. This may restrict the generalizability of the results. The topic addressed in this study is highly sensitive and often stigmatized within the Turkish context. Consequently, social desirability bias may have influenced the participants’ responses. Despite measures taken to ensure confidentiality, some students might have felt compelled to conceal their lack of knowledge or stigmatizing beliefs or hesitated to share personal experiences. Additionally, some socio-demographic variables -such as relationship status, number of children, and personal contraceptive use- were not collected as part of the data collection form. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings, as it may restrict the scope of demographic comparisons. Future research should address these limitations by including larger and more diverse samples and by employing mixed methods.

Conclusion

This study’s significant contribution is its examination of young individuals’ perceptions of contraceptive methods in Türkiye using a qualitative approach in contrast to the quantitative methods used in many studies [6]. This qualitative method allows for a deeper exploration of personal experiences and social perceptions. The findings are relevant not only to Türkiye, but also to other developing countries with similar sociocultural contexts, as evidenced by studies in Kenya and Nigeria showing similar socio-religious barriers to contraceptive use [7, 24]. Although conducted in Türkiye, this study enhances the understanding of contraceptive attitudes in developing countries, highlighting that barriers to social stigmatization and cultural norms are widespread. These insights could inform reproductive health initiatives in various global contexts. To raise awareness and promote understanding of this issue, comprehensive educational strategies on sexual health and contraceptive methods should be developed. The Ministry of Health, the Ministry of National Education, universities, and local governments play crucial roles in the design and implementation of these strategies. Scientifically accurate and accessible sexual health education programs should be integrated into school curricula, supplemented with seminars at universities and informative content disseminated to the public through media campaigns and social media platforms. Such initiatives will not only address knowledge gaps, but also reduce societal stigma, thereby encouraging more informed and widespread use of contraceptive methods.
