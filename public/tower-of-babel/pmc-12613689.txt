Predicting the critical micelle concentration of binary surfactant mixtures using machine learning
Authors: Aditya Choudhary, Saaketh Desai, Methun Kamruzzaman, Alexander Landera, Koushik Ghosh, Kunal Poorey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Surfactant mixtures play a critical role in industries such as drug delivery, cosmetics, firefighting foams, and lubrication, serving as foundational components of the global economy. Their performance hinges on micelle formation, a self-assembly process governed by the critical micelle concentration (CMC), which enables key functions like solubilization, emulsification, and targeted molecular delivery. However, rapidly and accurately predicting the CMC of mixtures remains a significant challenge due to the chemical diversity and nonlinear interactions between surfactants. Here, we introduce an artificial neural network (ANN)-based machine learning framework to predict the CMC of binary surfactant mixtures. Our workflow leverages cheminformatics-derived molecular descriptors for each surfactant component, which are then aggregated using strategies such as concatenation, arithmetic mean, and harmonic mean. We find that pairing the arithmetic mean strategy with ANN yields the best performance, effectively capturing complex molecular interactions and enabling dual predictive capabilities: (1) precise interpolation of CMC values at untested mole fractions within known mixtures, and (2) accurate prediction of complete CMC–composition profiles for entirely novel surfactant combinations. SHAP-based interpretability analysis highlights that features such as hydrophobic surface area, electronic topological descriptors, and headgroup basicity drive model predictions, aligning with core principles of surfactant chemistry and reinforcing the mechanistic validity of our model. Overall, this framework accelerates data-driven surfactant design by reducing experimental burden and enabling rapid, rational optimization of formulations across pharmaceuticals, personal care, environmental remediation, and enhanced oil recovery. Scientific contribution This study presents a novel machine learning framework that, for the first time, predicts full critical micelle concentration (CMC)–composition profiles for binary surfactant mixtures, including untrained systems. By strategically combining the features of individual components of mixtures using arithmetic mean, our artificial neural network model deciphers nonlinear interactions between chemically distinct surfactants, enabling accurate and generalizable CMC predictions. Introduction

Owing to their amphiphilic nature, surfactants spontaneously self-assemble into micelles above the critical micelle concentration (CMC) [1, 2]. The CMC is a fundamental physicochemical parameter that determines the effectiveness of surfactants as wetting agents, foaming agents, cleaning agents, flame retardants, emulsifiers, colloidal suspension stabilizers, and lubricants [3–6]. As a result, surfactants are indispensable across a diverse range of industrial and consumer applications, including pharmaceuticals, personal care products, firefighting foams, and enhanced oil recovery [7–10]. Despite their essential role, the rapid and accurate estimation of the CMC, which is a cornerstone for the rational design of surfactants with tailored performance, remains a significant challenge. While the CMCs of individual (single-component) surfactants are relatively well characterized [11] and increasingly accessible through high-throughput computational methods [12], predicting the CMC of surfactant mixtures continues to be a major hurdle in colloid and interface science. This difficulty arises from the non-ideal mixing behavior of surfactant combinations, which is governed by complex phenomena such as electrostatic screening, dynamic conformational rearrangements, and synergistic or antagonistic interactions among the headgroup and tailgroup moieties of the constituent surfactants [13, 14].

Such cooperative and competitive interactions within surfactant mixtures give rise to emergent interfacial activity, solubilization, and colloidal stability that are not derivable from the behavior of the individual surfactants. As a result, surfactant mixtures exhibit enhanced tunability and superior performance in industrial applications. For instance, Wang et al. demonstrated that mixing cationic and anionic surfactants reduces the CMC and oil–water interfacial tension, resulting in enhanced crude oil recovery, as compared to individual surfactants [15]. Similarly, Cagel et al. highlighted that in drug delivery systems, mixed surfactant micelles exhibit superior thermodynamic and kinetic stability compared to single-surfactant micelles, leading to improved drug retention and release profiles [16]. The efficacy of aqueous film-forming foams (AFFF) in fire suppression further illustrates the importance of rationally designed surfactant mixtures, where hydrocarbon and fluorocarbon surfactants simultaneously partition at the oil–water and air–water interfaces, respectively, to effectively suppress burning oil fires [17]. Collectively, these examples underscore the effectiveness of mixed surfactant systems, making them indispensable for pharmaceuticals, agriculture, personal care, chemical processing, and coatings industries [7–10].

Given the complex behavior of mixed surfactant systems and their essential role in practical formulations, the ability to accurately and readily estimate their CMC is important for guiding rational design. Unfortunately, the experimental determination of CMC via methods such as tensiometry [2], conductivity [18], and fluorescence spectrophotometry [19] is labor intensive, time-consuming, costly, and often limited by the availability of pure compounds and reproducible protocols. Computational approaches, including thermodynamic theories like self-consistent field theory [20] and molecular equations of state [21], offer rapid CMC estimations with minimal computation. However, their applicability is hindered by the calculation of complex thermodynamic bulk properties. Similarly, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provide detailed insights into surfactant behavior but are constrained by the need for highly accurate force fields and significant computational power to simulate large systems over extended timescales [22]. These constraints create an urgent need for reliable, high-throughput methodologies to screen surfactant mixtures with targeted CMC values.

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a transformative solution, offering unprecedented efficiency and scalability in rapidly predicting molecular properties. Within surfactant science, ML is gaining significant traction, with recent studies applying quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) models and advanced algorithms to predict the CMCs of single surfactants. For example, Mattei et al. developed a group-contribution (GC) model for predicting the CMC of nonionic surfactants, providing a useful framework for single-component systems [23]. Qin et al. employed graph convolutional neural networks (GCNs) on a dataset of 202 surfactants to predict the CMC value of single surfactants from their molecular structure, achieving high accuracy with an R2 of 0.92 and mean squared error (MSE) of 0.09 [12]. Similarly, Seddon et al. developed a hybrid model combining theoretical frameworks with gradient-boosted regressors to predict CMC, demonstrating improved generality, albeit on limited datasets [24]. Chen et al. classified surfactants by headgroup charges and identified light gradient boosting machine (LGBM) and gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) as the most effective models for ionic and non-ionic surfactants, respectively, achieving R2 values exceeding 0.94 and MSE of 0.06 [25]. Acknowledging the influence of temperature on CMC, Brozos et al. incorporated temperature effects into their model and utilized a graph neural networks (GNNs) on a dataset of 492 unique surfactants, achieving an impressive prediction accuracy with an R2 of 0.97 and MSE of 0.24 [26]. These advancements have largely focused on single surfactants, leaving a critical gap in predicting CMC for surfactant mixtures, a more realistic scenario for industrial applications. Addressing this gap requires moving beyond single-molecule frameworks and developing modeling strategies that can account for the multi-component nature of mixtures.

Extending these frameworks to molecular mixtures, however, presents unique challenges. Recent studies have shown that extending QSPR frameworks to molecular mixtures requires specialized treatment of multi-component information. For example, ML models that combine delumped molecular compositions with neural networks have significantly improved bulk property predictions of hydrocarbon mixtures compared to linear mixing rules [27]. The MolPool framework introduced permutation-invariant pooling of molecular representations, enabling accurate modeling of non-linear mixture behaviors such as cetane number and viscosity in renewable fuel blends [28]. Additionally, graph-based neural networks trained on curated experimental datasets have achieved state-of-the-art performance in predicting binary mixture viscosities as functions of temperature and composition, underscoring the importance of high-quality data curation [29]. Collectively, these studies highlight that QSPR modeling of mixtures demands representations beyond single-molecule descriptors, incorporating molecular complementarity, non-linear interactions, and composition-dependent effects. Yet, despite this progress, no ML models currently exist for predicting the CMC of binary surfactant mixtures. Bridging this gap is essential for enabling the data-driven formulation of high-performance surfactant mixtures, as existing models fall short in capturing the complex interplay of molecular packing, electrostatic interactions, and cooperative/competitive behaviors inherent to mixed systems.

To address this challenge, we introduce a ML framework specifically developed for predicting the CMC of binary surfactant systems. Our approach begins by expanding an existing single-surfactant dataset to strengthen chemical diversity and establish a strong foundation for enhanced baseline CMC predictive accuracy. Building on this foundation, we tackle the more challenging task of predicting CMCs in binary surfactant mixtures [30], a domain where curated data are scarce. To this end, we constructed a well-defined dataset of binary surfactant mixtures. For each mixture, we generated molecular descriptors for the individual surfactant components using either RDKit [31], Mordred [32], or PaDEL [33]. Furthermore, for each descriptor type, we rigorously evaluated multiple strategies for merging the features of the two components, ultimately identifying the most effective approach to capture the intricate interactions within surfactant mixtures. We then benchmarked the predictive performance of three machine learning models: random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and artificial neural networks (ANNs), on two key tasks: (i) interpolation of CMC values at untested mole fractions within known mixtures, and (ii) prediction of full CMC–composition profiles for novel surfactant pairs not seen during training. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study to address CMC prediction for binary surfactant systems using ML, providing a data-driven solution that bridges a critical gap in colloid and interface science. Our findings lay a robust foundation for the rational design and accelerated optimization of surfactant formulations across a broad spectrum of industrial and environmental applications.

Methodology

Data curation

We present a comprehensive dataset assembled to enable machine learning (ML) modeling of critical micelle concentration (CMC) in both single and binary surfactant systems. The single-surfactant dataset was constructed by augmenting the widely used collection curated by Qin et al. [25] with additional entries extracted from recent literature sources. For binary mixtures, CMC data were meticulously compiled from diverse peer-reviewed articles [34–59], ensuring a broad representation of surfactant chemistries, headgroup types, and chain architectures. Each entry in the dataset includes the molecular structures of the surfactants in SMILES format, the experimentally determined CMC (expressed as log CMC in μM), and the corresponding mole fractions (XA, XB) for binary systems. The complete datasets for both single and binary surfactant systems are openly available on our repository (https://github.com/snlai4chembiogroup/P2SURF).

Feature extraction

Accurate prediction of the CMC in surfactant mixtures hinges on the quality and diversity of molecular descriptors used to represent and encode chemical structure. To this end, we systematically evaluated three widely used cheminformatics packages RDKit [31], Mordred [32], and PaDEL [33], for their ability to generate features that enhance ML model performance. RDKit provides a robust set of 2D and 3D descriptors, including fingerprints and topological indices, which are widely used for molecular property prediction. However, its relatively modest feature space may not fully capture the complex structural nuances governing micellization. For instance, features such as ‘topological charge’ and ‘eccentric connectivity index’ may correlate with CMC, but are not computed by RDKit. Mordred significantly broadens this landscape, computing over 1800 descriptors that span constitutional, topological, geometrical, and electronic features. This high-dimensional representation is particularly suited for surfactants, where subtle electronic and spatial features critically influence CMC. PaDEL calculates the largest number of descriptors among the three tools, up to 1875, including nearly all Mordred descriptors along with several additional ones, notably a broader selection of molecular fingerprints. This extensive coverage increases the diversity of both molecular and fingerprint-based features, thereby enriching the feature space for model development.

Given the complex and non-linear relationship between surfactant structure and CMC, diversity in descriptor sets is essential. Our study rigorously compares descriptor sets offered by these packages in the context of surfactant CMC prediction, assessing how descriptor selection impacts model performance and interpretability. This approach underscores the importance of comprehensive, high-quality molecular representations for building predictive, generalizable ML models in surfactant science.

Feature engineering and aggregation

For binary surfactant mixtures, an essential challenge is encoding the interactions between two chemically distinct molecules in a manner that preserves both individual and collective properties relevant to micellization. To address this, we systematically evaluated three feature aggregation methods: (i) concatenation, (ii) arithmetic mean, and (iii) harmonic mean.

Feature selection

To ensure the efficiency and accuracy of the ML models, we eliminated redundant or irrelevant features using the BorutaPy package [60]. Originally introduced by Kursa et al., Boruta is a wrapper algorithm designed to identify all relevant features using tree-based estimators, such as random forest (RF) and extreme gradient boosting (xGBoost). The method introduces "shadow" features, which are randomly shuffled versions of the original descriptors, and compares their importance scores with those of the actual features during iterative model training. Features that consistently outperform their shadow counterparts are deemed relevant, while those that do not are rejected and removed from further consideration. This iterative process results in a refined set of molecular descriptors that are statistically significant for predicting log(CMC). The algorithm’s robustness lies in its parameter-free approach, making it superior to commonly used methods like recursive feature elimination and univariate feature selection.

In this study, we employed xGBoost as base estimators for BorutaPy to ensure optimal feature selection. The number of descriptors before and after feature selection for each aggregation scheme is provided in Table S1. Interestingly, although concatenation initially introduces a much larger number of descriptors, BorutaPy feature selection reduces them to a comparable number as obtained from arithmetic and harmonic mean aggregation. This outcome likely arises because concatenation introduces strong redundancy and multicollinearity, prompting BorutaPy to aggressively prune correlated descriptors, whereas arithmetic and harmonic mean strategies inherently compress correlated features into aggregated representations, enabling more features to survive selection.

Data splitting

For single surfactants, the dataset was randomly divided, allocating 80% of the data for model training and validation, while reserving the remaining 20% for independent testing. Specifically, the single-surfactant dataset consists of 440 unique surfactant SMILES, of which 352 were used for training/validation and 88 for testing. For binary surfactant mixtures, dataset consists of 979 unique surfactant–composition combinations. To rigorously assess the model’s capabilities for both interpolation and extrapolation, we implemented two distinct data splitting protocols. In the interpolation protocol, the model was trained on a subset of compositions within a given binary mixture series and evaluated on unmeasured compositions from the same series, thereby testing its ability to predict CMC values at intermediate mole fractions. In the extrapolation protocol, all data corresponding to a unique surfactant pair were withheld from training and used exclusively for testing. This stringent approach evaluates the model’s capacity to predict the entire CMC–composition profile for binary mixtures comprised of surfactant combinations not encountered during training. For both interpolation and extrapolation tasks, the training set comprised 852 data points, while the test set included 127 data points, representing 20% of the explicit binary surfactant mixture dataset.

To maximize the utility of available data and enhance model generalizability, single-surfactant data are also incorporated into the binary mixture dataset by representing each as a degenerate binary mixture—assigning identical SMILES strings to components A and B, with mole fractions XA = XB = 0.5. This choice ensures a symmetric representation and avoids introducing artificial asymmetry that could arise from concatenation-based feature construction, where the input matrix is explicitly formed as [FA, XA, FB, XB]. Notably, when descriptors are combined using arithmetic or harmonic mean operations, the exact choice of mole fractions has no effect since FA = FB for identical components. These single-surfactant entries are included in the training set for binary mixture modeling and excluded from the test set. This strategy provides the model with additional chemical diversity and structural information, enabling more robust learning in the context of limited binary mixture CMC data available in the literature.

Model development and evaluation

Three machine learning models: random forest (RF), xGBoost (gradient boosted decision tree model), and artificial neural networks (ANN), are assessed and compared based on a scalar figure of merit: the mean squared error (MSE) in predicting log CMC, on the test set. While some earlier studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of graph neural networks for predicting CMC of single surfactants [12], we focused on these simpler models due to their strong predictive performance, making the use of more complex structural models unnecessary for this task. For each of these models, we conducted hyperparameter optimization by using the validation set data, to ensure proper generalization and prevent overfitting. For the RF and xGBoost, we used a grid search strategy with five-fold cross-validation, whereas for ANN, we used a dedicated validation set (10% of training data). The optimized hyperparameters and model architecture are provided in Supporting Information (SI). We computed mean squared error (MSE) and coefficient of determination (R2) on the test sets to compare the model performance.

Results and discussion

CMC prediction of single surfactant system

We began our study by leveraging the existing CMC dataset of single-component surfactants curated by Qin et al. [12], supplementing it with additional surfactants extracted from recent literature. This resulted in an expanded dataset comprising 440 unique surfactants (see SI). To assess the distribution and coverage of the augmented dataset relative to the original, we conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 1a) on the entire (augmented) dataset. PCA revealed that our augmented dataset includes surfactants that populate regions of chemical space not represented in the Qin et al. dataset, thereby broadening the overall chemical diversity and potentially enhancing model generalizability. While the two-dimensional PCA projection simplifies the high-dimensional descriptor space, we found that a reduction to two dimensions captured 38% of the total variance. We then trained and evaluated three machine learning models: random forest (RF), XGBoost, and artificial neural network (ANN), on our augmented dataset using molecular descriptors. We benchmarked model performance using R2 and mean squared error (MSE) metrics (Figure S1). Among the tested models, the ANN model using RDKit-generated features outperformed the others, achieving an R2 of 0.907, an MSE of 0.137, and a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 7.81% on the test set. For comparison, a previous study employing a smaller dataset and a graph neural network (GNN) reported an R2 of 0.920 and an MSE of 0.090. [12]

To interpret the model’s predictions and assess the physical relevance of the selected features, we conducted SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [61] analysis (Fig. 3b). The SHAP summary plot reveals both the magnitude and directionality of each feature’s contribution to predicted log CMC values. Red points (high feature values) located to the right of the vertical axis indicate features that increase predicted CMC, while blue points (low feature values) on the left correspond to features that reduce CMC. The SHAP analysis (Figure S3) reveals that quaternary ammonium groups (fr_quatN) tend to increase the critical micelle concentration (CMC), likely due to their bulky structure introducing steric hindrance that impedes efficient packing of surfactant molecules into micelles. In contrast, electronic surface area descriptors such as VSA_Estate7 and PEOE_VSA6 are associated with lower CMC values, suggesting enhanced micelle stability through favorable electronic interactions. Additional contributors include imidazole fragments, which reduce CMC via π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding. These findings demonstrate that the model effectively captures key physicochemical principles, such as chemical structure and charge distribution, which govern surfactant aggregation.

To further evaluate the model’s generalizability, we assessed its performance across surfactant classes defined by headgroup charge (anionic, nonionic, and cationic). Across all three classes, the ANN model consistently demonstrated high fidelity, with the majority of predictions clustering closely around the parity line (Fig. 1b). Notably, our model performed exceptionally well for several chemically distinct compounds: for decyltrimethylammonium (C13H30N+), the predicted log CMC is 4.83, closely matching the experimental value of 4.72; for decanoate (C12H23O2−), the predicted value is 4.50 versus the true value of 4.39; and for xylitol (C14H28O6), the model predicted 3.67, nearly identical to the experimental value of 3.64. These compounds are utilized in a wide range of practical applications. Decyltrimethylammonium is a widely used cationic surfactant in disinfectant formulations, particularly effective against COVID-19 viruses [62]. Decanoate is an anionic surfactant employed to form hydrophobic ion pairs with hydrophilic drugs like tobramycin, enabling their incorporation into self-emulsifying drug delivery systems and improving oral bioavailability [63]. Xylitol, a naturally occurring sugar alcohol, is extensively used as a natural sweetener and in oral health products [64]. Accurate predictions for such diverse surfactants underscore the broad applicability of our framework.

However, three compounds stood out as prominent prediction outliers in Fig. 1b: 1,3-bis(3-decoxypyridin-1-ium-1-yl)propan-2-ol (C33H56N2O32+), dodecyl(2-hydroxyethyl)azanium (C14H32NO+), and 2-sulfotetradecanoic acid (C14H28O5S). These outliers likely reflect limitations in the model’s ability to capture rare structural motifs or unusual charge distributions. For instance, the gemini cationic surfactant (C33H56N2O32+) features two charged headgroups and a flexible spacer chain, deviating from typical single-headgroup surfactants. Similarly, highly functionalized molecules such as C14H28O5S may exhibit micellization behavior strongly influenced by pH [65] or ionic strength [66], features not explicitly accounted for in our descriptor set, which focuses solely on molecular structure of surfactants. These cases underscore the need to incorporate additional physicochemical parameters in future model development and to include a more diverse set of molecules to better capture the effects of ‘rare’ structural motifs.

CMC prediction of binary surfactant system

To model the CMC of binary surfactant mixtures, we combined the augmented single-surfactant dataset described earlier with a curated dataset of binary mixtures collected from literature. To enable the model to leverage the single-surfactant data for binary prediction, each single surfactant is represented as a pseudo-binary mixture by duplicating its SMILES string and assigning a mole fraction of 0.5 to each component. This approach allowed the model to learn from the rich chemical information encoded in the single-surfactant data while being trained to predict the behavior of binary mixtures. Each data point in our binary dataset is structured in the format: SMILESA, XA, SMILESB, XB, log (CMC), where SMILESA and SMILESB denote the molecular representations of the two surfactants, XA and XB are their mole fractions, and log (CMC) is the target property. The molecular features corresponding to these SMILES, denoted as FA and FB, are computed using cheminformatics packages and merged into a single input feature matrix using three distinct aggregation strategies, as described in the Methodology section. The resulting input feature vectors are then subjected to feature selection (or redundancy reduction) via BorutaPy.

With these curated inputs, we evaluated the predictive performance of our ML framework, focusing on two primary objectives, firstly, interpolating CMC values at untested mole fractions within known binary surfactant systems, and secondly, predicting complete CMC–composition profiles for entirely novel surfactant combinations not seen during training. The first task assesses the model’s ability to interpolate composition-dependent predictions within known systems, enabling targeted formulation without the need for exhaustive experimentation. The second tests the generalizability of our framework, extending our model’s predictive power to new binary systems where no experimental data exists.

Interpolating CMC at intermediate mole fractions

In this section, we evaluate the model’s performance in interpolating CMC values at unmeasured compositions within surfactant pairs present in the training set. For that, we randomly partitioned the input features into training, validation, and test subsets to facilitate model development and evaluation. Note that the complete single-surfactant dataset, which is represented by duplicating each surfactant’s SMILES string with equal mole fractions, is included in the training set to support learning of fundamental chemical features.

To further evaluate the model performance, we examined prediction accuracy across various surfactant pair types using a parity plot (Fig. 2d). We categorized the test data based on the ionic charge of each component in the surfactant mixture, as highlighted in the plot. The parity plot for the best-performing model shows a strong correlation between predicted and true log CMC values, with an R2 of 0.941 and mean squared error (MSE) of 0.099 across the entire test set. The model achieved a training MSE of 0.023 (R2 = 0.985) and validation MSE of 0.151 (R2 = 0.918). The small difference between validation and test performance indicates minimal overfitting. Additionally, the model predicts the CMC of binary mixtures with a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 7.86% on the test set, comparable to predictions for single-surfactant systems.

The SHAP analysis (Figure S5) reveals descriptors that capture the complexity of molecular interactions within surfactant mixtures. Both SCH-5 and nAromLAC are inversely related to CMC, indicating that the presence of specific structural motifs and larger aromatic clusters tends to lower the critical micelle concentration. These features likely enhance micellization by promoting favorable packing, π–π stacking, or electronic interactions between surfactant molecules. In contrast, descriptors associated with lipophilicity and polarizability, such as CrippenLogP and minssO, also show a negative impact on CMC, reinforcing the role of hydrophobic compatibility and electronic flexibility in stabilizing mixed micelles. These consistent patterns across SHAP distributions demonstrate that the model captures key physicochemical drivers of surfactant self-assembly. As a result, our model generalizes well across all categories, including homotypic (cationic–cationic, anionic–anionic, nonionic-nonionic) and heterotypic (cationic–anionic, cationic–nonionic, anionic-nonionic) systems. As expected, we clearly observed that mixtures comprising similarly charged surfactants (anionic–anionic and cationic–cationic) exhibit higher CMC values than oppositely charged mixtures (cationic–anionic), due to electrostatic repulsion hindering micellization [68, 69]. This well-established trend in surfactant science is accurately captured by our model.

Remarkably, cationic–anionic systems, which are known to exhibit the strongest deviations from ideality due to favorable electrostatic interactions [70], are predicted with consistently low error. This demonstrates the model's ability to capture nonideal mixing effects, which are typically challenging for data-driven models without explicit thermodynamic constraints [71]. Furthermore, despite the wide variation in log CMC observed for cationic–anionic mixtures, the model accurately interpolates within this broad compositional space, underscoring its robustness. This predictive consistency across diverse surfactant categories affirms the model’s potential as a design tool for surfactant formulation, particularly in industrial scenarios where experimental screening of every composition is impractical.

Predicting CMC-composition profile for unseen surfactant mixtures

While interpolation within known systems is critical for optimizing surfactant ratios, a more demanding and impactful task is to predict the full CMC–composition profile for entirely novel surfactant mixtures not encountered during training. To rigorously evaluate the model’s generalization ability, we ensured that all mole fraction variants of a given surfactant pair (SMILESA–SMILESB) were confined exclusively to either the training or test set. This strict data splitting strategy guarantees that the model is tested only on unseen combinations of surfactant chemistries, providing a robust assessment of its ability to predict CMC profiles for entirely new mixtures.

Among all tested ML pipelines, the combination of Mordred descriptors, feature aggregation via arithmetic mean and a fully connected ANN yielded the best predictive performance (Fig. 3a). This model achieved a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.24 on the test set. SHAP analysis of feature importance (Fig. 3b) identified several descriptors that strongly influenced the predicted CMC values. Among them, PEOE_VSA6, which quantifies the van der Waals surface area of atoms with specific partial charges, has a strong negative SHAP contribution. This indicates that surfactants with larger polarizable surface regions tend to trigger micelle formation, resulting in lower CMC values. This trend aligns with prior findings: for example, Borse et al. reported that increasing the headgroup polarity in surfactants enhances hydration, reduces electrostatic repulsion between headgroups, and facilitates tighter molecular packing, thereby lowering the CMC and stabilizing micelle formation. Another important feature, AATSC1s, is an autocorrelation descriptor that quantifies the spatial distribution of atomic properties across a molecule. Specifically, AATSC1s captures how the intrinsic electronic states (I-states) of directly bonded atom pairs are correlated, offering a topological summary of the electronic environment along the molecular backbone. Its positive SHAP value for this feature indicates that certain spatial arrangements of electronic states may disrupt efficient molecular packing, thereby impeding micelle formation and increasing the CMC.

The SLogP descriptor, a widely used proxy for molecular hydrophobicity, emerged as another key feature with a strong negative SHAP contribution – indicating that surfactants with higher SLogP values (i.e., greater hydrophobicity) tend to exhibit lower CMCs. This aligns with classical colloid science [77], where increased hydrophobicity enhances the thermodynamic drive for micelle formation by restricting unfavorable water-tail interactions. Two related descriptors, MATS3c and GATS2c, are both autocorrelation metrics weighted by atomic charges and highlight the role of charge distribution in modulating aggregation behavior. The descriptor Xch-5d, which captures molecular connectivity and branching, shows an inverse relationship with CMC in our model – higher values of Xch-5d correspond to lower CMC values. This finding seems counterintuitive as increased branching is conventionally thought to hinder micellization by introducing steric hindrance that disrupts efficient molecular packing [2]. However, this apparent discrepancy may stem from context-specific effects or underlying correlations with other features such as enhanced hydrophobic character or favorable electrostatic interactions that collectively facilitate aggregation and micelle formation. This underscores the value of nonlinear models in capturing complex, multi-feature interactions.

Descriptor counts such as nBase, AMID_N, and MID_N, representing the number of basic, amide, and imide nitrogen atoms respectively, are all found to be positively correlated with CMC. This suggests that the presence of these functional groups increases hydrogen-bonding capacity and aqueous solubility, thereby reducing the overall hydrophobic character of the surfactant. As a result, micellization/aggregation becomes thermodynamically unfavorable, consistent with foundational principles in surfactant chemistry and supported by prior studies [78, 79]. Finally, EState_VSA5, which encodes the electrotopological state of atoms within a specific surface area range, reinforced the role of electronic effects in surfactant self-assembly.

Interpreting feature importance not only confirms the physical relevance of the selected descriptors but also facilitates the rational design of surfactants with tailored aggregation properties. These results further demonstrate that ANNs offer superior simplicity through straightforward feature engineering using established physicochemical descriptors and simpler architectures that avoid the complexity of molecular graph construction and message-passing algorithms required by GNNs. For example, by explicitly incorporating chemically meaningful topological descriptors such as AATSC1s, our models achieve high predictive performance while maintaining interpretability, which is an important advantage over GNN-based approaches that rely on learned, often opaque representations of molecular structure.

Conclusions

In summary, this work presents a novel machine learning framework using an artificial neural network (ANN) for predicting the CMC profiles of binary surfactant mixtures, overcoming the challenges posed by non-ideal mixing behaviors that hinder rapid estimation via conventional methods. Our ANN-based framework offers dual predictive capabilities: (1) accurate interpolation of CMC values at untested mole fractions within known binary systems (MSE = 0.099), enabling precise optimization of surfactant ratios without the need for exhaustive experiments; and (2) precise prediction of complete CMC–composition profiles for entirely novel surfactant combinations (MSE = 0.236), thus facilitating rapid exploration of uncharted formulations and paving the way for the inverse design of high-performance surfactant mixtures. This advancement is achieved through a straightforward feature-merging strategy utilizing an arithmetic-mean approach, combined with a flexible ANN architecture capable of capturing the complex nonlinear interactions between molecular structure and micellization behavior. Unlike computationally intensive methods relying on molecular graph construction and message-passing algorithms (e.g., GNNs), this framework employs established physicochemical descriptors, providing a lightweight and scalable solution well-suited for high-throughput workflows and industrial applications.

Interpretability analysis using SHAP highlights that molecular features such as hydrophobic surface area, electronic topological patterns, and headgroup basicity play dominant roles in driving model predictions. These insights not only align with core principles of surfactant chemistry but also reinforce the mechanistic validity of our model, thereby guiding the rational design of binary mixtures. Moreover, the model demonstrates broad applicability by generalizing effectively across diverse surfactant classes, including anionic, cationic, and nonionic systems. While our current framework is limited to standard laboratory conditions, future work can extend its capabilities to include external parameters such as pH, temperature, and ionic strength.

In the broader context, the modular design of our framework allows for straightforward extension to predict other key physiochemical properties of multicomponent systems, such as surface tension, interfacial tension, and solubilization capacity, highlighting its broad applicability to formulation challenges beyond CMC prediction. Additionally, the ML models developed herein provide a foundation for inverse design strategies. By combining the interpretability of key molecular descriptors with optimization algorithms such as Bayesian optimization or genetic algorithms, our framework facilitates the rational modification of molecular structures and the exploration of entirely new surfactant chemistries. This integrated approach accelerates the design of novel surfactants and binary formulations with tailored functionalities, effectively bridging predictive modeling with practical formulation innovation. Future efforts may further expand these capabilities by evolving the framework into generative models or integrating it with high-throughput molecular dynamics simulations, opening new avenues for the computational discovery of surfactants with novel structures and enhanced functionalities.
