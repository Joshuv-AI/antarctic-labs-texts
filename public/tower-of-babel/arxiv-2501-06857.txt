A Counterfactual Cause in Situation Calculus
Daxin Liu, Vaishak Belle

Abstract
Perhaps the most popular modern formulation of actual causality is the HP account by Halpern and Pearl. Recent advancement has focused on extension of HP account to lift its limited expressiveness, in particular, Batusov and Soutchanski proposed a notion of actual achievement cause in the situation calculus, a rich first-order formalism of actions and changes. Among other things, the first-order nature allows for determining the cause of quantified effects in a given action history therein. While intuitively appealing, Batusov and Soutchanski’s account is not defined in a counterfactual perspective. In this paper, we propose a notion of cause based on counterfactual analysis. In the context of action history, we show that our notion of cause generalizes naturally to a notion of achievement cause. We analyze the relationship between our notion of the achievement cause and the achievement cause by Batusov and Soutchanski. Finally, we relate our account of cause to Halpern and Pearl’s account of actual causality. Particularly, we note some nuances in applying a counterfactual viewpoint to disjunctive effects, a common thorn in definitions of actual causes.

Introduction

The topic of causality
[
48
]
has been widely argued to play a central role in artificial intelligence, mainly because it determines and captures how the agent understands the world it operates in. Philosophers and machine learning people have argued for the need for
type causality
, also referred to as
general causality
, where we might be interested in knowing things like whether smoking causes cancer or asthma. But philosophers have also discussed the need for
actual causality

[
22
]
, sometimes also called
token causality
, which describes what sequence of events leads to something happening. E.g., in a legal setting, we wish to understand if the perpetrator intended to cause harm and whether the perpetrator carried out the harm to the victim or an accidental fire in the victim’s home.

As argued by Halpern and Hitchcock
[
24
]
, the topic of actual causation is a subject of intense study but also considerable disagreement because examples that make a case for one cause against the other can give authenticity or validity to one formalism versus the other. To a large extent, the study of actual causality in AI can be attributed to the importance of actual causality as an essential ingredient of the larger study of causation, primarily resting on David Hume’s account of
but-for
cause, i.e.
counterfactual
cause

[
31
]
. Essentially, this says that had event
A
not happened, then event
B
would not happen or would not be true. Such a formulation does not require that
A
is the unique cause for
B
. This is exemplified by the so-called structural equation model approach, which provides a rigorous mathematical framework for looking at actual causation by Pearl
[
46
,
47
]
. Since the original definition by Pearl, a number of modifications to the structural model account of actual causation have been introduced, perhaps most recently by Halpern and Pearl’s account (henceforth HP account)
[
26
,
21
,
22
]
.

Causation, and especially actual causation, remains an important topic for capturing blameworthiness and harm
[
15
]
. Although the structural equation model provides an attractive and arguably simple formalism for describing variables and their descendants, its simplicity becomes a limitation as it lacks temporal reasoning and quantifiers, and fails to distinguish actions from effects. Hence, it is difficult to work with once we go beyond atomic situations requiring one-step analysis. This motivated Pearl and his collaborators
[
29
]
to revisit the structural equation modeling approach in the language of the situation calculus, one of the most widely studied languages for reasoning about action and change. However, these too had problems as identified by Soutchanski
[
6
]
and led to providing a fresh and recent account of actual causality in the situation calculus.

Their key idea is to provide a definition of “achievement cause” which, given a history of actions and an outcome, tries to use regression to identify the sequence that leads to this event being true. This has been expanded to the epistemic context
[
32
]
. Although this is a very worthy start, what we ask in this paper is whether it is possible to characterize actual causality simply in terms of counterfactual analysis. We argue that a simple definition that almost completely lifts the “but-for” definition of causality, but in a rich action setting, is possible. Essentially, our definition looks very much like a reading of the definition of causality in some sense, closely following David Lewis’s intuition
[
38
]
, but also to some extent following the intuition of the Halpern-Pearl modified definition of structural equation models for actual causation
[
21
]
.

In fact, motivated by lifting the limited expressiveness of the HP account and adapting the elegant counterfactual analysis therein, Halpern and Pass
[
25
]
recently proposed to define abstract causes for frameworks where counterfactuals are defined:

…as long as we start with a framework that has a definition of counterfactual that a user is comfortable with and supports these constructs, we can essentially import the HP definition to that framework.

Hence, essentially, our approach follows this idea and adapts it to the framework of situation calculus action theories.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: in the next section, we present a modal logic that allows us to express action and changes. Based on it, we introduce our account of the actual cause in actions and analyze its application across various scenarios in Section
3
. We formally connect our account to Batusov and Soutchanski’s work in Section
4
. In Section
5
, we relate our account to the HP account and analyze the differences and limitations of our account. Finally, we discuss related works in Section
6
and conclude in Section
7
.

We do not think our account is strictly better than others. Nevertheless, since situation calculus offers a natural way to model actions, effects, and causation, our approach’s simplicity could benefit applications like Golog-based robot programming and future action languages requiring causal reasoning.

We start with a modal variant of the situation calculus, the logic

Basic Action Theory

To express the dynamic of a domain,

use a variant of the

•

•

to the frame problem.

We lump these axioms as

\Sigma=\Sigma_{0}\cup\Sigma_{ap}\cup\Sigma_{post}
. The following is a BAT for the block world domain.

could be

Projection by Regression

An important task in reasoning about action is projection, which is to decide what holds after an action. More formally, given a BAT
\Sigma
deciding if a query
\phi
holds after some action sequence
z
, that is, deciding if
\Sigma\models[z]\phi
. We want to ensure that the action sequence is executable (aka valid and/or legal).
So let
\mathit{exec}(\langle\rangle)=\textsc{True}
, and
\mathit{exec}(a\cdot z)=\mathit{Poss}(a)\land[a]\mathit{exec}(z)
. Then the projection problem is to determine if

\Sigma\models\mathit{exec}(z)\land[z]\phi.

(1)

One of the projection solutions is by regression. Regression systematically converts the query about the future,
[z]\phi
, into another logical equivalent one about now
\phi^{\prime}
. Namely, the regression
\mathfrak{R}[\Sigma,z,\phi]

of
\phi
wrt
z
under
\Sigma
is the weakest precondition of
[z]\phi
being true under
\Sigma
. If the context is clear, we also write
\mathfrak{R}[z,\phi]
instead of
\mathfrak{R}[\Sigma,z,\phi]
.
\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
adapts a similar solution of regression as in
[
49
]
, which recursively replaces fluents in the query about one-step future with their corresponding right-hand side (RHS) of the successor state axioms, which eventually generates a formula without the
[\cdot]
modality. Hence, the projection problem is reduced to checking if

\Sigma_{0}\models\mathfrak{R}[\Sigma,\langle\rangle,\mathit{exec}(z)\land[z]\phi],

(2)

where classical first-order reasoning applies. For example, to determine if the BAT
\Sigma
in Example
1
entails a query
[drop(C)]\mathit{Broken}(C)
, the regression
\mathfrak{R}[drop(C),\mathit{Broken}(C)]
replaces
\mathit{Broken}(C)
with the RHS of the successor state of
\mathit{Broken}
and instantiates it with the ground action
\mathit{drop}(C)
, resulting in a formula
\mathit{Fragile}(C)\vee\mathit{Broken}(C)
. Namely, after the action
\mathit{drop}(C)
,
C
is broken if and only if
C
is fragile or
C
is already broken before the action.

Counterfactual Achievement Cause

Batusov and Soutchanski in 2018
[
6
]
provided an account of
actual achievement cause
in the situation calculus, overcoming many problems that previous works
[
29
]
had in lifting the expressiveness of HP causality. Later,
Khan and Soutchanski in 2020
[
33
]

studied the sufficient and necessary conditions for actual achievement cause and provided an account that coincides with Batusov and Soutchanski’s definition but incorporates the notion of counterfactual analysis, an important aspect in actual causality
[
22
]
. In 2021, Khan and Lespérance
[
32
]
reconsidered the above definition and provided an account of actual achievement cause in the epistemic situation calculus in terms of modalities, among other things, which allows one to reason about knowledge about the cause, i.e., causal knowledge. A drawback that is common in the above proposal is that causality is defined recursively, and inferring the cause in a causal setting is intuitively non-trivial. The reason is that their notion of cause takes many aspects into account, such as
counterfactual
,
preemption
, and
direct or indirect cause
. Here we show that if we drop some of these features, we are still able to provide a simple account of counterfactual cause in the logic
\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
.

Minimal Cause

We start with the notion of minimal cause, which is based on the notion of counterfactual.

Definition 2

(Minimal Cause)
.

Given a BAT
\Sigma

\phi

1.

\Sigma\models\neg\phi

2.

\Sigma\models[z]\phi

3.

Clearly, this definition requires a notion of

Given two sequences

Intuitively, this criterion measures the number of actions needed for a goal from the initial state.

3.

Given two sequences

One could also draw comparisons to the HP definition, the original HP definition of a counterfactual cause:
where the negation of the formula (the negation of the goal) is set to hold. We call an intervention a cause when the negation of the formula holds, the intervention leads to the formula being true, and this intervention is minimal.

Minimal Cause in Narratives

By a

\mathcal{C}
, we mean a triple of BAT
\Sigma
, action sequence
z
, and goal
\phi
, i.e.
\mathcal{C}=\langle\Sigma,z,\phi\rangle
such that
\Sigma\models\mathit{exec}(z)\land[z]\phi
. Usually, the BAT
\Sigma
is fixed, and we write
\mathcal{C}=\langle z,\phi\rangle
for short.

By
z^{\prime}\subseteq z
, we mean
z^{\prime}
is a prefix subsequence of
z
, namely,
z^{\prime}\subseteq z
iff there exists
z^{\prime\prime}
such that
z=z^{\prime}\cdot z^{\prime\prime}

to be empty, we obtain the relation

.

Given a causal setting
\mathcal{C}=\langle z,\phi\rangle
, we define the

(or simply

) as the minimal prefix
z^{\prime}\subseteq z
that leads to
\phi
under
\Sigma
. For this, we need to consider the counterfactual of

being absent, namely, the sequence
z^{\prime\prime}=z\backslash z^{\prime}
obtained from

by removing the prefix
z^{\prime}
. However, simply checking if
z^{\prime\prime}
leads to
\phi
under
\Sigma
is insufficient, as

might contain actions that are now inexecutable due to the removal of
z^{\prime}
from

. We wish to filter these actions as well. We denote the result by

\mathit{Filter}(z\backslash z^{\prime})
and the function
\mathit{Filter}(\cdot)
is recursively defined as:

1.

\mathit{Filter}(\langle\rangle):=\langle\rangle
;

2.

for any prefix

, wlog, assume
z^{\prime\prime\prime}=z^{\star}\cdot a
, then

(a)

if
\Sigma\models[\mathit{Filter}(z^{\star})]\mathit{Poss}(a)
,
\mathit{Filter}(z^{\prime\prime\prime})=\mathit{Filter}(z^{\star})\cdot a
;

(b)

otherwise
\mathit{Filter}(z^{\prime\prime\prime})=\mathit{Filter}(z^{\star})
.

E.g., consider the action sequence
z=\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{drop}(C)\cdot\mathit{pickup}(D)\cdot\mathit{quench}(D)\cdot\mathit{drop}(D)
in Example

in
\Sigma
, the remaining executable action sequence of

is
\mathit{Filter}(z\backslash z^{\prime})=\mathit{pickup}(D)\cdot\mathit{quench}(D)\cdot\mathit{drop}(D)
as removing
\mathit{pickup}(C)
makes
\mathit{drop}(C)
impossible in
\Sigma
.

Definition 6

(Achievement Cause for Narratives)
.

Given a causal setting
\mathcal{C}=\langle z,\phi\rangle
, such that
\Sigma\models\neg\phi
and
\Sigma\models[z]\phi
. We call a prefix sequence
z^{\prime}
of an action sequence

, i.e.

, a cause under

if

1.

\Sigma\models[\mathit{Filter}(z\backslash z^{\prime})]\neg\phi
;

2.

\Sigma\models[z^{\prime\prime}]\phi
for all
z^{\prime\prime}
such that
z^{\prime}\subseteq z^{\prime\prime}\subseteq z
;

3.

no subsequence of
z^{\prime}
holds for Items 1 and 2.

Item 1 is a
sufficient
condition in terms of counterfactual, while Item 2 is a
necessary
condition. Item 3 is a minimal condition (in the sense of sequence length), namely, we are interested in the minimal prefix of
z
that satisfies Items 1 and 2. Note that, due to Item 3, the cause defined in this way is unique. Intuitively, the subsequence
z^{\prime}
is a cause of

\Sigma

\phi
always holds (Item 1), and in the counterfactual that it is absent, the remaining legal actions will not change the truth of
\phi
(Item 2). We show by examples that our notion of achievement cause is reasonable and useful.

Example 5

Consider the BAT
\Sigma
in Example
1

\phi_{1}:=\mathit{Broken}(C)
. Clearly, for the narrative

z=\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{drop}(C)\cdot\mathit{pickup}(D)
, we have
\Sigma\models\mathit{exec}(z)\land[z]\phi_{1}
and the achievement cause here is the prefix
z^{\prime}=\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{drop}(C)
: in the contingency of
z^{\prime}
being absent, the action
\mathit{pickup}(D)
alone would not cause
\mathit{Broken}(C)
under
\Sigma
. Hence, our notion of cause can successfully identify redundant actions in a narrative that are irrelevant to the achievement of a goal. ∎

Example 6
.

Now, consider the BAT
\Sigma
in Example
1
again, but this time, we consider another
disjunctive
goal
\phi_{2}:=\mathit{Holding}(C)\vee\mathit{Holding}(D)
, for the narrative
z=\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{pickup}(D)
, we have
\Sigma\models\mathit{exec}(z)\land[z]\phi_{2}
and the achievement cause here is the prefix
z^{\prime}=\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{pickup}(D)
: in the contingency of
z^{\prime}
being absent,
\mathit{Filter}(z\backslash z^{\prime})=\langle\rangle
, hence under which
\phi_{2}

\mathit{pickup}(C)

\mathit{pickup}(D)

\mathit{Holding}(D)
true, causing the goal
\phi_{2}
to hold. ∎

Admittedly, some might claim that this is counterintuitive as it is ultimately the action
\mathit{pickup}(C)
that achieves the goal. In fact, this is because counterfactual causation suffers in handling preemption (when one event achieves an effect before another can). Our notion of the cause will disregard the temporal order of occurrences of multiple atomic competing events and include all the competing events as a cause. This aligns with the latest version of the HP account of actual cause
[
21
]
where the joint of these events is identified as the cause and each individual event is called

. We come back to this in Section

.

Complexity of Reasoning about Minimal Causes

Before ending this section, we briefly touch on the reasoning complexity of the various minimal causes above. The problem is, in general, undecidable, even for the case of simple length-based minimality. This is because, as mentioned before, the minimal causes above correspond to the minimal plan; hence, reasoning about cause subsumes the planning problem in the situation calculus as a special case. The latter is known to be undecidable. In fact, to do planning, one needs to solve the projection problem as in Eq (
1
), which requires first-order reasoning as in Eq (
2
), hence, it is undecidable. Nevertheless, in practice, one might wish to consider decidable fragments such as
C^{2}
, the two-variable fragment with counting quantifiers
[
45
]
, or to impose constraints on the knowledge base
\Sigma_{0}
, say, to have complete knowledge, so that one could have a Prolog interpreter of the language and use Prolog for a reasoning engine as in
[
49
]
.

A more interesting case is for the minimal cause in narratives. Of course, the problem is still in general undecidable, yet the question we ask here is what is the complexity in terms of the size of the narratives if we have an oracle that can solve the projection problem in
O(1)
time. For a narrative
z
, let
|z|
be the number of actions in
z
. Naively, to reason about the achievement cause for a narrative
z
, one needs to check all the prefixes (
|z|
in total) of
z
, and for each such prefix
z^{\prime}
, one needs to check items 1 and 2 in Def.
6
, which in the worst case take time linear in
|z|
. Hence, the overall time complexity is
O(|z|^{2})
. Nevertheless, this procedure can be accelerated by a
bisection search. Instead of checking items 1 and 2 in Def.
6
step-by-step, one could start with the half-prefix of
z
and apply bisection search recursively. Hence, we have the following result:

Theorem 1
.

Given a causal setting
\mathcal{C}=\langle z,\phi\rangle
, such that
\Sigma\models\neg\phi
and
\Sigma\models[z]\phi
. Suppose further that we have an oracle that can solve the projection problem in Eq. (
1
) in
O(1)
time, the problem of computing the cause under causal setting
\mathcal{C}
can be solved in time
O(|z|\log|z|)
.

Batusov and Soutchanski

[

in the situation calculus. To compare the two definitions, we retrofit their account in the logic

Definition 7

(Actual Achievement Cause
[
6

)
.

A causal setting
\mathcal{C}=\langle z,\phi\rangle
satisfies the achievement conditions of
\phi
via the action-sequence pair
\langle a^{\star},z^{\star}\rangle
if and only if there exists a pair
\langle a^{\prime},z^{\prime}\rangle
such that

1.

\Sigma\models[z^{\prime}]\neg\phi

2.

for all
z^{\prime\prime}
s.t.

,
\Sigma\models[z^{\prime\prime}]\phi
;

3.

besides, it holds that (a) either
z^{\star}=z^{\prime}
and
a^{\star}=a^{\prime}

\langle\Sigma,z^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}[a^{\prime},\phi]\land\mathit{Poss}(a^{\prime})\rangle

in

Intuitively, the action
a^{\star}
executed after the sequence

is an achievement condition of

in the causal setting

if it is a
direct cause
(satisfying Item 1, Item 2, and Item 3(a) ), or it is a
indirect cause
that enable the execution of a direct cause (satisfying Item 1, Item 2, and Item 3(b)).

[
6
]
show that the achievement causes in a causal setting
\mathcal{C}
forms a finite sequence of action-sequence pairs
\mathit{BSChain}(\mathcal{C})
, which is called the achievement causal chain of
\mathcal{C}
.

Given an action sequence
z
, we define
\mathit{Chain}(z)
as
\mathit{Chain}(z):=\{\langle a^{\prime},z^{\prime}\rangle:z^{\prime}\cdot a^{\prime}\subseteq z\}
, namely,
\mathit{Chain}(z)
is the set of actions and together with the corresponding prefixes up to it.

Theorem 2
.

Given a causal setting
\mathcal{C}=\langle z,\phi\rangle
, let
\mathit{BSChain}(\mathcal{C})

by Definition
7
,
z^{\prime}
be the cause of
\phi

and

as Definition

, then
\mathit{BSChain}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathit{Chain}(z^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we suppose
\mathit{BSChain}(\mathcal{C})=\{\langle a_{1},z_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle a_{n},z_{n}\rangle\}.
It suffices to show that
z_{n}\cdot a_{n}\subseteq z^{\prime}
as
\mathit{Chain}(z^{\prime})
contains all actions and prefixes pairs of
z^{\prime}
. Since
\langle a_{n},z_{n}\rangle
is the last pair in
\mathit{BSChain}(\mathcal{C})
, action
a_{n}
executed in
z_{n}
is a direct cause of
\phi
, namely, we have: 1.
\Sigma\models[z_{n}]\neg\phi
; 2. for all
z^{\prime\prime}
such that
z_{n}\cdot a_{n}\subseteq z^{\prime\prime}\subseteq z
,
\Sigma\models[z^{\prime\prime}]\phi
.

Supposing
z_{n}\cdot a_{n}\not\subseteq z^{\prime}
, since
z_{n}\cdot a_{n}\subseteq z
and
z^{\prime}\subseteq z
, we have
z^{\prime}\subset z_{n}\cdot a_{n}
(hence

). By Def.

,
\Sigma\models[z^{\star}]
for all

z^{\prime}\subseteq z^{\star}\subseteq z

z^{\prime}\subseteq z_{n}\subseteq z

\Sigma\models[z_{n}]\phi
which contradicts with
\Sigma\models[z_{n}]\neg\phi

z_{n}\cdot a_{n}\not\subseteq z^{\prime}

Example 7

Consider a variant of the disjunctive goal in Example

This is because, after the action
\mathit{drop}(C)

\mathit{pickup}(C)

, hence it is a direct cause. Besides, the action
\mathit{pickup}(C)

. Meanwhile, for our notion of counterfactual achievement cause (of

)

\mathit{Chain}(z^{\prime})=\mathit{BSChain}(\mathcal{C})\cup\{\langle\mathit{pickup}(D),\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{drop}(C)\rangle\}

∎

5
Relation with HP Definition of Causality

Batusov and Soutchanski

[
6
]
had made a formal comparison between their account of actual achievement cause and the HP account of actual causality which can be summarized as (in the language of
\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
) for every (part of) cause in a causal model in the HP account, there exists a corresponding action-sequence pair that appear in the achievement casual chain of translated causal setting (corresponding to the HP casual model). However, as aforementioned, Batusov and Soutchanski’s account is not based on counterfactual analysis. We informally compared our notion of cause with the HP
modified
definition of actual causality
[
21
]
in terms of counterfactual analysis, especially in handling preemption or disjunctive goals.

The HP account of causality is based on the
structural equation model
. Below, we informally review the HP account and refer interested readers to
[
22
]
.
A causal model
\mathcal{M}
is a tuple
\langle\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V},\{f_{X}\}_{X\in\mathcal{V}}\rangle
where
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
are disjoint sets of
exogenous
and
endogenous
variables representing external or independent factors and internal factors. The value of each endogenous variable
X\in\mathcal{V}
is specified by a function
f_{X}
that may depend on exogenous variables and on endogenous variables that precede
X
with respect to a fixed order on
\mathcal{V}
. Moreover, the dependencies among variables are acyclic. A
context

\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu}
assigns values to all variables in
\mathcal{U}
. The
language
of the HP account consists of formulas of the form
X=x
(value assignment), boolean connectives, and formulas of the form
[\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muY}\xleftarrow{}\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muy}]\phi
(read as after intervening to set variables
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muY}
to values
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muy}
,
\phi
holds).
The truth of formulas is given as (boolean connectives are understood in the standard way):

•

(\mathcal{M},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu})\models X=x
if
f_{X}
has values
x
when setting
\mathcal{U}
according to
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu}
.

•

(\mathcal{M},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu})\models[\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muY}\xleftarrow{}\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muy}]\phi
if
(\mathcal{M}_{\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muY}\xleftarrow{}\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muy}},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu})\models\phi
where
\mathcal{M}_{\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muY}\xleftarrow{}\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muy}}
is the intervened model obtained from
\mathcal{M}
by replacing
f_{Y}
for
Y\in\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muY}
with the trivial function
f_{Y}=y
.

Definition 8
.

A conjunction of events
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muX}=\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0mux}
(
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muX}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
) is an
actual cause
in
(\mathcal{M},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu})
of a query
\phi
if all the following holds:

1.

(\mathcal{M},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu})\models\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muX}=\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0mux}
and
(\mathcal{M},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu})\models\phi
.

2.

There exists a set
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muW}
(disjoint with
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muX}
) of variables in
\mathcal{V}
with
(\mathcal{M},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu})\models\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muW}=\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muw}
and a set of values
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0mux}^{\prime}
such that
(\mathcal{M},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu})\models[\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muX}\xleftarrow{}\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0mux}^{\prime},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muW}\xleftarrow{}\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muw}]\neg\phi
.

3.

No proper sub-conjunction of
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muX}=\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0mux}
satisfies 1,2.

Depending on how contingencies are handled (Item 2), the HP account has multiple variants. The definition above is the latest version, i.e., the
modified
HP definition
[
21
]
, where we only consider those contingencies or counterfactuals where the un-intervented variables have their original values (
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muW}\xleftarrow{}\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muw}
). This is the same in spirit as our notion of achievement cause in narrative: when considering the counterfactual that a given action prefix in a narrative is absent, we would still consider the remaining possible actions in the narrative rather than other action sequences. The tuple
\langle\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muW},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muw},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0mux}^{\prime}\rangle

is called a witness to the fact that
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muX}=\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0mux}
is a cause of
\phi
and each conjunct of
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muX}=\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0mux}
is called
part of the cause
.

Example 8
.

Consider the well-known “Forest Fire” example from
[
27
,
22
]
with three endogenous variables:
MD
(match dropped by an arsonist),
L
(lightning strike), and
FF
(the forest is on fire). For the disjunctive case where either
MD=\textsc{True}
or
L=\textsc{True}
is sufficient to cause a forest fire, i.e.,
L=\textsc{True}
, the causal model
M
consists of 1 equation:
FF:=MD=\textsc{True}\vee L=\textsc{True}
. In the context
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu}
where both
MD=\textsc{True}
and
L=\textsc{True}
, the cause of
FF=\textsc{True}
is the conjunction of
MD=\textsc{True}
and
L=\textsc{True}
in the causal model
(\mathcal{M},\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu})
with a trivial witness
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muW}=\emptyset
. Neither
MD=\textsc{True}
nor
L=\textsc{True}
is a cause, they are but part of the cause.
∎

The HP account has many appealing points and has been successfully adapted in many ways, such as defining
blame and responsibility

[
15
]
, accounting for
evidence
in verification
[
3
]
, and model-checking
[
17
,
9
]
. Yet, it is also criticized for its limited expressiveness
[
29
]
, such as being unable to handle quantified effects compared to ours. The most severe issue is that the notion of cause there highly depends on how one models the domain, namely, the cause of a domain in a model might no longer be a cause when modeling the domain in another way. It is open what the criteria are in modeling a causal setting. Below, we show some possible and “reasonable” HP modeling of the disjunctive goal examples in Example
6
, and compare our notion of cause with the HP cause in terms of counterfactual analysis.

A possible HP modeling
M
of the goal
\phi_{2}:=\mathit{Holding}(C)\vee\mathit{Holding}(D)
and narrative
z=\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{pickup}(D)
in Example
6
is just like the “Forest Fire” example above. Namely, using two endogenous variables
PC
and
PD
for the two actions
\mathit{pickup}(C)
and
\mathit{pickup}(D)
and one endogenous variable
GL
for the goal. The equations are given as “
GL:=\textsc{True}\textrm{ if }PC=\textsc{True}\vee PD=\textsc{True}\textrm{ otherwise }\textsc{False}
”. We will further assume that two exogenous variables enable
PC
and
PD
, and omit the trivial enabling equations
f_{PC}
and
f_{PD}
. It is not hard to see that under such model
\mathcal{M}
and the context
\overrightarrow{\mkern 0.0muu}
where both two actions are enabled, the cause of
GL
is the conjunction of
PC=\textsc{True}
and
PD=\textsc{True}
, just as the “Forest Fire” example, and the cause corresponds to our notion of achievement cause as in Example
6
. This is because the above modeling disregards the temporal order of occurrences
PC
and
PD
, just as our notion of cause does.

Indeed, it is possible to add the temporal relation of competing events in the HP modeling by adding extra endogenous variables. E.g. one can add two endogenous variables
PCG
,
PDG
representing
PC
and
PD
causes the goal respectively, besides, add an equation “
PDG:=\textsc{True}\textrm{ if }PCG=\textsc{False}\land PD=\textsc{True}\textrm{ otherwise }\textsc{False}
” to the model, i.e.
PD
cause
GL
only if
PC
does not cause
GL
and
PD
happens. In this way, the HP account will indeed ascribe the cause of
GL
to
PC
. Nevertheless, there are problems. First, it is unclear how many such variables are needed. For the above examples, where the events
\mathit{pickup}(C)
and
\mathit{pickup}(D)
are atomic in achieving
\phi_{2}
, two variables suffice. Things become complicated when events are not atomic. In such cases, ground actions of competing events might interleave; hence it is unclear how to identify these events. Another problem lies in the meaning and relations among these additional variables. In the above example,
PCG
and
PDG
seem to hardcode the causal relation among competing events and goals (seems cheating in some sense), so it is unsurprising to derive
PC
as the cause. Lastly, it is unclear what the equations look like. For non-atomic events, actions might depend on each other; a natural thought is to specify
f_{X_{a}}
for variable
X_{a}
(supposing it corresponds to action
a
) by using variables of actions that
a
depends on. However, this is also problematic as equations in HP models encode not just
dependency
(or conditionals) but also causation. Namely, the equation
FF:=MD=\textsc{True}\vee L=\textsc{True}
no just says
FF

depends on

MD
and
L
, but also,
MD=\textsc{True}
or
L=\textsc{True}
will
cause

FF=\textsc{True}
. For the sequence
\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{drop}(C)
,
\mathit{drop}(C)
might depend on
\mathit{pickup}(C))
, but it does not mean that the occurrence of
\mathit{pickup}(C)
will cause the occurrence of
\mathit{drop}(C)
. The relation between dependency (conditionals) and causality has remained open in philosophy for a long time
[
42
,
20
,
2
]
.

All of this is to say that the comparison between the HP formalism and our proposal in terms of modeling capabilities is not exact. While it may be possible to pick one particular model from one formalism and translate it to a model in another formalism in a faithful way, there are multiple considerations that need to be taken into account, especially in the presence of durative actions and competing actions.

Limitation of the Proposed Achievement Cause

Compared to the HP account, where the actual cause depends on how one models the domain, our methods have uniform standard modeling: the basic action theory. Yet, this limits the applicability of the notion of counterfactual achievement cause. In particular, the proposed definition suffers in handling competing non-atomic events where actions of different events might be non-consecutive and interleave.

Example 9
.

Consider
\phi_{4}:=\mathit{Broken}(C)\vee\mathit{Broken}(D)
and
z=\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{drop}(C)\cdot\mathit{pickup}(D)\cdot\mathit{quench}(D)\cdot\mathit{drop}(D)
in the basic action theory
\Sigma
of example
1
. It is easy to check that
\Sigma\models\mathit{exec}(z)\land[z]\phi_{4}
. Here, the achievement cause is
z^{\prime}=\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{drop}(C)\cdot\mathit{pickup}(D)
: in the contingency of
z^{\prime}
being absent, the remaining action sequence
\mathit{quench}(D)\cdot\mathit{drop}(D)
is impossible, hence
\mathit{Filter}(z\backslash z^{\prime})=\langle\rangle
under which
\Sigma
does not entails
\phi_{4}
. It seems counter-intuitive as the cause is not
\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{drop}(C)
and contains one
redundant
action
\mathit{pickup}(D)
. Yet, it is not in the sense of counterfactual analysis: the redundant action
\mathit{pickup}(D)
serves to disable the contingency of
\mathit{pickup}(D)\cdot\mathit{quench}(D)\cdot\mathit{drop}(D)
under which
\phi_{4}
still holds in
\Sigma
as
\mathit{Broken}(D)
becomes true. However, in the contingency of
\mathit{pickup}(C)\cdot\mathit{drop}(C)\cdot\mathit{pickup}(D)
being absent, the remaining sequence
\mathit{quench}(D)\cdot\mathit{drop}(D)
is no longer executable under
\Sigma
. ∎

In the general case of multiple competing events (say, sequences of actions) to achieve the goal, the proposed notion of causes will include all the events (except the last one) and part of the last event. The fact that there are issues here is not surprising. This is precisely why the topic of causation remains an important and active area of philosophy, computer science, and artificial intelligence. What we have provided here is a first-order account that seems to have many reasonable features in an action language.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a notion of counterfactual cause in the situation calculus. In the context that a given action history leads to a goal, we show that our notion of cause generalizes naturally to a notion of achievement cause. We analyze the relationship between our notion of the achievement cause and existing works. Future works include extending the proposal to an epistemic setting, just like
[
32
]
. Besides, it is promising to investigate how our method can be adapted for robot programming in Golog and other applications that are being built on action languages and require actual causation.
