Exploring how textual complexity affects cognitive load during reading: an eye-tracking study
Authors: David Vaněček, Martin Kursch, Eyüp Şen, Inam Ur Rehman, Yilmaz Ilker Yorulmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reading constitutes a fundamental component of learning, and understanding cognitive load during reading is important both for explaining how learners engage with text and for enhancing the effectiveness of learning. The primary aim of this study is to examine how manipulated text complexity conditions influence eye-movement behavior, in order to better understand how readers’ gaze patterns reflect increases or decreases in cognitive load. Specifically, the study investigates whether key metrics (fixations, regressions, pupil area) and the behavioral indicator of reading duration differ systematically between easy and hard texts. The study examines how textual complexity affects cognitive load during reading using eye-tracking. The study included 33 university students (18 female, 15 male; M age = 35.6, SD = 12.4), all native Czech speakers. Participants completed a counterbalanced repeated-measures reading task in Czech in which text complexity was experimentally manipulated across lexical, syntactic, and semantic dimensions. Eye-movement data were collected during reading to capture behavioral patterns typically linked to variations in cognitive load, and participants subsequently provided self-reports of perceived mental effort using the NASA-TLX. Results showed that texts with greater complexity led to more fixations, more regressions, and longer reading durations, indicating higher cognitive load. NASA-TLX ratings likewise indicated greater perceived effort for the higher-complexity condition. However, pupillary metrics showed no meaningful differences between conditions. Overall, the findings suggest that gaze-based eye-tracking metrics are sensitive indicators of cognitive demand, while pupillary responses depend on specific experimental conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51704-7.

Introduction

Eye-tracking research has played a central role in advancing our understanding of the cognitive mechanisms involved in reading. Variations in gaze behavior, such as fixation duration and regression frequency, are sensitive to the cognitive demands1, and pupil dilation has been associated with mental activity2. Collectively, these metrics provide a real-time method for examining cognitive processes during learning. While pupil dilation is commonly interpreted as a psychophysiological index of mental effort, eye-movement measures such as fixations and regressions offer a more direct behavioral window into how readers interact with text during processing.

Understanding how textual complexity influences cognitive load is pedagogically significant, as excessive cognitive load can hinder comprehension, whereas an optimal level supports learning3,4. By examining how readers visually and cognitively respond to texts of varying complexity, this research seeks to contribute empirical evidence to ongoing scientific and pedagogical efforts that leverage process-oriented data to inform instructional design and personalized learning.

Cognitive load refers to the mental effort experienced during text processing. In empirical research, it is typically inferred from behavioral eye-movement measures (e.g., fixations, regressions, reading time), psychophysiological indices (e.g., pupil area), and subjective self-reports. In this study, cognitive load is defined as the mental effort readers experience while processing texts of varying complexity. To operationalize textual complexity in a controlled and theoretically interpretable manner, two expository Czech texts were constructed to differ systematically across lexical, syntactic, and semantic dimensions, while being matched on topic, genre, and overall length. This controlled manipulation allows for a more precise examination of whether core eye-movement metrics can detect systematic differences in cognitive processing. Given the growing interest in eye-tracking within learning analytics and cognitive load detection research, it is important to determine whether relatively simplified experimental configurations can yield theoretically meaningful and interpretable indicators of cognitive demand. The present study addresses this issue by testing whether key eye-movement metrics reliably differentiate between clearly defined levels of textual complexity under controlled conditions.

This study mainly presents both linguistic and methodological innovation. It addresses the English-centric bias of mainstream cognitive processing research by introducing Czech experimental materials. This cross-linguistic contrast is important, because fundamental typological differences are known to influence cognitive processing5. English, a predominantly analytic language (e.g., run as both verb and noun), differs from Czech, which is characterized by rich inflectional morphology (e.g., běhat [to run] vs. běh [a run])6 and relatively phonologically consistent orthography7. These linguistic characteristics allow for the validation of findings from English research in a different linguistic context. Furthermore, the study employs a multimodal assessment of cognitive load by combining eye-tracking metrics with NASA-TLX self-reports8, enhancing the reliability and interpretive depth of the findings. By contrasting easy and complex texts, the study provides empirical evidence on how eye-movement patterns (e.g., fixations, regressions) respond to controlled changes in complexity. This approach also makes it possible to evaluate whether gaze-based metrics can serve as indicators of cognitive demand during reading.

To situate these contributions within existing research, the following section outlines the theoretical foundations of cognitive load and its measurement. Cognitive load theory conceptualizes cognitive load as the mental effort imposed on working memory during text processing4, which can be assessed using behavioral, psychophysiological, and subjective measures. Traditionally, cognitive load has often been measured using subjective self-report instruments such as the NASA-TLX, although these methods have limitations for capturing real-time processing. Eye-tracking offers a complementary approach by providing continuous behavioral indicators of cognitive load9,10. At the same time, such metrics should be interpreted cautiously, as they reflect multiple overlapping constructs, such as cognitive effort, attention, and engagement. Therefore, eye-tracking measures are most informative when combined with additional indicators of cognitive load.

Eye-tracking studies commonly use fixation count and regression frequency as indicators of cognitive effort during reading11,12. Fixations reflect the allocation of visual attention and the required time to process textual information, whereas regressions (backward eye movements) are typically interpreted as indicators of rereading behavior, often occurring when readers encounter processing difficulty, ambiguity, or integration demands1,13. Together, these measures capture both forward progression and moments of disrupted comprehension, providing complementary insight into cognitive processing demands.

However, it is important to note that eye-tracking metrics such as fixation duration and gaze patterns are not uniquely specific to cognitive load and may also reflect related constructs, including attention and task engagement. For example, increased fixation duration or gaze concentration may occur when readers are highly engaged with relevant or interesting material, rather than experiencing cognitive overload. This distinction has also been underlined in studies of reading engagement, where similar eye-movement patterns were reported without necessarily indicating increased cognitive effort14.

To address this ambiguity, the present study adopts a multimodal approach to cognitive load measurement, in which gaze-based metrics are interpreted together with behavioral (reading duration) and subjective (NASA-TLX) measures. The convergence of these indicators provides a stronger basis for interpreting eye-movement patterns as reflecting cognitive load rather than engagement alone. This approach is particularly relevant because eye-tracking has been widely used to examine cognitive processes, underlying reading comprehension, word recognition, working memory, and inference generation15,16. Within this framework, text complexity, including lexical, syntactic, and semantic features17, represents a key factor influencing cognitive load and reading comprehension. More complex texts typically elicit more fixations, more regressions, and longer reading times, reflecting increased cognitive processing demands during reading18.

While eye-tracking is a well-established method in reading research, its value in the present study lies in linking textual complexity with cognitive load mechanisms. Cognitive load is closely related to task complexity and is often reflected in learners’ subjective experience of mental effort19, while also depending on the complexity of the learning material20.The complexity of instructional materials is a key determinant of mental effort, as readers tend to experience higher cognitive load when processing linguistically more complex texts21. Consistent with this view, increased text complexity has been associated with higher cognitive load, reflected in longer fixation and saccade durations, although not consistently in pupil size22. When selecting optimal learning materials, objective indicators of textual complexity may support the preliminary evaluation foreseen for cognitive load23. Despite these advances, it remains unclear how specific eye-tracking metrics respond to controlled variations in textual complexity, particularly in non-English contexts. Methodology

To examine how text complexity affects cognitive load, a quantitative research design was employed throughout the experimental process. As depicted in Fig. 1, the independent variables included the order of reading conditions, gender, age, average daily screen time, and use of glasses or lenses, while the dependent variables consisted of normalized reading duration, pupil area, number of fixations, number of regressions, self-estimated reading duration, and self-estimated cognitive load. This study utilized an eye-tracking experiment, in which a total of 33 university students participated on a voluntary basis. Participants were divided into two groups: one group read the easy text first, followed by the hard text, while the second group followed the reverse order. This design was adopted to reduce potential bias arising from the sequence of text difficulty. At the beginning of the experiment, participants completed a consent form along with a brief demographic questionnaire. Following each reading task, participants were asked to fill out a questionnaire estimating their perceived reading duration and cognitive load, separately for both the easy and hard text conditions.Fig. 1The research structure.

The research structure.

The dependent variables were derived from the raw eye-tracking data and processed through standardized procedures to ensure comparability across participants and reading conditions. Normalized reading duration was computed for each participant’s reading session and adjusted to account for differences in text length between conditions. To ensure a fair comparison between the easy and hard texts, reading time was normalized by total character count. Specifically, length-normalized reading duration was calculated as milliseconds per character (ms/character) by dividing each participant’s total reading time by the total number of characters in the respective text. This approach provides a transparent and interpretable measure of processing time per textual unit, enabling direct comparison across texts of unequal length.

Pupil area was calculated by estimating the area of the pupil using an elliptical model formula (π × [pupil width/2] × [pupil height/2]) separately for the left and right eyes. Measurements were recorded at a sampling rate of 15 data points per 100 ms (150 Hz). For each timestamp, elliptical area was computed for both eyes and a binocular pupil area value was obtained by averaging the left and right eye areas. All subsequent analyses were conducted using this combined binocular pupil measure. Pupil area was analyzed at the raw sample level and was not segmented based on fixation events; therefore, pupil-based metrics were independent of the fixation-detection procedure.

Prior to analysis, raw pupil data underwent preprocessing. Samples with missing values, zero values, or non-positive values were treated as invalid and coded as missing. Short signal losses due to blinks or tracking artifacts were linearly interpolated only when the gap duration did not exceed 200 ms; longer gaps were not interpolated and remained missing. This approach ensured that only brief, physiologically plausible interruptions were corrected while preserving data integrity. To control for slow drift and between-subject differences in tonic pupil size, subtractive baseline correction24 was applied. For each screen (treated as an experimental epoch), baseline pupil size was defined as the mean interpolated pupil area within the first 1000 ms following screen onset. Baseline-corrected pupil change was computed by subtracting this baseline value from subsequent samples within the same screen. Data retention was quantified for each participant and condition. Across participants, the percentage of missing raw samples generally remained low (typically below 10%), although a small number of participants exhibited higher levels of data loss (e.g., P14 and P33). After interpolation of short gaps, the vast majority of participant-condition datasets retained more than 95% of samples, and most exceeded 99% retention. Even in cases with higher initial missingness, interpolation substantially reduced data loss while avoiding overcorrection of longer gaps. These percentages are now reported to ensure transparency regarding data quality and preprocessing effects.

Fixations were identified using a dispersion-based algorithm consistent with the Identification by Dispersion Threshold (I-DT) procedure25. Consecutive gaze samples were grouped into the same fixation when spatial dispersion remained below predefined thresholds and the inter-sample interval did not exceed 100 ms. A minimum fixation duration of 100 ms was applied. Spatial dispersion thresholds corresponded to 96 pixels horizontally (≈ 2.47° of visual angle) and 60 pixels vertically (≈1.54°) at a viewing distance of 60 cm on a 24-inch monitor (1920 × 1200 px. Under this setup, 1° of visual angle corresponded to approximately 39 pixels. These threshold values fall within the range commonly reported for dispersion-based fixation detection procedures (approximately 0.5°–2.5° of visual angle; see25,26). All fixation- and regression-based metrics were computed within a predefined text Area of Interest (AOI) corresponding to the exact bounding box of the displayed text stimulus. The AOI was defined in pixel coordinates based on the stimulus layout, and only gaze samples and fixations falling within this region were included in the analyses.

The Number of Fixations was counted per participant and text type (easy and hard text). Regressions were defined at the fixation level as backward horizontal movements, operationalized as a fixation whose mean x-coordinate was lower than that of the immediately preceding fixation within the same text stimulus. To prevent routine return sweeps in multi-line reading from being counted as regressions, backward movements accompanied by a substantial downward shift in mean y-coordinate and a large leftward displacement were classified as return sweeps and excluded from regression counts.

These derived metrics served as independent variables of cognitive load measurement throughout the reading tasks. The eye-tracking metrics used in the present study reflect whole-text processing demands. Because region-based first-pass and second-pass measures were not computed, the findings should be interpreted as indexing overall cognitive load rather than specific processing stages. In addition to eye-tracking metrics, two self-reported measures were collected: Self-Estimated Reading Duration and Self-Estimated Cognitive Load. To assess perceived mental effort (Self-Estimated Cognitive Load), the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) was used. The NASA-TLX8 is a multidimensional subjective workload instrument used to assess perceived task demands. In reading research, the NASA-TLX assesses perceived workload across six dimensions (Mental Demand, Physical Demand, Temporal Demand, Performance, Effort, and Frustration) which together provide a multidimensional index of subjective cognitive load. In the present study, participants completed the NASA-TLX immediately after each reading task, and these subjective scores were analyzed in comparison with the objective eye-tracking measures.

Participants

The study involved 33 third-year university students who volunteered to take part in the eye-tracking experiment. Of these, 18 were female (54.5%) and 15 were male (45.5%), mirroring a balanced gender distribution. Participants’ ages ranged widely, with a mean of 35.6 years (SD = 12.4), reflecting the mature-student profile typical of the programme in which they were enrolled. An a priori power analysis was conducted using Jamovi (Version 2.6.44: https://www.jamovi.org/) for a paired-samples design assuming α = 0.05 and desired statistical power of 0.80 and an expected medium effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.50. The analysis indicated a required sample size of N = 34. The final sample consisted of 33 participants, yielding an achieved power of approximately 0.795, which is effectively equivalent to the target power and does not meaningfully affect the sensitivity of the study. A sensitivity analysis indicated that with a final sample of N = 33, the study was adequately powered to detect effects of approximately Cohen’s d = 0.50 or larger. Thus, the design was sufficiently sensitive to detect medium-to-large effects. However, smaller effects (d < 0.50), particularly for physiological measures such as pupil dilation, may not have been reliably detected under the present sample size.

All participants were enrolled in the bachelor’s level Teacher Training Programme for Future Teachers at the Masaryk Institute of Advanced Studies (MIAS), Czech Technical University (CTU) in Prague. They all were native speakers of Czech language and read both texts written in Czech language. There is no missing demographic data collected. To minimize order effects, the cohort was randomly divided into two reading-sequence groups. One group (easy-first) read the easy text before the hard text, while the second group (hard-first) completed the readings in the opposite order. All participants were in their third year of study and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision; glasses or contact‐lens use was recorded as a control variable. All experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee (Approval No. 0000-28/25/51902/EKČVUT). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Each student provided written informed consent and completed a brief demographic questionnaire. For manuscripts that include information or images that could lead to identification of a study participant, informed consent must be obtained for publication in an online open-access journal. Accordingly, informed consent for the publication of any potentially identifiable information or images was obtained from all participants.

Text materials and complexity

The experimental materials consisted of two expository Czech-language texts of comparable length. Both addressed the topic of climate change but differed in their specificity: one provided a general overview of the issue, while the other focused on a specific solution, the circular economy. Specifically, the easy text, titled “The Basics of Climate Change [Základy klimatické změny in Czech]”, explained the fundamental causes and everyday consequences of climate change. The second hard text, titled “Climate Change and Circular Economy [Klimatická změna a cirkulární ekonomika in Czech]”, discussed the topic through the lens of the circular economy and environmental policy. The easy text contained 225 words, and the hard text 230 words. The texts differed systematically in linguistic complexity to ensure that any observed effects could be attributed to textual complexity rather than differences in topic or length. Although such indices were not independently quantified for the present Czech-language materials due to limited availability of comprehensive norms, the manipulation of lexical sophistication in the hard text is consistent with increased processing demands typically associated with lower-frequency and more specialized vocabulary. These results support the intended manipulation of text complexity between the two reading conditions. Importantly, the observed differences reflect a multidimensional manipulation of textual difficulty at the lexical, syntactic, and conceptual levels. While specific psycholinguistic indices (e.g., Age of Acquisition and Neighborhood Density) were not independently quantified for the present Czech materials, the contrast between general-language vocabulary and specialized terminology suggests differences in overall processing demands.

Text complexity is inherently multidimensional, and features such as cohesion, abstractness, and semantic density are closely related to lexical and syntactic complexity. While the texts were matched on topic, genre (expository), and length, they were designed to differ across multiple dimensions. Therefore, the goal of the present study was not to isolate a single feature, but to establish a controlled contrast between two levels of overall textual complexity.

Design of the experiment

A counter-balanced, repeated-measures design was employed: every participant read both an easy and a hard text, while the order of presentation (easy-first versus hard-first) served as a between-subjects factor to control for sequence effects. Each experiment session began with informed-consent and a brief demographic form, followed by an eye-tracker calibration (a 9-point calibration procedure administered using the system’s built-in calibration routine; calibration quality was evaluated automatically through the device’s internal validation process, and recalibration was repeated when tracking quality did not meet the system’s acceptance criteria). Participants then completed the first reading task, after which they immediately filled out a short questionnaire assessing self-estimated reading duration and cognitive load. A two-minute rest period separated the readings, and the same procedure was repeated for the second text. The total duration of the experiment for each participant was approximately 30 min. All readings took place in a quiet, evenly lit room (within a semi-enclosed testing booth designed to reduce environmental distractions and maintain constant ambient illumination); ambient illumination was held constant to minimize extraneous pupil-dilation effects. Participants were seated 60 cm from a 24-inch monitor (1920 × 1200 px, 60 Hz) (with head position stabilized using a chin rest to minimize movement artifacts and maintain consistent viewing distance) and instructed to read silently at a natural pace without scrolling.

Gaze position and pupil size were recorded with the i4tracking system (Medicton Group Inc., Version 2.0 build 20221123 r167: https://i4tracking.eu/en/homepage-english/), a desktop remote eye-tracking camera mounted below the display to capture the face area from forehead to mouth. The device supports sampling rates of up to 300 fps with a native resolution of 2048 × 1088 px, a 20° field of view, 0.1° resolving power, and 0.5° absolute accuracy. In this study, raw images were acquired at 150 Hz to match the detection pipeline and reduce data volume. Two integrated infrared light sources illuminated the eyes, and reflected IR light enabled robust pupil detection under constant room lighting. The system operates at a viewing distance of 50–70 cm, tolerates head movements within a 10 × 10 × 10 cm volume, connects via USB 3.0, and measures 210 mm × 70 mm × 90 mm (w × h × d) with a weight of 400 g.

The resulting data contained the variables Screen_name, Time (ms), X_in_calibration_coordinates, Y_in_calibration_coordinates, Left_pupil_x, Left_pupil_y, Right_pupil_x, Right_pupil_y, Left_pupil_width, Left_pupil_height, and Right_pupil_width. These records formed the basis for computing normalized reading duration, interpolated pupil area and baseline-corrected pupil change, fixation counts, and regression counts. Each participant–text combination generated a separate comma-separated Excel file, which was immediately backed up to an encrypted institutional server for subsequent analysis. In this study, we followed the minimum criteria for reporting an eye-tracking study as outlined by Dunn et al.35, and aligned the reporting of apparatus, calibration, and environmental conditions with current best-practice recommendations for eye-tracking methodology36.

Operationalizing cognitive load through eye-tracking metrics

Specifically, four key variables were used in combination to estimate the cognitive load imposed by each text: normalized reading duration, number of regressions, interpolated pupil area and baseline-corrected pupil change, and number of fixations. Reading duration reflects the overall time required to process the material, serving as a proxy for processing fluency and task demand. The number of regressions defined as backward saccadic movements reflecting rereading behavior was used to capture localized comprehension difficulties or increased cognitive effort. Pupil measures, calculated via an elliptical formula from pupil width and height, provides a physiological correlation of mental workload, with larger dilations associated with higher cognitive demands. Lastly, the number of fixations represents the granularity of attention allocation, with elevated fixation counts signaling more intensive or fragmented information processing. Taken together, these metrics offer a complementary perspective on how readers engage with texts of varying complexity, enabling a composite understanding of the dynamic interplay between cognitive effort, and text complexity. It is essential to emphasize that these eye-tracking metrics do not provide a direct or exclusive measure of cognitive load. Instead, they function as indirect proxies of cognitive processing that may also capture aspects of attentional allocation and engagement. In this study, their interpretation as indicators of cognitive load is supported by their systematic variation across text complexity conditions and their alignment with subjective workload measures (NASA-TLX).

All eye-tracking variables were computed from raw gaze data exported from the i4tracking system and processed using custom scripts in R (version 2024.12.1 + 563: https://dailies.rstudio.com/version/2024.12.1+563/). The derived measures were subsequently analyzed in Jamovi (version 2.6.44). Given the within-subject design (each participant read both text conditions), condition differences were evaluated using paired-samples tests. Prior to inferential testing, normality of the difference scores was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of distribution plots. When normality assumptions were satisfied, paired-samples t-tests were conducted; when normality was violated, the nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied. Statistical significance was evaluated at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Complementary linear mixed-effects analyses with random intercepts for participants yielded identical conclusions to the paired-samples comparisons for the primary outcomes (fixations and regressions). Therefore, for parsimony, only the paired comparisons are presented in detail.

Results

In order to explore the impact of text complexity on cognitive load, a number of eye-tracking metrics, such as fixation count, regression count, reading duration, and pupillary metrics (pupil area and pupil area and baseline-corrected pupil change) were examined in relation to text difficulty for both easy and hard text conditions. The detailed results presented below show that behavioral eye-tracking metrics were sensitive to text complexity, whereas pupil-based measures were not. Participants exhibited significantly more fixations, more regressions, and longer normalized reading durations when reading the hard text compared to the easy text, and subjective time estimates were consistent with these objective differences. In contrast, neither mean pupil area nor baseline-corrected pupil change differed significantly between conditions.

Complementing the overall results, participants also provided subjective ratings of their mental effort after reading each text. Using the NASA-TLX8, participants reported substantially higher perceived mental effort for the hard text (M = 14.06) compared to the easy text (M = 4.79). These self-reports align with the behavioral data, further supporting the interpretation that the harder text was experienced as requiring significantly more mental effort. To further examine the relationship between subjective and behavioral & psychophysiological indicators of cognitive load, correlation analyses were conducted separately for each condition using Pearson’s r. In the easy condition, self-reported cognitive load was significantly negatively correlated with fixation count (r = − 0.383, p = .028). No other correlations reached statistical significance in this condition: the association with regression count was not significant (r = − 0.317, p = .072), and self-reported load was not significantly related to mean pupil area (r = − 0.116, p = .522). In the hard condition, self-reported cognitive load was not significantly correlated with fixation count (r = − 0.201, p = .262), regression count (r = − 0.038, p = .834), or mean pupil area (r = − 0.050, p = .784). Overall, apart from the significant negative association between subjective load and fixation count in the easy condition, no statistically significant relationships were observed between NASA-TLX scores and eye-tracking measures. Notably, a correlation of moderate magnitude (r = − 0.317), which is often considered psychologically meaningful in cognitive research, did not reach statistical significance in the present sample (N = 33), likely due to limited statistical power, as detecting correlations of this magnitude (r ≈ 0.30) with adequate power (1 − β = 0.80, α = 0.05) would require a substantially larger sample (approximately N ≈ 75–85). These findings suggest that, although mean differences across conditions followed a similar pattern, subjective and eye-tracking indicators reflect partially distinct aspects of cognitive load at the individual level.

To address potential order effects, we conducted additional linear mixed-effects analyses including Condition (easy vs. hard), Reading Order (Easy-first vs. Hard-first), and their interaction (Condition × Reading Order) as fixed effects, with random intercepts for participants. Reading order reflected the counterbalanced design in which one group read the easy text first and the other group read the hard text first. For fixation count, the main effect of Condition remained statistically significant, F(1, 31) = 40.67, p < .001 (Estimate = 15.24, SE = 2.39), indicating a higher number of fixations in the hard condition. Neither the main effect of Reading Order, F(1, 31) = 0.38, p = .541, nor the Condition × Reading Order interaction, F(1, 31) = 1.28, p = .266, reached statistical significance. A comparable pattern was observed for regression count. The main effect of Condition was significant, F(1, 31) = 29.75, p < .001 (Estimate = 6.05, SE = 1.11), reflecting more regressions in the hard condition. In contrast, neither the main effect of Reading Order, F(1, 31) = 0.36, p = .555, nor the interaction between Condition and Reading Order, F(1, 31) = 1.71, p = .201, was statistically significant. Overall, these results indicate that the observed complexity effects for fixation and regression counts were not attributable to reading order and that the counterbalancing procedure was effective. For baseline-corrected pupil change, the linear mixed-effects model revealed no significant main effect of Condition, F(1, 31) = 0.34, p = .566, and no significant main effect of Reading Order, F(1, 31) = 2.42, p = .130. The Condition × Reading Order interaction was also non-significant, F(1, 31) = 0.72, p = .402. These results indicate that task-evoked pupil dilation was not influenced by text complexity or presentation order. For reading duration, the linear mixed-effects model revealed a significant main effect of Condition, F(1, 31) = 40.30, p < .001, with longer reading times for the hard text compared to the easy text (Estimate = 17.34, SE = 2.73, 95% CI [11.90, 22.80]). The main effect of Reading Order was not significant, F(1, 31) = 0.02, p = .895. However, a significant Condition × Reading Order interaction was observed, F(1, 31) = 4.59, p = .040 (Estimate = − 11.70, SE = 5.46), indicating that although the hard text consistently required more time, the magnitude of this difference varied across order groups.

Overall, these results suggest that text complexity did not significantly influence pupil-based measures, implying that no change was observed in psychophysiological indicators of cognitive load based on the ocular metrics. These findings, however, may not contradict the behavioral and subjective data due to several methodological and theoretical considerations. The first is that, according to established literature, when the perceptual load is high, pupil diameter does not show significant effects on cognitive load37, a condition potentially met during the demanding task of reading hard text (Text 2). Second, the absence of a significant effect could be due to the fact that pupil-based measures is sensitive to factors other than cognitive load, such as ambient light and off-axis distortion38, which may have introduced variability and obscured any subtle physiological changes related to cognitive effort.

Conclusion

This research study addressed the research question, “How does text complexity affect eye behavior and cognitive load?” The results show that across metrics, a consistent pattern emerged for the behavioral indicators of cognitive load, whereas pupil-based measures did not show sensitivity to text complexity. Participants exhibited significantly more fixations and more regressions when reading the hard text compared to the easy text, with both effects robust across parametric and non-parametric tests, indicating increased processing demands and rereading behavior under higher complexity. Similarly, length-normalized reading duration (ms/character) was significantly longer for the hard text, confirming that greater textual complexity was associated with slower processing. Subjective estimates of reading time were also higher for the hard text and moderately correlated with objective reading duration, suggesting that participants were aware of increased effort.

In contrast, neither mean pupil area nor baseline-corrected pupil change differed significantly between conditions, despite overall dilation relative to baseline during reading. Taken together, the findings show that fixation count, regression count, and normalized reading duration were sensitive behavioral markers of the manipulated complexity contrast, whereas pupil-based physiological metrics did not reliably differentiate between easy and hard texts under the present experimental conditions. By doing so, our findings contribute empirical evidence that gaze-based metrics can function as indirect, real-time indicators of cognitive processing demands, particularly when interpreted alongside complementary behavioral and subjective measures. While further research is needed to establish their broader applicability for explaining how learners engage with text and for enhancing the effectiveness of learning. Intraclass correlation coefficients indicated substantial between-participant variability across metrics (ICC range = 0.50–0.78), and nearly complete participant-driven variance for pupil area (ICC = 0.996). Importantly, despite this variability, fixation count, regression count, and normalized reading duration showed robust and statistically significant fixed effects of text complexity, explaining between 19 and 25% of variance independently of individual differences. In contrast, pupil-based measures were largely dominated by baseline individual variability and did not demonstrate sensitivity to the manipulated complexity contrast.

This lack of a significant effect on pupil size, while seemingly contradictory to the behavioral data, could also be due to the high perceptual load of reading, which has been shown to obscure pupil diameter’s sensitivity to cognitive load37. Furthermore, this discrepancy may be explained by the specific nature of the linguistic complexity manipulated in the Czech texts. The difficulty in the hard text was primarily driven by syntactic and semantic factors, such as a higher density of dependent clauses, longer average sentence lengths, and a greater level of abstraction requiring readers to integrate implicit information. This type of complexity, which stems from the rich inflectional morphology and complex sentence structures of Czech, likely induces a more sustained and distributed (tonic) cognitive load as the reader works to build meaning across the entire sentence. This contrasts with the sudden, phasic load spikes often associated with encountering a single low-frequency word, which is more common in studies using analytic languages like English. However, this interpretation remains tentative and should be examined in future studies that directly compare linguistic structures across languages. It is plausible that pupil dilation is more sensitive to these immediate, phasic changes in effort, whereas the behavioral metrics of fixations, regressions and total reading time (which inherently measure integration and rereading) were better able to capture the sustained integrative effort required by the complex Czech text.

Theoretical implications and practical contributions

The present study contributes to the literature by providing a focused examination of how text complexity relates to cognitive load in a repeated-measures eye-tracking design. The findings indicate that texts with greater complexity were associated with more fixations, more regressions, and longer normalized reading times, whereas pupil-based measures did not reliably differentiate between conditions in the present design. These results suggest that, for short Czech expository texts and under controlled experimental conditions, gaze-based behavioral metrics may provide more sensitive indicators of complexity-related processing demands than pupillary responses. From a practical perspective, fixation count, regression count, and normalized reading time may help researchers and instructional designers identify texts that impose greater processing demands. In this way, these measures may support the selection or adaptation of reading materials to better match learners’ capabilities.

Limitations and future directions

Despite these findings, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

First, the study was carried out on a relatively small and homogeneous sample of adult university students, likely sharing a similar educational background, which limits the generalizability of the findings to broader populations, such as younger readers or individuals with diverse educational or linguistic backgrounds. Moreover, the use of small expository texts within a controlled laboratory context may not fully reflect natural reading settings.

Second, the study depended on a limited set of experimental materials, where one text demonstrated each complexity condition. Consequently, the results reveal variations between these particular stimuli rather than generalizable effects of textual complexity. Even though the manipulation was theoretically grounded and validated, future research should comprise multiple texts per condition to better capture variability in linguistic structures.

Third, cognitive load was assessed using behavioral and psychophysiological proxies rather than being calculated directly. Although fixation counts, regressions, and reading duration consistently differentiated between conditions, pupil-based measures did not indicate significant effects. This may reflect a lack of sensitivity of pupillary metrics under persistent reading conditions, as well as potential influences of individual and environmental factors.

Forth, eye-tracking measures may reflect not only cognitive load but also attentional engagement or interest, which cannot be fully disentangled within the present design.

Fifth, lexical complexity was primarily manipulated through differences in word frequency and the use of specialized, domain-specific academic vocabulary, while other psycholinguistic variables such as Age of Acquisition (AoA) and Neighborhood Density (NHD) were not individually controlled. This reflects the limited availability of comprehensive Czech-language psycholinguistic norms, as Czech remains underrepresented in this regard29, and variables such as orthographic neighborhood often require researcher-specific computation rather than standardized tools30. Consequently, although higher textual complexity was associated with increased behavioral indicators of cognitive load, the specific contribution of intended lexical properties cannot be determined. Future research should replicate these findings using materials based on more comprehensive Czech-language norms (e.g., AoA and NHD), enabling more precise operationalization of lexical complexity.

Finally, the study did not cover a direct assessment of reading comprehension or control for individual variances such as motivation, fatigue, or prior knowledge. Therefore, it remains unclear to what extent the perceived increases in cognitive load reveal productive processing leading to comprehension or processing difficulty without effective understanding.

Overall, these limitations advocate that while the study offers robust evidence that behavioral eye-tracking metrics are sensitive to variations in textual complexity, additional research is required to clarify the link between these measures, cognitive load, and reading outcomes across contexts.
