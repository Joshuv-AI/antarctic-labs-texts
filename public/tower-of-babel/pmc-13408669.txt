Closed-loop iron chelate recycling via molecularly imprinted hydrogels suppresses ferroptosis
Authors: Yubin Yao, Ting Ying, Chenyu Zong, Tao Ding, Qi Li, Zeyu Han, Fei Wang, Wenguo Cui, Mingzhu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of oxidative cell death, has emerged as a key driver of osteoarthritis, yet therapeutic strategies remain limited by the inability to safely eliminate iron–chelate complexes after treatment. Their local accumulation can lead to secondary iron release, oxidative stress, and sustained tissue damage. This study presents a hydrogel-based system that enables selective recognition and removal of these complexes through engineered molecular “memory” sites. By encoding the structural features of iron–chelate complexes into the hydrogel network, this system captures and clears them after cellular export, thereby preventing their re-entry and uncontrolled degradation. This approach markedly improves the efficiency and specificity of complex removal compared to non-imprinted materials. In cell and animal models, it restores iron balance, suppresses ferroptosis, and protects cartilage integrity. These findings establish a closed-loop strategy for regulating iron homeostasis and highlight a generalizable materials-based framework for treating iron-driven degenerative diseases.

Introduction

Ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death characterized by an imbalance in intracellular iron homeostasis, has garnered increasing attention for its role in osteoarthritis (OA)1–3. Among the therapeutic strategies for ferroptosis, chelation therapy has been considered an effective intervention4. By reducing the concentration of free iron ions both intracellularly and extracellularly, chelators can significantly suppress ROS generation and mitochondrial damage, thereby inhibiting the progression of ferroptosis5–7. However, despite showing efficacy in the early stages of ferroptosis, chelation agents face significant challenges regarding their metabolism and clearance in vivo8. Traditional iron chelators such as deferoxamine (DFO), deferiprone (DFX), and nanoparticle delivery systems often fail to eliminate the chelated iron in a timely manner9, particularly in environments like the joint cavity, which are enclosed or exhibit low blood flow10. As a result, chelates tend to accumulate in the local area, triggering a series of adverse reactions.

Firstly, due to local retention, the chelated complexes may be re-uptaken by surrounding cells via endocytosis11,12, leading to excessive iron depletion or the induction of oxidative stress, thus exacerbating secondary cell damage13. Secondly, many chelation materials, especially nanoparticles, exhibit sustained-release properties14,15. This means that, in the local environment, the chelated complexes may slowly release or degrade, leading to a re-release of iron ions, which could elevate the local iron concentration again and induce “secondary ferroptosis” or cause a ROS explosion16–18. In addition, chelated complexes may be recognized by the immune system as “danger signals,” triggering local immune activation and chronic inflammatory responses19–22, further aggravating tissue damage and pathological progression. Therefore, despite the theoretical potential of chelation therapy to intervene in ferroptosis, the lack of subsequent metabolic control and safe clearance of the chelated products significantly limits its therapeutic effectiveness and could potentially trigger a new cycle of cell damage10. This issue has become a major obstacle to the clinical application of ferroptosis intervention strategies, highlighting the urgent need to develop new systems capable of precisely recognizing and recycling iron chelates.

To address the aforementioned issue of chelate recycling, this study proposes a molecularly imprinted hydrogel microspheres (MIHM) system, which enables precise recognition and efficient recycling of chelated complexes after exocytosis, thus preventing their dispersion and reuptake (Fig. 1). Molecular imprinting technology (MIT) creates “molecular memory sites” in the hydrogel microspheres by using the template molecules (i.e., iron chelates) as recognition cores, and crosslinking functional monomers and crosslinkers23–25. This allows the prepared hydrogel microspheres to specifically bind to the target complexes. In this study, mesoporous polydopamine (mPDA) is utilized as a functional material for chelating intracellular iron overload. mPDA is rich in phenolic hydroxyl and amine groups, which can form stable complexes with Fe2+, while also possessing antioxidant properties26,27. Furthermore, by using the “mPDA–Fe” complex as a template molecule, hydrogel microspheres are prepared that can recognize its morphology and surface functional groups, enabling the complexes to be precisely recognized and efficiently adsorbed by MIHM once they are released into the extracellular space. Compared to the non-imprinted hydrogel microspheres (NIHM), MIHM demonstrated a 3-fold improvement in the recovery efficiency of mPDA-Fe. In addition, in multiple adsorption-desorption cycles, the deviation in its Q value was only 3% of the average value, indicating that MIHM not only exhibits high specificity for the substrate but also maintains robust stability throughout the adsorption process. This strategy not only effectively blocks the “free diffusion–cell reuptake” pathway of the chelates, but also utilizes the excellent biodegradability of the hydrogel material to enable subsequent self-clearing28. This closed-loop regulatory system realizes the complete therapeutic cycle from iron chelation, excretion to external recycling, breaking through the passive logic of traditional biological nanomaterials in vivo, which suffer from uncontrolled metabolism and insufficient clearance. Results

Association between osteoarthritis and ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is a form of programmed cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. It has recently been implicated in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including cancer and neurodegenerative disorders29,30. Growing evidence suggests a mechanistic link between ferroptosis and osteoarthritis (OA), drawing increasing scientific attention. Clinical studies have shown significantly elevated iron levels in the synovial fluid of OA patients. Serum ferritin concentrations also correlate positively with the severity of articular cartilage degeneration4,5,31. While clinical findings support the association between OA and ferroptosis, the molecular mechanisms underlying ferroptosis-induced cartilage degeneration remain poorly understood.

This study hypothesizes that disrupted iron homeostasis in chondrocytes during OA progression leads to intracellular iron overload, triggering ferroptotic cell death and contributing to cartilage degeneration. To test this hypothesis, bioinformatic analyses were performed using publicly available datasets. We compared the expression profiles of ferroptosis-related proteins in normal and OA cartilage samples from both human and rat models to elucidate the potential role of ferroptosis in OA pathophysiology.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed on RNA-sequencing data from 12 human knee cartilage samples—8 from OA patients and 4 from healthy controls—available in a public database. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a key suppressor of ferroptosis, regulates intracellular lipid hydroperoxide levels to prevent oxidative damage. Ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), a major subunit of ferritin, is essential for intracellular iron storage and the maintenance of iron homeostasis. To further validate ferroptosis involvement in OA, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for GPX4 and FTH1 was conducted on human OA and healthy cartilage tissues collected during orthopedic surgeries (Fig. 2B). Quantitative analysis (Fig. 2C) revealed significantly higher proportions of GPX4- and FTH1-positive areas in healthy cartilage compared to OA samples. To confirm these findings at the protein level, proteins were extracted from clinical cartilage tissues and analyzed by western blotting (WB). The results (Fig. 2D) showed that GPX4 and FTH1 expression levels in healthy cartilage were approximately threefold and twofold higher, respectively, than in OA cartilage. In addition, the expression of type II collagen—a key marker of cartilage integrity—was significantly reduced in OA cartilage compared to healthy tissue (Fig. 2E). To examine cross-species consistency, IHC analysis was extended to knee cartilage from healthy and OA-induced rat models. Consistent with the human data, GPX4 and FTH1 levels were significantly higher in healthy rat cartilage (Fig. 2F, G), further supporting the role of ferroptosis in OA pathogenesis.

To validate cartilage degeneration and confirm osteoarthritis (OA) pathology, Safranin O/Fast Green (S&O/FG) staining was performed on both human and rat cartilage samples. As shown in Fig. 2H, I, normal cartilage exhibited intense Safranin O staining, indicative of preserved proteoglycan content and intact cartilage architecture. In contrast, OA cartilage samples displayed markedly reduced Safranin O staining, accompanied by structural disorganization and thinning of the cartilage layer, consistent with characteristic features of OA-associated cartilage degeneration.

In parallel, ferritin expression was assessed by immunohistochemical staining in the same cartilage samples. Compared with normal cartilage, OA cartilage from both human and rat specimens showed a pronounced reduction in ferritin-positive staining (Fig. 2H, I). Quantitative analysis further confirmed a significant decrease in the ferritin-positive area in OA cartilage relative to normal controls (Fig. 2J, K), indicating impaired iron-storage capacity associated with OA progression.

Collectively, the results strongly suggest that ferroptosis contributes to the initiation and progression of OA and may represent a critical pathological mechanism underlying cartilage degeneration.

mPDA Attenuates chondrocyte ferroptosis via chelation of intracellular iron overload

Building on previous in vivo findings linking ferroptosis to OA, we investigated whether mesoporous polydopamine (mPDA) could attenuate chondrocyte ferroptosis by regulating intracellular iron homeostasis. Disruption of iron homeostasis is a key trigger of ferroptosis. Excess labile iron catalyzes Fenton reactions, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that cause oxidative damage and cell death32. mPDA possesses strong iron-chelating properties and binds free iron ions to form stable complexes, thereby reducing intracellular iron overload33.

To assess the anti-ferroptotic effects of mPDA, an in vitro chondrocyte ferroptosis model was established. Hemin, a classical ferroptosis inducer, promotes intracellular iron overload and lipid peroxidation, leading to ferroptotic cell death34,35. Based on CCK-8 assay results across varying hemin concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 1), 200 μM was selected to induce ferroptosis in chondrocytes. The protective effects of different mPDA concentrations against hemin-induced cytotoxicity were then evaluated. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, mPDA concentrations ≥ 20 mg/ml resulted in cell viabilities comparable to the control group, indicating this as the minimal effective dose. Therefore, 20 mg/ml mPDA was used in all subsequent experiments.

To assess the anti-ferroptotic efficacy of mPDA, chondrocytes were assigned to four groups: Control, Hemin, Hemin + mPDA, and Hemin + deferoxamine (Hemin + DFO). DFO, an FDA-approved iron chelator, served as a positive control. Intracellular ferrous iron (Fe2+) levels were first quantified using the FerroOrange probe. Fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 3A) and flow cytometry (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 3A) both revealed markedly elevated Fe2+ levels in the Hemin group, indicating disrupted iron homeostasis. Iron overload–induced ROS production is a hallmark of ferroptosis, primarily driven by hydroxyl radicals from Fenton reactions attacking polyunsaturated fatty acids in membrane lipids. To evaluate mPDA’s antioxidative capacity, intracellular ROS and lipid peroxidation were measured using fluorescent probes. Fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry (Fig. 3C–F and Supplementary Fig. 3B–C) both showed significant increases in ROS and lipid peroxidation in the Hemin group. Both mPDA and DFO significantly reduced ROS and lipid peroxidation, although levels remained slightly elevated in the Hemin + DFO group compared to the control. These results indicate that mPDA provides superior protection against oxidative stress and ferroptosis.

The superior efficacy of mPDA is supported by its intracellular accessibility and redox activity. Live-cell confocal imaging showed that Cy5-labeled mPDA was readily detected inside chondrocytes within 1 h. Its co-localization with LysoTracker-labeled lysosomes decreased over time, suggesting partial lysosomal escape and intracellular redistribution (Supplementary Fig. 4). In contrast, DFO is less membrane permeable and primarily chelates extracellular iron, which may limit its ability to rapidly reduce the labile intracellular iron pool. In addition, the catechol groups in mPDA provide intrinsic antioxidant capacity, which may further dampen ROS and lipid peroxidation.

Expression of key ferroptosis-related proteins was further examined across treatment groups. Western blotting (Fig. 3G, H) revealed significant downregulation of GPX4 and FTH1 in the Hemin group compared to all other groups. This decrease may be due to GPX4 depletion from lipid peroxidation and inhibited FTH1 synthesis. Conversely, transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1), which mediates iron uptake, was upregulated in the Hemin group, indicating a dysregulated feedback loop under oxidative stress. In addition, SLC7A11, a subunit of the system Xc⁻ antiporter essential for cystine import and glutathione synthesis, was significantly downregulated in the Hemin group, further impairing antioxidant defense and promoting ferroptosis sensitivity.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that mPDA exerts strong anti-ferroptotic effects by efficiently chelating intracellular iron and reducing ROS and lipid peroxidation, thereby restoring iron and redox homeostasis in chondrocytes more effectively than DFO.

mPDA Mitigates mitochondrial damage–induced cGAS–STING pathway activation during ferroptosis

Previous findings demonstrated that mPDA effectively protects chondrocytes from ferroptosis by chelating intracellular iron overload. To further investigate the underlying mechanisms, transcriptome sequencing was performed on chondrocytes from the Hemin group and Hemin + mPDA group. Differential gene expression analysis revealed significant transcriptional differences between the two groups, as shown by volcano plots, DEG bar charts, and hierarchical clustering heatmaps (Supplementary Figs. 5–7). Principal component analysis (PCA) confirmed strong reproducibility among biological replicates (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that, compared with the Hemin + mPDA group, the Hemin group exhibited significant enrichment of ferroptosis-related biological processes (Supplementary Fig. 9A), indicating a prominent involvement of iron-dependent cell death in OA-associated transcriptional alterations. Consistent with these findings, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified multiple inflammatory and stress-related signaling pathways as significantly enriched, including the TNF signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. 9B). Notably, the cytosolic DNA–sensing pathway was among the most significantly enriched pathways, suggesting activation of DNA-triggered inflammatory signaling in OA cartilage. The cGAS–STING pathway, a central component of the cytosolic DNA-sensing system, is activated when cGAS detects cytoplasmic DNA and synthesizes cGAMP, which then activates STING and initiates inflammatory signaling36–38. Mitochondrial damage, a typical response to oxidative stress, can release mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytoplasm, strongly activating the cGAS–STING pathway39–41. To examine this possibility, mitochondrial integrity was assessed in all treatment groups.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assessed using the JC-1 probe showed significant depolarization in the Hemin group, whereas Hemin + mPDA group and Hemin + DFO group-maintained potentials comparable to the control group (Fig. 4C). Transmission electron microscopy revealed that Hemin-treated mitochondria showed severe structural damage, including condensed matrices and cristae loss, while mitochondria in other groups remained intact (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that iron chelators protect mitochondria from oxidative stress-induced damage by reducing intracellular iron overload.

Live-cell confocal imaging showed sparse extranuclear, extramitochondrial DNA-associated puncta under basal conditions, whereas the Hemin group markedly increased the number of cytosolic DNA foci per cell (Fig. 4E, F). Notably, both mPDA and DFO pretreatment significantly reduced hemin-induced accumulation of cytosolic DNA foci, indicating effective preservation of mitochondrial integrity under iron overload conditions. These results suggest that both iron chelation strategies mitigate iron-induced mitochondrial damage and suppress the release of mtDNA-associated DNA into the cytosol.

To further validate the mechanism, expression levels of cGAS–STING components and cartilage matrix markers were examined. Western blotting (Fig. 4G, H) revealed strong activation of the cGAS–STING pathway in Hemin-treated chondrocytes, as indicated by elevated levels of p-STING and p-TBK1. This was accompanied by increased MMP13 and decreased type II collagen (Col II), indicating elevated inflammation and matrix degradation. Both mPDA and DFO suppressed pathway activation and partially reversed inflammation and matrix degradation, as evidenced by restored Col II expression. To further test causality, chondrocytes were treated with the mitochondrial uncoupler CCCP. CCCP robustly increased cGAS and enhanced phosphorylation of STING and TBK1. mPDA pretreatment significantly attenuated this activation (Supplementary Fig. 10), supporting that mitochondrial stress acts upstream of cGAS–STING signaling in chondrocytes.

Collectively, these results suggest that iron overload induces mitochondrial damage and mtDNA release, thereby activating the cGAS–STING pathway and amplifying inflammation and chondrocyte injury. Iron chelation therapy—especially with mPDA—offers promising protection against iron-induced cartilage degeneration by preserving mitochondrial integrity and preventing cGAS–STING–mediated inflammation.

Molecularly imprinted hydrogel microspheres enable specific recognition and reversible capture of mPDA–Fe chelates

Previous in vitro experiments confirmed the beneficial effects of mesoporous polydopamine (mPDA) in mitigating iron overload–induced cellular damage. However, osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic and complex disease, and the joint cavity is a relatively enclosed, poorly perfused space with limited vascular and lymphatic circulation. As a result, drug clearance and metabolism in the articular cavity are significantly slower than in other tissues42. Due to these factors, prolonged retention of mPDA–Fe complexes post-chelation raises potential safety concerns. mPDA’s intrinsic sustained-release properties may result in gradual re-release of chelated iron into the joint space, leading to re-accumulation of free iron ions43. These released ions may be reabsorbed by chondrocytes and synoviocytes, reinitiating oxidative damage and ferroptosis, and ultimately accelerating cartilage degeneration.

To address these limitations, effective removal of mPDA–Fe complexes from the joint environment is essential for establishing a complete and controlled therapeutic loop for OA. Accordingly, we developed a molecularly imprinted hydrogel microsphere (MIHM) system capable of specifically recognizing and efficiently recovering mPDA–Fe complexes using molecular imprinting technology. Chelate formation was first confirmed by characterizing mPDA and mPDA–Fe. Electron microscopy revealed distinct morphological differences: mPDA showed a well-defined mesoporous structure, while mPDA–Fe appeared less structured after chelation (Fig. 5A). Both had similar average particle sizes ( ~ 150–200 nm; Fig. 5B). mPDA carried a negative surface charge (– 34.28 mV), while mPDA–Fe complexes showed positive zeta potentials (10.90 and 16.99 mV for Fe2+ and Fe3+, respectively; Fig. 5C). Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping and spectral analysis (Fig. 5D–F) further confirmed successful mPDA–Fe complex formation. Using mPDA–Fe as a template, hybrid HAMA (hyaluronic acid methacrylate) microspheres were fabricated via a microfluidic strategy. After polymerization and template removal, the microspheres exhibited highly specific recognition sites for mPDA–Fe, constituting the MIHM system. Biocompatibility tests, including CCK-8 and live/dead staining (Supplementary Fig. 11), confirmed the favorable biocompatibility of both mPDA and MIHM.

Non-imprinted hydrogel microspheres (NIHM) served as controls for evaluating adsorption performance. Adsorption isotherms (Fig. 5H) showed that MIHM’s mPDA–Fe binding capacity increased with concentration up to 15 μM, followed by saturation. At 20 μM, MIHM reached a maximum adsorption capacity (Qmax) of 0.914 μM/g, significantly exceeding that of NIHM (0.321 μM/g). In adsorption kinetics tests (Fig. 5I), MIHM reached 90% equilibrium within 60 minutes, while NIHM required over 180 min to reach a lower capacity.

To further validate MIHM’s specificity, a competitive adsorption assay was conducted using equimolar quantities of MIHM and NIHM. Even under competition, MIHM retained high affinity for mPDA–Fe, with an imprinting factor (α) of 2.50 versus 0.46 for NIHM (Fig. 5J), indicating strong specificity unaffected by non-specific templates. Reusability and binding stability of MIHM were evaluated over six adsorption–desorption cycles (Fig. 5K). MIHM maintained consistent Q values (0.64–0.68), significantly outperforming NIHM (0.26–0.29), with stable imprinting factors (α = 2.24–2.58), confirming high reusability and specificity. Fluorescence imaging visualized selective binding, desorption, and rebinding of MIHM, directly validating its recognition capability (Fig. 5L).

Collectively, the results show that MIHM offers high specificity, high adsorption efficiency, and robust stability in recognizing and recovering mPDA–Fe complexes. This system represents a safe, effective, and reliable platform for precise post-chelation clearance in vivo, enabling a closed-loop therapeutic strategy for OA management.

Histological evaluation using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Safranin O–Fast Green (S&O) staining (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. 13A) further corroborated the micro-CT findings. Cartilage from the sham group displayed a smooth articular surface with intact hyaline cartilage and uniform proteoglycan staining. To further assess joint inflammation, synovial pathology was examined using low-magnification H&E staining, including both articular cartilage and adjacent synovium (Supplementary Fig. 14). Pronounced synovial hyperplasia and inflammatory cell infiltration were observed in the Hemin, mPDA, mPDA + NIHM, and DMM groups, whereas synovial architecture was largely preserved in the Sham, mPDA + MIHM, and DFO groups.

DFO treatment produced similar trends, further supporting the involvement of iron-dependent mechanisms in OA progression.

Compared with the sham group, the DMM-only group exhibited significantly increased expression of cGAS, p-STING, and p-TBK1, indicating activation of the cGAS–STING signaling axis during OA progression. The combined application of mPDA and MIHM provides superior therapeutic efficacy by simultaneously chelating excess iron and enabling selective recognition and removal of mPDA–Fe complexes, thereby mitigating ferroptosis and downstream inflammatory signaling in OA cartilage.

Discussion

Iron dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a key driver of osteoarthritis (OA), linking metabolic stress, ferroptosis, and chronic inflammation. Classical iron chelators such as deferoxamine (DFO) and deferiprone can reduce iron overload and ferroptosis-related injury in several disease models. However, their use in OA may be limited by rapid clearance, limited joint retention, and potential off-target exposure after repeated dosing.

In this study, DFO served as a positive control and partially alleviated OA pathology, supporting a central role for iron-dependent mechanisms. Notably, mPDA showed stronger and more sustained protection, especially when combined with MIHM. These results suggest that local iron scavenging is important and highlight the need to control the fate of chelated complexes within the joint microenvironment.

Mesoporous polydopamine (mPDA) differs from small-molecule chelators in both material properties and biological behavior. It has high iron-binding capacity, favorable biocompatibility, and intrinsic redox activity. After intra-articular injection, mPDA can remain locally for an extended period, supporting sustained suppression of iron-driven ferroptosis and downstream inflammatory signaling. However, chelation alone may not be sufficient if mPDA–Fe complexes are not properly handled. If such complexes degrade in situ, iron may be intermittently released and drive secondary ferroptotic stress.

MIHM addresses this limitation by selectively capturing mPDA–Fe complexes, thereby reducing delayed iron re-release and facilitating termination of iron-driven signaling. This functional control is supported by the degradation behavior of MIHM in simulated synovial fluid, where the hydrogel matrix undergoes slow and predictable erosion over several weeks without abrupt structural collapse (Supplementary Fig. 15). Such a degradation profile aligns well with the therapeutic time scale required for intra-articular intervention in OA.

Longitudinal in vivo imaging further revealed that MIHM exhibits sustained yet self-limited retention within the joint cavity, with approximately half of the initial signal persisting at four weeks and near-complete clearance by eight weeks (Supplementary Fig. 16). This retention–clearance behavior ensures effective local function while avoiding long-term accumulation.

The iron-binding behavior of mPDA was largely preserved under joint-mimicking conditions, with only a modest reduction in adsorption capacity observed in simulated synovial fluid compared with acetate buffer (Supplementary Fig. 17). This finding indicates that the complex biochemical composition of synovial fluid does not substantially compromise mPDA–Fe coordination, supporting the physiological relevance of the chelation mechanism.

A critical concern for degradable iron-chelating systems is whether material degradation leads to uncontrolled iron re-release, potentially disrupting local iron homeostasis. Time-resolved analyses of iron ion concentrations during both in vitro degradation and in vivo treatment provide important insights into this issue (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19). While free mPDA exhibited progressive iron accumulation and pronounced concentration fluctuations over time, the mPDA+MIHM system consistently maintained iron levels within a narrow and low-amplitude range throughout degradation.

Notably, no abrupt iron surge was detected during MIHM matrix disassembly, either in vitro or within the synovial fluid in vivo, indicating that iron coordination within the molecularly imprinted cavities remains stable even as the hydrogel network gradually degrades. As a result, iron concentrations in the mPDA+MIHM group remained well below levels associated with ferroptosis-prone microenvironments, in contrast to the sustained elevation observed in the mPDA-only group. Rather than acting as a degradable iron reservoir, MIHM functions as a dynamic iron-buffering system, in which gradual matrix degradation is coupled to continuous re-binding of iron within imprinted coordination cavities, thereby preventing secondary ferroptotic risk during material turnover. Consistent with the controlled clearance behavior, histological examination of major organs revealed no detectable pathological alterations across all treatment groups, indicating that the mPDA+MIHM system does not induce observable systemic toxicity during degradation and clearance (Supplementary Fig. 20).

Molecular imprinting technology (MIT) has been widely used in chemical sensing, catalysis, and drug delivery, yet its role as an active regulatory component in vivo remains less developed. Here, MIHM extends molecular imprinting from static recognition to a therapeutic function, acting as a post-chelation safeguard that selectively captures chelated intermediates and limits rebound iron stress. Notably, the recognition and adsorption performance of MIHM was largely independent of microsphere size within the tested range, indicating that chelate capture is governed by the density and affinity of imprinted binding sites rather than geometric factors (Supplementary Fig. 21).

Beyond osteoarthritis, the conceptual framework established in this study may be applicable to other diseases associated with iron dysregulation, including certain neurodegenerative disorders and cancers. Aberrant iron accumulation, ferroptosis susceptibility, and iron-driven inflammatory signaling are increasingly recognized as shared pathological features across these disease categories. From a design perspective, the MIHM platform integrates molecular selectivity with degradability, enabling closed-loop regulation of iron homeostasis rather than indiscriminate iron removal.

This principle could, in theory, be adapted to disease-specific contexts by tailoring imprinting motifs or delivery routes to distinct iron-rich microenvironments. For example, in oncology, MIHM-like systems could be co-designed to recognize Fe-based nanotherapeutics or ferroptosis-inducing agents, facilitating their localized clearance after treatment and thereby reducing systemic iron-related toxicity. In neurodegenerative conditions, where excess labile iron in the central nervous system has been implicated in disease progression, intrathecal or cerebrospinal fluid–compatible adaptations of such materials may offer a strategy for modulating ferroptotic stress.

Importantly, these potential extensions remain conceptual at this stage. Differences in biological microenvironment, iron trafficking dynamics, delivery constraints, and safety considerations will require disease-specific optimization and rigorous in vivo validation. Together, this work establishes MIHM not as a disease-specific solution, but as a modular strategy for precision iron regulation that may inspire future material designs targeting iron-driven pathologies.

In summary, this study establishes a closed-loop therapeutic strategy that integrates iron chelation, selective complex recognition, and controlled clearance to suppress ferroptosis-driven OA progression. By combining mPDA with MIHM, we demonstrate how material-based interventions can be engineered not only to neutralize pathological factors but also to regulate their metabolic fate, offering a generalizable blueprint for precision treatment of iron-mediated inflammatory diseases.

Methods

Ethics declaration

Clinical samples

The collection and use of human articular cartilage samples were approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. TREC2023-KY050). Human cartilage tissues were obtained from patients undergoing joint replacement or joint fusion surgery at the Department of Foot and Ankle Surgery, Beijing Tongren Hospital. A total of six osteoarthritic (OA) cartilage samples were collected from three patients diagnosed with osteoarthritis, and three normal cartilage samples were obtained from three healthy donors. All participants received a detailed explanation of the study objectives and experimental procedures prior to enrollment, and written informed consent was obtained from all donors. No financial compensation was provided to participants.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Phenotek Biotechnology (Protocol No. AUP-20240507-01) and were performed in compliance with all relevant ethical regulations for animal research. Forty-two male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (8 weeks old, 220–250 g) were used in this study. Animals were maintained under standard laboratory conditions at approximately 25 °C with a 12 h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. Osteoarthritis was induced in the right knee joint using the destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) model. Rats were randomly assigned to seven experimental groups (n = 6 per group): Sham, Hemin, mPDA, mPDA+NIHM, mPDA+MIHM, DMM, and DFO. All surgical procedures were performed under inhalation anesthesia with 2% isoflurane, and animal health status, joint swelling, and locomotor activity were monitored throughout the study.

Synthesis of mPDA nanoparticles

For the preparation of mesoporous polydopamine (mPDA) nanoparticles, a mixture was created by combining 65 mL of ultrapure water (ddH₂O), 60 mL of absolute ethanol, 360 mg of Pluronic F127 as a surfactant, and 360 mg of trimethylbenzene (TMB). Subsequently, 9 mL of Tris buffer (at 10 mg/mL) and 1 mL of dopamine hydrochloride solution (60 mg/mL) were incorporated sequentially. The reaction mixture was agitated at ambient temperature for 24 h. Upon completion, the product was aliquoted into six 50 mL centrifuge tubes, with 10 mL of acetone added to each for precipitation. Following vigorous mixing, centrifugation occurred at 11,000 rpm for 10 min, and the upper layer was removed. An ethanol-acetone blend (2:1 ratio) was introduced, followed by ultrasonication for 30 minutes to aid dispersion and extraction. This washing cycle—centrifugation and supernatant discard—was repeated thrice. After the last spin, ethanol was added in sufficient quantity, the particles were redispersed via sonication, and the suspension was preserved at 4 °C until needed.

Adsorption isotherm measurement of Fe2+ on mPDA nanoparticles

To assess the peak adsorption capability of mesoporous polydopamine (mPDA) nanoparticles toward ferrous ions (Fe²⁺), isotherms were generated through a colorimetric assay employing 1,10-phenanthroline spectrophotometry. Fresh FeCl₂ solutions at concentrations of 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 mM were formulated in an acetate buffer (HAc-NaAc, pH 6.5) to preserve Fe2+ stability. Each 1 mL aliquot was combined with 1 mg of pre-purified mPDA powder, which had been rinsed via ethanol centrifugation to eliminate contaminants. Incubation proceeded at 37 °C in a shaking incubator for 4–5 h to attain saturation. Samples were then spun at 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes to isolate supernatants. From each, 200 μL was pipetted into a 96-well plate, supplemented with 7 μL of 10 mM 1,10-phenanthroline, and mixed via vortexing for 20 s to develop color.

Absorbance of the Fe2+-phenanthroline complex, appearing orange, was recorded at 510 nm on a plate reader (e.g., BioTek Epoch). A standard curve from identically processed Fe2+ references facilitated quantification of remaining Fe2+. Adsorption was derived by deducting final from initial concentrations.

Isolation of primary rat chondrocytes

Chondrocytes were extracted from the joint cartilage of neonatal Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats aged 3–5 days. Under aseptic conditions post-euthanasia, knee joints from both sides were isolated, and cartilage was meticulously excised from femoral condyles and tibial platforms using fine surgical tools. Tissues were cleansed thrice in PBS with 1% penicillin-streptomycin to clear residues. Minced into ~ 1 mm³ pieces, the fragments underwent initial digestion in 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min with mild stirring to detach superficial elements. Supernatant was discarded, and further enzymatic breakdown occurred in 0.2% type II collagenase (Sigma, USA) dissolved in high-glucose Alpha-MEM for 6–8 h at 37 °C with constant gentle oscillation.

The digest was strained through a 70 μm mesh to filter debris, then centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min. Pelleted cells were resuspended in Alpha-MEM enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, then cultured in T-25 flasks at 37 °C in 5% CO2 humidified air. Medium refreshment occurred every 48 hours. Experiments utilized passages 1–2 to maintain cellular phenotype.

Cell viability assay for ferroptosis induction using Hemin

To identify suitable Hemin dosage and exposure time for triggering ferroptosis in rat chondrocytes, viability was quantified via Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8). Second-passage cells were plated at 5 × 10³ per well in 96-well formats and adhered overnight in Alpha-MEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Treatments involved Hemin at 5 μM, 10 μM, 20 μM, 40 μM, 80 μM, 160 μM, or 320 μM for 12, 24, or 48 hours at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Controls remained untreated, with n = 6 replicates per condition.

Post-exposure, the medium was aspirated, and 100 μL of fresh Alpha-MEM containing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation for 2 hours at 37 °C. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). The resulting OD values were used to evaluate cell viability across different treatment conditions. Dose- and time-dependent response profiles were analyzed to determine the optimal parameters for ferroptosis induction.

Screening of mPDA minimum effective dose via CCK-8 assay

From prior viability data, 200 μM Hemin was selected as the ferroptosis inducer, balancing cytotoxicity with assay feasibility. To pinpoint the lowest effective mPDA concentration countering Hemin-induced ferroptosis, another CCK-8 test was conducted. Passage-2 chondrocytes at 5 × 10³/well in 96-well plates adhered for 12 h. Pretreatment involved 100 μL mPDA-medium at 10–100 mg/mL (in 10 mg/mL increments) for 2 h.

Subsequently, 1 μL of 20 mM Hemin was added to each well to achieve a final concentration of 200 μM, followed by further incubation for 12, 24, or 48 h (n = 6 per group). After treatment, the culture medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh Alpha-MEM containing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent, and cells were incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. The OD at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). The resulting OD values were compared with those of the Hemin-only group to evaluate the protective effect of mPDA. The concentration that resulted in a marked increase in OD relative to the Hemin group was defined as the minimum effective dose for suppressing ferroptosis.

Evaluation of the biocompatibility of mPDA and MIHM via CCK-8 assay

Biocompatibility of mesoporous polydopamine (mPDA), molecularly imprinted hydrogel microspheres (MIHM), and their combination (mPDA+MIHM) toward rat chondrocytes was examined using CCK-8 (Dojindo, Japan). Passage-2 cells were seeded at 5 × 10³/well in 96-well plates in supplemented Alpha-MEM and settled overnight at 37 °C, 5% CO2.

Subsequently, cells were treated with the following conditions: mPDA (20 mg/mL), MIHM (2 mg/mL), and a combination of mPDA (20 mg/mL) with MIHM (2 mg/mL). Each experimental group included six replicates (n = 6), along with an untreated control group. Cells were incubated under these conditions for 12, 24, or 48 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

After treatment, the culture medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh Alpha-MEM containing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent, followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). The resulting OD values were directly compared among groups to assess relative cell viability, thereby reflecting the effects of mPDA, MIHM, and their combination on chondrocyte metabolic activity in a time-dependent manner.

Flow cytometry and fluorescence imaging of ROS, lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial membrane potential and intracellular Fe2+

The effects of mesoporous polydopamine (mPDA) and deferoxamine (DFO) on ferroptosis-associated markers, including reactive oxygen species (ROS), lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), and intracellular Fe²⁺ levels, were evaluated using flow cytometry and fluorescence imaging. Chondrocytes were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 12 h prior to treatment. Cells were divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): Control (untreated), Hemin (200 μM Hemin alone), Hemin + mPDA (20 mg/mL mPDA pretreatment followed by 200 μM Hemin), and Hemin + DFO (100 μM DFO pretreatment followed by 200 μM Hemin). Pretreatment with mPDA or DFO was performed for 2 h before Hemin exposure. Subsequently, Hemin was added to achieve a final concentration of 200 μM, and cells were incubated for a total of 48 h.

Following treatment, the culture medium was removed, and cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS. Fluorescent probes were prepared in serum-free medium according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Intracellular ROS levels were detected using H2DCFDA (Beyotime, China), lipid peroxidation was assessed using BODIPY™ 581/591 C11 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was evaluated using JC-1 dye (Beyotime, China), and intracellular Fe2+ levels were detected using FerroOrange (Dojindo, Japan). Cells were incubated with the corresponding probes at 37 °C for 20–30 min in the dark.

After staining, cells used for ROS, lipid peroxidation, and intracellular Fe²⁺ analyses were digested with trypsin, collected, and subjected to flow cytometry analysis. Mitochondrial membrane potential was analyzed by fluorescence microscopy based on the ratio of JC-1 aggregates to monomers. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity and flow cytometry data was performed using FlowJo software (v 10.0.7).

Public transcriptomic dataset analysis

Publicly available transcriptomic data from human chondrocytes were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database under accession number GSE246425. This dataset contains bulk RNA-seq profiles of human knee articular chondrocytes derived from healthy and osteoarthritic cartilage. Processed gene expression count data were downloaded from the GEO Series record and used for downstream analysis. Differentially expressed genes between healthy and osteoarthritic chondrocyte samples were identified using standard differential expression analysis. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R, and enriched pathways were ranked according to adjusted P-values to investigate biological pathways associated with osteoarthritis-related chondrocyte degeneration, with particular attention to ferroptosis-related pathways.

Transcriptome sequencing

Transcriptomic alterations from mPDA in ferroptotic chondrocytes were explored via RNA-seq. Samples came from Hemin (200 μM Hemin, 48 h) and Hemin + mPDA (20 mg/mL pretreatment + 200 μM Hemin, 48 h) groups (n = 3 each). RNA isolation used TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA), with quality checks on NanoDrop and Agilent 2100 (RIN > 7.0, OD260/280 1.8–2.0). Libraries were built with NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, USA) and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 for 150 bp paired reads.

Trimmomatic cleaned data, HISAT2 aligned to rat genome (Rnor_6.0), StringTie quantified as FPKM. DESeq2 identified differentials (adjusted p < 0.05, |log₂FC | ≥ 1). clusterProfiler in R handled GO/KEGG enrichments to reveal mPDA-modulated pathways.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for mitochondrial morphology analysis

Mitochondrial ultrastructure in ferroptosis and protective interventions was examined by TEM in Control, Hemin, Hemin + mPDA, and Hemin + DFO groups (n = 3). Post-48 h treatment, cells were trypsinized, pelleted at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and PBS-washed twice. Fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 M phosphate, pH 7.4) at 4 °C overnight followed, then 1% OsO₄ post-fixation for 1 h.

Dehydration in ethanol gradients, resin embedding, and polymerization preceded ultrathin sectioning (70–90 nm) on Leica EM UC7. Grids were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, imaged on JEOL JEM-1400 at 80–100 kV. Assessments targeted cristae, swelling, shrinkage, and membrane integrity in random fields.

Live-cell labeling and quantification of extramitochondrial mtDNA foci

Cells were seeded onto glass-bottom 24-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then assigned to four groups and treated as follows: Control; Hemin (200 μM hemin); Hemin + mPDA (cells pretreated with mPDA, 20 mg/mL, followed by 200 μM hemin); and Hemin + DFO (cells pretreated with deferoxamine (DFO), 100 μM, followed by 200 μM hemin). After 48 h of treatment, live-cell staining was performed sequentially to label mitochondria, DNA-associated signals, and nuclei.

For mitochondrial labeling, cells were incubated with MitoTracker Red (Beyotime, China) in complete culture medium for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). To label DNA-associated puncta in live cells, SYBR Green I (Invitrogen, S7563) was diluted 1:10,000 in complete culture medium and applied to cells (30 min, 37 °C, dark), followed by three PBS washes. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst dye (Beyotime, China) for 10 min in the dark, followed by three PBS washes. Cells were maintained in fresh culture medium during imaging, and images were acquired immediately using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Identical acquisition settings were applied across experimental groups to enable quantitative comparison.

Confocal images were processed and analyzed using Fiji ImageJ. Individual cells were delineated, and the cytoplasmic region of interest (ROI) was defined for each cell with reference to cell morphology and Hoechst nuclear staining. Binary masks for the nucleus (Hoechst), mitochondria (MitoTracker), and DNA-associated signals (SYBR Green I) were generated using consistent intensity thresholds across all groups. Extranuclear, extramitochondrial mtDNA-associated puncta were defined operationally by subtracting both the nuclear and mitochondrial masks from the total DNA-signal mask, retaining only puncta spatially excluded from the nucleus and mitochondrial networks. The number of extramitochondrial mtDNA-associated puncta was quantified on a per-cell basis.

Confocal imaging of cellular uptake and lysosomal escape of mPDA

To investigate the cellular internalization and intracellular trafficking of mPDA, including its ability to escape from lysosomes, mPDA was fluorescently labeled with Cy5 (Cy5–mPDA). Chondrocytes were seeded onto glass-bottom culture dishes and allowed to adhere for 24 h prior to imaging experiments.

For lysosomal labeling, cells were first incubated with LysoTracker™ Green DND-26 (Invitrogen, A66438) according to the manufacturer’s instructions to label acidic lysosomal compartments. Subsequently, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst dye. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), Cy5–mPDA was added to the culture medium, and cells were incubated under standard culture conditions.

Live-cell confocal laser scanning microscopy was performed at 1, 2, and 4 h after the addition of Cy5–mPDA to monitor its intracellular distribution and lysosomal association over time. All images were acquired using identical imaging parameters across time points to ensure quantitative comparability.

For quantitative analysis, confocal images were processed using Fiji ImageJ software. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated between the Cy5–mPDA (red) and LysoTracker (green) channels to assess the degree of spatial co-localization at each time point. Co-localization analysis was performed on multiple cells per condition, and data from three independent experiments were used for statistical analysis.

Functional validation of the cGAS–STING pathway

Chondrocytes were seeded and cultured for 24 h to allow cell attachment. Cells were then divided into three groups: Control (no treatment), CCCP, and mPDA + CCCP. For the mPDA + CCCP group, cells were pretreated with mPDA (20 mg/mL) for 30 min. Subsequently, both the CCCP group and the mPDA + CCCP group were stimulated with carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) (10 μM) for 6 h, whereas control cells received no treatment.

Following treatment, cells were collected and lysed for protein extraction. Activation of the cGAS–STING signaling pathway was evaluated by western blot analysis of key pathway components, including cGAS, phosphorylated STING (p-STING), and phosphorylated TBK1 (p-TBK1).

Preparation of molecularly imprinted hydrogel microspheres (MIHMs)

Lyophilized hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA) was dissolved in a pre-formed mPDA–Fe chelates solution to obtain a 4% (w/v) precursor solution and stirred for 24 h to ensure complete dissolution and homogeneity. Subsequently, lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) photoinitiator was added at a final concentration of 0.5% (w/v), followed by gentle stirring until fully dissolved.

Monodisperse hydrogel microspheres were generated using a flow-focusing microfluidic device (Supplementary Fig. 22). The microfluidic chip featured fixed channel dimensions (width: 230 μm; height: 150 μm). The HAMA precursor solution containing mPDA–Fe chelates served as the dispersed phase, while mineral oil supplemented with 6% (w/v) Span 80 was used as the continuous phase. Droplet formation was achieved under laminar flow conditions, and microsphere diameter was controlled by adjusting the flow-rate ratio between the two phases. For the standard MIHM preparation, flow rates were set to generate microspheres with an average diameter of approximately 200 μm.

The generated droplets were collected and immediately subjected to ultraviolet (UV) irradiation (365 nm) for 15 min to induce photopolymerization and stabilize the hydrogel structure. After crosslinking, the oil phase was removed by repeated washing with acetone, yielding mPDA@HAMA composite microspheres.

To remove the mPDA–Fe templates and generate molecularly imprinted binding sites, the microspheres were first washed three times with Tris buffer (pH 8.0–8.5) and subsequently incubated in 0.2 M hydrochloric acid (HCl). This elution step effectively removed embedded mPDA–Fe complexes without disrupting the hydrogel network. The resulting MIHMs contained three-dimensional imprinted cavities with spatial and chemical complementarity to mPDA–Fe chelates, enabling selective recognition and reversible binding.

Animal model and intra-articular injection protocol

Experiments adhered to approvals from Phenotek Biotechnology’s IACUC (AUP-20240507-01). SD rats were housed at ~ 25 °C with 12-hour light/dark cycles and ad libitum access.

OA was induced via DMM (Supplementary Fig. 23) in the right knees. Forty-two male SD rats (8 weeks, 220–250 g) were grouped (n = 6): Sham, Hemin, mPDA, mPDA + NIHM, mPDA+MIHM, DMM and DFO. Sham involved exposure without transection; others received medial meniscotibial ligament cut under 2% isoflurane. Injections (50 μL) occurred on days 7, 28, 49: Sham (PBS), Hemin (200 μmol/mL hemin/PBS), mPDA (20 mg/mL mPDA/PBS), mPDA + NIHM (20 mg/mL mPDA + 2 mg/mL NIHM), mPDA + MIHM (20 mg/mL mPDA + 2 mg/mL MIHM), DMM (PBS), DFO solution (200 mg/L). Monitoring covered swelling, activity; joints were harvested for analysis.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis

Subchondral bone alterations were imaged via Micro-CT on rat knees. At 4/8 weeks post-op, six rats per group underwent scanning (Bruker Skyscan 1176) under isoflurane: 5 μm voxel, 65 kV, 385 μA, 200 ms exposure.

NRecon reconstructed; CTAn analyzed medial tibial subchondral ROI for BV/TV (%), Tb.N (mm−1), Tb.Th (mm), Tb.Sp (mm), BMD (mg/cm3). CTVol visualized 3D structures to gauge remodeling and treatment efficacy.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis

At 8 weeks, euthanasia preceded knee harvest. Fixation (4% paraformaldehyde, 48 hours), decalcification (10% EDTA, pH 7.4, 8 weeks), dehydration, paraffin embedding, and 5 μm sectioning followed. HE stained for morphology; Safranin O-Fast Green for proteoglycans.

IHC involved deparaffinization, citrate retrieval (pH 6.0), 5% BSA block, overnight 4 °C primary incubation: cGAS (CST, No. 79978, 1:200), p-STING (CST, No. 50907, 1:200), p-TBK1 (CST, No. 5483, 1:200), MMP13 (Abcam, ab39012, 1:200), Col II (Abcam, ab307674, 1:200). HRP-secondaries, DAB development, hematoxylin counterstain ensued. Zeiss microscopy and ImageJ semi-quantification completed analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (v 10.2.0). Data are presented as means ± SD or means ± SEM. Exact sample sizes are indicated in the corresponding figure legends and Source Data file. Two-group comparisons employed a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test; multi-group via one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. Colocalization PCC via ImageJ Coloc2 (v 2.16.0). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact P-values are reported whenever possible; values smaller than 0.0001 are presented as P < 0.0001.
