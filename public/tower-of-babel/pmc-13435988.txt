Having or losing a family physician: a protocol for a survey-based study of patient’s continuity of primary care experience in Quebec, Canada
Authors: Maude Laberge, Micaël Carrier, Mylaine Breton, Élise Boulanger, Amédé Gogovor, Géraldine Layani, Paolo Landa, Marie-Eve Poitras, Nadia Sourial, Erin Strumpf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionContinuity of care with a family physician (FP) is a core attribute of effective primary care and is associated with improved health outcomes, reduced service use and lower healthcare costs. In Canada, an ageing FP workforce has led to an increasing number of patients losing their FP, raising concerns about access to care and disruptions in care trajectories. While the benefits of continuity are well documented, evidence on the consequences of discontinuity in primary care, particularly from the patient perspective, remains limited. Relational discontinuity occurs when there is a disruption in the relationship between an FP and a patient, which may be related to the FP’s retirement or relocation or to the closure of a clinic.ObjectiveThis study aims to assess the effects of a disruption in the relational continuity with an FP on patient’s experience and their patterns of healthcare service use.Methods and analysisA descriptive cross-sectional survey will be administered to adults residing in Quebec, Canada’s second most populous province. A total sample of 1000 respondents will be recruited, including approximately 500 individuals who have lost their FP within the past 3 years, and 500 individuals currently registered with an FP. The questionnaire covers sociodemographic characteristics, health status, access to care, use of healthcare services, out-of-pocket costs and perceived impacts of discontinuity. Descriptive and regression analyses will be used to compare experiences and perceived effects between groups and to explore equity-related differences.Ethics and disseminationThe study has received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Board involving human participants at Université Laval. Findings will be shared with policymakers and healthcare stakeholders to inform them on the patient-reported consequences of primary care discontinuity and support the development of strategies to mitigate its impacts.

Introduction

In many countries, family physicians (FPs) are increasingly leaving their practice. For many, it is for retirement. For others, they are moving or changing their practice.1 2 The departure of FPs is a cause for concern because, in the context of a shortage, they often leave thousands of patients without an FP. Indeed, FP rates, which stand at 2.8 per 1000 inhabitants in Canada3 and 2.6 per 1000 inhabitants in Quebec,4 compare unfavourably to Austria (5), Italy (5) or Germany.3 4 The shortage of FPs can limit access to primary care and also the continuity of care, fundamental attributes of effective healthcare systems.5 The issue is so concerning that access to an FP or to a family medicine group (FMG) has become a key election issue in several Canadian provinces.6 7 In Quebec, various strategies have been implemented to improve access to primary care, such as the implementation of interprofessional teams,8 9 formal registration with a FP,10 financial incentives9 11 and access points with centralised lists.12–15

Continuity of care is a fundamental element of efficient primary care which in turn is the foundation of high-performing health systems.16–21 The most widely adopted definition of continuity is that proposed by Haggerty, which is based on three dimensions.22 Managerial continuity is that which supports efficient logistics in the delivery of care, timely access and consistency of care. Information continuity is defined by the accessibility and transmission of information between members of a healthcare team and the use of past health information (diagnoses, prescriptions, test results, etc.) and services to better address the patient’s current needs. Finally, relational continuity is the relationship that develops over time between a healthcare provider (generally an FP or in some cases a nurse practitioner) and the patient, fostering a relationship of trust. Scientific data show that individuals with a high level of relational continuity with an FP adhere better to prescribed medications, receive more preventive care, use emergency services less frequently and experience fewer potentially avoidable hospitalisations.23

Improved continuity of care has been associated with lower healthcare costs in China,24 South Korea,25 the USA26 and British Columbia.27 A recent systematic review of the literature links relational continuity with lower healthcare costs internationally.28 Continuity of primary care is also associated with lower mortality in South Korea,25 fewer emergency department visits in Israel,29 fewer hospitalisations in the USA26 and fewer potentially avoidable hospitalisations in Germany.30 Another study conducted in Quebec showed that improved continuity of care appears to be particularly beneficial for older adults by reducing emergency department visits.31 A systematic review of the literature reports that continuity of care appears to be linked to better health outcomes and greater patient satisfaction.32 When access to their FP is difficult, patients turn to walk-in clinics or emergency departments.33 These options represent additional entry points and allow access to care, but they could negatively impact continuity of care, preventive care and chronic disease management, according to a literature review in the USA.34 In the UK, continuity appears to have declined,35 but this trend does not appear to have been examined in other contexts.

While there is considerable research on continuity with an FP and its importance, the literature on the effects of a discontinuity of care is much more limited. Relational discontinuity occurs when there is a disruption in the relationship between an FP and a patient, which may be related to the FP’s retirement or relocation,36 or to the closure of a clinic.37 38 Depending on the context, the patient may be registered with another FP more or less quickly (either through a bulk transfer by the departing FP or by finding a new FP themselves) or may become unattached.

Using Swiss data from 2007 to 2014, Bischof and Kaiser38 found that the closure of primary care clinics (typically following the retirement of the FP) led to a 12% decrease in visits to an FP for patients whose clinic had closed, but to increases in hospital visits of 6% and visits to specialists of 11%, suggesting a substitution of primary care services for specialist care.38 The total number of outpatient visits decreased overall by 3%, but the cost per visit increased by 4%, with no overall effect on costs. There were no effects on mortality or hospitalisations, but the researchers highlighted a fragmentation of care. Furthermore, these effects may vary depending on whether patients live in an area with a low or high density of physicians (more possible substitutions for hospital and specialist visits) and whether they have chronic diseases.38 In Norway, a similar substitution effect of visits from one FP to other doctors appeared to occur after a discontinuity. Furthermore, patients aged 65 and older were more affected by a discontinuity with an FP, notably with an increased risk of potentially avoidable hospitalisation.39 A substitution of healthcare services following a discontinuity was also observed in Austria, with increased use of primary care services among individuals who were quickly taken on by a new FP.36

Context of the study

The literature on these discontinuities comes from countries where there may be no shortage of FPs, or where interdisciplinary team practice is underdeveloped or nonexistent, meaning that patients whose FP retires must find another source of primary care. In Quebec, Canada’s second most populous province, the context is different. On the one hand, the majority of FPs work in interprofessional teams, which could have distinct effects on the use and healthcare of patients who lose their registered FP, as some may continue to have access to primary care at the FMG. On the other hand, there is a formal FP registration mechanism, which is not the case everywhere. However, registered patients report difficulties seeing their FP within a reasonable timeframe, which could limit the level of continuity. The scarcity of FPs means that patients may have difficulty finding a new FP, unlike in European contexts where the density of FPs is much higher. Finally, the ability to replace FPs with greater use of services from physicians of other specialties may be limited by the relative scarcity (compared with European contexts) of these other physicians.

In 2008, when the proportion of Quebecers without an FP was 26%,40 Quebec created centralised waiting lists for unattached patients to help those seeking an FP find one and facilitate coordination by FPs.12 41 However, in 2023, only 73.3% of Quebecers were registered with an FP, a decline from 82% in 2019.42 The ageing of the FP population in Quebec (11.5% are over 65 years of age and 24% are 60 or older43) implies retirements that will disrupt the continuity of the patient-physician relationship. Other reasons can also lead to a discontinuity, such as a relocation by the patient or the FP, or a change in the FP’s practice (private practice, telemedicine services, clinic not affiliated with Quebec’s public health insurance—the RAMQ).44 There may also be interruptions in practice due to extended sick leave or parental leave. However, there is very little data on the impacts of such a disruption in the relationship between a patient and an FP, particularly in a context where it is difficult to find an FP accepting new patients, as is the case in Quebec.

The objective of this study is to assess the effects of a disruption in continuity of care with an FP on patients’ experience and their patterns of healthcare service use. Specifically, a Patient-Reported Experience Measure questionnaire was developed to examine the experiences of people as they navigate and use primary care services, including those individuals who have lost their FP and people who are registered with a physician. This includes examining patients’ perceptions of the patient–FP relationship, the value they attribute to continuity of care and the strategies they adopt following the loss of their FP. Understanding the consequences of discontinuity in primary care is increasingly important, particularly in the context of an ageing FP workforce and growing patient concerns regarding access to longitudinal care. A clearer understanding of how discontinuity affects access to care, service utilisation and associated costs may inform the development of targeted strategies to mitigate its negative impacts on patients and the healthcare system.

Methods

Data collection methods

We will conduct a descriptive cross-sectional study among individual attached and unattached patients. The results will be reported using the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) guidelines developed and recommended for survey-based studies.45 This study uses questions from a survey developed by our team and applied to the general adult population in Quebec, Canada. Several questionnaires exist to assess patient experience, including questionnaires specific to primary care experience. This newly developed questionnaire includes questions addressing discontinuity and its perceived effects according to patient’s perspective. This section aims at highlighting the challenges faced by unattached patients. The questionnaire is based on a questionnaire members of our team recently developed to measure patient experience with Primary Care Access Points (GAP),46 with modifications adapted to our context (discontinuity or not). The GAP is an organisational innovation aimed at directing unattached patients to the most suitable professional or service.47 This questionnaire has been used with over 13 000 patients46 and demonstrated excellent validity as a social imperative as defined by Kinnear et al.48 Briefly, the questionnaire has five sections (figure 1), and the number of questions will depend on the participants’ responses.

Overview of survey sections assessing primary care access, service use and patient-reported impacts. GAP, Primary Care Access Points.

Section 1 of the patient survey includes sociodemographic questions (eg, age group, gender identity, sex). A key question in this section is when the person has lost their FP. This will allow us to distinguish respondents who have experienced a discontinuity from those who have not. This section includes questions about the person’s characteristics and identities, including their origins (Indigenous, non-Indigenous), immigration status, length of residence in Canada, place of residence and perception of their physical and mental health. These questions will allow us to consider equity, diversity and inclusion (EDI) in our analyses. Section 2 of the survey includes questions about whether the person has an FP in their view, for how long they have had their FP, their perceived importance of the FP, their health needs and ease of access to care. Section 3 contains questions about the use of health services, including reasons for consultation, where the person went to obtain services, types of health professionals consulted, services obtained (particularly in prevention, follow-up and disease management), costs incurred, emergency room visits and reasons for visits, as well as foregoing care and the reasons for doing so. Section 4 of the survey focuses specifically on the effects of discontinuity in care. In this section, questions will address delays and perceived delays in accessing an FP, FP substitutions and satisfaction with these substitutions, and perceptions of the outcomes of service use.

Patients were involved in the late stage of protocol development. Specifically, we recruited five patients to participate in the cognitive validation of the questionnaires to ensure their comprehensibility by patients with diverse educational backgrounds and varying levels of healthcare knowledge. A professional external to the research team conducted a cognitive validation with the patients through interviews while they completed the test, and collecting feedback as well as insights into their thought processes. The questionnaire was subsequently refined based on retrospective analysis. The questionnaire was then tested by a linguist and an expert in EDI. Finally, section 5 of the survey is the previously developed section about the GAP adapted to our context. This section is useful as patient that lost their FP might have a different experience with the GAP. We expect data collection to take place over a 3-week to 4-week period starting during the Spring of 2026.

Sample characteristics

The questionnaire will be administered, in French and in English to adults residing in Quebec. The target number of participants is 1000, half of whom will be patients who have lost their FP and the other half, individuals registered with an FP. The survey parameters were established with a polling firm (Léger), specifying a diverse sample in terms of characteristics such as gender, origin, place of residence and socioeconomic status. A pre-test with Léger was conducted to ensure representativity of the sample was attainable. This number allows to obtain a representative sample of both populations. Indeed, based on publicly available data on the percentage of the population registered with a family doctor currently and 3 years ago and population growth, it can be estimated that over 300 000 people lost their FP during this period. We estimate that 385 people will be needed to have a representative sample of this population (with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error), and the same applies to the sample required to ensure representativeness of patients with an FP, giving us a total of 770 people. Increasing this number to 1000 will allow us to balance the samples as needed to ensure the desired diversity in our sample.

Questionnaire administration

The questionnaire is built and hosted on the REDCAP platform, allowing the data to be stored in the home institution. Access and distribution of the questionnaire will be administered by a rewards-based market research platform (Léger, Canada) via their online platform. Special links were designed to take respondents from the Leger platform and return them afterwards with a specific tag, depending on whether they completed the survey, were screened out, or if the target quota was met. The questionnaire is available in French and English to reflect Quebec’s demographics (over 90% of the population speaks French). By clicking on the link, people will have summary information about the questionnaire including the estimated time to complete it (10–15 min), the contact details of members of the research team to contact with any questions, as well as consent to participate and the information regarding ethical approval. Consent will be obtained by asking participants to select either ‘I consent’ or ‘I do not consent’. Participants who select the latter will not proceed to the survey.

Statistical analyses

They will include the descriptive statistics of the participants' characteristics with some stratification of their status of registration with an FP and presence of discontinuity. The graphical representations will be used to facilitate the visualisation of the results that appear most relevant. Logistic regression will be performed to estimate the associations of variables with the probability of having lost one’s FP. This will allow the identification of factors associated with a discontinuity. Furthermore, the descriptive statistics related to the use of health services will be examined. The perceived effects will be described, and we will explore how individual characteristics affect these perceived effects, for example, gender or immigration status. Regressions on the various perceived effects will be performed to estimate which factors are associated with given outcomes. We expect the study to be completed by the end of December 2026.

Ethics and dissemination

This research was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Board of Université Laval (CÉRUL) (2025-523 A-1/03-02-2026). Dissemination will be conducted through presentations at scientific events and with publications in peer-reviewed journals. Our integrated knowledge transfer strategy includes meetings with policymakers to inform them of results throughout the study.
