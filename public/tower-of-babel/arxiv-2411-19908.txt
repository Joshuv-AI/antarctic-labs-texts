Another look at statistical inference with machine learning-imputed data
Jessica Gronsbelland, Jianhui Gaoand, Zachary R. McCawand, Yaqi Shiand, David Cheng

Abstract
From structural biology to epidemiology, predictions from machine learning (ML) models increasingly complement costly gold-standard data, enabling faster, more affordable, and scalable scientific inquiry. In response, prediction-based (PB) inference has emerged to support statistical analysis that combines a large volume of predicted data with a small amount of gold-standard data. The goals of PB inference are twofold: (i) to mitigate bias arising from prediction error and (ii) to improve efficiency relative to classical inference based solely on gold-standard data. While early PB inference methods primarily focused on bias mitigation, improving efficiency remains an active area of research. Motivated by connections between PB inference and longstanding problems in statistics and related fields, we draw on the two-phase sampling literature to introduce an approach for Z-estimation with ML-imputed outcomes that is guaranteed to match or exceed the efficiency of classical inference, regardless of prediction quality. We demonstrate the utility of our approach through theoretical and numerical analyses as well as an application to UK Biobank data. We further establish new connections between existing PB inference approaches and foundational and contemporary statistical methods.

Problem set-up

2.1
Notation & parameter of interest

We let
Y
denote a scalar outcome and
{\bf{X}}
a
p
-dimensional vector of covariates. We focus on Z-estimation and aim to conduct statistical inference on the
q
-dimensional parameter
\bm{\beta}^{*}
defined as the unique solution to the estimating equation:

\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}(Y,{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta})]=\bm{0}

where
\bm{\phi}(\cdot)
is a deterministic function that satisfies conditions C.1-C.7 in the Appendix. The Z-estimation framework is broad and encompasses maximum likelihood estimation, generalized estimating equations, and the generalized method of moments, among other common estimation approaches.

To conduct inference on
\bm{\beta}^{*}
, we assume that we observe a dataset in which
{\bf{X}}
is fully observed on all observations and
Y
is partially observed among a subset of observations indicated by
R=1
. In addition to data on
Y
and
{\bf{X}}
, we assume that predictions
\widehat{Y}=\hat{f}({\bf{Z}})
are available for all observations based on a pre-trained ML model
\hat{f}
and fully observed predictors
{\bf{Z}}
. The predictors
{\bf{Z}}
may or may not include
{\bf{X}}
. The assumption that
\hat{f}
is pre-trained implies that its training data are independent of the data used to conduct inference on
\bm{\beta}^{*}
. Consequently,
\hat{f}
is treated as ‘‘fixed’’ and variation arising from its estimation is not considered in downstream inference.
†
†

†

Approaches that account for uncertainty in the ML model have recently been explored.
20
,
65
The available data therefore consists of
n
independent and identically distributed (iid) observations
(R_{i},Y_{i}R_{i},{\bf{Z}}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},{\bf{X}}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
. We denote the labeling fraction as
\pi_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}
. For simplicity and to facilitate comparisons with recent work in PB inference, we assume that the outcomes are missing completely at random (MCAR) so that
\mathbb{P}(R=1\mid Y,{\bf{X}},{\bf{Z}})=\mathbb{P}(R=1)=\pi
for some
\pi\in(0,1)
.

Remark 1
.

With a few exceptions,
11
,
30
existing PB inference methods
4
,
6
,
37
,
20
assume that the available data consist of a labeled dataset with
n_{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
observations of
(Y_{i},{\bf{Z}}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},{\bf{X}}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
and an independent unlabeled dataset with
n_{\text{\tiny{unlab}}}
observations of
(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
where
n=n_{\text{\tiny{unlab}}}+n_{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
and
n_{\text{\tiny{lab}}}/n_{\text{\tiny{unlab}}}\to\rho
for
\rho\in(0,1)

. This set-up corresponds to a sampling scheme in which a fixed number

n_{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
. Our set-up assumes the observations are labeled by an independent stochastic mechanism.

2.2

Motivation for PB inference

To provide general motivation for PB inference, recall that the classical estimator of
\bm{\beta}^{*}
, denoted as
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
, uses only the labeled observations for estimation and is the solution to:

n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(Y_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta})\frac{R_{i}}{\pi_{n}}=\bm{0}.

While
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
is consistent for
\bm{\beta}^{*}
and enables valid inference, it has limited precision when the number of labeled observations is not large. In contrast, the predictions
\widehat{Y}
are available in the full data, leading to a much larger dataset for analysis. However, relying exclusively on
\widehat{Y}
for inference can result in biased estimation depending on the quality of predictions from
\hat{f}
. In so-called
naive
inference,
44

\widehat{Y}
is directly substituted for
Y
in downstream analysis.
55
,
39

The parameter
\bm{\gamma}^{*}
targeted by naive inference is thus the unique solution to:

\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\gamma})]=\bm{0}.

The corresponding estimator based on the full data, denoted as
\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{all}}}
, is the solution to:

n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\gamma})=\bm{0}.

As the distribution of
(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
may not coincide with that of
(Y,{\bf{X}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
, the relationship between
\bm{\beta}^{*}
and
\bm{\gamma}^{*}
is unclear, except under some restrictive situations.
For example, for least squares estimation,
\bm{\gamma}^{*}
coincides with
\bm{\beta}^{*}
when
{\bf{X}}
is included in
{\bf{Z}}
and the ML model perfectly captures the true regression (i.e., the true
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid{\bf{Z}}={\bm{z}})
is within the space of functions
\hat{f}({\bm{z}})
can represent)
.
The application of naive inference in scientific practice therefore has the potential to produce biased, though often very precise inferences, about
\bm{\beta}^{*}
. This can lead researchers to be more confident in the wrong answers, thereby undermining the scientific rationale for using predicted outcomes in statistical inference.

2.3

Existing PB inference methods

To overcome the significant limitations of naive inference, numerous PB inference methods have been proposed.
4
,
6
,
37
,
36
,
35
,
20
These methods leverage the full dataset and make use of both
Y
and
\widehat{Y}
in conducting inference on
\bm{\beta}^{*}
. For example, the first PB inference method, coined
PostPI
,
55
performs inference in two steps. First, the labeled data are used to model the relationship between
Y
and
\widehat{Y}
to address potential inaccuracies in the predictions. Next, this model is combined with the unlabeled data to perform inference on
\bm{\beta}^{*}
using either analytic or bootstrap methods. While an innovative approach that set the stage for subsequent methodological developments, Motwani and Witten
39
recently showed that
PostPI
provides valid inference only under stringent assumptions, such as when the ML model captures the true regression function.

Several years following the introduction of
PostPi
, Angelopoulos et al
4
proposed Prediction-Powered Inference (
PPI
).
Unlike its predecessor,
PPI
has the crucial property that inference remains valid regardless of the quality of the predictions. That is,
\hat{f}
need not
capture
the true regression function for the estimator to remain consistent for
\bm{\beta}^{*}
, for hypothesis tests to asymptotically control the type I error rate, or for confidence intervals to achieve the nominal coverage level. Formally, the
PPI
estimator of
\bm{\beta}^{*}
is the solution to the de-biased estimating equation:

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta})\frac{1-R_{i}}{1-\pi_{n}}-\left[\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta})-\bm{\phi}(Y_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta})\right]\frac{R_{i}}{\pi_{n}}=\bm{0}.

(1)

The trailing term of (
1
) is referred to by the authors as the “rectifier” and serves the purpose of correcting for potential bias in naive estimation. For the remainder of this paper, we consider a modified version of the
PPI
estimator, which we refer to as
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
, that solves an estimating equation in which the leading term in (
1
) is replaced with the average over the full data,
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta})
. This modification facilitates analytic comparisons with other estimators considered in this paper.

While
PPI
provides valid inference for
\bm{\beta}^{*}
, three separate studies simultaneously recognized that
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
can be less efficient than
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
and proposed to incorporate data-adaptive weights within the
PPI
estimation equation to address this limitation.
6
,
37
,
20
Formally, these proposals are solutions to:

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(Y_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta})\frac{R_{i}}{\pi_{n}}-\widehat{\bf{W}}\left[n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta})\frac{R_{i}}{\pi_{n}}-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta})\right]=\bm{0},

(2)

where
\widehat{\bf{W}}
is a
q\times q
weight matrix that controls the contribution of
\widehat{Y}
into estimation of
\bm{\beta}^{*}
. The first proposal, called
PPI++
,
6
utilizes a scalar weight to minimize the trace of the resulting estimator’s asymptotic covariance matrix.
‡
‡

‡

Computing confidence intervals for the
PPI
estimator is generally intractable, which is an issue that is also addressed by
PPI++
. See the supplementary materials of Angelopoulos et al.
4
for detailed algorithms.
The second proposal, called PoSt-Prediction Adaptive (
PSPA
) inference,
37
utilizes a diagonal weight matrix to minimize element-wise variance. The third proposal, called Prediction De-Correlated (
PDC
) inference,
20
utilizes a full
q\times q
matrix to attain the lowest variance among all estimators that are solutions to (
2
).
56
While
PPI++
,
PSPA
, and
PDC
achieve provable improvements over classical inference, estimators outside of the
PPI
framework (i.e., estimators that are not solutions to (
2
)) remain unexplored. We consider such an alternative estimator in Section
4
.

3

Asymptotic analysis of the
PPI
estimator

To motivate our proposal, we further analyze the
PPI
estimator and show that it can be viewed as an AIPW estimator.
AIPW estimators are asymptotically linear estimators with influence functions of the form
42
:

\displaystyle{\bf{IF}}(Y,\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\omega})={\bf{A}}\left\{\bm{\phi}(Y,{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})\frac{R}{\pi}+\bm{\omega}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}})\left(1-\frac{R}{\pi}\right)\right\},

(3)

where
\bm{\omega}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}})
is a square-integrable augmenting function of
\widehat{Y}
and
{\bf{X}}
and
{\bf{A}}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial\bm{\phi}(Y,{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})}{\partial\bm{\beta}^{\top}}\right]^{-1}
. An estimator whose influence function has the augmenting function:

\bm{\omega}^{\rm opt}(\hat{Y},{\bf{X}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\phi}(Y,{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})\mid\hat{Y},{\bf{X}}\right]

achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound
7
.
In Proposition
1
, we show that the influence function of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
takes the form of (
3
) for a specific choice of the augmenting function. This result formally establishes a similar finding in Angelopoulos et al
4
for mean estimation to the broader Z-estimation problem. The proof of this and other theoretical results are provided in the Appendix for completeness.

Proposition 1

(Asymptotic linearity of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
)
.

Under basic assumptions in the Appendix,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
is consistent such that
||\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}||=o_{p}(1)
. Moreover, it is asymptotically linear such that:

n^{1/2}\left(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\right)=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf{IF}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}(Y_{i},\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})+o_{p}(1),

where
{\bf{IF}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}(Y_{i},\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})={\bf{A}}\left[\bm{\phi}(Y_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})\frac{R_{i}}{\pi}+\left\{\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})\right]\right\}(1-\frac{R_{i}}{\pi})\right]
.

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
can thus be viewed as an AIPW estimator with
\bm{\omega}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}})=\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})\right]
. It can be shown that this choice closely approximates
\bm{\omega}^{\rm opt}(\hat{Y},{\bf{X}})
in specific cases, such as when estimating the coefficients of a linear regression model such that
\bm{\phi}(y,{\bf{x}};\bm{\beta})={\bf{x}}(y-{\bf{x}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\bm{\beta})
,
{\bf{X}}
is included in
{\bf{Z}}
, and the machine learning model
\hat{f}
captures the true regression function. However, in general, Proposition
1
shows that the
PPI
estimator is not the most efficient estimator among AIPW estimators. Moreover, several studies
6
,
37
,
20
have identified that
PPI
can even be outperformed by classical inference when prediction quality is low and/or the unlabeled sample size is insufficient. In Corollary
1.1
, we apply Proposition
1
to identify necessary and sufficient conditions for
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
to gain efficiency over
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
.

Corollary 1.1

(Efficiency of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
vs.
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
)
.

Let
{\bf{D}}_{12}=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\phi}(Y,{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\right]
and
{\bf{D}}_{22}=Var\left[\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})\right]
. The difference in the asymptotic variance between

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
and
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
is
\Delta_{\text{Var}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}},\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}})=(\pi^{-1}-1){\bf{A}}\left(2{\bf{D}}_{12}-{\bf{D}}_{22}\right){\bf{A}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
. Consequently,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
is more efficient than
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
if and only if
2{\bf{D}}_{12}-{\bf{D}}_{22}\succ\bm{0}
.

Remark 2
.

AIPW estimators are known to be model doubly robust in that they are
n^{1/2}
-consistent and asymptotically normal when either of two underlying nuisance parameters, typically a propensity score and a mean outcome function, are consistent at sufficiently fast rates
48
. For
PPI
, the mean outcome function is
\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})
and the propensity score function is effectively an estimate of the labeling probability
\pi_{n}
, which is
n^{1/2}
-consistent under MCAR. Due to model double robustness,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
remains
n^{1/2}
-consistent and asymptotically normal. However, as with other AIPW estimators, there is a contribution,
{\bf{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})\right](1-\frac{R_{i}}{\pi})
, to the influence function from estimating
\pi
with
\pi_{n}
. An analogous asymptotic expansion arises under alternative sampling schemes when
n_{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
is fixed and hence the propensity score is effectively known.
20
,
37
Lastly, of
\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}},\bm{\beta}^{*})=\bm{\omega}^{\text{\tiny{opt}}}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}})
, then
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})\right]=\bm{0}
and the estimator would reach the semiparametric efficiency bound.

4

The
CC
estimator

Building on the findings of the previous section, we now propose an estimator whose influence function has an augmenting function that guarantees its asymptotic variance is never larger than that of classical inference. Unlike the extensions of
PPI
, which augment the empirical estimating equation for
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
with information from
\widehat{Y}
, we propose to directly augment
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
.
§
§

§

Angelopoulos et al.
4
considered an augmented estimator for the special case of linear regression (see Algorithm S4 of the supplementary materials), which is generally distinct from the solution to (
1
).
Such augmented estimators have been widely studied in statistics and related areas as a means of improving efficiency, yet remain relatively underexplored in the context of PB inference (see Section
5
for further discussion). In particular, within the two-phase sampling literature, Chen and Chen
12
(
CC
) proposed an estimator for the regression coefficients of a GLM that leverages a small validation dataset with gold-standard measurements together with a larger primary dataset containing less-expensive proxy measurements. The
CC
estimator adds an additional term to the classical estimator that can be estimated from the proxy measurements and that is consistent for zero. This term is carefully designed to be correlated with the classical estimator in a way that guarantees a variance reduction.

To apply the this approach to the PB inference problem, let
{\bf{W}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}={\bf{C}}_{12}{\bf{C}}_{22}^{-1}{\bf{B}}^{-1}
where
{\bf{C}}_{12}=\text{Cov}[\bm{\phi}(Y,{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*}),\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\gamma}^{*})]
,
{\bf{C}}_{22}=\text{Var}[\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\gamma}^{*})]
,
and
{\bf{B}}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\gamma}^{*})}{\partial\bm{\gamma}^{\top}}\right]^{-1}
. We then define the
CC
estimator as

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}=\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}-\widehat{{\bf{A}}}\widehat{{\bf{W}}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}\left(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}-\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{all}}}\right).

where
\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
is the solution to
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\gamma})\frac{R_{i}}{\pi_{n}}=\bm{0}
,
\widehat{{\bf{A}}}
is a consistent estimator of
{\bf{A}}
, and
\widehat{{\bf{W}}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
is a consistent estimator of
{\bf{W}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
. Proposition
2
shows that
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
is asymptotically linear with an influence function resembling that of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
, but with a different augmenting function. This result was previously shown for GLMs in the original paper by Chen & Chen.
12
We present it here applied to the PB inference problem as a reference.

Proposition 2

(Asymptotic linearity of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
)
.

Under the assumptions in the Appendix,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
is asymptotically linear such that:

\displaystyle n^{1/2}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf{IF}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}(Y_{i},\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*},\bm{\gamma}^{*})+o_{p}(1),

where
{\bf{IF}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}(Y_{i},\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*},\bm{\gamma}^{*})={\bf{A}}\left\{\bm{\phi}(Y_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})\frac{R_{i}}{\pi}+{\bf{W}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}{\bf{B}}\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\gamma}^{*})(1-\frac{R_{i}}{\pi})\right\}
.

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
is therefore an AIPW estimator with

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}})=\text{Cov}[\bm{\phi}(Y,{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*}),\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\gamma}^{*})]\{\text{Var}[\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\gamma}^{*})]\}^{-1}\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\gamma}^{*}).

The augmenting function is the orthogonal projection of
\bm{\phi}(Y,{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})
onto the subspace spanned by
\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\gamma}^{*})
in the Hilbert space of mean-zero, square-integrable random variables. This projection effectively approximates
\bm{\omega}^{\text{\tiny{opt}}}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}})
as a linear combination of the components of
\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\gamma}^{*})
, yielding a closer approximation than
\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\gamma}^{*})
alone and protecting against potential efficiency loss. Corollary
2.1
demonstrates that, unlike
PPI
,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
is never asymptotically outperformed by
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
.

Corollary 2.1

(Efficiency of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
vs.
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
)
.

The difference in the asymptotic variance of

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
and
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
is
\Delta_{\text{Var}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}},\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}})={\bf{A}}{\bf{C}}_{12}{\bf{C}}_{22}^{-1}{\bf{C}}_{12}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf{A}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}(\pi^{-1}-1)
. Consequently,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
is at least as efficient as
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
and is more efficient if and only if
{\bf{C}}_{12}\neq\bm{0}
.

To understand Corollary
2.1
, we consider the simple setting of homoskedastic linear regression to illustrate how the
CC
estimator provides efficiency gains.

Example 1
(Homoskedastic Linear Regression).
Consider the setting of linear regression and let
\epsilon_{Y}=Y-{\bf{X}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\bm{\beta}^{*}
and
\epsilon_{\widehat{Y}}=\widehat{Y}-{\bf{X}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\bm{\gamma}^{*}
. Under homoskedasticity,
{\bf{C}}_{12}=\sigma_{Y,\widehat{Y}}\bm{\Sigma}_{{\bf{X}}}
and
{\bf{C}}_{22}=\sigma_{\widehat{Y}}^{2}\bm{\Sigma}_{{\bf{X}}}
where
\bm{\Sigma}_{{\bf{X}}}=\mathbb{E}({\bf{X}}{\bf{X}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}})
,
\sigma_{Y,\widehat{Y}}=\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{Y}\epsilon_{\widehat{Y}})
and
\sigma_{\widehat{Y}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{\widehat{Y}}^{2})
. Applying Corollary
2.1
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{Var}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}},\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}})

\displaystyle=\left(\pi^{-1}-1\right)\left(\frac{\sigma_{Y,\widehat{Y}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\widehat{Y}}^{2}}\right)\bm{\Sigma}_{{\bf{X}}}^{-1}=\left(\pi^{-1}-1\right)\sigma_{Y}^{2}\rho_{Y,\widehat{Y}}^{2}\bm{\Sigma}_{{\bf{X}}}^{-1}.

where
\rho_{Y,\widehat{Y}}=\sigma_{Y,\widehat{Y}}/\sigma_{Y}\sigma_{\widehat{Y}}
is the partial correlation of the residuals for
Y
and
\widehat{Y}
given
{\bf{X}}
.
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PPI}}}
is thus more efficient than
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}
when
\widehat{Y}
is a strong predictor for
Y
after adjusting for
{\bf{X}}
, though
\widehat{Y}
does not necessarily need to be derived from a ML model that estimates the true regression function. The improvement is stronger with increasing residual correlation of the residuals between
Y
and
\widehat{Y}
, greater variability in the residuals for
Y
, and a lower probability of labeling.

In addition to being more efficient than classical inference, the
CC
estimator has the lowest asymptotic variance among all weighted augmented estimators. This optimality property is suggested by the orthogonal projection interpretation and indicates that
\widehat{{\bf{A}}}\widehat{{\bf{W}}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
is a reasonable choice of weights for the augmented estimator. This result was mentioned in the original paper by Chen & Chen
12
and we provide a proof in the Appendix.

Proposition 3

(
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
is the most efficient among augmented estimators)
.

Let
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}(\widehat{{\bf{V}}})=\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}-\widehat{{\bf{V}}}(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}-\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{all}}})
be an alternative weighted augmented estimator with weights
\widehat{{\bf{V}}}
that are consistent for some
{\bf{V}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
. Then the difference in asymptotic variance is such that
\Delta_{\text{Var}}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}(\widehat{{\bf{V}}}),\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}})\succeq\bm{0}
.

4.1

Relationship to the
PPI
extensions

We now return to the extensions of
PPI
introduced in Section
2.3
, which have a similar goal of improving the efficiency of classical inference. We focus on the
PDC
estimator as it is known to be the most efficient among estimators that are solutions to the weighted estimating equation in (
2
).
56
. Specifically, let
{\bf{W}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}={\bf{D}}_{12}{\bf{D}}_{22}^{-1}
.
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}
is the solution to

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(Y_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta})\frac{R_{i}}{\pi_{n}}-\widehat{{\bf{W}}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}\left[n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta})\frac{R_{i}}{\pi_{n}}-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta})\right]=\bm{0}

where
\widehat{{\bf{W}}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}
is a consistent estimator of
{\bf{W}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}
. We show in Proposition
4
that, similar to the
PPI
and
CC

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}
is asymptotically linear with an AIPW influence function. The asymptotic normality of the
PDC
estimator has been shown in Gan et al.

Proposition 4

(Asymptotic linearity of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}
)
.

Under the basic assumptions in the Appendix,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}
is consistent such that
||\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}||=o_{p}(1)
. Moreover,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}
is asymptotically linear such that:

\displaystyle n^{1/2}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf{IF}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}(Y_{i},\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})+o_{p}(1),

where
{\bf{IF}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}(Y_{i},\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})={\bf{A}}\left[\bm{\phi}(Y_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})\frac{R_{i}}{\pi}+{\bf{W}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}\left\{\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})\right]\right\}(1-\frac{R_{i}}{\pi})\right]
.

Akin to the
CC
estimator, the
PDC

\bm{\omega}^{\text{\tiny{opt}}}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}})
with an orthogonal projection of
\bm{\phi}(Y,{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})
onto the subpace spanned by
\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}};\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\phi}(\widehat{Y}_{i},{\bf{X}}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})\right]
to protect against potential efficiency loss. The key difference between
CC
and

is that

does not estimate a separate
\bm{\gamma}^{*}
for the estimating equation in the augmenting function. It is not clear from analytic comparisons whether

or

and
\widehat{Y}
given
{\bf{X}}
,
CC
is more efficient than

. We evaluate this result numerically in our simulation studies in Section

.

Proposition 5

(Efficiency of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
vs.
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}
for GLMs)
.

Suppose that we seek to estimate regression parameters from a GLM with canonical link such that
\bm{\phi}(y,{\bf{x}};\bm{\beta})={\bf{x}}\left\{y-g({\bf{x}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\bm{\beta})\right\}
with
g(\cdot)
being the inverse link function. A sufficient condition for
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
to be at least as efficient as
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}}
such that
\Delta_{\text{Var}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{PDC}}},\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}})\succeq\bm{0}

\widehat{Y}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y\mid{\bf{X}})=g({\bf{X}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\bm{\beta}^{*})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{E}(\widehat{Y}\mid{\bf{X}})=g({\bf{X}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\bm{\gamma}^{*}).

5.1

Seemingly unrelated regression

The augmentation-based approach used by the
CC
estimator is related to several other estimators developed for linear regression, both historically and in recent work. In particular, it connects to seemingly unrelated regression (SUR), a framework introduced by economist Arnold Zellner in 1962 for the joint estimation of multiple related regression models
59
,
51
,
46
. In the current setting, the SUR problem can be written as

Y={\bf{X}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\epsilon\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{Y}={\bf{X}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\bm{\gamma}_{0}+\epsilon^{\prime}\quad

where
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon\mid{\bf{X}})=0
and
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon^{\prime}\mid{\bf{X}})=0.
Letting
W^{\text{\tiny{SUR}}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{Cov}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\mid{\bf{X}})]}{\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(\epsilon^{\prime}\mid{\bf{X}})]}
, Conniffe
13
proposed to estimate
\bm{\beta}_{0}
with:

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{SUR}}}=\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}-\widehat{W}^{\text{\tiny{SUR}}}\left(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}-\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{all}}}\right)\quad\mbox{where}\quad\widehat{W}^{\text{\tiny{SUR}}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{\tiny{lab}}}}(Y_{i}-{\bf{X}}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}})(\widehat{Y}_{i}-{\bf{X}}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{\tiny{lab}}}}(\widehat{Y}_{i}-{\bf{X}}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}})^{2}}.

In the homoskedastic case where
\text{Var}(\epsilon\mid{\bf{X}})
and
\text{Cov}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\mid{\bf{X}})
are constant and do not depend on
{\bf{X}}
,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
is asymptotically equivalent to
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{SUR}}}
.
12
Moreover, when the conditional distribution of
(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})
given
{\bf{X}}
is a zero-mean bivariate normal distribution with constant variance-covariance,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{SUR}}}
, and thus
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
as well, is the maximum likelihood estimator of
\bm{\beta}_{0}
and fully efficient under the assumed parametric model.
13
Moreover, the estimator still provides valid inference when the outcomes are Missing at Random (MAR). In other scenarios,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
achieves a lower asymptotic variance than
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{SUR}}}
due to Proposition
3
.

The SUR estimator was recently applied in a PB inference problem inspired by genome-wide association studies conducted with population biobanks. These studies often rely on pre-trained models to impute outcomes of interest (i.e., phenotypes or traits) as they are partially missing due to the time and expense of ascertainment.
35
The proposed method,
SynSurr
, assumes the true and predicted outcomes follow a bivariate normal distribution. The
SynSurr
estimator is equivalent to
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{SUR}}}
and asymptotically equivalent to
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{CC}}}
when
\text{Var}(\epsilon\mid{\bf{X}})
and
\text{Cov}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\mid{\bf{X}})
are constant. Building on
SynSurr
, Miao et al
38
proposed the
POP-GWAS
estimator for a variety of regression models utilizing the augmentation technique. The
POP-GWAS
estimator
¶

\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{unlab}}}

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{POP}}}=\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}-\widehat{W}^{\text{\tiny{POP}}}\left(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{lab}}}-\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{\tiny{all}}}\right)\quad\mbox{where}\quad\widehat{W}^{\text{\tiny{POP}}}=\widehat{\text{Corr}}(Y,\widehat{Y}\mid{\bf{X}}).

The weight,
\widehat{W}^{\text{\tiny{POP}}}

5.2

Augmented estimation for quantifying a treatment effect

Outside of linear regression, the augmented estimation approach has also been widely used for treatment effect estimation.
31
,
33
,
54
,
23
,
61
,
52
,
57
Many of these approaches build on methods from survey sampling.
9
,
45
For example, Tsiatis et al
54
consider covariate adjusted analyses for comparing two treatments in randomized clinical trials. The goal is to estimate the treatment effect
\Delta=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X=1)-\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X=0)
. A standard estimator of
\Delta
is
\widehat{\Delta}=\overline{Y}^{1}-\overline{Y}^{0}
, where
\overline{Y}^{1}=n_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}X_{i}
and
\overline{Y}^{0}=n_{0}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(1-X_{i})
are the empirical means of
Y
in the treated and control groups of sizes
n_{1}
and
n_{0}
, respectively. To utilize information from baseline covariates
{\bf{Z}}
for improving efficiency, Tsiatis et al
54
propose an augmented estimator of the treatment effect given by

\widehat{\Delta}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X})[n_{0}^{-1}h^{(0)}({\bf{Z}}_{i})+n_{1}^{-1}h^{(1)}({\bf{Z}}_{i})],

(4)

where
\bar{X}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and
h^{(k)}({\bf{Z}})
are arbitrary scalar functions of
{\bf{Z}}
for
k=0,1
. As
X
and
{\bf{Z}}
are independent due to randomization, the augmentation term in
4
converges in probability to 0, analogously to the augmentation term in the
CC
estimator. This technique has been applied in numerous settings, most recently for the analysis of time-to-event outcomes
62
and recurrent events.
25

5.3

Control variates in Monte Carlo simulations

Lastly, the augmentation technique has been utilized to improve the efficiency of Monte Carlo simulations. Rothery
43
considered using a control variate, a statistic whose properties are known, to improve precision when estimating power via simulation. In particular, suppose interest lies in estimating the power of a test
T
of
H_{0}:\theta=0
for some scalar parameter
\theta
. Let
S
denote an existing test of the same hypothesis whose power is known. Define
Y
as an indicator that test
T
rejects,
Z
as an indicator that test
S
rejects, and let
\mu_{Z}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}(Z)
denote the power of reference test
S
. For a set of
n
simulation replicates, the baseline estimator of power for
T
is simply
\overline{Y}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
while a control variate augmented estimator is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[Y_{i}-\lambda\{Z_{i}-\mu_{Z}\}\right].

(5)

The variance of the augmented estimator is minimized at
\lambda^{*}=\frac{\text{Cov}(Y,Z)}{\text{Var}(Z)}
.
43
,
16
The augmentation term in (
5
) reduces the variance of the unadjusted power estimate
\overline{Y}
in proportion to the correlation between
Y
and
Z
. Building on work from the survey sampling literature, a similar idea was later applied for semi-supervised estimation of means
60
and for causal effect estimation with multiple observational data sources
57
.

UK Biobank Analysis

We also evaluate the performance of the PB inference methods in an analysis focused on central obesity. Central obesity is excessive accumulation of body fat around the abdominal region and is strongly associated with cardiometabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. A major challenge in its study is the limited availability of direct body composition measurements. Android fat mass (AFM), measured via a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scan, is a key marker for assessing central obesity in clinical practice.
64
However, due to its high cost, DEXA-derived AFM is usually only measured in a small subset of participants in large-scale studies. For example, in the UK Biobank,
50
a prospective cohort study measuring genetic, lifestyle, and health information on approximately 500,000 participants in the UK, DEXA-derived AFM is incompletely ascertained, particularly among populations historically underrepresented in genetic research.
50
For instance, out of
5,397
South Asian participants in the UK Biobank, only
288
(5.3%) participants have undergone DEXA scans.

Our goal is to examine the association of AFM with age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) among the South Asian population. Previous research has indicated that this population is at higher risk of central obesity and that it is associated with cardiometabolic diseases at lower BMI thresholds than European populations.
49
Since DEXA-derived AFM is missing for most South Asian participants, it provides a natural opportunity to leverage ML-predicted AFM in downstream analyses. To illustrate the impact of different ML models on the efficiency of the PB inference methods, we trained two random forest models in the White British population (
32,460
participants) and used them to predict missing AFM values in the South Asian population. We built a “strong” prediction model that incorporated both the covariates of interest (i.e., age, sex, and BMI) as well as additional predictors, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, two bioelectrical impedance measurements of the legs, physical activity scores from the International Physical Activity Questionnaire, and sedentary behavior quantified as total hours spent driving, using a computer, and watching television. We built another “weak” prediction model that included only the additional predictors. The strong prediction model achieved an
R^{2}
of 0.8 while the weak prediction model had a substantially lower
R^{2}
of 0.5 in the South Asian subpopulation.

Discussion

We introduced the
CC
approach for Z-estimation problems with ML-imputed outcomes, which yields valid inference regardless of prediction quality while also achieving the lowest variance among weighted augmented estimators. Our theoretical and empirical results demonstrate that the
CC
estimator can provide practical improvements over classical estimation as well as the
PPI
estimator and its extensions, making it a preferred approach for applied PB inference tasks. More broadly, our work situates PB inference within semi-parametric efficiency theory and highlights connections to classical methods in statistics and related fields that are directly relevant to this modern problem.

For simplicity and to facilitate comparisons with existing PB inference methods, we assumed that the outcomes are MCAR. Extending the
CC
approach to accommodate more general missingness patterns would greatly enhance its practical applicability and has been a focus of recent work.
11
,
10
Additionally, the
CC
estimator is not guaranteed to outperform
PDC
in general. For instance, the result in Proposition
5
does not hold when the conditional mean of
Y
given
{\bf{X}}
follows a logistic regression model and
\widehat{Y}
are non-differentially misclassified versions of
Y
. In this case, the conditional mean of
\widehat{Y}
given
{\bf{X}}
involves a different link function that is dependent on its sensitivity and specificity for
Y
.
40
However, both estimators are special cases of recently proposed semi-supervised estimators that approximate
\omega^{\text{\tiny{opt}}}(\widehat{Y},{\bf{X}})
with an orthogonal projection of
\bm{\phi}(Y,X;\bm{\beta}^{*})
onto a carefully constructed basis
56
, suggesting a natural pathway for further improvement.

It is also important to note that the
CC
estimator requires estimation of a
q\times q
weight matrix, which can be unstable when labeled data are limited. In such cases, restricting the weight matrix to a simple structure, such as a scalar or diagonal matrix akin to
PPI++
or
PSPA
, may be beneficial in finite samples, albeit at some cost of asymptotic efficiency. Finally, although we focused on ML-imputed outcomes, the
CC
framework can accommodate ML-imputed covariates
30
and can be extended to handle multiple predictions of
Y
.
