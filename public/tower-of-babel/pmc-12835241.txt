Experimental realization of logical elastic bits as qubit analogues in a nonlinear oscillator
Authors: Kazi T. Mahmood, Abrar N.-E. Faiaz, M. Arif Hasan, Pierre A. Deymier, Keith Runge, Joshua A. Levine
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nonlinear mechanical oscillators can emulate qubit analogue algebra by leveraging multiple harmonics of large‑amplitude vibrations. We realize a logical elastic bit—a room temperature mechanical analogue of a qubit—in a two‑mass oscillator joined by a conical spring whose graded stiffness generates a robust sequence of phase‑coherent harmonics. From time‑series velocity measurements, a Fourier–projection maps the response onto the complete space of in‑phase and out‑of‑phase eigenvectors. The resulting complex coefficients define a Bloch‑sphere representation in which classical superpositions are directly controllable. Moreover, we gain more control over the coefficients by pairing the Fourier harmonics in different orders. When the paired Fourier components share a frequency, the coefficients are independent of time, producing tunable states that can serve as phase-defined memory. Pairing distinct harmonics introduces a beat frequency that drives deterministic precession of the Bloch vector, realizing single‑bit rotations (e.g., Pauli‑X and Hadamard analogues) without the need of additional external input, with time as the gate clock. By splitting the spectrum into blocks, a single resonator can host several elastic bits at once. The Hilbert space grows with the number of blocks while the hardware stays the same, allowing scalable architectures that show classical non-separable correlations. A linear mass-spring model yields closed‑form eigenfrequencies and Bloch‑angle formulas that overlay measured trajectories across resonance and provide design rules for state initialization and gate timing. All operations occur at ambient conditions and require no feedback or cryogenics, establishing a simple, reproducible route to quantum‑inspired logic in macroscopic mechanics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33387-8.

Introduction

Nonlinear dynamical systems display bifurcations, internal resonances, chaos, and amplitude‑dependent natural frequencies—behaviors absent in the linear regime1–5. When driven strongly, energy is redistributed among many Fourier components of the measured velocities, producing a high‑dimensional response whose amplitudes and phases can be shaped by the driver. Rather than being a modeling limitation, this spectral–spatial richness is a resource for vibration control, adaptive sensing, and information processing.

Harnessing that potential requires simultaneously solving two long-standing challenges. First, high-order nonlinear responses are highly sensitive to fabrication tolerances, material damping, and environmental drift, making reproducible operation difficult6–9. Second, even when stable responses can be obtained, one must embed them in a framework that protects the desired state against local perturbations. Topological mechanics provides a natural form of protection, since global phase winding and band-gap isolation make certain mechanical states robust against disorder that would otherwise disrupt their modes10–13. Yet general strategies for generating, manipulating, and scaling such topologically protected nonlinear states have remained elusive.

Here we show that a simple mass–conical‑spring overcomes these barriers and furnishes a purely mechanical analogue of a quantum bit, which we call an elastic bit. The conical spring’s graded stiffness produces deterministic nonlinearity without granular contacts or micro‑fabrication. Large‑amplitude excitation naturally populates a series of harmonics whose component phases can be static (time‑independent) or deliberately detuned (time‑varying). By projecting the velocity field onto orthonormal in‑phase and out‑of‑phase eigenvectors, each chosen pair of Fourier harmonic components maps to a complex coefficient pair on the Bloch sphere. We distinguish physical elastic bits, defined by projections of the full harmonic field, from logical elastic bits, defined by spectral blocks that behave as programmable two‑level subsystems.

Logical elastic bits are valuable for several reasons. (i) They provide room temperature platforms for prototyping gate operations without cryogenic or coherence overheads. (ii) They enable systematic exploration of control and scaling in the familiar Bloch‑sphere formalism. (iii) They complement recent acoustic “phi‑bit” demonstrations of qubit analogues and two- and three-bit gates in externally driven nonlinear waveguides, where states are also represented on the Bloch sphere and manipulated via phases of the drivers14–17. Our architecture retains these advantages while reducing hardware to a single conical spring. Through these, we advance four ideas: (1) A logical decomposition recasts the nonlinear response as a partial Fourier sum whose complex coefficients lie on the Bloch sphere. This construction provides a one‑to‑one mapping between physical modes (“Nonlinear response of the mass–conical‑spring oscillator”) and logical algorithms (“Logical elastic bits: spectral partitioning for programmable qubit analogues”). (2) When paired components share a frequency, the Bloch vector stays fixed in time. It can be adiabatically guided by slowly sweeping the drive frequency, where adiabaticity means changing it more slowly than the beat period. (3) Pairing distinct harmonics creates a beat frequency that drives deterministic Bloch‑vector precession, so single‑bit rotations follow from known waiting intervals—passive because no auxiliary control is applied once a pair is chosen. (4) By partitioning the spectrum, multiple logical bits can be hosted in a single resonator. The Hilbert space expands with the number of blocks, increasing logical capacity without requiring extra hardware.

Nonlinear response of the mass–conical‑spring oscillator

A two‑mass/two‑spring dimer comprising a conical inter‑spring and a linear driver spring provides the minimal mechanical lattice that can host elastic bits whose dynamics echo the recently established acoustic phi-bit platform18. In what follows, we show, step‑by‑step, how the nonlinear force–displacement law of the conical spring endows it with a high‑dimensional harmonic spectrum that, when recast in Hilbert space, supports Bloch‑sphere logic.

This measurement protocol provides the full spectral and modal information needed for the elastic-bit construction developed later in the paper. The nonlinear force–displacement response of the conical spring produces a hierarchy of harmonics whose amplitudes and phases are captured directly by the vibrometer. These harmonics, together with their modal phase relationships, form the basis of the time- and frequency-domain characterization presented in “Time-domain dynamics: asymmetric energy shuttling” and “Frequency‑domain signatures and the harmonic arrangement”. Their organization into in-phase and out-of-phase modal components is examined in “Modal phase topology”, and this modal structure is then recast as a two-level Hilbert-space representation in “Hilbert‑space representation of the nonlinear response”. Together, these sections show how the measured nonlinear response provides the ingredients from which both physical and logical elastic bits are constructed.

Time-domain dynamics: asymmetric energy shuttling

Figure 2a illustrates a representative response of the two-mass system driven at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{d}=65\:Hz$$\end{document} (the transmission spectrum of masses at frequency sweep in supplementary materials). At the beginning of the cycle, the shaker displaces Mass 1 toward Mass 2, shortening the conical spring so that both masses accelerate in the positive-velocity direction under compression. As the base motion reverses, the spring passes through its neutral length and is pulled into tension; because the active-coil diameter is narrower on the Mass-2 side, the tensile stiffness rises sharply, and the stored elastic energy migrates toward that end of the oscillator. During this interval, Mass 2 continues to move while Mass 1 momentarily hovers near rest, a clear signature of energy localization that corresponds to the plateau marked “3” in the time trace. When the external forcing changes sign once more, the spring relaxes back into compression, momentum is transferred back to Mass 1, and the sequence begins anew. The pronounced left–right asymmetry confirms that the graded stiffness of the conical spring—not merely geometric nonlinearity—governs the energy flow. Similar asymmetric phenomena have been reported in nonlinear granular media for nonlinear normal modes response19.

Logical elastic bits: spectral partitioning for programmable qubit analogues

Although physical elastic bits encode qubit-like states, extracting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\psi\:}_{0}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\psi\:}_{1}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} in Eq. To resolve that bottleneck, we introduce the logical elastic bit, defined not by the sum of all the harmonics but by blocks of the spectrum.

Static versus dynamic logical States

Logical bits inherit the dynamical richness of their physical elastic bit counterparts. If all \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{n}$$\end{document} within a block are equal (e.g., degenerate sidebands in a driven–damped system) then \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\psi\:}_{0}^{\left(p\right)}\:\mathrm{a}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{d}\:{\psi\:}_{1}^{\left(p\right)}$$\end{document} are constant and the logical Bloch vector is fixed—ideal for memory. Harmonics of differing orders cause predictable precession in the Bloch sphere. By waiting for a set time, one can realize rotations equivalent to Pauli-X, Hadamard, or phase gates without additional control.

The logical bit architecture leverages three experimental facts. First, nonlinearity ensures that higher-order harmonics remain phase-coherent with the excitation, preserving superposition integrity. Second, the relative amplitudes of those harmonics are tunable through driver amplitude, pre-compression, or modest geometric tweaks, yielding a convenient control dial for gate synthesis. Third, unlike true qubits, elastic bits operate at room temperature and are immune to decoherence; signal-to-noise, not phase collapse, sets the performance ceiling. Using frequency-domain partitioning and Hilbert-space mapping, we build a scalable mechanical platform. This platform captures the logic of multi-qubit quantum circuits while operating in the classical regime. Although each logical elastic bit is constructed from a disjoint spectral block, disjoint frequency support alone does not guarantee dynamical independence because all Fourier components are generated by the same nonlinear oscillator. In an ideal periodic signal with infinite observation time, Fourier components at different harmonics are orthogonal; however, in practice, finite-time estimation introduces spectral leakage and line broadening, which can create apparent cross-talk between adjacent blocks if the spectral resolution and windowing are insufficient28. Moreover, intrinsic nonlinearity can produce genuine inter-block coupling through harmonic energy transfer, internal resonance, and phase-coupling mechanisms (e.g., triad-type interactions), so the multi-bit state need not factorize even when blocks are non-overlapping in frequency. Because the coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\psi\:}_{0}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\psi\:}_{1}$$\end{document} in Eq. (4) are functions of harmonic amplitudes and phases produced by the same nonlinear dynamics, the measured multi-bit state is not guaranteed to be of tensor-product form. The experimental Bloch angles in Fig. 5 reflect these regimes directly: in frequency intervals (26–30 Hz) where third bit shows strong evolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{3}\left({\omega\:}_{d}\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varphi\:}_{3}\left({\omega\:}_{d}\right)$$\end{document} while the other two bits remain nearly stationary, indicating effectively weak cross-talk. Conversely, intervals where multiple bits evolve simultaneously, consistent with stronger nonlinear coupling across spectral partitions. Thus, spectral partitioning supports multiple logical elastic bits in one resonator, but their effective independence is an operating-regime property set by (i) spectral isolation in estimation and (ii) the strength of nonlinear inter-harmonic coupling.

Topological characterization of the logical elastic bit (Berry Phase)

Having established the elastic bit’s representation on the Bloch sphere and its two operational regimes—time-independent (degenerate pair) and time-dependent (detuned pair) (“Logical elastic bits: spectral partitioning for programmable qubit analogues” and “Single logical elastic bit: gate‑level control”)—we now quantify its topological aspect via the Berry phase. In our setting, the state is a two-component Bloch vector formed from the in-phase and out-of-phase eigenvectors. Its path on the Bloch sphere is traced either by sweeping the drive frequency (time-independent case) or by the dynamics of two detuned harmonics (time-dependent case). This construction is consistent with the logical elastic bit formalism introduced above, where spectral pairing maps directly to a Bloch vector with polar/azimuthal angles \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(\theta\:,\varphi\:\right)$$\end{document} (see “Logical elastic bits: spectral partitioning for programmable qubit analogues” and “Single logical elastic bit: gate‑level control”).

In the logical elastic bit, the paired modal components define a state on the Bloch sphere whose evolution under a frequency sweep or a beat cycle traces a closed path. The topological character of the logical bit is encoded not in the instantaneous amplitudes but in how this path winds around the sphere. The Berry phase provides the natural independent for quantifying this winding, since it measures the geometric angle associated with the enclosed solid angle of the trajectory. This makes it a direct indicator of topological behavior: distinct windings produce quantized or robust geometric phases even when the detailed dynamics vary. By evaluating the Berry phase of the measured Bloch-state trajectories, we capture the global structure of the logical bit’s evolution and identify its topological features in a way that amplitude and phase alone cannot reveal. Summary and concluding remarks

The logical elastic bit platform presented here establishes a mechanically simple and reproducible, ambient- condition bridge between nonlinear dynamics and quantum-inspired information science. By exciting a two-mass/conical-spring dimer into an arrangement of phase-locked harmonics and projecting its velocity field onto an orthonormal modal basis, we have shown that each selected pair of Fourier components maps one-to-one onto a Bloch vector. This Hilbert-space representation unlocks the full algebra of qubit manipulation in a purely classical medium. Time-independent superpositions arise when the paired harmonics share a common frequency, yielding fixed Bloch coordinates that can be swept adiabatically with the drive and therefore serve as phase-defined memory states. Time-dependent superpositions emerge when the harmonics are detuned; the ensuing beat frequency drives deterministic precession of the Bloch vector, passively enacting Pauli-X, Hadamard, and arbitrary single-bit rotations without external feedback. Crucially, segmenting the Fourier spectrum creates multiple independent elastic bits in a single resonator, expanding the accessible Hilbert space exponentially while keeping the mechanical footprint constant, and—importantly, enabling correlated (classically non-separable) states across bits. Further, a linearized dimer model furnishes closed-form eigenfrequencies and explicit Bloch-angle expressions that overlay the experimental trajectories with no adjustable parameters beyond measured damping, providing a predictive framework for device scaling and gate timing.

Compared with earlier granular or waveguide phi-bit systems, the conical-spring architecture delivers several practical advantages: (i) continuously graded stiffness ensures predictable nonlinearity and eliminates contact uncertainty; (ii) all operations occur at ambient conditions, obviating cryogenics and coherence overhead; and (iii) the entire control set; excitation amplitude, frequency, and phase—acts directly on macroscopic elastic modes that are inherently immune to quantum decoherence. These attributes position logical elastic bits as a compelling platform for prototyping quantum-inspired gates and exploring high-dimensional Hilbert spaces with conventional laboratory hardware.

Our findings chart a pragmatic middle path between classical signal processing and quantum computing. Elastic bits inherit the algebra of qubit rotations yet avoid cryogenic infrastructure and fragile coherence; error rates are governed by signal-to-noise rather than decoherence. The logical elastic bit elevates nonlinear vibration from a modeling curiosity to a robust information carrier, bridging continuum mechanics and quantum logic with a single, analytically tractable device. It can store and process information under ambient conditions and can allow state-agnostic initialization: by setting the amplitude ratio and phase offset (as demonstrated in21, the Bloch vector can be placed anywhere, and detuning then times the desired rotation. Together, these features provide a practical route to gate operations and classically non-separable correlations.
