Valorization of mango peel waste into phosphoric acid treated activated carbon for efficient removal of methyl orange from aqueous solution
Authors: Smruti Rekha Patra, Sneha Prabha Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Industrial wastewater contaminated with dyes pose a serious problem to the environment. So, it should be treated for dye removal before its release into the environment. Adsorption technique is a preferable method because of its efficiency and low-cost. The method becomes more attractive when various low-cost materials are used as adsorbents. Due to presence of various negatively charged functional groups on their surfaces, these materials are more specific towards cationic/positively charged pollutants. After suitable modification of the material, negatively charged pollutants can also be removed. Methyl orange (MO) is an anionic/negatively charged dye commonly found in most of the industrial wastewater. Mango peel (MP) is a significant waste produced from food processing industries. So the main objective of the present research is to remove MO from aqueous solution using MP as the adsorbent through adsorption technique. However, MO uptake on unmodified MP was only 22% and MP modified with phosphoric acid followed by carbonization (PMP-AC) showed 97.21% removal from a solution containing 50 mg/L MO. The MO adsorption on PMP-AC is optimized (contact time = 60 min and pH = 2.0). Both Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms are obeyed well with Langmuir monolayer uptake capacity of 23.2 mg/g. The modified material PMP-AC is properly characterized. P-O/P-O-C stretching from phosphate group bonded to C surface, aromatic C = C stretching, and aliphatic C-H bond are the functional groups present on the material surface. The interaction between PMP-AC and MO take place mainly through pore filling, electrostatic attraction, π-π interaction and hydrogen bonding. MO could be desorbed from the material using a 0.1 N NaOH solution, allowing reuse for up to two additional cycles. As per the observed outcomes, use of PMP-AC for MO removal is a successful, low-cost and eco-friendly process.

Introduction

Water is one of the most important basic needs of the total eco-system. However, this precious water gets polluted due to several reasons. Discharge of industrial wastewater into the environment adds various toxic pollutants in the water bodies [1]. These toxic substances are heavy metal ions, synthetic chemicals, dyes, suspended solids, and contagious microorganisms etc. [2]. Out of all these contaminations, dye contamination in water bodies is a common problem. The textile, food, paper, ink, leather refining and pigment industries are the main source of such type of pollution [3]. Globally, more than 100,000 commercial dyes are available, and nearly 280,000 tons of dyes are released into the environment through wastewater from printing and dyeing processes each year [4]. The concentration range of dyes in industrial wastewater varies in between 1 and 2500 mg/L [5]. Dyes are generally non-biodegradable [6]. Presence of dyes in the water bodies creates different environmental problems. They inhibit photosynthesis process. Most of the dyes are also extremely toxic, teratogenic, carcinogenic, and are dangerous to human health. Therefore, industries should treat their wastewater prior to its discharge into common water bodies [7]. Different methods such as chemical precipitation, membrane filtration, and ion exchange are applied for the dye treatment. However, these methods are not practically suitable because either of their cost, complexity or sludge generation [8]. The adsorption technique is a promising alternative to these methods, as it is low-cost, simple to operate, and highly efficient. It is applicable under a wide range of experimental conditions and does not require expensive equipment or skilled manpower [9].

In adsorption technique, contaminants/pollutants get accumulated on the surface of a solid material known as adsorbent [10]. The first step in this method is the selection of adsorbent which may be either naturally occurring (such as soil, ores or minerals, plant or animal parts or, their wastes, or microorganisms etc.) or synthetic (such as various polymeric or nanomaterials or their composites) [11, 12]. Natural materials are widely used in adsorption technology for the removal of dyes and other pollutants because of their easy availability, low-cost and eco-friendly nature. Most of the natural materials possess negatively charged functional groups (carboxyl, carbonyl, hydroxyl, phosphate, nitrate etc.) on their surfaces [13, 14]. So, they are more specific towards the removal of positively charged pollutants present in water and the mechanism is electrostatic force of attraction.

Research on cationic pollutants removal using low-cost materials is also comparatively more compared to anionic pollutants. Using Scopus database (scopus.com), the research reported (01.02.2026) on total dye removal using low-cost materials through adsorption technology was explored in all fields using the search keys removal followed by dyes, water, low-cost materials and adsorption technology sequentially. The distribution curve within the years 2010–2026 is shown as Fig. 1. It is found that total 86,448 articles (A) is published in this field. Filtering the search for removal, cationic dyes, water, low-cost materials and adsorption technology, the number of publications was found to be 35,075 (B). Using the same search keys used in the same sequence the number of publications for anionic dyes was found to be 29,153 (C). B and C are 40.1 and 33.7% of A respectively.

The affinity of adsorbent materials towards negatively charged pollutants can also be developed in certain specific conditions. The present investigation therefore aims to identify such specific conditions using an anionic/negatively charged dye methyl orange (MO). MO is an anionic azo dye. Among the different types of dyes, azo dyes are the most commonly used and almost 60–70% of the commercially used dyes belong to this class [15]. It contains azo group (–N = N–) as the primary chromophore [16]. MO is widely used in the laboratories as an acid-base indicator. It is also used in the paint, textile, fiber and cosmetic industries [17]. MO is extremely toxic and even trace amount of it is unsafe for living system [18]. Its presence in water causes various environmental disorders and health issues (allergies, hypersensitivity and dermatitis) like other dyes. The concentration of MO in industrial wastewater varies from few mg/L up to around 200 mg/L. More specifically, in textile industrial wastewater, MO is a common pollutant and its concentration ranges from 10 to 50 mg/L [19].

Various low-cost materials are already used by several researchers for the removal of MO using adsorption technique. Some of them are highlighted as follows. Green rust, which is a mixed valent iron mineral belonging to the LDH (layered double hydroxide) family can successfully remove MO from wastewater. Green rust is a low-cost and sustainable material used as adsorbent [20]. MO adsorption on various low-cost adsorbents is reviewed by Al-Kazragi et al. [21]. Here the authors have considered both unmodified and modified low-cost natural materials. The authors stated that adsorption is proved to be an efficient method compared to other techniques. After suitable modifications MO uptakes on natural materials are significantly improved. Anchote peel, an agricultural waste is reported to remove MO from aqueous system as a low-cost material [17]. Similarly, populous and jackfruit leaves can also be utilized to remove MO from aqueous solution [22, 23].

In the present investigation, Mango peel (MP) is chosen as a low-cost adsorbent for the removal of MO from the aqueous medium. Mango belongs to the family Anacardiaceae, genus Mangifera and species indica. It is a very popular fruit owing to its delightful taste, tremendous smell and health benefits. Due to high demand, its peels are accumulated as waste in food processing industries after consumption. So, it can be used as a significant low-cost, eco-friendly, green and sustainable adsorbent for the removal of various pollutants including dyes present in the wastewater [24]. Researchers have already identified it as a green material for the decontamination of wastewater. For example, inorganic metal nanoparticles fabricated from MP derived bioactives can be utilized for wastewater treatment [25]. Patra & Mishra have systematically reviewed the use of various forms of MP as adsorbents for the decontamination of wastewater [26]. Safranin O dye is reported to be removed by MP derived biosorbent. MP can also be used as a low-cost sustainable material for the removal of methylene blue from aqueous systems [24, 27]. However, from the existing literature, it is observed that MP is more specific towards cationic pollutants. So, the present investigation aims in testing and developing the affinity of MP towards the anionic dye MO.

The main objective of the present research is (a) to conduct adsorption experiments using MO as adsorbate and MP as adsorbent; (b) surface modification of MP to increase its efficiency; (c) selection of the best modified MP derived adsorbent; (d) characterization of the selected adsorbent for the surface properties determination; (e) optimization of the adsorption conditions for MO removal; (f) detailed isotherm/kinetic/thermodynamic studies to find out the mechanism of the process; (g) desorption studies to assess the circular economy.

The novelty of this work lies in the development of an efficient, low-cost adsorbent derived from mango peel biomass through phosphoric acid activation followed by carbonization for the removal of methyl orange. Very limited studies have explored mango peel as an adsorbent, and reports on its application either in unmodified or modified form for methyl orange removal are not available. Unlike raw mango peel, which exhibits negligible adsorption capacity, the modified material demonstrates significantly enhanced performance, highlighting the crucial role of chemical activation and thermal treatment. To the best of our knowledge, the use of this specific precursor–activation–carbonization approach for methyl orange removal has not been previously reported. The study further establishes clear structure–adsorption relationships through detailed characterization, supported by kinetic and isotherm analyses. In addition, regeneration and reuse experiments, along with cost estimation, provide insight into the material’s practical applicability and economic feasibility. Overall, this work presents a sustainable approach to agro-waste valorization through a simple synthesis route with a reduced environmental impact, demonstrating potential for real-world wastewater treatment applications.

Materials and methods

Material as adsorbent

Waste mango peel (MP) was collected after household consumption. It was cleaned thoroughly with tap water to remove the associated contaminants/dirts. The excess moisture was removed after sun drying it for 4 d. The remaining trace of moisture was removed after keeping these materials in the oven at 80 °C for 24 h. These dehydrated materials were powdered with the help of a domestic grinder. This is the unmodified form of MP which is stored in a desiccator for future use. The material is then modified as per the procedure represented in Fig. 2. The MP was soaked with 85% phosphoric acid (H3PO4) in 1:1 (g/mL) ratio. The excess liquid was removed by filtration. Then the material was dried in an oven at 80 °C for 24 h. This H3PO4 acid treated MP was heated in a furnace at 600 °C for 3 h. Then this thermo-chemically activated biochar was cleaned with distilled water followed by drying at 80 °C in an oven for 24 h. The detailed procedure is mentioned in Fig. 2. This H3PO4 activated MP carbon is used as adsorbent in the present investigation.

Adsorbate and other chemicals

Methyl orange (MO), AR grade, Merck Specialities Private Limited, C.I. 13,025, CAS 547-58-0, Mumbai, India was the adsorbate in the present investigation. Its other name is acid orange 52 with IUPAC name Sodium 4-{[4-(dimethylamino)phenyl]diazinyl}benzene-1-sulfonate. It has a molar mass of 327.33 g/mol and a chemical formula of C14H14N3NaO3S [28]. Its structure is mentioned in Fig. 3. A standard stock solution of 1 g/L MO dye was prepared dissolving 1 g dye in 1 L of double distilled water. This stock solution was diluted to various concentration levels required for the adsorption studies. The other chemicals used were potassium nitrate (KNO3) (≥ 99% pure, Merck Life Science Private Limited, Mumbai, India), hydrochloric acid (HCl) (35% pure, Loba Chemie Private Limited, Mumbai, India), sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (≥ 99% pure, Merck Life Science Private Limited, Mumbai, India), nitric acid (HNO3) (69% pure, Merck Life Science Private Limited, Mumbai, India) and phosphoric acid (H3PO4) (85% pure, Merck Life Science Private Limited, Mumbai, India).

Analysis and quality control

MO concentration was measured using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (double beam, Elico SL-244). All the glassware was soaked in chromic acid overnight for cleaning, followed by washing with deionized water and drying in an oven.

The point of zero charge (pzc) of the studied adsorbent was determined using salt addition method [30]. The specific surface area per unit weight of adsorbent was determined using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (B.E.T) method by a Nova Station A instrument (QuantachromeNovaWin©1994–2018). The porosity of the material was further estimated following the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method [32].

Desorption study

To prepare MO loaded adsorbent required for the desorption study, the adsorbent was treated with 50 mg/L of MO solution thrice at an adsorbent dosage of 10 g/L. The MO uptake on the adsorbent under study was estimated to be 18.5 mg/g. 0.1 N NaOH was taken as desorbing agent with an adsorbent dosage of 5 g/L. After desorption, each time the adsorbent was rinsed with double distilled water repeatedly for the removal of excess alkali. The material was then dried and kept for future use.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were conducted thrice to get accurate result, and the averages of the obtained results are presented for the calculations, with their corresponding standard deviations. Statistical methods were applied for the assessment of the consistency and feasibility of the experimental results. The suitability of the observed results towards the proposed models was assessed from their correlation coefficients and pertinent error analysis functions. Microsoft Excel software was used for the calculations whereas; graphs were plotted using Origin-8.5.

Results and discussion

Modification of the adsorbent

MO adsorption on unmodified MP at temperature = 30 °C, pH = 4, time = 120 min, solid: liquid ratio = 5 g/L, and MO concentration = 50 mg/L was found to be only 22%. The efficiency of low-cost materials is low which can be improved after suitable modifications [37]. Therefore, basing on literature, MP surface was modified in three different ways: (a) physical activation, (b) chemical activation, (c) physico-chemical activation [38]. In physical activation method, a fixed amount of MP was heated in muffle furnace at 400 °C for 2 h. Before keeping it in furnace, it was sun dried followed by heating in an oven at 70 °C for 6 h. After activation, the material was cooled to room temperature and then used for adsorption studies. Similar activation method is reported by Saha and his team during treatment of leather industry dye contaminated waste water with Tamarindus indica leaves [39]. Sulphuric acid was used as the chemical activating agent (solid: liquid ratio, g/mL = 1:1). Sulphuric acid treatment removes dirts and other impurities from the adsorbent’s surface increasing porosity, surface area and active sites. Chemical activation of biosorbents using sulphuric acid for the removal of malachite green dye is already reported [40]. Phosphoric acid is another modifying agent which is already used by various researchers [41]. The physico-chemical modification of MP was carried out using phosphoric acid followed by converting it to biochar (in muffle furnace at 600 °C for 3 h). MO adsorption was carried out on all these physically, chemically and physico-chemically activated biosorbents in the identical condition (mentioned above) to that of unmodified one. The adsorption % on different adsorbents are as follows; unmodified = 22, simple biochar (physically modified) = 0, H2SO4 treated MP (chemically modified) = 42.9, and H3PO4 treated MP biochar (physico-chemically modified) = 97.21. The result is shown as Fig. 5. The adsorbent prepared by physico-chemical method using H3PO4 as the modifying agent or H3PO4 treated MP derived activated carbon (PMP-AC) is found to be the best. So, in the present investigation, detailed studies on MO removal using PMP-AC have been carried out.

PMP-AC is an activated carbon with improved surface properties. H3PO4 is a chemical activator and acts as both oxidizing and dehydrating agent. During activation with it porosity is developed and the development of surface properties is directly related to H3PO4 concentration. During heating volatile matter is vaporized off increasing porosity and surface area further and convert it to a better adsorbent [42]. Higher activation temperature increases pore volume and surface area but the overall yield of the activated carbon may decrease. Due to this physico-chemical method, several new functional groups may also be introduced onto the adsorbent surface. These groups arise may be due to P incorporation, partial oxidation or, acid catalyzed dehydration. These functional groups may interact with MO either through electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding or co-ordinate covalent bonding as in metal complexes [43]. The physical activation through heat treatment decrease the adsorption capacity may be because of destruction of functional groups, collapse of pores, burning of C in air or effect on the structure and surface chemistry of the adsorbent [44]. During chemical activation by sulphuric acid, excessive acidic, negatively charged and structurally damaged surfaces may be created causing repulsion of MO from it [45].

Table 2 shows some important physicochemical properties of PMP-AC. The pH and pzc are found to be 2.24 and 3.75 respectively. Both are acidic in nature. Acidic nature of adsorbent indicates presence of higher concentration of H+ ions on it. It leads to increase in positivity of the adsorbent surface [46]. So, it may have higher affinity towards negatively charged adsorbates and may be in this case towards MO. The moisture content and mass loss on ignition are found to be 11.7 and 75.77% respectively. The bulk density is found to be 1.25 g/cm3. All these properties are suitable for a good adsorbent. Density has no direct relationship with adsorption capacity. However, an adsorbent with lower bulk density indicates the presence of more porosity in the material. For micro, meso and macroporous carbon bulk density is (< 0.85), (0.85–1.85) and (> 1) g/cm3 respectively [47]. So PMP-AC may be a macroporous substance. The factors such as specific surface area, pore size and pore volume play important role to determine quality of an adsorbent. The % moisture content is 11.7 indicating it to be a good sorbent (5–12% for ideal sorbent) and also ideal for storing. The % loss of mass on ignition for biosorbents varies from 50 to 90. It is a measure of organic and volatile matter present within it which gets decomposed during heating [48].

Measurement of surface area

Adsorption is a surface phenomenon. So, surface area has an important role in evaluating adsorption properties. BET (Brunauer–emmett–teller) method was used for surface area measurement considering N2 adsorption-desorption isotherm shown in Fig. 6. The shape of the isotherm and the H3-type hysteresis loop is characteristic of a Type IV isotherm, indicating the mesoporous nature of the material with slit-shaped pores. Pore volume and pore size were determined using BJH (Barrett-Joyner-Halenda) method. The surface area was found to be 383.5m2/g, whereas, pore volume and pore size were found to be 0.486 cm3/g and 3.4 nm respectively. The surface area of PMP-AC is almost 28 times higher compared to that of MP (13.455m2/g) [49]. The surface area obtained here is equivalent to that of raspberry derived activated carbon (380m2/g) [10]. The pore size 3.4 nm indicates mesoporous nature of the material and mesoporous materials are good adsorbents because of their remarkable surface area and pore size [50].

Effect of different parameters

Determination of optimum contact period

Fig. 9MO adsorption (%) with respect to contact time variation

MO adsorption (%) with respect to contact time variation

Optimum contact period has major significance in adsorption technology. Optimum contact time is the time required for the equilibrium to achieve. The contact period is also a major factor that determines cost of the process. To determine optimum contact period for the present system, experiments were carried out varying contact time from 0 to 120 min keeping other parameters constant (pH = 4.0, temperature = 30 °C, stirring speed = 200 rpm, initial MO concentration in solution = 50 mg/L, and PMP-AC concentration = 5 g/L). The result is shown as Fig. 9. It was observed that within first 5 min almost 86% adsorption occurs. Then it increased slowly up to 60 min where maximum adsorption (97.21%) occurred. Beyond 60 min no improvement in adsorption was observed. So, 60 min is considered to be the optimum contact period. Same trend was also observed during MO adsorption on natural jackfruit leaves powder [23]. Initially the active sites present on adsorbent surface (PMP-AC) are free and are ready to adsorb incoming adsorbate species (here is MO). Gradually, the active sites are filled up and after certain time equilibrium is achieved in the system. Equilibrium means both adsorption and desorption take place simultaneously. As a result, MO concentration remains constant both in solid and liquid phase.

Determination of optimum pH

Fig. 10MO adsorption (%) with respect to variation in pH

MO adsorption (%) with respect to variation in pH

The pH is the most important parameter in the adsorption experiments. The pH regulates the charge on the adsorbent surface along with the ionization of MO in the aqueous solution. The pH of dye contaminated wastewater varies widely due to the use of various chemicals during industrial processing and the range is beyond the normal pH range of naturally available water. The pH of dye contaminated water may reach the range from 2 to 12. But usually for adsorption experiments the pH is restricted to 10 to prevent the structural degradation of both dye and the adsorbents. The effect of pH on the present investigation was studied by varying the pH of the solution from 2 to 10. As seen from Fig. 10, at lowest pH, highest removal took place (pH 2, 98.8%). The removal % decreased gradually with increase of pH and at pH 10, it reached 75%. The pzc of PMP-AC is 3.75. Below pzc, the surface of PMP-AC is positively charged. As MO is an anionic dye, lower pH favors MO adsorption and the mechanism is electrostatic force of attraction. But at higher pH the adsorbent surface becomes negatively charged and does not favor adsorption of negatively charged MO on it. At higher pH also, excessive –OH− ions compete with MO for the same active adsorption sites so that extent of adsorption is reduced. MO adsorption on populous leaves also showed similar type of behavior with respect to pH [22].

Effect of adsorbent (PMP-AC) concentration

Fig. 11Effect of adsorbent amount on MO adsorption %

Effect of adsorbent amount on MO adsorption %

To study the effect of PMP-AC concentration in the present investigation, its concentration in the reaction vessel was varied from 1 to 10 g/L separately keeping other parameters constant (time = 60 min, pH = 4.0, temperature = 30 °C, and MO concentration = 50 mg/L). With the increase of adsorbent concentration, active sorption sites are increased favoring higher % of MO removal. However, the same amount of MO was shared among a greater number of active sites. So, MO uptake, mg/g decreased with the same variation in the adsorbent dose [59]. With variation of adsorbent dosages from 1 to 10 g/L, the uptakes were reduced from 45.98 to 4.93 mg/g and % of adsorption increased from 91.97 to 98.5. The result is shown in Fig. 11. At higher adsorbent concentration, the particles are aggregated with each other so that the total available surface area may not be exposed towards MO. It may lead to an increased diffusion path length towards active binding sites, thereby hindering the adsorption process and reducing MO uptake, mg/g. In order to avoid adsorbent wastage, its quantity should be optimized and in the present investigation most of the experiments are carried out using 5 g/L adsorbent dose.

Effect of MO concentration

The impact of MO concentration on its adsorption onto PMP-AC was studied by varying the MO concentration from 10 to 90 mg/L keeping other parameters constant (time = 60 min, pH = 4.0, temperature = 30 °C, and PMP-AC concentration = 5 g/L). With the increase of MO concentration, the driving force needed for mass transfer from aqueous to solid phase (adsorbent) increases. As a result adsorption quantity, mg/g increases. Even though MO concentration in the solution increases, fixed amount of PMP-AC provide limited adsorption sites and cannot remove MO proportionately. So, % of adsorption decreases. The result is shown as Fig. 12. When MO concentration in the solution increased from 10 to 90 mg/L, its uptake, mg/g increased from 1.98 to 17.13 and uptake, % decreased from 98.78 to 95.15.

Adsorption isotherms/kinetics

The adsorption isotherm describes the relationship between the amount of adsorbate adsorbed on the adsorbent surface (qe) and its concentration in the surrounding liquid medium (Ce) at constant temperature under equilibrium conditions. Different adsorption isotherms are Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin and Dubinin- Radushkevich (D.R) etc. Such relationships are represented graphically through mathematical models. These isotherms give information about the characteristics of the adsorbent surface and the adsorption process at equilibrium. Adsorption kinetics describe rate of the adsorption process as a function of time. It helps in understanding the underlying mechanism (physical/chemical adsorption). Different kinetic equations are Lagergren’s PFO, Ho & Mc-Kay’s PSO and Morris-Weber. The details of all these kinetic/isotherm equations are discussed earlier in the experimental section. Different parameters derived from all these kinetic/isotherm equations for the present adsorption system are shown in Table 4.

The correlation coefficient (R2) values of Ho & Mc-Kay’s PSO kinetics are found to be more compared to that of Lagergren’s PFO. So, PSO rate constants (k2) are taken into account for all the related calculations. For 30, 40 and 50 °C, k2 are found to be 0.262, 0.202 and 0.115 g/mg.min respectively. Weber Morris is otherwise known as intraparticle diffusion model. It analyses the movement of solute within the pores of the adsorbent as a function of time. It helps determine whether intraparticle diffusion is the rate limiting step. As mentioned in the experimental section, Weber Morris plot is drawn in between qt and t1/2. In the present case, the plot shows multiple linear segments which indicates that both boundary layer diffusion and intraparticle diffusion holds good at different stages.

Both the Freundlich and Langmuir isotherms fit the experimental data well, as evidenced by their correlation coefficient (R2) values shown in Table 4. PMP-AC contains both micro and meso pores in its structure. SEM also indicates a heterogeneous surface with multiple functional groups. But these characteristics go against Langmuir’s concept (homogeneous adsorbent surface, equivalent adsorption sites and unilayer adsorption). Since Langmuir isotherm also shows high R2 value, most probably adsorption occurs in micropores predominantly with limitations in experimental concentration ranges. The adsorption may be assumed to be nearly monolayer with finite no of strong adsorption sites. Langmuir monolayer adsorption capacity is 23.2 mg/g. The RL (separation factor) values are determined using Eq.[7] and are in the range of 0.02 to 0.14. So, this process is feasible in nature. Dubinin- Radushkevich (D.R.) isotherm describes adsorption equilibrium. An empirical model, that describes adsorption in microporous solids accounting for a pore-filling mechanism and a distribution of adsorption energies. It gives knowledge about the porosity of the adsorbent material and free energy of adsorption. It determines whether the process is physisorption or chemisorption in nature. It is a specific type of Langmuir isotherm which is applied both to homogeneous and heterogeneous surfaces. Xm’ represents maximum D.R. sorption capacity. K’ is the activity coefficient and is related to mean sorption energy (E). E is the free energy change required to transfer 1 mol of ions from infinity to the adsorbent surface. E is determined from K’ using Eq.[9] represented in experimental section. For physisorption, E < 8 kJ/mol and for chemisorption its values lie within 8 and 16 kJ/mol. In the present investigation the value of E is found to be 2.9 kJ/mol. So, the process may be physisorption in nature [60]. The details are shown in Table 4. According to Temkin isotherm, as the adsorbent surface is covered with the adsorbate molecules, adsorption heat decrease linearly. During adsorption binding energy is uniformly distributed and attains a maximum value. The Temkin parameters are listed in Table 4. Temkin heat of adsorption equal to 4.37 J/mol also indicates physisorption nature of the present adsorption system.

Effect of temperature on adsorption

Adsorption experiments were performed at three different temperatures (30, 40 and 50 °C) keeping other parameters constant at their optimum condition. The % of MO adsorption was found to be decreased with increase of temperature as seen in Fig. 13. It means it is an exothermic process which is commonly observed in most of the adsorption studies [61]. At higher temperature the mobility of adsorbate increases and therefore probability of contact between both adsorbent and adsorbate may decrease reducing extent of adsorption. Physical adsorption is usually exothermic in nature.

Fig. 13Effect of temperature on % of MO adsorption

Effect of temperature on % of MO adsorption

The temperature dependency of a particular adsorption system is well described by different thermodynamic parameters. These parameters are changes in Gibbs free energy (ΔG), enthalpy (ΔH), and entropy (ΔS). ΔG and ΔS represent spontaneity and randomness of the process respectively, whereas, ΔH gives information about exothermic/endothermic nature. These parameters are calculated using Eqs. 5 and 6. The thermodynamic parameters for the present system are shown in Table 5. The low value of activation energy indicates that the reaction is faster and easier to be initiated with low energy requirement. The negative activation energy and enthalpy change gives indication about the exothermic nature of the process. The ΔH value (−57.74 kJ/mol) suggests weak to moderate chemical interaction between PMP-AC and MO. However, mean free energy derived from D.R. isotherm suggest the process to be physisorption in nature. This contradiction can be explained by the heterogeneous nature of lignocellulosic materials derived adsorbents where multiple types of interaction occur simultaneously. The interaction is predominantly physisorption because of pore-filling mechanism through weak van der Waal’s force of interaction, electrostatic attraction or, hydrogen bonding. While some localized stronger interaction like surface complexation may also occur justifying chemisorption. Therefore, the adsorption process is predominantly physisorption with partial contribution from chemisorption [14]. Formation of stable product after adsorption is confirmed from negative entropy change. The negative free energy changes at all the studied temperatures indicate spontaneity of the process. So, 30 °C is the optimum temperature for the present adsorption system.

Mechanism of MO adsorption

During adsorption, both nature of adsorbent and adsorbate play important role. Usually, the adsorbent–adsorbate interaction takes place through synergistic effect from development of porosity (physical adsorption), electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding and π-π interaction. All probable interactions in between PMP-AC and MO are discussed below with a pictorial representation in Fig. 14.

i.Development of pores followed by physical adsorption: H3PO4 is a dehydrating agent. So, it dehydrates MP with the development of micro and mesoporous structure. In this way surface area and pore volume of the material increase. Post carbonization makes these pores stable. During interaction MO diffuse into the pores and are attracted to the PMP-AC structure by van der Waal’s forces of attraction. This phenomenon is of the type physisorption. It is the primary interaction mechanism in between PMP-AC and MO.ii.Electrostatic attraction: MO is an anionic dye and exists mainly as –SO3- in water. The oxygen containing functional groups present on PMP-AC get protonated at low pH. The –OH and –COOH groups present on unmodified MP are decomposed during preparation of PMP-AC at higher temperature. The only O containing functional groups are P-O or P-O-C. So, surface acidity is introduced by H3PO4. As a result, MO and PMP-AC interact with each other through electrostatic force of attraction. But this type of mechanism is strongly pH dependent and is favoured at low pH.iii.Hydrogen bonding: This type of interaction occurs between oxygen containing surface functional groups present on PMP-AC and –N = N–, –SO3- and aromatic N atoms present in MO. So, hydrogen bonding may be a probable mechanism between PMP-AC and MO. However, this type of bonding is secondary and weak in the present case due to availability of limited number of oxygen containing functional groups.iv.π-π interaction: After carbonization aromaticity and graphitic domains are developed on the adsorbent surface. MO also possess aromatic ring in its structure. So, π-π stacking interaction occurs between aromatic dye and C containing adsorbent surfaces. This is a dominant mechanism in the present case.

Similar type of mechanism is also reported by Ambaye et al. for the removal of various organic and inorganic pollutants on biochars [62].

However, Langmuir’s qm value alone is not enough to assess adsorption performance particularly for heterogeneous biomass based carbons. The efficiency of an adsorbent is dependent on its overall performance, mechanism and applicability [63]. The comparison is presented in Table 6. Since the presently studied adsorbent is a biomass based carbon, its qm comes in the range of lower to moderate. The qm values of other state-of-the-art adsorbents such as modified/functionalized activated carbon or advanced composites (magnetic, polymer hybrids) towards MO adsorption are much more (usually in the range of 50 to 1000 mg/g). Porous carbon material prepared from potassium citrate via calcination, with a specific surface area of 1436 m2/g, exhibits an MO uptake capacity of 927 mg/g [70]. But, these techniques are associated with limitations like use of expensive precursors, complex multi-step synthesis, higher cost, difficult regeneration, and harder to scale for real wastewater. The present study offers significant advantages in terms of sustainability, cost-effectiveness, and ease of synthesis, as the adsorbent is derived from agricultural waste and prepared via a simple activation process. Despite of its moderate adsorption capacity, the material demonstrates strong potential for practical wastewater treatment. In the present case, the effective adsorption is achieved under mild conditions (neutral pH, ambient temperature and low dosage). Other advantages of the process are faster kinetics, favorable surface functional groups and suitable pore structure for methyl orange removal. Although a high qm value indicates better performance, low-cost and scalable materials are more important for practical real world applications.

Desorption, cost and life cycle analysis of PMP-AC

MO adsorption on PMP-AC is favoured at lower pH. Therefore, it’s desorption is expected to be at higher pH. So 0.1 N NaOH is chosen as the desorbing agent for the present investigation. The same is also reported by several researchers for MO desorption from various adsorbents [71]. After regeneration of PMP-AC, it was reused in two more adsorption-desorption cycles. The MO adsorption in the 1 st cycle was 97.21% and in the next two cycles it was reduced to 86.19 and 71.46% respectively. The corresponding mass loss in both the cycles is 14% and 33% respectively. The experimental conditions for all the cycles were kept equivalent, i.e., temperature = 30 °C, stirring speed = 200 rpm, contact time = 60 min, pH = 4.0, and MO concentration = 50 mg/L. The observed mass loss and reduction in adsorption efficiency in the adsorption-desorption cycles may be due to a combination of both physical and chemical effects. Under alkaline condition, the adsorbent material is susceptible to partial dissolution/structural degradation particularly due to leaching of oxygen-containing surface functional groups including PO4−3. In addition, physical attrition during stirring makes the adsorbent material fragmented and the finer particles get lost during filtration/washing stages. The % decrease of MO removal in subsequent steps is due to some combined causes like adsorbent loss and thereby active site loss and alternation in pore-structure [72].

To select an adsorbent in a continuous adsorption-desorption cycle, cost analysis of the process is required. During preparation of PMP-AC, major cost includes (i) raw materials (MP and H3PO4) (ii) energy requirement (for drying, grinding, activation and carbonization). Use of MP based materials as adsorbent is fewer in the literature. Therefore, similar type of biosorbent generated from other equivalent biomaterials is considered for comparison. Production cost of biochar and activated carbon derived from biomaterials varies from 1.06 to 1.34 USD/kG [73]. Cost of tamarind coke activated carbon is 1.67 USD/kG at laboratory scale [74].

After estimating the production cost, its economic efficiency should be evaluated in terms of MO removal. For this evaluation, the adsorption capacity of PMP-AC (23.2 mg/g) should be considered. So, for the removal of 1 g of MO, 43.1 g of PMP-AC is required and the cost of 1 g MO removal onto PMP-AC will be in the range 0.052–0.22 USD. As per the literature it is cost effective which is comparable to the cost of MO removal on date palm spikelets (DPS) derived composite ZnO@DPS-AC (0.355–0.465 USD/g) [75]. In industrial scale, the cost will be reduced further due to bulk purchasing of materials at low price. So, it would be more practical in industrial scale due to its cost effectiveness.

The objective of life cycle analysis is to assess the environmental impact of the present research during MO removal. It starts from raw material MP waste collection followed by adsorbent preparation and then its application for MO removal. MP waste is used as precursor here. So resource depletion is minimal. Relatively low activation temperature indicates less energy consumption. Use of phosphoric acid has moderate impact towards chemical toxicity but it is less than that of commercial activated carbon preparation [76]. Impact towards global warming is low to moderate because it arises mainly from energy use. Due to waste valorization, the principle of waste management and circular economy is also greatly supported.

Conclusions and future challenges

The present research investigates the potential of H₃PO₄-treated mango peel derived activated carbon (PMP-AC) as an efficient, low-cost adsorbent for the removal of methyl orange dye from aqueous media. Natural low-cost materials are usually more specific towards cationic species. The present study proves that after suitable modification they are also capable of removing anionic pollutants successfully. The optimum contact time and pH are 60 min and 2.0 respectively. The process is spontaneous and exothermic in nature. The monolayer Langmuir MO uptake on PMP-AC is 23.2 mg/g. The interaction between MO and PMP-AC can be explained through pore filling, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction and π-π interaction. The used PMP-AC can be regenerated using 0.1 N NaOH and its reusability are tested for 2 more cycles. The improved adsorption performance after modification of the biosorbent suggests that simple chemical treatment can notably enhance the effectiveness of agricultural waste materials. The present research offers a dual benefit of waste valorization and environmental protection, providing a practical solution for reducing industrial dye pollution. Compared to other state-of-the-art techniques, the present material exhibits competitive adsorption capacity while emphasizing sustainability, cost-effectiveness, and eco-friendliness.

Certain limitations of the present research should be acknowledged. The study was carried out under laboratory conditions, which may vary when applied on a large scale. It does not consider the complexity of real dye-contaminated wastewater. The factors like presence of co-contaminants, and prolonged adsorbent stability were not considered. Recognizing these constraints, the reliability of the study can be strengthened by focusing on areas for further research. Additionally, optimization of the adsorbent preparation conditions and exploration of new surface modification techniques could improve the adsorption capacity further. Other dyes and emerging contaminants can also be tested using the present material at both pilot and industrial scales, so that it may lead to real-world water treatment solutions.
