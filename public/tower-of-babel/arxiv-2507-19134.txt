Short-Range Approximation to Casimir Wormholes Inspired by Scalar and Electric Fields
Remo Garattini, Athanasios G. Tzikas

Abstract
We investigate a static traversable wormhole sustained by a combination of a minimally coupled scalar field and an electric field, with exotic matter sourced by Casimir energy. Considering two scenarios, where the Casimir plate separation is either radially variable or fixed, we derive analytical near-throat solutions for both massless and massive scalar fields. To ensure consistency of the field equations, a thermal tensor is also incorporated, consisting solely of pressure terms that vanish at the throat. In all cases, we obtain well-behaved wormhole manifolds with throat sizes that scale proportionally with the number of elementary charges the wormhole can support.



I
Introduction

It is well established that a Traversable Wormhole (TW) requires exotic matter to be sustained. Since exotic matter has yet to be discovered, one may seek an alternative that behaves in a similar manner. In this context, the Casimir effect presents a promising candidate for mimicking exotic matter
Casimir
. The reason is the following: if two plane, parallel, closely spaced, uncharged metallic plates are placed in a vacuum, an attractive force arises between them. This force is generated by a negative energy, represented by

E^{\text{Ren}}(d)=-\frac{\hbar c\pi^{2}S}{720d^{3}}\,

(1)

where
S
is the surface of the plates,
d
is the distance between them and

E^{\text{Ren}}(d)
stands for the renormalized energy. This energy arises from the zero-point energy of the quantum electrodynamic field. A noteworthy property is that it suggests the presence of an equation of state of the form

P=\omega\rho
with
\omega=3\,
, where
P
stands for the pressure and
\rho
for the
energy density. Based on this property, a specific type of TW, termed as the Casimir Wormhole
CW
, has been investigated. Its geometric profile is described by the following functions

\Phi(r)={\ln}\left({\frac{3r}{3r+r_{0}}}\right)\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad b(r)=\frac{2}{3}r_{0}+\frac{r_{0}^{2}}{3r}

(2)

with
\Phi\!\left(r\right)
and
b(r)
representing the redshift
and the shape function of the wormhole respectively. Such a wormhole solution satisfies the Einstein Field Equations (EFE), provided that an additional inhomogeneous equation of state is introduced between the transverse pressure
p_{t}(r)
and the energy density
\rho(r)
, namely

p_{t}(r)=\omega_{t}\left(r\right)\rho\left(r\right).

(3)

Generalizations of the Casimir Wormhole have been studied by introducing temperature effects into the original Casimir result
HCW
, combining it with a scalar field
RGAZ
, and also incorporating rotations
RCW
. Another additional source was considered in
CCW
, where a static electric field was introduced. Yukawa deformations
YC
and Generalized Absurdly Benign Traversable Wormholes have also been examined
GABTW

.
Nevertheless, a combination of the three physical sources, namely the Casimir effect, electric field, and scalar field, has not been studied before. ;
BV
;
BV1

-

;

The purpose of this paper is to investigate the conditions under which these three physical sources can coexist. To proceed, we introduce the following spacetime metric in natural units

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-e^{2\Phi\left(r\right)}\,\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}{1-b(r)/r}+r^{2}\,(\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi^{2})\,

(4)

and

(b(r)-rb^{\prime}(r))/b^{2}(r)>0\,
. The prime symbol (

r

1-b(r)/r>0\,
. Furthermore, at the throat, the condition
b(r_{0})=r_{0}
must hold, and
the additional requirement

It is imperative that no horizons are present, which are
identified as the surfaces with
e^{2\Phi\left(r\right)}\rightarrow 0\,

\Phi\left(r\right)
must be finite everywhere.

Now with the help of
the line element (
4

\frac{b^{\prime}\left(r\right)}{r^{2}}=\kappa\rho\left(r\right)

(5)

\frac{2}{r}\left(1-\frac{b\left(r\right)}{r}\right)\Phi^{\prime}\left(r\right)-\frac{b\left(r\right)}{r^{3}}=\kappa p_{r}\left(r\right)

(6)

\left(1-\frac{b\left(r\right)}{r}\right)\left[\Phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(r\right)+\Phi^{\prime}\left(r\right)\left(\Phi^{\prime}\!\left(r\right)+\frac{1}{r}\right)\right]-\frac{b^{\prime}\left(r\right)r-b\left(r\right)}{2r^{2}}\left(\Phi^{\prime}\left(r\right)+\frac{1}{r}\right)=\kappa p_{t}(r)

(7)

in which
\kappa=8\pi G\,
,
\rho(r)
is the total energy density,
p_{r}(r)
is the total radial pressure, and
p_{t}(r)
is the total
tangential pressure. We can complete the EFE with the expression of the

reference frame as

p_{r}^{\prime}\left(r\right)=\frac{2}{r}\left(p_{t}\left(r\right)-p_{r}\left(r\right)\right)-\left(\rho\left(r\right)+p_{r}\left(r\right)\right)\Phi^{\prime}\left(r\right)\,.

(8)

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: In Sec.

The total SET of our system consists of four parts: one resulting from the Casimir setup, one from the scalar field, one from the electric field, and a fourth arising from relativistic thermodynamic assumptions, referred to as the thermal part. II
Structure of the Stress-Energy Tensor

Consider an anisotropic fluid described by the SET

T_{\mu\nu}=\rho(r)u_{\mu}u_{\nu}+p_{r}(r)n_{\mu}n_{\nu}+p_{t}(r)\sigma_{\mu\nu}

(9)

where
u_{\mu}
is the fluid four-velocity and
n_{\mu}
is a unit
spacelike vector orthogonal to
u_{\mu}\,
, i.e.,
n^{\mu}n_{\mu}=1\,
,
u^{\mu}u_{\mu}=-1\,
,
n^{\mu}u_{\mu}=0\,
. Here,

\sigma_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}+u_{\mu}u_{\nu}-n_{\mu}n_{\nu}

(10)

is a projection operator onto a two-surface orthogonal to
u_{\mu}
and
n_{\mu}\,
, i.e.,

u_{\mu}\sigma^{\mu\nu}\mathrm{v}_{\nu}=n_{\mu}\sigma^{\mu\nu}\mathrm{v}_{\nu}=0\qquad\forall\mathrm{v}_{\nu}\,.

(11)

The vector

can be represented as

n_{\mu}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-b(r)/r}}\left(0,1,0,0\right).

(12)

Before proceeding, it is useful to introduce two reference lengths:

r_{1}^{2}=\frac{\pi^{3}\ell_{\rm P}^{2}}{90}

(13)

and

r_{2}^{2}=\frac{GQ^{2}}{4\pi c^{4}\varepsilon_{0}}=\left(\frac{\hbar G}{c^{3}}\right)\frac{Q^{2}}{4\pi\hbar c\varepsilon_{0}}=\frac{Q^{2}\ell_{\rm P}^{2}}{4\pi\hbar c\varepsilon_{0}}\,.

(14)

Taking into account the quantization of charge,
Q=Ne
, where
e
is the elementary charge and
N
is an integer number, we find

r_{2}^{2}=\alpha N^{2}\ell_{\rm P}^{2}

(15)

with
\alpha\approx 1/137
being the fine structure constant. If
N\leq 6\,
, then
r_{1}>r_{2}\,
, while the opposite holds for
N\geq 7\,
. Exact equality is excluded (
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
), since it would correspond to
N\simeq 6.87\,
, which is not an integer. To proceed with a physically meaningful source, we now assume that the energy density is given by

\rho\left(r\right)=\rho_{S}\left(r\right)+\rho_{C}\left(r\right)+\rho_{E}\left(r\right)+\rho_{\tau}\left(r\right)=\rho_{S}\left(r\right)-\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\rho_{\tau}\left(r\right)

(16)

where
\rho_{S}\left(r\right)
is the scalar source,
\rho_{C}\left(r\right)
is the Casimir source,
\rho_{E}\left(r\right)
is the
electric source and
\rho_{\tau}(r)
is the thermal
energy density source. The thermal energy density is a part of
the thermal stress-energy tensor which arises from relativistic thermodynamic assumptions according to
Hayward
and includes also a radial component
\tau_{r}(r)
and a transverse component
\tau_{t}(r)
. The same decomposition
will be adopted for the rest of the SET, namely

p_{r}\left(r\right)=p_{r}^{S}\left(r\right)+p_{r}^{C}\left(r\right)+p_{r}^{E}\left(r\right)+\tau_{r}\left(r\right)=p_{r}^{S}\left(r\right)-\frac{3r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}-\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\tau_{r}\left(r\right)

(17)

and

p_{t}\left(r\right)=p_{t}^{S}\left(r\right)+p_{t}^{C}\left(r\right)+p_{t}^{E}\left(r\right)+\tau_{t}\left(r\right)=p_{t}^{S}\left(r\right)+\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\tau_{t}\left(r\right).

(18)

The scalar SET is different if we include a potential or if we exclude it.
The scalar field without a potential, hereafter referred to as the massless scalar
field, is governed by the scalar field equation
\nabla^{2}\psi=0
and is represented by the following quadruple
RGAZ

\displaystyle\left(\rho_{S}(r),p_{r}^{S}\left(r\right),p_{t}^{S}\left(r\right),p_{t}^{S}\left(r\right)\right)=\left(\frac{Ce^{-2\Phi(r)}}{2r^{4}},\frac{Ce^{-2\Phi(r)}}{2r^{4}},-\frac{Ce^{-2\Phi(r)}}{2r^{4}},-\frac{Ce^{-2\Phi(r)}}{2r^{4}}\right)\,,

(19)

while the scalar field with a potential, hereafter referred to as the massive
scalar field, is governed by the scalar field equation
\nabla^{2}\psi=\mathrm{d}V(\psi)/\mathrm{d}\psi
and is represented by this other quadruple:

\begin{array}[]{c}\rho_{S}(r)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{b(r)}{r}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}+V(\psi)\\
p_{r}^{S}(r)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{b(r)}{r}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}-V(\psi)\\
p_{t}^{S}(r)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{b(r)}{r}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}-V(\psi)\\
p_{t}^{S}(r)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{b(r)}{r}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}-V(\psi)\,.\end{array}

(20)

It is important to note that, in the case of a massless scalar field, the integration constant
C
is taken to be positive in order to avoid the introduction of
phantom
sources. Moreover, we assume the absence of additional thermal sources, implying that
\rho_{\tau}\left(r\right)=0\,
, even in the massive case. It should be emphasized that equations (
16
), (
17
) and
(
18

,

(21)

\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)=\frac{A^{3}\left(B\right)\left(2B\,r^{6}+\left(B-1\right)r_{0}r^{5}\right)+A^{2}\left(B\right)\left(8B^{2}-1\right)r_{0}^{2}r^{4}-A\left(B\right)r_{0}^{4}r\left(6Br+r_{0}\left(B-1\right)\right)+r_{0}^{6}}{2\left(A\left(B\right)4Br_{0}^{2}r^{2}+A^{2}\left(B\right)r^{4}-r_{0}^{4}\right)r^{4}\kappa A\left(B\right)}

(52)

where we have defined

A\left(B\right)=\sqrt{B\left(B+2\right)}+B+1\,.

(53)

Also in this case, we cannot find a point where
\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)

vanishes. To verify whether
\bar{r}
is compatible with the EFE, we note that there remains an unfixed parameter, namely the value of
C
. Using (
44
) and (
45
), the boundary
\bar{r}
becomes

\bar{r}=\frac{\sqrt{\sqrt{2C\kappa A\left(B\right)+4B^{2}r_{0}^{2}+r_{0}^{2}}-2Br_{0}}}{\sqrt{A\left(B\right)}}\sqrt{r_{0}}\,.

(54)

A compatible boundary must satisfy
\bar{r}>

r_{0}\,
. By defining the dimensionless constant

x=\frac{C\kappa}{r_{0}^{2}}\,

(55)

one gets

\bar{r}=\frac{\sqrt{\sqrt{2xA\left(B\right)+4B^{2}+1}-2B}}{\sqrt{A\left(B\right)}}r_{0}

(56)

and by plugging the value
B=0.425
inside
\bar{r}\,
, one
finds that
\bar{r}>r_{0}
if and only if
x>1.864\,
.

Last but not least, by considering the total radial pressure, the NEC takes the form

\rho(r)+p_{r}(r)=-\frac{(1-B)r_{0}}{\kappa r^{3}}

(57)

and is always violated for
B=0.425\,
, while numerical analysis shows that by using the tangential pressure, we get always
\rho(r)+p_{t}(r)>0\,
.

V
Near-throat solution for a massive scalar field with variable Casimir plates

When a potential is included as an additional source, the scalar field
equation becomes

\left(1-\frac{b(r)}{r}\right)\left[\psi^{\prime\prime}(r)+\left(\frac{2}{r}+\Phi^{\prime}(r)\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)\right]+\left(\frac{b(r)-rb^{\prime}(r)}{2r^{2}}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)=\frac{\mathrm{d}V(\psi)}{\mathrm{d}\psi}\,.

(58)

Since the equation of motion is considerably complicated with respect to the
massless case, such a complication is also transported to the SET, represented
by

\displaystyle\rho\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{b(r)}{r}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}+V(\psi)-\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}

(59)

\displaystyle p_{r}\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{b(r)}{r}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}-V(\psi)-\frac{3r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}-\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\tau_{r}\left(r\right)

(60)

\displaystyle p_{t}\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{b(r)}{r}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}-V(\psi)+\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\tau_{t}\left(r\right).

(61)

However, if we adopt the approximated profile of the shape function
\left(\ref{Ab(r)}\right)
, things are different because the SET reduces to

\displaystyle\rho\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=V(\psi)-\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}

(62)

\displaystyle p_{r}\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=-V(\psi)-\frac{3r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}-\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\tau_{r}\left(r\right)

(63)

\displaystyle p_{t}\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=-V(\psi)+\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\tau_{t}\left(r\right).

(64)

Moreover, the scalar field equation
\left(\ref{scalar_eom}\right)
can be
approximated with

\left(\frac{b(r)-rb^{\prime}(r)}{2r^{2}}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)=\frac{\mathrm{d}V(\psi)}{\mathrm{d}\psi}=\frac{V^{\prime}(r)}{\psi^{\prime}(r)}

(65)

leading to

\psi^{\prime}(r)=r\sqrt{\frac{2V^{\prime}(r)}{b(r)-rb^{\prime}(r)}}\,.

(66)

Since the scalar field depends only on the radial coordinate,
\psi=\psi(r)\,
, the potential can be expressed as a function of
r\,
, i.e.,
V(\psi)=V(r)\,
. It is interesting to note that, in this framework, the NEC becomes

\displaystyle\rho\left(r\right)+p_{r}\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\tau_{r}\left(r\right).

(67)

This means that the role of the thermal tensor is crucial to establish if
the NEC can be violated or not. To obtain this information, we begin by solving the first EFE (
5
), which yields

\frac{B}{r^{2}}=\kappa V(r)-\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{r^{4}}+\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{r^{4}}\,.

(68)

Solving
with respect to the potential, we can get its analytical representation in
terms of
r
, namely

V(r)=\frac{B}{\kappa r^{2}}+\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}-\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}\,.

(69)

The second EFE (
6
), within the same near-throat approximation and using (
69
), can be rewritten in terms of
\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)
as

\tau_{r}\left(r\right)=\frac{4r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\frac{r_{0}\left(B-1\right)}{\kappa r^{3}}\,.

(70)

Finally, from the
third EFE (
7
), we get

\displaystyle\tau_{t}\left(r\right)=\frac{r_{0}\left(1-B\right)}{2\kappa r^{2}}\left(\Phi\!^{\prime}\left(r\right)+\frac{1}{r}\right)+\frac{B}{\kappa r^{2}}-\frac{2r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}\,.

(71)

To further proceed, we need to use the SET conservation equation represented by

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{b(r)}{r}\right)\left[\psi^{\prime\prime}(r)\psi^{\prime}(r)+\left(\frac{2}{r}+\Phi\!^{\prime}\left(r\right)\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}\right]+\left(\frac{b(r)-rb^{\prime}(r)}{2r^{2}}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}-V^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)\kappa\,r^{5}-4rr_{1}^{2}\right)\Phi^{\prime}\!\left(r\right)+\tau_{r}^{\prime}\!\left(r\right)\kappa\,r^{5}+2\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)\kappa\,r^{4}-2\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)\kappa\,r^{4}+4r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa\,r^{5}}\,.

(72)

The first term of the above equation is identically zero because it coincides with the equation of motion for the scalar field (
58
), giving

\frac{\left(\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)\kappa\,r^{5}-4rr_{1}^{2}\right)\Phi^{\prime}\!\left(r\right)+\tau_{r}^{\prime}\!\left(r\right)\kappa\,r^{5}+2\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)\kappa\,r^{4}-2\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)\kappa\,r^{4}+4r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa\,r^{5}}\,=0\,.

(73)

This implies that the scalar field contribution is irrelevant in satisfying the SET conservation equation. To determine the profile of the redshift function, we simplify (
73
) using (
70
) and (
71
). This yields

\Phi^{\prime}(r)=\frac{B\,r^{2}+2r_{1}^{2}-2r_{2}^{2}}{r^{2}r_{0}\left(B-1\right)}

(74)

whose solution is

\Phi\!\left(r\right)=\frac{r^{2}B-2r_{1}^{2}+2r_{2}^{2}}{rr_{0}\left(B-1\right)}+K\,

(75)

with
K
being an integration constant. Since the location of the external boundary of such an approximation is
unknown, we may tempt to fix the external boundary
\bar{r}
in such a way that

\Phi\!\left(\bar{r}\right)=0\,
.
This implies
K=0
and

\bar{r}=\sqrt{\frac{2r_{1}^{2}-2r_{2}^{2}}{B}}\,,

(76)

but as we will show shortly, this does not provide a physically acceptable solution for
\bar{r}\,
.
Next, by plugging (
75
) into(
71
), one obtains

\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)=\frac{B\,r^{2}-2r_{1}^{2}-2r_{2}^{2}-r_{0}\left(B-1\right)r}{2r^{4}\kappa}\,.

(77)

Since the role of the thermal tensor is to balance the EFE, it is natural to consider minimizing its contribution at the throat. To this end, we impose

\tau_{t}\!\left(r_{0}\right)=\frac{r_{0}^{2}-2r_{1}^{2}-2r_{2}^{2}}{2r_{0}^{4}\kappa}=0

(78)

which yields a throat size that depends explicitly on the number of charges:

r_{0}=\sqrt{2\left(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}\right)}=\ell_{\rm P}\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{3}}{45}+\frac{2N^{2}}{137}}\,

(79)

after using the relations (
13
) and (
15
).
With the help of (
79
), the component
\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)
can be rearranged in the following way

\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)=\frac{\left(Br+r_{0}\right)\,\left(r-r_{0}\right)}{2r^{4}\kappa}\,.

(80)

At the other hand, if we apply again the same constraint, namely

\tau_{r}\left(r_{0}\right)=\frac{1}{\kappa r_{0}^{4}}\left(4r_{1}^{2}+r_{0}^{2}\left(B-1\right)\right)=0\,,

(81)

we obtain

r_{0}=\frac{2r_{1}}{\sqrt{1-B}}\,.

(82)

Now with the help of (
82
), we can
rearrange
\tau_{r}\left(r\right)
in the following way

\tau_{r}\left(r\right)=\frac{r_{0}\left(B-1\right)}{\kappa r^{4}}\left(r-r_{0}\right).

(83)

Note that the compatibility of the two values of the throat is possible if

B=\frac{r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}{r_{2}^{2}+r_{1}^{2}}\,.

(84)

As we can see, the constant
B
is always less than one, thereby satisfying the flare-out condition. It is also important to note that the thermal pressures
\tau_{r}\left(r\right)
and

\tau_{t}\left(r\right)
vanish only at the throat. There exists no
external boundary at which
\tau_{r}\left(r\right)
and
\tau_{t}\left(r\right)
can be set to zero. Unfortunately, the same conclusion applies to the redshift function
\Phi\!\left(\bar{r}\right)
. Specifically, inserting (
84
) into (
76
), one obtains

\bar{r}=\sqrt{-2(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2})}

(85)

which has no real solution for
\bar{r}
. Consequently, Eq. (
76
) must be discarded, implying that
\Phi(\bar{r})\neq 0
for any
\bar{r}>r_{0}\,
, except at the throat itself.
Since it is important to find a boundary, we adopt a different strategy.
Instead of searching for a vanishing point, we determine the location where one component of the thermal tensor has at least one stationary point. Examining
\tau_{r}(r)
, it is clear that the derivative
\tau_{r}^{\prime}\left(\bar{r}\right)
vanishes at

\bar{r}=\frac{4}{3}r_{0}\,.

(86)

This means that

\tau_{r}\left(\bar{r}\right)=\frac{27\left(B-1\right)}{256\kappa r_{0}^{2}}\,.

(87)

This value remains very small as long as
r_{2}\gg r_{1}
. On the other hand,
if we apply the same procedure to
\tau_{t}\left(r\right)
, we find

\tau_{t}^{\prime}\left(r\right)=\frac{-2B\,r^{2}+3rr_{0}\left(B-1\right)+4r_{0}^{2}}{2r^{5}\kappa}=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\bar{r}=\frac{\left(3\left(B-1\right)\pm\sqrt{9B^{2}+14B+9}\right)r_{0}}{4B}\,.

(88)

Only the positive solution can be accepted. When
B\in\left(0,1\right)
,

\bar{r}\in\left(4/3r_{0},\sqrt{2}r_{0}\right)
and

\tau_{t}\left(\bar{r}\right)=-\frac{8\left(B+3-\sqrt{9B^{2}+14B+9}\right)\left(3B+1+\sqrt{9B^{2}+14B+9}\right)B^{3}}{r_{0}^{2}\kappa\left(3\left(B-1\right)+\sqrt{9B^{2}+14B+9}\right)^{4}}\,.

(89)

When
B=0
then
\tau_{t}\left(\bar{r}\right)=0\,
. However,
B=0
must be
discarded. On the other hand, when
B\rightarrow 1
then

\tau_{t}\left(\bar{r}\right)=\frac{1}{8r_{0}^{2}\kappa}\,.

(90)

For both choices of
\bar{r}
, the contribution of the thermal tensor is
small. Nevertheless, due to the dependence on
B
in (
87
), it is easy to see that
\tau_{r}\left(\bar{r}\right)\rightarrow 0
when
B\rightarrow 1
.

However, a different situation arises when considering the total transverse pressure. From (
64
), we find

\displaystyle p_{t}\left(r\right)=\frac{-Br^{2}-r_{0}\left(B-1\right)r-r_{0}^{2}}{2\kappa r^{4}}

(91)

and, by implying
p_{t}\left(\bar{r}\right)=0
, we conclude to

\bar{r}=\frac{r_{0}}{2B}\left(\sqrt{5B^{2}-2B+1}+1-B\right).

(92)

If the boundary
\bar{r}
is close to
r_{0}\,
, then
B
has to be
close to unity. This is guaranteed by (
84
). The boundary
\bar{r}

represents the point where
p_{t}\left(r\right)
changes sign. It is important to emphasize that neither the radial pressure

p_{r}\left(r\right)
nor the energy density
\rho\left(r\right)
exhibit this behaviour. Therefore, we can formally regard the expression in (
92
) as defining the boundary
\bar{r}
of the TW.

As a final step, the NEC is violated through

\rho(r)+p_{r}(r)=-\frac{4r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r_{0}^{2}r^{3}}<0

(93)

as well as through the tangential pressure,
\rho(r)+p_{t}(r)<0\,
, as long as
N\leq 6
for the second expression. Nevertheless, this range for the number of electric charges implies
r_{2}<r_{1}\,
, while for
N\geq 7
(
r_{2}>r_{1}
), we get
\rho(r)+p_{t}(r)>0\,
.

Near-throat solution for a massive scalar field with fixed Casimir plates

In this section, we will consider the same source of the previous section but
with the Casimir plates treated as parametrically fixed. The new SET, within the same near-throat approximation, is given by

\displaystyle\rho\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=V(r)-\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa d^{4}}+\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}

(94)

\displaystyle p_{r}\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=-V(r)-\frac{3r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa d^{4}}-\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\tau_{r}\left(r\right)

(95)

\displaystyle p_{t}\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=-V(r)+\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa d^{4}}+\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\tau_{t}\left(r\right)

(96)

and the first EFE reduces to

\frac{B}{r^{2}}=\kappa V(r)-\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{d^{4}}+\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{r^{4}}\,

(97)

after considering the approximated profile (
22
). Solving with respect to the potential, we obtain

V(r)=\frac{B}{\kappa r^{2}}+\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa d^{4}}-\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}\,.

(98)

Using the above expression, we derive from the second EFE the form of the radial pressure

\tau_{r}\left(r\right)=\frac{4r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa d^{4}}+\frac{r_{0}\left(B-1\right)}{\kappa r^{3}}\,.

(99)

The third EFE gives us

\tau_{t}(r)=\left(\frac{b\left(r\right)-b^{\prime}\left(r\right)r}{2\kappa r^{2}}+\frac{r_{0}\left(1-B\right)}{2\kappa r^{2}}\right)\left(\Phi^{\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{r}\right)+\frac{2B}{\kappa r^{2}}-\frac{4r_{2}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}\,.

(100)

It is interesting to note that the third EFE does not develop a dependence on
the plates separation
d
. Following the previous section, we will use the SET
conservation which, in this case, is described by

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{b(r)}{r}\right)\left[\psi^{\prime\prime}(r)\psi^{\prime}(r)+\left(\frac{2}{r}+\Phi\!^{\prime}\left(r\right)\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}\right]+\left(\frac{b(r)-rb^{\prime}(r)}{2r^{2}}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}-V^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r\left(\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)\kappa\,d^{4}-4r_{1}^{2}\right)\Phi^{\prime}\!\left(r\right)+\tau_{r}^{\prime}\!\left(r\right)\kappa rd^{4}+2\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)\kappa d^{4}-2\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)\kappa d^{4}-8r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r\,d^{4}}\,.

(101)

Close to the throat, we can write

\displaystyle\left(\frac{b(r)-rb^{\prime}(r)}{2r^{2}}\right)\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}-V^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r\left(\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)\kappa\,d^{4}-4r_{1}^{2}\right)\Phi^{\prime}\!\left(r\right)+\tau_{r}^{\prime}\!\left(r\right)\kappa rd^{4}+2\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)\kappa d^{4}-2\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)\kappa d^{4}-8r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r\,d^{4}}\,

(102)

and with the help of (
66
), one finds that
(
102
) becomes

\frac{r\left(\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)\kappa\,d^{4}-4r_{1}^{2}\right)\Phi^{\prime}\!\left(r\right)+\tau_{r}^{\prime}\!\left(r\right)\kappa rd^{4}+2\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)\kappa d^{4}-2\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)\kappa d^{4}-8r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r\,d^{4}}\,=0\,.

(103)

Employing equations (
99
) and (
100
), from (
103
) we get

\Phi^{\prime}(r)=\frac{B\,r^{2}-2r_{2}^{2}}{r^{2}r_{0}\left(B-1\right)}

(104)

whose solution is

\Phi\!\left(r\right)=\frac{r^{2}B+2r_{2}^{2}}{rr_{0}\left(B-1\right)}+K\,

(105)

with
K
being an integration constant. Plugging the approximated redshift function (
105
)
into (
100
), we obtain

\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)=\frac{B\,r^{2}-2r_{2}^{2}-r_{0}\left(B-1\right)r}{2r^{4}\kappa}\,.

(106)

Even in the case of fixed plates, we desire to minimize the effect of the thermal
field, at least at the throat. For this purpose, we impose the condition

\tau_{t}\!\left(r_{0}\right)=\frac{r_{0}^{2}-2r_{2}^{2}}{2\kappa r_{0}^{4}}=0

(107)

which is satisfied if

r_{0}=\sqrt{2}r_{2}=\ell_{\rm P}N\sqrt{\frac{2}{137}}\,.

(108)

The throat size is again proportional to the charge number
N
. With the help of (
108
), we can rearrange
\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)
in the following manner

\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)=\frac{\left(\,r-r_{0}\right)\left(Br+r_{0}\right)}{2r^{4}\kappa}\,.

(109)

The component
\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)
vanishes not only at the throat but also at

r=-\frac{r_{0}}{B}\,.

(110)

Unfortunately, this can occur only if
B
is negative. By applying the same procedure on

\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)
, one gets

B\,r_{0}^{2}-2r_{2}^{2}=\frac{d^{2}\sqrt{1-B}}{2r_{1}}\,.

(111)

Using (
111

\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)

\tau_{r}\left(r\right)=\frac{\left(1-B\right)\left(r^{3}-r_{0}^{3}\right)}{\kappa r^{3}r_{0}^{2}}

(112)

which shows that it vanishes only at the throat. To proceed further, we need to verify whether the value of
r_{0}
given in (

B=1-\frac{8r_{2}^{2}r_{1}^{2}}{d^{4}}\,

(113)

satisfying always the flare-out condition. If we also impose

, we get

d>\sqrt{2\sqrt{2}r_{2}r_{1}}\,.

(114)

Since
d
is fixed by the physical setup, this imposes a constraint on
r_{2}
and, consequently, on the maximum number of charges the TW can accumulate:

N<12.5\left(\frac{d}{\ell_{\rm P}}\right)^{2}.

(115)

Since
B
cannot be negative,
we conclude that neither
\tau_{t}\!\left(r\right)
nor
\tau_{r}\!\left(r\right)
admit an external boundary where they vanish. Nevertheless, observing (
104

\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{r})=0\,
. This is located at

\bar{r}=r_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{B}}=\frac{r_{0}}{\sqrt{B}}\,.

(116)

Since

, it follows that
\bar{r}>r_{0}\,
. Therefore, the TW can be consistently defined within the interval

\left[r_{0},\bar{r}\right]\,
.
On the other hand, it is possible to adopt

and
\tau_{t}\left(r\right)

\tau_{r}\left(r\right)
has no stationary points, while
\tau_{t}\left(r\right)

\bar{r}=\frac{\left(3\left(B-1\right)+\sqrt{9B^{2}+14B+9}\right)r_{0}}{4B}

(117)

with

\tau_{t}\left(\bar{r}\right)=\frac{1}{8r_{0}^{2}\kappa}

(118)

since only the values of

close to

can be accepted.

Finally, we check the violation of the NEC. For the radial pressure,

\rho(r)+p_{r}(r)=-\frac{2r_{0}r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{3}(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2})}<0\,,

(119)

the NEC is always violated. In the case of the tangential pressure, we find that
\rho(r)+p_{t}(r)<0
only if
N\leq 3\,
, which sets an upper bound on the throat size at
N=3
(corresponding to
r_{0}\approx 0.36\ell_{\rm P}
). However, it is not necessary to impose such a restrictive sub-Planckian bound on the throat, since the NEC is already violated via the radial pressure, allowing us to set
N\geq 4\,
.

VII
Conclusions

In this paper we have explored how various physical sources can contribute to the sustainability of a traversable wormhole. Specifically, we have examined the combined effects of a scalar field (with and without a potential), a static electric field, and a Casimir source. This combination of sources generates a stress-energy tensor that significantly complicates the search for solutions to the Einstein field equations. To address this, we have restricted our analysis to a region near the throat. This approximation allows for a simplified form of the shape function, given by (
22
). Despite this simplification, it should be noted that any TW can be represented by (
22
) close to the throat. The only
difference between the various profiles is encoded in the coefficient
B
.
Once the form of the shape function was fixed, we divided our investigation into four parts; two without a potential for the scalar field and two with a potential. In both scenarios, we further distinguished the analysis by considering the Casimir source under two configurations; one where the plates vary radially and another where the plates are held parametrically fixed. For all cases, the introduction of an additional unknown tensor
-
the
thermal tensor
-
has been necessary to ensure consistency in the Einstein field equations. This tensor does not contribute to the energy density. It affects only the spatial pressure components and plays a fundamental role in the discussion of the null energy condition. Indeed, for the massless case, one finds that

\rho(r)+p_{r}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{c}\frac{Ce^{-2\Phi(r)}}{r^{4}}-\frac{4r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\tau_{r}\left(r\right)\\
\\
\frac{Ce^{-2\Phi(r)}}{r^{4}}-\frac{4r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa d^{4}}+\tau_{r}\left(r\right)\end{array}\right.,

(120)

while for the massive case, one finds

\rho(r)+p_{r}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{c}-\frac{4r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa r^{4}}+\tau_{r}\left(r\right)\\
\\
-\frac{4r_{1}^{2}}{\kappa d^{4}}+\tau_{r}\left(r\right)\end{array}\right..

(121)

It is interesting to note that in all four cases

\rho\left(r\right)+p_{r}\left(r\right)=-\frac{r_{0}\left(1-B\right)}{\kappa r^{3}}<0

(122)

due to the flare-out condition. Therefore, the NEC is always violated. To minimize the effects of the thermal tensor, we have imposed its vanishing at the throat. This condition has allowed us to determine the throat size in terms of the lengths
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
. In particular, we have observed that, in each case, the throat is
proportional to
r_{2}
or to a combination of
r_{1}
and

r_{2}
. This behaviour is significant because it allows the size of the throat to be adjusted. Although no external boundary is defined by a vanishing thermal tensor, an external boundary can be identified by examining the profile of the redshift function, at least in the massless case. In particular, when the plates vary, the value of
B_{-}

Since there is no natural boundary arising from the vanishing of the thermal stress tensor, we have investigated the existence of stationary points. Even though these points correspond to maximum values of the thermal stress tensor components, the actual values of its components can be very small.
A comment on this point is in order: the significance of identifying an external boundary lies in assessing the validity of the approximation adopted in this work. Indeed, since we have assumed the Einstein field equations to be valid close to the throat, it is important to understand the range over which this approximation remains valid. Given that our analysis is limited to a narrow region near the throat, we intend to revisit the same field equations in a future paper without relying on this approximation.

As a final remark, it is worth noting that the resulting wormhole solutions, in both the massless and massive cases, exhibit a throat size that increases with the strength of the electric field, or equivalently, with the number of elementary charges. It would be interesting though to investigate the full picture by including rotation effects. We leave this topic open for future research.
