Regenerative potential of Dental Pulp Stem Cells (DPSCs) in dental and periodontal tissue engineering: a systematic review of preclinical and clinical studies
Authors: Shima Asghari, Shaaf Rezaee, Negar Khaneshi, Mitra Rostami, Mandana Batebi, Fatemeh Heidari, Mina Moradi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) are multipotent mesenchymal stem cells with demonstrated potential for regenerating dental and periodontal tissues. This systematic review aimed to critically evaluate the regenerative potential, biological properties, and translational prospects of DPSCs in dental and periodontal tissue engineering, based on in vitro, in vivo, and clinical interventional studies published between 2010 and 2025. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Scopus using combinations of keywords related to DPSCs, dental/periodontal regeneration, and tissue engineering. Quality assessment was performed using the Downs and Black checklist, and the risk of bias was also evaluated with RoB 2 and ROBINS-I tools. A total of 12 interventional studies (9 experimental and 3 clinical trials) were included. DPSCs consistently indicated high proliferation, multilineage differentiation, immunomodulatory capacity, and resistance to inflammatory and senescence-inducing conditions compared to other mesenchymal stem cell sources. Preclinical studies demonstrated the enhanced regeneration of dental pulp, dentin, periodontal ligament, cementum, and alveolar bone using DPSCs, with or without scaffolds and bioactive cues. Clinical trials reported the safety of autologous DPSCs and improvements in pulp vitality, probing depth, clinical attachment level, and bone defect filling; however, sample sizes were limited, and evidence for superiority over standard therapies remains preliminary. DPSCs possess significant regenerative potential for dental and periodontal tissue engineering, supported by robust preclinical evidence and early clinical studies. Nevertheless, clinical translation is constrained by limited trial data, methodological heterogeneity, and regulatory challenges. Future research should focus on standardized protocols, long-term clinical outcomes, and integration with advanced tissue engineering strategies such as biomimetic scaffolds, organoids, and gene-editing approaches to fully realize the therapeutic potential of DPSCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08420-5.

Introduction

The regeneration of dental and periodontal tissues has become a central focus in contemporary restorative dentistry, driven by the increasing demand to preserve natural teeth and restore oral function [1, 2]. Conventional restorative approaches, such as fillings, veneers, and implants, primarily address structural defects but often fail to restore the full biological and functional integrity of the tissues [3, 4]. Advances in stem cell biology and tissue engineering have enabled biologically driven strategies that aim to achieve true tissue regeneration, rather than mere repair [2, 5, 6].

Dental and periodontal tissues such as enamel, dentin, pulp, cementum, and alveolar bone display varying regenerative capacities. Among these, dental pulp and periodontal tissues (periodontal ligament, cementum, and alveolar bone) are dynamic, the living tissues capable of responding to injury [7]. The pulp maintains tooth vitality through its rich vascular and neural network and contains multipotent stem cells critical for repair and regeneration [8]. Similarly, periodontal tissues provide structural support to teeth, and their loss due to caries, trauma, periodontitis, or aging can compromise oral health [9, 10].

True regeneration of damaged dental and periodontal tissues requires restoration of both structure and function [11, 12]. Traditional techniques, including guided tissue regeneration, bone grafting, and scaffold-based approaches, often fall short in achieving this goal [13]. Consequently, biological strategies leveraging stem cells, growth factors, scaffolds, and signaling molecules have been increasingly evaluated to promote the formation of functional, integrated tissue [14, 15].

Dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), a subset of mesenchymal stem cells derived from dental pulp, have emerged as a promising candidate for tissue engineering applications [16, 17]. First identified in the early 2000 s, DPSCs demonstrate high proliferative capacity, clonogenicity, and multipotency, enabling differentiation into odontoblasts, osteoblasts, adipocytes, and neural-like cells [18, 19]. Their accessibility from extracted teeth, minimal ethical concerns, and regenerative potential make them highly attractive for both preclinical and clinical applications [20, 21].

Preclinical studies have consistently demonstrated that DPSCs can differentiate into the odontoblast-like cells capable of dentin formation and secrete bioactive factors that promote angiogenesis, immunomodulation, and tissue repair [6, 22]. For example, studies have shown that DPSCs contribute to dentin-pulp complex formation, periodontal ligament regeneration, and alveolar bone repair in vitro and in vivo [23]. Early clinical trials have reported encouraging outcomes regarding safety and tissue integration, although standardized protocols for DPSCs application remain under development [24].

Recent evidence has been demonstrated the capacity of DPSCs to respond dynamically to bioactive cues and inflammatory microenvironments. Osorio et al. demonstrated that DPSCs exhibit adaptive behavior under inflammatory conditions, modulating proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation in response to cytokine exposure [25]. Similarly, López-García et al. reported that contemporary calcium silicate–based materials can stimulate anti-inflammatory responses and enhance the biological activity of DPSCs, indicating the importance of biomaterial–cell interactions in regenerative strategies [26]. These findings revealed that the regenerative efficacy of DPSCs is influenced not only by their intrinsic properties but also by extrinsic cues from scaffolds, growth factors, and the local microenvironment [25, 26].

Several studies have further documented the robustness of DPSCs across physiological and pathological conditions. Fawzy El-Sayed et al. and Fageeh revealed that DPSCs retain proliferative, osteogenic, and wound-healing capabilities even when derived from teeth affected by periodontitis, highlighting their therapeutic resilience [27, 28]. Gan et al. and Yuan et al. also demonstrated that DPSCs enhance angiogenesis and facilitate periodontal defect repair, confirming their translational potential [29, 30]. Additionally, both scaffold-free 3D DPSCs constructs and DPSCs-based cell sheets have been shown to promote pulp-like tissue formation and periodontal regeneration in preclinical models [31].

Despite the promising results, several challenges remain. Optimal scaffold design, signaling pathways, immune responses, and clinical standardization have yet to be fully elucidated. Furthermore, the integration of DPSCs with contemporary biomaterials and growth factors requires systematic evaluation to optimize regenerative outcomes. Addressing these gaps is essential to advance the safe and effective clinical translation of DPSCs-based therapies.

Considering the expanding evidence and potential of DPSCs in dental and periodontal tissue engineering, a systematic synthesis of current interventional studies is warranted. This review aimed to critically evaluate the regenerative potential of DPSCs, investigate their responses to various biological and biomaterial cues, and highlight translational challenges and opportunities for clinical application.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Appendix 1). This systematic review protocol was not registered in the PROSPERO database or any other registry. The review aimed to evaluate the regenerative potential of DPSCs in the repair and regeneration of dental and periodontal tissues. The primary research question was: “What is the current evidence regarding the efficacy, safety, and regenerative capacity of DPSCs in dental and periodontal tissue engineering?” This review included preclinical and clinical studies to provide a comprehensive assessment of biological properties and translational potential of DPSCs.

Literature search strategy

A systematic search was conducted across four major electronic databases: Web of Science, PubMed (MEDLINE), Embase, and Scopus. The search covered publications from January 2010 to June 2025. Search terms included “Dental Pulp Stem Cells,” “DPSCs,” “Dental Tissue Regeneration,” “Periodontal Tissue Regeneration,” “Stem Cells,” “Mesenchymal Stem Cells,” “Tissue Engineering,” and “Tissue Repair.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to combine keywords, ensuring a comprehensive retrieval of relevant literature. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: original experimental research, randomized or non-randomized clinical trials, in vitro or in vivo studies using DPSCs for dental or periodontal tissue regeneration, reporting relevant histological, molecular, radiographic, or clinical outcomes, and published in English in peer-reviewed journals between January 2010 and June 2025. Exclusion criteria included reviews, commentaries, editorials, conference abstracts without full data, studies using stem cells other than DPSCs (unless directly compared), and non-dental tissue regeneration studies.

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts for eligibility, followed by the full-text assessment of potentially relevant studies. Data extracted included study design, sample size, DPSCs source, scaffold/material used, intervention details, outcome measures, follow-up duration, and main findings. Excluded studies and reasons for exclusion were then documented and reported in the main text. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Quality assessment

The quality assessment of the included interventional studies was conducted using the Downs and Black checklist (Appendix 2). This tool was selected because it allowed the standardized appraisal of both randomized and non-randomized study designs within a single framework. The randomized clinical trials were evaluated using the full Downs and Black checklist, which consisted of 27 items with a maximum possible score of 28. This version assessed reporting quality, external validity, internal validity related to bias and confounding, and statistical power.

The experimental and non-randomized studies were also assessed using a modified version of the Downs and Black checklist, which included only the items relevant to non-randomized designs. For these studies, the maximum achievable score was 20. The same core domains such as reporting, external validity, internal validity (bias and confounding), and power were systematically evaluated.

For each included study, individual item scores were summed to generate a total quality score, and studies were subsequently categorized into high, moderate, or low quality based on their overall scores. This approach followed established practice in systematic reviews and ensured consistency in quality assessment across diverse study designs. The quality assessment process was applied uniformly to all included studies, and the results were summarized in a tabulated format to facilitate comparison and transparency [32].

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment of the included interventional studies was assessed using the standardized risk of bias assessment tools. Randomized controlled trials were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool version 2 (RoB 2), while non-randomized interventional studies were assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tool (ROBINS-I) [33].

For randomized controlled trials, bias was assessed across the domains of the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported results. Each domain was judged as low risk, some concerns, or the high risk of bias, and an overall risk of bias judgment was assigned for each study based on these domain-level assessments.

For non-randomized studies, bias was evaluated across seven domains, including bias due to confounding, the selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, the measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Each study was rated as having low, moderate, serious, or the critical risk of bias according to ROBINS-I guidance, and an overall risk of bias judgment was determined by the highest level of bias identified in any domain.

The assessments were conducted based on information reported in the published articles. The overall methodological quality of the evidence was summarized by study design, and the common sources of bias were identified to support the interpretation of the findings of this systematic review.

Data synthesis and interpretation

Due to heterogeneity in study designs, models, interventions, and outcomes, a qualitative synthesis was conducted. A meta-analysis was not feasible due to heterogeneity in study designs, outcomes, and reporting methods. Key findings were summarized narratively, with stratification by study type (in vitro, animal, and clinical) to maintain clarity and consistency. Moreover, comparative tables were used to demonstrate differences in regenerative outcomes, scaffold use, and DPSCs behavior.

Results

Description of the included studies

Following a comprehensive search of the selected databases a total of 512 potentially relevant articles were identified. After the removal of duplicates and application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 12 studies were included in this systematic review (Fig. 1). The included studies comprised 10 experimental studies and 2 randomized clinical trials (RCTs). The remaining studies were experimental or non-randomized in design and were assessed using the modified Downs and Black checklist. Most of these studies achieved moderate to high quality scores, ranging from 13/20 to 20/20. High scores were consistently observed in the reporting domain, indicating that study objectives, interventions, outcomes, and main findings were clearly described. The internal validity related to bias was generally rated as acceptable, reflecting appropriate outcome measurements and controlled experimental conditions.

The external validity scores were also relatively low in most experimental studies, as many investigations were conducted in vitro or in animal models, which limited the generalizability of findings to clinical settings. Moreover, the power calculations were not reported in the majority of non-randomized studies, which contributed to lower overall scores. Additionally, reduced scores were commonly associated with the lack of blinding, absence of sample size justification, and limited applicability to broader patient populations.

In summary, the quality assessment indicated that the included studies provided generally reliable evidence, with stronger methodological rigor observed in RCTs and acceptable but more limited validity reported in experimental studies.

Risk of bias assessment of the non-randomized studies using the ROBINS-I

For non-randomized studies, the overall risk of bias ranged from moderate to serious. Most studies were judged to have a moderate risk of bias, primarily due to confounding factors such as donor variability, age differences, disease status, species differences, or the absence of appropriate control groups. Bias in the selection of participants was generally low, except in Li et al. and Nakashima et al., where serious risk was identified due to highly selected cases and uncontrolled human pilot designs. The classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, and missing data were consistently judged as low risk across studies.

In addition, bias in outcome measurement was frequently rated as moderate, mainly because the blinding of outcome assessors was not reported and several outcomes relied on subjective histological or imaging assessments. The selection of reported results raised some concerns in most studies due to the lack of pre-specified analysis plans. Two studies, Li et al. and Nakashima et al. (2017) were reported to have an overall serious risk of bias, reflecting small sample sizes, the lack of control groups, and uncontrolled clinical designs.

Overall, the randomized evidence was limited but demonstrated moderate methodological quality, with recurring issues related to allocation concealment and blinding. The non-randomized evidence was predominantly affected by the moderate risk of bias, driven by small sample sizes, the lack of controls, the absence of blinding, and confounding factors. The highest risk of bias was observed in early-phase human pilot studies without control groups.

Biological properties and in vitro performance of DPSCs

Across the experimental studies, DPSCs consistently demonstrated robust proliferative capacity, clonogenicity, and multilineage differentiation potential. The comparative analyses also showed that DPSCs often outperformed non-dental MSCs, such as bone marrow–derived MSCs (BMMSCs), in terms of proliferation rate, resistance to senescence, and the maintenance of osteogenic potential during serial passaging. DPSCs also demonstrated resistance to inflammatory stimuli, including lipopolysaccharide-induced apoptosis, with favorable gene expression profiles related to cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and metabolic activity.

Several studies reported the influence of bioactive cues and adjunctive agents on DPSCs behavior. Platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) significantly enhanced DPSCs proliferation and promoted odonto-/osteogenic differentiation in a dose- and time-dependent manner, supporting its role as both a scaffold and a source of endogenous growth factors. Similarly, natural extracts and small bioactive molecules modulated DPSCs responses, with dose-dependent increases in proliferation and odontogenic/osteogenic gene expression observed in some models. However, not all bioactive agents exerted uniformly positive effects; for example, allantoin enhanced proliferation and wound-healing responses but inhibited osteogenic differentiation at higher concentrations, indicating the sensitivity of DPSCs to microenvironmental conditions.

Importantly, donor-related factors were addressed in a limited number of studies. DPSCs derived from teeth with mild periodontitis retained comparable morphology, surface marker expression, and differentiation potential to those from healthy donors, whereas age-related changes such as reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis were consistently observed the regardless of periodontal status. These findings revealed that donor age may be a more critical determinant of DPSCs function than mild inflammatory status.

In vivo regenerative outcomes in preclinical models

In vivo animal studies provided substantial evidence supporting the regenerative capacity of DPSCs in both dental pulp and periodontal environments. Scaffold-free and scaffold-based approaches were successfully employed. Three-dimensional DPSCs constructs demonstrated long-term cell viability, self-organization, and the ability to generate vascularized pulp-like tissue without exogenous growth factors following implantation in immunodeficient mice. These findings demonstrated the intrinsic capacity of DPSCs to orchestrate complex tissue regeneration.

Periodontal regeneration models further showed that DPSCs contributed to improved bone formation, periodontal tissue regeneration, and vascularization. In a miniature porcine periodontitis model, DPSCs resulted in superior periodontal regeneration compared with other MSCs sources, reinforcing their tissue-specific advantages. Nevertheless, comparative studies revealed conflicting evidence when DPSCs were evaluated against alternative stem cell sources. In a rat mandibular defect model, ADSCs induced faster and more extensive bone regeneration than DPSCs, despite DPSCs exhibiting superior angiogenic and migratory properties in vitro. These discrepancies showed that regenerative outcomes are highly context-dependent and influenced by defect type, scaffold selection, and targeted tissue.

Clinical evidence and translational findings

Two early-phase clinical studies and one randomized controlled trial directly evaluated DPSCs-based therapies. Autologous mobilized DPSCs transplanted into pulpectomized teeth demonstrated favorable safety profiles, the restoration of pulp vitality, the radiographic evidence of regenerated pulp-like tissue, and dentin formation in a small cohort of patients with irreversible pulpitis. No adverse events or toxicity were reported, supporting the feasibility of DPSCs-based pulp regeneration under GMP-compliant conditions.

In periodontal applications, a RCT using autologous DPSCs-rich micrografts showed statistically significant improvements in probing depth reduction, clinical attachment level gain, and radiographic bone fill compared with collagen scaffold alone. These results indicated a potential clinical benefit of DPSCs in periodontal regeneration. However, the small sample sizes, variability in defect morphology, and short- to mid-term follow-up periods limit the strength of these conclusions.

Synthesis and critical appraisal of findings

Overall, the included studies demonstrated that DPSCs possess favorable biological characteristics, including high proliferative capacity, multilineage differentiation, angiogenic potential, and relative resistance to inflammatory stress. These properties translated into encouraging regenerative outcomes in preclinical models and early clinical investigations. However, the findings also revealed substantial heterogeneity across studies in terms of cell sources, isolation and expansion protocols, scaffold composition, outcome measures, and in vivo models.

Conflicting results particularly in comparisons between DPSCs and other MSCs populations indicated the influence of experimental context and revealed the need for standardized methodologies. Moreover, the predominance of in vitro and animal studies, coupled with the limited number of well-powered randomized clinical trials, precludes definitive conclusions regarding the clinical superiority or routine clinical implementation of DPSCs-based therapies at this stage.

Discussion

The present systematic review synthesized current preclinical and clinical evidence regarding the regenerative potential of DPSCs in dental and periodontal tissue engineering, indicating their proliferative, differentiation, and immunomodulatory properties, as well as their translational promise.

The regeneration of dental and periodontal tissues is a major challenge in restorative and restorative dentistry [46]. Conventional treatments often involve the use of grafts, synthetic materials, or root canal procedures that aim to repair or replace damaged tissues, but may not fully restore the original function or structure [47]. Recent advances in regenerative medicine have revealed the potential of stem cell-based therapies to promote the regeneration of complex tissues, including dentin, pulp, cementum, and periodontal ligament [48].

Among the various sources of stem cells, DPSCs have emerged as a promising candidate due to their accessible origin, high proliferation capacity, and multilineage differentiation potential [49]. Isolated from the pulp tissue of extracted teeth, DPSCs demonstrate the ability to differentiate into odontoblast-like cells, dentin formation, as well as other cell types involved in dental tissue regeneration [20, 50]. Their immunomodulatory properties and ease of harvesting make DPSCs a valuable resource for the development of regenerative strategies aimed at restoring the structural integrity and biological function of damaged dental and periodontal tissues [14]. The use of DPSCs may revolutionize treatment paradigms for conditions such as pulp necrosis, caries, periodontal defects, and alveolar bone loss, ultimately improving patient outcomes and the quality of life [31].

DPSCs, as shown across multiple experimental studies, exhibit robust self-renewal, multilineage differentiation, and superior resistance to inflammatory stress compared to other MSCs sources. Ma et al. demonstrated that DPSCs maintained higher proliferation rates, lower senescence, and enhanced osteogenic potential relative to BMMSCs and ADSCs, particularly under lipopolysaccharide-induced stress, both in vitro and in a porcine model of periodontitis. These findings revealed the intrinsic resilience DPSCs to inflammatory microenvironments, a critical factor for successful tissue regeneration in diseased or damaged dental tissues [34]. Complementary work by Jin et al. comparing DPSCs with ADSCs revealed the relative strengths and limitations of different MSC sources, indicating that while DPSCs exhibit superior colony formation, migration, and angiogenic gene expression, ADSCs may outperform in terms of early osteogenic differentiation and bone volume regeneration in mandibular defects [41]. These comparative studies emphasized the importance of selecting the most appropriate stem cell source depending on the targeted regenerative application, whether it is pulp tissue reconstruction or alveolar bone regeneration [36].

The incorporation of bioactive scaffolds and cell-supportive matrices has emerged as a consistent strategy to enhance DPSCs-mediated regeneration. Chen et al. reported that PRF not only supported DPSCs proliferation but also promoted odonto-/osteogenic differentiation, leading to the formation of pulp-like tissue with regenerated dentin and vascular networks in canine and murine models [35]. Similarly, Itoh et al. demonstrated that scaffold-free 3D DPSCs constructs could self-organize into viable pulp-like tissues rich in vasculature and odontoblast-like cells without exogenous scaffolds or growth factors, highlighting DPSCs’ intrinsic capacity for tissue self-organization and functional integration [37]. These studies collectively demonstrated that the interaction between DPSCs and biomaterials or microenvironmental cues is a key determinant of regenerative outcomes, corroborating recent findings by López-García et al. regarding the modulation of DPSCs behavior by calcium silicate–based materials, and by Osorio et al. on cellular responses under inflammatory conditions [25, 26]. Integrating these perspectives provides a mechanistic understanding of inter-study variability and emphasizes the need for tailored biomaterials that synergize with DPSCs biology for predictable clinical outcomes [46].

The clinical translation of DPSCs-based therapies remains in its early stages, with limited but encouraging evidence from human trials. Nakashima et al. reported the safe application of autologous mobilized DPSCs (MDPSCs) for pulp regeneration in patients with irreversible pulpitis, demonstrating the restoration of pulp vitality and functional dentin formation without adverse events [39]. Ferrarotti et al. [40] further validated the clinical utility of DPSCs-rich micrografts in periodontal regeneration, showing significant improvements in probing depth reduction, clinical attachment levels, and bone defect filling compared to control sites. Nonetheless, with only a small number of trials and relatively short follow-up periods, it is premature to assert that DPSCs will “revolutionize” dental therapy [40]. The current evidence indicated potential benefits, but broader clinical trials with standardized protocols, long-term assessments, and cost-effectiveness analyses are required to substantiate efficacy and inform evidence-based clinical adoption [51].

Several factors contribute to the variability observed across preclinical and clinical studies. Donor heterogeneity, including age and periodontal status, influences DPSCs proliferation and differentiation, as demonstrated by Hu et al., who reported that DPSCs from patients with mild periodontitis retained comparable biological properties to healthy controls, although age-related declines in proliferation and osteogenic potential were reported [44]. Differences in isolation and culture protocols, scaffold composition, pre-differentiation status, and in vivo models further complicate direct comparisons between studies [52]. For instance, Cha et al. and Sanchez et al. reported that pre-differentiation of DPSCs did not increase total tissue formation but influenced the expression of odontoblastic markers, whereas PDLSCs showed enhanced cementum/PDL-like tissue formation under similar conditions, emphasizing the need for standardized preconditioning protocols to optimize outcomes [38, 42].

Emerging strategies such as natural bioactive compounds and scaffold innovations also show promise in modulating DPSCs behavior. Naik et al. revealed that the ethanol extracts of Andrographis paniculata enhanced osteogenic and odontogenic differentiation in a dose-dependent manner, while Özçınar et al. demonstrated that allantoin promoted proliferation and wound healing in DPSCs but inhibited osteogenic differentiation at higher concentrations, illustrating the nuanced effects of bioactive agents on DPSCs function [43, 45]. The synthesized findings indicated the potential of combining DPSCs with bioactive molecules or biomimetic scaffolds to fine-tune regenerative responses, particularly in challenging clinical microenvironments characterized by inflammation or limited vascularity [45, 50, 53].

Advances in organoid technologies, 3D bioprinting, gene-editing approaches, and biomimetic scaffold design may further enhance the translational potential of DPSCs [54]. These technologies could enable precise control over tissue architecture, vascularization, and differentiation cues, ultimately bridging the gap between preclinical success and predictable clinical outcomes [55]. However, successful implementation will require the careful consideration of regulatory frameworks, immunogenicity, cost, and the standardization of manufacturing processes to ensure patient safety and reproducibility [56, 57].

In summary, the preclinical and clinical evidence collectively revealed the considerable regenerative potential of DPSCs in both dental and periodontal tissue engineering. While DPSCs demonstrated favorable biological properties including high proliferation, multilineage differentiation, immunomodulatory capacity, and resistance to inflammation the translation of these findings into clinical practice remains limited by small patient cohorts, methodological heterogeneity, and insufficient long-term data. Future studies should prioritize the rigorous standardization of isolation, expansion, and scaffold protocols, comparative analyses with alternative MSCs sources, and the integration of emerging bioengineering technologies to optimize the therapeutic utility of DPSCs. Incorporating insights from recent literature on inflammatory responses and biomaterial interactions will further enhance the mechanistic understanding and guide the rational design of DPSCs-based regenerative therapies.

Future perspective

Future research on DPSCs should move beyond proof-of-concept studies and focus on refining biological understanding, improving methodological rigor, and addressing translational barriers that currently limit clinical adoption. The evidence synthesized in this review suggests that DPSCs possess intrinsic regenerative and immunomodulatory properties, yet their function is strongly influenced by microenvironmental conditions, donor-related factors, and experimental design. Therefore, a deeper mechanistic understanding of how DPSCs respond to inflammatory stimuli, aging, and disease-related sites is essential. Recent studies investigating DPSCs behavior under inflammatory conditions and their interactions with bioactive dental materials highlight the importance of adapting regenerative strategies to clinically relevant microenvironments, particularly in the cases of pulpitis and periodontitis where inflammation is unavoidable.

Standardization represents a critical priority for future research. Variability in cell isolation methods, expansion protocols, passage numbers, and scaffold composition hampers the comparison of studies and weakens the overall evidence base. The establishment of consensus guidelines for DPSCs harvesting, characterization, and quality control in accordance with Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) requirements will be essential to ensure reproducibility and facilitate regulatory approval. Furthermore, future systematic reviews and primary studies should adopt valid and design-specific tools to assess the risk of bias and clearly distinguish between in vitro, animal, and clinical evidence to enhance methodological transparency.

Technological innovations offer promising avenues for scaling up DPSCs-based restorative therapies. 3D culture systems, organoids, and scaffold-free constructs may better mimic natural pulpal and periodontal architectures and improve vascularization and functional integration, as demonstrated by preclinical studies included in this review. Advances in 3D bioprinting and biomimetic scaffolds can enable precise spatial organization of DPSCs with bioactive cues, promoting controlled differentiation and tissue-specific regeneration. In addition, smart and immune-modulating biomaterials such as calcium silicate-based systems may be used to modulate inflammation and enhance DPSCs survival and function in hostile microenvironments.

At the cellular and molecular level, emerging approaches such as gene editing technologies, epigenetic modulation, and preconditioning strategies may further optimize DPSCs regeneration function. These techniques can be used to enhance angiogenic, odontogenic, or osteogenic pathways or to enhance resistance to inflammatory stress and aging. However, such strategies need to be carefully evaluated for safety, ethical considerations, and long-term stability before clinical application.

From a clinical perspective, future efforts should prioritize well-designed and adequately powered randomized controlled trials with standardized outcome measures and longer follow-up periods. Comparative studies evaluating DPSCs against the other sources of dental and non-dental MSCs under the same conditions are needed, particularly to define the specific benefits of each. Besides, practical considerations including cost-effectiveness, scalability, safety variability, and regulatory pathways should be incorporated early in applied research to ensure that DPSCs-based therapies are not only biologically effective but also clinically and economically viable.

Limitations

Despite the promising regenerative potential of DPSCs demonstrated in the included studies, several limitations should be considered. The systematic review encompassed a diverse range of experimental models, including in vitro cell culture, animal models, and limited clinical pilot studies. Variability in study designs, stem cell sources, scaffolds, and outcome measures limits the ability to directly compare results. While initial clinical pilot studies indicate the safety and potential efficacy of autologous DPSCs, large-scale RCTs are scarce. This restricted definitive conclusions regarding long-term clinical outcomes, optimal protocols, and standardization necessary for routine clinical application.

Conclusion

This systematic review synthesized current interventional evidence on the regenerative potential of DPSCs in dental pulp, periodontal, and bone tissue engineering. Overall, the included in vitro studies, animal models, and limited clinical trials consistently demonstrated that DPSCs possess favorable biological characteristics, including high proliferative capacity, multilineage differentiation potential, angiogenic activity, and relative resistance to inflammatory and senescence-inducing stimuli. These properties support their suitability as a cell source for regenerative applications in dentistry.

Moreover, preclinical evidence indicated that DPSCs can contribute to pulp-like tissue formation, dentin deposition, periodontal regeneration, and bone repair, particularly when combined with supportive scaffolds, bioactive materials, or scaffold-free three-dimensional constructs. Several studies showed the importance of the microenvironment, showing that biomaterials, growth factor–rich matrices, and bioactive compounds can significantly modulate DPSCs behavior. Moreover, comparative studies revealed that DPSCs are not universally superior to other mesenchymal stem cell sources; for example, adipose-derived or periodontal ligament stem cells may demonstrate equal or greater regenerative outcomes in certain bone or periodontal models. These findings indicated that regenerative performance is highly context-dependent.

The review also demonstrated that clinical evidence is still preliminary. Two early-phase clinical investigations and one RCT provided that autologous DPSC-based therapies are feasible and appear safe, with the encouraging signals of efficacy in vital pulp therapy and periodontal regeneration. However, the small number of clinical studies, limited sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and heterogeneity in protocols preclude definitive conclusions regarding clinical effectiveness or routine clinical adoption. Importantly, the current level of evidence does not justify claims that DPSCs will transform clinical practice or should be prioritized over other established regenerative approaches.

In summary, DPSCs represent a promising and biologically versatile cell source for dental and periodontal tissue engineering, with solid preclinical support and emerging clinical evidence. Future progress toward clinical translation will depend on well-designed, adequately powered RCTs, standardized cell-processing and delivery protocols, robust risk-of-bias assessment, and clearer regulatory and cost-effectiveness considerations. Integrating advances in biomaterials, biomimetic scaffolds, and microenvironmental modulation particularly under inflammatory conditions may further enhance the regenerative potential of DPSCs. Until such evidence is available, DPSCs should be regarded as an experimental yet promising option rather than an established standard in regenerative dental therapy.
