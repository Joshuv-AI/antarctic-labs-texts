End-of-Life Education in Action: Evaluating Simulation-Based Palliative Care Training for Emergency Medicine Residents
Authors: Caroline H. Lee, Thaouyen Emily Pham, Jeanne Noble
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis simulation-based curriculum aimed to improve emergency medicine residents’ confidence and knowledge in applying palliative care principles in end-of-life scenarios in the emergency department.MethodsResidents participated in 4 simulation cases: (1) an elderly patient on hospice presenting with altered mental status, whose surrogate requests full intervention despite physician orders for life-sustaining treatment (POLST) indicating do not resuscitate/do not intubate and comfort-focused goals; (2) a patient with advanced dementia and hypotension, with a 2-year-old POLST indicating “Full Code,” requiring reassessment of goals of care; (3) a 3-year-old in cardiac arrest after drowning with no return of spontaneous circulation; and (4) a previously healthy 12-month-old found apneic and cyanotic with persistent arrest despite prolonged resuscitation. All cases and debriefs were performed in succession and completed in 1 hour. Critical actions included leading goals-of-care discussions, interpreting advance directives, transitioning to comfort measures, and delivering death notifications. Learning objectives focused on communication skills, symptom management, and ethical and legal considerations. Learners completed pre- and postsession surveys evaluating prior experience, self-reported confidence, and knowledge of palliative principles.ResultsThirty-one residents completed both surveys. Ninety percent reported feeling more prepared to lead end-of-life discussions. Knowledge scores increased from 72.9% to 79.4%. Qualitative feedback emphasized practicing difficult conversations, clear communication at the time of death, and a desire for further training in comfort-focused care and legal frameworks.DiscussionSimulation-based end-of-life education represents an effective method for preparing emergency medicine residents for complex palliative decision-making. Findings support curricular integration.

Introduction

Emergency medicine (EM) physicians routinely manage patients at the end of life (EOL), whether because of acute traumatic events or due to chronic illness progression. These encounters require providers to rapidly address not only the patients’ physical symptoms but also complex emotional, ethical, and communication challenges. As such, the ability to deliver primary palliative care—including managing symptoms, conducting goals-of-care discussions, and providing emotional support to patients and families—is an essential competency for EM physicians. National organizations, including the American College of Emergency Physicians, have emphasized the importance of high-quality EOL care in the emergency department (ED), and initiatives such as EPEC-EM and IPAL-EM have sought to expand training in this area.1–3 Although hospice and palliative medicine became an ACGME-recognized subspecialty in 2008, palliative care remains a core responsibility of all EM physicians, and related skills are required for EM board certification.4

Despite this recognition, the scope and quality of palliative care education in EM residency programs remain inconsistent. Surveys demonstrate that while most residents value palliative care training and desire more instruction, fewer than two-thirds report receiving formal education in key skills such as pain management, withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, and managing imminent death.5,6

Simulation-based training is an effective educational modality in both EM and palliative care, offering advantages over didactic instruction by improving practical skills, knowledge retention, and learner satisfaction.7 In palliative care education, simulation with structured debriefing allows residents to practice high-stakes communication and complex decision-making in a controlled, reflective environment.8 For this curriculum, simulation was selected due to its demonstrated effectiveness in teaching sensitive communication, clinical reasoning, and interprofessional teamwork.

Prior simulation-based curricula have targeted specific components of EOL communication relevant to EM. The GRIEV_ING mnemonic—Gather, Resources, Identify, Educate, Verify, Inquire, Nuts and bolts, Give—was developed specifically for the ED context and has been validated as both a teaching framework and assessment tool for death notification skills among EM residents, demonstrating improvements in confidence, competence, and communication. Hobgood et al.9,10 demonstrated that a 2-hour educational session using small-group, role-play, and didactic experiences improved resident confidence, competency, and communication skills in delivering death notifications. For goals-of-care conversations specifically, the REMAP framework—Reframe, Expect emotion, Map values, Align, Plan—has emerged as a widely adopted structured communication tool, particularly well suited to the time-constrained ED setting.11 Many other similar frameworks have been published and utilized as online resources.12 Despite this growing body of work, existing published curricula have largely addressed individual EOL communication skills in isolation—death notification, goals of care, or advance directive interpretation—rather than integrating these competencies across both adult and pediatric populations within a single simulation-based curriculum. The present curriculum addresses this gap by combining 4 cases spanning adult and pediatric EOL scenarios, targeting goals-of-care communication, advance directive application, comfort-focused symptom management, and death notification within a single structured session grounded in established communication frameworks.

Methods

Development

We developed a simulation workshop designed for learners to apply EOL principles with cases based on real de-identified patient cases from the ED. Learners were EM residents from each PGY level 1–4 at the University of California San Francisco (UCSF). Learners participated in this educational session during a dedicated residency education session. Participation in the session and in the research study was voluntary. This study was deemed exempt by the University of California Institutional Review Board (25-43308).

Two cases (cases 2 and 4) were piloted during prior simulation sessions, and the remaining cases (cases 1 and 3) were developed specifically for this session to address perceived gaps. All cases were reviewed by EM physicians with advanced training and/or fellowships in medical education and/or palliative care.

Two EM attending faculty facilitated the cases and accompanying debriefs. Debriefing allowed residents to self-reflect on the case; receive immediate, timely, and specific feedback from fellow learners and faculty; ask questions about EOL best practices; observe faculty facilitators’ role-play difficult conversations; and provide learners with the opportunity to repeat challenging conversations with a fellow learner.

Assessment of learners was conducted via survey to identify changes in confidence and knowledge regarding the critical skills related to EOL care.

Equipment/Environment

The simulation sessions were conducted at UCSF's Kanbar Center for Simulation and Clinical Skills, which simulated an ED setting. The adult patients were high-fidelity mannequins equipped with multiple lifelike features such as respiratory chest movement, blinking eyes, and programmed vocalization. The pediatric patients were age-concordant mannequins. For all cases, vital signs displayed on a bedside monitor were electronically adjusted dynamically by an operator in an adjacent room.

Personnel

Simulation center

The session was coordinated by the EM residency director of simulation education. A medical student assisted in the coordination and distribution of the assessment survey. Simulation center staff operated mannequins and monitoring systems remotely from an adjacent room.

Faculty

Four faculty facilitated the simulations. All faculty were board certified in EM and had extensive experience with EOL care in the ED. Two of the 4 faculty were board certified in pediatric EM. Two cases (1 pediatric, 1 adult) occurred concurrently, with 2 faculty members present for each case. One faculty member played a nurse/narrator, and the other played the dying patient's family member. Sessions also involved EM physicians with advanced training in palliative care to provide targeted feedback. The session can be scaled to run more cases concurrently if more faculty are available.

Implementation

The curriculum was delivered over two 4-hour sessions stratified by training level: PGY-1 and PGY-2 residents participated on one day and PGY-3 and PGY-4 residents on a separate day. Each session accommodated 8 simultaneous groups rotating through all 4 cases in succession. Four different simulation scenarios required the application of various EOL principles. A summary of the 4 simulation cases is presented in the following. Groups completed either the 2 adult cases (1 and 2) or 2 pediatric cases (3 and 4) with dedicated adult or pediatric faculty. Groups then switched, completing the remaining 2 cases with the respective faculty. Fifteen minutes were allocated per case, inclusive of debriefing. Facilitators chose how to split the 15 minutes between running the scenario and debriefing, depending on their group. As the cases were paired, often the second case scenario was shorter with a longer debrief. Each group completed all 4 cases within approximately 1 hour, allowing the full cohort to participate within the 4-hour session.

Case 1

An elderly woman with terminal brain cancer, currently enrolled in hospice care, is brought in by ambulance with altered mental status due to probable urosepsis. She is accompanied by her daughter, who serves as her durable power of attorney (DPOA) and is now requesting that “everything possible” be done to prolong her mother's life—despite the presence of a recent POLST form indicating do not resuscitate/do not intubate (DNR/DNI) status and comfort-focused goals of care.

Learners navigate a complex goals-of-care discussion with a surrogate decision maker. Learners apply communication strategies to explore the surrogate's concerns while upholding the integrity of the patient's expressed values. This case emphasizes the ethical and legal importance of honoring the patient's previously documented wishes, reinforcing the principle of respect for patient autonomy when a surrogate's requests conflict with the patient's clearly stated directives.

Case 2

A patient with advanced dementia and multiple medical comorbidities is brought in by ambulance from a skilled nursing facility, presenting with hypotension and respiratory distress. A 2-year-old POLST form is located, indicating the patient is “Full Code.” The team must engage the patient's family member in a critical decision: whether to pursue invasive interventions such as intubation and central line placement, or to prioritize comfort measures, allowing for a natural and dignified death.

Learners practice leading goals-of-care conversations, grounded in the patient's known values and preferences. Learners manage EOL dyspnea with symptom-directed care. In the debriefing, faculty highlight the limitations of an outdated POLST with changing clinical circumstances. Learners discuss the ethical and legal principles underpinning the concept that physicians are not obligated to provide interventions deemed medically futile, even if ordered on a POLST.

Case 3

A 3-year-old boy is brought to the ED in cardiac arrest after a drowning incident at a family picnic. Despite prolonged resuscitation, including intubation and multiple rounds of epinephrine, there is no return of spontaneous circulation. Learners must consider whether to continue resuscitation to allow the family time to be present at the bedside, assign a team member to provide emotional support and real-time explanations, and speak directly with the parents, preparing them for the likely outcome, before ending the resuscitation.

The scenario and debrief session emphasize the importance of welcoming family presence during pediatric resuscitation, engaging chaplaincy and/or social work early, and practicing compassionate, structured communication at the time of death. Learners discuss and practice the process of pausing for final team input, pronouncing death clearly, and then providing direct, empathetic communication to the grieving family using the child's name.

Case 4

A previously healthy 12-month-old girl is found apneic and cyanotic in her crib and arrives in the ED in cardiac arrest. Despite a prolonged resuscitation, there is no return of spontaneous circulation. Learners must interpret clinical data, including severely acidotic blood gas results, and apply evidence-informed criteria to support the decision to terminate resuscitative efforts.

Learners practice delivering news to grieving parents following a sudden, unexpected infant death. During debriefing, faculty facilitators first model communication strategies, emphasizing the importance of using the child's name, explicitly stating that the child has died, and allowing appropriate emotional expression from the provider without overwhelming the family. Learners then practice these conversations through role-play, focusing on compassionate, clear, and supportive communication in the face of profound loss.

Debriefing

Debriefing took place after each case, led by faculty facilitators. Debriefing followed the GAS—Gather, Analyze, Summarize—format, in which learners first reflected on their own performance before faculty provided targeted feedback and teaching points.13 Each debrief incorporated role-play to allow learners to practice and repeat difficult conversations with immediate feedback. Facilitators shared validated communication frameworks for goals-of-care conversations, delivering serious news, and death notification (REMAP, NURSE, SPIKES, Ask-Tell-Ask).12 Facilitators for the pediatric cases received additional guidance on creating a psychologically safe learning environment, given the emotionally intense nature of consecutive pediatric death scenarios. Debriefing guides are included in Appendix C.

Assessment

For our evaluation strategy, learners completed pre- and postsession surveys. Surveys included Likert-scale questions to assess self-reported confidence in discussing EOL decisions with patients and surrogate decision makers, communicating bad news, and managing pain and dyspnea at EOL. Additional true/false questions explored residents’ knowledge of hospice services, nuances of the POLST, and special considerations in pediatric cardiac arrest. Free-text responses captured feedback on the session's training utility and suggestions for improvement. This survey was developed to measure effectiveness of this simulation in achieving our educational objectives, consistent with Kirkpatrick level 2 assessment of “Learning.”14 The presurvey was distributed immediately prior to the first case. The postsurvey was distributed immediately following the debrief of the last case. Surveys are included in Appendix D.

Survey results were analyzed in Microsoft Excel. Thematic analysis of the free response answers was conducted as a group using inductive methodology and consensus debriefing to generate themes and identify representative quotes.

Results

Of the 41 participating residents, 31 (76%) completed both the pre- and postsimulation surveys and were included in the quantitative analysis. Participants represented all postgraduate training levels: 23% PGY-1 interns, 23% PGY-2 residents, 26% PGY-3 residents, and 29% PGY-4 residents. Learners reported a wide range of prior experience with EOL conversations: 65% estimated having previously led 1–10 goals-of-care discussions, 29% reported leading 10–20 discussions, and 6% reported more than 20.

Learner reactions to the simulation experience were overwhelmingly positive. Following the session, 90% of residents reported feeling more prepared to lead EOL discussions with patients and families. The proportion of learners who felt they had received adequate training in EOL conversations increased from 36% prior to the intervention to 68% posttraining.

Free-response feedback revealed that residents valued faculty-facilitated debriefings conducted immediately after each scenario. Learners described the simulation environment as particularly helpful for practicing emotionally difficult conversations aloud and experimenting with different language and approaches. One resident noted that although they had participated in EOL discussions during clinical care, the simulation allowed them “to come up with a script/approach when it's something we don't get that much practice with regularly.” Residents also identified the inclusion of both adult and pediatric cases as a critical strength of the curriculum.

Learners demonstrated improvements in self-assessed confidence across multiple palliative care domains. Following participation, residents reported increased confidence in conducting goals-of-care discussions, delivering serious news, interpreting advance directives, caring for hospice patients, and managing pain at EOL (Table).

Objective knowledge assessment showed modest improvement. The mean percentage of correctly answered true/false questions increased from 73% preintervention to 79% postintervention. Improvements were observed in questions addressing default treatment decisions in patients without advance directives, managing conflicts between a patient's previously expressed wishes and those of a DPOA, the ability to revoke hospice status, and recommendations regarding family presence during pediatric resuscitation. However, there was a slight decrease in correct responses to a question regarding legal obligations associated with “Full Code” status.

Residents suggested several areas for future refinement. Learners expressed interest in spacing EOL simulations throughout the academic year and recommended providing a content warning before each session due to the emotionally intense nature of the scenarios. Several residents also requested explicit review of the knowledge assessment questions during debriefings to improve clarity. One learner noted that spacing cases throughout the year might better reflect clinical practice, stating that it “would be better to practice having to think about these situations interspersed with doing full resuscitative efforts so that we're better at thinking about having these EOL discussions sooner when it's appropriate.”

Residents also identified topics for additional education, including pharmacologic management for comfort-focused care, decision-making around vasopressor use and antibiotics in patients with DNR or comfort-focused goals, and navigating scenarios involving family disagreement.

Discussion

We demonstrated that a dedicated simulation-based EOL curriculum can meaningfully improve EM residents’ confidence and self-reported proficiency in applying palliative care principles. Following the intervention, residents reported greater preparedness in core EOL skills, including discussing goals of care, interpreting advance directives, and managing pain. These findings support the value of simulation as a high-impact teaching method for complex clinical communication.

Residents valued the opportunity to practice difficult conversations in a realistic, reflective environment and expressed enthusiasm for pediatric scenarios, structured guidance on legal considerations, and clearer instruction on pain management at EOL. The overwhelmingly positive learner reception aligns with prior literature identifying simulation as one of the highest-rated educational modalities in palliative care education for EM residents, alongside bedside teaching and hospice and palliative medicine faculty mentorship.6 These findings emphasize that simulation is particularly well suited to practicing emotionally charged, ethically complex, and time-sensitive scenarios like those encountered in the ED.

While simulation effectively fostered experiential learning and confidence, gains in objective knowledge were more modest, suggesting that simulation alone may be insufficient for teaching certain legal and ethical concepts (eg, POLST limitations, medical futility, DPOA authority). Future iterations could consider integrating targeted mini-lectures or standardized debrief teaching points to improve knowledge acquisition while preserving the strengths of experiential learning.

The concentrated 4-case format of this pilot training differed from the residency program's previous curricular approach, which interspersed EOL cases throughout the year. While many residents appreciated the immersive nature of the focused session and instructors observed skill improvement across successive cases, others noted that the emotional intensity of the consecutive EOL experience was challenging. Both intensive and longitudinal approaches offer distinct benefits, without clear superiority of one model over the other. Although this format requires coordination among multiple simultaneous groups and dedicated faculty, each resident group completed all 4 cases in approximately 1 hour, making the curriculum more feasible than the overall session suggests. The modular design further supports scalability across different residency class sizes or use as individual cases implemented as standalone stations within existing simulation schedules for programs with resource constraints or distinct curricular gaps.

Regarding implementation, debriefing followed a GAS framework, selected to prioritize reflective learning and faculty-modeled role-play given the communication-focused objectives of this curriculum. While full Rapid Cycle Deliberate Practice was not feasible within the 15-minute case-debrief format, the curriculum captured elements of iterative practice by pairing thematically similar cases in succession, allowing learners to apply and refine communication skills across consecutive scenarios before rotating.15 The consecutive pediatric death cases warrant particular attention: the emotional intensity of back-to-back pediatric death scenarios requires skilled faculty debriefers with advanced training in both simulation facilitation and palliative care communication, and the 15-minute format, while logistically practical, limited time for deeper emotional processing. Future implementations should consider allocating additional time for the pediatric cases specifically, and facilitators are encouraged to prioritize psychological safety by acknowledging emotional weight at the outset and creating space for affective processing before transitioning to clinical feedback, as detailed in the facilitator guide (see Appendix C).

The curriculum has limitations related to assessment design. Assessment captured only Kirkpatrick level 2 outcomes—confidence and self-reported knowledge—as changes in clinical behavior (level 3) and patient outcomes (level 4) are difficult to assess following a single simulation session.14 Future iterations could incorporate these via structured faculty observation using a behaviorally anchored checklist during simulation to provide more objective measurement of skill acquisition and/or longitudinal follow-up. Outcomes relied on self-reported survey data subject to response and social desirability bias. Postintervention surveys completed immediately after the session cannot assess long-term retention or impact on clinical behavior. Knowledge assessment gains were modest and may have been affected by ambiguous survey items or inconsistent emphasis during debriefing. Variable simulation team sizes may have influenced individual engagement and learning, limiting generalizability across settings.

In conclusion, simulation-based training is an effective educational tool for enhancing EM residents’ proficiency in applying palliative care principles and conducting EOL conversations. Given the ongoing need to prepare EM physicians for the complexities of EOL care in emergency settings, simulation-based palliative care education should be considered a core component of EM residency curricula.
