Targeted analysis of purine derivatives in beer and fermented beverages using HPLC-QDA
Authors: Rahul Sen, Christian Schubert, Heena Rani, Scott Lafontaine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDietary purines from fermented beverages contribute to hyperuricemia and gout risk, yet factors controlling concentrations in alcoholic and non-alcoholic products remain unclear. Purine bases, purine nucleosides and pyrimidine nucleosides were quantified in 43 beverages using HPLC-QDA in single-ion recording (SIR) mode. Full-strength beers showed the highest concentrations (45.6–223.4 mg/l), dominated by guanosine (41–68%), reflecting the solubilization of barley malt-derived nucleic acids. In contrast, rice-based sake contained lower total purines (8.5–40.2 mg/l) with a xanthine-dominant profile, while distilled spirits, hard seltzers, and white wine contained no detectable purines. Non-alcoholic beers produced via membrane dealcoholization retained purines comparable to full-strength versions. However, substituting barley malt with alternative starch sources, such as rice, reduced total purines by approximately 25–51% in experimental non-alcoholic beers. Total purines correlated strongly with original extract, free amino nitrogen, alcohol, and barley malt proportion. Overall, purine concentration is governed primarily by raw material composition, and HPLC-QDA-SIR provides an accessible platform for routine screening.Keywords: Non-alcoholic beer, Purines, Gout, HPLC-QDA, Brewing adjunct

Introduction

Hyperuricemia and gout represent a rapidly escalating global public health burden. The prevalence of gout has surged to an estimated 55.8 million cases in 2020, representing a 150.6% increase in the total number of cases since 1990 (Cross et al., 2024). This increase was most pronounced in high-sociodemographic index (SDI) regions, particularly high-income areas such as North America, which exhibited the highest age-standardized prevalence globally (1719.8 per 100,000) and the steepest growth rate over the past three decades (Cross et al., 2024). Gout prevalence also displays marked sex differences, with rates approximately threefold higher in men than in women, largely attributed to the protective effects of estrogen, which promotes renal urate excretion and helps maintain lower circulating uric acid concentrations in females, particularly prior to menopause (Hak et al., 2010).While, population aging contributes to these trends (Dalbeth et al., 2021), the disproportionate rise observed in high-income nations suggests an important contribution of modifiable lifestyle factors, such as obesity and the consumption of purine-rich foods and beverages (Asghari et al., 2024).

Among alcoholic beverages, beer consistently contains substantially higher purine concentrations than wine or distilled spirits, a difference attributed to its cereal-based raw materials and yeast-derived biomass. Comparative analyses of alcoholic beverages has shown that beer ranks among the highest in total purine content, whereas distilled spirits typically exhibit purine levels near or below detection limits (Kaneko et al., 2009). Previous metabolomic studies reported the presence of purines (bases, nucleosides and nucleotides) in barley malt (Rani & Whitcomb, 2025) and incomplete assimilation of purine nucleosides by yeast during fermentation, allowing these compounds to remain in the final product (Almeida et al., 2021; Kaneko et al., 2014). Consistent with this compositional distinction, epidemiologic evidence indicates beverage-specific differences in gout risk, with beer consumption associated with approximately a 1.5-fold higher risk of gout compared with nondrinkers, whereas spirits consumption is associated with a more modest increase in risk (≈1.15-fold) (Choi et al., 2004). Taken together, these findings suggest that beer’s purine content is shaped primarily by its cereal-based raw materials and fermentation process. Therfore, purine exposure is relevant to non-alcoholic beer production as well.

In parallel with increasing health consciousness, the global non-alcoholic beer (NAB) market has expanded rapidly, driven by consumer demand for reduced-alcohol alternatives. NAB market valued approximately USD 22–24 billion in 2024–2025 and is projected to nearly double within the next decade, with a compound annual growth rate exceeding 7% (Pulidindi & Ahuja, 2025). Growing availability of NABs complicates consumer interpretation of beverage “risk proxies,” as NAB classification is defined by ethanol concentration but does not inherently constrain the non-volatile solute profile. Physical dealcoholization processes selectively remove ethanol while retaining many dissolved, non-volatile compounds, raising uncertainty as to whether NABs reliably provide reduced purine exposure. Although individual studies have reported purine contents in selected low or non-alcoholic beers, systematic comparative datasets spanning full-strength beers, NABs, and other fermented beverages remain limited in the US and particularly with respect to the influence of raw material selection and production strategy on purine retention.

Although barley malt is widely recognized as a major source of purines in beer, the potential of alternative starch sources to mitigate purine accumulation has not been comprehensively evaluated. During malting, germination activates nucleases that hydrolyze grain nucleic acids into phosphorylated nucleotide products, providing potential precursors of the soluble purine derivatives released during wort production (Brown & Ho, 1987). Past studies have increasingly incorporated non-traditional raw materials such fruits (Nardini & Garaguso, 2020), plants (Guglielmotti et al., 2020), alternative starch sources such as Buck wheat (Duliński et al., 2019), sorghum (Dlamini et al., 2020) and rice (Schubert et al., 2025) to modify fermentability and product attributes, yet their impact on purine composition under controlled brewing conditions remains poorly characterized (Radu et al., 2024).

Although several analytical approaches for purine quantification have been reported, many existing methods rely on extensive acid hydrolysis and enzymatic derivatization prior to analysis. In particular, traditional HPLC-UV methods typically require harsh hydrolysis and multi-step enzymatic treatments, with reported sample pretreatment times extending to several days (Fukuuchi et al., 2013). More advance LC-MS/MS methods offer improved sensitivity and selectivity for structurally similar purine bases, nucleosides and pyrimidines (Kamel et al., 2017), and depend on expensive triple-quadrupole systems that can be resource-intensive and may limit their routine application in large comparative screening studies spanning diverse beverage matrices. In contrast, single-quadrupole instruments offer lower technical complexity and simpler operation for targeted quantitative analysis (Xue et al., 2021). However, conventional single ion recording (SIR) does not provide the precursor-product ion transitions used in tandem mass spectrometry, limiting its ability to distinguish interfering compounds and confirm analyte identity (Xue et al., 2021).

To address these, the present study first developed and applied a targeted HPLC-QDA method operated in SIR mode as a practical, cost effective and accessible alternative to high-end tandem mass spectrometric approaches (Takanari & Natsuki, 2022) for purine profiling in complex beverage systems. Compared with conventional HPLC-UV, the QDA detector provides improved compound specificity through mass-selective detection while maintaining a simple analytical workflow. At the same time, it offers a more accessible and lower-complexity platform than LC-MS/MS (Yang & Rainville, 2018), making it well suited for routine targeted purine analysis in research, quality control, and industrial laboratories. This analytical methodology was then used to systematically quantify purine bases, nucleosides and pyrimidines across a diverse set of commercial alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages available in the United States, as well as experimentally produced NABs made with defined adjunct substitutions to modulate raw material composition. By integrating method development with controlled beverage formulation and broad product screening, this study enables direct evaluation of how raw material selection, processing strategy, and alcohol control influence final purine load, while establishing an analytical approach well suited for comparative screening and future formulation-driven studies.

Material and method

Chemicals and reagents

Analytical standards for purine bases (adenine, guanine, hypoxanthine, xanthine), purine nucleoside (guanosine, and adenosine) and pyrimidine nucleosides (cytidine and uridine) (purity >98%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA. LC-MS grade acetonitrile, Ammonium formate and formic acid were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA. Ultrapure water, type 1 (18.2 MΩ·cm) HPLC grade was produced using an ELGA water purification system (ELGA LabWater, High Wycombe, UK). All other reagents were of analytical grade.

Brewing enzymes used for experimental NAB production included Termamyl SC 4× (α-amylase), Ultraflo Prime (β-glucanase), Kerry Biomatex L (α-acetolactate decarboxylase), and Brewers Clarex (proline-specific endoprotease), obtained from Novozymes A/S (Bagsværd, Denmark) and DSM Food Specialties (Delft, Netherlands).

Sample collection and preparation

A total of 43 beverages representing diverse raw materials, fermentation types, and alcohol contents were analyzed (Table 1) including full-strength beers (n = 12), commercial NABs (n = 4), in-house experimental NABs (n = 10), sake (n = 6), wine (n = 2), cider (n = 1), and various distilled or blended. beverages (n = 8). Each commercial beverage represented a single commercially available product, and replicate analyses were performed to assess analytical reproducibility rather than production batch to batch variability. Commercial samples were purchased from local retail outlets in Fayetteville, AR, USA.

Production of experimental non-alcoholic beers

Experimental NABs were produced at the Center for Beverage Innovation, Department of Food Science, University of Arkansas (Fayetteville, AR, USA). NABs were brewed using a grist bill consisting of 50% barley malt (Batch 20,250,617, Epiphany Craft Malt LLC, Durham, NC, USA) and 50% of the respective adjunct material (Table 1; additionally one reference NAB with 100% barley malt). A two-vessel recirculating pilot brewery system (mash and boil kettle; Ss Brewtech, Wildomar, CA, USA), resulting in 40 L of cooled wort per batch was used for all the brewing trials. Each formulation was brewed once, and replicate measurements therefore represent analytical rather than independent brewing variability.

Milling was carried out using the Ss Brewtech grain mill with a roller gap of 1.25 mm for barley malt and 0.95 mm for the alternative starch grains. Cassava roots were manually peeled using a stainless-steel vegetable peeler and subsequently shredded using a Sammic Cutter-mixer & emulsifier KE-8 V (Sammic Co., Evanston, IL, USA). Alternative starch grains were pre-gelatinized at a adjunct-water ratio of 1:4 in the heating vessel. Pre-gelatinization was initiated by mashing in at 70 °C with the addition of Termamyl SC 4× (0.1 mL/kg total grist; Novozymes A/S, Novonesis Group, Bagsværd, Denmark), followed by heating to 85 °C and holding for 20 min. The gelatinized adjunct mash was subsequently cooled with brewing water in the same vessel to achieve a total mash-in temperature of 74 °C.

The primary grist (barley malt), along with unprocessed rice husks (10% of the total grist to improve lautering performance), was mashed to the pre gelatinized adjunct mash at 74 °C at a grist-water ratio of 1:4 (w/w), with Ultraflo Prime added at 0.05 mL/kg total grist (Novozymes). Following this mashing in process, the final saccharification rest was maintained at 74 °C for 60 min. Starch conversion after 60 min rest was assessed qualitatively using the iodine method on the mash (0.02 N iodine solution; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). The same time temperature and enzyme addition procedure was applied to all formulations. However, viscosity changes and the extent of enzymatic liquefaction were not quantitatively measured.

Lautering and sparging were conducted until a target pre-boil original gravity of 6.0°P ± 0.2°P was achieved. The original gravity was measured using an Anton Paar Easy Dens densitometer equipped with the Brew Meister software (iOS version 5.10.2; Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Austria). With the start of wort boiling, CTZ hops (14.4% α-acid; BSG Hops, Wapato, WA, USA) were added to achieve a final bitterness of 12.5 ± 1.5 bitterness units. After a 60 min boil, the wort gravity (6.5°P ± 0.25) and pH (4.65 ± 0.05) were adjusted. One Whirlfloc tablet (Kerry Group plc, Tralee, Ireland) was added at the start of whirlpool circulation, followed by a 15 min whirlpool rest.

The clarified wort was cooled using a single-stage heat exchanger and transferred to Ss Brewtech Unitanks (netto volume 53 L). Prior to yeast pitching, Kerry Biomatex L (α-acetolactate decarboxylase; 2 g/hL) and Brewers Clarex (2 g/hL; DSM Food Specialties B.V., Delft, Netherlands) were added. Fermentation was carried out using a maltose negative S. cerevisiae strain (Berkeley Yeast, Oakland, CA, USA) at a pitching rate of 6.5 × 106 cells/ml at 20 °C until a final ethanol concentration of 0.40% v/v ± 0.1% v/v was reached (repeated ethanol results on two consecutive days).

Following fermentation, NABs were transferred into 19.5 L stainless-steel kegs and pasteurized to a target of 40 pasteurization units. After cooling to 4 °C, carbonation was adjusted to 4.9 g/L using food-grade carbon dioxide, and the beers were stored at 4 °C until HPLC analysis.

Physicochemical characterization

Prior to analysis, carbonated samples were degassed in an ultrasonic bath for 20 min. Ethanol content (% v/v), specific gravity, and density were measured using an Anton Paar Alcolyzer ME. pH was measured using a calibrated digital pH meter. Free amino nitrogen (FAN) in all beverages was quantified using a previously published microplate method based on the Skalar segmented-flow protocol for full-scale malt analysis (Schmitt & Budde, 2012). Beverage samples and glycine standards (0–300 ppm) were diluted in 0.3% (w/v) Brij-35 and reacted sequentially with ascorbic acid and ninhydrin in a 96-well PCR plate. The reaction mixture was incubated at 90 °C for 15 min, followed by rapid cooling to room temperature. The reaction was then stopped by the addition of potassium iodate, and aliquots were transferred to a clear microplate. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a multimode microplate reader (Synergy HTX, BioTek Instruments). FAN concentrations were calculated using a glycine-based calibration curve.

Purines and pyrimidines analysis

Sample preparation

All samples were centrifuged at 16,000 ×g for 10 min at 10 °C to remove particulates, and supernatants were filtered through 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filters into amber HPLC vials. Samples were analyzed without further dilution unless concentrations exceeded the calibration range, in which case appropriate dilution with water was performed.

HPLC-QDA instrumentation and conditions

Purine and pyrimidine analysis was adapted from previously reported ion-pairing LC-MS methods with minor modifications (Yamaoka et al., 2010). Chromatographic separation was performed using a Waters ACQUITY Arc HPLC system coupled to a Waters ACQUITY QDA single-quadrupole mass detector (Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA). Separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm), attached to a HSS T3 guard column, with column temperature maintained at 40 °C, and sample manager temperature set at 10 °C. The QDA probe temperature was set to 600 °C with a capillary voltage of 0.8 kV and a cone voltage of 15 V. These settings were applied consistently to all standards and samples; a systematic comparison of alternative probe temperatures and cone voltages was not performed. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 10 mM ammonium formate buffer (pH 5.0) containing 1.25 mM dihexylammonium acetate (DHAA) and (B) acetonitrile containing 1.25 mM DHAA. Elution was performed at a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min using a linear gradient (waters curve 6) from 0 to 30% B over 40 min. Detection was conducted in SIR mode using positive electrospray ionization for all analytes except xanthine, which was monitored in negative ion mode. Monitored m/z values were, adenine (136), guanine (152), hypoxanthine (137), adenosine (268), guanosine (284), cytidine (244), uridine (245), and xanthine (153). SIR was selected because the analysis targeted predefined compounds with known m/z values, allowing greater dwell time and improved signal-to-noise ratio for the selected ions compared with full-scan acquisition. Analyte identification was based on agreement of the monitored ions and retention times with analytical standards. No hydrolysis step was performed; therefore, the method quantified the targeted free purine bases and nucleosides present in the beverage samples and did not quantify purines incorporated into nucleotides or intact nucleic acids. The reported total purine concentration was calculated as the sum of adenine, guanine, hypoxanthine, xanthine, adenosine, and guanosine and did not include bound nucleotides or purines incorporated into intact nucleic acids.

Quantification of individual compounds was performed using external calibration curves generated from aqueous standard solutions prepared across analyte specific concentration ranges encompassing the expected levels in beverage samples. Matrix-matched calibration, standard addition verification and internal-standard normalization were not used. Because sample preparation consisted only of centrifugation and filtration, without a solvent-extraction step, conventional extraction recovery was not evaluated. Calibration curves were fitted using linear regression expressed as y = mx + c. The calibration range, slope, intercept, R2, regression weighting, and back-calculated accuracy range for each analyte are provided in Table S1. Limits of detection (LOD) and limits of quantification (LOQ) were calculated as 3.3σ/S and 10σ/S, respectively, where σ represents the standard deviation of the analytical response and S represents the slope of the calibration curve and results are summarized in Table S1. Inter-day instrumental precision and agreement with nominal concentrations were evaluated using a mid-level mixed standard analyzed before and after the ten sample injections on each of four independent analytical days (n = 8 measurements per analyte). Precision was expressed as the %RSD across the eight measurements, while agreement was expressed as the measured concentration relative to the nominal concentration, with results provided in Table S2. These beginning and end of sequence measurements also served to monitor instrumental stability within each daily run. Intra-day method precision, matrix-specific spike recovery, matrix effects, robustness, and carryover were not formally evaluated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R statistical software (v4.5.2) with the tidyverse and agricolae packages. For the comparisons presented in Fig. 1, analytical replicates were averaged to obtain one value for each commercial product or experimental formulation. Mean total purine concentrations for full-strength beer, membrane processed commercial NAB, and experimental NAB were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test (p < 0.05). The distilled NAB and beverage categories with limited product representation, including wine and cider, were included for descriptive comparison only and were excluded from category-level statistical analyses. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated to explore association between purine concentrations and physicochemical parameters (Malt %, FAN, Alcohol). These correlations were considered exploratory and were not interpreted as independent predictive effects. Data Visualizations were performed on R using the ggplot2 package.

Results and discussion

Purine composition across different beverage categories

Among the commercial beverage products evaluated in this study, total purine concentrations varied significantly across beverage product styles (Table 1). Among the quantified pyrimidine nucleosides, cytidine and uridine were detected across the samples but were excluded from total purine calculations due to their pyrimidine structure and lack of involvement in uric acid metabolism. Unlike purines, which degrade to uric acid via xanthine oxidase, pyrimidines are catabolized into highly soluble products including β-amino acids (β-alanine and β-aminoisobutyrat), ammonia, and carbon dioxide, which are readily excreted and do not contribute to hyperuricemia (Chandel, 2021, Kiyoko, Aoyagi, Fukuuchi, Inazawa and Yamaoka, 2014). Therefore, the total purine metric was intended to reflect uric acid–relevant purine exposure and not total dietary nitrogen intake or assimilation.

Among all the samples studied, full-strength beers exhibited both the highest concentrations and widest range of total purines ranging from 45.6 ± 2.8 mg/l (beer 8) to 223.4 ± 11.1 mg/l (beer 6) (Table 1). Dark, barley malt-based beer styles consistently occupied the upper end of this spectrum, exemplified by beer 1 (182.9 ± 19.6 mg/l) and beer 2 at 199.9 ± 13.2 mg/l whereas lighter, adjunct (i.e. corn or rice)-rich lager styles beers (beer 3, beer 4, beer 10 and beer 12) were consistently lower (Table 1). These results indicate that ingredient composition, particularly the sugar/starch source(s) a beer is derived from significantly impacts the variability in the beer purine content. The observed concentration ranges are consistent with previously reported values for commercial beers, including 36.8 mg/l to 150 mg/l Chinese beer (Li et al., 2015), 60 to 175.8 mg/l in regular, 81.63 to 241.72 mg/l in locally brewed and 3.8 to 254.3 mg/l in low alcohol beers brewed in Japan (Kaneko et al., 2009). Across all beer, guanosine was the predominant purine base, contributing approximately 41–68% of total purines. To put this in perspective, based on the measured concentration of 223.4 ± 11.1 mg/L, a 12 oz. bottle of beer 6 would provide approximately 79.4 mg of purines per serving equivalent which equates to 19.8% of the 400 mg/day dietary purine intake recommended in Japan for the management of hyperuricemia and gout (Kaneko et al., 2024). This comparison is intended only to contextualize dietary purine exposure and should not be interpreted as a direct estimate of clinical gout risk.

The predominance of guanosine across diverse beer types is consistent with earlier reports identifying it as the major purine in barley malt-based beverages, reflecting both the nucleic acid composition of barley and its solubilization during malting and mashing (Markelj et al., 2016). Its persistence in finished beer may partly reflect limited uptake or utilization of purine nucleosides during fermentation. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, purine bases such as adenine, guanine, and hypoxanthine are transported through the purine-cytosine permease Fcy2p transporter and converted into nucleotides through salvage pathway enzymes involving adenine phosphoribosyl transferase (Apt1p) and hypoxanthine guanine phosphoribosyl transferase (Hpt1p) (Liu & Lu, 2024). These pathways primarily support the utilization of purine bases rather than directly demonstrating the uptake of guanosine. Limited utilization of extracellular purine nucleosides therefore provides a possible explanation for the observed guanosine predominance, although transport and metabolic changes during fermentation were not measured in this study.

Low or trace amounts of individual purine bases were observed in experimental NABs (2; 1,3–10) which were made with 100% rice malt and 50% alternative malt/ starch sources respectively. The nucleosides in these products were primarily guanosine (57–79%) and adenosine (13–30%). Together, these results suggest that limited yeast utilization of purine nucleosides contributes to their accumulation in beer like matrices, particularly when malt-derived nucleic acids/proteins remain a major source of purines.

Commercial NABs showed considerable variability in total purine concentration depending on sub-style (Fig. 1). NAB produced with the membrane filtration method (Group ab) were not statistically different from full-strength beers (group a), reflecting extensive overlap in purine concentrations between these product types (p > 0.05). Several commercial NABs displayed total purine concentrations comparable to their full-strength counterparts. For example, NAB 1 (168.5 ± 13.5 mg/l), NAB 2 (65.57 ± 2.5 mg/l) and NAB 3 (67.94 ± 9.4 mg/l) were found to have high purine concentration, comparable to their full strength counterparts beer 1 (182.86 ± 19.6 mg/l), beer 7 (93.06 ± 4.8 mg/l) and beer 4 (59.75 ± 5.9 mg/l), respectively (Table 1). These groupings (Fig. 1) demonstrate that non-alcoholic designation alone is not a reliable predictor of reduced purine content, whereas product style and formulation exert a stronger influence. Similar observations that ethanol removal does not necessarily reduce purine load have been reported previously for both low- and non-alcoholic beers (Kaneko et al., 2009).

Experimental NAB made using the maltose negative yeast (group b) and reduced barley malt showed significantly lower total purine concentrations than full-strength beers (p < 0.05), with values ranging from 0.7 ± 0.3 mg/l to 89.9 ± 7.9 mg/l and average concentrations below 50 mg/l (Fig. 1, Table 1). These reductions were consistent across alternative starch based experimental NABs and indicate that raw material composition and fermentation strategy exert a strong influence on final purine load. Commercial NAB 4 produced using the distillation process has very low amount of purines and differed significantly from full-strength beers (p < 0.05), while not differing from yeast controlled or membrane-processed NABs (Fig. 1). Together, these results further demonstrate that ethanol removal alone does not determine purine content; rather, formulation and processing conditions govern purine retention in beer-like matrices.

In contrast to barley-malted beverages, fermented rice and fruit-based beverages have lower total purine concentrations (Table 1). Total purine content in sake ranged from 8.5 ± 0.9 mg/l (sake 5) to 40.2 ± 3.7 mg/l (sake 1), values substantially lower than those observed for beer. In addition to reduced abundance, sake displayed a distinct individual purine profile. While guanosine predominated in barley-malted beers, sake purines were primarily composed of xanthine, contributing approximately 37–94%, and hypoxanthine accounting for an additional 7–24% of total purines. This specific purine profile aligns with the earlier reports by (Kaneko et al., 2009), who reported xanthine (56.3 to 79.1%) and hypoxanthine (19.6–39.2%) as dominant purines in Japanese sake. The absence of guanosine dominance in sake suggests that purine composition in sake is shaped by differences in raw material composition and fermentation biology inherent to rice-based processing, in contrast to the malt-based system characteristic of beer production.

Wine samples were consistently low, red wine (wine 2) had a total of 29.7 ± 1.0 mg/l total purine whereas no major purine bases were detected in white wine (wine 1), in which only trace levels of cytidine were observed (0.3 ± 0.1 mg/l). Other fermented beverages, including cider (0.9 ± 0.04 mg/l), malt beverages (2.1 ± 0.2 mg/l), vodka-based tea (1.7 ± 0.1 mg/l) and experimental NAB made with 100% malted rice (NAB Exp 2) (0.7 ± 0.3 mg/l) occupied lowest end of the observed ranges (Table 1). Distilled and blended beverages, including whiskey, tequila, soju, and sugar-based hard seltzers, contained no detectable purines. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing minimal purine content in wine, fruit-based fermented beverages, and distilled spirits due to the absence of barley malt derived nucleic acids and the non-volatile nature of purines (Kiyoko et al., 2014).

The elevated purine amount observed in barley malt-based beers is supported by the grain's high total protein content (8–13%), dominated by storage proteins, hordeins (40–50%) and glutelin (35–45%) (Senarathna et al., 2024). These proteins serve as rich source of glutamine (abundant in hordeins) and glycine (abundant in glutelin) (Jaeger et al., 2021), the two primary amino acid precursors utilized in the de novo biosynthesis of the purine ring (Bhagavan & Ha, 2015). During malting and mashing extensive modification and hydrolysis of malt components releases soluble nitrogenous compounds into the wort (Jaeger et al., 2021). However, the purines measured in the present study are more likely associated with the degradation and solubilization of pre-existing nucleic acids and nucleoprotein complexes in barley malt rather than direct de novo purine biosynthesis from released amino acids. This may also explain the lower purine concentrations observed when barley malt was partially replaced with alternative starch sources.

Taken together, these findings indicate that barley malt-based beers represent the greatest source of dietary purine exposure among the beverages evaluated, whereas sake, wine, cider, and other fermented beverages generally contribute lower purine amounts. Distilled spirits and hard seltzers contained little to no detectable purines because purine compounds are largely excluded during distillation. Therefore, from a purine exposure perspective, the beverages examined can be broadly ranked as beer followed by sake/wine/other fermented beverages and distilled beverages. However, this ranking reflects relative dietary purine exposure rather than overall gout risk, as ethanol itself may independently influence uric acid metabolism.

Factors associated with the overall purine concentration in beer

Pearson correlation analysis revealed strong and statistically significant relationships between brewing physicochemical parameters and final individual and total purine concentrations (Fig. 2). Total purine content showed strong significant positive correlations with original extract (°P; r = 0.85, p < 0.05), alcohol by volume (r = 0.78, p < 0.05), FAN (r = 0.69, p < 0.05). These associations indicate that purine load increases with wort density and malt-derived extract, consistent with the role of malted barley as the primary source of purine bases and nucleic acids in beer (Liu & Lu, 2025). In support of this interpretation, the proportion of barley malt in the grist exhibited a significant positive correlation with total purine concentration (r = 0.63, p < 0.05), supporting association between barley malt input and purine availability. The stronger association between total purines and original extract compared with alcohol content suggests that purine accumulation is related to the extraction of malt-derived compounds during wort production, although the independent contributions of these interrelated variables cannot be determined from the present pairwise analysis.

Although alcohol by volume exhibited a strong positive correlation with total purine concentration (Fig. 2), this relationship should be interpreted as covariation rather than a direct effect of ethanol on purine retention. Alcohol amount in beer reflects wort extract and fermentable substrate availability, both of which are determined by raw material input and processing conditions during wort production. Consequently, higher alcohol levels track with increased solubilization of barley malt derived purines during malting and mashing, leading to elevated purine concentrations in the final product. The observation that purine levels remain high in physically dealcoholized beers and low in biologically limited beers formulated with reduced malt input further supports the conclusion that ethanol content serves as a proxy for formulation and processing intensity rather than an independent determinant of purine load.

FAN, commonly used as an indicator of protein solubilization during mashing, was strongly correlated with adenine (r = 0.67, p < 0.05), Guanine (r = 0.56, p < 0.05), guanosine (r = 0.68, p < 0.05) hypoxanthine (r = 0.88, p < 0.05), xanthine (r = 0.64, p < 0.05) and total purines (r = 0.69, p < 0.05). Among these compounds, hypoxanthine exhibited the strongest association with FAN, suggesting a close relationship between nitrogen solubilization during mashing and the accumulation of purine compounds in wort and beer. This relationship suggests that processing conditions during wort production and peptide release also facilitate the liberation of intracellular purines and pyrimidines from malt-derived nucleoprotein complexes into the wort (Harris & Parsons, 1957). The co-variation of FAN and purine concentration indicates a shared association with solubilization processes occurring during mashing, rather than independent accumulation during fermentation.

Alternative starch % showed a significant negative correlation with total purine concentration (r = −0.63, p < 0.05) as well as individual purine measured. Because alternative starch and malt % are directly linked, the negative association with alternative starch mirrors the positive association with malt and should not be interpreted as an independent effect. Accordingly, this inverse relationship suggests that partial replacement of barley malt with alternative starch sources (e.g., corn, rice, cassava) may reduce purine load by lowering the contribution of nucleic acid rich barley malt. Similar trends have been reported previously, where substitution of barley malt with wheat grain resulted in reduced purine concentrations in beer (Liu & Lu, 2025). Consistent with these observations, experimental NABs produced in this study using 50% unmalted alternative starch substitution showed lower total purine concentrations compared to the 100% malt control, decreasing from 64.5 ± 2.3 mg/l (NAB EXP 5) to 31.4 ± 4.3–48.3 ± 0.3 mg/l depending on alternative starch type. Individual purines displayed correlation patterns like total purines, indicating that purine composition changes collectively with malt input rather than being selectively modulated (Fig. 2). Additional correlation statistics for individual purines and pyrimidines are provided in Fig. S1.

Production method and purine retention in nonalcoholic beer

Production method contributed to variability in purine retention among NABs (Table 1 and Fig. 1), although its effect was secondary to raw material composition. To enable direct comparisons between NABs and full strength beers, commercial NABs included in this study were selected together with the closest available full strength products from the same manufacturer and product family. This helped reduce but not eliminate variation arising from brand-specific formulations and provided a basis for examining purine concentrations in relation to production strategy. However, these commercial products were not confirmed to originate from the same wort or production batch and therefore should not be considered experimentally paired parent products. While all NABs in the current dataset-maintained ethanol concentrations below 0.5% v/v, no significant differences were observed between products produced via physical dealcoholization versus those brewed with maltose-negative yeast.Commercial NABs in this study predominantly employed physical dealcoholization, such as membrane filtration. Notably, products produced using membrane filtration did not exhibit reduced purine concentrations relative to their selected full-strength commercial counterparts: NAB 1 (168.5 ± 13.5 mg/l) remained comparable to Beer 1 (182.9 ± 19.6 mg/l); NAB 2 (65.6 ± 2.5 mg/l) to Beer 7 (93.1 ± 4.8 mg/l); and NAB 3 (67.9 ± 9.4 mg/l) to Beer 4 (59.8 ± 5.9 mg/l). Although these comparisons show that substantial purine concentrations remained in the membrane processed commercial NABs, details of membrane pore size, molecular-weight cut-off, fouling behavior and other parameters were unavailable. Consequently, the observed differences could not be attributed to specific membrane characteristics or operating conditions.

In contrast, the NAB produced using distillation (NAB 4) exhibited an approximately seven-fold lower total purine concentration (15.3 ± 1.8 mg/l) compared with the selected full-strength counterpart (113.2 ± 5.5 mg/l; beer 3, Table 1). However, because purines are non-volatile compounds and are not expected to be removed by evaporation (Almeida et al., 2021), we hypothesize that the lower purine concentration in NAB 4 primarily reflects differences in the base formulation rather than the distillation process itself. Nevertheless, the present study does not allow the individual effects of formulation and dealcoholization process to be completely separated, as commercial beers produced from the same wort using different dealcoholization technologies were not available. Future studies using a split-wort design, in which the same beer is subjected to different dealcoholization processes (e.g., membrane filtration and distillation), would provide a more direct evaluation of process-specific effects on purine retention. Supporting this interpretation, a light beer, beer 12 made with approximately 10% less malt than beer 3 but with same brewing method had lower original extract (8.3 P°) and FAN (69.7 ppm), accompanied by an approximately 44% reduction (113.21 ± 5.5 mg/l to 49.9 ± 3.5 mg/l) in total purine concentration relative to full strength beer 3 (10.7 P° and 113.21 ppm FAN) (Table 1). These results indicate that malt input and wort composition exert a stronger influence on purine retention than the specific alcohol-removal technology applied.

Experimental NABs produced using maltose-negative yeast strains (NAB Exp 1–10) further clarified the relative contributions of fermentation strategy and formulation. These strains produce minimal ethanol (0.3–0.5% v/v) without downstream alcohol removal, however, purine concentrations varied primarily with grist composition. Experimental NABs brewed with reduced barley malt content and adjunct substitution consistently exhibited low total and individual purine concentrations, whereas experimental NABs formulated with high barley malt loads specifically 100% barley malt (NAB 5, 64.52 ± 2.3 mg/l) or 50% unmalted barley (NAB 8, 68.83 ± 2.36 mg/l) retained elevated purine levels despite identical fermentation conditions. The particularly low total purine concentration of the 100% malted-rice NAB (NAB EXP 2; 0.71 ± 0.25 mg/L) compared with the formulation containing 50% raw rice and 50% barley malt (NAB EXP 10; 31.41 ± 4.78 mg/L) should not be interpreted as a simple dose dependent effect of rice substitution. These formulations differed both in the presence of barley malt and in the form of rice used. The absence of barley malt in NAB EXP 2 likely reduced the contribution of barley-derived nucleic acids and soluble purines. However, because each formulation was produced as a single batch and purines were not measured during intermediate processing stages, the mechanism underlying this difference could not be determined. Collectively, these findings indicate that raw material input dominates purine retention. The results highlight several practical formulation- and process-level strategies for reducing purine retention in beer-like matrices. Because overall purine content was driven primarily by malt-derived inputs and by the limited utilization of purine nucleosides during fermentation, effective interventions should focus on reducing nucleic acid availability or enhancing nucleoside assimilation. At the grist and raw material level, partial replacement of barley malt with low-protein, low-nucleic-acid alternative starch sources consistently resulted in lower total purine concentrations, representing an immediately actionable strategy for product reformulation. These reductions likely reflect both the lower intrinsic nucleic acid content of adjunct materials and their reduced contribution of soluble nucleosides during wort production. As a result, raw material selection represents one of the most direct and scalable approaches for mitigating purine content in finished beer-like beverages.

While partial replacement of barley malt with alternative starch sources offers a practical strategy for reducing purine content, such formulation changes may also influence the sensory characteristics of the final product (Cadenas et al., 2021). Barley malt is a major contributor to beer flavor, aroma, color, body, and mouthfeel, whereas increasing the proportion of cereal adjuncts such as rice, corn, or cassava can modify these attributes depending on the type and level of substitution. Previous studies have shown that moderate adjunct incorporation has relatively minor effects on sensory quality, whereas higher substitution levels may alter flavor profile, mouthfeel, and fermentation-derived volatile composition (Schubert, Sen, Rettberg and Lafontaine, 2025, Yorke, Cook and Ford, 2021). Therefore, the development of low-purine beers should balance nutritional benefits with sensory quality to ensure consumer acceptance. Although sensory evaluation was beyond the scope of the present study, future work integrating targeted purine analysis with descriptive sensory evaluation will help optimize formulations that achieve reduced purine content while maintaining desirable beer quality attributes.

From a fermentation standpoint, future work could explore yeast-based strategies aimed at enhancing nucleoside utilization, such as selecting or engineering strains with improved nucleoside transport or salvage capacity. Enzymatic approaches applied during wort production may offer an additional route, particularly strategies that convert purine nucleosides into bases or forms more readily assimilated by yeast. For example, the use of thermally stable phosphoribosyltransferase, rather than relying solely on nucleosidase-mediated cleavage, could theoretically promote intracellular salvage pathways and limit extracellular nucleoside accumulation. Although these strategies were not tested in the present study, they are consistent with the compositional trends observed here and highlight promising directions for future formulation-driven research. Also, the commercial beverage results were based on individual commercially available products rather than independent production batches. Therefore, the observed differences represent the products analyzed and should not be interpreted as estimates of batch-to-batch variability or as definitive characteristics of entire beverage categories.

Conclusion

This study establishes HPLC-QDA SIR as a practical, accessible method for routine targeted purine analysis across diverse beverage matrices. Our findings demonstrate that purine levels in fermented beverages are dictated primarily by raw material selection rather than fermentation kinetics or final ethanol concentration. Specifically, barley malt is the dominant contributor to purine load characterized by high guanosine levels, while rice-based sake exhibits a distinct xanthine-dominant profile. This divergence highlights how both precursor availability and fermentation biology determine the final metabolic fingerprint.

Critically, a “non-alcoholic” (NAB) label is not a reliable predictor of reduced purine content. Several commercial NABs retained purine concentrations comparable to full-strength counterparts, confirming that ethanol removal does not mitigate purine exposure. In contrast, substituting 50% of barley malt with low-nucleic-acid alternative starch (e.g., rice, cassava, or corn) resulted in approximately 25–51% lower total purine concentrations relative to the 100% barley malt control, depending on the adjunct source. Strong correlations between purine levels and parameters such as Original Extract, FAN, and malt percentage reinforce the mechanistic link between nucleoprotein solubilization and purine release during mashing.

Interpretation of these findings is limited by both the scope of the samples and the analytical approach. The commercial samples represented a selected range of products, limiting generalization to entire beverage categories, while each experimental formulation was produced as a single pilot-scale batch, so batch-to-batch variability could not be assessed. The analytical method also quantified only free purine bases and nucleosides, not nucleotides or purines incorporated into intact nucleic acids. Because matrix-specific validation was not performed across all beverage types, the measured values may not capture total purine content, and comparisons among beverage matrices should be interpreted cautiously.

Despite these limitations, from a dietary purine perspective, these findings suggest that beverages formulated with lower barley malt content generally provide lower purine exposure than conventional barley malt-based beers. However, purine concentration alone should not be interpreted as an indicator of the overall health impact of alcoholic beverages, as ethanol metabolism, individual susceptibility and other dietary and clinical factors also contribute to the risk of hyperuricemia and gout.While alcohol content correlates with purine load, this relationship is a result of strength of the wort and the percentage of barley malt used. These results provide a framework for developing “low-purine” beverages through informed grist optimization, targeted enzymatic degradation of nucleosides, or the selection of yeast strains with enhanced purine assimilation capacity.
