Regulatory Definitions and Classification of Biosimilar Medications Across the 6 Regions of the World Health Organization
Authors: Jonathan H. Watanabe, Michael W. Strand, Minal Walvekar, Rafael Alfonso-Cristancho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow do regulatory definitions and requirements for biosimilar medications differ among the 6 global regions of the World Health Organization (WHO)?FindingsThis scoping review, including guidelines from 19 countries, found that most emerging market and developing economies (as defined by the International Monetary Fund) follow the 2009 WHO guidance, but regulatory requirements vary among countries, particularly for comparability exercises, clinical and nonclinical data, and pharmacovigilance. Recent European, US, and WHO guidelines (2022) allow waiver of studies when appropriate analytical data are available, but few emerging market and developing economies have adopted similar waiver policies.MeaningThese findings highlight areas in which the global regulatory framework requires greater harmonization to optimize access to biosimilar medications and to improve support for policy research and cross-country comparisons.

Introduction

The development of biologic medicines has transformed the management of numerous chronic diseases, including cancer, autoimmune inflammatory conditions, and diabetes.1 The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) defines biologics as “generally large, complex molecules that are made from living sources such as bacteria, yeast, and animal cells.”2 In 2023, more than 15% of the medicines on the World Health Organization (WHO) essential medicines list were biologics, yet their high cost means that access is often limited, particularly in low- or middle-income countries where health budgets are constrained.1

Although the concept of a generic biologic is attractive for reducing costs, the size and complexity of biologics make this highly unlikely.3 For small molecules, chemically identical batches can be consistently manufactured using the same synthetic procedure.3 However, minor differences between batches are present in all biologics, even under identical manufacturing conditions.3 Instead of generics, there are biosimilars, which are defined by the FDA as “a biologic medication that is highly similar to and has no clinically meaningful differences from an existing FDA-approved biologic, called a reference product.”2 Thus, there may be minor differences in the clinically inactive components between a biosimilar and the reference biologic, but these differences must not affect its safety, purity, or potency.

Biosimilars are expected to be more affordable than originator biologics4,5 because development takes less time than would be required for a new biologic drug.6,7 The goal in biosimilar development is to demonstrate bioequivalence,3 which requires a lower threshold of evidence and a mitigated demand for lengthy, complex, expensive clinical trials compared with the requirements for the innovator biologic product, thus reducing both the cost and time associated with drug development.6 Since the approval in Europe of the first biosimilar in 2006 (somatropin),8 a range of biosimilar versions of widely used biologic drugs have become available across multiple indications, offering payers and patients additional options.9

Strict regulation is essential to ensure that biosimilars meet safety and efficacy standards that are comparable to the original reference products.2,10 Globally, the WHO has provided guidance on the regulatory approval process for biosimilars,11 detailing the proper evaluation for the approval of new biosimilars, including quality comparisons, stability studies, clinical and nonclinical studies, and pharmacovigilance. While many national regulatory bodies have their own protocols and policies, often based on guidance from the WHO, FDA, or European Medicines Agency (EMA),12 the global regulatory landscape remains inconsistent regarding policies and requirements, despite the availability of guidance on this topic.10,11,13,14

As biosimilars become more prevalent globally, decision-makers are increasingly compelled to consider how varying regulatory frameworks in other countries affect their assessment and interpretation of biosimilar data and the subsequent use of these biosimilars in clinical practice. For example, a clinician may presume that biosimilar data from another country has been generated based on similar definitions and evidence requirements to their own country, but this is not guaranteed and may result in bias regarding the biosimilar’s validated efficacy and safety. Therefore, there is a need for improved understanding of current differences in local guidelines to help advance the development of a standardized regulatory framework for biosimilars, particularly in middle-income countries (ie, those with a gross national income of US $1136 to $13 485 per capita [2022 data]15 or at least 75% of the global population) and also developing markets. The goal of this synthesis was to serve as a pragmatic, timely resource for policymakers, clinicians, payers, and manufacturers to inform clinical decision-making, forecasting, development planning, and strategy in the biosimilar space. The objective of this scoping review was to compare the regulations and policies governing biosimilars pertaining to elements of biosimilar development spanning the usage of reference products, quality analysis, nonclinical and clinical studies, extrapolation of indications, interchangeability, and pharmacovigilance delineated within the 2022 WHO guidance,11 that are increasingly important as more economies use this guidance as a framework, across the 6 global regions as defined by the WHO.16

Methods

Scope of Review

This review aimed to compare the regulations and policies governing biosimilar medications across key global regions, as they apply to the key elements of biologic similarity assessments and comparability to an originator product. We included the 6 regions defined by the WHO: Africa (AFR), Americas (AMR), South-East Asia (SEAR), Europe (EUR), Eastern Mediterranean (EMR), and Western Pacific (WPR). At time of study initiation, we selected the 2 most populous countries within each region that were also designated as emerging and developing economies (as defined by the International Monetary Fund).17 Emerging and developing economies included Nigeria and Tanzania in AFR; Brazil and Mexico in AMR; Egypt and Saudi Arabia in EMR; Poland and Turkey in EUR; India and Indonesia in SEAR; and China and the Philippines in WPR. A comparator group of 7 advanced economies (as defined by the International Monetary Fund)17 included Canada and the US in AMR; France, Germany, and the UK in EUR; and Japan and South Korea in WPR; these countries were selected because of their history of biosimilar development and approval, and their large populations.18,19,20,21,22 The UK was included additionally as a point of comparison with EMA countries (France and Germany) after its withdrawal from the EMA (post-Brexit).19,23 After 2021, the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency became the sole biosimilar regulatory body in the UK. This translated to modification of guidelines, including relaxation of additional scientific justification for extrapolation, removing the requirement for in vivo animal studies, and explicitly stating that a comparative efficacy study may not be necessary in most cases.19,23

Official regulatory documents for the relevant countries were taken from the National Regulatory Agency (NRA) or related agency or committee domain. To minimize the risk of selection bias, the review protocol was defined a priori and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses−Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) was used.24

Literature Review

To identify recent relevant peer-reviewed literature on this topic, we also conducted 3 separate literature searches on March 1, 2026, of the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), PubMed, and Scopus databases initiated with Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms relevant to biosimilars, the country or region in question, and costs or approval processes (eMethods and eTable 1 in Supplement 1 provide additional details). Articles and official guidance published on or before March 1, 2026, were included. All languages were included, and relevant non-English articles were translated into English using DeepL Translate (DeepL SE). The literature searches identified 1683 potential articles of which 86 met the inclusion criteria (eFigure 1 and the eAppendix in Supplement 1).7

Evidence Selection and Synthesis

The Covidence (Future Evidence Foundation) was used to organize, track, review, and quantify final results from the literature search, including removal of duplicates. The search results were exported into the bibliographic management tool, SciWheel, version 51.0.0 (Technology from SAGE, hosted on the LeanLibrary Workspace). The literature search results were independently screened by 2 reviewers (M.W.S. and M.W.), first by title and then by full-text review, to identify relevant articles regarding the specific regulatory elements of interest, including clinical and nonclinical study specifications, interchangeability guidance, and pharmacovigilance requirements. A third reviewer (J.H.W.) resolved any uncertain cases. Recent and salient guidelines were prioritized. Surveys and statistical frameworks were excluded from consideration unless otherwise indicated.

We assessed each country’s most current regulatory guidance (as of March 1, 2026) in the categories defined by WHO guidelines, specifically the concept and definition of biosimilarity; specifications for the reference product; analytical assessment; impurity; stability; nonclinical studies including in vitro, in vivo, immunogenicity, and toxic effects testing; clinical studies including pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (PK/PD), safety, efficacy, and immunogenicity; extrapolation of indications; interchangeability; and pharmacovigilance. Special attention was given to regional differences as well as differences between emerging and developing and advanced economies. Because of the potential for biosimilars to have an abbreviated approval pathway, effort was made to evaluate the extent to which emerging and developing economies waive certain clinical and nonclinical testing requirements, as these countries have the most to benefit from shorter and cheaper drug development regimes.

Comparative Regulatory Guidelines for Biosimilars

Definition of Biosimilarity

Of the 19 countries in the study sample, 17 (89%) have adopted the WHO’s biosimilar terminology; the exceptions were Indonesia, which uses the term follow-on biological in addition to biosimilar,41 and Tanzania, which uses the term similar biotherapeutic product (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).51 A total of 17 countries (89%; except India40 and South Korea43) defines biosimilarity according to the absence of differences in quality, safety, and efficacy between the biosimilar and the reference product, although in some countries (Egypt,34 Turkey,52 UK,53 and US13,14,54,55,56,57,59,60,61), this is heavily implied rather than explicitly stated. Most countries (n = 16 [84%]) require comparability exercises (by definition), but 3 (Mexico,45,46,47 Indonesia,41 and China30,31,32,33) do not explicitly include this requirement.

Product Attribute Requirements

Requirements related to product attributes vary widely among countries (Table 1). Guidelines were the most consistent across countries with regard to extrapolating data across indications. All countries except Saudi Arabia included guidance on this issue; most countries recommended a case-by-case analysis in which analytical and various clinical conditions should be met. Beyond the minimal requirement of similarity, NRA guidelines from these countries reflect the key elements of the EMA,36 FDA,13,54,56,62,63 and WHO guidance.11

Seventeen countries, but not Egypt34 nor Saudi Arabia,44 state that the strength and concentration of the biosimilar medication should be the same as that of the reference biologic; that is unless there is sufficient justification to make an exception (ie, if the total administered doses are equal, as in Japan,42 South Korea,43 and the UK53). Interchangeability guidelines were included in regulatory guidance from 5 of the 7 comparator countries (except Japan42 and South Korea43), whereas in the emerging and developing markets only Nigeria48 and Poland37 included interchangeability guidelines. The Nigerian guidance specifically discourages interchangeability and noted that medication substitution should not be undertaken by pharmacists without consulting the patient’s physician.

Specific guidance on naming or labeling was included in the guidance from 5 developing (China,33 Mexico,46 Nigeria,49 the Philippines,50 and Tanzania51) and 3 advanced economies (Canada,28 UK,53 and the US55,56). Guidance documents from 13 countries stated or implied that the same reference product should be used throughout each comparability study. For reference products that are not nationally licensed, countries may permit the use of nonlocal products sourced from jurisdictions with similar standards, if bridging data are available that justify the substitution of these products for the reference product in comparability studies.6 Seventeen countries had guidance on the use of nonlocal sourcing. China30,31 allows nonlocal sourcing of drugs that are unavailable domestically under special conditions but requires that clinical comparison studies be conducted using reference products licensed in China. Nonlocal products may be considered in India,40 Nigeria,49 the Philippines,50 Poland,36 and Turkey52 as long as they are sourced from jurisdictions of similar scientific and regulatory standards (eg, members of the International Council for Harmonization [ICH] in the case of India, Poland, Nigeria, and Turkey). Guidance from the Egyptian Drug Authority, issued in 2023, allows products from stringent authorities (eg, the European Union or the US) to be used,34 consistent with WHO guidance from 2022.11 Lastly, guidance in Brazil,25 Indonesia,41 and Tanzania51 states that nonlocal reference products may be considered if they come from countries with similar standards, but further details on specifics or bridging requirements are not provided.

Quality Comparisons

All surveyed countries require a complete analytical dossier that compares the biosimilar’s biological activity and physicochemical properties to the reference product; specifics of primary and higher order structures, amino acid sequences, terminal sequences, and posttranslational modifications should be included. The primary structure of the biosimilar should be identical to that of the reference product, but some countries, including Egypt,34 India,40Philippines,50 and Saudi Arabia,44 note that case-by-case flexibility is permitted for nonidentical lower order sequences. All countries require a biological assay to demonstrate the biosimilar’s mechanism of action, with precision set by NRAs.

A requirement for impurity testing was stated or implied in the guidance for all countries—the biosimilar dossier must describe all possible impurities that may be present due to the manufacturing process and provide sufficient analytical data to allow a close examination of purity. The EMA (and therefore, Poland),36 Egypt,34 and Saudi Arabia44 guidance specify the need for comparative accelerated and/or stress condition studies, and NRAs in Egypt,34 India,40 Nigeria,49 the Philippines,50 Poland,36 and Saudi Arabia44 explicitly require that additional risk assessments be performed if any new impurity is detected.

Stability testing under different stress conditions (including exposure to light, temperature, mechanical agitation, and humidity) is mandatory in all countries. Egypt,34 India,40 and Tanzania51 further require at least 3 batches to be studied. Most countries, but not Brazil,25,26,27 China,30,31 or Mexico,45,46,47 recommend storage and shelf-life studies.

In Vivo and Clinical Studies

Relevant nonclinical studies are consistently required for biosimilar registration, with the focus on analytical and preclinical testing, given that rigorous clinical testing is undertaken during development of the reference product. All the comparator advanced economies have abbreviated approval pathways in which in vitro, quality data, and/or clinical PK/PD data may be used to forgo other requirements, such as in vivo or immunogenicity tests (Table 2). As per the 2022 WHO guidelines,11 most of the developing market countries allow in vivo testing to be skipped; Saudi Arabia44 was the only country that did not clearly specify that these tests can be waived. China,30,31 Egypt,34 Indonesia,41 Philippines,50 Poland,36 and Turkey52 usually allow applicants to skip local tolerance tests, given adequate justification. Most other countries require these data to be provided, given the parenteral route of administration of most biologics.

Clinical efficacy and immunogenicity testing can be reduced in all advanced economies, but most emerging and developing economies in this analysis require some level of immunogenicity testing, with Tanzania requiring clinical efficacy testing.51 While none of the advanced economies’ guidelines require additional immunogenicity data beyond that generated by the PK/PD and the quality assessment (provided certain criteria are met), Egypt,34 Philippines,50 and Poland36 also specify details related to waiver justification. Guidance documents from Brazil,25 China,30,31 Mexico,46,47 and Turkey52 allude to reduced immunogenicity data but provided no clear further instruction.

China,30,31 Egypt,34 India,40 Indonesia,41 the Philippines,50 Poland,36 and Turkey52 allow clinical efficacy studies to be waived based on high confidence in functional and structural similarity observed in nonclinical analyses and satisfactory PK/PD studies in patients. India is notable in that these waivers are only extended to confirmatory efficacy trials; its guidance specifies that at least 1 randomized, parallel-group, blinded, clinical safety and efficacy study should be performed.40 The criteria allowing clinical efficacy studies to be waived are usually stringent and apply only when the biosimilar in question has been thoroughly characterized in comparison to the reference biologic. A randomized clinical trial using equivalence trial designs is recommended in cases when efficacy studies are required, although noninferiority designs are generally permitted with adequate justification.

Pharmacovigilance

Biosimilar regulations do not require that similarity to the reference product be re-established after approval. Both the reference biologic and biosimilar manufacturers are responsible for outlining pharmacovigilance. NRAs from the UK53 and several developing markets (Brazil,26 China,32 Egypt,34 India,40 Indonesia,41 Mexico,45 Nigeria,49 the Philippines,50 and Tanzania51) follow WHO guidelines11 by requiring that applications include a pharmacovigilance plan. Japan, the Philippines, and South Korea are the only countries included in this analysis that explicitly require postmarketing pharmacovigilance studies.42,43,50 The need for risk management plans and safety monitoring is generally specified by both emerging and developing and advanced economies (Table 3).

Discussion

This comparison of biosimilar regulatory frameworks across multiple global regions highlights key similarities and differences in regulatory requirements for biosimilars and provides timely regional comparisons across levels of economic development. These novel comparisons reveal patterns in guidance structure, level of detail, permissiveness for abbreviated approval pathways, reliance on clinical data, and the level of local and international cooperation. Although there is general agreement on key concepts, this analysis is consistent with previous research5,6,7,8 that found international regulatory inconsistencies. Identified inconsistencies include differences in the requirement for expensive, often redundant, switching studies when using a nonlocally sourced reference product; terminology; the complete meaning of an interchangeability designation; pharmacovigilance; and naming or labeling requirements.7,64,65,66,67 As a result, international differences complicate the approval process for biosimilars and may affect the availability of biosimilars in some countries if the regulatory requirements (eg, the need to demonstrate clinical efficacy) are onerous.

The choice of reference product is a key issue in biosimilar regulation guidance. Efficiency will increase if manufacturers are able to use the same comparability analyses for multiple applications in different countries, but some NRAs may not allow the use of data when the reference biologic is not licensed in a particular country. As shown in the current analysis, some countries will accept reference products from other countries as comparators, whereas others require evidence on the comparability of the local-licensed and externally sourced reference products.

Interchangeability is a key attribute of biosimilars that is granted once equivalent therapeutic performance to its reference product is demonstrated, and depending on the jurisdiction, it may then be switched by the prescriber or even substituted at the pharmacy. However, there is considerable inconsistency between guidance documents regarding these components, including whether switches can be made by a pharmacist without consulting the prescribing physician.68 EMA guidance grants interchangeability automatically,37 but European Union member states must determine themselves how to implement switching and substitution, whereas the WHO as well as Canada leave the decision of interchangeability itself to the individual NRAs and provinces or territories.11,29 The UK also automatically grants interchangeability to authorized biosimilars, but pharmacy-level substitution is not permitted, preferring the decision to switch be made between prescriber and patient.

Few emerging and developing economies include interchangeability in their guidance, with Nigeria48,49 and Poland37 being the rare exceptions. Despite their allowance, Nigeria’s National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control explicitly discourages interchangeability and substitution, citing potential complications to pharmacovigilance and risk management. Although Saudi Arabia’s 2017 Guideline on Biosimilar Products44 did not contain a statement on interchangeability, the 2025-issued draft version of the General Guideline on Regulatory and Scientific Requirements for Development and Approval of Biosimilars69 automatically grants interchangeability to approved biosimilars; however, this too has yet to be fully codified and is subject to change.

Biosimilar applications to the FDA must state whether they are seeking licensure for interchangeability.57,59 Applicants must provide sufficient evidence demonstrating (1) the biosimilar produces the same clinical result in any patient and (2) there is no increased risk due to switching, either in terms of safety or diminished efficacy. A totality of evidence approach is used to determine each of these points, sometimes requiring additional analytic, nonclinical, and/or clinical data. Once licensed for interchangeability, individual states decide whether or not the product can be used interchangeably without physician authorization.70 In June 2024, the FDA issued a draft guidance62 that no longer requires switching studies between the biosimilar and reference comparator. Recently, omalizumab-igec was granted interchangeability by the FDA and approved for use in Canada and the US, based on the data for the reference biologic, potentially supporting submissions in other countries.71

During the past decade, advances in analytical methods have allowed for effective characterization of the structural and functional qualities of biologic drugs,72 such that these data, combined with data from clinical PK/PD studies, may be used to justify the waiver of clinical efficacy and immunogenicity studies.73 The EMA, FDA, and WHO guidelines all list criteria under which these clinical studies may be replaced with analytical data and, given the years of experience and data, efforts are actively being made to reduce time and cost barriers imposed by these tests. In 2025, the FDA issued draft guidance that would reduce the need for clinical efficacy testing in favor of analytical justifications.63 However, few of the emerging and developing economies and advanced economies included in this review have officially adopted similar waivers into their guidelines. If existing data are to be used to forgo other requirements, the regulatory guidance needs to clearly define what the data requirements are and the circumstances under which supporting studies can be waived. Our analysis indicates that many guidance documents lack specificity and clarity in relation to these requirements.

Many efforts have been made to facilitate regional collaboration and to harmonize global biosimilar approval processes, with the most recent proposal at the time of writing from the ICH.74 The ICH states that differences in technical standards among countries are causing redundancies in testing and analytics, which increase costs and raises unnecessary barriers to biosimilar approval.74 Emerging and developing economies, often limited by resources, can benefit from the availability of established procedures from advanced economies for assessing new classes of biosimilars (eg, monoclonal antibodies, insulin). Collaboration to share data and resources is essential to maximize access to affordable medications, and large advanced economies, such as those included in this analysis, may lead in efforts to streamline and harmonize regulatory requirements.74

Limitations

Our analysis is not without limitations. Several official guidance documents were only available in the country-specific language, so it is possible that errors may have occurred during the translation to English. In addition, we could not be certain that we had collected the entirety of all official documents because some countries provided information in multiple separate documents, not uniformly harmonized. Guidance that has been drafted but not made publicly available would not have been integrated into our searches. At time of study initiation, Indonesia was a member of the WHO SEAR region; then in mid-2025, Indonesia transitioned to the WPR region. Notwithstanding, biosimilar regulatory guidance for Indonesia was collected while it was part of the SEAR region. Regardless, all 6 WHO regions remain represented in the analysis.

Conclusions

This scoping review found that there is a need to standardize the regulatory frameworks for biosimilars across global regions. While there are general agreements on key concepts, inconsistencies persist between and among countries, and several emerging and developing economies have yet to adopt the most recent approaches to biosimilar regulatory authorization. It is hoped that this research contributes to efforts to standardize regulatory frameworks and highlights nuances that can inform these efforts. Harmonizing the regulatory framework is important for not only providing clinicians with the evidence-based confidence to prescribe biosimilars for their patients, but for ensuring that compliance costs are minimized to optimize high-quality biosimilar access.
