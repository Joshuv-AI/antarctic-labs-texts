Nonlinear association between high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and carotid atherosclerosis risk in patients with type 2 diabetes: a retrospective analysis from China
Authors: Changchun Cao, Yuzhi Zheng, Fubing Zha, Yong Han, Haofei Hu, Meiling Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe objective of this study was to evaluate the effect of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) on carotid atherosclerosis (CAS) risk in patients with type 2 diabetes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 390 patients with type 2 diabetes hospitalized in medical institutions between January 2023 and March 2024. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to evaluate the effect of HDL-C on CAS. Nonlinear associations were examined using restricted cubic spline models.ResultsThe cohort demonstrated a CAS incidence of 66.41% (95% CI 61.70–71.12%). Multivariate regression demonstrated that higher HDL-C concentrations were associated with a lower risk of CAS (adjusted OR = 0.27, 95% CI 0.09–0.80, P = 0.0187). Notably, an L-shaped relationship was identified, with a threshold effect; a significant reduction in CAS risk was observed for HDL-C levels ≤ 1.62 mmol/L (adjusted OR = 0.10, 95% CI 0.03–0.42, P = 0.0014), while no significant association was detected above this level (adjusted OR = 5.34, 95% CI 0.25–114.45, P = 0.2844).ConclusionOur findings unveil an L-shaped correlation between HDL-C and CAS risk, with a distinct protective effect of HDL-C observed only below the inflection point. Our findings suggest the potential value of clinical trials aiming to elevate HDL-C as a therapeutic strategy for controlling CAS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03284-x.Keywords: High-density lipoprotein cholesterol, Carotid atherosclerosis, Nonlinear relationship

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes has become a pervasive global health issue, with its incidence escalating at an alarming rate across the world and posing a substantial public health threat in the modern era [1, 2]. The high prevalence of diabetes is primarily attributable to its propensity to induce both microvascular and macrovascular complications, which collectively heighten disease burden and mortality [3]. Carotid atherosclerosis (CAS)—characterized by intimal thickening and plaque deposition within the carotid arteries—functions as a widely accepted indicator of generalized atherosclerosis and is tightly linked to a heightened risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [4–6]. Diabetic individuals experience markedly heightened vulnerability to atherosclerotic disease progression relative to non-diabetic populations [7]. Persistent metabolic disturbances largely mediate this susceptibility, including abnormal lipid metabolism, systemic inflammation, and impaired insulin action. In addition, atherosclerotic CVD (ASCVD) represents the primary cause of death among diabetic patients [8]. Consequently, prompt identification and management of CAS in this population are essential for reducing the risk of ASCVD development.

High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) has historically been considered cardioprotective, based on robust epidemiological data revealing an inverse correlation between HDL-C and CVD events [9, 10]. Nonetheless, this conventional perspective has been called into question by randomized controlled trials and recent Mendelian randomization analyses, which have failed to demonstrate a clear causal linkage between HDL-C elevation and reduced CVD risk [11–17]. Clinical evidence has demonstrated that pharmacological agents aimed at elevating HDL-C concentrations, such as niacin and cholesteryl ester transfer protein inhibitors, have not been effective in reducing the incidence of cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events [15–17]. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that exceedingly high HDL-C levels neither confer additional vascular protection nor lower the risk of cardiovascular disease and may, paradoxically, relate to increased all-cause mortality [18]. These conflicting findings indicate a more intricate and nuanced relationship between HDL-C and atherosclerotic disease than previously appreciated, especially in diabetic cohorts. In light of these uncertainties, the present research seeks to clarify the association between HDL-C concentrations and the risk of carotid atherosclerosis in patients with type 2 diabetes.

Methods

Data source and study population

Our study utilized primary data kindly supplied by Yin Y et al. [19], which are publicly accessible under the Creative Commons Attribution License. This license permits unrestricted use, distribution, and reproduction in any format, provided appropriate credit is given to the original authors and source. Our analysis represents a secondary use of these publicly available datasets. We sincerely acknowledge and thank the original data providers for their significant contributions.

This investigation was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, which also authorized a waiver of written informed consent [19]. The original study was conducted in patients with type 2 diabetes hospitalized in the department of endocrinology at a medical institution in China: Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. The original study included 390 patients aged 18–79 years with a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes between January 2023 and March 2024. To maintain cohort homogeneity and reduce potential confounding influences on metabolic parameters, individuals were excluded from the original study if they exhibited incomplete data, undefined or suspected non-type 2 diabetes, severe impairment of consciousness, active malignancies, pancreatic or autoimmune disorders, acute diabetic complications, cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, hepatic cirrhosis, chronic renal insufficiency, thyroid dysfunction, or current use of anticoagulants, glucocorticoids, or immunosuppressive agents [19].

Data collection

Demographic and clinical information for all participants with diabetes was retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records [19]. Data encompassed age, sex, body measurements (height and weight), lifestyle factors (smoking and alcohol use), medical history (including autoimmune, thyroid disorders, hypertension, coronary artery disease, hepatic disease, malignancies, and renal disease), pharmacological treatments, and diabetes duration. Following an overnight fast of at least 8 h, venous blood was drawn to assess hematological indices (neutrophils and lymphocytes) and key biochemical markers, including triglycerides (TG), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), creatinine, albumin, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total cholesterol (TC), uric acid (UA), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and HDL-C, among others.

Carotid atherosclerosis

Carotid ultrasonography was performed with a standardized color Doppler system by certified sonographers [19]. Carotid atherosclerosis was identified by the presence of focal lesions extending into the arterial lumen, localized arterial wall thickening exceeding 50% relative to adjacent segments, or a maximum carotid intima–media thickness of at least 1 mm [19–21].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with Empower-Stats software. Participants’ demographics were analyzed employing the Chi-square test for categorical data, the Kruskal–Wallis test for skewed continuous data, and one-way ANOVA for normally distributed continuous data. Categorical data were employed as population proportions and percentages, while continuous data were applied as means ± standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges.

Multivariable logistic regression models were applied to examine the effect of HDL-C on the risk of CAS, with results reported as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Covariate selection was guided by previous literature, clinical experience, univariate analysis, and collinearity assessment [19, 22–26]. Three analytical models were developed: Model 1, which was unadjusted for confounders; Model 2, which accounted for age, BMI, gender, smoking, and drinking; and Model 3, which accounted for age, lymphocyte, HbA1c, drinking, hemoglobin, AST, neutrophils, LDL-C, hypertension, UA, creatinine, gender, TG, diabetes duration, albumin, BMI, smoking, and FPG. To evaluate potential nonlinear associations between HDL-C and CAS, we initially conducted restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses. The RCS models incorporated four knots positioned at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the observed HDL-C distribution. If a nonlinear relationship was identified, piecewise regression analysis based on the logistic regression models was conducted to determine the inflection point. Additionally, total cholesterol and ALT were excluded from the multivariable logistic regression models because of their collinearity with other variables (Table S1) [27].

Both obesity and hypertension are well-established independent risk factors for CAS with substantial pathophysiological effects on vascular structure and function [27, 28]. This investigation applied sensitivity analyses by excluding patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 and hypertension to ensure the robustness of the effect of HDL-C on CAS risk. The analysis in the BMI < 25 kg/m2 subgroup was designed to minimize confounding by obesity-related metabolic disturbances (including adipokine dysregulation, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation) and to determine whether the observed HDL-C–CAS association persists in individuals with lower cardiometabolic risk profiles. Similarly, the analysis in the non-hypertensive subgroup aimed to reduce the influence of blood pressure-related hemodynamic stress and vascular remodeling, which could potentially mask or modify lipid–atherosclerosis associations. This allowed us to better evaluate whether the relationship between HDL-C and CAS is independent of the effects of elevated blood pressure.

Subgroup analyses were applied to evaluate the effect of HDL-C on CAS risk across prespecified and exploratory subgroups, including age, BMI, HbA1c, hypertension, smoking, sex, drinking, and diabetes duration. Interaction terms were tested to identify potential effect modifications. All findings were reported in adherence to the STROBE guidelines. Statistical significance was established at a two-tailed P value of < 0.05.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort, comprising 390 patients with type 2 diabetes. The mean age was 51.17 ± 11.97 years, and the average HDL-C level was 1.08 ± 0.32 mmol/L. Of the participants, 259 (66.41%) were diagnosed with CAS. The cohort was stratified into three groups based on HDL-C tertiles. Compared to those in the lowest tertile (T1), individuals in the middle and highest HDL-C groups (T2 and T3) were more frequently female, older, and had a longer history of diabetes, lower body mass index, elevated HDL-C and total cholesterol concentrations, and lower rates of smoking. Conversely, participants in the lower HDL-C tertiles (T1 and T2) exhibited increased levels of creatinine, triglycerides, and uric acid compared to those in the highest tertile (T3).

As shown in Table 2, the overall cumulative incidence of carotid atherosclerosis (CAS) in the cohort was 66.41% (95% CI 61.70–71.12%). The effect of HDL-C on CAS

Multivariate logistic regression analyses evaluated the effect of HDL-C on incident CAS (Table 3). In unadjusted analyses, higher HDL-C was associated with a significantly lower risk of CAS (Model 1, OR: 0.50, 95% CI 0.26–0.96, P = 0.036). This correlation remained robust after adjustment for age, lymphocyte, HbA1c, drinking, hemoglobin, AST, neutrophils, LDL-C, hypertension, UA, creatinine, gender, TG, diabetes duration, albumin, BMI, smoking, and FPG (Model 3, adjusted OR: 0.27, 95% CI 0.09–0.80, P = 0.0187). When analyzing HDL-C by tertiles, participants in the highest tertile (T3) consistently exhibited reduced odds of CAS compared to those in T1 (Model 3, adjusted OR: 0.36; 95% CI 0.16–0.82, P = 0.0154).

Similarly, excluding those with hypertension (Model 5), the association persisted (adjusted OR: 0.18, 95% CI 0.04–0.85, P = 0.0307).

Nonlinear correlation

The RCS analysis identified a nonlinear correlation between HDL-C and CAS (Fig. 1 and Table 5), with an inflection point at 1.62 mmol/L. Below this threshold, higher HDL-C was markedly protective (adjusted OR: 0.10; 95% CI 0.03–0.42; P = 0.0014); whereas above 1.62 mmol/L, the effect of HDL-C on CAS was not statistically significant (adjusted OR: 5.34; 95% CI 0.25–114.45; P = 0.2844).

Discussion

In this retrospective study involving a cohort of patients with type 2 diabetes, we identified a negative effect of HDL-C on CAS risk. There is an L-shaped correlation between HDL-C and CAS. This inverse association was consistently observed across sensitivity analyses and prespecified subgroups. These findings provide new insights into the potential protective effect of HDL-C on vascular health among individuals with type 2 diabetes.

Atherosclerosis is a chronic, progressive inflammatory disorder distinguished by the deposition of lipids and ongoing inflammation within the arterial wall [29]. Several factors can accelerate its progression, including suboptimal glycemic control and reduced HDL-C concentrations [30, 31]. In individuals with diabetes, increased arterial stiffness and diminished vascular compliance further aggravate carotid atherosclerotic changes [32]. Notably, atherosclerosis underlies more than 80% of deaths and serious disabilities in this population, representing the principal driver of macrovascular complications [33]. HDL-C has been widely regarded as a protective lipid fraction, with substantial epidemiological evidence indicating that diminished levels correspond to an elevated risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [30, 34]. For example, a meta-analysis involving over 200,000 subjects demonstrated that lower HDL-C was linked to a higher risk of CAS [35]. Even in patients receiving intensive statin therapy to reduce LDL-C, HDL-C remains a significant predictor of CVD [36]. However, contemporary research has begun to challenge the presumed causative relationship between higher HDL-C and reduced cardiovascular risk. Findings from a cross-sectional Japanese study involving 657 individuals indicate that, after adjusting for confounders, HDL-C subfractions do not correlate with carotid atherosclerosis [37]. Similarly, Mendelian randomization involving over 20,000 myocardial infarction cases and approximately 95,000 controls reported no risk reduction for myocardial infarction with genetically higher HDL-C levels [11]. In addition, some investigations suggest that exceedingly high HDL-C concentrations neither enhance vascular protection nor decrease adverse outcomes and may, paradoxically, be linked with increased all-cause mortality. A meta-analysis of 17 cohorts encompassing nearly 20 million participants revealed that excessive HDL-C is associated with greater risks of stroke, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality [18]. Furthermore, a large prospective study from Copenhagen (n = 116,508) identified a U-shaped correlation between HDL-C and all-cause mortality for both genders, with heightened risk at both low and high HDL-C extremes [38]. Another longitudinal analysis of over 400,000 hypertensive and non-hypertensive individuals found that increased mortality risk with very high HDL-C was restricted to those with hypertension [39]. Our results indicate a significant inverse relationship between HDL cholesterol and CAS. However, the data also suggest that in patients with type 2 diabetes, maintaining HDL-C levels above a certain threshold may not confer additional vascular protective benefits.

Notably, our analysis identified an L-shaped correlation between HDL-C and CAS. For HDL-C concentrations below 1.62 mmol/L, each 1 mmol/L increase in HDL-C was linked to an approximately 90% reduction in CAS risk. However, elevations in HDL-C beyond 1.62 mmol/L did not confer additional benefit. These findings suggest that achieving and maintaining HDL-C levels near 1.62 mmol/L may help reduce the incidence of cardiovascular disease in patients with type 2 diabetes.

Current clinical guidelines—including those from the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA), the European Society of Cardiology/European Atherosclerosis Society (ESC/EAS), and the American Diabetes Association (ADA)—do not advocate for HDL-C elevation to specific numeric targets [40–42]. Instead, these guidelines emphasize the reduction of LDL-C, non-HDL-C, and apolipoprotein B (apoB) as primary lipid-modifying strategies [40–42]. While low HDL-C levels are acknowledged as a risk factor, there remains an absence of a universally accepted threshold above which additional vascular benefits are anticipated.

Previous large-scale studies within the general population have indicated nonlinear relationships between HDL-C and cardiovascular outcomes—often manifested as L-shaped or U-shaped curves—characterized by diminishing returns at elevated HDL-C concentrations [43–45]. However, few studies have provided definitive quantitative cutoffs, particularly within diabetic cohorts and concerning subclinical outcomes. Earlier reported inflection points typically span 70–80 mg/dL (approximately 1.8–2.1 mmol/L) [43–45]. This study advances existing knowledge by specifically analyzing the HDL-C and subclinical atherosclerosis relationship in individuals with type 2 diabetes. This high-risk subgroup is distinguished by HDL dysfunction, glycation, and a pro-inflammatory environment, factors that may decouple HDL-C concentration from its functional capacity. Thus, extrapolations from general population data may not be applicable in this context. Moreover, our methodology employed carotid ultrasonography to assess subclinical atherosclerosis by measuring carotid intima–media thickness and plaque presence, providing an early mechanistic insight into vascular burden that is highly predictive of future clinical events. This approach facilitated the detection of risk gradients that may precede overt clinical manifestations. Our data-driven analysis identified a specific HDL-C inflection point at 1.62 mmol/L (63 mg/dL) concerning subclinical atherosclerosis in patients with type 2 diabetes. This quantitative insight refines risk stratification for this population, a notable advancement over previous studies that have primarily reported nonlinear associations without specifying thresholds, especially in diabetic cohorts. Additionally, our findings imply that the “optimal zone” for HDL-C associated with reduced subclinical atherosclerosis may be lower in patients with type 2 diabetes compared to the general population. Importantly, HDL-C levels exceeding 1.62 mmol/L do not appear to confer further vascular advantages within this group. These observations provide a theoretical foundation for the early and tailored assessment of cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk and intervention strategies in a high-risk population. For clinicians, this threshold offers a more precise tool for risk assessment, presents opportunities for personalized intervention strategies, and enhances the early detection of atherosclerosis in patients with type 2 diabetes. Therefore, we advocate for routine HDL-C screening, particularly among patients approaching or below the 1.62 mmol/L threshold, recommend comprehensive cardiovascular risk evaluations, and suggest considering more proactive lipid management for patients within this at-risk category.

The mechanistic correlation between HDL-C and atherosclerosis is rooted in the diverse atheroprotective functions of HDL particles. HDL plays a critical anti-inflammatory role, mitigating endothelial dysfunction—an early and central event in atherogenesis [46, 47]. In addition, HDL protects the vascular endothelium and suppresses oxidative stress, thereby reducing the oxidative modification of low-density lipoprotein (LDL), a process pivotal for foam cell formation and plaque progression [46, 47]. Reverse cholesterol transport (RCT) represents a fundamental anti-atherogenic mechanism of HDL [46–49]. Through RCT, HDL mediates the efflux of cholesterol from macrophage-derived foam cells within the arterial intima, transporting it to the liver for excretion [46–49]. This not only prevents foam cell accumulation but also promotes plaque stabilization. Importantly, it is now recognized that HDL functionality—including cholesterol efflux capacity—rather than HDL-C concentration alone, serves as a more accurate indicator of its atheroprotective potential [45]. Elevated HDL-C concentrations do not necessarily confer greater atheroprotection. Additionally, type 2 diabetes is associated with various modifications in the structure and function of HDL particles. For instance, increased glycation and glycoxidation of HDL may impair its reverse cholesterol transport capabilities and diminish its anti-atherogenic potential [50]. The oxidative environment associated with the acute phase response in diabetes contributes to the transformation of HDL particles from anti-inflammatory to pro-inflammatory states [51–53]. Moreover, altered lipid exchange dynamics among lipoprotein particles, along with lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption, can influence HDL-C levels without necessarily enhancing its functional properties [54, 55].

Our study has several notable strengths. First, the HDL-C was analyzed as both continuous and categorical data, enabling a thorough assessment of its impact on CAS. Second, sophisticated statistical adjustments were implemented to attenuate the influence of potential confounding data. Third, this study established a curvilinear correlation, identifying the inflection point with precision.

Several limitations of this study merit consideration. Firstly, the cross-sectional design constrains the ability to definitively infer a causal relationship between HDL-C and CAS. Future research efforts will aim to extend the recruitment period to over 15 months to effectively capture seasonal variations and temporal trends, which may influence the relationship between HDL-C and atherosclerosis. Additionally, we will implement serial HDL-C measurements over time, rather than relying solely on single measurements, to account for biological variability, thereby providing a more precise assessment of long-term exposure effects. Secondly, the publicly available dataset utilized in this study fails to account for several critical confounders, including medication usage—particularly statins and other lipid-lowering agents—physical activity levels, and dietary habits. These factors can significantly alter HDL-C concentration and function, potentially exerting pleiotropic vascular effects that influence carotid atherosclerosis independently of HDL-C levels. Therefore, the impact of unmeasured confounders on the HDL-C and CAS relationship cannot be disregarded. To address this, we propose to conduct a prospective, multicenter investigation to gather comprehensive data on medication (including drug class, dosage, treatment duration, and adherence), physical activity, and dietary patterns. This will enable the integration of repeated HDL-C measurements alongside HDL-C functional indices to rigorously assess their relationship with carotid atherosclerosis. Thirdly, the nonlinear relationship documented between HDL-C and CAS (with a threshold of 1.62 mmol/L) was derived from a single-center, cross-sectional study that lacks external validation. We intend to further investigate this nonlinear relationship and the specified threshold effect by incorporating external datasets and conducting validation studies to reinforce our findings. Fourthly, this study's reliance on secondary analysis of publicly available data derived from hospitalized patients introduces potential selection bias due to the lack of specific hospitalization reasons. Consequently, caution must be exercised in generalizing our findings to the overall population of individuals with type 2 diabetes. We plan to adopt a community-based sampling strategy to secure a more representative sample in future studies, thereby enabling a deeper exploration of the HDL-C and CAS relationship. Fifthly, while the overall cohort exhibited a notable L-shaped association with an inflection point at 1.62 mmol/L, subgroup analyses by sex and age did not demonstrate statistically significant inflection points, likely due to the small sample sizes involved. Future research efforts with larger prospective samples will provide the necessary statistical power to conduct subgroup analyses and determine whether the HDL-C threshold for carotid atherosclerosis varies across sex, age, and other clinically relevant characteristics. Sixthly, in the subgroup analysis of individuals with HDL-C levels exceeding 1.62 mmol/L, the wide confidence intervals restricted our ability to draw robust conclusions within this range. Therefore, further studies with larger sample sizes and prospective designs are required to validate our findings and more precisely characterize the association between HDL-C and carotid atherosclerosis. Seventhly, our subgroup analyses revealed significant differences in the OR between drinkers and nondrinkers, recorded at 0.14 and 3.35, respectively. However, these differences were accompanied by nonsignificant p-values for the interaction effect, suggesting potential issues with insufficient statistical power arising from relatively small sample sizes in these subgroups. Accordingly, caution should be exercised when interpreting these results, as the observed OR may not be robust or stable. Eighthly, the original dataset derived from a study of a Chinese population lacks genetic information, which is critical given that HDL-C levels and lipid metabolism mechanisms may vary significantly across different ethnic groups and genetic backgrounds. Therefore, our study may not fully encapsulate the impact of ethnic and genetic factors on the HDL-C and CAS relationship. Future research will expand to include diverse ethnic groups and collect genetic information to more comprehensively explore this association. Ninthly, the data employed in this study originated from secondary sources, which may introduce potential inaccuracies or inconsistencies in the recorded information. Such information bias could adversely affect the validity of the observed associations.

Conclusion

Our study revealed an L-shaped correlation between HDL-C and CAS, wherein higher HDL-C concentrations were linked to reduced CAS risk only when HDL-C levels were below 1.62 mmol/L; beyond this threshold, no significant association was observed. Our results offer new perspectives on the lack of significant benefit observed in interventions aimed at increasing HDL-C levels in previous clinical trials and reinvigorate the potential of HDL-C as a therapeutic target for CAS.
