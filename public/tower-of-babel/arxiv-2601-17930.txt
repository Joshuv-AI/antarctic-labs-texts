A Rigorous and Self-Contained Proof of the Grover–Rudolph State Preparation Algorithm
Antonio Falcó, Daniela Falcó–Pomares, Hermann G. Matthies

Abstract
Preparing quantum states whose amplitudes encode classical probability distributions is a fundamental primitive in quantum algorithms based on amplitude encoding and amplitude estimation. Given a probability distribution { p k } k = 0 2 n − 1 \{p_{k}\}_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1} , the Grover–Rudolph procedure constructs an n n -qubit state | ψ ⟩ = ∑ k = 0 2 n − 1 p k ​ | k ⟩ \ket{\psi}=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\sqrt{p_{k}}\ket{k} by recursively applying families of controlled one-qubit rotations determined by a dyadic refinement of the target distribution. Despite its widespread use, the algorithm is often presented with informal correctness arguments and implicit conventions on the underlying dyadic tree. In this work we give a rigorous and self-contained analysis of the Grover–Rudolph construction: we formalize the dyadic probability tree, define the associated angle map via conditional masses, derive the resulting trigonometric factorizations, and prove by induction that the circuit prepares exactly the desired measurement law in the computational basis. As a complementary circuit-theoretic contribution, we show that each Grover–Rudolph stage is a uniformly controlled R y \mathrm{R}_{y} rotation on an active register and provide an explicit ancilla-free transpilation into the gate dictionary { R y ( ⋅ ) , X , CNOT ( ⋅ → ⋅ ) } \{\mathrm{R}_{y}(\cdot),X,\mathrm{CNOT}(\cdot\to\cdot)\} using Gray-code ladders and a Walsh–Hadamard angle transform.



Keywords:
Quantum state preparation; amplitude encoding; Grover–Rudolph algorithm; dyadic partitions;
uniformly controlled rotations; Gray code; ancilla-free transpilation; controlled rotations

1
Introduction

Preparing quantum states whose amplitudes encode classical data is a basic primitive in quantum
algorithm design. In particular,
amplitude encoding
of probability distributions underlies
quantum Monte Carlo–type routines and amplitude-estimation methods, where expectation values or
integrals are converted into success probabilities and then estimated quadratically faster than
classically in idealized settings (see, e.g., the amplitude-estimation framework of
Brassard–Høyer–Mosca–Tapp
[
1
]
).

A particularly influential and widely reused state-preparation procedure is due to Grover and
Rudolph
[
2
]
. Given a probability distribution over
2^{n}
outcomes (or, more generally, a nonnegative function on

[0,1]
sampled on a dyadic grid), their construction prepares an
n
-qubit state

\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}\sqrt{p_{z}}\,\ket{z}
by recursively refining a dyadic partition and applying, at each
level, a family of controlled one-qubit rotations whose angles are defined from conditional masses on a
binary tree. The algorithm is conceptually elegant and has become a standard reference point for
state preparation from classical distributions.

Despite its popularity, the Grover–Rudolph procedure is often presented at a high level, with correctness
justified informally or with implicit assumptions about the dyadic refinement and the controlled-gate
semantics. The first objective of this paper is therefore
mathematical
: we provide a rigorous and
self-contained treatment of the dyadic probability tree, the induced angle map, and the resulting
amplitude identities, culminating in a fully explicit proof that the circuit prepares the desired target
state. In particular, we isolate the key telescoping/trigonometric factorizations and prove them by
transparent induction, so that the overall correctness argument can be read independently of external
compilation folklore.

Grover and Rudolph’s original note
[
2
]
introduced the recursive dyadic construction that we formalize here. Beyond this specific algorithm,
state-preparation and multiplexing problems have been extensively studied in the circuit-synthesis
literature. A prominent line of work concerns
uniformly controlled one-qubit gates
(also called

multiplexed
or
uniformly controlled rotations
), for which Gray-code techniques yield
systematic decompositions into elementary gates without ancillas. In particular, the Gray-code ladder
idea appears as a standard tool for implementing uniformly controlled rotations and related multiqubit
structures (see, e.g., the uniformly controlled gate framework in
Bergholm
et al.

[
4
]
and the companion discussion of uniformly
controlled one-qubit gates in
[
3
]
).

The second objective of the present paper is to make this connection
fully explicit
in the
Grover–Rudolph setting: we show that each Grover–Rudolph stage is naturally a uniformly controlled

\mathrm{R}_{y}
gate acting on an
active register
, and we provide an explicit ancilla-free transpilation
into the elementary dictionary
\{\mathrm{R}_{y}(\cdot),X,\mathrm{CNOT}(\cdot\to\cdot)\}
via a Gray-code ladder. While such
Gray-code decompositions are well known in compilation contexts, including in modern toolchains, our
goal here is to integrate them into a
single
self-contained mathematical narrative: precise
definitions, correct-by-construction angle transforms (Walsh–Hadamard), and full proofs of correctness
at the operator level.

The main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

A rigorous dyadic-tree formalization of the Grover–Rudolph angle construction, including explicit
refinement identities and a clean inductive correctness proof of the prepared amplitudes.

•

A stage-wise “active-register” formulation of the circuit, clarifying exactly which
j
-qubit
unitary acts at each recursion depth and how it embeds into
\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
.

•

An explicit ancilla-free transpilation of each stage into
\{\mathrm{R}_{y}(\cdot),X,\mathrm{CNOT}\}
using a Gray-code
ladder and a Walsh–Hadamard angle transform, with full correctness proofs and implementable
pseudo-code.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
fixes notation and collects the required preliminaries, including the
dyadic partition formalism for probability masses, the embedding conventions for one-qubit gates, and
the selective (pattern-controlled) gate construction used throughout; it also states the Grover–Rudolph
state-preparation circuit and the main theorem.
Section
3
contains the rigorous and self-contained correctness proof, centered on the
trigonometric factorization of dyadic masses and the inductive amplitude formula that propagates across
Grover–Rudolph stages.
In Section
4
we provide a worked example together with an idealized (noise-free) circuit-level
verification of the construction.
Finally, Section
5
develops the active-register viewpoint and gives an explicit
ancilla-free transpilation of each Grover–Rudolph stage into the elementary gate dictionary

\{\mathrm{R}_{y}(\cdot),X,\mathrm{CNOT}(\cdot\to\cdot)\}
via Gray-code ladder decompositions, including implementable pseudo-code and
circuit diagrams.

2
Preliminary results and statement of the main theorem

Throughout the paper, we write
\mathbb{Z}_{2}:=\{0,1\}
and, for
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}:=\{0,1,\dots,2^{n}-1\},\qquad N:=2^{n}.

For a bitstring
\mathbf{z}=z_{1}\cdots z_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
we use the shorthand

|\mathbf{z}\rangle:=|z_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|z_{m}\rangle
for the corresponding
computational basis vector.

2.1
Binary representations and dyadic partitions

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
, let
b_{n}(k)=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
denote the
(length-
n
) binary representation of
k
, i.e.

k=b_{n}^{-1}(z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{n})=z_{1}2^{0}+z_{2}2^{1}+\cdots+z_{n}2^{n-1}.

For each level
1\leq\ell\leq n
and
0\leq k\leq 2^{\ell}
, define the dyadic grid points

z^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(k)}:=\frac{k}{2^{\ell}},\qquad\text{with the convention }z^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(2^{\ell})}:=1.

We then define the dyadic intervals

I^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(k)}:=\Big[z^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(k)},\,z^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(k+1)}\Big]=\Big[\frac{k}{2^{\ell}},\,\frac{k+1}{2^{\ell}}\Big],\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{\ell}}.

At the finest level
\ell=n
, we write

I_{k}:=I^{(n)}_{b_{n}(k)}=\Big[\frac{k}{2^{n}},\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\Big],\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}.

2.2
Quantum registers and one-qubit embeddings

We consider the
n
-qubit Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{n}:=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}},

endowed with the computational basis
\{\,|b_{n}(k)\rangle:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}\,\}
.
Given a one-qubit unitary
U\in\mathrm{U}(2)
and an index
1\leq j\leq n
, we define
the standard embedding (acting on the
j
-th qubit)

w_{j}^{(n)}(U):=\mathsf{I}_{2}^{\otimes(j-1)}\otimes U\otimes\mathsf{I}_{2}^{\otimes(n-j)}\in\mathrm{U}(2^{n}).

2.3
Selective and controlled one-qubit operations

Fix
1\leq\ell\leq n
and
U\in\mathrm{U}(2)
. For a pattern

\mathbf{z}=z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell-1}z_{\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}
,
we define the selective (rank-one supported) operator

\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{z}}(U):=|z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell-1}\rangle\langle z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell-1}|\otimes U\otimes|z_{\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}\rangle\langle z_{\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}|.

This operator acts as
w_{\ell}^{(n)}(U)
on basis states whose
(n-1)
remaining bits
match
\mathbf{z}
, and annihilates all other basis states. From
\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{z}}(U)

we build the corresponding controlled unitary (identity outside the selected branch)

\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{z}}(U):=\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{z}}(U)+\sum_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}\setminus\{\mathbf{z}\}}\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}(\mathsf{I}_{2})\;\in\;\mathrm{U}(2^{n}).

In particular,
\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{z}}(U)
applies
U
to qubit
\ell
if and only if the other
qubits match the control pattern
\mathbf{z}
, and otherwise acts as the identity.

2.4
Rotation primitives and amplitude encoding objective

We will use the real rotation gate
\mathsf{R}(\alpha)\in\mathrm{U}(2)
defined by

\mathsf{R}(\alpha):=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\
\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix},\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

Given a nonnegative density
\varrho:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
with
\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(x)\,dx=1
,
we define the target probabilities

p_{k}:=\int_{I_{k}}\varrho(x)\,dx,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}.

The state-preparation (amplitude-encoding) objective is to construct a circuit
U\in\mathrm{U}(2^{n})

such that

U|0\rangle^{\otimes n}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}}\sqrt{p_{k}}\,|b_{n}(k)\rangle,

up to a global phase.

2.5
Elementary identities for dyadic refinement

Fix
n\geq 1
and set
N:=2^{n}
.
For
m\geq 1
, denote by
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
the set of binary strings of length
m
.
For each
k\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{m}-1\}
we write

b_{m}(k)=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k=\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}\,2^{j-1}.

For
1\leq\ell\leq n
and
0\leq k\leq 2^{\ell}
, define the dyadic grid points

z^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(k)}:=\frac{k}{2^{\ell}},\qquad\text{with the convention }z^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(2^{\ell})}:=1,

and the associated dyadic intervals

I^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(k)}:=\Big[z^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(k)},\,z^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(k+1)}\Big]=\Big[\frac{k}{2^{\ell}},\,\frac{k+1}{2^{\ell}}\Big],\qquad k\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{\ell}-1\}.

Lemma 2.1

(Binary shift identities)
.

Let
1\leq\ell\leq n-1
and
k\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{\ell}-1\}
. Then

b_{\ell+1}(2k)=0\,b_{\ell}(k),\qquad b_{\ell+1}(2k+1)=1\,b_{\ell}(k),\qquad b_{\ell+1}(2k+2)=0\,b_{\ell}(k+1).

Proof.

Write
k=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}z_{j}2^{j-1}
, i.e.
b_{\ell}(k)=z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell}
. Then

2k=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}z_{j}2^{j}
, hence the binary expansion of
2k
in length
\ell+1

is
0z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell}=0\,b_{\ell}(k)
.
Similarly
2k+1=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}z_{j}2^{j}
yields
b_{\ell+1}(2k+1)=1\,b_{\ell}(k)
.
The identity for
2k+2
follows analogously.
∎

Corollary 2.2

(Dyadic refinement)
.

Let
1\leq\ell\leq n-1
and
k\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{\ell}-1\}
. Then

I^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(k)}=I^{(\ell+1)}_{\,b_{\ell+1}(2k)}\;\cup\;I^{(\ell+1)}_{\,b_{\ell+1}(2k+1)}=I^{(\ell+1)}_{\,0b_{\ell}(k)}\;\cup\;I^{(\ell+1)}_{\,1b_{\ell}(k)},

and the union is disjoint up to the common endpoint.
Consequently, for any integrable
\varrho
,

\int_{I^{(\ell)}_{b_{\ell}(k)}}\varrho(x)\,dx=\int_{I^{(\ell+1)}_{0b_{\ell}(k)}}\varrho(x)\,dx+\int_{I^{(\ell+1)}_{1b_{\ell}(k)}}\varrho(x)\,dx.

Lemma 2.3

(Suffix extraction via scaling)
.

Let
k\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{n}-1\}
with
b_{n}(k)=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{n}
.
For
1\leq s\leq n-1
we have

\Big\lfloor 2^{-s}k\Big\rfloor=\sum_{j=s+1}^{n}z_{j}\,2^{j-s-1},\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad b_{n-s}\!\Big(\lfloor 2^{-s}k\rfloor\Big)=z_{s+1}\cdots z_{n}.

Proof.

Using
k=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}2^{j-1}
,

2^{-s}k=\sum_{j=1}^{s}z_{j}2^{j-s-1}+\sum_{j=s+1}^{n}z_{j}2^{j-s-1}.

The first sum belongs to
[0,1)
, so taking the integer part yields the second sum,
which is exactly the claimed binary value.
∎

2.6
Measurement probabilities and statement of the main theorem

We briefly recall how probabilities are extracted from a quantum state, since this is the
criterion used to certify the correctness of the Grover–Rudolph construction.

A (mixed) quantum state is represented by a density matrix

\rho\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\qquad\rho\succeq 0,\qquad\tr(\rho)=1,

and pure states correspond to rank-one projectors
\rho=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
with
\|\psi\|=1
.
Given a unitary
U\in\mathrm{U}(N)
and an input state
\rho_{0}
, the output state is

\rho=U\rho_{0}U^{\star}.

For the computational-basis measurement, define the orthogonal projectors

\Pi_{k}:=\ket{b_n(k)}\bra{b_n(k)},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}},\qquad\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}}\Pi_{k}=I_{N}.

The associated outcome is a discrete random variable
\mathsf{A}
taking values in
\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
, and
the Born rule yields the probability law

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=k)=\tr(\rho\,\Pi_k),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}.

(2.1)

In particular, if
\rho=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
is pure then

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=k)=|\innerproduct{b_n(k)}{\psi}|^{2}.

Let
\varrho:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
be integrable with
\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(x)\,dx=1
and define

p_{k}:=\int_{I_{k}}\varrho(x)\,dx,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}.

Theorem 2.4

(Grover–Rudolph state preparation)
.

Let
n\geq 1
and set
N:=2^{n}
. Let
\varrho:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
satisfy
\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(x)\,dx=1

and define
\{p_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}}
by
p_{k}=\int_{I_{k}}\varrho(x)\,dx
.
Then there exists a quantum circuit
U\in\mathrm{U}(N)
, constructed as a product of
multi-controlled single-qubit rotations, such that for
\rho_{0}=\ket{0}^{\otimes n}\bra{0}^{\otimes n}

and
\rho:=U\rho_{0}U^{\star}
one has

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=k)=\tr(\rho\,\Pi_k)=p_{k},\qquad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}.

Moreover, the circuit can be chosen with
N-1
elementary controlled-rotation gates.

3
Proof of the Grover–Rudolph Theorem

Throughout this section we set
N=2^{n}
and we work in
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
with the
computational basis
\{\ket{b_n(k)}:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}\}
, initial state

\rho_{0}=\ket{b_n(0)}\bra{b_n(0)}=\ket{0}^{\otimes n}\bra{0}^{\otimes n}
, and the measurement
random variable
\mathsf{A}
associated with the projectors
\ket{b_n(k)}\bra{b_n(k)}
.
For a bit
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and an angle
x\in\mathbb{R}
we use

\mathsf{T}_{z}(x):=(\cos x)^{1-z}(\sin x)^{z}.

3.1
Dyadic integrals and trigonometric factorization

Let
\varrho:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
be a density, i.e.
\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(x)\,dx=1
.
For
m\geq 1
and a binary word
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
, let
k(w)\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{m}-1\}
be the unique
integer such that
b_{m}(k(w))=w
(equivalently, if
w=z_{1}\cdots z_{m}
then
k(w)=\sum_{r=1}^{m}z_{r}2^{r-1}
).
We associate to
w
the dyadic interval of length
2^{-m}

I_{w}:=\Big[\frac{k(w)}{2^{m}},\,\frac{k(w)+1}{2^{m}}\Big]\subset[0,1],

and define its probability mass by

p_{\mathbf{w}}:=\int_{I_{w}}\varrho(x)\,dx.

In particular, if
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
and
b_{n}(k)=w=z_{1}\cdots z_{n}
, then
k(w)=k
and thus

p_{z_{1}\cdots z_{n}}=p_{\mathbf{w}}=\int_{I_{w}}\varrho(x)\,dx=\int_{k/2^{n}}^{(k+1)/2^{n}}\varrho(x)\,dx.

By Corollary
2.2
, every dyadic interval indexed by a word

w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
with
m<n
splits into its two children
0w
and
1w
. Therefore, for the associated
probability masses we have the refinement identity

p_{\mathbf{w}}=\int_{I^{(m)}_{w}}\varrho(x)\,dx=\int_{I^{(m+1)}_{0w}}\varrho(x)\,dx+\int_{I^{(m+1)}_{1w}}\varrho(x)\,dx=p_{0\mathbf{w}}+p_{1\mathbf{w}}.

Angle assignment.

For each word
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
with
0\leq m\leq n-1
we define an angle
\theta_{\mathbf{w}}\in[0,\pi/2]
by

\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathbf{w}}:=\begin{cases}\dfrac{p_{0\mathbf{w}}}{p_{\mathbf{w}}},&p_{\mathbf{w}}>0,\\[8.99994pt]
0,&p_{\mathbf{w}}=0,\end{cases}\qquad\sin^{2}\theta_{\mathbf{w}}:=\begin{cases}\dfrac{p_{1\mathbf{w}}}{p_{\mathbf{w}}},&p_{\mathbf{w}}>0,\\[8.99994pt]
0,&p_{\mathbf{w}}=0,\end{cases}

so that
\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathbf{w}}+\sin^{2}\theta_{\mathbf{w}}=1
whenever
p_{\mathbf{w}}>0
.
(When
p_{\mathbf{w}}=0
all descendants have zero mass, and the choice is immaterial; the above convention is convenient
for bookkeeping.)
For the empty word
\emptyset
we write
\theta:=\theta_{\emptyset}
, where
\theta_{\emptyset}
is the
stage-
1
rotation angle determined by the top-level split of
[0,1]
into
[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]
and
[\tfrac{1}{2},1]
,
namely

\theta=\theta_{\emptyset}:=\arccos\sqrt{\frac{p_{0}}{p_{\emptyset}}}=\arccos\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1/2}\varrho(x)\,dx},

since
p_{\emptyset}=\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(x)\,dx=1.

Proposition 3.1

(Trigonometric factorization)
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
with
b_{n}(k)=z_{1}\cdots z_{n}
one has

p_{z_{1}\cdots z_{n}}=\mathsf{T}_{z_{1}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}})\,\mathsf{T}_{z_{2}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{3}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{n}})\,\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}^{2}(\theta).

(3.1)

Proof.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
and write
b_{n}(k)=z_{1}\cdots z_{n}
.
For
r\in\{1,\dots,n\}
let
w_{r}:=z_{r}z_{r+1}\cdots z_{n}
denote the length-
(n-r+1)
suffix,
and set
w_{n+1}:=\emptyset
. We prove by backward induction on
r
that

p_{w_{r}}=\mathsf{T}_{z_{r}}^{2}(\theta_{w_{r+1}})\,\mathsf{T}_{z_{r+1}}^{2}(\theta_{w_{r+2}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}^{2}(\theta_{w_{n}})\,\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}^{2}(\theta),

(3.2)

where
\theta=\theta_{\emptyset}
and, for any word
w
, the angle
\theta_{\mathbf{w}}
is defined by the convention
used in the manuscript (in particular, if
p_{\mathbf{w}}=0
we set
\theta_{\mathbf{w}}:=0
).

Base case (
r=n
).

We must show
p_{z_{n}}=\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}^{2}(\theta)
. This holds by the definition of
\theta=\theta_{\emptyset}
:

p_{0}=p_{\emptyset}\cos^{2}\theta=\cos^{2}\theta=\mathsf{T}_{0}^{2}(\theta),\qquad p_{1}=p_{\emptyset}\sin^{2}\theta=\sin^{2}\theta=\mathsf{T}_{1}^{2}(\theta),

since
p_{\emptyset}=\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(x)\,dx=1
.

Inductive step.

Assume (
3.2
) holds for
r+1
, and set
w:=w_{r+1}=z_{r+1}\cdots z_{n}
.
We prove (
3.2
) for
r
. There are two cases.

Case 1:
p_{\mathbf{w}}=0
.

By Corollary
2.2
(Dyadic refinement),
p_{0\mathbf{w}}+p_{1\mathbf{w}}=p_{\mathbf{w}}=0
, and since
all probabilities are nonnegative it follows that
p_{0\mathbf{w}}=p_{1\mathbf{w}}=0
. In particular,

p_{w_{r}}=p_{z_{r}w}=0
. On the other hand, our convention
\theta_{\mathbf{w}}=0
implies that

\mathsf{T}_{z_{r}}^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\in\{0,1\}
, and the right-hand side of (
3.2
) contains
the factor
\mathsf{T}_{z_{r}}^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})
multiplying the remaining terms. But the remaining product
equals
p_{\mathbf{w}}
by the induction hypothesis applied at level
r+1
(since its left-hand side is
p_{\mathbf{w}}
),
and therefore the whole right-hand side equals
\mathsf{T}_{z_{r}}^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\,p_{\mathbf{w}}=0
.
Hence (
3.2
) holds.

Case 2:
p_{\mathbf{w}}>0
.

By definition of
\theta_{\mathbf{w}}
we have

p_{0\mathbf{w}}=p_{\mathbf{w}}\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathbf{w}}=p_{\mathbf{w}}\,\mathsf{T}_{0}^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}}),\qquad p_{1\mathbf{w}}=p_{\mathbf{w}}\sin^{2}\theta_{\mathbf{w}}=p_{\mathbf{w}}\,\mathsf{T}_{1}^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}}).

Therefore, for
z_{r}\in\{0,1\}
,

p_{w_{r}}=p_{z_{r}w}=p_{\mathbf{w}}\,\mathsf{T}_{z_{r}}^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}}).

Applying the induction hypothesis at level
r+1
to
p_{\mathbf{w}}=p_{w_{r+1}}
yields

p_{w_{r}}=\mathsf{T}_{z_{r}}^{2}(\theta_{w_{r+1}})\,\mathsf{T}_{z_{r+1}}^{2}(\theta_{w_{r+2}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}^{2}(\theta_{w_{n}})\,\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}^{2}(\theta),

which is exactly (
3.2
).

This completes the induction. Taking
r=1
proves (
3.1
).
∎

3.2
Controlled rotations and state preparation

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
let

\mathsf{R}(\alpha):=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\
\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{U}(2).

For
j\in\{2,\dots,n\}
and a binary word
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}
, we denote by

\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{w}}^{(1)}\bigl(\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\bigr)\in\mathrm{U}(2^{j})

the multi-controlled one-qubit rotation acting on the
first
qubit, conditioned on the last

(j-1)
qubits being equal to
w
, and acting as the identity on all other basis states. Equivalently,

\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{w}}^{(1)}(\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}}))
applies
\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})
to qubit
1
if and only if the control register
(qubits
2,\dots,j
in the
j
-qubit active subregister, or, in the full
n
-qubit register, the last

(j-1)
wires) matches the pattern
w
.

Define the stage unitaries by

\mathsf{U}_{1}:=\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-1}}\otimes\mathsf{R}(\theta),\qquad\mathsf{U}_{j}:=\prod_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}}\Bigl(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-j}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{w}}^{(1)}\bigl(\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\bigr)\Bigr),\quad 2\leq j\leq n.

In particular, only the gates
\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{w}}^{(1)}(\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}}))
(with a fixed target qubit) are required for
the inductive construction, while
\mathsf{U}_{1}
is an uncontrolled rotation on the last qubit.

Finally, we define the full circuit

\mathsf{U}:=\mathsf{U}_{n}\mathsf{U}_{n-1}\cdots\mathsf{U}_{1}\in\mathrm{U}(2^{n}).

By construction,
\mathsf{U}_{j}
is a product of
2^{j-1}
elementary controlled-rotation gates, hence the
total number of elementary gates is

\sum_{j=1}^{n}2^{j-1}=2^{n}-1=N-1,

i.e.
\mathsf{U}
has circuit length
N-1
.

Lemma 3.2

(Inductive amplitude form)
.

Let
\ket{\psi_j}:=\mathsf{U}_{j}\mathsf{U}_{j-1}\cdots\mathsf{U}_{1}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
.
Then, for every
1\leq j\leq n
,

\ket{\psi_j}=\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-j)}\otimes\sum_{w=z_{n-j+1}\cdots z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j}}\Bigl(\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-j+1}}(\theta_{z_{n-j+2}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}(\theta_{z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\Bigr)\,\ket{w}.

In particular,

\ket{\psi_n}=\sum_{z_{1}\cdots z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}\Bigl(\mathsf{T}_{z_{1}}(\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\Bigr)\ket{z_1\cdots z_n}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
j
.

Base case (
j=1
).

By definition,
\mathsf{U}_{1}=\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-1}}\otimes\mathsf{R}(\theta)
acts only on the last qubit. Hence

\ket{\psi_1}=\mathsf{U}_{1}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}=\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\otimes\mathsf{R}(\theta)\ket{0}.

Since
\mathsf{R}(\theta)\ket{0}=\cos\theta\,\ket{0}+\sin\theta\,\ket{1}
, we obtain

\ket{\psi_1}=\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\otimes\bigl(\cos\theta\,\ket{0}+\sin\theta\,\ket{1}\bigr)=\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\otimes\sum_{z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\ket{z_n},

which is the claimed formula for
j=1
.

Inductive step.

Assume the statement holds for some
j-1
with
2\leq j\leq n
, i.e.

\ket{\psi_{j-1}}=\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-(j-1))}\otimes\sum_{w=z_{n-j+2}\cdots z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}}A_{w}\,\ket{w},

(3.3)

where, for each
w=z_{n-j+2}\cdots z_{n}
, the amplitude
A_{w}
is given by the product

A_{w}=\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-j+2}}(\theta_{z_{n-j+3}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}(\theta_{z_{n}})\,\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta).

(3.4)

At this stage, the basis vectors
\ket{w}
label the computational states of the last
(j-1)
qubits.
Their identification with suffixes of the global binary index follows from the fact that truncating
the first
(j-1)
bits of an integer corresponds to extracting its suffix.
More precisely, if
k\in\{0,\dots,2^{n}-1\}
has binary expansion
b_{n}(k)=z_{1}\cdots z_{n}
, then

b_{n-(j-1)}\!\bigl(\lfloor 2^{-(j-1)}k\rfloor\bigr)=z_{n-j+2}\cdots z_{n},

as shown in Lemma
2.3
.

It will be convenient to isolate the
(n-j+1)
-th qubit (the next qubit to be rotated at stage
j
).
Write the tensor product in (
3.3
) as

\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-(j-1))}=\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-j)}\otimes\ket{0},

so that

\ket{\psi_{j-1}}=\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-j)}\otimes\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}}A_{w}\,\bigl(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{w}\bigr),

(3.5)

where the factor
\ket{0}
corresponds to qubit
(n-j+1)
and
\ket{w}
to the last
(j-1)
qubits.

Action of
\mathsf{U}_{j}
on basis branches.

By definition,

\mathsf{U}_{j}=\prod_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}}\Bigl(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-j}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{w}}^{(1)}\bigl(\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\bigr)\Bigr).

Inside the active
j
-qubit register (qubits
(n-j+1),\dots,n
), the gate

\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{w}}^{(1)}(\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}}))\in\mathrm{U}(2^{j})
acts as follows:
it applies
\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})
to the
first
qubit of that register (i.e. qubit
(n-j+1)
)
if and only if the remaining
(j-1)
qubits are in the computational basis state
\ket{w}
, and
acts as the identity on all other computational basis states.
Consequently, for each
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}
,

\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{w}}^{(1)}\bigl(\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\bigr)\,\bigl(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{w}\bigr)=\bigl(\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\ket{0}\bigr)\otimes\ket{w},

(3.6)

and for
w^{\prime}\neq w
,

\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{w}}^{(1)}\bigl(\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\bigr)\,\bigl(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{w'}\bigr)=\ket{0}\otimes\ket{w'}.

Since the projectors onto distinct control branches are orthogonal, these controlled rotations act on
disjoint subspaces and therefore commute; hence the product over
w
applies the correct rotation in each
branch independently.

Splitting of amplitudes.

Applying
\mathsf{U}_{j}
to (
3.5
) and using (
3.6
) gives

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_j}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{U}_{j}\ket{\psi_{j-1}}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-j)}\otimes\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}}A_{w}\,\Bigl(\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\ket{0}\Bigr)\otimes\ket{w}.

Finally,
\mathsf{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\ket{0}=\cos\theta_{\mathbf{w}}\,\ket{0}+\sin\theta_{\mathbf{w}}\,\ket{1}=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\mathsf{T}_{z}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\ket{z}
, so

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_j}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-j)}\otimes\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}}A_{w}\,\sum_{z_{n-j+1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-j+1}}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\,\ket{z_{n-j+1}}\otimes\ket{w}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-j)}\otimes\sum_{z_{n-j+1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\;\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}}\Bigl(A_{w}\,\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-j+1}}(\theta_{\mathbf{w}})\Bigr)\,\ket{z_{n-j+1}w}.

Renaming the concatenated word
z_{n-j+1}w
as

w^{\prime}=z_{n-j+1}z_{n-j+2}\cdots z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j},

and substituting
A_{w}
from (
3.4
), we obtain exactly the claimed product form

\ket{\psi_j}=\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-j)}\otimes\sum_{w^{\prime}=z_{n-j+1}\cdots z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j}}\Bigl(\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-j+1}}(\theta_{z_{n-j+2}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}(\theta_{z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\Bigr)\,\ket{w'}.

This completes the induction. The final statement for
\ket{\psi_n}
is the special case
j=n
.
∎

3.3
Completion of the proof of Theorem
2.4

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Let
\rho:=\mathsf{U}\rho_{0}\mathsf{U}^{\star}=\ket{\psi_n}\bra{\psi_n}
, with
\ket{\psi_n}
as in
Lemma
3.2
. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
with
b_{n}(k)=z_{1}\cdots z_{n}
we compute

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=k)=\tr\bigl(\rho\,\ket{b_n(k)}\bra{b_n(k)}\bigr)=|\innerproduct{b_n(k)}{\psi_n}|^{2}=\mathsf{T}_{z_{1}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}^{2}(\theta).

By Proposition
3.1
, the right-hand side equals

p_{z_{1}\cdots z_{n}}=\int_{k/2^{n}}^{(k+1)/2^{n}}\varrho(x)\,dx
, hence

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=k)=\int_{k/2^{n}}^{(k+1)/2^{n}}\varrho(x)\,dx,\qquad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}.

Finally, the circuit length of
\mathsf{U}
is
N-1
by the gate count above. This proves the theorem.
∎

Discussion.

This example illustrates concretely how the Grover–Rudolph recursion translates dyadic integrals of

\varrho
into a hierarchy of controlled rotation angles.
Comparing the theoretical probabilities
\{p_{k}\}
with the empirical frequencies produced by the simulator,
we observe an overall good agreement. In particular, the maximum deviation occurs on the interval

[3/8,\,1/2]
, where the estimated probability is
0.240
whereas the exact value is
0.21875
,
yielding an absolute error of approximately
0.02125
. The minimum deviation is observed on

[1/2,\,5/8]
, with an absolute error of about
0.00275
.
These discrepancies are consistent with finite-shot sampling fluctuations and with the finite-precision
representation of rotation angles in the simulation.

A natural next step is to implement the same circuit on a real quantum device, where hardware noise,
gate calibration errors, and decoherence effects would provide a more realistic assessment of the method’s
practical performance. Nevertheless, even in this idealized setting, the example provides a transparent
verification of the construction and supports the theoretical results established in the previous sections.

5
Ancilla-free transpilation of the Grover–Rudolph circuit in a
\{\mathrm{R}_{y}(\cdot),X,\mathrm{CNOT}(\cdot\to\cdot)\}
gate dictionary

In practical implementations (e.g. on the Triangulum NMR device), the circuit
\mathsf{U}
in
Theorem
2.4
must be expressed over a fixed native gate dictionary.
Throughout this section we use the elementary gate set

\mathcal{G}:=\{\mathrm{R}_{y}(\cdot),\,X,\,\mathrm{CNOT}(\cdot\to\cdot)\},

where
\mathrm{CNOT}(c\to t)
denotes the standard two-qubit controlled-NOT (i.e. a controlled-
X
on the target
t

conditioned on the control
c
being in state
\ket{1}
).

5.1
Active-register viewpoint and target gates

At Grover–Rudolph stage
j\in\{2,\dots,n\}
the construction acts nontrivially only on the last
j
qubits of the

n
-qubit register. We therefore introduce the
active
j
-qubit register

(q_{1},\dots,q_{j}):=(n-j+1,\;n-j+2,\;\dots,\;n),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad q_{r}:=n-j+r,\ \ r=1,\dots,j,

so that any
G\in\mathrm{U}(2^{j})
acting on the active register is embedded into
\mathcal{H}_{n}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}

as
\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-j}}\otimes G\in\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
.

For
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}
we write

\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{w}^{(1)}\bigl(\mathsf{R}(\theta_{w})\bigr)\in\mathrm{U}(2^{j})

for the pattern-controlled one-qubit rotation acting on the
target

q_{1}
conditioned on the control register

(q_{2},\dots,q_{j})
being equal to
w
, and acting as the identity on all other computational basis states.

Dictionary gates.

For a qubit
q
we denote by
X_{q}
the Pauli-
X
gate on
q
, and by
\mathrm{R}_{y}(\alpha)_{q}
the
y
-rotation

\mathrm{R}_{y}(\alpha)=\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{i\alpha}{2}Y\Bigr)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha/2)&-\sin(\alpha/2)\\
\sin(\alpha/2)&\cos(\alpha/2)\end{pmatrix}.

For qubits
c
(control) and
t
(target), the standard
\mathrm{CNOT}(c\to t)
is defined by

\mathrm{CNOT}(c\to t)\ket{x}_{c}\ket{y}_{t}=\ket{x}_{c}\ket{y\oplus x}_{t},\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{2},

and acts as the identity on all other qubits.

CNOT in terms of fully pattern-controlled
X
.

If one wishes to express
\mathrm{CNOT}
using the manuscript’s fully pattern-controlled primitives

\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{v}(X)\in\mathrm{U}(2^{j})
(with
v\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}
specifying the entire control word on
(q_{2},\dots,q_{j})
),
then for
r\in\{1,\dots,j-1\}
one has

\mathrm{CNOT}(q_{r+1}\to q_{1})=\prod_{u\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-2}}\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{\,u_{1}\cdots u_{r-1}\,1\,u_{r}\cdots u_{j-2}}(X).

(5.1)

The factors commute because they act on pairwise orthogonal control subspaces, and exactly one factor is active on any
branch with the
r
-th control bit equal to
1
.

5.2
Stage-as-
\UCRY
compilation and Gray-code ladder

Rotation primitive.

Recall that

\mathsf{R}(\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\
\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix}=\mathrm{R}_{y}(2\alpha),

so at stage
j
it suffices to compile controlled
\mathrm{R}_{y}
rotations with angles
\phi_{w}:=2\theta_{w}
.

Uniformly controlled viewpoint.

Define the uniformly controlled
y
-rotation on the active register by

\UCRY(\bm{\phi}):=\prod_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}}\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{w}^{(1)}\!\bigl(\mathrm{R}_{y}(\phi_{w})\bigr)\ \in\ \mathrm{U}(2^{j}),\qquad\bm{\phi}=\{\phi_{w}\}_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}}.

With
\phi_{w}:=2\theta_{w}
, the active stage unitary is precisely

U_{j}^{\mathrm{(act)}}=\UCRY(\{2\theta_{w}\}_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{j-1}}),\qquad\text{and the full-$n$ embedding is }\ \mathsf{I}_{2^{n-j}}\otimes U_{j}^{\mathrm{(act)}}\in\mathrm{U}(2^{n}).

Gray-code conventions.

Let
(\mathbf{g}_{k})_{k=0}^{2^{m}-1}
be the standard (binary-reflected) Gray code on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
.
For
k\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{m}-1\}
define

\mathbf{g}_{k}:=b_{m}(k)\oplus b_{m}\!\left(\Big\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\Big\rfloor\right)\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m},\qquad\gamma(k):=b_{m}^{-1}(\mathbf{g}_{k})\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{m}-1\},

where
\oplus
denotes componentwise XOR on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
and
b_{m}^{-1}
is the inverse map
that sends a word
z_{1}\cdots z_{m}
to the integer
\sum_{r=1}^{m}z_{r}2^{r-1}
.
Then consecutive words differ in exactly one bit.
For
k\geq 1
set

d_{k}:=\gamma(k)\oplus\gamma(k-1)\in\{1,2,4,\dots,2^{m-1}\},

where
\oplus
now denotes bitwise XOR on integers; in particular
d_{k}
is a power of two.
Let
j(k)\in\{1,\dots,m\}
be the unique index such that
d_{k}=2^{\,j(k)-1}
.
Equivalently,
j(k)
is the unique bit position that flips from
\mathbf{g}_{k-1}
to
\mathbf{g}_{k}
.

(Gray-code ladder for a uniformly controlled

.

i.e.
\UCRY(\bm{\phi})
applies
\mathrm{R}_{y}(\phi_{w})
to
q_{1}

\ket{w}
.

Let
(\mathbf{g}_{k})_{k=0}^{2^{m}-1}
be the standard (binary-reflected) Gray code on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
and let

j(k)\in\{1,\dots,m\}
denote the unique bit that flips from

to
\mathbf{g}_{k}
.
Define a new angle list
\bm{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{v}\}_{v\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}}
by the Walsh–Hadamard transform

\UCRY

Fix a control word
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
and restrict the ladder circuit

\mathrm{R}_{y}(\alpha_{\mathbf{g}_{k}})\;\longmapsto\;\mathrm{R}_{y}\!\bigl((-1)^{S_{k-1}(\mathbf{u})}\,\alpha_{\mathbf{g}_{k}}\bigr).

For the binary-reflected Gray code, the parity

\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{g}_{k}\rangle:=\sum_{r=1}^{m}u_{r}(\mathbf{g}_{k})_{r}\pmod{2}

Conclusions

We have presented a rigorous and self-contained proof of correctness of the Grover–Rudolph state
preparation algorithm. The analysis makes explicit the dyadic refinement structure underlying the method,
derives a trigonometric factorization of the target probabilities in terms of conditional masses, and proves
by induction that the resulting hierarchy of controlled rotations produces exactly the desired measurement
law in the computational basis.

Beyond correctness, we discussed numerical considerations relevant for implementations, including how
finite-precision approximations of rotation angles affect the output distribution and how such errors
propagate through successive Grover–Rudolph stages.

Finally, as a practical complement, we described an ancilla-free compilation viewpoint in which each
Grover–Rudolph stage is treated as a uniformly controlled one-qubit rotation (multiplexor) admitting a
Gray-code ladder decomposition. This provides a direct route to gate-set transpilation on devices with a
native dictionary such as
\{\mathrm{R}_{y}(\cdot),X,\mathrm{CNOT}\}
, while remaining logically separate from the proof of the
Grover–Rudolph theorem.

Possible directions for future work include: (i) sharper a priori stability bounds that link angle quantization
to total-variation error in the output distribution; (ii) hardware-aware cost models for compiling the stage
multiplexors under connectivity constraints; and (iii) extensions to structured families of distributions
(e.g. log-concave or sparse) where additional synthesis savings may be achievable.
