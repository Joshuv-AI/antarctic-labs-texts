Postprandial hypotension is associated with increased blood pressure variability in patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Ju-Hee Lee, Jae-Hyeong Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostprandial hypotension (PPH), which is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, is a prevalent but often overlooked in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). In addition, its relationship with comprehensive 24-hour hemodynamic profiles remains poorly defined. We aimed to assess the prevalence and characteristics of PPH in patients with T2DM and to determine its association with systolic blood pressure (SBP) variability and circadian blood pressure (BP) patterns.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled T2DM patients with stable glycemic control. PPH was defined as a post-meal SBP fall of ≥ 20 mmHg or a drop to < 90 mmHg within 2 hours after a meal. Hemodynamic patterns, including SBP variability (standard deviation) and circadian rhythms, were analyzed via 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring.ResultsWe analyzed 67 T2DM patients (59.2 ± 11.9 years, 33 females). PPH was observed in 28 patients (41.8%). PPH occurred most frequently after dinner (64.3%), and less frequently after breakfast (25.9%). While average 24-hour BP levels were similar between groups, the PPH group exhibited significantly higher 24-hour SBP variability (21.4 ± 5.7 vs. 16.2 ± 4.3, P < 0.001) and a higher prevalence of morning surge (53.6% vs. 25.6%, P = 0.024). Multivariate logistic regression revealed that 24-hour SBP variability was the sole independent predictor of PPH (odds ratio, 1.211; 95% confidence interval, 1.043–1.406; P = 0.012).ConclusionsIn our cohort, PPH was found in 41.8% of patients with T2DM and was independently associated with increased SBP variability. These results suggest that BP variability could be a valuable marker for identifying individuals at higher risk for postprandial hemodynamic instability.Keywords: Postprandial hypotension, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Blood pressure variability, Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, Autonomic dysfunction

BACKGROUND

Postprandial hypotension (PPH), a significant drop in blood pressure (BP) following a meal, is common in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and is associated with dizziness, falls, and increased cardiovascular risk [1]. It is defined as a fall in systolic blood pressure (SBP) of ≥ 20 mmHg, or a decrease to < 90 mmHg when pre-prandial SBP ≥ 100 mmHg, within 2 hours of a meal [1,2]. The prevalence of PPH is approximately 30–67% of patients with T2DM, with higher rates in those with longstanding disease, autonomic neuropathy, or poor glycemic control [2,3,4].

The underlying mechanism involves splanchnic pooling of blood during digestion. In healthy individuals, this is counterbalanced by a compensatory increase in sympathetic activity and peripheral vasoconstriction. However, in patients with T2DM, autonomic neuropathy often impairs this baroreflex-mediated compensation, leading to systemic hypotension [4].

Despite its clinical significance, data regarding the relationship between PPH and comprehensive hemodynamic profiles obtained via 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) remains poorly understood. Specifically, the association between PPH and blood pressure variability (BPV), a marker of autonomic instability and increased cardiovascular risk, has not been fully elucidated in the T2DM population.

This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of PPH in this prospective cohort of patients with T2DM and to test the hypothesis that increased BPV and abnormal circadian BP patterns are associated with the occurrence of PPH.

METHODS

Study population

We prospectively enrolled adult patients aged 20 to 80 years who visited the outpatient cardiology clinic at Chungbuk National University Hospital between July 2020 and January 2023. Inclusion criteria required stable glycemic control (hemoglobin A1c < 8.5%) and maintenance of stable antihypertensive medication for at least 6 months prior to enrollment. To minimize the confounding effects of acute illness, patients were excluded if they had a history of acute coronary syndrome, cardiac surgery, or urgent percutaneous coronary intervention within 1 month before enrollment. Patients with systolic heart failure, defined as a documented left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 45%, as well as those with severe valvular heart disease or hemodynamically significant congenital heart disease were also excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included acute stroke within the previous month, end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis, and uncontrolled thyroid dysfunction. Patients were excluded if the investigators identified any significant clinical reason that might compromise the participant's safety or the integrity of the study data. We only enrolled patients who voluntarily signed informed consent forms to participate in the clinical trial; patients who did not consent were not included in this cohort.

A total of 82 patients were initially enrolled. Eight patients withdrew consent and were excluded prior to analysis. In addition, 7 patients were excluded due to inadequate documentation of meal timing in the standardized diary, which precluded accurate assessment of postprandial BP changes. Consequently, 67 patients were included in the final analysis. None of the enrolled patients reported typical symptoms suggestive of PPH, such as dizziness, lightheadedness, or syncope after meals, at the time of enrollment.

Study variables and definitions

Baseline demographic and medical data were obtained from the patients' records. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using height and weight. Hypertension was defined as treatment with antihypertensive medication for more than 6 months or a prior clinical diagnosis. Patients with T2DM were defined as those receiving active treatment with oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin, or by diagnostic laboratory criteria (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL or 2-hour postprandial glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL). Ischemic heart disease was identified if the patient had a history of coronary artery intervention with a documented clinical diagnosis. Dyslipidemia was defined as a prior clinical diagnosis of dyslipidemia, current use of lipid-lowering medication, or untreated lipid levels meeting the diagnostic criteria (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥ 100 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol < 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women) [5].

All echocardiographic examinations were performed using standard techniques with a 2.5 MHz probe as recommended by the American Society of Echocardiography [6]. Left ventricular volumes were calculated from the apical 4- and 2-chamber views, and LVEF was calculated from these values. Mitral inflow velocities were measured using pulsed-wave Doppler, and mitral annular velocities were estimated using tissue Doppler.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Chungbuk National University Hospital (IRB No. 2014-06-005), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki principles.

Ambulatory BP monitoring

All participants underwent 24-hour ABPM using a validated oscillometric device (TM-2430, A&D Company, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). During the monitoring period, patients were instructed to follow their habitual diet and daily activities. Standardized diaries were used to record meal times and sleep-wake periods. BP measurements were obtained at 30-minute intervals throughout the entire 24-hour monitoring period. A valid ABPM recording was defined as having at least 70% of scheduled BP readings successfully recorded, with a minimum of 20 valid daytime (awake) and 7 valid nighttime (asleep) measurements. All participants included in the final analysis met these validity criteria. Daytime and nighttime periods were defined individually for each participant based on self-reported sleep and wake times recorded in the standardized diary.

PPH was defined as a reduction in SBP of ≥ 20 mmHg, or a decrease to < 90 mmHg when preprandial SBP was ≥ 100 mmHg, within 2 hours of a meal. The magnitude of the fall was calculated as the difference between the mean SBP recorded during the 2-hour pre-meal period and the lowest SBP recorded during the subsequent 2-hour post-meal interval.

SBP variability was defined as the standard deviation (SD) of SBP measurements during the overall 24-hour cycle, the daytime (wake), and the nighttime (sleep) periods. Circadian patterns were assessed via dipping status and morning surge. Dipping status was classified based on the night-to-day SBP ratio. The morning surge was defined as the difference between the mean morning SBP (recorded during the first 2 hours after waking) and the lowest nighttime SBP (mean of 3 readings centered on the nocturnal nadir).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± SD. Differences between the PPH and non-PPH groups were analyzed using an independent student’s t-test. Categorical variables were presented as percentages and compared using the χ2 test, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed using the binary logistic regression to determine the correlation between the variables and PPH. Covariates included in the multivariate model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literatures. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

A total of 67 patients with T2DM were included in this analysis. The mean age was 59.2 ± 11.9 years, and females comprised 49.3% (n = 33). PPH was observed in 28 patients (41.8%). The baseline clinical characteristics of the PPH (+) and PPH (−) groups were largely comparable, except for medication use (Table 1). There were no significant differences in age, sex, duration of diabetes, BMI, or cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, or smoking status between the 2 groups. Laboratory parameters, including glycemic control and lipid profiles were similar between groups. Echocardiographic measures such as LVEF, and arterial stiffness (brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity) did not differ significantly. However, the use of dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4) inhibitors was significantly more frequent in the PPH (+) group than in the PPH (−) group (71.4% vs. 43.6%, P = 0.028).

Ambulatory BP monitoring and postprandial changes

Analysis of ABPM demonstrated distinct hemodynamic patterns in patients with PPH (Table 2). Mean 24-hour, daytime, and nighttime average systolic and diastolic BP values did not differ significantly between groups. In contrast, the PPH (+) group showed a significantly greater morning BP rise (15.0 ± 14.6 mmHg vs. 10.8 ± 10.8 mmHg, P = 0.038) and a higher prevalence of an exaggerated morning surge (53.6% vs. 25.6%, P = 0.024) compared with the PPH (−) group.

Consistent with the definition of PPH, the average postprandial SBP fall was markedly greater in the PPH (+) group (20.5 ± 11.8 mmHg) compared with the PPH (−) group (5.3 ± 7.0 mmHg, P < 0.001). When analyzed by mealtime, significant differences in postprandial BP reduction were observed after lunch and dinner (both P < 0.001), but not after breakfast (P = 0.410). Accordingly, the incidence of PPH events was the highest after dinner (64.3%), followed by lunch (63.6%) and breakfast (25.9%).

BP variability

Patients with PPH exhibited significantly greater BPV than those without PPH (Table 2). Twenty-four-hour SBP variability (SD) was significantly higher in the PPH (+) group than in the PPH (−) group (21.4 ± 5.7 vs. 16.2 ± 4.3, P < 0.001). Similar patterns were observed for daytime systolic and diastolic BPV, whereas nighttime BPV did not differ significantly between groups.

Determinants of PPH

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses are presented in Table 3. In univariate analysis, the presence of PPH was associated with the use of DPP4 inhibitors, exaggerated morning BP surge, and various measures of SBP variability assessed by SD. In multivariate analysis adjusting for age, sex, mean SBP, duration of diabetes, and antihypertensive medication use, 24-hour SBP variability remained the sole independent determinant of PPH (odds ratio, 1.211; 95% confidence interval, 1.043–1.406; P = 0.012). Mean SBP levels were not independently associated with PPH.

DISCUSSION

In this prospective ABPM-based study of patients with T2DM and stable glycemic control, PPH was found in 41.8% of the study population despite the absence of typical postprandial symptoms at enrollment. PPH is linked to BP instability, marked by higher 24-hour SBP variability and a higher morning surge prevalence, despite similar mean 24-hour BP levels between groups. Importantly, in the multivariable analysis, 24-hour SBP variability remained the sole independent determinant of PPH, suggesting that PPH may represent a BPV-related hemodynamic phenotype rather than a phenomenon driven primarily by BP level. These findings indicate that clinically stable patients with well-controlled diabetes may still exhibit postprandial BP instability not apparent from office BP levels alone.

Recent evidence suggests that the clinical significance of PPH may be underestimated, as PPH frequently occurs without overt symptoms. In the KAMOGAWA-HBP study, Kitae et al. [3] demonstrated that asymptomatic postprandial BP falls are common among patients with diabetes and are associated with abnormal BP regulation rather than with subjective symptoms. These observations highlight that PPH represents a subclinical hemodynamic abnormality that may remain undetected without ambulatory or home BP monitoring.

Our findings are consistent with and extend these observations by showing that increased BPV is closely associated with PPH, even in patients without symptomatic hypotension. Together, these results support the concept that PPH reflects an underlying phenotype of BP instability rather than an isolated postprandial event, as previously suggested in reviews emphasizing BP regulation failure rather than mean BP reduction [7].

The pathophysiology of PPH is complex and multifactorial, with impaired autonomic cardiovascular regulation considered a central mechanism [3,4,8,9]. Following meal ingestion, splanchnic vasodilation and blood pooling lead to a reduction in systemic vascular resistance and venous return [2,7]. In individuals with intact cardiovascular regulation, this hemodynamic challenge is counterbalanced by a compensatory sympathetic response, characterized by increases in heart rate, peripheral vascular resistance, and cardiac output, thereby maintaining systemic BP [9,10]. In patients with PPH, however, this compensatory response is blunted. Previous studies have demonstrated attenuated sympathetic activation and impaired baroreflex-mediated adjustments in response to PPH, suggesting that failure of short-term BP regulation underlies the development of PPH [1,9,10]. This concept aligns with the study's findings: higher BPV (a marker of BP instability) independently linked to PPH, while mean BP levels were not.

In addition to autonomic dysfunction, metabolic and hormonal factors may modulate postprandial BP responses. Intraduodenal glucose administration has been shown to decrease postprandial BP among older adults, without concomitant increase in heart rate or muscle sympathetic nerve activity, indicating that non-autonomic mechanisms may also contribute [11]. Several gastrointestinal hormones, including insulin, somatostatin and other vasoactive peptides, have been implicated in the regulation of splanchnic circulation and systemic vascular tone [2,12]. Insulin-induced vasodilation has been reported to provoke hypotensive episodes in patients with diabetic autonomic neuropathy [13], while somatostatin may attenuate PPH by suppressing the release of vasoactive gastrointestinal peptides [2,14].

The most notable finding of the present study is that BPV, rather than mean BP, was independently associated with PPH. Increased BPV might be associated with increased arterial stiffness, impaired endothelial function, and autonomic dysfunction, all of which may contribute to impaired cardiovascular adaptability [15,16]. Both increased BPV and PPH share autonomic dysfunction as a common underlying feature, and this close association supports the hypothesis that widespread impairment of autonomic cardiovascular regulation represents a unifying pathophysiological substrate [7,10]. Our findings extend this concept by demonstrating that PPH may represent one clinical manifestation of this BPV phenotype, particularly in patients with “brittle” BP regulation who are unable to maintain systemic pressure when challenged by the hemodynamic shifts induced by meal ingestion.

Interestingly, only daytime BPV, but not nighttime variability, was associated with PPH in our study. This finding is physiologically plausible, as PPH occurs during waking hours and is strongly influenced by dynamic behavioral and environmental factors, including meal ingestion, physical activity, and postural changes [17]. Daytime BPV may therefore better capture the dynamic cardiovascular challenges encountered during daily life, whereas nighttime variability primarily reflects sleep-related regulatory mechanisms [18]. Patients with heightened daytime BPV may thus be particularly vulnerable to postprandial BP falls, as their cardiovascular system lacks sufficient regulatory reserve to compensate for meal-related splanchnic blood pooling. The association between increased morning BP surge and PPH further supports the notion of heightened BP instability during periods of autonomic transition, reinforcing the link between impaired BP regulation and susceptibility to postprandial BP falls [17,19].

The relatively lower incidence of postprandial BP fall after breakfast observed in our study may reflect both physiological and behavioral factors. Morning BP is typically higher due to circadian variation in autonomic tone and the well-described morning BP surge, which may transiently enhance compensatory responses to meal-induced splanchnic blood pooling. In addition, breakfast may be smaller in volume or relatively lower in carbohydrate content compared with later meals, resulting in a lesser postprandial hemodynamic challenge. These explanations remain speculative, as meal composition and detailed autonomic parameters were not directly assessed in the present study.

From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the importance of BP monitoring in patients with T2DM. As asymptomatic PPH is common and cannot be reliably detected by office BP measurements alone, ABPM may help identify individuals at increased risk of PPH and related adverse events such as falls, syncope, stroke, coronary events, and all-cause mortality [8,20]. The risk is especially pronounced in patients with severe autonomic dysfunction, where BP may not recover for hours after meals, increasing the likelihood of serious cardiovascular events [8,20]. Strategies aimed to stabilize BP regulation, rather than focusing solely on lowering mean BP, may therefore be particularly relevant in this population, in line with contemporary perspectives on BPV as a therapeutic target [7].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between PPH and increased BPV. Second, although postprandial BP changes were assessed using ABPM with patient diaries, this approach may be less precise than standardized meal-based testing protocols. Third, clinical outcomes were not evaluated in the present cohort, limiting the ability to assess the prognostic implications of PPH. In addition, autonomic function was not directly measured, and thus mechanistic interpretations regarding impaired BP regulation remain inferential. Nevertheless, the consistent and independent association between BPV and PPH observed in this study supports the robustness of the present findings and provides a strong rationale for future prospective studies incorporating standardized assessments of autonomic function and long-term clinical outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

PPH was present in 41.8% of patients with T2DM and was independently associated with increased systolic BPV. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the long-term cardiovascular prognosis of PPH.
