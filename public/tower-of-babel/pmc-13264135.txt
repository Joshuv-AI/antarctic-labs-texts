Hesperetin-black phosphorus nanosheets targeting TAMs induce ferroptosis and reverse protumoral polarization to remodel the immune microenvironment
Authors: Liang Feng, Huan Wang, Zuodi Liang, Ying Tian, Yang Guo, Zhuo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study focused on developing a novel nanodelivery system, BP-PEG-S2P@H, with hesperetin-loaded black phosphorus nanosheets to target tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Through RNA sequencing and proteomic analyses, CEBPG upregulation was observed in TAMs, potentially controlling the SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis pathway. In vitro and in vivo experiments, including FerroOrange and malondialdehyde assays, demonstrated the induction of ferroptosis by BP-PEG-S2P@H. The nanoplatform facilitated the transition of TAMs from the M2 to the M1 subtype, enhancing IL-12 secretion while reducing IL-10 levels. Functional analyses confirmed the reversal of the pro-migratory TAM phenotype and inhibition of tumor cell invasion. In vivo imaging validated efficient tumor targeting, with immunohistochemical staining revealing decreased CEBPG/SLC7A11 expression in tumors, reduced macrophage infiltration, and enhanced CD8+ T cell activation, promoting the efficacy of PD-L1 immunotherapy. Biosafety assessments indicated no apparent toxicity, supporting the potential of the BP-PEG-S2P@H nanoplatform for TAM-targeted nano-immunotherapy in TNBC.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), defined by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2 expression, accounts for approximately 15%–20% of breast cancers. Owing to its aggressive behavior, high metastatic potential, and poor prognosis, TNBC remains a major clinical challenge [[1], [2], [3]]. Chemotherapy is still the mainstay of treatment, while effective targeted therapies remain limited [1,4]. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-1/PD-L1 have expanded therapeutic options, only a subset of patients benefits from these agents, largely because of the immunosuppressive tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [1,[4], [5], [6]]. Among the cellular components of the TIME, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are key mediators of immune suppression and contribute substantially to impaired antitumor immunity and resistance to immunotherapy [7]. Therefore, precise functional regulation of TAMs represents a promising strategy to enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy in TNBC.

TAMs exhibit remarkable phenotypic plasticity and can broadly polarize toward either pro-inflammatory M1-like or immunosuppressive M2-like states. M1 macrophages promote antitumor immune responses, whereas M2 macrophages support tumor progression, immune escape, and stromal remodeling through factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β [8]. In TNBC, TAMs are frequently enriched in the M2-like phenotype, thereby reinforcing an immunosuppressive microenvironment [9]. Importantly, M2-like TAMs can be reprogrammed toward an M1-like phenotype under appropriate stimuli, restoring antitumor immune activity [10]. In addition, inducing programmed cell death in TAMs, particularly ferroptosis, has emerged as an effective approach to disrupt macrophage-mediated immune suppression. Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of cell death driven by lipid peroxide accumulation and is closely associated with redox homeostasis [[11], [12], [13]]. Thus, simultaneously inducing TAM ferroptosis and reshaping macrophage polarization may offer a powerful strategy for remodeling the TIME.

SLC7A11, a core component of the cystine/glutamate antiporter system, regulates glutathione metabolism and antioxidant defense and serves as a major suppressor of ferroptosis [14]. CEBPG is an inflammation-related transcription factor that may regulate SLC7A11 expression in TAMs through transcriptional activation, although its regulatory mechanism remains unclear [15]. Nanotechnology provides an effective platform for targeted regulation of TAMs. Black phosphorus nanosheets (BP) have gained increasing attention because of their favorable biocompatibility, biodegradability, and drug-loading capacity [16,17]. In this study, BP was functionalized with PEG and the TAM-targeting S2P peptide, and further loaded with the natural polyphenol hesperetin to construct a multifunctional nanosystem, BP-PEG-S2P@H, designed to achieve TAM-targeted delivery and immune modulation.

In this study, we developed BP-PEG-S2P@H to overcome TAM-mediated immunosuppression in TNBC. By focusing on the CEBPG/SLC7A11 axis, we investigated its role in regulating TAM ferroptosis and macrophage polarization, and further evaluated its contribution to TIME remodeling. This study proposes an integrated therapeutic strategy combining targeted delivery, ferroptosis induction, and macrophage reprogramming. Our findings provide mechanistic insight into CEBPG-mediated ferroptosis regulation in TAMs and support the potential of BP-PEG-S2P@H as a safe, targeted, and immune-potentiating nanomedicine for precision immunotherapy in TNBC.

Materials and methods

Isolation and induction of TAMs and BMDMs

To investigate the role of TAMs in TNBC progression, a subcutaneous 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse model was established using female BALB/c mice aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 18–20 g under SPF conditions (Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., China). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (Approval No. CMUKT2025183). When tumor volumes reached approximately 300 mm3, tumor tissues were harvested, minced, and prepared into single-cell suspensions. Cells were stained with anti-F4/80-APC (123115, BioLegend, USA) and anti-CD11b-FITC (11-0112-82, eBioscience, USA), and TAMs were isolated using a FACSAria III cell sorter (BD Biosciences, USA). For bone marrow-derived macrophage (BMDM) induction, bone marrow cells were flushed from the femurs and tibias of mice and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (12633012, Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; A5256701, Gibco, USA) and 10 ng/mL M-CSF (AF-315-02, PeproTech, USA). After 7 days of differentiation, mature BMDMs were collected for subsequent experiments.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and data analysis

Total RNA was isolated from FACS-sorted TAMs (n = 3) and BMDMs (n = 3) using the RNeasy Mini Kit (74104, Qiagen). Samples demonstrating high RNA integrity (RIN >8) were subjected to library preparation and paired-end 150 bp sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument, generating a minimum of 20 million clean reads per biological replicate. Following initial quality control via FastQC (v0.11.9), adapter trimming and low-quality read filtering were executed using Trimmomatic (v0.39). The resulting clean reads were mapped to the GRCm38/mm10 murine reference genome via HISAT2 (v2.2.1), and gene-level read counts were extracted with featureCounts (v2.0.1). Differential expression profiling was carried out using DESeq2 (v1.34.0). Genes satisfying the criteria of |log2(fold change)| > 1 and an adjusted p-value (adjp) < 0.05 were classified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Proteomic analysis

To elucidate hesperetin-induced protein alterations, TAMs treated with either PBS or hesperetin (n = 3 per group) were lysed in SDS buffer (P0013G, Beyotime). After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 4 °C, 15 min), the supernatant protein concentrations were quantified via a BCA kit (23227, Thermo Fisher). Sample preparation for mass spectrometry followed the standard filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol. Briefly, 50 μg of total protein was dissolved in 8 M urea, reduced using 5 mM DTT (56 °C, 30 min), alkylated with 20 mM IAA (dark, RT, 30 min), and quenched with DTT. Solutions were then buffer-exchanged into 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate using 10 kDa cutoff filters (UFC501096, Millipore). Tryptic digestion (V5113, Promega) was performed overnight (16 h, 37 °C) at a 1:50 enzyme-to-substrate ratio. Following acidification with 1% formic acid (94318, Sigma-Aldrich), the resulting peptides were multiplexed using a TMT 11-plex kit (90110, Thermo Fisher). Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was conducted on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer coupled with an EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher) utilizing a 120-min gradient. Precursor (MS1) and fragment (MS2) ions were scanned at resolutions of 120,000 and 30,000, respectively. Peptide identification and quantification were processed via MaxQuant (v2.1.0.0), with significance thresholds set at |log2FC| > 1 and adjp <0.05.

Functional enrichment analysis

The identified DEGs and differentially expressed proteins were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway mapping. GO terms were subdivided into biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC). Pathway visualization and statistical testing were performed via the R package clusterProfiler (v4.4.4). Significance was determined using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction, with an adjusted q-value <0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Public data acquisition

Transcriptomic data (FPKM format) and matching clinical metadata for the TCGA-BRCA cohort were retrieved from the GDC Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) (data locked as of May 2025). Data downloading was facilitated by the GDC Data Transfer Tool (v1.6.0) via command-line execution. A final cohort of 1086 breast cancer cases was assembled, of which 1058 possessed comprehensive survival endpoints, including overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI).

Survival analysis

Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression subgroups according to the median transcript levels of CEBPG and SLC7A11. The prognostic impact on OS, DSS, and PFI was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier methodology coupled with log-rank testing. Hazard ratios (HR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. All statistical computing was performed in R (v4.3.1) utilizing the 'survival' (v3.5-7) and 'survminer' (v0.4.9) packages, with a two-sided p-value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Gene expression correlation analysis

To assess the correlation between CEBPG, SLC7A11, and M2 macrophage marker genes (MRC1, CD163, IL10) in tumor tissues, the FPKM values were extracted. Scatter plots were generated using the "ggpubr" package (v0.6.0) in R, and statistical testing was performed with Spearman's correlation coefficient. The plotting parameters were set to add = "reg.line" to display the fitted regression line, and both the correlation coefficient (r) and p-value were annotated on the plots.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

The CIBERSORT computational framework (v1.06) was employed to estimate the relative fractions of 22 infiltrating immune cell types within the TCGA-BRCA cohort based on the LM22 signature matrix. Following 1000 permutations, only cases exhibiting a p-value <0.05 were deemed statistically reliable and retained for subsequent evaluation. Spearman's correlation coefficients linking CEBPG or SLC7A11 transcript levels to the abundance of specific immune populations were calculated and visualized via the R 'corrplot' package (v0.92). Specifically, the estimated proportions of M0, M1, and M2 macrophages were isolated and correlated with CEBPG and SLC7A11 expression, followed by scatter plot generation via the 'ggplot2' package (v3.5.1).

Lentiviral transduction and siRNA transfection

To generate the CEBPG knockdown model, sh-CEBPG-1 and sh-CEBPG-2 lentiviral vectors were constructed using the pLKO.1 backbone (10878, Addgene, USA). Lentivirus packaging was performed in 293T cells (ATCC, #CRL-3216) by co-transfecting the shRNA lentiviral vectors with the packaging plasmid psPAX2 (12260, Addgene, USA) and the envelope plasmid pMD2.G (12259, Addgene, USA) using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Supernatants containing virions were harvested at 48 h. Primary murine TAMs were transduced at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50. Post-infection (48 h), cells underwent selection using 2 μg/mL puromycin (P8833, Sigma-Aldrich) for 14 days. Knockdown validation was performed via RT-qPCR (primer sequences listed in Table S1) [18].

To establish the CEBPG overexpression model, the full-length mouse CEBPG cDNA fragment (including a Kozak sequence and C-terminal HA tag) was cloned into the pcDNA3.1/Hygro(+) vector (V87020, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to form the oe-CEBPG vector, followed by identical lentiviral packaging and TAM transduction procedures. Overexpression was verified by RT-qPCR and immunoblotting. For subsequent pharmacological assays, stable TAMs were treated with either 50 μM hesperetin (HY-N0168, MedChemExpress) or a PBS vehicle for 48 h [19].

Furthermore, targeted gene silencing was achieved using specific siRNAs against SLC7A11 (5′-GGAACUUGUCUUUCAGAAUTT-3′) and STAB1 (5′-GGATCGTCTTCTACAACCA-3′), alongside a non-targeting control (si-NC) synthesized by Jili Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Cells (1 × 105/well in 6-well plates) were transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and harvested 48 h post-transfection [20,21].

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, USA) and reverse-transcribed with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (RR037A, Takara, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed to analyze the expression of CEBPG, SLC7A11, and PD-L1, with β-actin used as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2. Amplification was carried out using SYBR Green Master Mix (4385612, Applied Biosystems, USA) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Western blot (WB)

Total protein from tissue lysates and cultured cells was extracted using RIPA buffer (89901, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and concentrations were quantified via a BCA assay kit (23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equivalent protein masses were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels (4561093, Bio-Rad, USA) and then transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). After transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the corresponding primary antibodies (Table S3), including GPX4 (52455, CST, GPX4 Antibody | Cell Signaling Technology), IKKβ (8943, CST, IKK beta Antibody (D30C6) | Cell Signaling Technology), STAT6 (5397, CST, Stat6 (D3H4) Rabbit Monoclonal Antibody | Cell Signaling Technology), P-STAT6 (9361, CST, Phospho-Stat6 (Tyr641) Antibody | Cell Signaling Technology), ARG I (9242, CST, IkappaB alpha Antibody | Cell Signaling Technology), and iNOS (ab15323, Abcam, Anti-iNOS antibody, Rabbit polyclonal | Abcam). The membranes were then washed with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (TBST) (T9039, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ab172730, 1:1000, Abcam, UK) at room temperature for an appropriate duration. Finally, protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (WBKLS0500, Millipore, USA), and images were captured and subjected to densitometric analysis using a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA).

Preparation of tumor cell-conditioned medium (CM) and BMDM treatment

TNBC cell lines (4T1, CRL-2539; MDA-MB-231, HTB-26; BT-549, HTB-122; ATCC) were cultivated in 6 cm dishes to 70-80% confluence. Cells were subsequently washed and cultured in serum-free RPMI-1640 (C11875500BT, Gibco, USA) for an additional 24 h. The resulting supernatants were centrifuged (300 × g, 10 min) and passed through a 0.22 μm filter (SLGP033RS, Millipore, USA) to obtain CM. Mouse BMDMs were seeded and treated for 24 h with a 1:1 mixture of CM and fresh RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS, supplemented with either hesperetin (50 μM) or PBS.

Cells were fixed and blocked, then incubated overnight with CD206 (ab64693, 1:1000, Abcam, UK) and Ki67 (ab15580, 1:1000, Abcam, UK) primary antibodies, followed by nuclear staining with DAPI (D9542, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Fluorescence imagery was captured via a Nikon A1R confocal microscope to quantify the CD206+/Ki67+ cellular fraction as an indicator of M2 polarization.

Ferroptosis sensitivity assay (CCK-8 and live/dead staining)

BMDMs cultured under standard conditions or with CM from 4T1, MDA-MB-231, or BT-549 cells were treated with erastin (10 μM) or RSL3 (1 μM) (S7242, S8115, Selleck Chemicals, USA) for 48 h. Subsequently, cellular viability was evaluated by recording the absorbance at 450 nm utilizing a CCK-8 kit (CK04, Dojindo, Japan). To visualize live and dead cell populations, samples were stained with a Calcein-AM/PI mixture (40747ES76, Yeasen, China) for 30 min prior to fluorescence microscopy. To confirm ferroptosis specificity, parallel cohorts were co-treated with 2 μM of the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (S7243, Selleck Chemicals, USA).

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation assay

TAMs were treated for 48 h with PBS, Hesperetin, or Hesperetin plus Fer-1 (0.5 μM, MCE, HY-100579, Ferrostatin-1). After replacement with fresh complete medium, cells were incubated with 10 μg/mL DCFH-DA probe (H131224, Aladdin, China) for 30 min, washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (158127, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), counterstained with DAPI, and observed by confocal laser scanning microscopy [22].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for cellular ultrastructure observation

TAMs were treated with PBS or hesperetin for 48 h, then fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (340855, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After three PBS washes, the samples underwent secondary fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide (4 °C, 1.5 h). The specimens were then subjected to graded ethanol dehydration and subsequent acrylic resin embedding. Finally, ultrathin sections were contrasted using lead citrate and uranyl acetate before being imaged on a Hitachi H-7500 TEM (Hitachi, Japan).

Characterization of nanoparticles

Surface chemical compositions were evaluated via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) inside a UHV chamber (∼5 × 10−10 mbar) utilizing an Al Kα source (12.5 kV, 250 W) and a Phoibos 150 analyzer (all from SPECS GmbH, Berlin, Germany). Wide-scan and high-resolution spectra were collected at pass energies of 100 eV (1 eV steps) and 20 eV (0.1 eV steps), respectively. Binding energies were standardized to the adventitious C 1s peak (284.8 eV), and data deconvolution was performed via CasaXPS software (Casa Software Ltd., Teignmouth, UK). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS 50 spectrometer. UV–Vis–NIR absorption spectra were obtained using an Infinite M200 Pro TECAN GENIOS spectrophotometer. Drug loading efficiency and encapsulation efficiency were determined by analyzing the UV–Vis–NIR spectra of BP-PEG nanosheets before and after drug loading [23,24].

Live-cell mitochondrial staining

TAMs cultivated in confocal dishes were exposed to either PBS or hesperetin for 48 h. Following an HBSS wash, the cells were incubated with MitoTracker Red FM (M7512, Invivogen, USA) for 0.5 h. Mitochondrial distribution in live cells was immediately visualized using a Nikon N-SIM structured illumination microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) measurement

Post-treatment (48 h of PBS or hesperetin), TAMs were rinsed with PBS and stained with 5 mg/L JC-1 solution (C2006, Beyotime, China) for 20 min at 37 °C. After triple washing with the supplied JC-1 buffer, the ratio of red (J-aggregates) to green (J-monomers) fluorescence was determined via fluorescence microscopy.

Measurement of GSH and GSSG

Following overnight incubation in culture plates (3 × 105 cells/well), the cells were exposed to specific treatments for 24 h. Post-treatment, cells were rinsed with PBS and harvested into centrifuge tubes. A 5 μM C11-BODIPY working solution, diluted from a 50 μM stock (Invitrogen, D3861) using DMEM, was applied to the cells to replace the old media. Following a 30-min dark incubation at 37 °C, the cells were washed three times with PBS and resuspended in a 500 μL volume. The suspensions were then filtered, maintained on ice, and subjected to flow cytometric analysis (Beckman) utilizing the FL1 channel with a 488 nm laser [25].

FerroOrange staining

Intracellular Fe2+ levels were detected using the FerroOrange fluorescent probe (F374, Dojindo, Japan). Cells were incubated with 10 μmol/L FerroOrange at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min, washed with PBS, and imaged using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 880, Germany) at Ex/Em = 543/580 nm. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ/Fiji. CZI files were imported as hyperstacks and channels were separated for independent analysis. Regions of interest containing positively stained areas were manually outlined, with more than 50 cells analyzed per field.

Iron content measurement

Macrophage (TAM) Fe2+ concentrations were quantified employing a commercial iron assay kit (E1042, Applygen, China). Harvested TAMs were rinsed with cold PBS (4 °C) and subjected to a 30-min lysis on ice using the provided buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. After clearing the lysates via centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), 50 μL of the resulting supernatant or standard solutions were plated into 96-well plates. A 100 μL aliquot of the working reagent (comprising a chromogenic substrate and Fe2+ chelator) was then added. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at 25 °C protected from light. A SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA) was used to record the absorbance at 593 nm.

Lipid peroxidation product measurement (malondialdehyde [MDA])

Cellular MDA content, serving as a metric for lipid peroxidation, was quantified utilizing a TBARS assay kit (MAK085, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Cells were washed with cold PBS, lysed in buffer containing 1% protease inhibitors on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged (12,000 × g, 4 °C, 10 min). Subsequently, 100 μL of the cleared lysate was combined with an equal volume of TBA reagent (0.37% thiobarbituric acid, 15% trichloroacetic acid, and 0.25 M HCl). This mixture was heated to 95 °C for 1 h in the dark, then rapidly chilled in an ice bath for 10 min to stop the reaction. Following a final spin (10,000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C), the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance was read at 532 nm on a SpectraMax iD5 plate reader.

Lipid ROS detection

Intracellular lipid ROS levels were assessed using the BODIPY™ 581/591C11 probe (D3861, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cells were incubated with 10 μmol/L probe solution at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min, washed three times with pre-cooled PBS, and imaged using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 880, Germany). Fluorescence channels were separated into red (non-oxidized) and green (oxidized) signals in ImageJ software, and the green-to-red fluorescence intensity ratio (G/R) was calculated to quantify lipid ROS levels.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR) assay

Potential CEBPG binding motifs within the SLC7A11 promoter were initially predicted utilizing the JASPAR database. To experimentally confirm this interaction, ChIP assays were conducted. Briefly, 1% formaldehyde was utilized to cross-link TAMs for 10 min at ambient temperature, followed by glycine quenching. The chromatin from lysed cells was sheared via sonication to generate 200–500 bp fragments. These fragments were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C utilizing an anti-CEBPG antibody (12244-1-AP, Proteintech, USA) or an IgG isotype control (ab231712, Millipore, USA). DNA was isolated from the Protein A/G magnetic bead-captured immune complexes post-reverse cross-linking. Enrichment of the target promoter regions was evaluated by qPCR using site-specific primers, and the binding efficiency was calculated via the % Input method.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

SLC7A11 promoter activity was investigated by cloning wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) regulatory sequences into the pGL3-Basic plasmid (HG-VQP0121, Promega, USA). HEK293T cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were co-transfected with these constructs (1 μg) and the pRL-TK Renilla reference plasmid (0.1 μg, E2241, Promega) employing Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After a 48-h incubation, cell lysates were evaluated utilizing the Dual-Luciferase Reporter System (E1910, Promega). Firefly and Renilla luciferase substrates were sequentially added to measure promoter and internal control activities, respectively. Relative luminescence units (RLU) were acquired on a SpectraMax iD5 reader, and final promoter activities were normalized as the firefly/Renilla luminescence ratio.

Wound-healing assay

The migration capabilities of 4T1 cells (ATCC, TIB-71) in response to BP-PEG-S2P@H were assessed using a classic scratch assay. Cells were plated in 6-well dishes (Corning, USA) at 5 × 105 cells/well and grown until >90% confluency was achieved. A sterile 200 μL pipette tip was utilized to inflict a uniform vertical scratch, followed by two gentle PBS washes to eliminate detached debris. The experimental setups included Control, M2-CM, BP-PEG + M2-CM, and BP-PEG-S2P@H + M2-CM groups. The M2 conditioned medium (M2-CM) was prepared by diluting polarized BMDM supernatants 1:1 with complete RPMI-1640 media (Gibco, USA). The administration concentrations for both BP-PEG and BP-PEG-S2P@H were maintained at 50 μg/mL. Micrographs were taken immediately (0 h) and post-24 h incubation (37 °C, 5% CO2) using an Olympus IX73 inverted microscope. The extent of wound closure was quantified via ImageJ software across three independent biological replicates based on the formula: migration rate = [(initial wound area - remaining wound area)/initial wound area] × 100%.

Transwell assay

To assess BMDM motility, cells were initially exposed to 100 μg/mL of either BP-PEG or BP-PEG-S2P@H for a duration of 3 h. Following enumeration, 5 × 104 cells were plated into the top compartments of 8-μm pore Transwell inserts (3422, Corning, USA). The bottom compartments were supplied with RPMI-1640 containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) as a chemoattractant. Following a 24-h incubation period, cells remaining on the upper membrane surface were wiped away with cotton swabs. The cells that successfully migrated to the lower surface were subjected to a 30-min fixation using 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by a 20–30 min staining step with 0.1% crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime, China). After thorough PBS washing, migrating cells were counted under a microscope (Olympus IX73, Japan).

The migratory and invasive behaviors of 4T1 cells (ATCC, TIB-71) in response to treatments (50 μg/mL BP-PEG or BP-PEG-S2P@H) were similarly analyzed via Transwell systems. For cell migration profiling, 5 × 104 4T1 cells suspended in serum-free RPMI-1640 were placed directly into bare upper inserts. Conversely, for the invasion assays, the inserts were pre-coated with 50 μL of Matrigel (354234, Corning, USA) and allowed to gel at 37 °C for 30 min prior to the addition of cells. In both experimental paradigms, the lower reservoirs contained 10% FBS-supplemented media. Following a 24-h treatment phase, the non-penetrating cells on the top side of the porous membrane were mechanically removed. The translocated cells were subsequently fixed in anhydrous methanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 30 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, rinsed, and dried. Cellular quantification was performed across five randomly chosen microscopic fields (×200 magnification) per insert. Statistical evaluation was executed via GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.1, USA), with data derived from three independent biological replicates.

Preparation of BP

Liquid-phase exfoliation was utilized to manufacture black phosphorus (BP) nanosheets from bulk BP crystals (≥99.998% purity, Smart Elements, Austria) using anhydrous N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 328634, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as the solvent. Briefly, 100 mg of bulk BP powder was mixed into 50 mL of NMP, followed by 8 h of probe sonication (120 W, JY92-IIN, Scientz, China) under ice-cooling conditions. To eliminate unexfoliated bulk aggregates, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min. The collected supernatant was finally passed through a 0.22 μm filter membrane to yield a stable BP nanosheet dispersion characterized by an average particle size of approximately 150 nm.

Synthesis of BP-PEG-S2P

Synthesized S2P-PEG-NH2 (molecular formula: S2P-GGGC-PEG2000-NH2, GenScript, China) was added to the BP suspension (BP concentration, 1 mg/mL) at a mass ratio of 1:10. The mixture was stirred slowly at room temperature for 12 h to achieve polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification. BP-PEG-S2P was formed via electrostatic adsorption. The product was centrifuged and washed three times (12,000 rpm, 10 min each), then resuspended in sterile water for further use.

Construction of BP-PEG-S2P@H

Hesperetin was dissolved in ethanol and added dropwise to the BP-PEG-S2P solution at a drug-to-carrier mass ratio of 2:1. The mixture was shaken at room temperature under light-protected conditions for 12 h. Free hesperetin was removed using ultrafiltration tubes (MWCO 10 kDa, Millipore, USA) to obtain the loaded complex BP-PEG-S2P@H. Drug loading and encapsulation efficiency were determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu, Japan) at 286 nm.

TEM for nanomaterial morphology characterization

The morphology of BP, BP-PEG, and BP-PEG-S2P was examined using a TEM (HT7700, Hitachi, Japan). Samples were dropped onto copper grids, air-dried, and imaged at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. The lateral size distribution was determined from the acquired images, and image processing was performed using ImageJ software.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurement

BP and its derivatives were deposited onto freshly cleaved mica substrates, air-dried, and measured using an AFM (Dimension Icon, Bruker, USA) in tapping mode to determine nanosheet thickness. Fifty nanosheets were measured per sample, and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential analysis

A Nano ZS90 laser particle size analyzer (Malvern Instruments, UK) was used to monitor time-dependent changes in particle size and PDI of BP, BP-PEG, BP-PEG-S2P, and BP-PEG-S2P@H in PBS, DMEM, and PBS containing 10% FBS to assess colloidal stability. Zeta potential measurements were also performed to evaluate surface charge.

Phosphate release assay

BP-PEG-S2P was incubated in PBS containing 10% FBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C, and supernatants were collected at designated time points. Phosphate release was quantified using the molybdenum blue method with a phosphate assay kit (S0235S, Beyotime, China), and absorbance was measured at 650 nm.

Hesperetin release study

BP-PEG-S2P@H was incubated in PBS simulating the TME (pH 6.3) or PBS containing 0.1 or 1.0 mM H2O2 (both supplemented with 10% FBS) at 37 °C. At predefined intervals, supernatants were collected, diluted with ethanol, and analyzed at 286 nm to determine hesperetin release. The cumulative release percentage was calculated, and all assays were performed in triplicate.

Fluorescent labeling and TAM targeting assay

Cy7-NHS ester (C171360, Aladdin, China) was used to label BP-PEG or BP-PEG-S2P@H under light-protected conditions at 4 degrees C for 2 h. Unbound dye was removed using ultrafiltration tubes. BMDMs were polarized to the M2 phenotype by IL-4 (20 ng/mL, 48 h), then incubated with the Cy7-labeled materials for 6 h and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa, USA). Fluorescence intensity reflected uptake capacity. For receptor-knockdown experiments, Cy7-labeled materials were added to M2-BMDMs and si-STAB1 M2-BMDMs for 6 h before flow cytometric analysis.

Cell viability and cytotoxicity evaluation

The biocompatibility of BP-PEG-S2P@H with BMDMs and RAW264.7 cells (TIB-71, ATCC) was assessed using an MTT assay kit (M1020, Solarbio, China). Following a 24-h exposure to various concentrations of BP (0–200 μg/mL), the optical density was recorded at 550 nm, and cell viability was calculated relative to the untreated control cultures. Cytotoxicity of BP-PEG-S2P@H was further evaluated in M0 macrophages, TAMs, and 4T1 cells at concentrations of 50, 100, 200, 400, 600, and 800 μg/mL. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and dose-response curves were plotted accordingly.

Establishment and grouping of the TNBC xenograft model

A BALB/c mouse TNBC xenograft model was established using 4T1 murine breast cancer cells. Female BALB/c mice aged 6-8 weeks (n = 5/group, SPF grade, Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) were subcutaneously injected with 1 x 10^6 4T1 cells into the axilla. When tumors reached an initial volume of approximately 50-100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 5/group) using a computer-generated random number sequence, such as the RAND() function in Excel, to ensure no significant baseline differences among groups. During in vivo treatment, formulations were coded and concealed by an independent researcher not directly involved in dosing. Operators performing intraperitoneal injection, tail-vein injection, and caliper-based tumor measurement were fully blinded to group allocation. Subsequent evaluation of excised tissues, including flow cytometry analysis, quantitative immunofluorescence, and histological scoring, was completed by independent assessors unaware of sample identity.

For the therapeutic efficacy study, the four cohorts comprised: PBS + isotype, Hesperetin + isotype, PBS + anti-PD-L1 antibody (aPD-L1), and Hesperetin + aPD-L1. Hesperetin (50 mg/kg) was delivered via intraperitoneal injection every 48 h. The anti-PD-L1 antibody (BE0101, Bio X Cell, USA) or its equivalent isotype IgG control (BE0091, Bio X Cell, USA) was intravenously administered via the tail vein at a dose of 200 μg per mouse on a 3-day interval for a total of 5 cycles. Tumor dimensions were continuously monitored using calipers, and volumes were estimated employing the standard formula: V = 0.5 × length × width2 [26].

Intratumoral pharmacokinetic study

To evaluate the intratumoral distribution kinetics, 4T1-bearing mice were randomized into 12 temporal cohorts (n = 6 per group) and administered a single intravenous dose of BP-PEG-S2P@H (100 μL, 1 mg/mL). At pre-determined intervals (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 32 h post-injection), mice were deeply anesthetized employing 4% chloral hydrate and subjected to systemic perfusion with ice-cold saline prior to tumor excision. The harvested tissues were blotted dry, precisely weighed, and homogenized in a 4-fold volume of cold 20% aqueous acetonitrile (v/v). Control tissue homogenates were prepared identically from untreated mice. All homogenates were preserved at −80 °C until analysis.

Quantitative drug measurements were executed on a Finnigan Surveyor™ HPLC system coupled with a Finnigan TSQ Quantum Discovery Max mass spectrometer featuring an electrospray ionization (ESI) interface (Thermo Electron, San Jose, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Phenomenex Luna C18 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 3 μm; Torrance, CA, USA) maintained at 35 °C. An isocratic mobile phase consisting of 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile (50:50, v/v) was delivered at a flow rate of 300 μL/min, with a total analytical run time of 5.0 min. Xcalibur 2.0.7 software governed data acquisition.

Mass spectrometric detection operated in positive-ion multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The optimized ESI source parameters were: spray voltage 4.6 kV, capillary temperature 300 °C, sheath gas (nitrogen) 35 psi, auxiliary gas (nitrogen) 3 psi, and collision gas (argon) pressure 1.5 mTorr. Hesperetin was quantified utilizing a collision energy of 34 V and a specific precursor-to-product ion transition of m/z 272.9 → 152.9 [27].

In vivo bioluminescence imaging

4T1 cells stably expressing luciferase were used for model establishment. On day 7 post-implantation, mice were intraperitoneally injected with D-luciferin sodium salt (122799, PerkinElmer, USA) at 150 mg/kg every three days. Ten minutes after injection, real-time imaging was performed using the IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer, USA). Image acquisition and analysis were conducted with Living Image software (v4.5).

In vivo distribution and near-infrared fluorescence (NIRF) imaging of BP-PEG-S2P@H

To track the biodistribution of the nanocarriers, tumor-bearing mice were divided into three groups (n = 5): free DiR, BP-PEG/DiR, and BP-PEG-S2P@H/DiR. Samples were intravenously administered at equivalent DiR doses of 5 mg/kg. Whole-body macroscopic fluorescence mapping was conducted using the IVIS Spectrum system at 0, 1, 12, and 48 h post-administration (Ex/Em = 748/780 nm). The captured NIRF signals were processed using Living Image software (v4.5). Upon completion of the 48-h time point, the mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) alongside tumors were excised for ex vivo fluorescence imaging to quantify regional tissue accumulation.

Biosafety evaluation

Body weight was monitored to confirm the absence of significant toxicity differences among treatment groups. At the end of treatment, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin blocks. Sectioned tissues underwent standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (G1120, Solarbio, China). Histopathological examination was performed under an optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ci, Japan) to assess morphological changes.

Serum biochemical analysis

Following a 48-h interval post-final treatment, retro-orbital blood collection was performed on the mice. The obtained blood was subsequently centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 min) to isolate the serum. Serum levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), γ-glutamyltransferase (γ-GT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CRE) were determined using an automated biochemical analyzer (AU480, Beckman Coulter, USA).

Monocyte phenotype analysis

Murine peripheral blood from the experimental cohorts (BP-PEG and BP-PEG-S2P@H) was drawn directly into EDTA-coated Microtainer tubes (365974, BD, USA). Erythrocytes were eliminated by incubating the samples with a 1× RBC lysis buffer (R11814389001, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 5 min at ambient temperature with gentle agitation. Following two washing cycles in PBS supplemented with 1% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and centrifugation (300 × g, 5 min), the resulting cell pellets were reconstituted in 100 μL of PBS. The cellular suspensions were then labeled with anti-CD11b-APC (101212, BioLegend, USA) and anti-Ly6C-FITC (128006, BioLegend, USA) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Post-staining washes were conducted before acquiring data on a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (USA). Data were processed with FlowJo v10.8 software to determine the proportions of Ly6Chigh, Ly6Cmid, and Ly6Clow monocytes, thereby assessing the impact of each treatment group on monocyte differentiation profiles.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Tumor tissues embedded in paraffin were sectioned into 5-μm slices. Following a 30-min baking step at 60 °C, the slides underwent deparaffinization and sequential rehydration in descending concentrations of ethanol (100% down to 50%). To unmask antigens, the specimens were heated at 95 °C for 15 min immersed in a Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Once cooled to ambient temperature, the slides were rinsed thrice with PBS and treated with 5% BSA (A8020, Solarbio, China) for 1 h to block non-specific binding sites. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-F4/80 (ab6640, Abcam, UK), anti-IFNγ (BE0055, Bio X Cell), anti-GZMB (ab4059, Abcam, UK), anti-MMP-9 (ab283575, Abcam, UK), anti-CD4 (ab288724, Abcam, UK), anti-CD8α (ab316778, Abcam, UK), anti-Ki67 (ab15580, Abcam, UK), anti-PCNA (ab18197, Abcam, UK), anti-CEBPG (ab81098, Abcam, UK), and anti-SLC7A11 (ab175186, Abcam, UK). On the following day, the tissues were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488- (A-11034) or Alexa Fluor 594- (A-11012) conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 1 h protected from light. DAPI was applied for nuclear counterstaining. After final washing and mounting, the slides were visualized employing a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (Germany). Fluorescence quantification was conducted utilizing ImageJ software (v1.53t, NIH, USA).

For cellular immunofluorescence, BMDMs were seeded onto coverslips and treated with 4T1-conditioned medium in the presence of hesperetin or PBS for 24 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (T9284, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 5 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-PD-L1 antibody (ab213524, Abcam, UK). Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (A-11008, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was then applied for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ.

Flow cytometric analysis of TAM infiltration and drug uptake in tumor tissues

To evaluate immune cell infiltration, resected tumors were enzymatically degraded utilizing a mixture of 1 mg/mL collagenase IV (17104019, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (10104159001, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 1 h at 37 °C. The cellular suspensions were passed through 70-μm nylon meshes, followed by erythrocyte clearance utilizing an RBC lysis buffer. The resulting single-cell suspensions were washed and labeled with a specific fluorochrome panel: LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Aqua (L34957, Life Technologies), anti-CD3 (Alexa Fluor 488, 100210, BioLegend), anti-CD11b (550993, BD Biosciences), anti-CD11c (APC/Cy7, 561241, BD Biosciences), anti-Ly6G (127605, BioLegend), anti-F4/80 (PE/Cy7, 123113, BioLegend), anti-CD86 (Alexa Fluor 700, 105024, BioLegend), and anti-CD206 (APC, 141708, BioLegend). After a 30-min incubation, cells were washed, fixed, and analyzed on a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer. The data were interpreted using FlowJo software (Treestar). Following the exclusion of dead cells (Aqua-positive), the tumor microenvironment was profiled by quantifying the frequencies of T cells (CD3+), dendritic cells (CD11c+), and discrete TAM subpopulations (F4/80+), thus delineating M1 and M2 polarization states [28].

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Unless otherwise stated, in vitro experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates, with technical replicates included where appropriate. For in vivo experiments, each group generally contained five mice unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends.

Data normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed before statistical analysis. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test when data met the assumptions of normal distribution and equal variance. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used. When data did not conform to normal distribution, appropriate nonparametric tests were applied.

For transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, differential expression analyses were performed using the standard statistical pipelines of DESeq2 and MaxQuant, respectively, with multiple-testing correction applied to control the false discovery rate. Differentially expressed genes or proteins were defined using the thresholds |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05.

Considering the relatively small sample sizes in some experiments, including omics analyses with three biological replicates and animal studies with five mice per group, the reliability of the results was strengthened through biological replication, independent experimental validation, and cross-confirmation across in vitro, in vivo, and mechanistic assays. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 or the corresponding bioinformatics software. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Subsequently, BMDMs were cultured with CM derived from different tumor cell lines (4T1, MDA-MB-231, and BT-549). RT-qPCR and WB analyses showed that SLC7A11 expression was significantly increased in BMDMs exposed to tumor-derived CM compared with controls, with the most pronounced induction observed in the 4T1 CM group (Fig. 1D and E). Double immunofluorescence staining further showed that 4T1 CM markedly increased the proportion of CD206+Ki67+ macrophages, suggesting enhanced M2-like polarization and proliferative activity (Fig. 1F). These results indicate that the TNBC microenvironment promotes SLC7A11 expression and drives macrophages toward a protumor M2-like phenotype.

Subsequently, BMDMs cultured in either normal medium or 4T1 CM were treated with the ferroptosis inducers Erastin or RSL3. CCK-8 assays showed that BMDMs cultured in 4T1 CM exhibited greater viability (Fig. S1A–B). Live/dead cell staining revealed that the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 effectively attenuated Erastin- or RSL3-induced cytotoxicity (Fig. S1C–D). These findings indicate that culturing BMDMs in 4T1 CM reduces ferroptotic activity, thereby suppressing ferroptosis-induced cell death.

To evaluate the effect of hesperetin on ferroptotic activity in TAMs, intracellular Fe2+ levels in PBS, Hesperetin, and Hesperetin + Fer-1-treated TAMs were examined by FerroOrange staining (Fig. 1G) and an iron assay kit (Fig. 1H), while MDA content was measured using a commercial kit (Fig. 1I) and lipid ROS levels were detected by BODIPY-C11 staining (Fig. 1J). WB analysis revealed a marked decrease in SLC7A11 expression in the hesperetin group (Fig. 1K). Importantly, these effects were reversed by Fer-1, confirming that hesperetin induces ferroptosis in TAMs.

We then investigated whether hesperetin-induced ferroptosis contributed to macrophage polarization. Flow cytometric analysis showed that hesperetin treatment significantly reduced the proportion of CD206+ M2-like macrophages while increasing CD86+ M1-like macrophages compared with PBS treatment (Fig. 1L). However, Fer-1 largely reversed these changes, restoring M2-like polarization and reducing M1-like polarization (Fig. 1L). These data suggest that hesperetin promotes the transition of macrophages from an M2-like to an M1-like phenotype by inducing ferroptosis.

Because ferroptosis is closely associated with oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, intracellular ROS production and mitochondrial status were further examined. DCFH-DA staining showed that hesperetin significantly increased intracellular ROS levels (Fig. S2A). TEM revealed that hesperetin-treated cells exhibited mitochondrial shrinkage and disrupted cristae compared with controls, indicative of structural damage (Fig. S2B). MitoTracker staining and JC-1 assays demonstrated that hesperetin also impaired ΔΨm (Fig. S2C–D). These mitochondrial alterations further corroborate the role of hesperetin in regulating ferroptosis in TAMs.

Analysis of TCGA data further revealed that high CEBPG expression was significantly correlated with overall survival and progression-free interval in breast cancer patients (Fig. 2D). We further generated CEBPG-knockdown TAMs and confirmed the knockdown efficiency of sh-CEBPG-1 and sh-CEBPG-2 by RT-qPCR and WB (Fig. 2E and F). FerroOrange staining and iron assay results showed that CEBPG knockdown significantly increased intracellular Fe2+ levels compared with the sh-NC group (Fig. S3A and B). In parallel, MDA content and BODIPY-C11-detected lipid ROS levels were also markedly elevated following CEBPG depletion (Fig. S3C and D). These findings indicate that CEBPG knockdown enhances ferroptotic activity in TAMs.

Subsequently, the potential interaction between CEBPG and SLC7A11 was investigated. Binding sites between the SLC7A11 promoter and CEBPG were first predicted using the JASPAR database (Fig. 2G). ChIP-qPCR analysis further confirmed the binding of CEBPG to SLC7A11 (Fig. 2H). A dual-luciferase reporter assay was then performed with mutated binding sites to validate this interaction. CEBPG significantly enhanced the reporter activity of SLC7A11-WT but did not affect SLC7A11-MUT (Fig. 2I), demonstrating that CEBPG regulates SLC7A11 transcription.

To further determine whether the CEBPG/SLC7A11 axis mediates hesperetin-induced ferroptosis, CEBPG overexpression and SLC7A11 knockdown were introduced during hesperetin treatment. TAMs were divided into the PBS + oe-NC, hesperetin + oe-NC, hesperetin + oe-CEBPG, hesperetin + oe-CEBPG + siNC, and hesperetin + oe-CEBPG + siSLC7A11 groups (Fig. 2J). RT-qPCR and WB analyses showed that hesperetin markedly reduced CEBPG and SLC7A11 expression compared with PBS treatment. Conversely, CEBPG overexpression restored both CEBPG and SLC7A11 expression under hesperetin treatment (Fig. 2K and L).

Consistent with these expression changes, hesperetin significantly increased intracellular Fe2+ levels, MDA content, lipid ROS accumulation, and the GSSG/GSH ratio compared with the PBS + oe-NC group (Fig. 2M–Q). However, CEBPG overexpression attenuated these ferroptosis-related changes, indicating that CEBPG restoration partly blocked hesperetin-induced ferroptosis. Importantly, silencing SLC7A11 in CEBPG-overexpressing TAMs reversed this protective effect, leading to renewed increases in Fe2+ levels, MDA content, lipid ROS, and GSSG/GSH ratio compared with the hesperetin + oe-CEBPG + siNC group (Fig. 2M–Q).

Together, these results demonstrate that hesperetin promotes ferroptosis in TAMs primarily by inhibiting the CEBPG/SLC7A11 signaling axis.

Consistently, tumor growth curves and final tumor weights showed that hesperetin significantly suppressed tumor progression (Fig. 3F–H). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated that the proliferation markers Ki67 and PCNA were substantially reduced in tumors from the hesperetin group compared with those from PBS-treated mice (Fig. 3I and J). The impact of Hesperetin on macrophage infiltration was further assessed. Analysis of peripheral blood monocyte phenotypes showed that Hesperetin did not alter the frequency distribution of Ly6Chigh, Ly6Cmid, and Ly6Clow monocytes compared with PBS controls (Fig. S4A). However, F4/80 staining revealed a notable reduction in macrophage infiltration within tumors after hesperetin treatment (Fig. S4B). Flow cytometry further confirmed that the proportion of F4/80+CD11b+ macrophages was significantly lower in tumors from hesperetin-treated mice than in controls (Fig. S4C). In addition, Transwell assays showed that hesperetin impaired the migratory capacity of BMDMs (Fig. S4D). These results indicate that hesperetin may exert its antitumor effect partly by limiting TAM recruitment and reducing macrophage-mediated protumor activity.

To further explore the clinical relevance of the CEBPG/SLC7A11 axis in immune regulation, TCGA-BRCA data were analyzed. High expression of CEBPG and SLC7A11 was associated with poorer prognosis (Fig. S5A and B), and their expression levels were positively correlated (Fig. S5C). Both genes also showed positive correlations with M2 macrophage marker genes (Fig. S5D). Moreover, CIBERSORT-based analysis indicated that CEBPG and SLC7A11 expression levels were closely associated with macrophage infiltration in tumor tissues, particularly M2 macrophages (Fig. S5E–H), suggesting that this axis may participate in regulating TAM recruitment or polarization. These findings support the involvement of the CEBPG/SLC7A11 axis in TAM infiltration, M2-like polarization, and tumor immune evasion.

Hesperetin can partially enhance the efficacy of aPD-L1 immunotherapy in TNBC

RT-qPCR showed that PD-L1 expression was increased in BMDMs after hesperetin treatment, with the highest expression observed in cells cultured with tumor-derived CM (Fig. S6A). The morphology and dimensions of BP, BP-PEG, and BP-PEG-S2P were characterized using TEM and AFM. TEM images revealed that all three nanomaterials exhibited discrete nanosheet morphologies with lateral dimensions of approximately 150 nm (Fig. 5B). AFM images showed a slight increase in BP thickness after PEG modification, attributable to the surface coating of PEG-NH2 or S2P-PEG-NH2 (Fig. 5C and D). XPS analysis of BP-PEG-S2P showed characteristic peaks corresponding to P 2p, C 1s, O 1s, and S 2p. FT-IR further confirmed the introduction of the PEG and S2P peaks in BP-PEG-S2P (Fig. 5E and F). Moreover, time-dependent DLS analysis of particle size (Fig. 5G and Fig. S7A) and phosphate release measurements (Fig. 5H) indicated that PEG modification markedly improved BP colloidal stability, as bare BP degraded more rapidly in water and serum-containing solutions. Zeta potential measurements demonstrated that BP-PEG and BP-PEG-S2P exhibited lower surface charges than bare BP (Fig. 5I and Fig. S7B), further confirming the successful surface modification with PEG-NH2 or S2P-PEG-NH2.

BP-PEG-S2P was further loaded with hesperetin and termed BP-PEG-S2P@H (Fig. 5A). The results showed that BP-PEG-S2P@H exhibited favorable drug-loading capacity (LC = 18.3%, EE = 91%) (Fig. 5J) and good colloidal stability (Fig. 5G and Fig. S7A–B).

The hesperetin release profile was evaluated in PBS containing FBS to simulate the TME, characterized by mild acidity and elevated oxidative stress. The results demonstrated that BP-PEG-S2P@H released hesperetin in a pH- and oxidative stress-responsive manner (Fig. 5K and L). This responsive release was primarily attributed to the selective degradation of BP-PEG-S2P under these conditions (Fig. 5M).

The targeting capability of BP-PEG-S2P@H was then assessed. NIRF dye Cy7 was conjugated to BP-PEG-S2P@H and to BP-PEG lacking S2P peptide modification. BP-PEG-S2P@H-Cy7 was specifically internalized by TAMs, particularly the M2 phenotype, whereas BP-PEG-Cy7 exhibited minimal accumulation in TAMs (Fig. 5N–Q). To further verify the reliability of the targeting ability of our nanoparticles, we knocked down STAB1, the target of S2P, in BMDMs. The results showed that macrophage uptake of the nanoparticles was markedly reduced after STAB1 knockdown (Fig. 5R).

Cell viability assays indicated that BP exhibited no significant cytotoxicity toward BMDMs or RAW264.7 cells at the tested concentrations, confirming its favorable biocompatibility (Fig. S7C). Further investigations revealed that BP-PEG-S2P@H displayed dose-dependent cytotoxicity, selectively eliminating TAMs and M0 macrophages at higher concentrations while exerting no adverse effects on 4T1 cells, suggesting targeted TAM killing through induction of intracellular ferroptosis (Fig. S7D).

BP-PEG-S2P@H suppresses TNBC cell migration and invasion by promoting TAM ferroptosis and polarization

TAMs were divided into the Control, BP-PEG, and BP-PEG-S2P@H groups. RT-qPCR and WB analyses showed that BP-PEG alone had little effect on CEBPG and SLC7A11 expression compared with the control group. Hesperetin reduced the expression of both molecules, whereas BP-PEG-S2P@H produced the most pronounced inhibitory effect on CEBPG and SLC7A11, accompanied by significant changes in GPX4 protein expression (Fig. S8A–B).

Ferroptosis-related indicators were then evaluated. FerroOrange staining and iron assay results showed increased intracellular Fe2+ levels after BP-PEG-S2P@H treatment (Fig. S8C and D). Consistently, MDA content, lipid ROS accumulation, and the GSSG/GSH ratio were also significantly elevated in the BP-PEG-S2P@H group compared with the BP-PEG or hesperetin group (Fig. S8E–G). BP-PEG alone induced mild increases in ferroptosis-associated markers, suggesting that BP may exert a certain oxidative stress-regulating effect. However, BP-PEG-S2P@H showed a stronger ferroptosis-inducing capacity than either BP-PEG or hesperetin alone, indicating that the nanosystem synergistically enhances TAM ferroptosis, at least partly through inhibition of the CEBPG/SLC7A11 axis.

The effect of BP-PEG-S2P@H on macrophage polarization was further examined. RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with IL-4 to induce M2-like polarization and then treated with BP-PEG, hesperetin, or BP-PEG-S2P@H. Flow cytometry showed that all treatments increased the M1 marker CD86 and decreased the M2 marker CD206 compared with the control group, with BP-PEG-S2P@H producing the strongest effect (Fig. S9A and B). Confocal microscopy confirmed these results, showing enhanced CD86 fluorescence and reduced CD206 fluorescence after treatment, particularly in the BP-PEG-S2P@H group (Fig. S9C and D).

ELISA analysis further demonstrated that BP-PEG-S2P@H markedly increased secretion of the M1-associated cytokine IL-12 while reducing the M2-associated cytokine IL-10 (Fig. S9E and F). WB analysis of polarization-related signaling proteins showed reduced expression of M2-associated markers, including ArgI, together with increased expression of M1-related proteins such as iNOS (Fig. S9G). These findings indicate that BP-PEG-S2P@H effectively repolarizes macrophages from an M2-like phenotype toward an M1-like phenotype, with greater potency than single-agent treatment.

The in vitro anti-metastatic effect of BP-PEG-S2P@H was further evaluated using wound-healing and Transwell assays. 4T1 cells were divided into four groups: Control, M2, BP-PEG + M2, and BP-PEG-S2P@H + M2. In the wound-healing assay, both the Control and M2 CM groups exhibited enhanced migratory capacity, whereas BP-PEG-S2P@H + M2 treatment significantly suppressed 4T1 cell migration compared with BP-PEG + M2 (Fig. S10A–B). In the Transwell assay, the Control and M2 CM groups displayed nearly complete coverage of the membrane undersurface, indicative of strong invasive and migratory potential. In contrast, BP-PEG-S2P@H + M2 treatment markedly reduced the invasion and migration indices of 4T1 cells (Fig. S10C–D). These results suggest that BP-PEG-S2P@H inhibits the migration and invasion of highly metastatic 4T1 cells by reversing the protumor phenotype of TAMs.

Discussion

TNBC is characterized by the absence of ER, PR, and HER2 expression, which greatly restricts the availability of targeted therapeutic options. Although immune checkpoint blockade has expanded treatment strategies for TNBC, only a subset of patients derives durable benefit, largely due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [29,30]. Among the cellular components of the TME, M2-like TAMs are highly enriched and suppress antitumor T-cell activity through the secretion of immunosuppressive mediators, thereby contributing to immune tolerance and therapeutic resistance [31]. In this study, we targeted TAMs as a key immunoregulatory population and developed a strategy that simultaneously induced TAM ferroptosis and promoted M2-to-M1 phenotypic conversion, thereby remodeling the immune microenvironment and enhancing antitumor immunity.

BP nanosheets have attracted increasing attention as drug delivery platforms because of their large specific surface area, biodegradability, and high drug-loading capacity [16,17]. However, their instability under physiological conditions and lack of active targeting capacity limit their in vivo application. To overcome these limitations, PEG modification was introduced to improve colloidal stability and prolong circulation, while the S2P peptide was used to confer TAM-targeting ability. This active-targeting design markedly enhanced nanoparticle accumulation in tumor-associated immune cells compared with delivery systems relying mainly on the enhanced permeability and retention effect. Hesperetin, a natural flavonoid with antioxidant and immunomodulatory activities, has been limited by poor solubility and low bioavailability [32,33]. Loading hesperetin onto BP-PEG-S2P improved its delivery efficiency and enabled synergistic interaction with the BP nanoplatform. Compared with liposomes, which may suffer from drug leakage, or poorly degradable inorganic carriers, BP can degrade into relatively nontoxic phosphate products, supporting its biosafety as a nanocarrier [34]. Moreover, the mild oxidative stress generated during BP degradation may sensitize cells to ferroptosis and contribute to macrophage repolarization toward an antitumor phenotype [35]. Thus, BP-PEG-S2P@H functions not only as a targeted delivery vehicle but also as an active immunomodulatory platform.

Ferroptosis has been widely studied in tumor cells, with major attention given to regulators such as GPX4, ACSL4, and SLC7A11 [14,[36], [37], [38]]. However, the regulatory mechanisms governing ferroptosis in immunosuppressive TAMs remain insufficiently understood. Through transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, we identified CEBPG as a potential upstream regulator involved in TAM ferroptosis. Mechanistically, CEBPG directly bound to the SLC7A11 promoter and promoted its transcriptional activation. BP-PEG-S2P@H suppressed the CEBPG/SLC7A11 axis, leading to increased Fe2+ accumulation, lipid ROS generation, MDA production, and redox imbalance, ultimately inducing ferroptosis in TAMs. This finding extends the current understanding of ferroptosis from tumor cells to immunosuppressive myeloid cells and suggests that the CEBPG/SLC7A11 axis may represent a promising target for TAM-centered immunotherapy.

In addition to SLC7A11, CEBPG may participate in broader transcriptional regulation of ferroptosis-related genes, including GPX4, FTH1, and ACSL4, thereby contributing to a lipid peroxide defense network [39]. TAMs are characterized by active lipid metabolism and altered iron handling, which may render them particularly vulnerable to oxidative stress once redox-protective pathways are disrupted. In this context, inhibition of CEBPG may impair both cystine-dependent glutathione synthesis through SLC7A11 and the broader antioxidant buffering capacity of TAMs. This may explain why BP-PEG-S2P@H preferentially disrupts redox homeostasis in M2-like TAMs and promotes their ferroptotic susceptibility, while showing limited nonspecific cytotoxicity toward tumor cells. Nevertheless, whether CEBPG regulates additional ferroptosis-related targets in TAMs requires further validation.

Previous approaches to TAM reprogramming have often focused on single signaling pathways, which may be insufficient to simultaneously alter macrophage survival and function [40]. In contrast, BP-PEG-S2P@H exerted dual regulatory effects by inducing ferroptotic death in TAMs and reprogramming surviving macrophages from an M2-like to an M1-like phenotype. In vitro, BP-PEG-S2P@H increased IL-12 production while reducing IL-10 secretion, indicating a shift toward a pro-inflammatory macrophage state. In vivo, treatment reduced F4/80+ macrophage infiltration, decreased M2-like TAM abundance, enhanced M1-like macrophage polarization, and promoted CD8+ T-cell activation. These effects collectively reshaped the immunosuppressive TME and created a more favorable immune context for antitumor responses. Compared with strategies that only induce macrophage polarization, this combined “ferroptosis induction plus functional reprogramming” approach may provide more durable immune activation.

Another important finding of this study is that BP-PEG-S2P@H enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy. TAM infiltration and M2-like polarization are major contributors to immune checkpoint blockade resistance. By reducing TAM-mediated immunosuppression and increasing T-cell infiltration and effector activity, BP-PEG-S2P@H appeared to prime the tumor microenvironment for improved checkpoint blockade response. In the 4T1 xenograft model, BP-PEG-S2P@H combined with anti-PD-L1 produced stronger tumor inhibition than anti-PD-L1 monotherapy, supporting a treatment concept in which TAM-targeted microenvironmental remodeling precedes or accompanies immune checkpoint blockade. This strategy is consistent with the concept of immune priming and may provide a feasible approach to improving immunotherapy responsiveness in TNBC.

Serum biochemical analysis and H&E staining revealed no obvious toxicity. BP can be degraded into inorganic phosphate, and hesperetin itself has relatively high safety, both of which provide a solid safety basis for clinical application. Compared with some metal-based nanomaterials, this platform carries a lower metabolic risk. However, the long-term effects of immune stimulation and differences in responses under different patient immune backgrounds still need to be evaluated. In addition, the stability and consistency of the formulation during large-scale production remain key issues that must be resolved for future translation.

In summary, this study developed a TAM-targeted nanoplatform, BP-PEG-S2P@H, that suppresses the CEBPG/SLC7A11 axis, induces ferroptosis in TAMs, and promotes macrophage repolarization toward an antitumor phenotype. By reducing TAM-mediated immunosuppression and enhancing T-cell activation, BP-PEG-S2P@H effectively remodeled the TNBC immune microenvironment and synergistically improved the antitumor efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy. The scientific significance of this work lies in identifying a previously underexplored CEBPG/SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis mechanism in TAMs, while its translational value lies in providing a potential nano-immunotherapeutic strategy for immune-refractory TNBC. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the current findings were mainly obtained from TNBC models, and validation in additional tumor types remains necessary. Second, due to the lack of suitable TNBC clinical samples for TAM-specific analysis, we were unable to directly assess CEBPG expression in TAM-enriched regions of patient-derived TNBC tissues or examine its relationship with the immunosuppressive microenvironment, which limits the clinical generalizability of the findings. Third, the long-term safety of BP-PEG-S2P@H and its capacity to induce durable antitumor immune memory have not yet been fully evaluated. Finally, the relatively limited sample size may increase the influence of intragroup variability and the risk of false-positive or false-negative findings. Future studies should incorporate patient-derived models, clinical TNBC specimens, expanded safety evaluations, and optimized combination regimens to further support the clinical translation of this TAM-targeted nano-immunotherapy strategy.

Conclusion

This study developed a TAM-targeted, stimuli-responsive nanodelivery system, BP-PEG-S2P@H, which achieved precise release of hesperetin and effective immune modulation in TNBC. The findings demonstrated that hesperetin suppressed the CEBPG/SLC7A11 axis, thereby inducing ferroptosis in TAMs and promoting their M1 polarization, which markedly reduced the recruitment and protumor activity of M2-type TAMs, reversed the immunosuppressive microenvironment, and inhibited TNBC progression. BP-PEG-S2P@H not only improved the stability and targeting capacity of hesperetin but also synergistically enhanced its efficacy in combination with aPD-L1 immunotherapy, while exhibiting excellent biocompatibility and in vivo safety.
