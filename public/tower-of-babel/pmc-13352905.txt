Japanese hematologists’ and oncologists’ perceptions of patient preferences for shared decision-making
Authors: Yasuyuki Arai, Timothy Bolt, Hikaru Onishi, Michael LoPresti, Victoria Arguissain, Jörg Mahlich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesShared decision-making (SDM) is a key component of patient-centered oncology care, yet little is known about how Japanese physicians perceive their patients’ roles in treatment decisions for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). This study aimed to assess physicians’ assumptions about patient preferences regarding SDM and to identify factors associated with these perceptions.ResultsAn online survey was completed by 227 hematologists and oncologists in Japan who had managed at least one DLBCL case in the past year. Most physicians believed patients prefer collaborative decision-making: 34.4% selected joint decision-making, 31.7% patient-led after physician input, and 26.4% physician-led incorporating patient input. Few perceived patients as preferring full autonomy (7.5%), and none indicated fully physician-led decisions. Greater experience with refractory patients was linked to more physician-led perceptions (OR = 0.95 per patient, p = 0.0033). Female physicians more often endorsed patient-led preferences (OR = 0.39, p = 0.0436), while those aged ≥60 years favored physician-led models (OR = 2.33, p = 0.0454). University hospital affiliation showed a trend toward more patient-led perceptions (OR = 0.65, p = 0.087). Overall, Japanese physicians predominantly assumed patients prefer shared rather than extreme decision-making roles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07863-x.Keywords: Shared decision making, Clinical decision-making, Patient-centered care, Physician preference, Hematologists, CAR-T therapy

Introduction

Shared decision-making (SDM) is a collaborative process in which patients and healthcare providers work together to make medical decisions [1]. This approach, increasingly embraced in the healthcare community, relies on effective two-way communication and joint agreement on both therapeutic targets and management plans. When implemented effectively, SDM has been associated with improved patient satisfaction, greater adherence to therapy, and better clinical outcomes [2]. Importantly, supporting patient preferences is essential to the process. While many patients value active participation, some may prefer a more paternalistic model in which the physician takes the lead [3].

Studies conducted in the United States since the 1980s consistently show that most patients wish to be involved in treatment decisions, with positive effects on treatment adherence and health outcomes [4–9]. Similar findings have been reported in Europe [10, 11].

However, evidence from Japan is more limited. Early research suggested a predominantly passive role for Japanese patients, particularly among older adults who tended to trust their physicians to make treatment decisions. For example, a 1996 study found that patients aged ≥65 years often deferred decision-making to their doctors [12]. More recent Japanese studies, however, indicate a gradual shift toward collaborative decision-making, particularly among younger patients and in less serious disease contexts. For instance, a study of Japanese diabetic patients found that 71% preferred a collaborative role, 17% a passive role, and 12% an active role [13]. Preferences varied by age group and clinical scenario, with younger patients favoring patient-centered care for less severe conditions, while older patients were more likely to defer to their physicians [14]. Another study involving Japanese patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) reported that 61.6% preferred a collaborative role and 38.4% a passive role, with no participants choosing an active role in which they alone made the final decision. Younger age and employment outside the home were associated with stronger preferences for SDM [15].

This trend toward more active participation contrasts with traditional Japanese medical culture, which has historically favored physician-led decision-making [14].Recent findings suggest increasing openness among patients to autonomous or shared roles across different clinical indications [16–18]. However, a gap remains between patient preferences and physician perceptions. For example, in a study of prostate cancer patients, while nearly half of patients preferred SDM, only one-third of physicians assumed this to be the case [19]. Such mismatches have been linked to lower patient satisfaction, with correlations observed in conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and prostate cancer [20–22].

Against this background, the present study aims to investigate Japanese hematologists’ and oncologists’ perceptions of their patients’ preferred role in the medical decision-making process. We focus on physicians who treat patients diagnosed with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). DLBCL provides a unique context for studying shared decision making, as it is a therapeutic area in which multiple treatment options are available, each with distinct efficacy, safety, and burden profiles. As a result, treatment decisions inherently involve complex trade-offs that must balance potential clinical benefits against risks, tolerability, and resource use. For refractory patients Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMP) such as chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapies are available in Japan as well as more traditional therapies such as high-dose chemotherapy with Autologous Stem Cell Transplant (ASCT). ATMPs in particular are high costs treatments with acquisition cost of around 230,000 USD in Japan [23]. Given the substantial co-payments required, affordability may also need to be discussed as part of the SDM process. Although co-payment ceilings vary by income, higher-income patients must still pay 1% of total costs in addition to the standard ceiling [24].

Methods

As part of a treatment preference study [25], an online survey was carried out by INTAGE Healthcare Inc. (Tokyo, Japan) with physicians drawn from a panel maintained by PLAMED Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). The market-research panel includes roughly 900 hematologists, along with some oncologists, representing about 32% of Japan’s registered hematologists [26]. Eligible respondents had to have managed at least one case of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) in the preceding 12 months.

The study adhered to the approved protocol, International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) standards, relevant Japanese regulations, applicable clinical research guidelines, and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study received approval from the Toukeikai Kitamachi Clinic Ethics Review Board (approval no. KXJ09854) prior to the administration of the online survey, with informed consent obtained from all participants. Data quality checks were applied beyond eligibility screening, with respondents excluded if participants selected the same option for every task or if the reported age was incompatible with the stated years of clinical experience.

Drawing on a questionnaire developed by Baars et al. [10] on preferences for SDM, physicians were asked the following question: “Please indicate which of the following best describes your thoughts on treatment decisions for DLBCL patients.”

We assessed the association between conjectured decision-making preference and hematologists/oncologists and institutional characteristics using ordinal logistic regressions for each characteristic of interest, reporting odds ratios (OR). Before the study, with clinical advisors, the research team identified the following physician and hospital characteristics as potentially impacting responses: number of refractory patients treated, physician sex, physician age, CAR-T treatment experience (number of patients treated), hospital type (university hospital or not), hospital size, number of qualified CAR-T physicians, and geographic region. Estimates are reported in their original units (e.g., per refractory patient, per qualified CAR-T doctor at institution, per year of age) rather than normalized to a common scale, allowing direct interpretation of real-world effects rather than purely relative comparisons across factors.

Univariable regression analyses were applied to examine associations between physician characteristics and support for SDM given the exploratory nature of the research, the sample size, and the potential for correlation among physician- and institution-level characteristics. p-values are unadjusted for multiplicity, and associations should be interpreted as numeric trends rather than definitive statistical significance. Exposure-related variables such as the number of refractory patients treated were right-skewed and modestly correlated with institutional characteristics, including hospital size (Pearson r = 0.2170) and number of CAR-T-qualified physicians (Pearson r = 0.4173), which may reflect differences in case mix and referral patterns. Inclusion of multiple related predictors or interaction terms in a multivariable model could therefore increase multicollinearity and result in unstable coefficient estimates. Univariable analyses were used to allow each characteristic to be examined independently. We acknowledge that this approach does not fully account for potential residual confounding related to institutional characteristics or patient case mix.

Results

While the full sample characteristics are described elsewhere [27] we report the answers to the questions on SDM and relate them to physicians' characteristics. In the study we used the above ordinal categories for the answer to the question eliciting the responding physician’s perception of patients’ preferences. Of the 231 respondents (response rate of 25.7%), three respondents replied, “I do not know” and one respondent “I do not want to answer”, hence the subsequent analysis is undertaken with the 227 remaining respondents (Supplementary tables 1 and 2). Of the 5 response options for shared decision making, the extremes were least popular with no physician choosing 5 (“Patients would prefer that physicians make treatment decisions alone”) and 7.36% selecting the other extreme 1 (“I think patients prefer to make their own treatment decisions”) (Table 1).

In the univariate analysis, the number of refractory patients treated by the physician was consistently associated with preferences for SDM (OR = 0.95, p = 0.0033) (Table 2). Given that the reported number of refractory patients ranged from 0 to 100, this odds ratio, on a per-patient basis, represents a potential effect across the observed range. Among the results is that higher numbers of refractory patients were associated with a stronger belief that patients prefer physician-led decision-making with patient input rather than fully patient-led decisions. As the question is framed about the physician's expectation of patient preferences, this experience with many patients is of additional importance, as these physicians can be expected to have the largest pool of these patient interactions for DLBCL to draw on. Physician sex was also statistically significant: female physicians (OR = 0.39, p = 0.0436) were more likely to favor patient-led approaches, with 62.5% of female respondents selecting, “Patients prefer to make their own treatment decisions after hearing the physician’s opinion.” Notably, this effect was significant despite the small number of female respondents (16 of 214), suggesting a robust difference by physician sex among the 198 respondents reporting their sex.

Physician age showed a fairly consistent impact on SDM attitudes, with older physicians tending toward physician-led decision-making with patient input. The full range ordinal age variable by decade had an OR of 1.19, but lacked significance (p = 0.135). Only physicians aged ≥60 years reached statistical significance (OR = 2.33, p = 0.0454), while other age categories (≥40, ≥50) had odds ratios in the same direction but did not meet the 5% significance threshold.

Discussion

Shared decision-making (SDM) has gained increasing traction globally, including in Japan. In our study, 73.6% of physicians reported believing that their patients preferred a collaborative or active role in decision-making. This estimate is consistent with prior findings among Japanese oncologists and aligns with international evidence suggesting that most patients favor shared roles in medical decisions [28]. However, our results reflect physicians’ perceptions of patient preferences rather than patients’ actual preferences. A substantial body of literature indicates that physicians often misjudge patients’ desired level of involvement, frequently underestimating their willingness to participate [19, 29–31].Such gaps in perception have been documented across different clinical settings and may lead to systematic mismatches between how decisions are made and how patients would prefer them to be made.

These mismatches carry important clinical and ethical implications. Clinically, inaccurate assumptions about patient preferences may reduce adherence, limit patient engagement, and negatively affect outcomes [32]. Ethically, they raise concerns for patient autonomy and the validity of informed consent. Even when physicians intend to act in patients’ best interests, decisions based on incorrect assumptions may inadvertently reinforce paternalistic practices. Our findings therefore highlight not only the perceived support for SDM but also the need to critically examine how accurately these perceptions reflect patient realities.

Physician characteristics appear to shape these perceptions. Younger physicians were more likely to assume a patient preference for SDM, consistent with prior research suggesting that older physicians are more inclined toward paternalistic models [33]. Similarly, female physicians have been shown to engage more frequently in patient-centered communication, including greater elicitation of patient preferences and more participatory dialogue [34–36].These differences suggest that physicians’ assumptions may partly reflect their own communication styles and attitudes rather than objective assessments of patient preferences.

Practice setting also appears to influence perceptions. Physicians in university hospitals were more likely to assume patient interest in SDM, possibly reflecting institutional cultures that emphasize patient-centered care [37]. However, this association may be confounded by disease severity and case mix [18, 19]as patients treated in tertiary centers are often more complex and may have different informational needs and preferences. These structural and contextual factors may shape not only how decisions are made but also how physicians interpret patient preferences.

Despite growing support for SDM, implementation remains challenging. Evidence from other settings suggests that even when physicians endorse SDM in principle, paternalistic decision-making persists in practice, often justified by assumptions about patients’ capacity or willingness to participate [38]. In Japan, additional barriers exist. The process of informed consent is frequently characterized by one-sided information delivery rather than meaningful engagement [39], and studies indicate that many patients do not fully understand key aspects of their treatment when providing consent [40]. Although SDM consultations have been reimbursed in Japan since 2014, uptake remains low [41, 42], suggesting that structural support alone is insufficient without corresponding changes in clinical practice and communication.

The increasing availability of complex and high-cost treatments further underscores the importance of aligning decisions with patient preferences. In the context of DLBCL, options such as CAR-T cell therapy and autologous stem cell transplantation involve substantial trade-offs in terms of efficacy, toxicity, logistics, and financial burden. These decisions are highly preference sensitive. If physicians’ perceptions do not accurately reflect patients’ values regarding risk tolerance, quality of life, or financial considerations, treatment choices may fail to align with what matters most to patients.

Our study therefore points to a critical gap: while physicians perceive strong patient support for SDM, it remains unclear to what extent these perceptions correspond to actual patient preferences. Addressing this gap requires moving beyond physician-reported assumptions toward direct assessment of patient perspectives. Future research should explicitly compare physician perceptions with patient-reported preferences and examine how discrepancies influence decision-making processes and outcomes. Interventions such as structured preference elicitation tools, decision aids, and communication training may help reduce these mismatches.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size and moderate response rate limit statistical power and raise the possibility of selection bias. The exploratory design and reliance on univariable analyses preclude causal inference and do not adequately account for potential confounding. In addition, correlations between institutional characteristics and clinical exposure variables may have influenced the observed associations. Finally, the use of physician self-report introduces the risk of social desirability bias. Most importantly, the study captures only physicians’ perspectives and does not include patient-reported data, limiting conclusions about actual alignment between physicians and patients. In conclusion, although physicians report strong support for shared decision-making (SDM), these findings highlight the need to critically assess the accuracy of such perceptions. Ensuring that medical decisions truly reflect patient preferences requires not only endorsing SDM in principle but also systematically eliciting, understanding, and integrating patient perspectives into clinical practice.
