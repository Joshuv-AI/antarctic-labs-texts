Nurses’ Ethical Reasoning About Digital Ethical Risks: A Vignette‐Elicited Qualitative Study
Authors: Betül Bal, Nagihan Köroğlu Kaba, Emine Apaydin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis qualitative study aimed to explore how nurses reason ethically about potential digital ethical risks arising from the routine use of digital systems in clinical practice and the strategies they propose to address these risks in response to vignette‐based scenarios.BackgroundDigital systems shape nursing documentation, monitoring, communication, education, and decision‐making. Although they support care, digital ethical risks may arise when digital outputs are accepted without clinical verification, patients are not adequately informed, or data protection is weak.DesignA vignette‐elicited qualitative design was used.MethodsThe study was conducted in September 2025 with 16 nurses working in three public hospitals in Central Anatolia, Türkiye. Data were generated through face‐to‐face interviews using seven vignette‐based scenarios and analyzed using Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsThree main themes were generated through the analysis of nurses’ ethical reasoning in response to the vignette‐based scenarios: (i) nurses’ purposes for using digital systems, (ii) digital ethical risks in nursing, and (iii) strategies to prevent digital ethical risks. The findings within Themes 2 and 3 were organized into four subthemes—nonmaleficence and beneficence, autonomy and respect for persons, justice and equity, privacy and confidentiality—and further structured across three researcher‐developed categories: nurse, digital system, and organization.ConclusionsParticipants’ interpretations of the vignette‐based scenarios highlighted potential digital ethical risks that may require nurses to combine clinical judgment with ethical reasoning when using digital systems.Implications for NursingParticipants’ responses may inform digital ethics education by highlighting the importance of verifying digital data through bedside assessment, questioning potentially unsafe digital outputs, protecting confidentiality, obtaining meaningful consent, and recognizing potential risks in documentation, alarms, communication, and data sharing.Implications for Health PolicyParticipant‐identified priorities may inform institutional consideration of protocols for documentation, alarm management, mobile communication, image sharing, access control, patient information, accessibility, and accountability in digital nursing care.

Introduction

The integration of digital systems into healthcare is reshaping routine nursing practice. Digital systems comprise computing platforms, software, connectivity tools, sensors, and health information systems that support the collection, storage, access, transmission, use, and analysis of health‐related information (Ackerhans et al. 2024; Jeilani and Hussein 2025; Wei et al. 2025). In nursing practice, these systems include electronic health records, clinical monitoring and alarm systems, device interfaces, mobile communication tools, digital imaging systems, and emerging AI‐supported applications (Bal et al. 2026; Bayram et al. 2026; Jeilani and Hussein 2025; Wei et al. 2025). These systems support nurses’ documentation, patient monitoring, intra‐team communication, access to clinical information, and clinical decision‐making processes (Ackerhans et al. 2024; Bodur et al. 2025; Jeilani and Hussein 2025; Wei et al. 2025).

The routine use of digital systems supports nursing care but may also entail digital ethical risks. Electronic health records may improve access to patient information, yet documentation errors, copy‐paste practices, unclear information sources, and usability problems can threaten patient safety and information integrity (Bowman 2013; Al Bahrani and Medhi 2023; Corby et al. 2025). Clinical monitoring and alarm systems provide real‐time patient data, yet frequent alarms may contribute to alarm fatigue, missed alarms, and delayed clinical responses (Woo 2020). Mobile phones and messaging applications may support rapid team communication. At the same time, patient‐related information shared through these systems may raise concerns about privacy, confidentiality, information safety, professional boundaries, and delayed digital responses (de Jong et al. 2020; Vukušić Rukavina et al. 2021; Bal et al. 2026). This situation indicates that digital ethical risks in nursing should be evaluated within routine care technologies and everyday clinical workflows (Bayram et al. 2026).

Digital ethical risks may arise when ethical principles are compromised during the use, documentation, storage, transmission, sharing, or interpretation of patient‐related information in digital systems (Varkey 2021; ICN 2021; Liaw et al. 2020). In nursing practice, these risks can be evaluated through core clinical and professional ethics principles, including beneficence, nonmaleficence, autonomy, justice, informed consent, confidentiality, patient rights, and professional accountability (Varkey 2021; ICN 2021; ANA 2022). In digital systems, these principles extend to data quality, secure access, transparent data use, data‐sharing governance, and accountability across the data life cycle (Liaw et al. 2020). This expanding ethical domain requires nurses to evaluate the implications of digital practices for patient care within the context of clinical practice, ethical principles, and professional accountability (ICN 2021; ANA 2022).

Ethical reasoning provides the conceptual basis for understanding these judgments. It refers to nurses’ process of recognizing an ethical problem, evaluating its possible consequences, relating the situation to ethical principles and professional obligations, and identifying preventive or corrective actions (Varkey 2021; ICN 2021). Although digital ethical risks related to AI are increasingly discussed in healthcare and nursing, less is known about how nurses’ ethical reasoning is embedded in routine digital care (Almagharbeh et al. 2025; Hoelscher and Pugh 2025; Bayram et al. 2026). This gap is important because everyday digital practices may create ethically complex situations before they become formal breaches or reportable incidents (Woo 2020; de Jong et al. 2020; Vukušić Rukavina et al. 2021). Vignette‐based scenarios are therefore useful for examining how healthcare professionals interpret ethically sensitive practice situations and formulate possible responses (Tremblay et al. 2022). Accordingly, this study aimed to explore how nurses reason ethically about potential digital ethical risks arising from the routine use of digital systems in clinical practice and the strategies they proposed to address these risks in response to vignette‐based scenarios.

Aim

This qualitative study aimed to explore how nurses reason ethically about potential digital ethical risks arising from the routine use of digital systems in clinical practice and the strategies they proposed to address these risks in response to vignette‐based scenarios.

Methods

Design

This study used a vignette‐elicited qualitative design, and the data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2019). The study adopted a contextualist epistemological position, recognizing participants’ accounts as contextually situated and the researchers as active in interpreting patterns of meaning (Braun and Clarke 2022). Vignette‐based scenarios were used as elicitation tools to stimulate ethical reflection and discussion, and the analysis was conducted on interview data generated from participants’ responses. This approach enabled participants to articulate their ethical reasoning, judgments, and proposed strategies for addressing potential digital ethical risks in response to vignette‐based scenarios.

A qualitative design was selected because the study focused on exploring nurses’ interpretations of potential digital ethical risks, their ethical reasoning, and proposed strategies to address these risks. Vignette‐based scenarios are suitable for exploring sensitive and ethically complex issues because they provide hypothetical, practice‐related situations through which participants can discuss moral concerns and possible responses (Tremblay et al. 2022). Throughout the research process, the authors followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) to support comprehensive and transparent reporting (Tong et al. 2007; Supplementary Table S1).

Development and Use of Vignette‐Based Scenarios

The research team developed seven vignette‐based scenarios to reflect ethically challenging situations commonly encountered in digital nursing practice, including AI‐supported decision‐making, alarm management, digital documentation, patient autonomy, and data privacy. The development of the scenarios was informed by: contemporary literature on digital ethics and nursing ethics,the researchers’ clinical experience, andcore ethical principles (nonmaleficence and beneficence, autonomy and respect for persons, justice and equity, and privacy and confidentiality).

The vignette‐based scenarios were intentionally concise to encourage ethical reasoning rather than factual recall. Each scenario was followed by open‐ended questions (“What is the problem?” and “What measure should be taken?”) designed to elicit ethical judgments and proposed strategies for addressing potential digital ethical risks. Experts in nursing ethics reviewed the scenarios for clarity, clinical relevance, and alignment with the study aim before data collection. A brief pilot test was conducted with two nurses who were not included in the main sample. Minor revisions were made (e.g., simplifying wording and improving scenario clarity) before data collection.

Setting and Sample

Purposeful criterion sampling was used to recruit participants, selecting nurses who met predefined characteristics relevant to the research aim (Başkaya and Demir 2023). The study was conducted in three Ministry of Health public hospitals located in Central Anatolia, Türkiye. These hospitals were selected to ensure variation in clinical units and routine use of digital systems. All three hospitals were located in the same city and close to each other, which facilitated field access, coordination with units, and interview scheduling within a limited data collection period.

Eligible nurses were identified through unit nurse managers and staff rosters. Unit nurse managers helped the first author identify eligible clinical units and nurses who met the inclusion criteria; however, they were not involved in nurses’ decisions to participate. Participation was voluntary and at each nurse's discretion. The first author visited clinical units, provided verbal and written information about the study, and invited nurses to participate. Nurses who expressed interest were contacted individually, and face‐to‐face interviews were scheduled at a convenient time and location within the hospital setting. The hospitals' proximity enabled the first author to visit the study sites promptly and arrange interviews according to nurses’ shift patterns and availability. This organization supported recruitment and data collection without disrupting clinical care.

Of the 25 nurses approached, 16 provided written informed consent and participated in the study. Nine nurses declined because of workload or time constraints, and no incentives were offered. The adequacy of the sample was evaluated in terms of the depth, richness, and diversity of the interview data, as well as their capacity to address the study aim (Malterud et al. 2016). The resulting dataset was considered sufficiently rich and informative, with a final sample of 16 nurses.

Inclusion criteria were: (a) having at least one year of professional nursing experience; (b) active use of digital systems (e.g., electronic health records, clinical monitoring systems, or digital communication tools) in clinical practice; and (c) willingness to participate. Exclusion criteria included having speech, language, or hearing impairments that could hinder effective communication during interviews.

Data Collection

Data were collected in September 2025 through face‐to‐face, semi‐structured, in‐depth interviews conducted by the first author Betül Bal. All interviews were conducted in Turkish. Potential participants were informed about the study's aim, process, potential risks and benefits, and the need to record interviews. Interviews began only after participants signed the informed consent form.

Interviews were held in quiet, private rooms within participants’ workplaces. When more than one participant was available during the same hospital visit, interviews were conducted sequentially at that site. This approach helped complete the interviews within the data collection period while preserving voluntary participation and minimizing disruption to clinical duties.

Two instruments were used: a Nurse Information Form and a Semi‐Structured Interview Form developed by the authors. The Nurse Information Form included eight questions about age, gender, education, years of professional experience, work unit, digital systems used, duration of use of those systems, and the presence of any explicit institutional policy or guideline on digital ethics.

The Semi‐Structured Interview Form began with an introductory question exploring the purposes for which nurses used digital systems in their clinical practice. It then continued with seven vignette‐based scenarios focused on potential digital ethical risks in nursing settings and was finalized based on expert feedback (Table 1). Each interview lasted approximately 35–45 minutes and was audio‐recorded. Throughout the interviews, the first author (XX) applied facilitation techniques, including careful listening, paraphrasing, observing tone and facial expressions, and noting key points. Interviews were conducted in a quiet, private room within the participants’ workplaces to minimize external distractions.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke (2019). This approach was chosen because its interpretive and reflexive orientation was consistent with the study's aim of examining ethical reasoning and interpretive judgments elicited through vignette‐based qualitative data. The analysis incorporated both inductive and deductive elements (Braun and Clarke 2022; Proudfoot 2023). Initial coding and the development of the three main themes were primarily inductive and grounded in participants’ accounts. Theme 1 was developed from participants’ responses to the introductory question concerning the purposes for which they used digital systems in clinical practice.

In contrast, Themes 2 and 3 were developed primarily from participants’ ethical reasoning in response to the seven vignette‐based scenarios. The deductive component was introduced during the later stages of the analysis, when the four ethical principles that had informed the development of the scenarios were applied as subthemes to organize the findings within Themes 2 and 3. These subthemes comprised nonmaleficence and beneficence, autonomy and respect for persons, justice and equity, and privacy and confidentiality. Within each subtheme, the potential digital ethical risks and proposed strategies for addressing them were further organized into three categories: nurse, digital system, and organization. The researchers developed these categories to provide a structured representation of the levels at which participants identified potential digital ethical risks and proposed strategies for addressing them.

All interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim in Turkish within 24 hours by three authors (XX, XXX, and XX). Transcripts were checked against the audio recordings to ensure accuracy and completeness. Coding, theme development, and interpretation were conducted using the original Turkish transcripts. After the analysis had been completed, the participant quotations selected for inclusion in the manuscript were translated into English by an independent language expert who was not involved in the study. The researchers subsequently checked the English translations against the original Turkish quotations. Minor linguistic adjustments were made solely to improve readability while preserving participants’ intended meaning, emphasis, and tone. Participants’ demographic characteristics were summarized descriptively (Supplementary Table S2).

The analysis was guided by Braun and Clarke's six‐phase reflexive thematic analysis process, with recursive movement between phases as the researchers’ interpretations developed:

Data familiarization: The research team read and reread the transcripts to become familiar with the dataset, immerse themselves in the data, and record initial analytic observations and reflections on nurses’ responses to the vignette‐based scenarios.

Generating initial codes: The first author generated the initial codes across the entire dataset using a primarily inductive approach, focusing on meaningful features of the data related to the purposes of digital system use, potential digital ethical risks, and proposed strategies for addressing these risks. Coding emphasized participants’ language, ethical judgments, and reasoning processes.

Constructing candidate themes: Codes were examined for shared meaning and used to construct candidate themes that represent broader patterns across the dataset.

Reviewing and refining themes: Candidate themes were reviewed and developed through reflexive discussion among the research team in relation to both the coded data and the entire dataset. Themes were refined to strengthen internal coherence, conceptual clarity, and clear distinctions between themes. The research team engaged in iterative discussions to question and enrich interpretations, challenge assumptions, and enhance reflexivity throughout the analytic process.

Defining and naming themes: The three overarching themes were defined and named to reflect their central organizing concepts. During the later stages of the analysis, the four ethical principles—nonmaleficence and beneficence, autonomy and respect for persons, justice and equity, and privacy and confidentiality—were deductively applied as subthemes to organize the findings within Themes 2 and 3. The research team subsequently developed the nurse, digital system, and organization levels as a categorical framework to systematically illustrate the levels at which participants identified potential digital ethical risks and proposed strategies for addressing them.

Producing the report: Final themes were integrated into a coherent analytic narrative, supported by direct participant quotations that illustrated and substantiated the analytic interpretation. The final themes and the analytic organization of the findings are presented in Tables 2 and 3.

Data collection and initial analytic engagement were conducted concurrently. Transcription, familiarization, and preliminary analytic reflections undertaken during data collection informed the researchers’ ongoing engagement with the dataset.

Rigor and Trustworthiness

To support the trustworthiness of the study, Lincoln and Guba's (1985) four criteria—credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability—served as a guiding framework and were applied in a manner consistent with the study's contextualist epistemological position and reflexive analytic approach. Transcription accuracy was checked against the audio recordings to support data integrity. Interpretations were continuously discussed within the research team throughout the analytic process to refine themes and enhance reflexivity. These discussions encouraged critical reflection and consideration of alternative interpretations. Following theme development, a summary of the themes was shared with all 16 participants to obtain feedback. Four participants responded, indicating that the thematic account was consistent with their experiences; no additional interpretations were offered, and no analytic revisions were made. This process was undertaken to serve as an additional source of reflexive engagement.

Transferability was supported by providing transparent descriptions of the study context, participant characteristics, sampling, data collection, and analytic procedures. Participants were purposefully selected from various units and included variation in age, gender, and education. Direct quotations were included in the findings to demonstrate how the analytic interpretations were grounded in participants’ accounts and to enable readers to evaluate the relevance of the findings to other contexts.

Dependability was supported through the transparent description and documentation of the chosen methodological and analytic procedures. Confirmability was approached reflexively through the documentation of analytic decisions, ongoing consideration of the researchers’ assumptions, and research team discussions that questioned and developed interpretations.

Research Team and Reflexivity

The research team consisted of three nursing faculty members holding doctoral degrees, each with extensive clinical experience and previous studies in ethics. Their long‐term clinical exposure provided contextual familiarity with how digital systems are used, the ethical dilemmas that arise in these processes, and how these dilemmas affect both the individual and institutional levels. The researchers had no prior relationships with the participants. Recognizing that their clinical and academic backgrounds informed their engagement with the data, the researchers employed ongoing reflexive discussions and critical reflection throughout data collection and analysis to examine how their perspectives, assumptions, and experiences shaped the development of interpretations.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained (September 17, 2025–308446). All participants provided verbal and written informed consent before data collection. Audio recordings were stored on a password‐protected computer accessible only to the researchers. Participants were anonymized using codes (e.g., N1 and N2) rather than identifiable information, such as names or clinical units. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Findings

Participants

Sixteen nurses participated in the study. Their ages ranged from 27 to 46 years (mean = 33.1). Thirteen (81%) were women and three (19%) were men. Nine (56%) held bachelor's and seven (44%) held master's degrees. Six (38%) worked in the emergency department, three (19%) in intensive care, two (13%) in pediatrics, one (6%) in internal medicine, three (19%) in surgery, and one (6%) in the education/quality department. Professional experience ranged from 3 to 23 years (mean = 8.6) (Supplementary Table S2).

All nurses (100%) used electronic patient record systems, device interfaces, smartphones, and messaging platforms such as WhatsApp, Telegram, or Messenger. In addition, seven (44%) reported using mobile health and AI‐based tools (e.g., ChatGPT), five (31%) used clinical imaging or device‐connected software, and five (31%) used email. All (100%) reported taking photographs or videos with their personal phones during clinical care. On average, nurses reported using digital systems for 5.5 hours per shift (range = 1–14 hours).

Only two nurses (13%) stated that digital ethics policies or guidelines were introduced during institutional training, while 14 (88%) reported the absence of such resources in their workplaces (Supplementary Table S2).

Nurses’ Ethical Reasoning Regarding Potential Digital Ethical Risks

Analysis generated three main themes reflecting nurses’ ethical reasoning and evaluative judgments elicited through the vignette‐based scenarios regarding potential digital ethical risks: (i) nurses’ purposes for using digital systems, (ii) digital ethical risks in nursing, and (iii) strategies to prevent digital ethical risks (Tables 2 and 3).

Theme 1: Nurses’ Purposes for Using Digital Systems

Nurses described and justified their use of digital systems for various clinical and professional purposes. In patient care, these tools were perceived as supporting task prioritization, monitoring vital signs, managing medication, documenting interventions, and preparing care plans. AI‐based systems were conceptualized as decision‐support tools for clinical reasoning. Digital systems were also evaluated as facilitating early detection of complications, patient safety, professional boundaries, and accurate documentation. Personal phones, messaging applications such as WhatsApp, and email were considered pragmatically useful for rapid communication during shift changes and emergencies. Within these communication processes, participants described photographing patients’ wounds, skin lesions, or surgical sites and capturing images of monitor displays, medication orders, laboratory results, or patient records using personal phones. They also reported sharing patient images and identifiable information, such as patients’ names, diagnoses, or room numbers, through WhatsApp groups. Nurses further emphasized the use of digital systems for sharing educational materials and managing training or information dissemination.

Theme 2: Digital Ethical Risks in Nursing

This theme captured the potential digital ethical risks articulated by nurses in their interpretations of the vignette‐based scenarios. The risks were organized according to four subthemes: (i) nonmaleficence and beneficence, (ii) autonomy and respect for persons, (iii) justice and equity, and (iv) privacy and confidentiality. Within each subtheme, the findings were presented across three categories: nurse, digital system, and organization (Tables 2 and 3).

Subthemes 2.1: Nonmaleficence and Beneficence

Nurse‐Related Risks : Nurses interpreted the vignette‐based scenarios as showing excessive reliance on digital systems, which they perceived as posing risks to patient safety, particularly when bedside clinical observation was neglected. They identified potential risks associated with adding AI‐generated outputs to care plans without professional verification or synthesis, transferring clinical judgment to digital systems through blind trust, and silencing alarms without checking the patient. Participants reasoned that alarm fatigue could lead to delayed responses or missed critical findings.

Digital System–Related Risks : Nurses attributed the potential harm depicted in the scenarios to algorithmic and technical limitations of digital systems. They reasoned that AI‐supported systems could pose risks if they failed to identify disease‐specific or patient‐specific risk factors, misclassified patient risks, or produced outputs that lacked bedside observation, clinical context, and experiential judgment. Technical failures, software problems, insufficient calibration, delayed display of critical information, and unnecessary, false, or poorly classified alarms were also interpreted as potential sources of harm.

Organization‐Related Risks : Nurses interpreted limited institutional oversight of digital systems, including AI‐supported tools, as a potential contributor to the risks depicted in the scenarios. They also expressed concern about the absence of risk management procedures and clinical guidelines for the use of digital systems. Participants reasoned that insufficient nurse staffing and excessive workload could weaken timely responses to digital alerts and potentially compromise patient safety.

Subthemes 2.2. Autonomy and Respect for Persons

Nurse‐Related Risks : Participants interpreted the vignette‐based scenarios as insufficiently patient‐centered and under‐involved the patients, posing potential risks to patient autonomy. They considered the use of AI‐generated output without individualizing it to the patient's needs, preferences, and clinical context as potentially weakening patient‐centered care and respect for individual choice.

Digital System–Related Risks : Participants viewed digital systems that lacked prioritization criteria for patient information and care needs as potentially limiting autonomy. They also reasoned that device settings that could not be adapted to individual patient needs, preferences, or clinical conditions could limit personalized care and patient participation.

Organization‐Related Risks : Participants interpreted the use of unapproved AI systems and the absence of procedures for patient autonomy and information in the vignette‐based scenarios as raising ethical concerns. They reasoned that digital or AI‐supported decisions made without adequate patient information could weaken respect for autonomy and individual preferences.

Subthemes 2.3. Justice and Equity

Nurse‐Related Risks : Participants interpreted unfair patient prioritization in the vignette‐based scenarios as posing potential risks to justice and equity. They stated that determining the order of procedures based on personal preferences rather than clinical need could undermine fair care. In interpreting the scenarios, participants expressed concern that the needs of older adults and vulnerable patient groups could be overlooked in digital care decisions.

Digital System–Related Risks : Participants viewed inaccessible digital systems as posing a threat to equitable care for older and vulnerable patients. They reasoned that a non‐user‐friendly system design could make access to care more difficult. They also viewed reliance on digital applications as the primary route to services as potentially contributing to unequal access.

Organization‐Related Risks : Participants interpreted the organizational barriers presented in the scenarios as raising concerns about inequitable access to services. They reasoned that insufficient staffing and resource planning could limit equitable digital care. They also expressed concern that the absence of institutional procedures addressing equity and impartiality could undermine fair care delivery.

Subthemes 2.4. Privacy and Confidentiality

Nurse‐Related Risks : Nurses interpreted taking patient photos without explicit consent, sharing patient photos or images without authorization, and disclosing HIV status or other sensitive health information without patient permission in the vignette‐based scenarios as raising concerns about privacy and confidentiality. Participants also viewed the use of personal messaging applications to share identifiable patient information as potentially compromising confidentiality. Participants expressed concern that limited awareness of confidentiality, privacy, and data security responsibilities could increase the likelihood of such risks.

Digital System–Related Risks : Nurses viewed weak access control and authentication mechanisms in the scenarios as potential threats to privacy and confidentiality. They also reasoned that inadequate data protection measures could enable unauthorized storage or transfer of patient information. Participants interpreted these limitations as potential digital system–level threats to the protection of sensitive and identifiable health data.

Organization‐Related Risks : Nurses interpreted the absence of written policies and guidelines aligned with the Turkish Personal Data Protection Law (KVKK) requirements as a concern for privacy and confidentiality. They also expressed concern about insufficient institutional training, the absence of clear reporting, sanction, and disciplinary procedures, and inadequate auditing of digital data access, storage, and sharing. Limited organizational control over personal device use in clinical communication was viewed as a condition that could weaken privacy protection.

Theme 3: Strategies to Prevent Digital Ethical Risks

This theme captured the strategies that nurses proposed in response to the vignette‐based scenarios. The strategies were organized into the same four subthemes: nonmaleficence and beneficence; autonomy and respect for persons; justice and equity; and privacy and confidentiality. Within each subtheme, the proposed strategies were presented across the nurse, digital system, and organization categories (Tables 2 and 3).

Subthemes 3.1. Nonmaleficence and Beneficence

Nurse‐related Strategies : Nurses proposed that AI outputs should remain supportive rather than determinative. They considered the following actions appropriate: verifying digital outputs through clinical observation, developing care plans with professional judgment, checking patients before and after silencing alarms, and synthesizing digital information rather than copying it.

Digital System–Related Strategies : Participants proposed the following system safeguards as appropriate responses to the patient safety concerns raised in the scenarios: advisory AI prompts, real‐time error detection and correction, updated disease‐ and patient‐specific algorithms, regular testing and calibration, clinician override options, escalation pathways for critical alerts, and improved alarm classification.

Organization‐Related Strategies : Nurses proposed stronger institutional oversight of digital systems. They recommended lists of approved digital systems, patient safety governance for decision‐support systems, escalation pathways for unsafe outputs and system errors, staffing and workflow standards for timely alert responses, documentation of clinical rationale when digital recommendations are used, and review of digital safety incidents by quality, ethics, or patient safety committees.

Subthemes 3.2. Autonomy and Respect for Persons

Nurse‐Related Strategies : In response to the vignette‐based scenarios, participants considered it appropriate for patients and families to receive clear, understandable information about the use of digital systems and the processes for handling digital information. Family access to patient information was considered appropriate only with the patient's or a legal representative's consent. They also considered it appropriate to adapt explanations to patients’ understanding, digital literacy, and decision‐making capacity, and to respect and document patients’ consent, refusal, or preferences regarding digital care.

Digital System–Related Strategies : Participants identified multiple information access options as appropriate responses to the autonomy‐related concerns raised in the scenarios, including printed materials, telephone, SMS, and patient portals. They also considered the following system features appropriate: user‐friendly, accessible interfaces; accessibility features for older adults and vulnerable groups; and patient‐controlled preferences for information access and authorized family involvement.

Organization‐Related Strategies : When discussing the vignette‐based scenarios, participants identified institutional guidelines for accessible, patient‐centered digital information processes as appropriate organizational responses. They also viewed monitoring whether digital access processes support patients’ understanding, consent, and participation as important. Their responses further indicated that they considered procedures for patients with limited digital literacy, older adults, and vulnerable groups necessary, along with policies that address individual needs, decision‐making capacity, and authorized family involvement.

Subthemes 3.3. Justice and Equity

Nurse‐Related Strategies : In response to the vignette‐based scenarios, participants considered it appropriate for all patients to be evaluated fairly and promptly when digital records or alerts are used. They indicated that prioritization should be based on clinical urgency and care needs, without personal preference or convenience. They also identified paying particular attention to older adults and vulnerable patients and consistently using digital documentation and team communication as appropriate actions to promote fair task distribution and continuity of care.

Digital System–Related Strategies : Participants suggested standardized and transparent digital record management as an appropriate response to concerns about fair patient follow‐up. They identified the following system features as appropriate: objective prioritization criteria, automatic alerts for critical patient information, digital record functions that reduce missed, delayed, or selectively entered information, and traceable prioritization and documentation processes.

Organization‐Related Strategies : In considering the scenarios, participants identified institutional policies that incorporate standard prioritization criteria and principles of fairness and impartiality as appropriate organizational responses. They also identified staffing, workflow, and oversight mechanisms as appropriate measures for supporting timely and complete digital record entry. They further viewed consistent monitoring of digital prioritization practices, managerial responsibility, and corrective or disciplinary procedures as appropriate responses to situations in which digital practices could compromise equitable care.

Subthemes 3.4. Privacy and Confidentiality

Nurse‐Related Strategies : In response to the vignette‐based scenarios, participants proposed obtaining explicit written consent before taking, storing, or sharing patient photos, videos, images, or private health information. They cautioned against sharing patient information through social media, personal devices, or non‐approved communication channels. They emphasized that sensitive information, such as HIV status, should be handled with confidentiality, patient trust, and psychosocial sensitivity. Participants also suggested consulting institutional procedures or responsible units before sharing identifiable patient information.

Digital System–Related Strategies : Participants recommended secure, institution‐approved platforms for patient data exchange. They highlighted role‐based access control, user authentication, automatic audit trails, restrictions on exporting data to personal devices or external applications, and encryption of stored and transmitted patient information. Warning prompts for sensitive or identifiable data and secure transfer of AI‐supported outputs into electronic health records were also proposed.

Organization‐Related Strategies : Participants recommended written guidelines and protocols aligned with KVKK, patient rights, and institutional privacy standards. They also called for audit, reporting, sanction, disciplinary, and legal procedures for unauthorized access, disclosure, or sharing of patient data. They further proposed institutional support pathways, including psychosocial support for patients affected by privacy breaches.

Discussion

This study explored nurses’ ethical reasoning regarding potential digital ethical risks associated with the routine use of digital systems in clinical practice and the strategies they proposed for addressing these risks in response to vignette‐based scenarios. The findings are discussed under two main headings: (i) nurses’ purposes for using digital systems and (ii) nurses’ ethical reasoning regarding potential digital ethical risks and prevention strategies.

Nurses’ Purposes for Using Digital Systems

Participants’ accounts show that digital systems have become an integral part of the organization and delivery of everyday nursing care. Electronic records, monitoring devices, alarm systems, and digital decision‐support tools were used across interconnected processes, including patient prioritization, vital‐sign monitoring, medication management, care planning, documentation, and the early recognition of complications. Previous studies similarly demonstrate that electronic nursing records can strengthen documentation quality, traceability, and patient safety, although their benefits may be limited when system design does not correspond to nursing workflows or creates redundant documentation (Cho et al. 2024; Douma et al. 2024; Wahyuni et al. 2024). Likewise, monitoring and alarm systems provide clinically valuable real‐time information. Still, their effectiveness depends on nurses’ ability to interpret, prioritize, and respond to alerts, particularly when frequent non‐actionable alarms increase cognitive and workload demands (Xu et al. 2025). Accordingly, the value of digital systems appears to depend on the extent to which they are integrated into nurses’ clinical judgment and workflows.

A particularly important empirical finding was the incorporation of personal phones and messaging applications into patient‐related clinical communication. Personal devices were used to capture and share patient‐related images and identifiable clinical information through WhatsApp groups. Accordingly, these practices represent digital applications embedded in clinical care. This finding is consistent with evidence that nurses use personal smartphones to access patient care information and to communicate clinical information via phone, text, photographs, and videos (Bal et al. 2026; de Jong et al. 2020). Such practices have been interpreted as pragmatic workarounds that help nurses respond to the communication and information needs of clinical care (de Jong et al. 2020). The present study extends the existing evidence by identifying the specific types of patient‐related images and identifiable information that may be transmitted via personal devices and messaging groups.

Important ethical implications accompanied the practical value of these technologies. Personal phones and WhatsApp groups enabled rapid information exchange during shift handovers and emergencies, supporting continuity and coordination of care. However, recording and transferring patient‐related material through personally owned devices may move clinical information beyond institutionally controlled systems and raise questions concerning consent, confidentiality, secure storage, access, onward sharing, professional boundaries, and accountability. These concerns are consistent with the digital ethics literature, which identifies knowledge and awareness, digital ethical violations in nursing care, and violations of ethical principles as key dimensions of digital ethics and calls for nursing‐specific guidance, auditing, and monitoring mechanisms (Bayram et al. 2026). They also resonate with evidence that nurses may prefer informal or non‐official platforms when responding to the demands of digital communication. At the same time, gaps or interruptions in information sharing may affect continuity, collective clinical awareness, and patient safety (Bal et al. 2026). Accordingly, the use of personal devices may reflect a mismatch between the need for rapid clinical communication and the secure digital communication channels provided by healthcare institutions.

Participants positioned AI‐based systems as resources that could support clinical reasoning. This interpretation is consistent with qualitative evidence indicating that nurses recognize AI's potential to reduce workload, improve efficiency, and enhance patient care, while also expressing concerns about accountability, data security, patient safety, professional deskilling, and the dehumanization of care (Bodur et al. 2025). Participants’ use of digital systems to share educational materials and disseminate training information further indicates that these technologies also function as sources of professional knowledge and learning. However, evidence that digital health competencies vary among nurses and other healthcare professionals suggests that routine access to digital technologies does not, in itself, ensure their safe, critical, and ethically informed use (Longhini et al. 2024). Taken together, these findings, alongside evidence that formal and informal digital communication channels coexist in clinical care (Bal et al. 2026), portray contemporary nursing practice as a hybrid digital care environment in which institutionally provided systems, personal devices, messaging applications, and emerging AI tools are used together. The central challenge is therefore to ensure that this digital environment strengthens clinical judgment, ensures secure information exchange, supports professional accountability, and promotes safe nursing care.

Nurses’ Ethical Reasoning Regarding Potential Digital Ethical Risks and Prevention Strategies

In this section, potential digital ethical risks and the strategies proposed by nurses for addressing them are discussed together because participants’ responses to the vignette‐based scenarios demonstrated a direct connection between recognizing a potential risk and identifying an action they considered appropriate to prevent, reduce, or correct it. This section is structured around four subthemes—nonmaleficence, beneficence, autonomy–respect for persons, justice–equity, and privacy–confidentiality—to enable a comprehensive ethical evaluation of the potential digital risks identified by participants in the vignette‐based scenarios and the strategies they proposed to address them.

Nonmaleficence and Beneficence

Participants’ ethical reasoning in response to the vignette‐based scenarios suggested that potential digital ethical risks related to nonmaleficence and beneficence may arise when digital systems enter clinical decision‐making without sufficient clinical verification, timely professional response, and institutional oversight. This was considered particularly concerning because routine digital practices, such as electronic documentation, alarm management, interpretation of digital outputs, and technology‐assisted patient monitoring, are directly linked to patient safety. Prior research similarly indicates that poorly integrated digital systems may weaken clinical reasoning and create patient safety risks (Ackerhans et al. 2024; Bodur et al. 2025; Magrabi et al. 2019).

The present findings extend this evidence by identifying several mechanisms through which participants believed potential harm could occur, including over‐reliance on digital outputs, alarm fatigue, delayed or incomplete information, inadequate system calibration, and unclear professional or institutional accountability. To address these potential risks, participants proposed strategies that extended beyond individual vigilance, including the clinical verification of digital outputs, bedside assessment, clearly defined escalation pathways, periodic system testing, clinician override mechanisms, and institutional safety reviews. In this sense, digital systems were viewed as supporting beneficence when they complemented nurses’ clinical judgment and facilitated timely and safe care, but as potentially threatening nonmaleficence when they reduced direct observation, blurred responsibility, or delayed appropriate clinical action (Alnawafleh et al. 2025; Hoelscher and Pugh 2025).

Autonomy and Respect for Persons

Participants interpreted the vignette‐based scenarios as indicating that autonomy in digital care could potentially be weakened when patients’ access to technology, digital literacy, personal preferences, and decision‐making capacity were not adequately taken into account. They reasoned that, in such situations, care could be organized around system functions rather than patients’ understanding, consent, and individual needs. This interpretation is consistent with evidence showing that limited eHealth literacy and unequal use of patient portals can restrict access to health information and reduce patients’ ability to participate in care decisions (Deshpande et al. 2023; Yoon et al. 2024).

Participants’ interpretations highlighted the importance of incorporating patient‐centered digital information into routine nursing care. The strategies participants proposed—clear explanations, consent before family access, documentation of refusal or preference, printed or telephone‐based alternatives, accessible interfaces, and patient‐controlled information access—reflected their view that autonomy in digital care depends on more than the availability of technology. In their view, autonomy‐supportive digital care requires information that patients can understand, access options they can use, and institutional procedures that protect choice. Torgersen et al. (2024) similarly note that incorporating cybersecurity and data‐related risks into informed consent procedures can strengthen autonomous decision‐making.

In this respect, participants perceived adaptable digital systems as potentially supporting respect for persons, whereas unclear access, consent, and information processes were perceived as posing risks to autonomy.

Justice and Equity

Participants interpreted the vignette‐based scenarios as indicating that justice and equity in digital nursing care could potentially be compromised when digital workflows failed to clearly distinguish clinical urgency from convenience, personal preference, or incomplete documentation. Their vignette‐based evaluations suggested that this potential risk was not limited to individual bias but could also arise from how digital records, alerts, and access routes were designed and governed. This is particularly important because digital systems can increase transparency and continuity. Yet, the same systems may reproduce inequity when prioritization criteria are unclear, entries are delayed or selective, and older or vulnerable patients cannot use the main digital access route. Recent literature similarly shows that digital inequities are shaped by access, usability, literacy, trust, and the interaction between technology design and wider social or organizational conditions (Chidambaram et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2024; Alomar et al. 2024). Evidence on electronic health records also indicates that bias can be embedded in recorded data and can affect how patients are represented, interpreted, and followed in care (Boyd et al. 2023; Perets et al. 2025). This aligns with broader evidence that bias influences patient–clinician communication, clinical decision‐making, and institutional practices (Vela et al. 2022).

Participants’ responses emphasized the importance of making justice and equity visible within digital workflows. They proposed objective prioritization criteria, traceable documentation, critical information alerts, and consistent record entry as strategies that could potentially reduce the risk of preference‐based or delayed care. This interpretation is consistent with digital clinical safety guidance emphasizing risk management, governance, and continuous monitoring in health technologies (NHS England 2021, 2025). The clinical decision‐support literature also shows that oversight and regular review processes are necessary to prevent unintended inequities (Wright et al. 2011).

Privacy and Confidentiality

Participants’ interpretations of the digital care situations presented in the vignette‐based scenarios indicated that potential privacy and confidentiality risks could arise when identifiable patient information is photographed, shared, or transferred without explicit consent and secure institutional control. This is ethically critical because patient images, HIV status, and other sensitive health information can remain identifiable even when shared with clinical intent through personal devices, social media, or messaging applications. This aligns with evidence that social media and mobile communication create boundary, professionalism, and confidentiality risks for health professionals (Vukušić Rukavina et al. 2021), and with broader privacy literature emphasizing risks related to third‐party access, control of patient data, and inadequate safeguards in digital health systems (Murdoch 2021). The ICN Code of Ethics also frames privacy and confidentiality as core nursing obligations that extend to digital practice (ICN 2021).

This theme underscores the importance participants attributed to moving privacy protection from individual caution to enforceable digital governance. The strategies proposed by participants are consistent with recommended safeguards for health information systems (Shojaei et al. 2024). Written protocols aligned with KVKK, the Regulation on Personal Health Data, patient rights regulations, and institutional privacy standards can also clarify what may be accessed, shared, reported, and sanctioned in the event of a confidentiality breach (KVKK 2019). In this respect, participants proposed secure systems, clear rules, regular auditing, and institutional support for patients affected by unauthorized disclosure as appropriate responses to potential privacy and confidentiality risks.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Since the study was conducted in only three Ministry of Health hospitals in the Central Anatolia Region, nurses’ ethical perspectives and reasoning regarding digital ethics in other regions may not be fully represented. The voluntary nature of participant selection may have limited the involvement of nurses with sensitive or negative experiences. Additionally, the vignette‐based data collection method elicits ethical reasoning and evaluative judgments in response to hypothetical scenarios but may not fully capture actual clinical behaviors. Therefore, future research is recommended to revisit these themes across different regions and institutions, using studies that combine qualitative and quantitative data.

Implications for nursing Policy and Practice

The findings highlight the potential digital ethical risks articulated by participants in response to the vignette‐based scenarios, as well as the strategies they proposed at the nurse, digital system, and organization levels. At the nurse level, participants identified clinical verification of digital outputs, bedside assessment, professional synthesis of digital information, patient assessment before alarm silencing, clear patient information, informed consent, fair prioritization, and the secure handling of patient information as priorities for ethical digital care.

Participants’ responses suggest that digital ethics education could include clinical verification, ethical reasoning, alarm management, patient autonomy, digital literacy, privacy and confidentiality, equitable care, documentation responsibilities, and professional accountability. Such education could help nurses recognize and reason about the ethical dimensions of situations involving digital systems.

At the digital system and organization levels, secure institution‐approved platforms, role‐based access, authentication, audit trails, encryption, system testing and calibration, clinician override and escalation pathways, accessible interfaces, objective prioritization criteria, staffing and workflow arrangements, reporting procedures, sanctions, and institutional oversight emerged as matters for multidisciplinary and institutional consideration. Written guidelines aligned with KVKK and patient rights, procedures for the approval and use of digital systems, monitoring mechanisms, authorized family access, and institutional support following potential privacy breaches may be considered possible components of digital ethics governance.

Conclusions

This study provides insight into nurses’ ethical reasoning and evaluative judgments regarding potential digital ethical risks, as interpreted through vignette‐based scenarios, as well as the strategies they proposed to address these risks. Analysis generated three main themes: (i) nurses’ purposes for using digital systems, (ii) digital ethical risks in nursing, and (iii) strategies to prevent digital ethical risks. The findings show how participants interpreted potential digital ethical risks in relation to nonmaleficence and beneficence, autonomy and respect for persons, justice and equity, privacy and confidentiality, and how they located these risks at the nurse, digital system, and organization levels.

Participants identified excessive reliance on digital outputs, weakened clinical observation, alarm fatigue, unverified copied information, delayed or incomplete data, and limited system calibration as potential risks to nonmaleficence and beneficence. Regarding autonomy and respect for persons, insufficient patient‐centered information, unclear consent processes, limited consideration of digital literacy, and non‐individualized digital outputs were interpreted as potential ethical risks. In relation to justice and equity, unclear prioritization criteria, limited accessibility for older and vulnerable patients, non‐user‐friendly system designs, and inadequate staffing or workflow planning were viewed as potential contributors to inequitable digital care. Unauthorized sharing of patient images or sensitive health information, the use of personal devices, weak access controls, insecure digital infrastructures, and gaps in organizational policies were interpreted as potential risks to privacy and confidentiality.

In response to these potential risks, participants proposed verifying digital outputs through clinical assessment, maintaining bedside observation, establishing clear consent procedures, providing accessible information channels, using objective prioritization criteria, and adopting secure, institution‐approved platforms. They also identified role‐based access, audit trails, regular system monitoring, written guidelines, digital safety governance, reporting and sanction mechanisms, and institutional support pathways following potential privacy breaches as appropriate organizational responses.
