Rings in which all elements are the sum of a central element and an element from Δ ⁡ ( R ) \Delta(R)
Peter Danchev, Arash Javan, Omid Hasanzadeh, Ahmad Moussavi

Abstract
. We define and consider in-depth the so-called ” C ​ Δ C\Delta rings ” as those rings R R whose elements are a sum of an element in C ⁡ ( R ) C(R) and of an element in Δ ⁡ ( R ) \Delta(R) . Our achieved results somewhat strengthen these recently obtained by Ma-Wang-Leroy in Czechoslovak Math. J. (2024) as well as these due to Kurtulmaz-Halicioglu-Harmanci-Chen in Bull. Belg. Math. Soc. Simon Stevin (2019). Specifically, we succeeded to establish that exchange C ​ Δ C\Delta rings are always clean as well as that exchange CN rings are strongly clean. Likewise, we prove that, for any ring R R , the ring of formal power series R ⁡ [ [ x ] ] R[[x]] over R R is C ​ Δ C\Delta if, and only if, so is R R . And, furthermore, we show that, for any ring R R , if the polynomial ring R ⁡ [ x ] R[x] is a C ​ Δ C\Delta ring, then R R satisfies the Köthe conjecture. Some other closely related things concerning certain extensions of C ​ Δ C\Delta rings are also presented.

1.
Introduction and Basic Concepts

In the current paper, let throughout
R
denote an associative ring with identity element, which ring is
not
necessarily commutative. Typically, for such a ring
R
, the sets
U(R)
,
Nil(R)
and
C(R)
represent the set of invertible elements (i.e., the unit group of
R
), the set of nilpotent elements and the set of central elements in
R
, respectively. Additionally,
J(R)
denotes the Jacobson radical of
R
. Likewise, the whole ring of
n\times n
matrices over
R
and the ring of
n\times n
upper triangular matrices over
R
are, respectively, designed by
{\rm M}_{n}(R)
and
{\rm T}_{n}(R)
.

The key instrument of the present article unambiguously plays the set
\Delta(R)
, which was introduced by Lam in
[
10
, Exercise 4.24]
and was recently examined by Leroy-Matczuk in
[
11
]
. As pointed out by the authors in
[
11
, Theorem 3 and 6]
,
\Delta(R)
is the largest Jacobson radical’s subring of
R
that is closed with respect to multiplication by all units (as well as by quasi-invertible elements) of
R
. Also, the containment
J(R)\subseteq\Delta(R)
is fulfilled always. Moreover,
\Delta(R)=J(T)
, where
T
is the subring of
R
generated by all units of
R
, and the equality
\Delta(R)=J(R)
holds if, and only if,
\Delta(R)
is an ideal of
R
. Explicitly, an element
a
in a ring
R
is from
\Delta(R)
if
1-ua
is a unit for all
u\in U(R)
.

Furthermore, a ring
R
is said to be
CU
if each element
a\in R
has a decomposition
a=c+u
, where
c
is a central element and
u
is a unit – to be exact, this notion was introduced by Kurtulmaz et al. in
[
7
]
. They showed that, if
\mathbb{F}
is a field which is
not
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, then
M_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is a
CU
ring.

In the other vein, a ring
R
is called
CN
if each element
a
of
R
has a decomposition
a=c+n
, where
c
is a central element and
n
is a nilpotent element. This concept was introduced in
[
8
]
. They showed that every
CN
ring is
CU
, but the converse is definitely
not
true in general. They also characterized all elements in
M_{n}(R)
having a
CN
decomposition. They, moreover, proved for a division ring
R
that, if
M_{n}(R)
is a
CN
ring, then the cardinality of
C(R)
is strictly greater than
n
.

Similarly, an element
a\in R
has a
CJ
decomposition if
a=c+j
for some
c\in C(R)
and
j\in J(R)
and a ring
R
is called
CJ
if every element of
R
has a
CJ
decomposition. This notion was introduced by Ma et al. in
[
13
]
. They study the behavior of this class of rings under various ring extensions. In particular, they proved that, for any ring
R
, the subring
C(R)+J(R)
is always a
CJ
ring, and that if
R[x]
is a
CJ
ring, then
R
satisfies the Köthe conjecture.

So, as a non-trivial extension of
CJ
rings, we are motivated to introduce and investigate the new and more expanded concept of a
C\Delta
ring
, which will be the leitmotif of our study, like this: a ring
R
is called
C\Delta
if each element
a\in R
has a decomposition
a=c+r
, where
c\in C(R)
and
r\in\Delta(R)
. Apparently, all
CJ
rings are
C\Delta
, but the opposite implication does
not
hold in all generality as we will illustrate below.

In the following, we give definitions of some other subrings of
{\rm T}_{n}(R)
in order to discuss in the sequel whether they satisfy the
C\Delta
property:

D_{n}(R)=\left\{(a_{ij})\in M_{n}(R)\ \middle|\ \text{all diagonal entries of }(a_{ij})\text{ are equal}\right\};

V_{n}(R)=\left\{\sum_{i=j}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}e_{(i-j+1)i}\ \middle|\ a_{j}\in R\right\};

V_{n}^{k}(R)=\left\{\sum_{i=j}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}e_{(i-j+1)i}+\sum_{i=j}^{n-k}\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}a_{ij}e_{j(i+k+1)}\ \middle|\ x_{j},a_{ij}\in R\right\},

where
x_{ij}\in R,a_{js}\in R,\ 1\leq i\leq k,\ 1\leq j\leq n-k\ \text{and}\ k+1\leq s\leq n
;

D_{n}^{k}(R)=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}a_{ij}e_{ij}+\sum_{j=k+2}^{n}b_{(k+1)j}e_{(k+1)j}+cI_{n}\ \middle|\ a_{ij},b_{ij},c\in R\right\},

where
k=[\frac{n}{2}]
, i.e., satisfies
n=2k
when
n
is an even integer and
n=2k+1
when
n
is an odd integer.

Our principal work in what follows is planned thus: In the next section, we are concerned with the exploration of some crucial structural properties of

2.9

2.26

2.
Basic properties of
C\Delta
rings

In this section, we introduce the concept of a
C\Delta
ring and investigate its elementary properties. We also give relations between
C\Delta
rings and some other related rings. For this purpose, we need a series of preliminary technicalities.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any ring
R
, the following equality is true:

U(R)+\Delta(R)=U(R).

Proof.

We know that
U(R)\subseteq U(R)+J(R)\subseteq U(R)+\Delta(R)
. To treat the reciprocal, given
x\in U(R)+\Delta(R)
, we may write
x=u+r
, where
u\in U(R)
and
r\in\Delta(R)
. One easily checks that
x=u+r=u(1+u^{-1}r)\in U(R)
, as required.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
R
be a ring. For
n\geq 2
,

\Delta(M_{n}(R))=J(M_{n}(R))=M_{n}(J(R)).

Proof.

Knowing that, for all
n\geq 2
,
M_{n}(R)
is generated by its units, we apply
[
11
, Corollary 4]
to get that
\Delta(M_{n}(R))=J(M_{n}(R))
, as stated. ∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
R
be a ring. If
I\subseteq J(R)
and
\Delta(R/I)=0
, then
\Delta(R)=I=J(R)
.

Proof.

Since
J(R)\subseteq\Delta(R)
, it suffices to show that
\Delta(R)\subseteq I
. Choose
x\in\Delta(R)
and
x\notin I
. So,
x+I
is a non-zero element of
R/I
and hence
x+I\notin\Delta(R/I)
. Therefore, by definition of
\Delta
, there exists
y\in R
such that
(1+xy)+I
is not unit in
R/I
, but this is a contradiction. Then,
\Delta(R)\subseteq I
whence
\Delta(R)=I=J(R)
, as asserted.
∎

Two automatic consequences hold:

Corollary 2.4
.

For any ring
R
,
\Delta(R)=J(R)
if, and only if,
\Delta(R/J(R))=(0)
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
R
be a ring and
I\subseteq J(R)
. Then,
\dfrac{I+\Delta(R)}{I}=\Delta(R/I)
.

The following appeared to be obviously true:

Example 2.7
.

The following are fulfilled:

(i)

(ii)

Recall that a ring

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
R
be a
C\Delta
ring. Then, the following are valid:

(i)

For any ideal
I\subseteq J(R)
,
R/I
is
C\Delta
(in particular,
R/J(R)
is
C\Delta
).

(ii)

For any
a,b\in R
,
ab-ba\in\Delta(R)
.

(iii)

R
is Dedekind-finite.

(iv)

If
a^{2}\in\Delta(R)
, then
a\in\Delta(R)
.

(v)

R
is a
CU
ring.

(vi)

Always
Nil(R)\subseteq J(R)
and
Nil(R)\subseteq\Delta(R)
.

(vii)

If
a^{2}\in J(R)
, then
a\in J(R)
(in particular,
R/J(R)
is reduced).

(viii)

For any
e\in Id(R)
, the corner subring
eRe
is a
C\Delta
ring.

Proof.

(i)

It is straightforward and, therefore, we omit the details.

(ii)

Suppose
a=c+d
and
b=c^{\prime}+d^{\prime}
, where
c,c^{\prime}\in C(R)
and
d,d^{\prime}\in\Delta(R)
. Thus, we have
ab-ba=dd^{\prime}-d^{\prime}d\in\Delta(R)
.

(iii)

Let
a,b\in R
. So,
ab-ba\in\Delta(R)
by (ii). If
ab=1
, then
1-ba\in\Delta(R)
. Hence,
ba\in 1-\Delta(R)\subseteq U(R)
.

(iv)

Suppose
a=c+d
, where
c\in C(R)
and
d\in\Delta(R)
. Thus,
a^{2}=c^{2}+2cd+d^{2}
. On the other side, since
a^{2}\in\Delta(R)
, it must be that
(1-a)(1+a)=1-a^{2}\in U(R)
and hence
1+a\in U(R)
. So,

(1+a)-d=c+1\in U(R)+\Delta(R)\subseteq U(R).

Therefore,
(c+1)d\in\Delta(R)
whence
cd+d\in\Delta(R)
. But, since
\Delta(R)
is a subring of
R
, we derive
cd\in\Delta(R)
and so
c^{2}=a^{2}-2cd-d^{2}\in\Delta(R)
. Now, we show that
c\in\Delta(R)
. Given
u\in U(R)
, we may write
(1-uc)(1+uc)=1-u^{2}c^{2}\in U(R)
yielding
1-uc\in U(R)
and
c\in\Delta(R)
. Consequently,
a=c+d\in\Delta(R)
.

(v)

Choose
a\in R
. Then,
a-1=c+d
, where
c\in C(R)
and
d\in\Delta(R)
. So,
a=c+(1+d)
, where
1+d\in 1+\Delta(R)\subseteq U(R)
. Hence,
R
is
CU
.

(vi)

It is pretty clear, so we leave the arguments.

(vii)

Using (iv), we have
a\in\Delta(R)
. Take
r\in R
and write
r=c+d
, where
c\in C(R)
and
d\in\Delta(R)
. So, we obtain
(1-ra)=1-(c+d)a=1-ca-da
. Now, we show that
1-ca\in U(R)
. In fact,
(1-ca)(1+ca)=1-c^{2}a^{2}\in U(R)
(note that
a^{2}\in J(R)
) and hence
1-ca\in U(R)
. Therefore,

1-ra=(1-ca)-da\in U(R)+\Delta(R)\subseteq U(R).

This shows that
a\in J(R)
.

(viii)

Choosing
a\in eRe
, we write
a=c+d
, where
c\in C(R)
and
d\in\Delta(R)
. Since
a\in eRe
, we deduce
a=ec+ed=ce+de
forcing that
ed=de
. Evidently,
ec\in C(eRe)
. Now, we prove that
ed=de\in\Delta(eRe)
. For this purpose, assume that
u\in U(eRe)
, so
u^{\prime}=u+(1-e)\in U(R)
and, moreover,
v=1-u^{\prime}d\in U(R)
. But,
eve=ev=ve=e-ued
and, also,
(eve)(ev^{-1}e)=e
. Therefore,
ev=e-ued\in U(R)
and hence
ed=de\in\Delta(eRe)
.

∎

Recall that a ring
R
is

such that

Corollary 2.9
.

Let

be an exchange

ring. Then,

Proof.

Since

is exchange and all idempotents lift modulo

∎

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then, a
C\Delta
ring
R
is uniquely
C\Delta
(that is, each element has a unique
C\Delta
-decomposition) if, and only if,
\Delta(R)\cap C(R)=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
R
is uniquely
C\Delta
and that
c\in C\cap\Delta
. Let
0\not=r\in\Delta(R)
. We have
1+r=(1-c)+(c+r)
, where
1-c\in C(R)
and
c+r\in\Delta(R)
, because
\Delta(R)
is a subring of
R
. The uniqueness of the
C\Delta
-decomposition gives that

.

Conversely, if
C\cap\Delta=0
and

are two

decompositions, we infer
c-c^{\prime}=r^{\prime}-r\in C\cap\Delta=0

c=c^{\prime},r=r^{\prime}
, as required.
∎

Proposition 2.11

Let

(ii)

(i) Choose

. Finally,

and this insures that
R/J(R)
is reduced. Thus,
Nil(R)\subseteq J(R)
.

(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
. This is straightforward, because in Artinian rings we have always
J(R)\subseteq Nil(R)
.
∎

As finite rings are always Artinian, we directly extract the following consequence.

Corollary 2.20
.

Let
R
be a finite ring. Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

R
is a
CN
ring.

(ii)

R
is a
CJ
ring.

(iii)

R
is a
C\Delta
ring.

Let
Nil_{*}(R)
denote the
prime radical
(or, in other terms, the
lower nil-radical
) of a ring
R
, i.e., the intersection of all
prime ideals
of
R
. We know that
Nil_{*}(R)
is a nil-ideal of
R
. It is also long known that a ring
R
is called
2
-primal
if its lower nil-radical
Nil_{*}(R)
consists precisely of all the nilpotent elements of
R
, that is,
Nil_{*}(R)=Nil(R)
. For instance, it is well known that reduced rings and commutative rings are both
2
-primal.

For an endomorphism
\alpha
of a ring
R
,
R
is called
\alpha
-compatible
if, for any
a,b\in R
,
ab=0\Longleftrightarrow a\alpha(b)=0
, and in this case
\alpha
is clearly injective.

Let
R
be a ring and suppose
\alpha:R\to R
is a ring endomorphism. Then,
R[x;\alpha]
denotes the
ring of skew polynomial
over
R
with multiplication defined by
xr=\alpha(r)x
for all
r\in R
. In particular,
R[x]=R[x;1_{R}]
is the
ring of polynomials
over
R
.

Proposition 2.21
.

Let
R
be a 2-primal and
\alpha
-compatible ring. Then,

\Delta(R[x,\alpha])=\Delta(R)+Nil_{*}(R[x,\alpha])x.

Proof.

Writing that
f=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i}\in\Delta(R[x,\alpha])
, then, for every
u\in U(R)
, we have that
1-uf\in U(R[x,\alpha])
. Thus, an appeal to
[
1
, Corollary 2.14]
ensures that
1-ua_{0}\in U(R)
and, for every
1\leq i\leq n
,
ua_{i}\in Nil_{*}(R)
. Since
Nil_{*}(R)
is an ideal, we have
a_{0}\in\Delta(R)
and, for every
1\leq i\leq n
,
a_{i}\in Nil_{*}(R)
. But, as
R
is
2
-primal,
[
1
, Lemma 2.2]
is applicable to get that
Nil_{*}(R)[x,\alpha]=Nil_{*}(R[x,\alpha])
.

Conversely, assume
f\in\Delta(R)+Nil_{*}(R[x,\alpha])x
and
u\in U(R[x,\alpha])
. Then, a consultation with
[
1
, Corollary 2.14]
assures that
u\in U(R)+Nil_{*}(R[x,\alpha])x
. Since
R
is a
2
-primal ring, we have

1-uf\in U(R)+Nil_{*}(R[x,\alpha])x\subseteq U(R[x,\alpha]),

and thus
f\in\Delta(R[x,\alpha])
, as required.
∎

Lemma 2.22
.

Let
R
be a
CN
ring. Then, for every
e\in Id(R)
,
eRe
is too a
CN
ring.

Proof.

Letting
a\in eRe
, we then write
a=c+n
, where
c\in C(R)
and
n\in Nil(R)
. Since
a\in eRe
, we have
a=ec+en=ce+ne
and this, in turn, forces that
en=ne
. But,
ec\in C(eRe)
and we easily conclude that
en=ne\in Nil(eRe)
. Finally,
eRe
is a
CN
ring, as claimed.
∎

A ring
R
is called
semi-potent
if every one-sided ideal not contained in
J(R)
contains a non-zero idempotent.

Lemma 2.23
.

Let
R
be a semi-potent ring. Then, every
CN
ring is
CJ
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
Nil(R)\subseteq J(R)
. For this target, we establish that
\overline{R}=R/J(R)
is reduced. Since
R
is a semi-potent ring, we know that
\overline{R}
is also a semi-potent (and even a potent) ring.

Assume now that
0\neq x\in\overline{R}
with
x^{2}=0
. Thus,
[
12
, Theorem 2.1]
applies to get that there exists
e\in\overline{R}
such that
e\overline{R}e\cong M_{2}(S)
, where
S
is a non-zero ring. However, since
\overline{R}
is a
CN
ring in accordance with Lemma
2.22
),
e\overline{R}e
is also
CN
. However, on the other hand, it is known that
M_{2}(S)
is not a
CN
ring, which leads to a contradiction. Therefore,
\overline{R}
is a reduced ring, as promised.
∎

Corollary 2.24
.

Let
R
be a semi-potent
CN
ring. Then,
R/J(R)
is commutative.

Proof.

With Lemma
2.23
at hand, we conclude that
\overline{R}=R/J(R)
is reduced and hence
Nil(\overline{R})=(0)
. Since
R
is
CN
, it follows that
\overline{R}
is
CN
as well, and hence it is commutative. ∎

Lemma 2.25
.

Let
R
be a
CN
ring. Then,
R
is abelian.

Proof.

Suppose
e\in Id(R)
. So,
e=c+n
, where
c\in C(R)
and
n\in Nil(R)
. Therefore, we write
c-c^{2}=n-n^{2}-2cn\in Nil(R)
. Thus, owing to
[
18
, Lemma 3.5]
, there exists a monic polynomial
f(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
, where
{f(a)}^{2}=f(a)
and
m=c-f\in Nil(R)
. Since

, we have
f,m\in C(R)
. So,
e-f=m+n\in Nil(R)
. On the other side,
(e-f)^{3}=e-f
. This means that

, whence

is abelian, as stated.
∎

As a final consequence, we detect:

Corollary 2.26
.

Let

be an exchange

is strongly clean.

3.

We are here concentrated on certain non-trivial extensions of

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring. For any
n\geq 2
,
A\in M_{n}(R)
has a
C\Delta
-decomposition if, and only if,
A-cI_{n}\in M_{n}(J(R))
for some

.

Proof.

Assume that
A\in M_{n}(R)
has a
C\Delta
-decomposition. By assumption, there exists
c\in R
such that
A-cI_{n}\in M_{n}(J(R))
. Note that, for any
B\in M_{n}(R)
,
B
is central in
M_{n}(R)
precisely when there exists
c\in R
such that
B=cI_{n}
, as required.

Conversely, suppose that, for any
A\in M_{n}(R)
, there exists
c\in R
such that
A-cI_{n}\in M_{n}(J(R))
. As
cI_{n}
is central in
M_{n}(R)
, it follows that
A\in M_{n}(R)
has a
C\Delta
-decomposition, as needed.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
R
be a ring and

a positive integer. Then,
A\in M_{n}(R)
has a

-decomposition if, and only if, for each

,

has a

-decomposition.

Remark 3.3
.

Since
\Delta(M_{n}(R))=J(M_{n}(R))
for any

and

, we notice that

is never a

ring for any

.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring. Then,
T_{2}(R)
is a
C\Delta
ring if, and only if, for any
a,b\in R
, there exists
c\in R
such that

.

Proof.

Let
A=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{pmatrix}\in T_{2}(R)
. So,

has a
C\Delta
-decomposition if, and only if, there exist
C=\begin{pmatrix}c&0\\
0&c\end{pmatrix}\in C(T_{2}(R))
and

0&z\end{pmatrix}\in\Delta(T_{2}(R))
such that

. Thus,

if, and only if,

. Also,
A=C+D
is a
C\Delta
-decomposition if, and only if, there exists
c\in R
such that
A-cI_{2}\in T_{2}(R)
if, and only if,
a-c,b-c\in\Delta(R)
, as asked for.
∎

Example 3.5
.

Consider the ring
R=\mathbb{Z}
. So,

is

, but

is not
C\Delta
. Consider the ring

0&5\end{pmatrix}\in T_{2}(R)
. Then, one inspects that there is no

such that both

and

belong to

. So, we deduce that

is not
C\Delta
, as pursued.

Let
R
be a ring and suppose
\alpha:R\to R
is a ring endomorphism. Standardly,
R[[x;\alpha]]
denotes the ring of
skew formal power series
over

; that is, all formal power series in

with coefficients from

and with multiplication defined by

for all

. In particular,

is the

over

.

We now come to the following criterion.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
R
be a ring. The ring
R[[x;\alpha]]
is
C\Delta
if, and only if,
R
is
C\Delta
.

Proof.

Let
R[[x;\alpha]]
be
C\Delta
. Consider
I:=R[[x;\alpha]]x
. Then, one verifies that
I
is an ideal of
R[[x;\alpha]]
. Note also that a routine calculation leads to
J(R[[x;\alpha]])=J(R)+I
, so
I\subseteq J(R[[x;\alpha]])
. Since
R[[x;\alpha]]/I\cong R
, we derive with the aid of Proposition
2.8
(i) that
R
is
C\Delta
.

Conversely, let
R
be
C\Delta
and write
f(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+a_{2}x+\dots\in R[[x;\alpha]]
. Then,
a_{i}=c_{i}+r_{i}
, where
c_{i}\in C(R)
and
r_{i}\in\Delta(R)
for every
i
. Thus, we have
f(x)=g(x)+k(x)
, where

g(x)=c_{0}+c_{1}x+c_{2}x+\dots\in C(R)[[x;\alpha]]\subseteq C(R[[x;\alpha]])

and

k(x)=r_{0}+r_{1}x+r_{2}x+\dots\in\Delta(R)[[x;\alpha]]=\Delta(R[[x;\alpha]]).

This enables us that

is
C\Delta
, as expected.
∎

Our next automatic consequence sounds like this.

Corollary 3.7
.

A ring
R[[x]]
is
C\Delta
if, and only if, so is
R
.

We now have all the ingredients necessary to attack the truthfulness of the following main statement.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
R
be a
2
-primal ring and let
\alpha
be an endomorphism of
R
such that
R
is
\alpha
-compatible. The following are equivalent:

(i)

R
is a
CN
ring.

(ii)

R[x;\alpha]
is a
C\Delta
ring.

(iii)

R[x;\alpha]
is a
CJ
ring.

(iv)

R[x;\alpha]
is a
CN
ring.

Proof.

As
R
is
2
-primal,
R/Nil_{*}(R)
is reduced, and so we have

J(R[x;\alpha])=\operatorname{Nil}_{*}(R[x;\alpha])=\operatorname{Nil}_{*}(R)[x;\alpha].

Hence, points (iii) and (iv) are equivalent.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
. Supposing that
f=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i}\in R[x;\alpha]
, we have
a_{i}=c_{i}+n_{i}
, where
c_{i}\in C(R)
and
n_{i}\in Nil(R)=Nil_{*}(R)
for every
i
. Moreover, we know that
n_{0}\in Nil(R)\subseteq Nil_{*}(R)\subseteq J(R)\subseteq\Delta(R)
. Therefore, taking into account Proposition
2.21
, we write

f=\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}x^{i}+n_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}n_{i}x^{i}\in C(R)+\Delta(R)+Nil_{*}(R)[x;\alpha]x\subseteq C(R)+\Delta(R[x;\alpha])\subseteq

C(R[x;\alpha])+\Delta(R[x;\alpha]),

as required.

(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
. For every
a\in R
, we have that
ax=\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}x^{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{n}d_{i}x^{i}
. Thus,
a=c_{0}+d_{0}
, where
d_{0}\in Nil_{*}(R)
owing to Proposition
2.21
. Now, it suffices to demonstrate that
c_{0}\in C(R)
. For this aim, we set
f:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}x^{i}
. Since we know
rf=fr
for every
r\in R
, it gives that
rc_{0}=c_{0}r
. So,
c_{0}\in C(R)
and hence
R
is a
CN
ring, as required.

(i)\iff(iv)
. With a proof very close to the previous implication, the desired result can easily be obtained.
∎

As a direct consequence, we yield:

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
R
be a
2
-primal ring. Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

R
is a
CN
ring.

(ii)

R[x]
is a
C\Delta
ring.

(iii)

R[x]
is a
CJ
ring.

(iv)

R[x]
is a
CN
ring.

Let
Nil^{*}(R)
be the sum of all nil-ideals in
R
. We call
Nil^{*}(R)
the
upper nil-radical
of
R
. This is the largest nil-ideal of
R
. In order to remember the famous Köthe
conjecture
to the non-expert reader, we refer to
[
9
, 10.28]
for a more detailed information.

So, we are in a position to show validity of the following major assertion.

Theorem 3.10
.

If
R[x]
is a
C\Delta
ring, then
R
satisfies the Köthe
conjecture
.

Proof.

Assume that
R[x]
is
C\Delta
. It suffices to establish that
J(R[x])=Nil^{*}(R)[x]
. Indeed, since
J(R[x])=N[x]
, where
N=J(R[x])\cap R
, we show that
Nil^{*}(R)=N
. To that goal, given
a\in Nil^{*}(R)
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a^{k}=0
; thus,
a^{k}\in J(R[x])
. Since
R[x]
is
C\Delta
, the quotient
R[x]/J(R[x])
is reduced, which fact is enabled from Proposition
2.8
(vii). So, we conclude that
a\in J(R[x])
and this insures that
a\in N
. On the other hand, since
N
is a nil-ideal and
Nil^{*}(R)
is the largest nil-ideal of
R
, we infer
N\subseteq Nil^{*}(R)
. Therefore,
N=Nil^{*}(R)
, as required.
∎

The next two necessary and sufficient conditions are worthy of documentation.

Proposition 3.11
.

For any ring
R
and an integer

, the following are equivalent:

(i)

is
C\Delta
.

(ii)

D_{n}(R)
is

.

(iii)

.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
. Let

&&\ddots&&\\
0&&&&a\end{pmatrix}\in D_{n}(R).

Thanks to (i), there exist
c\in C(R)
and

such that

. Let

&c&&\\
&&\ddots&\\

&b&&\\
&&\ddots&\\

. Therefore,

(ii)\Rightarrow(i)

&a&&\\
\ &&\ddots&\\

.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let

if, and only if,

is

.

We know that

and, thanks to

We noticed earlier that, if
n\geq 2
, then
M_{n}(R)
is
not

C\Delta
. We now exhibit certain subrings of

that are

whenever

.

And so, let

We, thereby, arrive at the following technicality.

Lemma 3.13

Let

be a ring, and

. Then,

\Delta(L_{(s,t)}(R))=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&0&0\\

Assume that

For any element

Under our hypothesis, it must be that

,
1+dz
,

. Hence,

and

, as needed.

Conversely, suppose that

sc&d&te\\
0&0&f\end{pmatrix}\in\Delta(L_{(s,t)}(R)).

Thus, we have

,

,

. This unambiguously shows that

, as required.

Consider the following subring of

:

Proposition 3.14
.

Let

,

(i)

Then, there exist

and

such that

. Hence, for the next two matrices

0&0&r\end{pmatrix},

we detect that

and

. Therefore,

is the wanted

-decomposition of

in

.

For the converse, choose

and consider

Then, there exist

0&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&0&a\end{pmatrix}\in T_{n+m-1}(R):a,b_{i},d_{j},c_{i,j}\in R\right\}.

In this vein, let

be

\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c@{}}\begin{matrix}a&b_{1}&b_{2}&\cdots&b_{n-1}\\

0&0&a&\cdots&b_{n-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

\hline\cr\mbox{\Large 0}&\begin{matrix}a&c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{m-1}\\
0&a&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{m-2}\\
0&0&a&\cdots&c_{m-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&a\end{matrix}\end{array}\right)\in T_{n+m}(R):a,b_{i},c_{j}\in R\right\},

and

U_{n}(R)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}&b_{4}&\cdots&b_{n-1}\\
0&a&c_{1}&c_{2}&c_{3}&\cdots&c_{n-2}\\
0&0&a&b_{1}&b_{2}&\cdots&b_{n-3}\\
0&0&0&a&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{n-4}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&0&\cdots&a\end{pmatrix}\in T_{n}(R):a,b_{i},c_{j}\in R\right\}.
