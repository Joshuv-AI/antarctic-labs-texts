Hybrid Partial Least Squares Regression with Multiple Functional and Scalar Predictors
Jongmin Mun, Jeong Hoon Jang

Abstract
Motivated by renal imaging studies that combine renogram curves with pharmacokinetic and demographic covariates, we propose Hybrid partial least squares (Hybrid PLS) for simultaneous supervised dimension reduction and regression in the presence of cross-modality correlations. The proposed approach embeds multiple functional and scalar predictors into a unified hybrid Hilbert space and rigorously extends the nonlinear iterative PLS (NIPALS) algorithm. This theoretical development is complemented by a sample-level algorithm that incorporates roughness penalties to control smoothness. By exploiting the rank-one structure of the resulting optimization problem, the algorithm admits a computationally efficient closed-form solution that requires solving only linear systems at each iteration. We establish fundamental geometric properties of the proposed framework, including orthogonality of the latent scores and PLS directions. Extensive numerical studies on synthetic data, together with an application to a renal imaging study, validate these theoretical results and demonstrate the method’s ability to recover predictive structure under intermodal multicollinearity, yielding parsimonious low-dimensional representations. Keywords: Dimension reduction, functional data analysis, multiple data modalities, multivariate data analysis, multivariate functional data, partial least square.

Analyzing this hybrid model poses two fundamental challenges: achieving parsimony and mitigating multicollinearity.
Parsimonious representations are essential for interpretability, particularly when clinical scores and decision rules are of interest.
Multicollinearity is especially challenging in this setting, as it arises at multiple levels:
(i) within-function correlations, (ii) between-function correlations, (iii) correlations among scalar predictors,
and (iv) intermodal correlations between functional and scalar predictors.
Ignoring these dependencies can substantially inflate estimation variance, leading to overfitting and
degraded predictive performance at deployment.

Our motivating example is the Emory University renal study
[
12
,
28
]
, which collects two renogram curves (functional data) per kidney for the purpose of detecting kidney obstruction. Following intravenous injection of the tracer
99m
Tc-MAG3, the kidneys filter the tracer and transport it toward the bladder; renogram curves are obtained by tracking the tracer photon (MAG3) count within the kidneys over time. Owing to this tracer-based recording process, previous analyses have typically relied on scalar pharmacokinetic variables—summary measures characterizing tracer uptake, transport, and clearance, such as maximum intensity, time to minimum velocity, and area under the curve—to train predictive models. However, this strategy risks discarding subtle structural information contained in the full functional data, as illustrated in Figures
1
-(a) and
1
-(b). A more comprehensive approach is to jointly incorporate the complete functional curves and their scalar summaries within a PFLM, thereby fully leveraging their predictive potential. This joint modeling induces strong correlations between the two modalities, as shown in Figure
1
-(c), making this dataset an ideal test case for the setting considered here. Additional details on the dataset are provided in Section
6.4
.

Most existing methods are designed for homogeneous data structures.
High-dimensional Euclidean regression typically relies on structured regularization, such as lasso
[
48
]
,
SCAD
[

]
, or

[

Another class of methods relies on dimension reduction techniques. Principal component regression (PCR;

[

[
53
]
employs supervised dimension reduction.
Functional regression methods, by contrast, focus on processing functional predictors using basis expansions with roughness penalties, functional PCA
[
40
,
3
,
2
]
, or functional PLS
[
38
]
.

PFLM

[
43
,
32
,
34
]

integrate functional and scalar predictors through a two-step procedure: functional predictors are first converted into finite-dimensional Euclidean representations, followed by an integrated regression with scalar covariates. However, these approaches overlook the complex joint dependencies between predictors, and the functional component is processed in a response-agnostic manner, as the primary emphasis is on regression coefficient estimation rather than prediction.

To address these limitations, we propose a
hybrid PLS regression
framework. By constructing a unified Hilbert space that jointly represents functional and Euclidean predictors, our approach enables a hybrid extension of the nonlinear iterative partial least squares (NIPALS;
53
) algorithm. The proposed method alternates between (i) extracting joint latent directions that maximize covariance between the predictors and the response and (ii) residualizing both predictors and response. This framework naturally captures all four layers of correlation, preserves predictive power, and yields parsimonious representations. Moreover, by employing a rich basis expansion together with smoothness regularization of the hybrid PLS directions, the method borrows strength across predictors and accommodates dense or irregular functional data. The algorithm remains computationally efficient, relying solely on linear system solutions. The resulting hybrid PLS scores are mutually uncorrelated and provide a clinically interpretable representation of the data.

Related literature

The challenges of achieving parsimony and mitigating multicollinearity have been extensively studied in high-dimensional regression with Euclidean predictors. We briefly review the main methodological developments relevant to our work.

Integration of functional and scalar predictors has primarily been studied in the PFLM framework

2
Background on Partial Least Squares Regression

This section provides a brief introduction to Nonlinear Iterative Partial Least Squares (NIPALS;
53
), the standard algorithm for fitting PLS regression in Euclidean spaces. Consider the high-dimensional regression model:

Y_{i}=\bm{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}+\epsilon_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

where
\mathbf{Z}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
represents the predictor vector, typically in the regime where the dimension
p
far exceeds the sample size
n
(
p\gg n
).
To address high multicollinearity in
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
, a common approach is to approximate
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
using a low-dimensional score vector
\hat{\bm{\rho}}_{i}:=(\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[1]},\ldots,\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[L]})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
with mutually uncorrelated entries, which serve as coefficients with respect to a chosen set of basis vectors (or directions). Ordinary least squares (OLS) is then performed on these scores.
While principal component analysis (PCA) constructs these directions to capture only the variation within the predictors,
partial least squares (PLS) constructs them to explicitly maximize both predictor variation and the correlation with the response.
Let
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
denote the data matrix.
Assuming the data has been centered,
the
l
-th PLS direction
\hat{\bm{\xi}}^{[l]}
is defined as the solution to the constrained squared covariance maximization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}

\displaystyle\biggl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\mathbf{h},\mathbf{Z}_{i}\rangle Y_{i}\biggr)^{2}

(1)

s.t.

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{h}\|_{2}=1,~\mathbf{h}^{\top}(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z})\hat{\bm{\xi}}^{[j]}=0,\quad j=1,\ldots,l-1.

The NIPALS algorithm,
summarized in Algorithm
1
,
solves this by iteratively alternating between covariance maximization and residualization.

Extending Algorithm
1
to the hybrid model (
1
) requires jointly handling correlated functional and scalar components. A naive pointwise approach, which concatenates functional evaluations and scalar predictors into a single Euclidean vector, is computationally prohibitive, incompatible with irregular data, and prone to overfitting.
The extension to the hybrid setting faces three primary challenges :

1.

Independent variables consist of multiple highly structured functional images and scalar predictors.

2.

Sample sizes are small relative to the high dimensionality of the combined predictors.

3.

Existing PLS methods typically accommodate only functional predictors
[
38
,
16
]
or a single functional predictor with scalar covariates
[
52
]
, lacking a general framework for complex hybrid data.

The next section proposes a new framework to address these limitations.

3
Population-Level Hybrid PLS

We now formulate the population-level PLS algorithm by defining a unified Hilbert space for hybrid predictors. This framework allows us to extend the classical NIPALS algorithm, which relies solely on inner product operations, to the hybrid setting.

3.1
The Hybrid Hilbert Space

To treat functional and scalar predictors within a single mathematical structure, we introduce the product space
\mathbb{H}
.

Definition 3.1

(Hybrid Space)
.

Let
\mathbb{H}:=(\mathbb{L}^{2}[0,1])^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
An element
h\in\mathbb{H}
is an ordered tuple
h=(f_{1},\dots,f_{K},\mathbf{u})
.
For any
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{H}
, we define the inner product:

\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{1}f_{1k}(t)f_{2k}(t)\,dt+\omega\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{2},

(2)

where
\omega>0
is a weight parameter controlling the relative importance of the scalar component. This inner product induces the norm
\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}:=\langle h,h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{1/2}
.

The weight
\omega
addresses the heterogeneity between functional and scalar parts, particularly regarding measurement scales (discussed further in Section
4.1
).
For theoretical clarity, we assume
\omega=1
in this section, noting that all results generalize to any
\omega>0
.
Under this inner product,
\mathbb{H}
is a separable Hilbert space (see Appendix
B
for details). Our method naturally generalizes to settings where each functional predictor resides in a unique Hilbert space, potentially defined over distinct compact domains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with varying observation points. However, for notational simplicity, we assume a shared Hilbert space over
[0,1]
for all functional predictors.

We define the hybrid predictor
W=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{K},\mathbf{Z})
as a random element taking values in
\mathbb{H}
, measurable with respect to the Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathbbm{B}(\mathbb{H})
. The joint regression model (
1
) can then be written concisely as:

Y=\langle\beta,W\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\epsilon,\quad\text{where }\beta\in\mathbb{H}.

(3)

Throughout this section, we assume centered variables,
\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[W]=0
.

3.2
Covariance Operators and PLS Directions

The core of partial least squares (PLS) involves maximizing the squared covariance between the predictor and the response.
To characterize this at the population level, we first define the covariance operator
\Sigma_{W}
such that, for
u,v\in\mathbb{H}
,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{W}u

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle W,u\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\,W\big],\quad\langle\Sigma_{W}u,v\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle W,u\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\,\langle W,v\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\big].

Next, we define the cross-covariance vector

\Sigma_{YW}:=\mathbb{E}[\,YW\,]\in\mathbb{H},

which is composed of functional components and scalar components

\sigma_{YX}:=\big(\mathbb{E}[YX_{1}],\ldots,\mathbb{E}[YX_{K}]\big)\quad\sigma_{YZ}:=\big(\mathbb{E}[YZ_{1}],\ldots,\mathbb{E}[YZ_{p}]\big)^{\top}.

This, in turn, induces the cross-covariance operator

\mathcal{C}_{YW}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined as

\mathcal{C}_{YW}h:=\langle\Sigma_{YW},h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

Finally, we define the composite operator
\mathcal{U}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
as
\mathcal{U}:=\Sigma_{YW}\otimes\Sigma_{YW}
, which acts on any
h\in\mathbb{H}
via:

\mathcal{U}h=\mathcal{C}_{WY}(\mathcal{C}_{YW}h)=\mathcal{C}_{YW}(\langle\Sigma_{YW},h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}})=\Sigma_{YW}\,\langle\Sigma_{YW},h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=(\Sigma_{YW}\otimes\Sigma_{YW})h.

To ensure the constrained squared covariance maximization problem in NIPALS is well-posed in the hybrid space, we show that, under mild moment conditions,
\mathcal{U}
is a compact, self-adjoint, and positive-semidefinite operator. Proof of Lemma
3.1

is provided in Appendix
C.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If there exist finite constants
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\max\limits_{k=1,\ldots,K}\sup\limits_{t\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}[YX_{k}](t)^{2}<Q_{1}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\max_{r=1,\ldots,p}\mathbb{E}[YZ_{r}]^{2}<Q_{2}.

(4)

the operator

\mathcal{U}

is self-adjoint, positive-semidefinite and compact.

By the Hilbert-Schmidt theorem (e.g.,
27
, Theorem 4.2.4
), Lemma
3.1
guarantees that
\mathcal{U}
admits a complete orthonormal system of eigenfunctions
\{\xi_{(u)}\}_{u\in\mathbb{N}}
with corresponding non-negative eigenvalues
\kappa_{(1)}\geq\kappa_{(2)}\geq\cdots\to 0
.
The population PLS algorithm constructs directions by maximizing squared covariance. The following Theorem establishes that this optimization is equivalent to an eigenproblem for
\mathcal{U}
, ensuring its well-posedness via Lemma
3.1
.

Theorem 3.2
.

At the
l
-th step, the population PLS direction
\xi^{[l]}
is defined as the maximizer of the squared covariance:

\xi^{[l]}:=\arg\max_{h\in\mathbb{H},\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1}\operatorname{Cov}^{2}(\langle W^{[l]},h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},Y^{[l]}).

(5)

Under the condition of Lemma
3.1
,
this maximum is attained by the eigenfunction corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of the operator
\mathcal{U}^{[l]}:=\Sigma_{Y^{[l]}W^{[l]}}\otimes\Sigma_{Y^{[l]}W^{[l]}}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.2
is provided in Appendix
C.2
.
Based on this legitimate eigenproblem, we extend NIPALS to the hybrid setting as follows:

Theorem 3.3

(Properties of Population PLS)
.

The
l
-th PLS direction
\xi^{[l]}
, solving (
5
), equivalently satisfies the constrained maximization:

\xi^{[l]}:=\arg\max_{h\in\mathbb{H}}\operatorname{Cov}^{2}(\langle W,h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},Y),~s.t.~\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1,\langle h,\Sigma_{W}\,\xi^{[j]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=0,\,j<l.

Furthermore, the algorithm guarantees orthogonality of the scores and uncorrelated residuals. For every
s\geq 1
and every
j\leq s-1
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}\rho^{[s]}]=0

and

\mathbb{E}[Y^{[s]}\rho^{[j]}]=0.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
is provided in Appendix
C.3
.

.

The Hybrid PLS fitted value converges to

in the mean-squared sense:

\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\bigl\|\sum_{l=1}^{L}\nu^{[l]}\rho^{[l]}-Y\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\biggr]=0.

Proposed PLS Algorithm

We propose a sample version of hybrid NIPALS (Algorithm
3
) to efficiently compute PLS components for multiple dense or irregular functional predictors alongside scalar predictors. The algorithm alternates between two subroutines: regularized component estimation (Section
4.2.1
) and residualization (Section

).

4.1
Preliminary step: Finite-basis approximation, notations, and normalization

Let
\{b_{m}(t)\}_{m=1}^{M}
denote a basis of
\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,1])
with linearly independent second derivatives (e.g., cubic B-splines, Fourier series, or orthonormal polynomials). We employ this basis, truncated at a moderate size (e.g.,
M\approx 20
), to approximate the functional predictors
X_{ij}(t)
, regression coefficients
\beta_{j}(t)
, PLS directions
\xi_{j}(t)
, and residualization coefficients
\delta_{j}(t)
; iteration indices are suppressed here for clarity.
Consequently, all computations are performed within the finite-dimensional approximation space
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}:=\operatorname{span}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{M})^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We denote the approximated predictors for the
i
-th subject as
\widetilde{W}_{i}=(\widetilde{X}_{i1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{iK},\mathbf{Z}_{i})
.
We denote the scalar basis coefficients for these functions as
\{\theta_{ijm}\},\{\eta_{jm}\},\{\gamma_{jm}\},
and
\{\pi_{jm}\}
, respectively. Finally, we collect these coefficients into the corresponding
M
-dimensional vectors
\bm{\theta}_{ij},\bm{\eta}_{j},\bm{\gamma}_{j},\text{ and }\bm{\pi}_{j}
.

Matrix tuple Representation

We organize the predictor coefficients into matrices
\Theta_{j}:=(\bm{\theta}_{1j},\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{nj})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times M}
and stack the full set of predictors as
\Theta:=(\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{K},\mathbf{Z})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(MK+p)}
. The response vector is denoted by
\mathbf{y}:=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}
.Next, let
\mathbf{B},\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
be the Gram matrices containing the inner products of the basis functions and their second derivatives, defined by entries
B_{m,m^{\prime}}:=\int_{0}^{1}b_{m}(t)b_{m^{\prime}}(t)dt
and
B^{\prime\prime}_{m,m^{\prime}}:=\int_{0}^{1}b_{m}^{\prime\prime}(t)b_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime\prime}(t)dt
. We construct the corresponding block-diagonal matrices:

\mathbb{B}:=\operatorname{blkdiag}(\mathbf{B},\ldots,\mathbf{B},\mathbf{I}_{p}),\quad\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}:=\operatorname{blkdiag}(B^{\prime\prime},\ldots,B^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{I}_{p}).

(6)

The data at the
l
-th iteration (index omitted) is thus fully characterized by the tuple

(\mathbb{B},\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime},\Theta,\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{(MK+p)\times(MK+p)}\times\mathbb{R}^{(MK+p)\times(MK+p)}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times(MK+p)}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(7)

Although we employ a common basis for simplicity, the definitions of
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}
readily generalize to distinct bases for each predictor, facilitating the analysis of multiple dense or irregular functional predictors.

Data Preprocessing

To handle discrepancies in units and variation between functional and scalar predictors, we employ a two-step standardization. First, functional predictors are standardized to zero mean and unit integrated variance, and scalar predictors to zero mean and unit variance. Second, to balance the influence of the functional and scalar parts, we scale the scalar vector
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
by a factor
\omega^{1/2}
, where

\omega=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\widetilde{X}_{ik}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{Z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}.

This is equivalent to using a weighted inner product (
2
) in the hybrid space.

4.2
Iterative Steps

The algorithm iteratively constructs an orthonormal hybrid basis that captures predictor-response relationships. Each iteration consists of two steps: estimating the PLS direction (Section
4.2.1
) and residualizing the data (Section
4.2.2
). iteration indices are suppressed where unambiguous.

4.2.1
Step 1: Regularized estimation of PLS direction

Since the regression coefficient
\hat{\beta}
is derived as a linear combination of PLS directions (Section
4.3
), non-smooth PLS directions inherently yield a non-smooth
\hat{\beta}
, leading to potential overfitting and reduced interpretability. To address this, we estimate the PLS direction
\xi\in\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}
by maximizing the squared empirical covariance with the response, subject to a roughness constraint. Adopting the generalized smoothing framework
[
45
]
, this constraint regulates the complexity of the functional components while facilitating the borrowing of strength across them. Accordingly, at the
l
-th iteration (with indices suppressed and assuming prior residualization), our procedure solves the following optimization problem:

\hat{\xi}:=\arg\max_{\xi\in\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}}~\widehat{\textnormal{Cov}}^{2}\bigl(\langle\widetilde{W},\xi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},Y\bigr)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|\xi\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_{j}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\{\xi_{j}^{\prime\prime}(t)\bigr\}^{2}dt=1.

(8)

The smoothing parameters
\{\lambda_{j}\}
control the trade-off between covariance maximization and curve smoothness; notably, setting
\lambda_{j}=0
recovers the standard unregularized PLS solution. The resulting solution
\hat{\xi}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}
constitutes an ordered pair expanded as:

\hat{\xi}=\bigl(\hat{\xi}_{1}(t),\ldots,\hat{\xi}_{K}(t),\hat{\bm{\zeta}}\bigr)=\biggl(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{\gamma}_{1m},b_{m}(t),\ldots,\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{\gamma}_{Km},b_{m}(t),\hat{\bm{\zeta}}\biggr).

We define the correlation matrix

\mathbf{V}:=n^{-2}\big(\mathbb{B}\Theta^{\top}\mathbf{y}\big)\big(\mathbb{B}\Theta^{\top}\mathbf{y}\big)^{\top},

and the penalty matrix

\Lambda:=\operatorname{blkdiag}\big(\lambda_{1}\mathbf{I}_{M},\ldots,\lambda_{K}\mathbf{I}_{M},\mathbf{0}_{p\times p}\big).

The optimization in (
8
) is equivalent to the generalized Rayleigh quotient:

Theorem 4.1

(Regularized estimation of PLS component direction)
.

Let

(\mathbb{B},\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime},\Theta,\mathbf{y})

denote the data given at the
l
-th iteration, as defined in (
7
).

\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{(MK+p)\times(MK+p)}
be defined as:

\Lambda:=\operatorname{blkdiag}(\lambda_{1}\mathbf{I}_{M},\ldots,\lambda_{K}\mathbf{I}_{M},\mathbf{0}_{p\times p}),~\text{where}~\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{K}\geq 0.

(9)

Here,
\mathbf{0}_{p\times p}
denotes the
p\times p
zero matrix.
The coefficients of the squared covariance maximizer defined in (
8
),
are obtained as

\left(\hat{\gamma}_{11},\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_{1M},\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_{K1},\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_{KM},\hat{\bm{\zeta}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}\hskip-6.99997pt=\arg\hskip-6.00006pt\max_{\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{MK+p}}\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\bm{\xi}~~\text{s.t.}~~\bm{\xi}^{\top}\hskip-1.99997pt(\mathbb{B}+\Lambda\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime})\bm{\xi}=1.

(10)

The proof of Proposition
4.1
is provided in Appendix
D.1
. The constraint
\bm{\xi}^{\top}(\mathbb{B}+\Lambda\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime})\bm{\xi}=1
enforces orthonormality under a modified inner product (Section
5
). The parameter
\lambda_{k}
governs the smoothness-fit trade-off: setting
\lambda_{k}=0
recovers the unregularized solution (Proposition
D.1
), while
\lambda_{k}\to\infty
forces a linear structure. Values of
\{\lambda_{k}\}
are selected via cross-validation.

While generalized eigenproblems can be computationally unstable, the rank-one structure of
V
allows for an efficient closed-form solution solving linear systems for the functional and scalar components separately, followed by normalization by a common factor.

Theorem 4.2

(Closed-form solution)
.

Let us define

\mathbf{u}_{j}:=\mathbf{B}\Theta_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M},~j=1,\ldots,K,\quad\mathbf{v}:=\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\quad q:=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{j}+\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{v}.

The unique solution (up to sign) to (
10
) is:

\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,K;\qquad\hat{\bm{\zeta}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\mathbf{v}.

The proof of Proposition
4.2
is provided in Appendix
D.2
.
The normalization factor
q
couples the functional and scalar components, ensuring the solution captures the correlation structure between them.

4.2.2
Iterative step 2: residualization via hybrid-on-scalar regression

The second step of the
l
-th iteration residualizes both predictors and responses. First, we compute the PLS scores using the estimated direction
\widehat{\xi}^{[l]}
:

\hat{\rho}^{[l]}_{i}:=\langle\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]},\,\hat{\xi}^{[l]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

(11)

Assuming centered data, these scores possess a sample mean of zero. Next, we regress the current predictors and responses on these scores. The hybrid regression coefficient
\hat{\delta}^{[l]}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}
is estimated via unpenalized least squares:

\hat{\delta}^{[l]}:=\arg\min_{\delta\in\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]}-\hat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\,\delta\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}.

(12)

We omit roughness penalties in this step to ensure the orthonormality of the resulting PLS components (see Section
4.3
), relying instead on the smoothness of
\hat{\xi}^{[l]}
to regulate the regression coefficient
\beta
.
Simultaneously, the response regression coefficient
\hat{\nu}^{[l]}
is estimated via scalar least squares. The following lemma provides the closed-form updates, which mirror standard scalar PLS.

Lemma 4.3

(Closed-form solution)
.

Let
\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{[l]}:=(\hat{\rho}^{[l]}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\rho}^{[l]}_{n})^{\top}
. The predictors and responses for the
(l+1)
-th iteration are updated as:

\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l+1]}:=\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]}-\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\hat{\delta}^{[l]},\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{\delta}^{[l]}:=\frac{1}{\|\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{[l]}\|_{2}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]},

and

Y_{i}^{[l+1]}=Y_{i}^{[l]}-\hat{\nu}^{[l]}\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]},\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{\nu}^{[l]}:=\frac{\mathbf{y}^{[l]\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{[l]}}{\|\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{[l]}\|_{2}^{2}}.

(13)

Proof of Lemma
4.3
is provided in Appendix
D.3
. Note that since the inputs are centered, the residuals
\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l+1]}
and
Y_{i}^{[l+1]}
remain centered.

4.3
Final step: estimating the hybrid regression coefficient

The hybrid regression coefficient
\beta
in model (
3
) can be written
as a linear combination of PLS directions:

Lemma 4.4
.

Let us define

\widehat{\iota}^{[1]}:=\widehat{\xi}^{[1]}.

For
l\geq 2
, we recursively define:

\widehat{\iota}^{[l]}=\widehat{\xi}^{[l]}-\sum_{u=1}^{l-1}\langle\widehat{\delta}^{[u]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\widehat{\iota}^{[u]}.

Then we have:

\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}=\langle W_{i}^{[l]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\langle W_{i},\widehat{\iota}^{[l]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

Proof of Lemma
4.4
is provided in Appendix
E
.

Next, (
13
) leads to the following model:

Y_{i}=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\widehat{\nu}^{[l]}\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}+\epsilon_{i}.

This model lets us to express
Y_{i}

Y_{i}=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\widehat{\nu}^{[l]}\langle W_{i}^{[l]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\epsilon_{i}=\bigl\langle W_{i},\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\widehat{\nu}^{[l]}\widehat{\iota}^{[l]}\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\epsilon_{i},

which, given the uniqueness of
\beta

\widehat{\beta}=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\widehat{\nu}^{[l]}\widehat{\iota}^{[l]}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(\widehat{\nu}^{[l]}-\sum_{k=l+1}^{L}\widehat{\nu}^{[k]}\langle\widehat{\delta}^{[l]},\widehat{\xi}^{[k]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right)\widehat{\xi}^{[l]}.

(14)

5
Geometric Properties of the hybrid PLS

This section provides the mathematical properties that support the algorithm suggested in Section
4
. Specifically,
it demonstrates that our algorithm preserves the core properties of PLS, namely the orthonormality of the derived directions and the orthogonality of the scores.

A key property of PLS is that its directions are orthonormal and its scores orthogonal across iterations. Our regularized estimates preserve this property under a modified inner product that incorporates the roughness penalty, defined as follows:

Definition 5.1

(Roughness-sensitive inner product)
.

Given two hybrid predictors
W_{1}=(X_{11},\ldots,X_{1K},\mathbf{Z}_{1})
and
W_{2}=(X_{21},\ldots,X_{2K},\mathbf{Z}_{2})
, both elements of
\mathbb{H}
as defined in Definition
3.1
, and a roughness penalty matrix
\Lambda=\operatorname{blkdiag}(\lambda_{1}\mathbf{I}_{M},\ldots,\lambda_{K}\mathbf{I}_{M},\mathbf{0}_{p\times p})
, the roughness-sensitive inner product between
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
is defined as:

\langle W_{1},W_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H},\Lambda}:=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{1}X_{1k}(t)X_{2k}(t)\,dt+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}\int_{0}^{1}X^{\prime\prime}_{1k}(t)X^{\prime\prime}_{2k}(t)\,dt+\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{2}.

(15)

Based on this inner product, the following proposition states that the PLS component directions estimated from Proposition
4.1
are orthonormal.

Theorem 5.1

(Orthonormality of estimated PLS component directions)
.

The PLS component directions
\widehat{\xi}^{[1]},\widehat{\xi}^{[2]},\ldots,\widehat{\xi}^{[L]}
, estimated via Proposition
4.1
with a roughness penalty matrix
\Lambda=\operatorname{blkdiag}(\lambda_{1}\mathbf{I}_{M},\ldots,\lambda_{K}\mathbf{I}_{M},\mathbf{0}_{p\times p})
, are mutually orthonormal with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{H},\Lambda}
. That is,

\langle\widehat{\xi}^{[l_{1}]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l_{2}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H},\Lambda}=\mathbbm{1}(l_{1}=l_{2}),\quad l_{1},l_{2}=1,\ldots,L.

The proof of Proposition
5.1
is provided in Appendix
F.1
.

The next proposition states that the vectors of estimated PLS scores for different iteration numbers are mutually orthogonal.

Theorem 5.2
.

Recall from Lemma
4.3
that
\widehat{\bm{\rho}}^{[l]}
denote the
n
-dimensional vector whose elements consist of the
l
-th estimated PLS scores (
l=1,\ldots,L
) of
n
observations.
The vectors
\widehat{\bm{\rho}}^{[1]},\widehat{\bm{\rho}}^{[2]},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{\rho}}^{[L]}
are mutually orthogonal in the sense that

\widehat{\bm{\rho}}^{[l_{1}]\top}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}^{[l_{2}]}=0\quad\textnormal{for}\quad\;l_{1},l_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,L\},\;l_{1}\neq l_{2}.

The proof of Proposition
5.2
is provided in Appendix
F.2
.

6.3
Predictive Power and Parsimony on Synthetic Data

We evaluate the efficacy of our method in handling complex dependency structures through two synthetic experiments. We benchmark the predictive performance of Hybrid PLS against the PFLR method of
[
55
]
. This method fits FPCA separately for each functional predictor and combines the resulting scores with the scalar predictors for ordinary least squares regression. While this approach has been shown to be minimax optimal for prediction accuracy under the assumption of no multi-modal correlation, it is not explicitly designed to accommodate the complex dependency structures considered in this study.
To ensure a fair comparison, we extend their approach by also applying PCA to the scalar predictors; we refer to this modified benchmark as principal component regression (PCR) throughout this section.

Scenario 1:
Orthogonal Nuisance Variance.

i=1,\dots,400

and a nuisance factor
V_{i}
. To remove any predictive information,

was constructed as the residual from regressing a draw from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
onto the response
Y_{i}
, and then scaled by a factor of 5 to dominate total variance.
The response was defined as

Y_{i}=2U_{i}+\epsilon_{i},

where
\epsilon_{i}
is centered Gaussian noise with standard deviation equal to 5% of the sample standard deviation of
2U_{i}
.
Functional predictors were generated as

\displaystyle X_{i1}(t)

We evaluate our method on the Emory University renal study data

7
Discussion

In this article, we proposed a Hybrid Partial Least Squares (Hybrid PLS) framework to address the challenge of simultaneously modeling multiple functional and scalar predictors. By constructing a unified hybrid Hilbert space, we rigorously extended the classical NIPALS algorithm to jointly manage the complex dependency structures—particularly cross-modality correlations—that are often overlooked by conventional two-stage approaches. We established the theoretical well-posedness of the population-level problem and proved the consistency of the proposed estimator. From a computational perspective, we derived a closed-form solution for the regularized iterative steps, allowing the method to efficiently handle dense or irregular functional data while strictly enforcing geometric properties such as score orthogonality.
Our numerical studies and the application to the Emory University renal dataset highlight the practical advantages of this unified approach.
For future research direction, deriving theoretical convergence rates and minimax optimality bounds would provide a deeper understanding of its statistical efficiency relative to other high-dimensional regression techniques.

Appendix B
Separability of the hybrid space

Both
\mathbb{L}^{2}[0,1]
and
\mathbb{R}^{p}
are Hilbert spaces, with their inner products, norms, and metrics corresponding to the terms defined in equation (
2
).
Let us define the metric of
\mathbb{H}
as

d(h_{1},h_{2})=\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{\mathbb{H}}
.
A finite Cartesian product of Hilbert spaces, equipped with an
\ell_{2}
norm of the component metrics (which is our metric), is also a Hilbert space.
Furthermore, both of
\mathbb{L}^{2}[0,1]
and
\mathbb{R}^{p}
are separable.
For
\mathbb{L}^{2}[0,1]
, the set of all polynomials with rational coefficients is a countable and dense subset.
For
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the set of all vectors with rational components forms a countable and dense subset.
A finite Cartesian product of separable spaces is also separable.
Therefore
\mathbb{H}=\bigl(\mathbb{L}^{2}[0,1]\bigr)^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
is separable.

C.1
Proof of Lemma
3.1

Proof.

We first show that
the operator
\mathcal{C}_{YW}
is a compact operator under condition (
4
)
Any bounded linear functional from a Hilbert space to
\mathbb{R}
is a compact operator.
This is because the image of any bounded set under such a functional is a bounded set in
\mathbb{R}
,
and by Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem, every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence.
Therefore, to show that
\mathcal{C}_{YW}
is a compact operator, it suffices to show that

\mathcal{C}_{YW}
is a bounded linear functional.

Linearity.
The operator
\mathcal{C}_{YW}:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\mathcal{C}_{YW}h=\langle\Sigma_{YW},h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
.
For any
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{H}
and scalar
c\in\mathbb{R}
, the linearity of the inner product implies:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{YW}(h_{1}+h_{2})

\displaystyle=\langle\Sigma_{YW},h_{1}+h_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\langle\Sigma_{YW},h_{1}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\langle\Sigma_{YW},h_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\mathcal{C}_{YW}h_{1}+\mathcal{C}_{YW}h_{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{YW}(ch)

\displaystyle=\langle\Sigma_{YW},ch\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=c\langle\Sigma_{YW},h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=c\,\mathcal{C}_{YW}h.

Thus,
\mathcal{C}_{YW}
is a linear functional.

Boundedness.

To show that
\mathcal{C}_{YW}
is bounded, we need to find a finite constant
M
such that
|\mathcal{C}_{YW}h|\leq M\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}
for all
h\in\mathbb{H}
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have:

|\mathcal{C}_{YW}h|=|\langle\Sigma_{YW},h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|\leq\|\Sigma_{YW}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}.

Now, we must verify that
\|\Sigma_{YW}\|_{\mathbb{H}}
is finite.
Let
\mu([0,1])
denote a Lebesgue measure of
[0,1]
, and let
T=\max_{k=1,\ldots,K}\,\mu([0,1])
.
The squared norm of
\Sigma_{YW}
is given by:

\|\Sigma_{YW}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=\langle\Sigma_{YW},\Sigma_{YW}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[YX_{k}\right](t)^{2}\,dt+\sum_{r=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[YZ_{r}\right]^{2}<KTQ_{1}+pQ_{2}<\infty,

where the last inequality uses the condition (
4
).
Let
M=\sqrt{KTQ_{1}+pQ_{2}}
. Thus, we have shown that
|\mathcal{C}_{YW}h|\leq M\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}
for a finite constant
M
.

Next, we show that
\mathcal{U}
is self-adjoint, positive-semidefinite, and compact, in turn.

Self-adjoint.

For any
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{H}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{U}h_{1},\,h_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\bigl\langle\langle h_{1},\Sigma_{YW}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\Sigma_{YW},\,h_{2}\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\langle h_{1},\Sigma_{YW}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\langle\Sigma_{YW},h_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle h_{1},\,\langle\Sigma_{YW},h_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\Sigma_{YW}\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\langle h_{1},\mathcal{U}h_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

Positive-semidefinite.

For every
h\in\mathbb{H}
, we have

\langle\mathcal{U}h,\,h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\bigl\langle\langle\Sigma_{YW},\,h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\Sigma_{YW},\,h\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\langle\Sigma_{YW},\,h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq 0.

Compact.

We have shown that
\mathcal{C}_{YW}
is a compact operator under the condition (
4
).
The composition of two operators is compact if either operator is compact
(Theorem 4.1.3 of
27
). Therefore

\mathcal{U}:=\mathcal{C}_{WY}\circ\mathcal{C}_{YW}
is a compact operator.
This completes the proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Y^{[s]}\;=\;Y-\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\nu^{[k]}\,\rho^{[k]},\qquad\nu^{[k]}\;=\;\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y^{[k]}\rho^{[k]}]}{\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[k]})^{2}]}.

Compute the covariance of interest by expanding the residual:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[Y^{[s]}\,\rho^{[j]}\big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(Y-\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\nu^{[k]}\rho^{[k]}\Big)\,\rho^{[j]}\Big]=\mathbb{E}[Y\,\rho^{[j]}]\;-\;\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\nu^{[k]}\,\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[k]}\rho^{[j]}].

By the score orthogonality already established in Appendix
C.3.1
,
we have
\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[k]}\rho^{[j]}]=0
for every
k\neq j
. Thus only the
k=j
term survives in the sum and we obtain

\mathbb{E}\big[Y^{[s]}\,\rho^{[j]}\big]=\mathbb{E}[Y\,\rho^{[j]}]-\nu^{[j]}\,\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[j]})^{2}].

It remains to identify
\mathbb{E}[Y\,\rho^{[j]}]
. Using the definition of the
(j)
-th residual response,

Y=Y^{[j]}+\sum_{m=1}^{j-1}\nu^{[m]}\rho^{[m]},

and again using score orthogonality
\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[m]}\rho^{[j]}]=0
for
m<j
, we find

\mathbb{E}[Y\,\rho^{[j]}]=\mathbb{E}[Y^{[j]}\,\rho^{[j]}].

Substitute this into the previous display and use the definition of
\nu^{[j]}
:

\mathbb{E}\big[Y^{[s]}\,\rho^{[j]}\big]=\mathbb{E}[Y^{[j]}\,\rho^{[j]}]-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y^{[j]}\rho^{[j]}]}{\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[j]})^{2}]}\,\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[j]})^{2}]=0.

This completes the proof of orthogonality between scores and residualized responses.
∎

C.3.3
Equivalence of the problems

Proof.

Recall the residualization definitions (for fixed
l\geq 2
):

W^{[l]}\;=\;W-\sum_{j=1}^{\,l-1}\rho^{[j]}\,\delta^{[j]},\qquad Y^{[l]}\;=\;Y-\sum_{k=1}^{\,l-1}\nu^{[k]}\,\rho^{[k]},

where

\rho^{[j]}=\langle W^{[j]},\xi^{[j]}\rangle,\quad\delta^{[j]}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}W^{[j]}]}{\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[j]})^{2}]},\quad\nu^{[j]}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}Y^{[j]}]}{\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[j]})^{2}]}.

Since
W,Y
are centered,
so are
W^{l},Y^{l}
.
Thus covariances are expectations of products.
Fix any
w\in\mathbb{H}
. Using linearity,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\!\big(\langle W^{[l]},w\rangle,\;Y^{[l]}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\langle W,w\rangle-\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\rho^{[j]}\langle\delta^{[j]},w\rangle\big)\big(Y-\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\nu^{[k]}\rho^{[k]}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\langle W,w\rangle Y]-\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\nu^{[k]}\,\mathbb{E}[\langle W,w\rangle\rho^{[k]}]-\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\langle\delta^{[j]},w\rangle\,\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}Y]}_{:=S_{1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\underbrace{+\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\langle\delta^{[j]},w\rangle\,\nu^{[k]}\,\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}\rho^{[k]}]}_{:=S_{2}}.

We claim that
S_{1}=S_{2}
.
For
S_{1}
, first note that, since

Y=Y^{[l]}+\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\nu^{[k]}\rho^{[k]}
, for each
j\leq l-1
we have

\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}Y]=\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}Y^{[l]}]+\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\nu^{[k]}\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}\rho^{[k]}]=\nu^{[j]}\,\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[j]})^{2}],

where the last equality uses

\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}Y^{[l]}]=0
(Appendix
C.3.2
) and

\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}\rho^{[k]}]=0
for
j\neq k
(Appendix
C.3.1
).
Therefore we have

S_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\langle\delta^{[j]},w\rangle\nu^{[j]}\,\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[j]})^{2}].

For
S_{2}
,
since
\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}\rho^{[k]}]=0
for
j\neq k
(Appendix
C.3.1
), we have:

S_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\langle\delta^{[j]},w\rangle\,\nu^{[k]}\,\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}\rho^{[k]}]=\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\langle\delta^{[j]},w\rangle\,\nu^{[j]}\,\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[j]})^{2}].

Since
S_{1}=S_{2}
,
and

\mathbb{E}[\langle W,w\rangle Y]=\operatorname{Cov}(\langle W,w\rangle,Y)
, we have

\operatorname{Cov}\big(\langle W^{[l]},w\rangle,\;Y^{[l]}\big)=\operatorname{Cov}(\langle W,w\rangle,Y)-\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\nu^{[k]}\,\operatorname{Cov}(\langle W,w\rangle,\rho^{[k]}).

Therefore we are solving

\displaystyle\max_{\|w\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1}~

\displaystyle\Big\{\operatorname{Cov}(\langle W,w\rangle,Y)-\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\nu^{[k]}\,\operatorname{Cov}(\langle W,w\rangle,\rho^{[k]})\Big\}^{2}

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle\nu^{[k]}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[k]})^{2}]}\mathbb{E}[Y^{[l]}\rho^{[k]}],~k=1,\ldots,l-1

\displaystyle\rho^{[k]}=\langle\xi^{[k]},W^{[k]}\rangle,~k=1,\ldots,l-1

\displaystyle\delta^{[k]}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[k]})^{2}]}\mathbb{E}[W^{[k]}\rho^{[k]}],~k=1,\ldots,l-1

\displaystyle W^{[k+1]}=W^{[k]}-\rho^{[k]}\,\delta^{[k]},~k=1,\ldots,l-1

\displaystyle Y^{[k]}=Y^{[k-1]}-\nu^{[k-1]}\,\rho^{[k-1]},~k=1,\ldots,l-1

Since the constraints imply
that for every
s\geq 1
and every
j\leq s-1
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\rho^{[j]}\rho^{[s]}]=\langle w,\Sigma_{W}\,\xi^{[k]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\operatorname{Cov}(\langle W,w\rangle,\rho^{[k]})=0
(Appendix
C.3.1
),
the above problem is equivalent to

\displaystyle\max_{\|w\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1}~

\displaystyle\Big\{\operatorname{Cov}(\langle W,w\rangle,Y)\Big\}^{2}

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle\nu^{[k]}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[k]})^{2}]}\mathbb{E}[Y^{[l]}\rho^{[k]}],~k=1,\ldots,l-1

\displaystyle\rho^{[k]}=\langle\xi^{[k]},W^{[k]}\rangle,~k=1,\ldots,l-1

\displaystyle\delta^{[k]}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[k]})^{2}]}\mathbb{E}[W^{[k]}\rho^{[k]}],~k=1,\ldots,l-1

\displaystyle W^{[k+1]}=W^{[k]}-\rho^{[k]}\,\delta^{[k]},~k=1,\ldots,l-1

\displaystyle Y^{[k]}=Y^{[k-1]}-\nu^{[k-1]}\,\rho^{[k-1]},~k=1,\ldots,l-1

\displaystyle\langle w,\Sigma_{W}\,\xi^{[k]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=0,~k=1,\ldots,l-1.

Removing constraints that does not affect the objective function, the above optimization problem is equivalent to

\displaystyle\max_{\|w\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1}~

\displaystyle\Big\{\operatorname{Cov}(\langle W,w\rangle,Y)\Big\}^{2}

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle\langle w,\Sigma_{W}\,\xi^{[k]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=0,~k=1,\ldots,l-1.

This completes
the proof of Proposition
3.3
.
∎

C.4
Proof of Theorem
3.4

Proof.

Iterating the residualization
Y^{[l]}=Y^{[l-1]}-\nu^{[l-1]}\rho^{[l-1]}
for
l=1,\dots,L
, we obtain

Y=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\nu^{[j]}\rho^{[j]}+Y^{[L]}=Y_{\mathrm{PLS},L-1}+Y^{[L]}.

(16)

Consider the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
norm of the remaining residual
Y^{[L]}
:

\mathbb{E}[(Y^{[L]})^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(Y^{[L-1]}-\nu^{[L-1]}\rho^{[L-1]})^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(Y^{[L-1]})^{2}]-(\nu^{[L-1]})^{2}\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[L-1]})^{2}].

Thus, the residual variance is nonnegative and monotonically decreasing with
L
:

0\leq\mathbb{E}[(Y^{[L]})^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(Y^{[L-1]})^{2}]\leq\cdots\leq\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]<\infty.

Therefore, the series
\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(\nu^{[l]})^{2}\mathbb{E}[(\rho^{[l]})^{2}]
converges and the residual variance
\mathbb{E}[(Y^{[L]})^{2}]\to 0
as
L\to\infty
.
From (
16
), the mean squared error of the PLS approximation is

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|Y_{\mathrm{PLS},L-1}-Y\|^{2}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}[(Y^{[L]})^{2}]\to 0\quad\text{as }L\to\infty.

This completes the proof of Theorem
3.4
.
∎

Proof of Section
4.2

This section provides the proofs for the core problem formulations and computational schemes of the iterative steps for estimating the PLS direction and performing residualization.

derive the eigenproblem formulations for regularized PLS direction estimation.
Appendices

and

D.1
Proof of Proposition
4.1

We begin by establishing the result for the unregularized case, which serves as the foundation for the general problem. The objective is to estimate the hybrid PLS direction
\xi\in\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}
by maximizing the squared empirical covariance with the response. This approach fully leverages the continuous nature of the functional components and the hybrid structure of the data.The PLS direction is estimated by the unit-norm vector
\xi\in\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}
that maximizes the squared empirical covariance between the PLS scores
\langle\widetilde{W}_{i},\xi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
and the responses
Y_{i}
:

\widehat{\textnormal{Cov}}(\langle\widetilde{W},\xi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},Y):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\langle\widetilde{W}_{i},\xi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

(17)

The estimator is defined as:

\hat{\xi}:=\arg\max_{\xi\in\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}}~\widehat{\textnormal{Cov}}^{2}\bigl(\langle\widetilde{W},\xi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},Y\bigr)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|\xi\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1.

(18)

The candidate direction
\xi
admits the finite-basis expansion:

\xi=\bigl(\xi_{1}(t),\ldots,\xi_{K}(t),\bm{\zeta}\bigr)=\biggl(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\gamma_{1m},b_{m}(t),\ldots,\sum_{m=1}^{M}\gamma_{Km},b_{m}(t),\bm{\zeta}\biggr).

Solving for the optimal coefficients is equivalent to the maximization problem in (
18
). The following proposition reformulates this procedure as a generalized Rayleigh quotient.

Theorem D.1
.

Let

(\mathbb{B},\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime},\Theta,\mathbf{y})

denote the observed data defined in (
7
).
At the
l
-th iteration of the PLS algorithm,
the coefficients of the squared covariance maximizer defined in (
18
),
is obtained as

\left(\hat{\gamma}_{11},\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_{1M},\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_{K1},\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_{KM},\hat{\bm{\zeta}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}=\arg\max_{\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{MK+p}}\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\bm{\xi}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{B}\bm{\xi}=1.

(19)

Proof.

Let
\xi=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{K},\bm{\zeta})\in\mathbb{H}
. Assume that each functional component
\xi_{j}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,1])
lies in the span of the basis functions
{b_{1}(t),\ldots,b_{M}(t)}
and can be written as

\xi_{j}(t):=\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{jm}\,b_{m}(t),\quad j=1,\ldots,K,

with coefficient vectors
\bm{\gamma}_{j}:=(d_{j1},\ldots,d_{jM})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.

Objective function

The empirical covariance is
computed as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\textnormal{Cov}}(\langle\widetilde{W},\xi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\langle\widetilde{W}_{i},\xi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\langle\widetilde{X}_{ij},\xi_{j}\rangle+\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\zeta}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\biggl(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{X}_{ij}(t)\,\xi_{j}(t)\,dt\biggr)+\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}(\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\biggl\{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{1}\biggl(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\theta_{ijm}b_{m}(t)\biggr)\biggl(\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}d_{jm^{\prime}}b_{m^{\prime}}(t)\biggr)dt\biggr\}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{y}^{\top}Z\bm{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\biggl\{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\theta_{ijm}d_{jm^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}b_{m}(t)b_{m^{\prime}}(t)dt\biggr\}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{y}^{\top}Z\bm{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\biggl\{\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{ij}^{\top}B\bm{\gamma}_{j})\biggr\}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{y}^{\top}Z\bm{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\biggl(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\Theta_{j}B\bm{\gamma}_{j}+\mathbf{y}^{\top}Z\bm{\zeta}\biggr)

Building on this computation, the squared empirical covariance is
expressed as the quadratic form involving the matrix
\mathbf{V}
.

\displaystyle\widehat{\textnormal{Cov}}^{2}(\langle\widetilde{W},\xi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\biggl(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\Theta_{j}B\bm{\gamma}_{j}+\mathbf{y}^{\top}Z\bm{\zeta}\biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}(\mathbf{y}^{\top}\Theta\mathbb{B}\bm{\xi})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bm{\xi}^{\top}(\mathbb{B}\Theta^{\top}\mathbf{y})(\mathbb{B}\Theta^{\top}\mathbf{y})^{\top}\bm{\xi}

\displaystyle=\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\bm{\xi}.

(20)

Constraint

The squared norm of
\xi
in the hybrid Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}

is computed as follows:

\displaystyle\langle{\xi},{\xi}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{1}\xi_{j}(t)\,\xi_{j}(t)\,dt+\bm{\zeta}^{\top}\bm{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{1}\biggl(\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{jm}b_{m}(t)\biggr)\biggl(\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}d_{jm^{\prime}}b_{m^{\prime}}(t)\biggr)dt+\bm{\zeta}^{\top}\bm{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}d_{jm}d_{jm^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}b_{m}(t)b_{m^{\prime}}(t)dt+\bm{\zeta}^{\top}\bm{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\bm{\gamma}_{j}^{\top}B\bm{\gamma}_{j}+\bm{\zeta}^{\top}\bm{\zeta},

\displaystyle=\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{B}\bm{\xi}

(21)

Therefore, the covariance maximization problem (
18
) is equivalent to the generalized Raleigh quotient (
19
). This completes the proof of Proposition
D.1
.
∎

Now, for the regularized case, we only need to deal with the new constraint.
The penalization term
can be written in matrix form as:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_{j}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\{\hat{\xi}_{j}^{\prime\prime}(t)\bigr\}^{2}dt

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_{j}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\{\sum_{l=1}^{M}\theta_{ijl}\,b_{l}^{\prime\prime}(t)\bigr\}^{2}dt

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_{j}\sum_{l=1}^{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\theta_{ijl}\theta_{ijm}\int_{0}^{1}\,b_{l}^{\prime\prime}(t)b_{m}^{\prime\prime}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_{j}\bm{\gamma}_{j}^{\top}B^{\prime\prime}\bm{\gamma}_{j}

\displaystyle=\bm{\xi}^{\top}\Lambda\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}\bm{\xi},

(22)

where
\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}
is defined in (
6
), and
\Lambda
is defined in (
9
).
Combining this with (
21
),
we can compute

\|\xi\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_{j}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\{\hat{\xi}_{j}^{\prime\prime}(t)\bigr\}^{2}dt=\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{B}\bm{\xi}+\bm{\xi}^{\top}\Lambda\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}\bm{\xi}=\bm{\xi}^{\top}(\mathbb{B}+\Lambda\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime})\bm{\xi}.

This computation along with (
20
) implies that the covariance maximization problem
(
8
) is equivalent to the generalized Raleigh quotient (
10
). This completes the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

D.2
Proof of Proposition
4.2

Proof.

The optimization problem
(
10
) can be written as

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\gamma}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\gamma}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{M},\;\bm{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\Theta_{j}B\bm{\gamma}_{j}+\mathbf{y}^{\top}Z\bm{\zeta}\right)^{2},

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\bm{\gamma}_{j}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})\bm{\gamma}_{j}+\bm{\zeta}^{\top}\bm{\zeta}=1.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{j}:=B\Theta_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
\mathbf{v}:=Z^{\top}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. These are fixed problem data. Then the objective becomes

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{j}+\mathbf{v}^{\top}\bm{\zeta}\right)^{2}.

The objective function is continuous. The constraint defines the boundary of an ellipsoid in a finite-dimensional Euclidean space, the feasible set is compact. By the Weierstrass Extreme Value Theorem (see, e.g., Theorem 2.3.1 in
4
), a global maximizer exists. The constraint function is continuously differentiable and its gradient vanishes only at the origin, which is not feasible.
Thus the gradient is nonzero at all feasible points.
Hence, the Linear Independence Constraint Qualification (LICQ) holds, and the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions are necessary for local optimality (see, e.g., Theorem 5.3.1 in
4
).

Define the Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}(\{\bm{\gamma}_{j}\},\bm{\zeta},\mu):=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{j}+\mathbf{v}^{\top}\bm{\zeta}\right)^{2}-\mu\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}\bm{\gamma}_{j}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})\bm{\gamma}_{j}+\bm{\zeta}^{\top}\bm{\zeta}-1\right].

Let
s:=\dfrac{2}{n^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{j}+\mathbf{v}^{\top}\bm{\zeta}\right)
. The KKT conditions require that:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\gamma}_{j}}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=s\mathbf{u}_{j}-2\mu(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})\bm{\gamma}_{j}=0,\quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,K,

(23)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\zeta}}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=s\mathbf{v}-2\mu\bm{\zeta}=0.

(24)

From (
23
) and (
24
), we have

(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})\bm{\gamma}_{j}=\frac{s}{2\mu}\mathbf{u}_{j},\quad\bm{\zeta}=\frac{s}{2\mu}\mathbf{v},\quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,K.

This implies that for any local maximizer, there exists
c\neq 0
such that

\bm{\gamma}_{j}=c(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{j},\quad\bm{\zeta}=c\mathbf{v},\quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,K.

Since we assumed in Section
4.1
that the functions
\{b_{m}(t)\}
and their second derivatives are linearly independent, both
B
and
B^{\prime\prime}
are positive definite (see, for example, Theorem 273 of
21
). As positive definiteness is preserved under conic combinations,
B+\lambda_{j}B^{\prime\prime}
is also positive definite.

A local maximizer must also be primal feasible. Substituting the conditions above in to the primal constraint:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(c(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{j}\right)^{\top}(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})\left(c(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{j}\right)+c^{2}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow c^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{j}+\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right)

\displaystyle=1.

Solving for
c^{2}
gives:

c^{2}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{j}+\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right)^{-1}.

Hence, the unique maximizer (up to sign) is:

\bm{\gamma}_{j}^{\ast}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{j},\quad\bm{\zeta}^{\ast}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\mathbf{v},

where

q:=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{j}+\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{v}.

This completes the proof of Proposition
D.2
.

∎

D.3
Proof of Lemma
4.3

For notational simplicity, we omit the iteration superscript
[l]
.
Recall from (
11
) that

\hat{\rho}_{i}=\langle\widetilde{W}_{i},\,\hat{\xi}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
.
Using the basis expansion coefficient notation from
Section

4.1
, the full vector of PLS scores
\hat{\bm{\rho}}=(\hat{\rho}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\rho}_{n})^{\top}

is computed through the following matrix multiplication:

\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j})^{\top}\mathbf{B}\,\Theta_{j}^{\top}+\bm{\zeta}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}.

(25)

The least square criterion can be decomposed as follows:

\displaystyle\operatorname{SSE}(\delta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]}-\hat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\,\delta,\,\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]}-\hat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\,\delta\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]},\,\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}_{A}\underbrace{-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\langle\delta,\,\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}_{B_{1}(\bm{\delta})}\underbrace{+(\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}})\langle\delta,\,\delta\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}_{B_{2}(\bm{\delta})}

Here, part
A
does not contain
\delta
, so it does not contribute to the minimization problem.

Let
\bm{\delta}:=(\pi_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\pi_{K}^{\top},\bm{\chi}^{\top})^{\top}
be the
MK+p
-dimensional concatenated vector of basis coefficients and the scalar part of
\bm{\delta}
.
Abusing notation, we treat
\operatorname{SSE}
as a function of
\bm{\delta}
.
Next, We will now demonstrate that the combined term
B_{1}(\bm{\delta})+B_{2}(\bm{\delta})
is a quadratic function of
\bm{\delta}
, by expanding its component functions:

\displaystyle B_{1}(\bm{\delta})

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\rho}_{i}\langle\delta,\,\widetilde{W}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=-2\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\Theta_{j}\mathbf{B}\bm{\pi}_{j}+\mathbf{Z}\bm{\chi}\biggr),

\displaystyle B_{2}(\bm{\delta})

\displaystyle=(\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}})\langle\delta,\,\delta\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=(\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}})\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\pi_{j}+\bm{\chi}^{\top}\bm{\chi}\biggr),

Now we compute the gradients:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\pi}_{j}}B_{1}(\bm{\delta})

\displaystyle=-2\mathbf{B}\Theta_{j}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}},~j=1,\ldots,K,\quad\nabla_{\bm{\chi}}B_{1}(\bm{\delta})=-2\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\pi}_{j}}B_{2}(\bm{\delta})

\displaystyle=2(\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}})\mathbf{B}\bm{\pi}_{j},~j=1,\ldots,K,\quad\nabla_{\bm{\chi}}B_{2}(\bm{\delta})=2(\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}})\bm{\chi},

The Hessian of

\operatorname{SSE}(\bm{\delta})
is then given by

2(\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}})\mathbf{B}.

Since
\mathbf{B}
is positive definite,
\operatorname{SSE}(\delta)
is convex. Consequently, the gradient vanishes at its unique minimizer:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\pi}_{j}}\operatorname{SSE}(\hat{\bm{\delta}})

\displaystyle=-2\mathbf{B}\Theta_{j}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}}+2(\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}})\mathbf{B}\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{j}=0,~j=1,\ldots,K,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\chi}}\operatorname{SSE}(\hat{\bm{\delta}})

\displaystyle=-2\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}}+2(\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}})\hat{\bm{\chi}}=0,

providing the following closed-form solution:

\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{j}\\
=\bigl\{(\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}})\mathbf{B}\bigr\}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\Theta_{j}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}}=\frac{1}{\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}}}\Theta_{j}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}}~j=1,\ldots,K,\quad\hat{\bm{\chi}}=\frac{1}{(\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}})}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}}.

Expanding with respect to these coefficients, we obtain

\delta^{[l]}=\frac{1}{\|\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{[l]}\|_{2}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]}.

On the other hand since
\mathbf{y}^{[l]}
and

\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{[l]}
are zero-mean,
it is well known that the least square estimate of linear regression coefficient is computed as
\hat{\nu}^{[l]}=\frac{\mathbf{y}^{[l]\top}\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{[l]}}{\|\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{[l]}\|_{2}^{2}}
.
This completes the proof of Lemma
4.3
.

Appendix E
Proof of Lemma
4.4

Proof.

We prove the lemma using mathematical induction.

Base case (
l=1
).

For
l=1
, the lemma states
\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[1]}=\langle W_{i},\widehat{\iota}^{[1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
. Since
\widehat{\iota}^{[1]}:=\widehat{\xi}^{[1]}
and
W^{[1]}_{i}=W_{i}
, the base case holds.

Inductive steps.
Assume
\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[u]}=\langle W_{i},\widehat{\iota}^{[u]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
for
u=1,\ldots,l
. We want to show that
\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l+1]}=\langle W_{i},\widehat{\iota}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
.
Recall from (
11
) and Lemma
4.3
that we have

\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l+1]}=\langle\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l+1]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},\quad\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l+1]}=\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]}-\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\widehat{\delta}^{[l]}

(26)

Combining these two, we have

\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l+1]}=\langle\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]}-\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\widehat{\delta}^{[l]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\langle\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\langle\widehat{\delta}^{[l]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

(27)

Next,
we can express
\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]}
in terms of the original predictor
W_{i}
by recursively applying Lemma
4.3
:

\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l]}=\widetilde{W}_{i}^{[l-1]}-\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l-1]}\widehat{\delta}^{[l-1]}=\ldots=W_{i}-\sum_{u=1}^{l-1}\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[u]}\widehat{\delta}^{[u]}

Substituting this into our equation (
27
), we have:

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l+1]}

\displaystyle=\left\langle W_{i}-\sum_{u=1}^{l-1}\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[u]}\widehat{\delta}^{[u]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\langle\widehat{\delta}^{[l]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\langle W_{i},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\sum_{u=1}^{l-1}\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[u]}\langle\widehat{\delta}^{[u]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l]}\langle\widehat{\delta}^{[l]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\langle W_{i},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\sum_{u=1}^{l}\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[u]}\langle\widehat{\delta}^{[u]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\langle W_{i},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\sum_{u=1}^{l}\langle\widehat{\delta}^{[u]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\langle W_{i},\widehat{\iota}^{[u]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\langle W_{i},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\left\langle W_{i},\sum_{u=1}^{l}\langle\widehat{\delta}^{[u]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\widehat{\iota}^{[u]}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle W_{i},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}-\sum_{u=1}^{l}\langle\widehat{\delta}^{[u]},\widehat{\xi}^{[l+1]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\widehat{\iota}^{[u]}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[l+1]}.

where step
(i)
uses the inductive hypothesis
\langle W_{i},\widehat{\iota}^{[u]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\widehat{\rho}_{i}^{[u]}
for all
u\leq l
.
By the principle of mathematical induction, the lemma holds for all
l\geq 1
. This completes the proof of Lemma
4.4
.
