Quantitative characterization of cracking and debonding behaviors of sandstone containing an inverted U-shaped inclusion
Authors: Xianghua Liu, Zhe Pan, Ke Zhang, Kai Zhang, Lei Xiong, Zhi Liu, Wenhuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo better investigate the reinforcement effect of inclusions on inverted U-shaped openings, sandstone specimens with an U-shaped opening were fabracation, considering two cases of filled and unfilled opening. Uniaxial compressive tests combined with digital image correlation (DIC) monitoring were performed on these specimens. Three quantitative methods were introduced to decipher DIC measurements. The inclusion has a significant effect on enhancing the strength and elastic modulus of the specimens but cannot change the ultimate failure mode. The strain distribution around the opening was analyzed by extracting the strain data. The presence of inclusions changes the strain distribution and reduces the concentration level, thereby causing a change in the crack initiation position and a reduction in the crack initiation stress. The change rate of variance (CROV) was proposed to describe the strain field evolution quantitatively, which is found to exhibit abnormal features in response to cracking events. The alteration in the cracking process caused by the inclusion changes this response feature, and the first cusp in CROV associated with crack initiation is defined as the precursor point. A novel approach was used to reveal the debonding mechanism between rock and inclusions. The relative opening zones are concentrated around the sidewalls, and the relative squeezing zones are located at the opening top and bottom. The crack initiates from the relative opening zone and, in turn, changes the initiation area from the relative opening state into the relative squeezing state.Keywords: Inclusion, Cracking behavior, Digital image correlation, Strain dispersion, Debonding mechanismSubject terms: Civil engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Underground openings have been extensively used in various engineering projects, such as mining, transportation, and hydropower. The existence of these underground openings may induce stress redistribution in rock masses, which becomes the main reason for instability and failure in most practical cases1,2. Therefore, the stability of underground openings has long been an essential issue in rock engineering.

Researchers usually model underground openings using rock specimens containing artificial openings for testing and analysis. Fundamental studies have been done by Lajtai and Lajtai3 and Cater et al.4. They found three types of cracks that occur around the periphery of openings, involving the tensile crack, shear crack, and remote crack. Some studies have been done on the influence of the size and shape of the openings on the mechanical response5–13. Zhao et al.6 and Wang et al.7 reported that the mechanical properties of specimens are related to the opening size by conducting experimental tests on specimens containing openings of different sizes. Zhao et al.8 and Zeng et al.9 experimentally and numerically studied the mechanical response of rocks containing different opening shapes. Wang et al.10,11 studied the stress response and distribution of rocks containing circular and rectangular openings. Zhang et al.12 and Cai et al.13 found that the primary tensile cracks always start from the opening top and bottom, which is independent of opening shapes. The opening number, opening arrangement14–17, and loading conditions18,19 are also factors that affect the mechanical response of rocks. Scholars have used various numerical approaches, including the PFC20–22, RFPA23,24, FLAC25,26, XFEM27, and BBM28, to analyze the whole failure process and cracking mechanism of the specimens with openings in detail and repeatedly.

During the construction and operation of engineering projects, the effects of underground openings are inevitable. Filling is an effective means to address these problems and can increase the stability and safety of underground openings29–31. However, the reinforcement mechanism of this method is still not very clear, which leads to engineering design and construction technology being ahead of basic theory. To overcome these limitations, several studies have investigated the reinforcement effect of inclusions. Janeiro and Einstein32 studied the mechanical properties of inclusions on the mechanical behaviors of the rock-like specimens with openings. Zhang et al.27,33 studied the cracking behaviors of specimens with circular and square inclusions. Janeiro and Einstein34 and Zhu et al.35 discussed the effects of the types, mechanical properties, and shapes of inclusions on the fracturing behavior and basic crack types of specimens with single and double openings. Zhu et al.36, Zhang et al.37, and Zhou et al.38 employed different theoretical models to analyze the stress distribution around the openings and inclusions. These studies focused on studying the reinforcement of inclusions on specimens with openings from the perspective of theoretical, experimental, and numerical simulation, indicating that the inclusion exerts a significant influence on the mechanical response of the specimens with openings.

The digital image correlation (DIC) is a non-contact optical measurement technique that enables visualizing and characterizing cracking behavior and helps elucidate damage mechanisms39–42. Scholars generally obtain the deformation and strain fields of rock or rock-like specimens through DIC measurements to identify cracking behavior39–42. In fact, the progression from strain localization to macrocracks is a gradual evolutionary process, and strain localization does not directly represent macroscopic cracking. Thus, the identified results are influenced by observer experience43. To further extract key information related to fracture mechanisms from DIC measurements, several novel approaches have been presented to extend DIC applications. Zhang and Wong44 and Liu et al.45 combined DIC with displacement vector methods to characterize the fracture nature of newly formed cracks directly. On the basis of unitary and multivariate statistical methods, Song et al.46, Zhang et al.47, and Liu et al.48 quantitatively described strain dispersion and discussed the relationship between cracking behavior and strain anomalies. Liu et al.49 grayed out the strain field and proposed grayscale parameters on the basis of the Gaussian distribution function to characterize the development of the strain divergence phenomenon.

Although the abovementioned studies have provided an understanding of the reinforcement effect of inclusions, two aspects still need to be further explored:

Most previous studies focused on specimens containing openings and inclusions of conventional geometries such as circles, ovals, and rectangles, but the inverted U-shape is representative of cross-sectional structures in mines, tunnels, and roadway engineering50,51, and the related studies are relatively rare.

Few previous experimental studies used dynamic, non-contact, full-field monitoring methods, which limit the multiscale understanding of the differences in cracking behaviors of rocks with and without inclusions. However, studies that employed monitoring methods such as DIC also did not delve deeply into measurement information to provide quantitative explanations of the interaction mechanisms between rocks and inclusions and their reinforcement effects.

However, studies that employed monitoring methods such as DIC also did not delve deeply into measurement information to provide quantitative explanations of the interaction mechanisms between rocks and inclusions and their reinforcement effects.

To this end, uniaxial compression tests are carried out on sandstone specimens containing an inverted U-shaped opening, considering unfilled and filled conditions. The deformation and failure behaviors of the specimens are captured by DIC measurements. Three DIC-based quantitative methods are used to reveal the cracking mechanisms, including strain extraction, strain dispersion characterization, and relative displacement analysis. The change rate of variance of the strain field (CROV) is applied to identify the precursor information. Based on the virtual extensometers, the relative displacement between the inclusion and the rock is captured to analyze their debonding mechanism. The influence of the inclusion on the stress and strain distribution is further studied based on DIC measurements and FEM simulations.

Methodology

Physical specimen fabrication

Red sandstone is a typical sedimentary rock that is widely distributed in China and highly relevant to engineering projects52. Compared with other types of rocks, it has a better homogeneous and isotropic nature, thereby ensuring the reliability and repeatability of experimental results53. Red sandstone is selected as the test object in this study, which is specimens from Kunming City, Yunnan Province, China. To check the microstructure and mineral composition, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) testing are performed on the sampled rock. The selected red sandstone has a medium grain structure and relatively uniform grain size (see Fig. 1a). The main mineral composition of the red sandstone is primarily quartz, K-feldspar, hematite, calcite, and plagioclase (see Fig. 1b). The compressive strength and elastic modulus of the sandstone are 75.05 MPa and 7.08 GPa, respectively.

SEM and XRD results of the sandstone used in this study: (a) Microstructure characteristic and (b) Mineral components.

All the specimens are cut to the same dimensions: 120 mm in height, 60 mm in width, and 20 mm in thickness. In accordance with recommendations from the International Society for Rock Mechanics (ISRM), each of the six specimen surfaces is polished to be smooth and straight. Numerous studies have investigated the deformation and failure behavior of rock masses containing circular, square, trapezoidal and elliptical openings9–13. However, inverted U-shaped openings are common cross-sectional structures in practical underground engineering50,51. This research aims to examine how inclusions affect the mechanical behavior of rocks with an inverted U-shaped opening. To this end, two different conditions are considered: one where the opening is unfilled (referred to as type A) and another where the opening is filled with inclusions (referred to as type B). The inverted U-shaped opening is composed of a semicircular segment with a diameter of 6.2 mm, two vertical sidewalls each 9.3 mm in height, and a floor with a length of 12.4 mm, as shown in Fig. 2.

Geometries of the specimens: (a) Type A specimen and (b) Type B specimen.

A professional high-pressure water jet cutting machine is used to prepare the inverted U-shaped hole in the center of the specimen. Type A specimens are obtained by cutting out the opening according to the design, whereas type B specimens need to be further filled with inclusions on this basis. The inclusions are made of gypsum powder, barite powder, quartz sand, and purified water, and the mass proportions in the specimens are 1.0:3.0:0.7:1.0. To determine the basic mechanical properties of the used inclusion, uniaxial compression and Brazilian splitting tests of cylindrical specimens are conducted. The bulk density, uniaxial compressive strength, elastic modulus, tensile strength, and Poisson’s ratio are 1852 kg/m3, 3.69 MPa, 0.35 GPa, 0.37 MPa, and 0.30, respectively.

According to previous studies16,27,33,35,42,43,45,48,49, two identical specimens per type are tested. All rock specimens are extracted from the same rock block, and the rock processing and flaw cutting procedures are rigorously controlled following the recommendations of the International Society for Rock Mechanics (ISRM). If experimental results show inconsistency, additional specimens are prepared and tested. The mechanical properties of the tested specimens are listed in Table 1. The standard deviation of uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) and elastic modulus (E) is pretty small, indicating better result reproducibility. To achieve the subsequent DIC measurement, black and white spray paintings are applied on the target surface to form a random speckle pattern.

Uniaxial compression testing

As shown in Fig. 3, the experimental setup used in this study consists of a loading system and an observation system. Uniaxial compression testing is one of the most fundamental and common types of testing in the field of rock mechanics24. Many scholars applied this testing method to study the mechanical behavior of rocks and rock-like materials containing openings9–16,20,24,25,27. A universal testing machine (WDW-100, China) is applied for the experiments. To minimize friction, the upper and lower ends of the rock specimens are lubricated. To simulate quasistatic loading conditions, the crosshead speed of the machine is set to 0.005 mm·s− 1. During loading, a digital image capture system is placed in front of the specimen surface with a speckle pattern. The system contains a CCD camera (WP-UF500, China) with a graphic resolution of 2592 × 1944 pixels. It should be noted that the DIC is an advanced application of computer vision, and its measurement accuracy depends on the quality of captured images. Thus, two LED lights are set around the CCD camera to provide stable, cool illumination, thereby improving he brightness and contrast of digital images.

Uniaxial compression testing system.

Digital image correlation method

The digital image correlation (DIC) method utilizes spraying random speckles on the object surface to measure data such as deformation displacement by precisely matching the corresponding points in the speckle images before and after the deformation of the object39–42. Compared with other traditional contact measurement techniques, DIC has various advantages, including high reliability, wide measuring range, and efficient computations, and thus it has been widely used in basic testing of rock materials. After testing, the captured images are processed to obtain the deformation field using the Ncorr. This software is an open-source 2D-DIC code, whose calculation errors are less than 0.05%. Four steps are needed for calculation: sub-region deformation, nonlinear optimization, reliability guidance, and strain calculation. To convert pixel-based displacements from DIC into the physical unit, unit conversion is conducted in the Ncorr software. A calibration line of known pixel length is defined on the specimen in the Unit conversion module in Ncorr, and then, input the actual physical length, the software yields a conversion factor. In this study, the conversion factor is 0.082 units/pixel, indicating that 1 pixel corresponds to 0.082 mm of physical displacement.

Results and analysis

Macromechanical properties

Figure 5 shows the stress–strain curves of typical specimens. It can be seen from the figure that the stress and strain relationship of specimens before and after filling displays similar trends. According to the deformation and failure process, the curve can be generally divided into the original defect compaction stage, linear-elastic deformation stage, crack initiation and propagation stage, and postpeak failure stage. Figure 6 presents the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) and elastic modulus (E), with detailed values for each specimen listed in Table 1. The average values of these two mechanical parameters for type A specimens are 57.66 MPa and 6.14 GPa, respectively, whereas those for type B specimens are 64.11 MPa and 6.66 GPa, respectively. Compared with those of the type A specimens, the UCS and E of the type B specimens increased by 11.19% and 8.50%, respectively. This indicates that the inclusion increases the mechanical properties of the specimens with openings. Importantly, although the UCS and E of the inclusions are only 3.69 MPa and 0.35 GPa, respectively, the increases in these two mechanical parameters after filling are 6.45 MPa and 0.52 GPa.

Deformation and failure behavior

Quantitatively identifying crack initiation locations, development paths, and final coalescence patterns can be achieved by capturing strain localization zones47–49. Figures 7 and 8 present the cracking evolution and corresponding maximum principal strain fields (𝜀1) and minimum principal strain fields (𝜀3) of type A and type B specimens. Five typical points are selected for analysis, which are marked in Fig. 5 as blue dots and labeled with capital letters A (initial closure, at an axial strain of 0.20%), B (elastic deformation, at an axial strain of 0.70%), C (crack initiation), D (peak stress) and E (ultimate failure) on the stress‒strain curves. For Ncorr, the convention used is that tensile results in a positive value, whereas compressive leads to a negative value.

Crack evolution and corresponding principal strain field of type A specimen.

Crack evolution and corresponding principal strain field of type B specimen.

Figure 7 illustrates the crack evolution and corresponding maximum and minimum principal strain fields of the type A specimen. At point A, the DIC measurements indicate that the sample deforms more or less uniformly because the rock is behaving elastically. When the load reaches point B, no obvious macrocracks are observed on the specimen surface, but the strain localization zone is concentrated around the opening top and bottom. With the load increasing to point C, tensile cracks are found to initiate from the location of strain concentration, that is, the opening top and bottom, accompanied by the development of strain localization zones. Moreover, spalling cracks also occur at the opening sidewalls, causing the inner surrounding rock to tend to spall. When the external load increases to the peak stress (point D), spalling with the bursting of rock debris occurs at these positions, and tensile cracks appear at the spalling area. Hereafter (point E), the shear cracks initiate from the opening sidewalls and develop toward the diagonal of the specimen that behaves in an unstable manner, which finally coalesces with the inverted U-shaped opening to form a shear sliding failure surface. Moreover, the principal strain fields suggest that an entire shearing failure zone has formed.

Figure 8 illustrates the crack evolution and corresponding maximum and minimum principal strain fields of the type B specimen. At point A, the specimen deforms nearly uniformly. With the load reaching point B, the strain localization zone is concentrated within the inclusion because of its low mechanical properties. When the load increases to point C, the tensile crack subsequently develops from the bottom right vertex of the opening, and the spalling crack first occurs within the inclusions. The initiation position of the first macrocrack is different for the two types of specimens. When the external load increases to the peak stress (point D), a tensile crack and corresponding high strain zone are observed at the bottom right of the opening. At the same time, a remote crack occurs at the bottom right end of the specimen. During postpeak failure (point E), shear cracks initiate at both sidewalls of the opening and propagate quickly to cut through the rock bridge. The final shear failure occurs through the connection of debonding cracks between the rock matrix and inclusions, and shear cracks coalesce.

From the above analysis, the effects of the inclusions on the mechanical behavior of the specimen can be summarized. (1) Inclusion has a substantial influence on crack initiation behavior: the tensile crack first initiates from the top and bottom of the opening in the type A specimen, whereas it occurs at the opening vertex in the B specimen. The crack initiation stresses of the specimens before and after filling are 54.91 MPa and 63.90 MPa, respectively. (2) The inclusion significantly affects the cracking behavior around the opening: for the type A specimen, spalling aligned parallel to the sidewall occurs around the inner surrounding rock and is induced by crack initiation and propagation. For the type B specimen, no fracturing occurs at the opening sidewalls, but spalling cracks can be found within the inclusion. (3) The final failure pattern of the two types of specimens is diagonal shear sliding. This is because the inclusions are very small, approximately 0.05 times greater than those of the sandstone. Owing to the low strength of the inclusions, they do not have the ability to alter the failure pattern.

Previous studies have shown that rock fracturing tends to initiate around openings, which is associated with strain anomalies during external loading20,56. To further quantitatively analyze the effect of the inclusion on the strain distribution around the opening, a sampling line is set to extract the maximum principal strain (𝜀1). As presented in Fig. 9, the sampling line is set al.ong the opening circumference, which contains sixteen measurement points (P1, P2, P3,…, and P16). Figure 10 presents the extracted strain data at axial strains of 20%, 70%, and 90%. The selected typical strain levels are all before crack initiation. To provide a more intuitive display, the strain distribution is plotted in Fig. 11. In the figure, the red line represents the maximum principal stress of the type A specimen, whereas the blue line represents the maximum principal stress of the type B specimen.

Principal stress distribution around the opening

Numerical simulations are widely recognized as an effective approach for investigating stress distribution around openings13,58. In this study, the Finite Element Method (FEM) is employed to perform such simulations. FEM is a computational technique used to approximate solutions to boundary value problems in engineering and mathematical physics. Two-dimensional numerical models of Type A and Type B specimens are generated using ABAQUS. The modelling domain of the specimen is classified using 4-node quadrilateral elements. Due to the inverted-U-shaped opening, the meshing of the model is considerably difficult. Therefore, the free-mesh technique available in ABAQUS is employed, which allows for refined mesh generation in regions with complex geometry. The advancing front algorithm is selected for meshing, as it forms elements with relatively uniform size and aligns effectively with the seed position. The total number of elements generated for specimens A and B are 42,564 and 43,700, respectively. The basic physical and mechanical parameters of the bedrock and inclusions in the models are assigned based on experimental results. A displacement-controlled loading condition is applied to the models.

Figure 12 shows the maximum (σ1) and minimum (σ3) principal stress fields for the two types of specimens at an axial loading displacement of 2.5 × 10− 3 mm. The inclusions did not alter the stress distribution around the U-shaped opening. The maximum principal stress concentration zone intersects the upper and lower boundaries of the U-shaped opening, forming an approximate “X” shape, and the tensile stress is concentrated at the upper vertex and lower floor of the opening. The minimum principal stress is distributed on the sidewalls, and the maximum compressive stress is located at the arch springings and the bottom vertex of the opening. However, the inclusions improve the stress environment around the opening, as evidenced by the decrease in principal stresses. The maximum values of σ1 of type A and B specimens are 0.142 MPa and 0.112 MPa, respectively, and the minimum values of σ3 of these two types of specimens are 0.432 MPa and 0.357 MPa.

Distributions of the principal maximum and minimum stresses: (a) Type A specimen and (b) Type B specimen.

Although FEM simulations can analyze how inclusions affect stress distributions around the inverted U-shaped opening, establishing corresponding theoretical models remains essential. Such models may provide analytical stress solutions for unfilled and filled conditions, enabling direct comparison with simulation results. Wu et al.10 and Tan et al.59 derived analytical stress solutions for unfilled complex-shaped openings based on complex variable theory. Thus, extending this method to the above study may more comprehensively reveal stress redistribution mechanisms induced by the U-shaped inclusions.

Relationship between strain dispersion and cracking behavior

Figure 13 presents the variance evolution of the variance of two types of strain field data. When the specimens are in the initial compaction and elastic deformation stages (point A to point C), the variance of the two kinds of strain fields first approaches zero because the strain field distribution on the specimen surface is relatively uniform at the beginning of the loading period. The appearance of the strain localization zone around the opening leads to a small increase in the variance of the strain field. As the external load reaches tensile crack initiation (point C to point D), the variance of the strain field shows an accelerated increase because the specimen enters the stable crack propagation stage. Owing to more crack initiation and propagation, the strain localization zone extends and develops, which further increases the dispersion of the strain field data. As the external load reaches shear crack initiation (point D to point E), the variance of the strain field increases abruptly again and shows a steep vertical pattern. This is because the specimen enters the unstable crack expansion stage, which breaks the stress and strain equilibrium state and leads to a sudden change in the degree of dispersion of the strain field data.

Figure 14 illustrates the change rate of variance of the maximum principal strain field of the type A and B specimens during loading. Before crack initiation, the CROVs of the two types of specimens are close to zero. As shown in Fig. 13, the initial increase in the variance of the strain field corresponds to fracture process zone (FPZ) formation rather than macrocrack initiation. The FPZ is defined as the transition zone between discontinuous macrocracks and continuous material49. During the formation and early development of the FPZ, microcracks are formed in the zone and increase in number, but do not coalesce; thus, although the FPZ causes strain concentration, limited strain dispersion results in slight changes in CROVs. When macrocracks initiate, complete FPZ formation and macrocrack emergence occur due to rapid microcrack propagation. The variance of the strain field accelerates, reflected in the CROV‒strain curve by the appearance of obvious sharp points. This is because the cracking events are coupled with strain redistribution, which may lead to abrupt changes in strain dispersion. During shear crack initiation and unstable propagation, the FPZ shows a rapid development trend. The variance of the strain field consequently exhibits dramatic divergence, another sharp point or near-steepening increase in the CROV‒strain curves. These indicate that the CROV shows low sensitivity to the initial formation of FPZ induced by microcracks, but can accurately capture the complete formation of FPZ caused by macrocracks. It should be noted that the main difference in the CROV for the type A and B samples is that another sharp point appears in the type A specimen before the appearance of shear cracks. This is because type B specimens store more energy before failure than type A specimens, which need to be dissipated through more cracking events.

Previous studies used statistical indicators to quantitatively characterize the differentiation in horizontal, vertical, and shear strain fields, including standard deviation and variance of the strain fields27,47,49,59. They found that the first sharp point in such curves of the statistical indicators consistently coincides with macroscopic crack initiation. This correlation arises because the cracking induces pronounced displacement changes, which increase the strain concentration degree within pre-existing strain localization zones. Statistically, such localized strain variations increase data heterogeneity, thereby triggering the abrupt increase in these indicators. As shown in Fig. 14, a similar phenomenon is observed in the CROV‒strain curve. To check the effectiveness, Fig. 14 also indicates the crack evolution corresponding to the appearance of the sharp points, and macroscopic crack initiation can be observed. Thus, the first sharp point in the CROV‒strain curve is defined as the precursor point. To quantitatively compare the precursor recognition ability of the precursor points for type A and B specimens, the precursor response coefficient T is introduced:

where εp is the strain corresponding to the peak stress, and εc is the strain corresponding to crack initiation. The smaller T is, the longer the time from crack initiation to final failure, and the closer the precursor point is to the peak, and vice versa.

According to the above equation, the average value of the precursor response coefficient of the type A specimen is 0.041, whereas that of the type B specimen is 0.088, demonstrating that the presence of inclusions increases the precursor response coefficient of the specimen larger. After the inverted U-shaped opening is filled with inclusions, the integrity and load-bearing capacity of the specimen are enhanced. Under external loads, the inclusions play a passive supporting role and increase the strain concentration around the opening, thereby delaying crack initiation, propagation and coalescence. Through monitoring the CROV of the strain field on the rock surface, the limitations of the traditional buried point monitoring method can be overcome, providing new ideas and methods for noncontact monitoring and early warning in rock engineering.

Debonding mechanism between rock and inclusion

Previous studies suggest that debonding can be attributed to the relative separation between inclusions and rocks, which is associated mainly with tensile behavior32,33. Therefore, quantitatively characterizing the normal relative displacement between inclusions and rocks is expected to identify the interface debonding state. To this end, sixteen groups of measurement points are set al.ong the opening, named G1, G2, …, and G16, as shown in Fig. 15a. Each group contains two measurement points, which are arranged in the rock matrix and the inclusion, respectively. For the measurement points at the opening sidewalls (G4, G5, G6, G12, G13, and G14), the normal relative displacement (n) is the difference in the horizontal displacement (u) between the two points. For the measurement points at the opening top and bottom (G1, G8, G9, and G10), the normal relative displacement (n) is the difference in the vertical displacement (v) between the two points. For the other points (G2, G3, G7, G11, G15, and G16), the normal relative displacement (n) is the difference in the normal displacement obtained via coordinate conversion of the horizontal displacement (u) and vertical displacement (v), as shown in Fig. 15b.

(a) Arrangement of measurement points around the opening and (b) Coordinate transformation.

Notably, the above calculations are performed by subtracting the data at the inclusion from the data in the rock matrix. Therefore, according to the sign convention applied in Ncorr, the following rules can be summarized: (1) For G2, G3, G4, G5, G6, G7, G8, G9, and G10, the positive values of the normal relative displacement represent the squeezing state, whereas the negative values indicate the opening state. (2) For G1, G11, G12, G13, G14, G15, and G16, the positive value of the normal relative displacement represents the squeezing state, whereas the negative values indicate the opening state. Based on this, two different zones, including the relative opening zone and the relative squeezing zone, are defined. The relative opening zone represents that the measurement points within the zone are under the opening state, that is, the normal displacement within the inclusion is smaller than that within the rock matrix, causing the relative opening in the interface between the inclusion and rock. The relative squeezing zone represents that the measurement points within the zone are under the squeezing state, that is, the normal displacement within the inclusion is larger than that within the rock matrix, causing the relative squeezing in the interface between the inclusion and the rock.

Figure 16 presents the normal relative displacement–the axial strain of the sixteen groups. The normal relative displacements of all the measurement points are close to 0 at the early loading stage but then exhibit variable characteristics in response to strain anomalies with increasing compressive loading. The normal relative displacements of G3, G4, G5, and G6 gradually increase, whereas those of G11, G12, and G13 exhibit the opposite trend. The normal relative displacements of G2, G8, G9, and G10 gradually decrease, whereas those of G1 and G16 exhibit the opposite trend. This indicates that G3, G4, G5, G6, G11, G12, and G13 are in the opening state, whereas G1, G2, G8, G9, G10, G15 and G16 are in the squeezing state. However, the relative displacement of G7 and G14 changes from positive to negative and from negative to positive, respectively, during crack initiation. This indicates that the states of G7 and G14 change from opening to squeezing.

On the basis of the above analysis, the debonding mechanism between inclusions and rock can be summarized in Fig. 17. In the figure, the blanchedalmond area refers to the rock matrix, and the gray area refers to the inclusion. The red and blue dashed regions are used to characterize the opening and squeezing zones, respectively. The green line indicates the newly formed cracks. The debonding behavior between the inclusion and rock matrix can be divided into two stages during loading. Before macrocrack initiation, the opening zone is concentrated at both sidewalls, whereas the squeezing zone is located at the opening top and bottom, as shown in Fig. 17a. After macrocrack initiation, the state of the crack initiation and propagation area changes from opening to squeezing, whereas that of other zones is maintained, as shown in Fig. 17b, indicating that macrocrack occurrence influences debonding behavior. This finding is in reasonable agreement with previous studies; that is, the opening zone may induce debonding between the inclusion and rock and, thus, is prone to crack initiation32,60. The main reason for the state changing from opening to squeezing is likely that crack initiation results in relatively large rock matrix deformation toward the inclusion, thereby providing a force that behaves as a pressure on the interface between the inclusion and rock matrix.

Reinforcement mechanism of inclusion

After inclusions have filled inside inverted U-shaped openings, a structure with complex properties is formed because of the mechanical interaction between the rock matrix and inclusions, which enhances the structural integrity of rocks. When the rock matrix deforms toward the opening under external loads, the inclusion offers passive support to limit its deformation, thereby improving the mechanical properties, strain and stress distribution and concentration level of the rock, as described in “Macromechanical properties”, “Principal strain distribution around the opening” and “Principal stress distribution around the opening”. Because of the passive support from inclusions, crack initiation and progression are inhibited, and the final failure is delayed, as described in “Relationship between strain dispersion and cracking behavior”. However, deformation incongruity appears at the rock-inclusion interface due to the large difference in mechanical properties, which may cause debonding failure at the interface, as described in “Debonding mechanism between rock and inclusion”.

Using cemented materials with low-strength fill underground openings is an effective method to prevent hazards and secondary disasters, such as rockslides, rockbursts, and collapses. A validation of the reinforcement effect of inclusions is provided in this study. Previous works have indicated that the compressive strength of inclusions varies from 0.2 to 4 MPa, whereas that of the surrounding rock mass varies from 5 MPa to 240 MPa in many backfill stopes61. Although the compressive strength of the inclusions is much lower than that of rock masses, it has a significant effect on stabilizing the mine. The findings in this paper are in agreement with those of previous numerical simulations and field measurements of backfilled stopes in underground mines30,62.

In this study, the compressive strength and elastic modulus of the rock specimens with an inverted U-shaped inclusion increased by 11.19% and 8.50% when the strength of the inclusions is equal to 4.92% of the rock strength. This indicates that the reinforcement of inclusions is significant for the inverted U-shaped opening compared with other shaped openings. In addition, the inclusion is found to significantly affect the crack initiation and propagation in this study, which is in reasonable agreement with that reported by Zhu et al.35.

Limitations and future works

The mechanical response of rock specimens is influenced by opening dimensions and inclusion properties. However, this study used only a single inverted U-shaped opening size, one inclusion type, and two specimens per group. Moreover, while real openings experience both vertical and horizontal stresses, the experiments employed only uniaxial compression. Therefore, future work should test more opening dimensions and inclusion types, increase specimens per group, and implement biaxial compression to better represent field conditions.

Compared to other precursor indicators such as AE activity and acoustic velocity changes obtained by acoustic emission (AE) and microseismic (MS) monitoring53, the CROV developed in this study overcomes limitations requiring complex sensor installation. However, the accuracy of the DIC-based method is closely related to speckle quality and testing environment, and specimens may show different patterns due to homogeneity. Future studies should consider these factors to ensure reliable performance in complex conditions.

Conclusion

The stress‒strain relationships of sandstone specimens with an opening before and after filling are similar. A complex interaction occurs between the rock matrix and inclusions, causing a significant improvement in the mechanical properties. Compared with those of the samples without inclusions, the compressive strength and elastic modulus of the specimens with inclusions increased by 11.19% and 8.50%, respectively.

Strain extraction analysis is conducted to provide deep insight into the effects of inclusions on the strain distribution. The presence of inclusions changes the strain distribution and reduces the concentration around the opening, thereby altering the crack initiation position and reducing the crack initiation stress. Owing to its low strength, the inclusion cannot change the final failure mode of the specimen.

The first sharp point in the CROV associated with crack initiation is identified as a precursor point. The precursor response coefficient of the samples with inclusions is greater than that of specimens without inclusions, indicating that inclusion inhibits the cracking process.
