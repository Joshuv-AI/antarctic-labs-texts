ZBTB7B modulates the androgen receptor as an upstream regulator via colocalization and direct binding in LNCaP prostate cancer cells
Authors: Eunji Im, Su-Yeon Park, Deok-Yong Sim, Ah Reum Cho, Bum Ju Kil, Bonglee Kim, Bum-Sang Shim, Sung-Hoon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThough ZBTB7B is overexpressed in breast and prostate cancers and dysregulates CD8 T cell response, there is no report on the close relationship between ZBTB7B and androgen receptor (AR) yet. This study aimed to investigate the molecular interaction between ZBTB7B and AR and to determine its role in prostate cancer progression.MethodsProstate cancer cell lines (AR-dependent LNCaP and AR-independent DU145) were subjected to 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay, clonogenic assay, and cell cycle analysis. Protein-protein interaction was examined using immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence. Protein stability was assessed by cycloheximide chase and ubiquitination assays. RNA interference was employed to deplete ZBTB7B or AR, and tissue expression patterns were analyzed by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and human tissue microarray.ResultsZBTB7B was overexpressed in prostate cancer cells and tissues with poor prognosis by human tissue microarray and TCGA analysis. However, ZBTB7B depletion suppressed viability, the number of colonies and increased G1 arrest in AR dependent LNCaP cells, but not in AR independent DU145 cells. Interestingly, ZBTB7B depletion suppressed the expression of AR and prostate specific antigen (PSA) in LNCaP cells, while AR depletion did not affect ZBTB7B. Furthermore, AR inhibitor finasteride and AR activator dihydrotestosterone (DHT) did not affect ZBTB7B. However, ZBTB7B was colocalized with AR by immunofluorescence and was bound to AR by immunoprecipitation. Consistently, ZBTB7B depletion attenuated the nuclear translocation and stability of AR through its degradation and also promoted AR degradation by ubiquitination assay. Notably, N-terminal domain (NTD) of AR is requisite for binding with ZBTB7B in HEK293 cells, not AR-DNA-binding domain (DBD) or AR-ligand-binding domain (LBD) in HEK293 cells.ConclusionsOverall, these findings provide a novel insight that ZBTB7B promotes prostate cancer progression as a potent oncogene via colocalization and binding with AR.Keywords: ZBTB7B, androgen receptor (AR), finasteride, dihydrotestosterone (DHT), N-terminal domain of the androgen receptor (AR-NTD), prostate cancer

Highlight box

Key findings

• ZBTB7B modulates the androgen receptor (AR) as an upstream regulator via colocalization and direct binding in LNCaP prostate cancer cells.

What is known and what is new?

• ZBTB7B was overexpressed in breast and prostate cancers and dysregulates CD8 T cell response.

• This study is the first to demonstrate that ZBTB7B directly interacts with AR, regulates its nuclear translocation and stability, and thereby promotes prostate cancer progression.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings identify ZBTB7B as a novel oncogenic regulator of AR signaling.

• ZBTB7B should be considered a promising therapeutic target and biomarker for AR-dependent prostate cancer, supporting the need to expand treatment strategies beyond direct AR inhibition to include upstream regulators such as ZBTB7B.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is known as one of the most common cancers in males and the second leading cause of cancer death in Cancer Statistics 2021 (1). Though surgery (2), radiation therapy (3), and androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) (4) have been used for prostate cancer treatment, the survival rate of the patients with advanced prostate cancer still remains poor so far (5). It is well known that reactivation of androgen receptor (AR) signaling is critically involved in the mortality of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPCa) despite low levels of circulating androgen (6,7).

AR modulates biological functions in the normal prostate and its dysfunction promotes prostate cancer progression (8-10). AR is composed of four domains (8,11), such as N-terminal domain (NTD), DNA-binding domain (DBD) and ligand-binding domain (LBD). AR-LBD contains the transcriptional region and binds to agonists such as testosterone and dihydrotestosterone (DHT), by directly regulating androgen (12,13), whereas the transcriptional activation domain of AR is located in the N-terminus. Also, AR-DBD is highly conserved among the different steroid receptors and is composed of two zinc fingers (8).

The zinc finger and BTB consist of various transcription factors such as ZBTB1, ZBTB17 and ZBTB7B (14). ZBTB7B, called the transcription factor T-helper-inducing POZ/Krueppel-like factor (ThPOK), acts as a transcription factor, a significant element of immature T cell precursors (15) and a potent oncogene in cancer progression and proliferation (16). Also, it is overexpressed in prostate cancer (17) and breast cancer (18). Accumulating evidence reveals that ZBTB7B in peripheral CD4+ T cells suppresses CD8-lineage genes such as CD8 and cytotoxic effector perforin or granzyme B, and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) during effector differentiation (19). Conversely, ZBTB7B depletion is required for robust CD8 T cells response (20). In contrast, Xia et al. (21) indicated that overexpression of ThPOK or ZBTB7B suppressed the growth of gastric cancer via T-cell proliferation. Also, Zhang et al. (6) demonstrated that ZBTB7B regulated the IRS1 and insulin-induced activation of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)-sterol regulatory element binding protein (SREBP) signaling in the mammary glands. Nonetheless, oncogenic roles of ZBTB7B have never been explored in prostate cancers to date. Hence, in the present study, oncogenic mechanism of ZBTB7B was investigated in prostate cancer cells in association with AR/prostate specific antigen (PSA) signaling for their colocalization, stability, ubiquitination, and binding. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1365/rc).

Methods

Cell culture and reagents

LNCaP, DU145, and HEK293 cell lines were obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB, Seoul, Korea). LNCaP and DU145 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Welgene, Gyeongsan, Korea) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (100×). HEK293 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Welgene) with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (100×).

TCGA analysis and Kaplan-Meier survival rates

ZBTB7B mRNA expression was analyzed using human prostate cancer clinical data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) by RStudio (Boston, MA, USA). Disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) analyses were performed using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database.

RNA interference

LNCaP, DU145, and HEK293 cells were seeded overnight and transfected with ZBTB7B siRNA, AR siRNA, or a negative control (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea) using INTERFERin® (Polyplus, Bas-Rhin, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Expression vectors (His-AR, HA-Ub, Flag-ZBTB7B, pEGFP-AR, HA-AR-NTD, HA-AR-NTD-DBD, and HA-AR-DBD-LBD) were purchased from Addgene (Cambridge, MA, USA). LNCaP and HEK293 cells were also transfected using TurboFect™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cell viability assay

LNCaP and DU145 cells transfected with control or ZBTB7B siRNA were cultured for 1–3 days. 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) solution (1 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added for 2 h at 37 ℃. Formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL of DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Colony formation assay

LNCaP and DU145 cells transfected with control or ZBTB7B siRNA for 48 h were seeded in six-well plates (7×103 cells/well) and cultured for 2 weeks. Colonies were fixed and stained using Diff-Quik solution (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan), air-dried, and counted.

Cell cycle analysis

LNCaP and DU145 cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection, fixed in 70% ethanol overnight at −20 ℃, treated with RNase A (1 mg/mL, 30 min, 37 ℃), and stained with propidium iodide (PI, 50 µg/mL). DNA content was analyzed using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Western blotting

LNCaP and DU145 cells transfected with ZBTB7B or AR siRNA were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma, MO, USA). Lysates were quantified using a DC Protein Assay kit (Bio-Rad) and separated by SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer to nitrocellulose membranes (GE Healthcare, Marlborough, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk or BSA and incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against AR, PSA, ZBTB7B, Flag, His, HA, Lamin B (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), or β-actin (Sigma). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) were applied, and signals were detected with ECL reagent using an Amersham Imager 600 (Cytiva, BW, Germany).

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from LNCaP cells using QIAzol (Qiagen, MD, USA). cDNA was synthesized and amplified on a LightCycler™ (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Primers were synthesized by Bioneer, and the sequences were as follows:

ZBTB7B F: 5'-CCTGTCTGCCACAAGATCATCC-3', ZBTB7B R: 5'-GCATGTGGATCTTCAGCTTGTCG-3', AR F: 5'-ATGGTGAGCAGAGTGCCCTATC-3', AR R: 5'-ATGGTCCCTGGCAGTCTCCAAA-3'.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction

LNCaP cells transfected with negative control or ZBTB7B siRNA were harvested, and cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions were isolated using the NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cell pellets were resuspended in 200 µL of Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent I (CERI) and incubated on ice for 10 min, followed by the addition of Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent II and incubation for 1 min on ice. After centrifugation for 5 min, the supernatant (cytoplasmic fraction) was collected. The remaining pellet was resuspended in nuclear extraction reagent (NER), incubated on ice for 40 min with intermittent mixing, and centrifuged to obtain the nuclear fraction. Extracted fractions were stored on ice and used for downstream assays.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-IP was performed as previously described (22). Briefly, whole-cell lysates (250 µg) were incubated at 4 ℃ individually in separate Co-IP experiments with 2 µg of the indicated antibodies [anti-ZBTB7B, anti-His, anti-HA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), or anti-AR (BD Biosciences)] together with 15 µL protein G agarose (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or anti-HA magnetic beads. Incubation was carried out for 2–3 days for agarose-based reactions or 4 h for magnetic bead-based reactions at 4 ℃. The immunocomplexes were washed three times with 1% NP-40 washing buffer, and the precipitated proteins, along with input lysates (25 µg), were analyzed by Western blotting.

Ubiquitination assay

As previously described (23), HEK293 cells were co-transfected with siRNAs (siCTL or siZBTB7B) and plasmids (pcDNA3.0, His-AR, HA-Ub, ± ZBTB7B). After treatment with MG132 (20 µm, 2 h), cells were lysed and immunoprecipitated with anti-HA antibody/HA-magnetic beads, followed by immunoblotting with anti-His, AR, HA, and β-actin antibodies.

Cycloheximide assay

LNCaP cells transfected with control or ZBTB7B siRNA for 48 h were treated with cycloheximide (50 µg/mL; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) for the indicated times. Protein stability was assessed by Western blotting (24).

Immunofluorescence

LNCaP cells (6×104) were cultured for 48 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. After blocking with 3% BSA, cells were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against ZBTB7B (1:500; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and AR (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology). Alexa Fluor 488- and 546-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1,000; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) were applied for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (10 µg/mL; Sigma), and images were obtained using a FLUOVIEW FV10i confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Tissue microarray and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Prostate cancer tissue microarrays (78 adenocarcinoma, 8 adjacent normal, 8 normal tissues) were purchased from US Biomax (Derwood, MD, USA). Sections (4 µm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Slides were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with anti-ZBTB7B (1:200) and anti-AR (15 µg) mouse monoclonal antibodies. Secondary goat anti-mouse antibodies (1:200) were applied for 1 h, followed by DAB staining. Images were captured using a Leica SCN400 system (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Hesse, Germany). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test. Differences were considered significant at P<0.05. TCGA survival data were analyzed in R, and OS was assessed by Kaplan-Meier analysis.

Results

ZBTB7B was highly expressed in prostate cancer cell lines and human tissues

To explore the role of ZBTB7B in prostate cancers, the expression level of ZBTB7B was evaluated in human prostate cancer tissues and normal prostate tissues by TCGA. As shown in Figure 1A, ZBTB7B was highly expressed at mRNA level in prostate cancer tissues than in normal tissues. Moreover, the Kaplan-Meier DFS and OS analyses indicate high expression of ZBTB7B in the prostate cancer patients, implying poor prognosis (Figure 1B). Of note, ZBTB7B and AR were overexpressed in androgen-dependent cell lines LNCaP cells, rather than in PC3 and DU145 cells by qRT-PCR and Western blotting (Figure 1C,1D). Consistently, human tissue array reveals that ZBTB7B was overexpressed in prostate cancer tissues compared to normal control by IHC (Figure 1E).

Differentially expressed profile in ZBTB7B depleted LNCaP cells by genomic analysis

To reveal ZBTB7B related genes, next geneartion analysis was performed in ZBTB7B depleted LNCaP cells. We confirm the reduced expression of ZBTB7B in LNCaP cells transfected with ZBTB7B siRNA (Figure 2A). Scatter plot reveals the decreased expression of ZBTB7B, AR and PSA by over two folds (Figure 2B). In addition, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis shows the related signaling, such as cell cycle (8.13%), cell proliferation (7.14%), DNA repair (6.84%), cell migration (5.39%) and cell differentiation (5.0%) (Figure 2C). Also, upregulated (NKX3-1, ELL2, MAF, CD200) and downregulated (KLK3, ACSL3, KLK2, CENPN) genes in ZBTB7B depleted LNCaP cells by next geneartion analysis (Figure 2D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis indicates the repressed signaling pathway (Green) and induced signaling pathway (Red). Especially, ZBTB7B depletion induced genes are more associated with metabolic pathways and cancer related pathways (Figure 2E).

ZBTB7B depletion reduced viability, the number of colonies and increased G1 arrest in prostate cancer cells

To investigate the oncogenic potential of ZBTB7B in prostate cancer cells, MTT assay, colony formation assay and cell cycle analysis were conducted in DU145 and LNCaP cells transfected with ZBTB7B siRNA or negative control siRNA. The expression of ZBTB7B was decreased at protein and mRNA levels in LNCaP cells by transfection of ZBTB7B siRNA, but not in DU145 cells (Figure 3A,3B). Also, ZBTB7B depletion attenuated the viability in a time-dependent (Figure 3C) and suppressed the number of colonies in LNCaP cells compared to DU145 cells (Figure 3D). Similarly, as shown in Figure 3E, ZBTB7B depletion significantly increased G1 arrest in LNCaP cells, but not in DU145 cells.

ZBTB7B binds to NTD domain of AR as an upstream protein in LNCaP or HEK-293 cells

To check whether ZBTB7B interacts with AR, correlation between ZBTB7B and AR was analysed via cBioPortal database. Here the correlation between ZBTB7B and AR was positively correlated (P<0.001, r=0.28) at mRNA level in prostate cancers (Figure 4A). Of note, ZBTB7B depletion downregulated AR (Figure 4B), while AR depletion did not affect ZBTB7B at mRNA level (Figure 4C) in LNCaP cells. Consistently, ZBTB7B depletion reduced the expression of AR (Figure 4D) in LNCaP cells and also reduced the production of PSA in serum of LNCaP cells by ELISA (Figure 4E). AR depletion did not affect ZBTB7B in LNCaP cells (Figure 4F). Notably, ZBTB7B depletion reduced the stability of AR in a time course by cycloheximide assay (Figure 4G). AR consists of NH2-terminal transactivation domain [NTD or transactivation domain (TAD)], DNA-binding domain (DBD) and LBD (Figure 4H). In HEK293 cells cotransfected with Flag-ZBTB7B, HA-HA-AR-NTD, HA-HA-AR-NTD-DBD and HA-HA-AR-DBD-LBD plasmids, ZBTB7B binds to the NTD alone or NTD-DBD construct, but not to LBD (Figure 4I), implying that the NTD of AR is requisite for binding with ZBTB7B.

Effect of finasteride or DHT on the expression of ZBTB7B in LNCaP cells

To demonstrate whether AR inhibitor finasteride or AR activator DHT affects ZBTB7B, Western blotting was performed in LNCaP cells. LNCaP cells were treated with finasteride and/or DHT. Here finasteride attenuated the expression of AR and PSA but did not affect ZBTB7B for 24, 48, and 72 h (Figure 5A). In contrast, DHT significantly upregulated the expression of AR and PSA, but did not affect ZBTB7B as well (Figure 5B). Likewise, ZBTB7B depletion attenuated the expression of AR and PSA in LNCaP cells, while finasteride or DHT regulated the expression of AR and PSA in LNCaP cells (Figure 5C). Furthermore, ZBTB7B binds to AR by IP regardless of DHT presence in LNCaP cells (Figure 5D). To explore the mechanism that ZBTB7B modulates AR-mediated transcription activity, immunofluorescence (IF), immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination assays were performed. IF reveals that ZBTB7B is colocalized in the nucleus with AR, while ZBTB7B (green) depletion reduces the number of AR (red) in LNCaP cells (Figure 5E). It is observed that AR and ZBTB7B exist both in the cytosol and nucleus, and ZBTB7B depletion reduced the nuclear expression of AR and PSA in LNCaP cells (Figure 5F). Then the effects of ZBTB7B depletion on AR degradation were assessed in LNCaP cells. Herein, AR expression was induced by proteasome inhibitor MG132, while ZBTB7B depletion reduced the expression of AR in LNCaP cells (Figure 5G). Furthermore, AR degradation was enhanced by ZBTB7B depletion by ubiquitination assay (Figure 5H).

Discussion

Though ZBTB7B is involved in cancer progression and proliferation via inhibition of CD8 T cells (15), the oncogenic role and molecular mechanism of ZBTB7B are not elucidated in prostate cancers to date. In the current study, ZBTB7B was overexpressed in prostate cancer cells and tissues with poor prognosis by human tissue microarray and TCGA analysis, implying oncogenic potential of ZBTB7B. However, ZBTB7B was highly expressed in androgen-dependent cell line LNCaP cells, but not in androgen-independent cell lines DU145 and PC3 cells, indicating close association with AR signaling. Consistently, ZBTB7B depletion suppressed viability, the number of colonies and increased G1 arrest in LNCaP cells rather than in DU145 cells.

Accumulating evidence reveals that ADT has been used for the primary treatment for metastatic prostate cancer, since androgen function is mediated by the AR, a hormone activated transcription factor (9,22) and also AR signaling is critically involved in prostate cancer proliferation, differentiation and growth (25). Additionally, PSA is associated with prostate cancer progression, since PSA is known to modulate AR signaling (26). Here, ZBTB7B depletion suppressed the expression of AR and PSA in LNCaP cells, while AR depletion did not affect ZBTB7B, demonstrating that ZBTB7B regulates AR as its upstream protein. Finasteride is reported to inhibit PSA and AR in LNCaP cells, while DHT, the natural androgen, has the highest affinity with AR (27). Furthermore, 5α-reductase inhibitor finasteride and AR activator DHT did not affect ZBTB7B, implying ZBTB7B as an upstream of AR. Consistently, we have found that ZBTB7B was colocalized with AR by IF and was bound to AR by immunoprecipitation, indicating colocalization and binding between ZBTB7B and AR.

A key step of AR action is AR nuclear translocation, while AR is limited in the cytoplasm in the absence of ligand in androgen-sensitive cells (28,29). Consistently, ZBTB7B depletion reduced the nuclear translocation of AR compared to untreated control. Consistently, ZBTB7B depletion reduced the stability of AR in a time course by cycloheximide assay. Furthermore, ZBTB7B depletion promoted AR degradation in HEK293 cells by ubiquitination assay, demonstrating that ZBTB7B modulates nuclear translocation and degradation of AR.

AR domains are composed of NTD or TAD, DBD and LBD (8). However, development of active drugs is not clinically active targeting the DBD and TAD due to multiple factors (30). Also, enzalutamide, bicalutamide and apalutamide are known to act as AR antagonists (31). Interestingly, binding assay reveals that ZBTB7B is bound to AR-NTD and NTD-DBD, but not AR-LBD in HEK293 cells, demonstrating AR-NTD domain is requisite for binding with ZBTB7B in AR-dependent prostate cancer cells.

Taken together, these findings provide a novel insight that ZBTB7B promotes prostate cancer progression as a potent oncogene via colocalization and binding with AR, though further animal studies and human clinical trials are required to validate the in vitro oncogenic role of ZBTB7B in association with AR in the future.

Conclusions

ZBTB7B has been implicated in cancer progression, including through effects on CD8⁺ T cells (15), but its oncogenic role and mechanism in prostate cancer have remained unclear. In this study, ZBTB7B was overexpressed in prostate cancer tissues and associated with poorer prognosis in TCGA analyses, supporting oncogenic potential. Notably, ZBTB7B expression was high in the androgen-dependent LNCaP line but low in androgen-independent DU145 and PC3 cells, suggesting a link to AR signaling. Functionally, ZBTB7B knockdown reduced viability and colony formation and increased G1 arrest more prominently in LNCaP than in DU145 cells.

ADT is a mainstay for metastatic prostate cancer because AR, a ligand-activated transcription factor, drives proliferation, differentiation, and growth (9,22,25). Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is a canonical AR target gene and clinical biomarker (26). Consistent with an upstream relationship, ZBTB7B depletion decreased AR and PSA protein in LNCaP cells, whereas AR knockdown did not alter ZBTB7B. In addition, neither the 5α-reductase inhibitor finasteride nor DHT affected ZBTB7B levels, further indicating that ZBTB7B acts upstream of AR. Mechanistically, ZBTB7B colocalized with AR by IF and co-immunoprecipitated with AR, indicating a physical interaction.

A key step in AR action is ligand-dependent nuclear translocation (28,29). ZBTB7B knockdown reduced AR nuclear accumulation, decreased AR protein stability in cycloheximide chase assays, and enhanced AR ubiquitination and degradation in HEK293-based assays. Together, these data suggest that ZBTB7B promotes AR stabilization and nuclear trafficking. Domain mapping indicated that ZBTB7B binds AR’s N-terminal transactivation domain (NTD) and NTD-DBD, but not the LBD, in line with the challenges of clinically targeting AR DBD/TAD (30) and with the use of LBD-directed antagonists such as enzalutamide, bicalutamide, and apalutamide (31). The preferential interaction with AR-NTD supports a model in which ZBTB7B enhances AR function through N-terminal engagement rather than ligand-binding modulation.

However, we did not directly quantify AR transcriptional activity under ZBTB7B perturbation, which represents one of the limitations of this study. To address this, AR element (ARE)-driven luciferase reporter assay is required with PSA-as Luc/MMTV-Luc + pRL-TK in LNCaP (endogenous AR) and HEK293 (AR co-expression) cells with ZBTB7B knockdown/overexpression plasmids, along with quantification of AR target genes (KLK3/PSA, TMPRSS2, FKBP5) by qRT-PCR and at canonical AREs by ChIP-qPCR in the future. Also, given the pivotal role of AR signaling in prostate cancer progression, our findings suggest that combining AR inhibitors with ZBTB7B silencing represents a promising translational strategy for prostate cancer, warranting validation in xenograft/PDX models and clinical trials.
