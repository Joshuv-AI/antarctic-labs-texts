Algebraic groups over free and hyperbolic groups
Vincent Guirardel, Chloé Perin

Abstract
We define an algebraic group over a group G G to be a variety - that is, a subset of G d G^{d} defined by equations over G G - endowed with a group law whose coordinates can be expressed as word maps. In the case where G G is a torsion-free hyperbolic group and the underlying variety is irreducible we give a complete description of all algebraic groups.



1
Introduction

In classical algebraic geometry, an algebraic group over a field
k
is an algebraic variety endowed with a group law which can be expressed by polynomials in the coordinates. We ask what happens when we replace the field
k
by a group
\Gamma
, and polynomials by word maps with constants in
\Gamma
.

More precisely, given a group
\Gamma
, an equation over
\Gamma
in the variables

x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
is an expression of the form
w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=1
,
where
w

is a word in the variables
x_{1}^{\pm},\dots,x_{d}^{\pm}
with constants in
\Gamma
,
i. e. an element of the free product
\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle
.
Given a set
\Sigma
of such words, the variety
V
defined by
\Sigma
is the subset of
\Gamma^{d}
consisting of the
d
-tuples
(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})\in\Gamma^{d}

satisfying all the equations

w(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})=1
for
w\in\Sigma
.
An algebraic map
F:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
between two algebraic varieties
V\subset\Gamma^{d}
and
V^{\prime}\subset\Gamma^{d^{\prime}}
is a map such that there exist words
w_{1},\dots,w_{d^{\prime}}\in\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle

such that for all
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})\in V
,

F(v)=(w_{1}(v),\dots,w_{d^{\prime}}(v))
.
An isomorphism of algebraic varieties
F:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
is a bijection such that
F
and
F^{-1}
are algebraic maps.
When
\Gamma
is a non-elementary torsion-free hyperbolic group,
the set of algebraic varieties is the collection of closed sets for
a topology, called the Zariski topology (the hypothesis on
\Gamma
ensures that
this is stable under finite unions, see
[
BMR99
, §3.1]
).

We define an algebraic group over
\Gamma
as a group
V
which is a variety
and whose multiplication law
\mu:V\times V\to V
is an algebraic map.
It would be natural to ask additionally that the map
V\rightarrow V
sending and element to its inverse
is also an algebraic map, but we actually don’t require this as our results hold without
this assumption (and in fact imply that this extra condition automatically holds).

Example 1.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be any group. Then

\Gamma
itself is an algebraic group
for its standard multiplication law
\mu(u,v)=uv
,
which is simply the algebraic map
\Gamma^{2}\rightarrow\Gamma
associated to the word
xy\in\Gamma*\langle x,y\rangle
.
We note that the inverse
v\mapsto v^{-1}
is also an algebraic map.

Example 1.2
.

If
a\in\Gamma
is any element, then its centralizer
V=Z_{\Gamma}(a)

is an algebraic group with underlying variety defined by the equation
xax^{-1}a^{-1}=1
,
and endowed with the standard multiplication law.
Similarly, the centralizer of any subset of
\Gamma
is an algebraic group.
Now if
\Gamma
is a torsion-free hyperbolic group,
any centralizer is either the trivial group, the whole group
\Gamma
, or a maximal cyclic subgroup
\langle c\rangle=Z(c)
.

Example 1.3
.

The direct product of finitely many algebraic groups over
\Gamma
is again an algebraic group.
Thus, given
a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}\in\Gamma\setminus\{1\}
and
n_{1},\dots,n_{l},r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the product

\Gamma^{r}\times Z(a_{1})^{n_{1}}\times\dots\times Z(a_{l})^{n_{l}}

is also an algebraic group.

Example 1.4
.

If
a
is conjugate to
a^{\prime}=bab^{-1}
then
V=Z(a)
is isomorphic to
V^{\prime}=Z(a^{\prime})
as an algebraic group, the isomorphism
v\mapsto bvb^{-1}
and its inverse being algebraic maps.
For this reason, if
\Gamma
is a torsion-free hyperbolic group,
any group as in Example
1.3
is isomorphic, as an algebraic group, to
a group of the form

\Gamma^{r}\times\langle c_{1}\rangle^{n_{1}}\times\dots\times\langle c_{l}\rangle^{n_{l}}

where
\langle c_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle c_{l}\rangle
are non-conjugate maximal cyclic groups.

Example 1.5
.

If
V
is an algebraic group and
F:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
is an isomorphism of algebraic varieties,
one can transport the multiplication law by
F
.
For example, conjugating the standard multiplication law on
V=\Gamma^{2}
by the algebraic map

F:\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{bmatrix}\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}u_{2}\\
u_{2}u_{1}u_{2}\end{bmatrix}
,
one gets a seemingly complicated multiplication law
\circledast
on
V^{\prime}=V=\Gamma^{2}
given by

\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{bmatrix}\circledast\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{2}u_{2}^{-1}v_{1}^{2}v_{2}^{-1}u_{2}u_{1}^{-1}v_{2}v_{1}^{-1}\\
u_{2}u_{1}^{-1}v_{2}v_{1}^{-1}u_{1}^{2}u_{2}^{-1}v_{1}^{2}v_{2}^{-1}u_{2}u_{1}^{-1}v_{2}v_{1}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}

but this is just an algebraic group isomorphic to
\Gamma^{2}
with its standard multiplication law.
Here we say that two algebraic groups
(V,\mu),(V^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
are isomorphic (as algebraic groups over
\Gamma
)
if there is an isomorphism of algebraic varieties
F:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
which is a group isomorphism.

In this paper we work over a fixed torsion-free hyperbolic group
\Gamma
.
A variety
V
is called
irreducible
if it is not the union of two proper subvarieties.
For an algebraic group, it is irreducible if and only if it is connected for the Zariski topology (see Section
4.3
).
We thus say that an algebraic group
V
is
connected
if the underlying variety is connected, or equivalently irreducible.
Focusing on connected algebraic groups is not a big restriction since any algebraic group has a finite index subgroup which is connected (this is a consequence of equational Noetherianity due to Sela
[
Sel09
]
, see Section
4.3
).
All the examples of algebraic groups given above are connected.

Our main theorem gives a classification of all connected algebraic groups over
\Gamma
,
and says that all of them come from the examples above.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a torsion-free hyperbolic group.
Let
V
be a connected algebraic group over
\Gamma
.

Then there exist non-conjugate maximal cyclic subgroups
\langle c_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle c_{l}\rangle
of
\Gamma
, and integers
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
n_{1},\ldots,n_{l}\in{\mathbb{N}}\setminus\{0\}
such that

V
is isomorphic, as an algebraic group, to

\Gamma^{r}\times\langle c_{1}\rangle^{n_{1}}\times\ldots\times\langle c_{l}\rangle^{n_{l}}

where the multiplication law is the standard coordinate-wise product.

Moreover, this gives a complete classification of connected algebraic groups up to isomorphism:
the conjugacy classes of the groups
\langle c_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle c_{l}\rangle
and the exponents
n_{i}
and
r
are uniquely determined
(up to permutation) by the isomorphism class of
(V,\mu)
as an algebraic group.

Remark 1.7
.

As mentioned above, if
V
is an algebraic group which is not assumed to be connected,
then the connected component of
V
containing the neutral element
is a finite index subgroup which is itself a connected algebraic group.
However, with the definition given above,
this remark is not enough to deduce a description of all algebraic groups from our result on connected ones. Indeed,
it is not clear even which finite groups can be realised as algebraic groups:
every finite set is an algebraic set but its is not clear whether a given group law can
be expressed as a word map.

With this in mind, it would be more natural to enlarge the category of algebraic groups by allowing the group law to be
given by word maps only locally. For connected algebraic groups, this definition is equivalent
to the previous one. However, it has the advantage of being stable under taking supergroups of finite index: with the new definition,
if
G
has a finite index subgroup which is isomorphic to a connected algebraic group,
then
G
itself is isomorphic to an algebraic group.

Other notions and questions

One can consider more general notions.
A group
(V,\mu)
is
definable
over a structure
\Gamma
(such as a field, or a group)
if
V
is a subset of
\Gamma^{d}
definable by a first order formula,
and
\mu
(or more precisely the graph of
\mu
) is also definable in this way.
Even more generally, a group
(V,\mu)
is
interpretable
over
\Gamma

if
V=\tilde{V}/\sim
where
\tilde{V}
is a definable subset and
\sim

is a definable equivalence relation, and similarly for the graph of
\mu
.

Over an algebraically closed field, the Weil-Hrushovski theorem states
that any interpretable group is an algebraic group
(see
[
Poi01
]
Theorem 4.13, with (3) from §4.1),
where the notion of algebraic group here should not be restricted to affine algebraic groups but should also include abelian varieties for example.

Let us now consider groups interpretable over a base group
\Gamma
.
If

\Gamma
is a group such as
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})

or the Heisenberg group
\Gamma=H_{3}({\mathbb{C}})
(or more generally an algebraic group over
{\mathbb{C}}
, thought of as an abstract group),
then any interpretable group over
\Gamma
will also be interpretable over
{\mathbb{C}}
. Thus
the theorem by Weil-Hrushovski shows that all interpretable groups
over
\Gamma
are actually isomorphic to algebraic groups over
{\mathbb{C}}
.
On the other hand, starting from the Heisenberg group
\Gamma=H_{3}(R)

over a ring
R
, then the ring
(R,+,.)
is interpretable over the group
\Gamma

[
Mal65
]
,
so conversely any algebraic group over
{\mathbb{C}}
is interpretable over
H_{3}({\mathbb{C}})
.
But taking
R={\mathbb{Z}}
, one gets that arithmetic is interpretable over
H_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})

and it easily follows that every finitely presented group
is interpretable over
H_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})
.

Returning to the case where
\Gamma
is a torsion-free hyperbolic group (and even a free group), we ask:

Problem 1.8
.

Let
\Gamma
be a torsion-free hyperbolic group. Classify the definable and interpretable groups over
\Gamma
.

We note that Byron and Sklinos proved,
relying on Sela’s work on the first order theory of free groups, that no infinite field is
interpretable over the free group
[
DBS19
,
Skl24
,
Sel06
,
Sel24
]
.

Ingredients of the proof.

Our proof of Theorem
1.6
uses
basic tools from algebraic geometry over groups
[
BMR99
]
.
The central notion is that of the
group of functions

L_{V}
over a variety
V
(also called
coordinate group
in the literature).
For instance, in the case where
\Gamma
is a free group, coordinate groups
L_{V}
are precisely finitely generated residually free groups. When
V
is irreducible (see below),

L_{V}
is actually a
limit group
, a class of well behaved groups
which has been widely studied
[
Sel01
,
KM98
,
Sel09
,
GW18
]
.
The group
L_{V}
contains a preferred copy of
\Gamma
corresponding to the constant functions
(except for the degenerate case
V=\emptyset
).
Given a point
v
in
V
, the evaluation homomorphism
L_{V}\rightarrow\Gamma

is defined by evaluating a function

f\in L_{V}
at the point
v
.
This gives a bijective correspondence which identifies
V

with the set
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
of all homomorphisms
L_{V}\to\Gamma

that restrict to the identity on the preferred copy of
\Gamma
in
L_{V}
.

Of fundamental importance will be the functoriality of the correspondence between
V
and
L_{V}
: a morphism of algebraic varieties
\alpha:V\to W
corresponds to a group morphism
\alpha^{*}:L_{W}\to L_{V}
, and conversely.
In particular, the group of automorphisms of
V
(as an algebraic variety)
is isomorphic to
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
, the group of automorphisms of
L_{V}

that are the identity on
\Gamma
.
For example, if
V
is homogeneous under the action of its automorphism group,
then
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
acts transitively on
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
.
Using tools such as Grushko decompositions and JSJ decompositions, which help describe automorphisms of
L_{V}
,
we get strong
restrictions on
L_{V}
and thus on
V
.

However this is not enough to complete the description of algebraic groups, since
there are varieties
V
that are homogeneous but do not correspond
to algebraic groups over
\Gamma
.
In Section
5.2
for instance, we describe a variety
V
over
\Gamma
a free group,
on which there is a free transitive action
of the mapping class group of a surface, but it follows from our classification that
V
does not admit a structure of algebraic group.

If
V
is an algebraic group, its action on itself yields a representation
of
V
into the group
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
.
The fact that this representation comes from an algebraic action of an algebraic
group gives strong restrictions on the image of
V
in
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
.
For example, in algebraic geometry over a field
k
, if an algebraic group acts
algebraically on the affine space
k^{n}
, then any orbit for its action on the ring of functions
k[X_{1},\dots X_{n}]

has bounded degree.
The analogous statement here says that the action of
V
on the group of functions
L_{V}

has bounded stretch:
there exists a constant
C
such that all elements of
V
act
on
L_{V}
with Lipschitz constant at most
C

(see Lemma
7.5
) where the length of elements of
L_{V}

is measured in a word metric
where all elements of
\Gamma\setminus\{1\}
have length 1.

Because of the lack of local finiteness of the metric involved,
this does not imply finiteness of the image of
V
.
For instance, if
L_{V}=\Gamma*\langle X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\rangle
,
and given two tuples
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}),b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in\Gamma
,
the automorphism
\tau_{a,b}:X_{i}\mapsto a_{i}X_{i}b_{i}

is an automorphism whose powers have bounded Lipschitz constants,
and which usually has infinite order.
Still, this gives strong restrictions on the representation of
V
, and for instance, staying in the case where
L_{V}=\Gamma*\langle X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\rangle
,
it prevents its image from containing complicated automorphisms such as
fully irreducible automorphisms
(in the sense of free products relative to
\Gamma
).
Applying a result by Handel and Mosher
[
HM20
]

and generalized by Horbez and the first author
[
GH22
]
,
one deduces that there is a triangular structure invariant under the image of
V
in
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
(the image of
V
looks like a unipotent group).

The endgame is then algebraic: using the fact that the action of
V

on
V
is triangular, one can then express the associativity of the group law to deduce that our algebraic group is isomorphic to a standard one.

Organization of the paper

The paper is structured as follows: in Section
2
, we survey tools of algebraic geometry over groups and the function group

L_{V}
associated to a variety
V
.

In Section
3
, we recall some results about
\Gamma
-limit groups, in particular we define
the JSJ decomposition for such groups and give some of their properties.

In Section
4
, we consider algebraic groups over
\Gamma
. We prove a useful formula expressing the group law under the identification of
V
with
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
, and express homogeneity of the underlying variety under this same identification.

We use this in Section
5
to deduce a number of constraints that the JSJ decomposition of the function group
L_{V}
of a homogeneous irreducible variety must satisfy.

In Section
6
,
we construct a canonical algebraic subgroup
(W,\mu|_{W\times W})
of
(V,\mu)

associated to the abelian factors in
V
.
We deduce that the JSJ decomposition of
L_{W}
and of
L_{V}

have no rigid vertices other than the one containing the constants group
\Gamma
.

In Section
7
, we introduce the bounded stretch argument
and use it to show that the JSJ decomposition of
L_{V}
cannot contain any surface type vertices either.

This enables us in Section
8
to give a complete description of the various possibilities for the variety
V
, via a description of the JSJ decomposition of
L_{V}
.

Finally, in Section
9
,
we use the bounded stretch argument to find a triangular structure
of the image of
V
and
V^{2}
in
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
,
and use it to find a change of coordinates
under which the group law becomes the standard coordinate-wise product,
thus completing the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

2
Algebraic geometric tools over groups

In all this paper, we fix
\Gamma
a non elementary torsion-free hyperbolic group.

2.1
\Gamma
-groups.

Following
[
BMR99
]
,
a
\Gamma
-group

G
is a group
G
together
with a specified monomorphism
\iota:\Gamma\rightarrow G
.
A

\Gamma
-homomorphism

\varphi:G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
is a homomorphism that commutes with the specified embeddings.
We will usually keep
\iota
implicit, and identify
\Gamma
with its image;
thus, a
\Gamma
-homomorphism is a homomorphism that restricts to the identity on
\Gamma
.
We denote by
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(G,G^{\prime})
the set of
\Gamma
-homomorphisms.

For instance
\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle
(where
\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle
is the free group on the
x_{i}
’s) is a
\Gamma
-group
whose elements are called
\Gamma
-words:
they can be viewed as
words in the variables
x_{1}^{\pm},\dots,x_{d}^{\pm}
with coefficients in
\Gamma
.

A subset
S
of a
\Gamma
-group
G
is a
\Gamma
-generating set
if

\Gamma\cup S
generates
G
as a group.

Definition 2.1
.

A
\Gamma
-group
G
is residually
\Gamma
if for any
g\in G\setminus\{1\}
there exists a
\Gamma
-homomorphism
h:G\to\Gamma
such that
h(g)\neq 1
.

See Definition
2.11
for the stronger notion of being fully residually
\Gamma
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a
\Gamma
-group
G
, its
largest residually
\Gamma
quotient
is the group

G/K\quad\text{where }K=\bigcap_{h\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(G,\Gamma)}\ker h

Clearly,
G/K
is residually
\Gamma
and if
G\to G^{\prime}
is a
\Gamma
-homomorphism to
a
\Gamma
-group
G^{\prime}
which is residually
\Gamma
,
it factors through
G/K
.
If
G=\langle\Gamma,x_{1},\dots,x_{d}|\Sigma\rangle
, then
K
is called the
radical
of
\Sigma

in
[
BMR99
]
.

2.2
Variety over
\Gamma
.

A system of equations over
\Gamma
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
is a set of words

\Sigma=\{w_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\mid i\in I\}\subset\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle.

The variety
V_{\Sigma}\subseteq\Gamma^{d}
it defines is

V_{\Sigma}=\{(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})\in\Gamma^{d}\mid w_{i}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})=1\textrm{ for each }i\in I\}.

Following Lemma 6(6) p. 49 and Proposition 1 p. 31 in
[
BMR99
]
,
since
\Gamma
is CSA and non abelian, the collection of varieties is stable under finite union and is the collection of closed sets of a topology on
\Gamma^{d}
, called the Zariski topology (see Theorem 3 p. 50 in
[
BMR99
]
).

2.3
Group of functions.

Let
V
be a subset of
\Gamma^{d}
. A word map on
V
is a map
f:V\to\Gamma
for which there exists a word

w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle

such that for
all
(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})\in V
,

f(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})=w(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})
.

For example, the projection on the
k
-th coordinate
V\to\Gamma,(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})\mapsto v_{k}
is a word map. We denote it by
X_{k}
.

Definition 2.3

(Group of functions)
.

The group of functions
L_{V}
on
V
is the set of word maps
f:V\to\Gamma
, endowed with multiplication in the range: given
f,f^{\prime}\in L_{V}
we define
ff^{\prime}:V\to\Gamma
by setting
ff^{\prime}(v)=f(v)f^{\prime}(v)
.

The group
L_{V}
comes with the
\Gamma
-generating family
(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
formed by the coordinate functions.

By definition of a word map, there is a surjective map
\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle\rightarrow L_{V}
sending a word
w
to the corresponding word map, and sending the generator
x_{k}

to the coordinate map
X_{k}
. Thus,
(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
is indeed a
\Gamma
-generating family of
L_{V}
.

The group of functions on
V
is also known as its
coordinate group

[
BMR99
]
.

All the varieties we consider will be non empty. In this case,
L_{V}
is a
\Gamma
-group where
\Gamma
embeds in
L_{V}
as the set of constant maps.

2.4
Equations of the variety and functions on
V
.

If
V
is a variety defined by
\Sigma=\{w_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\mid i\in I\}
, then the map
\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle\rightarrow L_{V}
vanishes on each
w_{i}
, hence
factors through the group
L_{\Sigma}=\langle\Gamma,x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\mid w_{i},i\in I\rangle
.

Given a point
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})\in V
, we denote the evaluation homomorphism by
h_{v}:\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle\rightarrow\Gamma
, it is defined by

h_{v}(w)=w(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})
for any word
w\in\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle
.
This is a
\Gamma
-homomorphism so
h_{v}\in{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle,\Gamma)
.

Every evaluation homomorphism
h_{v}
factors through
L_{\Sigma}

and through
L_{V}
.
We still call the factored map
L_{\Sigma}\rightarrow\Gamma
or
L_{V}\rightarrow\Gamma
an evaluation homomorphism and denote it by
h_{v}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Every
h\in{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{\Sigma},\Gamma)

agrees with some evaluation homomorphism
h_{v}:L_{\Sigma}\to\Gamma
.

Proof.

Given
h\in{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{\Sigma},\Gamma)
,
consider the point
v_{h}=(h(\bar{x}_{1}),\dots,h(\bar{x}_{d}))\in\Gamma^{d}

where we denote by
\bar{x}_{i}\in L_{\Sigma}
the image of
x_{i}

under the morphism
\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle\rightarrow L_{\Sigma}
.
The point
v_{h}
lies in the variety
V
.
Indeed, for each
w_{i}\in\Sigma
,
we have
w_{i}(h(\bar{x}_{1}),\dots,h(\bar{x}_{d}))=h(w_{i}(\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{d}))=1
because
h
is a
\Gamma
-morphism.
We check that
h=h_{v_{h}}
:
given any word
w\in\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle
with image
\bar{w}\in L_{\Sigma}
, we have

h_{v_{h}}(\bar{w})=w(h(\bar{x}_{1}),\dots,h(\bar{x}_{d}))=h(w(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{d}))=h(\bar{w}).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Two words in
\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle
represent the same function
on
V
if and only if they have the same image under all evaluations maps
h_{v}
for
v\in V
.
Thus one can alternatively describe
L_{V}
as the quotient

L_{V}=L_{\Sigma}/\bigcap_{v\in V}\ker h_{v}.

By construction,
L_{V}
is residually
\Gamma
: for every function
f\in L_{V}\setminus\{1\}

there is a
\Gamma
-morphism
L_{V}\rightarrow\Gamma
which does not kill
f
, namely the evaluation map
h_{v}

at a point
v\in V
where
f
does not vanish.

As an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.4
we get

Corollary 2.5
.

If
V
the variety defined by the set of equations
\Sigma
, then

L_{V}
is the largest
quotient of
L_{\Sigma}
that is residually
\Gamma
.

In particular, the canonical map from
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
to
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{\Sigma},\Gamma)
is a bijection.

Example 2.6
.

Fix an element
c\in\Gamma\setminus\{1\}
which is not a proper power so that
Z(c)=\langle c\rangle
.
Let
V=Z(c)^{d}\subset\Gamma^{d}

be the variety defined by the system of equations
\Sigma=\{[x_{1},c],\dots,[x_{d},c]\}
.
Here
L_{\Sigma}=\langle\Gamma,x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\mid[x_{1},c],\ldots,[x_{d},c]\rangle

and

L_{V}\simeq\Gamma*_{\langle c\rangle}(\langle c\rangle\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})

is its largest residually
\Gamma
quotient (see for instance
[
BMR02
, Theorem 1]
to see it is indeed residually
\Gamma
).

2.5
Correspondence
V\leftrightarrow{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
.

Consider the maps

\left\{\begin{matrix}V&\rightarrow&{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)\\
v&\mapsto&h_{v}\end{matrix}\right.\text{\quad and \quad}\left\{\begin{matrix}{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)&\rightarrow&V\\
h&\mapsto&v_{h}=(h(X_{1}),\dots,h(X_{d}))\end{matrix}\right.

The first associates to a point of
V
its evaluation map, the second is the map
h\mapsto v_{h}
that appears in the proof of the previous lemma, up to the bijection
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)\rightarrow{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{\Sigma},\Gamma)
mentioned in Corollary
2.5
.

We claim that these maps are inverses of each other.
Indeed, we already proved in Lemma
2.4
that
h_{v_{h}}=h
for all
h\in{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{\Sigma},\Gamma)
. This is true on
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
via the canonical bijection between
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{\Sigma},\Gamma)
and
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
.
For the other direction, we consider
v\in V
and
h_{v}:L_{V}\rightarrow\Gamma
the corresponding evaluation morphism.
We have

v_{h_{v}}=(h_{v}(X_{1}),\dots,h_{v}(X_{d}))=(X_{1}(v),\dots,X_{d}(v))=v.

2.6
Algebraic maps.

Let
V\subseteq\Gamma^{d},V^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma^{d^{\prime}}
be algebraic varieties over
\Gamma
. An algebraic map from
V
to
V^{\prime}
is a map

F:V\to V^{\prime}

such that there exist word maps
f_{1},\dots,f_{d^{\prime}}:V\rightarrow\Gamma
such that

F(v)=(f_{1}(v),\ldots,f_{d^{\prime}}(v))
for all
v\in V
.
An algebraic automorphism
F:V\rightarrow V
is a bijective algebraic map whose inverse is an algebraic map.

Recalling that elements of
L_{V}
(respectively
L_{V^{\prime}}
) are word maps
V\to\Gamma
(respectively
V^{\prime}\to\Gamma
)
any algebraic map
F:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
induces a
\Gamma
-homomorphism

F^{*}:\left\{\begin{matrix}L_{V^{\prime}}&\to&L_{V}\\
f&\mapsto&f\circ F.\end{matrix}\right.

Note that given a variety
V^{\prime\prime}
and an algebraic map
G:V^{\prime}\to V^{\prime\prime}
, we have
(G\circ F)^{*}=F^{*}\circ G^{*}
.
In other words, there is a contravariant functor from the category of algebraic varieties over
\Gamma
to the category of
\Gamma
-groups that are residually
\Gamma
.

Conversely, given a
\Gamma
-homomorphism
\theta:L_{V^{\prime}}\to L_{V}

(with
\Gamma
-generating families
(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
and
(X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{d^{\prime}})
of
L_{V}
and
L_{V^{\prime}}
respectively)
there is an induced
map
\theta_{*}:{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)\to{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V^{\prime}},\Gamma)
given by
h\mapsto h\circ\theta
. Via the identification between
V
and
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
(respectively between
V^{\prime}
and
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V^{\prime}},\Gamma)
)
induced by the
\Gamma
-generating families,
this can be seen as an algebraic map
V\to V^{\prime}
:
the image of
X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{d^{\prime}}\in L_{V^{\prime}}
under
\theta
can be written
using the
\Gamma
-generating set

\{X_{1},\dots,X_{d}\}
of
L_{V}
, say
\theta(X^{\prime}_{i})=u_{i}(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})

for some
\Gamma
-words
u_{i}\in\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle
,
and the algebraic map
V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
is defined by

(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})\in V\mapsto(u_{1}(v_{1},\dots,v_{d}),\dots,u_{d^{\prime}}(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})).

In particular, there is an isomorphism between
the group
{\mathrm{Aut}}(V)
of algebraic automorphisms of the variety
V
, and the group

{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
of
\Gamma
-automorphisms of the
\Gamma
-group
L_{V}
.

2.7
Noetherianity and irreducible varieties.

Sela proved the following

Theorem 2.7

(
[
Sel09
]
)
.

Torsion-free hyperbolic groups are equationally Noetherian: for any system of equations
\Sigma\subset\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
,
there exists a finite subsystem
\Sigma^{\prime}\subset\Sigma

such that
V_{\Sigma}=V_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
.

Equivalently, any descending chain of varieties
V_{1}\supset V_{2}\supset V_{3}\ldots
is stationary.

Definition 2.8
.

A non-empty variety is irreducible if it cannot be written as a finite union of proper subvarieties.

As a straightforward corollary of Theorem
2.7
we get that any variety
V
can be written as a finite union
V_{1}\cup\ldots\cup V_{s}

of irreducible varieties.
Additionally, there is a unique such decomposition for which
s
is minimal
(equivalently, no inclusion
V_{i}\subset V_{j}
holds for
i\neq j
): in this case,
V_{1},\dots,V_{s}
are called the
irreducible components
of
V
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
G,G^{\prime}
be two
\Gamma
-groups. A collection
{\mathcal{H}}
of
\Gamma
-homomorphisms

G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
is
discriminating
if
for every finite subset
F\subset G

there exists
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that
h
is injective in restriction to
F
.

Remark 2.10
.

For a sequence of
\Gamma
-homomorphisms,
we will use the following variant of this definition:
a sequence of
\Gamma
-homomorphisms
h_{i}:G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
(
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
) is discriminating if
for every finite subset
F\subset G
, for all
i
large enough,
h_{i}

is injective in restriction to
F
.
Note that for
G
countable, a collection
{\mathcal{H}}
of
\Gamma
-homomorphisms is discriminating if and only if it contains a discriminating sequence.

Equivalently,
{\mathcal{H}}
is discriminating if for any finite collection of
non-trivial elements
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in G\setminus\{1\}
,
there exists
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that
h(g_{1}),\dots,h(g_{n})
are all non-trivial.

Definition 2.11
.

A
\Gamma
-group
G
is
fully residually
\Gamma
if
it admits a discriminating family of
\Gamma
-homomorphisms to
\Gamma

(equivalently
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(G,\Gamma)
is discriminating).

Sela proved that the class of finitely generated
\Gamma
-groups which are fully residually
\Gamma
coincide with that of
\Gamma
-limit groups
[
Sel09
]

(actually, in
[
Sel09
]
§1, Sela works with equations which do not involve constants in
\Gamma
, so the
\Gamma
-limit groups there don’t come with a preferred copy
\Gamma
).

The following lemma
is a slight variation on
[
BMR99
, Theorem D2]
.

Lemma 2.12
.

The variety
V
is irreducible if and only if
L_{V}
is fully residually
\Gamma
.

In this case, if
V_{0}\subset V
is a dense subset (for the Zariski topology),
then the family of evaluation homomorphisms
\{h_{v}|v\in V_{0}\}\subset\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
is discriminating on
L_{V}
.

Proof.

We first prove the second assertion by contradiction: we assume that
V
is irreducible,
but that
\{h_{v}|v\in V_{0}\}
is not discriminating.
Then there exists
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in L_{V}\setminus\{1\}

such that for each
v\in V_{0}
, there exists
i\leq n
such that
h_{v}(f_{i})=f_{i}(v)=1
.
Denote by
V_{i}=f_{i}^{-1}(\{1\})
the subvariety of
V

defined by
f_{i}
.
In other words,
V_{0}\subset\bigcup_{i}V_{i}
.
Since
V_{0}
is dense and the
V_{i}
are closed, we get
V=\bigcup_{i}V_{i}

so by irreducibility,
V=V_{i}
for some
i\leq n
.
This means that
f_{i}
vanishes on
V
, i. e.
f_{i}=1
a contradiction.

This proves the second assertion. The direct implication of the first assertion follows.

Conversely, assume that
V=V_{1}\cup\dots\cup V_{n}
is a union of proper subvarieties.
For each
i\leq n
, consider a word map
f_{i}\in L_{V}\setminus\{1\}
that vanishes on
V_{i}
.
Then for any point
v\in V
,
v
lies in
V_{i}
for some
i\leq n
and the evaluation homomorphism
h_{v}
vanishes on
f_{i}
.
This shows that the collection of evaluation homomorphisms is not discriminating. Since by Lemma
2.4
every
\Gamma
-morphism to
\Gamma
is an evaluation homomorphism, this proves the lemma.
∎

2.8
Direct products

Let
V_{1},V_{2}
be two non-empty algebraic varieties,
and let
V=V_{1}\times V_{2}
be the product variety.
We now describe the group of functions
L_{V}
in terms of
L_{V_{1}}
and
L_{V_{2}}
.

For
i\leq 2
, denote by
p_{i}:V\rightarrow V_{i}
the
i
-th projection.
Recall that the map
p_{i}^{*}:L_{V_{i}}\rightarrow L_{V}

sends a word map
f:V_{i}\rightarrow\Gamma
to
f\circ p_{i}
.
The two maps
p_{1}^{*},p_{2}^{*}
are injective, they agree on
\Gamma
,
and thus yield a
\Gamma
-homomorphism
\varphi:L_{V_{1}}*_{\Gamma}L_{V_{2}}\rightarrow L_{V}
.

Lemma 2.13
.

L_{V}
is the maximal residually
\Gamma
quotient of
L_{V_{1}}*_{\Gamma}L_{V_{2}}
.

More precisely, the map
L_{V_{1}}*_{\Gamma}L_{V_{2}}\rightarrow L_{V}
is surjective and its kernel is exactly
the intersection of all kernels of
\Gamma
-homomorphism
L_{V_{1}}*_{\Gamma}L_{V_{2}}\rightarrow\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma_{1}\subset\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\rangle
(resp.
\Sigma_{2}\subset\Gamma*\langle y_{1},\dots,y_{d^{\prime}}\rangle
)
be a system of equations defining
V_{1}
(resp.
V_{2}
).
By Lemma
2.4
,
L_{V_{1}}
is the maximal residually-
\Gamma
quotient of the group

L_{\Sigma_{1}}=\langle\Gamma,x_{1},\dots,x_{d}|\Sigma_{1}\rangle

(i. e. its quotient by the intersection
K_{1}
of all
\Gamma
-homomorphisms
L_{\Sigma_{1}}\to\Gamma
), and similarly for
L_{V_{2}}
.
Since
\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}\subset\Gamma*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{1},\dots,y_{d^{\prime}}\rangle

is a system of equations defining
V
,

L_{V}
is the maximal residually
\Gamma
quotient of

L_{\Sigma_{1}}*_{\Gamma}L_{\Sigma_{2}}=\langle\Gamma,x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{1},\dots,y_{d^{\prime}}|\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}\rangle.

Clearly
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
die in
L_{V}
so

L_{V}
is also the maximal residually
\Gamma
quotient of
L_{V_{1}}*_{\Gamma}L_{V_{2}}
.

∎

Remark 2.14
.

One can prove that if
V_{1}
and

.

.

The description given by the lemma is not very explicit.

3
\Gamma
-limit groups

3.1
Description of the JSJ decomposition of
\Gamma
-limit groups

In this section we follow the terminology of
[
GL17
]
.

Let
L
be a
\Gamma
-limit group.
Given two collections
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}}
of subgroups of
L
,
an
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}})
-tree is a tree with a minimal action of
L

such that each edge stabilizer lies in
{\mathcal{A}}
, and each group in
{\mathcal{H}}
is elliptic.
A subgroup
H\subset L
is
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}})
-universally elliptic if it is elliptic in every
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}})
-tree.
An
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}})
-JSJ decomposition is an
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}})
-tree whose edge groups are

({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}})
-universally ellipic, and maximal for domination (given two
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}})
-trees

T
,
T^{\prime}
, we say that
T
dominates
T^{\prime}
if vertex stabilizers of
T
are elliptic in
T^{\prime}
).

In what follows, we will take for
{\mathcal{A}}
the collection of abelian subgroups of
L
.
Denoting by
{\mathcal{A}}_{nc}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
the subcollection of all non-cyclic abelian groups,
we will take
{\mathcal{H}}=\{\Gamma\}\cup{\mathcal{A}}_{nc}
. Thus
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}})
-trees
are abelian splittings of
L
relative to
\Gamma
and to non-cyclic abelian groups.

If
L
is freely indecomposable relative to
\Gamma
,
it has a canonical JSJ tree over abelian groups relative to
\Gamma
and
to non-cyclic abelian groups.
Indeed
L
is torsion-free and CSA (because
\Gamma
is) thus Theorem 9.5 of
[
GL17
]
implies that the JSJ deformation space of
L
relative to
\Gamma
and to non-cyclic abelian subgroups exists.
Its tree of cylinders is itself a JSJ tree, and is invariant by
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L)
.
We denote by
\Lambda
the corresponding graph of groups.
We call the decomposition
L=\pi_{1}(\Lambda)
the
canonical JSJ decomposition
of
L
.
We will denote by
v_{\Gamma}
the image in
\Lambda
of the unique vertex
\tilde{v}_{\Gamma}
of the JSJ tree
fixed by
\Gamma
. We denote by
G_{v_{\Gamma}}=G_{\tilde{v}_{\Gamma}}
the corresponding vertex group (which contains
\Gamma
).

If we drop the assumption of free indecomposability,
we choose
L=L_{0}*\ldots*L_{r}*{\mathbb{F}}_{s}
a Grushko decomposition relative to
\Gamma

with
\Gamma\subset L_{0}
.

As mentioned above, we can consider the canonical JSJ-decomposition
L_{0}=\pi_{1}(\Lambda_{0})
relative to
\Gamma
and non-cyclic abelian groups.
For each
i\geq 1
,
L_{i}
has no preferred copy of
\Gamma
(it contains no conjugate of
\Gamma
).
There is an
{\mathrm{Aut}}(L_{i})
-invariant JSJ-decomposition
L_{i}=\pi_{1}(\Lambda_{i})
relative to non-cyclic abelian groups.

The following definition will be convenient.

Definition 3.1

(Standard JSJ decomposition)
.

Gluing a wedge of
s
edges with trivial edge group on
v_{\Gamma}
, and connecting
v_{\Gamma}
to each graph of groups
\Lambda_{i}
(
i>0
) using edges with trivial edge group (see Figure
1
,
one obtains a splitting of
L
.
We call such a splitting a
standard JSJ decomposition
of
L
.

Figure 1:
A typical standard JSJ decomposition. Blue edges have trivial edge group, black edges have non-trivial abelian edge group.

A standard JSJ decomposition of
L
is indeed a JSJ decomposition of
L
over abelian groups relative to
\Gamma
and to non-cyclic abelian groups (but it is not invariant under

{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L)
in general).

Trees of cylinders can be characterized in the following way:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G=\pi_{1}(\Lambda)
be an abelian splitting of a CSA group
G
without trivial edge groups.

The tree corresponding to
\Lambda
is its own tree of cylinders if and only if
it is bipartite between vertices with abelian and non-abelian stabilizer,
and for every vertex
v
with non-abelian stabilizer,

•

for every edge
e
incident on
v
, the group
G_{e}
is maximal abelian in
G_{v}
;

•

for every pair of edges
e\neq e^{\prime}
incident on
v
,

G_{e}
is not
G_{v}
-conjugate to
G_{e^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

In this context, cylinders have maximal abelian stabilizers, thus it is easy to see that a tree of cylinder will satisfy the conclusions of the lemma.

For the other direction, note that the given conditions imply that cylinders are exactly the set of edges adjacent to a common abelian vertex. Applying the tree of cylinders construction thus yields an isomorphic tree.
∎

We gather some properties of the standard JSJ decomposition which we will use.

A vertex
v
in an abelian splitting
L=\pi_{1}(\Lambda)
is
rigid
if
G_{v}
is elliptic in every abelian splitting of
L
relative to
\Gamma
and to non-cyclic abelian groups. It is

otherwise.

The vertex

is

if its vertex group
G_{v}
is abelian.
It is
of surface type
(see QH with trivial fiber in
[

GL17
]
) if

can be written as the fundamental group of a surface

so that each incident edge group
is conjugate to a subgroup of the fundamental group of a boundary component of
\Sigma

(orbifolds are allowed in
[
GL17
]
, but they don’t occur here because the groups we consider are torsion-free).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
L
be a
\Gamma
-group which is also a
\Gamma
-limit group.
A standard JSJ decomposition
\Lambda
for

satisfies the following

1.

non-abelian flexible vertex groups are of surface type;

2.

if

is
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}})
-universally elliptic
and is contained in a surface type vertex group of

, it is contained in a boundary component;

3.

the vertex group containing
\Gamma
is rigid;

4.

any edge

with nontrivial

joins a vertex with abelian group to a vertex with non abelian group;

5.

for any non-abelian vertex

, and incident edge

, if

is nontrivial it is maximal abelian (and thus malnormal) in

;

6.

for any non-abelian vertex

, and every pair of edges

is not
G_{v}
-conjugate to

.

Proof.

Item
1

is given by Theorem 9.5 of
[
GL17
]
.
Item
2
appears as point (3) of Theorem 9.4 of
[
GL17
]
, and implies
item

by considering the case where

(recall that we assume that

is a non-elementary hyperbolic group).
Items
4
,

and

.
∎

]

.

Let

4
Basic facts about algebraic groups

4.1
Algebraic groups over
\Gamma
.

Note that if
V\subset\Gamma^{d}
is a variety, then
V\times V
is a variety in
\Gamma^{2d}
.

Definition 4.1
.

An algebraic group
(V,\mu)
over
\Gamma
is an algebraic variety
V

together with a group law
\mu:V\times V\rightarrow V
which is an algebraic map.

Note that we could have required in addition that the inverse map
V\rightarrow V
, defined by
g\mapsto g^{-1}
, be an algebraic map. In fact, when
\Gamma
is torsion-free hyperbolic and
V
is irreducible, our classification result shows that the inverse map is automatically algebraic.

We denote the group law on
V
by
\circledast
, the inverse of an element
v\in V
by
\check{v}
, and the identity element by
e
. For any
v\in V
, left and right multiplication by
v
give algebraic automorphisms
\lambda_{v}:V\rightarrow V,x\mapsto v\circledast x
and
\rho_{v}:V\rightarrow V,x\mapsto x\circledast v
.

For any
v,w\in V
we have by associativity
\lambda_{v}\circ\lambda_{w}=\lambda_{v\circledast w}
but
\rho_{v}\circ\rho_{w}=\rho_{w\circledast v}
.
We also note that

\rho_{\check{v}}=\rho_{v}^{-1}
and

\lambda_{\check{v}}=\lambda^{-1}_{v}
.

This induces automorphisms

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\rho_{v}^{*}:L_{V}&\rightarrow&L_{V}\\
f&\mapsto&f\circ\rho_{v}\end{array}\right.\text{\quad and \quad}\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{v}^{*}:L_{V}&\rightarrow&L_{V}\\
f&\mapsto&f\circ\lambda_{v}\end{array}\right..

and therefore gives two representations of the algebraic group
V
into
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}V&\rightarrow&{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})\\
v&\mapsto&\rho^{*}_{v}\end{array}\right.\text{\quad and \quad}\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}V&\rightarrow&{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})\\
v&\mapsto&(\lambda^{*}_{v})^{-1}\end{array}\right.

4.2
The group law formula

Let
V
be an algebraic group. Any point
v\in V
corresponds to a
\Gamma
-homomorphism
h_{v}\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
.
We wish to understand the group law when points of
V
are interpreted as elements of
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
.
We denote by
e
the identity element of
V
and by
h_{e}\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
the corresponding
\Gamma
-homomorphism.

Lemma 4.2
.

For all
v,v^{\prime}\in V
, one has

h_{v\circledast v^{\prime}}=h_{v}\circ\rho_{v^{\prime}}^{*}.

In particular, for all
v\in V
, one has
h_{v}=h_{e}\circ\rho_{v}^{*}
.

Proof.

For any
f\in L_{V}
(viewed as a function on
V
),

h_{v}\circ\rho_{v^{\prime}}^{*}(f)=h_{v}(f\circ\rho_{v^{\prime}})=f\circ\rho_{v^{\prime}}(v)=f(v\circledast v^{\prime})=h_{v\circledast v^{\prime}}(f)
.
∎

As an immediate consequence of the two equalities in Lemma
4.2

we get

Corollary 4.3
.

For all
v,v^{\prime}\in V
we have

h_{v\circledast v^{\prime}}=h_{v}\circ\rho_{v^{\prime}}^{*}=h_{e}\circ\rho_{v}^{*}\circ\rho_{v^{\prime}}^{*}

The following is a straightforward but useful consequence of the transitivity of the action of
G
on itself
by right multiplication.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
V
is an algebraic group, then for all
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
, there exists
v\in V
such that

h^{\prime}=h\circ\rho_{v}^{*}
.

In particular, if
V
is a connected algebraic group, then for any
h\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
the family of
\Gamma
-homomorphisms
\{h\circ\rho_{v}^{*}\mid v\in V\}
is discriminating.

Proof.

Using the correspondence between
V
and
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
, the transitivity of the right action of
V
on itself
translates into the first assertion.

The second part follows from the fact that the family of all morphisms in
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
is discriminating
because
L_{V}
is a
\Gamma
-limit group.
∎

4.3
Connected components of an algebraic group.

Let
(V,\mu)
be an algebraic group over
\Gamma
.
The following easy remarks are completely standard.

Let
V=V_{1}\cup\ldots\cup V_{s}
be the unique (minimal) decomposition of
V
as a finite union of irreducible varieties.
Then the irreducible components are disjoint. Indeed, as a consequence of homogeneity all the points in
V
are contained in the same number
k
of irreducible components
V_{j}
. If
k>1
we have
V_{1}=(V_{1}\cap V_{2})\cup\ldots\cup(V_{1}\cap V_{s})
, which contradicts the irreducibility of
V_{1}
.

It follows that the decomposition of
V
into irreducible components is also its decomposition into connected components
for the Zariski topology.

The connected component containing
e
is a finite index algebraic subgroup of
V
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
V
be a connected algebraic group, and let
V_{0}\subset V
be a finite index (not necessarily algebraic) subgroup of
V
.
Then
V_{0}
is dense in
V
.

Proof.

If
W
is a subvariety of
V
containing
V_{0}
,
then finitely many translates of
W
cover
V
. Since
V
is irreducible,
W=V
.
∎

5
Homogeneous varieties

Algebraic groups are in particular homogeneous varieties.
In this section, we collect a few consequences of homogeneity.

Definition 5.1
.

A variety
V
is
homogeneous
if the group of algebraic automorphisms
V\rightarrow V

acts transitively on
V
.

Question 5.2
.

What can we say about homogeneous varieties in general?
Can we classify them ?

5.1
Homogeneity

The following is the reformulation of the definition of homogeneity using the correspondence
V\leftrightarrow{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
.

Lemma 5.3
.

V
is homogeneous if and only if
for all
h,h^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{Hom}}(L_{V},\Gamma)
, there exists
\alpha\in{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
with
h\circ\alpha=h^{\prime}
.∎

We saw in Lemma
2.12
that
V
is irreducible as an algebraic variety if and only if
the family
{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
is discriminating. Together with the previous lemma this gives

Corollary 5.4
.

If
V
is a homogeneous irreducible variety, for every
h\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
, the family

\{h\circ\alpha\mid\alpha\in{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})\}
is discriminating.∎

Note that Lemma
4.4
gives a more precise version of this result in the case where
V
is an algebraic group.

The following lemma says in particular that Corollary
5.4
still holds if one replaces

{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
by a finite index subgroup.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
h\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
and let
M
be a subgroup of
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
.
Assume that
\{h\circ\alpha\mid\alpha\in M\}
is a discriminating family.

Then for any finite index subgroup
M_{0}
of
M
, the subfamily

\{h\circ\alpha\mid\alpha\in M_{0}\}
is still discriminating.

Proof.

Write
M=M_{0}\varphi_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup M_{0}\varphi_{n}
for some
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\in M
.
Fix a finite set
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\}\subseteq L_{V}
.
Since
\{h\circ\alpha|\alpha\in M\}
is discriminating,
there exists
\alpha\in M
such that
h\circ\alpha
does not kill any
\varphi^{-1}_{i}(g_{j})
.
Let
k
be such that
\alpha=\alpha_{0}\varphi_{k}
for some
\alpha_{0}\in M_{0}
.
Then
h\circ\alpha_{0}
does not kill any
g_{j}
.
∎

5.2
An example of homogeneous variety

Let
\Gamma
be a torsion-free hyperbolic group and let
c\in\Gamma\setminus\{1\}
be such that

\langle c\rangle
is a maximal cyclic group of
\Gamma
and
such that any endomorphism of
\Gamma
fixing
c
is an automorphism (such a
c
is known as a
test element
in
\Gamma
).
Then the double
L_{V}=\Gamma*_{c}\bar{\Gamma}
is a
\Gamma
-limit group,
and it is the function group of a homogeneous variety: for any
h\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
we have that
h_{|\bar{\Gamma}}

sends
\bar{c}
to
c
, so it must be an isomorphism.
Therefore
h=r\circ\sigma
where
r
is the canonical retraction and
\sigma\in{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
.

For instance, one can take for
\Gamma
the free group
\Gamma={\mathbb{F}}_{2g}=\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{2g}\rangle
, and let
c
be the product of commutators
[a_{1},a_{2}]\dots[a_{2g-1},a_{2g}]
.
In this case, the double
L_{V}
is the fundamental group of an orientable surface of genus
2g
, and
\Gamma\subset L_{V}
is the fundamental group of a subsurface of genus
g
with one boundary component.
The variety
V
itself is the subset of
\Gamma^{2g}
defined by the equation

V=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{2g})|[x_{1},x_{2}]\dots[x_{2g-1},x_{2g}]=[a_{1},a_{2}]\dots[a_{2g-1},a_{2g}]\}.

The automorphism group
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
is isomorphic to the mapping class
group of the surface of genus
g
with one boundary component.
This group acts freely on
V=\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
because
\Gamma
-homomorphisms
L_{V}\rightarrow\Gamma
are injective on
\bar{\Gamma}
.
In particular, points of
V
are in bijection with elements of the mapping class group.

There are very many other test elements in a free group
[
ST17
]
.
In particular, there exist test elements
c
for which
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
is
a rigid vertex of the amalgam
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}*_{c}\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
(take a test element
c
whose cyclic reduction contains all subwords of length 3 and apply
[
CM15
]
).
In this case,
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
still acts freely and transitively
on
V
, but it is virtually cyclic.

5.3
Grushko decomposition for a homogeneous variety

Proposition 5.6
.

If
V
is homogeneous and irreducible,
the Grushko decomposition of
L_{V}
relative to
\Gamma
has the form
L_{V}=L^{0}_{V}*{\mathbb{F}}_{r}
with
\Gamma\subset L^{0}_{V}
,
and
L^{0}_{V}
is the group of functions of a homogeneous irreducible variety.

Proof.

Let
L_{V}=L^{0}_{V}*G_{1}*\dots*G_{n}*{\mathbb{F}}_{r}
be the Grushko decomposition of
L_{V}
relative to
\Gamma
, with
\Gamma\subset L^{0}_{V}
, and
note that
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
permutes the conjugacy classes of the factors
G_{1},\dots,G_{n}
.
Choose any
\Gamma
-homomorphism
h_{0}\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L^{0}_{V},\Gamma)
, and extend it to a
\Gamma
-homomorphism
h\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
that kills
G_{1},\dots,G_{n}
.
Since
V
is homogeneous, the set of
\Gamma
-homomorphisms
\{h\circ\alpha\mid\alpha\in{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})\}
is discriminating
by Corollary
5.4
.
But all of them kill
G_{1}
,…,
G_{n}
.
This prevents this family from being discriminating unless
n=0
.

As a subgroup of
L_{V}
,
L_{V}^{0}
is fully residually
\Gamma
. Thus by Lemma
2.12
, the variety
associated to
L_{V}^{0}
is irreducible.
To prove that this variety is homogeneous,
it is enough by Lemma
5.3

to check that
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V}^{0})
acts transitively on
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L^{0}_{V},\Gamma)
.
Given
h_{0},h^{\prime}_{0}\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L^{0}_{V},\Gamma)
, extend them to
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
by killing the factor
{\mathbb{F}}_{r}
.
By homogeneity of
V
, there exists
\alpha\in{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
such that
h^{\prime}=h\circ\alpha
.
Since
\alpha
preserves
L^{0}_{V}
, we get that
h^{\prime}_{0}=h_{0}\circ\alpha_{|L_{V}^{0}}
.
∎

5.4
JSJ decomposition for a homogeneous variety

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
V
be an irreducible variety
and let
\Lambda
be a standard JSJ decomposition
of its group of functions
L_{V}
.

If
V
is homogeneous, then all the edge groups of
\Lambda
are cyclic
and every
\Gamma
-homomorphism
h\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
is injective
in restriction to all edge groups and rigid groups of
\Lambda
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.6
, it is enough to show the result in the case where
L_{V}
is one ended.

Let
{\mathrm{Aut}}^{0}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
be the subgroup of finite index of
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})

consisting of automorphisms that act as the identity on the graph
\Lambda

(recalling that the canonical JSJ tree is invariant under automorphisms).

Let
h\in{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
. By Corollary
5.4
and Lemma
5.5
,
the family
\{h\circ\alpha\mid\alpha\in{\mathrm{Aut}}^{0}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})\}
is discriminating.
Thus, by Remark
2.10
,
there exists a sequence of automorphisms
\alpha_{i}\in{\mathrm{Aut}}^{0}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
such that
h\circ\alpha_{i}
is a discriminating sequence.

Let
R\subset L_{V}
be a rigid vertex group of
\Lambda
, and
E_{1},\dots,E_{p}\subset R
the collection of incident edge groups.
Since each
E_{k}
is abelian,
h\circ\alpha_{i}(E_{k})
is cyclic.
Up to removing finitely many terms in the sequence we may assume that
h\circ\alpha_{i}(E_{k})
is non-trivial for all
i,k
.
Since for all
i
,
\alpha_{i}(E_{k})
is conjugate to
E_{k}
in
L_{V}
,
there exist maximal cyclic groups
\langle u_{k}\rangle\subset\Gamma
such that for all
i
,

h\circ\alpha_{i}(E_{k})
is conjugate in
\langle u_{k}\rangle
.

We now view
\Gamma
as hyperbolic relative to
\langle u_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle u_{k}\rangle
,
and we look at
an action of
\Gamma
on a space with a corresponding collection of
disjoint horoballs as in
[
Bow12
]
.
Assume first that the homomorphisms
h\circ\alpha_{i}
are pairwise distinct modulo conjugation in
\Gamma
.
Since
R
is non-abelian,
h\circ\alpha_{i}(R)
is non-parabolic for
i
large enough.
By
[
BS08
, Theorem 1.2]
(whose proof also works for a discriminating sequence of homomorphisms),
we get a non-trivial action of
R

on an
{\mathbb{R}}
-tree relative to
E_{1},\dots,E_{p}

with abelian arc stabilizers.
Since
abelian subgroups of
L_{V}
are finitely generated,
the hypotheses of
[
Gui08
, Theorem 5.1, Corollary 5.2]
are satisfied, and this action yields an abelian splitting of
R
relative to
E_{1},\dots,E_{p}
.
Taking a tree of cylinders for commutation as in
[
GL11
, Proposition 6.3]
,
one gets a non-trivial abelian splitting of
R
relative to
E_{1},\dots,E_{p}
and to all non-cyclic abelian subgroups of
R
,
contradicting its
rigidity.

Thus, up to conjugating and taking a subsequence, we
may assume that
(h\circ\alpha_{i})_{|R}
does not depend on
i
.
Since it is discriminating, this implies that
(h\circ\alpha_{i})_{|R}
is injective, hence so is
h_{|R}
.

This shows that
h
is injective on rigid vertex groups of
\Lambda
.
It follows that every edge group
E
of
\Lambda
is cyclic because
every edge of
\Lambda
is adjacent to a rigid or surface vertex.
Since
h\circ\alpha_{i}
is discriminating and
\Gamma
is torsion-free,

h\circ\alpha_{i}
is injective on
E
for
i
large enough.
Since
\alpha_{i}
preserves the conjugacy class of
E
, this shows that

h
is injective on
E
.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
V
be a homogeneous irreducible variety over
\Gamma
.
Then a standard
JSJ decomposition
\Lambda
of
L_{V}
relative to
\Gamma
satisfies the following:

1.

in each abelian vertex group, the subgroup generated by
incident edge groups is cyclic;

2.

G_{v_{\Gamma}}=\Gamma
;

3.

for every non-trivial edge group
G_{e}
incident on
v_{\Gamma}
,

G_{e}
is maximal cyclic in
L_{V}

4.

If
e,e^{\prime}
are distinct edges incident on
v_{\Gamma}
, then
G_{e}
does not commute with any
L_{V}
-conjugate of
G_{e^{\prime}}
.
In particular, for every abelian vertex
v
adjacent to
v_{\Gamma}
, there is at most one edge joining
v
to
v_{\Gamma}
.

Proof.

Using Proposition
5.6
, we may assume without loss
of generality that
L_{V}
is freely indecomposable relative to
\Gamma
,
and that
L_{V}=\pi_{1}(\Lambda)
is the canonical JSJ decomposition of
L_{V}
.

Let
{\mathrm{Aut}}^{0}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
be the subgroup of
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})

consisting of automorphisms that act as the identity on the graph
\Lambda
and whose restriction to each edge group is a conjugation. The edge groups of
\Lambda
are cyclic by Proposition
5.7
so this is a finite index subgroup of
{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
.

Let
h\in{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
. By Corollary
5.4
and Lemma
5.5
,
there exists a sequence of automorphisms
\alpha_{i}\in{\mathrm{Aut}}^{0}_{\Gamma}(L_{V})
such that
h\circ\alpha_{i}
is a discriminating sequence.

To prove (
1
),
let
A
be an abelian vertex group of
\Lambda
, and
B\subset A
be the subgroup generated by incident edge groups.
Each
\alpha_{i}
preserves the conjugacy class of
A
and acts by conjugation on each incident edge group. Using CSA property, it easily follows that
\alpha_{i}
acts by conjugation on
B
. Thus,

\ker(h\circ\alpha_{i})_{|B}=\ker h_{|B}
and does not depend on
i
.
Since this is a discriminating sequence,
h_{|B}
is injective. Since
\Gamma
is hyperbolic, it
follows that
B
is cyclic.

For (
2
), recall that
G_{v_{\Gamma}}
denotes the vertex group that contains
\Gamma
, and
consider any
h\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
.
The vertex group
G_{v_{\Gamma}}
is rigid by Proposition
3.3
(
3
).
By Proposition
5.7
,
h
is injective on
G_{v_{\Gamma}}\supset\Gamma
.
On the other hand, being a
\Gamma
-morphism,
h
is the identity on
\Gamma
, so
G_{v_{\Gamma}}=\Gamma
.

To prove (
3
), consider an edge
e
incident on
v_{\Gamma}
.
By Proposition
5.7
,
G_{e}
is infinite cyclic.
By Lemma
3.3
(
5
),

G_{e}
is maximal abelian hence maximal cyclic in
G_{v_{\Gamma}}=\Gamma
.
Assuming that
G_{e}
is not maximal cyclic in
L_{V}
,
consider
c\in L_{V}
and
k>1
such that
G_{e}=\langle c^{k}\rangle
.
Choose some
h\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
(there exists one because
L_{V}
is a
\Gamma
-limit group),
and view it as a retraction
L_{V}\rightarrow\Gamma
.
Since
c^{k}\in\Gamma
, we have
c^{k}=h(c^{k})=h(c)^{k}

contradicting that that
G_{e}
is maximal cyclic in
\Gamma
.

To prove (
4
), consider two distinct edges
e,e^{\prime}

incident on
v_{\Gamma}
whose edge groups have commuting conjugates in
L_{V}
.
View
G_{e}
and
G_{e^{\prime}}
as subgroups of
\Gamma
.
Using a retraction
L_{V}\rightarrow\Gamma
as above, we see that

G_{e}
and
G_{e^{\prime}}
have commuting conjugates in
\Gamma
.
By Lemma
3.3
(
6
),
this implies
e=e^{\prime}
.
∎

6
Extension of centralizers and algebraic subgroups

The goal of this section is Theorem
6.8
showing that the JSJ decomposition associated to a connected algebraic group
V

has no rigid vertex except for
v_{\Gamma}
.
We will first associate to
V
an algebraic subgroup
W_{0}\subset V

whose associated limit group is obtained by replacing some abelian vertex groups by the cyclic subgroup generated
by incident edge groups. This will be used to slightly simplify some arguments including the description of
L_{V\times V}

6.1
An algebraic subgroup associated to an extension of centralizers

Let
V
be a connected algebraic group. Let
\Lambda
be a standard JSJ decomposition of
L_{V}
(we don’t assume that
L_{V}
is one-ended).

Denote by

.
Using Lemma

Let

We will apply the following lemma to the

Lemma 6.1
.

Let

Let

For each

We now define the variety and limit group obtained by trimming a collection of abelian groups in

7
Bounded stretch and application to vertices of surface type

7.1
Length and stretch

Let
L
be a
\Gamma
-group with a finite
\Gamma
-generating set
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, i. e. such that
L=\langle\Gamma,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\rangle
.

Definition 7.1
.

Given
f\in L
, we define the length
\ell(f)
of
f
to be the minimal length of a word on the alphabet
\{X^{\pm 1}_{1},\ldots,X^{\pm 1}_{n}\}\cup(\Gamma\setminus\{1\})
representing
f
.

Remark 7.2
.

The length of
f
should be thought of as an analogue of the degree of a polynomial
(although the length of
aX_{1}X_{2}bX_{2}^{-2}
is 6 while its degree should rather be 4).

Definition 7.3
.

Let
\alpha\in{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L)
. We define the
stretch
of
\alpha

(with respect to the
\Gamma
-generating set
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
of
L
)
as

{\mathrm{Lip}}(\alpha)=\sup_{f\in L\setminus\{1\}}\frac{\ell(\alpha(f))}{\ell(f)}

=\max\{1,\ell(\alpha(X_{1})),\dots,\ell(\alpha(X_{n}))\}

Remark 7.4
.

The equivalence between the two definitions follows from the fact
that
\Gamma\cup\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
is a generating set of
L
,
so the stretch of
\alpha
is bounded by the length of their images.

The following lemma is a key observation.
It generalizes the classical fact of algebraic geometry saying that
if
{\mathbb{G}}\curvearrowright X
is an algebraic action of an algebraic group on an algebraic variety
X
,
for every function
f
on
X
, the
{\mathbb{G}}
-orbit of
f
spans a finite dimensional vector space
(
[
Bri09
]
).
If
X
is the affine space, this means that the
{\mathbb{G}}
-orbit of a polynomial
f
has bounded degree.

We say that an algebraic group
G
acts algebraically on a variety
X
if the
map
G\times X\rightarrow X
is an algebraic map.
Given
g\in G
, we denote by
\tau_{g}:x\in X\mapsto g.x\in X
,
and by
\tau_{g}^{*}\in{\mathrm{Aut}}_{\Gamma}(L_{X})
the automorphism
sending
f\in L_{X}
to
f\circ\tau_{g}:z\mapsto f(g.x)
.

Lemma 7.5

(Bounded stretch)
.

Let
G
be an algebraic group acting algebraically on an algebraic variety
X
.

Then
there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
g\in G
,
{\mathrm{Lip}}(\tau_{g}^{*})\leq C
.

Equivalently, for every function
f\in L_{X}
, and every
g\in G
,
\ell(f\circ\tau_{g})\leq C\ell(f)
.

Proof.

View
G
(resp
X
) as a subset of
\Gamma^{n}
(resp
\Gamma^{p}
),
and denote by
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}:G\rightarrow\Gamma
(resp
X_{1},\dots,X_{p}:X\rightarrow\Gamma
)
the coordinate functions – sending
(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in G
to
z_{i}
.
The action map
\alpha:G\times X\rightarrow X
can be written as
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{p})

where each
\alpha_{i}
is a word on
n+p
variables
\alpha_{i}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n},x_{1},\dots,x_{p})
.

Given
g\in G
, the map
\tau_{g}:X\rightarrow X
is defined by
\tau_{g}(.)=\alpha(g,.)
.
Thus,
\tau_{g}^{*}(X_{i})=X_{i}\circ\tau_{g}=X_{i}(\alpha(g,.))=\alpha_{i}(g,.)=\alpha_{i}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n},X_{1},\dots,X_{p})\in L_{X}
.
Thus
\ell(\tau_{g}^{*}(X_{i}))
is bounded by the length of
\alpha_{i}
as a word in
n+p
variables.
This bound does not depend on
g
, which proves the lemma.
∎

Given an element
g
acting isometrically on a metric space
Y
, we denote by

||g||_{Y}=\inf_{y\in Y}d(y,gy)
its translation length.
By the triangle inequality, we obtain:

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
G
be an algebraic group acting algebraically on an algebraic variety
X
. Consider an action of
L_{X}
by isometries on a metric space

, such that
\Gamma
has bounded orbits.

Then there exists a constant

such that
for all

and

,
||\tau_{g}^{*}(f)||_{Y}\leq K\ell(f)
.

Proof.

Endow

with the word metric
\ell
on the infinite generating set
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{p}\}\cup\Gamma\setminus\{1\}
.
Fix a basepoint
*\in Y

given by

is

-Lipschitz
where

and on the distances

.
Thus, using Lemma

, for all

and

∎

The goal of this section is the following result.

Otherwise, since

Consider closed curves

Let

\Gamma

9
Classification of the multiplication law

Theorem
8.1
gives a full description of the underlying variety of an algebraic group
(V,\mu)
over
\Gamma
. The aim of this section is to complete the classification of algebraic groups
by classifying the possible multiplication laws on
V
.
Our goal is to find a system of coordinates
in which
\mu
is the standard multiplication law.

Starting with
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\in L_{V}
such that
L_{V}=\langle\Gamma,T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle
,
we get an embedding
V=\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)
into
\Gamma^{n}
via
h\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma}(L_{V},\Gamma)\mapsto(h(T_{1}),\dots,h(T_{n}))
.
This way,
T_{i}
becomes the
i
-th coordinate function on
V
, and we call
(T_{1},\dots,T_{n})

a system of coordinates on
V
.

Such a system induces a system of coordinates
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
for
V\times V
as follows.
Denoting by
p_{1},p_{2}:V\times V\rightarrow V
the two projections,
we define
X_{i}=T_{i}\circ p_{1}
and
Y_{i}=T_{i}\circ p_{2}
.
Equivalently,
X_{i}=p_{1}^{*}(T_{i})
and
Y_{i}=p_{2}^{*}(T_{i})
.
Viewing
L_{V\times V}
as a quotient of
L_{V}*_{\Gamma}L_{V}
,

X_{i}
(resp.
Y_{i}
)
is the image of
T_{i}
under the left (resp. right) embedding of
L_{V}
into
L_{V\times V}
.

With these coordinates, if we write the multiplication law

\mu:V\times V\rightarrow V

by

\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{bmatrix}\circledast\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
y_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1}(\underline{x},\underline{y})\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{n}(\underline{x},\underline{y})\end{bmatrix},

then the comultiplication law
\mu^{*}:L_{V}\rightarrow L_{V\times V}
is defined on the generating set
by

\mu^{*}:\left\{\begin{matrix}T_{1}\mapsto\mu^{*}(T_{1})=\mu_{1}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})\\
\vdots\\
T_{n}\mapsto\mu^{*}(T_{n})=\mu_{n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})\end{matrix}\right.

Our goal is to find coordinates
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\in L_{V}
in which
\mu
is the standard multiplication law
i.e.,
\mu^{*}(T_{i})=X_{i}Y_{i}
for all
i\leq n
.

Remark 9.1
.

If we change the coordinate system
(T_{1},\dots,T_{n})
on
V
, we need to change the coordinate system on
V^{2}
accordingly.
For instance, if we change
T_{i}
to
T^{\prime}_{i}=T_{i}^{-1}
,
then
X_{i}
changes to
X^{\prime}_{i}=p_{1}^{*}(T^{\prime}_{i})=X_{i}^{-1}
and similarly,

Y_{i}
changes to
Y^{\prime}_{i}=Y_{i}^{-1}
.
If we change
T_{i}
to
T^{\prime}_{i}=uT_{i}v
for some
u,v\in L_{V}
,
then
X_{i}
changes to
X^{\prime}_{i}=u^{(X)}X_{i}v^{(X)}

where
u^{(X)}=p_{1}^{*}(u)\in L_{V^{2}}
and
v^{(X)}=p_{1}^{*}(v)
are the copies of
u
and
v

under the left embedding of
L_{V}
into
L_{V^{2}}
; there are similar changes for

.

Just as for

, the system of coordinates
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
on

also induces a system of coordinates on

(in this order)
as the image of
T_{i}
under the left, middle, and right embedding of
L_{V}

in
L_{V^{3}}
respectively.
This corresponds to coordinates associated to the embedding

V\times V\times V\subset\Gamma^{n}\times\Gamma^{n}\times\Gamma^{n}

Let
(\underline{x},\underline{z},\underline{y})
be a point of

.
Associativity then reads

(\underline{x}\circledast\underline{z})\circledast\underline{y}=\underline{x}\circledast(\underline{z}\circledast\underline{y})\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\mu(\mu(\underline{x},\underline{z}),\underline{y})=\mu(\underline{x},\mu(\underline{z},\underline{y}))

where

At the level of function groups, the maps induced by

9.2
Multiplication law on the abelian part

Recall that we defined a coordinate system

Proposition 9.3

With the bases chosen above, for all
j\leq l
,

Proof.

Fix

Since

commutes with

commutes with

and

Denote by

the maps defined by

and
v\mapsto(v,e)
.
At the level of function groups,

is induced by the map

which is

on the left factor
and the identity on the right factor (sending

and

For

Theorem 9.4

Let
\Gamma

Proof of Proposition
