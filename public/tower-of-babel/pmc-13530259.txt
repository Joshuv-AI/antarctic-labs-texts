MOO-IDS: multi-objective optimization-based lightweight intrusion detection system for in-vehicle networks
Authors: Yali Hao, He Bai, A Siya, Jiaqi Zhang, Xueliang Fu, Honghui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the proliferation of intelligent connected vehicles, the Controller Area Network (CAN) bus, as the backbone of in-vehicle communication, is vulnerable to cyberattacks due to lack of authentication and encryption. Existing Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) exhibit limitations in addressing data imbalance, complex attack recognition, and resource-constrained deployment. This paper proposes a lightweight intrusion detection system based on multi-objective optimization, termed MOO-IDS. The system integrates a Hybrid Deep Restricted Boltzmann Machine Generator (HyDRBM-Gen) to mitigate attack sample scarcity and class imbalance in vehicular network data. To address targeted ID fabrication and masquerade attacks, two novel features, ID Frequency (IDF) and ID-Data Correlation (IDC), are introduced. An Adaptive-Enhanced Non-Dominated Sorting Dung Beetle Optimizer (AE-NSDBO) optimizes XGBoost hyperparameters to achieve an optimal balance among F1-score, model size, and inference latency, enabling the optimized XGBoost to accomplish intrusion detection. Experimental results on the real-world dataset Real ORNL Automotive Dynamometer (ROAD) demonstrate that MOO-IDS achieves outstanding performance, with a weighted-average F1-score of 0.9907, a model size of only 0.199 MB, and substantially reduced inference latency. The model also performs excellently on the Car Hacking and Survival Analysis datasets. This study provides a high-precision, lightweight, and strongly generalizable multi-class intrusion detection model for resource-constrained vehicular environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-68795-x.Keywords: In-vehicle network, Intrusion detection system, Controller area network, Multi-objective optimization, Machine learningSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With the rapid evolution of intelligent connected vehicles and autonomous driving technologies, modern vehicles have developed into highly integrated and complex Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) comprising numerous Electronic Control Units (ECUs) and millions of lines of software code1. The Controller Area Network (CAN) bus, leveraging its advantages such as low cost and high reliability, has become the dominant in-vehicle communication protocol, covering safety-critical subsystems such as powertrain and chassis control2. However, the CAN bus lacks authentication and encryption by design. Its broadcast-based architecture, where messages carry no explicit receiver address and sender identification is limited to the CAN ID, prevents receiving nodes from verifying message legitimacy3.

To address the inherent security vulnerabilities of the CAN bus, intrusion detection systems (IDS) have become the mainstream approach for in-vehicle cybersecurity, as they require no modifications to existing bus protocols, offer flexible deployment, and suit resource-constrained environments4. Recently, data-driven IDS have gradually replaced rule-based methods, offering better adaptability through autonomous learning5. However, in-vehicle environments impose stringent constraints on model lightweightness and real-time performance. Existing data-driven solutions still face notable limitations6: on real-world datasets like ROAD, detection accuracy remains insufficient when confronting complex and highly concealed attacks; meanwhile, some deep learning models, despite powerful feature extraction, suffer from large parameter sizes and high inference latency, hindering deployment on resource-constrained ECUs. Indeed, the trade-off between detection accuracy and resource overhead is not unique to vehicular networks. In resource-constrained dynamic network scenarios such as UAV communications7 and aerial-ground integrated ad hoc networks8, researchers commonly face the challenge of multi-objective joint optimization, where effectively balancing accuracy loss against efficiency gains remains the core issue9. To this end, this paper introduces the concept of multi-objective optimization into the field of vehicular intrusion detection, seeking a superior balance between detection performance and deployment feasibility.

To address the limitations of existing in-vehicle IDS, traditional machine learning models offer advantages in simplicity, low computational overhead, and interpretability, making them more suitable for resource-constrained embedded platforms. Therefore, this paper proposes a lightweight multi-objective optimization IDS based on machine learning, termed MOO-IDS. By synthesizing samples to balance data distribution and enhancing features to strengthen attack representation, this solution synergistically improves detection performance and deployment feasibility. Background and related work

This section introduces the working principles and security vulnerabilities of the CAN protocol, analyzes typical attack types targeting this bus, and reviews existing research on intrusion detection systems.

Fundamentals of the CAN protocol

Developed by Bosch in the 1980s10, the CAN protocol employs a message-based broadcast communication mechanism where all ECUs are connected in parallel to a shared bus, forming a multi-master network structure. Messages are identified by a unique CAN ID, which defines message content and determines bus arbitration priority, with lower values indicating higher priority.

The structure of a standard CAN data frame is shown in Fig. 1 It consists of a Start of Frame (SOF) bit, an arbitration field, a control field, a CRC field, an ACK field, and an End of Frame (EOF) field. The identifier (ID) in the arbitration field occupies 11 bits in the standard format and extendable to 29 bits in the extended format. The data field carries a payload of 0 to 8 bytes.

Typical attack types targeting the CAN bus

Due to the inherent security flaws of the CAN bus protocol, attackers with access to the bus can launch various attacks. The five most common attack types are as follows:

Denial-of-Service (DoS) Attack: The attacker continuously transmits high-priority messages (e.g., 0 × 000) to dominate the bus11, disrupting vehicle functions.

Fuzzy Attack: Exploiting the lack of authentication on the CAN bus12, the attacker injects messages with random IDs and payloads at high frequency, causing unpredictable system faults.

Related work

IDS have emerged as a critical defense line in automotive cybersecurity research, evolving from reliance on prior knowledge to data-driven modeling. Early rule-based and statistical feature detection methods primarily identify anomalies through predefined rules or thresholds. In terms of physical feature utilization, intrinsic ECU characteristics such as voltage fingerprinting14 and clock skew15 are employed to detect masquerade and bus-off attacks. For statistical feature analysis, information entropy16, Hamming distance17, and anomaly scores derived from dynamic data entropy with multi-dimensional fusion18 are applied to detect various injection and fuzzy attack with relatively low computational complexity. However, these methods heavily depend on manual feature selection and parameter calibration, exhibiting poor generalization and insufficient adaptability to sophisticated stealthy attacks.

Traditional machine learning, integrating feature engineering with classical classification algorithms, has become a significant research direction in this domain. Aksu et al.19 employed a modified genetic algorithm (MGA) to optimize feature subsets, combined with decision trees to alleviate the bottleneck of redundant features. Park et al.20 designed a multi-label hierarchical classification (MLHC) model, integrating decision trees and random forests to achieve multi-class classification detection. Ileri et al.21 proposed the MetaCAN framework, employing hybrid metaheuristic algorithms to optimize XGBoost hyperparameters. However, Aloraini et al.22 pointed out that traditional machine learning methods suffer from unstable classification performance under adversarial attacks and depend heavily on manual feature engineering, failing to fully capture complex temporal dependencies, which provides guidance for follow-up research.

With the advancement of deep learning, time-series modeling methods have become mainstream owing to their ability to automatically learn temporal patterns from raw message sequences. Hossain et al.23 utilized LSTM to learn temporal features of CAN messages, achieving high-precision detection. Tariq et al.24 used a rule-integrated RNN for temporal modeling. Lo et al.25 proposed HyDL-IDS, combining CNN-based spatial feature extraction with LSTM-based temporal modeling. Desta et al.26 introduced Rec-CNN, which transforms CAN ID sequences into images using recurrence plots and captures temporal dependencies through convolutional neural networks. However, the aforementioned methods still have drawbacks: they rely on large volumes of high-quality labeled data for training, incur substantial computational overhead, and are hard to deploy in resource-constrained in-vehicle environments.

To fully exploit communication correlation features among ECUs on the CAN bus, graph-based intrusion detection methods map CAN IDs to nodes and construct communication relationships as edges to mine spatial correlation features. Islam et al.27 proposed the GGNB model, integrating PageRank and Gaussian Naive Bayes to achieve lightweight classification. Park et al.28 utilized graph structural features, combining threshold classifiers and random forests to detect and classify attacks. Ye et al.29 introduced GDT-IDS, incorporating graph-theoretic features and employing decision trees for attack detection. Al-Absi et al.30 first fused graph convolutional networks with the Transformer architecture, subsequently proposing the use of dynamic spatial-temporal graph transformers to adaptively learn evolutionary patterns31. Even so, graph-based methods still have limitations: they rely on prior knowledge for graph construction, adapt poorly to dynamic topologies, and entail high computational complexity.

Meanwhile, Transformer-based methods have been widely adopted due to their capability to capture long-range dependencies. Wei et al.32 proposed the AMAEID model, combining attention mechanisms with denoising autoencoders to enhance feature extraction efficiency. Nguyen et al.33 designed a dual-mode attention network, integrating transfer learning to adapt to multiple vehicle models. Zhang et al.34 constructed a two-stage Transformer within a federated framework to tackle data imbalance. Wang et al.35 introduced Swin Transformer combined with wavelet transform to improve detection efficiency. However, Transformers involve large parameter sizes and depend on large-scale training data, posing challenges for deployment in resource-constrained environments.

This section has systematically reviewed the mainstream technical trajectory in the field of in-vehicle network intrusion detection. Despite notable progress, existing methods still face challenges in recognizing sophisticated attacks and achieving lightweight deployment. This paper proposes a multi-class intrusion detection framework that is lightweight, high-precision, and strongly generalizable.

Methodology

Overall system architecture

This study proposes a three-stage intelligent detection system, termed MOO-IDS, designed for CAN bus intrusion detection. The overall architecture is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Overall architecture of our proposed MOO-IDS.

The framework consists of three modules: data generation, feature engineering, and multi-objective optimization mechanism. The data generation module synthesizes high-quality attack samples using the proposed HyDRBM-Gen, mitigating the weak generalization ability of models caused by insufficient attack data. The feature engineering module enables collaborative detection of sophisticated attacks by constructing dual-dimensional features based on temporal behavior and data content. The multi-objective optimization mechanism jointly optimizes detection accuracy, model complexity, and inference efficiency, ensuring that the model maintains high classification performance while meeting the stringent requirements of in-vehicle devices for real-time operation and low resource consumption.

Data preprocessing and dual-dimensional feature engineering

To ensure data quality and construct effective discriminative features, this study first performs data cleaning on raw CAN logs, including handling missing values (removing unparsable message lines) and unifying data length (padding data fields shorter than 8 bytes to 8 bytes with 0 × 00 from the right end). After cleaning, the timestamp t, arbitration identifier ID, data length code DLC, and data payload Data of each message are parsed and extracted as basic fields.

Given the high complexity and stealth of masquerade attacks and targeted ID fabrication attacks on the in-vehicle CAN bus, traditional single-dimensional features fail to provide sufficient discriminative information. To effectively distinguish between these two attack types, this paper introduces two critical features based on their attack mechanisms: ID Frequency (IDF) and ID-Data Correlation (IDC). Attack data augmentation based on hybrid deep RBM

To address the constraints imposed by scarce attack samples and class imbalance in the in-vehicle CAN network on IDS performance, this study proposes a Hybrid Deep Restricted Boltzmann Machine Generator (HyDRBM-Gen). This model aims to adaptively select the optimal generation strategy according to the scale of raw attack data, synthesize high-quality and diverse attack samples, and provide balanced and sufficient training data for the IDS model.

The Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM) is an energy-based generative stochastic neural network proposed by Smolensky in 198636. As an unsupervised learning model, it captures core data patterns to generate low-dimensional, highly discriminative features37. Compared with Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), RBM avoids mode collapse and is well-suited for learning discrete binary data, aligning with CAN frame characteristics38,39.

The RBM employs a two-layer undirected probabilistic graph comprising a visible layer and a hidden layer, with no intra-layer connections and only full inter-layer connections, effectively reducing computational complexity40. This model characterizes the joint probability distribution of visible and hidden state vectors via an energy function , reflecting the intrinsic statistical regularities of data41:

where and denote the bias vectors of the visible and hidden layers, respectively, and represents the inter-layer weight matrix.

However, the standard RBM exhibits limited representational capacity, struggling to effectively capture multi-level, nonlinear feature structures in complex attack patterns. Therefore, this study proposes HyDRBM-Gen, a deep RBM-based attack data generator.

At the core of HyDRBM-Gen is a deep network of multiple stacked RBM (structure shown in Fig. 3, with three stacked RBMs as an example), where the hidden layer units of the preceding RBM share the same set of units with the visible layer units of the subsequent RBM, forming a chain structure for feature abstraction layer by layer. During training, the model learns multi level feature representations of data from low level bit patterns to high level semantic patterns through layer wise greedy unsupervised pre-training; during generation, random sampling is performed from the top hidden layer and reconstructed layer by layer from top to bottom, ultimately synthesizing samples consistent with the original data distribution.

The structure of deep RBM.

This generator operates at the fine-grained attack subclass level, adaptively selecting a generation strategy based on each subclass’s sample size, generating data separately and then merging the generated samples with the original ones to construct a more balanced dataset. Different sample sizes have fundamentally different requirements for generative models: scarce samples easily cause overfitting, while shallow models fail to capture complex distributions when samples are sufficient, meaning a unified strategy cannot achieve optimal results across all sample scales.

Based on statistical analysis of the sample sizes ( N) of different attack types in the dataset, the attack types are categorized into small sample, medium sample, and large sample classes according to their natural distribution characteristics.This classification also takes into account the feasibility of RBM training: small-sample classes are handled with shallow network structures to avoid overfitting, whereas large-sample classes, with sufficient data, support deep RBMs to capture more complex feature patterns. HyDRBM-Gen dynamically selects the corresponding generation path according to the sample size labels, as shown in part (b) of Fig. 2.

AE-NSDBO: a multi-objective optimization algorithm for XGBoost

As the core of MOO-IDS, AE-NSDBO aims to address the multi-constraint trade-offs among detection accuracy, model size, and inference latency in in-vehicle environments. Traditional single-objective optimization struggles to comprehensively balance such conflicting objectives. This algorithm integrates non-dominated sorting into DBO, while introducing dynamic weight adjustment, adaptive phase detection and hybrid mutation strategies to enhance global exploration capability and local exploitation capabilities in high-dimensional complex search spaces. This section first formulates the multi-objective optimization problem, then introduces the baseline model XGBoost, the DBO algorithm, and the non-dominated sorting mechanism, and finally presents the complete implementation pathway of AE-NSDBO.

Dung Beetle Optimizer (DBO) and non-dominated sorting mechanism

The Dung Beetle Optimizer (DBO) is a novel metaheuristic optimization algorithm proposed by Xue and Shen, inspired by the natural behaviors of dung beetles45. Through mathematical modeling, these bionic behaviors are transformed into iterative search mechanisms, effectively coordinating global exploration and local exploitation, demonstrating strong optimization capability and robustness in complex optimization problems46. The algorithm achieves population position updates by simulating four core behaviors: the rolling ball behavior enables large-scale exploration, ensuring coverage of more potentially high-quality solution regions47; the breeding and foraging behaviors focus on local exploitation, further intensifying the search in high-quality regions; the stealing behavior introduces random perturbations to maintain population diversity and avoid premature convergence.

The synergy and dynamic role allocation of these behaviors enable DBO to achieve an adaptive balance between exploration and exploitation during the search process. Leveraging its efficient bionic search mechanism48, DBO can efficiently search for optimal hyperparameter combinations for classifiers.

Since traditional DBO is a single-objective optimization algorithm, while the current problem belongs to multi-objective optimization, the non-dominated sorting mechanism is introduced to construct a multi-objective optimization framework. This mechanism stratifies population individuals through dominance relationships, maintaining the distribution diversity of the solution set49, thereby effectively guiding the search process toward the Pareto front, where solutions in the set are mutually non-dominated in the objective space50.

The dominance relationship is defined as follows: for any two solutions and , if is not inferior to in all objectives and is strictly superior to in at least one objective, then is said to dominate (denoted as )51. Based on this, non-dominated sorting partitions the population into different fronts and calculates crowding distances, preferentially retaining solutions with larger crowding distances to ensure broad distribution of the solution set52.

This study integrates the non-dominated sorting mechanism with DBO, leveraging the efficient search capability of DBO to expand the coverage of solutions, while employing the non-dominated sorting mechanism to select balanced solutions, forming an improved algorithm framework tailored for multi-objective optimization requirements, providing core support for the subsequent construction of the AE-NSDBO algorithm.

Experimental environment

For the ROAD dataset, all comparison models were reproduced by us under identical experimental conditions. For the Survival Analysis and Car-Hacking datasets, we reproduced the comparison models whose source code was publicly available; for a few methods whose source code was not disclosed, we directly cited the results reported in their original papers.

Experimental platform

The models in this experiment are implemented in Python 3.10.18 using the PyTorch 2.8.0 framework. The experiments are conducted on a computer running Windows 10, equipped with an Intel Core i5-6500 processor, 16 GB of RAM, a 1 TB hard drive, and an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1660 graphics card.

Experimental parameter configuration

The parameters involved in this experiment are mainly divided into two categories: the HyDRBM-Gen generator parameters and the AE-NSDBO optimization algorithm parameters.

HyDRBM-Gen generator parameters: The deep RBM employs layer-wise greedy pre-training. The number of pre-training epochs for each RBM is set to 50, the learning rate is set to 0.001, and the number of Gibbs sampling steps in the contrastive divergence algorithm is set to 1. For attack types with extremely small sample sizes, KDE with default configuration is employed for smoothing-based augmentation to reach a sample size sufficient for effective RBM training. The random binary perturbation rate ε introduced during large-sample generation is set to 0.01.

AE-NSDBO optimization algorithm parameters: The population size is set to 20, and the maximum number of iterations is set to 30. In evolutionary computation, the dynamic mutation probability is typically controlled within the range of 0.1 to 0.5, which has been widely adopted as a reference basis for balancing exploration and exploitation56. Based on this, this paper adopts a mutation probability that decreases with the search stage in the AE-NSDBO algorithm, a “coarse‑to‑fine” parameter adjustment strategy consistent with the commonly adopted practice in evolutionary computation57: in the exploration stage, a relatively high mutation probability of 0.3 is set, with polynomial mutation as the dominant operator to promote extensive searching across the solution space. After entering the balance stage, the mutation probability is reduced to 0.2, where Gaussian mutation and Cauchy mutation are combined. The Cauchy mutation enhances global exploration capability while the Gaussian mutation ensures local convergence accuracy, and their combination effectively balances search breadth and precision58. In the exploitation stage, the mutation probability is further reduced to 0.1, and small-step Gaussian mutation is adopted for fine-tuning to accelerate convergence to the optimal solution.

In addition, XGBoost in this experiment adopts the multi‑class objective function multi: softmax. The inference latency is measured in a per‑sample manner (batch size = 1), with repeated runs on the test set samples. Outliers are removed using the Interquartile Range (IQR) method, and the remaining results are averaged to ensure the stability of the measurements.

Impact of hyperparameters on model performance

To analyze the impact of each hyperparameter on model performance, Fig. 10 presents the relationship between each hyperparameter and the F1-score (Y-axis) as well as model size (color mapping) during the AE-NSDBO optimization process, where the red star marks the finally selected optimal solution. Overall, all parameters have a relatively limited impact on the F1-score within the search range, but their influence on model size varies significantly. Among them, n_estimators and max_depth are the primary parameters affecting model size: when n_estimators increases from 50 to 200, model size grows substantially while F1 remains nearly unchanged; max_depth exhibits a similar trend from 3 to 10, where F1 improvement is limited but model size increases noticeably. In contrast, min_child_weight and gamma show the opposite pattern: larger parameter values result in smaller models while F1 remains largely stable. This is because these two parameters constrain tree growth by regulating the minimum sum of instance weights required for a leaf node to split and the minimum loss reduction required for a node to split, respectively, thereby effectively controlling model complexity. Therefore, prioritizing larger values for min_child_weight and gamma helps achieve model lightweighting without significantly compromising accuracy. The scatter distributions of other parameters are relatively dispersed, with no significant direct impact on the objectives, yet they exhibit complex interactions with key parameters, such as the coupling between learning rate and the number of trees. This observation indicates that the AE-NSDBO algorithm achieves an effective balance between performance and resource consumption, demonstrating the practical value of multi-objective optimization. The finally selected optimal solution is a balanced configuration that comprehensively considers the joint effects of all parameters, with the corresponding parameter values listed in Table 8.Although inference latency is not directly shown in the figure, it is highly positively correlated with model complexity and computational cost, thereby optimizing model size correspondingly reduces inference latency. Furthermore, although the optimal parameters vary across datasets due to differences in data distribution, the parameter sensitivity patterns remain largely consistent, indicating that the proposed multi-objective optimization framework exhibits stable adaptability to different data distributions.

Convergence analysis of AE-NSDBO

To further evaluate the convergence performance of AE-NSDBO, we compare it with the classical multi-objective optimization algorithm NSGA-II, with both algorithms adopting the same population size (20) and maximum number of generations (30). As shown in Fig. 11, the two solid curves represent the convergence trajectories of AE-NSDBO and NSGA-II, respectively, with the dashed lines indicating the generations at which each algorithm stabilizes: AE-NSDBO converges at approximately 15 generations, while NSGA-II converges at approximately 22 generations.

Comparison of hypervolume (HV) convergence curves between AE-NSDBO and NSGA-II on the ROAD dataset.

It can be observed from the figure that AE-NSDBO rapidly approaches the Pareto front within the first 10 generations and stabilizes after about 15 generations, demonstrating a faster overall convergence speed than NSGA-II. This advantage is attributed to its adaptive phase detection mechanism, which dynamically adjusts the search strategy according to population diversity and convergence status, effectively balancing exploration and exploitation. In contrast, the convergence speed of NSGA-II is relatively moderate, gradually stabilizing only after about 22 generations.Therefore, the maximum number of generations is set to 30 in this study, ensuring optimization effectiveness while maintaining computational efficiency. In terms of population size, as shown in Fig. 10, the 30 Pareto-optimal solutions generated under this configuration are uniformly distributed across all hyperparameter dimensions, covering the effective range of the search space. This indicates that a population size of 20 is sufficient to maintain population diversity and avoid premature convergence, further validating the rationality of the algorithm configuration.

In summary, MOO-IDS effectively balances detection accuracy and model efficiency, with the AE-NSDBO optimization algorithm demonstrating fast convergence speed and favorable search stability, achieving a high-performance intrusion detection solution suitable for resource-constrained in-vehicle environments.

Conclusion and future work

The proposed MOO-IDS effectively alleviates the scarcity of attack samples and mitigates class imbalance issues through HyDRBM-Gen. The introduced IDF and IDC features enhance the representation of complex attacks from the dimensions of temporal behavior and content statistics. Meanwhile, the AE-NSDBO algorithm achieves multi-objective synergistic optimization of F1-score, model size, and inference latency. Experimental results demonstrate that MOO-IDS achieves excellent performance across all three datasets. On the Survival Analysis and Car-Hacking datasets, it attains F1-scores of at least 0.9993 for all attack types. Even on the ROAD dataset, which presents more complex attack patterns and higher recognition difficulty, the F1-scores across various attack types remain above 0.96. Meanwhile, the model size is as low as 0.157 MB and inference latency is as low as 0.360 ms. Compared with mainstream models, the proposed method achieves comparable or superior detection accuracy while reducing model size by one to three orders of magnitude and improving inference speed by over two orders of magnitude, fully meeting the deployment requirements of resource-constrained vehicular environments and providing a high-precision, lightweight, and strongly generalizable multi-class intrusion detection solution for intelligent connected vehicles.

Although MOO-IDS has achieved significant breakthroughs in detection accuracy, model efficiency, and cross-dataset generalization, there remains room for further expansion and optimization as in-vehicle network technologies evolve and attack methods become more sophisticated. Future work will proceed in two directions: first, in-depth investigation of the quantitative impact of adaptive mechanisms and hybrid mutation strategies in AE-NSDBO, and on this basis, integration with federated learning, differential privacy, and model compression techniques to achieve collaborative optimization of privacy protection and lightweight deployment; second, construction of an end-to-end feature extraction model fusing spatiotemporal convolutional networks and Transformer, extending to multi-protocol scenarios such as in-vehicle Ethernet to enhance robustness against novel attacks.
