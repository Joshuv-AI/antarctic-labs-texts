Gaze behavior during closed-captioned movie viewing adapts to absent audio through more frequent switching between text and scene
Authors: Anna Behler, Megan E. J. Campbell, Brodi Bathgate, Chanelle Noble, Johan van der Meer, Saurabh Sonkusare, Michael Breakspear
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Closed captions, originally for hearing-impaired audiences, are now increasingly used, adding an additional visual element to the multisensory experience of movie viewing. This study examines how gaze behavior adapts to the absence of auditory information by testing whether increased caption engagement reflects more frequent switching between closed captions and the visual scene, longer uninterrupted reading intervals, or both. Using eye-tracking, we analyzed saccades and fixations in designated areas of interest as participants viewed a 20-minute movie with alternating presence and absence of audio. Viewers integrated the two visual input streams by frequently shifting their gaze between the captions and the scene. The balance between reading captions and watching visuals shifts in the absence of sound by adjusting transition probabilities (i.e., increasing gaze switching between captions and scene, rather than changing the duration of reading intervals). This suggests that increased caption engagement in the absence of sound reflects more frequent sampling of captions, rather than longer uninterrupted reading. Modeling the probabilistic characteristics of fixations and saccades revealed more consistent and focused eye movement patterns on the caption text compared to the visual scene, highlighting distinct reading and gazing behaviours. Notably, these differences remained stable regardless of the presence or absence of sound. In addition to the availability of audio, we observed the presence of dialogue and the emotional content also influence caption engagement. These findings highlight how viewers adapt to missing audio through more frequent switching between text and scene while maintaining low-level properties of eye movements.

Introduction

Watching a movie is multisensory experience, in which narrative and emotional content are communicated through visual and auditory input streams (Sonkusare, Breakspear, & Guo, 2019). With the rapid rise of streaming platforms, the use of closed captions (a full text transcript of dialogue, music, and sound) has become very common, extending their original purpose for hearing-impaired individuals. Their increasing adoption reflects broader shifts in media consumption habits (Barry, 2023; Friedman, 2023). The presentation of closed captions introduces an additional visual component, challenging the viewer's ability to rapidly detect relevant and interesting visual elements. The distribution of visual attention between the dynamic scene and displayed text potentially increases cognitive load and might limit information processing (Alzahabi & Becker, 2013). Yet, the integration of these inputs typically appears seamless to the viewer (d'Ydewalle & De Bruycker, 2007).

Captions are known to attract viewers' gaze in a largely automatic fashion, even when they are not required for comprehension (d'Ydewalle, Praet, Verfaillie, & Van Rensbergen, 1991; d'Ydewalle & De Bruycker, 2007). During a typical 90-minute feature film, a viewer may shift their gaze over 20,000 times to process visual information (Smith, 2013). The viewers' gaze tends to focus towards the center of the screen, influenced by the film director's techniques (Tseng, Carmi, Cameron, Munoz, & Itti, 2009), or onto specific elements, for example to improve effectiveness of TV advertisements (Brasel & Gips, 2014). These cinematic techniques reduce variability in eye movements among viewers (Hasson et al., 2008). Eye movements are essential for encoding scene information and memory formation (Hannula et al., 2010), yet comprehension can still vary significantly among viewers (Hutson, Smith, Magliano, & Loschky, 2017). In movies, visual and auditory streams interact dynamically, and gaze behavior depends on attentional focus. For example, eye movements tend to decrease when individuals listen attentively to speech (Cui & Herrmann, 2023). This visual-auditory interaction is also modulated by arousal during movie watching (Yu, Sun, & Ding, 2022). Incorporating closed captions adds a third information stream, competing with the visual scenes and theoretically rendering the audio partly redundant. The nature of this multimodal sensory experience raises new questions about the drivers of visual attention and how the brain optimizes information acquisition. The availability of audio has been shown to modulate subtitle reading behavior: Liao, Yu, Kruger, & Reichle (2022) demonstrated that the presence of same-language audio reduced the fixation time on intralingual subtitles, whereas Szarkowska et al. (2024) found that the absence of sound increased fixation counts and total reading time on subtitles, while also increasing cognitive load and reducing immersion and enjoyment. Although it is known that captions draw viewer attention even when auditory information renders them redundant, the underlying mechanisms—the distribution of fixations, saccades and reading versus scene gazing transitions—are not well understood.

Eye movement patterns, i.e. characteristics of saccades and fixations, differ markedly between reading and viewing natural scenes (d'Ydewalle & De Bruycker, 2007; Roberts, Wallis, & Breakspear, 2013). This suggests that the processes by which information is extracted through eye movements may differ between watching dynamic naturalistic scenes and reading (Magliano, Loschky, Clinton, & Larson, 2013). Our eyes cannot simultaneously fixate on both scenes and text while watching a movie with closed captions. This necessitates viewers to rapidly switch their focus between the on-screen action and the closed captions—a demanding task that involves complex cognitive functions.

Although attention allocation between captions and the scene is well documented, less is known about the adaptation mechanisms of gaze re-allocation when auditory information becomes unavailable. Increased caption engagement in the absence of sound could reflect longer uninterrupted reading of captions, more frequent gaze shifts between captions and the scene, or both. Disambiguating these alternatives is important because they reflect different mechanisms of audiovisual adaptation. In addition, less is known about how the probabilistic characteristics of fixations and saccades differ between these two viewing modes within the same naturalistic stimulus, and how these are modulated by audio availability. Much of the existing eye-tracking literature has focused on dialogue-only intra-lingual subtitles. In contrast, the now commonly used closed captions provide textual descriptions of spoken dialogue plus relevant non-speech auditory information, such as the tone of music, non-speech vocalizations and relevant sound descriptions of actions such as a closing car door or screeching tires. This distinction is increasingly relevant because caption use has expanded beyond deaf and hard-of-hearing access and has become common among hearing viewers in everyday streaming contexts. To address these gaps, we characterized eye movement behaviors during movie viewing with closed captions, examining the statistical properties of eye movements on both captions and the visual scene. The distributional properties of eye movements (e.g., the shape of fixation duration and saccade amplitude distributions) have been used to characterize gaze behavior in both reading (Engbert, Nuthmann, Richter, & Kliegl, 2005; Staub, White, Drieghe, Hollway, & Rayner, 2010) and scene viewing (Ho-Phuoc, Guyader, Landragin, & Guerin-Dugue, 2012; Tatler & Vincent, 2009), revealing effects that are not apparent at the mean level (Guy, Lancry-Dayan, & Pertzov, 2020). However, these approaches have not been applied to compare reading and gazing within the same dynamic stimulus. Our aim was to examine how these probabilistic characteristics manifest and are modulated in a multimodal context when both text and scene are presented dynamically. Additionally, because naturalistic movie viewing is shaped by narrative, social, and visual features, we modeled available frame-level annotations, including dialogue, scene changes, and positive or negative scenes and faces, that likely influence the likelihood of engaging with captions.

Methods

Participants

Healthy adults were recruited through the University undergraduate psychology course, and via convenience sampling. Participants were compensated for their time with either course-credits or entered a draw for an $AUD 100 gift voucher. Inclusion criteria were an age between 18 to 50 and fluency in English. Participants were excluded if they had any history of psychological or neurological illness or reported difficulties with understanding or following English instructions.

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of Newcastle and informed written consent was obtained for all participants.

In this exploratory study, no formal power analysis was conducted. The sample size was informed by previous eye-tracking studies using similar stimuli and designs, which have reliably identified within-participant effects with 20–30 participants (Cui & Herrmann, 2023; Ross & Kowler, 2013). In total, 22 participants took part in the experiment. Of these, four participants were excluded prior to any analysis due to inadequate eye tracking data quality as indicated by a data loss > 10% (two participants) or less than 30 valid saccades in one or more conditions (two participants). The final analyzed sample comprised 18 participants, which is marginally fewer than previous studies, though Ross & Kowler (2013) included one experiment with the same number. The remaining participants were aged 21–40 years (mean age 28.2 ± 4.6 years), 61% identified as female. Seventeen percent of participants had a postgraduate degree (Masters or PhD) as their highest level of education, 56% had an undergraduate degree (Bachelors), 11% had some non-university tertiary education, and 17% had a Year 12 High School Certificate. All 18 participants reported to be fluent in written and spoken English. Fifteen participants reported English as their first language (L1), two participants were raised with English as a second language (L2) and one participant was raised bilingually. The remainder reported English as their first language. On a scale from 1–10 (with 1 being very low confidence, and 10 being very high confidence), average self-rated English reading confidence was 9.2 for L1 speakers, 8.5 for L2 speakers, and the bilingual participant reported 10. All reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing.

Procedure

After providing informed consent, participants were given a short demographic questionnaire, before being seated comfortably in a light-attenuated booth to view the movie. The height of the desk was adjusted to suit the stature of each participant, with a chinrest ensuring all participants had their head in a similar position relative to the eye-tracker and monitor. Participants were given headphones and sound-levels were slightly adjusted to comfort level as needed. The movie was shown after eye-tracking calibration. Participants then completed a post-movie questionnaire.

Experimental paradigm

In the experimental session, the 20-minute independent movie “The Butterfly Circus” was used as stimulus. The movie was selected for its strong emotional storyline, and comprehensive scene annotations established in previous research (Nguyen et al., 2017; van der Meer, Breakspear, Chang, Sonkusare, & Cocchi, 2020). In short, “The Butterfly Circus” tells the story of a man born without limbs who is encouraged by the showman of a renowned circus to overcome self-doubt and reach his own potential. Basic frame annotations accompanying the stimuli included: dialogue, scene changes, the presence of positive or negative faces, and positive or negative scenes. Frame markers were derived from manual annotations indicating the onset and offset of features.

A transcript generated using Adobe Premiere Pro was used to display captions in time with dialogue, with manual additions for non-dialogue audio cues (e.g., “ominous music”). Non-dialogue cues were included to reduce variability in visual salience in the caption area and to avoid attention shifts triggered by abrupt appearances and disappearances of text. All captions were presented in reference to Australian Broadcast Standards (Commonwealth of Australia (Australian Communications and Media Authority), 2024), which aim to ensure readability, accuracy, and comprehensibility of captions. Caption text appeared in white sans-serif font on a black ribbon located horizontally centered below the video frame. Captions were presented in static text blocks rather than in word or line increments. In total, 401 captions were displayed across the 20-minute movie. The mean presentation speed was 9.43 characters per second (CPS; SD = 6.17; median = 8.24; range, 0.78–44.42), with 94.3% of captions at or below 20 CPS. Speeds below 20 CPS are generally recommended (Kruger, Wisniewska, & Liao, 2022). Consecutive captions were presented immediately, hence, with no caption-free frames.

Caption content for each frame was categorized as dialogue if they comprised spoken words or non-dialogue if they contained the description of sound or music. In total, the stimuli contained 401 caption events. Of these, 228 were dialogue-only captions (56.9%; 4877 characters), 162 were sound-description-only captions (40.4%; 3488 characters), and 11 were mixed captions (2.7%; 128 dialogue characters and 171 sound-description characters).

To manipulate the audio content, the film was divided into eight 2-minute, 30-second segments and the sound was muted for half of the segments. A logical index comprising markers for positive and negative facial expressions that were displayed on screen, positive and negative scenes, spoken language, and captions was employed to determine the optimal sequence for muting and unmuting. Details about markers can be found in (van der Meer et al., 2020). The segments were then arranged in an “AABABABB” pattern. Group A participants (n = 9) heard sound during “A” segments and experienced silence during “B” segments; Group B (n = 9) had the inverse audio arrangement. This approach ensured that each group condition received a balanced experience of the film and that any differences in caption-level features were counterbalanced across the presence and absence of audio. Average caption presentation speed was comparable across the two segment types (A segments: 9.72 CPS; B segments: 9.14 CPS). A segments contained 125 dialogue-only captions (62.8%), 72 sound-description-only captions (36.2%), and two mixed captions (1.0%), whereas B segments contained 103 dialogue-only captions (51.0%), 90 sound-description-only captions (44.6%), and nine mixed captions (4.5%). Four of 401 caption events (1.0%) spanned a sound on/off segment boundary. These were assigned to the sound condition in which the majority of the caption's display duration fell.

Post-movie questionnaire

To confirm participants were engaged with the movie during the eye-tracking recording, participants were asked to answer a post-movie questionnaire comprising 16 questions about the subjective appraisal of the movie and the closed captions and provide a brief plot summary in their own words. The full list of survey questions is provided in Supplementary Material. Engagement while movie watching was estimated from questions one to nine. Responses were converted into a Likert scale with numerical values from 1 to 5 (inverted for questions 1 and 7). Engagement with the movie was estimated from the sum of these scores, thus ranging from 9 (least engagement) to 45 (most engagement). Attitude toward captions was estimated from questions 10, 11, and 13 in similar manner, leading to a maximal score of 15.

To account for familiarity with the stimulus, rather than to exclude participants, participants were asked if they had already seen the movie before participating in the study. Familiar viewers may anticipate events and therefore show different engagement with captions. Participants were also requested to indicate the audio quality (Very Poor/Poor/Average/Good/Very Good), to confirm that the sound on/off conditions were experienced as intended (i.e., confirming that no audio playback errors occurred to interfere with sound-on segments).

Eye tracking

The stimuli were displayed on a 24” Samsung monitor with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 and a refresh rate of 60 Hz using PsychoPy. Participants were positioned with their eyes being 92 cm away from the monitor and used a chin and headrest for stability. Despite the relatively large distance, all participants were able to comfortably read the closed captions. An SR Research EyeLink 1000 Plus eye tracker was placed directly below the center of the screen. Calibration required participants to focus on targets within a 13-point grid, aiming for a symmetrical calibration pattern. Misalignments indicated calibration issues. Validation followed, aiming for an average accuracy error below 1.0° visual angle and a maximum of less than 2.0°. Both eyes were recorded at a 1000 Hz sampling rate. Data loss was calculated as the percentage of data points with lost pupil relative to the total recording duration.

Data processing

Before identifying quantifiable eye movement events (i.e., fixations and saccades), all blinks automatically identified by the EyeLink System were extended by excluding an additional 50 ms of gaze position data before onset and after offset, which is consistent with established preprocessing defaults in widely used toolkits such as MNE-Python (Larson et al., 2025). Blinks are typically accompanied by involuntarily saccades-like eye movements while the lids are moving. A visual quality check was conducted to ensure data quality and comparability of movements for both eyes, and to manually exclude any other artefacts due to noise. Although binocular gaze was recorded, only one eye was used for analysis to ensure data quality and consistency. The eye with fewer artifacts and more stable tracking across the entire recording was selected per participant (Hooge, Holleman, Haukes, & Hessels, 2019).

Fixations and saccades were identified using established classification criteria: A saccade was said to be in progress if eye-movement velocity exceeded 30°/s for longer than 10 ms (Costela & Woods, 2019). Fixations were identified based on a total dispersion threshold over minimum 50 ms while velocity did not exceed 20°/s during the fixation (Salvucci & Goldberg, 2000). These established thresholds minimize the risk of misclassification between fixations and saccades. Counts of fixations and saccades were used for further analysis, as well as fixation durations and saccade amplitude. Because the visual content was dynamic, visual information could change significantly during a blink. We therefore treat pre- and post-blink fixations as independent.

Areas of interest

Two Areas of Interest (AOI) were defined for each frame of the movie (Figure 1). The “gazing” AOI constitutes the dynamic video display. The “reading” AOI was identified by placing a box around the line of text in the lower black ribbon. As the length of the displayed text changed throughout the movie, the Optical Character Recognition (OCR) module “EasyOCR” version 1.6.2 was used for frame-wise detection of text. To buffer eye-tracking inaccuracies, a margin of 15 pixels was added to each side of the bounding boxes provided by OCR to define reading AOIs. Based on those AOIs, fixations were classified as “reading,” “gazing,” or “outside.” Saccade labels included: “switching from reading to gazing” and “switching from gazing to reading” based on start and end points. Analyses of fixation and saccade proportions used all labeled events. Distributional modeling was applied to all labeled fixations and to saccades within AOIs.

Statistical analysis

To address hypotheses I and II (i.e., to examine how auditory input and AOI influenced fixation behavior), we applied linear mixed-effects models allowing random intercepts for each participant which are known to be well suited for the analysis of eye movements in subtitle settings (Silva, Orrego-Carmona, & Szarkowska, 2022). These models were based on participant-level condition summaries, rather than individual caption events, and therefore did not include caption-level random effects. Caption-event-level analyses are reported separately in the two-part GEE model described below.

First, fixation proportions were modeled as a function of AOI (gazing vs. reading) and sound condition (on vs. off), plus their interaction. A second model was applied separately to assess the role of semantic relevance within the reading AOI, testing for main effects and interactions of sound condition and meaningfulness of the displayed text. Importantly, no video frames were omitted from the analysis, as text was displayed in every frame, either reflecting dialogue or as a description of music or sound, to avoid sudden on-screen text appearance acting as an unintended gaze attractor.

Transition probabilities between AOIs were calculated based on saccade counts using saccade start and end points and respective saccade labels to test hypothesis III. A paired samples t-test was used to compare transition probabilities across sound conditions. Based on existing pairs of switching saccades (i.e., one saccade landing in the reading AOI and one leaving the reading AOI), the duration of reading periods was calculated based on the end and start times of respective switching saccades. Accordingly, missing data intervals of maximal 300 ms were included. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for within-subject comparisons of reading dwell times (which are not normally distributed). Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was calculated between median reading dwell time and the composite score of questionnaire answers.

We sought to gain insights into the statistical properties of eye movements across different viewing conditions by modeling the probability density functions of saccades and fixations (Hypothesis IV). The distribution of saccade amplitudes is known to be positively skewed with a long tail, often well-represented by a gamma distribution (Ho-Phuoc et al., 2012; Tatler & Vincent, 2009). The shape parameter of a gamma distribution reflects the distribution's skewness and spread, and the scale parameter represents the degree to which the distribution is stretched. The mode provides a measure of the most likely fixation duration or saccade amplitude, capturing the central tendency in these behaviors. The mode of gamma functions is calculated as: Mode = (Shape −1) ⋅ Scale. The probability density of fixation duration and saccade amplitudes at the individual level for each sound condition and AOI was hence fitted with a gamma distribution. To ensure sufficient data for reliable parameter estimation, only participants with at least 30 valid events for each condition were included. This threshold balances the need for statistical stability with the practicalities of trial-level data. Therefore two more participants had to be excluded (as noted in Participants section), resulting in a final analyzed sample of 18 participants. Parameters of fitted gamma distributions (shape, scale, and mode) were extracted per participant and condition. These parameters were then analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with AOI, sound condition, and their interaction as fixed effects. All models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood, and p-values were obtained from the model outputs.

To assess the effects of movie features on caption reading, we first extracted reading behavior at the caption level. An individual closed caption event is defined by the start and end time of each distinct text present in the movie. The total duration that individual captions were displayed varied between 0.63 s and 21.00 s, with an average display duration of 2.98 s (SD = 2.20 s). For each caption event and participant, we computed the valid viewing time (the time during which gaze was detected anywhere on the screen), the time spent reading captions (the summed duration fixations and/or saccades labeled “reading” occurring within the caption AOI during that caption event) and reading incidence as a binary event. Participants were grouped by their sound pattern conditions (see Experimental paradigm for details). Each caption event was annotated with proportion of content features being present: sound on, use of language, positive or negative scene content, positive or negative faces. In addition, the occurrence of scene changes while a caption being displayed was annotated as a binary measure. We then analyzed the factors influencing both the probability and duration of subtitle reading while accounting for the non-independence of repeated measures with a two-part Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) model (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013), clustered by participant. The GEE model was chosen to provide robust standard errors by accounting for the likely autocorrelation within subjects (repeated observations). The first part of the model addressed the question of “what predicts the occurrence of caption reading happening at all?”. A binomial GEE model (logistic link) was used to analyses the movie annotations influencing the binary outcome, i.e., reading or no reading of a caption: Log(Reading) ∼ sound + proportion dialogue × sound pattern group + proportion positive scenes × sound pattern group + proportion negative scenes × sound pattern group + proportion positive faces × sound pattern group + proportion negative faces × sound_pattern_group + number of scene changes × sound_pattern_group

This model addressed the question of “given a participant chooses to read, what predicts the duration of that reading?” as follows: Log(Cumulative Reading Duration) ∼ sound + proportion dialogue × sound pattern group + proportion positive scenes × sound pattern group + proportion negative scenes × sound pattern group + proportion positive faces × sound pattern group + proportion negative faces × sound_pattern_group + number of scene changes × sound_pattern_group + offset(Log(Valid Viewing Time))

The natural logarithm of the valid viewing time was included as a required offset in the model formula. This mathematical relationship scales the outcome, such that the model coefficients predict the rate or proportion of reading time per unit of available viewing time, rather than the raw duration. Both GEE models used an Independence working covariance structure, with standard errors calculated using the robust estimator.

Results

Viewer preferences toward closed captions

Fifty percent of participants reported no distraction from the closed captions, with the remaining 50% indicated some level of distraction. Of these, 44% described it as slight and 6% as moderate. No participants reported higher levels of distraction (“Quite” or “Extremely”). The self-reported use of closed captions in everyday life showed variations across different media. For movies, 22% of participants reported that they always opt in closed captions, 22% often, 17% sometimes, 33% rarely, and 6% never. In contrast, for other content like social media clips, only 6% reported that they always use captions, 28% often, 33% sometimes, 17% rarely, and 17% never use them. The reasons for the use of closed captions are diverse: 56% find close captions helpful in understanding actors whose accents might be challenging to understand, 44% find captions necessary to comprehend dialogue without having to increase the volume and 39% opt in for closed captions to overcome distracting background noises. For those watching with others, 22% enable captions to accommodate viewers with hearing difficulties or language difficulties. Furthermore, for those with a first language other than English (11%), captions are invaluable for following rapid dialogue and enhancing understanding of the content. Interestingly, one participant noted that being distracted by other visual information or mind wandering makes following dialogue difficult, and having it presented as text helps them keep track.

Drivers of caption engagement

Across all participants, the dataset comprised 23,199 fixations on the visual scene (gazing AOI) without sound and 26,304 with sound, and 9069 fixations on captions (reading AOI) without sound and 5013 with sound. The visual patterns in the fixation heat map qualitatively indicated a division of visual attention between the central scene and the closed caption text (Figure 2A). Note that the text in the reading AOI was continuously present throughout the movie. In the absence of the movie's audio, concentration of fixations shifted towards caption text. A mixed-effects model of the effects of sound presence and AOI on the proportion of fixations revealed a significant main effect of AOI (Table 1). Participants allocated a larger proportion of their fixations to the visual scene compared to captions (β = 0.115, p < 0.0001). The model intercept (β = 0.069, p < 0.0001) reflects the estimated proportion of fixations to captions in the absence of sound. The presence of sound significantly reduced the proportion of fixations to captions (β = −0.030, p < 0.0001), indicating that audio reduced reliance on written information. A significant interaction (β = 0.055, p < 0.0001) indicated that sound shifted gaze away from captions and toward the scene, suggesting a functional reallocation of visual attention in response to available auditory input (Figure 2B).

A second model, restricted to the reading AOI, showed that the presence of dialogue in the closed captions increased caption fixations (β = 0.020, p < 0.0001), while sound again reduced them (β = −0.028, p = 0.0001) as reported in Table 2. As evident with the analysis of fixation count, the absence of sound systematically shifts visual attention away from scene and towards the caption text. Saccade counts differed significantly between sound conditions for all categories (Table 3). Fewer saccades were directed within the gazing area in the absence of sound, while more saccades were placed within the reading AOI.

To further characterize this dynamic, we analyzed transition probabilities based on saccade start and end points. This analysis reveals a higher probability of remaining in the gazing AOI when the sound is on (rather than switching to reading) and a higher probability of staying in the reading AOI when sound is off (rather than switching to gazing; Figure 2D).

Although saccades revealed shifts between captions and the scene, they do not capture how long attention remained within the reading AOI once engaged. To further characterize the dynamics of attentional allocation, we analyzed reading dwell times as the time between the landing of a switching saccade in the reading AOI to a switching saccade leaving it. Across all participants, the reading ranged from 0.060 s to 3.029 s when the sound was on, and from 0.055 s to 5.242 s when the sound was off. On the individual level, no participant showed a significant difference in reading duration between sound conditions.

Probabilistic modeling

Statistical characteristics of fixation durations showed a strong association with the AOI in which the fixations occurred (Figure 3). Parameters of the individual probability density functions, shape, scale and mode, were significantly associated with the AOI, indicating distinct fixation behaviors (Table 4). For the shape parameter, fixations in the reading AOI had significantly higher values than those in the gazing AOI (β = −1.580, p < 0.0001), indicating less skewed and more peaked duration distributions during reading. Sound had no significant main effect (β = 0.425, p = 0.06), and there was no significant interaction between AOI and sound (β = −0.478, p = 0.13). The scale parameter showed a similar pattern: it was significantly larger for gazing than reading (β = 0.063, p < 0.0001), consistent with more temporally dispersed fixations during scene viewing. There was no significant effect of sound (β = 0.002, p = 0.85) nor interaction between AOI and sound (β = 0.008, p = 0.59) on the scale parameter. Finally, analysis of the mode parameter revealed no significant main effects of AOI (β = 0.007, p = 0.16), or sound (β = −0.006, p = 0.24), and no significant interaction (β = 0.007, p = 0.33). This suggests that the most likely fixation duration was relatively stable across conditions, even though the distributional shape differed.

Drivers of caption engagement

We next sought to disentangle the influence of individual preferences from that of stimulus-driven factors on gaze behavior. We first tested whether participants’ attitudes toward the movie or captions were associated with the time spent in reading instances. The individual median reading duration was not significantly correlated with the participant's attitude towards the movie (r = −0.33, p = 0.18) regardless of the sound conditions (sound on: r = −0.39, p = 0.11; sound off: r = −0.38, p = 0.12). The individual median reading duration was also not significantly correlated with the attitude towards captions (r = 0.09, p = 0.72).

To identify if specific movie features influence the engagement with closed captions, we used a two-part model which predicts both the choice to read a caption and the subsequent duration of reading. Across all participants, in 2959 of 7204 total caption events (41.07%) no eye movements in the reading AOI were recorded. The two-part model separates the incidence of reading and the duration of reading (intensity). Although the visual appearance of the stimuli was the same for every participant (including the presence of closed captions continuously throughout the entire movie), two different patterns of alternating sound on/off segments were used (see Methods) and included in the models. The results for both models are summarized in Table 6. As expected, participants had a significantly lower odds of reading a caption at all when sound was present (β = −0.535, p < 0.001). Regarding the movie feature annotations, both the proportion of positively valenced facial expressions and negatively valenced facial expressions significantly increased the odds of reading the caption (β = 0.335, p = 0.008; β = 0.3100, p = 0.013, respectively). Additionally, a higher presence of dialogue significantly increased the odds of reading (β = 0.117, p = 0.027). The occurrence of a scene change during a caption event did not significantly affect the initial odds of reading (β = −0.003, p = 0.969). This analysis also revealed significant interactions with the alternating sound on/off pattern, although the overall caption reading did not differ significantly between sound pattern groups (p > 0.10). The generally positive effect of positive faces on caption reading incidence was significantly reduced for sound on/off group A (β = −0.383, p = 0.048). Conversely, the effect of positively valenced scenes became significantly positive only for the sound on/off group A (β = 0.460, p = 0.003), suggesting these participants were more likely to initiate reading when the scene contained a positive valence.

In a next step (i.e., part 2 of the model), the 4,245 caption events where reading occurred were analyzed to identify factors influencing the intensity of reading. Consistent with the reading incidence model, availability of audio significantly decreased reading intensity (β = −0.358, p < 0.001). A higher proportion of dialogue significantly increased the reading rate (β = 0.163, p < 0.001). None of the main effects for sound pattern groups or the emotionally valenced faces and scenes showed a significant effect on the reading rate (all p > 0.10). Finally, no interaction effect was observed (all p > 0.10).

Discussion

This study examined how viewers allocate gaze between closed captions and the visual scene when auditory information is intermittently unavailable during naturalistic movie viewing. The key finding is that adaptation to missing audio was expressed primarily through increased gaze switching between caption text and visual scene rather than through prolonged uninterrupted reading of captions. When sound was absent, viewers shifted more frequently between the scene and the caption area, but the duration of individual caption-reading episodes remained comparatively stable. This suggests that viewers compensate for missing auditory information by sampling captions more often while preserving the temporal structure of individual reading episodes.

Our analyses indicate that probabilistic eye movement patterns differ significantly when reading closed captions versus gazing freely at visual scenes. Notably, the statistical properties of these patterns are not significantly influenced by the presence of the movie's audio. These findings underline that reading and gazing behaviors in this context are low-level characteristics of eye movement control, reflecting basic attentional mechanisms driven by visual features of text and scenes. In the absence of the movie's audio, viewers integrate textual information and scenes through more frequent gaze switches between scene and text instead of adjusting reading dwell times, suggesting adaptive attentional mechanisms. Additionally, dialogue, emotive aspects and narrative features of the movie significantly influence which captions attract gaze, highlighting the role of content in driving saccade timing.

The influence of additional text in the form of closed captions serves as strong attractor of gaze, convergent with previous studies (Ross & Kowler, 2013; Szarkowska, Krejtz, Klyszejko, & Wieczorek, 2011). Our study confirms that captions draw viewer attention even when auditory information renders them redundant. When sound was absent, counts of saccades and fixations on closed captions increased, suggesting a compensatory mechanism to integrate missing auditory information through visual input. This aligns with the idea of flexible attention allocation, where the brain dynamically adapts visual processing strategies to fill in missing sensory information (Alencar, Butler, & Lomber, 2019; Miall, Afanasyeva, Cole, & Mason, 2021).

Modeling probabilistic eye movement behavior with gamma functions revealed distinct differences between reading and gazing. Eye movements within the reading AOIs showed less skewed gamma distributions with sharper peaks compared to the gazing AOI, indicating less variability in eye movements. Specifically, the higher shape and smaller scale parameters suggests that fixation durations and saccade amplitudes on closed captions are more consistent and cluster closely around the mode. This reflects the regular and systematic nature of reading, where saccades and fixations follow a more predictable pattern as the eyes move systematically across the text (McDonald & Shillcock, 2003). Given that reading is a highly automated skill in adults (Justino & Kolinsky, 2023), the consistent statistical properties throughout movie segments with and without audio, the consistent statistical properties throughout movie segments with and without audio indicate a reading-like pattern of eye movement control, even though dynamic caption reading may involve higher rates of word skipping than static text reading (Lopukhina, van Heuven, Crowley, & Rastle, 2025). Crucially, these patterns remain clearly distinct from those observed during scene viewing, which showed more dispersed and variable saccades and fixations. This suggests that even with sound, individuals are deeply engaged with textual information, eventually enhancing comprehension (Gernsbacher, 2015). The differences in eye movement behavior between reading and gazing AOI reflects the close connection between eye movement control, cognition and the viewing task (Henderson, 2003). Importantly, the probabilistic characteristics of fixation durations and saccade amplitudes were not affected by the presence or absence of sound.

Two possible strategies could underlie the increased visual attention to captions when the sound is off. The first would be to spend a longer uninterrupted dwell time (duration per single visit) on captions without changing the number of gaze switches. The second would involve increasing the number of switches while keeping dwell times for each reading event consistent. Our findings support the latter strategy, showing that when sound was absent, participants engaged in more frequent switching saccades without significantly altering the uninterrupted dwell time per reading event. We also found that the cumulative time spent reading a caption was significantly higher when the sound was off. Importantly, scene changes are known to influence transition probabilities between AOIs and increasing gaze shifts between the scene and text (Krejtz, Szarkowska, & Krejtz, 2013). Our analysis confirmed that these disruptions were associated with reduced rates of sustained caption reading. Crucially, our counterbalanced stimulus design effectively controls for this factor. This strengthens the interpretation that the observed increase in gaze switching is not merely stimulus-driven, but rather reflects an adaptive strategy to compensate for the absence of audio information. Participants appear to dynamically reallocate visual attention to ensure access to dialogue, highlighting the flexibility and efficiency of audiovisual integration in naturalistic stimuli contexts. Subjective reports indicated that participants did not experience distraction or extra effort associated with the presence of closed captions and the associated frequent visual shifts. This aligns with the notion that subtitle viewing is cognitively efficient and does not entail a trade-off between image processing and subtitle processing (Kruger, Hefer, & Matthew, 2013; Perego, Del Missier, & Stragà, 2018). The efficient integration of textual and visual information may be facilitated by audiovisual redundancy, where overlapping information across modalities enhances comprehension without overloading cognitive resources (Adesope & Nesbit, 2012; Brasel & Gips, 2014).

Beyond the expected influence of sound, stylistic elements and the presence of dialogue also served as catalysts for the initiation of reading captions. To ensure a balanced design, we counterbalanced all available movie annotations across sound conditions. As a result, participants watched the movie with one of two alternating sound on/off patterns. The influence of emotional features was not uniform across these two groups, with interactions observed for positive faces and positively valenced scenes. These interactions likely reflect the counterbalanced design, in which each group experienced different combinations of emotional content and audio availability at different points in the narrative arc. The stimulus was a short movie and included a complete narrative arc with key turning points and plot developments. Varying levels of tension and engagement throughout the story may have influenced how participants interacted with the captions dependent on when the experienced segments without audio occurred throughout the movie. The build-up and resolution of tension across the narrative arc could provide additional insights into the dynamic visual interaction between scene and caption text in future research.

Limitations

There are several important caveats to our findings. First, eye-tracking data quality inevitably varied across participants. To address this issue, the analysis focused predominantly on within-subject, across-condition comparisons. This assumes that each individual's data quality remains consistent over time and is better at accommodating between subject variations in core data quality than pooled group contrasts. However, future studies with larger samples will be important to confirm and extend these findings. Gaze classification based on AOIs can be affected by the shape and size of those AOIs, especially due to eye tracking inaccuracy (Hessels, Kemner, van den Boomen, & Hooge, 2016; Wass, Forssman, & Leppänen, 2014). We added a margin to the tight bounding boxes around caption text provided by OCR to account for minor errors. However, there is no consensus in the field regarding optimal AOI margins. In this study, we used a high-accuracy eye tracker, and all included participants had calibration errors below 1° of visual angle resulting in a setting under which AOI size does not meaningfully contribute to Type II errors (Vehlen, Standard, & Domes, 2022). Given the spatial proximity of captions and visual scene (Figure 1), our analyses might have benefited from using relatively small AOIs (Orquin, Ashby, & Clarke, 2016).

Second, to ensure a consistent visual layout captions were present in every frame. This means that some caption parameters such as presentation speed (cps) deviated from standard guidelines. Occasionally, very short captions may have limited full reading, whereas very long captions may have allowed re-reading or disengagement. However, presentations speeds of captions were counterbalanced across groups and thus stimulus features, suggesting that caption speed is unlikely to explain the main sound-related contrast.

Third, we used basic probabilistic properties of eye movements for reading (and gazing) rather than classical reading patterns characterized by specific sequences of horizontal saccades and fixations (Engbert et al., 2005; Just & Carpenter, 1980; Reichle, Rayner, & Pollatsek, 2003). This method does not fully capture lexical processing of closed captions, including word skipping or per-word gaze durations, which are known to be influenced by subtitle speed and impact comprehension (Kruger et al., 2022; Liao, Yu, Reichle, & Kruger, 2021; Liao et al., 2022). However, our focus was on broader patterns of reallocation of visual attention during movie viewing with closed captions associated with the availability of auditory input. This abstraction enabled a direct comparison between reading and gazing behaviour. A limitation of this approach is that we did not incorporate conventional eye-tracking metrics commonly used in subtitle research, such as word dwell time (Krejtz et al. 2016, Liao, Kruger, & Doherty, 2020) or word hit count (Szarkowska, Krejtz, Pilipczuk, Dutka, and Kruger, 2016). While our probabilistic approach reveals higher-order regularities in reading versus non-reading gaze, future work could complement it by analysing moment-to-moment gaze events to more precisely analyses of lexical aspects of closed caption reading, as well as gaze behaviour around transitions between consecutive captions, which may function as partial onset events despite the continuous presence of text. Such future research could provide deeper insights into how textual information is processed alongside dynamic visuals (Rayner, 2009).

Fourth, there are several limitations related to our sample. Participants were recruited from a university undergraduate psychology course and by convenience sampling. While participants varied in age (21–40 years) and educational background, the sample is not broadly representative of the general population, and younger, university-educated individuals may differ from the wider population in reading habits, media consumption, and familiarity with closed captions, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. We also included non-native speakers among our participants, although they self-reported fluency in English. This may introduce variability in reading and comprehension strategies, potentially affecting eye movement behaviors during closed caption processing (Szarkowska et al. 2024). With only two L2 and one bilingual participant, our sample did not permit a meaningful separate analysis by language background. The primary sound-related analyses were based on within-participant comparisons. Nevertheless, L1/L2 differences may influence absolute levels of caption engagement. Our analytic approach would accommodate future studies with larger and more diverse samples could examine how L1/L2 language background modulates gaze behavior during captioned movie viewing.

Although similar eye movements indicate a commonality of visual processing across individuals, it does not necessarily imply similar brain responses or thoughts (Hasson et al., 2008; Wallace et al., 2025). Concurrently acquired functional brain activity (such as Electroencephalogram or functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging) would provide more direct insights into the shared and unique functional networks of the gaze versus reading behaviors. Manipulating closed captions—such as through the use of pseudo-words, or misaligning the caption timing - could shed light on the mechanisms of multisensory integration and attention allocation during movie viewing with closed captions. Movie-viewing paradigms offer valuable insights into multisensory integration that extend beyond the constraints of classic eye-tracking or psychophysical task offering more generalization (Nau, Schmid, Kaplan, Baker, & Kravitz, 2024; Sonkusare et al., 2019; van der Meer et al. 2020). Although not as varied as real-world environments, movies approximate the dynamic and complex nature of everyday sensory inputs. Coupled with the inherently captivating aspects of movies such as narrative and socially salient content (Rider, Coutrot, Pellicano, Dakin, & Mareschal, 2018; Rooney, Benson, & Hennessy, 2012), this audiovisual milieu engages attention in a more passive and life-like way than the task-driven processes required to respond to sparse and repetitive stimuli of more classical paradigms.

Conclusions

Closed captions are increasingly used beyond their original purpose, shaping visual attention and cognitive processing during movie viewing. Our findings reveal that while eye movement patterns differ between reading captions and gazing at scenes, they remain stable regardless of audio presence. Viewers adapt to missing auditory input by increasing gaze shifts rather than altering reading duration, suggesting flexible attention mechanisms. These results highlight how textual and visual information are efficiently integrated, reinforcing the role of content in guiding attention within multisensory experiences. Understanding these dynamics is essential as caption use continues to grow in everyday media consumption.
