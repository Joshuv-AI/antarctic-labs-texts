Genetic variations underlying bitter and sweet taste perception are associated with food and beverage intake habits and taste-modifying behaviors among Dutch adults with a prior myocardial infarction
Authors: Julie E Gervis, Monica Mars, Kamalita Pertiwi, Luc Heerkens, Alice H Lichtenstein, Johanna M Geleijnse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTaste perception influences eating behaviors and varies partly based on genetics. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified many variants related to bitter, sweet, salt, sour, and umami perception (taste-related variants), but whether these variants inform eating behaviors among patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD) is unknown. This study investigated the associations of taste-related variants with eating behaviors in adults with a prior myocardial infarction (MI) and explored the potential for sex-specific effects.MethodsAnalyzed, were 4,204 Dutch adults with a prior MI from the Alpha Omega Cohort (60–80 years; 21% female; BMI: 27.7 ± 3.8 kg/m2). We selected 39 taste-related variants identified in prior GWAS and determined their associations with reported intake frequency of 19 food and beverage groups and 7 taste-modifying behaviors (e.g. adding sugar to coffee) using multivariable regression models adjusted for demographic, lifestyle and socioeconomic factors.Main findings32/39 taste-related variants were nominally (p < 0.05) associated with at least one food or beverage group or taste-modifying behavior, of which 6 passed Bonferroni adjustment (p < 0.05/19 or 0.05/7, respectively). The strongest associations were for rs1726866––a functional variant in the TAS2R38 bitter taste receptor gene––for which each additional G allele related to higher bitter perception was associated with less frequent coffee (β = −1.1 servings/week; 95% CI: −1.75, −0.42) and more frequent tea intake (β = 1.0 servings/week: 95% CI: 0.42, 1.64). Other significant associations were identified for bitter-related variants with less frequent legume intake and addition of full-fat milk to coffee and sweet-related variants with more frequent red/orange vegetable and full-fat dairy intake. Exploratory interaction analyses also suggested potential sex-specific effects; for example, rs1726866 (G) was associated with adding less sugar to coffee among females, but more among males.ConclusionsBitter- and sweet-related variants were associated with food and beverage intake habits and taste-modifying behaviors in adults with a prior MI, with some evidence for sex-specificity. These findings support the relevance of taste-related variants to eating behaviors among CVD patients and suggest the benefit of leveraging these genetic factors when tailoring dietary recommendations for reducing secondary cardiovascular risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12263-026-00814-y.Keywords: Taste perception, Genetics, Dietary intake, Precision nutrition

Introduction

Gaining insights into the genetic factors that predispose individuals to make specific food and beverage choices is critical to facilitating development of more effective precision nutrition strategies for chronic disease risk reduction. In modern food environments characterized by an abundance of foods and beverages with enhanced hedonic value and taste [1], genetic variations underlying taste perception provide plausible candidates for investigation [2, 3].

Taste perception is an evolutionary driver of food avoidance, selection, preference and intake [4]. It reflects individuals’ ability to detect basic tastes––sweet, salt, sour, bitter, and umami––and the intensity with which individuals perceive suprathreshold concentrations of each taste. Although perception of all 5 tastes synergistically informs dietary habits [5, 6], relationships between taste perceptions, food preferences and intake habits differ widely among individuals. While many individuals with higher bitter taste perception avoid bitter-tasting foods like coffee and cruciferous vegetables [7, 8], others with similar bitter taste like the bitterness of these foods and consume them frequently [9]. Some individuals also modify the taste of foods and beverages to accommodate their taste perceptions and preferences––for example, adding sugar or cream to coffee can mask its bitterness or adding table salt to meals can enhance overall flavors [10, 11]. Thus, beyond food and beverage choices, taste perceptions drive taste-modifying behaviors that ultimately shape overall diet quality [12].

According to family and twin studies, taste perceptions are moderately-to-highly heritable, with an estimated 20–70% of phenotypic variability explained by genetic variation [13–15]. This is further supported by recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) that identified many variants related to taste perception (herein referred to as taste-related variants) [16–20], some of which have been mapped to canonical taste receptor genes (e.g., TAS2R38 bitter taste receptor), while others have been mapped to genes with broader functional relevance to the gustatory system though peripheral pathways of cell-to-cell signaling, nutrient metabolism and central reward systems. Several of these variants have been subsequently related to dietary intake [21, 22] and taste-modifying habits [11] albeit with some inconsistency [23] and evidence of sex-specificity [24]. However, as these associations have been predominantly tested in healthy adults, it is unknown whether taste-related variants influence dietary habits in other health or environmental contexts, such as in patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD) who may be exposed to physiological and pharmacological stressors that modify their taste perceptions [25, 26].

Here, we performed a secondary analysis using data from a patient cohort, the Alpha Omega Cohort (AOC), comprised of ~ 4800 Dutch adults who experienced a myocardial infarction (MI) within 10 years prior to recruitment [27, 28]. We selected variants previously associated with taste perception in GWAS (measured using suprathreshold intensity tests) to represent taste-related variants. Our primary objective was to determine the associations of taste-related variants with food and beverage intake habits and taste-modifying behaviors to better understand the potential benefits of leveraging taste-related variants when developing tailored dietary recommendations for risk reduction in patient populations. An exploratory objective was to test for sex-specific effects of taste-related variants on dietary habits, based on evidence of sexual dimorphisms in taste-related genes [24, 29].

Methods

Study participants

Recruited for the AOC (2002–2006) were Dutch adults aged 60 to 80 years residing in the Netherlands who experienced a clinically diagnosed MI within the 10 years (average 4 years) prior to study entry (registered at ClinicalTrials.gov as NCT03192410 in 06/2017) [27, 28]. Of the 4,837 participants enrolled at baseline, 4,221 had complete genotyping, dietary and relevant confounder data and were included in the analyses (see Supplementary Fig. 1). All participants provided written informed consent for long-term follow-up. The trial was approved by a central medical ethics committee (Haga Hospital, Leyenburg, The Hague, Netherlands), by the ethics committee at each participating hospital, and for the current data analyses, the Tufts University Health Sciences Institutional Review Board.

Taste-related variants

Genotyping in the AOC

DNA was isolated from buffy coats and genome-wide genotyping was performed using an Illumina (2016) Infinium Global Screening Array, as described previously [30, 31]. Imputation was performed using the 1000 Genomes project European reference panel. Genetic quality control included restricting variants to those in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (p > 1 × 10−6), with a minor allele frequency > 0.05, genotyping call rate > 0.95, and imputation quality r2 > 0.80.

Selection of taste-related variants

Taste-related variants were selected based on prior GWAS for perception of five basic tastes (sweet, salt, sour, bitter, umami) [16–20], identified from GWAS Catalog [32]. Given the variety of tastants used to measure perception of each taste in prior GWAS, representative tastants were specified a priori for each taste to improve trait specificity (sucrose for sweet, sodium chloride for salt, citric acid for sour, 6-n-propylthiouracil [PROP] or phenylthiocarbamide [PTC] for bitter, and monopotassium glutamate [MPG] for umami). Variants with genome-wide (p < 5 × 10−8) or suggestive (p < 5 × 10−5) associations with one of the six predetermined tastants in prior GWAS which were the strongest (lowest p-value) among those in linkage disequilibrium (LD; r2>0.10) in the 1000 Genomes project EUR population (phase 1 v3; N = 514) were selected for analysis. All variants were coded using an additive model of inheritance based on the dosage of alleles related to higher taste perception (0, 1, or 2), to align with variant coding in the discovery GWAS and to maximize statistical power (compared to dominant or recessive models) [33].

Of the 95 taste-related variants identified, 39 passed selection criteria and genetic quality control (Supplementary Fig. 2). A summary of their locations, mapped genes, allele frequencies (in AOC and the 1000 Genomes Project EUR population, for comparison) and GWAS effect estimates is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Included were 22 sweet-, 2 salt-, 2 sour-, 9 bitter- and 4 umami-related variants, reflecting five basic tastes.

Dietary assessment

Dietary habits were assessed with a 203-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) administered at baseline (2002–2006), reflecting an extended version of a previously validated FFQ developed for the AOC [34, 35]. The FFQ assessed habitual intake frequency of 203 foods and beverages during the prior month, using standardized, pre-specified portion sizes, and included a subset of 11 questions on the frequency and amount of taste-modifying behaviors commonly used when consuming coffee and tea. Completed FFQs were reviewed and quality checked by trained dieticians; missing or unclear information was clarified via phone call. Intake frequency of each item was estimated in servings per week (sev/wk) from the FFQ. Energy intake was estimated in kcal per day by linking FFQ items to the 2006 Dutch food composition database [36] and multiplying by standard portion sizes and intake frequencies.

Food and beverage intake habits

Habitual food and beverage intake was captured as the intake frequencies (in servings/week) of 19 food and beverage groups, defined by collapsing individual items from the FFQ based on the 2015 Dutch Healthy Diet index [37]. Additional sub-groups were defined to capture taste-related characteristics of items within each group. These included: vegetables (sub-groups: cruciferous, red/orange, other), fruits (sub-groups: citrus, non-citrus), whole grains, legumes, nuts, dairy (sub-groups: full-fat [FF] milk/yogurt; low-fat [LF] milk/yogurt; FF cheese; LF cheese), fish, refined grains, red meat, processed meat, coffee, tea and sugar-sweetened beverages. To minimize the impact of extreme values and non-parametric (i.e. skewed) distributions, intake frequencies were winsorized to 5 SD and a log10(x + 1)-transformation was applied to the data for statistical comparisons.

Taste-modifying behaviors

To capture taste-modifying behaviors of coffee and tea, 7 habits were defined from the FFQ data based on the expected impact of each modification to the beverage’s taste (informed by the “Smaak, Vet, Tekstuur” [SVT] dataset, containing relative taste properties of common foods and beverages consumed in the Netherlands) [38]. Behaviors included addition of sugar to coffee; creamer or milk (subgroups: FF, LF, other) to coffee; sugar to cappuccino; and sugar to tea. Values were coded as missing for participants reporting no coffee or tea consumption (n = 192 and 809, respectively). For descriptive analyses, proportions of individuals reporting each behavior were assessed as binary (yes/no) outcomes. For primary analyses, among individuals reporting each behavior, frequencies were assessed as continuous outcomes (in servings/week).

Demographic, lifestyle and health-related covariates

Demographic and lifestyle characteristics were assessed using self-administered participant questionnaires at baseline [27]. For this analysis, smoking status was defined as current, former, or never cigarette smoker. Physical activity was assessed using the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly [39] and categorized as low (<3 METs on all days), intermediate (≥3 METs on <5 days/week) or high (≥3 METs on ≥5 days/week) [40]. Alcohol intake (g) was quantified from the FFQ (1 drink = 10 g) and categorized as none, light (0–1 drink/day), moderate (1–2 drinks/d) or heavy (>2 drinks/day). Socioeconomic status was represented by educational attainment, defined as low (primary or secondary), moderate (higher secondary or lower tertiary), or high (higher tertiary). Anthropometric measures, including height (m), weight (kg) and waist circumference (cm), were also assessed at baseline [27]; BMI was calculated from height and weight as kg/m2. For descriptive analyses, we also included clinical measures such as systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP, respectively; mmHg), self-reported medication use (hypertension, lipid-lowering, glucose control) and diabetes status (combined as type I or II), assessed at baseline, as previously described [27].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R (v 4.1.2). Descriptive statistics (mean and SD; median and percentiles; n and percentages) were used to summarize participants’ demographic, behavioral, health and dietary characteristics overall and by sex. Pairwise Student’s T-tests and Chi Square tests were used to compare continuous and categorical variables, respectively, by sex. Analyses of variance (ANOVA) and Fischer’s Exact tests were used to compare continuous variables by categorical genotypes.

For primary analyses, generalized linear models were used to determine the associations of each taste-related variant as the dosage of alleles for higher taste perception (continuous: 0, 1, 2) with food and beverage intake frequencies and taste-modifying behaviors as independent continuous outcomes in servings per week (sev/wk). Associations were first tested in a base model, controlling for age, sex, total energy intake (kcal/day) and top 5 genetic principal components (PCs) to account for population stratification (base model). A stepwise framework was then used to additionally adjust for BMI as a potential mediator or collider (BMI model); and for smoking status exposure, alcohol use, physical activity level and education level as potential taste or diet confounders (lifestyle/SES model). Results were presented as effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CI) reflecting the mean change in reported frequency of each behavior per additional allele related to higher taste perception. Statistical significance was defined using Bonferroni-adjusted thresholds of p < 2.6 × 10−3 (0.05/19) for associations with food and beverage intake habits and p < 7.1 × 10−3 (0.05/7) for taste-modifying behaviors. For more intuitive interpretations of effect estimates with confounder adjustment, significant associations were also summarized as estimated marginal means (SE) at 0, 1, or 2 taste increasing alleles.

For exploratory analyses of sex-specific effects, a cross-product variant-by-sex term was entered into the base model and Extra Sum of Squares F-tests were used to determine global significance of the interaction term. When significant interactions were identified, sex-stratified analyses were performed to tabulate sex-specific effect estimates, 95% CIs, and estimated marginal means using the base model, then the BMI and lifestyle/SES models as a sensitivity analysis. Statistical significance was set a p < 0.05 given the hypothesis-generating nature of this analysis.

Results

Patient characteristics in the AOC

AOC participants (N = 4,204) were 69.0 ± 5.6 years (mean ± SD), had a BMI of 27.7 ± 3.8 kg/m2 and 21% were female (Table 1). A majority were non or former smokers (84%), were not diagnosed with diabetes (83%) and were taking blood pressure- or lipid-lowering medications (99 and 87%, respectively). Compared to males, females smoked less, consumed less alcohol, engaged in less physical activity, attained lower levels of education and had higher rates of diabetes (type I or type II).

Description of eating behaviors among AOC patients

On average, AOC participants reported more frequent intakes of whole and refined grains, full-fat cheese, processed meat, other vegetables and non-citrus fruits and less frequent intakes of low-fat cheese, legumes, nuts and fish (Table 2). Coffee was also more commonly consumed than tea. Across sexes, when adjusting for total energy intake, females reported more frequent intakes of fruits, low-fat dairy and tea and less frequent intake of processed meat than males.

Among coffee or tea drinkers, the most common taste-modifying behavior was adding cream to coffee (49%); the least common was adding full-fat milk to coffee (1.2%). A similar proportion of individuals reported adding sugar to coffee or tea (29% and 28%) (Table 2). While a similar proportion of females and males reported drinking coffee, females were more likely to be tea drinkers than males (85% vs. 80%) and were less likely to add sugar to either coffee (15% vs 33%) or tea (14% vs 32%).

Associations of taste-related variants with habitual food and beverage intake

Of the 39 taste-related variants, 32 were nominally associated (p < 0.05) with intake frequency of at least one food or beverage group in the base model, of which, 5 variant-diet associations passed Bonferroni adjustment (p < 2.6 × 10−3) (Fig. 1) (see Supplementary Table 2 for full summary statistics for associations with p < 0.01). The strongest associations were with bitter-related variant rs1726866 in the TAS2R38 bitter taste receptor gene (A > G; effect allele [EA] for higher bitter taste perception = G), for which each additional G allele was associated with 1.0 (95% CI: 0.42, 1.64) more servings per week of tea and 1.1 (95% CI: −1.75, −0.42) fewer servings per week of coffee. Associations were also identified for bitter-related variant rs6761655 (G > A; EA for higher bitter taste perception = A) with less frequent legume intake (p = 1.2 × 10−3); sweet-related variant rs7602024 (C > T; EA for higher sweet perception = T) with more frequent red/orange vegetable intake (p = 1.1 × 10−3), and sweet-related variant rs994178 (A > G, EA for higher sweet perception = G) with more frequent full-fat dairy intake (p = 1.1 × 10−3).

After additional adjustment for BMI, all five variant-diet associations remained significant below the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold. Associations of taste-related variants with taste-modifying behaviors

Analyses of taste-modifying behaviors of coffee and tea focused on bitter-related variant rs1726866 as the only taste-related variant to be significantly associated with reported intake frequency of both beverages. Individuals with the genotype related to higher bitter taste perception (GG genotype) were more likely to consume any tea (p = 7.5 × 10−3) or coffee (p = 0.06) than those with genotypes related to lower bitter taste perception (AA or AG genotype) (Supplementary Table 3). Among coffee (N = 4012) or tea (N = 3395) drinkers, while there were similar proportions of individuals reporting each taste-modifying behavior across genotypes (all p > 0.05), when restricting to those reporting each behavior, individuals with the GG genotype added less full-fat milk to coffee (p = 0.033) and more sugar to tea (p = 0.029), than individuals with the GA or AA genotype.

After adjusting for age, sex, total energy intake, genetic PCs, (base model covariates) and coffee or tea intake, the inverse association between rs1726866 (G) with full-fat milk added to coffee remained significant below the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold (β, per G allele for higher bitter taste perception: −0.79; 95% CI: −1.2, −0.39; p = 6.5 × 10−4), while the positive association with adding sugar to tea was attenuated (p = 0.649) (Fig. 2). Further adjusting for BMI and lifestyle/SES covariates produced similar results (Supplementary Table 4).

Exploratory sex interaction analysis

In exploratory analyses testing for sex-specific associations of taste-related variants with dietary habits, five interactions were identified as significant (p < 0.05), of which two passed a more stringent Bonferroni-corrected threshold (Fig. 3). These included interactions between sweet-related variant rs994178 (C > G) with low-fat cheese intake (Pinteraction = 1.3 × 10−4) and sour-related variant rs837231 (G > C) with other vegetable intake (Pinteraction = 4.5 × 10−4). Both interactions were robust to further adjustment for BMI and lifestyle/SES confounders (Supplementary Table 5), and in sex-stratified analyses, significant associations were only identified among females but not among males.

A significant interaction between rs1726866 with taste-modifying behaviors by sex was also identified for adding sugar to tea (Pinteraction = 2.6×10−3): each additional G allele related to higher bitter perception was associated with adding less sugar to tea among females, and more sugar to tea among males, though the magnitudes of effects were small (Fig. 3). The association remained robust after further adjustment for BMI and lifestyle/SES covariates (Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated whether genetic variations underlying bitter, sweet, salt, sour and umami taste perceptions were associated with reported food and beverage intake habits and taste-modifying behaviors of coffee and tea among ~4,000 Dutch adults with a prior MI. We identified several significant taste-related variant-diet associations, with some evidence of sex-specificity, particularly for bitter-related variant rs1726866 and coffee and tea intake habits; as well as other bitter- and sweet-related variants with legume, red/orange vegetable and dairy intake frequency. Collectively, these findings support the relevance of previously identified taste-related variants in population-based cohorts to food and beverage intake habits and taste-modifying behaviors in a patient population at elevated CVD risk.

The strongest observed associations were for rs1726866 in the TAS2R38 bitter taste receptor gene. This variant––part of a functional haplotype conferring phenotypic variability in bitter taste perception of PROP and PTC [41]––was associated with less frequent coffee and more frequent tea intake. Concordant associations have been reported in the UK Biobank [11], a population-based cohort of ~500k adults residing in the UK, providing independent replication. In the AOC, rs1726866 was also associated with adding less full-fat milk to coffee among coffee drinkers. Prior studies among UK adults have also reported inverse associations of rs1726866 with “milk coffee” [11] and dietary fat [42] intake. However, these associations have not been consistently identified in US [11] or other European cohorts [43], where there may be cultural differences in coffee and tea drinking habits or in the coffee and tea additions habitually available to consumers (e.g., range of dairy products readily available at brewed coffee purveyors). Thus, while these findings support an association between rs1726866 and bitter taste perception with coffee and tea intake habits, they also underscore the need to consider the cultural and behavioral contexts and broader food environments in which genetic-taste-diet associations are evaluated.

Within the AOC, we identified several taste-related variant-diet associations that have not been previously reported. These included bitter-related variants rs6761655 with less frequent legume intake and sweet-related variants rs7602024 and rs994178 with more frequent red/orange vegetable and full-fat dairy intakes, respectively. None of these variants have been identified in prior GWAS of dietary habits [44–46], though this may be because their allelic effect sizes are not detectable at genome-wide significance (p < 5×10−8). All four associations were robust to adjustment for BMI, lifestyle and SES factors and are supported by evidence linking phenotypic variability in bitter and sweet taste perception to vegetable, dairy and dietary fat intake [47, 48]. However, none of these variants are mapped to genes with known functions in ingestive behaviors (e.g., DIRC3, RPL7P13 and TMEM72–AS1), nor are they related to alterations in gene expression which could provide biological plausibility (based on publicly available data in the Genotype-Tissue Expression [GTEx] database) [49]. Further replication and experimental studies are needed to inform whether these loci reflect spurious associations or provide preliminary evidence of novel sensory mechanisms underlying eating behaviors.

Our exploratory analyses suggested several potential sex-specific effects of taste-related variants on dietary habits. For example, we found evidence of differential rs1726866 effects on sugar addition to tea: among coffee drinkers, females with more bitter taste alleles (G) reported adding less sugar to coffee while males reported adding more. This could suggest that rs1726866 (G) confers greater acceptance or liking for the bitter taste of coffee in females than males, though the precise explanation or underlying mechanisms remain unknown. We also found stronger taste-related variant-diet associations among females than males, which was surprising given the larger sample size and statistical power in the male subsample. Prior studies have reported sex-specific differences in taste perception, most often with females having higher perceptions than males [24]. This has been partially attributed to sex-specific neural responses to taste stimuli [29] and hormonal differences [50], though the latter is unlikely to explain our results, as females in the AOC were likely post-menopausal. Another explanation may stem from sex-specific lifestyle habits. In the AOC, men reported higher tobacco exposure than women, which may have reduced their taste perceptions [51, 52] and attenuated taste-related variant effects on dietary habits. Further consideration of sex-specific and environmental contexts will be important when integrating taste-related variants into nutrition guidance, particularly for older patient populations who may have diminished taste perceptions [24].

A key strength of our approach was the focus on variants identified in GWAS for taste perception rather than for dietary intake. Large-scale GWAS of diet have reported hundreds of loci related to dietary preferences [46] and intakes [44, 45], many of which are highly pleiotropic and likely indirectly affect diet through lifestyle, socioeconomic or obesity-related factors [53]. In contrast, taste-related variants that inform upstream predictors of diet have more plausible direct effects and provide stronger causal anchors for tailoring nutrition recommendations. This aligns with the shift toward using functional (instead of genome-wide) loci in diet Mendelian Randomization studies [54]. Fully interpreting these genetic effects, however, requires an understanding of the mechanisms driving the identified associations. Particularly, clarifying the extent to which non-taste-related pathways contribute to these associations can inform how to translate taste-related variant-diet associations so as to advance precision nutrition efforts. For example, TAS2R38 bitter taste receptor variants may influence eating behaviors through bitter taste perception, or through non-taste-related pathways of appetite hormone secretion [55] and postprandial glycemia [56].

Our results must be interpreted in the context of several limitations. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences; and without taste perception data in the AOC, we could not confirm the portability of taste-related variants for capturing taste perceptions in this cohort. To focus on specific variant-diet relations, we did not consider combined or interactive effects of multiple variants or tastes which may more comprehensively capture the influence of taste perception on eating behaviors [5, 6, 21]. We also did not consider adherence to different diet patterns, such as vegetarianism, that likely shape food choices, though this is unlikely to be a major source of variability in the AOC dietary data as fewer than 5% of patients reported following a prescribed diet at the time of dietary assessment. Relying on self-reported dietary data introduced the potential for recall bias, and seasonal fluctuations in food availability and differences in timing of data collection relative to patients’ MI events may have introduced additional sources of variability. While the FFQ used to collect the dietary data was also not formally validated, it was based on an FFQ designed and validated for the AOC [34, 35] and was expanded to better capture variation in diet habits over time. Estimates from the FFQ also show expected associations with alcohol and coffee intake, fatty acid intake and cardiometabolic risk factors [30, 57, 58], supporting its validity. Statistical power to detect main and sex-specific effects was limited by the modest sample size and unequal sex distributions; and we could not account for the potentially altered taste perceptions and reduced taste bud regenerations from high exposure to polypharmacy and chronic inflammation among AOC participants [25], which potentially diluted the detectable genetic signal. Finally, our focus on older Dutch adults residing throughout the Netherlands with a prior MI limits the generalizability of our findings; however, this also enhanced the novelty and clinical relevance of our study to a patient population at elevated CVD risk. Future replication of these findings in independent cohorts and extrapolation to broader measures of diet quality and downstream cardiometabolic risk factors will be required to translate these insights into tailored dietary recommendations for secondary cardiovascular risk reduction in patient populations.

Conclusions

Identifying genetic factors that shape eating behaviors is essential for advancing precision nutrition strategies designed to reduce cardiometabolic disease risk. Among ~4000 adult patients with a prior MI, we identified several significant associations between variants previously related to bitter and sweet taste perception with reported frequency of food and beverage intakes and taste-modifying behaviors, with some evidence of sex-specificity. We also provide candidate loci for replication and experimental investigations which may shed light on novel sensory mechanisms in eating behaviors. Collectively, these findings support the benefit of leveraging taste-related variants when tailoring dietary recommendations for cardiovascular disease patients, providing new approaches to advance the effectiveness of precision nutrition strategies for reducing secondary cardiovascular disease risk.
