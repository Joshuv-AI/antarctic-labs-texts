Quantum Information Ordering and Differential Privacy
Ayanava Dasgupta, Naqueeb Ahmad Warsi, Masahito Hayashi

Abstract
We study quantum differential privacy (QDP) by defining a notion of the order of informativeness between two pairs of quantum states. In particular, we show that if the hypothesis testing divergence of the one pair dominates over that of the other pair, then this dominance holds for every f f -divergence. This approach completely characterizes ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) -QDP mechanisms by identifying the most informative ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) -DP quantum state pairs. We apply this to analyze the stability of quantum differentially private learning algorithms, generalizing classical results to the case δ > 0 \delta>0 . Additionally, we study precise limits for privatized hypothesis testing and privatized quantum parameter estimation, including tight upper-bounds on the quantum Fisher information under QDP. Finally, we establish near-optimal contraction bounds for differentially private quantum channels with respect to the hockey-stick divergence.

Introduction

A fundamental challenge in modern machine learning is the
trade-off between privacy and information extraction
.
In this work, we explicitly treat both sides:
privacy
(ensuring that algorithmic outputs do not reveal significant information about the input data
of the respondents) and the investigator’s goal to
extract as much useful information as possible
from data for accurate learning and estimation.
With the rapid advancement of machine learning, a key concern is about ensuring the
privacy
of learning algorithms, meaning that their outputs should not reveal significant information about the input data.
Differential privacy (DP) provides a rigorous mathematical framework to balance these opposing requirements.
Accordingly, we structure our contributions in three steps:
first step
(privacy),
second step
(information extraction under privacy constraints), and

third step
, the quantum channel setup, where the situation is more complicated,
and we mark the transition to each step explicitly in the text.

First step: privacy.

This step develops the privacy side of the trade-off from the
respondent
’s perspective by studying the
stability

[
1
,
2
]
of learning algorithms.
From the respondent’s viewpoint, privacy means that the inclusion or exclusion of their individual data should not materially affect the mechanism’s output, so that they can contribute data without fear of singled-out inference.
An algorithm is considered stable if its output does not change drastically when a single respondent’s data is changed; this point-wise insensitivity is precisely the respondent-centric guarantee we seek.
Differential privacy ((
\varepsilon,\delta
)-DP)
[
3
,
4
]
formalizes this requirement as a strong, mathematically precise form of stability: by ensuring that the output states (distributions) corresponding to any two neighboring datasets are close, an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm guarantees that no respondent can be reliably distinguished from the output alone
[
5
]
.

In the classical case, the stability of learning algorithms was studied in
[
6
]
only for the pure
\varepsilon
-DP (
\delta=0
) case.
The main aim of this manuscript is to generalize the result of
[
6
]
to the quantum learning scenario for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP.
To achieve this, we build a framework for studying quantum differentially private mechanisms ((
\varepsilon,\delta
)-DP).
This framework is inspired by
[
7
]
and is related to the problem of hypothesis testing (see for example,
[
8
]
).
The main aim of differential privacy is to ensure that the outputs of a private mechanism corresponding to two neighboring inputs are hard to distinguish.
To understand this, consider the following scenario.
Suppose inside a database of datasets, we have two datasets
S
and
T
such that there is exactly one individual, say
A
is present in set
S
and not in
T
, i.e.,
S\setminus(S\cap T)=\{A\}
and suppose there is a random oracle which is attached to the dataset
D\in\{S,T\}
. Any such oracle can be modeled by a conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}^{(D)}(O\mid I)
, where
O
is the output corresponding to the input query
I
. From the perspective of an attacker, the only way it can break the privacy of this oracle is by performing a hypothesis test on the output of the oracle to detect whether the individual
A
is a member of
(d)
or not.
From this discussion, it implies that the more informative the oracle is about the datasets, the less private it is.

To understand this notion of an oracle being more informative, consider a pair of oracles modeled as
\mathbb{P}^{(D)}(O\mid I)
and
\mathbb{Q}^{(D)}(O\mid I)
. We say that
\mathbb{P}^{(D)}(O\mid I)
is more informative than
\mathbb{Q}^{(D)}(O\mid I)
(represented as the order
\mathbb{P}^{(D)}(O\mid I)\succeq\mathbb{Q}^{(D)}(O\mid I)
or simply
\mathbb{P}^{(D)}(O\mid I)
dominates
\mathbb{Q}^{(D)}(O\mid I)
) if the pair
\{\mathbb{P}^{(S)}(O\mid I),\mathbb{P}^{(T)}(O\mid I)\}
is statistically easier to distinguish as compared to the pair
\{\mathbb{Q}^{(S)}(O\mid I),\mathbb{Q}^{(T)}(O\mid I)\}
. In
1951
, Blackwell
[
9
,
10
]
formalized this notion of comparing “informativeness” between two statistical experiments, where in
[
10
, Theorem 12.2.2]
, he showed that
\mathbb{P}^{(D)}(O\mid I)\succeq\mathbb{Q}^{(D)}(O\mid I)
if and only if there exists a Markov kernel
\mathcal{M}
such that
\mathbb{Q}^{(S)}(O\mid I)=\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{(S)}(O\mid I))
and
\mathbb{Q}^{(T)}(O\mid I)=\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{(T)}(O\mid I))
. Further, using the data-processing inequality (monotonicity property) we have
D_{f}(\mathbb{P}^{(S)}(O\mid I)\|\mathbb{P}^{(T)}(O\mid I))\geq D_{f}(\mathbb{Q}^{(S)}(O\mid I)\|\mathbb{Q}^{(T)}(O\mid I))
, where
D_{f}
is the
f
-divergences
[
11
]
. For the case when these oracles are differentially private (with the same privacy parameters), it is obvious that the above monotonicity would help us obtain security bounds of the less informative in terms of the one that is more informative.

Likewise, in the quantum case, we consider two quantum oracles whose outputs are represented by
\{\rho^{S},\rho^{T}\}
and
\{\sigma^{S},\sigma^{T}\}
. We say that
\{\rho^{S},\rho^{T}\}
is more informative than
\{\sigma^{S},\sigma^{T}\}
(represented as the order
\{\rho^{S},\rho^{T}\}\succeq\{\sigma^{S},\sigma^{T}\}
or simply
\{\rho^{S},\rho^{T}\}
dominates
\{\sigma^{S},\sigma^{T}\}
) if the pair
\{\rho^{S},\rho^{T}\}
is easier to distinguish as compared to the pair
\{\sigma^{S},\sigma^{T}\}
. From this, similar to the classical case, one would like to conclude that
D_{f}(\rho^{S}\|\rho^{T})\geq D_{f}(\sigma^{S}\|\sigma^{T})
, where
D_{f}
is the quantum
f
-divergences
[
12
]
. In Theorem
2
of this manuscript, we show that this conclusion holds,
but not via the monotonicity of quantum
f
-divergence
. Rather, we show that if the quantum hockey stick divergence
[
13
]
between the pair
\{\rho^{S},\rho^{T}\}
dominates over
\{\sigma^{S},\sigma^{T}\}
for every hockey-stick divergence parameter, then
D_{f}(\rho^{S}\|\rho^{T})\geq D_{f}(\sigma^{S}\|\sigma^{T})
holds. We note here that we do not use the monotonicity of
f
-divergences because that even if
\{\rho^{S},\rho^{T}\}\succeq\{\sigma^{S},\sigma^{T}\}
, it follows from
[
14
]
that there does not always exist a CPTP map which maps
\{\rho^{S},\rho^{T}\}
to
\{\sigma^{S},\sigma^{T}\}
. Further, Matsumoto in
[
15
]
showed that the existence of even a PTP (positive trace preserving) map can not be guaranteed.

Second step: information extraction under privacy constraints.

We now analyze, under the
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP constraints, how much useful information an investigator can still extract.
That is, we investigate the statistical inference of quantum privatized parameter using our quantum
f
-divergence dominance result
(Theorem
2
).
This is a common task where we want to guess a hidden number or parameter from data that has been made private.
How well the investigator can guess this number is limited by how much information the privacy method keeps.
In this manucript, this limit is examined through two different viewpoints: binary hypothesis testing and parameter estimation.

In the former scenario, we begin with the one-shot setting, where the investigator tries to distinguish between two nearby sources
\rho_{\theta_{0}}
and
\rho_{\theta_{1}}
(parametrized by two private parameters
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
) after their encoding has been privatized through an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanism. Although mainly theoretical, this scenario exposes the operational effect of privacy constraints. We show that, among all
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanisms, the hypothesis testing divergence is maximized by the weakest (most informative) privatized mechanism, and this optimality carries over to KL, hockey stick Rényi (see Definition
14
) and measured Rényi divergences. We then extend the analysis to the asymptotic regime and establish that the same privatized mechanism remains optimal. This yields a unified characterization of the fundamental limits of hypothesis testing under differential privacy.

In the regime of classical private parameter estimation, the limit of the investigator’s ability to extract information is measured by a quantity called the Fisher information. In the quantum case, we use a related measure called the Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative (SLD) Fisher information to judge performance
[
16
]
.
By using the idea of our “weakest” (most informative) private method, we can figure out the exact maximum SLD Fisher information the investigator can get while still respecting the
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP rule.
This work improves on past studies that only looked at the simpler case where
\delta=0

[
17
]
, and sets a hard limit on how accurate quantum estimations can be under the privacy condition.

Third step:
We have two kinds of extensions.

In the privacy of the quantum learning framework,
we can relax the
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP condition
by trusting the party, the data processor, who performs the learning algorithm.
That is,
motivated by the quantum learning framework of
[
18
,
19
]
, we propose a framework for a quantum learning algorithm which is “
1
-neighbor"
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. For this proposed learning framework, we study its stability by obtaining an upper-bound on the Holevo information (between the training data and the quantum output of the algorithm). Further, we show that for
\delta=0
, our upper-bound recovers
[
6
, Proposition 2]
in the classical case.

Another interesting problem
is to analyze quantum channels which are
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. Its classical version has been extensively studied in the literature via the contraction of output divergence-measured with respect to the output distributions induced by
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP channels-in comparison to the input divergence, which is defined over the input distributions of these channels; see, for example,
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
, and the references therein.
This contraction is studied using an integral representation which involves the hockey stick divergence
[
11
]
. We leverage our framework to first establish an almost tight upper-bound on the contraction coefficient of any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP CPTP map with respect to the hockey stick divergence.

In the process of studying the contraction for divergences, we observe that in the classical case, there exists a pure
(\varepsilon+\log(1/1-\delta))
-DP pair of distributions which can be obtained by
truncating
the original
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP pair. Further, we show that this truncated pair is not far (
O(\delta)
, in
L_{1}
distance) from the original pair. We use this truncated pair to obtain a meaningful upper-bound on the relative entropy (when it is well-defined) of the original
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP pair of distributions using the continuity of the relative entropy. This upper-bound recovers the known result for
\delta=0,

[
22
,
7
]
as a special case.

Moreover, these contraction-based analyses serve to connect the channel-level action with both privacy guarantees and investigator-agnostic limits on information extraction, by bounding how much input distinctions can be suppressed at the output, we obtain direct, worst-case control of output distinguishability (useful for hypothesis-testing-based privacy statements) and of output information quantities (useful for bounding mutual information, Fisher-information-based estimation limits).

Organization of this Manuscript and our Contributions

The rest of this manuscript is organized as follows:

•

In Section
II
, we list the essential notations, definitions, and facts which will be used throughout this manuscript.

•

f

D^{\alpha}_{H}{(\rho\|\sigma)}\leq-\log\max\left\{1-\delta-e^{\varepsilon}\alpha,e^{-\varepsilon}(1-\delta-\alpha),0\right\}

where

\rho

Using this in Lemma

-DP quantum states that dominates all other. Finally, in Theorem
2
, we show that the
f
-divergence of any pair of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP quantum states can be upper-bounded by that of the “weakest” (most-informative) pair of

-DP quantum states.

In Section

, we apply our framework to the regime of the quantum hypothesis testing and the quantum parameter estimation under privacy constraints. In particular, in Theorem

-DP mechanism.

In Section

In Section

Definition 4

(Classical minimun type-II error)

Given a pair of probability distributions

\beta(\alpha|P_{1}\|P_{2}):=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]\\

Definition 5

(Classical hypothesis testing divergence)
.

For any pair of probability distributions

Fact 16
.

Consider
\rho,\rho^{\prime},\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
.
Then, the following inequality holds:

D(\rho\|\sigma)\leq D(\rho\|\rho^{\prime})+D_{\max}(\rho^{\prime}\|\sigma).

(29)

Proof.

By the definition of the max-relative entropy, we have

\rho^{\prime}\leq e^{D_{\max}(\rho^{\prime}\|\sigma)}\,\sigma.

Equivalently,

\sigma\geq e^{-D_{\max}(\rho^{\prime}\|\sigma)}\,\rho^{\prime}.

Since the logarithm is operator monotone, this implies

\log\sigma\succeq\log\rho^{\prime}-D_{\max}(\rho^{\prime}\|\sigma)\,\mathbb{I}.

Multiplying both sides by
-\rho
and taking the trace (which reverses the inequality), we obtain

-\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\log\sigma]\leq-\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\log\rho^{\prime}]+D_{\max}(\rho^{\prime}\|\sigma).

Adding
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\log\rho]
to both sides gives

\mathrm{Tr}[\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)]\leq\mathrm{Tr}[\rho(\log\rho-\log\rho^{\prime})]+D_{\max}(\rho^{\prime}\|\sigma),

which can be written as

D(\rho\|\sigma)\leq D(\rho\|\rho^{\prime})+D_{\max}(\rho^{\prime}\|\sigma).

This completes the proof of Fact
16
.

(2)

Taking the maximum over all

Quantum Information Ordering and Differential Privacy

IV-A

Quantum ordering of informativeness

In
[
9
,
10
]
, Blackwell introduced a notion of an ordering of classical informativeness between two pairs of distributions
\{P_{2},P_{2}\}
and
\{Q_{1},Q_{2}\}
. In particular, he proved the following proposition,

Proposition 1

(Blackwell’s Theorem of Informativeness Ordering
[
10
, Theorems
12.2.2
&
12.4.2
]
)
.

Given two pairs of probability distributions
\left(P_{1},P_{2}\right)
and
\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)
over two finite sets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, respectively, the following statements are equivalent,

1.

\left\{P_{1},P_{2}\right\}\succeq_{B}\left\{Q_{1},Q_{2}\right\}
.

2.

D^{\alpha}_{H}{(P_{1}\|P_{2})}\geq D^{\alpha}_{H}{(Q_{1}\|Q_{2})}
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
, where
D^{\alpha}_{H}{(P_{1}\|P_{2})}
and
D^{\alpha}_{H}{(Q_{1}\|Q_{2})}
are the classical hypothesis testing divergence of order
\alpha
(see Definition
5
) between the pair of distributions
\left\{P_{1},P_{2}\right\}
and
\left\{Q_{1},Q_{2}\right\}
respectively.

3.

There exists a stochastic map
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
such that
Q_{i}=\mathcal{T}(P_{i})
for each
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Analogous to Blackwell’s order of classical informativeness (as mentioned in Proposition
1
), we discuss a quantum version of it, which provides an ordering of quantum statistical experiments based on their distinguishability. Using the quantum hypothesis testing divergence as the underlying measure, we formalize when one pair of quantum states can be considered more informative than another. We further establish that this dominance relation implies a corresponding ordering across all quantum
f
-divergences, thereby linking hypothesis testing quantities to a broad class of information-theoretic measures.

We now make the following definition.

Definition 22

(Quantum information order)
.

Consider two pairs of quantum states
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
. We say that the pair
\left(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\right)
dominates (or, is more informative than) the pair
\left(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\right)
(denoted as
\left\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\right\}\succeq_{Q}\left\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\right\}
), if for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, the following holds,

D^{\alpha}_{H}{(\rho_{1}\|\rho_{2})}\geq D^{\alpha}_{H}{(\sigma_{1}\|\sigma_{2})}.

(57)

Intuitively, the order
\left\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\right\}\succeq_{Q}\left\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\right\}
indicates that the states
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
are more distinguishable as compared to the states
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
.

Unlike the classical case, in the quantum setting, from the results of
[
15
]
and of
[
14
, Lemma
18
& Corollary
19
]
, it is known that (
57
)
does not
always imply the existence of a CP-TP map
\mathcal{E}
such that
\sigma_{i}=\mathcal{E}(\rho_{i})
for each
i\in\{1,2\}
. This yields that we
can not
invoke the monotonicity property of quantum divergences (see Definition
18
) to conclude that
\mathbb{D}(\rho_{1}\|\rho_{2})\geq\mathbb{D}(\sigma_{1}\|\sigma_{2})
.

However, we can circumvent this issue by directly showing that if for any two pairs of quantum states
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
, (
57

f
-divergence (see Definition
12
) between the former pair dominates that of the latter pair. We formalize this claim in the Theorem below.

Theorem 1
.

Consider two pairs of quantum states
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
, for which the following holds,

D^{\alpha}_{H}{(\rho_{1}\|\rho_{2})}\geq D^{\alpha}_{H}{(\sigma_{1}\|\sigma_{2})},\forall\alpha\in[0,1].

(58)

Then, for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
(see Definition
12

D_{f}(\rho_{1}\|\rho_{2})\geq D_{f}(\sigma_{1}\|\sigma_{2}),

whenever
D_{f}
is well-defined (see Definition
12
) for the pairs
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})

Before proceeding with the proof of Theorem

Consider

IV-B

Differential privacy: A quantum hypothesis testing perspective

In this subsection, we consider differential privacy from a quantum hypothesis testing perspective. The main goal of quantum differential privacy is to ensure that a quantum mechanism produces output states that are nearly indistinguishable when applied to neighboring quantum inputs. Formally, for two neighboring quantum inputs producing output states
\rho,\sigma
, the distinguishability between
\rho
and
\sigma
must be limited by the privacy parameters. Intuitively, this means that even if an investigator (adversary) knows the mechanism and observes its output, the investigator should not be able to reliably infer which quantum input (respondent) generated it. More formally, with high probability, no hypothesis test—regardless of the investigator’s strategy—can reliably infer the respondent’s individual contribution from the output.

Motivated by the close relation between privacy and hypothesis testing, in the lemma below, we give an equivalent condition for a pair of quantum states to be
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP (see Definition
19

D^{\alpha}_{H}{(\rho\|\sigma)}\leq-\log f_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\alpha),\forall\alpha\in[0,1],

(63)

or, equivalently,

\beta(\alpha|\rho\|\sigma)\geq f_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\alpha),\forall\alpha\in[0,1],

(64)

f_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\alpha):=\max\left\{1-\delta-e^{\varepsilon}\alpha,e^{-\varepsilon}(1-\delta-\alpha),0\right\},

for any
\alpha\in[0,1]

-B

In the previous setting,

To discuss this case,

Untrusted data processor

One may consider the scenario where the third party, namely the data processor, cannot be trusted.
To address this case, we study the untrusted-data-processor scenario.
In such a scenario, the learning algorithm must be independent of the training data
s
and is represented by the family of maps
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
.
Accordingly, we adopt the following notation in place of (
103
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\rho_{s}):=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{N}_{w}(\rho_{s})\otimes|w\rangle\langle w|,

(119)

which can be regarded as the algorithm itself.
Since the investigator has access only to
\mathcal{N}_{w}(\rho_{s})
, Definition
24
is modified as follows.

Definition 26
.

An algorithm
\mathcal{N}
is said to be a
1
-neighbor
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP support-consistent learning algorithm with an untrusted data processor if and only if the following conditions hold:

1.

The quantum instrument
\mathcal{N}
must satisfy
1
-neighbor
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. That is, for every
s\overset{1}{\sim}s^{\prime}
(see Definition
3
), the following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\Lambda\mathcal{N}(\rho_{s})]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{Tr}[\Lambda\mathcal{N}(\rho_{s^{\prime}})]+\delta,

(120)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\Lambda\mathcal{N}(\rho_{s^{\prime}})]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{Tr}[\Lambda\mathcal{N}(\rho_{s})]+\delta,

(121)

for every
0\preceq\Lambda\preceq\mathbb{I}
.
These conditions ensure privacy during the learning process.

2.

The algorithm must be support-consistent. Formally, for each
s\overset{1}{\sim}s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
, we require:

\operatorname{supp}(\mathcal{N}(\rho_{s}))=\operatorname{supp}(\mathcal{N}(\rho_{s^{\prime}})).

(122)

Since
1
-neighbor
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP support-consistent learning algorithms with an untrusted data processor form a special subclass of
1
-neighbor
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP support-consistent learning algorithms, any algorithm in this subclass satisfies all properties of the general class.

However, there remains a possibility that the untrusted data processor possesses additional information about the training data
s
, even though they only have access to the learning output
\mathcal{N}(\rho_{s})
.
To mitigate this risk, we introduce the following concept.

Definition 27
.

Consider two learning algorithms
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
and
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}\}_{w}
.
We say that the algorithm
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}\}_{w}
is more informative with respect to the states
\{\rho_{s}\}_{s}
than the algorithm
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
if there exists a family of CP-TP maps
\{\Gamma_{w}\}_{w}
such that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{w}\circ\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}(\rho_{s})=\mathcal{N}_{w}(\rho_{s})\quad\text{for all }w\text{ and }s.

(123)

Furthermore,
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}\}_{w}
is said to be strictly more informative with respect to
\{\rho_{s}\}_{s}
than
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
if
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}\}_{w}
is more informative than
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
, but the converse does not hold.

Finally, we say that a learning algorithm
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
is information-theoretically admissible ITA with respect to the states
\{\rho_{s}\}_{s}
if no other learning algorithm is strictly more informative than
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
.

If a learning algorithm
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
is not ITA, then the investigator could construct another algorithm
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}\}_{w}
that is strictly more informative with respect to
\{\rho_{s}\}_{s}
than
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
.
In such cases, we cannot assume that the investigator executes
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
, and therefore the notion of
1
-neighbor
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP cannot be applied to
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
.

Conversely, when
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
is ITA and the investigator requires its output, we can reasonably assume that the investigator performs the algorithm
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
.

In fact, the concept “more informative” is similar to the opposite concept of “degraded”
[
45
,
38
]
.
However, in order that
\mathcal{N}_{w}
is degraded of
\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}
,
the relation (
123
) needs to hold for any input state
instead of specific states
\{\rho_{s}\}_{s}

while our “more informative” requires the relation (
123
) only
for the states
\{\rho_{s}\}_{s}
.
Therefore,
if
\mathcal{N}_{w}
is degraded of
\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}
,

\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}
is more informative with respect to the states

Lemma 5
.

Assume that all states
\{\rho_{s}\}_{s}
commute with each other.
If no CP-TP map
\Gamma
satisfies

\Gamma\left(\sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{N}_{w}(\rho_{s})\otimes|w\rangle\langle w|\right)=\rho_{s}\quad\text{for any }s,

then the learning algorithm
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
is not ITA.

Proof.

Choose a basis
\{|x\rangle\}
that diagonalizes
\rho_{s}
.
In this basis, the state can be expressed as

\rho_{s}=\sum_{x}P_{X|s}(x)\,|x\rangle\langle x|

(124)

on system
X
.
Next, define the instrument
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}\}_{w}
by

\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}(|x\rangle\langle x|):=|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes\mathcal{N}_{w}(|x\rangle\langle x|),

(125)

where the output system is
XB^{\prime}
.
Recall that the original learning algorithm
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
outputs on system
B^{\prime}
.
Under this construction,
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}\}_{w}
is strictly more informative than
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
.

Suppose, for contradiction, that
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
is more informative than
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}\}_{w}
.
Then there exist CP-TP maps
\{\Gamma_{w}\}_{w}
such that

\Gamma_{w}(\mathcal{N}_{w}(\rho_{s}))=\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}(\rho_{s})\quad\text{for all }s.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{B^{\prime}W}\Bigg[\sum_{w}\Gamma_{w}(\mathcal{N}_{w}(\rho_{s}))\otimes|w\rangle\langle w|\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{B^{\prime}W}\Bigg[\sum_{w}\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}\Bigg(\sum_{x}P_{X|s}(x)|x\rangle\langle x|\Bigg)\otimes|w\rangle\langle w|\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{B^{\prime}W}\Bigg[\sum_{x}P_{X|s}(x)\sum_{w}\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}(|x\rangle\langle x|)\otimes|w\rangle\langle w|\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{B^{\prime}W}\Bigg[\sum_{x}P_{X|s}(x)|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes\sum_{w}\mathcal{N}_{w}(|x\rangle\langle x|)\otimes|w\rangle\langle w|\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x}P_{X|s}(x)|x\rangle\langle x|=\rho_{s},

(126)

which contradicts the assumption that no CP-TP map
\Gamma
satisfies

\Gamma\left(\sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{N}_{w}(\rho_{s})\otimes|w\rangle\langle w|\right)=\rho_{s}.

Therefore,
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}^{\prime}\}_{w}
is strictly more informative than
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
, and thus
\{\mathcal{N}_{w}\}_{w}
is not ITA.

In the classical setting, the above lemma implies that if a learning algorithm is ITA, then the investigator can recover the original information
\rho_{s}
.

can be replaced by the same condition with
\mathcal{N}_{s}(\rho_{s})
substituted by
\rho_{s}
.
This indicates that privacy cannot be relaxed by employing the learning algorithm.

However, in the quantum setting, ITA learning algorithms do exist.
For example, let
{\cal Z}=\{0,1\}
and define
\rho_{z}:=|\phi_{z,p}\rangle\langle\phi_{z,p}|
, where

|\phi_{z,p}\rangle:=\sqrt{1-p}|0\rangle+(-1)^{z}\sqrt{p}|1\rangle.

for

.
Let

In this case, any more informative algorithm with respect to
\{\rho_{s}\}_{s}
requires the same measurement.
Thus, it is meaningful to consider the security condition in Definition

In summary, the quantum setting provides a genuine advantage for Definition

In this section, we show some other applications of the dominance of quantum informativeness as discussed in Section

-DP mechanisms is to determine upper-bounds on the divergence with respect to the output induced by the mechanisms. To do this, we can easily invoke Theorem

(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP channel (defined below) for the case when at least the support of one of the output distributions is a subset of the support of the other distribution. We will accomplish this with the help of the integral representation of the relative entropy mentioned in Fact

(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP channels for the hockey stick divergence.

Towards this, we first formulate the channel-based setup of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP in both classical and quantum settings.
In the classical case, a channel from the system
\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{Y}

Assume that a respondent generates private data in
\mathcal{X}
and converts it to

\mathcal{Y}
via the channel

and an investigator can access only the system
\mathcal{Y}

In this case,
a
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP channel is formulated
as a generalization of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP in the following way.

Definition 28
.

A classical channel
P_{Y|X}

is defined to be
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP (locally differentially private) for some fixed
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
\delta\in[0,1]

if any pair of
x\neq x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
satisfies

P_{Y|X}(S|x)\leq e^{\varepsilon}P_{Y|X}(S|x^{\prime})+\delta,

(132)

for every subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
.
Further, for
\delta=0
, we denote
\mathcal{K}
to be a pure
\varepsilon
-LDP or just
\varepsilon
-LDP channel.

Now, we write the channel by using the map
\mathcal{K}
from
a distribution on
\mathcal{X}
to a distribution on
\mathcal{Y}

as
\mathcal{K}(P)(y):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{Y|X}(y|x)P(x)
.
This definition is rewritten as follows.

Lemma 6
.

A classical channel
\mathcal{K}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP,
if and only if
any pair of distributions
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
satisfies

\mathcal{K}(P)(S)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{K}(Q)(S)+\delta,

(133)

for every subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof.

When the condition (
133
) holds,
the classical channel
\mathcal{K}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP
by considering the case when
P
and
Q
are delta distributions.

Assume that
the classical channel
\mathcal{K}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP.
Given
x\in\mathcal{X}
, (

P_{Y|X}(S|x)\leq e^{\varepsilon}P_{Y|X}(S|x^{\prime})+\delta
for any
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Thus,

P_{Y|X}(S|x)=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}Q(x^{\prime})P_{Y|X}(S|x)\leq\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}Q(x^{\prime})(e^{\varepsilon}P_{Y|X}(S|x^{\prime})+\delta)=e^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{K}(Q)(S)+\delta

Then, we have

\mathcal{K}(P)(S)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P(x)P_{Y|X}(S|x)\leq=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P(x)(e^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{K}(Q)(S)+\delta)=e^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{K}(Q)(S)+\delta

-LDP channel is relaxed version of the standard definition of

-LDP channel

-LDP channels.
Now, as a quantum generalization of (

(

Remark 11
.

It is important to note that the bound obtained in Theorem

\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon+\log\frac{1}{1-\delta}

\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\tanh(\varepsilon)){\left\lVert P_{X}-Q_{X}\right\rVert}_{1}

VIII
Conclusion

In this work, motivated by Blackwell’s order of informativeness
[
9
,
10
]
between two statistical experiments, we define an order of informativeness in the quantum setting, which is based on the hypothesis testing divergence dominance. In the classical case, Blackwell’s order implies that there always exists a Markov kernel that maps the more informative statistical experiment to the lesser informative statistical experiment. However, in the quantum case, a CPTP map may not always exists
[
15
]
from a more informative pair of quantum states to the lesser informative pair of quantum states. Despite the lack of existence of such CPTP maps, we still upper-bound the
f
-divergence of lesser informative pair of quantum states by that of a more informative pair of quantum states. This approach allows us to fully characterize
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-QDP mechanisms.

We use this ordering to study the stability of differentially private quantum learning algorithms. Our stability result also generalizes (in the sense that
\delta>0
) the existing bound in the classical settings.

We also study the problem of quantum privatized hypothesis testing and quantum privatized parameter estimation, where the trade-off between privacy and statistical utility is characterized via the quantum hypothesis testing divergence and the quantum Fisher information, respectively. We derived explicit expressions for the maximal Fisher information achievable under quantum
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP constraints, thereby quantifying the fundamental limits of parameter estimation in the presence of quantum privacy mechanisms.

Further, we derive near-optimal bounds on the contraction coefficient of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP CP-TP maps with respect to the hockey stick divergence. This allows us to prove bounds on the relative entropy between the output pair induced by any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP classical channels.

We begin by invoking the operator monotonicity of the function

.
Since

Using (

\displaystyle D(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)\right]

Since (

114

To derive the tighter inequality, we now consider the following series of inequalities,

\displaystyle D(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho\log\rho-\rho\log\sigma\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho\log\rho-\rho\log\left(\sum_{b=1}^{m}P(b)\sigma_{b}\right)\right]
