Comprehensiveness and validity of a multidimensional assessment of patients with fibromyalgia: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Thomas Benz, Susanne Lehmann, Peter S. Sandor, Felix Angst
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFibromyalgia is a complex syndrome affecting all dimensions of biopsychosocial health and quality-of-life. The aim of the study was to quantify the cross-sectional and longitudinal validity of single measurement scales in specific construct domains and to examine how they combine to build a comprehensive, multidimensional outcome assessment of fibromyalgia before and after a standardized interdisciplinary pain program.MethodsThis prospective cohort study assessed 123 patients using 8 measures: the Short Form 36 (SF-36), the Multidimensional Pain Inventory (MPI), the Symptom Checklist-90-Revised (SCL-90-R), the Quantification Inventory for Somatoform Syndromes (QUISS), the Fibromyalgia Health Assessment Questionnaire (FHAQ), the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ), the Coping Strategy Questionnaire (CSQ), and the 6-Minute Walking Distance (6MWD). The comprehensiveness of the constructs and their overlap were quantified cross-sectionally and longitudinally by bivariate correlations, explorative factor analysis, and effect sizes (ESs).ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 48.6 years (+/-9.8); 86.2% were female. Factor analysis revealed 2 factors with a totally explained variance of 44.5%-64.5%. At the baseline and follow-up cross-sectional analyses, mental health and psychosocial factors loaded more strongly on the first, mental health factor, especially the 4 SCL-90-R scales (factor loads 0.75-0.90) than on the second, predominantly physical function factor. Longitudinally (score differences), the predominantly physical function factor was dominant, with loads of 0.61 (FHAQ), 0.66 (6MWD), and SF-36 and MPI pain (0.68 each). Maximum cross-sectional correlations were up to 0.89 between depression and anxiety, up to 0.87 between pain and interference on the MPI (0.79 longitudinally), and up to 0.83 between depression and catastrophizing. Longitudinally all scales showed improvements. The greatest effects were measured on MPI Pain severity (ES = 0.78) and Interference (0.71).ConclusionsAll 23 health parameters examined had moderate to high factor loads on at least one factor, evidence of the multidimensionality of fibromyalgia. Mental health and function explained the substantial, partially comparable variances of the clinical characteristics. Both domains are almost equally important in describing the syndrome. An accurate description of fibromyalgia requires comprehensive assessment of a broad spectrum of constructs, including fatigue, somatization and coping strategies, particularly catastrophizing, in addition to the traditional pain, function and mental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09270-y.

Background

Fibromyalgia is characterized by chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain and a wide range of other symptoms, including joint stiffness, sleep disturbances, fatigue, reduced function, cognitive difficulties, anxiety, depression, and other manifestations [1–4]. The burden of the condition is substantial and includes reduced health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL), impairment, disability, and limited participation [1, 4]. Fibromyalgia affects persons of all ages, predominantly females; it has an estimated mean point prevalence in the general population worldwide of 2.7% (range 0.4% to 9.3%) [5, 6].

The development of the diagnostic criteria of fibromyalgia in the past 30 years illustrates the diagnostic challenges and the complexity of the syndrome [4, 7, 8]. The European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) recommendations for the management of fibromyalgia favor a biopsychosocial approach, with comprehensive assessment of pain, function and psychosocial factors and multidisciplinary treatment [9]. Furthermore, to achieve the primary goal of improving HRQoL, an individualized management strategy adapted to personal goals is recommended, combining non-pharmacological and, if necessary, pharmacological treatment modalities [8, 9]. So far, there is no consensus and only limited evidence as to the best approach and content of multidisciplinary treatment programs. Moreover, a systematic review found only limited scientific evidence for the effectiveness of multidisciplinary rehabilitation programs in working age adults with fibromyalgia [10, 11]. Nevertheless, several interdisciplinary, biopsychosocial rehabilitation programs reported positive treatment effects on pain intensity, symptom impact and disability [12–16].

Core outcome measurement sets for clinical trials have been published for various chronic pain conditions, such as fibromyalgia, low back pain, and chronic pain in general, as well as for interdisciplinary multimodal pain therapy [17–21]. The recommendations by the Initiative on Methods, Measurement, and Pain Assessment in Clinical Trials (IMMPACT) list 6 outcome domains for chronic pain [17]. To assess the efficacy and effectiveness of interdisciplinary multimodal pain therapy, the VAPAIN consensus process resulted in agreement on 8 core outcome domains to be used in every study [20]. Specifically for the assessment of physical function and participation, IMMPACT and the OMERACT (Outcome Measures in Rheumatology Clinical Trials) initiative recommend the use of disease specific measures combined with generic measures of physical functioning, to be assessed by the 3 domains of self-reported physical functioning, examiner measured physical function and activity levels [21].

However, only the OMERACT initiative proposed core domains specifically for fibromyalgia [18]. OMERACT agreed that all fibromyalgia clinical trials should include the domains pain, tenderness, fatigue, sleep disturbance, patient global response, and multidimensional function. They further recommended that cognitive dysfunction and depression should be measured in some trials; and they classified stiffness, anxiety, functional imaging, and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers as domains of research interest for discretionary inclusion in trials [18]. A wide variety of measurement instruments have been proposed and used to assess these core domains in fibromyalgia trials, but there is as yet no agreement on a particular set of outcome measures for the condition [22, 23].

Fibromyalgia is known to be complex, as is the biopsychosocial approach recommended to manage it. The use of single outcome measures that assess and quantify individual symptoms is arguably not the optimum approach for describing fibromyalgia fully [22]. The aim of the study was to quantify the cross-sectional and longitudinal validity of single measurement scales in specific construct domains and to examine how they combine to build a comprehensive, multidimensional outcome assessment of fibromyalgia before and after a standardized interdisciplinary pain program. We hypothesized that in the fibromyalgia construct psychosocial factors, such as depression, anxiety and somatization, would be dominant. It was further hypothesized that these psychosocial factors would demonstrate large construct overlaps and explain much of the variance of the syndrome.

Methods

Patients

The patients in this prospective cohort study were recruited at the pain center of the “Rehaklinik” rehabilitation clinic in Bad Zurzach, Switzerland. On their admission to the clinic, all patients attending the Zurzach Interdisciplinary Pain Program (ZISP) between April 2011 and August 2023, aged ≥ 18 years and with persistent pain for ≥ 3 months were invited to participate in this study. All patients with chronic widespread pain, fibromyalgia or chronic pain in several quadrants of the body were asked to fill out the fibromyalgia diagnostic criteria questionnaire [24]. Only those patients fulfilling the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 2016 diagnostic criteria for fibromyalgia were included, irrespective of the initial admission diagnosis of the referring family physician, internist or rheumatologist [24]. These criteria were: ((Widespread Pain Index (WPI) ≥ 7 AND Symptom Severity Score (SSS) ≥ 5) OR (WPI 4–6 AND SSS ≥ 9) present for at least 3 months). Exclusion criteria were severe somatic or mental conditions that prevented participation in the pain program, insufficient German language reading and writing skills, impeding completion of the assessment questionnaire, and refusal to participate in the pain program or the study.

Before joining the ZISP and based on their admission reports, potential participants had already been contacted by telephone to evaluate the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients whose German was too weak to follow the group program (an exclusion criterion of the ZISP) were admitted to a different, individualized pain treatment program in our clinic. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the Local Ethics Commission (Health Department of Aarau, Switzerland, EK AG 2008/026).

Intervention

The ZISP is a standardized inpatient pain management program for the treatment of chronic musculoskeletal pain disorders, such as fibromyalgia. The comprehensive and interdisciplinary program includes non-pharmacological treatment modalities with a specific focus on active patient participation, patient education, and self-management strategies. The treatment is tailored to individual symptoms and disability and is based on individual goal setting. It includes non-pharmacological therapies for fibromyalgia recommended by the EULAR, such as aerobic and strengthening training, behavioral therapies, spa therapy, meditative movement therapies, such as Qigong/tai chi [9]. Further therapies are active physiotherapy, individual psychotherapy, participation in a pain coping group, information and education sessions on the pathophysiology of pain mechanisms and the management of chronic disabling pain, relaxation therapy, humor therapy (till June 2021), horticultural therapy, nursing care, and regular medical consultations, including pharmacotherapy. Details of the group-based program of 4 weeks’ duration have been published elsewhere [12, 13, 25].

Measures

The assessment of our pain program (ZISP) began in 1998, based on clinical experience and the available assessments [13]. At that time, there were no recommended core outcome sets for clinical trials. Taking into account the experiences of that period, especially the results of the responsiveness study and the IMMPACT recommendations of 2005, the assessment set was revised and the present study was started in 2011 [13, 17, 26]. Weakly responsive scales were eliminated and fresh assessments added with the goal of designing a more complete assessment of the complex biopsychosocial construct of fibromyalgia. Psychometric properties were published in our previous paper [26]. Cronbach’s alpha values of the scales included in the assessment set are reported in Table 3.

Sociodemographic data, including potentially confounding parameters, such as age, gender, occupation (working capacity), living situation, sports habits, and formal education attainment levels, were recorded on admission to the clinic, using a standardized form that has been used in many previous studies [12, 13]. Comorbidities were retrieved from the patient’s medical history.

The assessment set comprised the following: the Medical Outcomes Study Short Form 36 Health Survey (SF-36), a generic patient-reported outcome measurement instrument, is a commonly used and widely recommended and tested self-report questionnaire for HRQoL in chronic pain conditions including fibromyalgia [28, 29]. It comprehensively measures physical, mental and psychosocial health in 36 items categorized in 8 health domains: Bodily pain, Physical functioning, Role physical, General health, Vitality, Social functioning, Role emotional, and Mental health [30]. The German version was used [31]. Scores on the scale range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better functioning.

The West Haven-Yale Multidimensional Pain Inventory (MPI) self-report questionnaire assesses psychosocial and behavioral aspects of pain. The 51 items of the MPI are divided into 11 subscales and grouped into 3 sections: (1) Pain impact: Pain severity, Interference due to pain, Life control, Affective distress (synonymously described as negative mood), Support, (2) Response by significant others: Negative, Solicitous and Distracting responses, and (3) Activities: Household chores, Outdoor Work, Activities away from home, and Social activities [32]. The German version of this questionnaire was shown to be internally consistent and valid in chronic pain patients [33]. In this study, based on previous responsiveness analysis of the MPI and to reduce the patient response burden, only the Pain impact section of the MPI was used [26].

The Symptom Checklist 90-Revised (SCL-90-R) is a self-report instrument that measures a wide range of symptoms of psychological distress and psychiatric illness across 9 domains with 90 items on a 5-point scale ranging from none to extreme [34–36]. It has been widely used in patients with fibromyalgia and other conditions. In this study, 4 scales were assessed: Somatization (12 items), Anxiety (10 items), Depression (13 items) and Anger-Hostility (6 items). Its validity is documented by its extensive use in psychiatric conditions. The Swiss Standard German version of 1976 used in several studies was used in this study [37, 38]. This 1976 Version is a word for word translation of the SCL-90-R published by the developer Derogatis LR [36].

The Quantification Inventory for Somatoform Syndromes (QUISS) assesses syndrome severity and treatment outcome in clinical and research settings for common cognitive and behavioral domains of somatoform disorders [39]. Both the patient- and the observer-rated versions of the original QUISS consist of 18 items, can be used separately, and have shown satisfactory psychometric properties [39]. In this study, only item 2, comprising 41 somatoform symptoms from the German, patient-rated version was used [40]. The score was calculated by summarizing the individual items/listed complaints of item 2. To facilitate comparison with the other instruments, it was converted to a score ranging from 0 (= maximum number of somatoform symptoms (= 41)) to 100 (= no somatoform symptoms).

The Fibromyalgia Health Assessment Questionnaire (FHAQ) is an 8-item self-report instrument derived from the Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ), measuring fibromyalgia-specific functional impairment [41]. Each question rates a specific activity, such as bending, dressing, getting up from a chair, reaching overhead, etc. The items are scored between 0 (= no impairment/performed without difficulty) and 3 (= unable to perform). The total score is reported as the mean of the 8 items. For the purposes of comparability with the other scales, the scoring system of the FHAQ has been inverted so that 0 = maximum, 100 = no impairment for the calculations in this paper. The validated German version of the FHAQ was used in this study [42].

The Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ) was developed to assess symptoms and problems associated with fibromyalgia and response to therapy [43]. This self-report questionnaire includes questions on physical activity, work/housework, pain, fatigue, morning tiredness, stiffness, anxiety, and depression. It has good psychometric properties and has been translated into several languages [43–45]. The original total score of all items ranges from 0 (= no impairment) to 100 (= maximum impairment) and was classified as a useful outcome measure of functional disability and health in clinical trials in fibromyalgia [46]. Here, too, for comparability purposes the scoring system of the FIQ was inverted (0 = maximum, 100 = no impairment) and applied to the validated German version of the original FIQ [44]. An updated and revised version of the FIQ (FIQ-R) with good psychometric properties was published in English in 2009 [47]. A translated, cross-culturally adapted version of the FIQ-R questionnaire was not available in German when this study started. Therefore, the reliable and valid German version of the original FIQ was used throughout.

The Coping Strategy Questionnaire (CSQ) self-rates pain management strategies by six cognitive pain management scales (attentional distraction, reinterpreting, self-instructions, ignoring, praying and hoping, catastrophizing) and two behavioral pain coping scales (activity enhancement and pain behavior) [48]. This study used only the 6 questions on the catastrophizing scale and one question on Pain control, based on previous responsiveness analysis [49]. The validated German version of the CSQ was used in this paper [50].

In addition to the PROMs described above, the 6-Minute Walking Distance test (6MWD) was used as a performance-based measure (PBM). The 6MWD quantifies the distance walked in 6 min on a pre-measured 100-meter flat distance. This easy-to-use functional capacity test requires minimal equipment: a pre-measured flat walking surface with interval markers every 5 m, markers at the end of the walking distance, a timer, and a chair for resting if needed. Longer walking distances indicate better performance [51]. The 6MWD is a reliable measurement in fibromyalgia and recommended for assessing physical function in chronic pain trials [21, 52].

Sample size

We chose two criteria to determine the sample size: first, a factor analysis should comprise at least 5 cases per variable, i.e. 5*23(scales) = 115 patients, in order to result in finite, valid data [53]. Second, patient recruitment was continued until the sample reached the size where small effects (ES according to Kazis ≥ 0.21) were statistically significant from 0, which means that the 95% confidence interval excluded 0 [54].

Analysis

Patients’ health and HRQoL were measured on admission to the clinic, setting the baseline measurement before therapy, and again on discharge from the pain program after 4 weeks’ treatment (follow-up). In order to determine the scales, the instrument-specific “missing rules” had to be fulfilled: at least 50% of the items had to be completed for each of the SF-36 scales and 2/3 to 5/6 of the items of the SCL-90-R in accordance with that instrument’s manual [31, 55]. For the other instruments no missing rules were provided in the initial description of the original questionnaires; we therefore applied the 2/3 rule (completion of 67% of the items required to determine the score), as previously reported [56].

As mentioned, to allow comparison of the different measurement instruments, all scores were transformed into 0 = maximum pain/no function/worst limitation/worst health to 100 = no pain/full function/no limitation/best health, which is the score range originally described for the SF-36. The exception is the 6MWD, where a higher value = longer distance walked and better performance [30]. All analyses were performed using the statistical software package IBM SPSS 29.01 for Windows® (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Mean values with standard deviations and, for effect quantification, effect sizes (ESs) according to Kazis and standardized response mean (SRMs) according to Liang were calculated. The ES according to Kazis is defined as the score difference (follow-up – baseline) divided by the standard deviation of the baseline score and is identical to the Glass’s Delta [54, 57]. The SRM is defined as the score difference (follow-up – baseline) divided by the standard deviation of the score differences (follow-up – baseline) and is identical to t/√n, t = t value for the pair-wise one-sample t-test of the score differences, n = number of patients [54, 58]. For both the ES and the SRM a positive value of >0.80 is considered as large, 0.50–0.79 moderate, 0.20–0.49 small, and 0.00–0.19 very small improvements. Negative values reflect worsening.

To measure construct validity, bivariate Pearson correlations and factor analyses were calculated [59–61]. The correlation reflects the strength of the association between two variables. There is no general rule for the classification of correlation coefficients, but a correlation coefficient (r) above 0.75 can be considered an excellent association, 0.50–0.75 moderate to good, 0.25–0.50 fair, and 0.00–0.25 little or no relationship [60].

Exploratory factor analysis is a multivariate correlation analysis aimed at reducing the number of dimensions so as to facilitate data overview, identify common constructs and explain the nature of their interrelations [53, 60, 62, 63]. Since fibromyalgia represents a broad spectrum of physical, psychological and cognitive symptoms, factor analysis helps to identify and validate the underlying common dimensions, which contribute to capture the syndrome [63]. Factor analysis helps to elucidate the fibromyalgia syndrome’s multidimensionality and supports the development of robust assessment instruments. It helps to explain the dimensions of the clinical experience and quantifies interrelated constructs [63].

Principal component analysis was applied using Varimax rotation with Kaiser normalization. For the extraction of the number of factors of the factor analysis, “Velicer’s minimum average partial (MAP) test” and “parallel analysis” were used [53]. The factor load reflects the construct convergence of the scale to the major underlying, common dimension of the factor. It can be interpreted as correlation and is in fact the orthogonal vector projection of the scale on the factor. Large factor loads indicate the representation of the same underlying construct, whereas small factor loads do not [53].

Factor analysis revealed 2 factors, mental health and function(+), for the baseline scores, follow-up scores and score differences (difference follow-up – baseline score) (Table 4). The total explained variance was 52.5% at baseline, 64.5% at follow-up, and 44.5% for the score differences. Overall, comparable scores loaded on the two factors, but not all scores loaded on the same factor in each of the 3 analyses.

Cross-sectional construct validity

At baseline, excellent associations and strong correlations (r ≥ 0.75) were observed between SF-36 Mental health and SCL-90-R Depression (0.75), between MPI Negative mood and Interference (0.75), between SCL-90-R Anxiety and SCL-90-R Depression (0.80), and between SCL-90-R Depression and CSQ Catastrophizing (0.77) (Additional file 1). Out of the total of 276 cross-sectional correlations, 63 (= 22.8%) were moderate to good (0.75–0.50). All other correlations (213/276 = 72.2%) were fair or showed little or no relationship (< 0.50).

At follow-up, in 26/276 (12.4%) correlations the associations were excellent (Additional file 2). In 131/276 (= 47.5%) correlations the associations were moderate to good. All other correlations (119/190 = 43.1%) were fair or showed little or no association (< 0.50).

Longitudinal construct validity

In the longitudinal construct validity (difference follow-up – baseline score), the highest and the only strong longitudinal correlation (r ≥ 0.75) was between MPI Interference and MPI Pain severity with r = 0.79 (Additional file 3). Moderate to good correlations were observed in 30/276 (= 10.9%) correlations. All other correlations (245/276 = 88.8%) were fair or showed little or no relationship (r < 0.50).

Discussion

This study investigated the comprehensiveness of a multidimensional, biopsychosocial assessment of patients with fibromyalgia. A set of 7 PROMs and 1 PBM, comprising 23 dimensions, was used to determine the cross-sectional and longitudinal validity of these measures, with much of the variance explained through factor analysis. Patients were assessed before and after participation in the ZISP, a standardized interdisciplinary pain management program for chronic musculoskeletal conditions, including fibromyalgia. Validity was quantified by inter-comparison of the scales of each validated instrument, as there is no “gold standard” for the evaluation of a specific construct of a scale [61].

There was improvement on all 23 health dimensions, with effect sizes (ES) ranging from ES = 0.18 (MPI Social and away from home activities) to 0.78 (MPI Pain severity) (Table 3). The most conspicuous improvements were in pain and pain-related disability (interference), the main symptom of fibromyalgia. In terms of the SRMs, the strongest effects (SRM ≥ 0.60) were observed on SF-36 Mental health, MPI Interference, SCL-90-R Depression and Anger-hostility, and CSQ Catastrophizing.

Accurate description of the broad symptomatology by a limited number of health dimensions is more difficult in fibromyalgia than in other disorders [62, 64–66]. Factor analysis identified 2 factors (mental health and function(+)) for the cross-sectional and the longitudinal data with inverse content of the dimensions. Mental health was the dominant factor in the cross-sectional analysis at baseline and at follow-up, explaining a variance of around 33% in both cases. This factor was mainly defined by all 4 SCL-90-R scales, with loads between 0.75 and 0.90, and to a lesser extent by high loads of the SF-36 Mental Health and the CSQ Catastrophizing scales.

Although its name suggests that it measures a similar domain, the MPI Negative mood scale seems to have a different construct. Mental health dimensions appear to play a major role in the description of the fibromyalgia syndrome, with anxiety being at least as important as depression [64, 65]. The key role of mental health is supported by the definition of fibromyalgia, which includes symptoms of depression and somatization in the symptom severity score [24].

The second factor (function(+)) was mainly defined by physical function and other consequences of chronic pain (pain interference). In addition, this domain is characterized by Negative mood (MPI), number of somatoform symptoms (QUISS), Fatigue (SF-36 Vitality), Life control (MPI), and Social & away from home activities (MPI), which are dimensions of consequences of pain. While less dominant at baseline, this domain had equal weight at follow-up and explained more of the variance than the mental health factor.

This finding contrasts with our parallel analysis of patients with chronic low back pain, where the mental health domain was consistently dominant [62]. This divergent result may be attributable to fibromyalgia’s heavier burden/higher symptom levels with regard to function and the above listed dimensions of pain consequences compared to chronic low back pain, even though the pain levels were comparable. Factor analysis helps condense fibromyalgia’s multidimensional symptomatology into two manageable health domains by dimension reduction. This facilitating a better grasp and overview of the syndrome.

In the longitudinal measurement of the score differences, the 2-factor construct established in the cross-sectional measurement remained stable, but the function(+) factor explained somewhat more variance than the mental health factor. This is consistent with the somewhat greater improvements in pain and function compared to the other health dimensions. This may be explained by the fact that our pain program was dominated by physical and exercise therapy modalities and that the elements of psychotherapy and tuition in coping strategies focused on pain relief and functional improvement. Studies comparing the effects of therapy in fibromyalgia show that exercise and aerobic training have the largest effects on pain and function, while exercise and all other therapy modalities result in relatively lower effects on the psychosocial dimension [9].

The FHAQ, for example, focuses on the measurement of physical function, as did its predecessor the original HAQ, which was developed to measure functional impairment in patients with rheumatoid arthritis [41, 42]. This is borne out by the factor analysis and by the relatively high correlations of the FHAQ to the 6MWD and SF-36 Physical function. In contrast, the FIQ loads almost equally on both factors in all 3 factor analyses (Table 4). This corresponds to the construct of the FIQ, which was initially designed to cover all dimensions of the biopsychosocial health components equally [45]. This is confirmed by the correlation analysis in our data and in 5 comparable studies (Table 5). The 4 physical scales of the SF-36 (Physical functioning, Role physical, Bodily Pain, and General health) and the 4 psychosocial scales (Vitality, Social functioning, Role emotional, Mental health) showed comparable levels of correlation to the FIQ in the studies listed [67, 68].

While the mental health construct is predominantly defined by the 4 SCL-90-R scales (Somatization, Anxiety, Depression and Anger-hostility), their factor loads and correlations to the MPI Negative mood scale are surprisingly low, especially at baseline. One explanation might be the different lengths of the scales. The MPI Negative Mood scale consists of just 3 items: one item each for mood, irritation, and anxiety, whereas the SCL-90-R has 13 items for mood/depression, 6 items for irritation/anger, and 10 items for anxiety. This issue has been discussed in greater detail in Angst et al., 2024 [71].

Our parallel study of low back pain patients mentioned above, with the same intervention and aims, did not find the low construct overlap of the MPI Negative mood with the SCL-90-R in fibromyalgia [62]. The SF-36, MPI and SCL-90-R scales and the 6MWD were assessed, in addition to condition-specific instruments in both studies. The factor analysis for low back pain also revealed 2 factors for the baseline and follow-up scores and the score differences, but the pattern was similar in all 3 analyses. The first factor included psychosocial scores, with explained variances between 33.5% and 48.9%, which is higher than in the present study. The second factor included function and other consequences of chronic pain (pain interference) with explained variances ranging from 10.5% to 12.3%. The total explained variances between 44.0% (score differences) and 60.9% (follow-up scores), were, however, in a comparable range. The smaller differences in the variances explained by the first and the second factors in the present study indicate that in patients with fibromyalgia, mental health is closer to function and other consequences of chronic pain (pain interference), and that both need to be given equal weight in rehabilitation programs. This is also confirmed by the factor analysis of the score differences, where the two factors, respectively the loads of many scales, inversely load on the two factors of the score differences.

A strength of our study lies in the comparison of a large number of generic and condition-specific scales, both self- and clinician-rated, combined in a single set for the cross-sectional and longitudinal assessment of fibromyalgia. The wide range of complementary measurements and the comparison by factor and correlation analysis is to our knowledge unique in fibromyalgia literature. The scales of the instruments used have been validated in fibromyalgia trials and good psychometric properties are reported for most of them. The 6MWD was added as a PBM. The assessment set covers the whole construct of the biopsychosocial model and broad parts of the contents of the three core set recommendations (see Table 1). The strict ACR criteria of 2016 provided a compact cohort.

A possible limitation of our study is that the data were collected in one setting, which might limit the generalizability of the results. Moreover, this cohort represents a highly symptomatic and chronic subgroup of fibromyalgia patients, with long disease duration and elevated affective symptom scores, as shown in the comparison between patients’ and norm scores (Table 4 in [71]). However, the specific nature of our setting offers valuable insights into more severe clinical presentations, which may also apply to other populations and less intensive treatment contexts.

Moreover, while the measurements in this study covered a broad range of symptoms of fibromyalgia, the sleep disturbance dimension was not included although the OMERACT initiative recommended that it be assessed in all clinical trials in fibromyalgia [18]. Nevertheless, a number of aspects related to disturbed sleep are assessed by several of the measures in our study (Table 1). For example, the FIQ measures sleep disturbances indirectly. Fatigue, often a consequence of poor sleep, is measured by the SF-36 Vitality scale, the QUISS, and the FIQ. A loss of energy and vitality is captured by the SCL-90-R. Moreover, in both the DSM-5 and ICD-11, fatigue is a recognized symptom of depression and anxiety, and is therefore further covered through these broader constructs [71–73]. Future studies should include a specific instrument for sleep disturbances.

Conclusions

All 23 health parameters examined had moderate to high factor loads on at least one factor, evidence of the multidimensionality of fibromyalgia. Mental health and function explained the substantial, partially comparable variances of the clinical characteristics. Both domains are almost equally important in describing the syndrome, as demonstrated by the FIQ. An accurate description of fibromyalgia requires comprehensive assessment of a broad spectrum of constructs, including fatigue, somatization and coping strategies, particularly catastrophizing, in addition to the traditional dimensions of pain, function and mental health. In some special cases, the 6MWD provides valuable supplementary information.
