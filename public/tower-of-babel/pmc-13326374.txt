A study on the correlation between cardiac magnetic resonance characteristics and myocardial fibrosis in overweight and obese individuals
Authors: Lulu Liu, Yuheng Yao, Hongqing Yu, Jian Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOverweight and obesity are major risk factors for adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) can detect subclinical cardiac dysfunction through myocardial strain and quantify epicardial adipose tissue volume (EATV). This study aimed to investigate the differences in cardiac structure, function, myocardial strain, and EATV among individuals with normal weight, overweight, and obesity using CMR, and to evaluate their value for myocardial fibrosis (LGE positivity).MethodsIn this study, 192 subjects undergoing CMR were classified into control (n = 77), overweight (n = 37), and obesity (n = 78) groups based on body mass index (BMI). CMR-derived parameters included cardiac function, left ventricular global/segment strain (radial, circumferential, longitudinal), left atrial strain, EATV, and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). Differences among groups were compared. Spearman correlation analyzed relationships between BMI, EATV, and strain parameters. Logistic regression and ROC curve analyses assessed factors independently associated with LGE positivity.ResultsCompared to the control group, the overweight and obesity groups showed significantly increased left ventricular volumes, mass, and EATV, but decreased left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), global longitudinal strain (GLS), and left atrial reservoir strain (εs), with the most pronounced changes in the obesity group. EATV was highly correlated with BMI (r = 0.72). Both BMI and EATV showed low-to-moderate inverse correlations with impaired myocardial strain parameters (|r| = 0.22–0.31). Multivariate logistic regression identified higher BMI (OR = 1.22, 95% CI:1.07–1.40), larger EATV (OR = 1.03, 95% CI:1.01–1.04), and lower left ventricular apical segment radial strain (A-PRS) (OR = 0.93, 95% CI:0.87–0.98) as factors independently associated with LGE positivity.ConclusionsOverweight and obesity are associated with adverse cardiac remodeling, subclinical systolic dysfunction, impaired myocardial strain, and increased EATV. BMI, EATV, and A-PRS are factors independently associated with myocardial fibrosis. These findings underscore the presence of early cardiac alterations in the overweight stage, highlighting the need for early cardiovascular risk assessment in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02384-2.

Background

Globally, the prevalence of overweight and obesity continues to rise, presenting a significant public health challenge [1, 2]. Obesity is not only a major risk factor for metabolic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension, but also a key independent risk factor contributing to increased incidence and mortality from cardiovascular disease [3–5]. Under prolonged obesity, the heart undergoes a series of structural and functional remodelling to adapt to increased metabolic demands and heightened haemodynamic load. These adaptations include left ventricular hypertrophy, cardiac chamber enlargement, and early subclinical systolic dysfunction. Such alterations may ultimately progress to severe clinical outcomes such as heart failure [6]. Previous studies have demonstrated a significant positive correlation between weight gain and the rising incidence of heart failure [7]. Current research has revealed that overweight and obesity can induce subclinical alterations in ventricular structure and function [8].

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) is widely recognised as the gold standard for assessing cardiac structure, function, and myocardial tissue due to its high spatial resolution, superior tissue characterisation, and absence of radiation exposure [9]. In recent years, CMR strain tracking has gained widespread application. This non-invasive technique quantitatively assesses myocardial strain, a parameter demonstrating greater sensitivity than conventional ejection fraction for detecting early, subtle impairments in myocardial contractility [10, 11]. Concurrently, CMR serves as an accurate method for quantifying epicardial adipose tissue volume (EATV). As a metabolically active visceral fat, abnormal accumulation of epicardial fat directly contributes to myocardial fibrosis, insulin resistance, and atherosclerosis through local lipotoxicity, inflammatory cytokine release, and paracrine effects [12].

Myocardial fibrosis constitutes a common pathological basis for the progression of multiple cardiac diseases towards poor prognosis. Late Gadolinium Enhancement (LGE) technology in CMR enables non-invasive identification and assessment of myocardial fibrosis [13]. However, the association between subclinical cardiac alterations and myocardial fibrosis in overweight and obese populations remains incompletely elucidated, particularly concerning integrated predictive studies combining myocardial strain with epicardial fat volume. This study aims to systematically compare differences in cardiac structure, function, global and segmental myocardial strain, and epicardial fat volume across populations with varying body mass indices using CMR techniques. It further analyses the correlation between BMI, EATV with strain parameters, and further explore the value of these imaging parameters for myocardial fibrosis (LGE positivity). This aims to provide more precise imaging evidence for early cardiovascular risk stratification and intervention in overweight and obese populations.

Methods

Study population

This study is a retrospective analysis, participants underwent CMR examination at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical College between September 2023 and December 2025 were enrolled. The obese group comprised 78 cases (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2), Additionally, 37 overweight individuals (24 ≤ BMI < 28 kg/m2) and 77 normal-weight individuals (18.5 ≤ BMI < 24 kg/m2) were randomly selected to form the overweight group and control group respectively, ensuring matching for age and gender. The primary clinical indications for testing include: atypical chest pain or dyspnea with no abnormalities on echocardiography, and ECG abnormalities (such as nonspecific ST-T changes) suggestive of cardiomyopathy, as well as some volunteers from the health screening center. None of the participants had a known history of cardiovascular disease; all were undergoing their first diagnostic evaluation or risk stratification. Exclusion criteria: (1) History of cardiovascular disease, such as atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, valvular heart disease, primary cardiomyopathy, congenital heart disease, or severe arrhythmias; (2) Concurrent hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, or other conditions; (3) Poor CMR image quality rendering measurement impossible.Body mass index was assessed according to the definition standards for the Chinese population [14] (Fig. 1). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was reviewed and approved by the Research and New Technology Ethics Committee of Yiji Mountain Hospital, Wannan Medical College: (2023) Lun Shen Sheng Yan Di (01). All participating patients have signed informed consent documents.

Baseline data

Collect baseline clinical data from participants, encompassing medical history, anthropometric measurements, body surface area (BSA), and blood pressure at rest. Blood pressure measurement strictly adheres to standardised operating procedures: two standardised readings are taken by rigorously trained researchers using a calibrated electronic sphygmomanometer, repeated at two-minute intervals, with the final blood pressure value being the mean of the two readings. Laboratory investigations included complete blood count and biochemical profiles, specifically: fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), uric acid (UA), haematocrit (HCT), haemoglobin (Hb), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and cardiac troponin I (cTnI).

CMR analysis

CMR parameters

All images were acquired using a Siemens MAGNETOM Vida 3.0T MRI scanner. Sequence parameters: Steady-state free precession (SSFP) sequence with retrospective ECG gating. Key parameters: TR/TE 3.4/1.7 ms, flip angle 45°, slice thickness 8 mm, slice spacing 1.6 mm, field of view 315 × 315 mm², matrix 256 × 256, temporal resolution 30–40 ms.

LGE acquisition parameters: Acquisition is performed 10–15 min after contrast agent injection using a phase-sensitive inversion recovery (PSIR) gradient-echo sequence. Key parameters: TR/TE 5.2/2.3 ms, flip angle 20°, slice thickness 8 mm, field of view consistent with the cine sequence; inversion time is adjusted individually based on myocardial suppression (typically 260–340 ms).

Contrast agent protocol: Gadolinium diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid (Gd-DTPA) is administered at a dose of 0.2 mmol/kg body weight with an injection rate of 2 mL/s, followed by 20 mL of saline to flush the line. Acquire long-axis images in the 2-view, 3-view, and 4-view planes, as well as a series of short-axis images of the left ventricle, 10 min after injection of the contrast agent.

Cardiac function analysis

All images underwent post-processing using CVI42 (V5.6.2) software. Endocardial and epicardial contours were manually delineated on short-axis, two-chamber, three-chamber, and four-chamber cine images, followed by analysis using the software’s corresponding modules. Left ventricular function parameters comprised: left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV), left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), left ventricular stroke volume (LVSV), left ventricular cardiac output (LVCO), left ventricular cardiac index (LVCI), and left ventricular mass (LVM). Left atrial function parameters are derived from the biventricular and four-chamber cine sequences, primarily comprising: left atrial minimum volume (LAVmin), left atrial maximum volume (LAVmax), and left atrial ejection fraction (LAEF).

Myocardial strain analysis

Perform left ventricular myocardial strain analysis within the feature tracking module of CVI42 software. At end-diastole across all series, the endocardial and epicardial contours were delineated for each layer, excluding papillary muscles and the moderator band. Peak myocardial strain parameters were automatically extracted for the basal, mid, and apical segments of the left ventricle, as well as for the entire segment. These parameters included peak radial strain (PRS), peak circumferential strain (PCS), and peak longitudinal strain (PLS). Left atrial strain analysis utilised feature tracking technology. Initially, end-diastolic endocardial and epicardial boundaries of the left atrium were manually delineated in four-chamber and two-chamber long-axis views within the cine sequence. Subsequently, automated tracking algorithms processed these data to derive the left atrial time-strain relationship curve (Fig. 2). When delineating atrial volume and strain, the openings of the pulmonary veins and the left atrial appendage must be strictly excluded; the contour should terminate at the junction of the pulmonary veins and the atrium and at the base of the atrial appendage. Tracking and analysis should be performed only within the main body of the atrium, and a two-person review should be conducted to ensure the accuracy of the boundaries. The left atrial strain parameter system encompasses the following metrics: Reserve strain (εs)—the maximum strain during left atrial diastolic filling, reflecting left atrial reserve capacity; Conduit strain (εe)—the change in strain from mitral valve opening to the onset of atrial contraction, reflecting the left atrium’s function as a conduit for the passive transport of blood from the pulmonary veins to the left ventricle; Pump strain (εa)—the change in strain during atrial contraction, reflecting the left atrium’s auxiliary pumping function [15]. The conduit phase strain (εe) is defined as the change in strain from mitral valve opening to the onset of atrial contraction (marked by the onset of the P wave on the electrocardiogram).

Epicardial adipose tissue

Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) appears on imaging as a high-signal region between the myocardium and pericardium. EAT measurement covers all short-axis planes of the entire left ventricle, from the apex to the base (including the plane at the base where the ventricle is no longer visible). At end-diastole in each plane, manually trace the boundary between the epicardium and the pericardial wall; the area between the two constitutes the EAT (Fig. 3). The software then automatically calculates the EATV.

Assessment of left ventricular myocardial fibrosis(LGE positivity)

LGE images were independently assessed by two experienced radiologists who were blinded to all clinical data. LGE positivity was determined based on visual identification of abnormal signal enhancement in a non-ischemic pattern. In cases of disagreement, a third experienced radiologist was consulted to reach a consensus and ensure diagnostic consistency. Classification of LGE patterns: We classify LGE-positive findings into two categories: (1) Ischemic pattern: involving subendocardial regions and consistent with the distribution of coronary blood supply; (2) Non-ischemic pattern: Includes mid-myocardial, epicardial, or focal patchy enhancement that does not follow the coronary artery distribution. Exclusion of ischemic scarring: Based on the strict exclusion criteria of this study, all individuals with a known history of coronary artery disease (including myocardial infarction and revascularization) were excluded.

Repeatability analysis

To assess the reliability of the measurements, we conducted intra-observer and inter-observer reproducibility analyses: A single technician performed all initial image measurements, while reliability was assessed using a random sample of 20 cases: the same technician evaluated intra-observer consistency by repeating the measurements one month later, and inter-observer consistency was assessed by a second technician. Throughout the analysis, both technicians remained blinded to each other’s results to prevent measurement bias. Intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC) were used to assess consistency.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0. For normally distributed quantitative data, results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons among multiple groups employed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When variances were homogeneous, pairwise comparisons were performed using the LSD method; when variances were heterogeneous, Tamhane’s T2 method was applied. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (M) and interquartile range (Q1, Q3). Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, with pairwise comparisons between groups analysed using Dunn’s test and Bonferroni correction applied to adjust P-values. Count data were expressed as case numbers and analysed using chi-square tests. Spearman’s correlation analysis assessed associations between BMI, EATV, and myocardial strain parameters, with correlation heatmaps generated. To investigate relationships between parameters and LGE, univariate logistic regression was initially performed. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate binary logistic regression model to identify factors independently associated with LGE. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P > 0.05 indicating good fit), and multicollinearity was diagnosed via variance inflation factors (VIF), with variables exceeding VIF > 10 excluded. The predictive efficacy of relevant indicators for left ventricular delayed enhancement (LGE) was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. ROC curve analysis: SPSS 26.0 was used to calculate the AUC, confidence intervals, sensitivity, specificity, and Youden’s index for each indicator, and to determine the optimal cutoff value. The ROC curve plots were generated and optimized using MedCalc 22.0 software. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05.

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis between BMI, EATV, and myocardial strain parameters is presented in Supplementary Fig. S1. BMI was negatively correlated with radial strain parameters (r range: −0.24 to − 0.31) and positively correlated with certain circumferential and longitudinal strain parameters (r range: 0.16 to 0.30), indicating reduced myocardial contractility with increasing BMI. EATV was negatively correlated with left atrial strain parameters (εs: r = − 0.18; εe: r = − 0.19) and positively correlated with left ventricular global longitudinal strain (r = 0.30, P < 0.001), suggesting that increased epicardial fat is associated with impaired atrial and ventricular function. Additionally, BMI showed a strong positive correlation with EATV (r = 0.72, P < 0.001).

Logistic regression analysis is shown in Supplementary Table S2. Univariate analysis revealed that several parameters, including εa, M-PRS, A-PRS, G-PRS, and EATV, were significantly associated with LGE (all P < 0.05). After adjusting for confounders (multicollinearity assessed by variance inflation factor, with detailed VIF values provided in Supplementary Table S3), multivariate analysis identified EATV (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.10, P < 0.001), BMI (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.07–1.40, P = 0.004), and A-PRS (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.86–0.99, P = 0.044) as factors independently associated with LGE (Hosmer-Lemeshow test, P = 0.312).

Discussion

We employed cardiac magnetic resonance imaging to systematically compare cardiac structure, function, myocardial strain, and EATV across distinct BMI groups and to investigate their associations with myocardial fibrosis (LGE positivity). The results showed that higher BMI was associated with adverse cardiac remodeling, reduced function, widespread impairment of myocardial strain, and increased epicardial adipose tissue volume. These alterations were closely associated with the presence of myocardial fibrosis.

Consistent with previous reports [16, 17], left ventricular and left atrial volumes and left ventricular mass were significantly increased in the overweight and obese groups compared with controls, whereas left ventricular and left atrial ejection fractions were reduced. Increased hemodynamic load and neurohumoral activation are key mechanisms underlying obesity‑related left ventricular hypertrophy and chamber enlargement [18], and such adaptive remodeling is common even in asymptomatic obese individuals [19]. Notably, significant changes in echocardiographic parameters were already observed in overweight participants, suggesting that cardiac remodeling begins before the onset of obesity, in line with large community‑based findings that overweight is an independent risk factor for left ventricular hypertrophy and systolic dysfunction [20]. Left atrial enlargement is an early marker of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and atrial fibrosis and predicts future atrial fibrillation and heart failure [21]. These findings underscore the importance of precise CMR‑based quantification of cardiac structure for early detection of obesity‑related myocardial pathology. Furthermore, recent studies have shown that GLS is significantly impaired in obese individuals without known cardiovascular disease, even when left ventricular ejection fraction and mass index remain normal [22], highlighting the superior sensitivity of myocardial strain over conventional functional parameters—a finding consistent with the widespread strain reduction observed in our study.

Myocardial strain parameters are sensitive markers of contractile function [23]. In this study, global and segmental left ventricular strain parameters, as well as left atrial strain parameters, were significantly reduced in the overweight and obese groups. Correlation analyses showed mild negative correlations between BMI and mid‑ventricular, global, and apical radial strain (r = − 0.31, − 0.28, and − 0.24, respectively), indicating an association between greater adiposity and diminished radial contractility. BMI was positively correlated with certain circumferential and longitudinal strain parameters. Because circumferential and longitudinal strains are normally negative, these positive correlations imply that the absolute values of these strain parameters decrease with increasing BMI, reflecting impaired myocardial shortening. Strain reduction was more pronounced in obese individuals with LGE positivity, suggesting that myocardial fibrosis may contribute to mechanical dysfunction. Recent studies have also demonstrated that impaired left atrial strain is independently associated with new‑onset atrial fibrillation [24, 25], supporting the inclusion of strain analysis in risk stratification [26]. In our study, left atrial εs, εe, and εa were reduced in overweight and obese individuals and were negatively correlated with BMI, consistent with the notion that obesity affects both ventricular and atrial function early in its course.

We observed a strong positive correlation between EATV and BMI (r = 0.72, P < 0.001), as well as negative correlations between EATV and several myocardial strain parameters. Specifically, EATV was moderately correlated with left ventricular global longitudinal strain (r = − 0.30) and mildly correlated with left atrial reservoir and conduit strain (r = − 0.18 and − 0.19, respectively). In the multivariate regression model, despite the strong correlation between BMI and EATV, VIF remained below 2.5, indicating that multicollinearity did not meaningfully affect model stability. Both BMI and EATV were retained as factors independently associated with LGE, suggesting that systemic obesity and local epicardial fat deposition may influence myocardial fibrosis through partially distinct pathways. Epicardial fat can directly affect the adjacent myocardium by releasing free fatty acids and pro‑inflammatory mediators that promote interstitial fibrosis [27], and the independent association of EATV with LGE positivity in our study supports a direct role of epicardial fat in myocardial fibrosis [28]. Recent large‑scale CMR studies have distinguished the cardiac effects of systemic obesity from those of visceral fat distribution; for example, in the UK Biobank cohort, visceral adipose tissue was independently associated with smaller left ventricular end‑diastolic volume and lower ejection fraction, particularly in males [29]. Although our study focused on epicardial rather than visceral adipose tissue, these observations suggest that ectopic fat deposition—whether visceral or epicardial—may contribute more directly to adverse cardiac remodeling and functional decline than systemic obesity alone. Another study in uncomplicated obese adults similarly identified visceral adipose tissue as an independent predictor of left ventricular global longitudinal strain and diastolic strain rate [30].

Multivariate regression and ROC analysis identified BMI, EATV, and apical radial strain (A-PRS) as factors independently associated with LGE positivity. Although A-PRS showed only a mild correlation with BMI in univariate analysis (r = − 0.24), it retained independent value in the multivariate model, suggesting that localized apical contractility captures a distinct dimension of fibrosis risk beyond global adiposity. The apical myocardium may be particularly susceptible to early functional alterations and fibrosis under obesity‑related metabolic stress due to its unique biomechanical environment. The thinner apical wall experiences higher wall stress according to Laplace’s law, which may predispose it to mechanical dysfunction under chronic hemodynamic load [31]. Additionally, epicardial adipose tissue releases inflammatory mediators that act on the myocardium via paracrine signaling; the thin‑walled apex may be especially vulnerable to such inflammatory insults [32, 33]. As a distal perfusion territory of the coronary arteries, the apex is also more susceptible to ischemia in the setting of obesity‑related microvascular dysfunction [34]. These factors collectively position the apex as an “early warning site” for obesity‑related myocardial injury, a notion supported by the independent association of A-PRS with LGE positivity in our study. Although global longitudinal strain is widely recognized as a sensitive marker of cardiac function, segmental strain analysis—particularly of the apex—may provide incremental information for detecting early focal pathologies such as microvascular dysfunction or incipient fibrosis [35, 36]. This finding suggests that in future clinical research and risk stratification, detailed analysis of myocardial segmental strain may provide additional incremental information beyond focusing solely on global strain parameters.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single‑center study, which limits generalizability; multicenter prospective validation is warranted. Second, the sample size was relatively modest, raising the possibility of type II error. Third, CMR feature tracking results may be influenced by image spatial resolution and the use of contrast agents; although we used standardized acquisition and post‑processing protocols to ensure consistency, technical standardization remains an ongoing consideration. Fourth, the hospital‑based design introduces referral bias, as all participants underwent CMR for specific clinical indications; despite excluding individuals with known cardiovascular disease, the findings may not fully represent asymptomatic overweight or obese individuals in the general population. Finally, LGE was assessed as a binary variable; while this approach effectively identifies focal fibrosis, it does not quantify fibrosis burden and may be insensitive to early or diffuse changes. Future studies incorporating quantitative techniques such as T1 mapping are needed.

Conclusion

Overweight and obese individuals exhibit significant cardiac remodeling, reduced systolic function, and impaired myocardial strain, which are closely associated with increased epicardial adipose tissue volume. BMI, EATV, and A-PRS of the left ventricle are factors independently associated with myocardial fibrosis. This study demonstrates that subclinical structural and functional alterations in the heart occur even during the overweight stage, suggesting that cardiovascular risk management in overweight populations warrants attention.
