Association Between Extreme Climate Exposures and Bodily Pain: Evidence from the Chinese Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study
Authors: Shilong Zhang, Yaoyang Ban, Feng Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsExtreme climate events may be associated with increased bodily pain, particularly among aging populations. However, individual-level evidence linking specific climate exposures to pain outcomes remains limited. This study examined the association between extreme climate exposure and pain in different body regions among middle-aged and older Chinese adults.MethodsThis study used data from the Chinese Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, 2011–2020), which included 65,693 person-wave observations. Pain outcomes across the 15 body regions were self-reported. Climate exposure indices (extreme temperature, rainfall, drought, and the composite Climate Physical Risk Index [CPRI]) were derived from city-level meteorological data. Generalized additive mixed models were used to assess associations, adjusting for demographics, lifestyle, health status, cognitive function, frailty, and chronic disease.ResultsThe composite CPRI was significantly associated with higher likelihood of reporting pain across most body regions (e.g., most severe neck pain, β = 0.0121). Extreme low temperatures and rainfall were significantly associated with pain in the knee, chest, and stomach. Vulnerability was significantly increased among rural residents, older adults (≥ 65 years), women, and those reporting poorer health or greater frailty. These findings remained robust in the sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsExposure to extreme climatic conditions is significantly associated with higher likelihood of reporting pain across multiple body regions among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. These associations are primarily immediate, suggesting that timely public health strategies might be beneficial in mitigating potential climate-related discomfort during extreme weather events. Increased vulnerability among older adults, women, rural populations, and frail individuals also highlights the need for targeted intervention strategies to mitigate climate-related health risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-026-00586-z.

Introduction

The increasing frequency and intensity of extreme climate events, such as extreme heat and cold, are associated with increased risks of morbidity and mortality and pose significant global public health challenges, especially for middle-aged and older patients [1, 2]. While most of the literature has focused on immediate health events such as cardiovascular or respiratory crises, chronic manifestations such as pain have received less attention despite their profound impact on quality of life [3–5].

The pathophysiological pathways linking weather to pain are complex and vary distinctly by the type of climate exposure. For instance, extreme cold is known to induce peripheral vasoconstriction and increase synovial fluid viscosity, thereby exacerbating joint stiffness and pain [6]. Conversely, extreme heat may trigger systemic inflammatory cascades and alter neurosensory stress responses [7]. Heavy precipitation and high humidity can induce barometric pressure changes that cause the expansion of inflamed joint tissues, while severe droughts are linked with airborne particulate matter and psychological distress, both of which can amplify pain perception [8, 9]. To systematically quantify these multidimensional environmental threats, researchers utilize the Climate Physical Risk Index (CPRI). The CPRI is a composite measure that aggregates individual hazards—such as extreme low and high temperatures, heavy rainfall, and drought—into a unified indicator of environmental stress, making it highly relevant for capturing the cumulative and compounding nature of climate exposures on human health [10].

In China, this climate-health nexus requires urgent attention. The country is experiencing unprecedented demographic aging, with the population aged 60 and over projected to reach 400 million by 2040 [11]. Furthermore, China’s vast geographic footprint makes it highly susceptible to intensifying climate hazards. Specifically, the northern regions face frequent heatwaves and droughts, while the south endures severe floods and heavy precipitation [12, 13]. Concurrently, epidemiological evidence highlights a substantial burden of multi-site bodily pain among Chinese older adults. These high prevalence rates reflect a growing public health crisis [14]. Despite this, robust individual-level evidence linking specific environmental risk exposures to pain outcomes in this population remains scarce. Furthermore, the health impacts of these extreme exposures are not uniformly distributed; individual susceptibility is significantly modified by key vulnerability indicators. Advanced age and pre-existing chronic diseases impair the body’s homeostatic and thermoregulatory reserves, while physical frailty exacerbates functional decline under environmental stress [15]. Sex differences may drive distinct physiological and behavioral responses to weather extremes. Additionally, stark urban-rural disparities in China dictate access to climate-adaptive infrastructure (e.g., indoor heating, air conditioning, and high-quality healthcare), making geographic residence a critical modifier of environmental risk [16].

Therefore, utilizing longitudinal, nationally representative data from the Chinese Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, 2011–2020) linked to high-resolution city-level climate exposure indices, this study aimed to: (1) assess the associations between specific extreme climate conditions (temperature, precipitation, drought) and the CPRI with self-reported pain outcomes across multiple body regions; and (2) examine whether these associations are modified by key indicators of vulnerability, including frailty status, chronic disease burden, age, sex, and place of residence.

Methods

Study Design and Study Population

This study used data from CHARLS (2011–2020), a publicly available, nationally representative, longitudinal survey of individuals aged 45 years and older in mainland China (https://charls.pku.edu.cn/en/). The baseline survey was conducted in 2011 (wave 1), followed by subsequent waves of 2013 (wave 2), 2015 (wave 3), 2018 (wave 4), and 2020 (wave 5). CHARLS used a multistage stratified sampling strategy, covering 150 districts and 450 municipalities in 28 provinces. Participants were interviewed face-to-face using structured questionnaires to collect information on demographics, health status, lifestyle, and social and economic factors [17].

In this study, we included respondents aged 45 years and older who participated in at least two of the five waves and had complete data on key variables of interest. To ensure the stability of the environmental exposure assessments, individuals whose place of residence changed between waves were excluded. Because detailed home addresses were not available, environmental exposures were assessed at the city level, assuming that participants remained in the same city throughout the observation period. This study linked individual-level health data from CHARLS with city-level climate exposure data, resulting in an analytical dataset of 86 cities common to both databases (Supplementary Fig. 1).

From an initial pool of 96,629 person-wave observations across the five survey waves, data attrition occurred in two primary stages to ensure high-fidelity exposure matching and data quality. First, 19,573 observations were excluded due to spatial linkage failures, comprising participants whose residential locations were not covered by the 86 analytical cities (n = 17,200) or those with unresolvable administrative boundary mismatches (n = 2,373). Second, 11,363 observations were excluded due to severe missingness, specifically lacking pain outcome assessments (n = 4,800) or having more than 10% missing data across key covariates, such as the frailty index, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle factors (n = 6,563), as well as a negligible number of off-season (winter) interviews omitted to maintain seasonal homogeneity. This rigorous step-by-step exclusion process yielded a final analytical sample of 65,693 person-wave observations. Any remaining minor missing values (under 10%) in this final cohort were appropriately addressed via multiple imputation using the mice package (Fig. 1).

The reduction to 86 cities was not due to arbitrary selection, but rather driven by spatial data availability constraints. Specifically, the third-party climate dataset relies on valid global meteorological stations. Several county-level administrative units in the CHARLS baseline either lacked sufficient nearby NOAA meteorological station coverage for the entire study period or could not be seamlessly aggregated to the corresponding prefecture-level city codes due to administrative boundary restructurings between the survey wave and the climate dataset generation. Consequently, only participants residing in areas with highly reliable, overlapping meteorological and administrative data were retained.

Construction of Climate Physical Risk Indicators

This study used annual data to quantify the climate-related physical risks of Chinese cities from 1993 to 2023 [10]. The dataset includes five core indicators that reflect distinct dimensions of climate-related physical hazards: the Climate Physical Risk Index (CPRI), a composite metric representing overall exposure to climate-related physical risk; the Extreme Low Temperature Days (LTD), a standardized measure indicating the frequency and severity of extreme cold events; the Extreme High Temperature Days (HTD), a standardized measure capturing the occurrence and intensity of extreme heat events; the Extreme Rainfall Days (ERD), a standardized indicator of excessive precipitation events; and the Extreme Drought Days (EED), a standardized indicator quantifying prolonged low-humidity conditions [10, 18].

These indices are based on daily meteorological observations from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s (NOAA) Global Summary of the Day (GSOD) dataset. The key input variables include daily average temperature (T), precipitation (R), and dew point–derived relative humidity (H) as key variables.

Pain Outcome Ascertainment

Pain outcomes were assessed using the initial screening question: “Have you often been bothered by pain in any part of your body?” Response options ranged from (1) not at all, (2) a little, (3) somewhat, (4) quite a bit, to (5) very much. Interviewers instructed participants to consider pain in all parts of the body when responding. Participants who answered “not at all” were classified as the non-pain control group. Those who endorsed any level of pain (options 2 through 5) constituted the pain group. For these participants, a follow-up question was asked: “In what part of your body do you feel pain in? Please list all that apply.” The listed body parts included the head, shoulders, arms, wrists, fingers, chest, stomach, back, waist, hips, legs, knees, ankles, toes, and neck. In the site-specific analyses, participants who explicitly selected a given anatomical site from this list were classified into the pain group for that respective region.

Covariate Ascertainment

Baseline demographic and health-related covariates collected for all participants included sex (male or female), age (years), marital status (married, divorced, never married, widowed, separated, cohabiting, or married but living separately), residence (urban or rural), and geographic region (eastern, northeastern, central, or western China). The geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) were also recorded to control for potential spatial clustering effects. Educational level was classified as high (college or above), middle (high school), or low (below junior high school). Employment status was defined as currently employed or unemployed, and economic status was determined by pension receipt (yes or no). Lifestyle behaviors assessed included smoking status (yes or no), alcohol consumption (yes or no), nighttime sleep duration (in hours), and daytime nap duration (in hours).

Participants’ health status was evaluated comprehensively. Self-reported health was assessed using a scale ranging from excellent to very poor (excellent, good, fair, poor, or very poor) [19]. Dependency in activities of daily living was categorized as independent, mildly dependent, moderately dependent, or severely dependent. The number of chronic diseases was calculated from self-reported conditions, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and arthritis, each indicated as present or absent. Cognitive function was evaluated via standardized neuropsychological tests covering multiple cognitive domains, including immediate and delayed word recall (memory), orientation to time (temporal awareness), and serial sevens subtraction (attention and working memory) [20, 21]. A composite cognitive score was computed by combining these tasks, with all scores standardized as z-scores for comparability. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), ranging from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. Functional limitations were further assessed using the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scales. ADL scores ranged from 0 to 6, representing the number of basic self-care tasks with which participants reported difficulty (e.g., dressing, bathing, and eating). The IADL scores also ranged from 0 to 6, reflecting limitations in tasks such as cooking, managing finances, and using the telephone. Social isolation was measured using a validated composite score ranging from 0 to 4, based on the frequency of contact with family and participation in social activities, where higher scores indicate greater social isolation [22].

Frailty was operationalized using a cumulative deficit model based on the frailty index approach [23]. A total of 32 health-related variables were included to capture physiological, functional, and psychosocial deficits, such as chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, stroke, cancer, arthritis), sensory impairments (vision and hearing), self-rated health, depressive symptoms (CESD-10 score ≥ 11), cognitive deficits, difficulties with ADLs, IADLs, and physical functioning. Each item was coded as a binary indicator (1 = deficit; 0 = no deficit). Cognitive deficits were calculated using the following formula: Cognitive Score = (14 - (Word Recall Score/2 + Orientation Score))/14. The final frailty index was derived by summing the total number of deficits divided by the number of non-missing items and multiplying by 100 to yield a percentage (range 0–100). Participants with missing data for more than one item were excluded from the frailty index calculation [24].

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). To stringently control for the inflation of Type I error associated with multiple hypothesis testing—specifically, the simultaneous examination of 15 distinct anatomical sites across 5 climate exposure indices—a Bonferroni correction was applied to the analyses. Consequently, all reported p-values and 95% confidence intervals reflect these adjusted estimates, and statistical significance was strictly defined based on the Bonferroni-corrected thresholds.

Descriptive analyses were performed to characterize the study participants. Continuous variables were summarized using the mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), whereas categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Missing data were handled by multiple imputation within a Bayesian framework using the mouse package in R [25]. Missing variables were conditionally imputed using an iterative procedure. Predictive mean matching was used to preserve the original data distribution by imputing missing values with observations that had similar predicted means. Variables with more than 10% missing data were excluded prior to imputation. Ridge regularization (0.5) was applied to improve model stability. Although the CHARLS dataset is longitudinal, our primary analysis focused on the associations between climate exposures and pain outcomes reported in the same year. Therefore, the analytical framework is best interpreted as a repeated-measures analysis of short-term associations rather than a causal inference of longitudinal effects.

We utilized Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) with a logit link function to examine the associations between extreme climate exposure and reported pain. where Painiₜ denotes the binary pain outcome for individual i at time t. The term sₖ(·) represents the thin-plate regression splines used for continuous variables (age and calendar year) to capture potential non-linearities. Crucially, we included ui as a random intercept for each participant to account for the correlation inherent in repeated measures within the same individual over time. Additionally, v_city was included as a random intercept at the city level to account for spatial clustering and unobserved geographic heterogeneity. This dual-level random effect structure ensures that the standard errors are appropriately estimated for our multilevel data. GAMMs with a logit link were used to examine the associations between five indicators of extreme climate exposure (LTD, HTD, ERD, EED, and CPRI) derived from the year preceding the survey and reported pain across 15 anatomical sites.

To evaluate short-term associations, we strictly defined the 12-month period immediately preceding the survey (year t-1) as the primary exposure window. Delayed associations were assessed separately using exposures from the second year (year t-2) and the third year (year t-3) prior to the outcome assessment. Additionally, to capture the cumulative environmental burden, we calculated a ‘3-year cumulative’ metric, representing the aggregate sum of exposures across years t-1, t-2, and t-3. Given the biennial waves of the CHARLS survey, these windows were specifically defined to reflect the cumulative and legacy impacts of environmental stress leading up to each assessment, rather than attempting to isolate incident pain events. Crucially, each exposure window—the primary year (t-1), the specific lagged years (t-2 or t-3), and the cumulative period—was analyzed in separate regression models to ensure no multicollinearity existed between these metrics. Smooth spline functions for age and calendar year were included to account for potential non-linear relationships. The functional forms of the continuous predictors (age, year, and primary exposure) were systematically evaluated via visual inspection, spline modeling, likelihood ratio tests, and information criteria (AIC/BIC).

This comprehensive approach ensured optimal model specification, balancing goodness-of-fit with interpretability, and led to the inclusion of spline terms for variables showing significant nonlinearity, while retaining linearity for primary exposures where appropriate (Supplementary Table 1). GAMMs are particularly suited to this analytical framework, as they effectively handle repeated measures over time and account for the geographic clustering inherent in the dataset [26, 27].

All models were adjusted for baseline demographic factors (age, sex, marital status, education, and employment status), lifestyle behaviors (smoking, alcohol use, and nighttime sleep duration), cognitive function, frailty status, depressive symptoms (CESD-10), and the number of chronic diseases.

Subgroup analyses stratified by place of residence (urban vs. rural), sex, age group (< 65 years vs. ≥65 years), frailty status, and health status were conducted to explore the heterogeneity in the associations between extreme temperature exposure and pain outcomes. Results from the GAMMs are presented as regression coefficients (β), along with their corresponding standard errors (SEs). The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated as β ± 1.96 × SE.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the results. First, the cluster-level sensitivity analysis replaced the city-level random effects (CityID) with community-level random effects (CommunityID) to assess the effect of hierarchical structuring. Second, the covariate adjustment sensitivity analysis excluded the frailty index from the model to determine its influence on the associations. Third, a sample-based robustness analysis was performed by randomly selecting 80% of the original dataset (without replacement) and re-estimating the models across all pain outcomes. Furthermore, to rigorously eliminate potential confounding introduced by behavioral changes, psychosocial stress, and healthcare-seeking disruptions during the COVID-19 pandemic, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by entirely excluding all observations collected during the 2020 survey wave (Wave 5). Finally, to evaluate the robustness of the equal-weighting scheme employed for the CPRI, we performed a sensitivity analysis using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The four individual climate sub-indices (LTD, HTD, ERD, and EED) were first standardized to ensure comparability. We then extracted the first principal component (PC1), which represents a data-driven, variance-weighted composite index that captures the maximum shared variance among the sub-metrics. This weighted index was then substituted for the original unweighted CPRI in our primary regression models across all anatomical sites to verify the stability of our findings and to ensure that the observed associations were not artifactually driven by the equal-weighting assumption.

Results

Study Sample Characteristics

A comprehensive comparison of baseline characteristics between the retained analytical sample (n = 65,693) and the excluded participants (n = 19,573) was conducted to evaluate potential selection bias (Supplementary Table 2). The results indicated that both groups were well-balanced in age (60.54 ± 10.43 vs. 60.51 ± 9.91 years, p = 0.742). While the excluded group exhibited slightly lower frailty index scores (17.97 ± 12.11 vs. 18.62 ± 13.80) and fewer chronic diseases (1.73 ± 1.60 vs. 1.96 ± 2.07), the absolute differences were minimal, suggesting a similar clinical health profile. The exclusion of these participants was primarily driven by the lack of meteorological station coverage in their respective regions (predominantly rural and remote areas), rather than systematic demographic shifts (Supplementary Table 2).

Following the linkage procedure, the final analytical sample included 65,693 person-wave observations, consisting of 38,731 observations in the non-pain group and 26,962 observations in the pain group (Supplementary Table 3). Compared with the non-pain group, participants in the pain group were older (61.45 vs. 59.90 years) and had a higher proportion of females (60.8% vs. 56.5%), urban residents (44.8% vs. 35.7%), and unemployed individuals (37.4% vs. 35.8%) (all p < 0.001).

The pain group presented a higher prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and arthritis (all p < 0.001). Functional limitations were more common, with fewer individuals reporting no limitations in ADLs (69.6% vs. 88.9%) or IADLs (66.6% vs. 84.7%). Cognitive impairment was more common (28.3% vs. 21.0%). Outpatient visits were more common in the pain group (25.4% vs. 14.1%, p < 0.001).

Participants in the pain group also had higher Frailty index scores (24.86 vs. 14.28), higher CESD-10 scores (11.21 vs. 7.42), shorter nighttime sleep duration (5.80 vs. 6.39 h), and slightly shorter daytime naps (36.42 vs. 39.86 min) (all p < 0.001). The burden of chronic disease was also greater, with fewer reporting zero conditions (14.3% vs. 33.8%) and more reporting multiple conditions.

Pain observations were associated with greater environmental exposures, including higher levels of HTD, ERD, and CPRI across various lag and cumulative periods (all p < 0.001).

Associations Between Extreme Climate Exposures and Pain

Extreme exposure was positively associated with pain. For example, the LTD was 0.0068 (95% CI: 0.0014 to 0.0122, p = 0.013), the ERD was 0.0027 (95% CI: 0.0010 to 0.0043, p = 0.001) for headache, the LTD was 0.0073 (95% CI: 0.0028 to 0.0118, p = 0.010), and the ERD was 0.0040 (95% CI: 0.0009 to 0.0077, p < 0.001) for arm pain (Fig. 2). In contrast, the evidence for cumulative and delayed associations were less consistent and generally weaker. For example, the 3-year cumulative LTD estimate for back pain was 0.0024 (95% CI: 0.0012–0.0036, p = 0.038) while the delayed LTD effect for headache was 0.0056 (95% CI: 0.0024–0.0088, p = 0.019). A similar delayed effect was observed for ERD for headache at 0.0027 (95% CI: 0.0009 to 0.0045, p = 0.003) (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3).

Subgroup Analyses

Interaction terms were incorporated between the five environmental exposure indices (LTD, HTD, ERD, EED, and CPRI) and five predefined modifiers: sex, age group (age ≥ 65 vs. <65 years), residence (rural vs. urban), self-rated health, and frailty status across all 15 pain-related outcomes. Of the 375 interaction combinations tested, 335 (89.3%) remained statistically significant after Bonferroni adjustment (p < 0.05), with a substantial majority achieving p < 0.001. Notably, ERD and EED showed significant interactions across all modifiers for every outcome (100% significance rate). These findings suggest that the associations between environmental exposures and pain symptoms are significantly non-uniform, being markedly influenced by individual vulnerability and socioeconomic contexts. These robust interaction results justify the necessity of our subsequent stratified analyses (Supplementary Table 4).

The positive associations between the CPRI and pain were generally stronger among adults aged ≥ 65 years than in those aged < 65 years, particularly for the upper body regions (Fig. 4A). For example, the association with neck pain was more pronounced in the older group (β = 0.0137; 95% CI, 0.0059–0.0214) than in the younger group (β = 0.0114; 95% CI, 0.0055–0.0173). Consistent age-related differences were generally not observed for the lagged or cumulative CPRI effects. For example, the lagged association with headache was β = 0.0099 in adults aged ≥ 65 years and β = 0.0063 in those aged < 65 years. Similar patterns, in which age appeared to modify immediate but not consistently lagged or cumulative effects, were also observed for individual climate exposures, including LTD, HTD, ERD, and EED (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5).

Females showed stronger and more consistent immediate CPRI-pain associations across multiple sites (Fig. 4B). For waist pain, the association was β = 0.0101 for females and β = 0.0080 for males. Greater sex-based differences were observed for toe and leg pain. In contrast, lagged and cumulative CPRI effects were generally comparable between the sexes, with no consistent pattern of sex differences. This pattern of greater female susceptibility, primarily for immediate effects, was also broadly evident for LTD, HTD, ERD, and EED, although less consistently for lagged and cumulative effects (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7).

Compared to their urban counterparts, the residential context played a critical role, as rural residents showed stronger associations between CPRI and pain outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 8). For example, for headache, rural participants showed a significant association (β = 0.0109, 95% CI: 0.0051–0.0167, p < 0.001), whereas the effect in urban areas was not significant (β = 0.0022, 95% CI: − 0.0048–0.0092). Similarly, for stomach pain, the rural effect was notable (β = 0.0145, 95% CI: 0.0074–0.0216), but no significant association was found among urban residents (β = 0.0026, 95% CI: − 0.0055–0.0107). Other pain sites, such as back pain (rural: β = 0.0114, 95% CI: 0.0048–0.0181; urban: β = 0.0038, 95% CI: − 0.0040–0.0116) and neck pain (rural: β = 0.0173, 95% CI: 0.0107–0.0238; urban: β = 0.0074, 95% CI: 0.0004–0.0143), also demonstrated consistently stronger rural effects. In contrast to these immediate effects, the patterns for lagged and cumulative effects were mixed. For example, the 3-year cumulative effect on neck pain was significant only in rural areas (β = 0.0069, 95% CI: 0.0036–0.0102) but not in urban areas (β = 0.0032, 95% CI: − 0.0001–0.0064). The pronounced rural disadvantage for immediate climate effects was consistent across the LTD, HTD, ERD, and EED (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Self-reported health status at baseline significantly modified the direct association between CPRI and pain. Compared with those reporting good health, those reporting poor health showed stronger effects (Fig. 4C). For example, the immediate association between CPRI use and back pain was β = 0.0101 [95% CI: 0.0041–0.0161] in the poor health group and β = 0.0063 [95% CI: − 0.0083–0.0209] in the good health group. This pattern extended to cumulative effects (Supplementary Fig. 10), and for the 3-year cumulative CPRI and neck pain, the association was significant only in the poor health group (β = 0.0047 [95% CI: 0.0022–0.0072]) versus the good health group (β = 0.0034 [95% CI: − 0.0024–0.0092]). Lagged and cumulative climate effects did not show a consistent disadvantage among participants who reported poorer health across LTD, HTD, ERD, and EED (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Frailty status also modulated climate-pain associations (Fig. 4D). Higher frailty scores corresponded to stronger immediate effects of CPRI pain. For example, for immediate CPRI and back pain, the estimate was β = 0.0114 [95% CI: 0.0061–0.0167] in the high-frailty group and β = 0.0090 [95% CI: 0.0042–0.0139] in the low-frailty group. Similarly, for neck pain, the association was slightly stronger in the high-frailty group (β = 0.0107 [95% CI: 0.0059–0.0155]) than in the low-frailty group (β = 0.0100 [95% CI: 0.0056–0.0144]). Increased vulnerability among those with poorer health or greater frailty was consistently observed for the individual climate exposures LTD, HTD, ERD, and EED for both immediate and cumulative effects (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13).

Sensitivity Analyses

In the sensitivity analyses, we first replaced the clustering variable from city ID to community ID to evaluate the potential effects of spatial aggregation (Fig. 5). The CPRI remained significantly associated with pain in several upper body regions, including the arm (β = 0.0103, 95% CI: 0.0059–0.0148, p < 0.001), wrist (β = 0.0064, 95% CI: 0.0013–0.0114, p = 0.013), finger (β = 0.0074, 95% CI: 0.0023–0.0125, p = 0.005), and shoulder (β = 0.0079, 95% CI: 0.0037–0.0121, p < 0.001). Associations were also detected in the hip (β = 0.0072, 95% CI: 0.0016–0.0129, p = 0.012) and knee (β = 0.0076, 95% CI: 0.0035–0.0118, p < 0.001).

ERD demonstrated pervasive and highly significant positive associations with pain across multiple regions, with the largest effects observed in the neck (β = 0.0071, 95% CI: 0.0055–0.0087, p < 0.001), arm (β = 0.0069, 95% CI: 0.0054–0.0084, p < 0.001), back (β = 0.0063, 95% CI: 0.0049–0.0078, p < 0.001), and chest (β = 0.0057, 95% CI: 0.0038–0.0076, p < 0.001). Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first systematic examination of the associations between multiple extreme climate exposures and reported pain in different body regions in a large, nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older adults. Our findings indicate significant associations between exposure to extreme cold and high humidity, and increased reports of pain across the majority of body regions examined. This suggests a broad and potentially pervasive link between these environmental stressors on physical health and discomfort [28]. Physiologically, LTD and ERD demonstrated distinct site-specificity (e.g., knee, neck, and shoulder), which might be explained by potential mechanical pathways, although our cross-sectional data cannot confirm such mechanisms. Extreme cold increases synovial fluid viscosity and induces peripheral vasoconstriction, directly exacerbating joint stiffness [29]; meanwhile, sudden barometric pressure drops associated with heavy rainfall can cause the transient expansion of inflamed joint tissues, thereby triggering pain flare-ups in specific anatomical regions [30]. However, these findings do not rule out the potential effects of extreme heat or dry conditions, which may be related to pain outcomes through different physiological pathways or may manifest in different ways in different populations or in different contexts [31]. This inconsistency likely reflects a fundamental difference in the pathophysiological pathways of varying climate risks. In contrast to the localized physical associations of cold and rain, extreme heat and drought operate primarily through diffuse, systemic pathways—such as heat stress-induced systemic inflammation, sleep deprivation, and the psychosocial stress driven by drought conditions [32]. These factors tend to lower the generalized systemic pain threshold rather than acting directly on specific joints, making their independent signals more difficult to capture in regression models targeting highly localized anatomical sites.

In addition, this study utilized the CPRI. Although it was initially conceptualized to evaluate broad socio-economic impacts, the index itself is grounded in objective, high-frequency meteorological thresholds (e.g., historical percentiles for temperature and humidity), rendering it a robust proxy for environmental epidemiology [10]. A central tension emerges when comparing the highly variable, site-specific patterns of individual sub-indices with the pervasively consistent associations observed for the composite CPRI. Rather than merely attenuating meaningful heterogeneity between specific climate hazards, we argue that this divergence indicates the composite index captures a critical, synergistic dimension of environmental stress. The CPRI effectively operationalizes the “cumulative environmental allostatic load” that individual metrics miss. While a single climate hazard may merely act as an acute trigger for localized pain flare-ups, chronic exposure to a highly volatile environment with intersecting extreme climates continuously taxes the body’s adaptive mechanisms [33]. Over time, this exhausts physiological reserves, ultimately leading to neuroendocrine dysregulation and central sensitization [34]. This explains why the CPRI, reflecting the totality of environmental adversity, lowers the generalized systemic pain threshold and serves as a much more robust predictor of widespread bodily pain than any single weather component [35]. Interestingly, our analysis found limited evidence of delayed or cumulative effects of extreme climate exposures on pain outcomes. This observation suggests that the adverse health implications associated with these exposures may predominantly manifest in the short term, thereby highlighting the potential value of timely public health interventions and responsive mitigation strategies to protect vulnerable populations [36].

Overall, our approach allowed for a more nuanced understanding of climate-health vulnerability in the context of aging. This provides preliminary evidence that may help inform targeted public health strategies in China and potentially other rapidly aging LMICs facing similar climate challenges.

Age-Related Insights

Advancing age significantly modulated the association between CPRI score and reported pain, although this age effect varied markedly across body regions. Increased vulnerability with increasing age was most evident for the upper body and trunk (arms, neck, chest); individuals aged ≥ 65 years had significantly stronger CPRI-pain associations than those aged 45–64 years. This finding is consistent with established gerontological evidence suggesting that age-related changes increase the sensitivity to environmental stressors [37]. Conversely, this pattern of age-related enhancement was not evident for the knees. While knee pain was linked with CPRI, this link held true for both middle-aged (45–64) and older (≥ 65) groups, suggesting that knee vulnerability to climate effects is established by middle age within this group and doesn’t increase with age [38]. In essence, aging is associated with an increased risk of climate-related pain, selectively in the upper body/trunk but not in the knee, highlighting the body’s differential response to climate change.

Sex-Based Variations

The link between extreme climate exposure and pain was stronger for female than male participants, with women experiencing more climate-related pain, especially in the back and hips. This is consistent with large-scale data showing higher bodily pain prevalence and burden among older women worldwide, possibly due to hormonal factors, differences in pain-processing pathways, and psychosocial influences that may be associated with heightened susceptibility [16, 39]. These findings support clinical observations and research highlighting the female sex as a significant risk factor for severe or widespread pain syndromes in later life. Therefore, the observed differences underscore that older women are particularly vulnerable to climate-related pain, offering insights for further exploring tailored clinical advice and preventive measures [40].

Urban-Rural Disparities

The geographical context significantly shaped vulnerability, with rural residents showing stronger associations between CPRI and pain. This may reflect higher baseline pain levels, more physically demanding work, and reduced access to healthcare. Increased exposure because of housing and occupational patterns likely compounded the association of climate extremes, in line with prior research emphasizing the role of environmental and socioeconomic factors in heightening rural vulnerability [41]. Our results highlight the need for considering geographic and infrastructural contexts in climate-health assessments [42].

Influence of Health Status

Participants with poorer self-reported health and higher frailty scores had significantly stronger associations between the CPRI scores and pain. This finding is consistent with the well-established concept of frailty, as described by Huang et al. [43], as a state of diminished physiological reserves and increased vulnerability to external stressors [44, 45]. People with pre-existing health problems are less able to cope with extreme weather changes, which can lead to increased susceptibility to pain. Research has shown that chronic disease and disability are associated with higher levels of pain [46, 47]. This suggests that people with pre-existing health problems may have a lower threshold for pain, making them more sensitive to environmental factors such as temperature and humidity. These findings suggest the potential utility of frailty assessments in climate change-related health risk assessments, as recommended in geriatric care [48].

Public Health Implications

The findings have several public health implications. The predominantly immediate nature of the observed associations suggests that extreme weather events are associated with immediate increases in pain. However, there may be additional delayed or cumulative effects. Therefore, interventions could consider focusing on short-term forecasting and rapid response. This is consistent with strategies for other weather-sensitive conditions [49]. For example, warning systems during extreme conditions may help vulnerable individuals take protective action. Second, the robust association between the CPRI and pain suggests that it can effectively detect environmental health risks, more so than individual exposures. Consistent with environmental epidemiology, our results highlight the value of using an integrated index. Third, the results show that public health strategies may benefit from being tailored to specific populations. Our findings align with the principle of individual susceptibility, suggesting that physiological heterogeneity significantly moderates the human response to environmental determinants. This is important in the context of climate change. Finally, even non-lethal climate stressors such as extreme heat and rain can have a real association on people’s daily lives [50]. As climate variability potentially increases, integrating climate risk considerations into chronic pain management and health policy for older adults is critical. By highlighting these links, this study provides evidence for proactive interventions aimed at mitigating climate-related health burdens in aging populations and contributes to the growing field of climate change and health adaptation research.

This study had several limitations. First, exposure assessments rely on city-level climate data, which may not capture microclimatic variations or individual-level differences in exposure. City-level climate assignment, constrained by meteorological station coverage, reduced the analytical sample to 86 cities—a limitation inherent to the exposure data that cannot be fully eliminated. A comparison of included versus excluded participants revealed differences in demographics, geography, and health status that were statistically significant but of negligible magnitude, supporting the representativeness of the retained sample. Nevertheless, aggregating exposure to the city level inevitably introduces non-differential misclassification, which would bias estimates toward the null. The reported associations may therefore be conservative. Second, due to data limitations, we could not account for behavioral and environmental modifiers, such as physical activity, housing quality, or access to temperature control, which may influence pain outcomes [51]. Third, although CHARLS provides a nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, generalizability to other countries or populations should be approached with caution, given the varying demographic, climatic, and health system contexts. Furthermore, while we utilized longitudinal data, our primary analysis focused on short-term associations. As with any observational study, we cannot strictly infer causality. Although it is biologically implausible for individual pain to influence climate events (reverse causation), behavioral adaptations in response to pain (e.g., staying indoors) might influence exposure classification, potentially biasing the observed associations. Because we did not adjust for prior-wave baseline pain status, our models cannot definitively distinguish whether extreme climate exposures trigger incident (new-onset) pain or exacerbate pre-existing chronic pain conditions. This is a clinically and mechanistically meaningful distinction that warrants future investigation using high-frequency longitudinal study designs. Furthermore, we acknowledge a temporal resolution mismatch between the exposure and outcome variables. While pain outcomes were ascertained on specific interview dates, climate exposures were measured as annual averages. Future research utilizing high-resolution daily meteorological data linked with daily pain assessments is warranted to investigate these acute temporal associations. Finally, the assessment of pain in this study relied on a single self-reported screening question rather than a clinically validated psychometric instrument. This measure lacks the psychometric depth to definitively differentiate between acute pain flare-ups and long-term bodily pain syndromes. While such single-item measures are common in large-scale national surveys like CHARLS and provide a valuable proxy for general pain burden in aging populations, our results should be interpreted as associations with ‘self-reported bodily pain’ rather than clinically diagnosed chronic pain.

Conclusions

This large, nationally representative study suggests an association between extreme climatic conditions and higher likelihood of reporting pain in multiple body regions among middle-aged and older adults in China. Older adults, women, rural residents, and those with poor/severe frailty were most affected, and further research is key to understanding the health impacts of climate adversity. As climate variability increases, targeting climate-sensitive health outcomes such as pain may improve efforts to support healthy aging and resilience.
