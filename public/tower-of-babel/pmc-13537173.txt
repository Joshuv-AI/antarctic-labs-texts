An ADDIE Model-Based Training Program for Enhancing Public Health Competencies in General Practice Residency: Development, Validation, and Preliminary Evaluation
Authors: Jianli Ge, Xiaoliang Sun, Min Ding, Yang Li, Shasha Geng, Xin Chen, Yingqian Zhu, Xiaoming Sun, Chi Chen, Hua Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneral practitioners (GPs) play a pivotal role in delivering public health services at the primary care level, yet standardized residency training often inadequately addresses public health competency development. This study aimed to systematically develop a public health service capability training course for GP residents using the ADDIE model and to preliminarily evaluate its effectiveness.MethodsThis study employed a non-randomized controlled trial design. A structured training program was developed following the five-phase ADDIE model. In the Analysis phase, competency gaps were identified through stakeholder needs assessment and literature review; in the Design phase, a 13-module curriculum was validated via two-round Delphi consensus (expert authority coefficient Cr = 0.921); in the Development phase, teaching materials, case libraries, and assessment tools were produced; the Implementation phase delivered the training through monthly 5-hour sessions over 13 months; and the Evaluation phase assessed outcomes using formative and phased assessments. The curriculum integrated case-based learning, problem-based learning, and simulation-based teaching. The intervention group (n = 32, 2023–2024 cohort) received the ADDIE-based training, while the control group (n = 22, 2021–2022 cohort) received conventional lecture-based training. Competencies were assessed at baseline, immediately post-training, and at 3-month follow-up, along with the annual Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE, scored on a 100-point scale).ResultsWithin the intervention group (n= 32), formative assessment scores improved significantly from baseline to 3-month follow-up across all three domains (theoretical: 69.03 ± 6.65 to 84.59 ± 3.81; clinical: 67.40 ± 6.18 to 84.27 ± 3.30; practical: 74.78 ± 4.39 to 84.85 ± 3.76; all P < 0.001). For the phased assessment, the intervention group outperformed the historical control group (n = 22) on annual OSCE (71.88 ± 10.84 vs 62.07 ± 4.71; mean difference = 9.80, 95% CI: 4.29–15.29; P = 0.039; Cohen’s d = 0.99), with greater improvement from baseline (Δ = 12.47 ± 4.27 vs 8.96 ± 3.51; P = 0.002; Cohen’s d = 0.82). Course satisfaction was high (mean 4.43–4.83 on a 5-point scale).ConclusionThe ADDIE-based training program demonstrated preliminary feasibility and potential effectiveness in enhancing GP residents’ public health service competencies. However, as a single-center, non-randomized pilot study with a modest sample size, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.Keywords: ADDIE model, general practice, public health competency, residency training, curriculum development

Introduction

The global health landscape is undergoing a profound transformation, driven by accelerating population aging, the dual rising burden of chronic and infectious diseases, and growing demands for public health services. These challenges underscore a critical shortage in both the quantity and quality of health human resources worldwide, including in China.1–6 This workforce deficit is particularly pronounced in primary care settings, where the gap between service demands and workforce competency has persisted and even widened. Given this, a robust public health system is fundamental to safeguarding population health, serving as a cornerstone of national public security.

Effective public health practice is crucial for controlling health risk factors, strengthening disease prevention, reducing the incidence of both infectious and chronic diseases, improving health literacy, and ultimately enhancing overall population health.7,8 General practice, serving as the foundation of the health care service network and the “gatekeeper” of residents’ health, faces the urgent task of integrating clinical care with public health services. General practitioners (GPs) are pivotal in delivering primary health care and coordinating medical services. However, they often exhibit challenges such as limited public health awareness, inadequate professional skills, and underdeveloped capabilities in early warning and monitoring.7,8

Existing studies have documented these competency deficits, yet most have focused on describing problems rather than developing and evaluating systematic training solutions, and even fewer have been contextualized within China’s standardized residency training framework. Specifically, available public health training within this system has been criticized for fragmented content delivery, over-reliance on passive lectures, and insufficient integration with clinical practice9- approaches that often fail to cultivate the core competencies required for community-based public health practice. Therefore, a key issue in reforming general practice education is how to effectively cultivate GPs’ critical thinking and comprehensive competencies in public health service, enabling them to better address patients’ integrated medical and public health needs.10 The standardized residency training system is the primary pathway for GP training in China11 However, as noted above, public health training within this system often remains insufficient, lacking structured instructional design and practical competency orientation.9

To address these shortcomings, this study makes three contributions: (1) it systematically constructs a competency-based public health training curriculum for GP residents using the ADDIE instructional design model—a systematic framework that has been underutilized in GP education; (2) it validates the curriculum content through a rigorous two-round Delphi expert consensus process; and (3) it provides a preliminary evaluation of the curriculum’s effectiveness using a non-randomized controlled design with historical controls. Unlike prior studies that relied on fragmented content delivery, this course integrates competency-based modules and interactive pedagogies to bridge the theory-practice gap, thereby offering a replicable model for reforming GP public health training.

Materials and Methods

Study Aim

This study aimed to develop, implement, and preliminarily evaluate a training program designed to enhance public health service capabilities among general practitioner residents, based on the ADDIE model.

Primary Objective

To assess the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of the developed training program in improving GP residents’ public health competencies.

Secondary Objectives

(1) To establish the content validity of the course curriculum through expert consensus;

(2) To compare the annual clinical competency (as measured by Objective Structured Clinical Examination, OSCE) scores between the intervention group and the control group;

(3) To evaluate participants’ satisfaction with the course.

The study sought to address the following research questions:

(1) Content Applicability: Is the ADDIE-based training course scientifically valid and practically applicable in the context of GP residency training?

(2) Comparative Effectiveness: Compared to traditional teaching methods, does this training program lead to superior outcomes in theoretical knowledge, clinical application, skills and overall clinical competency (OSCE performance)?

(3) Impact Trajectory: What are the immediate, short-term, and medium to long-term effects of the training on the development and retention of participants’ public health competencies?

ADDIE Model

The ADDIE model, a systematic and learner-centered instructional design framework, was employed to guide the development of the training course.12 This validated model encompasses five interconnected and iterative phases: Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation. Within this framework, the Analysis and Design phases establish the foundation; Development and Implementation constitute the core constructive process; and the Evaluation phase ensures the quality and effectiveness of the outcomes. The ADDIE model has been widely applied and validated across diverse educational fields.13

ADDIE-Analysis Phase

In January 2023, a dedicated nine-member research team was convened to develop the curriculum. The team composition was designed to ensure comprehensive expertise and practical relevance: one director from the Teaching and Training Office (overall supervision); two teaching management staff (preliminary evaluations and coordination); four general practice faculty members (course design and development); and two general practitioners from primary health care centers (practical applicability).

Guided by the ADDIE model, the team first conducted a comprehensive needs analysis involving key stakeholders (Table 1). Subsequently, a systematic literature review was performed. The team searched Chinese (CNKI, Wanfang Database) and English (PubMed, Web of Science, Embase) databases using combinations of keywords such as “general practitioners”, “public health”, “competency”, “training”, and “curriculum”. A total of 3641 articles were initially retrieved. After screening titles and abstracts and removing duplicates and irrelevant publications, 11 full-text articles were included for in-depth review. The research team analyzed these articles to synthesize identified gaps in general practitioners’ public health competencies, prevailing training needs, effective instructional methods, and relevant knowledge domains.

Training Needs Analysis for the Course

Based on the needs analysis and literature synthesis, the initial curriculum framework was structured. Informed by Miller’s pyramid of clinical competence (“knows”, “knows how”, “shows how”, “does”) as the theoretical foundation,14 the framework was organized into three core teaching modules: Theoretical Instruction, Case Studies, and Practical Training.

ADDIE-Design Phase

To establish content validity and finalize the course curriculum, a Delphi expert consultation process was conducted.

Consultation Instrument

A structured questionnaire was developed, comprising three sections: (1) Basic Information Form: Collected demographic and professional data of the experts (eg, age, highest degree, major, professional title, workplace, field of expertise, and years of experience).

(2) Expert Authority Coefficient (Cr) Self-Assessment Form: Assessed the experts’ authority level based on two dimensions: their familiarity (Cs) with the subject and the basis of their judgment (Ca). Familiarity was rated on a 5-point scale (from “Very unfamiliar” to “Very familiar”). The basis of judgment was quantified across four aspects: practical experience, theoretical analysis, reference to domestic and international knowledge, and intuitive judgment. The authority coefficient (Cr) was calculated as Cr = (Cs + Ca) / 2.

(3) Course Content Evaluation Form: This core section presented the draft training course indicators. Experts were asked to rate the importance and accessibility of each indicator using a 10-point Likert scale (1 = “Very unimportant/Very difficult to obtain”; 10 = “Very important/Very easy to obtain”). An open-ended comment box was provided for each item to solicit qualitative feedback and suggestions for modification.

The three-level indicator system ultimately determined in this stage provides the basic framework for curriculum design and teaching evaluation tools.

ADDIE-Development Phase

The objective of this phase was to transform the finalized three-tier indicator system and core module framework from the Design phase into executable, teachable instructional materials and resources. This encompassed four key tasks:

(1) Development of Teaching Materials: A comprehensive instructor and learner package was created, including an instructor’s manual with session plans, student learning materials with compiled theoretical knowledge resources, and multimedia slide decks to support didactic sessions.

(2) Construction of a Case Library: A repository of structured teaching cases for CBL/PBL sessions was developed. Each case contained predefined learning objectives, clinical background information, guiding questions for discussion, reference answers, and key teaching points.

(3) Development of an Assessment Toolkit: To enable rigorous evaluation aligned with course objectives, a suite of formative assessment tools was created, including theoretical knowledge tests (A/B versions), clinical skills operation checklists, scripts for Standardized Patient (SP) encounters, and course satisfaction questionnaires.

(4) Resource Validation and Piloting: All developed materials underwent an internal review and a pilot teaching session conducted by the research team and selected Delphi experts. This step ensured the scientific accuracy, teaching applicability, and alignment of the final resources with the curriculum framework established through expert consensus.

ADDIE-Implementation Phase

Study Design and Participants

Due to the sequential and queue-based nature of the resident physician program, this study employed a non-randomized design with a control group. Although this method was pragmatic, it carried the risk of time bias, such as changes in exam difficulty, teacher composition, or unmeasured queue specific characteristics. The reason for choosing the control group was that the curriculum reform was implemented at the institutional level for all residents in a specific academic year, excluding concurrent randomization within the same cohort. Limitations would be taken into account when interpreting the results.

As a preliminary, single-center feasibility study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed; the sample comprised all eligible first-year GP residents in the respective training cohorts.

Participants were first-year GP residents undergoing standardized training at Shanghai East Hospital, Tongji University School of Medicine. Those enrolled in the 2023–2024 academic year constituted the intervention group (n=32) and received the newly developed Public Health Service Capability Training Course. Residents from the 2021–2022 cohort served as the control group (n=22) and received traditional public health lectures as part of their standard curriculum. No residents were excluded, and no residents withdrew or were lost to follow-up.

Training Implementation

(1) Goal and Framework: The training aimed to enhance the public health service competencies of GP residents, with learning objectives structured according to the WONCA Tree framework.

(2) Faculty: All instructors possessed valid teaching certificates, had over five years of clinical teaching experience, and held intermediate or senior professional titles.

(3) Procedure: While both groups completed the core residency curriculum, the educational intervention differed. The control group received conventional didactic lectures on public health topics. The intervention group participated in the structured training course developed in this study (Table 2).

Schedule of Training Course

(4) Intervention – The Training Course: The course for the intervention group adopted an integrated, diversified, and longitudinal approach, combining theoretical learning, clinical case analysis, and practical skill training.

Format and Mentorship: Training was delivered monthly in 5-hour sessions, organized into small groups (3 residents per group). A dual-mentorship model was implemented, involving co-teaching by GP supervisors from both primary health care centers and the comprehensive hospital. The entire training program spanned 13 months, with one session per month, resulting in a total of 65 hours of instruction. This structured schedule ensured that the training did not interfere with routine clinical rotations while maintaining continuity and sufficient exposure to the course content.

Assessment-Driven Learning: A pre-training questionnaire assessed baseline knowledge and learning needs. Immediate post-training evaluations gauged knowledge retention and comprehension.

Pedagogical Methods: Case-based sessions utilized a hybrid CBL/PBL model, where residents actively engaged with structured cases and problem-solving scenarios, facilitated by instructors.

Blinding: the historical control design and the use of routine institutional examination data made blinding unfeasible.

ADDIE-Evaluation Phase

A multi-modal evaluation strategy was implemented.

Formative Assessment (Intervention Group, Pre-, Post-, and Follow-Up)

Assessments were conducted before training, immediately after training, and at 3-month follow-up, covering three competency domains: theoretical knowledge, clinical application, and practical skills. The maximum score for each domain was 100 points.

Theoretical knowledge: Online testing was adopted, with feedback provided on incorrect answers. The test question bank was derived from the National Standardized Training Program for Resident Physicians, with questions set by expert panels. Content validity was evaluated by experts, and the pre-test internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) was 0.76.

Clinical application: Standardized patients (SP) were used for assessment, with two instructors independently evaluating residents’ abilities in history gathering, preliminary diagnosis, and treatment recommendations using a structured scoring sheet. The content validity index (CVI) of the scoring sheet was 0.90, and the inter-rater reliability (ICC) was 0.82.

Practical skills: Operational assessments were conducted using high-fidelity simulation tools.

Patient education: This was evaluated by dual mentors, SP, and third-party reviewers through case summaries.

Phased Assessment (Both Groups)

Annual Clinical Competence Assessment – Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE): The OSCE is a well-validated assessment tool in medical education, and its multi-station design ensures good content validity. It comprehensively evaluates key competencies such as history taking, physical examination, diagnosis and treatment, SOAP note writing, doctor-patient communication, and clinical procedures.15,16 Each station was independently scored by two trained examiners using a unified 100-point scale. Among the OSCE stations, three were specifically designed to evaluate public health-related competencies: health education and counseling, chronic disease management and follow-up planning, and doctor-patient communication with a focus on shared decision-making.

Annual Theoretical Knowledge Assessment: The standardized unified examination of public health-related knowledge (maximum score 100), organized by the Shanghai Resident Physician Training Affairs Center, was adopted to objectively compare the theoretical proficiency of the two groups.

Course Satisfaction Assessment (Intervention Group Only)

A post-course survey was administered, consisting of four items: perceived learning outcomes, overall course satisfaction, simulation training evaluation, and perceived course value. A five-point Likert scale was used (1 = very dissatisfied to 5 = very satisfied). The internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) of the survey was 0.88.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). All tests were two-tailed, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

The analysis strategy was structured as follows:

Inter-group Comparisons (Intervention vs Control):

Baseline: Chi-square tests and independent-samples t-tests were used to examine differences in general characteristics and baseline scores between the two groups.

Post-training Effect: Independent-samples t-tests were employed to analyze the differences in annual OSCE scores between the intervention and control groups after the training intervention. Cohen’s and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for between-group comparisons.

Intra-group Comparisons (Within the Intervention Group): Changes in outcome scores across the three assessment time points (before training, immediately after training, and at 3-month follow-up) were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) for each competency domain (theoretical knowledge, clinical application, and practical skills). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. Paired-sample t-tests were used to compare the magnitude of improvement from baseline to immediate post-training (Δ1) versus baseline to 3-month follow-up (Δ2) within the intervention group.

Results

All 54 participants completed the study with no missing data at any assessment time point.

Expert Consultation and Course Series Settings

Building on the framework and needs identified in the Analysis phase and integrating relevant literature on GP training and public health competency development, the research team employed the Delphi method to establish content validity for the course.17 Fifteen experts from fields including public health, disease prevention and control, medical education, and general practice were invited to participate. The panel consisted of 5 males and 10 females, with a mean age of 42.12 ± 4.35 years and a mean work experience of 15.31 ± 2.13 years. Their educational backgrounds included 4 bachelor’s degrees,7 master’s degrees, and 4 doctoral degrees. In terms of professional titles, 3 held intermediate-level and 12 held senior-level positions.

Two rounds of structured questionnaire consultations were conducted via email, with effective response rates of 100.0% for both rounds. To assess the quality of the consultation, the following metrics were calculated:

Expert Authority Coefficient (Cr): 0.878 in the first round and 0.921 in the second round.

Kendall’s Coefficient of Concordance (W): 0.308 (χ2 = 87.386, P < 0.001) in the first round and 0.429 (χ2 = 89.823, P < 0.001) in the second round.

A threshold method was applied to screen the course indicators, with criteria set as: mean importance score ≥ 8.0, coefficient of variation (CV) ≤ 0.250, and full-score ratio ≥ 30.0%.18,19 All proposed indicators met these criteria in the final round. These results indicate a high level of consensus among the experts and the reliability of the consultation process.

Following the two rounds, the final training curriculum was established, comprising 13 core modules. These modules were structured across three competency levels: theoretical knowledge, clinical application, and practical skills (Table 3).

Course Evaluation

Baseline Characteristics of Participants

This study enrolled 54 first-year GP residents who underwent standardized training at Shanghai East Hospital, Tongji University School of Medicine. Participants were divided into two groups based on their training cohort:

Intervention group (n=32): Residents enrolled in the 2023–2024 academic year who received the newly developed Public Health Service Capability Training Course. This group consisted of 7 males (21.88%) and 25 females, with a mean age of 23.47 ± 0.80 years.

Control group (n=22): Residents from the 2021–2022 cohort who received the conventional training program. This group consisted of 6 males (27.27%) and 16 females, with a mean age of 23.59 ± 0.79 years.

No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of gender distribution (χ2 = 0.208, P = 0.648) or age (t = 0.551, P = 0.584). The baseline clinical knowledge and ability assessment scores (on a 100-point scale) were also comparable, with scores of 73.85 ± 9.98 for the intervention group and 72.50 ± 9.39 for the control group (t = 0.498, P = 0.621).

Formative Assessment (Within-Intervention-Group)

To evaluate the longitudinal effects of the training, competency scores across three dimensions—theoretical knowledge, clinical application, and practical skills—were assessed at three time points: before training (baseline), immediately after training, and at 3-month follow-up.

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of time for all three competency dimensions (F = 101.193, 171.336, and 201.121, respectively; all P < 0.001). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction confirmed that scores at both post-training assessments were significantly higher than baseline scores (all P < 0.001).

To assess the consolidation of learning gains, paired-sample t-tests were conducted to compare the magnitude of improvement from baseline to the immediate post-training assessment (Δ1) with the improvement from baseline to the 3-month follow-up (Δ2). The results indicated that the gains observed at 3-month follow-up were significantly greater than the immediate gains in all domains: theoretical knowledge (t = 3.320, P = 0.003), clinical application (t = 3.067, P = 0.006), and practical skills (t = 2.891, P = 0.009) (Table 4).

Course Satisfaction Evaluation (Intervention Group)

Participant satisfaction with the training course was evaluated using a standardized questionnaire based on a 5-point Likert scale. The satisfaction scores across four domains were all high, presented in descending order: perceived learning outcomes (4.827 ± 0.675), simulation training evaluation (4.812 ± 0.348), overall course satisfaction (4.71 4± 0.520), and perceived course value (4.430 ± 0.485).

Discussion

Course Design: Addressing Competency Gaps Through the ADDIE Model

In the context of general practice training, public health service competency is a critical indicator of a physician’s preparedness for primary care. This study applied the ADDIE instructional design model to systematically develop a training course aimed at bridging specific competency gaps among GP residents.

The efficacy of this approach hinged on the accuracy of the initial Analysis phase. To avoid reliance on a single data source, a multi-method needs assessment was conducted. This combined an objective evaluation of residents’ existing public health capabilities with a self-assessment survey of practicing GPs regarding frontline challenges. This integrated analysis identified three core competency shortfalls: chronic disease management (long-term follow-up and intervention planning), targeted health services for key populations, and public health emergency response. These practice-derived gaps directly informed the subsequent Design and Development phases, ensuring the curriculum was grounded in actual service demands.

To ensure scientific rigor, the Delphi method was employed during these phases. A panel of experts from public health policy, disease control, medical education, and clinical practice (response rate > 80%, authority coefficient Cr > 0.85) reached a consensus on a final curriculum comprising 13 core modules. These were structured into a progressive framework across three dimensions: theoretical knowledge, clinical application, and practical skills. This structure, guided by the ADDIE model, created a closed loop from theory to practice, establishing a foundation for targeted training.

The sustained improvement observed at the 3-month follow-up and the superior OSCE performance were associated with specific pedagogical decisions within the ADDIE framework. The Analysis phase identified competency gaps that informed case-based and simulation-based content; the Design phase operationalized Miller’s pyramid14 by structuring learning objectives across “knows”, “knows how”, and “shows how”; and the Development phase integrated KWL strategies and multi-source feedback to promote active, reflective learning. This alignment between instructional design and educational theory likely contributed to the consolidation of competencies beyond immediate post-training gains.

Training Efficacy: Association with Competency Improvement

The primary outcome of this curriculum redesign was a significant enhancement in resident competency, as evidenced by both formative and phased assessments. At baseline, the intervention and control groups were comparable in all measured characteristics and initial clinical knowledge scores (P > 0.05). Following the intervention, the intervention group demonstrated superior performance.

Short-term gains were captured through formative assessment across three time points. Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of time on scores for theoretical knowledge, clinical application, and practical skills (all P < 0.001, Table 4). More importantly, paired-sample t-tests showed that the magnitude of improvement from baseline to the 3-month follow-up (Δ2) was significantly greater than the immediate post-training gain (Δ1) in all three domains (all P < 0.001, Table 4). This finding suggests not only immediate knowledge acquisition but continued consolidation and integration of learning over time, which may be related to the curriculum’s emphasis on application and feedback.

Between-group comparison was assessed via the annual OSCE. The intervention group achieved a significantly higher mean score (71.88 ± 10.84) compared to the control group (62.07 ± 4.71) (t = 2.420, P = 0.039; Cohen’s d = 0.99). This superior performance in a comprehensive clinical skills examination suggests an association between the ADDIE-based training and measurable clinical ability.

These results may be linked to specific pedagogical innovations. First, the adoption of KWL (Know, Want to know, Learned) strategies20 promoted self-directed learning by activating prior knowledge and targeting personal learning gaps. Second, a multi-method evaluation system (eg, 360-degree feedback) combined with blended learning (PBL,CBL,high-fidelity simulation) created a “practice-feedback-optimization” cycle. This aligns with Miller’s Pyramid framework, effectively facilitating the progression from “knows” to “shows how”.18,19,21 The sustained improvement at 3-month follow-up and superior OSCE performance ultimately suggest that the curriculum may facilitate the transition from knowledge mastery to durable competency building.

Comparison with Previous Studies and Learner Experience

Compared to prior ADDIE-based interventions in medical education, which often focus on a single skill or course component, our curriculum systematically integrated multiple pedagogical strategies (CBL, PBL, simulation) within a unified framework. Zhang et al13 demonstrated the effectiveness of ADDIE in neurology training; Das et al22 used ADDIE for a pediatric obesity module; and Malan et al23 for behavior change counseling training. Our findings extend these by specifically addressing the public health competency gap for GPs and incorporating formal Delphi validation. The structured nature of the ADDIE model allowed for a deliberate alignment between identified needs (Analysis), learning objectives (Design), and assessment methods (Evaluation).18,21 This systematic alignment likely explains the sustained improvement observed, as opposed to traditional lecture-based training, which often lacks this integrated, practice-driven loop.

The curriculum’s pedagogical philosophy, centered on practice-orientation and interactive teaching, received strong validation from participant evaluations. Overall satisfaction was high across all four surveyed domains (mean scores > 4.4/5). Residents rated perceived learning outcomes (4.827 ± 0.675) and simulation training (4.812 ± 0.348) highly.

This high satisfaction likely stems from the tangible bridge built between theory and clinical practice. The extensive use of real-world case analyses (CBL/PBL), simulated public health scenarios, and standardized patient interactions provided “immersive” practice opportunities. This shift from passive listening to active engagement directly addresses the common challenge of “knowing but not being able to do”. Furthermore, the multi-source, real-time feedback mechanism (from mentors, peers, and simulated patients) created an immediate “practice-feedback-optimization” loop, allowing for rapid identification and correction of skill gaps, thereby enhancing learning efficacy.13

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence that an ADDIE model-based training program is feasible and potentially effective for improving public health competencies among GP residents. The intervention group demonstrated significantly higher OSCE scores and greater improvement from baseline compared to the control group, along with sustained gains at 3-month follow-up and high participant satisfaction. These findings suggest that the ADDIE framework may be a valuable approach for structuring public health training in GP residency education.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study has several limitations. The non-randomized, historical control design and lack of assessor blinding may introduce bias. The single-center setting and modest sample size limit generalizability, and no power calculation was performed. The 3-month follow-up supports only short-term retention, and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. Future research should employ randomized or prospective controlled designs with larger, multi-center samples, longer follow-up, blinded assessment, and patient-centered outcomes to confirm these preliminary findings.
