Impact of hydrostatic pressure on the physical attributes of Lutetium-based perovskite oxide for optoelectronic applications
Authors: Hudabia Murtaza, Ahmed B. M. Ibrahim, Quratul Ain, Junaid Munir, M. A. Habib, Mokhtar S. S. Al-Salimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pressure modulation in perovskite oxides enables precise tuning of the physical attributes of the material. This controllability allows optimization of conductivity and bandgap. Such adaptability enhances their potential in energy storage, optoelectronics, and multifunctional device technologies. This manuscript reports the tuning of the physical attributes of LuGaO3 using the Wien2K code. The pressure range is selected from 0 GPa to 32 GPa with an increment of 8 GPa in each iteration. The FP-LAPW and mBJ approximation is utilized to analyze the accurate electronic structures. At ambient pressure, the structure of the studied oxide remains thermodynamically and structurally stable. The elastic attributes of LuGaO3 report a significant reduction in the compressional forces as pressure is increased. LuGaO3 remains ductile as pressure is increased till 32 GPa. The studied oxide reports a transition of the mechanical attributes from anisotropic nature to isotropic as the pressure is increased. The electronic properties of LuGaO3 report a decline in the electronic bandgap from 5.02 eV to 2.69 eV as pressure is varied from 0 GPa to 32 GPa, which is also evident from TDOS and PDOS plots. Furthermore, this bandgap reduction leads to the shifting of optical properties from the higher energy region (UV) towards the visible region. This redshift enhances the ability of LuGaO3 to absorb and interact with visible light, potentially improving its performance in light-harvesting and optoelectronic devices, making it a promising candidate for photovoltaics, photo-catalysis, visible-light photo-detectors, light-emitting diodes, and optical sensors.

Introduction

The energy crisis hinders rapid industrial growth worldwide. Energy has a strong effect on the sustainability of the environment and ecology, and is widely regarded as the most critical environmental concern of our time1. Power consumption has significantly increased during the past 50 years, which can be linked to the rise of industry and the world’s population2,3. One of the most promising alternative energy sources being investigated by researchers to eventually replace fossil fuels is solar energy4,5. According to the International Energy Agency, fossil fuels remain the main source for achieving these goals6. Nonetheless, fossil fuels constitute the primary source of hazardous CO2 emissions in the environment. The use of a variety of fossil fuels causes CO2 emissions, which exacerbate environmental problems. It contributes significantly to global warming and environmental degradation7,8. Right now, solar power is the best substitute for conventional energy sources and the most practical way to solve the energy crisis. Photovoltaic conversion or the transformation of sunlight into electricity, is the foundation of solar technology, which has various applications9. The search for breakthrough materials with remarkable properties has spurred the creation of new technologies10. The goal is to develop novel, abundant, and nontoxic materials that can be employed in the process of turning sunlight into usable energy11,12. Perovskite materials possess exceptional physical properties, which make them an attractive choice of material for many modern applications13. Furthermore, perovskite oxides offer high flexibility in cation and anion substitutions, leading to enhanced physical attributes which make them ideal for use in electronic devices, energy storage, catalysis, and optoelectronics14–16. Numerous cations can be substituted with ABO3, which has a major impact on their chemical and physical characteristics17. Perovskite oxides possess a changeable chemical composition making them highly adaptable and flexible in fabrication18. DFT-GGA calculations using the CASTEP code report that PrVO3 and CeVO3 are half-metallic materials with magnetic moments in the range of 4 to 3 µB19. Magnetic materials Sr2TbBiO6 and Sr2TbSbO6 possess magnetic moments in the range of 6 to 5 µB, whereas their mechanical properties report that these oxides are brittle20. The experimental and theoretical findings of LaCaO3 report it as a remarkable perovskite catalyst for the generation of methanol and hydrogen21. An indirect bandgap of 21.5 eV is noticed for Sr2YBiO6 with strong UV-visible absorption, making it a strong candidate for UV–visible photovoltaic22. Half-metallic nature is demonstrated by the GGA + U approximation for LaMnO3 and LaFeO3. Furthermore, their elastic properties reveal that both these magnetic materials are ductile23. Perovskite oxides AVO3 (A = Ca, La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Y) offer higher charge capacity at lower operating voltages, making these viable for usage in high energy density electrodes24. Computational analysis of LaFeO3 shows a major reduction in the bandgap from 2.30 eV to 2.14 eV when the material is doped with “Yb”25. Ba2PrXO6 (X = Ir, Pt) reports a transition of bandgap from metallic to semiconductors with strong UV absorption potential26. The electronic properties of PuMgO3 report a half-metallic nature with its possible usage in the optoelectronic and energy-related applications27. Numerous perovskite oxides were studied on a fundamental level extensively, but; their behavior under applied pressure is virtually unstudied. Most of these materials have incredible physical properties but are not considered in the experimental practices because of their very high band gaps. One of the useful methods of adjusting the physical properties of such materials to special performance needs is the application of pressure. By realizing this possibility, in the recent past, researchers started investigating the pressure-dependent behavior of different perovskite oxides with new possibilities of their application in the future in more advanced optoelectronic, energy conversion, and storage technologies. For example, La2NiMnO6 reports transitioning from being brittle to ductile as pressure is applied28. The first principle analysis of RbTaO3 under 0-150 GPa was examined using SP-DFT. The study revealed that the electronic bandgap changes from indirect to direct when pressure is applied29. A significant increase in the mechanical properties of GdAlO3 is noted as the pressure increases. Moreover, it is also noticed that the electronic bandgap for GdAlO3 widens as pressure is increased30. For CaSnO3 the bandgap is enhanced from 3.8 eV to 6.6 eV for the pressure from 0 to 120 GPa31. SrTiO3 reports enhanced ductility under pressure from 0 to 100 GPa32. DFT analysis of XNbO3 (X = Na, K) under 0–50 GPa reveals structural compression with lattice parameter reduction, stable cubic phase, and XRD peak shifts to higher angles33. The VASP program is used to address the thermal properties of LaFeO3 up to 100 GPa. This trend demonstrates that as pressure is raised the melting temperature of the studied oxide increases34. Using the Wien2K code with FP-LAPW and mBJ, AcGaO3 was studied under 0–30 GPa, revealing decreased elastic constants, weaker bonding, and reduced Debye/melting temperatures with pressure. The indirect bandgap narrows, and optical responses shift to lower energies with reduced absorption and conductivity, making AcGaO3 a promising material for optoelectronic and energy storage applications35. Materials with large band gaps are often overlooked in research and technology due to perceived limitations in certain functional applications. However, hydrostatic pressure offers an effective way to tune these band gaps to meet specific requirements, unlocking new opportunities for their use in advanced technologies. As a versatile external parameter, pressure enables precise control over a material’s physical attributes without altering its chemical composition. In this work, we employ DFT-based computation to investigate how hydrostatic pressure influences the physical attributes of LuGaO3, evaluating its potential for optoelectronic.

Simulation technique

The first principles analysis of the studied oxide LuGaO3 under pressure is obtained through the DFT-based code, Wien2K. The FP-LAPW approach, along with the mBJ, is used to assess the physical attributes of LuGaO3. The FP-LAPW technique is a highly accurate approach to analyzing the Kohn-Sham equations36. In this work, LuGaO3 was subjected to hydrostatic pressure by uniformly scaling the lattice parameters to reach the target pressures, which ranged from 0 to 32 GPa with 8 GPa increments. Complete structural optimization was carried out at each pressure point, enabling simultaneous relaxation of the atomic positions and lattice parameters. The total energy, forces on atoms, and stress tensor were converged to high precision, with energy convergence set to less than 10− 6 eV per atom and force convergence below 0.01 eV/Å. This process guarantees that the computed structures match the equilibrium configuration under the given pressure. The structural stability of the studied oxide is assessed through the GGA approximation38. For SCF calculations, the product of the radius of the non-overlapping spheres with the plane wave cutoff in the interstitial region is selected to be 6.5, whereas Lmax and Gmax are selected as 11 and 20 Ry1/2, which are high enough to capture the atomic orbital character accurately. The muffin-tin radii are obtained as Lu = 2.5, Ga = 2.12 and O = 0.89 (a.u)−1, respectively. To ensure high computational accuracy, a dense k-point mesh consisting of 1500 points is employed within the first Brillouin zone. The fine sampling allows an accurate integration over the reciprocal space, which is critical in obtaining accurate values of the total energy and the electronic density of states. Overlapping of spheres in the muffin area is avoided by the choice of energy cutoff − 7 Rydberg. In order to accurately capture the pressure-dependent changes in physical properties, the structural relaxations were repeated independently at each pressure point. The pressure applied in the simulations is isotropic, representing a uniform hydrostatic compression. Additionally, the optical properties are evaluated using the Kramers-Kronig relations.

Structural properties

The structure of LuGaO3 is based on the generic formula of perovskite oxide (ABO3)39. In our studied oxide, the “A” site is occupied by the rare earth element “Lu” and the “B” site is occupied by “Ga”, which is a transition element. The studied oxide possesses a cubic formation with the Pm3m space group. Lu3+ is coordinated by twelve equivalent O2− ions, forming LuO12 cuboctahedra. These LuO12 units are interconnected by sharing corners with twelve other LuO12 cuboctahedra, sharing faces with six additional LuO12 cuboctahedra, and also sharing faces with eight GaO6 octahedra. This structure suggests that the studied oxide is highly symmetric and possesses strong structural integrity. The occupation of the Lu3+ and Ga3+ at the A-site and B-site in the Pm3m space group indicates an ideal perovskite framework in which the LuO12 and GaO6 octahedra form a continuous network through corner and face sharing connections. Figure 1(a) reports the crystal structure of LuGaO3, which is obtained via VESTA software. Furthermore, the atomic bond length and atomic positions are also assessed through the VESTA software40. The atomic bond lengths between Lu-O are 2.66 Å, whereas for Ga-O, the bond length is 1.88 Å. The obtained bond lengths suggest that Ga-O possesses a stronger Columbic interaction as compared to Lu-O. This is due to the smaller ionic radius and is placed at the B-site in the studied oxide, where it is octahedrally linked to oxygen, leading to stronger bond strength. The Wyckoff atomic positions for “Lu”, “Ga” and “O” are noted as 1a (0,0,0), 1b (1/2,1/2,1/2) and 3c (1/2,1/2,0). Moreover, the structure of LuGaO3 is optimized via variation in its volume to obtain the ground state energy of the studied oxide. The volume optimization involves calculating the total energy of a material at different unit cell volumes. The energy-volume data is then fitted using an equation of state, typically the Birch-Murnaghan equation41. The volume corresponding to the minimum energy is identified as the equilibrium volume. This process helps determine the most stable structural configuration of the material and the optimization curve of the studied oxide is presented in Fig. 1(b). Figure 2 illustrates the variation of the lattice constant of LuGaO3 with increasing applied pressure. Interestingly, the lattice constant shows a rising trend as pressure escalates from 0 GPa to 32 GPa. At 0 GPa, the lattice constant is approximately 3.80 Å, and it gradually increases to about 4.19 Å at 32 GPa as reported in Table 2. The observed expansion suggests an anomalous behavior, possibly due to internal structural rearrangements. The rate of increase becomes steeper at higher pressures, indicating non-linear behavior. This trend may imply enhanced interatomic repulsion or structural instability under compression. The rise in lattice parameter in LuGaO3 can be explained by internal structural rearrangements, despite the fact that lattice contraction is usually predicted under hydrostatic pressure. Pressure drives the structure toward greater symmetry in deformed perovskites by reducing the octahedral tilting of Ga2 units. The pseudo-cubic lattice parameter may appear to grow as a result of this tilting suppression. This behavior is also influenced by pressure-induced changes in bond angles rather than bond lengths. These findings align with reports of anisotropic compressibility in comparable perovskite systems. Consequently, rather than actual lattice expansion, the observed trend represents internal structural relaxation.

Mechanical attributes

The resilience level of the material to the external strains is analyzed through its mechanical attributes. The mechanical properties help in assessing the material’s hardness, endurance to cracks, ductility and stiffness43. We have studied the impact of increasing pressure (0–32 GPa) on the mechanical attributes of LuGaO3. The elastic constants help in determining the material’s mechanical properties and for LuGaO3, three elastic constants C11, C12 and C44 are computed. These three elastic constants are sufficient to analyze the mechanical durability of a cubic material7. C11 elastic constant helps in addressing the compressional effects caused by external forces in the longitudinal direction, whereas C12 accounts for compressional effects in the transverse direction. C44 helps in determining the material’s resistance to shear deformation44. Table 3 indicates the computed mechanical properties of LuGaO3 under the influence of pressure. The elastic constants for the studied oxide reveal a dropping trend as pressure is increased, revealing that the material becomes less resistant to the compressional effects occurring in the longitudinal and transverse directions, as well as to shear deformation45. Figure 3(a) reports the variation of elastic constants of LuGaO3 as pressure is enhanced. The material’s endurance to volumetric changes in the presence of external influence is assessed via its Bulk modulus46. The reported values of the bulk modulus indicate a significant reduction in the value, which suggests that the material’s response towards volumetric changes in the presence of external influence declines as pressure is increased from 0 GPa to 32 GPa. The shear modulus aids in analyzing the material’s ability to resist changes occurring in its shape as the deforming forces are applied47. With increasing pressure, the material’s resistance to shape deformations declines. The rigidity and stiffness of the material are analyzed through its Young’s modulus48. The rigidness of the studied material reports a declining trend as pressure is enhanced from 0 GPa to 32 GPa, which indicates that the studied oxide becomes easier to deform. The materials anisotropic nature (0 < A < 1) and isotropic nature (A = 0 or A = 1) is determined through the materials anisotropy factor49. For LuGaO3, the anisotropic factor reports an increasing trend as pressure is increased from 0 GPa to 32 GPa, which suggests that the studied oxide is transitioning from an anisotropic material to an isotropic material. The materials’ ductile and brittle nature is typically assessed via three general criteria, of which the most common is Pugh’s ratio50. Ductile materials have a B/G ratio higher than 1.75; below this, the material is considered brittle. The studied oxide reports ductile behavior as pressure is increased from 0 GPa to 32 GPa, which is also illustrated in Fig. 3(b). It is also noticed that at 8 GPa, the material’s ductility declines and when pressure is raised to 16 GPa and 18 GPa. At 32 GPa, the studied oxide reports a slight decline in the ductility, which suggests that if pressure is enhanced any further, the studied oxide will become brittle. An alternative criterion for evaluating the ductile or brittle nature of a material is Poisson’s ratio. In this approach, materials exhibiting a Poisson’s ratio exceeding 0.26 are regarded as ductile, whereas those below 0.26 are categorized as brittle51. For the studied oxide, Poisson’s ratio remains greater than 0.26 as pressure is raised from 0 GPa to 32 GPa. The same variation is noticed in ductility for Poisson’s ratio as is observed in Pugh’s ratio. Third, the final criterion is the Cauchy’s pressure, which also depicts the material’s ductile and brittle nature with the aid of C12 and C44 elastic constants. For C12 > C44, the material is labeled as ductile, whereas for C12 < C44, the material is termed as brittle52. With applied pressure, the Cauchy pressure values demonstrate a ductile nature for LuGaO3. The declining trend in LuGaO3 can be explained by pressure-induced structural instability and bond weakening. The decrease in C11, C12, and C44 indicates a gradual softening of the lattice, perhaps due to internal structural rearrangements and the suppression of octahedral tilting. These modifications tend to lower bulk, shear, and Young’s moduli by decreasing resistance to both volume and shear deformation. The notable decline in Cauchy’s pressure further suggests decreased mechanical stability under pressure, suggesting a transition to a metastable or mechanically soft state. Furthermore, the growing directional dependence of elastic response under pressure is supported by the variation in the anisotropy factor. Therefore, rather than typical stiffening behavior, the observed patterns represent pressure-driven lattice softening.

Industrial and thermodynamic parameters

The hardness factor reports the material’s endurance to external indentations and scratching53. The hardness factor is the report on the material’s resistance to external indentations and scratching, and this is largely determined by the strength of the interatomic bonding and the resistance to plastic deformation. The larger the value of hardness, the greater the degree of bonding between atoms, as well as the greater the mechanical strength, which means that the material could tolerate mechanical loads and environmental rupturing of the surface in the course of handling or using a device. Therefore, materials that show increased hardness are deemed to be more appropriate in situations that need structural integrity, wear resistance and long-term mechanical integrity, especially under extreme conditions like high pressure or temperature. From the obtained hardness factor values, it is noticed that as pressure is raised from 0 GPa to 32 GPa, the hardness factor value for LuGaO3 decreases gradually, which suggests that at lower pressures the material possesses higher endurance to external indentations. The machinability index (B/C44) reports the level of human effort needed to design or manufacture the studied material54. Materials with a lower machinability index tend to require more effort for their manufacturing, whereas higher values of the machinability index suggest that the material is convenient to work with and can easily be manufactured. The reported values of the machinability index for the studied oxide suggest that as pressure is raised from 0 GPa to 32 GPa, the material’s machinability index drops gradually. This pattern implies that more human effort is required to manufacture LuGaO3 as pressure is increased. Elastic waves are mechanical energy waves that travel through a material by causing temporary deformation, returning the material to its original state once the wave has passed55. The longitudinal and transverse waves are the types of elastic waves which propagate through a material by causing particles to oscillate either parallel or perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation. Table 4 indicates that as pressure increases, these velocities decline significantly, indicating a softening of the material’s elastic stiffness. This reduction in sound velocities under pressure reflects a weakening of interatomic bonding and a decrease in mechanical rigidity. The Debye temperature reveals the material’s stiffness and also provides an insight into lattice vibrations56. According to the Debye temperature values of the studied oxide declines as pressure is increased from 0 GPa to 32 GPa, which suggests a decline in the studied material. The melting temperature signifies the heat needed to change the physical form of the material57. The melting temperature for LuGaO3 also reports a decreasing trend as pressure is increased, which implies that the interatomic bonding between the materials is becoming weaker.

Electronic properties

Pressure modulation is an extremely effective approach in tuning the bandgap of the material58. This section reports the pressure modifications on the electronic bandgap of LuGaO3. The pressure (GPa) values are selected as 0, 8, 16, 24 and 32. Figure 4 reports the reduction in electronic bandgap of LuGaO3 with increasing pressure. At ambient pressure, LuGaO3 possesses an indirect bandgap of 5.02 eV, which reports that it’s an insulator as shown in Fig. 4(a). As pressure is increased to 8 GPa, the bandgap reduces to 4.58 eV, which implies its transitioning from an insulator to a wide band gap semiconductor as reported in Fig. 4(b). From 0 GPa to 8 GPa s significant change is noticed in the conduction band’s dispersion curves, whereas the valence band’s dispersion curves remain unaffected. Furthermore, the bandgap of LuGaO3 declines to 4.29 eV when the pressure is raised to 16 GPa, as presented in Fig. 4(c). This suggests that as pressure is increased till 16 GPa, the studied oxide reports semiconducting behavior. The studied oxide reports a significant decline in the bandgap to 3.65 eV and 2.69 eV as pressure is increased to 24 GPa and 32 GPa, as demonstrated in Fig. 4(d-e). With pressure increasing from 16 GPa to 32 GPa, a prominent change in the materials’ conduction band dispersion curves is noticed. Table 5; Fig. 5 report the tabular and pictorial representation of the band gap decline of LuGaO3 with decreasing pressure. This pattern suggests that with increasing pressure, the material tends to transition from a non-metallic to a metallic character. The total density of states reports the empty and occupied energy states by the electrons in the material59 and for LuGaO3, the TDOS plots are presented in Fig. 4. The TDOS plots for the studied oxide perfectly match the dispersion curves present in both intermediate bands. After the analysis of the TDOS, it’s crucial to analyze the partial density of states, which provides an insight into which state contributes the most in total density plots60. For the studied oxide, Fig. 4 reports the PDOS plots with pressure modulation. Slight contribution of Lu-s states is noticed in the conduction band, whereas in the valence band, Lu-s states exhibit dominance. Moreover, the O-s states are visible in the valence band only. The tunable bandgap of LuGaO3 under pressure makes it suitable for pressure-sensitive optoelectronic devices and tunable photo-detectors. Its transition from insulator to semiconductor also suggests potential applications in high pressure sensors and strain-engineered electronic devices.

The optical response of LuGaO3 is evaluated using the Kramers–Kronig formalism, which provides a reliable framework for describing the interaction of electromagnetic radiation with solids. The extinction coefficient plots for the studied oxide are demonstrated in Fig. 6 (d). The extinction coefficient peaks are shown to systematically shift from higher to lower photon energies as pressure increases. This implies that at lower photon energies under compression, the material’s capacity to absorb and attenuate light becomes more noticeable in the visible region. The optical conductivity shows the material’s ability to conduct charge carriers during photon excitation. The optical conductivity spectra of LuGaO3 displayed in Fig. 6 (e) display maximum peaks at 7.74 eV (0 GPa), 7.03 eV (8 GPa), 6.87 eV (16 GPa), 6.46 eV (24 GPa), and 5.86 eV (32 GPa), respectively. This progressive shift of the conductivity peaks toward lower photon energies with increasing pressure reflects the narrowing of the electronic bandgap, which facilitates optical transitions at reduced energies. Therefore, the observed shift and peak enhancement at lower energies indicate that, under compression, LuGaO3 becomes more responsive to lower-energy photons, improving its potential for optoelectronic devices. Reflectivity is the fraction of falling light that is reflected by the substance’s surface and for the studied oxide, it is presented in Fig. 6(f). The static reflectivity of LuGaO3 is found to be 0.057 (0 GPa), 0.063 (8 GPa), 0.066 (16 GPa), 0.068 (24 GPa) and 0.071 (24 GPa), respectively. This trend indicates that, as pressure is applied, the reflectivity of LuGaO3 increases slowly. The increase in the static reflectivity is a sign of an improvement in the surface optical properties of the studied oxide and this could be explained by changes in the electronic structure upon applying pressure. The higher pressure reflectance, hence, means a smaller optical band gap and an increased metallic or semi-metallic nature, which means that LuGaO3 is more sensitive to incident electromagnetic radiation at high pressures. Furthermore, notable reflectivity features are observed across the visible to UV region, with the highest peak occurring at 13.56 eV, as pressure increases from 0 GPa to 32 GPa. The increase in static reflectivity with pressure is attributed to the narrowing of the bandgap, which enhances the interaction of the material with incident photons. Higher reflectivity in the visible from the UV range implies that, under compression, LuGaO3 could be more effective in photonic devices where efficient light reflection is required. The absorption coefficient measures how well a material absorbs light at particular photon energy. It shows how quickly the light’s intensity drops as it passes through the material. The absorption coefficient for the studied oxide is plotted in Fig. 6 (g). The absorption coefficient spectra of LuGaO3 exhibit pronounced peaks at 13.56 eV as pressure increases from 0 GPa to 32 GPa, indicating strong photon absorption. The peak position remaining in the high-energy range suggests that fundamental optical transitions are primarily driven by higher-energy photons. An energy loss peak in optical spectra refers to the photon energy at which electrons in a material lose the maximum amount of energy due to inelastic scattering, typically associated with plasma oscillations. The energy loss function for the studied oxide is presented in Fig. 6(h). The energy loss spectra of LuGaO3 reveal prominent peaks at 12.72 eV, 10.08 eV, and 9.80 eV for pressures of 0 GPa, 8 GPa, and 16 GPa, respectively. At 24 GPa and 32 GPa, additional significant peaks appear at 9.04 eV and 8.06 eV, with the corresponding maximum peaks recorded at 13.15 eV and 12.23 eV. The band gap reduction directly influences the optical properties, which show a shift toward lower photon energies (redshift), indicating easier electronic transitions. Consequently, increased pressure enhances optical response, higher absorption, conductivity, and reflectivity because electrons require less energy to transition from the valence to the conduction band, consistent with the narrowing of the band gap. These findings suggest improved interaction with lower-energy photons; however, in practical implementation, it would require further experimental validation to evaluate whether LuGaO3 may be considered for optoelectronic applications.

Conclusion

This publication reports the alteration of the physical attributes of LuGaO3 using the Wien2K code. The pressure range is chosen from 0 GPa to 32 GPa with an increase of 8 GPa in each cycle. The precise electronic structures under the influence of pressure are analyzed using the FP-LAPW and mBJ approximation. The investigated structure LuGaO3 is structurally and thermodynamically stable at room pressure. According to LuGaO3’s elastic properties, compressional forces significantly decrease with increasing pressure. As the pressure is raised to 32 GPa, LuGaO3 maintains its ductility. Furthermore, the thermodynamic traits report that the studied oxide becomes more prone to external scratching and softer as pressure is increased till 32 GPa. The electronic bandgap of LuGaO3 decreases from 5.02 eV to 2.69 eV, which is also evident from TDOS/PDOS plots. With applied pressure, the studied oxide show transition form insulator to semiconductor. Furthermore, the optical response shows a considerable redshift from high-energy to lower photon energies, indicating increased activity in the visible area. The pressure-driven spectrum shift makes LuGaO3 a promising material for optoelectronic applications like photovoltaics, visible-range photodetectors, light-emitting diodes, and sophisticated optical sensing technologies.
