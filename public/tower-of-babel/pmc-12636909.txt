Implementation science research in the intensive care unit: a scoping review
Authors: Yingxue Sun, Min Ding, Cong-cong Liu, Wen-shuo Dong, Yv Xia, Lei Wei

Introduction

Implementation science as a bridge between theory and practice enables researchers and policymakers to effectively translate theory into practice, ensuring that the results of scientific research are used effectively.1 This, in turn, contributes to social progress and fosters scientific and technological innovation. Research findings are often not translated into practical applications and supported by evidence before they are adopted and implemented in public health and medical practice. Implementation science, an interdisciplinary field that has emerged to address the adoption, implementation and maintenance of evidence-based practices (EBPs) in frontline work, aims to bridge the knowledge-practice gap and facilitate the adoption and application of EBPs. Studies have shown that it takes an average of 17–20 years to translate clinical innovations into practice and that less than 50% of clinical innovations are generalisable.2 Reducing the gap between evidence and practice, disseminating and translating strong evidence into practice and adopting and applying it on the frontline have become urgent issues.

Intensive care units (ICUs) are specialised wards that care for critically ill and complex patients, characterised by wide individualised variations and continuous 24-hour monitoring. Current developments in EBPs for the management of mechanical ventilation, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), delirium and central catheter bloodstream infections in the ICU have made great strides.3,6 However, the long gap between evidence and practice, as well as the slow pace of integrating EBP into routine care, limits the impact of these advances.7 In the ICU, implementation science aims to translate clinical evidence into actual clinical practice to improve the quality of care and prognosis of critically ill patients. This study integrates multidisciplinary development of new approaches to close this gap. In the ICU, implementation science can accelerate EBP and rapidly translate the latest research findings into clinical practice; implementation science can help develop and promote standardised operating procedures; implementation science emphasises continuous assessment and improvement, and it may be possible to establish a feedback mechanism in the ICU to assess the effectiveness of nursing interventions regularly, make adjustments based on feedback and develop a culture of continuous improvement. Implementation science provides a systematic framework for ICU care to improve the quality of care and prognosis of critically ill patients.

The unique characteristics of ICU wards make it difficult for standardised implementation strategies to be fully applied to every patient, thereby increasing the difficulty of implementing scientific applications.8 9 The stressful and demanding workload of healthcare workers may lead to poor acceptance and compliance with new strategies and programmes. The lack of an organisational climate and incentives to support the implementation of science can also affect the effectiveness of implementation science.10 There are many challenges to the development of implementation science in the ICU.

This review aims to provide a broad overview of the theoretical framework of implementation science in the ICU, the determinants affecting implementation results, the intervention guided by a theoretical framework and the assessment of implementation effectiveness based on an implementation science perspective to further advance the implementation of evidence in the field of ICU care.

Methods

Overview

This review was based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and their structured approach provided by the guidelines.11 This review was based on the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-SCR). The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Registry of Systematic Reviews on 15 December 2024 (registration number: CRD42024614246).

Search strategy

Searches were performed using PubMed, Cochrane Library, EBSCO and Web of Science electronic databases. This review was conducted by combining the terms ‘implementation science’, ‘evidence-based practice’, ‘ICU’, ‘intensive care unit’ and ‘clinical research’. The search was conducted at the beginning of 15 December 2024. See online supplemental appendix 1 for the complete database search strategy.

Inclusion criteria

Papers that met all of the following criteria were included: (1) implementation science-related studies, (2) ICU-related studies, (3) primary research and (4) articles published in English in peer-reviewed journals. To meet inclusion criteria for this review, it was not required that the research reports use the language or conventions of IS, but the data presented had to conceptually address the domains of implementation science.12 13

Excluded from the review were non-original research articles, including reviews, studies that were not implementation science studies.

Selection of an article

Two researchers conducted the literature search by referring to the above search strategies, and one researcher removed duplicates using EndNote. Two independent investigators screened the retrieved studies based on their titles and abstracts using eligibility criteria. Subsequently, the same two researchers conducted a full-text screening. Implementation scientific framework comparative analysis

Theoretical framework of implementation science

Seven studies applied the consolidated framework for implementation research (CFIR),15 16 18 20 22 23 25 two studies applied the i-PARIHS,17 27 one study applied the translating research into practice (TRIP),26 one study applied the exploration, preparation, implementation, sustain (EPIS),24 one study applied the reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation and maintenance (RE-AIM)19 and one study applied the behaviour change wheel (BCW).21 Damschroder et al28 proposed CFIR in 2009 and consists of 5 main dimensions, and 39 components, including intervention characteristics, individual characteristics, external environment, internal environment and implementation process, which is a strong theoretical guide for identifying various barriers and facilitators in the process of evidence-based implementation, as well as how the factors interact with each other to influence implementation and effectiveness. It has strong theoretical guidance for identifying barriers and facilitators in the process of evidence implementation, and how the interaction between factors affects implementation.29 The core conceptual equation of i-PARIHS is ‘SI=Facn(I+R+C)’, with ‘SI’ being the successful application of evidence, ‘Facn’ being facilitation, ‘I’ being change, ‘R’ being recipients and ‘C’ being the organisational environment.30 The success of an EBP initiative depends on three major elements: the level and nature of evidence, the organisational environment in which the evidence is applied and the facilitation of the translation of evidence into practice.30 The TRIP model, proposed in 2008, consists of seven parts: evidence identification and prioritisation, contextualised adaptation and adjustment, multilevel implementation strategies, collaborative stakeholder engagement, scaled diffusion and dissemination, continuous support and infrastructure, dynamic monitoring and iterative optimisation.31 Through the combination of structured steps and dynamic feedback, the model aims to achieve an efficient and lasting transformation from evidence to practice. EPIS was proposed in 2011 by Moullin et al.32 The main components include four well-defined phases describing the implementation process, the identification of the external system and internal organisational environments and their correlates, the innovation factors associated with the characteristics of the EBP being implemented and the bridging factors connecting the EBPs, the dynamics, complexity and interactions of the external and internal environments.32 In 1999, American scholars Glasgow et al proposed the RE-AIM framework, which consists of five dimensions: accessibility, effectiveness, adoption, implementation and sustainability, and uses a structured approach to evaluate the implementation of the implementation comprehensively.33 The BCW is an integrative model developed from 19 behaviour change frameworks for designing interventions, with 6 overarching domains of competence, opportunity and motivation-behaviour (COM-B) and 14 domains of the theoretical domains framework (TDF), which expands the COM-B 6 central domains to delineate further the factors influencing the target behaviour.34 The intervention function includes nine strategies that can be used to change behaviour. Specific COM-B and TDF domains are associated with specific intervention functions.

Intervention guided by the theoretical framework

The seven studies included in this paper used CFIR to organise and analyse the data into lists of facilitators and barriers under CFIR headings. Similar facilitators and barriers were categorised and coded to form major themes. Thematic analysis was used to identify themes, which were then mapped to CFIR intervention characteristics, CFIR individual characteristics, CFIR internal environment, CFIR external environment and CFIR implementation process. For example, this study developed interventions to address barriers to implementation, including minimal blood draws, use of a closed-loop system to reduce discarded blood, use of microtubes and standardised blood cultures.23 The two studies included in this paper constructed a change process model based on i-PARIHS, which consists of multiple stages, including planning, decision to adopt change, implementation and facilitation and sustainability. The study focused on barriers and facilitators to clinical practice change and transfusion in the ICU according to the i-PARIHS framework, constructed around the i-PARIHS constructs (evidence-based intervention, recipient, context and facilitation) and associated subconstructs.17 The study was guided by the TRIP framework, which identifies checklists of improvement behaviours, identifies barriers to implementation, develops interventions, develops new blood culture procurement criteria, implements chlorhexidine baths and dressings, uses alcohol-impregnated caps, routinely changes central venous catheters and evaluates the effectiveness of implementation.26 The study was guided by the EPIS framework. Participants described the ICU structure and quality improvement priorities, as well as previous experiences in planning and implementing quality improvement efforts, and discussed the adaptability of the EBP in the hospital setting, available resources to support quality improvement, staff openness to the programme and working relationships between site and ICU innovation team members.24 A list of a priori codes derived from the RE-AIM model was used to understand how feedback could be used to improve the intervention.19 Coding was conducted for key activities (communication, meetings, decision-making) that produce outputs (deliverables) and drive partnership outcomes (completion of goals, improvement of programmes/interventions, improved research/quality control/intervention delivery capacity, improved clinical care, sustainable partnership infrastructure). The study used the BCW to examine specific COM-B and TDF domains associated with specific intervention functions to develop a corresponding intervention.21

Effectiveness of implementation and determinants of effectiveness

With the implementation of scientific guidance, all included ICU-related research questions improved significantly. The transfusion rate decreased from 32.1% to 20.7% guided by the CFIR, and 93% of ICU nurses expressed a willingness to use the interventions in the long term, suggesting that implementation of the scientific method facilitates the selection of an intervention strategy and enhances its acceptance.23 Guided by the CFIR, this study found that the primary facilitator for promoting recovery for ICU patients was using the patient’s story to create interpersonal connections for other clinicians and hospital administrators; barriers were limitations in patient access to outpatient services and funding. The primary facilitator for establishing peer support groups was the development of successful processes and systems; barriers were patient and family participation rates. The application of implementation science methods can help to establish postcritical care unit outpatient and peer support programmes and reduce the burden of postcritical care syndrome.15 Guided by the CFIR, the study identified four organisational characteristics that contribute to healthcare-acquired infections: lack of neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission protocols, overcrowding, understaffing and equipment shortages; resource constraints in the NICU reflecting a social climate that devalues medical care for infants and respondents were highly motivated to prevent healthcare-acquired infections but expressed serious concerns about poststudy sustainability.16 This study applied CFIR to assess the barriers and facilitators to the implementation of integrated family care, the inclusion of parents in the NICU care team, both of which facilitated the implementation of integrated family care in the NICU.18 Guided by the CFIR, this study found that hospital support, bedside clinician buy-in and leadership facilitated the implementation of the prone position, suggesting that the successful implementation of the prone position during the COVID-19 pandemic could serve as an important evidence-based nursing practice for the application of implementation science in critical care to improve the quality of ICU care.20 This study found that the identified determinants of successful implementation, guided by CFIR, were related to individual characteristics of healthcare professionals, ward characteristics and the healthcare system. Weaning boards can serve as a useful tool for mechanical ventilation in the ICU, facilitating knowledge sharing among professionals and supporting patient-oriented care.22 This study found that both the CFIR internal setting and the Organizational Readiness for Implementing Change (ORIC) measure scored above 3, leadership engagement scored the highest, leadership support, a supportive team culture and a positive learning climate were important prerequisites for implementing family care initiatives, and that a collaborative team process, a supportive organisational structure and adequate competencies were critical to the implementation of complex family support interventions.25

Guided by the i-PARIHS framework, paediatric ICUs were found to be high-risk environments for change in clinical practice: complexity of team-based care, high-risk care limiting acceptability of change, variable readiness for change, limited unit conditions and emotional harm in the PICU setting were found to be barriers to change.17 Guided by the i-PARIHS framework, this study found that alternatives to physical restraint were rarely used before physical restraint was initiated; 7.2% of patients received alternative restraints; prophylactic physical restraints were used on mechanically ventilated, sedated or unconscious patients: 71% of patients received prophylactic restraints; nurses missed clinical records: 75% of discrete observations of patients using physical restraints were not documented; limited discussion of physical restraints during rounds: the use of physical restraints was discussed in 10% of daytime interprofessional team rounds.27

Guided by the TRIP framework, the rate of central venous-associated bloodstream infections in the ICU decreased from 15.5% to 0% after the use of the implementation of a scientific framework to guide the interventions.26 Guided by the EPIS framework, the study found that at the provider level, structural characteristics (hospital size, critical care staffing) did not vary by EBP implementation rate and that differences in staffing patterns and culture varied by implementation; at the clinician team level, time and financial resources were the main barriers and differences in attitudes and autonomy to change across sites were key factors affecting programme implementation.24

The study found that clinical academic collaboration, guided by the RE-AIM model, increased family engagement, empowerment and effective care management. Clinical Academic Collaboration completed rigorous evidence-based nursing research on family-centred care. Promote excellence in nursing practice, research and education by accelerating knowledge translation and earlier infusion of implementation science principles into the development of evidence-based nursing interventions.19

The study found an overall lack of consensus on EBP for patients with hypoxemic respiratory failure (HRF) and ARDS in the ICU, guided by the BCW, a lack of certain interventions, procedures, and clinical information critical to the pathway and social influences (social/professional roles and identities, environmental contexts and resources, physical skills, beliefs about competence, memory, attention and decision-making) among other factors as determinants. Improving care pathways for HRF and ARDS in the ICU based on the implementation of scientific strategies can help to improve the quality of patient care and increase the onset of adherence to evidence-based care.21

Discussion

Implementation science has gained widespread attention and importance internationally, but it is still in the early stages of development compared with traditional disciplines. Studies have shown that the evidence applied in practice is not widely accessible. However, a substantial gap remains between theoretical approaches and practice.35

Applicability and comparative analysis of theoretical frameworks

Based on the findings summarised in this paper, the CFIR dimensions of ‘Internal Environment (Organizational Support)’ and ‘Process (Leadership Engagement)’ effectively explain the decline in ICU healthcare-associated infection rates (interviews revealed direct leadership promotion and improved cross-departmental collaboration).16 The core purpose of the CFIR is to identify and evaluate key factors influencing intervention implementation systematically. Its comprehensive framework facilitates multidimensional, full-cycle management. One study used the CFIR to identify expediters and barriers to intervention implementation, improve colorectal cancer screening and provide recommendations for improving future interventions.36 Another study used the CFIR to evaluate expediters and barriers to the early implementation of outpatient speciality palliative care, as well as associated clinical characteristics.37 i-PARIHS focuses primarily on the ‘facilitator’ element (where doctors and nurses assess the timing and indications for physical restraints), effectively improving the management of physical restraints for ICU patients.27 i-PARIHS emphasises the core role of the ‘facilitator’ in achieving multifactorial transformation. Applying i-PARIHS to establish a paediatric pain collaborative group to co-design innovative solutions for paediatric emergency room pain care facilitates a broad examination of research evidence, clinician and family experiences, and local assessment data to develop a critical assessment of the context and culture of pain management, thereby generating innovation and facilitating the implementation of a cycle of action focus that has been effectively applied in transforming pain care in an emergency.38 TRIP provides a stepwise pathway for knowledge translation, involving the identification of the problem (high catheter-related infection rates), evaluation of the evidence (improved behavioural checklists), adaptation of the evidence (establishing new blood culture collection standards) and integration of the evidence (designating and implementing comprehensive procedures). By sequentially addressing these issues, this approach effectively reduced the rates of catheter-related bloodstream infections.26 This theoretical framework has also been explored in other fields. An Australian surfing team implemented a four-phase applied research model to bridge the gap between scientific insights and practical applications in the daily training environment, ensuring timely and impactful high-performance results.39 This study also identifies the limitations inherent in single theoretical frameworks. The RE-AIM evaluation demonstrated high intervention coverage but low long-term retention rates, highlighting the importance of evaluation rather than intervention design.19 While the RE-AIM ‘retention’ metric reflects outcomes, it fails to explain ‘why retention rates are low’. The limitations of the RE-AIM framework are also noted in this study.40 By integrating the ‘sustainment phase’ elements of the EPIS framework (ICU quality continuous improvement) in this study, we found that low retention rates stem from external ‘internal environment’ factors (lack of experience in planning and implementing quality improvement initiatives).24 The EPIS explicitly analyses these dynamic factors, enabling seamless task integration across all phases of the project. One study demonstrated how community pharmacies can use EPIS assessments to support participating pharmacists at each stage of the process.41 This study designed optimal care intervention pathways (BCW-based strategies) for HFR and ARDS, focusing on the behavioural change technique pathway while overlooking the underlying motivational factors of ‘patient trust in interventions’ (such as scepticism regarding healthcare professionals’ expertise).21 Combined with the CFIR’s ‘individual characteristics’ (eg, patient health literacy), this has led to BCW interventions being prone to failure due to external interference. This study describes the limitations of using the BCW as an intervention to improve family-centred care in adult audiology services.42

Practical implications of complementary theoretical frameworks

Given the limitations of the single frameworks revealed in the findings, a collaborative framework approach is proposed, in which the dynamic implementation process requires the integrated application of the EPIS, CFIR and i-PARIHS. EPIS’s phased structure provides a temporal framework, while CFIR’s 41 subdimensions refine specific influencing factors at each stage. Additionally, i-PARIHS’s ‘facilitator’ role explains the key drivers during phase transitions. This tripartite integration enables more systematic guidance for the full lifecycle management of complex interventions. Behavioural intervention design requires the complementary application of the BCW and CFIR: the BCW addresses the technical question of ‘how to change behaviour’, while the CFIR concurrently analyses ‘external barriers to behavioural change’. Their combination prevents intervention failure owing to ‘technically feasible but environmentally infeasible’ scenarios. From an implementation science perspective, the barriers to and facilitators of evidence implementation in ICU nursing remain unclear.43 In-depth research on the underlying mechanisms of barriers is required, with a preference for mixed-method research designs across all implementation phases, to further advance the application and translation of evidence into clinical practice.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. This is the first comprehensive review to examine implementation science in the ICU. This exploratory approach prevents in-depth bias analyses in individual studies. Potential biases arose from the omission of quality assessments, exclusion of non-English studies and reliance on only four databases. Multiple independent reviewers and software-assisted screening reduced screening bias.

Conclusion

This review provides an overview of the theoretical framework of implementation science in ICU nursing, the determinants affecting implementation outcomes, the intervention guided by the theoretical framework of implementation science and assessing implementation effectiveness, providing a theoretical foundation and methodological guidance for future research in ICU nursing through implementation science. Implementation science is an available resource for ICU nurses to translate evidence into practice, and through the practice of implementation science research, ICU nurses can build a solid evidence base for clinical practice and solve clinical nursing problems. In the future, more researchers are needed to conduct more in-depth implementation science studies on ICU nursing to facilitate evidence translation and improve the quality of nursing practice.
