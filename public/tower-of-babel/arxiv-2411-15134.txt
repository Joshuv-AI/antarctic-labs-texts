Toric invariance of vertically parametrized systems
Elisenda Feliu and Oskar Henriksson

Abstract
. We consider the problem of deciding whether the solution sets of a parametrized polynomial system are toric in the sense that they admit a monomial parametrization. We focus on vertically parametrized systems, which are sparse systems where we allow linear dependencies between coefficients in front of the same monomial. We give a matroid-theoretic characterization of the maximal-dimensional torus for which all solution sets are invariant under componentwise multiplication. Building on this, we provide necessary conditions and sufficient conditions for when the solution sets are unions of finitely many or a unique coset of this torus. The motivation of this work comes from the theory of reaction networks, where toric structure of the steady state system substantially simplifies the determination of multistationarity; here, we show that this is also the case for absolute concentration robustness and steady state invariants.



1.
Introduction

Toric varieties are central objects in algebraic geometry, and appear naturally in many applications, including polynomial system solving
[
62
,
66
]
, statistics
[
30
]
, phylogenetics
[
63
]
, and chemical reaction network theory
[
15
]
. Much is known about the geometry of toric varieties
[
20
]
,
but effectively deciding whether a given variety is toric from an implicit description remains a hard problem, which has recently been approached from the point of view of, e.g., symbolic computation
[
28
]
and Lie theory
[
49
,
43
]
.

This paper focuses on deciding whether the
positive
zero sets
of a
parametric
polynomial system have toric structure.
More precisely,
for a parametric system
F\in{\mathbb{R}}[\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{m},x_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm}]^{s}
(with parameters
\kappa
and variables
x
)
we consider the set
\mathcal{Z}_{>0}
of parameters
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
for which
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
is nonempty. We say that
F

is
toric
over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
if
there is an exponent matrix
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
such that for all
\kappa\in\mathcal{Z}_{>0}
,
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
admits a monomial parametrization of the form

{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{d}\to\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa}),\quad t\mapsto\alpha_{\kappa}\circ t^{A}\,

for some
\alpha_{\kappa}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{n}
depending on
\kappa
, and where
\circ
denotes componentwise multiplication.
In this case, we view
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
as a coset of the multiplicative subgroup
\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0}=\{t^{A}:t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{d}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}
and write

\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})=\alpha_{\kappa}\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0}.

Detecting whether a system is toric in this sense
can be formulated as a quantifier elimination problem
[
56
]
, which gives a detection algorithm, albeit at a substantial computational cost. Another general but computationally intense approach is considered in
[
60
,
16
,
57
]
, which give sufficient conditions in terms of Gröbner bases and comprehensive Gröbner systems.

Here, we focus on the special case when
F
is
vertically parametrized
in the language of
[
41
,
24
]
, in the sense that it can be written as

F=C(\kappa\circ x^{M})\,,

where each row of
C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s\times m}
encodes a linear combination of
m
monomials with exponents given by the columns of
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
and scaled by the parameters
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{m})
.

.

It is not hard to see that the system is toric over

as, for each

, the positive zero locus admits the parametrization

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{>0}\to\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})\,,\quad(t_{1},t_{2})\mapsto\left(t_{1},t_{2},\tfrac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{2}+\kappa_{3}}\,t_{1}t_{2},\tfrac{\kappa_{3}(\kappa_{5}+\kappa_{6})}{\kappa_{4}\kappa_{6}},\tfrac{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{3}}{\kappa_{6}(\kappa_{2}+\kappa_{3})}\,t_{1}t_{2}\right).\qed

Vertically parametrized systems describe the steady states of reaction networks and the critical points of multivariate polynomials. They include sparse polynomial systems with fixed support, but the framework also allows for linear dependencies among the coefficients of the same monomial.
Previous work on vertically parametrized systems has addressed the generic root count
[
41
]
and tropical homotopies for polynomial system solving
[
34
]
. Algebraic-geometric properties such as dimension and nondegeneracy were treated in
[
24
]
, where it was shown that generic solvability, in the sense that

\mathcal{Z}_{>0}
has nonempty interior, is equivalent to the linear algebra condition

(1.1)

\rk(C\diag(w)M^{\top})=\rk(C)\>\>\text{for some $w\in\ker(C)$\,. }

For reaction networks, knowing that the steady state system is toric over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
is especially relevant, since it simplifies the analysis of key problems such as multistationarity. In this context, several
sufficient
conditions to guarantee that the steady state system

is toric are known. This includes binomiality of the ideal
\langle F_{\kappa}\rangle
for all
\kappa

[

,
17
]
, as well as the concept of
complex-balanced equilibria

[
36
,
15
]
, and the related notions of deficiency
[
22
,
11
]
and disguised toric dynamical systems
[
10
]
.

In this work, we first investigate
the

condition that

, meaning that

\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0}

Equivalently, each

This property can be seen as the positive analog of being a

over the complex numbers

]
.

.

Our approach to detecting and ruling out toric structure has the following three steps, the theoretical foundations of which are summarized in parts (i)-(iii) of
Theorem

A
.
First, we determine the unique (up to row operations) maximal-rank matrix
A
for which
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
is invariant for all
\kappa
through the linear algebra condition in (i). Secondly, in part (ii), we characterize when the dimension of
\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0}
and of
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
generically agree, to determine whether
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
is generically a finite union of
\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0}
-cosets. Importantly, part (i) and (ii) together give a way to rule out
F
being toric. Finally, we derive a range of sufficient conditions for
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
to consist of a single coset. These conditions come from the observation that, by Birch’s Theorem, each coset intersects each parallel translate of
\ker(A)
precisely once, thus giving rise to the
coset counting system
appearing in the disjoint union in (iii).

Theorem A

(
Theorems

3.7
,
4.7
and
5.3
and
Proposition

6.1
)
.

Let
F=C(\kappa\circ x^{M})
be a vertically parametrized system defined by
M=[\,M_{1}\,M_{2}\cdots M_{m}\,]\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
and
C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s\times m}
with
\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{m}\neq\varnothing
, and let
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
.

(i)

(Invariance)
If
C^{\prime}
is the reduced row echelon form of
C
, then
F
is invariant with respect to
A
if and only if

A(M_{i}-M_{j})=0\quad\text{for all $i,j\in\supp(v)$ and all rows $v$ of $C^{\prime}$}.

(ii)

(Locally toric systems)
Suppose
F
is invariant with respect to
A
and that (
1.1
) holds.
Then
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
is a union of finitely many
\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0}
-cosets for generic
\kappa\in\mathcal{Z}_{>0}
if and only if

{\rk(C)+\rk(A)=n\,.}

(iii)

(Counting cosets) Suppose
F
is invariant with respect to
A
. Then, for each
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
and each
b\in A({\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0})
, it holds that

\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})=\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa},\,Ax-b)}\alpha\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0}\,.

Invariance is the topic of
Sections

3
and
4
. We prove part (i) by characterizing the stronger notion of invariance of the zero locus over
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
, and showing that invariance of the positive zero locus is equivalent to invariance of the complex zero locus. This is not true for general parametric systems; see
Example

3.13
. Notably, part (i) shows that invariance only depends on the
column matroid
of
C
(rather than the specific entries of
C
) and on the affine span of subsets of columns of
M
determined by the
connected components
of this matroid. In particular, invariance can only arise
if the system, after putting
C
in reduced row echelon form, has the property that each polynomial has exponent vectors belonging to a parallel translate of
\row(A)^{\perp}
. This generalizes the well-known fact that a polynomial is quasihomogeneous if and only if its Newton polytope is contained in an affine hyperplane
[
29
, Ch. 6.1]
. In informal terms, this means that invariance reflects that the system “effectively depends on fewer than
n
variables” as discussed in
Remark

4.10
. This is not the case for arbitrary parametric systems; see
Example

4.11
.

Part (ii) is the topic of
Section

5
and the coset counting system of part (iii) is discussed in
Section

6
. Here, we also derive bounds on the number of cosets and sufficient conditions for
F
to be toric. These conditions are gathered in
Algorithm

2
and implemented in our Julia package
ToricVerticalSystems.jl
.

In
Section

7
, we study consequences of invariance and (local) toric structure in the context of reaction network theory and use our approach to provide new mathematical insights to previously established results. After recalling the basic terminology in
Section

7.1
, we study in

,

, and

. In general, these are computationally challenging properties to detect or rule out, but with (locally) toric structure for a known exponent matrix

,
the analysis simplifies substantially.

In

7.3
, we introduce a model-reduction technique, based on the concept of intermediates from
[

,

]
, that preserves invariance. In
Section

7.4
, we
apply our algorithms to biochemically relevant networks from the database ODEbase
[

]
as a case study. The latter shows that our conditions are often enough to conclusively

for realistic networks.

Finally, in

, we connect our results to more specialized notions of toric structure in the literature: quasithermostatic networks (and the related notion of
toric dynamical systems
of
[

]
), and networks with binomial steady state ideal (also known as

in
[

]
). In particular,

2.
Vertically parametrized systems

Throughout, we work with Laurent polynomials with coefficients in
\mathbbm{k}\in\{{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\}
.
We consider
vertically parametrized systems
(or
vertical systems
for short), which are parametric systems of the form

(2.1)

F=C(\kappa\circ x^{M})\in\mathbbm{k}[\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{m},x_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm}]^{s},

consisting of
s\leq n
polynomials with parameters
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{m})
and variables

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, encoded by a coefficient matrix
C\in\mathbbm{k}^{s\times m}
and an exponent matrix
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
.
The componentwise product
\kappa\circ x^{M}
indicates that the monomial encoded by the
i
th column of
M
is scaled by
\kappa_{i}
, while the rows of
C
give linear combinations of the scaled monomials. An important feature is that
F
is linear in the parameters and that each parameter always accompanies the same monomial (though a monomial is accompanied by different parameters if
M
has repeated columns).
We also consider
augmented vertically parametrized systems
of the form

\left(C(\kappa\circ x^{M}),\,Lx-b\right)\in\mathbbm{k}[\kappa,b,x^{\pm}]^{s+d},\qquad s+d\leq n\,,

where we also allow for
d\geq 0
affine linear equations, encoded by a coefficient matrix
L\in\mathbbm{k}^{d\times n}

and parametric constant terms
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{d})
. Geometrically, this corresponds to intersecting the variety given by the vertical system
C(\kappa\circ x^{M})
by a parallel translate of
\ker(L)
.

For an augmented vertical system
F
, we denote the specialization at
(\kappa,b)\in\mathbbm{k}^{m+d}
by

F_{\kappa,b}=F(\kappa,b,\cdot)\in\mathbbm{k}[x^{\pm}]^{s+d}\,.

Although our primary interest is in the positive zeros of
F
, it is convenient to work in a more general setting. We will therefore consider the set of zeros over a multiplicative group of scalars
{\mathbb{G}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0},{\mathbb{R}}^{*},{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\}
, and the corresponding solvability locus, denoted by

\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa,b})=\{x\in{\mathbb{G}}^{n}:F_{\kappa,b}(x)=0\}\,,\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{{\mathbb{G}}}=\{(\kappa,b)\in{\mathbb{G}}^{m}\times\mathbbm{k}^{d}:\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa,b})\neq\varnothing\}\,.

We implicitly consider the ground field to be
\mathbbm{k}={\mathbb{R}}
if
{\mathbb{G}}
is
{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
or
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
, and
\mathbbm{k}={\mathbb{C}}
if
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{C}}^{*}
. We will often abbreviate “
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
” by “
{>\!0}
” in superscripts and subscripts.

Key results from
[
24
]
on generic dimension and nondegeneracy for augmented vertical systems are reviewed next, as they play an important role later on.

For an augmented vertical system
F\in\mathbbm{k}[\kappa,b,x^{\pm}]^{s+d}
, we say that a zero
x\in{\mathbb{G}}^{n}
of
F_{\kappa,b}
is
nondegenerate
if the Jacobian
J_{F_{\kappa,b}}(x)
has rank
s+d
. A nondegenerate zero of
F_{\kappa,b}
is in particular a nonsingular point of
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa,b})
.
We recall that
\dim(\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa,b}))\geq n-s-d
for all
(\kappa,b)\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{m}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
such that
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa,b})\neq\varnothing
,
and if in addition, all irreducible components contain a nondegenerate zero, then
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa,b})
has pure dimension
n-s-d
(cf.
[
19
, §9.6, Thm. 9]
).

A summary of the main conclusions from
[
24
]
is given in the next proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{G}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0},{\mathbb{R}}^{*},{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\}
and
F\in\mathbbm{k}[\kappa,b,x^{\pm}]^{s+d}
be an augmented vertical system with defining matrices
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
,
C\in\mathbbm{k}^{s\times m}
of rank
s
with
\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{G}}^{m}\neq\varnothing
, and
L\in\mathbbm{k}^{d\times n}
.
Consider the rank condition

(2.2)

\rk\begin{bmatrix}C\diag(w)M^{\top}\diag(h)\\
L\end{bmatrix}=s+d\quad\text{for some }w\in\ker(C)\text{ and }h\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}.

Then one of the following two scenarios occurs:

(i)

If (
2.2
) holds, then

\mathcal{Z}_{{\mathbb{G}}}
has nonempty Euclidean interior. Furthermore,
there exists a nonempty Zariski open subset
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{Z}_{{\mathbb{G}}}
such that for all
(\kappa,b)\in\mathcal{U}
, it holds that

\dim(\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa,b}))=\dim(\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa,b}))=n-s-d

and that all zeros of
F_{\kappa,b}
in
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
are nondegenerate. If in addition (
2.2
) holds for all
w\in\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{G}}^{m}
and
h\in{\mathbb{G}}^{n}
, then we can take
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbb{G}}
.

(ii)

If (
2.2
) does not hold, then
\mathcal{Z}_{{\mathbb{G}}}
is contained in a hypersurface, and, for all
(\kappa,b)\in\mathcal{Z}_{{\mathbb{G}}}
, it holds that
\dim(\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa,b}))>n-s-d
and that all zeros of
F_{\kappa,b}
in
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
are degenerate.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem 3.7, together with Propositions 2.11, 3.2 and 3.11, and Remark 3.6 in
[
24
]
.
∎

In view of
Proposition

, we say that
an augmented vertical system
F\in\mathbbm{k}[\kappa,b,x^{\pm}]^{s+d}
is

{\mathbb{G}}
if
\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{G}}^{m}\neq\varnothing
and (

) holds. This is equivalent to

having a nondegenerate zero in
{\mathbb{G}}
for some parameter choice and also to

\mathcal{Z}_{{\mathbb{G}}}
being Zariski dense in
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
.
If (
2.2
) does not hold, then we say that

is

.
Observe that nondegeneracy of

over
{\mathbb{G}}
is equivalent to nondegeneracy over
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
, as long as
\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{G}}^{m}\neq\varnothing
.

When
F
is a vertical system, condition (
2.2
) reduces to

(2.3)

\rk(C\diag(w)M^{\top})=s\quad\text{for some }w\in\ker(C)\,.

.

For the vertical system in

and

Remark 2.3

(Freely parametrized systems)
.

As discussed in
[
24
, §3.6]
, vertical systems include
freely parametrized systems
, obtained by fixing the support and letting all coefficients vary freely. Given support sets
\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{s}\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, the corresponding freely parametrized system is the vertical system given by

C=\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&C_{s}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{with}\quad C_{i}=[1\ \cdots\ 1]\in{\mathbb{C}}^{1\times\#\mathcal{S}_{i}},\quad\text{and}\quad M=[M_{1}\ \cdots\ M_{s}]\,,

where the columns
M_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times\#\mathcal{S}_{i}}
are the elements of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
(in some fixed order).
Restricting to
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
allows us to consider systems with fixed support and coefficients with fixed sign by specifying the signs of the
C_{i}
in the construction above (coefficients of free sign are included by repeating the monomial with opposite signs). For instance, the vertical system

(2.4)

F=(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}+\kappa_{3}x_{2}^{4}-2\kappa_{4}x_{1}^{6}\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}[\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3},\kappa_{4},x_{1}^{\pm},x_{2}^{\pm}]

consisting of a single polynomial
can be thought of as a generic system with support
x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}
,
x_{2}^{4}
and
x_{1}^{6}
, where the coefficient of
x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}
may take arbitrary signs, and the coefficients of
x_{2}^{4}
and
x_{1}^{6}
are fixed to
+
and
-
, respectively. (We will revisit this system in
Example

6.8
.)

3.
Toric invariance and cosets

In this section, we define the various forms of toric invariance that we study in this work.

Definition 3.1
.

The
{\mathbb{G}}
-torus
and
torus
associated with
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
are, respectively,

\mathcal{T}_{A}^{\mathbb{G}}=\{t^{A}:t\in{\mathbb{G}}^{d}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{G}}^{n},\qquad\mathcal{T}_{A}=\mathcal{T}_{A}^{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}=\{t^{A}:t\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{d}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\,.

We view
\mathcal{T}_{A}^{{\mathbb{G}}}
as a multiplicative subgroup of
{\mathbb{G}}^{n}
and consider the cosets
\alpha\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}^{{\mathbb{G}}}
for
\alpha\in{\mathbb{G}}^{n}
.
Note that the exponent matrix
A
can be replaced by any other matrix with the same row lattice
\row_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
(and over
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
and
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
, it suffices that the row spans over
{\mathbb{Q}}
agree).
Also note that over
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
, a coset
\alpha\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}
is the same as a
very affine scaled toric variety
.

We now use cosets to define the three notions of invariance illustrated in
Figure

1.1
.

Definition 3.2
.

For
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
,
{\mathbb{G}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0},{\mathbb{R}}^{*},{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\}
, and a set
X\subseteq{\mathbb{G}}^{n}
, we say that:

•

X
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{G}}
if
X\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}^{{\mathbb{G}}}\subseteq X

(i.e.,
x\circ t^{A}\in X
for all
x\in X
and
t\in{\mathbb{G}}^{d}
). In this case,
X
is a union of
\mathcal{T}_{A}^{{\mathbb{G}}}
-cosets. We denote the set of these cosets by
X/\mathcal{T}_{A}^{{\mathbb{G}}}
.

•

X
is
locally
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric over
{\mathbb{G}}
if
X
is

\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{G}}
and
1\leq\#(X/\mathcal{T}_{A}^{{\mathbb{G}}})<\infty
.

•

X
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric over
{\mathbb{G}}
if it is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{G}}
and
\#(X/\mathcal{T}_{A}^{{\mathbb{G}}})=1
.

Definition 3.3
.

For
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
,
{\mathbb{G}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0},{\mathbb{R}}^{*},{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\}
, and a vertical system
F\in\mathbbm{k}[\kappa,x^{\pm}]^{s}
, we say:

•

F
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{G}}
if
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{G}}
for all
\kappa\in{\mathbb{G}}^{m}
, that is,

(3.1)

F_{\kappa}(x\circ t^{A})=0,\qquad\text{for all}\quad\kappa\in{\mathbb{G}}^{m},\ x\in\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa}),\text{ and }t\in{\mathbb{G}}^{d}\,.

•

F
is
(generically) (locally)
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric over
{\mathbb{G}}
if
F
is nondegenerate over
{\mathbb{G}}
and
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})
is (locally)
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric over
{\mathbb{G}}
(generically) for
\kappa\in\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbb{G}}
.

If
{\mathbb{G}}
is omitted, it is implicitly assumed that
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.

Example 3.4
.

The vertical system

in

\mathcal{Z}_{>0}={\mathbb{R}}^{6}_{>0}
and is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric over

. However, this is not required in our definition of

-toric, and such a choice of
\alpha_{\kappa}
might not exist (cf.
Example

).
∎

Example 3.5
.

The vertical system defined by the polynomial

∎

.

Consider the following vertical system with

In the light of
Definition

3.3
, we take the following two-step approach to decide whether a vertical system
F
is toric over
{\mathbb{G}}
:

(1)

Find a maximal-rank matrix
A
such that
F
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{G}}
, i.e., satisfies (
3.1
). Under mild assumptions, this comes down to a linear algebra condition, which we discuss in
Section

4
.

(2)

Bound
\#(\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})/\mathcal{T}_{A}^{{\mathbb{G}}})
by using concepts from
polyhedral and real algebraic geometry;
this is the topic of
Sections

5
and
6
.

A key observation is that the study of invariance can be reduced to the case
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
{\mathbb{G}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0},{\mathbb{R}}^{*},{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\}
and consider a vertical system
F\in\mathbbm{k}[\kappa,x^{\pm}]^{s}
as in (
2.1
). Let
\mathcal{K}\subseteq{\mathbb{G}}^{m}
be a Euclidean open set such that
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})\neq\varnothing
for some
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
. For
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{G}}
for all
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
.

(ii)

F
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{G}}
.

(iii)

F
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant (over
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
).

Proof.

The implications (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) are clear. For the implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii), form the complex incidence variety

\mathcal{I}=\{(\kappa,x)\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{m}\times({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}:F_{\kappa}(x)=0\}\,,

which by
[
24
, Thm. 3.1]
is irreducible and smooth.
Taking Zariski closures in the complex torus, we have

\overline{{\mathbb{G}}^{d}}=({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{d}
and
\overline{\mathcal{I}\cap(\mathcal{K}\times{\mathbb{G}}^{n})}=\mathcal{I}
; this is obvious in the complex case, whereas in the real case, we use that
a Euclidean open subset of the real part of an irreducible smooth complex variety defined by polynomials with real coefficients is dense (see
[
54
, Thm. 6.5]
and
[
5
, Prop. 3.3.6]
).
With this in place, for
the map

\Phi{\colon}({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{d}\times\mathcal{I}\to({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{m}\times({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}\,,\quad(t,(\kappa,x))\mapsto(\kappa,x\circ t^{A})\,.

we have, using (i), that

\displaystyle\Phi(({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{d}\times\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(\overline{{\mathbb{G}}^{d}\times(\mathcal{I}\cap(\mathcal{K}\times{\mathbb{G}}^{n}))}\right)\subseteq\overline{\Phi\left(\overline{{\mathbb{G}}^{d}\times(\mathcal{I}\cap(\mathcal{K}\times{\mathbb{G}}^{n}))}\right)}

\displaystyle=\overline{\Phi({\mathbb{G}}^{d}\times(\mathcal{I}\cap(\mathcal{K}\times{\mathbb{G}}^{n})))}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{I}\cap(\mathcal{K}\times{\mathbb{G}}^{n})}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,,

which is equivalent to (iii) by (
3.1
).
∎

For common parameter sets
\mathcal{K}
, checking that
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})\neq\varnothing
for some
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
boils down to a simple computation, as the next lemma indicates.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
{\mathbb{G}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0},{\mathbb{R}}^{*},{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\}
and
F
be a vertical system defined by matrices
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
and
C\in\mathbbm{k}^{s\times m}
of rank
s
. Let
\mathcal{K}\subseteq{\mathbb{G}}^{m}
.
We have that (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) for the following statements:

(i)

\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})\neq\varnothing
for some
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
.

(ii)

\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{G}}^{m}\neq\varnothing
.

Furthermore, if
\mathcal{K}
is such that

(3.2)

\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{G}}^{m}\subseteq\{\kappa\circ x^{M}:\kappa\in\mathcal{K},x\in{\mathbb{G}}^{n}\}\,,

then it also holds that (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i).
In particular, (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) when
\mathcal{K}={\mathbb{G}}^{m}
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): If
x\in\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})
for
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
, then
\kappa\circ x^{M}\in\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{G}}^{m}
.
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Let
v\in\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{G}}^{m}
. By (
3.2
), there exists
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
and
x\in{\mathbb{G}}^{n}
, such that
\kappa\circ x^{M}=v
, which implies
x\in\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})
.
Finally if
\mathcal{K}={\mathbb{G}}^{m}
, for any
v\in\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{G}}^{m}
,
we have
v=v\circ\mathbbm{1}^{M}
, with
\mathbbm{1}
the vector of all ones.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

Over
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
, it is a well-known fact (see, e.g.,
[
22
]
,
[
47
, §3.2]
) that

\alpha\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}:\log(x)-\log(\alpha)\in\ker(A)\}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}:x^{B}=\alpha^{B}\}\,,

where
B\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times(n-\rk(A))}
is a matrix whose columns form a basis for
\ker_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(A)
. Cosets of this form are sometimes called
log-parametrized
sets in the context of reaction networks
[
38
]
and
log-linear
or
log-affine
sets in the context of algebraic statistics, see, e.g.,
[
65
, Ch. 6–7]
.

Remark 3.10
.

When
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
, any ideal
I\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}[x]
generated by binomials
ax^{u}-bx^{v}
for
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
and
u,v\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
gives a toric zero locus
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(I)
(see, e.g.,
[
16
, Prop. 5.2]
).
When
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n\times m}
, a computational sufficient condition for
a vertical system
F
to be
generically
toric over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
is therefore that a reduced Gröbner basis for the ideal
\langle F\rangle\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}(\kappa)[x]
is binomial. If the binomial Gröbner basis specializes for each
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
, the conclusion can be strengthened to
F
being toric

[
61
, Rk. 2.4]
. A particularly simple special case where we can immediately assert that
F
is toric
is when putting
C
in reduced row echelon form gives a matrix where all rows have supports of size 2.

We conclude the section with some additional examples to illustrate some subtleties of the concepts defined above.

Example 3.11
.

To see that a system can be generically toric but not toric,
consider the linear vertical system

F=\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\kappa_{1}x_{1}-\kappa_{2}x_{2}\\
\kappa_{1}x_{1}-\kappa_{3}x_{2}\end{array}\right]\quad\in{\mathbb{C}}[\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3},x_{1}^{\pm},x_{2}^{\pm}]^{2}.

As the zero set is generically one point,
F
is generically
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric with
A
the empty matrix. However, when
\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{3}=1
, the number of
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-cosets is infinite as
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa})
is one dimensional.
∎

Example 3.12
.

The linear dependencies among the coefficients of a vertically parametrized system play an important role in whether the system is toric. Consider the following two systems, with the same support but different dependencies:

\left[{\small\begin{array}[]{l}-3\kappa_{1}x_{1}^{6}+3\kappa_{2}x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}+3\kappa_{3}x_{2}^{4}-\kappa_{4}x_{1}+\kappa_{5}x_{3}^{5}\\
\kappa_{1}x_{1}^{6}-\kappa_{2}x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}-\kappa_{3}x_{2}^{4}+\kappa_{4}x_{1}-\kappa_{5}x_{3}^{5}\end{array}}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\left[{\small\begin{array}[]{l}-\kappa_{1}x_{1}^{6}+\kappa_{2}x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}+\kappa_{3}x_{2}^{4}-\kappa_{4}x_{1}+\kappa_{5}x_{3}^{5}\\
\kappa_{6}x_{1}^{6}-\kappa_{7}x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}-\kappa_{8}x_{2}^{4}+\kappa_{9}x_{1}-\kappa_{10}x_{3}^{5}\end{array}}\right]\,.

The first system is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
for
A=[\,10\ \ 15\ \ 2\,]
, whereas the second is not toric.
∎

Example 3.13
.

A key ingredient in the proof of

Theorem

3.7
is that the incidence variety of a vertical system is irreducible. Failure of this condition may give rise to systems displaying invariance over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
but not over
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
. A simple example is the system with one entry

F=(\kappa_{1}x_{1}-\kappa_{2}x_{2})(\kappa_{3}x_{1}^{2}+\kappa_{4}x_{2}^{4})\,,

which is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
for
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix}
, but
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa})
consists of three connected components, only one of which has

-invariance.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

We only focus on the zeros with nonzero or positive coordinates as the behavior on the coordinate hyperplanes might vary drastically. If such solutions are of interest, one may perform a systematic case-by-case analysis, where different combinations of variables are set to zero, and the resulting system is studied with the methods of this paper.

4.
Characterization of toric invariance

We now give a characterization of toric invariance of a vertically parametrized system
F
.
The key object is the
toric invariance lattice
, defined as

\mathcal{L}_{F}=\left\{a\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}:F\text{ is }\mathcal{T}_{a}\text{-invariant}\right\}\,,

which is an abelian subgroup of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. In the language of toric geometry,
\mathcal{L}_{F}
is the
cocharacter lattice
of the maximal subtorus of
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
that stabilizes all of the varieties
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa})
. In concrete terms, we obtain the unique maximal-dimensional torus
\mathcal{T}_{A}
for which the system
F
is invariant by taking the rows of
A
to be any
{\mathbb{Z}}
-basis for
\mathcal{L}_{F}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{G}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0},{\mathbb{R}}^{*},{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\}
,
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
, and
F
be a vertical system
such that
\mathcal{Z}_{{\mathbb{G}}}\neq\varnothing
.
Then
F
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{G}}
if and only if
\row_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{F}
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of
Theorem

3.7
,
Lemma

3.8
, and the discussion above.
∎

A stronger condition for a row vector
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
to belong to
\mathcal{L}_{F}
is that every entry of
F
is
quasihomogeneous
with weights given by
a
, in the sense that there exists
b\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{s}
such that

{F_{\kappa}(x\circ t^{a})=F_{\kappa}(x)\circ t^{b}\,\quad\text{holds in }{\mathbb{C}}[t^{\pm},x^{\pm}]\,\text{ for all $\kappa\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{m}$}.}

Lemma 4.2
.

A vertical system
F
with defining matrices
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
and
C\in{\mathbb{C}}^{s\times m}
of rank
s
is quasihomogeneous with weights given by a row vector
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
if and only if
a
is perpendicular to the affine hull of the Newton polytope of each of the
s
polynomials in
F
:

(4.1)

a\,M_{j}=a\,M_{j_{0}}\quad\text{for all }j,j_{0}\in\supp(C_{i*})\text{ and all }i\in[s]\,.

Proof.

Follows from
[
29
, Prop. 6.1.2(a)]
.
∎

Lemma

4.2
gives a sufficient condition for invariance, which depends on the supports of the rows of
C
. We might obtain additional sufficient conditions by performing row operations on
C
, as these preserve
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
but may change the support of the rows. The main message of this section is that these sufficient conditions, relying on quasihomogeneity, together completely characterize
\mathcal{T}_{a}
-invariance. In practice, it is enough to consider quasihomogeneity
when
C
is in reduced row echelon form.

The structure of
\ker(C)
is captured by the column matroid
\mathcal{M}_{C}
of
C
, which is the matroid on
[m]
whose circuits are the minimal nonempty index sets of linearly dependent columns of
C
.

Definition 4.3
.

The
matroid partition
defined by a matrix
C\in{\mathbb{C}}^{s\times m}
is the partition on
[m]
whose blocks are the connected components of the column matroid
\mathcal{M}_{C}
of
C
. In other words, the blocks are the equivalence classes of the equivalence relation
\sim
on
[m]
generated by

j\sim j_{0}\quad\text{if }j,j_{0}\in c\quad\text{ for some circuit $c$ of $\mathcal{M}_{C}$}\,.

By
[
67
, Thm. 5.2.1]
the blocks of the matroid partition are also the connected components of the dual matroid of
\mathcal{M}_{C}
, whose circuits are the minimal supports of nonzero vectors in
\row(C)
.
The following easy lemma tells us that we do not need to find all circuits to determine the matroid partition: a basis consisting of vectors with minimal support, for example given by the rows of
C
if it is in reduced row echelon form, suffices.
We include the proof for completeness.

Note that a nonzero vector
v\in V
in a vector space
V
having

minimal support
means that the zero vector is the only vector of
V
with support strictly contained in
\supp(v)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
C\in{\mathbb{C}}^{s\times m}
and
\mathcal{V}
be
a basis of
\ker(C)
or of
\row(C)
consisting of vectors of minimal support.
The blocks of the matroid partition are the equivalence classes of the equivalence relation
\sim
on
[m]
generated by

(4.2)

j\sim j_{0}\quad\text{if }j,j_{0}\in\supp(v)\quad\text{ for some $v\in\mathcal{V}$}\,.

Proof.

Let
V=\row(C)
or
V=\ker(C)
such that
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}\}
is a basis of
V
.
For a nonzero vector
w\in V
of minimal support, let
\rho
be a block
of the partition defined by (
4.2
) such that
\supp(w)\cap\rho\neq\varnothing
. It is enough to show that
\supp(w)\subseteq\rho
.
As
\mathcal{V}
is a basis of
V
, we can write

w=\sum_{\supp(v_{j})\subseteq\rho}\lambda_{j}v_{j}+\sum_{\supp(v_{j})\cap\rho=\varnothing}\lambda_{j}v_{j}\,.

The two summands have disjoint index sets and define vectors in
V
.
As
w
has minimal support and the first summand is nonzero, the second summand must vanish.
In particular,

\supp(w)\subseteq\rho
as desired.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The matroid partition has previously appeared in the reaction networks literature under the term
the finest independent decomposition

[
33
, Thm. 3.3]
, referring to Feinberg’s notion of
independent decompositions
of a network
[
21
]
.

Remark 4.6
.

If
we write the matroid partition as
[m]=\rho_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\rho_{\theta}
and for
v\in\ker(C)
, we let
v^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
, for all
i\in[\theta]
, be the unique vectors with
v=v^{(1)}+\dots+v^{(\theta)}
and
\supp(v^{(i)})\subseteq\rho_{i}
, then
v^{(i)}\in\ker(C)
as well for all
i
. In other words,

\ker(C)
is, after a suitable coordinate permutation, the direct product of the kernels of the matrices
C^{(i)}
obtained from
C
by considering the columns with indices in
\rho_{i}
.
In particular

{\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa})=\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa}^{(1)})\cap\dots\cap\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa}^{(\theta)})\,}

where the vertical system
F^{(i)}
is defined by considering
C^{(i)}
and the exponent matrix
M^{(i)}
defined analogously.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
F
be a vertical system with defining matrices
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
and
C\in{\mathbb{C}}^{s\times m}
of rank
s
with
\ker(C)\cap({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{m}\neq\varnothing
.
Let
[m]=\rho_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\rho_{\theta}
be its matroid partition and
\mathcal{L}_{F}
the toric invariance lattice.
Then
a\in\mathcal{L}_{F}
if and only if
aM_{j}=aM_{j_{0}}
for all
j,j_{0}\in\rho_{i}
and all
i=1,\dots,\theta
.
Equivalently, it holds that

\mathcal{L}_{F}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\theta}\spn_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\{M_{j}-M_{j_{0}}:j,j_{0}\in\rho_{i}\}^{\perp}\,.

Proof.

The reverse implication follows from the facts that replacing
C
by a matrix
C^{\prime}
in reduced row echelon form with the same row span as
C
in
F
does not change
\mathcal{L}_{F}
, and that

Lemmas

4.2
and
4.4
imply that
C^{\prime}(\kappa\circ x^{M})
is quasihomogeneous with weights given by
a
.

For the forward implication, by (
3.1
),
a\in\mathcal{L}_{F}
if and only if

(\kappa\circ x^{M})\circ t^{aM}\in\ker(C)
for all
t\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
if
\kappa\circ x^{M}\in\ker(C)
.
As (
3.2
) holds with equality for
\mathcal{K}=({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{m}
and
{\mathbb{G}}={\mathbb{C}}^{*}
,
and using that
\ker(C)\cap({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{m}
is Zariski dense in
\ker(C)
, it holds that

C(v\circ t^{aM})=0\quad\text{ for all }v\in\ker(C)\text{ and }\ t\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\,.

This can only happen if all coefficients of
C(v\circ t^{aM})
as a polynomial in
t
are zero.
Thus, for any nonzero
v\in\ker(C)
and
j_{0}\in\supp(v)
, the coefficient of
t^{aM_{j_{0}}}
must be zero:

\sum_{j\in\supp(v),\ aM_{j}=aM_{j_{0}}}C_{j}v_{j}=0\,.

We obtain a nonzero vector
w\in\ker(C)
by setting

w_{j}=v_{j}
if
aM_{j}=aM_{j_{0}}
and
0
otherwise. Taking
v
with minimal support,
we conclude that
w=v
and hence

aM_{j}=aM_{j_{0}}
for all
j\in\supp(v)
. The conclusion now follows from the definition of the matroid partition.

The last statement is immediate.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

If the support of each row of
C
is contained in one block of the matroid partition, then, by
Theorem

4.7
, we have quasihomogeneity for all weights in the toric invariance lattice. This is for example the case when the matroid partition is trivial or when the rows of the coefficient matrix
C
have pairwise disjoint support, as is the case for freely parametrized systems.

Remark 4.9
.

To find a matrix
A
of maximal rank for which a vertical system
F=C(\kappa\circ x^{M})
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant, we consider the Cayley matrix
\widehat{M}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{(n+\theta)\times m}
, obtained by appending to
M
, for each block
\rho
of the matroid partition, the row with
1
’s for the indices in
\rho
and zero otherwise. Let
\widehat{A}

Remark 4.10
.

Theorem

4.7
implies that invariance simply encodes that the system depends, in a sense, on fewer than
n
variables.
The key insight is that by
Theorem

4.7
, the affine span of the columns of the matrices
M^{(i)}
defined in
Remark

4.6
is contained in a parallel translate of
\row(A)^{\perp}
.
By assuming that
C
is in reduced row echelon form, choosing an index
j_{i}\in\rho_{i}
for each
i
, and multiplying by
x^{-M_{j_{i}}}
the entries of
F
that correspond to a row of
C
with support in
\rho_{i}
,
we obtain an equivalent system whose exponents affinely span
\row(A)^{\perp}
.

By a monomial change of coordinates, this system can be transformed to a system depending on
n-\rk(A)
variables and with
\rk(A)
free variables.
Hence, in the new variables, the zero set is a union of translates of a linear subspace of dimension
\rk(A)
, and there is one set per zero of the system in
n-\rk(A)
variables.
Reversing the change of coordinates, the linear subspace becomes
\mathcal{T}_{A}
.
Observe that invariance does not imply that the affine span of the columns of
M
is not full dimensional, unless the matroid partition is trivial.

5.
Locally toric systems

Having invariance for vertical systems determined, in this section, we give a complete characterization of systems that are generically locally toric, as well as sufficient conditions for
a system to be locally toric.
A useful observation is that if
F
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant, then

(5.1)

\dim(\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa}))\geq\dim(\mathcal{T}_{A})=\rk(A)\,,

for all
\kappa\in\mathcal{Z}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}
. If in addition
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa})
is locally
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric, then
\dim(\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa}))=\rk(A)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
F
be a vertical system with defining matrices
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
and
C\in{\mathbb{C}}^{s\times m}
of rank
s
.
Assume that
F
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant for a matrix
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
of rank
d
.
If
F
is nondegenerate over
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
,
then
s+d\leq n
.

Proof.

This follows from (
5.1
),
as
\dim(\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa}))=n-s
for some
\kappa
by
Proposition

2.1
.
∎

The inequality in

might not hold when

is degenerate, as

shows. The following lemma will be at the core of several results below.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
{\mathbb{G}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0},{\mathbb{R}}^{*},{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\}
, and
F
be a vertical system defined by
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
and

of rank
s
, which is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant for a matrix

of rank
d

Assume that for a fixed

and all irreducible components of
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa})

contain a nondegenerate zero of

{\mathbb{G}}^{n}

is locally
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric over

if and only if

.

Proof.

As
F
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant, it is also
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{G}}
by

3.7
.
Let
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}Y_{i}
be the union of the irreducible components of

\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa})
that intersect
{\mathbb{G}}^{n}
. The condition on nondegeneracy guarantees that
\dim(Y_{i})=n-s
for all

and that

\overline{\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}Y_{i}
, where the overline denotes the Zariski closure in
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}

For a coset
\alpha\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}^{\mathbb{G}}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})
,
we have
\alpha\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}
must be contained in

for some

by irreducibility.
As
\dim(\alpha\circ\mathcal{T}_{A})=d
, if

, then
\alpha\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}=Y_{i}
and the system is locally toric. Conversely, if

\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})
is locally
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric over
{\mathbb{G}}
, then
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{G}}}(F_{\kappa})=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}^{\mathbb{G}}

. Hence,

n-s=\dim(\bigcup\nolimits_{i=1}^{\ell}Y_{i})=\dim\left(\overline{\bigsqcup\nolimits_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}^{{\mathbb{G}}}}\right)=\dim\left(\bigsqcup\nolimits_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}\right)=d\,.\qed

, then the coset counting system has generically a finite number of zeros and hence the generic number of cosets is bounded above by the mixed volume by Bernstein’s theorem
[
6
]
(see also
[
18
, §7.5]
, as well as
[
27
]
where Bernstein’s theorem is applied to a similar system). Sharper bounds that take into account the dependencies among the parametric coefficients arise from Newton–Okunkov bodies
[
53
]
and tropical methods
[
41
,
34
]
. This type of bounds can be quite far from the number of positive solutions, as they count solutions over
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
, but if the bound is
1
, then we conclude that
F
is generically
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric.

In the next two subsections, we will discuss two other approaches to assert the system is toric, that is, to confirm that the coset counting system has at most one solution for all
\kappa
.
We end this subsection with a short discussion on nondegeneracy of the coset counting system.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
F
be a vertical system that is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant for a matrix
A
of full row rank, and let
H
be the coset counting system (
6.1
). Let
\kappa\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{m}
and
x^{*}\in\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa})
be such that
A\diag(x^{*})A^{\top}
is invertible (this is true for any
x^{*}\in\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
). Then

\rk(J_{H_{\kappa,Ax^{*}}}(x^{*}))=\rk(J_{F_{\kappa}}(x^{*}))+\rk(A)\,.

In particular,

x^{*}
is a nondegenerate zero of
F_{\kappa}
if and only if it is a nondegenerate zero of
H_{\kappa,Ax^{*}}
.

Proof.

The assumed invertibility gives that we have a direct sum decomposition

(6.2)

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}=\ker(A)\oplus\im(\diag(x^{*})A^{\top})\,.

Furthermore, by invariance, it holds that
F(x^{*}\circ t^{A})=0
for all
t\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{d}
, which gives

0=\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}F(x^{*}\circ t^{A})|_{t=(1,\dots,1)}=J_{F_{\kappa}}(x^{*})\diag(x^{*})A^{\top}.

The lemma now follows by intersecting both sides of (
6.2
) with
\ker(J_{F_{\kappa}}(x^{*}))
, which yields

\ker(J_{F_{\kappa}}(x^{*}))=\ker(J_{H_{\kappa,Ax^{*}}}(x^{*}))\oplus\im(\diag(x^{*})A^{\top}),

as
\ker(J_{H_{\kappa,Ax^{*}}}(x^{*}))=\ker(J_{F_{\kappa}}(x^{*}))\cap\ker(A)
.
∎

Lemma

6.3
has the following interesting consequence for generic nondegeneracy.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
F
be a vertical system that is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant for a matrix
A
of full row rank, and let
H
be the coset counting system (
6.1
). Then
F
is nondegenerate over
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
if and only if
H
is nondegenerate over
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.

Proof.

The reverse implication is immediate, so we focus on the forward implication. Nondegeneracy of
F
implies that there is a nonempty Zariski open subset
\mathcal{U}
of the incidence variety
\mathcal{I}=\{(\kappa,x)\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{m}\times({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}:F_{\kappa}(x)=0\}
consisting of pairs
(\kappa,x)
such that
x
is a nondegenerate zero of
F_{\kappa}
.
Since the projection
p{\colon}\mathcal{I}\to({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
is surjective, it follows that
p(\mathcal{U})
is a nonempty Zariski open subset of
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
. By denseness,
p(\mathcal{U})
contains some
x^{*}\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
such that
A\diag(x^{*})A^{\top}
is invertible. The desired conclusion now follows from
Lemma

6.3
.
∎

6.2.
Injectivity

Chemical reaction network theory has provided several methods to decide whether an augmented vertically parametrized system
(C(\kappa\circ x^{M}),Lx-b)
has two or more positive zeros for a choice of parameter values.
One of the simplest methods decides whether the map
x\mapsto(C(\kappa\circ x^{M}),Lx)
is injective on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}
for all positive
\kappa
(see
[
68
,
47
]
), and gives rise to the following sufficient criterion for a vertical system to be toric over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
. Here, we formulate it in terms of a symbolic determinant, but we note that it can equivalently be formulated in terms of sign vectors, as discussed in
[
47
]
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
F
be a vertical system with defining matrices
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
and
C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s\times m}
of rank
s
with
\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}\neq\varnothing
.
Assume that
F
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant for a full rank matrix
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{(n-s)\times n}
.
For variables
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m})
and
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
, form the symbolic matrix

{Q}_{\mu,\lambda}:=\begin{bmatrix}C\diag(\mu)M^{\top}\diag(\lambda)\\

If
\det(Q_{\mu,\lambda})
is a nonzero polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[\mu,\lambda]
, with all nonzero coefficients having the same sign,

is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
.

Proof.

It follows from
Proposition

2.1
combined with

that

is nondegenerate over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
.
By
[

, Thm. 2.13]
, the assumption on
\det(Q_{\mu,\lambda})
is equivalent to
the polynomial map

being injective on

for all
x^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}
and
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
. This implies that for the coset counting system

in (
6.1
),
\#\mathbb{V}_{>0}(H_{\kappa,b})\leq 1
for all

and

, and the statement follows from
Proposition

6.1
.
∎

6.3.
Constant number of cosets.

In practice, it often holds that

\#(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})/\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0})
is constant with respect to
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
. When this is the case, for all
\kappa
,
\#(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})/\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0})
can be inferred from
the number of zeros of the coset counting system
H
from (
6.1
) for any fixed
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
.

The following is a sufficient criterion for
\#(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})/\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0})
to be constant for all
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
.
In intuitive terms, it rules out the three ways in which the number of zeros of
H
can change as the parameters vary:
(i) a zero crossing a coordinate hyperplane, (ii) a degenerate zero arising, or (iii) a zero going to infinity.

Proposition 6.7
.

Let
F
be a vertical system with defining matrices
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n\times m}
and
C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s\times m}
of rank
s
with
\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}\neq\varnothing
. Assume that
F
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant for a matrix
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{(n-s)\times n}
of rank
n-s
. Suppose the following hold:

(i)

\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{R}}}{(C(\kappa\circ x^{M}),Ax-b)}\cap({\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0})=\varnothing
for all
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
and some
b\in A({\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0})
.

(ii)

\rk(C\diag(w)M^{\top})=s
for all
w\in\ker(C)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
.

(iii)

\row(A)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}\neq\varnothing
.

Then
\#(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})/\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0})
is constant with respect to
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
, and in particular
\mathcal{Z}_{>0}={\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
.

Proof.

Let
H
be the coset counting system from (
6.1
) and consider the incidence correspondence

\mathcal{I}=\{(\kappa,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}:H_{\kappa,b}(x)=0\}

as well as the projection
\pi{\colon}\mathcal{I}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
to parameter space. Condition (i) gives that
\mathcal{I}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{m}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}
.
Condition (ii) implies that
\rk(J_{F_{\kappa}}(x^{*}))=s
for all
(\kappa,x^{*})\in\mathcal{I}

and hence, by
Lemma

6.3
, all zeros of
H_{\kappa,b}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}
are nondegenerate. It follows now from
[
24
, Prop. 3.3]
that
\pi
is an open map and lacks critical points.

We now prove that
\pi
is surjective, i.e., that
\mathcal{Z}_{>0}={\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{m}
. Since
\pi(\mathcal{I})
is nonempty and open, and
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
is connected, it suffices to prove that
\pi(\mathcal{I})
is closed. Condition (iii) gives that
\mathcal{P}:=\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(Ax-b)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
is compact
[
9
]
, which implies that the canonical projection
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}\times\mathcal{P}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
is a closed map. Since
\mathcal{I}
is closed in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}\times\mathcal{P}
, this shows that
\pi(\mathcal{I})
is closed.

Our next goal is to show that
\#\pi^{-1}(\kappa)
is constant for all
\kappa
. As
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
is connected, it suffices to show that the cardinality is locally constant. Given
\kappa^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
, write

\pi^{-1}(\kappa^{*})=\{(\kappa^{*},x_{1}),\ldots,(\kappa^{*},x_{\ell})\}
for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\in\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa^{*}})
. The absence of critical points for
\pi
allows us to find, for each
i\in[\ell]
, an open neighborhood
U_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
of
(\kappa^{*},x_{i})
and an open neighborhood
V_{i}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
of
\kappa^{*}
such that
\pi_{|U_{i}}{\colon}U_{i}\to V_{i}
is a homeomorphism.
These open sets can be chosen such that
U_{i}\cap U_{j}=\varnothing
for
i\neq j
. Furthermore,
by letting
V=\cap_{i=1}^{\ell}V_{i}
and replacing
U_{i}
by
(\pi_{|U_{i}})^{-1}(V)
, it holds that
\pi(U_{i})=V
for all
i\in[\ell]
.
Now, form the set
Q=\mathcal{I}\setminus(U_{1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{\ell})
, which is closed in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}\times\mathcal{P}
, which in turn gives that
\pi(Q)
is closed in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
.
It now holds that
W:=V\setminus\pi(Q)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
is an open neighborhood of
\kappa^{*}
such that
\#\pi^{-1}(\kappa)=\ell
for all
\kappa\in W
.
∎

Condition

7.1.
Reaction networks

A
reaction network
on an ordered set
\mathcal{S}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
of species is a collection of
m
reactions between formal nonnegative linear combinations of the species (called complexes):

(7.1)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{ij}X_{i}\longrightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{ij}X_{i}\,,\qquad j\in[m]\,,

where
\alpha_{ij},\beta_{ij}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
.
The net production of the species in each of the reactions is encoded by the
stoichiometric matrix

N=(\beta_{ij}-\alpha_{ij})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
.

The concentration of the respective species is denoted by a vector
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.
Under common assumptions,
these concentrations vary according to an autonomous ordinary differential equation system of the form

(7.2)

\tfrac{dx}{dt}=N(\kappa\circ x^{M})\,,\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}\,,

where
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times m}
is called the kinetic matrix and
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
is a vector of rate constants, which typically are viewed as unknown parameters.

The main example of this construction arises under the mass-action assumption, where
M=(\alpha_{ij})
is the reactant matrix consisting of the coefficients of the left-hand sides of the reactions. In this case,
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
is forward-invariant by the ODE system (
7.2
).

Letting
s=\rk(N)
, we choose a matrix
C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s\times m}
of rank
s
such that
\ker(C)=\ker(N)
. Then, the steady states of (
7.2
) are the zeros of the vertically parametrized system

F=C(\kappa\circ x^{M})\in{\mathbb{R}}[\kappa,x^{\pm}]^{s}\,.

We call any such system
the steady state system
(with a choice of
C
implicitly made).
One is particularly interested in steady states with
strictly positive
entries.

The trajectories of (
7.2
) are confined in parallel translates of
\im(N)
, which can be written as

(7.3)

\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}:Lx-b=0\},\qquad b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-s}

for a matrix

L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n-s)\times n}
whose rows form a basis for the left-kernel of
N
.
We call such a matrix
L
a
conservation law matrix
.
The sets in (
7.3
) are called
stoichiometric compatibility classes
. The steady states in a given stoichiometric compatibility class are therefore the positive zeros of the augmented vertical system

(7.4)

(C(\kappa\circ x^{M}),Lx-b)\in{\mathbb{R}}[\kappa,b,x^{\pm}]^{n}\,.

7.2.
Consequences of toric invariance

As alluded to in the introduction, the following three algebraic properties play a central role in the
(algebraic)
study of reaction networks:

•

The network is said to have the capacity for
multistationarity
if system (
7.4
) admits at least two positive zeros for some choice of
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
and
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-s}
.

•

A network is said to have
absolute concentration robustness
(or
ACR
for short) with respect to
X_{i}
if

\pi_{i}(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa}))
consists of at most a single point for all
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
, where
\pi_{i}{\colon}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
denotes the canonical projection onto the
i
th factor.

•

A polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{R}}(\kappa)[x^{\pm}]

is said to be a
steady state invariant
if for all
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
,
f
specializes to

Multistationarity.
Multistationarity might imply that trajectories converge to different steady states for the same parameter values and has been associated with robust cellular decision making.
Deciding upon multistationarity corresponds to the general problem of determining whether an augmented vertical system has multiple positive zeros for some choice of parameter values. This is, in general, a challenging quantifier elimination problem. However, under the assumption of toric invariance, it simplifies to a sign condition.

Absolute concentration robustness.
ACR means that the concentration
x_{i}
at steady state is independent from initial conditions of the system. Because of this, ACR is believed to be a mechanism that contributes to the remarkable robustness many biological systems display to changes in their environment
[
59
]
.

The problem of ACR has been studied with various algebraic techniques
[
55
,
42
,
54
,
31
,
25
]
.
In general, this requires determining whether the elimination ideal
I(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa}))\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x_{i}^{\pm}]
has a linear polynomial
x_{i}-c_{\kappa}
for some

for all
\kappa
,
which is a highly challenging computational problem
[
31
]
.

A weaker notion is that of

, where one requires
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
being contained in finitely many translates of the

th coordinate hyperplane
[
54
]
, corresponding to
I(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa}))\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x_{i}^{\pm}]

being nonzero for all
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
. A simpler problem is to decide whether local ACR arises generically in
\kappa
, which can be addressed using the ideal generated by
F
, see
[
25
]
.

Under the hypothesis of
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariance, constant coordinates in

Steady state invariants.

Steady state invariants may be used to discriminate between different reaction networks that may be underlying a mechanism. The ideas behind this approach were introduced in
[
32
,
48
]
and further developed for example in
[
35
,
42
]
.

For a subset of indices
J=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{r}\}\subseteq[n]
, which correspond to experimentally measurable concentrations, assume we have
a sample of the observable variables at steady state, obtained from a fixed but unknown
\kappa
. Each sample point corresponds then to a point of
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
.

Now assume
there is a steady state invariant
f
that only depends on

x_{J}=(x_{j_{1}},\dots,x_{j_{r}})
and has support consisting of the monomials
x_{J}^{u_{1}},\dots,x_{J}^{u_{p}}
for
u_{1},\ldots,u_{p}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
. We can then test the hypothesis that the sample points come from
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
by statistically checking whether the image of the sample under the map

\hat{x}\mapsto(\hat{x}^{u_{1}},\dots,\hat{x}^{u_{p}})
lies on a hyperplane in
{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
through the origin; see
Figure

7.1
(c).
This is a parameter-free method, that does not require knowing
\kappa
nor the coefficients of the invariant
f
, but instead only its support.

The goal is thus to find a tuple
of monomials
(x_{J}^{u_{1}},\dots,x_{J}^{u_{p}})
that constitutes the support of a polynomial in
I(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa}))\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x_{J}^{\pm}]
for all
\kappa\in\mathcal{Z}_{>0}
.
A common method is to study the elimination ideal

\langle F\rangle\cap{\mathbb{R}}(\kappa)[x_{J}^{\pm}]
with Gröbner bases methods. However, this will give polynomials vanishing on the entire complex varieties
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}(F_{\kappa})
, and hence might miss useful invariants that only vanish on the positive parts (cf.
below).

Under the assumption of
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariance, the analysis simplifies substantially to studying the kernel of the submatrix
A_{J}
consisting of the columns of
A
with index in
J
.
The key observation is that

I(\alpha\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0})=\langle x^{u}-\alpha^{u}:u\in\ker_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)\rangle
,
from which we obtain

(7.5)

I(\alpha\circ\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0})\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x_{J}^{\pm}]=I(\alpha_{J}\circ\mathcal{T}_{A_{J}}^{>0})=\big\langle x_{J}^{u}-\alpha_{J}^{u}:u\in\ker_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A_{J})\big\rangle\,.

Hence, if
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
is a disjoint union of

\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0}
-cosets, then
I(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa}))\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x_{J}^{\pm}]
is the intersection of one ideal of the form (
7.5
) per coset.
This gives rise to the following result (which, incidentally, recovers
Proposition

7.3
about ACR when
J
is a singleton).

Proposition 7.4

(Polynomial relations)
.

Let
F
be a vertical system that is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant for a matrix
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
of rank
d
and let
J\subseteq[n]
. Then the following holds:

(i)

If

for some
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}

.

(ii)

Suppose

. Then
I(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa}))\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x_{J}^{\pm}]\neq\{0\}
for any
\kappa
such that
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
is locally
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
.

Under the assumption that
F
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric and that
\mathcal{Z}_{>0}\neq\varnothing

•

•

For all nonzero vectors

For steady state systems that are
\mathcal{T}_{A}

is constant at the sample points for appropriate nonzero vectors

For the best numerical and statistical performance for noisy data, one should use vectors in
\ker_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A_{J})
of small length, found systematically via, e.g., the Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász algorithm
[
44
]
.

7.3.
Network reduction and toric invariance

For a reaction network with mass-action kinetics,
the search for toric invariance of the steady state system can be simplified by removing
single-input intermediates
, a concept that we review now.
For a more detailed presentation we refer to
[
26
,
60
,
61
]
.

Given a network with set of species
\mathcal{S}
, a
choice of intermediates
is a partition
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{X}\sqcup\mathcal{Y}
of the set of species into a set of
non-intermediates

\mathcal{X}
and a set of
intermediates

\mathcal{Y}
, with the following properties:

(i)

Each species
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
only appears in complexes where the coefficients sum to 1.

(ii)

For each
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
there exists a sequence of reactions

(7.6)

c\to Y_{1}\to\cdots\to Y_{i}\to Y\to Y_{i+1}\to\cdots\to Y_{r}\to c^{\prime}

with
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{r}\in\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{Y\}
(there might be repetitions) and
c,c^{\prime}
are complexes in the non-intermediates
\mathcal{X}
.

The non-intermediate complex
c
in (
7.6
) is called an
input complex
of
Y
. A
single-input intermediate
has by definition a unique input complex.
Note that there might be several possible choices of intermediates for a given network. One of the key ideas in the theory of intermediates is that some properties of the network are preserved in a simpler
reduced network
defined as follows:
one removes the intermediates, and all reactions involving intermediates, and adds a reaction
c\to c^{\prime}
for every sequence of reactions as (
7.6
)

[
26
]
.

By letting
x,y
denote the vectors of concentrations of the species in
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
respectively, the key idea is that condition (i) ensures that, with mass-action kinetics, the entries of
y
appear linearly in the ODE system (
7.2
). Then condition (ii) ensures that the steady state system has a unique zero in
y
, which in addition is a polynomial in
x
with coefficients being rational functions in
\kappa
with all coefficients positive.
Plugging the expressions of
y
at steady state into the remaining ODE equations (for
x
), one obtains the ODE system for the reduced network for a choice of rate constants given as a vector of rational functions
\varphi(\kappa)
in the original rate constants.
When
Y_{i}
is a single-input intermediate, the expression takes the form
y_{i}=\psi_{i}(\kappa)x^{c}
, where
c
is the vector of coefficients of the unique input of
Y_{i}
.

As a convention, we order the species such that the vector of concentrations is
(x,y)
, that is,
so that the non-intermediates come before the intermediates. We use a tilde to denote quantities and objects that correspond to the reduced network.

Proposition 7.8
.

For a reaction network consider a choice of intermediates
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{X}\sqcup\mathcal{Y}
with
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{\ell}\}
consisting of single-input intermediates. Let
F
be the steady state system.
Let
B\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n\times\ell}
be the matrix where the
i
th column is the coefficient vector in
\mathcal{X}
of the unique input complex of the
i
th intermediate. Then the following holds:

(i)

There are rational maps
\psi{\colon}{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}_{>0}
and
\varphi{\colon}{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\tilde{m}}_{>0}
such that we have a bijection

\Phi_{\kappa}{\colon}\mathbb{V}_{>0}(\tilde{F}_{\varphi(\kappa)})\to\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa}),\hskip 9.24994ptx\mapsto(x,\psi(\kappa)\circ x^{B}).

(ii)

If
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
for
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times(n+\ell)}
, then
A=[\tilde{A}\,|\,\tilde{A}B]
with
\tilde{A}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
.

(iii)

With the notation in (ii),
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(\tilde{F}_{\varphi(\kappa)})

is
\mathcal{T}_{\tilde{A}}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
if and only if

\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
is
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-invariant over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
. Furthermore,
\Phi_{\kappa}
descends to a bijection

\mathbb{V}_{>0}(\tilde{F}_{\varphi(\kappa)})/\mathcal{T}_{\tilde{A}}^{>0}\to\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})/\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0}.

(iv)

If
\tilde{F}
is (generically/locally)
\mathcal{T}_{\tilde{A}}
-toric over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
, then
F
is (generically/locally)
\mathcal{T}_{A}
-toric over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
. The reverse implication holds also if
\varphi
is surjective.

Proof.

Statement (i) is shown in
[
26
]
, see also
[
60
,
61
]
.
For statement (ii),
let
y\in\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
and write it as
y=\Phi_{\kappa}(x)
for the unique
x\in\mathbb{V}_{>0}(\tilde{F}_{\varphi(\kappa)})
.
By writing
A=[\tilde{A}\,|\,\tilde{A}^{\prime}]
with
\tilde{A}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
, we have

\Phi_{\kappa}(x)\circ t^{A}=(x,\psi(\kappa)\circ x^{B})\circ(t^{\tilde{A}},t^{\tilde{A}^{\prime}})=(x\circ t^{\tilde{A}},\psi(\kappa)\circ x^{B}\circ t^{\tilde{A}^{\prime}}).

By hypothesis,
\Phi_{\kappa}(x)\circ t^{A}\in\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
,
hence it belongs to the image of
\Phi_{\kappa}
. Therefore

\psi(\kappa)\circ x^{B}\circ t^{\tilde{A}^{\prime}}=\psi(\kappa)\circ(x\circ t^{\tilde{A}})^{B}=\psi(\kappa)\circ x^{B}\circ t^{\tilde{A}B},

and as this holds for all

, we must have that
\tilde{A}^{\prime}=\tilde{A}B
, giving (ii).

Statement (iii) is now a consequence of the equality
\Phi_{\kappa}(x\circ t^{\tilde{A}})=\Phi_{\kappa}(x)\circ t^{A}\,.

Finally, (iv) follows from (iii) as
\#(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(\tilde{F}_{\varphi(\kappa)})/\mathcal{T}_{\tilde{A}}^{>0})=\#(\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})/\mathcal{T}_{A}^{>0})
.

7.4.
Case study: Networks from ODEbase

To illustrate the applicability of our results for realistic networks, we have applied our algorithms to the networks from the repository of biological and biomedical models BioModels
[
51
]
, using the stoichiometric matrices and reactant matrices collected in the database ODEbase
[
45
]
.

In our analysis, we work under the assumption of mass-action kinetics for all models (regardless of the exact kinetic model registered in BioModels).
We have considered all 69 nonlinear networks in ODEbase that satisfy

m\leq 100\,,\hskip 18.49988ptn-\rk(N)>0\,,\hskip 18.49988pt\text{and}\hskip 18.49988pt\ker(N)\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}\neq\varnothing\,.

For each such network we have applied
Algorithm

2
to the steady state system. If the network had single-input intermediates, we computed a matrix
\tilde{A}
with invariance for the reduced network, and attempted to prove that the reduced network was toric, before proceeding with Step
2
.

The Github repository of this paper contains the output of the computations for each of the analyzed networks. We here report some summarized data:

•

For 38 networks, we rule out that the system is (locally) toric.

•

For 31 networks, we verify that the system is locally toric, and for 30 of them, we verify that it is toric.

•

For the remaining network that is locally toric (
835
), none of our conditions for being toric are satisfied, and the mixed volume bound is 46 (but the steady state ideal is binomial for all positive rate constants and hence it is toric).

•

Out of the toric networks, seven are not covered by the Deficiency One Theorem, and two are verified to be non-binomial.

•

We verify capacity for multistationarity for 2 networks, and preclude multistationarity for 27 of them. We verify local ACR for 15 networks, and ACR for 14 of them.

•

In total, 56 of the investigated networks are quasihomogeneous with respect to all weights in the toric invariance lattice. Out of these, 14 have a trivial matroid partition.

7.5.
Other flavors of toric structure

In this final section, we view our results in the context of some previous approaches to determine toric structures in reaction networks. In this subsection, the reaction networks are taken with mass-action kinetics, and hence
M
is the reactant matrix.

Quasithermostatic networks and deficiency theory.

A special case where the toric structure plays an important role in classical monostationarity results is when the vectors in the toric invariance lattice span the left kernel of the stoichiometric matrix.
A network with some choice of rate constants is said to be
quasithermostatic
if the set of positive steady states is of the form
x^{*}\circ\mathcal{T}_{L}^{>0}
for some
x^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}
and a conservation law matrix
L
as in (
7.3
)
[
36
, §4]
. In this case, monostationarity follows directly from
Proposition

6.1
.

An important sufficient condition for quasithermostaticity is that the network is
complex-balanced
(or a
toric dynamical system
in the language of
[
15
]
). A characterization for when this happens for all choices of positive rate constants is given by the Deficiency Zero Theorem
[
39
]
. Another sufficient condition for quasithermostaticity for all rate constants is given by the Deficiency One Theorem
[
22
]
.

To connect our results to this body of work, we review and reprove some basic facts about quasithermostatic networks from the point of view of partitions and toric invariance. Similar statements appear in
[
39
,
36
,
40
,
22
]
and more recently in
[
12
, §3.1]
.

In what follows, we let
r
be the number of complexes of a given reaction network and
m
the number of reactions. The
linkage classes
of a network are the connected components of the network digraph which has the complexes as vertices and the reactions as edges. This results in a
linkage class partition
of
[m]=\gamma_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\gamma_{\ell}
, where two indices are in the same subset if the corresponding reactions belong to the same linkage class.
A network is
weakly reversible
if all linkage classes are strongly connected, and the
deficiency
of the network is

\delta:=r-s-\ell\geq 0
.

Proposition 7.12
.

Consider a network with
\ell
linkage classes, its steady state system
F
, and
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n-s)\times n}
a matrix defining the stoichiometric compatibility classes.

(i)

If the matroid partition of the steady state system is finer than the linkage class partition, then
F
is
\mathcal{T}_{L}
-invariant.

(ii)

Statement (i) holds if the network is connected.

(iii)

Statement (i) holds if there is a direct sum decomposition
\im(N)=\im(N_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\im(N_{\ell})
, where
N_{i}
is the stoichiometric matrix of the
i
th linkage class.

(iv)

If the network is weakly reversible with deficiency zero or satisfies the conditions of the deficiency one theorem from
[
22
]
, then
F
is locally
\mathcal{T}_{L}
-toric.

(v)

If
F
is
\mathcal{T}_{L}
-invariant, then for fixed
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
, the cardinality of
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa},Lx-b)
does not depend on
b
.
In particular
F
is
\mathcal{T}_{L}
-toric over
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
if and only if
F
is locally
\mathcal{T}_{L}
-toric and the network does not have the capacity for multistationarity.

Proof.

Let
Y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times r}
be the matrix whose columns are the coefficients of all complexes that appear in the network in some chosen order. The columns of
M
are among the columns of
Y
.
Let
C_{G}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r\times m}
be the incidence matrix of the network seen as a directed graph: the entry
(i,j)
is
1
,
-1
or
0
, if the
i
th complex is on the right, left, or does not occur in the
j
th reaction, respectively.
Let
[m]=\gamma_{1}\sqcup\dots\gamma_{\ell}
be the linkage class partition.
For each
k\in[\ell]
, construct the vector
u_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}

with

1
for the indices in
\gamma_{k}
and zero otherwise.
These vectors generate
\ker(C_{G}^{\top})
.

It is easy to see that
N=YC_{G}
, hence
0=L\,YC_{G}
by hypothesis, and the rows of
LY
belong to the left-kernel of
C_{G}
. In particular columns of
L\,Y
corresponding to complexes
in the same linkage class are all equal. It follows that a row
a
of
L

satisfies
aY_{i}=aY_{j}
if
i,j\in\gamma_{k}
for some
k
. Statement (i) now follows from
Theorem

4.7
, using that the matroid partition is finer than the linkage class partition.

For (ii), if the network is connected, then
\ell=1
, hence (i) holds.
For (iii), the condition is equivalent to
\ker(N)=\ker(N_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\ker(N_{\ell})
. Hence, a circuit of the column matroid of
N

must be contained in a block of the linkage class partition and (i) holds.
For (iv), if the deficiency is zero, then
\ker(N)=\ker(C_{G})
, see, e.g.,
[
22
, Lem. 6.1.4]
. After a suitable reordering of the complexes and reactions,
C_{G}
is a block diagonal matrix, which gives that (iii) applies.
Condition (iii) holds under the setting of the deficiency one theorem by hypothesis.
We note also that the property in
Proposition

5.5
holds for networks of deficiency zero and in the setting of the deficiency one theorem
[
23
, §15.2 and §17.1]
, from where we conclude that
F
is locally
\mathcal{T}_{L}
-toric.
For (v), we simply note that
(F_{\kappa},Lx-b)
is the coset counting system.
∎

A natural generalization of complex-balancing is that the network gives rise to the same ODEs as a complex balanced network (which is called being
disguised toric
in
[
10
]
). The set of rate constants for which the network is disguised toric is called the
disguised toric locus
, and has rich dynamical and geometric properties (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
for a recent overview). The methods developed in this paper give an easy-to-check
necessary
condition for the disguised toric locus to have nonempty Euclidean interior, namely that
Algorithm

1
returns a conservation law matrix
L
and
n=s+d
.

Proposition 7.13
.

If a network is disguised toric for rate constants in a nonempty Euclidean open set
U\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
, then it is generically locally
\mathcal{T}_{L}
-toric, where
L
is a conservation law matrix.

Proof.

For each
\kappa\in U
, the network being disguised toric means that
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(N(\kappa\circ x^{M}))
is
\mathcal{T}_{A_{\kappa}}
-toric for
A_{\kappa}
a full rank matrix such that
A_{\kappa}N(\kappa\circ x^{M})=0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}
.
Furthermore, since complex balanced networks have positive steady states,
U\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{>0}
, and it follows from
Proposition

2.1
that the steady state system is nondegenerate. Hence, there is a nonempty open subset
V\subseteq U
of rate constants for which the network is both nondegenerate and disguised toric.
Nondegeneracy implies now that
A_{\kappa}=L
for all
\kappa\in V
(see
[
25
, §3.4]
) and we have quasithermostaticity. By
Theorem

3.7
, it follows that we have
\mathcal{T}_{L}
-invariance for all
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
, and
Theorem

5.3
then gives that the system is generically locally
\mathcal{T}_{L}
-toric.
∎

Networks with binomial steady state ideals.

By
Remark

3.10
, a sufficient condition for a network being generically toric is that the polynomial ideal
\langle F_{\kappa}\rangle
is binomial over the field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\kappa)
of rational functions in the rate constants.
In
[
46
]
, networks with binomial steady state ideals are said to have
toric steady states
, and the authors give a sufficient condition for this to hold for all rate constants.
In what follows, we will revisit this condition from the point of view of the techniques developed in the present paper.
In particular, we will see that both works involve partitions, and that the one used in
[
46
]
can be seen as a coarsening of the matroid partition, which is consistent with the fact that binomiality of
\langle F_{\kappa}\rangle
is a stronger property than
\mathbb{V}_{>0}(F_{\kappa})
admitting a monomial parametrization (cf.
Remark

3.10
).

We begin by writing the steady state system in the form

F_{\kappa}=\Sigma_{\kappa}x^{Y},

where
\Sigma_{\kappa}\in{\mathbb{Q}}(\kappa)^{n\times p}
is the coefficient matrix and
Y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times p}
has exactly one column per reactant complex of the network (the matrix
\Sigma_{\kappa}
in
[
46
]
might have some additional zero columns, but these are irrelevant for the results under discussion). Note that
m\geq p
.

Condition 3.1 in
[
46
]
asks for the existence of a basis
b^{1},\dots,b^{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}_{\geq 0}
for
\ker(\Sigma_{\kappa})
such that their supports
I_{1},\dots,I_{d}
form a partition of
[p]
.
When this is the case, the system is toric with respect to the maximal-rank matrix
A
such that
AY_{i}=AY_{j}
whenever
i,j
belong to the same subset
I_{k}
(see
[
46
, Thm. 3.11]
). This construction resembles
Theorem

4.7
.
To understand the connection, we need first to assume that the partition is independent of
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
.
Then, the vectors
b^{1},\dots,b^{d}
are rational functions in
\kappa
, and by multiplying by the denominators if necessary, we can assume they are polynomial and hence continuous functions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.

Let
\iota\colon[m]\rightarrow[p]
where
\iota(i)
is the index of the column of
Y
that has
M_{i}
as column.
The hypothesis of
[
46
]
gives then that

(7.16)

AM_{i}=AM_{j}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{if $\iota(i),\iota(j)\in I_{k}$ for some $k\in[d]$}.

Note that
\iota
is surjective and
\iota^{-1}
induces a partition of
[m]
.
The connection between
[
46
]
and this work stems from the fact that the matroid partition is finer than that induced by
\iota^{-1}
, which we show next.

Let
K_{\kappa}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times p}
be the matrix such that
\kappa\circ x^{M}=K_{\kappa}x^{Y}
, and more explicitly,
(K_{\kappa})_{i,\iota(i)}=\kappa_{i}
with all other entries equal to zero. It follows that

\Sigma_{\kappa}=NK_{\kappa}\,
, and that
K_{\kappa}
has rank
p
for all
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
.
By construction, it holds that
v_{\kappa,j}:=K_{\kappa}\,b^{j}\in\ker(N)
for all
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{>0}
, and
\supp(v_{\kappa,j})=\iota^{-1}(I_{j})
.
By continuity, if some entries of
\kappa
are set to zero, the vector
v_{\kappa,j}
still belongs to
\ker(N)
.

As
K_{\kappa}(1,\dots,1)^{\top}=\kappa
, any vector in
\ker(N)
belongs to
\im(K_{\kappa})
for some
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Hence

\ker(N)=\bigcup_{\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\im(K_{\kappa})\cap\ker(N)=\bigcup_{\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}K_{\kappa}(\ker(\Sigma_{\kappa}))\,,

where in the last equality we use that
K_{\kappa}
has maximal column rank.
Using that
b^{1},\dots,b^{d}
form a basis for
\ker(\Sigma_{\kappa})
, and that the vectors

v_{\kappa,j}
,
v_{\kappa,i}
have disjoint support if
i\neq j
,
we obtain that the support of any minimal-support vector in
\ker(N)
is contained in one of
\supp(v_{\kappa,j})=\iota^{-1}(I_{j})
.
This implies that the matroid partition is finer than that induced by
\iota^{-1}
as desired.

Example 7.14
.
