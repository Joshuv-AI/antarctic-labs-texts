Small CD63 + CD81+ extracellular vesicles induce autophagy via the FOXO3a pathway in pancreatic cancer cells during starvation
Authors: Daniel Grasso, Maximiliano A Diaz, Laura Sobrado, Valentín Yzetta, Marcela A Cucher, Maria L Cerutti, Daniela L Papademetrio, Elida Alvarez, Maria Noé Garcia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), the most common histological subtype of pancreatic cancer, is an aggressive malignancy expected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths by 2040. A hallmark of PDACs is the highly desmoplastic and hypovascularized nature of its microenvironment, which enables PDAC cells to adapt and survive under conditions of low oxygen and nutrient deprivation through various cellular mechanisms. Among these processes, autophagy has emerged as a key response mechanism to cope with these adverse conditions. Consequently, a deeper understanding of the molecular events driving autophagy could pave the way for the development of new and more effective treatments for PDAC. In this context, we provide evidence of novel pathway mediated by extracellular vesicles (EVs) that promotes autophagy in a paracrine manner in PDAC cells in response to starvation. Our findings indicate that, under starvation conditions, EVs-associated tetraspanins, namely CD9, CD63 and CD81, mobilize towards domains-like structures within the plasma membrane of PDAC cells. Correspondingly, a specific release of small EVs (sEVs) is observed in the starved cells. Notably, sEVs derived from starved cells, but not those from cells at basal conditions, strongly induce the autophagy pathway in PDAC cells cultured with optimal nutritional media. Interestingly, this ability to induce autophagy is specific to CD63/CD81 double-positive sEVs and is effective even in non-tumoral pancreatic cells. This sEVs-mediated autophagy is, at least partially, mediated by the activation of the FOXO3a pathway. It is worth noting that, although EVs release returns to basal level after 1 h of recovery following starvation, these EVs retain a capacity to induce autophagy, suggesting a decupling between quantity and quality of secreted vesicles. Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the most frequent type of pancreatic cancer, with a five-years survival rate of only 13%1–3. The global rise in PDAC incidence, combined with the lack of early diagnostic tools and minimal advancements in the therapeutic approaches, make this disease an increasing public health concern1,2,4. PDAC is characterized by highly aggressive tumor cells embedded within a prominently desmoplastic and poorly vascularized tumor microenvironment (TME). This unique setting allows PDAC to adapt and thrive under conditions of limited nutrient availability5.

Regarding intercellular communications, recent years have brought about a revolutionary understanding of the significant role extracellular vesicles (EVs) play in cell biology6. EVs have been implicated in numerous physiological and pathological processes, including neurodegeneration and cancer7,8. These EVs are small membranous structures, in the nanometric scale, without replicative capability that are released by all cell types studied so far. Indeed, EVs have been detected in most human biological fluids, such as blood, urine, breast milk and semen9,10. EVs constitute a heterogeneous population, and current methodologies are yet unable to fully distinguish between all subtypes of EVs. Nevertheless, there are two main classifications: one based on the site of vesicles biogenesis, identifying microvesicles as those generated from the plasma membrane and exosomes as originating from the endo-lysosomal system10. Another classification considers vesicle size, categorizing them as small EV (sEVs) under 200 nm, medium EVs ranging between 200 and 800 nm, and large EVs for more than > 800 nm10,11. Additionally, EVs feature an array of transmembrane proteins known as tetraspanins12,13. Among these, CD9, CD63 and CD81 are commonly employed as EV markers; however, the distribution of these proteins is highly variable, with some EV populations displaying different combinations or lacking them entirely14,15. In any case, all EVs share the ability to transport various biological molecules, including proteins and nucleic acids, which exert specific effects on recipient cells either locally or in distant tissues. Notably, the profile of EVs released by cells and the messages they carry change depending on the cells type and its homeostatic condition. Thus, EVs hold critical relevance in cancer biology, serving as potential biomarkers and pharmacological targets. In pancreatic carcinogenesis, EVs play a profound role in transmitting resistance mechanisms, remodeling of TME, promoting cellular migration, and initiating pre-metastatic niches6,16,17.

Macroautophagy (referred to as autophagy hereafter) is a homeostatic process responsible for the degradation and recycling of cellular components, including entire organelles18. Autophagy is triggered under conditions of nutrients deprivation, absence of growth factors, or various stress situations such as organelle damage. Briefly, autophagy begins with the formation of an initial membrane known as the omegasome18. This membrane expands, engulfing the targeted cargo, that eventually forms a double membrane vesicle called the autophagosome. After potential interactions with the endosomal trafficking system, the autophagosome ultimately fuses with a lysosome, where its contents are degraded18. Autophagy serves as a survival mechanism, enabling cells to withstand stressful conditions. This process is particularly relevant in cancer biology, as it provides tumor cells with a means to resist stress and is closely linked to chemotherapeutic resistance in PDAC19,20.

In this study, we demonstrate that PDAC cells submitted to starvation can transmit resistance to other cells in a paracrine manner, inducing autophagy even in cells in optimal nutritional conditions through sEVs. This mechanism involves the activation of the FOXO3a pathway by CD63/CD81 double-positive sEVs derived from starved PDAC cells. These findings are crucial for understanding the intricate communication among tumor cells within the complex TME of PDAC and the resistance mechanisms associated with this devastating disease.

Results

Starvation of pancreatic cancer cells induces intracellular mobilization of extracellular vesicles-related tetraspanins

Within the heterogeneity of EVs populations, it is well-established that most include common tetraspanin proteins such as CD63, CD81 and CD912,13. These transmembrane proteins are widely used as markers for EVs, as they are integrated along the various routes of EVs biogenesis. To investigate the response of pancreatic cancer cells to starvation, cultures of the pancreatic cancer cell line Panc-1 were subjected to 1 h of nutrient deprivation. The starvation condition was induced by incubating the cultures in EBSS medium (Earl’s Balanced Salt Solution), leaving them without amino acids and growth factors for one hour. This strategy is widely used for the physiological induction of autophagy and cellular fasting states (see Suppl. Figure 1)21. Subsequently, the intracellular distribution of EV-associated tetraspanin proteins (CD63, CD81 and CD9) was observed. Using immunofluorescence followed by confocal microscopy, we identified a rapid response of these tetraspanins, which exhibited a distinct redistribution within the cells (Fig. 1A). Under basal conditions, tetraspanins displayed a disperse pattern throughout the cytoplasm, this was particularly noticeable in CD81 or were more concentrated within perinuclear structures for CD63 and CD9. In contrast, upon starvation, these proteins moved prominently towards the plasma membrane. This mobilization was quantified by measuring fluorescence intensity along the cell borders, revealing an increased presence of all three tetraspanins at the membrane (Fig. 1A). Moreover, detailed observations of the plasma membrane highlighted the formation of microdomains by mobilized tetraspanins (Fig. 1B). Many immunofluorescence samples from starved Panc-1 cells also demonstrated an increase in extracellular tiny dots positive for one or more of the tetraspanin being labeled (Fig. 1C). This phenomenon may correlate with an elevated release of EVs. Taken together, these findings suggest a rapid cellular response to starvation, involving the mobilization of tetraspanins towards microdomains on the plasma membrane in a manner consistent with enhanced EVs release.

Starvation induces a significant increase in extracellular vesicles release in pancreatic cancer cells

Following the observation of intracellular tetraspanins mobilization, it became essential to determine whether this cellular response correlates with a measurable increase in EVs release during starvation. To investigate this, conditioned media from Panc-1 cell cultures subjected to starvation were analyzed. Vesicles were stained with a fluorescent dye and quantified by image cytometry, as described in the methods section. Results confirmed our supposition, demonstrating a significant increase in the total EVs present in conditioned media. This increase was noticeable as early as 30 min of starvation and became even more pronounced after 1 h of nutrient deprivation (p < 0,05 and p < 0,0001, respectively, compared to basal conditions) (Fig. 2A). To validate that observed elevation in EVs release was indeed a direct response to nutrient deprivation, starvation conditions were reversed by reintroducing complete medium for 1–2 h of recovery. Notably, the data revealed that that the total EVs release returned to basal levels after the recovery period, underscoring the specificity of the cellular response to starvation (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, characterization of released EVs using dynamic light scattering (DLS) indicated that starvation induced a shift in the vesicle size profile, favoring smaller vesicle populations (Suppl. Figure 2A). To further explore whether starvation-induced EVs release pertained to a specific EV fraction, EVs were purified and size-fractioned via ultracentrifugation, and the resulting vesicles were quantified through image cytometry. As illustrated in Fig. 2C, a significant increase was observed in vesicles smaller than 200 nm, classified as small EVs (sEVs) (defined here as < 200 nm; medium EVs 200–800 nm; large EVs > 800 nm), in response to starvation (p < 0,0001). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were detected in medium or large EVs fractions (Fig. 2C). For additional confirmation, sEVs from various treatment conditions were isolated via ultracentrifugation and analyzed using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). This approach validated the increment of sEVs under starvation conditions and their subsequent return to basal levels upon the reintroduction of complete medium (Fig. 2D). Interestingly, the NTA analysis also revealed a considerable reduction in the zeta potential of sEVs under these treatments, which could suggest changes in membrane composition or EVs corona structure (Suppl. Figure 2B). The results were corroborated in two additional pancreatic cancer cell lines, MiaPaca-2 and BxPC-3, where starvation similarly induced an increase in sEVs release (Suppl. Figure 2C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that pancreatic cancer cells exhibit a specific response to starvation through the enhanced release of small EVs.

Pancreatic cancer cells respond to starvation with the tetraspanin-specific release of small extracellular vesicles

Our findings reveal that starvation induces a significant increase in the release of sEVs from pancreatic cancer cells. To begin, the characteristics of the sEVs isolated through our ultracentrifugation protocol were carefully examined. The isolated vesicles exhibit the typical cup-shaped morphology under transmission electron microscopy, and their presence was confirmed by western blot analysis, which showed positivity for EV-associated tetraspanins (Suppl. Figure 3A). To further characterize the vesicles released in response to starvation, sEVs samples underwent a series of immunofluorescence analyses employing pairs of antibodies targeting the tetraspanins CD63, CD9 and CD81, followed by image cytometry quantification. As illustrated in Fig. 3A, starvation stimulated an increase in sEVs positive for either CD9 (CD9+) or CD81 (CD81+), with minimal involvement of double-positive vesicles (CD9+/CD81+). A similar pattern was observed for the CD9+/CD63+ pair, where the increase was predominantly sustained by each individual population, with limited contributions from double-positive vesicles (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, for the pair CD63 and CD81, an increase in signal was detected for both tetraspanins in the isolated sEVs from starved Panc-1 cells, alongside a notable rise in the double-positive CD63+/CD81+ population (Fig. 3C). These results suggest that the total CD81+ sEVs population undergoes a significant increase in response to starvation, with a proportional rise in the CD81+/CD63+ double positive vesicles, which in turn drives the observed increase in total CD63+ sEVs. Figure 3D provides an integrative plot offering a clearer visualization of each sEVs population. Finally, the results were validated by magnetic beads immunocapture of sEVs using antibodies specific to each tetraspanins, followed by flow cytometry analysis. This approach involves capturing sEVs with one tetraspanin antibody and then revealing them with another, yielding consistent findings for the double-positive tetraspanin populations (Suppl. Figure 3B). In summary, pancreatic cancer cells subjected to starvation release distinct populations of sEVs that are positive for CD9, CD63 or CD81, as well as sEVs that exhibit CD63/CD81 double positivity.

CD63/CD81 double positive small extracellular vesicles from starved pancreatic cancer cells induce autophagy even in non-starved cells

Since nutrient deprivation triggers several cellular response programs, we wondered whether EVs released from pancreatic cancer cells under starvation have the potential to activate one of these nutrients stress responsive pathways, such as autophagy, in recipient cells. In other words, whether autophagy can be induced by a direct effect of EVs that came from starved cells. To explore this question, we designed an experimental setup where EVs from Panc-1 cells were purified under different starvation conditions and subsequently incubated in new cultures with optimal nutritional medium, with autophagy evaluated after 3 h of EVs uptake (Fig. 4A). In a preliminary approach, we quantified the fluorescent metachromatic shift of the acidotrophic stain acridine orange (AO) in live Panc-1 cell cultures receiving EVs from five distinct conditions: basal (B-EVs), 30 min starvation (s30-EVs), 1 h starvation (s1-EVs), 1 h of recovery (r1-EVs) and 2 h recovery (r2-EVs). Despite being a rather screening method, the results revealed a significant increase in the autophagosomes-associated signals for EVs isolated from starved Panc1-cells (Suppl. Figure 4A). To obtain a more specific and sensitive determination, we repeated the experiments and analyzed the LC3 cellular pattern. Upon autophagy activation, LC3 is cleaved, lipidated and incorporated into the autophagosomal membrane. Under basal conditions, LC3 exhibits a diffuse pattern distribution, which shift to a punctate pattern upon autophagy activation due to its recruitment to the autophagosomes18,21. Consequently, Panc-1 cultures were transfected with the red fluorescent protein-tagged LC3 plasmid (LC3-RFP) and exposed to conditioned media from starved cultures. Notably, the conditioned media from starved Panc-1 cultures demonstrated a strong capacity to induce autophagy in the same cell line under optimal nutrient availability (Fig. 4B). It is very particularly interesting to note that, although EVs release due to starvation become observable after 30 min, those vesicles are not yet capable of significantly triggering the autophagy response in recipient cells. Conversely, after 1 h of recovery, when we previously demonstrated a return to baseline EVs release levels (Fig. 2B), a pronounced induction of LC3 punctate distribution still occurs (Fig. 4B). These results suggest a possible decoupling between the quantity and quality of EVs released in response to starvation.

To ensure that autophagy induction was mediated specifically by EVs and not by other components in the conditioned media, such as supermeres or unknown factors, we repeated the experiments using purified EVs fractions. As shown in, Fig. 4C, medium and large EVs from starved Panc-1 cells had no significant effect on autophagy induction. However, the fraction corresponding to small EVs proved to be a potent autophagy inducer. Additionally, we evaluated the effect of sEVs on the autophagy flux, as the accumulation of LC3 puncta could potentially reflect autophagosome accumulation caused by an autophagy flux blockade rather than true induction. To differentiate between these possibilities, we utilized the mCherry-EGFP-LC3B plasmid, which expresses a dual-tagged LC3 protein. This approach allows discrimination between cytoplasmic autophagosomes, emitting both red and green fluorescence, and those already in the lysosomal compartment, where acidic conditions quench green fluorescence, leaving only red fluorescence detectable. The results, consistent with the LC3-RFP analysis, confirmed that the autophagic flux is effectively induced by sEVs from starved Panc-1 cells (Suppl. Figure 4B). Notably, Miapca-2 cells exhibited similar behavior, with autophagic flux induced by sEVs from starved cultures, whereas BxPC-3 cells showed no significant LC3 aggregation effects (Suppl. Figure 4C).

For further characterization of the EVs involved in autophagy induction, we subjected purified sEVs from starved Panc-1 cells to depletion of CD63 and/or CD81-positive populations using tetraspanin-specific antibody-attached magnetic beads. When the sEVs fraction was depleted of CD63 + vesicles, leaving CD81+ sEVs but excluding CD63+ and CD63+/CD81+, no autophagy induction was observed, regardless of whether the small vesicles originated from basal Panc-1 cells (B-sEVs) or 1 h starved cells (s1-sEVs) (Fig. 4D). In contrast, CD63+ sEVs from starved cells strongly induced autophagy, suggesting that the effect is mediated by CD63 positive or CD63/CD81 double-positive vesicles (Fig. 4D). Further resolving this, Fig. 4D demonstrates that depleting CD81+ sEVs, including CD81+ and CD63+/CD81 + vesicles, abolishes the autophagy-inducing properties, thereby identifying CD63/CD81 double-positive sEVs as the primary candidates responsible for autophagy induction.

In another set of experiments, we investigated whether sEVs derived from starved Panc-1 cells could trigger autophagy even in non-tumoral cells. We selected the immortalized non-tumoral human pancreatic cell line hTERT-HPNE, and cultures of these cells, maintained in optimal medium, were incubated with sEVs from Panc-1 cells. Autophagy was initially assessed using AO staining, which revealed an increase in cytoplasmic acidic vesicles and autophagosomes-associated signal ratios following the uptake of sEVs from starved Panc-1 cells (Fig. 4E and Suppl. Figure 4D). These results were corroborated by analyzing the LC3 pattern, showing a strong autophagy induction in hTER-HPNE cells incubated with sEVs derived from starved Panc-1 cultures (Fig. 4E).

Autophagy induced by small extracellular vesicles from starved pancreatic cancer cells is mediated by the FOXO3a pathway

FOXO3a is a transcription factor involved in the transcriptional activation of several genes within the autophagy pathway22. We decided to evaluate the FOXO3a pathway due to existing reports describing its activation induced by other EVs23–25. Through western blot analysis, we observed an increased amount of FOXO3a in non-starved Panc-1 culture after the uptake of sEVs derived from starved Panc-1 cells (s30-sEVs and s1-sEVs) (Suppl. Figure 5). Furthermore, a residual induction was detected in cells incubated with sEVs from recovery conditions (r1-sEVs and r2-sEVs) (Suppl. Figure 5). Importantly, FOXO3a transcriptional activity is primarily regulated by the modulation of its cytoplasm-to-nucleus shuttling22. Using immunofluorescence, we evaluated FOXO3a localization in Panc-1, MiaPaca-2 and BxPC-3 cells, focusing on the incubation duration with purified sEVs obtained from starved cultures (Fig. 5A). In Panc-1 cells, a rapid nuclear shuttling of FOXO3a was observed as early as 1 h after incubation, with levels decreasing after 4 h. MiaPaca-2 cells displayed a later and more progressive response, showing a significant effect at 3 h post-contact with the sEVs and maintaining elevated levels for at least the 5 h time point. Conversely, no FOXO3a movement was observed in BxPC-3 cell cultures for any of the studied incubation times (Fig. 5A). Given that FOXO1 has also been implicated in the regulation of autophagy22, we next examined whether sEVs from starved cells induced FOXO1 activation through nuclear translocation. Although FOXO1 has been associated with autophagy regulation in different cellular contexts, we did not observe any detectable nuclear accumulation of FOXO1 following treatment. These results indicate that, in contrast to FOXO3a, FOXO1 is not activated via nuclear translocation under our experimental conditions, suggesting a selective involvement of FOXO3a in the autophagic response induced by the sEVs. Lastly, cells incubated with sEVs from starved cultures exhibited an accumulation of ATG16L1-positive perinuclear puncta, confirming autophagosome biogenesis and the initiation of autophagy (Fig. 5B). Taken together, these findings suggest that sEVs released by pancreatic cancer cells in response to starvation stimulate the autophagy pathway through the transcriptional activation of the FOXO3a pathway.

Discussion

Pancreatic cancer prevalence continues to rise, with projections suggesting it will become the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths by 20401. Most PDAC are highly desmoplastic and hypovascularized, meaning cancer cells often experience hypoxic and nutrient-poor conditions. Our aim was to examine PDAC cells response to starvation. Using immunofluorescence, we observed rapid mobilization of the EVs-associated tetraspanins (CD9, CD63 and CD81) to the plasma membrane upon starvation, along with an increase in extracellular signals, indicating EV-mediated release of these proteins. Image cytometry and nanoparticle tracking analysis confirmed a swift, significant rise EVs release after 30–60 min of starvation. Notably, returning cells to nutritive media restored baseline values, demonstrating the specificity of this response. EVs are a diverse group of vesicles (30 nm to 1 μm) classified by size into small, medium, and large15. In our analysis, PDAC cells released small EVs during starvation, indicating a targeted cellular response to environmental changes that stops once conditions normalize.

EVs serves as platforms for intercellular communication, whether among neighboring cells or distant tissues26. They carry various macromolecules, including proteins and nucleic acids, that exert effector functions on recipient cells26. Under stress conditions such as starvation, cells activate numerous response programs, with the autophagy pathway being a primary one27,28. Recognizing that autophagy in PDAC cells under starvation might be transmitted to other cells, we conducted uptake experiments, exposing fresh cell cultures to EVs derived from starved cells. Remarkably, we found that conditioned media from starved Panc-1 cells is a potent inducer of autophagy in fresh pancreatic cancer cells under optimal nutritional conditions. Repeating these experiments across different EVs fractions revealed that sEVs were capable of inducing autophagy. This effect was noted in both Panc-1 and MiaPaca-2 cells but not for BxPC-3 cells. Moreover, sEVs from starved PDAC cells induced autophagy in non-tumoral human pancreatic cells (hTER-HPNE) grown in complete medium, underscoring the potential for EVs to exert effects not only locally but also on distant tissues, while our starvation-driven EV profiling was focused on PDAC cells and HPNE served only as a recipient model. These findings with hTER-HPNE suggest that tumoral EVs may influence non-tumoral areas of the pancreas.

For further characterization, we subjected sEVs from starved cells to tetraspanin-specific depletion of subpopulations using magnetic beads conjugated with antibodies against distinct tetraspanins. Intriguingly, depletion of CD63- and CD81-positive sEVs subpopulations abolished their autophagy-inducing capacity. This result implies that the CD63/CD81 double-positive subset is uniquely responsible for this functional effect. This finding is particularly noteworthy because increased levels of CD63/CD81 double-positive EVs have been detected in the plasma of patients with late-stage lung cancer and in bronchial epithelial cells exposed to cigarette smoke extracts29,30. Current evidence indicates that the functional effects associated with tumor-derived EVs-such as enhancing tumor growth and influencing the tumor microenvironment-stem not from the total population of released EVs but from specific subpopulations31. Our results further support this perspective, reinforcing the concept that tumor cells actively produce distinct and functionally specialized subpopulations of EVs in response to defined stimuli, such as starvation in this case. Moving forward, future studies are expected to investigate the possible link between CD63/CD81 double-positive vesicles and cellular stress-response mechanisms, including autophagy induction mediated by FOXO proteins.

Interestingly, sEVs derived from 1 h recovery conditions still exhibited strong autophagy-inducing capabilities, despite EVs quantity returning to baseline in prior experiments. This suggests a decupling of quantity and quality, indicating that EVs-mediated autophagy is not directly proportional to the released amount but rather to the intrinsic characteristics of the vesicles themselves. This hypothesis is further supported by the change in z-potential observed in NTA studies for starvation-derived vesicles. Published studies have linked z-potential changes to the composition of the vesicle membrane and/or corona32,33. Thus, the data presented here suggest a potential independence between vesicles quantity and their intrinsic properties or composition.

The role of EVs-mediated intercellular communication within the TME is increasingly recognized as vital for understanding tumor behavior and its ability to acquire resistance to environmental conditions and therapies6. Autophagy appears to be one such mechanism implicated in EVs-mediated cellular responses to adverse scenarios. For example, EVs from bone marrow stem cells modulate autophagy, preventing glucocorticoids-induced epithelial cell injury in the same tissue34. In prostate cancer, small EVs derived from stromal cells activate the AMPK pathway and autophagy, conferring radioresistance to cancerous cells35. Interestingly, EVs from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (huMSCs) protect microglia from ischemia/reperfusion by inducing FOXO3a, which subsequently enhances mitophagy (selective autophagy of mitochondria)24. FOXO3a, a member of the Forkhead box O (FOXO) family of transcription factors, is involved in several critical cellular processes, including the transcriptional activation of autophagy. Cytoplasm-localized FOXO3a can be phosphorylated, enabling its nuclear translocation for transcription activity22. FOXO3a initiates autophagy by upregulating various autophagy-related genes and indirectly enhancing FOXO1 transcription, which activates TFEB, a key transcription factor for autophagy22,36. Several studies have also reported the activation of FOXO3a pathway by EVs in different contexts23,24. Notably, small EVs from the pancreatic cancer cell line BxPC3 have been shown to modulate Treg immune response through a mechanism involving nuclear translocation of FOXO1a and FOXO3a25. In our study, we observed that sEVs from starved PDAC cells induce FOXO3a expression alongside its nuclear translocation. This aligns with a concurrent increase in ATG16L1 signal, key member of the ATG16-12-5 autophagy complex, exhibited in puncta patterns compatible with autophagosome biogenesis. Thus, the FOXO3a pathway appears to play a role in sEVs-mediated autophagy induction in PDAC.

It is noteworthy that sEVs from BxPC-3 cells do not trigger FOXO3a mobilization. Unlike PDAC cell lines such as Panc-1 and MiaPaca-2, BxPC-3 cells harbor a wild-type version of Kras, and despite responding to starvation with increased EV release, they fail to mobilize FOXO3a or induce autophagy in recipient cells. Future studies should investigate whether this differential behavior is linked to the specific oncogenic profile of this cell line. Additionally, as mentioned earlier, EVs from BxPC-3 cells have been reported to induce FOXO3a mobilization in immune cells, prompting further exploration to understand whether this discrepancy arises from different contexts or EVs selectivity.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that starved PDAC cells utilize CD63+/CD81+ sEVs to induce FOXO3a-mediated autophagy in recipient cells, even under optimal nutritional conditions. Something similar has been reported for hypoxia, where PDAC cells respond to low oxygen levels by increasing the release of sEVs, which subsequently promote the survival of neighboring cells37. This suggests that cancerous cells may contribute to population-wide resistance within the nutrient-limited PDAC microenvironment. Furthermore, autophagy has been associated with chemotherapy resistance19,38,39, making these results particularly significant for understanding PDAC´s resistance to treatment. Future research is necessary to provide a comprehensive overview and explore how these novel findings could be leveraged to improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Cell cultures

Cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified chamber with 5% CO2 and were harvested at 80–90% confluency. PANC-1 and MiaPaca-2 cell lines (purchased to ATCC, Cat. CRL-1469 and CRL-1420) were cultured in DMEM high glucose medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. 12100046), while BxPC-3 cells (purchased to ATCC, Cat. CRL-1687) were cultured in RPMI medium (Gibco 23400-021). For the PDAC cell lines, the medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Internegocios S.A.) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. 15140122). The hTERT-HPNE cell line (purchased to ATCC Cat. CRL-4023) was cultured in a complete growth medium consisting of 75% DMEM without glucose (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. 11966025) supplemented with 2mM L-glutamine, 25% medium M3 Base (Incell Corporation LLC Cat. M300F-500), 5% fetal bovine serum, 10 ng/mL human recombinant EGF (R&D Systems Cat. 236-EG) and 5.5 mM D-glucose (1 g/L) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. 15140122). All cultures were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination prior to use. For cellular starvation (STV), the cultures were washed twice with PBS and then incubated for either 30 min–1 h with Earl’s Balanced Salt Solution (EBSS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. 14155063). This treatment induces acute starvation, activating autophagy due to deprivation of nutrients and growth factors21,40. Similarly, for the basal samples (Basal), the cultures were washed twice with PBS and then incubated with complete medium for the indicated times. For the recovery experiments (R1 and R2), following starvation, the EBSS was replaced with complete medium supplemented with 10% EVs-depleted fetal bovine serum. In all conditions, for EVs collection, the supernatant was obtained from 9 × 106 total secreting cells, distributed across four 10 cm plates at 80% confluence. The collected EVs correspond to those secreted by the cells during 1 h of incubation for each condition, except for the 30-minute time point.

Extracellular vesicles purification

EVs were isolated from 10 cm dishes of cells culture supernatants using differential centrifugation and ultracentrifugation protocols. Briefly, conditioned media were subjected to three consecutive centrifugations at 300 x g for 20 min at 4 °C (Thermo Fisher Scientific IEC-MULTI RF #084670F, fixed angle rotor 8850), and the pellet was discarded. The supernatants were then centrifuged at 2000 x g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in PBS and labeled as the large EVs fraction (lEVs). Subsequently, the supernatant from this step underwent another centrifugation at 10,000 x g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS to be labeled as the medium EVs fraction (mEVs). Finally, the supernatant from the previous medium EVs step was centrifuged once more at 180,000 x g for 2 h at 4 °C (HIMAC CP80NX, fixed angle rotor P45AT) to isolate small EVs (sEVs), which were collected by pellet resuspension. Each of the fractions obtained (lEVs, mEVs, and sEVs) was washed at least twice with PBS.

Experiments using conditioned media, the cell culture supernatants were subjected to three consecutive centrifugations 300 x g for 20 min at 4 °C, with pellets discarded at each step. The final supernatants were then centrifuged at 5000 x g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was used for experimentation.

EVs characterization and quantification

Image cytometry-based EV quantification and tetraspanin immunofluorescence

EVs image cytometry protocol was adapted from Schürz et al.41. Briefly, EVs were incubated either with 1 µM CellTracker Red CMTPX Dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. C34552) or 7 µM CFSE (Thermo Fisher Scientific C1157) for 30 min at 37 °C. 10 µl of stained EVs were incubated on microscope slides with 12 reaction wells (Thermo Fisher Scientific – Marienfeld – Cat. 10777124) in humidity chamber at room temperature for 30 min to allow for adherence. After washing with PBS, the adhered EVs were fixed with 4% praformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, washed again, and mounted for microscopy. Images, with at least 10 fields per well, were acquired with an EVOS M7000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. AMF7000) and analyzed with the EVAnalyzer plugin41 of FIJI software (NIH – ImageJ). When detection involved specific tetraspanins, the EVs fixed on the microscopy slides were incubated for 1 h in a blocking solution (10% EVs-dpleted FBS in PBS). They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in humidity chamber with primary antibodies (rabbit anti-CD63 Abbexa abx231490 1:500, goat anti-CD81 Santa Cruz Biotech. sc-31234 1:300, mouse anti-CD9 Santa Cruz Biotech. sc-13118 1:300) in the blocking solution. After washing in PBS, the slides were incubated for 2 h with conjugated secondary antibodies AlexaFluor 488 or 594 (Thermo Fiseher Scientific) diluted 1:1000.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), size distribution and zeta potential

Physical properties of EVs were evaluated by a nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) ZetaView PMX-230-Z TWIN (Particle Metrix). Briefly, samples were diluted in naoparticle-free water immediately before measurement; size distribution and concentration were determined with a 488 nm laser using the following: (i) acquisition parameters: Sensitivity 80, Shutter 100, Frame Rate 30, Positions 11, Cycles 1, Resolution Med; (ii) Post-acquisition parameters: Max Area 1000, Min Area 10, Min Brightness 30, Tracelength 15, nm/Class 5, Classes/Decade 64. Zeta potential was determined also by a 488 nm laser using the following: (i) Acquisition parameters: Sensitivity 80, Shutter 100, Frame Rate 30, Positions 2, Cycles 2, Resolution High, Pulse Continuous < 2 mS/cm; (ii) post-acquisition parameters: Max Area 1000, Min Area 10, Min Brightness 30, Tracelength 15, ZP/Class 2.0 mV, Max ZP 200.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were performed using the Zetasizer Nano S (Malvern Panalytical) at UNSAM. Seven measurements per sample were obtained using the cuvette Zen2112 (15 ul) under General Purpose SOP Analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For transmission electron microscopy, the fixed samples were adsorbed onto grids and incubated with 10% uranyl acetate for 2 min. After incubation, the grids were washed with distillated water, allowed to dry, and subsequently examined under a Carl Zeiss C-10 electron microscope (LANAIS-MIE, UBA).

Bead-based flow cytometry immunocapture

Flow cytometry analysis was performed using protein G coated magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. 10003D), which were incubated with specific tetraspanin antibodies (mouse anti-CD63 BD 556019, goat anti-CD81 Santa Cruz Biotech. sc-31234, mouse anti-CD9 Santa Cruz Biotech. sc-13118). After EVs immunocaptures, EVs-beads were detected using a second antibody targeting another tetraspanin, followed by conjugated secondary antibody Alexa Flour 647 (ThermoFisher A-21245, A-21235, A-21447). The analysis was conducted on a FACS Calubur (BD) at the Fundación Instituto Leloir, and data were processed using FlowJo software. The protocol followed the strategy and controls outlined by Campos Silva et al.29,42,43. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was measured, and relative fluorescence intensity (RFI) and stain index (SI) were calculated for each sample as: RFI= (MFI sample)/(MFI isotype control (IgG)) and SI= (MFI sample – MFI isotype control)/(2 × SD isotype). RFI > 1 and SI > 0 correspond to positive signal for the tested protein on the EVs-Beads.

EVs uptake assays

Cells in optimal nutritional conditions were incubated with 50% standard completed medium and 50% purified EVs in PBS, adjusted to achieve a final concentration of 10% EVs-free FBS. The incubation process was concluded after the stipulated time to proceed with the corresponding assay.

Autophagy assays

For rapid screening we used a modification of the methods described by Thomé et al. based on the acidotrophic fluorescent dye acridine orange (AO)44. Briefly, after treatments, cells in 96 multiwell plates were incubated with 1 µg/ml of AO (Sigma-Aldrich Cat. A8097) for 5 min. After staining, cells were immediately scanned in a Sapphire Biomolecular Imager (Azure Biosystems) with filters for red and green channels (520/566BP24 and 488/518BP22 respectively). Analysis of the green to red fluorescence intensity rate (G/R FI rate) was calculated.

LC3 puncta formation assay was performed in cells cultured on 24-multiwell plate with 0.12 mm round glass coverslips until 50–70% confluency. Cells were transfected with RFP-LC3B plasmid (Addgene #200943) using TransIntro PL (TransGen Biotech Cat. FT301-01). Culture media was replaced 4 h after transfection and cells were cultured 24 h prior to further experiments. Following treatments, cells were first washed with PBS, fixed with ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and washed twice againg with PBS. Fixed cells were mounted for image acquisition. Red fluorescent dots in the cytoplasm of transfected cells were counted. The values ​​expressed represent the average of LC3-RFP dots per cell from 10 fields evaluated. In another sets of experiments, autophagic flux was assessed using the tandem fluorescent reporter mCherry-EGFP-LC3B (Addgene plasmid #22418). This assay is based on the differential pH sensitivity of EGFP and mCherry, allowing discrimination between autophagosomes and autolysosomes, as the EGFP signal is quenched upon lysosomal acidification while mCherry remains stable21. Cells were transfected as described above, and live-cell imaging was performed. Fluorescent signal areas in the red (mCherry) and green (EGFP) channels were quantified, and the red-to-green fluorescent ratio was calculated as a measure of autophagic flux. Images were acquired using an EVOS M7000 imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. AMF7000) and analyzed with FIJI software (NIH, ImageJ).

Western blot

After treatment, cells cultured in 10 cm plates were placed on ice and incubated with ice-cold modified RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 0.5% deoxycholate, 1% NP-40) supplemented with 1% beta-mercaptoethanol. Lysates were centrifuged at 10000 x g for 20 min at 4° C transferring the supernatants to a fresh tube. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford protein assay (Bio-Rad Cat. #5000006). The whole-cell extracts were separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (GVS PVDF Cat. 1214429). Following this, the membranes were blocked in 3% skimmed milk dissolved in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C (FOXO3a Cell Signaling #2497 1:1000; phosphor-4E-BP1 (Thr70) Cell Signaling #9455 1:1000; phospho-p70 S6 kinase (Thr389) Cell Signaling #9205 1:1000; phosphor-S6 ribosomal protein (Ser240/244) Cell Signaling #2215 1:1000; SQSTM1/p62 Cell Signaling #88588 1:1000). Fluorescent secondary antibodies AzureSpectra 700 and 800 (Azure Biosystems) were employed and blots were normalized by total line protein content using the TotalStain Q (PDVF) solution (Azure Biosystems Cat. AC2226). For EVs characterization, sEVs purified from supernatants of 9 × 106 total secreting cells, were resuspended in RIPA buffer. Then lysates were processed as described above. Membranes were probed for CD63 (Abbexa abx231490 1:400), CD81 (Santa Cruz Biotech. sc-31234 1:300), CD9 (Santa Cruz Biotech. sc-13118 1:300), Calnexin (BD #610524 1:500), and Tsg101 (BD #612697 1:600). In all the cases, images were acquired with a Sapphire Biomolecular Imager (Azure Biosystems) and quantified with FIJI software (NIH - ImageJ).

Cells Immunofluorescence

For immunostaining, cells were grown on a 24 multiwell plate containing 0.12 mm round glass coverslips. The cells were washed with PBS and fixed with ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. After fixation, the cells were permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocked with 10% FBS (blocking solution) in PBS for 60 min. They were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C in blocking solution (rabbit anti-CD63 Abbexa abx231490 1:500, goat anti-CD81 Santa Cruz Biotech. sc-31234 1:300, mouse anti-CD9 Santa Cruz Biotech. sc-13118 1:300, rabbit anti-ATG16L1 Cell Signaling #8089 1:500; anti-FOXO3a Cell Signaling #2497 1:600; FOXO1 Cell Signaling #2880 1:500). Following this, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated for 2 h with appropriate conjugated secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 488 or 594 (Thermo Fiseher Scientific) diluted 1:1000. Nuclear staining was performed by incubating the cells with DAPI at a concentration of 1:10000 for 10 min in PBS. To determine fluorescence intensity at the cell borders, a fixed-width line selection tool was used to generate the ROIs, where the signal was subsequently quantified. Slides were imaged by confocal microscopy (Carl Zeiss LSM800) and analyzed by FIJI software (NIH – ImageJ).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently conducted three times. Statistical significance tests were 2-tailed, with the significance level set at 0.05. Comparisons between two groups were evaluated using Student’s unpaired t test for normally distributed data. For non-parametric samples, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. One-way ANOVA was used to determine significance among multiple groups, with Bonferroni, Scheffé or Tukey Post hoc tests applied when appropriate. For datasets where a group did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed. In cases where two variables were compared between groups, an Analysis of Deviance (Type II Wald chi-square tests) was performed. In all figures: ns, p-value > 0.05; ∗, p-value < 0.05; ∗∗, p-value < 0.01; ∗∗∗, p-value < 0.001. Statistical analysis was performed using R Statistical Software (R Core Team 2021) and GraphPad Prism.
