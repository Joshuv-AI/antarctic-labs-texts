Predictive utility and associated challenges of adolescent arm scarring in suicide risk assessment
Authors: Yuan-Hong Shi, Chen-Xu Tong, Wei Xu, Ping Tong, Lu Yang, Yong-Long Zhang, Li-Ping Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is common and associated with higher suicide risk. Self-injury scars hold valuable clinical insights, but research in this area remains limited. This study aims to examine possible correlations between scar characteristics and both suicide risk and symptom severity.MethodsAdolescents with arms self-injury scars were enrolled in the study. Scar characteristics were quantitatively evaluated. The suicide behavior questionnaire-revised (SBQ-R) and the adolescent non-suicidal self-Injury assessment questionnaire (ANSAQ) were utilized to assess suicide risk and symptom severity. Statistical analyses were conducted to explore the possible links between scar characteristics and both suicide risk and symptom severity.ResultsTotal 198 cases were enrolled, representing 47.8% of adolescent patients and 7.6% of all patients, with 77.3% classified as NSSI. The total scar score was linked to Self-injury frequency, ANSAQ scores, Days of last injury and Course of disease. Logistic regression showed that total scar score, ANSAQ score, and Days of last injury were linked to suicide risk. In contrast, SBQ-R score, Course of disease, Days of last injury, and Frequency were associated with symptom severity. ROC analysis showed that only ANSAQ scores predicted suicide risk, while SBQ-R scores and Frequency predicted symptom severity.ConclusionThe self-injury scars among adolescents are associated with specific clinical features and suicide risk, but their value for predicting suicide or assessing disease severity remains limited.Clinical trial numberThe study was registered with chictr.org.cn: ChiCTR2600117633, on 2025/03/04.Keywords: Adolescents, Scar, NSSI, Suicide risk, Symptom severity

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is classified as an independent mental disorder in the DSM-5 (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders) [1, 2]. The prevalence rate of NSSI among adolescents is approximately 20%, with up to 50% comorbid with mental health disorders like depression [3–6]. Although NSSI is typically not driven by suicidal ideation, it is linked to a higher risk of suicide and impaired cognitive functioning [7–12]. It frequently co-occurs with mental health disorders such as depression, bipolar disorder (BD), and borderline personality disorder (BPD) [13, 14].

The pathogenesis of non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) remains inadequately understood, and as of now, no conclusive biomarkers have been identified. Physical scars stemming from self-injurious behaviors function as crucial diagnostic indicators for NSSI and stand as objective clinical manifestations. However, research in this area remains limited. Current studies on scars fall into the following categories: (1) Scar assessment scales. These encompass tools for evaluating self - harm behaviors, such as the Adolescent Nonsuicidal Self - Injury Assessment Questionnaire(ANSAQ) [1, 15, 16]. Few scales have been developed specifically to assess scars from NSSI. Two identified tools are the Multidimensional Non-Suicidal Self-Injury Scar Scale (NSSI-ScarS) [17] and the Non-Suicidal Self-Injury Scar Cognition Scale (NSSI-SCS) [18]. (2) Research has also explored the link between scars and NSSI risk factors, clinical symptoms, and prognosis. However, findings are inconsistent, and few longitudinal studies exist [19, 20]. (3) Much research has focused on the mechanisms behind NSSI, few studies have examined the psychological and behavioral significance of scar formation.

Self-harming behaviors often involve cutting the skin on limbs with sharp objects such as knives or nails [21]. Scars contain valuable external features that can aid in diagnosing NSSI and related comorbidities, assessing suicide risk and severity, and predicting prognosis. Scars may also reflect risk factors [4, 22] and underlying mechanisms like pain perception, reward pathways, the opioid system, neurotransmitter activity, and genetic influences [10, 23–25]. However, more research is needed to fully understand these connections. We suggest that scar features, such as number, location, and size, may relate to suicide risk, diagnosis, co-occurring mental conditions, and the severity of NSSI. To test this, we will recruit adolescents with a history of scarring from self-injury and record their scar characteristics. Participants will be divided into two groups: those with NSSI only and those with past suicide attempts. We will compare scar features between the groups and analyze how they relate to suicide risk and symptom severity using correlation, regression, and ROC curve analyses to assess their potential as predictive markers.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our Hospital (2025ky033). All participants provided written informed consent (including the publication of anonymized scar images) in line with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participant screening and data collection took place in the Department of Clinical Psychology at our Hospital. On the day of enrollment, all eligible participants completed demographic and scar-related questionnaires, scar photography, and scale assessments.

Participants

The patient inclusion process is shown in Fig. 1. All patients treated at our hospital’s Department of Clinical Psychology between March 1, 2025, and September 30, 2025, were considered for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) aged 6–23 years (This age range includes children, adolescents, and young adults—all referred to as “adolescents” in this article for simplicity); (2) had scars on the arms; (3) scars resulted from self-harm; (4) could communicate effectively and complete the assessment scales independently or with minimal help, and understood the content; (5) had not received any treatment, including biological, physical, or psychological interventions, within two weeks prior to inclusion; (6) volunteered to participate with written informed consent from both the patient and legal guardian.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Severe organic brain disorders or significant systemic medical conditions; (2) Self-harming behaviors linked to other psychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia, autism, intellectual disability, impulse control disorder, or substance abuse; (3) Tattoos, scars unrelated to NSSI, physical trauma, or deformities on the arms; (4) Incomplete data collection or participant/guardian withdrawal from the study.

Participants meeting inclusion criteria were assigned to the Suicide or NSSI group based on the primary intent of their self-harm - suicidal or non-suicidal. Suicidal intent was assessed via structured clinical interviews conducted independently by at least two board-certified psychiatrists. Group assignment required consensus between the two raters; if they disagreed, a third senior psychiatrist performed a re-evaluation and made the final decision.

Assessments

Scar assessments

The evaluation of scars was conducted in two stages. First, each participant’s scars were photographed. To standardize the process, a custom device was used for capturing images of arm scars, as shown in Fig. 1A. The device includes a Canon digital camera mounted on a box with fixed screws for adjustable positioning. It also features an internal lighting system to ensure consistent illumination and uniform image quality. This setup helped maintain standardized photographic conditions for all participants.

The photographs were transferred to a computer, and scar characteristics were assessed and scored. To illustrate these characteristics, we selected 24 representative images grouped into four types, as shown in Fig. 2. Scars A1-A6 are superficial, recent, numerous, mostly linear, and arranged in regular patterns. Scars B1-B6 are older and more established, often combined with superficial marks. Scars C1-C6 are deep and high-risk. Scars D1-D6 exhibit characteristics indicative of deliberate shaping. The assessment of scars is based on NSSI - ScarS [17]. The evaluation encompasses seven dimensions, with total scores ranging from 7 to 25. (Table 1).

Demographic variables and self-injury related characteristics

Demographic variables included gender and age. Factors related to self-injurious behavior were Frequency (episodes per month), Days of last injury, Course of disease (months since first occurrence), and Comorbidity (specifically depression, BD and BPD; the scores are shown in Table 1). Data were collected on the day of enrollment.

Scale assessment

Two standardized scales were used to assess NSSI and suicide risk. The ANSAQ includes a behavioral and a functional section. In this study, only the behavioral section was used to measure NSSI severity. Higher scores indicate more severe NSSI. The ANSAQ has strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.900 [1]. The Suicide Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised (SBQ-R) is a four-item self-report scale that assesses lifetime suicidal behavior, suicidal thoughts, attempts, and threats in the past year, as well as the likelihood of future suicide. Higher scores indicate higher suicide risk, with a score of ≥ 7 suggesting significant risk. The SBQ-R has a Cronbach’s α of 0.768 in the general population [26]. Both assessments were conducted under the supervision of a psychiatrist on the day of enrollment.

Statistical analysis

Count data were presented as percentages and compared using chi-square tests. Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean (standard deviation, SD) and compared using independent t-tests. Non-normally distributed data were log-transformed to meet normality assumptions before analysis. Scar characteristics and clinical data (including demographic factors, factors related to self-injury, and ANSAQ and SBQ-R scales) were compared between NSSI and suicide groups. Pearson and Spearman correlations were used for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Binary logistic regression models used SBQ-R and ANSAQ scores (dichotomized at the 75th percentile) as dependent variables, with clinical and scar-related indicators as independent variables. The ROC curve was used to assess the predictive performance of variables in the logistic regression model for suicide risk and illness severity in self-harming patients. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSSAU with two-tailed tests, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Inclusion procedure and self-injury scar characteristics in participants

During the study period, 13,147 patients were admitted, including 2,609 first-time patients. Among them, 2,178 were adolescents, with 414 being first-time admissions (15.9% of all first-time patients). Of these adolescents, 198 had self-harm scars, accounting for 47.8% of the adolescent group for initial consultation and 7.6% of all patients for initial consultation. Among those with self-harm scars, 153 had engaged in NSSI, representing 77.3% of the scar group, 37.0% of the adolescent population, and 5.9% of all patients. Additionally, 45 cases were classified as suicide attempts, making up 22.7% of those with scars, 10.9% of the adolescent group, and 1.7% of all patients. Based on inclusion criteria, 86 NSSI patients and 29 suicide attempt patients were enrolled in the study (Fig. 1).

Relevant factors influencing suicide risk and NSSI symptoms

Associations between scar characteristics and clinical data

The correlation analysis between clinical data and scar characteristics was conducted. Results (Fig. 3) showed that Total scar scores were positively correlated wtih Frequency (r = 0.281, P = 0.002; Fig. 3B) and ANSAQ score (r = 0.225, P = 0.016; Fig. 3E), and negatively correlated with Days of last injury (r=-0.532, P < 0.001; Fig. 3C) and Course of disease (r=-0.263, P = 0.004; Fig. 3D). Among the seven scar characteristics, Numbers exhibited the strongest correlation with four of the nine clinical indicators. Among the clinical indicators, Days of last injury demonstrated the highest correlation with four of the scar characteristics (Fig. 3A).

Logistic regression analysis of factors influencing suicide risk and NSSI symptoms

Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to assess how various factors affect suicide risk and NSSI symptoms in patients. SBQ-R and ANSAQ scores were used as dependent variables, with the 75th percentile as the cut-off (Of the 115 participants, only five scored below 7 on the SBQ-R, suggesting limited discriminative ability. Based on prior evidence and our preliminary analysis, we therefore set the cutoff at the 75th percentile—applied consistently to both the SBQ-R and ANSAQ scores). Independent variables included clinical data and total scar scores (Due to redundancy of sub-indices and total score in scar assessment, they were excluded from statistical analysis). Results showed that total scar scores (odds ratios (OR): 1.408; 95%CI: 1.061 ~ 1.869; P = 0.018), ANSAQ scores (OR: 1.177; 95%CI: 1.081 ~ 1.281; P < 0.001), and Days of last injury (OR: 1.658; 95%CI: 1.158 ~ 2.373; P = 0.006) were significantly linked to suicide risk (Fig. 4A). For NSSI symptoms, significant predictors were SBQ-R scores (OR: 1.331; 95%CI: 1.137 ~ 1.558; P < 0.001), Course of disease (OR: 1.033; 95%CI:1.004 ~ 1.063; P = 0.026), Days of last injury (OR: 0.706; 95%CI:0.505 ~ 0.987; P = 0.041) and Frequency (OR: 1.088; 95%CI:1.016 ~ 1.165; P = 0.016) (Fig. 4C).

ROC curve of the factors affecting the prediction rate of suicide risk and NSSI symptoms

ROC curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive performance of suicide risk and NSSI symptom indicators using binary logistic regression. Three suicide risk indicators were analyzed: Total scores of scar, ANSAQ scores and Days of last injury; four indicators were assessed for NSSI symptoms: SBQ-R scores, Course of disease, Days of last injury and Frequency (Table 3). As shown in Fig. 4B and D, all three suicide risk indicators had AUC values above 0.5. However, only ANSAQ scores showed significant predictive accuracy (AUC: 0.741; P < 0.001). The combined model improved prediction (AUC: 0.781; P < 0.001). Among the NSSI symptom indicators, Days of last injury had an AUC below 0.5, and Course of disease showed no statistical significance (P > 0.05). SBQ-R scores (AUC: 0.748; P < 0.001) and Frequency (AUC: 0.666; P = 0.002) demonstrated significant predictive value. The combined model achieved the highest accuracy (AUC: 0.840; P < 0.001).

Discussion

Our study found that NSSI patients make up 37.0% of adolescent patients and 5.9% of the total population. All NSSI patients in our study had co-occurring mental disorders, though the prevalence was lower than in other studies [3–7, 27, 28], likely due to stricter inclusion criteria requiring visible scars. Among adolescents who self-harmed, 22.7% did so under suicidal thoughts, suggesting suicide attempts.Research on this topic is limited. Most suicide studies do not focus on scars, while NSSI 10 studies often exclude those with suicidal intent. Certainly, there are exceptions [9, 29]. However, few studies directly compare suicide and NSSI or investigate scar prevalence.

Self-harm takes many forms [4, 21, 30], cutting is the most common, making up 70 ~ 97% of cases [29]. It can occur on any body part, but the arms are most frequently affected [30]. Therefore, we focused on arm scars in our research. Our study compared scars and clinical features between patients with NSSI and those with suicide attempts. Results showed no significant differences between the two groups except Scar treatment and SBQ-R scores. The Scar treatment had only one case per group, making the sample size inadequate and severely limiting statistical power. Overall, there were few significant differences in clinical or scar-related features between the two groups, suggesting substantial overlap. Therefore, data from both groups will be combined for further correlation and regression analyses. Moreover, this study conducts a combined analysis to assess how self-harm scarring - regardless of whether it meets NSSI criteria - predicts suicide risk and symptom severity.

We conducted a correlation analysis between scar characteristics and clinical data, revealing significant associations, though the strength varied across indicators. Although some studies find no link between scar characteristics and the suicide risk or severity of NSSI [31, 32], others report positive correlations. Another study found that scar characteristics offered more useful insights than NSSI severity measures in assessing risk [33]. Earlier studies also found that certain scar traits (like self-harm frequency, method, pain, injury severity, and scarring) were linked to higher suicide risk [19, 20, 34]. However, these studies are dated, and recent research on the connection between NSSI scars and suicide risk is still limited.

Earlier research suggested that the presence and number of NSSI scars could predict suicidal ideation and attempts [35]. In our study, however, further binary logistic regression analysis showed that the total scar score was associated with suicide risk but did not significantly predict it in ROC analysis. However, ANSAQ, SBQ-R, and some clinical features - such as frequency of self-harm and timing of the most recent incident - significantly predict both suicide risk and clinical severity. This study aimed to examine whether self-harm scars predict suicide risk and symptom severity in NSSI individuals, but the results did not support the hypotheses. Possible explanations for this inconsistency may be due to limitations in the scar assessment methodology and statistical methods. Although this study found no statistically significant link between self-harm scars and suicide risk, the clinical value of these scars remains substantial. We identified strong associations between scar features—such as total scar score, number of scars, location, time of self-injury, and scar-related medical care—and key clinical factors including patient age, self-harm frequency, course of disease, and symptom severity. As enduring physical markers of NSSI, scars are more than aftereffects: they visibly reflect core psychological difficulties—impaired emotion regulation, intense psychological distress, maladaptive self-concept, and growing suicidal capability [36, 37].

This study is confronted with several limitations: (1) Limited sample size. Small sample size (n = 115) limits statistical power, validity, and generalizability of results. The logistic regression models are likely underpowered, and confidence intervals should be interpreted cautiously. (2) Cross-sectional design. This cross-sectional study cannot establish temporal precedence, so its predictive validity should be interpreted cautiously. A longitudinal study tracking scar characteristics and later suicidal ideation or behavior would yield more robust, generalizable findings. (3) Inadequate assessment methods for suicide risk and NSSI symptoms. The study used only one scale to assess suicide risk and NSSI symptoms, limiting the depth of evaluation. (4) Incomplete control of confounding variables. Participants with depression, BD, or BPD were included—conditions with heterogeneous clinical presentations that may bias results. Age differences and motives for self-harm were also inadequately controlled. (5) Absence of a control group. Without a comparison group of individuals with non-self-harm scars, causal inferences about the link between self-harm scars and suicide risk are unsupported. (6) Selection bias and generalizability: The wide age range of participants, under-reporting of scars due to self-concealment, and selection bias from including only hospital-attending patients. Race, geographic location, and environmental influences were omitted from analysis, limiting generalizability.

Adolescent suicide is a critical global public health issue requiring urgent action [38]. NSSI is highly prevalent among adolescents and strongly linked to increased suicide risk. Self-harm scars are crucial clinical indicators, however, many adolescents hide their scars [17], and only a few seek help on their own or are noticed by parents. Early identification of these scars and assessment of their characteristics may be help evaluate symptom severity and suicide risk, enabling timely interventions to protect adolescent mental health. Therefore, future research should focus on two areas. First, leverage advanced information technology—such as SAM and nnU-Net for scar image segmentation, combined with prompt engineering, CNNs, and contrastive learning—to uncover the clinical value of scar features, especially their ability to predict suicide risk and symptom severity [39–41]. Traditional statistical methods are inadequate for such high-dimensional image data; machine learning and deep learning [42, 43] offer more robust predictive modeling. Second, a standardized scar assessment tool should be developed from existing research and integrated into routine adolescent health screenings for wider use.

Conclusion

The study found that 47.8% of adolescent patients and 7.6% of all patients had self-harm scars, with NSSI accounting for 77.3% of cases. No significant differences were observed between the NSSI and suicide attempt groups in most variables, except for gender, scar treatment, and SBQ-R scores. The total scar score was significantly associated with Frequency, ANSAQ scores, Day of last injury, and Course of disease. Logistic regression showed that scar characteristics were linked to suicide risk but not to symptom severity. ROC analysis revealed limited predictive value of scars for suicide risk or symptoms. These findings suggest that interpreting scar-related information requires more specialized and precise methods.
