Level of involvement of four selected cytochrome P450s (CYPs) in pyrethroid-resistant Anopheles gambiae (s.s.) and Anopheles coluzzii across Côte d’Ivoire

ABSTRACT
In Côte d'Ivoire, there is a high intensity of pyrethroid resistance in malaria vectors which may threaten successful vector control. Molecular studies of the target site mutation in the voltage-gated sodium channel (VGSC) 1014F show that the gene frequencies of these mutations are high, widely spread across Côte d’Ivoire, and even fixed in some areas. To further characterize insecticide resistance in Côte d’Ivoire beyond target site mutations, the metabolic resistance mechanism was explored. Overexpression of the metabolic enzyme cytochrome CYP6M2, CYP6P3, CYP6P4, and CYP6P5 was investigated in Anopheles gambiae (s.s.) and An. coluzzii collected from 30 insecticide resistance monitoring sites across the country in 2021 and 2022. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assay was carried out using RNA-later preserved 60 surviving mosquitoes from each site after phenotypic susceptibility tests. Additionally, a subsample of about 100 mosquitoes from each site was identified to species by PCR, and the frequency of the VGSC 1014F and 1014S was determined. All four CYPs were found to be overexpressed in Côte d’Ivoire with at least one CYP statistically significantly overexpressed in 27 of the 30 sites investigated compared to the susceptible An. gambiae (s.s.) Kisumu (P < 0.005). CYP6M2 was overexpressed in 89% of sites and was the sole overexpressed gene in 10 sites, while the overexpression of CYP6P3 was found in only 10 sites. CYP6P4 and CYP6P5 were overexpressed in 16 and 13 sites, respectively. Furthermore, seven sites (Adzopé, Bongouanou, Daloa, Gagnoa, Guiglo Jacqueville, and Sassandra) had overexpression of all four CYPs. Overall, An. coluzzii showed higher overexpression of CYPs than An. gambiae (s.s.). This study highlights the involvement of selected CYPs in insecticide resistance where target site mutation genes are already present, suggesting that insecticide resistance is complex and multifaceted at a molecular level. Where feasible, it may be helpful to include metabolic resistance surveillance to further characterize insecticide resistance.

Introduction

Insecticide resistance in malaria vectors is primarily caused by two major mechanisms, target site resistance altering the affinity of insecticide binding sites, and metabolic resistance, referring to the activity of enzymes that metabolize (detoxify) or sequester insecticides (Ingham et al., 2014).

Target site resistance includes the voltage-gated sodium channel (VGSC) single amino acid substitutions (1014F, 1014S, and N1575Y) linked to both pyrethroids and dichloro-diphenyl-trichloroethane (DDT) resistance known as knockdown resistance (kdr), and the acetylcholinesterase resistance allele (ace-1) amino acid substitution G119S, that confers resistance to carbamates and organophosphate insecticides. Conversely, metabolic resistance is associated with an increase in insecticide biotransformation, detoxification, and elimination rates, generally caused by elevated activity of members of three large multigene enzyme families of cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (P450s), glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) and carboxylesterases (COEs) that reduce insecticide toxic potency (Khan et al., 2020).

Metabolic resistance associated with increased levels of P450 activity is very common in insecticide resistance and has been associated with major classes of insecticides used for both public health vector and agricultural pest control including pyrethroids, organophosphates, carbamates, organochlorines, and recently neonicotinoids (Puinean et al., 2010; Jones et al., 2011; Zoh et al., 2021). Multiple cytochrome P450s (CYPs) specific genes can be found in different geographical locations in the same or different vector species and can be vector species or insect-specific (Nauen et al., 2022). CYPs that confer insecticide resistance, typically members of the CYP6 and CYP9 families, have been associated with resistance not only in Anopheles spp. mosquitoes but also in Aedes spp. and other pests (Feyereisen, 1999; David et al., 2013; Stevenson et al., 2011; Liu, 2015; Matowo et al., 2022; Nauen et al., 2022). CYPs constitute the largest gene superfamily and are found in a variety of organs and tissues (Feyereisen, 2005). CYP6P3 has been previously associated with pyrethroid resistance in West African An. gambiae (s.l.) (Djouaka et al., 2008). In Cameroon, CYP6P3 and CYP6P4 overexpression was also reported to be associated with DDT resistance in Douala City (Fossog Tene et al., 2013).

While it has been established that the VGSC and ace-1 are widely spread across Côte d’Ivoire (Ahoua Alou et al., 2010; Camara et al., 2018; Fodjo et al., 2018; Zoh et al., 2018; Kouassi et al., 2020; Wolie et al., 2021; Wipf et al., 2022), the extent of metabolic resistance is less well described in the country. Edi and colleagues reported in Tiassalé that CYP6P3, CYP6P5, CYP6P4, and CYP6M2 were highly expressed in Anopheles gambiae (s.l.) mosquitoes resistant to DDT, pyrethroid, and carbamates, suggesting the potential for these genes to act across three insecticide classes (Edi et al., 2012, 2014a). Studies conducted in the same region of Agneby-Tiassa in the South-East of the country by Edi et al. (2012, 2014a) in Tiassalé and Meiwald et al. (2022) in Agboville reported the overexpression of CYP6P4, CYP6P3, and CYP6Z1. More recently Kouamé et al. (2023) reported the overexpression of CYP6P3 gene in An. gambiae (s.l.) from the same locality of Agboville in mosquitoes surviving after WHO susceptibility tests against deltamethrin in comparison to dead and control mosquitoes.

In this study, insecticide resistance monitoring data from 2021 to 2022 were analyzed to determine the levels of CYP6P3, CYP6P5, CYP6P4, and CYP6M2 gene expression in An. gambiae (s.s.) and An. coluzzii samples from the 30 sites in Côte d’Ivoire. The goal of the study was to further characterize the resistance mechanism in An. gambiae (s.l.) from Côte d’Ivoire and explore CYP overexpression as a resistance marker.

Materials and methods

Study sites

The study was conducted within a routine insecticide resistance monitoring data collection across Côte d’Ivoire. The selected study sites were from 27 of the 31 regions that compose the country to ensure the insecticide resistance data was nationally representative. WHO insecticide susceptibility tests

Insecticide susceptibility tests were conducted on 2–5-day-old adult female An. gambiae (s.l.) reared from field-collected larvae from each of the sites between June and September of each year. Multiple larval habitats were sampled for each site, and collected larvae were pooled and reared to adults at a field laboratory that was provided by each district health center. The resistance status of the local vectors against the diagnostic concentrations of pyrethroids [alpha-cypermethrin (0.05%), deltamethrin (0.05%), and permethrin (0.75%)] was determined by exposing the mosquitoes to insecticide-treated papers using standard test procedures defined by the WHO (WHO, 2016). When resistance was confirmed, synergist assays were conducted using 1-h pre-exposure to 4% piperonyl butoxide (PBO) before each pyrethroid to assess the implication of mono-oxygenase enzymes in the vector population’s resistance (WHO, 2016).

Mosquito sample analysis for CYP gene detection

For metabolic gene detection, 60 randomly selected An. gambiae (s.l.) mosquitoes (30 mosquitoes surviving pyrethroid insecticide diagnostic dose exposure and 30 from control tubes from WHO susceptibility testing) from each site were preserved in RNAlater, brought to the laboratory, and stored directly at −80 °C. The overexpression of key detoxification genes (four cytochromes P450s: CYP6M2, CYP6P3, CYP6P4, and CYP6P5) for An. gambiae (s.s.) and An. coluzzii were determined in each site using qRT-PCR. Ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted from 3 biological replicates (pool of 10 specimens) of pyrethroid-resistant, unexposed control mosquitoes and the fully susceptible insectary Kisumu strain of An. gambiae (s.s.). The susceptible insectary mosquitoes were used to assess the relative fold change in the level of expression of each gene. One (1) μg total RNA from each of the three biological replicates from field-resistant, unexposed control, and laboratory-susceptible mosquitoes was used as the template for complementary deoxyribonucleic acid (cDNA) synthesis using Superscript III (Invitrogen, New York, USA) with oligo-dT20 and RNase H, following the manufacturer’s instructions. A serial dilution of cDNA was used to establish standard curves for each gene to assess PCR efficiency and quantitative differences between samples. The qPCR amplification was carried out in a Biorad CFX qPCR system (BioRad Technologies, Singapore, Republic of Singapore) using Brilliant III Ultra-Fast SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix and following a standardized protocol (Kwiatkowska et al., 2013). A total of 10 ng of cDNA from each sample was used as a template in a 3-step program involving a denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min followed by 40 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C and 10 s at 60 °C and a last step of 1 min at 95 °C, 30 s at 55 °C, and 30 s at 95 °C.

Mosquito species identification and voltage-gated sodium channel resistance gene

Between 50 and 100 additional morphologically identified An. gambiae (s.l.) were selected each year among the surviving and dead from the WHO susceptibility testing, from each surveyed site to determine specific members of the species present. The DNA of each mosquito was extracted using the protocol of Collins et al. (1987). Anopheles gambiae complex species were identified as An. coluzzii, An. gambiae (s.s.), or hybrids of both species following the Short-Interspersed Element protocol described by Santolamazza et al. (2008). Furthermore, each mosquito was molecularly analyzed using PCR methods to characterize the VGSC 1014F and 1014S mutations using the Taqman protocol of Bass et al. (2007).

Data analysis

Insecticide resistance status was defined following WHO criteria with mortality after 24 h for pyrethroids < 90% as confirmed resistance, between 90% and < 98% as possible resistance, and ≥ 98% as susceptible. Mortality was corrected using Abbott’s formula when the mortality of the control tubes was above 5% and less than 20% (Abbott, 1987).

The level of gene expression was assessed using the relative fold change measurement which compares the expression of a candidate gene in resistant mosquitoes relative to the level of expression in the susceptible insectary strain (An. gambiae (s.s.) Kisumu). The relative expression and fold change of each target gene were calculated according to the 2-ΔΔCT method (Rao et al., 2013), incorporating PCR efficiency after normalization with the housekeeping genes RSP7 (ribosomal protein S7, AGAP010592) and actin. In practice, output raw CT-values from the real-time PCR machine are normalized based on efficiency correction, and then the average ΔCT value (CT Target – CT Control) was calculated across control genes. A graphic representation of fold change frequency was generated for each locality using Excel® and presented overall, and statistically compared to the susceptible An. gambiae (s.s.) Kisumu strain using the one-way ANOVA test to compare the means of the 2-ΔΔCT for the biological replicates (2-ΔΔCT (fold change)) values. The correlation between species and each CYP overexpression level was estimated for An. coluzzii and An. gambiae (s.s.) by categorizing the highest species percentage identified in each locality. Thus, the dominant species from the molecular species identification (either An. gambiae (s.s.) or An. coluzzii) at a site was considered for the specific species in the analysis. The mean CYP gene overexpression of each gene at all sites and species-specific was calculated, plotted, and compared using the one-sample Wilcoxon test at a 5% significance level P-value. All CYP analyses and graphical representation for species were done using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA, www.graphpad.com).

Characterization of the voltage-gated sodium channel mutations

The voltage-gated sodium channel (VGSC) 1014F was found at all sites at a high frequency except in Tingrela. The frequency recorded in 20 of the 30 sites was above 50%. The mutation was already fixed in Odienné (100%) and almost fixed in Korhogo (98%) and Bouna (96%). The VGSC 1014S was low within the vector populations with the highest frequency recorded in Touba (14%) and Sassandra (10%). CYP expression

At least one CYP out of the tested four CYPs was found to be overexpressed in 27 sites out of the 30 investigated in Côte d’Ivoire. Overall, the CYP genes were statistically significantly overexpressed in resistant An. gambiae (s.s.) and An. coluzzii than the susceptible strain in all sites where they were expressed. However, each gene was overexpressed to a different degree in each site and there were three sites (Man, Yopougon, and Yamoussoukro) where none of the genes were overexpressed compared to the susceptible strain (Supplementary file S3).

All four genes investigated were overexpressed in seven sites: Adzopé, Bongouanou, Daloa, Gagnoa, Guiglo Jacqueville, and Sassandra. CYP6M2 was the only gene statistically significantly overexpressed in ten sites (Beoumi, Bouake, Bouna, Dabakala, Korhogo, Odienné, Nassian, Sakassou, Seguela, and Tingrela) out of the 24 where the gene was found, while CYP6P4 was the only gene found overexpressed in San Pedro. The An. gambiae (s.l.) population of Bouafle (which was mainly An. coluzzii) overexpressed both CYP6M2 and CYP6P4. All other sites recorded three of the four genes (Fig. 7). Furthermore, although CYP6M2 was spread across the country, the gene was mostly overexpressed in the northern sites than the southern. Discussion

Very low levels of mortality were observed in the pyrethroid susceptibility tests across all sites yet the VGSC mutation genes, although at high frequency for some sites, are not universally fixed. This suggests that other mechanisms besides target site resistance are involved. The PBO synergist assay showed a significant increase in mortality at all sites for almost all tested pyrethroids, implying the probable involvement of mono-oxygenases in the pyrethroid resistance observed across the country (Kouassi et al., 2020; Kouamé et al., 2023). This was particularly apparent for Tingrela, Guiglo, Bouafle, and Beoumi. In this study, four genes screened for metabolic resistance (CYP6M2, CYP6P3, CYP6P4, and CYP6P5) were overexpressed in most of the sites surveyed in Côte d’Ivoire, though they were expressed differently according to site and Anopheles species. Of the 30 sites surveyed, only three did not record any CYP overexpression suggesting that the resistance observed and reported in those sites (Kouassi et al., 2020, 2023) may be driven by target site resistance mechanisms, with or without the involvement of other genes such as glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), esterases, or other P450s not evaluated. There was no direct correlation between sites with the highest VGSC frequencies and overexpression of any of the CYPs, nor between the highest increase of mortality using pre-exposure to PBO and overexpression of CYPs, while Gagnoa showed less increase in mortality but still recording overexpression of all four CYPs. The highest VGSC 1014F frequency was recorded on Odienné, but only CYP6M2 was statistically significantly overexpressed at that site and similar to Tingrela where the lowest VGSC 1014F frequency was recorded. High overexpression of all four genes investigated was recorded at seven sites (Adzopé, Bongouanou, Daloa, Gagnoa, Guiglo Jacqueville, and Sassandra) in addition to variable frequencies of VGSC 1014F observed (between 23.0% in Guiglo and 74.0% in Jacqueville). Data thus far suggest that both metabolic resistance and target site resistance are contributing to the high level of pyrethroid resistance observed.

CYP6M2, which was the main gene overexpressed at 24 of the sites, is known to induce a cross-resistance between different classes of insecticides that include pyrethroids, organochlorines, and carbamates, and contributes to resistance in addition to VGSC and ace-1 target site resistance mechanisms (Djouaka et al., 2008; Fotso-Toguem et al., 2022). CYP6P3 was overexpressed in ten sites and CYP6P4 in the largest number of sites (16) after CYP6M2, and CYP6P5 in 13 sites. All these CYPs were reported to be involved in pyrethroid resistance by metabolizing alpha-cypermethrin, deltamethrin, and permethrin (Djouaka et al., 2008; Yunta et al., 2019).

Anopheles gambiae (s.s.) and An. coluzzii were the main vectors found at the sites. Anopheles coluzzii was identified in 29 of the 30 sites surveyed and represented the only vector in five sites while An. gambiae (s.s.) was recorded in 25 sites and mostly in sympatry with An. coluzzii. Across all sites, Anopheles coluzzii overexpressed all four genes investigated more than An. gambiae (s.s.), though CYP6P5 was found to be overexpressed similarly by both species. High pyrethroid resistance and target site resistance mechanism VGSC L1014F mutation are almost universal across Côte d’Ivoire (Edi et al., 2012, 2014b; Camara et al., 2018; Kouassi et al., 2020) and have long threatened the vector control tools and efforts carried out by the country’s National Malaria Control Programme. Metabolic resistance of the main vectors to other and new classes of insecticides used in public health further limits the choice of strategies and control tools to fight the vectors in the country.

Our results support the need for continued investigation and consideration of the resistance gene profiles to guide specific and targeted vector control strategies, which may require a combination of tools to overcome the multiple resistance mechanisms. It is known that the more resistance mechanisms are present in a vector population, the more difficult it is to control (Ranson and Lissenden, 2016; Lindsay et al., 2021). The vector control tools currently used to overcome pyrethroid-resistant vector populations, such as either dual insecticide ingredient or PBO insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and new insecticides for indoor residual spraying (neonicotinoid or broflanilide-based insecticides), may be jeopardized by multiple resistance mechanisms, and especially by metabolic resistance. As PBO improves pyrethroid potency by inhibiting the action of the P450 enzymes in pyrethroid-resistant mosquitoes, PBO-ITN deployment is supported by the evidence of P450 overexpression (Corbel et al., 2010; Dadzie et al., 2017). Conversely, dual-active ingredient (AI) ITNs that include chlorfenapyr require mosquito activation of cytochrome P450 monooxygenase enzymes to convert the inactive ingredient to its active form (Ngufor et al., 2016, 2017). In both scenarios, the impact of P450s such as those investigated could be an additional key indicator for ITN selection, particularly for PBO-ITNs.

In Côte d’Ivoire, the NMCP is moving forward with the stratified mass distribution of dual active ingredient ITNs and PBO ITNs alongside standard ITNs guided by the resistance intensity and PBO synergist assay data for An. gambiae (s.l.) in each district (Kouassi et al., 2023). Therefore, the present data may contribute additional information to improve the national insecticide management plan and guide PBO ITN purchase and district-targeted distribution during future ITN procurements for mass and routine distributions.

This study has a few limitations that may overestimate the role these four CYPs play in conferring insecticide resistance. While we only focused on four CYPs, the family of metabolic enzymes includes a range of genes such as GSTs and esterases in addition to the large family of CYPs. Focusing only on previously identified CYPs may downplay the involvement of other CYP genes. Also, there was a decreased sample size that comes with pooling as well as using other tested mosquitoes to determine species composition instead of the CYP-tested samples. However, this study is still useful for resistance characterization. Furthermore, insecticide resistance is multifaceted, and this study only focused on oxidases. Other insecticide resistance mechanisms may also be important to consider. In future studies, it may be useful to investigate resistance pathways to determine how different mechanisms may be working in concert to confer resistance rather than looking at any single resistance mechanism alone.

Conclusions

The detection of CYPs in areas where target site mutation genes are already present showed that additional genes linked to vector metabolic resistance are also evolving in Côte d’Ivoire. Routine surveillance of additional CYPs could contribute to the understanding of any emerging resistance to new insecticides across the country that cannot be explained by target site mutation frequencies as there are several gene modifications at different locus that the vector populations are expressing. Monitoring CYPs that could confer chlorfenapyr resistance is especially important in the context of the wide distribution of IG2 nets to control pyrethroid-resistant vectors. Detection of increasing overexpression of CYPs could be considered for inclusion in insecticide resistance management plans to guide allocation of ITN types considering the level of overexpression of the CYPs that could affect the effectiveness of PBO and dual active ITNs. This study provides information on CYPs and VGSC but including other genes such as GSTs and esterases could provide additional information to the resistance profile of vector populations across the country. Moving forward, as new vector control strategies with or without pyrethroid are put in place, tracking efforts should place greater emphasis on metabolic resistance genes over VGSC mechanisms that are widely spread in the vector population to support vector control decision-making.
