Non-coding RNAs in Parkinson's Disease: Pathogenesis, Exosomes, and Therapeutic Horizons
Authors: Niloufar Rezaei, Maryam Zivari Fard, Mansure Kazemi, Behrang Alani, Mahdi Noureddini, Mahdi Rafiyan, Ashkan Bahrami, Mohammad Sepehr Yazdani, Reza Eshraghi, Tahereh Mazoochi, Hamed Mirzaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the loss of dopaminergic neurons and the accumulation of α-synuclein. Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs)—including microRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, and circular RNAs—have emerged as critical regulators in PD pathogenesis by modulating pathways such as neuroinflammation, mitochondrial function, and protein clearance. Furthermore, exosomal ncRNAs facilitate intercellular communication, propagating pathological signals but also offering therapeutic potential. This review synthesizes the current understanding of ncRNA involvement in PD, structuring the analysis around key pathogenic mechanisms. We provide a critical perspective on the strengths and weaknesses of the current evidence, evaluate the major challenges facing the field—including biomarker validation and therapeutic delivery—and propose a path forward for future research. A deeper, more integrated understanding of these ncRNA networks is essential for developing novel diagnostics and treatments to halt the progression of PD.Keywords: Parkinson's disease, ncRNAs, lncRNA, Exosome, miRNA, cirRNA

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a complex, progressive neurodegenerative disorder, first described by Parkinson (1969). It is the second most common neurodegenerative condition worldwide, surpassed only by Alzheimer's disease (AD; Mhyre et al. 2012; Ou et al. 2021; Bloem et al. 2021). Epidemiological studies indicate a rising incidence of PD over the past decade, driven by factors such as an aging population and lifestyle changes (Ben-Shlomo et al. 2024). This condition profoundly diminishes the quality of life for both patients and their caregivers and places a substantial burden on society. The current absence of curative treatments necessitates pioneering research into innovative therapeutic options (Li et al. 2020c).

The pathological hallmarks of PD are the progressive loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra (SN) and the presence of cytoplasmic inclusions known as Lewy bodies, which are primarily composed of aggregated α-synuclein protein. Although dopaminergic cell death is central to the motor symptoms of PD, the pathology extends to many other regions of the nervous system, contributing to a wide range of non-motor symptoms (Simon et al. 2020). Despite decades of research, the precise molecular mechanisms that initiate and drive the progression of PD remain incompletely understood.

Recent advances in genomics have revealed that the vast majority of the human genome is transcribed into non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), molecules that are not translated into proteins (Consortium et al. 2007; Consortium 2004; Kapranov et al. 2007). This discovery has triggered a paradigm shift, transforming our view of RNA from a simple intermediary in protein synthesis to a critical operational molecule with diverse regulatory functions in gene expression and genome organization (Nemeth et al. 2024; Khan et al. 2022). While the earliest ncRNAs like tRNAs and rRNAs were identified in the 1950s, the field exploded with the discovery of microRNAs (miRNAs) in the early 2000s, which highlighted their profound role in post-transcriptional gene regulation. The main classes of ncRNAs implicated in PD, which are the focus of this review, include miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs.

The regulatory networks of ncRNAs extend beyond the single cell through their transport within extracellular vesicles, particularly exosomes. Exosomes are small (30–150 nm) membrane-bound vesicles of endocytic origin that are secreted by nearly all cell types (Yang, Zhang et al. 2020a, b, c, d). They are formed within multivesicular bodies (MVBs) and are released into the extracellular space when MVBs fuse with the plasma membrane. Crucially, exosomes carry a cargo of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids—including miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs—derived from their cell of origin (Marima et al. 2024; Ye et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2020a). By delivering this cargo to recipient cells, exosomes act as key mediators of intercellular communication, influencing the physiology and pathology of surrounding cells, including neurons and glia (Tielking et al. 2019; Huo et al. 2021; Pascual et al. 2020).

Given the complex etiopathogenesis of PD, ncRNAs and their exosomal transport represent a critical and rapidly evolving area of research. Dysregulation of specific ncRNAs has been shown to directly impact core PD pathologies, suggesting they may serve as valuable diagnostic biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets (Zhang et al. 2022c). This review will synthesize the current evidence for the roles of miRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and exosomal ncRNAs in PD. To move beyond a simple enumeration of findings, we will structure our analysis around key pathogenic mechanisms, including α-synuclein regulation, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Furthermore, we will provide a critical perspective on the state of the field, highlighting robust findings, existing controversies, and the major challenges that must be overcome to translate this research into clinical practice.

The ncRNA Landscape in Parkinson's Disease: An Overview

Among the most studied ncRNAs are microRNAs (miRNAs), which are short, single-stranded RNAs (~ 22 nucleotides) that primarily function by binding to the 3′-untranslated region (3′-UTR) of target messenger RNAs (mRNAs), leading to their degradation or translational repression (Elangovan et al. 2023; Singh and Khatri 2024; Uppsala et al. 2023). The biogenesis of miRNAs is a multi-step process beginning in the nucleus, where a primary miRNA (pri-miRNA) transcript is cleaved by the Drosha-DGCR8 complex into a precursor hairpin (pre-miRNA). After exportation to the cytoplasm, the Dicer enzyme processes the pre-miRNA into a mature miRNA duplex, which is then loaded into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) to execute its gene-silencing function. Beyond this canonical role, miRNAs are involved in a wide array of biological processes, including immune regulation and development (Leitao and Enguita 2022).

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a diverse class of transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that lack significant protein-coding potential (Chodurska and Kunej 2025). Although often transcribed and spliced similarly to mRNAs, lncRNAs fold into complex three-dimensional structures that enable them to perform a wide range of regulatory functions. They can act in cis to regulate the transcription of nearby genes or in trans by interacting with DNA, proteins, or other RNA molecules. Their mechanisms of action are varied, including acting as molecular sponges for miRNAs, serving as scaffolds for protein complexes, or guiding chromatin-modifying enzymes to specific genomic loci (Chen and Kim 2024; Mattick et al. 2023).

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are a unique class of ncRNAs characterized by a covalently closed-loop structure, formed by a “back-splicing” event where a downstream 5′ splice site joins with an upstream 3′ splice site. This circular conformation renders them highly resistant to exonuclease-mediated degradation, resulting in greater stability compared to their linear RNA counterparts (Doxakis 2022). CircRNAs can exert their biological functions through several mechanisms, most notably by acting as ‘sponges’ for miRNAs, thereby sequestering them and preventing them from binding to their mRNA targets. They can also regulate transcription and interact with RNA-binding proteins (Ma et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2024).

Core Pathogenic Mechanisms Modulated by ncRNAs

Regulation of α-Synuclein and Protein Clearance

Alterations in the gene encoding α-synuclein (SNCA) are a primary genetic factor in PD, and even modest increases in its expression can drive aggregation and toxicity. Consequently, miRNAs that regulate SNCA expression are of significant interest. Several miRNAs, including miR-7 and miR-153, have been predicted and validated to bind directly to the 3′-UTR of SNCA mRNA, thereby reducing its translation. The diagnostic potential of such miRNAs has been explored in patient biofluids. For instance, reduced levels of SNCA-targeting miRNAs, specifically miR-153 and miR-223, were found in the saliva and plasma of PD patients (Cressatti et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2022a). In the study by Wu et al., plasma miR-223 levels demonstrated a remarkable 86.2% accuracy in distinguishing PD patients from healthy controls, and miR-153 levels correlated with disease duration and motor symptom severity (Wu et al. 2022a). Further supporting this Zhang et al., confirmed that another brain-enriched miRNA, miR-433, was significantly downregulated in the substantia nigra of PD patients, showing a direct inverse correlation with SNCA mRNA levels (Zhang et al. 2017). Autophagy is a critical cellular degradation process responsible for clearing damaged organelles and aggregation-prone proteins like α-synuclein. Its impairment is a known hallmark of PD. Several miRNAs have been shown to disrupt this process. Dong et al. demonstrated that miR-214-3p, which is upregulated in PD models, directly targets and inhibits ATG3, a key gene required for autophagosome elongation. This inhibition promoted dopaminergic neuron death, a process that could be rescued by restoring ATG3 levels (Dong et al. 2024). The link between autophagy and neuroinflammation is particularly crucial. For instance, Lv et al. found that miR-3473b was upregulated in microglia in response to inflammatory stimuli. This miRNA was shown to suppress autophagy by indirectly regulating ULK1, a critical autophagy initiation kinase, via its direct target TREM2, a microglial receptor implicated in PD (Lv et al. 2021b). These studies highlight how miRNA dysregulation can cripple the cell's ability to manage toxic protein buildup. Together, these studies illustrate a clear principle: specific miRNAs, such as miR-214-3p and miR-3473b, can directly compromise neuronal health by targeting key components of the autophagy machinery, including essential proteins like ATG3 and ULK1. One of the most widely studied functions of lncRNAs in PD is their role as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) or ‘miRNA sponges,’ whereby they bind to and sequester miRNAs, preventing them from acting on their target mRNAs. For example, Liu et al. (2017a, b) elucidated the pathogenic role of the lncRNA MALAT1 using a classic in vitro PD model of MPP+-treated SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells. They first established that knocking down MALAT1 protected these cells from apoptosis. Critically, they then confirmed the direct 'sponge' mechanism using a dual-luciferase reporter assay, which showed a direct binding interaction between MALAT1 and the neuroprotective miR-124. This sponging action was shown to promote dopaminergic cell death (Liu et al. 2017a). Similarly, the lncRNA NEAT1—also upregulated in PD—was shown to exert its neurotoxic effects through the NEAT1/miR-212-3p/AXIN1 axis, leading to increased apoptosis and inflammation (Liu et al. 2021). Other complex ceRNA networks have been identified, such as the lncRNA SNHG8, which is downregulated in PD. Overexpression of SNHG8 was protective, an effect mediated by its sponging of miR-421-3p, which in turn increased the expression of its target, the sorting nexin SNX8, thereby mitigating mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum stress (Zhou et al. 2023a).

The regulation of cell survival and death pathways is critical in PD, and lncRNAs are deeply involved in this balance. The lncRNA BDNF-AS, an antisense transcript to the neurotrophic factor BDNF, was found to be upregulated in PD models. Its inhibition protected against dopaminergic cell loss by reducing both apoptosis and excessive autophagy. This effect was mediated through its sponging of miR-125b-5p, demonstrating a direct link between a lncRNA and core cell survival pathways (Fan et al. 2020). Conversely, some lncRNAs play a protective role. The lncRNA NORAD, which was downregulated in cellular models of PD, was shown to be neuroprotective. Overexpression of NORAD rescued cells from MPP+-induced apoptosis by reducing caspase 3/7 activity and decreasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, suggesting it is a vital component of the cellular stress response (Song et al. 2019).

Together, these findings illustrate a key principle of PD pathogenesis: the cellular machinery for protein quality control is under intense, multi-layered regulation by the non-coding genome. While miRNAs often act as direct post-transcriptional repressors of key autophagy components or SNCA itself, lncRNAs can function as competing endogenous RNAs that modulate the activity of these same miRNAs. Recent studies continue to highlight the central role of miRNAs in pathways essential for neuronal homeostasis, with their dysregulation being a key feature in synucleinopathies (Ala 2020). This complex interplay, where ncRNA networks are deeply integrated into the circuits controlling protein aggregation, represents a critical area for future therapeutic development (Zhang et al. 2019c).

The most frequently documented function of circRNAs in PD is their role as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), where they sequester miRNAs and thus regulate the expression of the miRNA's target genes. A clear neuroprotective example is circDLGAP4, which was found to be downregulated in PD models. Feng et al. (2020) demonstrated its therapeutic potential through a series of mechanistic experiments. In vitro, overexpressing circDLGAP4 in MPP+-treated neurons enhanced cell viability (as measured by MTT assay) and reduced levels of the apoptosis marker cleaved caspase-3. They confirmed this effect was mediated by sponging miR-134-5p, which subsequently led to increased protein levels of its target, the transcription factor CREB, and its pro-survival downstream effectors (Feng et al. 2020). Conversely, other circRNAs contribute to pathology. circSAMD4A, which is upregulated in PD models, was shown to promote dopaminergic neuron death and autophagy by sponging miR-29c-3p and thereby modulating the AMPK/mTOR pathway (Wang et al. 2021g). The circ-Pank1/miR-7a-5p axis provides another direct link to core PD pathology, where an increase in circ-Pank1 sequesters miR-7a-5p, leading to elevated levels of α-synuclein (Liu et al. 2022b). This sponge mechanism is not limited to mammalian models; an early study in Caenorhabditis elegans showed that circzip-2 contributes to α-synuclein aggregation by sequestering miR-60 (Kumar et al. 2018).

Mitochondrial Dysfunction and Oxidative Stress

Mitochondrial failure and the resulting oxidative stress are central events in the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons. The PINK1-PRKN pathway, which governs the selective removal of damaged mitochondria via autophagy (mitophagy), is often impaired in both familial and sporadic PD. Markham et al. identified the miR-29 family as a potent inhibitor of this protective pathway. They found that miR-29 directly targets ATG9A, a gene essential for initiating mitophagy, and that EOPD-associated variants of ATG9A failed to rescue the defects caused by miR-29 (Markham et al. 2024). Beyond mitophagy, miRNAs also regulate core mitochondrial biogenesis and function. Baghi et al. showed that miR-376a levels are altered in PD models and patient PBMCs. This miRNA targets key mitochondrial regulators PGC1α and TFAM, and its dysregulation was linked to mitochondrial deficits and disease severity (Baghi et al. 2020). Furthermore, miRNAs can influence other major PD-related kinases tied to mitochondrial health, such as LRRK2. The study by Rahimmi et al. showed that miR-185, which is downregulated in PD models, can protect neurons by regulating the LRRK2 pathway (Rahimmi et al. 2019).

This connection is further supported by findings that link specific miRNA profiles in the substantia nigra to the mitochondrial deficits that are a hallmark of PD pathology (Guedes et al. 2023; Tryphena et al. 2023a).

Circular RNAs also play a key role in mitochondrial quality control. Beyond simply acting as sponges, circRNAs are integrated into larger networks that control fundamental cellular processes crucial for neuronal survival. A prime example is circEPS15, which was found to be downregulated in the plasma of PD patients and correlated with motor symptom severity. Zhou et al. demonstrated that circEPS15 exerts a neuroprotective effect by sponging miR-24-3p, which subsequently stabilizes the expression of PINK1. This action enhances PINK1-PRKN-mediated mitophagy, facilitating the clearance of damaged mitochondria and preserving neuronal health (Zhou et al. 2023b). This study elegantly connects a circRNA to a core pathological mechanism in PD. Other circRNAs contribute to neurotoxicity by dysregulating key signaling hubs. For example, circ_0054220 was found to be upregulated in PD and functions by sponging miR-625 and miR-145, leading to an increase in their shared target, the High Mobility Group A1 (HMGA1) protein, which subsequently promotes neuronal dysfunction (Zhong et al. 2024). Neuroinflammation

Chronic activation of microglia and astrocytes creates a toxic, inflammatory environment that accelerates neuronal death in PD. MiRNAs are potent modulators of these inflammatory cascades. A compelling example comes from Wang et al., who provided compelling in vivo evidence for the neuroprotective role of miR-218-5p. Using an MPTP-induced mouse model of PD, they delivered miR-218-5p directly to the substantia nigra via an adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector. This intervention not only preserved dopaminergic neurons, as quantified by tyrosine hydroxylase (TH+) cell counts, but also markedly suppressed microglial activation. Mechanistically, they demonstrated that miR-218-5p directly targeted Ddx41, a sensor that triggers the pro-inflammatory type-I interferon (IFN-I) signaling pathway. By inhibiting Ddx41, miR-218-5p effectively dampened this entire inflammatory cascade, providing a clear mechanistic link between a single miRNA and a key neuroinflammatory pathway in PD (Wang et al. 2024). This work provides a clear mechanistic link between a single miRNA and a key neuroinflammatory pathway in PD.

Neuroinflammation driven by activated microglia is a key contributor to the progressive nature of PD. Several lncRNAs have been identified as critical regulators of these inflammatory pathways. Ma et al. investigated the lncRNA RMST, which was upregulated in the substantia nigra of PD rats. They demonstrated that inhibiting RMST reduced microglial and astrocyte activation, suppressed oxidative stress, and protected dopaminergic neurons, likely by modulating the Toll-like receptor (TLR)/NF-κB signaling pathway (Ma et al. 2021a). The lncRNA MEG8, which is downregulated in the blood of PD patients, also plays a role in neuroinflammation. MEG8 was shown to act via the miR-485-3p/FBXO45 axis, and its overexpression in microglial cells suppressed the production of inflammatory mediators (Lin et al. 2024). These examples, focusing on named lncRNAs like RMST and MEG8 (and even the well-known HOTAIR), demonstrate that these molecules can act as critical hubs in neuroinflammatory pathways, often by modulating master regulatory cascades such as the TLR/NF-κB axis (Fig. 4). In addition to miRNAs, lncRNAs have emerged as powerful regulators of the inflammatory state in glial cells, often acting as critical hubs in signaling pathways that respond to pathogenic stimuli.

Exosomal ncRNAs: Mediators of Intercellular Communication

Exosomes are small (30–150 nm), membrane-bound vesicles that function as natural nanocarriers for intercellular communication. Formed within multivesicular bodies and released by nearly all cell types, they carry a cargo of lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids derived from their parent cell (Gurunathan et al. 2019). Crucially, this cargo includes a diverse array of ncRNAs (Fig. 5). By traveling through biofluids and being taken up by recipient cells, exosomes can deliver these functional ncRNAs to distant sites, influencing the physiology and pathology of the surrounding microenvironment (Sun et al. 2018b; Yang et al. 2022a; Li et al. 2021a). In the context of PD, exosomes play a fascinating dual role: they can act as vehicles for the propagation of pathogenic factors, but they also hold immense promise as a source of biomarkers and a novel platform for therapeutic delivery. Exosomes as Vehicles for Pathogenic ncRNAs and Protein Aggregates

A growing body of evidence suggests that exosomes are key players in the “prion-like” spread of α-synuclein pathology between neurons (Xia et al. 2019b; Howitt and Hill 2016; Mavroeidi et al. 2022). Beyond simply transporting the toxic protein itself, exosomes also carry pathogenic ncRNAs that can amplify the damage in recipient cells. A clear example was provided by Zhou et al., who found that exosomes released from cells overexpressing α-synuclein were enriched with miR-19a-3p. When these exosomes were taken up by microglia, the transferred miR-19a-3p inhibited the PTEN/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, leading to impaired autophagy in the recipient microglia (Zhou et al. 2019). This demonstrates a mechanism where exosomes not only spread the toxic protein but also deliver a specific ncRNA that cripples the recipient cell's ability to clear it (Singh et al. 2024; Kadbhane et al. 2021; Tryphena et al. 2023b).

Exosomes as a Source of Disease Biomarkers

Because exosomes can cross the blood–brain barrier and carry cargo from the CNS into the peripheral circulation, their contents are a highly attractive source for liquid biopsy biomarkers. Jiang et al. found that serum-derived exosomes from PD mice contained elevated levels of miR-137. They showed that these exosomes could deliver miR-137 to healthy neurons, where it suppressed its target OXR1 and induced oxidative stress (Jiang et al. 2019). This finding connects a measurable exosomal miRNA in the blood to a direct pathogenic mechanism. Human studies have identified similar biomarker candidates, including elevated hsa-miR-4639-5p (which targets the PD-related gene DJ-1) and lnc-MKRN2-42:1 in the plasma exosomes of PD patients (Wang et al. 2020b; He et al. 2023a).

Exosomes in Neuroprotection and Therapeutic Strategies

In contrast to their pathogenic role, exosomes can also be vehicles for neuroprotective signals. Shakespeare et al. discovered that exosomes derived from healthy astrocytes (ADEXs) were able to protect dopaminergic neurons from MPP+-induced cell death. They identified that this protective effect was mediated by the exosomal transfer of miR-200a-3p, which suppressed the pro-apoptotic kinase MKK4 in recipient neurons (Shakespear et al. 2020).

This natural therapeutic potential is being actively harnessed using mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). MSC-derived exosomes are packed with anti-inflammatory and pro-survival molecules, including protective ncRNAs, and are being explored as a cell-free therapy for PD. For example, He et al. demonstrated that exosomes from MSCs overexpressing miR-100a-5p could protect neurons from oxidative stress by modulating the Nox4/ROS/Nrf2 signaling pathway (He et al. 2023b). This approach offers a potential strategy for delivering therapeutic ncRNAs to the brain using a natural, well-tolerated delivery system. Translational Implications

From a diagnostic standpoint, transitioning a promising miRNA biomarker from the lab to the clinic requires overcoming major hurdles. These include developing universally standardized protocols for sample collection and quantification (RT-qPCR vs. sequencing), establishing definitive diagnostic cutoff values with high sensitivity and specificity, and validating that the biomarker can distinguish PD from other neurodegenerative disorders like Multiple System Atrophy (MSA) or Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB).

From a therapeutic perspective, the primary obstacles are delivery and specificity. Systemically administered RNA therapeutics must be engineered to cross the blood–brain barrier and enter specific cell types (e.g., dopaminergic neurons or microglia) (Shirmast et al. 2024; Jiao et al. 2024). Furthermore, because a single miRNA can influence hundreds of mRNA targets, modulating its activity carries a high risk of unintended off-target effects (Diener et al. 2022; Seok et al. 2018). These challenges in translation, however, are not unique to miRNAs and are a recurring theme in the study of other ncRNA classes, such as lncRNAs.

The exceptional stability of circRNAs in biofluids makes them highly attractive biomarker candidates for PD. Unlike linear mRNAs, which are rapidly degraded, the closed-loop structure of circRNAs allows them to persist in blood, CSF, and urine, potentially providing a more reliable and non-invasive snapshot of the disease state in the brain. The negative correlation of circEPS15 with motor severity is a promising step in this direction (Zhou et al. 2023b).

ncRNA-Based Therapeutic Strategies

The unique ability of ncRNAs to fine-tune entire gene networks makes them attractive therapeutic targets. Several strategies are being explored to harness their potential, drawing lessons from advances in other neurological diseases.

Therapeutic Modalities: The primary approaches involve either restoring beneficial ncRNAs or inhibiting detrimental ones. For downregulated protective miRNAs, synthetic miRNA mimics can be introduced to replenish cellular levels. Conversely, for upregulated pathogenic ncRNAs, several inhibitory strategies exist. These include antagomirs (chemically modified inhibitors that block miRNA function) and, most prominently, Antisense Oligonucleotides (ASOs). ASOs are designed to bind specifically to a target lncRNA or miRNA, leading to its degradation via RNase H or sterically blocking its function (Ling et al. 2013; Seyhan 2024; Ratti et al. 2020).

The Delivery Challenge the single greatest hurdle for all ncRNA therapeutics in PD is delivery to the brain. Any systemic therapy must cross the highly restrictive blood–brain barrier (BBB). Furthermore, once in the CNS, the therapeutic must reach the correct cells (e.g., dopaminergic neurons) and avoid widespread off-target effects, a significant concern given that one miRNA can influence hundreds of genes. Finally, naked RNA molecules are quickly degraded in the body, requiring chemical modifications or encapsulation in delivery vehicles like lipid nanoparticles to ensure stability (Sadat Razavi et al. 2025; Saadh et al. 2025; Li et al. 2022b).

Clinical Landscape and Comparative Perspective currently, there are no ncRNA-based drugs in clinical trials specifically for Parkinson's disease, highlighting the nascent stage of this field. However, the path has been paved by success in other neurodegenerative disorders. The approval of the ASO drug Spinraza (Nusinersen) for spinal muscular atrophy proved that RNA-targeted drugs can be effective for neurological conditions. Compared to other emerging PD therapies like gene therapy (which often replaces a single gene) or immunotherapy (which targets a specific protein like α-synuclein), ncRNA strategies offer the potential to modulate an entire pathogenic pathway (e.g., neuroinflammation) back toward homeostasis, representing a fundamentally different therapeutic paradigm (Shahid 2025; Mansour and El-Khatib 2025; McCartan et al. 2023).

Challenges and Future Directions

The explosion of research into non-coding RNAs has fundamentally reshaped our understanding of the molecular landscape of Parkinson's disease. As this review has synthesized, different classes of ncRNAs converge to regulate core pathological pathways, including α-synuclein proteostasis, mitochondrial quality control, and neuroinflammation. However, translating these complex findings into tangible clinical benefits requires overcoming significant hurdles that span from basic mechanistic understanding to clinical application.

A foundational challenge is the lack of truly translational models. Many findings originate from toxin-based in vitro or acute rodent models that fail to fully recapitulate the slow, progressive, and multifactorial nature of sporadic human PD. This contributes to a lack of consistent findings across studies and complicates the validation of therapeutic targets. The challenge of mechanistic validation is particularly acute for lncRNAs and circRNAs. While the ‘miRNA sponge’ or ‘ceRNA’ hypothesis is widely reported, its physiological relevance is often debated due to stoichiometric requirements (Denzler et al. 2014). Rigorous validation using techniques like RIP-seq and ChIRP-seq is needed to uncover their true functions, which may be more complex than simple sponging.

These basic science challenges extend directly to the development of ncRNAs as clinical tools. For biomarkers, the path to the clinic is fraught with obstacles. Key issues include specificity (distinguishing PD from other synucleinopathies), the lack of standardized protocols for sample processing and quantification, and the difficulty in confirming the biological origin of ncRNAs found in peripheral biofluids (Vijiaratnam and Foltynie 2023). A signal from the blood does not guarantee a signal from the brain.

For ncRNA-based therapeutics, the practical hurdles are immense. The single greatest obstacle remains delivery across the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Furthermore, ensuring cell-type specificity and avoiding widespread off-target effects are paramount for safety (Winkle et al. 2021; Poller et al. 2023). This is especially true for miRNAs, which can regulate hundreds of genes simultaneously.

To navigate this complex landscape, a strategic path forward is required. The field must evolve from discovery-focused studies to a new phase of deep mechanistic validation using more clinically relevant platforms, such as patient-derived iPSC models. Embracing a multi-omics approach—integrating ncRNA data with proteomics, metabolomics, and genomics—will be essential to build a complete picture of the disrupted molecular networks. Finally, harnessing natural delivery systems like exosomes represents a promising frontier (Salido-Guadarrama et al. 2023; Rotroff and Motsinger-Reif 2016). Engineering MSC-derived exosomes to carry a specific cargo of therapeutic ncRNAs offers a potential solution to the critical delivery challenge, although significant hurdles in purification, loading, and large-scale manufacturing must first be overcome (Chavda et al. 2023).

Shared ncRNA Signatures Across Neurodegenerative Diseases

While this review has focused on Parkinson's disease, it is crucial to recognize that many of the underlying pathological processes are not unique to PD. Core cellular deficits including impaired protein clearance, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and chronic neuroinflammation are common features across a spectrum of neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Huntington's disease (HD). Consequently, it is not surprising that many of the ncRNAs that regulate these fundamental stress pathways are also dysregulated across these different conditions.

For example, miR-155 is a well-established master regulator of inflammation, and its upregulation in microglia is a common pathological signature found not only in PD models but also in AD and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS; Zingale et al. 2021; Asl et al. 2025; Song and Lee 2015). Similarly, the stress-response lncRNA NEAT1 is upregulated in multiple neurodegenerative contexts, suggesting it is part of a generalized cellular response to neuronal injury (Wu et al. 2022b; Esmaeili et al. 2025). Even ncRNAs that target disease-specific proteins can have overlapping functional consequences. For example, miR-7 targets SCNA in PD, while the lncRNA BACE1-AS regulates the production of amyloid-beta in AD (Li et al. 2019; Titze-de-Almeida and Titze-de-Almeida 2018; Shaheen et al. 2024). Although the primary targets are different, both ncRNAs modulate the core pathogenic process of toxic protein accumulation.

This overlap has critical implications for the future of ncRNA-based medicine. From a diagnostic perspective, it highlights a major challenge: a biomarker that reflects a general process like neuroinflammation (e.g., miR-155) may lack the specificity required to differentiate PD from AD. This underscores the need for diagnostic panels that combine both disease-specific ncRNAs (like those targeting SCNA) and pathway-specific ncRNAs to achieve a precise molecular diagnosis. Conversely, from a therapeutic standpoint, this overlap presents an opportunity. An ncRNA-based therapeutic designed to quell neuroinflammation or boost mitochondrial health could potentially serve as a “pan-neurodegenerative” treatment, offering benefits across multiple diseases that share these downstream pathologies.

Conclusion

Non-coding RNAs are undeniably integral players in the pathogenesis of Parkinson's disease, orchestrating core processes from α-synuclein proteostasis and mitochondrial function to neuroinflammation. Their dysregulation offers a rich source of potential biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets that could revolutionize how we diagnose and treat this devastating disorder. While significant hurdles in clinical translation—particularly concerning validation, delivery, and specificity—remain, an integrated, systems-level approach holds the potential to finally unlock the power of ncRNAs. A future where ncRNA-based diagnostics and precisely targeted exosomal therapies can halt the progression of Parkinson's disease is no longer a distant dream, but an achievable goal for the next generation of research.
