Cancer immunotherapy by adenosinergic CD39 and CD73 as emerging immune checkpoints
Authors: Xinyu Yin, Ying Yang, Songjiang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracellular adenosine has recently been identified as an immunological checkpoint mediator that hinders the immune response to malignancies. The production of adenosine is facilitated by the enzymatic activity of CD39 and CD73. There is an increasing body of evidence suggesting that CD39 and CD73, which are considered to be emerging immunological checkpoints, can transform adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-mediated pro-inflammatory tumor microenvironment (TME) into an adenosine-mediated immunosuppressive through purinergic pathway. The aim of this study was to review macrophages-based cancer immunotherapy by adenosinergic CD39 and CD73 as emerging immune checkpoints. We searched the PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Medline (Ovid) databases to find relevant studies between December 30, 2010, and December 30, 2024. The search strategy encompassed utilization of Cancer, Immune checkpoints, CD39, CD73, Immunotherapy as keywords. The case reports, reviews, evaluations, originals, and earlier meta-analyses were all included in the study. The inclusion criteria were the researches that investigated the functions of the adenosinergic immunological checkpoints CD39 and CD73 in cancer immunotherapy. Clinical studies have examined small molecule inhibitors or monoclonal antibodies that target the CD39/CD73 pathway in recent years. Cancer immunotherapy might profit from therapeutic inhibition of CD39 and CD73’s enzymatic activity, given that these two proteins are crucial for the conversion of ATP into adenosine. Multiple types of monoclonal antibodies have been developed and shown to inhibit CD39 or CD73. One benefit of using monoclonal antibodies to block CD39 and CD73 is that antibody medications usually have lengthy serum half-lives. Inhibiting CD39 and CD73 may contribute to limiting the progression of tumors and boost anti-tumor responses. Currently, one of the most promising strategies in immuno-oncology is to target CD39 and CD73 due to their extensive immune regulatory effects on tumor immunity.Keywords: Cancer, Immune checkpoints, CD39, CD73, Immunotherapy

Introduction

The notion of immunological homeostasis pertains to the delicate balance between the activation and inhibition of the immune system. Immunotherapy by monoclonal antibodies is designed to effectively identify and eliminate infections during an illness while minimizing exaggerated and incorrect immune responses that could potentially harm the body’s tissues. A growing body of academic literature indicates a strong correlation between the disruption of immunological balance and the majority of cancer cases [1]. The principal function of immunological checkpoints is their ability to govern tissue damage during the innate immune system’s physiological response to combat pathogenic infections, primarily by modulating the inflammatory response. The immune checkpoint pathways have considerable activity within cancer cells, hence enabling the evasion of immunological responses by malignant tumors [2]. The immune system utilizes various immunological checkpoint molecules comprising ligand-receptor pairs. These chemical substances have the capacity to either impede or enhance immune responses. These compounds are broadly distributed across several cell types, encompassing immune cells, antigen-presenting cells, tumor cells, and other cellular entities. As a result, they assume a pivotal function in controlling adaptive immune system development, specifically T cells and innate immune cells. An expanding corpus of scholarly literature has revealed an increasingly diverse set of immunological checkpoints, including but not limited to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3), T cell immunoglobulin domain and mucin domain-3 (TIM-3), and tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) [3, 4]. The application of immune checkpoint blockade therapy, which aims to activate the suppressed immune response against tumors, represents a notable advancement in oncological intervention [5].

The proteins CD39 and CD73 play a crucial role in the enzymatic conversion of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to adenosine. Consequently, they fulfill a vital role in the regulation of immunological suppression. The enzymatic conversion of adenosine diphosphate (ADP) to adenosine is attributed to CD73, whereas CD39 enhances the conversion of ATP to ADP [6, 7]. The significant involvement of CD39 and CD73 in the synthesis of adenosine, resulting in the creation of an immunosuppressive environment, is widely recognized [8]. Previous studies have demonstrated the anti-inflammatory properties of adenosine, particularly when it is released into the extracellular environment [9]. The significance of the interaction between specific T cell receptors and adenosine in the inhibition of antitumor immune response is quite significant. Tumor formation was reported to be suppressed following the inhibition of CD39 and CD73. The control of immunological reactions can be achieved by reducing the enzymatic activity of CD39 and CD73, suppressing adenosine production [10, 11]. The efficacy of immunotherapies involving CD39 and CD73 can be ascribed to their capacity to impede the production of immunosuppressive adenosine and the degradation of extracellular ATP. The application of this specific strategy exhibits potential as an immunotherapeutic approach that can augment the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors in the context of combination therapy. This article succinctly summarizes the cellular and molecular mechanisms linked to the CD39 and CD73 pathways. Moreover, the primary objective of this work is to investigate the current status of therapeutic interventions targeting the CD39/CD73 adenosine pathway while also proposing prospective strategies to enhance the efficacy of cancer treatment in forthcoming advancements.

Methodology

In the present article, we reviewed the application of adenosinergic CD39 and CD73 as new immunological checkpoints in macrophage-based cancer immunotherapy. Between December 30, 2010, and December 30, 2024, we thoroughly searched all of the major databases, including Medline (Ovid), Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science. With an emphasis on examining the purposes and applications of CD39 and CD73 in cancer immunotherapy, our search included research that was published in all languages. Cancer, Immune checkpoints, CD39, CD73, and immunotherapy were all included as keywords in the literature’s title and abstract as part of the search strategy. Additionally, we evaluated two additional sources to confirm the studies’ applicability: (I) the references of numerous approved review papers and (II) the references of articles that might be qualified. The inclusion/exclusion criteria include: an assortment of research that investigated the functions of the adenosinergic immunological checkpoints CD39 and CD73 in cancer immunotherapy. Moreover, case reports, reviews, evaluations, originals, and earlier meta-analyses were all included in the study. The article that covered the most important subjects was chosen for inclusion when several publications with comparable results from investigations had been discovered. Examining the titles, abstracts, and full texts of the articles that were obtained as part of the review process for the search results. Included in the statistical population were conclusive research that satisfied the predefined inclusion criteria.

Biology and biochemistry CD39/CD73

CD39 is a crucial transmembrane enzyme that relies on the presence of calcium (Ca2+) and magnesium (Mg2+) ions for its functionality. The process operates through the catalysis of ATP phosphohydrolysis, leading to the synthesis of adenosine monophosphate [12, 13]. The protein CD39 is composed of a total of 510 amino acids, which includes seven notable N-glycosylation sites, 11 cysteine residues, and sites that are situated within the inner membrane [14–16]. CD39 is comprised of five conserved regions called apyrase domains, which are essential for the formation of the active site and the catalytic process involved in the phosphohydrolysis of extracellular nucleotides [17–19]. CD39 is predominantly expressed by several cell types, including endothelial cells, immunological cells, such as dendritic cells (DCs), Langerhans cells, neutrophils, B cells, monocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, macrophages, mesangial cells, and regulatory T (Tregs) cells [20–22]. The expression of CD39 is modulated by multiple variables, such as proinflammatory cytokines, hypoxia, oxidative stress, specificity protein 1 (Sp1), and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) [23, 24]. The enzyme CD73, which is anchored to glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI), plays a pivotal role in facilitating the conversion of adenosine monophosphate (AMP) to adenosine and inorganic phosphate [25]. After the elimination of the GPI anchor, the soluble form of CD73 has a function comparable to that of its membrane-bound form [26]. CD73 consists of three discrete domains: an N-terminal domain containing metal-binding sites, a C-terminus domain housing the catalytic site, and a short alpha helix connecting the N and C-terminal domains [27, 28]. CD73 is a GPI-anchored molecule with a molecular weight of 70 kD. The regulation of adenosinergic signaling is greatly impacted by its engagement [29, 30]. CD73 has been detected in multiple organs and cell types, including the brain, colon, kidney, heart, liver, lung, and leukocytes [31–33].

Tumor microenvironment

The tumor microenvironment comprises several cellular components, such as tumor cells, stromal cells, endothelial cells, and immune cells, which demonstrate increased expression of CD39 and CD73 [34]. It is crucial to acknowledge that this particular gene has been documented to have increased levels of expression inside a hypoxic tumor microenvironment. In addition, current research has uncovered that Tregs significantly impact the upregulation of CD39 and CD73 expression in response to adenosine signaling [35, 36]. The process described above establishes a feedback loop that sustains the generation of adenosine and the immunosuppressive state inside the tumor microenvironment [37, 38]. Elevated levels of CD39 and CD73 have been observed in neoplastic cells, immunological cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts. Furthermore, it is crucial to recognize that tumors consistently release exosomes that include CD39 and CD73, leading to a increased presence of ectonucleotidases inside the distinct tumor microenvironment.

Recent studies have yielded empirical findings suggesting that exosomes originating from cancer cells manifest the presence of CD39 and CD73 proteins on their cellular membrane. Moreover, exosomes originating from several cancer types demonstrate significant hydrolytic activity towards ATP and AMP-phosphate. Prior studies have demonstrated that the observed elevation in adenosine concentrations inside the tumor microenvironment can be ascribed to enzymatic processes [39, 40]. The expression of CD39 and CD73 on myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) inside the tumor microenvironment is subject to regulation by transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1) [41, 42]. Recent studies have demonstrated that increased levels of immunosuppressive adenosine inside the tumor microenvironment play a substantial role in enabling cancer cells to evade the immune system. Hence, the CD39/CD73/adenosine pathway facilitates the buildup of increased extracellular adenosine concentrations inside the tumor microenvironment [43].

Human cancers

Solid tumours

Several studies have suggested that CD39 may serve as a prognostic indicator, as it is found in both human malignancies and the immune cells that infiltrate these tumors. Pancreatic cancer cases have exhibited a notable tendency towards the upregulation of CD39 in both the vascular and stromal constituents. According to a study conducted by researchers [44], an empirical investigation has revealed a significant correlation between increased levels of CD39 mRNA in tissue and improved long-term survival outcomes following tumor removal. The investigation of rectal cancer in human patients has revealed a significant increase in the expression of CD39 in both primary tumors and metastatic sites. Nevertheless, a proposition was presented, positing that the concurrent assessment of CD39 and CD73 would yield a more precise prognostic marker in contrast to the sole evaluation of CD39 [45].

The upregulation of CD73 has been associated with multiple signaling pathways that are involved in the advancement of tumors. These pathways include Wnt [46], epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [47], TP53 [48] or Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS) mutations [49], TGF-β [50], and loss of estrogen receptor signaling [51]. As a result, the accumulation of adenosine within the tumor, facilitated by the enzyme CD73, impedes the immune system’s ability to effectively launch responses against the tumor [39]. A comprehensive examination has been undertaken to explore the correlation between the expression of CD73 and various clinicopathological characteristics in multiple cancer types, including melanoma, breast cancer, acute lymphocytic leukemia, chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), glioblastoma, head and neck cancers, high-grade serous ovarian cancer, endometrial cancer, colorectal cancer, prostate cancer, bladder cancer, gastric cancer, kidney cancer, and pancreatic carcinomas [52, 53]. The results of these analyses consistently indicate a notable correlation between increased levels of CD73 expression in the tumor microenvironment and unfavorable clinical outcomes. Recent studies have demonstrated that individuals who have received a diagnosis of cancer exhibit elevated levels of soluble CD73 in their plasma, in contrast to persons who are in a state of good health [54]. Moreover, it has been observed that persons diagnosed with acute inflammatory pancreatitis exhibit increased concentrations of soluble CD73 in their circulatory system [55]. Hence, the identification of increased concentrations of soluble CD73 in the bloodstream has the potential to serve as a diagnostic indicator for inflammation in tissues or tumors.

The interplay between neoplastic cells and their surrounding microenvironment is a pivotal factor in shaping the progression and metastasis of the tumor [56]. CD39 has been observed in invading immune cells and cancer cells in several human malignancies, such as lung cancer, squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck, clear cell carcinoma of the kidney, rectal adenocarcinoma, thyroid cancer, breast cancer, and multiform glioblastoma solid tumors. According to empirical research findings [57], a notable association has been observed between increased CD39 expression and an adverse prognosis. The prognostic implications of CD73 expression within the tumor microenvironment have been examined in a range of tumor types, including lung squamous cell carcinoma, pheochromocytoma, pancreatic cancer, bladder urothelial carcinoma, esophageal carcinoma, gastric adenocarcinoma, thyroid carcinoma, and pleomorphic glioblastoma. The conducted study has demonstrated a notable correlation between elevated CD73 expression and the progression of metastasis, leading to a reduction in the duration until recurrence [58]. Several studies have provided evidence of an increase in the expression of CD39 and CD73 in various cancer types as compared to the surrounding normal tissues. Furthermore, previous studies have provided evidence of a significant association between the activation of the CD39/CD73 pathway and the presence of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, as well as a negative prognosis, in cancer patients [59]. Therefore, it is crucial to emphasize the importance of CD39 and CD73 in the regulatory mechanisms that control the proliferation, differentiation, migration, and infiltration of malignant cells [60, 61]. Previous studies have demonstrated a positive association between increased levels of CD39 and CD73 expression and the ability of cancer cells to evade immune detection. The observed phenomena can be ascribed to the role of these chemicals in facilitating the infiltration of MDSCs and Treg cells into the specific tumor microenvironment [61, 62].

Surgically excised tumors of hepatocellular carcinoma, gastric carcinoma, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma have exhibited increased levels of CD39. Based on the extant empirical evidence, it may be inferred that there exists a positive correlation between increased levels of CD39 expression and an augmented probability of postoperative recurrence and/or unfavorable overall survival outcomes. In addition, a recent empirical study has provided empirical evidence suggesting that the inclusion of FoxP3 + Tregs expressing CD39 demonstrates improved prognostic effectiveness in predicting the survival rate in gastric cancer and the time until recurrence in hepatocellular carcinoma when compared to using only FoxP3 + Tregs [63]. The results of the research suggest a possible correlation between a negative prognosis and the existence of rectal adenocarcinoma in patients with tumors that exhibit increased CD73 expression and diminished CD39 expression. Conversely, those afflicted with malignancies characterized by increased levels of CD73hiCD39lo and CD73loCD39hi exhibit a more favorable prognosis [47]. The association established between the expression of CD73 in gastrointestinal neuroendocrine tumors and the presence of PD-L1 indicates that CD73 could potentially function as a valuable prognostic indicator for predicting the effectiveness of targeted therapy involving PD-1/PD-L1 [64]. The potential incorporation of CD73 and CD39 in prognostic evaluation shows potential for augmenting comprehension of the suitable therapeutic strategy, as it considers the involvement of the adenosine energy axis in the advancement of cancer [7]. Additional research is necessary to attain a thorough comprehension of the implications linked to the manifestation of adenosine receptors on the prognosis of cancer.

Hematological malignancies

In the context of hematological malignancies, previous studies have demonstrated a higher prevalence of CD39+, CD4+, and CD8 + cells in follicular lymphoma, as opposed to their significantly reduced presence in healthy or reactive lymph nodes, as well as in normal peripheral blood [65]. The research undertaken by the scientists has established a favorable correlation between elevated CD39 expression on CD4 + and CD8 + cells derived from individuals diagnosed with CLL and a higher stage of the ailment [66].

Cancer immunotherapy

A considerable amount of research has been conducted, which suggests that inside the tumor microenvironment, many cell types, such as cancer cells, immune cell subsets, and stromal cells, demonstrate increased expression of CD39 and CD73. The prevailing idea suggests that the observed upregulation of these molecules functions as an adaptive mechanism employed by these cells to hinder the immune response directed toward cancerous cells. The methodology outlined in the aforementioned study induces a rise in adenosine concentrations, inhibiting immune reactions targeting tumors [40]. Consequently, there has been a growing inclination towards employing the CD39/CD73 axis as an immunotherapeutic approach to diminish adenosine levels and facilitate the transition of cells from a pro-tumoral condition to an anti-tumoral immune response (Fig. 2). Given the claimed role of CD73 and CD39 as adhesion molecules, it is reasonable to propose that the effectiveness of inhibiting these proteins with targeted antibodies is not only contingent upon their enzymatic activity and subsequent adenosine generation [67–69]. Multiple studies have reported evidence indicating that the suppression of CD73 could impede the mobility of cancer cells, a critical factor in tumor invasiveness [70, 71], by diminishing their capacity to adhere to the extracellular matrix. The recognized function of CD73 involves promoting the release of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in cancerous cells through pathways regulated by adenosine. The initiation of this route triggers the onset of angiogenesis in the particular tumor microenvironment, promoting the metastatic spread of malignant cells [72]. A substantial body of research has provided empirical evidence that supports a positive link between the expression and/or activity of CD73 and the capacity of malignant cells to adhere to the extracellular matrix and invade various tissues. Previous studies have demonstrated that inhibiting CD39 and CD73 enzymatic activity significantly reduces tumor development and metastasis by decreasing tumor angiogenesis [73].

The CD39/CD73 pathway can regulate anti-tumoral immune. In the tumor microenvironment, the elevated expression of CD39 and CD73 can convert ATP into adenosine. Adenosine can stimulate adenosine receptors, which can lead to the inhibition of anti-tumoral immune responses and the activation of immune suppressor cells. Besides, adenosine can promote the migration and the development of tumoral cells

Furthermore, considerable empirical data indicates that the inhibition of CD73 can directly augment the effectiveness of conventional therapeutic approaches. The desired result can be achieved by employing particular strategies that directly focus on cancer cells or by decreasing adenosine levels, thus indirectly enhancing the effectiveness of immune cells in inhibiting tumor growth. The abovementioned technique has shown promise in improving the immunosuppressed microenvironment [74]. There is an ongoing evaluation of the inhibitory impacts exerted on CD73 by applying monoclonal antibodies. Significantly, the examined antibodies encompass oleclumab, CPI-006, AK119, TJ004309, NZV929, and BMS-986,179. Moreover, ongoing clinical trials are underway to assess synthetic inhibitors’ effectiveness, such as LY3475070 and AB-680. The current evaluation of the bifunctional monoclonal antibody GS-1423, which exhibits selectivity for the CD73 and TGF-β pathways, is in progress. The primary focus of research regarding these substances revolves around their potential utilization as a combined treatment approach for patients diagnosed with advanced cancer. Monoclonal antibodies have demonstrated a reduced incidence of off-target effects compared to naturally occurring or chemically synthesized inhibitors targeting CD73. In contrast to monoclonal antibodies, conventional molecular inhibitors such as ARL67156/POM-1 (CD39), AMPCP (CD73), and their derivatives have been utilized to impede various purinergic targets in preclinical contexts [75].

Combination therapy

Combination therapy by CD39

Concurrently with notable advancements in the clinical field and the extensive implementation of research pertaining to combination therapy, several strategies involving the simultaneous administration of multiple medications are being utilized in clinical settings [76]. Considering its unique capacity to regulate homeostasis within the tumor microenvironment through the preservation of pro-inflammatory ATP and the reduction of adenosine accumulation, it is advisable to prioritize the suppression of CD39 in the context of combination therapy.

There is a growing acknowledgment of the potential effectiveness of combining the ATP-adenosine axis with chemotherapy. Prior research has yielded findings about the correlation between the release of ATP resulting from cellular breakdown or disintegration and its influence on the immune response against cancer. The phenomenon of cell death induced by chemotherapy medicines, such as cisplatin, oxaliplatin, and mitoxantrone, has been acknowledged by the scientific community. The aforementioned procedure results in the passive liberation of immunostimulatory ATP [77]. Prior research has documented findings that indicate the potential of CD39 expression on diverse cell types to effectively inhibit immune responses against malignancies triggered by immunogenic chemotherapy. Therefore, there exists a compelling rationale for the utilization of immunogenic chemotherapy in conjunction with agents that augment or sustain ATP levels, such as CD39 inhibitors. Moreover, as mentioned earlier, a number of chemotherapeutic agents have been observed to have a significant effect on the upregulation of CD39 expression. Previous research has indicated an increase in the level of CD39 expression in malignancies that have undergone therapy with cytarabine and anthracyclines [51, 78]. CD39 has been identified as the causal component responsible for the emergence of resistance to cytarabine in animal models using patient-derived xenografts. Previous studies conducted on living organisms have provided empirical evidence that POM-1 has shown effectiveness as a countermeasure against the resistance indicated earlier. Multiple studies have consistently demonstrated that the upregulation of CD39 in different preclinical tumor models results in the emergence of chemoresistance by suppressing adaptive antitumor immune responses [79, 80]. Extensive research has been conducted on the impact of CD39 on the efficacy of chemotherapy in several preclinical models, demonstrating its capacity to diminish both the activity and effectiveness of the treatment. The aforementioned outcome is attained via the process of adenosine-mediated immunosuppression and the subsequent decrease in the immunostimulatory impacts of ATP [34, 81]. The combined administration of chemotherapy and CD39 inhibitors has exhibited notable efficacy. The proposed treatment approach entails the inhibition of CD39 in order to impede the degradation of ATP, hence leading to an augmented buildup of ATP inside the tumor microenvironment subsequent to chemotherapy. In the current setting, it is crucial to determine the chemotherapeutic medicines that demonstrate the ability to induce immunogenic cell death or augment the expression of CD39. Therefore, these chemical substances are regarded as more beneficial therapeutic strategies for various categories of cancer [78]. Without a doubt, chemotherapy continues to be the preferred therapeutic modality for a wide array of malignant malignancies. Given the contextual information supplied, the optimal therapeutic strategy for effectively controlling these malignant circumstances is the concurrent utilization of CD39 inhibition in conjunction with chemotherapy. The investigation of a potential therapeutic methodology centered on the selective targeting of the protein CD39 and the evaluation of its expression in malignant tissue exhibits potential as a feasible tactic for tackling the problem of drug resistance in cancer patients receiving chemotherapy.

Multiple preclinical studies have presented findings supporting that the ATP-adenosine pathway, specifically CD39 and CD73, demonstrates a combined or incremental effect when concurrently delivered with anti-PD1/PD-L1 therapy [82, 83]. The clinical trial findings revealed that the concurrent administration of MEDI9447, an antibody with a special focus on CD73, in conjunction with durvalumab, an antibody that targets PD1, led to enhanced clinical outcomes among individuals diagnosed with pancreatic and colorectal cancer. Therefore, it may be inferred that there is a lack of alignment between the adenosine pathway and immune checkpoint blockade when utilized as a strategy for combination therapy. Moreover, previous preclinical studies have shown that the simultaneous administration of anti-PD1 therapy and CD39 enzymatic inhibition led to a significant reduction in tumor development in animal models with immunotherapy-resistant cancers [6, 69]. Multiple clinical trials, such as NCT04306900, NCT04261075, NCT04336098, and NCT03884556, have reported results that provide evidence in favor of the proposition that anti-PD1 therapy substantially augments the sensitivity and response rates of immunotherapy. However, additional research is necessary to determine the potential mechanisms contributing to the increased vulnerability of CD39-deficient rodents to immunotherapy. The combined administration of CD39 blocking and anti-PD1/PD-L1 therapy has been observed to enhance efficacy to a large degree. This conclusion is backed by evidence derived from both preclinical models and clinical investigations. However, the administration of anti-PD1 therapy in mice that lack CD39 demonstrated a significant delay in tumor progression, ultimately leading to complete tumor eradication in 70% of mice with pre-existing tumors. According to a study conducted in preclinical settings [84], the administration of the mouse CD39-specific inhibitor antibody Vx26102 has demonstrated the ability to effectively modulate the activity of CD39 within a living organism. Consequently, this intervention has shown promise in augmenting the efficacy of anti-PD1 therapy. The current clinical trials, namely trials 86 and 87, are being undertaken to examine the possible synergistic outcomes of combination therapy. The continuous nature of clinical research in this particular domain currently presents difficulties in precisely ascertaining response rates for monotherapy and the suggested combination of CD39 inhibition with anti-PD1/PD-L1 therapy. Evidence indicates the presence of CD39 alongside other immunological checkpoint markers such as CTLA-4, TIM3, TIGIT, and LAG3 inside a specific population of cells that play a role in immune responses. This implies the possibility of achieving a synergistic benefit by employing combinatorial interactions. Previous studies have demonstrated that the concurrent administration of antibodies directed against CTLA-4 and TIM3, in conjunction with the suppression of CD39, elicits synergistic benefits in preclinical murine models [5, 85]. The effectiveness of treatment procedures, such as chemotherapy and radiation, is reduced in cold tumors due to the lack of infiltrating polyfunctional immune effectors that specifically identify and target the tumor. The activation of these effectors is specifically induced by the potentially deleterious techniques employed in chemotherapy and radiation therapy [86]. The augmentation of the regulatory function of NK cells in the control of tumor metastasis is achieved by suppressing CD39 activity in conjunction with the existence of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-15 and IL-2 [81].

In a manner akin to the action of chemotherapy, radiation induces the release of ATP from numerous degenerating neoplastic cells. Following this, ATP undergoes enzymatic conversion assisted by the CD39 and CD73 enzymes, leading to the synthesis of adenosine. The adenosine-mediated pathways have the potential to hinder the immune responses directed toward cancers that are triggered by radiation [87]. Therefore, there is a strong rationale for employing a diverse range of inhibitors that selectively target the adenosine system in conjunction with radiation therapy in order to attain improved effectiveness in the management of neoplasms. Additional research is necessary to explore the potential integration of CD39 inhibitors within the context of radiation therapy. Nevertheless, it is crucial to exercise prudence when evaluating the possible therapeutic strategies involving the combination of CD39 targeting and radiotherapy, given the current research findings that emphasize the supportive function of host CD39 in the context of radiotherapy [88].

Combination therapy by CD73

In recent times, CD73 has garnered significant attention as a prospective candidate for a novel therapeutic strategy incorporating combination therapy, owing to its pivotal involvement in the immune response against malignancies [89, 90]. Regarding this issue, in addition to conventional therapy, there is continuous assessment of various anti-CD73 antibodies and other selective small-molecule inhibitors. The study’s findings suggest potential avenues for enhancing therapeutic strategies that target CD73-mediated adenosine signaling in combination with existing cancer therapies.

Previous studies have documented the correlation between CD73 and multidrug resistance [91]. The study revealed a significant association between the presence of CD73 and the acquisition of resistance to doxorubicin in patients afflicted with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). The treatment of doxorubicin led to an increase in the expression of CD73, which subsequently resulted in the inhibition of CD8 + T cells [51]. The study reported an increased abundance of CD47+/CD73+/PDL + cells in TNBC cells following treatment with adjunctive chemotherapeutic agents, namely carboplatin, gemcitabine, and paclitaxel [92]. The investigation into the sensitivity of chemotherapeutic drugs in NCI-60 cell lines revealed a negative correlation between the expression of CD73 and the vulnerability to different chemotherapeutic agents. In addition, it was noted that there was an elevation in the expression of CD73 in ovarian cancer cells that exhibited resistance to platinum-based chemotherapy [93]. Moreover, there have been reports suggesting that the cytokine interleukin-6 (IL-6) derived from mesenchymal stem/stromal cells has a significant role in facilitating resistance to cisplatin in nasopharyngeal cancer by upregulating CD73 [94]. The rise in CD73 expression seen subsequent to chemotherapy treatment appears to be a method utilized by the organism to counterbalance the excessive release of ATP by neoplastic cells in reaction to chemotherapy [80, 95]. Moreover, it is apparent that the conveyance of adenosine via CD73 results in the suppression of ABC transporters and P-glycoprotein, a transporter accountable for the expulsion of drugs. Hence, this particular mechanism contributes to the emergence of drug resistance [96, 97].

Several studies have demonstrated enhanced efficacy in certain mouse tumor models when combining anti-CD73 with PD-L1 and/or anti-CTLA-4, as opposed to utilizing anti-CD73 monotherapy [98–100]. The findings from a comprehensive examination of the immune system suggest that individuals diagnosed with glioblastoma who have undergone anti-PD-1 therapy exhibit a notable presence of macrophages characterized by elevated levels of CD73. The researchers discovered that the lack of CD73 resulted in an increased efficacy of anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapies in a murine model of glioblastoma [101]. The simultaneous blockade of CD73 and CTLA-4 with an antagonistic monoclonal antibody resulted in the augmentation of anticancer effects. The combined administration of adenosine 5’-(α, β-methylene)diphosphate (APCP) and anti-CTLA-4 antibody exhibited significant inhibition of melanoma tumor growth in mice, surpassing the individual effects of APCP or anti-CTLA-4 antibody treatment in isolation. The simultaneous suppression of CD73 and CTLA-4 led to a notable increase in the number of CD8 + tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). Similarly, it was shown that the inhibition of CD73 and CTLA-4 led to a reduction in the number of regulatory T cells [99, 102].

Moreover, it is worth noting that radiation therapy has the capacity to influence the immune response in addition to its direct cytotoxic impact on cancer cells [103]. The exposure to radiation led to the initiation of programmed cell death (apoptosis) in NK cells, T cells, and B cells, as well as the simultaneous attraction and stimulation of DCs [104]. The observed variation in immunological regulation resulting from radiation exposure is most likely attributable to the relative proportions of ATP and adenosine. It is important to acknowledge that there exists a link between increased CD73 enzymatic activity in lung tissue exposed to radiation and the occurrence of pulmonary fibrosis, a notable detrimental consequence of thoracic irradiation. The infusion of anti-CD73 antibodies led to a notable reduction in radiation-induced lung fibrosis, as reported in a study [105]. This discovery suggests that the inhibition of CD73 may be a promising strategy for alleviating the pulmonary adverse effects commonly observed in the treatment of thoracic cancer. Additionally, it was noted that the application of radiotherapy led to an increase in the expression of CD73 in human breast cancer cells. Furthermore, a study indicated that the concurrent inhibition of CD73 and radiation demonstrated an enhanced modulation of antitumor T cell responses [106]. Nevertheless, recent research has demonstrated that the pharmacological inhibition or ablation of CD73 can successfully reinstate the proliferative capacity of human bladder cancer cells, leading to a reduction in their susceptibility to radiation [107].

Clinical trials

Clinical trials in CD39

Several phase I clinical trials have been undertaken to evaluate the effectiveness of CD39 monoclonal antibodies or other inhibitors, either as monotherapy or in combination with other inhibitors, for various types of solid tumor malignancies. The current clinical trials involve three specific monoclonal antibodies, specifically JS019, ES014, and PUR001. The antibodies being discussed are characterized by their respective trial registration codes, namely NCT05508373, NCT05381935, and NCT05234853. In addition, there are now active clinical trials aimed at evaluating the effectiveness of four CD39 antagonists, specifically IPH5201, SRF617, ES002023, and TTX-030. The aforementioned trials have been duly documented and allocated the corresponding trial registration numbers: NCT04261075, NCT04336098, ES002023, and TTX-030. The preliminary findings have demonstrated promising indications within the realm of cancer therapy. Despite being a component of the immunotherapy field, CD39 inhibitors have received limited attention in the arena of clinical research (Table 1).

Cancer immunotherapy by adenosinergic CD39 and CD73 Blockade in the clinic

Given the present state of clinical research pertaining to anti-CD39 cancer immunotherapy, it is imperative to augment our comprehension of its mechanism of action and potential adverse consequences. Previous studies have demonstrated that the inhibition of CD39 leads to increased activation of immune cells, a broader diversity of T cells in the peripheral circulation, and an enhanced presence of T cells in tumor biopsies [108]. The primary objective of the phase 1 clinical trial (NCT04336098) was to evaluate the preliminary safety profile of SRF617, a CD39-specific inhibitor, when delivered as monotherapy at a dosage of 1400 mg. Additionally, the study investigated the safety attributes of SRF617 when administered concurrently with gemcitabine + nab-paclitaxel or pembrolizumab. The research encompassed a group of 55 individuals who were clinically diagnosed with advanced solid tumors. The initial findings suggest that a single patient diagnosed with pancreatic cancer exhibited a partial response [109].

The current study involved recruiting a cohort of 204 individuals who had received a diagnosis of advanced solid tumors, with the aim of their participation in a phase I clinical trial. The principal aim of this clinical trial was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of IPH5201, a monoclonal antibody engineered to selectively block the activity of the CD39 protein. Furthermore, a clinical trial in phase I, identified as NCT03884556, has produced empirical data that supports the feasibility of further investigating supplementary inhibitors of CD39, particularly TTX-030, in individuals suffering from advanced or metastatic solid malignancies. At present, a considerable quantity of active clinical trials is focused on assessing the effectiveness of various small-molecule inhibitors that selectively target CD39. The administration of the CD39 inhibitor ES002023 demonstrates the augmentation of the immune response against malignancy. The mechanism of action of the inhibitor involves enhancing the stability of extracellular ATP, a chemical well-known for its pro-inflammatory properties. Furthermore, it serves to hinder the synthesis of immunosuppressive adenosine inside the particular tumor microenvironment. The current focus of inquiry pertains to the mechanism of action, which is currently undergoing evaluation in a clinical trial registered with the identification NCT05075564).

Clinical trials in CD73

A multitude of treatment trials have been conducted with monoclonal antibodies that specifically target CD73, with the objective of addressing various types of solid tumors. The injection of these antibodies has been reported in several therapeutic applications, both independently and in conjunction with medications that particularly target PD-L1 or PD-1. Oleclumab, MEDI9447, AK119, HLX23, IPH5301, Sym042, CPI-0006, IBI325, PT199, JAB-BX102, TJ004309, NZV930, INCA00186, and BMS-986,179 represent a diverse range of antibody compounds. The association between anti-CD73 and moderate side effects, which have been demonstrated to be manageable or acceptable, has been documented in several clinical trials (NCT02503774, NCT03381274, NCT03616886, NCT03611556, and NCT03334617). The preliminary findings have yielded inconclusive outcomes, as certain examinations have exhibited encouraging signs of disease control, but others have failed to establish unequivocal therapeutic advantages (Table 1).

The study consisted of a cohort of 77 individuals diagnosed with colorectal cancer, 73 individuals diagnosed with pancreatic adenocarcinoma, and 42 individuals diagnosed with lung cancer. The participants in this study were enlisted as volunteers in a phase I clinical trial (NCT02503774) with the objective of investigating the effects of anti-CD73 therapy, either as a standalone intervention or in conjunction with anti-PD-L1 therapy. A comparative analysis was conducted on a cohort of individuals diagnosed with colorectal cancer (n = 9), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (n = 8), and lung cancer (n = 9) who demonstrated stable disease. The study’s results indicated that within the cohort of patients diagnosed with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutation (EGFRm) lung cancer, four subjects demonstrated a discernible and medically significant reaction to the provided pharmaceutical intervention. From a comprehensive perspective, the therapeutic approach utilizing anti-CD73/anti-PD-L1 medicines has exhibited positive results in terms of inhibiting tumor growth in individuals afflicted with lung cancer. However, the effectiveness of this therapeutic strategy in persons with colorectal cancer and pancreatic adenocarcinoma is still unclear [110].

The main objective of the phase Ib/II clinical trial (NCT03381274) was to assess the safety and efficacy of anti-CD73 in conjunction with third-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) in a distinct cohort of patients diagnosed with advanced EGFRm lung cancer who had received prior treatment. A group consisting of five persons had therapy with a dosage of 1500 mg of anti-CD73 antibody, resulting in a clinical benefit/response rate of 75% and an objective response rate of 25%. On the other hand, a group consisting of 21 participants who were administered a dosage of 3000 mg of anti-CD73 antibody demonstrated a clinical benefit/response rate of 82.4% and an objective response rate of 11.8%. The group of patients who were administered a larger dosage of anti-CD73 exhibited a median duration of progression-free survival of 7.4 months, whereas the group of patients who did not receive anti-CD73 revealed a median progression-free survival duration of 2.8 months [111].

The primary aim of the clinical trial, which was registered with the code NCT03954704, was to evaluate the efficacy of a bifunctional antibody known as GS-1423 and AGEN1423 [14]. This antibody was designed to selectively target CD73 and TGF-β in a cohort of patients who had been diagnosed with advanced solid tumors. There exists a notion proposing that the immunosuppressive cytokine TGF-β possesses the capability to augment the effectiveness of therapy by promoting the upregulation of CD73 expression via the mediation of adenosine [112, 113]. The initial assessment of a group including 21 individuals indicated the presence of unfavorable outcomes, which ranged in intensity from mild to severe and, in some cases, led to fatalities [114]. The researchers discovered that the stability of the bifunctional antibody in the bloodstream was significant. Moreover, the study exhibited a notable affinity for the binding of B cell CD73 in individuals who were administered a dosage ranging from 20 to 45 mg/Kg. Within the sample, including 17 patients who underwent the initial response assessment, the findings revealed that 4.8% of the participants showed a partial response, 33.3% exhibited stable disease, and 42.9% experienced disease progression [114].

Limitations

The therapeutic strategy of selectively targeting CD39 and CD73 demonstrates promise because it has the ability to enhance the buildup of extracellular ATP while simultaneously reducing the levels of extracellular adenosine. The aforementioned phenomenon of dual action is of significant importance in the progression of a robust immune response against cancer. However, it is imperative to acknowledge the limitations associated with inhibitors and antibodies targeting CD39 and CD73 in the context of immunotherapy. The primary constraint is the challenge of creating inhibitors that demonstrate robust efficacy and safety, as well as a notable degree of selectivity, particularly in regard to the targeting of CD39 and CD73. The poor efficacy of traditional inhibitors arises from their lack of specificity in selectively reducing the activities of CD39 and CD73 [112]. The latest group of CD39 inhibitors, which has attracted significant academic attention, principally functions by inhibiting the cellular absorption mechanism and only operates inside the extracellular environment [113, 115]. The utilization of low molecular weight inhibitors that selectively target CD39 and CD73 has been constrained to areas distinguished by increased ATP levels as a result of competitive inhibition [116]. Furthermore, there is a need for a thorough examination of the potential adverse effects associated with these drugs that have yet to be undertaken. Another problem that arises is related to the restricted accessibility of commercially available antibodies that are particularly engineered to target CD39 and CD73. The primary objective of antibodies is to selectively target extracellular CD39 and CD73, leading to restricted effectiveness against soluble variants of CD39 and CD73. The constraint indicated above poses significant challenges for potential scientific initiatives and clinical investigations [34]. Additional research is necessary to explore the unintended consequences associated with the use of monoclonal antibodies.

The complex arrangement of the purinergic network and the significant possibility of negative consequences present obstacles to the effective incorporation of adenosine receptor blockage in therapeutic approaches. The absence of CD39 and CD73 has been associated with the dysregulation of several physiological processes, including angiogenesis, coagulation, inflammation, and glucose tolerance. The primary objective of the study was to examine the role of the signaling molecules CD39 and CD73 in regulating adhesion. Hence, the potential inhibition of CD39 and CD73 may obstruct the attachment of malignant cells to the extracellular matrix, thereby restricting their ability to move, a critical factor in tumor invasion. The inhibition of CD39 and CD73 may begin platelet activity, potentially resulting in thrombotic events. The significant academic worth lies in the chemicals generated by activated platelets, which play a crucial role in the breakdown of the extracellular matrix and the establishment of the metastatic niche [117]. Nevertheless, it is crucial to conduct more investigation through preclinical or clinical trials to thoroughly assess the intrinsic negative consequences linked to the suppression of CD39 and CD73. The potential consequences encompass symptoms such as weariness, cognitive impairment, increased body temperature, and pruritus. The aforementioned elements pose potential hazards associated with the suppression of CD39. Given the underlying assumption that the tumor microenvironment demonstrates an elevated abundance of extracellular enzymes, it is plausible to argue that the potential advantages linked to these therapeutic medicines could outweigh any probable drawbacks they would involve.

Future perspective and direction

Within the ever-evolving field of cancer immunotherapy, the use of targeted therapeutics aimed at CD39 and CD73 serves as a prime example of the discovery of new immune targets resulting from fundamentally revolutionary therapeutic approaches. The discovery of previously unidentified immune checkpoint targets, specifically CD39 and CD73, has been shown to hold substantial importance within the realm of cancer immunotherapy [118, 119]. The primary mechanism by which the CD39/CD73 pathway exerts its immunosuppressive effect is by facilitating the synthesis of adenosine. During the previous quadrennial interval, researchers have effectively generated monoclonal antibodies that have a distinct affinity for the binding of CD39 and CD73. The antibodies now under investigation have exhibited a significant capacity to hinder the progression of tumors in preclinical cancer models, as supported by relevant citations from publications [69, 119, 120]. Nevertheless, it is crucial to acknowledge that the existing body of research concerning the utilization of immunological checkpoints in cancer primarily relies on the utilization of rat tumor models. Moreover, additional research is necessary to assess the effectiveness of specific approaches. At now, there exists an ongoing clinical investigation concerning more than fifty therapeutic combination medicines that aim to selectively modulate the ATP-adenosine pathway by the utilization of CD39 or CD73 antagonists [121, 122]. The current state of clinical studies investigating combination therapy is experiencing a notable and rapid transformation. In the domain of cancer therapy, it is imperative to underscore methodologies that offer a synergistic benefit in the context of CD39 targeting. In contemporary times, there has been an observable increase in the number of pharmacological compounds that have a distinct preference for binding to CD39 and CD73 receptors. Consequently, clinical research was later initiated. There is an expectation that a greater number of assessments will be conducted on supplementary drugs in the foreseeable future [119, 123].

Targeting the CD39/CD73 pathways for cancer treatment has demonstrated considerable promise in both in vitro and animal model studies; however, implementing these findings into clinical practice would require an enhanced understanding of how adenosine controls the cancer microenvironment. Nevertheless, one of the deficiencies in the scientific community is that adenosine stimulates the formation of cancer by influencing the cancer stroma; its direct effects on cancer cells can occasionally not be consistent. The comprehension of a range of intricate research techniques is also crucial when analyzing tumor inhibition of adenosine pathways mediated by cancer stroma. These techniques involve conditional deletion of adenosine receptors or metabolic enzymes in immune or endothelial cells, silencing adenosine receptors or metabolic enzymes in xenograft or allograft before inoculation, and using a three-dimensional cell culture model involving cancer cells that constitute their microenvironment. The intrinsic impact of the adenosine system additionally depends on an assortment of factors, such as the type of cancer, the adenosine receptor subtypes expressed by cancer cells, and studies of proliferation, apoptosis, or metastasis. For instance, a specific tumor may express multiple adenosine receptors; in this case, adenosine therapy should take into account the competing proliferative and antiproliferative (or pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic) roles associated with various receptors.

Clinical trials incorporating adenosine medications should also be predicated on a deeper comprehension of specific human cancers in addition to preclinical research. Such adenosine therapy can be determined by biomarker-based tumor surveillance, which may involve diverse adenosine receptors, metabolic enzymes, and uptake systems. In combination with the examination of possible polymorphisms in the human adenosine system, we anticipate that these methods will enable us to thoroughly investigate the potential of adenosine therapy in the treatment of cancer patients. Numerous preclinical and clinical investigations will expand and enhance the application of CD39/CD73 pathway inhibitors. The combination regimen’s effectiveness with other immune checkpoint inhibitors has also been established and assessed in preclinical research. Since the generation of adenosine is dependent on hypoxia and cell renewal, it should be beneficial to block this pathway when combined with treatments that encourage hypoxia and cell death in the tumor microenvironment. These include hypoxic conditions caused by radiation therapy and chemotherapy medications, particularly those that stimulate ATP release (a process known as immunogenic chemotherapy). Its broad medicinal application may be indicated by the broad spectrum of immunological responses mediated by the CD39/CD73 signaling pathway.

Discussion

Overexpression of CD39 and CD73 has been identified in many human malignancies. The presence of CD39 and CD73 triggered anti-tumor responses, including the inhibition of CD8 + cytotoxic T cell activities and the suppression of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells. The enzymatic activity of CD39 and CD73 also prevented the decomposition of cancer cells and the survival of T lymphocytes specific to tumors. The ongoing discovery of CD39 and CD73 in human cancer cells lends credence to the hypothesis that these molecules could be targets for cancer immunotherapy. It is possible to disrupt the enzymatic activity of CD39 and CD73 by employing monoclonal antibodies, siRNA, and small molecules. The inhibitory effects of CD39 and CD73 were alleviated when small compounds, siRNA, and monoclonal antibodies inhibited these proteins. An assortment of monoclonal antibodies has been developed that have been demonstrated to inhibit CD39 or CD73. The antibodies in question are all monoclonal. One benefit of using monoclonal antibodies to block CD39 and CD73 is that antibody medications usually have lengthy serum half-lives. Since monoclonal antibodies are injected into the bloodstream and circulate before they reach their target, this feature is advantageous. When compared to blocking CD39 or CD73 alone, blocking both CD39 and CD73 does not enhance anti-tumor responses. In comparison to blocking only CD39 or CD73, blocking both could result in superior results because blocking only one already disrupts adenosine production. Combination therapy appears to be a potential treatment option for cancer. Nonetheless, research should be conducted to observe the adverse effects of CD39 and CD73 in patients. The potential negative effects of combination therapy must also be investigated. CD39 and CD73 are recognized in immune cells alongside cancer cells. Monoclonal antibodies that block CD39 and CD73 may disrupt other biological functions. Tissue-specific monoclonal antibodies must be produced and identified for the purpose of preventing this.

A recent preclinical study showed that inhibition of CD39 and CD73 using locked nucleic acid-modified antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) inhibited ATP degradation and suppression of T-cell proliferation, which is seen with accumulation of adenosine metabolites, CD39 was reduced by 98% when treated with 5 µM CD39 ASO and 95% with 2.5 µM CD39 + 2.5 µM CD73 ASO compared with the control. T cells that were treated with CD73 ASO alone showed a 70% reduction in CD73 expression compared with the control. Additionally, treated T cells were able to escape inhibition of proliferation and apoptosis induced by adenosine metabolites. ASO-mediated knockdown of CD39 and CD73-induced suppression of proliferation was evident up to a concentration of 400 µM, which is comparable to what is measured at tumor sites [124]. Another study showed a synergistic effect of co-targeting CD73 and adenosine A2A receptor through antibody-directed therapies. Gene-targeted mice deficient in both A2AR and CD73 had a reduction in tumor size (15 mm2 compared with 50 mm2 in single gene knockout and 80 mm2 in wild-type mice). This result was similarly observed with tumor mass (50 mg compared with 100–150 mg in single gene knockout and 300 mg in wild-type mice). Additionally, 40% of dual-gene knockout mice rejected tumors compared with 7% in single-gene knockout mice. The prolonged survival was up to 80 days post inoculation compared with approximately 40 days in the control, and 50–60 days in single therapy groups [125].

Presently, CD39- and CD73-blocking monoclonal antibody are undergoing clinical trials. Several clinical trials are investigating the use of these antibodies as both monotherapy and as a component of combinational therapy. At this time, the results of a clinical trial for participants receiving oral osimertinib with oleclumab (anti-CD73 antibody) demonstrated that treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were 60.0% of patients receiving 1500 mg oleclumab treatment and 85.7% of the participants receiving 3000 mg oleclumab treatment. Such reported events were generally mild with the most common including rash, stomatitis, diarrhea and paronychia. Additionally, for patients who received 3000 mg oleclumab treatment, the median progression-free survival was 7.4 months and the median overall survival was 24.8 months. The performance of 3000 mg oleclumab regiment as both a safe and effective immunotherapeutic agent has since prompted its use as the recommended phase II dose in subsequent clinical trials [126]. This finding is critical since advanced-stage cancers is regularly able to generate resistance to the current standard of care, which requires additional therapeutic agents in combination therapy to maximize positive outcomes in such cases [127]. Meanwhile, 55 patients with advanced solid tumors enrolled in a phase 1 dose-escalation study of SRF617 (anti-CD39 antibody) demonstrated that the most treatment-emergent adverse effect in monotherapy were fatigue (35%), nausea (22%), and constipation (19%). The most common adverse effects in combination with pembrolizumab (anti-PD1) were pruritus (33%), diarrhea, myalgia, anemia, cough, and dizziness (22%, each) [109]. In addition, IPH5201 (anti-CD39 antibody) was administered to 57 patients and was generally well-tolerated as monotherapy or when used in combination with dupilumab; 66.7% of patients has TRAEs, and the most common events were reactions to infusion (21.1%) and fatigue (17.5%). Median PFS was 1.4 months and median OS was 8.2 months; 22 (38%) patients had stable disease but no patient was observed to have a partial or complete response [128].

Conclusion

The existing comprehension of the therapeutic capacity of CD39 and CD73-targeted therapy in individuals with cancer is limited, despite the significant anti-cancer efficacy observed in preclinical experiments when employed alongside immune checkpoint inhibition or agonist immunotherapies. Moreover, it is plausible to consider the utilization of therapeutic approaches to specifically address the succeeding series of occurrences specifically by modulating the functioning of adenosine deaminase. This can be accomplished by manipulating adenosine metabolism, which is mediated by CD39 and CD73. To assess the potential therapeutic advantages of targeting the adenosinergic axis facilitated by CD39 and CD73 in patients with aggressive malignancies, it is imperative to carry out continuous clinical trials that encompass a comprehensive examination of CD39 and CD73 expression, their respective functionalities, and the subsequent cascade of events occurring within the tumor microenvironment. Currently, ongoing clinical trials are being conducted to assess the effectiveness of anti-CD39 and anti-CD73 treatment. Future research should focus on detailed mechanistic studies and biomarker-driven patient stratification to elucidate synergistic interactions in combination therapies.
