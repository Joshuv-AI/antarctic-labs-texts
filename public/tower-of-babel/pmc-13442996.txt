Small‐scale adaptation to geothermal soil heating in a perennial herb revealed by combining crosses and transplantations
Authors: Alicia Valdés, Vigdís F Helmutsdóttir, Bryndís Marteinsdóttir, Johan Ehrlén
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Identifying environmental factors associated with local adaptation and traits under selection is key to linking evolutionary processes to the environment. While reciprocal transplantation studies and provenance experiments often have demonstrated adaptation at relatively large spatial scales, adaptation can also occur at very small spatial scales.Combining a crossing experiment with field transplantations, we investigated whether Cerastium fontanum has adapted to geothermally induced small‐scale soil temperature differences. Offspring representing a wide range of parental soil temperatures were transplanted across the same temperature range, and traits and fitness components were measured over 2 yr. We evaluated the relationship between plant performance and soil temperatures, the degree of adaptation to their source thermal environment, and the dependence of adaptation on flowering time.Survival, flowering incidence, and overall fitness were lower in warmer soils. However, adaptation to temperature was asymmetric; while all plants performed well at colder sites, individuals from colder origins performed poorly at warmer sites. Flowering time and fitness varied in relation to soil temperature, as well as the difference between planting and source thermal environments.Our findings indicate that small‐scale variation in soil temperature underlies fine‐scale adaptation and provides important knowledge to understand evolutionary effects of microclimatic variation. Keywords: climatic variation, fitness, geothermal ecosystems, Iceland, local adaptation, plant phenology, selective agents, soil temperature

Introduction

Populations in disparate environments often undergo divergent selection, leading to genetic differentiation and local adaptation (de Jong, 1990; Falconer & Mackay, 2009; Hereford, 2009; Kawecki & Ebert, 2004; Savolainen et al., 2007). Local adaptation, that is a fitness advantage of resident vs foreign genotypes in the local environment, might evolve in response to a wide variety of factors, including climate and other abiotic factors, as well as species interactions (Wadgymar et al., 2022). Local adaptation can occur at multiple spatial scales (Belotte et al., 2003; Richardson et al., 2014; Valdés et al., 2019; Arietta & Skelly, 2021), and identifying the environmental factors associated with adaptation at different spatial scales is important to understand evolutionary processes, and to predict responses to environmental changes (Wilczek et al., 2014; Anderson & Wadgymar, 2020; Wadgymar et al., 2022).

Climatic conditions such as temperature constitute important selective agents in many systems (Frank et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2019; Lortie & Hierro, 2022; Wadgymar et al., 2022). In temperate environments with short growing seasons, spring temperature conditions are likely to be an important selective agent, and the timing of development and reproduction are often traits under selection (Munguía‐Rosas et al., 2011; Austen et al., 2017). In plants, an early onset of growth and reproduction in spring provides advantages in terms of a longer period for resource acquisition and seed development, but also entails costs, such as an increased risk of exposure to late frosts. A favorable balance of this trade‐off will depend on spring climatic conditions and how these are related to growing season length (Hereford, 2009; Buckley et al., 2021; Dittmar & Schemske, 2023). Importantly, the production of phenotypes that enhance fitness across variable climatic conditions might prevent phenotypes from being fully adapted to conditions in each single year (Milner et al., 1999; Benning & Moeller, 2019). Moreover, due to life history trade‐offs, local phenotypes might not be fully adapted during all life cycle phases (Buckley et al., 2021; Dittmar & Schemske, 2023). Studies of adaptation should therefore ideally consider multiple fitness components and be carried out during several years (Hereford, 2009).

Reciprocal transplantation experiments have shown that adaptation to the local environment is widespread (Leimu & Fischer, 2008; Johnson et al., 2022; Wadgymar et al., 2022). While many reciprocal transplantation studies and provenance experiments have demonstrated adaptation at relatively large spatial scales, adaptation has also been shown to occur at very small spatial scales (Antonovics & Bradshaw, 1970; Schemske, 1984; Sork et al., 1993; Knight & Miller, 2004; Kolis et al., 2022). However, local adaptation is not adaptation to a geographic location, but rather adaptation to the specific set of environmental conditions at that site. To better understand the selection resulting in local adaptation, it is therefore essential to not only demonstrate that populations are locally adapted, but also to identify the selective agents and traits involved. Several studies have examined the environmental factors and traits associated with adaptation by using provenance experiments, including numerous source populations transplanted to field gardens representing a gradient of the putative environmental factor (Risk et al., 2021; Wadgymar et al., 2022). However, reciprocal transplantations or provenance experiments often rely on correlational evidence and can only identify putative agents of selection. To gain a better understanding of the environmental factors associated with local adaptation and the traits under selection, carrying out manipulative experiments under realistic conditions or taking advantage of natural experiments therefore constitute important tools (Wadgymar et al., 2022).

High‐latitude geothermal ecosystems, consisting of mosaics of unheated areas and areas heated by geothermal steam, have been used to study plant adaptation to high temperatures at small spatial scales in Yellowstone (Lekberg et al., 2012; Hendrick et al., 2016; Kolis et al., 2022) and Iceland (O'Gorman et al., 2014; Leblans et al., 2017; Valdés et al., 2019; Ehrlén et al., 2023). These systems, with large temperature differences over distances of a few meters and little apparent variation in other factors, can be considered natural experiments and offer unique opportunities to study adaptation to temperature. In unheated areas, low temperatures constrain growing season length (Thorhallsdottir, 1998), and selection likely favors a more rapid growth and an earlier flowering than in heated areas, where growing seasons are longer. While conditions in heated areas reduce the constraints put by short growing seasons, they are likely to be stressful in other respects. For example, in heated areas soil temperature is largely decoupled from air temperature, and thus constitutes a less reliable cue for spring advancement than on unheated soils (Ehrlén et al., 2023). Furthermore, soil temperature affects sessile plants, which are exposed to temperatures only in their immediate neighborhood, differently from mobile, and this can influence the synchrony of plant flowering and pollinator activity (Valdés et al., 2022). The importance of these potential disadvantages depends on spring weather conditions and is thus likely to vary between years. Moreover, over longer time periods the spatial pattern of geothermal heating can change (Carotenuto et al., 2016; Sigurdsson et al., 2016), meaning that current heating conditions often do not span a sufficiently long time to allow for plants to have become well adapted to their current temperature environment.

In the perennial herb Cerastium fontanum, selection favors earlier flowering on unheated soils but later flowering on geothermally heated soils in some years (Ehrlén et al., 2023). Accordingly, other studies with the same system have shown that there is additive genetic differentiation in flowering time (Valdés et al., 2019, 2025). Phenotypic plasticity in response to soil temperature results in earlier flowering of plants on warmer soils in the field, whereas, at a given temperature, plants originating from warmer soils flower than those from colder soils. Environmental and genetic variation in relation to soil temperature thus follows a counter‐gradient pattern (cf. Conover & Schultz, 1995).

In this study, we combined a crossing design with a transplantation experiment, to examine whether the perennial herb Cerastium fontanum is adapted to small‐scale differences in soil temperature in a geothermally heated sub‐arctic area in Iceland. F1‐plants from 94 crosses of parent plants originating from a large range of soil temperatures and from 41 self‐pollinated individuals were transplanted to eight different field plots differing in mean soil temperature, but also harboring considerable variation in soil temperature within plots. This design yielded offspring representing a continuous gradient of parental soil temperatures that were transplanted to a similar continuous gradient of soil temperatures. To assess the extent of adaptation in relation to temperature conditions, we recorded individual plant responses in terms of four fitness components: survival, flowering incidence in surviving individuals, fruiting incidence in flowering individuals and total number of seeds in fruiting individuals. We then used Aster models to assess effects on overall fitness and structural equation models to evaluate to what extent the observed effects depended on flowering time.

We made the following three predictions: (1) Plants on average perform worse, that is have lower fitness, under the more stressful conditions on warmer soils. (2) Plants are adapted to their local thermal environment, that is have a higher fitness when transplanted to soils with temperatures that deviate less from the temperature at the parental sites than when transplanted to sites with temperatures that deviate more. (3) Flowering time mediates fitness effects of soil temperature, and effects of differences in soil temperature between parental and transplantation sites.

Materials and Methods

Study site and species

The study was conducted in Hengill, a sub‐arctic high‐elevation geothermal area in SW‐Iceland (64°03′N; 21°18′W, c. 360 mean above sea level). Hengill is a relatively young central volcano, situated at the heart of the established Hengill volcanic system, that last erupted c. 2000 yr ago (Zakharova & Spichak, 2012). In Iceland, geothermal areas lie close to the tectonic plate boundaries of the Mid‐Atlantic ridge, where magma chambers reside at relatively shallow depths, warming the groundwater. Accumulating hot water creates high‐pressure areas that warm the bedrock and soil above (Barbier, 2002). In the Hengill area, the proximity to the surface of heated channels can result in soil temperatures rising to more than 50°C above ambient (Perron, 2017). In the study area (c. 1 km2), soil temperature can range from no geothermal heating to over 20°C above ambient within short distances (10–20 m), without significant differences in soil chemistry, elevation, or vegetation type (O'Gorman et al., 2014).

The study species, Cerastium fontanum (Caryophyllaceae), is a common perennial herb in sub‐arctic and arctic ecosystems. The main part of its global distribution area encompasses unheated soils, while occurrences on heated soils are very restricted. In the study area, individuals produce 1–30 stems that are 10–30 cm long. Each stem carries one to six flowers that flower asynchronously. Flowering starts in late May and often continues through June and July. Flowering time strongly depends on soil temperature, being earlier on warmer soils. This suggests that phenological assortative mating restricts gene flow between areas differing in soil temperatures (Ehrlén et al., 2023). Cerastium fontanum is pollinated by various insect families (Fitter & Peat, 1994). It is self‐compatible, but the selfing rate in the study area is not known. Fruits mature from July to August, and the species has no specialized mechanism for seed dispersal (Kristinsson, 2010). In the study area, each fruit contains c. 40 seeds on average.

Experimental design

Seed collections and temperature measurements in the field

In May 2017, 420 C. fontanum individuals along the entire temperature gradient in the study area were haphazardly picked and marked with numbered plastic tags (Supporting Information Fig. S1). To avoid biasing, marking occurred before flower buds appeared, and some marked individuals never flowered. At the same time, soil temperature at 10 cm depth was measured within 2 cm from each marked individual with a digital multi‐purpose thermometer. Previous studies with this system have shown that instant measures of soil temperature provide adequate descriptions of soil temperature conditions during longer time periods (Ehrlén et al., 2023). When seeds matured, up to three fruits were collected from the 251 individuals that had flowered. Fruits were placed in separate paper envelopes, dried, and stored cold in air‐tight containers.

Cultivation of first‐generation plants

Seeds were sown in commercial nutrient‐poor soil in the glasshouse at Stockholm University in January 2018 (80 seeds per mother plant, or all seeds if fewer were available, distributed among four pots). In March 2018, up to eight seedlings per mother plant were transferred to individual pots, which were then moved to the common garden at Stockholm University (59°22′N; 18°03′E) in June 2018, and placed in randomly designated positions. Pots were kept in the common garden until March 2020, allowing plants to grow and reach a reproductive state.

Crossings

We carried out a crossing experiment to reduce maternal effects and at the same time achieve a continuous distribution of mid‐parental temperature values. We crossed individuals originating from different temperature environments using a North Carolina type II (NCII) crossing design (Lynch & Walsh, 1998). Plants were brought from the common garden to the glasshouse in March 2020 and divided into four different groups based on the soil temperature of maternal plants. Among plants that produced at least 16 flower buds, we randomly picked 12 individuals from each of these four groups to be used for the crossings. This resulted in 48 plants, representing 48 different maternal families from the entire soil temperature range at their sites of origin, of which 24 were used as dams and 24 as sires (Fig. S1). The crossing design consisted of six replicate NCII blocks, each with four dams and four sires. The sires and dams for each block were randomly picked from each of the four temperature groups so that each group was represented among both sires and dams (Table S1). As a few plants did not produce 16 flowers, the final design consisted of 100 different dam × sire combinations (see details in Table S1). The crossing scheme was designed to ensure that each dam received pollen from sires with diverse origin temperatures, and each sire sired seeds on dams with diverse origin temperatures (Fig. S1; Table S1). The magnitude of the differences in origin temperature between dams and sires did not affect fitness (results not shown). Flowers on dams were emasculated by removing the anthers at several occasions every day, as soon as flowers were about to open. To perform cross‐pollinations, we removed the dehisced anthers from sires with tweezers and rubbed them against the stigma of one flower on the dam, cleaning tweezers with ethanol between pollinations. We marked recipient flowers with colored threads to keep track of pollen donors (sires). In 46 of the 48 plants used for the crossings, which had additional flowers beyond the 16 used for the crossings, we also performed self‐pollinations. This was done to increase the number of parental combinations, as well as to allow us to test for differences between crossed and selfed progenies. After pollination treatments, we kept plants in the glasshouse and collected seeds from each hand‐pollinated flower as fruits matured. Seeds were stored in separate paper envelopes in a dry, dark and cool environment. Part of the seeds obtained from the cross‐pollinations were used in a glasshouse heating experiment used to investigate genetic differences in flowering time and thermal plasticity of flowering time (Valdés et al., 2025).

Cultivation of F1 plants from controlled crosses

In early June 2021, up to 40 seeds from each of the 100 crosses and 46 selfings were sown in a glasshouse in Reykjavík, Iceland. In cases where less than 40 seeds were available from a crossing or selfing, we sowed all available seeds. The aim was to produce 12 seedlings per seed family (i.e. each crossing or selfing) to be planted at sites representing three predetermined temperature categories with four replicates per site category. Seeds were sown into pot trays with slots measuring 5 × 5 × 9 cm (225 ml). A mixture of half garden soil and half nutrient‐poor sowing soil was used to mimic the nutrient levels of their natural habitat. Glasshouse conditions were warm (c. 20°C) and no synthetic lights were used. We were able to get 858 seedlings representing 94 of 100 crossings and 41 of 46 self‐pollinations for the transplantation experiment. Before being planted to the field in September–October, plants were taken outside for 1–2 wk of acclimation. Daily mean temperatures in Reykjavík over this time period were c. 6–9°C (The Icelandic Meterological Office, data not shown).

Transplantation of F1 plants to the field

In autumn 2021, the 858 seedlings were transplanted into eight different field plots (Fig. S1). Plots were selected based on soil temperature measurements taken at 10 cm soil depth during late summer 2021, while also trying to keep other factors, such as slope, aspect, moisture, and the composition and height of the vegetation constant. Mean soil temperatures varied widely between plots, ranging from 11 to 34°C, and temperatures also varied within plots (the temperature range within plots, i.e. the difference between maximum and minimum temperature, varied between 2 and 36°C). The transplantation plots thus altogether represented a continuous variation along the temperature range over which C. fontanum grows in the study area. To distribute the seedlings from each family over the entire temperature gradient, the plots were broadly categorized as hot (> 25°C), intermediate (15–24°C) or cold (< 14°C). One of the original plots included two parts that had clearly different temperatures and was therefore treated as two different plots. This resulted in totally nine plots, three plots per temperature category, in the transplantation experiment. The available seedlings for each seed family were distributed evenly across these three temperature categories, that is with up to four replicates of a given cross per category. Within temperature categories, the four replicates were distributed among different plots. Within each plot, positions for representatives of each cross were assigned at random in a grid‐system, with 50 cm between transects, and 40 cm between plants within each transect line, leaving positions empty where wild C. fontanum plants were already present in the near vicinity. Coordinates for each planted seedling were recorded within the plot structures. This design resulted in one or two replicates per crossing/self‐pollination in each plot.

Data collection

Temperature recordings

Soil temperature at 10 cm depth was measured within 2 cm from each transplanted individual with a digital multi‐purpose thermometer, at least two times per year (28–30 June and 25–28 July in 2022, and 5–6 June and 15–16 August in 2023). These recording dates correspond approximately to the time of mean peak flowering for the whole area and the time of fruit maturation in each year, respectively. The average of the two soil temperature measurements for each plant and year was used as an estimate of soil temperature at the planting site. For some individuals (22 plants in 2022 and 1 plant in 2023), only one temperature measurement was available in one of the years. In these cases, we imputed the second temperature measurement by using the relationship between the first and second temperature values in individuals for which both measurements were available in that year (R 2 > 0.9 for linear regressions). In total, we were able to get estimates of temperature at the planting site for 814 individuals in 2022 and 837 individuals in 2023.

Plant recordings

All individuals were followed from 27 May to 15 September in 2022, and from 24 May to 4 September in 2023. Plant survival was recorded at the beginning of the sampling period. Floral development was monitored once a week during the sampling period. At each recording, the number of buds and flowers was counted, and the stage of the most advanced bud or flower on each plant was assigned to one of eight development stages. Based on this information, the most likely first flowering date (FFD) was assigned to each plant (see appendix S1 in Ehrlén et al., 2023 for details). At the time of fruit maturation, the total number of fruits developed was counted, and when available, three mature fruits were collected and brought to the laboratory, where the number of viable seeds was counted. When fewer fruits were available, all fruits were collected and counted. We estimated the total number of seeds as the product of the total number of fruits and the mean number of seeds per fruit. For nine individuals that produced fruits, we had information about the number of fruits produced but lacked information about the number of seeds per fruit. For these individuals, we used the mean total number of seeds of individuals within the same plot and year as an estimate of the total number of seeds produced. We excluded one individual that represented an extreme outlier with total number of seeds = 15 768 (Z‐score = 24).

Statistical analyses

Data analyses were performed using R v.4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024).

For each individual, we calculated mid‐parental temperature values as the mean of the temperatures at the source sites for the seeds used to raise sires and dams. We then calculated, for each individual and year, the difference between this mid‐parental temperature value, and the temperature at planting site, described above. A large value of this temperature difference means that the transplantation site deviates greatly from the average temperature of the individual's parents' source sites, and a small value means that transplantation and parents' source sites are relatively similar regarding soil temperature. A positive value of temperature difference means that the transplantation site was warmer than the parental sites, and a negative value means that the transplantation site was colder than the parental sites. If plants perform worse under the more stressful conditions that prevail on warmer soils (Prediction 1), we expect fitness components and overall fitness to decrease with temperature at the planting site. If plants are adapted to their thermal environment (Prediction 2), we expect fitness components and overall fitness to be highest when the temperature difference is close to zero (i.e. a negative quadratic effect of temperature difference).

We first assessed the effects of temperature at the planting site and temperature difference in each of the two study years on each of four multiplicative fitness components – survival (0 for dead, 1 for alive), flowering incidence in surviving individuals (0 for no flowers produced, 1 for one or more flowers produced), fruiting incidence in flowering individuals (0 for no mature fruits produced, 1 for one or more mature fruits produced), and total number of seeds produced in fruiting individuals. For these analyses, we fitted generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) using the glmmTMB package (Brooks et al., 2017). We used binomial distributions for survival, flowering and fruiting incidence, and a negative binomial distribution for total number of seeds. The negative binomial distribution fitted the actual distribution of total number of seeds in fruiting individuals better than a Poisson distribution (Fig. S2) and allowed us to account for overdispersion. The predictors included were temperature at the planting site, temperature difference (linear and quadratic terms), and mating type (cross‐ vs self‐pollinated) to test for potential effects of pollination mode. All models included plot as a random effect. We checked all GLMMs for multicollinearity using the performance package (Lüdecke et al., 2021) and found it not to be an issue in our models (Variance Inflation Factor < 5). We conducted model diagnostics using the dharma package (Hartig, 2022).

To measure local adaptation, it is key to use integrative measures of fitness that include all or most of the important fitness components. We therefore used Aster models to evaluate the effects of temperature at the planting site and temperature difference on overall fitness (Geyer et al., 2007; Shaw et al., 2008; Shaw & Geyer, 2010). The Aster statistical framework makes it possible to calculate such integrative measures by linking sequential fitness components, each modeled with its appropriate statistical distribution. We fitted one Aster model for each study year. Our Aster models incorporated the four fitness components mentioned above. Survival, flowering incidence in surviving individuals and fruiting incidence in flowering individuals were modeled as Bernouilli variables, and total number of seeds in fruiting individuals was modeled as a zero‐truncated negative binomial variable. As predictors, models included temperature at the planting site, temperature difference (linear and quadratic terms), mating type, and plot. In the Aster models, plot was included as a fixed instead of random effect because random‐effects Aster models do not allow to use the zero‐truncated negative binomial distribution (Geyer, 2023), and this distribution fitted the actual distribution of total number of seeds better than a Poisson distribution (Fig. S2). To estimate their effects on fitness, each predictor was fit at the level of total seed production in the model (Shaw et al., 2008). We determined the significance of each predictor by comparing sub‐models without the term of interest to the full model using likelihood ratio tests (LRTs). Due to the relatively high correlation between temperature at the planting site and temperature difference (Pearson's r was 0.76 in 2022 and 0.78 in 2023), we also refitted the GLMMs for fitness components and the Aster models including only one of these two variables in the model at the time, that is either temperature at the planting site or temperature difference (both linear and quadratic terms). To visualize the effects in our Aster models, we generated predictions across the observed ranges of temperature at the planting site and temperature difference and obtained uncertainty estimates using nonparametric bootstrapping (1000 bootstrap resamples per model).

To test whether a significant fraction of the fitness effects of temperature at the planting site and temperature difference were mediated by differences in flowering time (Prediction 3), we used piecewise structural equation modelling (piecewise SEM, Lefcheck, 2016). We fitted one SEM for each study year. Because we used FFD as a measure of flowering time, these models included only the subset of individuals that flowered in the study year. The fitness variable used was thus total number of seeds in flowering individuals, that is the product of the fitness components fruiting incidence in flowering individuals and total number of seeds in fruiting individuals. Each SEM consisted of two component models: (1) a linear mixed model of FFD as a function of temperature at the planting site, temperature difference (linear and quadratic terms) and mating type, and (2) a GLMM with negative binomial error distribution of fitness as a function of temperature at the planting site, temperature difference (linear and quadratic terms), mating type and FFD. A random effect of plot was included in all component models. Because the SEMs were saturated (i.e. there were no missing paths), we did not use a goodness‐of‐fit statistic for model evaluation, but directly tested significances of path coefficients.

Results

Of 857 individuals transplanted to the field in 2021, 95% survived to 2022, 74% of surviving plants flowered, and 97% of flowering plants produced at least one mature fruit. From 2022 to 2023, 65% of plants survived, 78% of surviving plants flowered, and 97% of flowering plants produced at least one mature fruit (Fig. S3). Total seed production in plants that produced at least one mature fruit ranged from 1 to 6183 in 2022 (mean ± SD = 535.3 ± 732.6) and from 2 to 2730 in 2023 (mean ± SD = 305.2 ± 386.4). Survival and total seed production were significantly higher in 2022 than in 2023 (Fig. S4).

Higher temperature at the planting site was associated with a lower survival in 2023, and with a lower incidence of flowering in both study years (Table 1; Fig. 1). Fruiting incidence and total number of seeds increased with higher soil temperatures in 2022, but not in 2023. The Aster model showed that the overall fitness increased significantly with temperature at the planting site in 2022, while it decreased significantly in 2023 (Table 2; Fig. 2).

A larger difference in temperature between the site of transplantation and the parental sites was associated with a lower incidence of fruiting in 2022, but only for individuals transplanted to sites that were warmer than their parental sites. In both years, larger temperature differences were associated with a lower total number of seeds in individuals transplanted to warmer sites, while the opposite was true for individuals transplanted to colder sites (Table 1; Fig. 1). In 2023, survival was lower in individuals experiencing smaller temperature differences. Overall fitness decreased with increasing temperature difference in both years in individuals transplanted to warmer sites (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Plants generated by self‐pollination had a lower incidence of flowering than those generated by cross‐pollination in both years, and a lower survival and lower total number of seeds in 2023 (Table 1). Overall fitness differed significantly between the mating types in 2023, being lower in plants generated by self‐pollination (Table 2).

Models for fitness components including only one of the predictors, temperature at the planting site or temperature difference, differed from the full models in some respects (Table S2; Fig. S5). In Aster models fitted with only one of these two variables at a time, fitness decreased significantly with temperature difference in 2023 (Table S3; Fig. S6).

Piecewise SEM models showed that plants flowered earlier (lower FFD) at higher transplantation site temperatures, and that earlier flowering plants had higher fitness (Fig. 3). Apart from the effect mediated by flowering time, in 2022 there was a direct positive effect of temperature at planting site on fitness. The effects of temperature difference were mediated by flowering time in 2023; plants with a smaller difference between temperature at transplantation and parental sites flowered earlier than plants with a larger difference (quadratic effect significant), and earlier flowering plants had higher fitness (Fig. 3). In 2022, there was a direct negative effect of temperature difference on fitness, but this effect was not significantly associated with flowering time (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, we found evidence of adaptation to differing soil temperature over short distances in a perennial herb inhabiting a geothermally heated sub‐arctic grassland. This adaptation to temperature was asymmetric; individuals transplanted to sites that were warmer than their source environments had a lower fitness than those transplanted to sites that were similar, or colder than their parental environment. Soil temperature at planting site and temperature difference between planting site and sites of origin both influenced flowering time, and earlier flowering plants had higher fitness. Our results thus show that plants are adapted to local soil temperature, and that soil temperature influences selection on flowering time. These findings suggest that changes in flowering phenology constitute an important adaptation to warmer conditions, and that adaptations of traits to warmer conditions are neutral under cooler conditions.

Soil temperature had significant effects on plants performance in both study years, but the direction of the effects differed between the 2 yr. In geothermal ecosystems, thermal conditions in heated areas allow for longer growing seasons than in unheated areas (Leblans et al., 2017). In accordance with positive effects on fitness of such a longer growing season, we found that C. fontanum individuals growing at higher soil temperatures had a higher overall fitness in 2022. However, in 2023 overall fitness decreased with temperature at the planting site, suggesting that other negative effects of soil temperature can outweigh the advantages. One possible cause of negative fitness effects in warm soils, is that the decoupling between soil and air temperatures in warmer soils makes soil temperature a poor cue for spring advancement, leading to increased risk of frost damage during early development (Inouye, 2008; Leblans et al., 2017; Ehrlén et al., 2023). This pattern is consistent with our finding that both survival (in 1 yr) and flowering incidence of C. fontanum decreased with increasing temperature at planting site. Another potential reason why soil heating is associated with a lower fitness is that it might disrupt interactions with pollinators; plants in warmer soils may flower earlier than their pollinators become active (Valdés et al., 2022). This might happen either because sessile plants are exposed to temperatures of a smaller area than mobile pollinators and therefore experience a stronger warming, or because plant development is more sensitive to soil temperature than is insect development (Forrest & Thomson, 2011; Kharouba & Vellend, 2015; Pyke et al., 2016; Thackeray et al., 2016). Finally, higher soil temperatures might have direct negative physiological effects on plant fitness (Crous, 2019). Soil temperature could also potentially affect plants, positively or negatively, through temperature‐mediated changes in soil properties. However, previous studies in this area have not found any significant differences in other abiotic factors, such as soil chemistry, elevation, or vegetation type related to soil temperature (O'Gorman et al., 2014).

We found evidence of adaptation to small‐scale variation in soil temperature; plants originating from warm soils outperformed plants from cold soils at warm transplantation sites (local vs foreign criterion, Kawecki & Ebert, 2004). Our study aligns with previous evidence of ecotypic differentiation and adaptation to local climatic conditions (López‐Goldar & Agrawal, 2021; Lortie & Hierro, 2022; Wadgymar et al., 2022). In addition, it indicates adaptation at a very small spatial scale, as a large part of the investigated temperature range occurs over distances of only tens of meters (Fig. S1, Valdés et al., 2025). Several previous studies with the herb Mimulus guttatus have found adaptation to geothermal soil heating over distances of a few hundred meters (Lekberg et al., 2012; Hendrick et al., 2016; Kolis et al., 2022). Other studies have found adaptation over small spatial scales in response also to metal contamination and other edaphic factors (McNeilly, 1968; Antonovics & Bradshaw, 1970; Rajakaruna, 2018), climate (Arietta & Skelly, 2021), and soil bacteria (Belotte et al., 2003). In our study area, this small‐scale adaptation appears to prevail in spite of some level of gene flow. Gene flow is facilitated by the short distances between heated and nonheated areas, but at the same time limited by phenological assortative mating and short‐distance dispersal of pollen and seeds in C. fontanum (Fitter & Peat, 1994; Weis & Kossler, 2004).

Interestingly, our results indicated that adaptation to soil temperature was asymmetric; warm‐origin plants had higher fitness than cold‐origin plants in warm environments, and performed as well as cold‐origin plants under cold conditions. Several previous studies have found evidence of asymmetric adaptation to environmental conditions (Mercer et al., 2008; Burford et al., 2014; Gosden et al., 2015; Ishizuka et al., 2021; Mainali et al., 2023). Our findings suggest that an earlier flowering time confers a fitness advantage in heated soils but is near neutral in unheated soils. Such conditional neutrality, where alleles entail a fitness advantage in one environment but are neutral in the contrasting environment, has been reported in several systems (Anderson et al., 2013, 2014; Wadgymar et al., 2017). In our system, the apparent lack of costs of heat tolerance would imply that stable polymorphisms and habitat specialization are less likely to develop than in the situation where there is a clear performance trade‐off between thermal habitats, that is antagonistic pleiotropy (Anderson et al., 2013, 2014). Although local adaptation has been suggested to be more often caused by conditional neutrality than by genetic trade‐offs (Wadgymar et al., 2017), there might be a bias against detection of genetic trade‐offs, as it is difficult to demonstrate that fitness effects of alleles are reversed in contrasting environments (Anderson et al., 2013). In this sense, a disadvantage of warm‐origin plants in cold environments in our study might have been too small to be detected. An artificial heating experiment with geothermal and nonthermal ecotypes of Agrostis stolonifera from the same geothermal ecosystem indeed found that increased heat tolerance of geothermal plants was associated with reduced performance under cooler conditions (Nuppenau et al., 2025). This suggests adaptations to geothermal heating in this ecosystem differ between species regarding the degree to which alleles favorable in heated soils have negative effects under unheated conditions, and that negative effects sometimes are small or nonexistent.

The results of our study have a couple of important methodological implications. First, patterns of adaptation to soil temperature differed considerably among different fitness components. This result is in line with previous findings (e.g. Zhang et al., 2021), and points at the importance of including multiple fitness components in assessments of local adaptation. Second, relationships between fitness and temperature at the planting site differed between the two study years, being positive in 2022, and negative in 2023. Differences in the effects of soil temperature between years are possibly associated with weather conditions. In our study, summer temperatures were slightly warmer in 2023 than in 2022 (The Icelandic Meterological Office, data not shown). Because the overall effects of soil temperature on fitness differed between years, temperature‐mediated selection is likely to also differ between years. Temporally varying selection has been documented frequently (e.g. Siepielski et al., 2009; Delph & Kelly, 2014), and implies that in our study system we should not expect adaptation to a certain soil temperature habitat to remain temporally consistent in the face of varying conditions (Benning & Moeller, 2019).

In addition to identifying soil temperature as a selective agent, we found that effects on fitness were partly mediated by flowering time, suggesting that selection on this trait is temperature‐dependent and that it has important fitness effects. In our study species, longer growing seasons, an increased risk of experiencing cold air temperatures at a given soil temperature, and mismatches with pollinators might have contributed to selection for an altered flowering time on heated soils. We found that flowering time mediated the effect of temperature difference in one of the two study years, when individuals planted in sites more similar to their thermal origin flowered earlier and had higher fitness. Moreover, a significant fraction of the effect of temperature at planting site on fitness was mediated by flowering time in both study years. In the analysis examining flowering time, individuals transplanted to warm sites flowered earlier and produced a higher number of seeds. This appears to be the opposite to the decrease in overall fitness with temperature at planting site observed in the Aster model. The difference is likely due to the fact that only flowering individuals were included in the former analysis, and the fitness measure did thus not include survival and flowering incidence. Similarly, but at a larger geographical scale, previous studies investigating selection on traits potentially associated with local adaptation found that selection on flowering time varied with elevation in Potentilla glandulosa (Clausen & Hiesey, 1958; Núñez‐Farfán & Schlichting, 2005). Based on the results of our study, we cannot ascertain that the observed association between flowering time and fitness differences represents a causal relationship. However, previous studies with the same study system have shown that flowering time is partly genetically based and that additive genetic variation for flowering time is associated with soil temperature in a counter‐gradient way (Valdés et al., 2019, 2025). Taken together with the results of this study, this strongly suggests that flowering time is a trait associated with adaptation to soil temperature. Results with other perennial species have shown earlier flowering in populations from higher latitudes, suggesting that flowering time might more generally be an important trait for local adaptation in response to temperature conditions (e.g. Turesson, 1930; Thorhallsdottir, 1998; Weber & Schmid, 1998; Olsson & Ågren, 2002). Our study adds to this previous evidence by showing that adaptation of flowering time to the local climate can occur not only along latitudinal gradients but also over distances of only a few meters.

Our study demonstrated local adaptation to geothermally induced differences in soil temperature in a perennial plant over spatial scales of only a few tens of meters, and identified flowering time as a key trait associated with this adaptation. Our results also show that adaptation was asymmetric; plants from heated soils performed well on cold soils while plants from cold soils did not perform well on warm soils. This suggests that trait adaptations to warm soils do not confer disadvantages on unheated soils. Lastly, our results suggest that phenological traits, such as flowering time, can be important traits associated with adaptation to temperature. A broader implication of our results is that adaptation to microclimate might be important in thermally variable environments. Given recent ample evidence of small‐scale variation in climate (e.g. Kemppinen et al., 2024), such local adaptation might constitute an important source of genetic variation within populations in many systems. In conclusion, our study highlights the importance of identifying the selective agents underlying local adaptation, as well as the traits under selection that are involved in this process. These findings suggest that plant populations can adapt to climate warming through rapid and fine‐scale evolutionary changes in flowering phenology.
