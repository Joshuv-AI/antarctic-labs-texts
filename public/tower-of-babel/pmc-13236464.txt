Simulation, artificial intelligence, and competency-based frameworks in veterinary medical education: a narrative review
Authors: Hui Zhang, Aoyun Li, Ying Li, Fazul Nabi, Hailong Dong, Yongjiang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceVeterinary medical education is rapidly evolving through the adoption of simulation, virtual reality (VR), artificial intelligence (AI), and competency-based frameworks. These innovations aim to address long-standing challenges such as limited patient availability, variable case exposure, and inconsistent assessment practices across institutions.ObservationsExisting studies show that high-fidelity simulators and VR improve procedural confidence, skill acquisition, and learner satisfaction while reducing the dependence on live animals. AI-assisted learning systems support decision-making and personalized instruction but raise concerns about accuracy, bias, and data governance. Competency-based education frameworks, including entrustable professional activities and programmatic assessment, can improve workplace readiness, but their application varies widely between veterinary schools. Persistent barriers include unequal access to digital resources, high implementation costs, and limited faculty training.Conclusions and RelevanceEvidence from the literature suggests that, when integrated appropriately, simulation technologies, digital learning environments, and AI-assisted tools collectively enhance the key elements of veterinary training. These approaches can support safer clinical practice, help standardize skill development, and broaden access to quality training, particularly in resource-limited settings. Instead of hypothesizing new mechanisms, this review uses current evidence to highlight practical implications for curriculum design and clinical preparedness. Continued investment in faculty development, ethical use of AI, and infrastructure is essential for achieving sustainable, technology-enhanced veterinary education.Keywords: Blended learning; education, veterinary; virtual reality; entrustable professional activities; faculty development

INTRODUCTION

Veterinary medical education is rapidly evolving to improve the clinical competence, reduce reliance on live animals, and integrate technology-enhanced learning. Simulation-based training has become a key approach to strengthening skill acquisition and overall graduate readiness. A recent meta-analysis of 71 studies reported a moderate overall effect (Hedges’ g = 0.49), showing that simulation training consistently leads to superior learning outcomes compared to traditional instructions [1]. This effect spans a wide spectrum of veterinary clinical activities, including suturing, dissection, minimally invasive surgery, anesthesia and airway management, vascular access, rectal and reproductive examinations, radiographic interpretation, and clinical decision-making tasks [1]. Other studies similarly reported reduced dependence on live animals and cadavers, with improved student competence when high-fidelity simulators are incorporated into the curricula [2,3]. These trends align with broader movements in health professions education, emphasizing structured, high-fidelity experiential learning for enhancing technical and non-technical skills [4,5].

Advanced modalities, such as virtual reality (VR), improve procedural confidence and clinical reasoning. VR-based cardiac dissection and canine endotracheal intubation training showed better performance than video-based or traditional methods [6,7]. Low-fidelity tools, including obstetric boxes, also enhance procedural proficiency and learner confidence [4]. These results show that instructional format, hands-on, virtual, or blended, significantly influences clinical skill development and student readiness.

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic accelerated the adoption of remote and digitally mediated learning. Peer-designed virtual case simulations significantly enhanced clinical judgment, communication, and decision-making of fourth-year veterinary students in Japan’s six-year curriculum, with marked improvements on the Modified Lasater Clinical Judgment Rubric [7]. Peer role-play interventions also enhanced communication skills and reflective learning, supporting more authentic engagement with clinical scenarios [8].

In parallel, artificial intelligence (AI) offers new opportunities and challenges. Veterinary AI applications, such as imaging interpretation, disease prediction, and administrative automation, now report diagnostic accuracies above 90% in some areas [9,10]. Nevertheless, concerns about algorithmic bias, data quality, transparency, and ethical use remain underexplored in veterinary contexts, as have been highlighted in the general education literature [11]. Although large language model (LLM)-based educational tools have been well studied in human health professions, where issues of privacy, fairness, and reproducibility are well documented [12], veterinary education is only beginning to examine these risks. Previous reviews highlight the need for ethical, transparent, and context-appropriate AI adoption to prevent misinterpretations and protect academic integrity, as reported in the general education literature [12] and in veterinary-specific contexts [13].

These developments illustrate a clear trajectory, i.e., veterinary education is shifting toward integrated, technology-enhanced pedagogies in which simulations, digital environments, and AI systems intersect. Existing reviews have addressed these elements separately, but none have discussed them within an integrated conceptual perspective. This transition also brings challenges, including instructor training, tool validation, and thoughtful curricular integration. Therefore, this narrative review consolidates evidence across simulation technologies, AI applications, virtual learning approaches, and competency-based frameworks. In addition, it identifies pedagogical and ethical constraints, examines how these domains interact, and outlines future directions and integrative perspectives to inform the ongoing evolution of veterinary education.

METHODS

This narrative review synthesizes the peer-reviewed literature on simulation-based veterinary education, virtual and augmented reality, artificial intelligence-supported learning, digital teaching tools, and competency-based instructional frameworks. The publications evaluated were identified through searches of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar between January 2000 and December 2024, a period chosen to encompass the emergence of modern simulation and digital technologies in veterinary training. The search terms included combinations of “veterinary education,” “simulation,” “virtual reality,” “artificial intelligence,” “competency-based education,” “entrustable professional activities,” “digital learning,” and “technology-enhanced learning.”

No Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram or formal screening procedure was applied because this was a narrative review rather than a systematic review. Studies were selected based on their conceptual and pedagogical relevance, their contribution to historical or current trends, and their alignment with the thematic scope of this review. Priority was given to empirical studies, narrative or scoping reviews, consensus statements, and educational position papers. Literature that was not peer reviewed (except for one preprint) and non-English publications were excluded. This approach enabled an integrative and interpretive use of data that connects foundational literature with emerging evidence in technology-enhanced veterinary education.

OBSERVATIONS

Veterinary education through history

Veterinary education historically relied on apprenticeship and experiential learning, reflected in the maxim “see one, do one, teach one,” as described in the medical education literature [14]. Since the establishment of the first veterinary school in Lyon in 1761, apprenticeship-based clinical training dominated into the late 20th century [15]. Its strengths included progressive responsibility, professional identity formation, and close faculty–student interaction, as described in the veterinary education literature [15,16] and general learning theory [17]. Nevertheless, this model increasingly showed limitations such as unequal case exposure, reliance on variable clinical caseloads, welfare concerns regarding invasive procedures on live animals, and inconsistent preparedness for diverse practice environments [14,18,19,20,21].

By the early 2000s, these constraints prompted the development of clinical skills laboratories using low- and high-fidelity models that provided standardized, low-risk opportunities to practice procedures such as intubation, suturing, and catheterization. These laboratories improved technical skills, reduced anxiety, and supported animal welfare, marking a shift toward evidence-based and simulation-supported pedagogy [1,2,18,19,20,22].

Simultaneously, rural workforce shortages, exacerbated by graduates’ preference for urban companion-animal practice, highlighted the gaps in exposure to rural and mixed-animal contexts [5,23]. Workplace-based learning (WBL) emerged to address these needs, integrating supervised clinical immersion, feedback-rich environments, and competency-based assessment [16,24]. Hybrid curricula then evolved, combining simulation training with structured placements, framed within the scaffolded and spiral learning designs to promote progressive competency development [25].

Situated learning theory further emphasized the value of communities of practice involving private practitioners, clients, and rural agencies, where mentorship and continuity across placements improved the quality of feedback and learner confidence [24,26].

Despite these advances, challenges persist. For example, simulations cannot fully replicate unpredictable live-animal scenarios; rural placements carry logistical and financial burdens, and assessment practices vary considerably. Entrustable professional activities (EPAs), increasingly explored in veterinary contexts, offer structured, observable units of professional practice to support more consistent entrustment decisions [5,16,21,23]. EPAs are units of professional work, such as taking history, performing an examination, or developing a diagnostic plan that learners can be entrusted to perform independently once they demonstrate sufficient competence [27].

Therefore, veterinary education has shifted from informal apprenticeships toward hybrid, competency-based models that integrate simulation, WBL, and structured assessment to enhance safety, equity of exposure, and workforce readiness. Immersive simulation-based learning

Simulation-based approaches have expanded substantially in veterinary education, providing safer and more consistent opportunities to develop technical and non-technical skills. Early simulators ranged from simple task trainers to full-scale mannequins replicating the anatomical and physiological features, enabling repeated practice of procedures such as intubation, suturing, and catheter placement without risk to patients [18]. Clinical skills laboratories equipped with these models have become increasingly common, enhancing learning, supporting animal welfare, and complementing live-animal training [18,28].

Manikins for canine, bovine, and equine practice have clear educational value. A VR haptic equine colic simulator allows repeated rectal palpation and internal examinations without stressing live horses [29,30], while similar models for reproductive exams and venipuncture improve confidence and procedural competence [2]. Surveys of veterinary schools in North America and Europe report the widespread use of dog and cow simulators for injections, venipuncture, catheterization, and basic examinations, with most laboratories offering open access for self-directed practice and feedback-based learning [31]. Simulation training also improves preparedness in complex tasks. Dedicated sessions for anesthetic induction significantly increased students’ confidence and self-rated readiness, with scores rising from 2.1 to 4.2 out of 5 [32]. High-fidelity and immersive veterinary emergency simulations, such as dystocia-based training modules and other high-stakes scenarios, provide structured opportunities to develop clinical decision-making under pressure while supporting communication and teamwork skills [33,34,35]. Moreover, learners exposed to high-fidelity simulations show improved non-technical performance [32]. Simulation-based objective structural clinical examination objective structured clinical examinations (OSCEs) in veterinary education strengthen the assessment reliability by standardizing scenarios at the “shows how” level of Miller’s pyramid of competence [36,37]. Pedagogically, simulations align with scaffolded and spiral learning, progressing from low- to high-fidelity tasks and culminating in OSCE-style evaluations [22]. Proficiency-based progression and mastery learning models also yield better live-animal performance and reduced variability [38,39]. Nevertheless, simulations cannot fully replicate unpredictable behavior in live animals. For example, VR-trained students scored higher on knowledge but achieved intubation success comparable to video-trained peers during real procedures, highlighting the limits in adaptive skill development [7]. Therefore, simulation-based learning markedly enhances technical, non-technical, and assessment outcomes, but optimal training requires balancing simulation with structured live-animal experience. VR and standardized patients

VR has attracted interest in veterinary education as an immersive platform for practicing clinical and procedural skills without risking patient safety. VR developers incorporate evidence-based principles such as interactivity, feedback loops, graded difficulty, and real-time guidance to enhance learning [40]. In a study evaluating canine anesthesia induction and intubation simulators, students trained with video plus VR achieved higher descriptive test scores (median 63.3/100 vs. 51.5/100, p = 0.01) and similar real-world intubation success (84.3% vs. 77.4%), with high usability ratings [7]. Another randomized trial examining VR-based anesthesia machine training showed slightly lower VR exam scores and cybersickness, likely due to the cognitive load, but strong user satisfaction, comparable training time, and positive engagement were noted [41]. VR also has potential for communication and clinical-reasoning training. In medical education, AI-driven virtual patient systems have been validated for training purposes. For example, SOPHIE is an AI-based virtual platform that provides automated feedback on communication performance [42]. Veterinary counterparts remain limited, but standardized client (SC) programs using trained actors or peers to simulate pet-owner interactions offer parallel benefits. Studies have shown that SC-based training yields higher OSCE communication scores than traditional methods, with students valuing authenticity and consistent responses [43]. Audio-recorded SC encounters, including sensitive topics such as euthanasia, provide rich and standardized assessment opportunities [44]. Longitudinal SC exposure (up to 27 encounters) improves confidence, empathy, history taking, and adaptability [45]. Despite the educational advantages, scaling VR and SC programs remains challenging. High-fidelity VR systems require substantial investment, maintenance, and dedicated space [40], while SC programs demand actor recruitment, scenario development, scheduling, and staff oversight [43]. Technical issues further complicate adoption, such as VR glitches, cybersickness, connectivity problems, and challenges with maintaining standardization [41]. Instructors’ education and development are also critical because educators must competently operate VR tools, interpret analytics, and conduct structured debriefing, as reported in the health care education literature [46] and veterinary contexts [47]. More recently, AI-driven adaptive learning systems are increasingly being incorporated into veterinary curricula, supporting knowledge retention and practical skills development, particularly in rural or instructor-limited training environments [48]. These studies show that VR and standardized patients enhance technical and communication competencies when integrated within blended curricula. Institutions that combine VR, SC programs, and supervised live clinical experiences supported by adequate staffing and instructors’ training achieve the strongest educational outcomes. Digital & distance education

The COVID-19 pandemic forced a rapid transition to online learning across veterinary education, imposing significant challenges for delivering traditionally hands-on content. In Egypt, approximately two-thirds of the 500 surveyed students reported enthusiasm for online anatomy and found digital materials useful. On the other hand, fewer than half believed virtual anatomy could replace face-to-face dissection, emphasizing the need for 3-dimensional (3D) tools and instructor-guided sessions [49]. Similarly, a study of 684 students in Iran reported that most were comfortable with online platforms, but only 14% felt that virtual teaching was sufficient for practical anatomy, with 82% reporting compromised hands-on learning despite preserved theoretical understanding [50]. These findings highlight the need for hybrid curricula integrating digital and in-person modalities.

Post-pandemic institutions adopted flipped and peer-assisted approaches. A flipped physiology module improved the exam scores by 16% and was appreciated for applied learning [51]. A peer-assisted flipped anatomy model improved collaboration, reflection, and formative feedback, highlighting the adaptability of active learning strategies in medical education [52]. This study hypothesized that this can serve as a basis for application in veterinary education.

Clinical rotations that rely heavily on patient interaction were the most substantial disruption. A fully virtual fourth-year rotation delivered via Zoom, Canvas, and Echo360 received higher student ratings than previous in-person cohorts owing to increased feedback and enhanced theoretical discussions, but it remained insufficient for hands-on and communication skills [53]. Remote OSCEs using home-based models achieved comparable pass rates and low rates of academic misconduct, highlighting their feasibility when supervision and structure are strong [54].

Significant challenges persisted, including limited internet access, device shortages, fatigue, loneliness, and reduced interaction, which were common, particularly in resource-constrained settings [55]. Students also experienced concentration difficulties and “digital fatigue,” mirroring the findings in human medical education [56].

Despite these limitations, remote and blended learning offered lasting benefits, including flexible pacing and access to recorded content. Innovations such as 3D anatomical sketching and virtual dissection in human medical education highlight the potential for veterinary adoption as supplements to laboratory teaching [57]. Therefore, these online formats effectively support theoretical learning, but essential clinical and practical skills require hybrid delivery.

Generative AI and automation

The use of ChatGPT and other LLMs in veterinary education has increased rapidly, offering opportunities and challenges. A recent survey of Australian Doctor of Veterinary Medicine students reported that more than 75% were already using ChatGPT or similar tools, finding outputs practical, though not always accurate. Students who completed a structured appraisal task showed a clearer understanding of the benefits and limitations of AI, supporting the need for formal curricular integration [58].

LLMs have been used to generate scenarios, write questions, and provide learning support. In an equine medicine course, students used ChatGPT to develop and critique multiple-choice questions, and 77% reported improved understanding of complex concepts and 68% noted better critical thinking [59]. Nevertheless, LLMs underperform on veterinary examinations because GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 achieved 55% and 77%, respectively, both below student performance (86%), and were prone to generating plausible but incorrect answers [60]. These limitations suggest that unsupervised reliance on LLMs risks inaccurate learning and flawed clinical reasoning.

Several educational advantages exist, such as on-demand tutoring, rapid scenario creation, and assistance with summaries, case reflections, and Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan (SOAP) note drafting [12,13]. LLMs can simulate patient cases and adapt explanations to diverse learning styles, allowing greater focus on reasoning. Nevertheless, risks such as hallucinations, bias, academic misconduct, and overreliance remain significant [58,60]. Digital literacy gaps and subscription barriers further complicate equitable adoption, as highlighted in the general education literature [12]. Ethical concerns around privacy, transparency, and data quality are also insufficiently addressed in veterinary settings, drawing from insights in general education [12] and human medicine and healthcare [61].

Overreliance on AI may weaken critical thinking skills; evidence from broader higher education shows that the uncritical use of ChatGPT can bypass essential cognitive processes [12,62]. Best practices emphasize human-in-the-loop (human oversight of AI-generated outputs to verify accuracy, ensure safety, and guide learning) supervision, guided AI use, and post-generation critique. In a study at City University of Hong Kong (CityU), a structured AI-supported multiple-choice question (MCQ) activity required veterinary students to use ChatGPT to generate, critique, and refine assessment questions, followed by guided reflection on the accuracy, reasoning quality, and limitations of AI-generated content [59]. Exercises, such as the CityU MCQ activity, are most effective when embedded within reflective analysis frameworks [59]. The instructor’s education and development are essential for safe integration. Educators must understand the LLM behavior, detect inaccuracies, and uphold policies addressing authorship, data security, and academic integrity [12,13,61]. AI literacy training covering bias detection, hallucination awareness, and appropriate use contexts supports responsible adoption [12,58]. Studies showed that Generative AI can enhance engagement and efficiency when paired with critical reflection, clear guidelines, instructor oversight, and ethical safeguards [12,13,56,59]. Simulation, virtual learning environments, and generative AI are often discussed as discrete educational technologies, but their effective and sustainable use in veterinary education requires a structured curricular and assessment framework. Competency-Based Veterinary Education (CBVE) and Evidence-Based Veterinary Medicine (EBVM) provide this integrative structure by linking technology-enhanced learning activities, such as simulation-based training, AI-supported exercises, and digital assessments to observable competencies, longitudinal feedback, and entrustment decisions. In this context, digital portfolios, programmatic assessment systems, and technology-enabled feedback mechanisms serve as the connective infrastructure that aligns educational technologies with competency development and graduate readiness, as discussed in healthcare literature [61] and veterinary education frameworks [63].

Competency-based and evidence-based curriculum

Competency-based and evidence-based veterinary education aims to ensure graduates are prepared for real-world clinical responsibilities. The CBVE framework developed by the American Association of Veterinary Medical Colleges (AAVMC) defines domains, such as clinical reasoning, communication, and professionalism, aligning curricula and assessments with observable outcomes and supporting learner progression through milestones and EPAs [63,64,65].

CBVE emphasizes longitudinal development rather than episodic teaching [24]. Programmatic assessment commonly integrates direct observation tools, such as mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise, a structured, real-time workplace-based assessment of a student’s clinical encounter, multisource feedback, an assessment method in which feedback is gathered from supervisors, peers, clients, and other team members, and self-assessment within digital portfolios reviewed by mentors and competency committees, improving the assessment reliability and feedback quality [66]. OSCEs continue to play a central role in evaluating technical and communication skills [67]. The AAVMC CBVE Assessment Toolkit 2024 outlines appropriate mapping of assessment tools to competency domains, including entrustment and supervision scales [64]. Repeated simulation and Direct Observation of Procedural Skills-based assessment further improve the procedural performance and reduce errors in live-animal settings [21,39].

Evidence-Based Veterinary Medicine (EBVM) strengthens these frameworks by developing skills in literature searching, critical appraisal, and evidence application [68]. On the other hand, studies in North America indicate inconsistent EBVM delivery due to the instructor’s workload and limited access to evidence resources [69]. Proposed solutions include librarian collaboration, point-of-care databases, and incorporating evidence appraisal into case-based teaching [70]. Embedding EBVM within CBVE ensures that students perform clinical tasks based on the best available evidence [64].

Digital portfolios capture competency progression across multiple assessment modalities, supporting formative coaching and summative decisions, as evidenced in veterinary contexts [66] and the medical education literature [71]. EPAs such as history taking, diagnostic planning, and anesthesia management offer structured, workplace-based assessments that enhance clarity and guide entrustment decisions, as described in medical education literature [71] and veterinary contexts [72].

Implementation challenges include the instructors’ education and development in assessment literacy, the maintenance of digital portfolio systems, and the standardization of entrustment benchmarks across programs, drawing from medical education models [71] and veterinary frameworks [73]. Despite these challenges, institutions adopting CBVE report improved coherence between the curricula and graduate outcomes, supported by structured portfolios and regular feedback [66,73]. The CBVE Assessment Toolkit further enables the alignment of assessment strategies with competency goals [64].

DISCUSSION

This review shows that technology-enhanced veterinary education spanning simulation, virtual and distance learning, generative AI, and competency-based frameworks offers substantial benefits, but its implementation remains uneven across global contexts. Despite the strong evidence that simulations improve procedural performance, reduce errors, and enhance non-technical skills, widespread adoption is limited by high infrastructure costs, maintenance demands, and the need for structured instructional environments. Studies in health and veterinary education consistently report that the initial and recurrent simulation expenses can exceed $170,000–$270,000 per training cycle, placing substantial strain on institutions in low- and middle-income regions where personnel and facility costs are major barriers [74,75]. Even when simulators are available, underutilization often stems from inadequate scheduling systems, insufficient instructor preparation, and suboptimal instructional design, reflecting a broader trend in healthcare education in which sustained outcomes depend heavily on environmental and pedagogical readiness [76].

The education and development of instructors emerge as critical determinants of successful reform. High-fidelity simulation, blended pedagogies, competency-based assessment, and AI-enhanced instruction require educators who understand alignment between learning goals, activities, and assessments. Evidence highlights that instructor training influences the effectiveness of simulation and AI tools more than the technologies themselves [23]. On the other hand, resource constraints often limit access to formal training in digital pedagogy, instructional design, and competency-based education. Significant disparities have been documented across veterinary programs, including Korean institutions, where uneven instructor preparation has resulted in variable graduate readiness [77]. Comparable challenges have affected the adoption of the AAVMC CBVE framework and international competency models, such as the World Organisation for Animal Health “Day 1 Competencies,” which face inconsistent uptake due to limited institutional capacity and infrastructure [73,78]. In the future, blended and AI-enabled learning models offer promising platforms for scalable, inclusive veterinary education. Hybrid instruction incorporating simulation and asynchronous modules has improved student confidence and knowledge acquisition, as reported in a veterinary communication course reporting significant improvement in post-course scores [79]. AI-driven adaptive learning systems, already effective in human medical training through tools such as MedSimAI [80], are beginning to enhance veterinary curricula by improving knowledge retention and practical skills, particularly in rural settings with limited availability of instructors [48]. Nevertheless, responsible integration requires ethical governance, human oversight, transparency, and institutional policies on algorithmic use, privacy, and data security.

A major gap remains the lack of veterinary-specific research validating the long-term outcomes of blended and AI-supported educational models. Current evidence is largely derived from the human medical literature, underscoring the need for veterinary research agendas focused on simulation OSCEs, digital portfolios, and adaptive learning analytics across diverse contexts [72].

Sustainable progress will depend on coordinated investment in infrastructure, equitable connectivity, instructor development, and cross-institutional collaborations. Shared simulation libraries, interoperable digital platforms, and standardized competency frameworks can reduce the inequities and improve global consistency. Recent comparative analyses of veterinary education systems also emphasize that disparities in curriculum structure, practical training resources, and evaluation standards across countries require coordinated international reforms to prevent the widening gaps in graduate competence [81]. If this strategic support is unavailable, the field risks fragmentation and persistent disparities in graduate competence. Through deliberate development and ethical implementation, veterinary education is positioned to achieve a globally connected, evidence-based, and technologically enabled model capable of meeting emerging challenges in animal and public health.
