The Tutopatch® assisted surgery for complex strabismus study: TASCS‐study
Authors: Ires A. W. Verhees, Hinke Marijke Jellema, Anne‐Marie E. L. Langenhorst, B. Jeroen Klevering, Laurentius J. van Rijn, Annabel L. W. Groot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the efficacy of tendon elongation using bovine pericardium (Tutopatch®) in complex strabismus surgeries, with a specific focus on longer follow‐up of postoperative outcomes and patient satisfaction.MethodsIn this pre‐post cohort study, the records of all patients who underwent tendon elongation strabismus surgery with Tutopatch® at Radboudumc and Amsterdam UMC were retrospectively reviewed. Eligible patients were invited for a single prospective follow‐up visit to assess long‐term outcomes. Strabismus angle (success defined as ≤5.71° of orthotropia) and duction limitations (converted to a pseudo‐quantitative scale ranging from 0 to 5) were evaluated preoperatively, 1 week postoperatively, 3 months postoperatively and at longer intervals where possible. Patient satisfaction was measured on a Visual Analogue Scale at the prospective follow‐up.ResultsSixty‐nine participants (mean age 41.3 ± 18.4 years) were included. Fifty‐one patients had childhood onset and eighteen patients had acquired onset of strabismus. Tutopatch® was predominantly used to correct the horizontal deviation. Long‐term follow‐up data (>17.4 weeks) were available for 49 patients (71%), with a median follow‐up of 124 weeks (range 19.9–431 weeks) and surgical success was achieved in 73% (near) and 60% (distance). Duction limitations significantly increased postoperatively, especially in exodeviations, but improved over time and seemed to have minimal impact on ocular alignment in primary position. Patient satisfaction was very high (median VAS 9.0), with only weak‐to‐moderate correlation with ocular alignment.ConclusionTutopatch® is a viable surgical option for tendon elongation in complex strabismus cases with favorable long‐term ocular alignment, mild duction limitation and high patient satisfaction.

INTRODUCTION

Strabismus has a tendency to recur, and multiple surgeries may be needed, resulting in more complex procedures to achieve proper ocular alignment and patient satisfaction (Kushner, 2014; Lee et al., 2023). In cases where further muscle repositioning is not possible, alternative techniques must be considered, as attaching the muscle behind the globe equator may result in globe retraction instead of globe movement, termed ‘duction limitation’ (Coats & Olitsky, 2017). To address this, hang‐back sutures may be used, allowing the muscle to ‘hang back’ without direct attachment to the globe while maintaining the muscle origin anterior to the globe equator (Potter & Nelson, 1990). An alternative to this technique is tendon elongation, which enables the correction of larger strabismus angles and maintains a proper arc of contact. This technique utilizes various materials such as polytetrafluoroethylene (Gore‐Tex), silicone and fascia lata (Langmann et al., 2006; Talebnejad et al., 2008; Wright, 2000). Another option is bovine pericardium (Tutopatch®). This material has been used for a longer period in other medical fields, such as in cardiology for heart valve repair (Kidane et al., 2009), but remains relatively new in ophthalmology.

Four studies particularly focused on patients with Graves' orbitopathy have demonstrated that the use of Tutopatch® in strabismus surgery yields positive results, with minimal complications (Esser et al., 2011; Jellema et al., 2025; Oeverhaus et al., 2018; Wipf et al., 2018). Despite these promising results, the application of Tutopatch® in other types of strabismus surgery remains underexplored (Elabbasy et al., 2022; Hedergott et al., 2021; van Rijn et al., 2016), and further research is crucial to determine its broader applicability. Due to the tendency of strabismus to recur, long‐term outcomes are essential for evaluating sustained ocular alignment. Follow‐up periods in prior studies were limited (Hedergott et al., 2021; van Rijn et al., 2016). Factors such as Tutopatch® length, which may influence surgical success, have also not been thoroughly explored in studies involving this material. In addition to clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction is a key determinant of surgical success. The surgery can improve visual function, facial appearance and psychosocial well‐being. Previous research highlights that patients often experience enhanced self‐esteem, improved social interactions and reduced anxiety after strabismus surgery, with notable gains in confidence, relationships and overall quality of life (Al Shehri et al., 2020; Arblaster et al., 2024).

This study aims to address these gaps by further investigating the efficacy of tendon elongation using Tutopatch® in complex, non‐Graves' orbitopathy strabismus cases, with a specific focus on a more extended follow‐up period and patient satisfaction.

METHODS

Study design

This pre‐post cohort study consisted of two components: (1) a retrospective data analysis regarding a cohort of patients who underwent strabismus surgery with Tutopatch® and (2) a single prospective orthoptic evaluation of eligible patients from the same cohort to extend the follow‐up period. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association 2013, Brazil). A waiver was obtained from the Medical Ethics Review committees of both Radboudumc and Amsterdam UMC.

Study population

All patients who underwent strabismus surgery with Tutopatch® at Radboudumc between 1 January 2021 and 1 September 2024 (performed by ALWG) and at Amsterdam UMC between 1 January 2016 and 1 September 2024 (performed by LJvR) were included. Eligible patients were invited for a one‐time follow‐up examination between 1 March 2025 and 11 April 2025. Inclusion criterion was: patients aged ≥18 years at the time of inclusion. Exclusion criteria were: patients with Graves' orbitopathy and patients who underwent revision surgery on the muscle where Tutopatch® was applied.

Surgery

For each patient, tendon elongation was determined to be the most suitable, and often the only, surgical option. Patients were informed about the nature and purpose of Tutopatch® (Tutogen Medical GmbH, Neuenkirchen am Brand, Germany) a biological graft composed of bovine pericardium that has been processed and preserved for medical use. Verbal informed consent was obtained for both the surgery and its use. The required amount of Tutopatch® was based on a dose‐response method described by van Rijn et al. (2016) in which the length equalled half the intended additional recession. Axial length was taken into account, with adjustments made for notably short or long eyes (Gräf et al., 1994). Graft length was measured intraoperatively from suture to suture, allowing for application with an estimated accuracy of approximately 0.5 mm. For further surgical details, see van Rijn et al. (2016).

Study procedures

For the prospective part, patients who underwent strabismus surgery after the Tutopatch procedure were excluded as their postoperative outcomes could not reliably be attributed to the initial intervention. All remaining eligible patients were invited to participate in a one‐time prospective evaluation.

Reliability assessment of duction scores

A measurement validation was done to assess the measurement of duction limitation, as patients were clinically assessed by different orthoptists in the two centres. In both clinics, duction limitations are routinely graded using the descriptive categories: none, trace, small, moderate, large and very large. For analysis purposes, these clinical judgements were converted to a pseudo‐quantitative scale ranging from 0 to 5, where 0 indicates no limitation and 5 indicates a very large limitation.

To evaluate the consistency of this scale between and within orthoptists, we used a standardized set of digitally manipulated photographs, as described by van Rijn et al. (2016) These images were reassessed in the current study to determine whether orthoptists from different clinical centres apply the same grading in a comparable manner. Eight orthoptists (three from Radboudumc and five from Amsterdam UMC) independently rated the full image set twice, with a two‐week interval between assessments, to assess both inter‐ and intra‐rater reliability.

Data collection

Retrospective data were extracted from the electronic patient records and included demographics (age, gender), ocular history and diagnoses, details of the surgical procedure and if present complications, target angle and the reason for using Tutopatch®. Orthoptic exam including: strabismus angle at near and distance (standardized methods per clinic, see below), measured preoperatively, approximately 1 week and approximately 3 months (between 2 and 4 months) postoperatively and when additional postoperative time points were available, duction limitations; ocular motility in 4 positions of gaze (up, down, right and left) and if available 9‐gaze photographs (See Vision, 2024); monocular visual acuity; stereopsis (using TNO or Titmus Fly test); fusion ability (measured with prism bars with step vergence method) (Lança & Rowe, 2019); axial length or refractive spherical equivalent.

Standardized methods per clinic for measuring the strabismus angle

Strabismus angle was measured at near (30 cm) with the alternate prism cover test (APCT) or with a custom‐made Maddox device (Amsterdam UMC only), and at distance (5 m, 6 m or infinity) with the APCT. In cases of poor vision in one eye, the Krimsky test was used (Choi & Kushner, 1998). For analysis, the prism outcome was converted to degrees with the formula: strabismus angle in degree = arctan(prism dioptre/100).

Stereopsis and fusion

Since no ‘golden’ standard exists for categorizing stereopsis or fusional vergence amplitudes, the following classification criteria were applied to facilitate statistical comparison across participants. These thresholds were based on commonly used cut‐off values reported in the literature (Lança & Rowe, 2019; Mohan & Sharma, 2022; O'Connor et al., 2010). Stereopsis was defined as normal when ≤120 arcseconds, reduced when >120 arcseconds and absent when not measurable. Fusion was defined as normal with a total fusion range of ≥42Δ at near (12Δ base‐in to 30Δ base‐out) and ≥26Δ at distance (8Δ base‐in to 18Δ base‐out); reduced when below these thresholds; and absent when no fusion was present.

Prospective data were collected with consent during the voluntary follow‐up visit and included strabismus angle at near and distance, duction limitations, ocular motility in 4 positions of gaze and 9‐gaze photographs (See Vision, 2024), monocular visual acuity of both eyes, patient satisfaction with ocular alignment using a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) from 0 (very dissatisfied) to 10 (very satisfied).

Parameters

There is no universal clinical definition of success in terms of ocular alignment (Al‐Jabri et al., 2021; Chiu et al., 2014). However, a deviation within 5.71° (≤10Δ) of orthotropia (0°) is commonly considered clinically acceptable (Elliott & Shafiq, 2013; Hatt et al., 2016; MacKenzie et al., 2016; Rowe & Noonan, 2017). Therefore, the primary outcome was the proportion of patients with a postoperative strabismus angle ≤5.71° of 0°, and with the predefined target angle.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics and study outcomes. The primary outcome was the proportion of success (≤5.71° of 0° and target angle), with 95% confidence intervals calculated using the Wilson method for proportions. Associations between potential risk factors (e.g., stereopsis, fusion, visual acuity) and unsuccessful outcomes were evaluated using chi‐squared tests. Associations between an unsuccessful outcome and the number of prior strabismus surgeries, Tutopatch® length and axial length were performed with independent‐samples t‐tests and one‐way anova.

Duction limitations were analysed on a pseudo‐quantitative 0–5 scale. Changes between pre‐ and postoperative scores were assessed using McNemar's test, in which no duction limitation was defined as success. Linear mixed models were used to analyse stability of the strabismus angle and duction scores across postoperative follow‐up points. The dependent variable was either the strabismus angle or the duction limitation score. Time was included as a fixed effect, and individual subjects were included as random intercepts to account for within‐subject correlations due to repeated measurements.

Patient satisfaction was analysed descriptively. Normality was assessed, and median (range) values were reported accordingly. The association between ocular deviation and patient satisfaction was evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients. The level of agreement of duction scores, both intra‐ and inter‐observer variability, was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). Correlation coefficients were interpreted based on absolute values (|r|), with thresholds for very strong (≥0.9–1.0), strong (≥0.7 <0.9), moderate (≥0.5 <0.7), weak (≥0.3 <0.5) and no correlation (<0.3).

RESULTS

Sixty‐nine participants with a mean age of 41.3 ± 18.4 years were included in this study (Table 1 and Tables S1–, S4). Nine participants were only included in the retrospective part of the study, as they underwent strabismus surgery after the Tutopatch® procedure. The remaining 60 participants were invited for a prospective follow‐up examination, of whom 31 participated. Long‐term data (>4 months i.e. >17.4 weeks postoperative) were available for 49 participants (71%), with a median follow‐up of 124 weeks (range 19.9–431 weeks).

Patient characteristics.

Fifty‐one patients had childhood onset of strabismus. Eighteen patients had acquired onset of strabismus: four following ocular trauma, three due to sixth nerve palsy, three after retinal detachment, three associated with high myopia, two following glaucoma implant (Baerveldt) surgery, one due to a brain tumour and one after monocular vision loss from hypertensive retinopathy. Many patients with childhood‐onset strabismus were nevertheless clinically complex, mostly due to restricted ductions related to multiple previous strabismus procedures. Tutopatch® was mainly applied for horizontal correction, while vertical deviations were addressed using standard surgical techniques when indicated. In most cases, Tutopatch® was used because patients did not want surgery on the sound eye or conventional surgery was insufficient (see Table 1). Three patients had ‘other reason’ for using Tutopatch®: one for vertical displacement of the medial and lateral rectus muscle, one to align the only seeing eye in the primary position and one to allow multiple procedures on the same eye during a single surgery.

Tutopatch® was applied to the lateral rectus muscle in 54 patients and to the medial rectus muscle in 13 patients. One patient received a Tutopatch® graft on both lateral rectus muscles, and another patient received a Tutopatch® graft on the left lateral rectus and the left superior rectus muscle. The median Tutopatch® length was 4.0 mm (range 1.0–11.5 mm). An overview of the individual Tutopatch® lengths used can be found in Tables S1 and S3.

Duction limitation inter‐ and intra‐rater reliability

Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) indicated very strong correlation in scoring duction limitations. Intra‐rater reliability among orthoptists varied between ICC = 0.914 and ICC = 0.973, while inter‐rater reliability was ICC = 0.923 at the first rating round and ICC = 0.929 at the second round.

Strabismus angle

Figure 1 presents the mean strabismus angles for both near and distance fixation in patients with exodeviation and esodeviation. The data show a substantial reduction in deviation shortly after surgery, with an overall stable alignment over time. Since the sample size was too small to allow reliable subgroup analyses, changes in ocular alignment over time were evaluated in the total study population. From 3 months postoperative onward, no statistically significant changes were observed in near and distance deviation. In contrast, when including measurements from approximately 1 week postoperative onward, a statistically significant change was observed for near deviation (LMM, F = 4.76, p = 0.011), driven by an initial higher angle at 1 week (1.11°, 95% CI −0.87 to 3.09) compared to 3 months postoperatively (−0.92°, 95% CI −2.96 to 1.13). For distance deviation, no significant differences were found across timepoints.

Proportion of success ≤5.71° of 0°.

One patient received Tutopatch® on both lateral rectus muscles (participant 40, Tables S1 and S2). Preoperatively, the strabismus angle was 16° exophoria at near and 14° exophoria at distance, with no abduction limitation. At approximately 1 week postoperative, near ocular alignment was orthotropic, while distance showed 3.4° esotropia with diplopia and trace right and moderate left abduction limitations observed. At approximately 3 months postoperative, the esotropia and duction limitations progressed. Unfortunately, the patient underwent reoperation.

One patient received Tutopatch® on both the left lateral rectus (3.7 mm) and left superior rectus (4.0 mm) muscles (participant 30, S1 and S2). Preoperatively, the vertical deviation was 10.2°L/R at near and distance. At long‐term follow‐up (161.3 weeks), the deviation was 3.4°L/R at both distances, indicating surgical success (≤5.71° of 0°). No duction limitations of the superior rectus were noted pre‐ or postoperatively. The patient rated satisfaction with a VAS score of 9, very satisfied.

Duction limitation

In both the total group and the exodeviation subgroup, a statistically significant increase in duction limitation was observed at approximately 3 months postoperatively compared to preoperative (p < 0.001) (total group preoperatively 0.22 ± 0.54 vs. 3 months postoperatively 1.44 ± 0.90; exodeviation subgroup preoperatively 0.21 ± 0.53 vs. 3 months postoperatively 1.55 ± 0.83) and was also observed when preoperatively was compared to long‐term follow‐up (p < 0.001) (total group long‐term 1.26 ± 0.97; exodeviation subgroup long‐term 1.37 ± 0.93). In the esodeviation group, this effect was also statistically significant at approximately 3 months postoperatively (p = 0.05) (preoperatively 0.15 ± 0.38 vs. 3 months 1.0 ± 1.1), but missed statistical significance at long‐term follow‐up (p = 0.08) (long‐term 0.77 ± 1.1), although the patient sample was considerably smaller.

From 3 months postoperative onward, no statistically significant changes were observed in duction limitation. When including measurements from approximately 1 week postoperative onward, there was a significant decrease over time (LMM, F = 6.20, p = 0.003), from 1.60 (95% CI 1.35–1.84) at 1 week to 1.43 (95% CI 1.17–1.68) at 3 months and 1.01 (95% CI 0.64–1.38) at final follow‐up.

Most patients had no or only mild duction limitations before surgery (Figures 2 and 3). In the exodeviation group, limitations increased in most cases to trace or small at 1 week postoperatively and improved or remained stable after 3 months. In the smaller esodeviation group, a similar but less pronounced pattern was observed, with most patients showing no limitations at long‐term follow‐up.

Associations of success

No statistically significant associations were found between fusion, stereopsis or visual acuity (≥0.3 in both eyes vs. <0.3 in one eye) and surgical success (≤5.71° from the target angle) in any group. In the exodeviation group, an evident trend towards higher surgical success was observed in patients with a visual acuity of ≥0.3 in both eyes at 3 months. However, this trend was not sustained at long‐term follow‐up.

No significant association was found between the number of prior strabismus surgeries and surgical success (≤5.71° of 0°), although more previous surgeries were associated with greater duction limitations at long‐term follow‐up (p = 0.02).

Tutopatch® length and axial length showed no significant association with surgical success (≤5.71° of 0°) or duction limitations.

Patient‐reported satisfaction

Patient satisfaction with ocular alignment was assessed in 31 participants using a visual analogue scale (VAS). The median satisfaction score was 9.0 (range 5.5–10.0). Satisfaction correlated weakly with measured near deviation (r = −0.45) and moderately with distance deviation (r = −0.50).

DISCUSSION

This pre‐post cohort study aimed to evaluate the long‐term efficacy of tendon elongation using bovine pericardium (Tutopatch®) on the strabismus angle, duction limitations and patient satisfaction in patients who have undergone complex strabismus surgery. It is the largest and longest study to date reporting on these outcomes, showing that the use of Tutopatch® results in a stable reduction of the strabismus angle with around a 70% success rate. Small duction limitations in the direction of the Tutopatch®‐treated muscle were observed, yet these seemed to have minimal impact on ocular alignment in the primary position or on patient‐reported satisfaction. These limitations tended to improve over time, suggesting partial recovery of motility.

A key strength of this study is the long‐term follow‐up period, as strabismus can recur at any point in life. While the previous study by Hedergott et al. (2021) reported the longest follow‐up to date with a maximum of 261 weeks (5 years), the present study extends this to 431 weeks (8.3 years). Of the patients included, 49 had follow‐ups beyond 4 months, with a median of 124 weeks. This provides valuable insight into the durability of surgical outcomes following tendon elongation with Tutopatch®.

Literature on the use of Tutopatch® in complex strabismus surgery is limited, with only a few published studies addressing its application in broader clinical indications than Graves' orbitopathy (Elabbasy et al., 2022; Hedergott et al., 2021; van Rijn et al., 2016). Van Rijn et al. (2016) reported fairly good outcomes in a much smaller relatively heterogeneous group, with a surgical success rate of 73.5% (25/34 participants) at the last follow‐up (range 6–205 weeks). In the exodeviation subgroup, preoperative abduction limitations of the Tutopatch®‐treated lateral rectus muscle were minimal. At the first postoperative follow‐up, they were frequently observed but showed significant improvement over time. This was not analysed for the esodeviation group. These findings were similar to our study. Hedergott et al. (2021) described ocular alignment in a diverse cohort of patients, including those with Duane syndrome and cranial nerve palsies. They achieved a surgical success rate of 52.3% (23/44 participants, derived from their supplementary dataset) at long‐term follow‐up (range 5–261 weeks). This was lower than in our study, possibly due to the inclusion of other strabismus types. Duction limitations were measured in degrees as a continuous variable but were not subjected to statistical analysis. The authors implied that postoperative duction limitations were most pronounced in restrictive motility disorders, such as Duane syndrome. Elabbasy et al. (2022) evaluated nine patients with third nerve palsy, all of whom had undergone prior conventional strabismus surgery. Tutopatch® was also used to facilitate a lateral splitting procedure. They reported lower success rates than in our study: 37.5% (3/8 participants) at 3 months postoperatively and 50% (2/4 participants) at the final follow‐up (range 26–191 weeks). This was likely due to the neurogenic strabismus population and the small sample size. More recently, Pelińska et al. (2025) reported on the use of Tutopatch® (8–10 mm) for superior oblique tendon elongation in paediatric patients with congenital Brown syndrome. Although the indication and surgical target differed from our cohort, as no patients with Brown syndrome were included in our study, they reported significant improvement of elevation in adduction and abnormal head posture in the majority of their ten patients at final follow‐up (range 22–104 weeks).

An alternative technique to tendon elongation is the use of large hang‐back sutures, where the attachment point of the suture is placed anterior to the equator, but the muscle itself hangs behind it. Animal studies have demonstrated that such techniques can be unpredictable, with larger hang‐back recessions showing a tendency for greater unintended anterior muscle displacement (Ohtsuki et al., 1994; Repka et al., 1990; Wysenbeek et al., 2004). To our knowledge, data on the long‐term outcomes of large hang‐back recessions are lacking. Recently, Mohamed et al. (2025) evaluated hang‐back sutures (3–8 mm augmentation after 7 mm conventional recession) in patients with large‐angle exotropia (≥40Δ), reporting a surgical success rate of 67.6% (23/34 participants) at 6 months, with a considerably shorter follow‐up than in our study. Five participants were noted to have abduction limitations, although no further details or statistical analysis were provided. In another pilot study, Pujari et al. (2023) compared two surgical approaches for extra‐large exotropia (>60Δ): both groups underwent a 9 mm lateral rectus recession combined with medial rectus resection, followed by either a muscle transplant using the resected medial rectus segment or an augmented hang‐back suture (5–6.5 mm). They reported a surgical success rate of 80% at a median follow‐up of around 8 months in both groups. The authors cautioned against loops exceeding 6 mm due to an increased risk of duction limitations. In contrast, our study found no significant association between Tutopatch® graft length and either surgical success or duction limitation. These findings suggest that tendon elongation using Tutopatch® may provide more predictable and stable outcomes than large hang‐back techniques.

A small residual deviation may be intentionally aimed for to optimize postoperative comfort, avoid diplopia or prevent consecutive deviations. For the analyses regarding the correlation between visual acuity, stereopsis and fusion, comparison with the target angle was considered more appropriate. No meaningful conclusions could be drawn due to the small study population, particularly the low number of participants with good fusion or stereopsis, and the method of ocular alignment measurement that did not allow for differentiation between manifest and latent components of the deviation. While reduced visual acuity, stereopsis or fusion may theoretically lead to poorer outcomes, good alignment was also observed in patients lacking these functions in our study. Additionally, we speculate that in these cases a small duction limitation may help prevent recurrence of strabismus, particularly in recurrent exodeviations.

In addition to clinical measures, patient satisfaction is a key determinant of surgical success, given its direct impact on quality of life (Al Shehri et al., 2020). The study by van Rijn et al. (2016) addressed satisfaction, although the method of data collection is unclear. Almost all patients were satisfied with their results. In this study, patient‐reported satisfaction was similarly high. No participant rated their individual outcome with respect to ocular alignment below 5.5 on a VAS, even among those with a residual deviation >5.71°. The correlation between strabismus angle and satisfaction was weak to moderate, suggesting that alignment alone does not entirely predict perceived success. Other factors, such as improved symmetry, relief of diplopia or psychosocial adaptation, may contribute to subjective satisfaction. For example, visibility thresholds for strabismus vary depending on the type of deviation and observer factors. Larson et al. (2003) found that esotropia (ET), exotropia (XT) and hypertropia were reliably detected at ≥7.1°, while hypotropia required a higher threshold of 11.3°, based on ratings by both lay and professional observers. Chan et al. (2016) reported that thresholds varied by ethnicity and deviation type: in White and Black models, esotropia was less detectable than exotropia (e.g., 13.1°ET vs. 7.7°XT for white models; 11.8°ET vs. 9.3°XT for black models). In contrast, Asian models showed the reverse trend, with esotropia detected at lower thresholds than exotropia (8.1°ΔET vs. 11.8°ΔXT). These findings, based on assessments by non‐healthcare professionals, suggest that a surgical success threshold of ≤5.71° from 0° may be overly stringent from a cosmetic or patient‐perceived standpoint.

Possible limitations of the study include the fact that at Amsterdam UMC the angle was mainly measured in degrees with a custom‐made Maddox device at a distance of 30 centimetres. While no published research exists on the custom‐made Maddox device, internal studies conducted at Amsterdam UMC have confirmed its reliability and validity in clinical practice. No research has been conducted on the inter‐ and intra‐observer reliability of the APCT between clinicians at Radboudumc and/or Amsterdam UMC. Research has shown that the APCT is a widely accepted, reliable method for assessing ocular deviation (PEDIG, 2009; Zou et al., 2022). Previous studies have shown that the inter‐ and intra‐observer variability of the APCT can vary somewhat, depending on the examiner's experience, strabismus type or patient age; however, overall agreement between and within experienced examiners is generally good (de Jongh et al., 2014; Johns et al., 2004; Rainey et al., 1998). A limitation of alternating measurements is that it does not separate manifest from latent deviation, which may have led to an underestimation of surgical success in this study. However, since most patients in our cohort presented with manifest tropia, this potential bias is expected to be minimal.

Another limitation is the use of intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) to assess inter‐ and intra‐rater reliability for ordinal duction limitation scores, which may have slightly overestimated agreement. While ICC is commonly used and suitable for comparing multiple raters, it assumes interval‐level data. Although weighted kappa is more appropriate for ordinal data, its application across eight raters is complex. ICC was therefore chosen as the most feasible and interpretable method.

CONCLUSION

The use of Tutopatch® for tendon elongation in complex strabismus surgery is a viable technique offering favourable long‐term alignment and high patient satisfaction. Clinicians should be aware that mild duction limitations may occur postoperatively; however, these often improve over time and appear to have a limited impact on perceived success. As strabismus can recur at any point in life, future studies should include even longer follow‐up periods. Ideally, they should include more strabismus types, such as esodeviations, to establish a study population in which statistically significant results may be observed.
