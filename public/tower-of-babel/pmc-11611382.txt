Post-tonsillectomy hemorrhage in children
Authors: Jennifer M. Siu, Evan J. Propst, Nikolaus E. Wolter
Published: 2024

Post-tonsillectomy hemorrhage can occur within the first 14 days after surgery1

The incidence ranges from 2% to 5%, and the severity varies from blood-tinged sputum to major hemorrhage.1 Causes may include scab sloughing, trauma from food ingestion, and infection of the tonsil bed.1

Bleeding can be life-threatening

Lethal post-tonsillectomy hemorrhage has been reported at a rate of 7 per 100 000 operations overall and 117 per 100 000 operations among children with complex chronic conditions.2 Patients should return to the emergency department urgently if they notice any postoperative bleeding, including blood-tinged sputum, hemoptysis, or hematemesis.3 Abdominal pain and nausea may be the only symptoms if blood has been swallowed.3

Preventative measures include adequate hydration and oral intake, appropriate analgesia, minimization of cough or strain, and avoidance of vigorous activity

Males; older children; and children with obesity, coagulation disorders, or a history of chronic tonsillitis are at increased risk of hemorrhage.4 It is unclear whether nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs increase the risk of bleeding.5

Patients with post-tonsillectomy hemorrhage should be assessed urgently

Examination should occur with the patient sitting upright, with suction available, and an intravenous line should be placed for potential volume resuscitation. Manipulation, coughing, or straining can destabilize clots or cause emesis of swallowed blood. Minor bleeding or clotting in the tonsillar fossa may become serious, and close monitoring is required. Investigations include hemoglobin, hematocrit, and blood type and screen. Direct pressure can be applied to the tonsillar bed using gauze on a finger or a hemostat.3 The patient can be in prone position or on their side to avoid aspiration.

Many patients require surgical management

After primary evaluation and stabilization, management by an otolaryngologist may include close observation or operative treatment.1 In the operating room, hemorrhage can be managed with a combination of suction cautery, suture ligation, various hemostatic agents, and suctioning of the stomach to remove any swallowed blood.
