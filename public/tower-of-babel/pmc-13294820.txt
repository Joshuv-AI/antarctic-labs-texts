Neurodevelopmental and behavioral correlates of household structure independent of socioeconomic status
Authors: Kaitlynn E Ellis, Amar Camara, Camille Archer, Yulan D Chen, Antonia N Kaczkurkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionParental socioeconomic status (SES) is associated with children’s brain development and mental health but does not fully capture variation in caregiving environments. Household structure, including the number of caregivers in the home, may also shape children’s experiences through differences in supervision, support, and daily demands. Because household structure and SES are closely linked, it remains unclear whether differences in child outcomes reflect economic conditions, caregiving context, or both. This study examined whether household structure is associated with psychopathology symptoms and brain structure after accounting for SES.MethodsParticipants included 7890 children (ages 9–10) from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study living in households with one or two caregivers. Associations between household structure, psychopathology, and gray matter volume were examined while controlling for age, sex, site, family income, and parental education. Sensitivity analyses included additional caregiving and mental health factors. Mediation analyses tested whether total cortical and subcortical volumes were associated with the relationship between household structure and psychopathology. For all analyses, multiple comparisons were controlled using false discovery rate correction.ResultsHouseholds with two caregivers had, on average, higher income and parental education. After adjustment for SES and covariates, differences were observed in externalizing behaviors and attention-related symptoms. Small but widespread differences in regional brain volumes were also observed and remained robust in sensitivity analyses. Total cortical and subcortical volumes were associated with both household structure and behavioral outcomes, with small but significant indirect effects indicating significant mediation.DiscussionAlthough effect sizes were small, findings suggest that aspects of caregiving context captured by household structure are associated with child development above and beyond SES. Brain structure differences may represent one pathway linking caregiving context to behavioral outcomes, though effects were modest. Further work is needed to identify specific, modifiable processes underlying these associations.

Introduction

Children’s neural and behavioral development is shaped by the social, educational, and material resources available to them (McLoyd, 1998; Hurt and Betancourt, 2016; Rakesh et al., 2023). These resources are often indexed by parental socioeconomic status (SES), which includes income, education level, occupational characteristics, and neighborhood context. Low SES has been associated with greater internalizing and externalizing symptoms in children, as well as reductions in cortical and subcortical gray matter volume (Letourneau et al., 2013; Brito and Noble, 2014; Carneiro et al., 2016; Buckley et al., 2019; Rakesh et al., 2023). However, SES does not fully capture variability in children’s caregiving environments, which are shaped by multiple co-occurring familial and contextual factors.

One such factor is household structure, operationalized here as the number of caregivers in the home (one versus two). This dimension is developmentally relevant as the number of caregivers available in a child’s daily environment may shape caregiving capacity in ways not fully captured by SES. Prior work suggests that family structure is associated with differences in parental time investment, parental involvement, and children’s exposure to family instability, all of which may influence behavioral development (Fomby and Cherlin, 2007; Ryan et al., 2015; Bibler, 2020). The number of caregivers may also affect the distribution of household and childrearing responsibilities, potentially shaping consistency in supervision, routines, and emotional support. These processes are important as parental monitoring and family routines have been linked to lower externalizing and behavioral problems in children and adolescents (Li et al., 2000; Smetana, 2008; Liu and Merritt, 2021). In addition, caregiving demands and stress may influence parenting behaviors and children’s socioemotional functioning, suggesting that caregiver availability and caregiver well-being are relevant pathways through which household structure could relate to child outcomes (Neece et al., 2012; Masarik and Conger, 2017). These potential pathways suggest that household structure may be an important contextual factor in children’s development.

Household structure and socioeconomic conditions are also closely intertwined. Families with one caregiver in the home are more likely to experience lower income and occupational disparities (Koops et al., 2021). These socioeconomic differences may compound other family level stressors in ways that shape child development. For example, Sarsour and colleagues found that family structure moderated the association between SES and executive functioning, such that children from low-SES households with one caregiver showed poorer inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility (Sarsour et al., 2011). Similarly, children exposed to multiple co-occurring risks, including low SES, high family stress, and one-caregiver household structure, showed reduced gray matter volume and poorer cognitive performance (Chad‐Friedman et al., 2021). Given that these factors often cluster together, studies that do not account for SES cannot determine whether observed associations with household structure reflect caregiving context, material resources, or their combination.

Studies that have examined household structure and child psychopathology while accounting for SES have yielded mixed results. Some work suggests that children in households with one caregiver show higher levels of depressive and externalizing symptoms even after socioeconomic factors are considered (Daryanani et al., 2016a, b). Similarly, caregiver status has been associated with internalizing and externalizing symptoms independent of poverty exposure (Comeau and Boyle, 2018). In contrast, other studies suggest that associations between household structure and child mental health are attenuated or no longer significant after accounting for socioeconomic indicators (Fergusson et al., 2007; Faulconer et al., 2022). Together, these divergent findings underscore ongoing uncertainty about whether household structure is associated with child mental health beyond SES, and whether these associations differ across emotional and behavioral domains. Inconsistencies across studies may reflect methodological differences, including sample size, developmental age range, and variation in the measurement of family level and contextual covariates. Moreover, little is known about whether household structure is associated with children’s structural brain development after accounting for SES. Clarifying these relationships may help identify which aspects of children’s caregiving environments are linked to neurodevelopmental and mental health outcomes beyond socioeconomic conditions.

Given evidence that household structure may be associated with both behavioral outcomes and neurodevelopment, it is possible that brain structure partially accounts for associations between caregiving context and psychopathology. Prior mediation work provides support for testing brain structure as a potential neurodevelopmental correlate linking children’s environments with behavioral outcomes. Studies have shown that structural brain measures statistically mediate associations between socioeconomic context and ADHD symptoms, between family SES and depression-related traits, and between neighborhood poverty and externalizing symptoms (Yang et al., 2016; Machlin et al., 2020; Maxwell et al., 2023). Related mediation work has shown that aspects of the family environment, including family functioning and parental monitoring, are associated with child behavioral problems through differences in brain structure, supporting the use of neurostructural measures as potential mediators linking caregiving context with psychopathology (Gong et al., 2021; Xerxa et al., 2021). However, to our knowledge, no study has tested whether brain structure mediates the association between household composition and child psychopathology after accounting for SES.

The current study addresses this gap by examining the number of caregivers in the household, brain structure, and psychopathology symptoms in a large sample of children while accounting for family income and parental education. Consistent with prior literature, we focus on households with one or two caregivers while recognizing that children’s caregiving environments may include a broader range of family and household arrangements. Specifically, we evaluated whether children in households with one caregiver differed from children in households with two caregivers in emotional symptoms, behavioral dysregulation, social functioning problems, and regional brain volume after controlling for age, sex, site, family income, and parental education. We also tested the robustness of these findings by examining parental monitoring, perceived caregiver warmth, total child psychopathology, parental mental health, and intracranial volume as additional covariates. Finally, we conducted exploratory analyses to test whether cortical and subcortical brain volume mediated associations between household structure and psychopathology outcomes.

Method

Sample

The current study utilized baseline data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (version 5.1), a large, longitudinal sample of children recruited from 21 sites across the USA. Participants included 11 868 children (ages 9–10) who completed neuroimaging scans and various cognitive and behavioral assessments (Barch et al., 2021). Informed consent and assent were obtained from all caregivers and minors prior to study participation. From the baseline sample, household structure data were available for 10 784 children. To approximate households with one versus two caregivers in the home, we restricted the sample to children whose caregivers reported being either “married” or “never married” (N = 8 724; Table 1). Participants with missing data on our variables of interest were excluded from analyses, resulting in a sample of 7929 for behavioral analyses. Participants who failed to meet the ABCD Study’s neuroimaging quality assurance metrics were additionally excluded, resulting in a sample of 7890 for volume analyses. The Institutional Review Board at Vanderbilt University approved the use of this de-identified dataset.

Measures

Household structure

Household structure was operationalized as the number of caregivers in the home and approximated using responses from the caregiver demographics survey. Caregivers were asked, “Are you now married, widowed, divorced, separated, never married, or living with a partner?” Several response options introduce ambiguity regarding the child’s current and historical caregiving context. For example, caregivers who are widowed, divorced, or separated may have previously lived with another caregiver for part or most of the child’s life, making it difficult to classify the child’s caregiving environment. Similarly, responses indicating that a caregiver is “living with a partner” do not specify the duration of the cohabiting relationship or the partner’s role in caregiving, which could represent either a long-term two-caregiver household or a more recent transition. To reduce these sources of heterogeneity and improve interpretability and consistency with prior studies, we restricted the sample to caregivers who endorsed being “married” or “never married.” These categories were used as proxies for households with two caregivers or one caregiver in the home, respectively. Table S1 provides a comparison of all included and excluded household groups.

Socioeconomic status

Parental SES was assessed using data on family income and parental education level from the caregiver demographics survey. Caregivers were asked to report their total family income before taxes and deductions, as well as the highest level of education or degree completed by both the primary caregiver and their partner (if present).

Psychopathology symptoms

Psychopathology symptoms were measured using the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL), a standardized caregiver-report measure that assesses children’s behavioral and emotional problems over the past 6 months (Achenbach, 2009). The CBCL includes 112 structured items, along with one open-ended item that allows caregivers to report additional concerns not captured by the structured prompts. Items are rated on a three-point Likert scale: 0 (Not True), 1 (Somewhat/Sometimes True), and 2 (Very True/Often True). Scores are grouped into eight CBCL syndrome scales (anxious/depressed, withdrawn/depressed, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems, rule-breaking behavior, and aggressive behavior) with summary scores for internalizing, externalizing, and total problems, as well as six DSM-oriented scales: affective problems (primarily consisting of depressive symptoms), anxiety problems, somatic problems, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) problems, oppositional defiant problems, and conduct problems.

Parental warmth

Perceived parental warmth was measured using the acceptance subscale of the Children’s Report of Parental Behavior Inventory (CRPBI; Schaefer, 1965). Youth rated the extent to which their caregiver demonstrated warmth, affection, and acceptance. A composite score was calculated as the mean of five items, with higher scores indicating greater perceived parental warmth. Consistent with ABCD data quality guidelines, scores were considered valid when youth completed a minimum of four items. The CRPBI acceptance subscale has demonstrated strong reliability and validity across developmental stages and has been widely used to assess parental warmth and support in relation to youth emotional and behavioral outcomes (Barber et al., 1994; Barber and Olsen, 1997).

Parental monitoring

Parental monitoring was assessed using a youth-reported measure capturing caregivers’ knowledge of the child’s whereabouts, activities, and peer interactions adapted from previous work (Karoly et al., 2016). A composite parental monitoring score was computed as the mean of five items (e.g. “How often do your parents/guardians know where you are?”), with higher scores reflecting greater parental monitoring. Youth-reported parental monitoring has been shown to be a reliable indicator of caregiver supervision and engagement and is associated with a range of child and adolescent behavioral outcomes (Karoly et al., 2016).

Parental mental health

Parental mental health history was assessed using items from the Family History Assessment Module of the ABCD Study. Primary caregivers reported whether any biological relative had a history of specific psychiatric conditions using binary responses (1 = Yes, 0 = No). Possible conditions included depression, mania, psychosis, nervous breakdown, antisocial behavior, alcohol use problems, drug use problems, suicide attempt or completion, and history of psychiatric hospitalization or treatment. For the present analyses, we restricted responses to biological mothers and fathers and summed the total number of endorsed conditions across both parents to create a cumulative parental mental health risk score. Thus, higher values reflect a greater number of psychiatric conditions reported across biological parents.

Image acquisition, quality assurance, and processing

Imaging protocols for this study were designed by the ABCD Data Analysis and Informatics Center (DAIC) and the ABCD Imaging Acquisition Workgroup (Casey et al., 2018; Hagler et al., 2019). Imaging data were acquired across the 21 ABCD data collection sites using multiple 3-Tesla (3T) scanner models: General Electric Discovery MR750, the Siemens Prisma, the Siemens Prisma Fit, the Philips Achieva dStream, and the Philips Ingenia. The DAIC performed centralized processing, which included preprocessing, brain segmentation, and morphometric analyses (Hagler et al., 2019). Images underwent basic preprocessing steps, including reorientation, motion correction, intensity correction, skull stripping, and spatial normalization. Rigorous quality control procedures, involving both automated and manual checks by trained technicians, were used to evaluate scan quality and included identifying abnormal scans, motion artifacts, white matter underestimation, pial overestimation, and inhomogeneity. Previous work provides detailed descriptions of these methods (Hagler et al., 2019). Images were parcellated into 68 cortical and 19 subcortical regions using well-established atlases (Fischl et al., 2002; Desikan et al., 2006).

Data analysis

The overarching goal of the analyses was to determine whether household structure was associated with child psychopathology and brain structure beyond socioeconomic differences (Fig. 1). Data analyses were performed using Mplus version 8.4. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was used to account for multiple comparisons, with statistical significance set at q < 0.05. All code for these analyses can be found at https://github.com/VU-BRAINS-lab/Ellis_household-structure_ses.

Psychopathology analyses

Multiple regression models tested household structure, defined as one versus two caregivers in the home, as a predictor of CBCL psychopathology scales, with age, sex, ABCD Study site, family income, and parental education included as covariates. Sensitivity analyses then tested whether associations between household structure and child psychopathology differed by sex. Measures of parental monitoring, perceived caregiver warmth, and parental mental health were also added as covariates to examine whether parenting-related factors and familial psychiatric history influenced associations between household structure and psychopathology symptoms.

Neurostructural analyses

Using structural equation modeling, household structure was examined as a predictor of gray matter volume across 68 cortical and 19 subcortical regions, controlling for age, sex, family income, parental education, and site/scanner. Models included a combined site–scanner variable provided by the ABCD Study, which jointly accounts for study site and MRI scanner characteristics (including manufacturer and model) to control for site- and imaging-related variability. To account for family dependence, including siblings, twins, and triplets, data were clustered by family membership. All models were weighted using post-stratification weights to align the sample with US population demographics and non-participation weights to account for differences among excluded participants, as our prior work has shown that those excluded for failure to meet neuroimaging quality control are younger, more likely to be male, and have more psychopathology (Durham et al., 2021).

Sensitivity analyses for the neurostructural models then tested whether brain-volume associations remained significant after adding parental monitoring and perceived caregiver warmth as additional covariates. Total child psychopathology was associated with household structure; therefore, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses with total CBCL problems included as a covariate in the neurostructural models. We also conducted analyses accounting for parental mental health to examine whether observed associations were independent of familial psychiatric history. Finally, to account for differences in overall cranial size, intracranial volume was included as a covariate.

Mediation analyses

Exploratory mediation analyses were conducted to determine whether global brain volume statistically mediated links between household structure and child psychopathology. Because these analyses were secondary, mediation models were restricted to outcomes that showed a significant total association with household structure in the primary regression analyses. Thus, this approach focused mediation testing on behavioral domains for which there was already evidence of household-structure differences. These outcomes included attention problems, rule-breaking behavior, DSM-oriented ADHD problems, and DSM-oriented conduct problems.

Total cortical volume and total subcortical volume were tested as separate mediators, resulting in eight mediation models. Each model estimated the association between household structure and brain volume, the association between brain volume and psychopathology symptoms while accounting for household structure, the direct association between household structure and psychopathology symptoms, and the indirect effect through brain volume. Models included the same covariates used in the primary analyses, including age, sex, family income, parental education, parental monitoring, perceived caregiver warmth, parental mental health, and site/scanner covariates.

All mediation models used the MLR estimator with complex survey correction, clustering by family membership, stratification by ABCD Study site, and post-stratification weights. Indirect effects were estimated in Mplus using MODEL INDIRECT. Given the cross-sectional design, these analyses were interpreted as exploratory tests of statistical mediation rather than evidence of causal pathways.

Results

Household structure predicts youth psychopathology symptoms

Household structure (one versus two caregivers in the home) was tested as a predictor of family income, parental education, and the CBCL scales. Children from households with two caregivers had significantly higher family income and parental education levels (P-values ≤ 0.01). After controlling for age, sex, site, family income, and parental education, household structure remained a significant predictor of CBCL attention problems, rule-breaking behavior, total externalizing problems, and the DSM-oriented scales of ADHD and conduct problems (Table 2). Children from households with two caregivers had fewer symptoms in these domains. Household structure was not significantly associated with the CBCL syndrome scales for anxious/depressed symptoms, withdrawn/depressed symptoms, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, or aggressive behavior, nor with total internalizing problems. Household structure was also not a significant predictor of the DSM-oriented affective (depression), anxiety, oppositional defiant, or somatic problem scales. When measures of parental monitoring and perceived caregiver warmth were added as covariates, the pattern of results remained consistent (Table S2). After controlling for parental mental health history, the total externalizing problems scale was no longer significantly predicted by household structure (Table S3).

Sex differences in the relationship between household structure and psychopathology

We examined interactions with sex to determine whether this pattern of results differed between males and females. Sex did not significantly interact with household structure in predicting psychopathology symptoms across the nine CBCL subscales, even after controlling for age, site, family income, parental education, and the main effect for sex (Table S4). When measures of parental monitoring and perceived caregiver warmth were added as covariates, sex significantly interacted with household structure in predicting DSM-oriented conduct problems (Table S5). Specifically, males were reported to have greater conduct problems, and this relationship was stronger in households with one caregiver relative to households with two caregivers. This pattern of results remained consistent when parent mental health history was added as a covariate (Table S6).

Household composition predicts youth brain structure

Using structural equation modeling, household structure was examined as a predictor of gray matter volume. After FDR correction and controlling for age, sex, family income, parental education, and site/scanner, children from households with two caregivers showed significantly larger gray matter volumes in widespread cortical and subcortical regions compared to children from households with one caregiver, independent of socioeconomic status (Table S7; Fig. 2). Significant cortical effects were observed in frontal regions, including lateral orbitofrontal, right caudal middle frontal, frontal pole, caudal anterior cingulate, right rostral anterior cingulate, posterior cingulate, isthmus cingulate, precentral, pars opercularis, pars orbitalis, and left pars triangularis, with the left paracentral lobule bridging the frontal and parietal lobes. Significant parietal regions included superior and inferior parietal, postcentral, precuneus, and supramarginal gyrus. Temporal regions with significant associations included inferior and middle temporal gyri, fusiform, entorhinal, and parahippocampal areas. Additionally, significant occipital regions included the cuneus as well as lateral occipital and lingual cortices. Subcortical differences included the caudate, putamen, hippocampus, amygdala, and cerebellum. These results indicate that household structure is associated with broad differences in cortical and subcortical gray matter volume, even after accounting for socioeconomic factors.

We conducted sensitivity analyses with measures of parental monitoring and warmth to examine whether positive parenting practices influenced the relationship between household structure and gray matter volume. After FDR correction and adjustment for age, sex, family income, parental education, site/scanner model, parental monitoring, and caregiver warmth, the overall pattern of results remained similar, with the right paracentral lobule and left ventral diencephalon emerging as additional significant regions in children from households with two caregivers (Table S8). Additionally, we conducted sensitivity analyses with total CBCL problems as an additional covariate to account for the association between psychopathology and household structure. The pattern of results remained consistent with only the left ventral diencephalon emerging as an additional significant region in children from households with two caregivers (Table S9). Adding parental mental health as an additional covariate revealed a similar pattern to the results with total CBCL problems, with only the right posterior cingulate losing significance and the right temporal pole gaining significance (Table S10). Finally, we conducted sensitivity analyses with intracranial volume included as an additional covariate to account for overall cranial size. Significant associations remained across 21 frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital regions (Table S11). Additionally, the left rostral middle frontal gyrus and left superior temporal gyrus emerged as significantly smaller regions in children from households with two caregivers.

Volume mediates the relationship between household structure and psychopathology

We performed exploratory mediation analyses to test whether global brain volume statistically mediated associations between household structure and CBCL psychopathology outcomes. Total cortical volume and total subcortical volume were each tested as mediators for the four CBCL scales that were significantly associated with household structure in the primary regression analyses: attention problems, rule-breaking behavior, DSM-oriented conduct problems, and DSM-oriented ADHD problems. Across these domains, total cortical and subcortical volume mediated the association between household structure and behavioral outcomes (Table S12). Children from households with two caregivers exhibited greater total cortical and subcortical brain volumes (a paths, P-values ≤ 0.001), which were in turn associated with lower levels of attention problems, conduct problems, ADHD symptoms, and rule-breaking behavior (b paths, P-values ≤ 0.001). Indirect effects were small but significant across all outcomes, and direct effects remained significant.

Discussion

The current study examined whether household structure was associated with differences in brain volume and psychopathology problems beyond SES. Households with two caregivers had higher family income and parental education. After accounting for SES, household structure was associated with attention and externalizing-related problems, including ADHD problems, rule-breaking behavior, and conduct problems. Neurostructural analyses showed widespread differences in gray matter volume across cortical and subcortical regions, which were largely robust across sensitivity analyses, although fewer regions survived correction after intracranial volume was included. Evidence for sex differences was limited to conduct problems in secondary models. Exploratory mediation analyses showed small but significant indirect effects through total cortical and subcortical volume for associations between household structure and attention and externalizing-related outcomes.

These results expand upon previous literature suggesting that family structure is a significant predictor of brain and behavioral development in children beyond the effects of socioeconomic status (Daryanani et al., 2016a; Comeau and Boyle, 2018). After controlling for family income and parental education level in the current study, household structure significantly predicted CBCL subscales, especially within the attention and externalizing domains. Daryanani and colleagues found a similar pattern of results, where more children from households with one caregiver met the criteria for externalizing disorders, such as ADHD, oppositional defiant disorder, and conduct disorder, compared to those from households with two caregivers (Daryanani et al., 2016a). Interestingly, the associations found in the current study were specific to attention and externalizing-related problems rather than reflecting broad elevations across psychopathology domains.

Household structure was not significantly associated with internalizing symptoms in the current study. This is contrary to prior work showing a relationship between one-parent households and greater rumination and depressive symptoms in adolescents (Daryanani et al., 2016a, b). One possible reason for the lack of significant internalizing results in the present study is that household structure, as measured here, may be more closely related to caregiving processes that support behavioral regulation, such as monitoring, routines, and scaffolding of attention and self-control, than to internalizing symptoms. It is also possible that internalizing symptoms are less readily captured by caregiver report at this developmental stage (9–10 years) or may emerge more strongly later in adolescence (Durham et al., 2021, 2023). Thus, these analyses should be repeated in later waves of the ABCD Study as participants progress through adolescence, when internalizing symptoms may become more prominent and more reliably assessed.

Additionally, we extend the findings of Chad‐Friedman et al. (2021) by demonstrating that household structure, beyond SES differences, is associated with gray matter volume across cortical and subcortical structures. At the systems level, the affected regions map onto several large-scale networks including the default mode network (e.g. precuneus, inferior parietal, and medial temporal regions), the frontoparietal control network (e.g. rostral middle frontal and superior parietal regions), and limbic and striatal systems (e.g. parahippocampal regions and caudate). These networks are broadly implicated in self-referential processing, executive control, socioemotional functioning, and reward-related behavior, which are processes that are closely aligned with the externalizing and attention symptoms observed in the present study (Marek and Dosenbach, 2018; Elliott et al., 2022; Menon, 2023). The observed associations between household structure and widespread differences in cortical and subcortical gray matter volume are consistent with developmental models emphasizing the sensitivity of these neural systems to early environmental stressors (Miguel et al., 2019; Jeong et al., 2021, 2023, 2024), and suggest that the number of caregivers may be linked to multiple neurodevelopmental systems rather than a circumscribed neural circuit.

The exploratory mediation analyses provide an initial test of whether global brain volume statistically accounts for links between household structure and behavioral outcomes. Across attention problems, rule-breaking behavior, DSM-oriented ADHD problems, and DSM-oriented conduct problems, total cortical and subcortical volume showed small but significant indirect effects. Specifically, children from households with two caregivers had greater total cortical and subcortical volumes, which were in turn associated with lower levels of externalizing- and attention-related problems. These findings are consistent with prior mediation work showing that structural brain measures can statistically mediate associations between socioeconomic or family-contextual factors and child behavioral outcomes, including ADHD and externalizing symptoms (Machlin et al., 2020; Gong et al., 2021; Maxwell et al., 2023). However, the present results should be interpreted cautiously. Direct effects of household structure remained significant, indicating that global brain volume accounted for only a small portion of the association between household structure and psychopathology. Moreover, because these analyses were cross-sectional, they do not establish a causal developmental pathway. Rather, they suggest that neurostructural differences may be one correlate of the broader association between caregiving context and behavioral functioning. Future longitudinal work is needed to determine whether differences in caregiving context precede changes in brain development, whether brain development contributes to later behavioral outcomes, or whether these associations reflect shared influences from other unmeasured family or environmental factors.

We observed limited evidence that associations between household structure and psychopathology differed by sex. Specifically, sex interacted with household structure in predicting DSM-oriented conduct problems only after parental monitoring and caregiver warmth were included as covariates, and this pattern remained after accounting for parental mental health. Given that this interaction emerged in sensitivity analyses and was specific to one outcome, it should be interpreted cautiously. This finding may reflect outcome-specific variability rather than a robust sex difference. Future work should test whether sex moderates the associations between caregiving context and behavioral outcomes using more detailed measures of family processes.

From a clinical and public health perspective, these findings underscore the importance of considering household structure as one component of the caregiving context when assessing child mental health and development. All children may benefit from early screening for attentional difficulties and behavioral regulation problems, even when socioeconomic resources are relatively stable. Interventions that provide targeted support to caregivers, such as access to childcare resources, parenting support programs, and stress-reduction interventions, may help mitigate these risks. Moreover, school- and community-based programs that offer consistent support and opportunities for self-regulation and social engagement may be particularly beneficial. Recognizing household structure as a distinct developmental context, rather than a proxy for socioeconomic disadvantage, may improve the precision of prevention and intervention efforts aimed at promoting child mental health and neurodevelopment. Importantly, these findings reflect modest population-level associations and should not be overinterpreted at the level of individual families. Many households with one caregiver provide stable, supportive, and enriching environments, and factors such as extended family involvement, community support, caregiver well-being, and parenting quality likely play a larger role in shaping child outcomes than household structure alone.

The current study has several strengths. We utilized a large, diverse sample of youth within a narrow age range, which enhances the generalizability of our results and minimizes age-related effects. Additionally, while the ABCD Study approximately mirrors US population demographics, we used post-stratification weights to better align the sample with US demographic and socioeconomic distributions. Further, the current study addresses a gap in the literature by demonstrating that household structure is associated with neurodevelopmental and behavioral outcomes beyond SES and other relevant factors.

The current study also has several important limitations. First, household structure was approximated using caregiver marital status. We restricted analyses to children whose caregivers identified as either “married” or “never married,” excluding caregivers who endorsed being “widowed,” “divorced,” “separated,” or “living with a partner.” These excluded categories introduce ambiguity regarding current caregiving roles, financial contributions, and the timing or duration of household transitions. For example, cohabiting partners may vary in their involvement in caregiving and household resources, whereas widowed, divorced, or separated caregivers may reflect either recent or longstanding changes in household structure. Because the dataset does not capture the timing, duration, or caregiving involvement associated with these arrangements, we could not reliably classify children’s exposure to one- versus two-caregiver household contexts. This restriction may limit generalizability, as households with one caregiver can vary widely in transition histories, stressors, and available supports. Future work should assess household transitions prospectively, as developmental outcomes may depend on both household composition and children’s cumulative exposure to different caregiving arrangements.

Second, household structure should not be interpreted as a direct measure of parenting quality or caregiving support. Although sensitivity analyses included parental monitoring and perceived caregiver warmth, these measures do not capture the full range of caregiving processes that may shape child development. Changes in household structure may also be beneficial or protective under certain circumstances; for example, living with one stable caregiver may be preferable to remaining in an adverse two-caregiver environment. In addition, the presence of two adults in the home does not indicate how caregiving responsibilities are distributed, nor does it capture the degree of involvement from each caregiver. The current study also did not assess the contributions of extended family members, such as grandparents, older siblings, or other relatives, who may provide substantial caregiving, emotional support, and household stability. These contributions may be particularly important in households where caregiving responsibilities may be distributed across a broader family network. Future studies should more directly measure caregiving involvement, extended family support, and how household structure is experienced by children and families.

Third, although we adjusted for family income and parental education, residual socioeconomic and contextual confounding remains possible. Household structure is embedded within broader neighborhood and community contexts that may influence both caregiving environments and child development. For example, we did not include neighborhood-level socioeconomic disadvantage, such as the area deprivation index (ADI), which captures dimensions of neighborhood deprivation not reflected in family income or parental education alone. Because neighborhood disadvantage may be associated with access to resources, school quality, safety, environmental stressors, and health-related opportunities, future work should incorporate ADI or similar neighborhood-level measures to better separate household-level caregiving context from broader structural conditions.

Fourth, neuroimaging analyses were restricted to gray matter volume. Other neurostructural measures, including cortical thickness and surface area, have been associated with SES and may provide complementary information about neurodevelopmental variation (Chad‐Friedman et al., 2021; Rakesh et al., 2023). Future work should examine whether household structure is associated with these additional neuroimaging phenotypes.

Finally, the cross-sectional and observational design of this study precludes causal inference. Although we statistically adjusted for multiple covariates, this approach cannot account for all unmeasured or residual confounding. This limitation is especially relevant for the mediation analyses, which should be interpreted as exploratory tests of statistical indirect effects rather than evidence of causal mechanisms. Longitudinal analyses are needed to clarify the temporal ordering of household structure, brain development, and psychopathology outcomes. Importantly, although the observed associations were statistically significant, effect sizes were small, indicating modest population-level differences rather than large or deterministic effects at the level of individual children or families.

In conclusion, the results of this study suggest that household structure is associated with variation in brain volume and psychopathology symptoms in children, independent of SES. These findings highlight household structure as one dimension of the caregiving context that may capture aspects of children’s daily environments not fully reflected by income or parental education. Exploratory mediation analyses showed small indirect effects through total cortical and subcortical volume, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional design. Longitudinal analyses with future waves of the ABCD Study are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics of these associations. Finally, additional research is needed to identify modifiable sources of support within families, schools, and communities that could promote positive developmental outcomes across diverse household contexts.
