Matrix Quantum Mechanics and Entanglement Entropy: A Review
Jackson R. Fliss ♭,♮ and Alexander Frenkel ♯

Abstract
We review aspects of entanglement entropy in the quantum mechanics of N × N N\times N matrices, i.e. matrix quantum mechanics (MQM), at large N N . In doing so we review standard models of MQM and their relation to string theory, D-brane physics, and emergent non-commutative geometries. We overview, in generality, definitions of subsystems and entanglement entropies in theories with gauge redundancy and discuss the additional structure required for definining subsystems in MQMs possessing a U ⁡ ( N ) U(N) gauge redundancy. In connecting these subsystems to non-commutative geometry, we review several works on ‘target space entanglement,’ and entanglement in non-commutative field theories, highlighting the conditions in which target space entanglement entropy displays an ‘area law’ at large N N . We summarize several example calculations of entanglement entropy in non-commutative geometries and MQMs. We review recent work in connecting the area law entanglement of MQM to the Ryu-Takayanagi formula, highlighting the conditions in which U ⁡ ( N ) U(N) invariance implies a minimal area formula for the entanglement entropy at large N N . Finally, we make comments on open questions and research directions.

Since then, many pieces of intuition have emerged for how exactly a fluctuating spacetime emerges from the degrees of freedom of its holographic dual. We touch on only a couple of them here. One of the best-established correspondences is the observation of Ryu and Takayanagi that entanglement entropy of geometric subregions on the boundary is given by minimal-area surfaces in the bulk

This standard picture of holography, where the deep interior of emergent geometry emerges from the IR of the holographic dual and the boundary is emergent from the UV, cannot be the whole story. This intuition only describes the radial direction of the emergent geometry, but

This additional intuition comes from matrix eigenvalues – the large

The matrices describe branes and the configuration of open strings between them, and the eigenvalues of the matrices correspond to the distribution of branes along a particular dimension. Close to a stack of branes, within an order 1 number of string lengths, the branes will fluctuate away from the center of mass of the stack, giving some width to the eigenvalue distribution of the matrices. In the

Taken together, these two pictures of emergent geometry in holography complement each other. Geometry above the curvature scale scale seems to emerge from the energy spectrum and entanglement structure of the boundary theory, in a manner almost agnostic as to the precise field content of the dual theory, whereas geometry below the energy scale seems to emerge from the
N\times N

It would be great to find a unifying picture of the emergence of geometry, where the entanglement structure of the matrix degrees of freedom gives rise to geometry below the AdS lengthscale, just like the CFT entanglement structure gives rise to geometry above the AdS lengthscale. This article reviews recent progress towards this goal – developments in understanding subalgebras and area-law entanglement structure emerging from the quantum mechanics of matrix systems, i.e.

A brisk review of MQM models in string and M-theory

In a theory of matrix quantum mechanics (MQM) the classical degrees of freedom of the systems are
N\times N
Hermitian matrices
X^{i}
, with labels
X^{i}
running from
1

D

The matrices

admit an adjoint action of the unitary group

X^{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}\,X^{i}\,\mathcal{U}^{{\dagger}}.

(2.1)

We consider Lagrangians symmetric under this action, typically of the single trace form so that an ‘t Hooft expansion of the type described in the introduction applies:

L=\Tr[\sum_{i}\dot{X}_{i}^{2}-V(X^{i})]~,

(2.2)

where

is some analytic function of the
X^{i}
.
Once we quantize the theory, the generators of the symmetry (
2.1
) are

G_{ab}=2\mathsf{i}\sum_{i}\left(:\mathrel{[X^{i},\Pi^{i}]}:\right)_{ab},

(2.3)

with

the conjugate momentum to
X^{i}
. The normal ordering symbol places the position

The Hilbert space of the theory is the space of square-integrable functions of the matrices,

. The symmetry (
2.1

). As we highlighted in §

, both choices produce interesting stringy physics
[

]
, with non-singlets often associated to sources of open strings.

The non-commutative plane:

4
Subsystems and entanglement

Entanglement is a measure of how quantum information is distributed across separate
subsystems
in a quantum theory. What we define as a ‘subsystem’ is a matter of choice and depends on the type of question one wishes to ask of a physical theory. A subsystem could consist of a particular collection of particles, a collection of sites in a lattice system, or a more abstract division of internal degrees of freedom. However, in a local quantum field theory we are typically interested in how information is spread across differing spatial regions. As we will soon see the declaration of a ‘subsystem’ is more interesting in matrix quantum mechanics for the reason that space itself emerges from internal degrees of freedom. Precisely defining a partition of matrix degrees of freedom that results in a spatial subsystem is one goal of this section.

Before doing so, we will more concretely define what is meant when we use the word ‘subsystem.’ A subsystem,
\Sigma
, in any quantum theory is defined through a factorization of its Hilbert space,
\mathcal{H}
. At its simplest level such a factorization takes the form

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma}}~,

(4.1)

where the factor
\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}
can be identified with the degrees of freedom of the subsystem,
\Sigma
, and
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma}}
with its complement,
\overline{\Sigma}
. While such a factorization cleanly separates the degrees of freedom of
\Sigma
from its complement,
\overline{\Sigma}
, this does not imply every states is a product of a state in
\Sigma
and a state in
\overline{\Sigma}
. A generic state,
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
, is entangled and shares information across
\Sigma
and
\overline{\Sigma}
in the form of a superposition of such products. One measure for this entanglement is the
entanglement entropy
defined in the following way.

From
|\psi\rangle
we form its density matrix,
\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
and reduce it to subsystem
\Sigma
by “tracing out”
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma}}
. There is no subtlety in this precisely because
\mathcal{H}
is a tensor product, (
4.1
). This defines the reduced density matrix
\rho_{\Sigma}
:

\rho_{\Sigma}=\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma}}}\rho~.

(4.2)

While
\rho
is a projector onto a pure state, when
|\psi\rangle
is entangled
\rho_{\Sigma}
will be a probabilistic mixture indicating a loss of information in forgetting
\overline{\Sigma}
. The von Neumann entropy of
\rho_{\Sigma}
provides a measure of this information loss which is the entanglement entropy:

S_{\Sigma}=-\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}}\left(\rho_{\Sigma}\log\rho_{\Sigma}\right)~.

(4.3)

When
\Sigma
is a spatial subregion of a local quantum field the above basic steps are rife with subtleties and obstructions. At a basic level this is because the vacuum of a local quantum field theory is entangled at all length scales about the boundary of
\Sigma
(the
entangling surface
) which leads to a UV divergence in
S_{\Sigma}
. In many cases the tensor factorization (
4.1
) can be defined in a cutoff sense leading to regulated entanglement entropy. A more modern perspective is to define entanglement entropy with respect to an algebra of observables. Given an algebra of operators
\mathcal{A}
acting on
\mathcal{H}
, we assign a subalgebra
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}\subset\mathcal{A}
as the operators with support within
\Sigma
. The reduced density matrix can be assigned as the state in
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
reproducing the expectation values for all operators
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}

\tr\left(\rho_{\Sigma}\,\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}\right)=\tr\left(\rho\,\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}\right)~,\qquad\forall~\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}~,

(4.4)

and its entanglement entropy can be computed through (
4.3
). According to the classification of von Neumann algebras, the subalgebra associated to a spatial region of a local quantum field theory is type-III and does not possess a trace
[
7
,
123
,
65
,
80
]
; this is a restatement of the above divergence. Computing (
4.3
) still requires regulating. A comprehensive study of the evolving study of entanglement entropy in quantum field theory is not within the scope of this review article.

The extended Hilbert space

The lack of factorization of the Hilbert space of physical states means we need to provide a prescription for defining reduced density matrices and for computing entanglement entropies. There is no canonical prescription and in principle the entanglement entropy that we compute will depend on our prescription. In essence what we want is to embed the Hilbert space of physical states into a larger
extended Hilbert space
admitting a tensor factorization:

\mathcal{H}\overset{\mathcal{I}}{\hookrightarrow}\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext}}=\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext},\Sigma}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext},\overline{\Sigma}}~.

(4.6)

Precisely because gauge invariance precludes the factorization (
4.1
), the extended Hilbert space contains non-physical, gauge-variant, states and the physical Hilbert space,
\mathcal{H}
, is embedded only as a subspace. The embedding,
\mathcal{I}
, is what is known as a
factorization map
. Given the structure of (
4.5
) one natural embedding is given by

\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext}}=\left(\bigoplus_{q}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,q}\right)\otimes\left(\bigoplus_{q}\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},q}\right)~,

(4.7)

although there can exist multiple inequivalent factorization maps. Under the image of
\mathcal{I}
, any physical state is represented on a factorized Hilbert space; from there a reduced density matrix and entanglement entropy can be computed per the procedure outlined in equations (
4.2
) and (
4.3
). In doing so it is important to appreciate that the resulting entanglement entropy is sensitive to our choice of factorization map. The factorization (
4.7
) is natural from the point of view of the structure (
4.5
): given a basis
|m_{q},\bar{m}_{q}\rangle
of
\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,q}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},q}
, a state

|\psi\rangle=\sum_{q}\sum_{m_{q},\bar{m}_{q}}\psi^{(q)}_{m_{q},\bar{m}_{q}}|m_{q},\bar{m}_{q}\rangle\equiv\sum_{q}|\psi^{(q)}\rangle~,

(4.8)

embeds trivially to a state
|\tilde{\psi}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext}}

|\tilde{\psi}\rangle=\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}\sum_{m_{q},\bar{m}_{q^{\prime}}}\tilde{\psi}^{(q,q^{\prime})}_{m_{q},\bar{m}_{q^{\prime}}}|m_{q},m_{q^{\prime}}\rangle~,\qquad\tilde{\psi}^{(q,q^{\prime})}_{m_{q},\bar{m}_{q^{\prime}}}=\delta^{qq^{\prime}}\,\psi^{(q)}_{m_{q},\bar{m}_{q}}~.

(4.9)

Within this embedding the reduced density matrix takes the form

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}=\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext},\overline{\Sigma}}}|\tilde{\psi}\rangle\langle\tilde{\psi}|=\sum_{q}\sum_{\bar{m}_{q}}\left(\psi^{(q)}_{n_{q},\bar{m}_{q}}\right)^{\ast}\psi^{(q)}_{m_{q},\bar{m}_{q}}|m_{q}\rangle\langle n_{q}|=\sum_{q}\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},q}}|\psi^{(q)}\rangle\langle\psi^{(q)}|~.

(4.10)

That is the density matrix reduced in the extended Hilbert space is equivalent to an orthogonal sum over the physical state reduced on each sector of (
4.5
). Because the individual
|\psi^{(q)}\rangle
’s are not normalized to unity (indeed it is the sum over their normalizations that must be unity) we can write

|\psi^{(q)}\rangle\equiv\sqrt{p_{q}}|\hat{\psi}^{(q)}\rangle~,\qquad\langle\hat{\psi}^{(q)}|\hat{\psi}^{(q)}\rangle=1~,\qquad\sum_{q}p_{q}=1~.

(4.11)

That is
\{p_{q}\}
define a classical probability distribution and the reduced density matrix is a classical mixture of density matrices

\tilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}=\sum_{q}p_{q}~\hat{\rho}_{\Sigma,q}~,\qquad\hat{\rho}_{\Sigma,q}\equiv\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},q}}|\hat{\psi}^{(q)}\rangle\langle\psi^{(q)}|~.

(4.12)

The resulting entanglement entropy contains a contribution of a classical Shannon entropy of the probability distribution plus a weighted sum over entanglement entropies in each selection sector:

S_{\Sigma}=-\sum_{q}p_{q}\log p_{q}+\sum_{q}p_{q}\,S^{(q)}_{\Sigma}~,\qquad S^{(q)}_{\Sigma}\equiv-\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,q}}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\Sigma,q}\log\hat{\rho}_{\Sigma,q}\right)~.

(4.13)

Edge modes and area laws

Within the extended Hilbert space what were previously gauge ‘symmetries’ now act as global symmetries. More specifically let the gauge group be
\mathcal{G}
, and call the generator of a local gauge transformation,
\hat{G}(x)
. This generator does not necessarily annihilate all states in
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext}}
; it is only required to annihilate the physical states within
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H})
. The states within
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext}}
that carry the action of
\hat{G}(x)
correspond to new degrees of freedom. Because gauge symmetries act locally it is easy to argue that the states with non-trivial
\mathcal{G}
action are localized to the boundary between
\Sigma
and
\overline{\Sigma}
: gauge transformations acting strictly within the interior of either of
\Sigma
or
\overline{\Sigma}
do not spoil the factorization of
\mathcal{H}
. It is instead the gauge transformations localized directly at the entangling surface,
\partial\Sigma
, that act simultaneously on
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext},\Sigma}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext},\overline{\Sigma}}
. Thus
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext}}
will contain degrees of freedom that are localized to
\partial\Sigma
and carry a representation of the gauge group restricted to
\partial\Sigma
. These are
edge modes
.

Each factor,
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext},\Sigma}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext},\overline{\Sigma}}
, will contain edge mode degrees of freedom. Physical states in
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{H})
will precisely correlate the edge modes of
\Sigma
with those of
\overline{\Sigma}
such that the global state is invariant under gauge transformations. Thus there is a significant amount of entanglement across
\partial\Sigma
in physical states carried by edge modes. To be more specific, the generator of a gauge transformation acting on
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext}}
will take the form

\hat{G}_{i}=\hat{G}_{\Sigma,i}\otimes\hat{\mathbb{1}}_{\overline{\Sigma},i}+\hat{\mathbb{1}}_{\Sigma,i}\otimes\hat{G}_{\overline{\Sigma},i}~.

(4.14)

The index
i
labels the differing gauge generators that
\tilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}
must be a singlet under. In a lattice gauge theory this could be, for instance, all the links that cross the entangling surface,
\partial\Sigma
. Both
\hat{G}_{\Sigma,i}
and
\hat{G}_{\overline{\Sigma},i}
generate
\mathcal{G}
actions at
\partial\Sigma
and physical states are invariant under the diagonal of these two actions. In particular the image of global state in
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext}}
,
\tilde{\rho}=\mathcal{I}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}
, satisfies

\llbracket\hat{G}_{i},\tilde{\rho}\rrbracket=0~,

(4.15)

which then implies that the reduced states are singlets as well:

\llbracket\hat{G}_{\Sigma,i},\tilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}\rrbracket=0~.

(4.16)

This implies that
\tilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}
breaks up into a sum over representation identities of
\mathcal{G}
:

\tilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}=\bigoplus_{\mu_{i}}p_{\mu_{i}}\frac{\hat{\mathbb{1}}_{\mu_{i}}}{\text{d}_{\mu_{i}}}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{\mu_{i}}~,

(4.17)

where we index representations by
\mu_{i}
and their representation dimensions by
\text{d}_{\mu_{i}}
. Lastly,
p_{\mu_{i}}
are set of probability measures such that
\sum_{\mu_{i}}p_{\mu_{i}}=1
, and
\hat{\rho}_{\mu_{i}}
is the portion of the state in the
\mu_{i}
block that is a singlet under the gauge generators contained strictly in
\Sigma
. Note that this structure of reduced density matrix is commensurate with the generic structure of the physical Hilbert space given by (
4.5
).

We see that gauge invariance enforces that within the extended Hilbert space, the state is
maximally entangled
amongst the edge modes at
\partial\Sigma
in a given representation. The entanglement entropy of the state takes the generic form

S_{\Sigma}=-\sum_{\mu_{i}}p_{\mu_{i}}\log p_{\mu_{i}}+\sum_{\mu_{i}}p_{\mu_{i}}\log\text{d}_{\mu_{i}}-\sum_{\mu_{i}}p_{\mu_{i}}\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,\mu_{i}}}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\mu_{i}}\log\hat{\rho}_{\mu_{i}}\right)~.

(4.18)

The first term is often called the
Shannon term
as it takes the form of the Shannon entropy of a classical probability distribution. The last term is a weighted sum over ‘bulk’ entanglement arising from gauge singlet degrees of freedom within the interiors of
\Sigma
and
\overline{\Sigma}
. This middle term arises from entanglement at the edge
\partial\Sigma
and because gauge transformations are local, it is extensive in the area of
\partial\Sigma
(i.e. recall in a lattice gauge theory the sum
i
would range over all links crossing the entangling surface). Thus term term is responsible for an edge mode entanglement entropy that scales with
\absolutevalue{\pa\Sg}
, the area of
\partial\Sigma
:

S_{\Sigma,\text{edge}}\equiv-\sum_{\mu_{i}}p_{\mu_{i}}\log p_{\mu_{i}}+\sum_{\mu_{i}}p_{\mu_{i}}\log\text{d}_{\mu_{i}}\sim\frac{|\partial\Sigma|}{\epsilon^{d-2}}~,

(4.19)

where
\epsilon
is a dimensionful short distance cutoff (such as a lattice spacing). This behavior is what we mean when say
‘area law’
entanglement.

Gauge invariant subalgebras

The above description is, in words, indicating that there is not a (uniquely) determined notion of localizing degrees of physical degrees of freedom with a subregion,
\Sigma
, or its complement,
\overline{\Sigma}
. This has an operational meaning in terms of the subalgebras,
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
, acting on physical states, which we briefly review now.

Given an assignment
\Sigma\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
of a subalgebra of gauge-invariant operators to a subregion
\Sigma
, we then associate to
\overline{\Sigma}
, the commutant:

\mathcal{A}_{\overline{\Sigma}}:=\left(\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}\right)^{\mathsf{c}}=\left\{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{A}~\big|~\llbracket\mathcal{O},\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}\rrbracket=0~,~\forall~\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}\right\}

(4.20)

This definition is in keeping with locality in a quantum field theory: operators at spacelike distances commute. The tension of gauge-invariance and the ability to locally isolate degrees of freedom is then manifested that these two subalgebras have overlap. That is they possess an intersection called
the center
which, by definition, consists of operators that commute with
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\overline{\Sigma}}
:

\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\overline{\Sigma}}~,\qquad\llbracket\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma},\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma},\mathcal{A}_{\overline{\Sigma}}\rrbracket=0~.

(4.21)

Operators in
\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma}
can be simultaneously diagonalized and take the form of projectors on their mutual eigenbasis:

\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma}=\text{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{{\bm{\Pi}}_{q}\}~,\qquad\sum_{q}{\bm{\Pi}}_{q}=\mathbb{1}~.

(4.22)

These are precisely the projectors that isolate a given block of (
4.5
). For a given state,
\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
with an associated reduced density matrix
\rho_{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
satisfying (
4.4
), we can work in the eigenbasis of
\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma}
to write

\rho_{\Sigma}=\bigoplus_{q}\,p_{\Sigma,q}\,\hat{\rho}_{\Sigma,q}~,

(4.23)

We have separated off coefficients such that each sub-block of
\rho_{\Sigma}
is a normalized and positive density matrix:

\tr\hat{\rho}_{\Sigma,q}=1~.

(4.24)

Normalization and positivity of the full density matrix then implies that
\{p_{\Sigma,q}\}
define a probability distribution. This is, in fact, the same distribution as defined in (
4.11
). Subsequently, the von Neumann entropy of
\rho_{\Sigma}
displays the, now familiar, Shannon contribution as well as weighted sum over entanglement entropies:

S_{\Sigma}=-\sum_{q}p_{q}\log p_{q}-\sum_{q}\tr\left(\rho_{\Sigma,q}\log\rho_{\Sigma,q}\right)~.

(4.25)

5
Target space entanglement

The above discussion has focussed on the subsystems given by local partitions of the base space that a physical system is defined on. However for models of matrix quantum mechanics there is no base space to partition; indeed, as we have emphasized above, a intriguing consideration of these models is that spacetime is not the arena in which the dynamics takes place, but instead emerges from the dynamics of the model itself. In the language of string theory and sigma models, the spacetime is part of the
target space
of MQM. Indeed, the base space of MQM is a point and so there is no meaningful notion of ‘spatial subsystem’ with respect to which we can partition a state. Thus in investigating how the connection between entanglement and locality emerges from MQM, we will need to investigate target space entanglement. Some of what we have established in the previous section also applies here: the target space itself will typically have gauge invariances that will prevent strict factorization of the physical Hilbert space. There will also be new subtleties that we will have to consider.

In a not completely unrelated fashion to the above, the notion of entanglement in target space also plays a role in string theory where the target space of the strings is precisely the real space of the low-energy effective supergravity. There is a growing body of literature developing and investigating target space entanglement in string theory
[
90
,
86
,
53
,
14
,
101
,
141
,
52
,
3
,
107
]
. In this review we will focus solely on the aspects most applicable to MQM and unfortunately omit a review of this literature, however we encourage the interested reader to see the papers cited above. We will build intuition for the problem with single and multiple particles propagating in a
\mathbb{R}^{D}
target space before moving to matrices.

5.1
Single particle

The simplest scenario to begin our discussion of target space entanglement is the quantum mechanics of a single particle. Configurations of the system are given by the position of the particle at a given point in time,
\vec{x}(t)
, which is the coordinates of a target space, which for simplicity we will take to be
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. The quantum Hilbert space is

\mathcal{H}_{1}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})~.

(5.1)

Indeed consider the single-particle Hilbert space spanned by
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
wavefunctions,
|\psi\rangle
. We can also use the delta function normalizable basis
|\vec{x}\rangle
. Given a subregion of
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, this basis makes clear that is the one-particle space is actually a tensor sum:

\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},1}~,\qquad\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,1}:=\text{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\left\{|\vec{x}\rangle~\big|~\vec{x}\in\Sigma\right\}

(5.2)

and a similar definition for
\mathcal{H}_{1,\overline{\Sigma}}
. While perhaps not immediately obvious, (
5.2
) actually fits into the general Hilbert space structure we defined above, (
4.5
). Indeed defining
\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,0}=\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},0}:=\mathbb{C}
, then
\mathcal{H}_{1}
can be written as

\mathcal{H}_{1}=\bigoplus_{q=0,1}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,q}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},1-q}\right)~.

(5.3)

We can view
\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma/\overline{\Sigma},q}
as the Hilbert space of
q
particles with
\Sigma/\overline{\Sigma}
as their target. Here the lack of tensor factorization stems entirely from the global constraint of particle number conservation – if the particle is localized in
\Sigma
it is not localized in
\overline{\Sigma}
.

It will be useful notation for later to define a target space subregion theta function,
{\bm{\theta}}_{\Sigma}
, associated to
\Sigma
as

{\bm{\theta}}_{\Sigma}(\vec{x}):=\theta(f_{\Sigma}(\vec{x})-\mathsf{c})~,\qquad{\bm{\theta}}_{\overline{\Sigma}}(\vec{x}):=1-{\bm{\theta}}_{\Sigma}(\vec{x})

(5.4)

where
\theta
is the standard Heaviside theta function, and
f_{\Sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function whose level set defines
\partial\Sigma
, i.e.
\partial\Sigma=\{f_{\Sigma}(\vec{x})=\mathsf{c}\}
and
f_{\Sigma}(\vec{x})>\mathsf{c}
for
\vec{x}\in\Sigma
and
f_{\Sigma}(\vec{x})<\mathsf{c}
for
\vec{x}\in\overline{\Sigma}
.

In the language of subalgebras, this decomposition of the Hilbert space has a natural action under the subalgebras

\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}=\mathfrak{U}\left\{|\vec{x}\rangle\langle\vec{x}^{\prime}|~\big|~\vec{x},\vec{x}^{\prime}\in\Sigma\right\}~,\qquad\mathcal{A}_{\overline{\Sigma}}=\mathfrak{U}\left\{|\vec{x}\rangle\langle\vec{x}^{\prime}|~\big|~\vec{x},\vec{x}^{\prime}\in\overline{\Sigma}\right\}~.

(5.5)

where
\mathfrak{U}
denotes the universal enveloping algebra and it should be tacitly understood that the identity on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is included in the generation of this algebra. Associated with these subalgebras are projectors

{\bm{\Pi}}_{\Sigma}=\int_{\Sigma}\differential^{D}\vec{x}\,|\vec{x}\rangle\langle\vec{x}|=\int\differential^{D}\vec{x}~{\bm{\theta}}_{\Sigma}(\vec{x})|\vec{x}\rangle\langle\vec{x}|~,\qquad{\bm{\Pi}}_{\overline{\Sigma}}=\int_{\overline{\Sigma}}\differential^{D}\vec{x}\,|\vec{x}\rangle\langle\vec{x}|=\int\differential^{D}\vec{x}~{\bm{\theta}}_{\overline{\Sigma}}(\vec{x})|\vec{x}\rangle\langle\vec{x}|~,

(5.6)

which are the natural promotion of (
5.4
) to Hilbert space operators and from which we can project any operator,
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
, acting on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to the corresponding subalgebra through
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{A/\overline{\Sigma}}={\bm{\Pi}}_{A/\overline{\Sigma}}\,\hat{\mathcal{O}}\,{\bm{\Pi}}_{A/\overline{\Sigma}}
. These subalgebras contain a common center

\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\overline{\Sigma}}=\text{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\left\{\Pi_{\Sigma},\Pi_{\overline{\Sigma}}\right\}~,

(5.7)

which is a restatement of the lack of factorization of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

From our previous section, we can write down a natural candidate extended Hilbert space for the one-particle system, (
5.3
), as

\mathcal{H}_{1,\text{ext}}:=\left(\bigoplus_{q=0,1}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,q}\right)\otimes\left(\bigoplus_{q=0,1}\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},q}\right)~.

(5.8)

In this Hilbert space we have relaxed the constraint of particle number conservation: in
\mathcal{H}_{1,\text{ext}}
we can have either one particle in
\Sigma
, one in
\overline{\Sigma}
, one in both, or none at all.

Let us now describe the factorization map of physical states from
\mathcal{H}_{1}
into
\mathcal{H}_{1,\text{ext}}
. A generic state of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
can be written as

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\int\,\differential^{D}\vec{x}~\psi(\vec{x})|\vec{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\,\differential^{D}\vec{x}~{\bm{\theta}}_{\Sigma}(\vec{x})~\psi(\vec{x})|\vec{x}\rangle+\int\differential^{D}\vec{x}~{\bm{\theta}}_{\overline{\Sigma}}(\vec{x})~\psi(\vec{x})|\vec{x}\rangle

\displaystyle:=|\psi_{\Sigma}\rangle+|\psi_{\overline{\Sigma}}\rangle~.

(5.9)

The probability to find the particle in
\Sigma
or
\overline{\Sigma}
is given by

p_{\Sigma,1}=\int\differential^{D}\vec{x}~{\bm{\theta}}_{\Sigma}(\vec{x})~\absolutevalue{\psi(\vec x)}^{2}~,\qquad p_{\overline{\Sigma},1}=\int\differential^{D}\vec{x}~{\bm{\theta}}_{\overline{\Sigma}}(\vec{x})~\absolutevalue{\psi(\vec x)}^{2}~,

(5.10)

which are equivalent, by number conservation, to finding no particles in
\overline{\Sigma}
or
\Sigma
, respectively:

p_{\overline{\Sigma},0}=p_{\Sigma,1}=1-p_{\overline{\Sigma},1}~,\qquad p_{\Sigma,0}=p_{\overline{\Sigma},1}=1-p_{\Sigma,1}~.

(5.11)

We can then designate the image of
|\psi\rangle
in
\mathcal{H}_{1,\text{ext}}
as

\widetilde{|\psi\rangle}=\left(\sqrt{p_{\Sigma,0}}\oplus|\psi_{\Sigma}\rangle\right)\otimes\left(\sqrt{p_{\overline{\Sigma},0}}\oplus|\psi_{\overline{\Sigma}}\rangle\right)~.

(5.12)

It is easy to verify that this is an isometry:

\widetilde{\langle\psi}|\widetilde{\psi\rangle}=\left(p_{\Sigma,0}+p_{\Sigma,1}\right)\left(p_{\overline{\Sigma},0}+p_{\overline{\Sigma},1}\right)=1~.

(5.13)

An interesting feature of this factorization map is that, strictly speaking, (
5.12
) is a product state on the state between
\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma}}
. However the entanglement entropy of
|\psi\rangle
is still non-zero: in the form we illustrated above, (
4.18
), this entanglement arises as the classical, Shannon term induced by the particle number conservation. Let us show this now. It is easy to work out that the reduced density matrix is

\widetilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}=p_{\Sigma,0}\oplus|\psi_{\Sigma}\rangle\langle\psi_{\Sigma}|~.

(5.14)

Although
|\psi_{\Sigma}\rangle\langle\psi_{\Sigma}|
looks to be a rank-one projector, it is not quite since
|\psi_{\Sigma}\rangle
is not normalized – its norm is
\langle\psi_{\Sigma}|\psi_{\Sigma}\rangle=p_{\Sigma,1}
. Thus the von Neumann entropy of
\widetilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}
is

S_{\Sigma}=-p_{\Sigma,0}\log p_{\Sigma,0}-p_{\Sigma,1}\log p_{\Sigma,1}~.

(5.15)

We pause to note that we could have arrived at (
5.14
) algebraically from the projectors spanning the center, (
5.7
): notice that
{\bm{\Pi}}_{\overline{\Sigma}}
and
{\bm{\Pi}}_{\Sigma}
are the projectors onto the
\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,0}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},0}
blocks of (
5.3
), respectively. The full density matrix,
\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
, then takes the form

\rho=\left({\bm{\Pi}}_{\overline{\Sigma}}\rho{\bm{\Pi}}_{\overline{\Sigma}}\right)\oplus\left({\bm{\Pi}}_{\Sigma}\rho{\bm{\Pi}}_{\Sigma}\right)=\left(|\psi_{\overline{\Sigma}}\rangle\langle\psi_{\overline{\Sigma}}|\right)\oplus\left(|\psi_{\Sigma}\rangle\langle\psi_{\Sigma}|\right)

(5.16)

whose reduction on each
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},q}
on each block yields (
5.14
).

5.2
N
identical particles

We now increase the complexity by considering the quantum mechanics of multiple identical particles. Much of this section will follow
[
132
,
44
]
. We designate our identical particles by positions
\{\vec{x}_{a}\}_{a=1,\ldots,N}
. The quantum Hilbert space is given by

\mathcal{H}_{N}=\left(\bigotimes_{a=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{1}^{(a)}\right)/\mathfrak{S}_{N}~,

(5.17)

where each
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{(a)}
is the one-particle Hilbert space from before and
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
is the symmetric group of
N
elements. An element
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
acts on the collection of single-particle wavefunctions symmetrically or anti-symmetrically depending on if the particles are bosons or fermions, respectively:

\displaystyle\sigma~\circ~\psi_{\text{boson}}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2},\ldots,\vec{x}_{N})

\displaystyle=\psi_{\text{boson}}(\vec{x}_{\sigma(1)},\vec{x}_{\sigma(2)},\ldots,\vec{x}_{\sigma(N)}),

\displaystyle\sigma~\circ~\psi_{\text{fermion}}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2},\ldots,\vec{x}_{N})

\displaystyle=\text{sgn}(\sigma)\psi_{\text{fermion}}(\vec{x}_{\sigma(1)},\vec{x}_{\sigma(2)},\ldots,\vec{x}_{\sigma(N)})~.

(5.18)

We will work with bosons in what follows, although fermions only require a simple modification.

We can view (
5.17
) as treating
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
as a gauge redundancy: physical states of
\mathcal{H}_{N}
are wavefunctions of
N
variables, with the additional constraint that they are symmetric under permutations. This is an additional target space gauge redundancy in comparison to the single-particle space. We can identify
\mathcal{H}_{N}
as the image of
N
-fold tensor product under the projector,
P_{\mathfrak{S}_{N}}

\mathcal{H}_{N}=P_{\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\left(\bigotimes_{a=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{1}^{(a)}\right)~.

(5.19)

For bosonic particles
P_{\mathfrak{S}_{N}}
can be expressed in terms of basis states as

P_{\mathfrak{S}_{N}}=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\int\differential^{D}\vec{x}_{1}\differential^{D}\vec{x}_{2}\ldots\differential^{D}\vec{x}_{N}~|\vec{x}_{\sigma(1)}\vec{x}_{\sigma(2)}\ldots\vec{x}_{\sigma(N)}\rangle\langle\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2},\ldots,\vec{x}_{N}|~.

(5.20)

We will be interested once again in partitioning the target space into a region,
\Sigma
, and its complement,
\overline{\Sigma}
. Our warmup for the one-particle system acts as a guide here: we first decompose each one-particle factor of (
5.17
) as

\mathcal{H}_{N}=\left(\bigotimes_{a=1}^{N}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,1}^{(a)}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},1}^{(a)}\right)\right)/\mathfrak{S}_{N}~,

(5.21)

which we can further decompose into

\mathcal{H}_{N}=\bigoplus_{a=0}^{N}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,a}\otimes_{\text{S}}\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},N-a}\right)~,\qquad\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,a}:=\left(\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,1}\right)^{\otimes a}/\mathfrak{S}_{i}~.

(5.22)

where
\otimes_{\text{S}}
is the symmetrized tensor product. This structure is slightly different than what was discussed in typical gauge theories, (
5.3
): it is an additional structure of gauge redundancy that arises in target space entanglements. We will see this structure to a richer extent when we consider MQM.

We can cast (
5.22
) in the form of (
4.5
) by embedding it into

\mathcal{H}_{N}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{N}=\bigoplus_{a=0}^{N}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma,a}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma},N-a}\right)~,

(5.23)

with the symmetric tensor factor replaced with the standard one. We can think of
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
as a collection of ‘partially gauge-fixed’ Hilbert spaces with
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
broken to
\mathfrak{S}_{a}\times\mathfrak{S}_{N-a}
in each block by putting the first
a
particles,
\{\vec{x}_{1},\ldots,\vec{x}_{a}\}
, in
\Sigma
.
\mathcal{H}_{N}
can once again be isolated from
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
by the action of the projector, (
5.20
). Going the other direction, states of the
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
invariant space can be embedded into
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
by imposing
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
invariance at the level of the wavefunction. We will illustrate this shortly.

Using the projector (
5.20
), we can associate the following
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
-invariant subalgebra of operators,
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
, to
\Sigma

[
132

:

We now describe how for partially gauge-fixed states the decomposition (

6
Entanglement in emergent non-commutative geometries

In the previous section we set up the necessary background to discuss the target space entanglement of MQMs by introducing a notion of factorization of matrix entries. We illustrated two examples, the
c=1
matrix model and the matrix quantum Hall, where this entanglement entropy led to interpretation of a entanglement entropy in an emergent target space. The matrix quantum Hall example is particularly interesting because as we reviewed in §
3.3
, the target space of this model can be thought as the coordinates of a non-commutative disc. Correspondingly the fluctuations about the classical background is a non-commutative Chern-Simons field theory. As we reviewed in §
3

[
156

In all of the examples to come, we may think of a non-commutative geometry as

geometries of a MQM. That is, there may be a quantum wavefunction,

, that is strongly peaked around a particular configuration,

, satisfying one of the fuzzy space algebras, (
3.12

3.20

3.22
). Small fluctuations about this peak are described by an effective field theory living on a background fuzzy space. In considering entanglement on these spaces we may consider the entanglement of degrees of some subset of those degrees of freedom (such as the scalar sector of that effective field theory) or the fluctuations in their entirety. In some cases, particularly when a non-commutative space arises a D-brane worldvolume theory, the large
N
limit of that space and its effective field theory admit a holographic dual
[
87
,
125
]
. Within certain regimes where the supergravity approximation can be trusted, the dual geometries are standard (albeit, curved, non-AdS) geometries. For simple choices of subregion,
\Sigma

[
149
]

S_{\Sigma}=\frac{\text{Area}[\Gamma_{\Sigma}]}{4G_{N}}~,

(6.1)

where
\Gamma_{\Sigma}
is a bulk surface of minimal area anchored to
\partial\Sigma
at the boundary, and
G_{N}
is the bulk gravitational constant
[
21
,
62
,
110
,
6
]

\partial\Sigma

\partial\Sigma

In this review we will instead focus on the effective field theory side of the story. Example: the non-commutative sphere, part 2

7
Minimal areas from MQM entanglement

Up to this point we have considered entanglement in MQMs and non-commutative spaces with factorizations that ‘fix’ an entangling region in target space. In terms of the
U(N)
gauge redundancy of MQM, the factorizations we have dealt with have gauge-fixed the subset of gauge transformations that move the entangling surface. In the previous section we have argued, on general grounds, how the remaining gauge transformations preserving the entangling surface lead to non-commutative edge modes with have an area law entanglement in sufficiently nice semi-classical states.

In this section we loosen the gauge fixing and look at the effect of invariance under maps that act on the location of the entangling surface. We will find that doing so will lead us to consider a gauge orbit’s worth of area laws. Under suitable conditions, that we will describe below, the integral over this orbit can admit a saddle-point approximation resulting in it being dominated by a subregion of minimal area. The result is the expression of the entanglement entropy as a minimal area, much akin to the Ryu-Takanagi formula for holographic entanglement entropy. However in contrast to holographic entanglement entropy, the minimization is not over entangling regions anchored to any boundary system, but however over regions with fixed total volume:

S_{\Sigma}\sim\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Sigma\,,\\
\absolutevalue{\Sg}\text{ fixed}\end{subarray}}\absolutevalue{\pa\Sg}\log\frac{\absolutevalue{\Sg}}{\absolutevalue{\pa\Sg}}~.

(7.1)

Let us briefly describe how this result is reached. We begin by revisiting the factorization map described in §
5.4
. In that section we described how the physical Hilbert space of states could be embedded into an extended Hilbert space consisting of all Hermitian matrix elements an space invariant under some subgroup
G\subseteq U(N)
. For the choice
G=U(N)
, the subalgebra considered in this section is equivalent to
[
70
]
. A general choice of
G
may be viewed as an interpolation between
[
70
]
and the target space entanglement approach reviewed in §
5
. A physical state,
|\psi\rangle
, can be embedded as a
G-
invariant state
|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext}}
by starting with a state with completely gauge-fixed matrix elements and averaging it over its
G
-orbit:

|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle=\int_{G}\differential\mathcal{G}\,|\mathcal{G}\rangle|\widetilde{\psi}_{\text{gf}}\rangle~.

(7.2)

We wish to reduce this state based on the factorization of the extended Hilbert space determined by a fiducial
M\times M
block of matrix degrees of freedom, (
5.72
). We saw that the
U(M)\times U(N-M)
portion of the orbit integral leads a reduced density matrix that is maximally entangled over edge modes valued a representation
\mu
of
U(M)
and living at a fixed entangling cut. The portion of this orbit integral over elements
\mathcal{V}\in G/(U(M)\times U(N-M))
change the matrix block; they are so-called ‘wiggle modes,’ acting as a volume preserving diffeomorphisms that warp
\Sigma
to new a subregion with a distinct dominant representation,
\mu_{(\mathcal{V})}
and corresponding area,
\absolutevalue{\pa\Sg_{(\mc V)}}
. Over all we will find that density matrix reduced in the extended Hilbert space takes the form

\widetilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}\sim\oplus\hskip-10.59999pt\displaystyle\int\limits\differential{\mc V}\left[\frac{\mathds{1}_{\upmu_{\mathcal{V}}}}{\differential_{\upmu_{\mathcal{V}}}}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{\mu_{\mathcal{V}}}\right].

(7.3)

The ‘
{\oplus\hskip-10.59999pt\displaystyle\int}
’ notation here indicates that reduced states at different
\mathcal{V}
are (nearly) distinguishable. This assumption additionally leads to a Rényi entropy of the form

S^{(n)}_{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{1-n}\log\tr\widetilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}^{n}\sim\frac{1}{1-n}\log\int\differential\mathcal{V}\,\exp\left(-(n-1)\log\differential_{\mu_{\mathcal{V}}}\right)~,

(7.4)

where, as before, we have focussed on the contribution from the representation dimension. Holding,
n>1
fixed, we can approximate this integral by saddle point to find

S_{\Sigma}^{(n)}\sim\min_{\mathcal{V}}\log\differential_{\mu_{\mathcal{V}}}\sim\min_{\mathcal{V}}\absolutevalue{\pa\Sg_{\mc V}}\log\frac{\absolutevalue{\Sg_{\mc V}}}{\absolutevalue{\pa\Sg_{\mc V}}}~,

(7.5)

where in the final step we have used the general connection between representation dimension and areas from §
6.2
. This is precisely the minimization formula for the entanglement entropy that we advertised, (
7.1
).

There are several technial assumptions that have gone into this simple roadmap that we will make clear in this section. The first, and perhaps most important, is the existence of a saddle point in the integral (
7.4
). This assumption is, in fact, typically false. The reason is that, despite our abuse of terminology, most elements of
U(N)
are non-geometric when interpreted as a volume preserving ‘diffeomorphisms’: indeed a element exchanging a single matrix entry of
X^{i}
exchanges a Planck-sized unit of area on the non-commutative background. These non-geometric elements of
U(N)
are so numerous that they wash out any saddle-point approximation to the integral.

In order to arrive at (
7.1
) it will be necessary to ‘coarse-grain’ over gauge transformations to eliminate such non-geometric maps while also preserving invariance under honest (volume preserving) diffeomorphisms in the continuum limit. Fortunately, this process only represents a mild extension of the technology we have established in Sections
5.4
and
6.2
. In particular we will implement the coarse-graining by considering embeddings of physical states not as an
U(N)
invariant subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext}}
, but instead a subspace invariant under an appropriate subgroup,
G\subset U(N)
, with
U(N)/G
gauge-fixed. For reasons that will be clear below, we will sometimes refer to
G
as the ‘frame transformation group.’ We implement this embedding as before, by starting with a fully gauge-fixed state and averaging over its
G
-orbit, e.g. (
7.2
). However, in the case that
U(M)\times U(N-M)
is no longer a proper subgroup of
G
, we will want to implement
U(M)\times U(N-M)
invariance separately and so our embedding into the extended Hilbert space is, in total,

|\psi\rangle\rightarrow|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle=\int_{U(M)}\differential\mathcal{U}\int_{U(N-M)}\differential\bar{\mathcal{U}}\int_{G}\differential\mathcal{G}\,|\mathcal{U}\bar{\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{G}\rangle|\widetilde{\psi}_{\text{gf}}\rangle~.

(7.6)

We can notate

H:=G\cap U(M)~,\qquad\bar{H}:=G\cap U(N-M)~,\qquad\mathsf{F}:=(H\times\bar{H})\backslash G

(7.7)

(here
\mathsf{F}
is a left-quotient). We can subsume the

G

U(M)\times U(N-M)

|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle=\int_{\mathsf{F}}\differential\mathcal{V}|\widetilde{\psi}_{\mathcal{V}}\rangle~,\qquad|\widetilde{\psi}_{\mathcal{V}}\rangle=\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}\int\differential\mathcal{U}\differential\bar{\mathcal{U}}|\mathcal{U}\bar{\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{V}\rangle|\widetilde{\psi}_{\text{gf}}\rangle~,

(7.8)

and where
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
is a potential measure in splitting
\int_{G}\differential\mathcal{G}
into integrals over
H\times\bar{H}
and
\mathsf{F}
. The state
|\tilde{\psi}_{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
is precisely the type of
U(M)\times U(N-M)
invariant state that we considered in §
6.2
that leads to edge modes with area law entanglement. Reducing (
7.8
) within
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ext}}
with the factorization (
5.72
) leads to

\widetilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}=\int\differential\mathcal{V}\differential\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\,\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\overline{\Sigma}}}\left(|\widetilde{\psi}_{\mathcal{V}}\rangle\langle\widetilde{\psi}_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}|\right)~.

(7.9)

In
[
63
]
, it was argued that (
7.9
) is strongly supported at
\mathcal{V}\approx\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, i.e. that it is roughly proportional to
\delta(\mathcal{V}-\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
, in semi-classical states of sufficiently tame MQMs. This leads to the block-integral decomposition of
\widetilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}
in (
7.3

7.5
). However, this is
a priori
an assumption whose breaking has interesting physical consequences; see
[
63

for elaboration.

Let us pause to discuss the physics of this replica symmetry assumption in the context of contrasting the
U(M)\times U(N-M)
integral vs. the integral over coarse-grained frame transformations. It is useful to consider what physical information a low-energy observer with access to a subregion
\Sigma
can distinguish. The assumption of replica symmetry implies that the reduced state in the form (
7.3
) is nearly perfectly distinguishable between different
\mathcal{V}

\mathsf{F}
acts non-unitarily on the reduced density matrix and changes its eigenvalues. Coarse-graining
U(N)\rightarrow G
ensures that an observer with reduced state
\widetilde{\rho}_{\Sigma}
cannot distinguish between Planck-sized maps changing the subregion. Elements of
U(M)\times U(N-M)
however have a different flavor: they act entirely within a subregion, preserving the information of the state (that is they act unitarily on reduced state at a give
\mathcal{V}
). The integral over this subgroup ensures that the reduced state is invariant under such subregion preserving redundancies.

Frame averaging and coarse-grained QRFs

The algebra we described above is defined in relation to a

orbit of a gauge-fixed configuration,
X^{i}_{\text{gf}}
, which we think of as an (incomplete) QRF. More generally we can construct an analogous subregion algebra by gauge-fixing to the QRF in a different orientation,
\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\,X^{i}_{\text{gf}}\mathcal{U}

\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}X^{i}_{\text{gf}}\mathcal{U}

\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma,\mathcal{U}}
are just as physical as before, i.e. there is no canonical preference to which orientation of QRF we dress this physical data.

It is natural in this context to consider physical quantities that are averaged over the QRF orientation. This is fitting with notions of ‘bulk subsystems’ in holography and quantum gravity. In AdS/CFT diffeomorphism invariant subregions are defined by a boundary subregion,
A

\Sigma

An additional subtlety that we have mentioned above is many rotated QRF orientations,

do not act continuously or differentiably when interpreted as volume preserving maps. As a technical hurdle, the proliferation of these elements overwhelm the saddle-point leading to a minimal area formula. On an interpretational level, we might expect that coarse-graining over QRFs is needed for arriving at semi-classical quantities at low energies: the effective state of a low-energy observer should not be sensitive to Planck-sized uncertainties of their QRF. Averaging over a subgroup,

The generic term

To estimate the generic term, we calculate the average

•

The Casimir is self-averaging:

\frac{\overline{\langle C_{2s}\rangle^{2}}-\overline{\langle C_{2s}\rangle}^{2}}{\overline{\langle C_{2s}\rangle}^{2}}\ll 1~.

(7.34)

This establishes that quantum distribution of irreps have the same average over
\mathsf{F}
.

•

The variance of Casimir

is self-averaging:

\frac{\overline{\left(\Delta^{2}_{C_{2s}}\right)^{2}}-\left(\overline{\Delta^{2}_{C_{2s}}}\right)^{2}}{\left(\overline{\Delta^{2}_{C_{2s}}}\right)^{2}}\ll 1~.

(7.35)

This establishes that the quantum distribution of irreps have the same width. This and the above bullet also justify treating
\overline{\exp(-I_n)}
as
\exp(-\overline{I}_n)
.

•

The variance is small:

\frac{\overline{\Delta_{C_{2s}}^{2}}}{\overline{\langle C_{2s}\rangle}^{2}}\ll 1~.

(7.36)

This establishes that the generic quantum state is dominated by a single irrep,
\mu_{\star}
.

8
Conclusion

In this article we reviewed a growing body of research in how geometric features arise from the entanglement of quantum mechanical matrices at large
N
. We motivated this study in §
1
through a brief history of the connections between the ’t Hooft limit of MQMs and string theory. In §
2
we summarized several standard MQM models that play a key role in string theory and M-theory. The relevance of MQMs to D-brane physics then motivated a discussion in §
3
on non-commutative geometries and their matrix representations. Central to this discussion was the realization of the
U(N)
symmetry of MQMs as volume preserving diffeomorphisms (and symplectomorphisms, more generally) acting on non-commutative spaces in the large
N
limit. In §
4
we built the necessary scaffolding for investigating entanglement in MQM by overviewing how subsystems and entanglement entropies are defined in the presence of gauge redundancy. In §
5
we discussed the target space interpretation of this entanglement, starting with a review of the target space entanglement of identical particles and building up to a notion of entanglement in MQM based upon a gauge-fixed subsets of matrix degrees of freedom. We then illustrated in §
6
how target space entanglement can be realized geometrically on a non-commutative space, starting from a simple non-commutative scalar field theory and then presenting general criteria for when the entanglement entropy can take on an ‘area law’ form in an emergent geometry. Lastly in §
7
, we drew connections between this area law and entanglement entropy in quantum gravity, and in particular the celebrated Ryu-Takayanagi formula for holographic entanglement entropy, by describing the conditions in which
U(N)
invariance leads to the expression of the entanglement entropy as a minimal area formula.

The history of MQM in string theory and holography is rich, and the study of entanglement in these areas has proven to be extremely fruitful. As such there are many topics that we unfortunately unable to cover in this review. While technically not a model of quantum mechanics, notably absent from our review in §
2
is a discussion of the matrix integral of Ishibashi, Kawai, Kitazawa, and Tsuchiya
[
102
]
which has a strong relevance in string theory and D-brane physics and has recently seen a small renaissance in its holographic understanding
[
85
,
84
,
118
,
119
]
. Similarly, as we noted in beginning of §
5
on target space entanglement, we have chosen to omit a discussion on target space entanglement in string theory (e.g.
[
90
,
86
,
53
,
14
,
101
,
141
,
52
,
3
,
107
]
) although the topic is clearly relevant to various aspects of this review and important to understanding entropy in quantum gravity more broadly.

In writing this review, it was very clear to us that the topic of entanglement entropy in MQM is far from a closed and fully understood subject. Despite the progress highlighted in this review, deeper connections between matrix and target space entanglement to AdS/CFT and D-brane holography more broadly, how a genuine Ryu-Takayanagi formula emerges, and the use of entanglement in probing the internal spaces of AdS/CFT are still being explored
[
12
,
75
,
109
,
83
,
70
,
43
]
. More mysterious is how target space entanglement is expressed in the flat limits of various matrix models. This reflects both the technical difficulties of working with the nine matrices of the BFSS model as well as the broader mystery of how flat space physics and Lorentz invariance manifests itself in the BFSS model beyond scattering amplitudes
[
170
]
. Further, it would be interesting to understand how the target space entanglement discussed in this review might apply to black hole states in MQM, which are conjectured to be thermal excitations of sub-blocks
[
18
,
19
,
99
,
27
,
70
,
1
]
, and perhaps recover an entanglement of
\frac{A}{4G_{N}}
. We hope that many of these questions will be better understood in the near future.
