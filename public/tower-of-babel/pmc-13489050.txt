Immunopathogenesis of Sickle Cell Disease: Mechanisms of Immune Dysregulation and Clinical Consequences, a Narrative Review
Authors: Simeon Ikechukwu Egba, Virginus Agozie Umeh, Prince Ogochukwu Alaebo, Uchenna Valentine Chukwuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSickle cell disease (SCD) is a hereditary haemoglobin abnormality due to a point mutation in the β-globin gene resulting in the production of haemoglobin S. Polymerization of deoxygenated hemoglobin S results in red cell sickling, hemolysis, endothelial injury, ischemia-reperfusion damage, vaso-occlusion, and, in addition to being a hemolytic anaemia and vaso-occlusive condition, SCD is now considered a chronic inflammatory disease with significant immunological abnormalities. This review explores the pathophysiological and immunological basis of SCD, with particular emphasis on how hemolysis, inflammation, innate and adaptive immunity, vulnerability to infection and their implications for current and emerging therapeutics. SCD is characterised by sustained innate and adaptive immune responses, including leukocytosis; neutrophil and monocyte activation; changes in cytokine profiles; complement activation; and T- and B-cell dysfunction. Danger-associated molecular patterns released during hemolysis, including activate inflammasomes, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction through toll-like receptors. Repeat vaso-occlusion maintains sterile inflammation and facilitates coagulation, immune regulation, and vascular damage. Also present are functional asplenia and defective humoral immunity, which predisposes to infection, particularly that caused by encapsulated bacteria. These disruptions help cause vaso-occlusive crises, acute chest syndrome, stroke, pulmonary hypertension, leg ulcers, nephropathy and long-term organ damage. Immunological dysregulation lies at the core of the pathophysiology and complications of SCD. A more detailed understanding of the immune landscape can enhance disease management and inform treatment, including drug administration, transfusion, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and gene-based therapies.Keywords: sickle cell disease, inflammation, innate immunity, adaptive immunity, hemolysis

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is one of the most prevalent life-threatening monogenic disorders and represents a major, yet unequally distributed, global public health burden.1 The Global Burden of Disease study (2021) estimated that in 2021 almost 7,740,000 individuals were living with SCD worldwide, and almost 515,000 SCD affected infants were born in 2021. In fact, the majority of the burden (~80%) is found in sub-Saharan Africa, although SCD is also prevalent in India, the Middle East, the Mediterranean region, and among populations of African descent in Europe and the Americas.2 Estimates of mortality additionally show the under-recognition of the disease. While in 2021 around 34,400 deaths were reported directly due to SCD, a modelling study on the overall mortality burden of SCD estimated around 376,000 deaths caused by SCD, including 81,100 among children aged less than five years.1,2 These data highlight the ongoing inequities in newborn screening, early diagnosis, infection prevention, access to hydroxyurea, transfusion services, and comprehensive specialist care. SCD is a group of inherited haemoglobin disorders resulting from pathogenic variants in the β-globin gene (HBB). In the case of sickle cell anaemia, the mutation is a single nucleotide substitution leading to the replacement of glutamic in position 6 of the β-globin chain with valine, and subsequently a haemoglobin S (HbS).3 During deoxygenation, HbS polymerises, resulting in dehydration of the red cells, damage to the membrane, loss of cell deformability and the typical sickled appearance of the cells.4 Abnormally shaped red blood cells are damaged and have a decreased life span and abnormal interaction with leukocytes, platelets, endothelial cells and plasma proteins. These mechanisms lead to microvascular occlusion, ischaemia, and recurrent vasculocclusive complications as well as the gradual involvement of brain, lungs, kidneys, liver, spleen, bones, and cardiovascular system.4,5 It was thought that the pathogenesis of SCD was a primarily mechanical phenomenon resulting from erythrocytes sickling and small blood vessel obstruction. Though being basic, this model is lacking to be sufficient to explain the large clinical variability seen in SCD or the presence of vasc abnormalities in clinically stable periods. What was once considered a reactive model of SCD as a chronic, sterile thrombo-inflammatory disease is now known to be active and that immune regulation is not just a secondary phenomenon to vaso-occlusion-induced tissue damage, but one that is involved in the initiation, amplification and persistence of this disease.6–8 Chronic intra- and extravascular haemolysis leads to the release of cell-free haemoglobin, labile haem, iron and mitochondrial DNA, erythrocyte membrane fragments and other damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to the circulation. These mediators activate pattern-recognition receptors (such as Toll-like receptors), activate inflammasome pathways, induce complement pathways, induce oxidative stress, decrease nitric oxide bioavailability, activate endothelial cells, and recruit leukocytes.7–10 Damage from repeated ischaemia–reperfusion exacerbates this effect, producing a perpetuating vicious cycle of haemolysis, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, coagulation activation and vaso-occlusion. Additional sources of immune perturbation include functional asplenia, altered integrity of intestinal barrier, recurrent infection, transfusion exposure and the occurrence of alloimmunisation.9–11 This chronic immune activation has clinically relevant aspects that impact all the acute and chronic complications of SCD. Activated neutrophils, monocytes, platelets, and endothelial cells play a role in promoting cell adhesion, microvascular stasis, and thus reduce the threshold for painful vaso-occlusive crises. Neutrophil extracellular traps, activation of inflammatory cytokines, complement activation and pulmonary endothelial injury could play a role in the pathogenesis and progression of acute chest syndrome.8,12 Cerebral endothelial dysfunction, chronic anaemia, abnormal blood flow, leukocyte adhesion and thrombo-inflammation are involved in overt stroke and silent cerebral infarction. Ambiguous inflammatory and ischaemia – reperfusion episodes over time cause progressive renal, pulmonary, hepatic, neurological and cardiovascular damage.5,12 Immune dysregulation thus presents a plausible mechanism for the connection between the molecular impact of HbS polymerisation and the clinical severity of SCD which varies amongst individuals with similar SCD genotypes.

There are several emerging concepts which are further expanding some ideas of SCD immunopathology. It is becoming more apparent that complement activation is a mediator of endothelial damage, coagulation, haemolysis and vaso-occlusive phenomena. Cells can send out extracellular vesicles containing haem, phosphatidylserine, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids and other inflammatory or procoagulant cargo that can be transferred between cells.13,14 Moreover, re-ignition of immune responses to haem and other endogenous danger signals may impel trained immunity, the metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming of mature myeloid cells and haematopoietic progenitors that instigates enhanced inflammatory and procoagulant reactions.15 The emerging concept of immunometabolism is also beginning to unveil how changes in glycolysis, mitochondrial function, oxidative phosphorylation, lipogenesis and redox state impact on erythrocyte function and in activation of immune cells. At the same time, transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, lipidomic, epigenomic and single-cell based technologies are starting to delineate previously unrecognized cellular states and molecular signatures associated to vaso-occlusion, organ dysfunction and therapeutic response.16,17 These advances could help identify clinically useful biomarkers and therapeutic targets targeted towards the immune system. Prior reviews have offered valuable reports of individual innate or adaptive immune abnormalities, of specific inflammatory pathways or of the thrombotic aspects of inflammation in SCD. However, the mechanisms generally have been examined individually and, more recently, fields like trained immunity, extracellular-vesicle signalling, complement and immunometabolic reprogramming have not been regularly reported in the context of clinical phenotypes and therapeutic applications.6,18 Therefore, there is a lack of knowledge about the overall contribution of all these processes including innate immunity, adaptive immunity, erythrocytes, endothelium, platelets, coagulation cascades and systemic metabolic signals in the acute complications and progressive organ injury. This review aims to fill this gap with an integrated picture of innate and adaptive immune dysregulation in SCD and relate these to vaso-occlusive crises, acute chest syndrome, stroke, susceptibility to infections, alloimmunisation and chronic organ damage. It also considers new concepts, such as complement activation, the concept of trained immunity and extracellular vesicles, and their relevance as potential biomarkers, patient stratification and therapeutic development (immunometabolism and multi-omics technologies). This review seeks to attempt to put into a clinically oriented perspective the established immunological mechanisms but also the recently discovered mechanistic findings relating to SCD and how treatment is evolving to incorporate and consolidate these findings and mechanisms so that SCD can be viewed within a framework of both inflammatory vasculopathy and systemic immune dysregulation syndrome.

Methodology

Review Design

This is a narrative mini-review research that sought to summarize existing evidence on immunological dysregulation in sickle cell disease (SCD). The review is based on mechanistic understanding, immune mechanisms, and clinical consequences but not quantitative synthesis.

Review Objectives

The objectives of this review were to:

1. Explain the processes of immune dysregulation in SCD.

2. Discuss the functions of innate and adaptive immunity on the progression of diseases.

3. Emphasize the role of hemolysis-produced products in inflammation and immune response.

4. Comment on the clinical consequences of immune dysfunction, such as infections and complication.

5. Overview existing and new treatment measures to address immune pathways in SCD.

Search Strategy

Electronic databases, such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, were searched through literature. Relevant articles in English that were published between 2010 and 2026 were accessed. The search was limited to peer-reviewed articles, both original research and review articles, and clinical studies. Other sources were determined through screening of reference lists of the selected articles.

Keywords

Refinement of the search was done using combinations of the following keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH): sickle cell disease, immunological dysregulation, inflammation, innate immunity, adaptive immunity, hemolysis, cytokines, toll-like receptors, inflammasomes, oxidative stress, immune dysfunction of SCD.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

Research on sickle cell disease and immunity.

Articles on innate and/or adaptive immunity in SCD.

Research on inflammation, cytokines, or immune signaling pathways.

Original research articles, reviews, and clinical studies (peer-reviewed).

Articles written in English between 2010 and 2026.

Exclusion Criteria

Studies not related to immunological aspects of SCD.

Articles, editorials, or abstracts of conferences lacking adequate data that are not peer-reviewed.

Duplicate publications.

Articles whose results are ambiguous or meaningless.

Articles written in other languages and those published earlier than 2010.

Pathobiological Basis of Immune Stimulation for SCD

The immunological abnormalities of SCD are initiated by the sickled red blood cell. Polymerisation of haemoglobin S is damaging the erythrocyte membrane, favours the exposure of phosphatidylserine, and shortens the life of the cell.19 Hemolysis releases free haemoglobin and heme, which are central mediators of inflammation. Free haemoglobin scavengesf nitric oxidonreducing vasodilation and thereby contributingtes to endothelial dysfunction.20 Heme serves as a strong damage-associateddamage-associated molecular pattern that activates immune cells, induces oxidative stress, and increases the expression of adhesion molecules on endothelial cells. Repeated vaso-occlusion leads to tissue ischemia, precipitating in reperfusion, which is known to generate reactive oxygen species and inflammatory mediators. Endothelial cells react by increasing the expression of selectins, Vascular Cell Adhesion Molecule-1 (VCAM-1), Intercellular Adhesion Molecule-1 (ICAM-1) and tissue factor in order to further promote leukocyte adhesion and thrombosis.21 This leads to a vicious cycle where inflammation influences vaso-occlusion and oocyte influences inflammation. Another important component is the presence of circulating extracellular vesicles of red cells, platelets, leukocytes and endothelial cells. These vesicles contain proinflammatory lipids, proteins and nucleic acids and may exacerbate coagulation and immune activation.22,23 At the same time, chronic hypoxia, oxidative stress and tissue damage keep the immune system in a state of basal immune alertness.24 As a result even under conditions of so-called steady state, many patients with SCD have a high level of inflammatory markers (Table 1).

Innate Immune System Dysregulation

Innate immune dysregulation is an integral characteristic of sickle cell disease (SCD) and is instrumental in the disorder’s manifestation as both a hemolytic anemia and a chronic inflammatory vasculopathy.7 The activating state of the innate immune system in SCD is a key feature and while this may be explained by persistent activation of the innate immune system-even during those periods of time previously described as being the clinical steady state. This persists activation driven by the hemolysis repeated or repeated ischemia/reperfusion injury, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction and the release of damage associated molecules continuously in the circulation.27 As a result, several innate immune effectors, especially neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, platelets and endothelial cells, become active participants in the pathogenesis of vascular occlusion, tissue injury and progressive organ damage28 (Table 2).

Neutrophils

Neutrophils are one of the most important cellular mediators of the inflammatory response in SCD. Leukocytosis is not uncommon, and increased white blood cell count has been linked to severe disease, recurrent vaso-occlusive crisis, stroke, and high death rates for many years.30 However, the contribution of neutrophils is not just their increased numbers. In SCD, there is activated phenotypes of neutrophils; increased adhesion to vascular endothelium, increased release of ROS, increased interaction with platelets and sickled erythrocytes. These adhesive and oxidative properties make neutrophils powerful microvascular obstruction amplifiers. By binding to endothelial surfaces and trapping rigid red cells, they are a direct part of the problem by impairing the blood flow and causing local tissue ischemia.31 In addition, a novel important mediator of sterile inflammation in SCD are neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which are composed of decondensed chromatin and antimicrobial granular proteins. Although NET formation may have antimicrobial benefit, excessive NET release can cause damage to vascular tissues and also activates coagulation pathways and worsen thrombosis.25 Thus, neutrophils create a vital link between inflammation, vascular injury and thrombo-occlusive pathology.

Monocytes and Macrophages

Monocytes and macrophages are also chronically activated in SCD and significantly contribute to the inflammatory state.32 Circulating monocytes mediate the production of an array of proinflammatory cytokines, such as tumour necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-1 beta, and interleukin-6, which in turn are responsible for further endothelial induction and leukocyte recruitment. These cells are very sensitive to heme and other signals derived from hemolysis, and they can become polarised into proinflammatory functional states.26 Repeated erythrophagocytosis also changes macrophage metabolism, iron handling, and redox balance, thereby reinforcing inflammatory signaling.32 In organs like the spleen, liver, and bone marrow, macrophagesmacrophages play an important role in the clearance of damaged or senescent erythrocytes. In SCD, however, the striking excess of hemolysis causes this protective process to become a source of immune stimulation, that persists for a long time.33 The result is a chronic inflammatory cycle that destroys red cells, which in turn feed monocyte and macrophage activation.

Endothelial Cells as Participants of the Immune System

Endothelial cells, although not classical immune cells, are major involved immunological cells of SCD.34 They are highly sensitive to heme, oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines and hypoxia. In response to such stimuli, adhesion molecules such as selectins, VCAM-1, and ICAM-1 are expressed on the endothelium, along with chemokines that recruit circulating leukocytes. Activated endothelial cells are thus both sensors and effectors of inflammation.41 They lead to a proadhesive vascular surface, encouraging neutrophils, monocytes, platelets, and sickled erythrocytes to be bound to one another and cause vaso-occlusion.42 Endothelial dysfunction in SCD is therefore not only a vascular abnormality but also an immunological abnormality, as it orchestrates the recruitment of cells, the amplification of inflammation, and thrombogenic signalling within the microcirculation.

Platelet and Thromboinflammation

Platelets would also play a significant role in innate immune dysregulation in SCD. Rather than responding specifically to hemostasis, platelets in SCD are chronically activated and release mediators that promote endothelial activation and leukocyte adhesion.35 Platelet-neutrophil aggregates have been increasingly recognised as important components of the process of thromboinflammation, in which coagulation and inflammation become intimately coupled.36 These aggregates increase vascular obstruction and help explain why vaso-occlusion is not merely a result of aberrant red cell shape but is a multicellular event involving leukocyte, platelet, and endothelial cell interactions, as well as coagulation pathway interactions.

Role of Hemolysis, Heme and Inflammasome Activation

A major upstream factor in this process is chronic hemolysis and the accumulation of free heme.37 Under physiological conditions, haptoglobin and hemopexin bind free haemoglobin and heme. In SCD, persistent hemolysis overwhelms these scavenging systems, and therefore, toxic products of hemolysis are left to roam free and circulate.38 Free heme activates toll-like receptor 4 signalling, promotes oxidative stress and increased endothelial permeability, and stimulates leukocyte recruitment. It can also stimulate the activation of the inflammasome complex of the NOD-like receptor family, pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3), with the release of interleukin-1 beta and interleukin-18, and link sterile tissue injury to the activation of systemic immunity.39 In parallel, heme and iron catalyse oxidative reactions that damage lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, further disrupting immune system homeostasis. For this reason, hemolysis in SCD should be considered not only a cause of chronic anaemia but also a primary initiator of innate inflammatory dysfunction. Overall, innate immune dysfunction in SCD represents a self-propagating cycle of hemolysis, cellular activation, vascular injury, and inflammation that underlies many of the more serious complications of this disease.

Complement activation, inflammasome signaling, and adaptive immune abnormalities have all recently emerged as potential mechanisms in sickle cell disease (SCD) but their relative roles in disease progression remain unknown.25 Most solid evidence is seen for the presence of complement activation, where increased levels of circulating parameters of the alternative pathway, enhanced complement activation by the sickle erythrocytes, and increased complement activation during the vaso-occlusive episode have been demonstrated.40 However, many investigations are with small or cross sectional cohorts, and some mechanistic findings have been from ex vivo systems or animal models.29,43 Therefore, it is unclear if complement activation is a primary event leading to vaso-occlusion and organ injury or a downstream event induced by the hemolysis, endothelial damage and coagulation activation. Thus, more extensive studies in larger cohorts of patients and clinical efficacy trials will be necessary before NLRP3 could be considered a validated marker of disease progression.

Cytokine Conturbation and Chronic Inflammation

Patients with SCD often have elevated circulating levels of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as tumour necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-1 beta, interleukin-6, interleukin-8, interferon-gamma, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1.48 These mediators promote the maintenance of leukocyte activation, endothelial adhesion, fever, and pain sensitisation as well as organ injury. Anti-inflammatory pathways are present; however, they often cannot balance the inflammatory burden. The cytokine landscape may differ depending on disease state, age, treatment, and the presence of complications.49 Inflammatory cytokines usually rise even further during vaso-occlusive crises and acute chest syndrome. This and similar work would suggest that the acute complications of SCD may not be isolated events but flares of an already keyed up immune system. Chronic inflammation has more far-reaching and systemic metabolic effects. It plays a role in impaired growth, impaired endothelial function, catabolism and progressive organ damage.25 It may also impact on neuroinflammation, chronicity of pain and psychosocial outcomes. Therefore, the cytokine dysregulation in SCD is not only a lab finding but it is a clinically relevant feature of the disease (Table 2).

Abnormalities of the Adaptive Immune System

Adaptive immune abnormalities are becoming perceived as increasingly important contributors to SCD pathophysiology.50 The disorder is classically defined with characteristics of abnormal hemoglobin polymerization and chronic hemolysis. It is now becoming evident that immune dysregulation goes beyond innate inflammatory pathways. It is equally marked by profound perturbations in lymphocyte function, humoral immunity, splenic immune activity, and host defense.5 These adaptations in the immune system are important clinically because they contribute to chronic inflammation, susceptibility to infection, responses to vaccination and transfusion, as well as contributing to indirect tissue and organ damage and significant disease complications (Table 3).

Adaptive immune changes include T-cell activation, regulatory T-cell (Treg) function, and alterations in lymphocyte subset, B-cell phenotype, and humoral immunity.51 However the results not always being the same, it is possible that the results of different studies are affected by age, genotype, degree of splenic function, infection, transfusion history, vaccination, hydroxyurea therapy and presence of samples taken in steady state or acute crisis. Some studies are cross sectional and mostly conducted in small and/or clinically selected populations which make causal interpretations and generalisability more difficult.52,53 Additionally, some adaptive abnormalities may be a response to chronic inflammation or functional asplenia instead of primary causes of vascular injury. They have more clearly defined clinical associations, including susceptibility to infection, vaccine responses, and transfusion related alloimmunization and it is less clear about their direct involvement in progressive organ damage.54,55

In general, these pathways can be considered as interactive elements of a comprehensive network of thrombo-inflammatory responses instead of discrete mechanisms (Figure 2). Variations in how the studies were conducted, who was studied, who received what treatments, when samples were taken, and how the labs conducted their assays likely explain some of the inconsistencies. Future studies of a longitudinal, multicentre nature or using an intervention will then be required to determine if these immune changes are causative, biomarkers to assess the severity of disease, compensatory, or due to already present SCD pathologies.

T Lymphocyte Dysregulation

T glycoprotein lymphocyte (T cell) dysregulation is one of the most important manifestations of adaptive immune disturbance in SCD.6,56 Several studies have reported abnormalities in T-cell subsets, activation markers and the regulatory function. Patients with SCD frequently demonstrate evidence of increased T cell activation suggesting that there is chronic exposure of the adaptive immune system to inflammatory stimulation. This may be secondary to chronic hemolysis, repeated ischemia and reperfusion injury, oxidative stress, recurrent infections and chronic exposure to antigens from injured tissues and transfused blood products.57 Alterations in the balance between helper T cells and cytotoxic T cells have also been described as well as impaired regulatory T cell activity. Function of reduced regulatory T cells are important in particular because these cells normally keep excessive immune activation in check and keep the immune system tolerant. When this regulatory force is blunted, chronic inflammation may continue unabated and reinforce this already activated innate immune environment. In addition, abnormal T cell polarization may play a contributing role to the susceptibility to infection, and perhaps in exaggerated inflammatory response.57 The exact pattern of T-cell dysfunction is likely to be different based on age, severity of the disease, exposure to therapy, transfusion history, and clinical state. For example, in patients in a steady-state condition there may be low level, chronic stimulation of the immune system, while in acute crisis patients there may be more intense lymphocyte stimulation.34 These adaptive changes may also have practical implications in vaccine responsiveness, alloimmunization and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, where effective immune regulation is elusive in order to have a good outcome. Thus, abnormalities of T cells in SCD are not just secondary laboratory findings but part of the general immunopathological picture of the disease.

B Cells and Humoral Immunity

B-cell and humoral immune abnormalities are not as consistently characterized but are equally important as shown by the splenic dysfunction.58 The spleen is a primary organ for filtration of the blood stream containing pathogens, in the removal of opsonized organisms, and in the antibody response to encapsulated bacteria. In SCD, there is recurrent vaso-occlusive injury within the spleen subjecting the organ to progressive infarction, and often progresses to functional asplenia early in life. This severely induces humoral immune defense dysfunction.59 As the spleen function decreases, the body has less ability to develop efficient antibody-mediated reactions, especially against encapsulated organisms such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae and Neisseria Meningitidis. Reduced marginal zone function within the spleen may also impair the function of the immunoglobulin-mediated cleansing of bacteria.60 Humoral immune weakness in SCD may be exacerbated due to recurrent infections, nutritional deficiencies, chronic inflammation and repeated exposure to transfusion. These factors may cause some alteration in B-cell maturation and antibody production and immune memory, although the extent of impairment may vary from patient to patient. Clinically, these abnormalities are sufficient justification for the central role of prophylactic penicillin, pneumococcal and other routine vaccinations, and early infection surveillance in standard SCD management. In this context, adaptive immune dysfunction means directly into preventable morbidity and mortality. One of the most obvious clinical manifestations of impairment of adaptive immune function in SCD is an increased susceptibility to infection.61 Functional asplenia can develop during childhood, and leaves patients very susceptible to invasive bloodstream infection. Without the efficient splenic filtration and opsonization of cells, infections can burn briskly and be life-threatening. Although the classical cause of concern are encapsulated bacteria, the infectious burden in SCD is more extensive. Important pathogens include salmonella species of salmonella causing osteomyelitis, parvovirus B19 causing aplastic crisis, malaria currently endemic regions and a variety of respiratory pathogens.62 Infection in SCD is especially dangerous because it is not only a manifestation of immune vulnerability but can also trigger vaso-occlusive crisis through the development of fever, dehydration, hypoxia, and the increased process of inflammation. Infection is therefore itself a consequence and trigger of immune dysregulation.

Functional Asplenia and Susceptibility to Infection

These adaptive and infection related abnormalities also intersect with the major clinical complications of SCD.63 Immune dysregulation is involved with vaso-occlusive crisis, acute chest syndrome, stroke, pulmonary hypertension, nephropathy, chronic leg ulcers, chronic pain. In each, the abnormal signaling by the immune system interacts with dysfunction of the vascular system, hemolysis and the activation of the endothelium to exacerbate tissue damage.64 This supports the human message that part of the pathogenesis of SCD is that SCD is not a simple red cell disorder but also a systemic inflammatory and immunological disease where adaptive immune abnormalities play an important pathogenic contribution.

Future Directions

Future studies should go beyond immune abnormalities in the context of SCD to determine their causal and prognostic importance. Larger, longitudinal and multicentre studies for evaluation of the contribution of complement activation, inflammasome signalling, extracellular-vesicle activity and adaptive immune changes in steady state, acute crises, progressive organ injury and treatment exposure are required. It will be necessary, as well, to develop standardized approaches for measuring immune and inflammatory biomarkers to facilitate across research studies and to uncover clinically meaningful patient subgroups. The use of single-cell technologies, in combination with genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, lipidomic, and epigenomic methods, holds great promise in elucidating and defining distinct immunological endotypes and why patients with similar genotypes have markedly different courses of disease. These approaches should be supplemented by mechanistic studies and well designed clinical trials that will assess if immune abnormalities are causative or markers of disease severity, compensation or result of pre-existing tissue damage. Particular emphasis should be placed on complement- and inflammasome-targeted drugs, targeting heme-scavenging pathways, targeting of leukocyte–platelet–endothelial interactions and the long-term immunological impact of hydroxyurea, transfusion, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation and gene-based interventions. In summary, these discoveries could lead to the development of validated biomarkers and specific treatment strategies, which could enhance the precision of SCD risk categorization and personalized treatment.

Conclusion

Immunological dysregulation is a key feature of sickle cell disease and a major determinant of clinical severity of the disease. Chronic hemolysis, heme release, oxidative stress, endothelial activation, ischemia-reperfusion injury and functional asplenia add up to a state of persistent inflammation of both the innate and adaptive immune systems. Neutrophils, monocytes, platelets, cytokines, inflammasomes and endothelial cells are all involved in a self-perpetuating cycle of immune activation and vascular injury. At the same time, dysfunction of the spleen and impairment of humoral immunity enhance the susceptibility to serious infection. These disturbances of the immune systems are not secondary curiosities. They are essential causes of pain, organ destruction, acute chest syndrome, stroke, nephropathy and other complications. A better understanding of the immunobiology of SCD is critical in order to improve the prognosis of SCD, refine biomarkers for targeted therapies. Future advances in care will depend on incorporating hematology, immunology, vascular biology and infection prevention into one model of sickle cell disease.
