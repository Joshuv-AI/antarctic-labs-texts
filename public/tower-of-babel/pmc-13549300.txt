Association Between Habitual Khat (Catha edulis) Chewing and Oral Reactive Hyperplastic Lesions: A Cross-Sectional Clinicopathological Study in Yemen
Authors: Ammar M Al-ashwal, Shaima’a Abdulraqeeb Yahya Rajeh, Rofaida Jubarah, Mohammed M Al Moaleem, Haneen G Al-Affari, Alaa Khaled, Lana Sayal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the clinicopathological characteristics of oral reactive hyperplastic lesions (RHLs) and evaluate their association with habitual khat (Catha edulis) chewing in a Yemeni dental population.MethodsThis cross-sectional clinicopathological study included 1197 Yemeni patients with 48 histopathologically confirmed RHLs. Demographic characteristics; periodontal status; oral hygiene; and behavioral habits, including khat chewing and smoking, were recorded. Clinical lesion features and histopathological diagnoses were assessed. Associations between khat-chewing status and clinicopathological variables were analyzed by using Pearson’s chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.ResultsHabitual khat chewing was significantly associated with male gender (P = 0.015), smoking status (P = 0.002), and periodontitis (P = 0.005). Among lesion-related variables, lesion size was significantly associated with khat chewing, with lesions larger than 1 cm occurring more frequently among khat chewers than among nonchewers (P = 0.026). No significant associations were identified between khat chewing and lesion anatomical site (P = 0.604), clinical course (P = 1.000), or histopathological diagnosis (P = 0.762).ConclusionHabitual khat chewing was associated with increased periodontal destruction and large oral RHLs but showed no association with lesion location, clinical behavior, or histopathological subtype. These findings suggest that khat acts as a chronic local irritant that may amplify reactive tissue proliferation without altering the underlying histopathological pattern of RHLs.Clinical RelevanceThe assessment of khat-chewing habits should be incorporated into routine oral examinations because chronic exposure may contribute to increased lesion burden and periodontal deterioration.

Introduction

Reactive hyperplastic lesions (RHLs) comprise a heterogeneous group of nonneoplastic oral soft-tissue proliferations that develop secondary to chronic low-grade irritation, persistent inflammatory stimuli, or repetitive tissue injury, reflecting an exaggerated localized reparative response.1–3

RHLs arise through a multifactorial pathogenic process that is predominantly initiated by chronic local irritation and, to a lesser extent, systemic predisposing factors. Persistent exposure to local irritants, including plaque and calculus deposits, carious teeth, sharp tooth cusps, food impaction, ill-fitting prostheses, defective restorations, and parafunctional oral habits, elicits continuous tissue injury and chronic inflammatory stimulation.2,4,5 This prolonged reparative response promotes connective tissue proliferation, extracellular matrix deposition, and progressive fibrous maturation, culminating in localized reactive soft-tissue overgrowths. Parafunctional behaviors, such as cheek biting and bruxism, may further exacerbate these processes by maintaining repetitive mechanical trauma to the affected tissues.2,4–7

In addition to local irritative factors, systemic influences may modulate the host tissue response and predispose individuals to the development of RHLs. Hormonal fluctuations during puberty and pregnancy contribute to vascular hyperplastic responses likely through their effects on vascular permeability, angiogenesis, and inflammatory signaling pathways. Furthermore, chronic periodontal inflammatory diseases, particularly gingivitis and periodontitis, create a sustained proinflammatory microenvironment that facilitates the reactive proliferation of connective tissue and formation of localized oral overgrowths.6,8,9

Khat (Catha edulis) chewing is a prevalent sociocultural habit in Yemen and an important local irritative factor affecting oral mucosal health.10 Khat contains psychoactive alkaloids, primarily cathinone and cathine, and is typically chewed for prolonged periods with unilateral bolus retained in the buccal vestibule. Habitual use significantly compromises periodontal health, leading to increased attachment loss and gingival recession, which establishes a chronic inflammatory environment.11–13 Chronic exposure to the khat bolus may induce oral mucosal alterations through repetitive mechanical trauma; chemical irritation from its bioactive constituents; and associated physiological effects, including xerostomia and local vasoconstriction.11,13,14 Furthermore, irritation induced by khat occurs through two distinct pathways: the physical plant material acts as a repetitive mechanical abrasive, while the chemical alkaloids can alter local microvascular behaviour through vasoconstriction. This distinction is critical, as mechanical trauma may primarily drive physical lesion expansion, whereas chemical constituents modify the localized tissue response and healing capacity.11,12

The accurate diagnosis of RHLs cannot rely solely on clinical assessment because these lesions frequently exhibit overlapping clinical features and variable clinicopathological correlations. Consequently, histopathological examination remains the gold standard for establishing a definitive diagnosis and excluding other reactive or neoplastic entities.2,4,15 Excisional biopsy is the preferred management strategy for small localized reactive lesions because it facilitates complete lesion excision while simultaneously providing an adequate specimen for definitive histopathological diagnosis.16–18

While the prevalence of RHLs has been widely documented, data examining their clinicopathological spectrum in relation to region-specific risk factors and behavioral habits remain scarce, particularly in Yemen, where khat chewing constitutes a unique and prevalent exposure.1,2 A significant gap remains in the literature regarding how region behavioural habits modulate their clinicopathological spectrum. Specifically, the role of habitual khat chewing has not been systematically compared between chewers and non-chewers to determine its influence on the clinical behaviour or histopathological phenotype of RHLs.1–3 This study addresses this gap by providing a novel clinicopathological characterization, highlighting the unique contribution of khat as a regional modifier of lesion size and periodontal health. Therefore, the present cross-sectional study aims to characterize the clinical and histopathological features of RHLs and investigate their association with habitual khat chewing and related behavioural factors in a Yemeni dental population.

Methodology

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This research employed an observational cross-sectional clinical design to investigate the influence of khat chewing on the development of RHLs. It involved a sample of 1197 Yemeni dental patients recruited through convenience sampling. They included all patients who attended dental clinics of Sana’a University and two major public hospitals (Aljomhori and Althawra) in Sana’a, Yemen, between February 2024 and June 2024. This study received formal approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Sana’a University (Reference No. 263). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from adult participants prior to their enrollment in the study. For participants younger than 18 years, parental informed consent was obtained from one of their parents or legal guardians prior to enrollment.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This study included Yemeni patients of both genders aged over five years old who presented for dental treatment with RHLs. Khat and nonkhat chewers were included. Participants in the khat-chewing group were required to have a history of regular khat chewing for at least 3–5 years, with chewing sessions lasting no less than 5 h per day and occurring at least five days per week. These thresholds were selected to ensure the inclusion of only chronic, high frequency users. Patients younger than five years, edentulous individuals, and non-Yemeni patients were excluded to reflect the sociocultural characteristics of the local population.

Sample Size Justification

The sample size for this study was derived through the large-scale screening of 1197 Yemeni dental patients attending Sana’a University and major public hospitals between February 2024 and June 2024. From the screened population, 48 patients (4%) presenting with clinical signs of RHLs underwent excisional biopsy for histopathological confirmation. This cohort (n = 48) was previously utilized to describe the prevalence of oral RHLs as reported recently.1

However, the present study represents a distinct scientific investigation; whereas the previous report established the general prevalence of RHLs, this work provides a dedicated clinicopathological analysis focusing on how habitual khat chewing modifies the lesion phenotype. Data Collection

Demographic and Clinical Data

Demographic information, including age, sex, marital status, and occupation, was collected from all participants by using a structured data collection form. Information regarding behavioral and oral habits, including khat chewing, cigarette smoking, and shammah (smokeless tobacco) use, was also recorded. In addition, potential local irritative factors, such as cheek biting, bruxism, and oral hygiene status, were documented to assess their possible associations with lesion development. A comprehensive medical history was obtained. It included systemic health conditions, pregnancy and lactation status when applicable, current medication use, and any reported drug or substance allergies.

Clinical Examination

A total of 48 patients with histopathologically confirmed RHLs were included in the final analysis. Information regarding khat-chewing status was recorded for all participants and subsequently incorporated into the statistical analyses. All participants underwent a standardized clinical examination and excisional biopsy procedure.

Intraoral examinations were performed by a single calibrated examiner using a standard dental chair under optimal illumination. The examination included the systematic inspection and palpation of the labial mucosa, alveolar mucosa, buccal mucosa, and tongue to identify exophytic soft tissue lesions. For each lesion, the anatomical location, maximum clinical diameter, color, surface characteristics, consistency, and morphology (sessile or pedunculated) were documented (Figure 2). Following clinical assessment, excisional biopsy was performed for all lesions. Tissue specimens were immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Richard-Allan Scientific, the USA) and submitted to a specialized oral pathology laboratory for histopathological examination. Histopathological findings were used to establish the definitive diagnosis and confirm the clinicopathological characterization of each lesion.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed by using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, the USA). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. The associations of khat-chewing status with demographic, clinical, and histopathological variables were evaluated by using Pearson’s chi-squared test. When the expected frequency in any cell was less than five, Fisher’s exact test was applied as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. Given the small sample size, Fisher’s exact test was preferentially used when the assumptions of the chi-squared test were not met.

Discussion

The present cross-sectional clinicopathological study investigated the association between the habitual chewing of khat and the clinical and oral histopathological characteristics of RHL in a Yemeni population. Its findings demonstrated significant associations between khat-chewing status and sex, smoking status, periodontal status, and lesion size. By contrast, no significant associations were observed between khat-chewing status and lesion anatomical location, clinical course, morphology, or histopathological diagnosis. Collectively, these findings suggest that khat chewing may influence the severity and extent of reactive tissue proliferation without substantially altering the underlying histopathological pattern of the lesions.

Demographic Characteristics

The study population had a mean age of 37.0 ± 16.1 years, which falls within the age range commonly reported for RHLs in previous clinicopathological investigations.19,20 The predominance of male patients observed in the present study is more likely attributable to the high prevalence of khat chewing and tobacco use among Yemeni men than to any intrinsic biological susceptibility to lesion development.21 Notably, the sex distribution of RHLs varies considerably across different populations and study settings, with some studies reporting a female predominance and others a male predominance,22–24 suggesting that demographic, behavioral, and sociocultural factors may play a substantial role in shaping disease patterns. These observations underscore the importance of considering population-specific risk exposures when interpreting the epidemiological characteristics of RHLs.

Association Between Khat Chewing and Demographic/Clinical Variables

A significant association was identified between khat-chewing status and smoking, supporting previous evidence that these habits frequently coexist within the same behavioral profile. This clustering of risk behaviors may potentiate chronic oral irritation and inflammatory burden.25,26 Furthermore, the observed association between khat chewing and periodontitis is consistent with evidence from previous epidemiological and systematic reviews linking habitual khat use to periodontal attachment loss, gingival recession, increased pocket depth, and compromised periodontal health. The prolonged retention of the khat bolus, together with associated xerostomia and reduced oral hygiene practices, may contribute to the establishment of a chronic inflammatory environment that favors periodontal tissue destruction.12,27 The clustering of khat chewing with smoking and periodontitis suggests a synergistic inflammatory environment. While khat provides a mechanical abrasive stimulus, tobacco smoke introduces chemical carcinogens and irritants that induce oxidative stress and impair microvascular perfusion. This synergy likely exacerbates the reactive proliferation of connective tissue, as evidenced by the significantly larger lesion sizes in chewers who also smoke.28

Association Between Khat Chewing and Clinical and Histopathological Characteristics

One of the most clinically relevant findings of the present study is the significant association between khat chewing and large lesion size. Individuals with a history of habitual khat use were significantly more likely to present with lesions exceeding 1 cm in diameter than nonchewers.14,29 Although the cross-sectional nature of this study does not permit conclusions regarding causality, this association may indicate a contributory role of chronic khat-related mucosal irritation in lesion progression. The prolonged retention of the khat bolus within the oral cavity exposes localized tissues to persistent mechanical trauma and chronic inflammatory stimulation, potentially contributing to tissue remodeling and progressive lesion enlargement.30 Collectively, these processes could facilitate continued tissue proliferation and lesion enlargement over time. The observed relationship supports the concept that khat acts as a chronic, nonspecific irritant that amplifies reactive tissue responses rather than inducing a distinct pathological entity.

No significant association was observed between khat-chewing status and lesion location. This finding suggests that although khat-related irritation may contribute to lesion development and enlargement, the anatomical distribution of RHLs is likely influenced by a complex interplay of local irritative factors. Previous studies have identified dental plaque, calculus, traumatic occlusion, defective restorations, and other forms of chronic mechanical irritation as important determinants of lesion initiation and localization. Consequently, lesion location may be governed primarily by the site of local tissue injury rather than by khat exposure alone.19,31

Likewise, the lack of significant associations between khat-chewing status and lesion morphology or clinical behavior suggests that khat use is unlikely to impart a distinct clinical presentation to RHLs. Despite its potential role as a chronic irritative stimulus, khat exposure does not appear to alter the fundamental clinical characteristics of these lesions. This finding is consistent with the well-recognized clinical heterogeneity and substantial phenotypic overlap among RHLs, regardless of the underlying irritant. Consequently, the comparable distribution of sessile and pedunculated lesions observed among khat chewers and nonchewers may reflect shared reactive pathways governing lesion development rather than the influence of a specific etiological factor.19,32

Histopathological analysis revealed no significant association between khat chewing and lesion subtype. The similar distribution of irritative fibroma, pyogenic granuloma, and peripheral giant cell granuloma among khat chewers and nonchewers suggests that khat does not determine the specific histopathological pattern of RHLs. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution; given that 91.6% of the cases were irritational fibromas, the sample size was insufficient to evaluate rarer subtypes effectively.

Strengths of the Study

This study’s main strengths include the histopathological confirmation of all lesions, an approach that enhanced diagnostic accuracy, and the inclusion of khat chewers and nonchewers, allowing the direct comparison of clinicopathological characteristics. Additionally, the assessment of demographic, periodontal, clinical, and histopathological variables provided a comprehensive evaluation of RHLs. The study also contributes valuable data from Yemen, wherein evidence on the relationship between khat chewing and RHLs remains limited.

Limitations of the Study

The cross-sectional design of this work precludes causal inference, and the small sample size, together with convenience sampling, may limit the generalizability of the findings and introduce selection bias. Furthermore, detailed measures of khat exposure were not assessed, and residual confounding from smoking, oral hygiene practices, periodontal status, and other local irritants cannot be completely excluded.

Future Research

Future research should consider extending the study period to increase the sample size, thereby further strengthening the statistical robustness and generalizability of the results. Furthermore, the future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter prospective studies to clarify further the role of khat chewing in the initiation, progression, and biological behavior of RHLs. The use of standardized and detailed measures of khat exposure, including chewing duration, frequency, intensity, cumulative exposure, and chewing laterality, would facilitate the investigation of potential dose–response relationships and exposure-dependent effects. In addition, the integration of advanced histopathological techniques and molecular analyses targeting pathways involved in inflammation, angiogenesis, fibrosis, and tissue remodeling may provide deep insights into the mechanisms through which khat influences oral tissue responses. Future studies should also incorporate rigorous adjustment for potential confounding variables, including tobacco use, oral hygiene status, periodontal health, and other sources of chronic local irritation, to delineate accurately the independent contribution of khat chewing to the pathogenesis of RHLs. Such approaches may help establish a comprehensive understanding of the biological and clinical implications of chronic khat use in oral health.

Conclusion

Habitual khat chewing was associated with periodontal disease and large RHLs but showed no significant relationship with lesion location, clinical characteristics, or histopathological diagnosis. Collectively, these findings support the concept that khat functions primarily as a chronic nonspecific irritative stimulus that may exacerbate reactive oral tissue responses rather than determine a distinct clinicopathological phenotype. Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify its role in the pathogenesis of RHLs and inform targeted preventive strategies in khat-chewing populations.
