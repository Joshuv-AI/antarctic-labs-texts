Fractional Divisibility of Spheres with Partially Generic Sets of Rotations
Jan Grebík, Christian Ikenmeyer, Oleg Pikhurko

Abstract
We say that an r r -tuple ( γ 1 , … , γ r ) (\gamma_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\gamma_{r}) of special orthogonal d × d d\times d matrices fractionally divides the ( d − 1 ) (d-1) -dimensional sphere 𝕊 d − 1 {\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1} if there is a non-constant function f ∈ L 2 ​ ( 𝕊 d − 1 ) f\in L^{2}({\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}) such that its translations by γ 1 , … , γ r \gamma_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\gamma_{r} sum up to the constant-1 function. Our main result shows, informally speaking, that fractional divisibility is impossible if at least r / 2 r/2 rotations are “generic”.

Introduction

Let an integer
d\geqslant 2
be fixed throughout the paper. Let
\mathrm{SO}(d)
denote the group of
d\times d
special orthogonal real matrices.
The elements of this group are naturally identified with orientation-preserving
isometries of the Euclidean unit sphere

{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}:=\{{\mathbold x}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{d}:\|{\mathbold x}\|_{2}=1\},

where the action
\gamma.{\mathbold x}
of
\gamma\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
on the (column) vector
{\mathbold x}\in{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
is the matrix product
\gamma{\mathbold x}\in{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
.
Also,
\mathrm{SO}(d)
has the natural left action on the functions on
{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
, namely

(1.1)

(\gamma.f)({\mathbold v}):=f(\gamma^{-1}.{\mathbold v}),\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for }\gamma\in\mathrm{SO}(d),\ f:{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}\to{\mathbbm{R}}\mbox{ and }{\mathbold v}\in{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}.

Let
\mu
denote the
spherical measure
on
{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}
, which can be defined as the
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure with respect to the geodesic distance
on the sphere. This measure is invariant under the action
\mathrm{SO}(d){\curvearrowright}{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
, so the action in (
1.1
) preserves the Hilbert space
L^{2}({{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}},\mu)
.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{d,r}
be the set of
r
-tuples
{\mathbold\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\gamma_{r})\in\mathrm{SO}(d)^{r}

such that
{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
is
fractionally
{\mathbold\gamma}
-divisible
(when we also say that
{\mathbold\gamma}

fractionally divides

{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
), meaning that there is a function
f\in L^{2}({{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}},\mu)
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{i}.f
is equal to
1

\mu
-a.e. and
f
differs from
1/r
on a set of positive measure (or, equivalently,
f
is not a constant function
\mu
-a.e.).

This notion was studied in Conley, Grebík and Pikhurko
[
CGP24
]
who proved in
[
CGP24
, Theorem 1.1]
that, for any
d,r\geqslant 2
, the set
{\mathcal{D}}_{d,r}
cannot contain any
generic

r
-tuple
{\mathbold\gamma}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)^{r}
, where the notion “generic” is defined in the algebraic geometry sense. Here we do not give the exact definition but point out that the set of non-generic elements of the topological group
\mathrm{SO}(d)^{r}
both is meager and has Haar measure 0, see
[
CGP24
, Lemma 1.3]
. Thus we get the following corollary.

Corollary 1.1

(Conley, Grebík and Pikhurko
[
CGP24
]
)
.

For any integers
d,r\geqslant 2
, the subset
{\mathcal{D}}_{d,r}
of the compact group
\mathrm{SO}(d)^{r}

is both meager and null.

We say that an
r
-tuple of matrices
(\gamma_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\gamma_{r})\in\mathrm{SO}(d)^{r}

measurably divides

{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
if there is a measurable subset
A
of
({{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}},\mu)
such that its translates
\gamma_{1}.A,\dots,\gamma_{r}.A
(defined in the obvious way) form a partition of
{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
, without a single point being omitted or multiply covered. The authors of
[
CGP24
]
were motivated by the question of Wagon
[
Wag93
, Question 4.15]
(which is Question 5.15 in the newer edition
[
TW16
]
of the book) whether there is a triple of rotations in
\mathrm{SO}(3)
that
measurably divides
{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
. (The related question whether the Axiom of Choice can be avoided here altogether, not even on a
\mu
-null set, was earlier asked by Mycielski
[
Myc57a
,
Myc57b
]
.)
The obvious more general version of the question of Wagon, where we consider arbitrary pairs
(r,d)
, has known answer for all
d\not=3
(see
[
CGP24
, Page 26]
for references to these results). However, the cases when
d=3
and
r\geqslant 3
still remain open. Since measurable divisibility is a stronger version of fractional divisibility (namely, we additionally require that
f
is
\{0,1\}
-valued and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{i}.f
is 1 everywhere),
[
CGP24
, Theorem 1.1]
implies that any
r
-tuple of matrices achieving it must have entries that satisfy some non-trivial polynomial relation with integer coefficients.

Fractional divisibility of the real line
\mathbb{R}
, better known under the name
(translational) tilings by a function
, has been extensively studied from the perspective of Fourier analysis, see the survey
[
KL21
]
.
A key role in the seminal results
[
KL96
,
LW96
]
is played by periodic sets of translations; this, for a given period, corresponds to fractional division under the action of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
on the circle
{\mathbbm{S}}^{1}
.
This special case, when
d=2
, connects our investigation with this area; however, note that when
d>2
there is a stark difference between commutative and non-commutative divisibility, see for instance the references in
[
KL21
, Section 6.4]
and the results about translational tilings of tori in
[
GGRT23
]
.

The main result of this note (Theorem
1.2
) is to strengthen Corollary
1.1
by allowing an adversary to fix some number of matrices. With this in mind, we define
f^{\mathrm{top}}(d,r)
(resp.
f^{\mathrm{Haar}}(d,r)
) to be the smallest integer
\ell
such that for every
(r-\ell)
-tuple
{\mathbold\gamma}^{\prime}=(\gamma_{\ell+1},\dots,\gamma_{r})\in\mathrm{SO}(d)^{r-\ell}
the section

{\mathcal{D}}_{d,r}({\mathbold\gamma}^{\prime}):=\{(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{\ell})\in\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}:(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{r})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{d,r}\}

(which consists of those
\ell
-tuples
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{\ell})\in\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
such that the extended
r
-tuple
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{r})
fractionally divides
{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
) is a meager (resp. null) subset of the group
\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
.
Thus Corollary
1.1
gives that each of these functions is at most
r
.
Note that, by the Kuratowski-Ulam and Fubini-Tonelli Theorems, if
f^{\mathrm{top}}(d,r)\leqslant\ell^{\prime}\leqslant r
or
f^{\mathrm{Haar}}(d,r)\leqslant\ell^{\prime}\leqslant r
, then
\ell^{\prime}
also satisfies the defining condition.
Thus the following theorem, the main result of this note, strengthens Corollary
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
d\geqslant 2
and
r\geqslant 2
, each of
f^{\mathrm{top}}(d,r)
and
f^{\mathrm{Haar}}(d,r)
is at most
\ell
, where
\ell:=\lfloor r/2\rfloor
if
d\geqslant 3
and
\ell:=1
if
d=2
.

Let us provide some lower bounds on these functions.

Proposition 1.3
.

It holds
f^{\mathrm{top}}(d,r),f^{\mathrm{Haar}}(d,r)\geqslant 1
for any
d,r\geqslant 2
. Also,
f^{\mathrm{top}}(d,4),f^{\mathrm{Haar}}(d,4)\geqslant 2
for every odd
d\geqslant 3
.

Thus, by Theorem
1.2
and Proposition
1.3
, we know the following values:

\displaystyle f^{\mathrm{top}}(2,r)\ =\ f^{\mathrm{Haar}}(2,r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{any $r\geqslant 2$,}

\displaystyle f^{\mathrm{top}}(d,3)\ =\ f^{\mathrm{Haar}}(d,3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{any $d\geqslant 2$,}

\displaystyle f^{\mathrm{top}}(d,4)\ =\ f^{\mathrm{Haar}}(d,4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{any odd $d\geqslant 3$.}

There is little empirical data but, if the authors are to guess, it seems plausible that each of
f^{\mathrm{top}}(d,r)
and
f^{\mathrm{Haar}}(d,r)
is at most
2
for all
d\geqslant 3
.

2
Spherical harmonics

Our proofs rely on some basic facts about spherical harmonics. For a detailed introduction to this topic, we refer to the book by Groemer
[
Gro96
]
.
Here, we only state the definitions and results that we need. Recall that an integer
d\geqslant 2
is fixed throughout this paper. So the dependence on
d
is often omitted.

A polynomial
p\in{\mathbbm{R}}[{\mathbold x}]
in
{\mathbold x}=(x_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{d})
is called
harmonic
if
\mathop{\mathrm{\Delta}}p=0
, where

\mathop{\mathrm{\Delta}}:=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}+\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{d}^{2}}

is the
Laplace operator
. A
spherical harmonic
is a function from
{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
to the reals which is the restriction to
{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}
of a harmonic polynomial on
{\mathbbm{R}}^{d}
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be the vector space of all spherical harmonics.
For an integer
n\geqslant 0
, let
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
be the linear subspace consisting of all functions
f:{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}\to{\mathbbm{R}}
that are the restrictions to
{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}
of some harmonic polynomial
p
which is homogeneous of degree
n
, where we regard the zero polynomial as homogeneous of any degree. By
[
Gro96
, Lemma 3.1.3]
, the homogeneous degree-
n
polynomial
p
is uniquely determined by
f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
, so we may switch between these two representations without mention.

In order to state some further properties of spherical harmonics, we need the following definitions. Recall that
\mu
denotes the spherical measure on
{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}
. Let the shorthand
a.e.
stand for
\mu
-almost everywhere. Let
\sigma_{d}
denote the total measure of the sphere:

\sigma_{d}:=\mu({\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1})=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}.

By
[
Gro96
, Lemma 1.3.1]
, the density
\rho
of the push-forward of
\mu
under the projection to any coordinate axis is

(2.1)

\rho(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma_{d-1}\,(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2},&-1<t<1,\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

An important role is played by the
Gegenbauer polynomials

(P_{0},P_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
which are obtained from
(1,t,t^{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
by the Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization process on
L^{2}([-1,1],\rho(t)\,\mathrm{d}t)
, except they are normalised to assume value 1 at
t=1
(instead of being unit vectors in the
L^{2}
-norm). In the special case
d=3
(when
\rho
is the constant function), we get the
Legendre polynomials
.
Of course, the degree of
P_{n}
is exactly
n
.

We are now ready to collect some properties that we will need (with references to the statements in
[
Gro96
]
that imply them).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
. For every integer
n\geqslant 0
the following holds.

1.

The space
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
is invariant under the action of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
(
[
Gro96
, Proposition 3.2.4]
).

2.

The dimension of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
is

N_{n}:=\binom{d+n-1}{n}-\binom{d+n-3}{n-2},

where we agree that
\binom{d+n-3}{n-2}=0
for
n=0
or
1
(
[
Gro96
, Theorem 3.1.4]
).

3.

For every
{\mathbold v}\in{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}
, the function
P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}}:{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}\to{\mathbbm{R}}
, defined by

(2.2)

P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}}({\mathbold x}):=P_{n}({\mathbold v}\cdot{\mathbold x}),\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for }{\mathbold x}\in{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1},

belongs to
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
, where
{\mathbold v}\cdot{\mathbold x}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}x_{i}
denotes the scalar product of vectors in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{d}
(
[
Gro96
, Theorem 3.3.3]
).

4.

There is a choice of
{\mathbold v}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,{\mathbold v}_{N_{n}}\in{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}
such that the functions
P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}_{i}}
,
i\in[N_{n}]
, form a basis of the vector space
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
(
[
Gro96
, Theorem 3.3.14]
).

5.

For every
{\mathbold u},{\mathbold v}\in{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}
, we have
\langle P_{n}^{{\mathbold u}},P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}}\rangle=\frac{\sigma_{d}}{N_{n}}P_{n}({\mathbold u}\cdot{\mathbold v})
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the scalar product on
L^{2}({\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1},\mu)
(
[
Gro96
, Proposition 3.3.6 and Theorem 3.4.1]
).

6.

The subspaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{0},{\mathcal{H}}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
of
L^{2}({\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1},\mu)
are pairwise orthogonal
(
[
Gro96
, Theorem 3.2.1]
) and the linear span of their union is dense in
L^{2}({\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1},\mu)
(by
[
Gro96
, Corollary 3.2.7]
).

We will need the following characterization of the set
{\mathcal{D}}_{d,r}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d,r\geqslant 2
and take any
{\mathbold\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{r})\in\mathrm{SO}(d)^{r}
. For an integer
n\geqslant 1
, define

(3.1)

{\mathbold L}_{n}(g):=\sum_{s=1}^{r}\gamma_{s}.g,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for $g\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}$},

and note that
{\mathbold L}_{n}(g)\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
by Lemma
2.1
1
.
Then the
r
-tuple
{\mathbold\gamma}
fractionally divides
{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
if and only if there is an integer
n\geqslant 1
such that the linear map
{\mathbold L}_{n}:{\mathcal{H}}_{n}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
is not invertible

Proof.
Note that
{\mathbold L}_{n}
is a linear operator on
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
. Let
{\mathbold L}_{n}^{*}:{\mathcal{H}}_{n}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
denote its adjoint operator with respect to the scalar product inherited from
L^{2}({{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}},\mu)
. Since the action of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
preserves the measure
\mu
, we have that
{\mathbold L}_{n}^{*}(g)=\sum_{s=1}^{r}\gamma_{s}^{-1}.g
for
g\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
. For a unit vector
{\mathbold v}\in{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}
, define

(3.2)

G^{{\mathbold v}}_{n,{\mathbold\gamma}}:={\mathbold L}_{n}^{*}(P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}}).

Note that
G^{{\mathbold v}}_{n,{\mathbold\gamma}}=\sum_{s=1}^{r}P_{n}^{\gamma_{s}^{-1}.{\mathbold v}}
, since it holds
for all
\gamma\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
and
{\mathbold x}\in{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
that

\big(\gamma^{-1}.P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}}\big)({\mathbold x})=P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}}(\gamma.{\mathbold x})=P_{n}({\mathbold v}\cdot(\gamma.{\mathbold x}))=P_{n}((\gamma^{-1}.{\mathbold v})\cdot{\mathbold x})=\big(P_{n}^{\gamma^{-1}.{\mathbold v}}\big)({\mathbold x}).

Suppose first that there is a non-constant
f\in L^{2}({\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1},\mu)
such that
\sum_{s=1}^{r}\gamma_{s}.f=1
a.e. By Lemma
2.1
6
, we have the direct orthogonal sum
{\mathcal{H}}=\oplus_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
. Thus we can uniquely write
f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}F_{n}
in
L^{2}({\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1},\mu)
with
F_{n}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
for every
n\geqslant 0
. Since the action of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
preserves each space
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
by Lemma
2.1
1
as well as the scalar product on
L^{2}({\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1},\mu)
, we have that

\gamma.f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma.F_{n}
is the harmonic expansion of
\gamma.f\in L^{2}({\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1},\mu)
.

Since the sum
\sum_{s=1}^{r}\gamma_{s}.f
is a constant function
1
a.e. and
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
is exactly the set of constant functions on
{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
, we have
by Lemma
2.1
6
and the invariance of the scalar product under
\mathrm{SO}(d)
that, for every integer
n\geqslant 1
and every vector
{\mathbold v}\in{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}},1\rangle\ =\ \langle P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}},\gamma_{1}.f+\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt+\gamma_{r}.f\rangle\ =\ \langle P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}},\gamma_{1}.F_{n}+\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt+\gamma_{r}.F_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}},{\mathbold L}_{n}(F_{n})\rangle\ =\ \langle{\mathbold L}_{n}^{*}(P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}}),F_{n}\rangle\ =\ \langle G^{{\mathbold v}}_{n,{\mathbold\gamma}},F_{n}\rangle.

Since
f:{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}\to{\mathbbm{R}}
is not a constant function
\mu
-a.e., there is
n\geqslant 1
such that

. Thus the functions
G^{{\mathbold v}}_{n,{\mathbold\gamma}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
for

do not span the whole space. It follows from Lemma
2.1
4
that
{\mathbold L}_{n}^{*}
is not surjective and thus not invertible; of course, the same applies to

.

Conversely, suppose that
{\mathbold L}_{n}
is not invertible for some

. By the finite dimensionality of

there is a non-zero polynomial

with
{\mathbold L}_{n}(g)=0
. The last identity means that
\sum_{s=1}^{r}\gamma_{s}.g=0
. Thus, for example,

shows that
{\mathbold\gamma}
fractionally divides
{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Note that the function
f=\frac{1}{r}+g
obtained in the end of the proof of Lemma

.

Proof of Theorem

.

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{d}}\langle P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}_{i}},P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}_{j}}\rangle,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for }i,j\in[N_{n}].

Let us see what happens if we let

to

\sum_{s=1}^{r}\gamma_{s}.g=(\ell+1)\gamma_{\ell+1}.g+\sum_{s=\ell+2}^{r}\gamma_{s}.g\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}.

Looking at the norm coming from
L^{2}({{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}},\mu)\supseteq{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
and using that the action of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
on this space preserves the norm, we get by the Triangle Inequality that, for all
g\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}\setminus\{0\}
,

\|{\mathbold L}_{n}(g)\|_{2}\geqslant(\ell+1)\|\gamma_{\ell+1}.g\|_{2}-\sum_{s=\ell+2}^{r}\|\gamma_{s}.g\|_{2}=(2\ell+2-r)\,\|g\|_{2}>0.

By the finite dimensionality of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
, the linear map
{\mathbold L}_{n}
is invertible.
Furthermore, note that the invertible adjoint map
{\mathbold L}_{n}^{*}
sends the basis vectors
P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}_{1}},\dots,P_{n}^{{\mathbold v}_{N_{n}}}
to respectively
G^{{\mathbold v}_{1}}_{n,{\mathbold\gamma}^{\prime}},\dots,G^{{\mathbold v}_{N_{n}}}_{n,{\mathbold\gamma}^{\prime}}
. Thus the latter vectors are linearly independent (which was observed earlier to be equivalent to
\det L\not=0
). Thus the polynomial
\det L
is non-zero when evaluated at the constant
\ell
-tuple
(\gamma_{\ell+1},\dots,\gamma_{\ell+1})\in\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
.

We are now ready to show that, for each
n\geqslant 1
, the set
Z_{n}
(that consists of those
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{\ell})\in\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
on which
\det L
vanishes) is both null and meager. Informally speaking, this is true since the zero set of a non-vanishing polynomial
\det L
on the irreducible variety
\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
must have strictly smaller dimension than the variety itself. For the sake of completeness, we present a proof of this intuitively clear result (which is obtained by an easy adaptation of the proof of
[
CGP24
, Lemma 1.3]
).

Clearly,
Z_{n}
is a closed subset of of
\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
so, in particular, it is measurable.
First, we prove that
Z_{n}
is a null set with respect to the Haar measure
\nu
on
\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
.
Let us recall how the Haar measure
\nu
can be constructed for the group
\Gamma:=\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
(and, in fact, for any real Lie group), following the presentation in
[
Kna02
, Sections VIII.1–2]
. Namely, choose some linear basis for the Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{so}(d))^{\ell}
viewed as the tangent space
T_{(I_{d},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,I_{d})}
at the identity
(I_{d},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,I_{d})\in\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
and, using the translations of these vectors, turn them into left-invariant vector fields

X_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,X_{m}
. (Note that the Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{so}(d))^{\ell}
, that consists of all
\ell
-tuples of skew-symmetric matrices, has dimension
m=\binom{d}{2}\ell
as a vector space.) For each
{\mathbold\gamma}\in\Gamma
, let
e_{1}({\mathbold\gamma}),\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,e_{m}({\mathbold\gamma})\in T_{{\mathbold\gamma}}^{*}
be the dual basis to
(X_{1}({\mathbold\gamma}),\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,X_{m}({\mathbold\gamma}))
. Then
\omega=e_{1}\wedge\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\wedge e_{m}
(the skew-symmetric product) is a smooth
m
form on
\Gamma
, which is positive and left-invariant and thus
defines a Borel left-invariant non-zero measure on
\Gamma
(
[
Kna02
, Theorem 8.21]
). By the uniqueness, this has to be a multiple of the Haar measure
\nu
. In particular, any smooth submanifold of
\Gamma
of dimension (as a manifold) less than
m
has zero Haar measure (
[
Kna02
, Equation (8.25)]
).

The variety
\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
is irreducible and has dimension
m=\binom{d}{2}\ell
as a real algebraic variety. This is a well-known fact for
\ell=1
and the proof of this fact for an arbitrary
\ell
is carefully spelled out in
[
CGP24
, Lemma 8.2]
.

The set
Z_{n}\subsetneq\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
, as an algebraic variety, has dimension smaller than
m
which follows from the definition of the
algebraic dimension
of a variety as the maximum length of a strictly nested chains of non-empty irreducible varieties. (Indeed, a chain for
Z_{n}
extends to a larger chain for
\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
by adding
\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}\supsetneq Z_{n}
at the top.) The standard results in the theory of
(semi-)
algebraic sets give
that every bounded variety of algebraic dimension
k
in some
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
admits a finite partition, with each part being a smooth
semialgebraic manifold in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
of dimension at most
k
, see e.g.
[
BPR06
, Theorem 5.38]
.
Apply this result to each of finitely many irreducible components
Z
of
Z_{n}
.
The dimension
s
of each obtained manifold
S
is at most
\dim Z
. Indeed, pick a point
{\mathbold s}\in S
and the projection from
S
on some
s
coordinates which is a homeomorphism around
{\mathbold s}
. Observe that these
s
coordinates are algebraically independent in the function field
{\mathbbm{R}}(Z)
(because a non-zero polynomial on
{\mathbbm{R}}^{s}
cannot vanish on a non-empty open set). By
[
Has07
, Proposition 7.27]
, the algebraic dimension of
Z
is at least
s
, as claimed.

Thus we covered
Z_{n}
by finitely many manifolds of dimension less than
m
. As it was observed earlier, each such manifold has Haar measure 0 (namely, by
[
Kna02
, Equation (8.25)]
). We conclude that the Haar measure of
Z_{n}
is indeed zero.

Let us show that
Z_{n}
is meager in
\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
. Since
Z_{n}
is closed, it is enough to show that the relative interior
U
of
Z_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
is empty. Suppose on the contrary that
U\not=\emptyset
. Since the compact group
\mathrm{SO}(d)^{\ell}
acts transitively on itself by homeomorphisms, finitely many translates of
U
cover the whole group. As the Haar measure
\nu
is invariant under this action, we have that
\nu(U)>0
. However, this contradicts the identity
\nu(Z_{n})=0
that we have already proved.

This proves the theorem for
d\geqslant 3
.

Finally, suppose that
d=2
. While the above arguments (for
d\geqslant 3
) also apply for
d=2
, we get in fact the stronger conclusion that each section is countable
by explicitly writing the action of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
on
L^{2}({\mathbbm{S}}^{1},\mu)
. Here, we identify
\mathrm{SO}(2)
with the circle
{\mathbbm{T}}:={\mathbbm{R}}/2\pi{\mathbbm{Z}}
(where we take reals modulo
2\pi
) by corresponding each rotation matrix
\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\\
\sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{array}\right)\in\mathrm{SO}(2)
to its angle
\phi\in{\mathbbm{T}}
. Take any integer
n\geqslant 1
. It is well-known (see e.g.
[
Gro96
, Section 3.1]
) that the dimension of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
is
2
and we can take
c,s\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
as its basis vectors, where
c,s
are the (unique) homogeneous degree-
n
polynomials that satisfy
c(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha):=\cos(n\alpha)
and
s(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha):=\sin(n\alpha)
for every
\alpha\in[0,2\pi)
.
Then the action of
\phi\in{\mathbbm{T}}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
in the basis
(c,s)
is given by the matrix

M_{\phi}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos(n\phi)&-\sin(n\phi)\\
\sin(n\phi)&\cos(n\phi)\end{array}\right).

Indeed, the action of
\phi\in{\mathbbm{T}}
on, for example,
c\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
is the function

\cos(n(x-\phi))=\cos(n\phi)c(x)+\sin(n\phi)s(x),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in{\mathbbm{T}}.

Recall that we are given
\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{r}\in\mathrm{SO}(2)
.
Let the linear map
g\mapsto\sum_{s=2}^{r}\gamma_{s}.g
from
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
to itself be given in the basis
(c,s)
by the matrix
K=(K_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{2}
.
We vary only
\gamma_{1}=M_{\phi}
for
\phi\in{\mathbbm{T}}
. Let
x:=\cos(n\phi)
and
y:=\sin(n\phi)=\pm\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
. Suppose that
y=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
with the other case being analogous. Consider the linear map
{\mathbold L}_{n}
that sends
g\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
to
\sum_{s=1}^{r}\gamma_{s}.g
.
As in the case
d\geqslant 3
, we will be done if we show that the set of those
\phi\in{\mathbbm{T}}
for which
{\mathbold L}_{n}c
and
{\mathbold L}_{n}s
are linearly dependent is both null and meager. The matrix of
{\mathbold L}_{n}
in the basis
(c,s)
is

M_{\phi}+K
. Its determinant is

\displaystyle\det(M_{\phi}+K)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x+K_{1,1})(x+K_{2,2})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle(-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}+K_{1,2})(\sqrt{1-x^{2}}+K_{2,1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-x^{2}}(K_{2,1}-K_{1,2})+x(K_{1,1}+K_{2,2})+\det K+1.

It is enough to show that the set of
x\in[-1,1]
for which this is 0 is countable, since the map
\phi\mapsto\cos(n\phi)
has countable pre-images (in fact, each has at most
2n
elements). The equation
\det(M_{\phi}+K)=0
implies that

(K_{1,2}-K_{2,1})^{2}(1-x^{2})=(x(K_{1,1}+K_{2,2})+\det K+1)^{2}.

To satisfy this polynomial identity for for an infinite set of
x
, it must be the case that the coefficients at all powers of
x
match. By looking at the terms linear in
x
, we conclude that
(K_{1,1}+K_{2,2})(\det K+1)=0
. One of the factors is 0 and either case implies that
K_{1,2}=K_{2,1}
which in turn implies that
\det K=-1
and
K_{1,1}+K_{2,2}=0
. Thus
K=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
b&-a\end{array}\right)
for some
a,b\in{\mathbbm{R}}
. Note that
K
is a linear combination of some matrices
M_{\psi}
. As each matrix
M_{\psi}
has equal diagonal entries and opposite off-diagonal entries, we conclude that
a=b=0
, contradicting
\det K=-1
. Thus the set of
x
for which
{\mathbold L}_{n}c,{\mathbold L}_{n}s\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
are linearly dependent is in fact finite and so is its pre-image in
{\mathbbm{T}}
, as desired. This finishes the proof of the theorem.∎

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

We start by showing that
f^{\mathrm{top}}(d,r)>0
and
f^{\mathrm{Haar}}(d,r)>0
for every
d,r\geqslant 2
.
By definition, this is the same as showing that we can always find an
r
-tuple of rotations that fractionally divides
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Given arbitrary
d,r\geqslant 2
, take
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{r}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
that are the rotations of the
(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane by angles
\frac{2\pi m}{r}
, for
m\in\{0,\dots,r-1\}

(and fix every other standard basis vector).
Define
f:{{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}}\to\{0,1\}
by
f({\mathbold x}):=1
if there are
\rho>0
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi/r)
such that
(x_{1},x_{2})=(\rho\cos\theta,\rho\sin\theta)
, and let
f({\mathbold x}):=0
otherwise. This
f
shows that
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{r})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{d,r}
, so each of
f^{\mathrm{top}}(d,r)
and
f^{\mathrm{Haar}}(d,r)
is at least
1
.

Let us consider the case when
d=2m+1\geqslant 3
is odd and
r=4
. Let
D(c_{1},\dots,c_{d})
denote the diagonal matrix with
c_{1},\dots,c_{d}
on the diagonal and let
c^{(k)}
denote the sequence where
c
is repeated
k
times.

Let
\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}
be the diagonal matrices

D((-1)^{(2m-1)},-1,1),\ D((-1)^{(2m-1)},1,-1)\mbox{ and }D((1)^{(2m-1)},-1,-1).

We have that their sum is
D((-1)^{(d)})
, the diagonal matrix with all diagonal entries equal to
-1
. We can naturally identify the space
{\mathbbm{R}}^{d}
with
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
by corresponding
{\mathbold u}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{d}
to the linear map
{\mathbold x}\mapsto{\mathbold x}\cdot{\mathbold u}
. Under this identification the action of
\gamma\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
is given by the usual matrix product.
Thus

\sum_{s=2}^{4}\gamma_{s}.g=-g
for every
g\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
.

Let us show that the section
{\mathcal{D}}_{d,4}(\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4})
is the whole group
\mathrm{SO}(d)
. Take any
\gamma_{1}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
. Let
{\mathbold u}\in{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}
be a fixed point of
\gamma_{1}
, which exists since
d
is odd. Then the function
g:{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}\to{\mathbbm{R}}
which sends
{\mathbold x}
to
{\mathbold u}\cdot{\mathbold x}
is a non-zero element of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
with
\gamma_{1}.g=g
and thus
\sum_{s=1}^{4}\gamma_{s}.g=0
. We can now invoke Lemma
3.1
(or just observe that e.g.
f=\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{4}g
is a non-constant function that shows fractional divisibility).
∎

4
Concluding remarks

Our definition of fractional divisibility allows
f
to be an arbitrary function in
L^{2}({{\mathbbm{S}}^{d-1}},\mu)
. Of course, by varying this requirements and/or adding constraints such that the values of
f
are restricted to
[0,1]
, we get potentially different notions. Since the main result of this note (Theorem
1.2
) is about the non-existence of
f
, we picked the largest natural class of functions for which our proof works. This definition is rather natural; also, it admits an alternative characterization (as given by Lemma
3.1
) and is not affected when we add some extra restrictions on
f
(as discussed in Remark
3.2
).
