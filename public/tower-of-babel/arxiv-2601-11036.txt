Self-Augmented Mixture-of-Experts for QoS Prediction
Kecheng Cai, Chao Peng, Chenyang Xu, Xia Chen, Yi Wang, Shuo Shi, Qiyuan Liang

Abstract
Quality of Service (QoS) prediction is one of the most fundamental problems in service computing and personalized recommendation. In the problem, there is a set of users and services, each associated with a set of descriptive features. Interactions between users and services produce feedback values, typically represented as numerical QoS metrics such as response time or availability. Given the observed feedback for a subset of user-service pairs, the goal is to predict the QoS values for the remaining pairs. A key challenge in QoS prediction is the inherent sparsity of user-service interactions, as only a small subset of feedback values is typically observed. To address this, we propose a self-augmented strategy that leverages a model’s own predictions for iterative refinement. In particular, we partially mask the predicted values and feed them back into the model to predict again. Building on this idea, we design a self-augmented mixture-of-experts model, where multiple expert networks iteratively and collaboratively estimate QoS values. We find that the iterative augmentation process naturally aligns with the MoE architecture by enabling inter-expert communication: in the second round, each expert receives the first-round predictions and refines its output accordingly. Experiments on benchmark datasets show that our method outperforms existing baselines and achieves competitive results.

Introduction

Quality of Service (QoS) is a set of metrics to evaluate the performance of web services
[
1
]
. It includes non-functional metrics such as response time, throughput, and availability, and is used to differentiate the quality of services that have the same functionality. With the rapid growth of cloud computing
[
2
]
and web services
[
3
]
, the ability to accurately predict QoS has become increasingly important for optimizing resource allocation and improving user experience. The prediction of QoS
[
12
,
25
]
involves estimating the performance metrics of the services based on historical invocation records. These predictions enable service providers to dynamically allocate resources, improve service availability, and ensure a seamless user experience, particularly in large-scale distributed systems where service quality can vary significantly due to network conditions, server loads, and user behavior
[
23
]
.

In typical scenarios, users invoke web services and provide measurable feedback such as response time or availability
[
14
]
. These interactions naturally form a sparse user-service matrix, where each entry reflects an observed QoS value. Predicting missing entries in this matrix—i.e., estimating QoS for unseen user-service pairs—has become a core problem in the field
[
4
,
5
]
.

Traditional methods approach this problem via collaborative filtering (CF)
[
22
,
24
]
and matrix factorization (MF)
[
9
,
13
,
16
]
, which estimate missing QoS values based on similarity among users/services or low-rank latent representations. While efficient, these models often struggle with complex patterns and perform poorly under high sparsity.

To overcome these limitations, recent works have proposed deep learning methods, including neural collaborative filtering
[
7
]
, neural matrix factorization
[
26
]
, and graph-based or attention-based models
[
18
,
6
,
10
]
. These approaches aim to capture high-order dependencies and contextual signals through expressive architectures. However, many of them tightly couple representation learning and final prediction, which may hinder robustness.

In contrast, our method decouples representation learning and prediction: we use matrix factorization solely as a semantic encoder to extract user and service embeddings, while QoS prediction is delegated to a neural Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) network. This design enables a self-augmentation strategy that iteratively refines latent structure and prediction quality through model-guided feedback. Building on this foundation, our framework introduces new mechanisms for enhancing robustness and performance under extreme sparsity, as detailed below.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose a self-augmented
refill mechanism
that improves latent representation learning by iteratively predicting and updating missing QoS values. This process leverages a dedicated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture, enabling experts to specialize and refine their predictions collaboratively.

•

We introduce a
pseudo-labeling strategy
as another self-argument strategy, in which selected model-predicted entries are incorporated back into training supervision. This self-augmentation phase further enhances model generalization and robustness in the face of severe data sparsity.

•

Extensive experiments on QoS datasets demonstrate that our method significantly outperforms existing state-of-the-art approaches, validating the effectiveness of our dual-stage self-augmentation design.

2
Preliminary

2.1
Problem Formulation

In the task of
Quality of Service (QoS) prediction
, we consider a setting with
m
users and
n
Web services. Let
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{m}\}
and
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\}
denote the sets of users and services, respectively. Each user or service may be associated with descriptive features, such as geographic location, usage patterns, or network context.

User-service interactions generate measurable QoS feedback, such as response time or throughput. These interactions are represented by a matrix
R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, where
R_{ij}
denotes the QoS value observed when user
u_{i}
invokes service
s_{j}
.

2.2
Neural Network-Based Modeling

Modern approaches increasingly adopt neural architectures to model the QoS prediction task. These methods aim to learn expressive representations of users and services by jointly leveraging two types of information:

•

Attribute-based information
, such as user demographics, service category, or location. In earlier matrix factorization methods, these signals often appeared as bias terms or regularization priors; neural models incorporate them more flexibly through learnable embeddings or attention mechanisms.

•

Collaborative information
extracted from the observed QoS matrix, including user-service interaction patterns and latent feedback signals (e.g., reputation).

Formally, given a user
u_{i}
and a service
s_{j}
, we obtain their latent representations via embedding functions:

\mathbf{u}_{i}=\phi_{u}(u_{i})+\psi_{u}(i),\quad\mathbf{s}_{j}=\phi_{s}(s_{j})+\psi_{s}(j),

(1)

where
\phi_{u}
and
\phi_{s}
incorporate side features (e.g., location, category), and
\psi_{u}(i),\psi_{s}(j)
represent collaborative embeddings derived from interaction patterns in the QoS matrix
R
, such as user reputation or service-level consistency.

The enriched embedding pair
(\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{j})
is then fed into a prediction network
f_{\theta}(\cdot)
to estimate the QoS value:

\hat{R}_{ij}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{j}).

(2)

The model is trained by minimizing a supervised loss over observed entries:

\min_{\theta}\sum_{(i,j)\in\Omega}\ell\left(R_{ij},\hat{R}_{ij}\right),

(3)

where
\Omega
is the set of observed user-service pairs, and
\ell
is typically the squared or absolute error.

This framework unifies structural insights from classical models (e.g., matrix factorization) with the flexibility of neural networks, allowing the model to capture both individual preferences and complex interaction patterns for accurate QoS prediction.

3
Methodology

This section details our proposed framework for Quality of Service (QoS) prediction under sparse user-service interactions. Our approach is built upon a self-augmentation paradigm, where model predictions are iteratively refined and reused to improve representation quality and downstream accuracy.

The architecture comprises three interdependent components: a representation module that encodes user and service semantics from incomplete matrices, a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) network that performs adaptive prediction over learned embeddings, and a training mechanism that incorporates both observed and pseudo-labeled data to enhance robustness. We describe each of these components in the following subsections.

3.1
Self-Augmented Representation Learning

A fundamental challenge in QoS prediction lies in the extreme sparsity of the user-service matrix
R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, where only a small subset of entries is observed. Despite this, we aim to learn effective latent representations for users and services that encode both collaborative signals and contextual attributes. Instead of relying on complex hand-crafted strategies to extract information from sparse matrices, we adopt a more direct approach: using matrix factorization to encode collaborative structure, and enhancing it through a self-augmented refill mechanism that progressively completes the matrix via model-guided prediction. An overview of our framework—including the matrix factorization module, feature fusion network, and the MoE predictor—is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Collaborative Representation via MF.

We begin by applying low-rank matrix factorization directly to the observed matrix
R
(restricted to entries in
\Omega
), obtaining preference-aware user embeddings
U^{(1)}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and quality-aware service embeddings
S^{(1)}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. These embeddings encode collaborative structure and act as semantic representations of user and service. In practice, we employ a truncated singular value decomposition (SVD) to extract these factors without gradient-based training.

Prediction and Refill.

To enhance representation capacity, we enrich each latent pair
(U^{(1)}_{i},S^{(1)}_{j})
with side information through embedding functions
\phi_{u}(u_{i})
and
\phi_{s}(s_{j})
. The resulting features are passed to a neural prediction module
f_{\theta}
to estimate QoS values:

\hat{R}^{(1)}_{ij}=f_{\theta}\left(U^{(1)}_{i},S^{(1)}_{j},\phi_{u}(u_{i}),\phi_{s}(s_{j})\right).

(4)

The network is trained using supervision only from observed entries
(i,j)\in\Omega
. Once trained, it is applied to predict the unobserved entries
(i,j)\notin\Omega
, yielding an updated matrix:

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}^{(1)}_{ij}=\begin{cases}R_{ij},&(i,j)\in\Omega,\\
\hat{R}^{(1)}_{ij},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(5)

Self-Augmentation Semantics.

In our framework, matrix factorization is used solely as a latent feature extractor. This design makes the learned embeddings particularly sensitive to the distribution of unobserved entries in the matrix. If missing values are left as zeros or other arbitrary constants, they may introduce artificial bias into the collaborative structure, leading to distorted user or service representations.

In contrast, our refill mechanism provides a data-adaptive alternative: missing values are estimated from the model itself, producing pseudo entries that are statistically aligned with the learned latent structure. Formally, we treat each unobserved entry
(i,j)\notin\Omega
as a latent variable whose posterior expectation is estimated by the model:

\mathbb{E}[R_{ij}\mid R_{\Omega}]\approx\hat{R}^{(1)}_{ij},\quad(i,j)\notin\Omega.

(6)

Compared to static placeholders, these model-informed estimates reduce the injection of structured noise and yield a softened supervision signal. Let
\epsilon^{\text{prior}}_{ij}=R_{ij}^{*}-c_{ij}
and
\epsilon^{\text{posterior}}_{ij}=R_{ij}^{*}-\hat{R}^{(1)}_{ij}
denote the prediction errors under static fill
c_{ij}
(e.g., 0) and model-based refill
\hat{R}^{(1)}_{ij}
respectively, with
R_{ij}^{*}
the true (unknown) value. Then under reasonable smoothness assumptions:

\mathbb{E}[|\epsilon^{\text{posterior}}_{ij}|]<\mathbb{E}[|\epsilon^{\text{prior}}_{ij}|],\quad\text{for most }(i,j)\notin\Omega,

(7)

which implies that refill introduces less noise into representation learning than arbitrary constants.

The resulting pseudo-complete matrix
\widetilde{R}^{(1)}
thus better preserves structural consistency and enhances both expert specialization and generalization in the following stages.

3.2
Expert Collaboration via MoE Architecture

To leverage the enriched user-service matrix from the refill process, we employ a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture as the prediction module. MoE is particularly suitable in our setting, as it can model diverse interaction patterns and naturally align with the iterative structure of self-augmentation.

Expert Specialization and Iterative Communication.

The two-stage refill process provides a natural foundation for expert collaboration. In the first stage, multiple experts are trained independently on the initially filled matrix
\widetilde{R}^{(0)}
, each developing its own inductive bias and regional focus. In the second stage, the model is retrained on the refined matrix
\widetilde{R}^{(1)}
, allowing experts to adjust their predictions based on patterns learned previously—effectively introducing a form of implicit communication.

Emergent Expert Diversity.

Preliminary experiments suggest that different experts exhibit specialization in distinct regions of the QoS matrix. As Figure

3.3
Self-Augmented Training with Pseudo Labels

In earlier stages of our framework, the training process relies solely on observed entries
(i,j)\in\Omega
to supervise the prediction network, even after the refill procedure produces a fully populated matrix
\widetilde{R}^{(1)}
. However, this overlooks a potentially valuable signal: the model’s own predictions on unobserved entries.

To exploit this, we introduce a self-augmented training strategy that includes a subset of filled entries as pseudo labels. Specifically, let
\bar{\Omega}=\{(i,j)\notin\Omega\}
denote the set of originally unobserved entries. From this set, we sample a subset
\Omega^{\prime}
such that:

\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\bar{\Omega},\quad\text{and}\quad\forall(i,j)\in\Omega^{\prime}:u_{i},s_{j}\in\text{Train-ID set}.

(8)

That is, we only select entries where both the user and service have appeared in the original training set, ensuring compatibility with learned representations.

Let
\hat{R}^{(1)}
denote the predictions obtained after the first-stage model. We construct an augmented supervision set:

\tilde{\Omega}=\Omega\cup\Omega^{\prime},

with corresponding labels

\displaystyle Y_{ij}=\begin{cases}R_{ij},&(i,j)\in\Omega,\\
\hat{R}^{(1)}_{ij},&(i,j)\in\Omega^{\prime}.\end{cases}

(9)

The model is then trained by minimizing:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{aug}}=\sum_{(i,j)\in\tilde{\Omega}}\ell\left(\hat{R}_{ij},Y_{ij}\right),

(10)

where
\ell
denotes a standard regression loss (e.g.,
\ell_{2}
). While pseudo labels
\hat{R}^{(1)}_{ij}
inevitably contain bias from the previous model, we observe empirically that including them improves test-time generalization.

This effect can be interpreted as a trade-off between bias and variance: although pseudo labels introduce noise, the increase in data volume and distributional coverage improves the robustness of the learned predictor.

3.4
Implementation Details and Feature Fusion Strategy

This subsection introduces several supporting components that, while not central to our methodological contributions, are necessary for enabling effective training and stable performance.

User and Service ID Embedding.

Each user
u_{i}
and service
s_{j}
is associated with two types of side information: (1) inherent features, such as geographic location or service category, denoted as
u\_feat_{i}
and
s\_feat_{j}
, and (2) collaborative features derived from matrix factorization, denoted as
u\_mf_{i}
and
s\_mf_{j}
. In addition to these, we introduce learnable ID-based embeddings
u\_id_{i}
and
s\_id_{j}
for each user and service, respectively.

Although user/service IDs are often regarded as non-semantic symbols, we revisit their role in the context of sparse QoS prediction. Given the limited side information and data density, ID embeddings offer a lightweight yet effective way to encode entity-specific signals, helping the model distinguish among diverse users and services. This enhances representation expressiveness and improves generalization under data scarcity. Empirically, we find that incorporating ID embeddings consistently benefits overall performance.

Two-Tower Feature Fusion.

To construct rich representations for users and services, we adopt a two-tower architecture that independently encodes each entity type before their interaction is modeled downstream. Each tower processes three sources of input:

•

ID Embeddings:
Each user and service is associated with a unique, learnable ID embedding, providing index-level differentiation among entities.

•

Side Features:
Structured attributes (e.g., location, region, category) are linearly projected into the hidden space, forming semantic feature vectors.

•

Matrix Factorization Embeddings:
Latent factors extracted via matrix factorization are used to capture collaborative signals from the QoS matrix.

The three feature types are concatenated separately for users and services, and passed through dedicated linear layers to obtain the final embeddings:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}_{i}=\text{Mixer}_{u}([u\_id_{i}\|u\_feat_{i}\|u\_mf_{i}]),\\
\quad\mathbf{s}_{j}=\text{Mixer}_{s}([s\_id_{j}\|s\_feat_{j}\|s\_mf_{j}]).\end{aligned}

(11)

Note that the two towers do not project users and services into a shared representation space. Instead, each embedding is constructed in its own context-specific subspace, allowing the downstream prediction module to model interactions flexibly based on asymmetric input information.

This architectural separation helps preserve entity-specific inductive biases while maintaining modularity and interpretability—especially valuable in settings with heterogeneous features and sparse supervision.

4
Experiments

We conduct comprehensive experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed self-augmentation framework for QoS prediction. The goal is to assess how each component—refill and augmented supervision—contributes to the model’s predictive performance under realistic sparse conditions.

4.1
Experimental Setup

Evaluation Objective.

All models are trained to predict QoS values (e.g., response time or throughput) for unobserved user-service pairs. Performance is evaluated on held-out test sets using standard regression metrics, including Mean Absolute Error (MAE, Equation
12
) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE, Equation
13
). Lower values indicate better performance.

\displaystyle\text{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|R_{ij}-\hat{R}_{ij}\right|

(12)

\displaystyle\text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_{ij}-\hat{R}_{ij})^{2}},

(13)

Model Variants.

To assess the impact of each self-augmentation component, we evaluate the following model variants:

•

MF-MoE
: A baseline model that performs standard matrix factorization to extract latent user/service embeddings, followed by a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) predictor. The model is trained solely on observed entries without any self-augmentation.

•

SA-MoE
-
: Introduces the first-stage self-augmentation mechanism. The model is initially trained on observed entries, then uses its own predictions to refill the unobserved entries, and is subsequently retrained on the updated matrix.

•

SA-MoE
: The complete version of our framework. In addition to the refill-based retraining, it further incorporates selected pseudo-labeled entries (from previously unobserved positions) into the training set, enabling fully end-to-end self-augmentation.

Training Process.

All models share the same architecture and optimization settings. To ensure fair comparison, we constrain the total number of training epochs across all variants. Specifically,
MF-MoE
is trained for 100 epochs on the initial training set, while both
SA-MoE
-
and
SA-MoE
adopt a two-stage training schedule: the first-stage model is trained for 40 epochs on the initially filled matrix, and the second-stage model is retrained for another 60 epochs on the augmented matrix, respectively. All results are averaged over 4 different random seeds.

Where the squaring of errors means that larger deviations in predictions are penalized more severely. Hyperparameter Analysis

We further investigate the sensitivity of SA-MoE and its variants to different hyperparameter choices, specifically focusing on the two most influential parameters in the self-augmentation process: the matrix refill ratio (
r_{m}
) and the pseudo-label ratio (
r_{p}
).

The matrix refill ratio (
r_{m}
) refers to the proportion of missing entries in the training set (for users or services present in the training data) that are filled during the refill process. The pseudo-label ratio (
r_{p}
) defines the proportion of unobserved entries for which model-generated predictions are treated as pseudo-labels and included in the training set. Both parameters have a significant impact on the training speed and overall model performance. In the main experiments, both ratios were set to 0.4.

In this section, we explore how varying
r_{m}
and
r_{p}
affects model performance, particularly with higher refill and pseudo-label rates. We aim to identify the optimal trade-off between enhancing data coverage and maintaining training efficiency.

Effect of Varying Matrix Refill Ratio (
r_{m}
)

As shown in Figure
3
, the performance of
SA-MoE
-
exhibits a non-monotonic trend as the matrix refill ratio (
r_{m}
) increases. Specifically, as
r_{m}
rises from 0.4 to 1.0, the model’s performance first decreases and then improves. This suggests a complex interaction between the noise introduced by the refill mechanism and the additional information it provides to the model.

A possible explanation for this behavior is that moderate levels of refill (around 0.7) introduce noise into the matrix, distorting the collaborative signals and leading to poorer performance. In contrast, when the refill ratio is low (0.4), the model lacks sufficient data to refine the matrix effectively. On the other hand, a higher refill ratio (1.0) introduces more information to the model, allowing it to learn better from the augmented data. Thus, both low and high levels of refill appear to help the model by either minimizing noise or providing more informative data, respectively. This suggests that there is an optimal balance between noise and information quantity, with extreme values on either side providing a suboptimal learning environment.

Conclusion and Future Work

We propose
SA-MoE
, a self-augmented hybrid framework for QoS prediction that combines matrix factorization with a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) network. The method introduces two key components: (1) a
refill mechanism
that uses model predictions to iteratively complete sparse QoS matrices, and (2) a
pseudo-label strategy
that incorporates selected predictions as additional supervision.

Together, these techniques improve latent representation quality and enhance generalization under data sparsity. Experiments confirm that SA-MoE consistently outperforms baseline methods across various settings.

Future Work.

We plan to extend this framework in two directions:
(1) applying self-augmentation to other sparse learning tasks, such as recommendation and feedback modeling;
(2) adapting SA-MoE to dynamic user-service environments where user/service sets evolve and fixed IDs are unavailable.

Baselines.

We compare our proposed model against several widely adopted and representative methods for Web service QoS prediction. Below, we provide a brief description of each baseline:

•

UIPCC

[
22
]
: A hybrid collaborative filtering method that combines both user-based and item-based Pearson correlation strategies. It leverages the similarity between users and services to estimate unobserved QoS values.

•

PMF

[
13
]
: Probabilistic Matrix Factorization introduces Gaussian priors to user and item latent factors. It models the user-service matrix probabilistically, making it more robust to sparsity and noise.

•

NFMF

[
19
]
: Neural Factorization Machine for Matrix Factorization enhances traditional MF by integrating deep neural layers to capture non-linear and high-order interactions between users and services.

•

RAHN

[
6
]
: Reputation-Aware Hybrid Network employs attention mechanisms to integrate multiple user-service interaction features, including reputational signals. It enhances robustness to noise by filtering out unreliable patterns.

•

GraphMF

[
11
]
: A graph-based matrix factorization framework that constructs user-service graphs and applies graph convolutional operations to learn better collaborative embeddings from local graph neighborhoods.

•

TAN

[
10
]
: Topology-Aware Network uses the communication topology of users and services as a contextual graph structure to enrich feature representations. It emphasizes multi-hop relational modeling for improved QoS prediction.

Dataset.

To validate the performance of MF-MoE, we conduct experiments on the large-scale real-world dataset WS-DREAM
[
20
]
, which is a widely used benchmark for QoS prediction in web services. It contains real-world QoS data, including response time (RT) and throughput (TP), collected from distributed users invoking various web services. Specifically, as shown in

For each density setting, the corresponding QoS data samples were harnessed as training data, while the remaining samples were employed as test data to evaluate the accuracy of QoS prediction.

and weight decay of

Expert Modules and Hyperparameters in MoE.

The Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) component in our framework consists of three heterogeneous expert networks, each designed to capture distinct patterns in the user-service interaction space. Their configurations are as follows:

•

Attention Expert:
A single-layer multi-head self-attention mechanism, implemented via
MultiheadAttention

[
17
]
in PyTorch. We use
2
attention heads and apply a dropout of
0.5
to the attention weights. The input embedding dimension is set to 32, consistent with other modules.

•

LHUC Expert:
A two-stage modulation network inspired by LHUC
[
15
]
. It consists of two parallel pathways for users and services, each with two fully connected layers followed by batch normalization. The output is adaptively scaled using a gating mechanism based on item embeddings:

\texttt{output}=\texttt{BN}_{2}\left(2\cdot\sigma(W_{4}\cdot s_{j})\cdot\texttt{ReLU}(W_{2}\cdot\texttt{BN}_{1}(...))\right)

(14)

where
\sigma
denotes the sigmoid function. This expert is particularly effective at enhancing representation flexibility through sample-wise modulation.

•

MLP Expert:
A standard three-layer feedforward neural network with hidden sizes
[64,64,32]
. Each intermediate layer is followed by a ReLU activation.

Gating Mechanism and Output Layer.

To adaptively combine expert outputs, we introduce a learnable gating network. This gate computes a softmax-weighted distribution over the three expert outputs, based on the concatenated input embeddings of users and services:

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\text{Softmax}\left(W_{g}[\mathbf{u}_{i}\|\mathbf{s}_{j}]\right),\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(15)

where
W_{g}
is a trainable weight matrix in the gating layer. Each expert output is first reshaped and then weighted accordingly:

\mathbf{o}_{\text{final}}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\alpha_{k}\cdot\mathbf{o}^{(k)}.

(16)

This formulation ensures dynamic expert selection, allowing different expert networks to dominate for different user-service pairs.

Finally, the aggregated vector
\mathbf{o}_{\text{final}}
is passed through a task-specific prediction head:

\hat{y}_{ij}=\tanh(W_{\text{out}}\cdot\mathbf{o}_{\text{final}}),

(17)

where
W_{\text{out}}
denotes a linear transformation and
\tanh
provides bounded output for stable regression. This pipeline supports the learning of robust, expert-aware predictions for Quality of Service (QoS) values.

More detailed architectural and training configurations can be found in our source code.
