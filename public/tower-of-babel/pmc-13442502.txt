Identifying marker-trait associations for wheat stem sawfly resistance
Authors: Mik Hammers, Zachary J. Winn, Brian Rice, Punya Nachappa, Katherine Frels, Geoff Morris, Richard Esten Mason
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key messageTwo novel WSS resistance loci were identified on chromosomes 2B and 5A. Characterization of WSS resistance loci will improve breeders’ ability to select to reduce yield loss due to WSS.AbstractWheat stem sawfly (WSS) is a native grass feeding pest of winter wheat (Triticum aestivum L) which is difficult to control since most of its life cycle occurs within the stem of wheat plants. The only well-characterized genetic resistance to WSS is the solid stem locus (Sst1) on chromosome 3B, which exhibits environmental variability. It is critical to identify novel forms of genetic resistance outside of Sst1 to improve the overall resistance of wheat to WSS. In this study, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) were performed on lines in the Colorado State University wheat breeding program grown between 2014 and 2025 field seasons for three traits of interest: heading date (HD), WSS damage in the form of stem cutting (CUT), and stem solidity (SOLID). Significant marker trait associations (MTA) were identified on chromosomes 2B, 2D, 3A, 3B, 5A, and 5D for CUT and 2A, 3B, 4B, and 6A for SOLID. Significant MTA from these GWAS were used to identify beneficial allelic combinations (AC) for WSS resistance. The stem cutting ACs which had the lowest damage estimates were those in which lines possessed the resistant haplotype at every locus assessed (CUT = 2.35, error = 0.21, N = 56). Lines that had all resistant alleles in the stem solidity ACs showed the same superior estimate (SOLID = 14.7, error = 0.78, N = 22). The positive effects of the identified small-effect MTA on CUT and SOLID indicated the importance of including these loci when breeding for WSS resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00122-026-05317-3.

Introduction

Wheat stem sawfly (Cephus cinctus Norton) (WSS) is a native grass-feeding insect pest of many agronomically relevant grasses including common wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), durum wheat (Triticum durum), barley (Hordeum vulgare), and triticale (× Triticosecale Wittmack) (Beres et al. 2012).Wheat stem sawfly has been noted as a pest of spring wheat in the Northern United States (US) since the nineteenth century. However, due to changes in climatic patterns, WSS began emerging earlier in the season in the latter twentieth century and have adapted to regularly infest hard winter wheat of the Great Plains region (Peirce et al. 2022). Currently, the annual estimated financial loss due to WSS in the Great Plains and Canada is approximately $350 million United States dollars (Beres et al. 2011).

Most of the WSS life cycle occurs within the stem of the host, making cultural or chemical control difficult (Beres et al. 2011). In late spring, WSS oviposit their eggs within the stem (Ainslie 1920). Once hatched, the larvae feed on the parenchymal tissues of the plant while also cannibalizing other larvae until one larva remains. The remaining sawfly larva then eats its way towards the base of the stem where it creates a hibernaculum, or “stub” to pupate and overwinter. During the formation of the hibernaculum, the larva will chew the surrounding stem and fill the opening with frass (Ainslie 1920; Beres et al. 2007). Once infested wheat has senesced, the weakened stems are predisposed to lodging which disrupts conventional agricultural practices and reduces yield.

Currently, the most widely utilized genetic resistance to WSS in released wheat cultivars is derived from the SSt1 locus on the long arm of chromosome 3B which confers the “solid stem” phenotype (Cook et al. 2004; Nilsen et al. 2017, 2020). Likewise, there is a less frequently utilized homologue to SSt1, Qssmsub-3DL, located on the long arm of chromosome 3D (Lanning et al. 2006). In non-solid stem wheat, the center of each stem is comprised of ground tissue consisting of parenchyma cells which normally break down during maturation (Ainslie 1920). These undifferentiated parenchyma cells constitute the pith. When the pith inside the wheat stem breaks down it leaves a central cavity called the internodal lacuna (Kebrom et al. 2012; Kirby, n.d.). In solid and semi-solid stemmed wheat cultivars, these parenchyma cells do not break down entirely which increases the mortality of WSS eggs through mechanical interference (Beres et al. 2012; Holmes & Peterson 1960, 1961), increases the mortality of eggs and neonates through increased stem moisture (Holmes & Peterson 1964), and hinders the ability of WSS larvae to move about the stem and feed (Evert et al. 2006; Kemp 1934). The decreased damage to semi-solid stem or solid stem plants reduces lodging caused by WSS damage, thus mitigating some yield losses (Beres et al. 2007).

The molecular mechanism conferring the SSt1 solid stem phenotype is attributed to increased expression of the gene TdDof (TRITD3Bv1G280530) (Nilsen et al. 2020). Nilsen et al (2020) demonstrated that at least 3 copies of the TdDof transcription factor confer the solid stem phenotype, and that a unique 25 base pair insertion sequence was present between each gene copy. Increased expression of SSt1 explained 76% of the variance for stem solidity in a doubled haploid winter wheat population derived from a solid stem by hollow stem cross (Cook et al. 2004). The solid stem phenotype conferred by SSt1 is also influenced by environmental variables such as temperature, light intensity, moisture, and precipitation (Beres et al. 2012). Lower temperatures and light intensity (Holmes 1984) as well as increased moisture and precipitation were found to decrease stem solidity (Platt & Farstad 1946). There are mixed reports about the effects of the solid stem trait on grain yield (Cook et al. 2019; Sherman et al. 2015).

It is crucial to find additional sources of WSS resistance, especially those not related to solid stem. Important sources of non-solid stem resistance could be related to host non-preference or other agronomic traits such as straw strength. It is also important to improve our understanding and consistency of solid stem expression. Increasing sources of genetic resistance to WSS may enhance the overall stability and durability of WSS resistance (MacIntosh 2019). In prior studies, Talbert et al. (2014) used two wheat recombinant inbred lines (RILs), ‘Scholar’ and ‘Conan’, that show varying resistance to WSS despite both having the solid stem SSt1 locus solid allele. It was found that while both varieties expressed solid stems, Conan had lower infestation and cutting scores than Scholar, despite having more hollow stems than Scholar. The authors found that these two lines had different alleles of the SSt1 locus, which could affect WSS resistance; this difference in WSS resistance was also compounded by a separate quantitative trait locus (QTL) on chromosome 4A. The QTL on 4A (wmc161) showed little effect on solid stem expression, but appeared to provide additive resistance with 20% reduced cutting in infested stems in the ‘Conan’ haplotype (Talbert et al. 2014).

Cook et al. (2017) identified 26 haplotypes for the SSt1 locus using nine kompetitive allele specific PCR (polymerase chain reaction) (KASP) assays. Three of these differing haplotypes included the solid stem SSt1 allele most used in breeding programs derived from S-615 (the Conan allele) and the hollow stem allele. All other haplotypes identified were novel to the study and suggest that novel recombination events and haplotypes from diverse wheat germplasm could be used as alternative sources for solid stem resistance.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) are a commonly used method for analyzing the genetic architecture of traits (Korte & Farlow 2013). At the time of this publication, there is only one peer-reviewed article that outlines a GWAS for WSS resistance (Joukhadar et al. 2013). Joukhadar et al. (2013) used a population of 134 wheat genotypes that included landraces, synthetic hexaploid wheat, and some elite germplasm and identified five genomic regions on chromosomes 1D, 3BL, 5BL, 6BS, and 7AS associated with number of cut stems. The marker-trait associations (MTA) identified on 3BL and 5BL were identified as most-likely linked to the SSt1 locus and the major effect vernalization locus VrnB1, respectively. However, the three remaining MTA were novel to the study. While there are multiple QTL studies for WSS resistance (Cook et al. 2019; Sherman et al. 2010; Varella et al. 2015; Varella et al. 2019a, b), comparing these existing results to a GWAS on a new population of elite and agronomically viable lines could potentially assist in identifying consistent QTL for WSS resistance across different populations or uncover previously unidentified MTAs.

The Colorado State University (CSU) Wheat Breeding and Genetics program has collected trait information on nurseries screened for WSS resistance including both cutting scores (CUT) and stem solidity ratings (SOLID) since the 2014 field season. While the solid stem phenotype conferred by SSt1 on chromosome 3BL is present in the program, work has been ongoing to further characterize genomic regions related to solid stem and non-solid stem resistance within the program. It has been hypothesized that genetic variation for solidity in the CSU germplasm is oligogenic and that non-solid stem resistance exists in the CSU germplasm because of the observed variation in levels of cutting and solidity amongst cultivars with and without SSt1 (Supplemental Table 1). Materials and methods

Germplasm

The population used in this study included 2,503 lines from the CSU wheat breeding and genetics program that had unbalanced phenotypic data for stem solidity or WSS cutting damage across many years. Germplasm included a range of generations ranging from F3 bulk populations, preliminary yield nurseries, advanced yield nurseries, and elite breeding program germplasm and released cultivars. All data was collected between 2014 and 2025 and totaled 11,502 observations in 45 unique site-year environments (Table 1). Not all lines were replicated in each environment within a given year.

Data collection

Observations were collected in Sidney, NE, Hemingford, NE, Grant, NE, Mead, NE, Orchard, CO, New Raymer, CO, Roggen, CO, Akron, CO, Genoa, CO, and Fort Collins, CO (Supplemental Table 2). Lines were planted in replicated breeding program field trials with included checks for row by column analysis. All data was collected as part of routine breeding program note taking for design and release of winter wheat cultivars. Heading date (HD) was defined as the number of days from January 1st (Julian day) that half the wheat heads in a plot had fully emerged from the flag leaf. Plant height was measure in centimeters by selecting a representative portion of the plot and measuring to the average height from the base of the plants to the top of the wheat spike not including awns. Cutting (CUT; i.e., lodging due to WSS damage) was scored on an ordinal visual scale ranging from one to nine where one was no visible cutting and nine was completely cut. Solidity (SOLID) was rated by collecting 20 stems randomly from each plot. A cross section was taken from each internode of the stem and rated on a visual ordinal scale of solidity from one to five where one was completely hollow and five was completely solid. An average is then taken across all internodal segments present on each stem from five to 25 where five is completely hollow throughout the whole length of the stem and 25 is completely solid throughout the whole length of the stem. A maximum of four internodal ratings were taken from each stem. Ratings for stems with less than four internodal regions were adjusted based on number of internodal ratings to meet the equivalent 5 to 25 scale. The rating for each plot is then taken by averaging the stem solidity across all 20 stems (Varella et al. 2016). Genotyping

Lines originating from CSU were sequenced via genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS). Ten seeds were planted for each line and roughly one cm tissue samples were collected from each plant for deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction. The DNA was extracted and quantified from the samples using ThermoFisher’s MagMax plant DNA and PicoGreen kits (ThermoFisher Scientific, Walthram, Massachusetts, USA), respectively. Extracted DNA was normalized to a concentration of 20 ng µL-1. Sequencing libraries were prepped following the protocol from Poland et al. (2012) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) at 384-plex density per lane. Reads were then aligned to the RefSeq Chinese Spring wheat reference sequence v2.1 (Appels et al. 2018) using the burrow-wheel aligner (Li & Durbin 2009). The reads obtained from the alignment were processed using the TASSEL 2.0 standalone pipeline (Glaubitz et al. 2014).

All single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were filtered using the following parameters: SNPs with greater than 20% missing data were removed; SNPs with greater than 10% heterozygosity were removed; SNPs with a read depth of zero or greater than 100 were removed; SNPs with a site allele frequency less than 5% or greater than 95% were removed, and unaligned or indel SNPs were removed; Only biallelic SNPs were retained. After filtering, 22,207 high-quality SNPs remained. Missing data was imputed using Beagle V 5.4 (Browning et al. 2018). A synthetic wheat biparental cross between ‘W7984’ and ‘Opata’ was used to estimate the recombination distance-based map for imputation due to its established use for high-density mapping (Gutierrez-Gonzalez et al. 2019).

Statistical analysis

For this analysis, within and across environment adjusted means were calculated for individuals in the population for HD, HT, CUT, and SOLID. These across environment adjusted means were used to perform GWASs to determine significant MTA for all four traits. From these MTA, additional GWASs were performed for both CUT and SOLID using these MTA as fixed effects. From these additional GWASs that utilized previous markers as fixed effects, MTA that were consistently significant across multiple CUT or SOLID GWAS models were used to develop ACs to determine the effects of pyramiding smaller effect CUT and SOLID loci with the SSt1 locus on chromosome 3B for WSS resistance. GWAS with marker covariates

Pruned markers from initial GWAS were used as fixed effects in CUT and SOLID models to identify loci for CUT and SOLID that were not associated with the SSt1 locus, HD, or HT. Heading date and HT markers were included as fixed effects due to their known relationship with WSS resistance loci (Bathini et al. 2023). Additionally, GWAS models were run utilizing populations that separated hollow stem or solid stem individuals based on if they had the hollow stem or solid stem allele at the most significant SSt1 locus marker (S3B_844887153). Based on these parameters, six additional GWASs were performed: CUT with 5 pruned CUT markers as fixed effects (C1), CUT with the 5 pruned CUT markers, 6 pruned HD markers, and 2 pruned HT markers as fixed effects (C2), CUT using only individuals with the hollow stem allele at the SSt1 locus (C3), SOLID with the 30 pruned SOLID markers as fixed effects (S1), SOLID with the 30 pruned SOLID, 6 pruned HD markers, and 2 HT markers as fixed effects (S2), and SOLID using only individuals with the solid stem allele at the SSt1 locus (S3).

Loci significant at the Bonferroni threshold or within the exploratory threshold from all models were compiled. If multiple markers from the same region were significant in the model, the SNP with the highest −log10(p) was selected as the peak marker for that region. Any significant markers that were present on the same chromosome as significant HD or HT markers were assessed to ensure they were associated with WSS resistance and not HD or HT. To do this, the LD was calculated between the significant CUT or SOLID marker and the nearby HD and HT markers to confirm HD or HT markers were not included for further analyses. Markers identified on chromosome 2D had their LD calculated between the marker closest to Ppd-D1(S2D_33652726) based on the gene specified in the GrainGenes database (TraesCS2D02G079600). Additionally, markers identified on chromosome 4B had their LD calculated between the marker closest to Rht-B1(S4B_28587008) based on the gene specified in the GrainGenes database (TraesCS4B02G043100) (Yao et al. 2022).

AC marker analysis

The allelic combinations were assessed in a linear mixed model (LMM) using ASReml-R version 4.1.0.160 (Butler et al. 2023) using the same structure as the markers effects data, but with PCs and ACs being used as fixed effects instead of PCs and markers. These models were used to predict the response variable (CUT or SOLID) for each AC to assess which resistant/susceptible marker combinations effect resistance to WSS.

Allelic combination analysis

Significant MTA from C1, C2, C3, S1, S2, and S3 were assessed to determine which of the MTA appear consistently across models and were not associated with HD or HT. Markers that fit this criteria were then used to create ACs to determine the effect of using both small effect resistant loci with the SSt1 locus on WSS resistance. Of all loci above the Bonferroni or within the exploratory threshold for either CUT or SOLID identified in the fixed effect GWASs, only five were on the same chromosome as a significant HD or HT marker. These markers were on chromosome 2B and 3A and had LD r2 values ranging from 0.001 to 0.04 with the exception of the peak marker from the C3 model (S2B_65833439), which had an LD r2 of 0.64 with the peak HD marker on chromosome 2B. Because of this, the S2B_65833439 marker was removed from further analysis. The low LD r2 of all other markers indicated the other loci identified for CUT and SOLID were not associated with HD or HT (Supplemental Table 6). Additionally, the LD r2 for peak CUT and SOLID markers on chromosome 2D had LD r2 values ranging from 0.006 to 0.01 with the peak PPd1 marker, indicating these were also unlinked.

Seven identified CUT markers were used to develop CUT ACs and included markers from 2B, 2D, 3A, 3B, 5A, and 5D. After stepwise linear regression identified the markers that had a significant effect on CUT at the Bonferroni threshold (0.005 ≥ p-value), there were no significant epistatic effects (Supplemental Table 7). Five markers remained in the AC group (S3B_844887153, S2B_564527, S3A_610265762, S5A_705490182, S5D_62660804) (Supplemental Table 8) with the 3B marker representing SSt1. From these five markers, 29 unique CUT ACs were developed and were used to predict of group means and standard errors for CUT and SOLID (Supplemental Table 9). The ACs with the lowest CUT predictions all had the solid stem allele at the SSt1 locus with the AC possessing all five resistance alleles having the lowest CUT prediction (CUT = 2.35, error = 0.21, n = 56). In contrast, the AC possessing the hollow stem allele at the SSt1 locus and susceptible alleles at all other loci had a predicted CUT of 5.8 (error = 0.4, n = 14). AC marker analysis

To further investigate the markers associated with each AC, individual marker effects were extracted for both CUT and SOLID from the stepwise linear regression models used to determine ACs (Table 4). For the five markers associated with CUT ACs, two of the resistant alleles are the major allele in the population (S2B_5645271 and S5D_62660804). The CUT marker with the largest effect on CUT was associated with the SSt1 locus (S3B_844887153) and had a CUT effect of -0.94 with a standard error of 0.12. All four other markers outside of the SSt1 marker had high SOLID standard errors relative to their estimated SOLID effect. However, the SSt1 resistant allele had an estimated effect of 3.63 on SOLID with a standard error of 0.41, which was expected. Additionally, not all resistant alleles for CUT had SOLID estimates that indicated increased SOLID in those alleles. For the markers associated with the SOLID ACs, all three resistant alleles were the minor allele in the population, and all three resistant alleles also predicted lower CUT. However, similar to the CUT markers, the estimates for CUT using the SOLID AC markers had high standard errors relative to the CUT estimates. The only exception was the SSt1 marker (S3B_844887153), which had similar estimates for CUT and SOLID in both AC models (CUT AC model: CUT Estimate = -0.94 ± 0.12, SOLID Estimate = 3.63 ± 0.41; SOLID AC model: CUT Estimate = -0.91 ± 0.13, SOLID Estimate = 3.70 ± 0.40).

Discussion

This study utilized GWAS to identify marker associations for WSS cutting (CUT) and stem solidity (SOLID), two traits used to measure and identify WSS resistance. The solid stem locus, SSt1, on chromosome 3B (Nilsen et al. 2020) was identified across several models even when used as a fixed effect. However, multiple MTA outside of the SSt1 locus that were identified in this study were also identified across multiple models, indicating stability of non-SSt1 resistance in the population. These MTA were then used to develop allelic combinations (ACs) to assess the impact of including small effect resistant loci with the SSt1 locus to improve WSS resistance.

In this manuscript, MTA for both CUT and SOLID were identified on chromosome 2D, however, neither were linked to the Ppd1 locus. While HD MTA have been found in multiple WSS studies (Sherman et al. 2010; Varella et al. 2015), significant MTA related to CUT or SOLID in this study on the same chromosome as significant HD MTA were found to share near-zero LD, reducing the likelihood of MTA for CUT and SOLID being confounded with HD. Multiple studies have identified novel QTL for WSS resistance, with some loci being identified on the same chromosomes as loci in this study (Supplemental Table 12). Multiple studies have also identified loci on chromosome 2D. The QTL identified by Varella et al. (2017) (Qwss.msub-2D) was associated with antixenosis. The alleles at Qwss.msub-2D were associated with probing behavior and plant cues related to the host plant selection process and influenced oviposition choices afer probing. These plant cues could be related to such as puncture resistance of the stem (Varella et al. 2019a, b).

Sherman et al. (2010) identified two QTL on chromosome 2D: One associated with HD and the Ppd-D1 locus and one only associated with CUT and infestation, which could support the findings of altered oviposition behavior reported by Varella et al. (2019a, b). Additionally, the QTL on 2D identified by Nilsen et al. (2017) (Qss.usw-2D) was associated with solidity. While later heading date is associated with reduced WSS damage (Sherman et al. 2010), supporting literature indicates that additional resistance mechanisms are associated with the same genomic region. Due to lack of available marker sequence data for all similar studies, it could not be determined if the loci identified on the same chromosomes were the same as previous literature. However, it does indicate that resistance identified in other populations could be the same resistance present in the CSU breeding program germplasm.

While no MTA identified in this manuscript were associated with known plant height loci, previous research does indicate a link between plant height, cutting score, and stem solidity (Graham & Frels 2025; Lanning et al. 2012). Buteler et al. (2009) identified that WSS prefer taller stems when selecting host plants for oviposition. Additionally, Graham & Frels (2025) identified a negative correlation between plant height and solidity. However, Lanning et al. (2012) found mixed result for dwarfing genes in wheat where germplasm in sets of NILs with the Rht-B1b and Rht-D1b alleles had increased solidity compared to the wild type. In the same study, germplasm with Rht8 had decreased solidity compared to the wild type. One significant MTA (S4B_480704002) in this analysis fell on the same chromosome as Rht-B1 (4B) (Peng et al. 1999) and one (S3A_610265762) fell on the same chromosome as a known plant height QTL (Xgwm369–XwPt-4725)(Griffiths et al. 2012). However, the linkage between the identified MTA and markers associated with Rht-B1 and Xgwm369–XwPt-4725 were low, indicating the significant MTA were associated with WSS resistance traits instead of plant height.

While there was not available data to determine if the QTL identified in previous literature were associated with the same MTA in the present study, there were many previously reported QTL identified on the same chromosomes as our MTA (Supplemental Table 12). Nilsen et al. (2017) identified another minor QTL for solidity on chromosome 5A (Qss.usw-5A), which was identified on the same chromosome as the CUT MTA identified in this study on chromosome 5A. However, there were no significant loci identified on chromosome 5A for the SOLID GWAS. Additionally, Varella et al.(2019a, b) identified a QTL on chromosome 6A associated with increased WSS mortality, which may be related to the 6A locus identified for solidity in the present work. Other studies identified additional MTAs that were located on the same chromosomes as CUT MTA identified in this study. Varella et al. (2015) identified QTL on chromosomes 2A and 3A associated with larval mortality and one on chromosome 5D associated with early stem solidity in spring wheat. Since early stem solidity has not been consistently measured in the population, it could not be directly associated with the same mechanism of resistance. However, early stem solidity is a known form of WSS resistance (Varella et al. 2015; Varella et al. 2019a, b), which could be the driver of the identified MTA in the CSU germplasm.

In addition to previous literature related to WSS resistance (Joukhadar et al. 2013; Nilsen et al. 2017; Sherman et al. 2010; Varella et al. 2015; Varella et al. 2019a, b), literature related to other agronomic traits such as straw strength and lodging were assessed to determine possible connections to identified MTA, since evidence shows the two may be related (Bathini et al. 2023). Multiple studies identified loci on chromosome 3A for a variety of different agronomic traits including stem strength (QSs-3A) (Hai et al. 2005), stem wall width and stem material strength (Ren et al. 2025), and lodging resistance (Dreccer et al. 2022). However, the closest 3A lodging QTL was over 100Mbp distal to the significant marker identified on 3A in the CUT fixed effects models. Ren et al., (2025) also identified a QTL associated with stem wall width on chromosome 6A. Additionally, loci on chromosomes 3A and 6A are associated with plant height, which can lead to reduced lodging (Curtis & Halford 2014).

Hai et al. (2005) identified a QTL on chromosome 2D (QCwt-2D) associated with culm wall thickness and explained 9.6% of phenotypic variation for stem wall thickness, which mirrors findings for stem solidity on chromosome 2D by (Nilsen et al. 2017) and the present study. For lodging, additional QTL for lodging resistance was identified on chromosome 2D (Excalibur_rep_c102984_157) (Dreccer et al. 2022), and chromosomes 2B and 4B (LI 2-4B-19)(Ren et al. 2025) with the 2B QTL (qSSE-2B-7, qLI 1-2B-21, qLI 3-2B-9; qSSE-2B-8, qLI 1-2B-22, qLI 3-2B-10) being in the same region as the significant CUT marker from this study (S2B_5645271). Similar MTA on chromosome 2B were identified in a study examining stem properties that included loci for culm wall thickness, stem area, pith diameter, and pith area (Ávila et al. 2021). These MTA were co-localized with previously identified QTL lodging or stem diameter according to the ‘Svevo’ genome browser QTL track. While there is no published literature relating specific agronomic traits such as culm all thickness to WSS resistance, these traits could be associated with the probing and oviposition behaviors (Buteler et al. 2009) exhibited by WSS. Additionally, traits related to stem strength and solidity are associated with lodging resistance (Bathini et al. 2023; Kong et al. 2013), which is important to mitigating loss due to WSS damage. Understanding agronomic traits that improve WSS resistance is important for helping breeders combine as many beneficial resistance alleles as possible.

In this study, markers that were identified in multiple of the C1, C2, C3 and S1, S2, S3 models were assessed for their effects on CUT and SOLID. Using the developed ACs to predict CUT and SOLID indicated that small effect would be beneficial for WSS resistance. While the solid stem allele is important to reduce CUT in this population, ACs with lower predicted CUT predictions when small effect loci were included support evidence that there is non-solid stem resistance for WSS. Additionally, while the CUT ACs with all resistant alleles or mostly resistant alleles at the smaller effect MTA had the lowest CUT scores, they did not have predictions for the highest SOLID ratings (Fig. 6, Supplemental Table 9). As expected, the 12 highest SOLID ratings had the resistant allele at the SSt1 locus. However, the CUT AC with all five resistant alleles only had the 9th highest predicted SOLID (predicted SOLID = 11.1, error = 0.63) (Supplemental Table 9) and the CUT AC with the highest predicted SOLID had all resistant alleles with the susceptible allele at the 2B locus (predicted SOLID = 13.1, error = 0.81). These results indicate that the markers in the CUT AC provide resistance to WSS outside of the solid stem trait.

Unlike the CUT ACs, the SOLID AC with all three susceptible alleles and the SOLID AC with all three resistant alleles both have the lowest and highest predicted SOLID and the highest and lowest predicted CUT, respectively (Fig. 6). For both SOLID and CUT, the four SOLID ACs with the solid stem allele at the SSt1 locus showed the highest resistance with the SOLID and CUT predictions (Supplemental Table 11). These results imply that the solid stem allele is a reliable form of WSS resistance in this population across years. While there is literature identifying and evaluating superior haplotypes for the solid stem trait (Cook et al. 2017), literature looking at allelic combinations across different chromosomes could not be identified.

The individual markers used for each AC were also assessed for their additive effect on both CUT and SOLID (Table 4). None of the epistatic interactions for the markers were significant, which aligns with previous research pertaining to the genetic architecture of solid stem resistance (Bainsla et al. 2020). Of the five markers in the CUT AC, two of the resistant alleles were the major allele in the population and had the second and third largest estimated effect on CUT (S5D_62660804, CUT effect = −0.68, error = 0.16; S2B_5645271, CUT effect = −0.57, error = 0.10) after the SSt1 solid stem allele (S3B_844887153, CUT effect = −0.94, error = 0.12). All five CUT markers were also assessed for their effect on SOLID, however, only the SSt1 allele had a notable estimated effect on SOLID and a standard error that was not the same size or larger than the estimated effect. The large standard errors may be due to the reduced sample size of SOLID data compared to CUT data in the population. The inability of the CUT markers to accurately predict SOLID in the population may also indicate the presence of non-solid stem resistance associated with the MTA.

Of the three MTA used in the SOLID AC, the SSt1 marker had the largest effect on SOLID (S3B_844887153, SOLID effect = 3.7, error = 0.4). However, the markers not associated with SSt1 still had notable effects on SOLID with an effect size of a 2.2 increase (error = 0.5) in SOLID for S4B_480704002 and an effect size of a 1.21 increase (error = 0.3) in SOLID for S2D_654117623. Resistance markers for WSS were identified on chromosome 2D by (Varella et al. 2017), but it was associated with host plant attractiveness and oviposition rates instead of solidity. Like the CUT AC markers that were used to estimate SOLID, the SOLID AC markers (outside of the SSt1 locus) had low estimated effects on CUT with higher standard errors (S2D_654117623, CUT effect = −0.09, error = 0.09; S4B_480704002, CUT effect = −0.06, error = 0.16). Since the SSt1 locus constitutes a large portion of the phenotypic variance in solidity ratings of designed populations (Nilsen et al. 2020), the lower effect of the other SOLID markers on CUT may be attributed to them being overshadowed by the large effect of the SSt1 solid stem allele. While the MTA outside of the SSt1 locus are all smaller effect, considering favorable alleles for selections could have implications for improved WSS resistance.

While the only overlapping locus between the CUT and SOLID models was the SSt1 locus, one limitation of this study is the use of breeding program data. Due to the nature of the dataset, all data used for the SOLID models and predictions is a sub-sample of a broader population that contains lines related to the WSS resistance portion of the breeding program, which heavily emphasizes the solid stem allele at the SSt1 locus. However, the data utilized for the CUT models includes lines not associated with WSS nurseries that were grown in areas that experience WSS infestation. Because of this, the CUT datasets were more representative of a broader wheat breeding program and contain many of the same lines with SOLID dataset, which means the results of these two models are not independent in terms of germplasm, only trait assessed. However, despite this overlap, these findings were still beneficial for understanding the need to utilizing multiple WSS resistance loci in breeding efforts for developing WSS resistant cultivars.

Conclusions

Overall, the findings of this study combined with the results of previous literature indicate that there are multiple WSS resistance MTA that are stable across populations and environments. This in combination with the AC evaluation show potential for the use of GBS markers to be used for marker assisted selection (MAS) in addition with other markers such as the SSt1 locus on chromosome 3BL to select superior ACs in breeding populations. Since the solid stem trait and WSS resistance are environmentally variable year to year, it is important for breeders to identify and implement additional loci that could confer more consistent resistance across years.
