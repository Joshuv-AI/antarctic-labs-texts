SQUAMOSA-Promoter Binding Protein Like 10/14 (OsSPL10/14) Regulate Salt Tolerance by Modulating Reactive Oxygen Species Homeostasis in Rice (Oryza Sativa L.)
Authors: Chaowei Fang, Yuanqing Nie, Minglan Zhu, Xinyi Yu, Liwen Jia, Jiayi Li, Xin Shu, Taotao Zhu, Weihong Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil salinization seriously impacts the growth and development, yield, and grain quality of crops. Elucidating the molecular underlying of salt tolerance is crucial for advancing stress-resistant molecular breeding research in crops. Here, we identified a salt stress-responsive gene, SQUAMOSA-promoter binding protein like 10 (OsSPL10), and characterized its molecular function in conferring salt tolerance to rice. Firstly, we generated and characterized three distinct types of spl10 mutants using CRISPR/Cas9 mutagenesis. In spl10 mutant, the leaf withered rate was lower than that in wild type (WT), the plant height and fresh weight per plant of spl10 were higher than WT under salt stress, indicating that spl10 exhibits tolerance to salt stress. Based on biochemical and physiological assays, the OsSPL10/14 complex was identified as a key regulator of salt stress response in rice by modulating the homeostasis of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Besides, the RNA-Seq assay confirmed that OsSPL10 may be involved in plant hormone signal transduction and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways under salt stress, providing valuable insights for exploring the downstream target genes of OsSPL10. These findings offer novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying salt tolerance mediated by the SPL transcription factor in plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-025-00878-1.Keywords: OsSPL10/14, Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, Plant hormone transduction, Rice, ROS homeostasis, Salt stress

Introduction

Soil salinization is a severely detrimental environmental stressor that impacts seed germination, crop growth, and agricultural productivity (Van Zelm et al. 2020). With rice being a staple food for more than half of the global population, rice is especially vulnerable to salt stress, which disrupts cellular homeostasis and impairs physiological processes. It is critical to understand the salt tolerance molecular mechanisms in rice for developing resilient cultivars capable of thriving in saline environments (Ullah et al. 2022, 2024). However, progress in molecular breeding has been slow, hampered by a scarcity of elite germplasm and key genetic determinants that balance growth and stress tolerance (Liang et al. 2024).

Exposure to a high-salinity environment would trigger a series of detrimental physiological responses in plants, including ionic toxicity, osmotic stress-induced imbalance, disruption of nutrient homeostasis, and accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Munns and Tester 2008; Deinlein et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2015). Osmotic stress represents the predominant challenge for plants in saline environments, which disrupting cellular water relations and reducing turgor pressure (Horie et al. 2011). Subsequently, excessive Na⁺ accumulation leads to ionic toxicity, which displaces essential ions like K⁺, impairs metabolic processes, and damages chloroplasts, constraining photosynthetic capacity (Van Zelm et al. 2020).

To cope, plants activate osmoregulatory defense mechanisms by de novo biosynthesis and compartmentalization of compatible solutes, such as proline and glycine betaine (Park et al. 2016; Mehta and Vyas 2023). Salinity exposure induces the overproduction of ROS in various plant cellular compartments, leading to the generation of cytotoxic radicals (Van Zelm et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2022). To counteract oxidative stress, plants orchestrate a sophisticated ROS scavenging network involving both enzymatic (e.g., SOD, APX, GR) and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems (You and Chan 2015; Li et al. 2019; Siddiqui et al. 2019; Goodarzi et al. 2020). Additionally, plants also enhance their stress resistance by means of hormonal regulation and precise control of gene expression. For example, abscisic acid (ABA) can induce stomatal closure in plants, thereby reducing water evaporation and regulating ionic balance as well as antioxidant defense mechanisms (Zhao et al. 2022; Jiang et al. 2024; Raghavendra et al. 2010).

The SQUAMOSA Promoter-Binding Protein-Like (SPL) gene family represents a phylogenetically conserved group of plant-specific transcription factors characterized by the signature SBP domain (He et al. 2022). The modular SBP domain contains three distinct structural motifs including a bipartite zinc finger architecture comprising twin Cys3His zinc-binding clusters, a conserved α-helical nuclear localization signal (NLS), and a flexible inter-zinc β-hairpin loop that mediates sequence-specific DNA recognition at GTAC core motifs through major groove interactions (Yamasaki et al. 2006; Cai et al. 2018). The SPL genes regulate various processes, including plant growth and development, secondary metabolism, signal transduction, and stress responses (Wang et al. 2009; Cai et al. 2018; He et al. 2024; Poethig and Fouracre 2024; Huang et al. 2025). Among the 19 SPL genes in rice, OsSPL2 affects the heading stage and negatively regulates the oxidative stress tolerance (Chen et al. 2014; Yue et al. 2017). OsSPL3 regulates the occurrence of adventitia by participating in auxin transport and signal transduction (Shao et al. 2019). OsSPL4 positively regulates rice blast resistance, promotes the expression of virus-related genes and increases the accumulation of H2O2 (Zhang et al. 2022). OsSPL9 regulates the antiviral ability of rice and enhances the absorption capacity of Cu (Tang et al. 2016; Yao et al. 2019). The ideal plant type genes OsSPL14/IPA1 negatively regulates the salt tolerance, drought tolerance, cold tolerance, and rice blast (Jia et al. 2022b; Wang et al. 2018). OsSPL7/14/17 synergistically regulates the length of rice stalks. Additionally, OsSPL14/17 regulates the elongation of rice roots under nitrate nitrogen nutrition (Sun et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2021). OsSPL10 affects trichome formation and negatively controls salt and drought stress (Lan et al. 2015; Li et al. 2023b). However, the molecular mechanism by which OsSPL10 regulates salt tolerance of rice under salt stress conditions remains unclear. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutagenesis seems to be an excellent method for functional analysis and characterization of OsSPL10 (Razalli et al. 2025).

In this study, we explore the function of OsSPL10 and its interaction with OsSPL14 in salt tolerance of rice. Using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutagenesis, we generated spl10 mutants and characterized their phenotypic and physiological response to salt stress. Furthermore, we investigated the molecular pathways by assessing ROS homeostasis and transcriptomic profiling. Our findings indicate that OsSPL10 negative control salt stress of rice along with OsSPL14 and provide new insights into molecular mechanisms of OsSPL10 and OsSPL14 underlying salt stress in rice.

Results

OsSPL10 Negatively Regulates Salt Tolerance

To explore the spatial-temporal expression pattern of OsSPL10 gene, qRT-PCR analysis was performed on various tissues and organs at seedings and reproductive growth stages. The results revealed that OsSPL10 was mainly expressed in roots and stems, but weakly expressed in developing seeds (Fig. 1A). To investigate whether OsSPL10 is response to salt stress, the 14-day-old seedlings were treated with nutrient solutions containing 120 mM NaCl. In roots, the expression of OsSPL10 was rapidly down-regulated upon treatment, but was subsequently up-regulated after 9 h of exposure by NaCl (Fig. 1B). This dynamic expression pattern suggests that OsSPL10 is involved in the early response to salt stress in rice.

To explore the function of OsSPL10, three mutant lines (spl10-1, spl10-2, and spl10-3, the spl10-3 was used further study, so, it was also named spl10 thereafter) were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology. In spl10-1, 1 bp (T) insertion was detected at position + 51 (Fig. 1C and Fig. S1A). In spl10-2 and spl10-3, 1 bp (T) and 2 bp (CG) deletion were detected at positions + 50 and + 51 ~ + 52 of the first exon of OsSPL10 gene, respectively. All three mutations resulted in the premature termination of OsSPL10 protein translation (Fig. S2). Under salt stress conditions in soil, the spl10 mutants exhibited better growth than WT (Fig. 1D). Moreover, the phenotypic investigated of 14-day seeding treated by 120 mM NaCl for 7-day revealed that WT plants showed severe wilting, whereas spl10 mutants maintained relatively normal growth. We employed the Standard Evaluation System (SES) for rice to quantitatively assess salt tolerance (Gregorio et al. 1997). The WT plants were scored as susceptible (SES = 7), characterized by severe leaf rolling and wilting, while the spl10 mutants were scored as tolerant (SES = 3), displaying only minor leaf symptoms (Fig. 1E and S1B). This was consistent with a significantly lower leaf withering rate in the mutants (Fig. 1E and F). Besides, the plant height and fresh weight per plant were significantly higher in the spl10 mutants than in WT (Fig. 1F). These results implying that OsSPL10 is a negative regulator of salt stress in rice.

OsSLP10 Interact with OsSPL14 in Rice

OsSPL14, a squamosa promoter binding protein-like transcription factor, has been previously reported to negatively regulates the salt tolerance of rice (Jia et al. 2022b). Given that OsSPL10 belongs to the same family and exhibits a similar negative regulatory function under salt stress, we hypothesized that OsSPL10 and OsSPL14 might physically interact to form a regulatory complex.

To test this hypothesis, we performed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H), luciferase complementation imaging (LCI), and bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays to validate this protein–protein interactions (PPIs). These results from all three experiments confirmed that OsSPL10 and OsSPL14 interact and form a complex in nucleus (Fig. 2A and C).

Furthermore, qPCR analysis showed that the expression of OsSPL14 was significantly reduced in WT under salt stress, and the spl10 mutation significantly increased OsSPL14 transcripts, however, it was no significantly difference in spl10 under salt stress or not (Fig. 2D). Suggesting that OsSPL14 might affect salt tolerance along with OsSPL10, synergistically (Li et al. 2021a).

Loss Function of OsSPL10 Leads to Less ROS Under Salt Stress

Salt stress is associated with the rapid production of ROS in plants (Yang and Guo 2018), In rice, OsSPL14, a target of miR529a, has been shown to negatively regulates the balance of ROS, and its upregulation reduces oxidative stress tolerance (Yue et al. 2017). Given the functional similarity between OsSPL10 and OsSPL14, we speculate that OsSPL10 might also participate in regulating ROS homeostasis under salt stress in rice.

In order to test the above conjecture, the NBT and DAB staining were performed to assess superoxide and hydrogen peroxide accumulation. The result showed that there was no obvious difference in O2− content in roots of WT and spl10 at control condition (Fig. 3A). However, after treatment with 120 mM NaCl, leaves of spl10 mutants exhibited markedly lighter NBT and DAB staining than those of WT (Fig. 3A), indicating lower ROS accumulation. Consistent with these observations, the malondialdehyde (MDA) content in shoots, a marker of oxidative damage, was increased significantly in both WT andspl10under salt stress, but was significantly lower in spl10 than in WT (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was significantly higher in spl10 mutants under salt stress (Fig. 3C). Consistently, the qPCR results showed that the catalase-related gene OsCatA (Li et al. 2021b), which function to eliminate H2O2 and maintain the ROS homeostasis, was highly expression in spl10 under salt stress (Fig. 3D). The ascorbate peroxidase-related genes OsAPX2 (Wu et al. 2018) and OsAPX8 (Hong et al. 2007; Jiang et al. 2016) were significantly downregulated in spl10 under salt stress (Fig. 3D). Collectively, those results demonstrate that OsSPL10 modulates salt stress by influencing in ROS homeostasis in rice.

RNA-Seq Analysis of WT and spl10 Under Salt Stress

To explore the biological function of OsSPL10, the RNA-Seq analysis was performed using the roots tissues of WT and spl10 under control and salt-stressed conditions. The samples of salt-treated for 6 h of WT and spl10 were designated as WT-salt and spl10-salt, respectively, while untreated controls were labeled as WT-control and spl10-control. The principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to evaluate the clustering of groups, the result showed that the excellent reproducibility among biological replicates and noticeable differences between WT and spl10 samples (P < 0.05). The PCA1 accounted for 51.83% of the variance, and PCA2 for 37.96% of the variance (Fig. 4A).

Differential gene expression analysis identified the highest number of DEGs in the WT-salt vs. WT-control comparison, with 898 upregulated and 837 downregulated genes. In contrast, the fewest DEGs were observed in spl10-salt vs. WT-control, with only 256 upregulated and 253 downregulated genes (Fig. 4B). All total of 27 DEGs were common to all four comparison groups (Fig. 4C).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that the 1095 DEGs in spl10-salt vs. WT-salt were primarily associated with response to stress in biological process (BP), cell in cellular component (CC), and oxygen binding in molecular function (MF) (Fig. 4D). DEGs from WT-salt vs. WT-control and spl10-salt vs. spl10-control were enriched in metabolic process (BP), while their CC and MF terms were consistent with those in spl10-salt vs. WT-salt (Fig. S3 and Fig. 4D). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that DEGs in spl10-salt vs. WT-salt were mainly involved in plant hormone signal transduction and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Fig. 4E). Both WT-salt vs. WT-control and spl10-salt vs. spl10-control were predominantly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction (Fig. S3).

Based on these findings, we conjecture that OsSPL10 might participate in plant hormone transduction and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways to regulate salt tolerance in rice. Moreover, plant hormone transduction and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways might also influence the ROS metabolism in plant under abiotic stress (Arnao and Hernández-Ruiz 2019; Li et al. 2023a; Demiwal et al. 2024).

To validate this hypothesis, we detected the expression of plant hormone transduction and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway-related genes in WT and spl10 under control and salt stress conditions. The heatmap showed that those 30 genes were classified into two clusters (I and II) based on gene expressions. Cluster I including nine highly expressed genes related to plant hormone transduction and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways, could be further divided into two subclusters: I-1 and I-2, which consist of three and six genes, respectively. Among them, OsCCR10 (LOC_Os02g56700) (Bang et al. 2022) and OsGH3-2 (LOC_Os01g55940) ( Du et al. [2012]) were related to abiotic stress (Fig. 5A). The expression patterns of those genes in Cluster I were also confirmed by qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 5B). The Cluster II were also divided into two subclusters: II-1 and II-2, which including four and seventeen genes (Fig. 5A). Implying that these genes may play important roles in salt tolerance of rice, which provided strong clues for the studying the downstream target genes of OsSPL10.

Combining all our findings together, we established a proposed working model of OsSPL10 and OsSPL14 synergistically regulate salt tolerance in rice (Fig. 6). Firstly, OsSPL10 physically interacts with OsSPL14 to form a heteromeric complex in the nucleus (Fig. 2). Under salt stress, the heteromeric complex modulates ROS homeostasis by activating SOD activity and the expression of the catalase gene OsCatA, and suppressing chloroplast ascorbate peroxidase genes OsAPX2 and OsAPX8, thereby reducing oxidative damage in spl10 mutants (Fig. 3). Transcriptome analysis reveals that OsSPL10 significantly influences the salt stress response by modulating genes involved in plant hormone signal transduction (e.g., OsGH3-2) and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (e.g., OsCCR10) pathways crucial for defense and adaptation (Figs. 4 and 5), which might influence the ROS homeostasis in rice, thus regulating the salt tolerance in spl10 plants.

Discussion

OsSPL10 Is a Negative Regulator of Salt Stress Response

SPL transcription factors are involved in the response to salt stress in plants. In Arabidopsis, AtSPL9 is ubiquitinated by AtHOS1 to modulate salinity and delay flowering (Jiao et al. 2024), and also mediates freezing tolerance by regulating the expression of AtCBF2 (Bang et al. 2022). In apple, mdm-MIR156a targets MdSPL13 to activating MdWRKY100, thereby enhancing salt tolerance (Ma et al. 2021). Similarly, ZaSPL21 promotes flowering and improves salt tolerance in transgenic Nicotiana benthamiana (Li et al. 2025), while CpSPL5 and CpSPL8 negatively regulate salt tolerance in codonopsis pilosula by inhibiting SOS pathway (Li et al. 2024). Overexpression of AhSPL5 in Arabidopsis can enhance salt tolerance by enhancing its ROS-scavenging capability and positively regulating the expression of stress-responsive genes (Sun et al. 2024). MsSPL8 improves the biomass yield and enhances salt and drought in alfalfa (Gou et al. 2018). OsSPL14 is dephosphorylated and degraded by OsMPK4, and improves the salt tolerance of rice, ultimately (Jia et al. 2022b).

In this study, we created the mutant of spl10 using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing strategy in cultivated japonica Xinfeng 2, which exhibited the similar phenotype with the previous study that spl10 mutant was salt tolerance (Fig. 1) (Lan et al. 2019). Under salt stress (120 mM NaCl), spl10 mutants exhibited significantly better growth, reduced leaf withering, higher plant height, and greater fresh weight compared to WT (Fig. 1D-F). This robust phenotypic advantage unequivocally positions OsSPL10 as a negative regulator of salt tolerance. The specific spatial-temporal expression pattern of OsSPL10, predominantly in roots and stems (Fig. 1A), sites critical for sensing and responding to soil salinity, and its rapid transcriptional downregulation followed by later upregulation upon salt exposure (Fig. 1B), further underscores its involvement in the early stress signaling cascade. This biphasic expression pattern is intriguing and suggests potential roles in both immediate stress perception and later adaptive or recovery phases, warranting further investigation into the specific triggers and regulators involved.

OsSPL10 Interacts with OsSPL14 To Form a Regulatory Complex in Regulating Salt Stress by Maintaining the ROS Homeostasis in Rice

Although the functions of OsSPL10 have been reported in rice, the regulatory mechanism to salt stress is not investigated (Lan et al. 2015; Li et al. 2023b). The functional similarity of OsSPL10 to the known salt tolerance negative regulator OsSPL14 (Jia et al. 2022b). Led us to hypothesize a physical and functional interaction. This hypothesis was strongly supported by three independent protein-protein interaction assays: Yeast Two-Hybrid (Y2H), Luciferase Complementation Imaging (LCI), and Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BiFC) (Fig. 2A-C). These results demonstrate that OsSPL10 and OsSPL14 physically interact, likely forming a heteromeric complex within the nucleus. This interaction provides a crucial mechanistic link, suggesting that their combined action, potentially as a transcriptional complex, is central to their negative regulatory role. While OsSPL14 expression was significantly reduced by salt stress in WT, this reduction was absent in the spl10 mutant, and its transcript levels were constitutively higher (Fig. 2D). This indicates that OsSPL10 influences OsSPL14 transcript abundance, likely indirectly as OsSPL10 is an activating transcription factor) (Li et al. 2023b). The data suggest synergistic action where OsSPL10 modulates the expression or stability of the OsSPL10/14 complex components, rather than OsSPL14 being a direct downstream target of OsSPL10. This complex formation represents a key node in the salt stress regulatory network.

Salt stress invariably leads to the accumulation of damaging ROS in plants. Given the established link between OsSPL14 and ROS balance (Yue et al. 2017) and the interaction between OsSPL10 and OsSPL14, we investigated ROS dynamics in the spl10 mutant. Under salt stress, spl10 mutants exhibited significantly reduced accumulation of superoxide (O₂⁻) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in leaves compared to WT, as visualized by NBT and DAB staining, respectively (Fig. 3A). This was accompanied by lower levels of Malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker for membrane lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage, in spl10 leaves (Fig. 3B). Crucially, spl10 mutants displayed significantly higher activity of Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), a key enzyme detoxifying O₂⁻ (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, qPCR analysis revealed enhanced expression of the catalase gene OsCatA (Li et al. 2021b), responsible for H₂O₂ decomposition, in salt-stressed spl10 (Fig. 3D). Conversely, expression of ascorbate peroxidase genes OsAPX2 and OsAPX8 (Wu et al. 2018; Jiang et al. 2016), involved in the ascorbate-glutathione cycle for H₂O₂ scavenging, was downregulated in spl10 under stress (Fig. 3D). This coordinated response–increased SOD activity and OsCatA expression coupled with reduced APX expression and lower ROS accumulation – demonstrates that loss of OsSPL10 function enhances the antioxidant capacity in plants, particularly bolstering SOD/CAT-mediated ROS detoxification. This efficient management of ROS-induced oxidative damage is a major contributing factor to the observed salt tolerance in spl10 mutants.

OsSPL10 Might Participate in Plant Hormone Transduction and Phenylpropanoid Biosynthesis Pathways Under Salt Stress

Plant hormones are small chemicals that play a crucial role in plant growth and development, each plant hormone plays sophisticated and efficient roles at different growth and development status, and different environmental conditions (Ku et al. 2018; Cortleven et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2020). In plants, phytohormones are closely related to salt stress. For example, the ABA signaling gates salt-induced responses of plant roots and involved in regulation of osmosis, ions, and ROS under salt stress (Umezawa et al. 2009; Golldack et al. 2014; Yu et al. 2020), auxin-mediated root growth plasticity in response to salt stress (Liu et al. 2015). Indole-3-acetic-acid (IAA) can mitigate NaCl toxicity by regulating endogenous secondary metabolites and Ascorbate-Glutathione (AsA-GSH) Cycle (Kang et al. 2019; Vimal et al. 2023). Besides, the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway is a major secondary metabolic pathway in the salt response of plants (Jia et al. 2022a). Here, we confirmed that OsSPL10 might be involved in the processes of plant hormone transduction and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis to regulate salt stress by KEGG enrichment analysis based on RNA-Seq data (Fig. 4E).

Validation of specific genes within these pathways by qRT-PCR confirmed distinct expression clusters (Fig. 5A, B). Cluster I contained genes highly expressed in spl10-salt compared to WT-salt, including key players like OsCCR10 (Cinnamoyl-CoA reductase, involved in lignin biosynthesis-phenylpropanoid pathway) (Bang et al. 2022) and OsGH3-2 (an IAA-amido synthetase modulating auxin homeostasis - hormone pathway) (Du et al., 2012), both linked to abiotic stress tolerance. The upregulation of phenylpropanoid pathway genes in the tolerant spl10 mutant under stress suggests enhanced production of compounds like lignin (for strengthening cell walls) and flavonoids (acting as antioxidants and signaling molecules). The modulation of hormone signaling genes (OsGH3-2) indicates that OsSPL10 likely influences salt tolerance by interfering with hormone balance (e.g., auxin), known to be crucial for growth-defense trade-offs under stress. Cluster II genes, showing lower expression in spl10-salt, represent another set potentially repressed by the loss of OsSPL10 to promote tolerance. These DEG clusters provide strong candidates for downstream effectors mediating negative regulatory role of OsSPL10 in salt tolerance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified the molecular mechanism of the rice SQUAMOSA Promoter-Binding Protein-Like (SPL) gene OsSPL10. Loss-of-function mutants exhibit significantly enhanced salt tolerance phenotypes. Besides, OsSPL10 physically interacts with another SPL protein, OsSPL14, forming a likely heteromeric complex in the nucleus. OsSPL10 negatively modulates ROS homeostasis under salt stress, primarily by increasing SOD activity and the expression of the catalase gene OsCatA in spl10 mutant, leading to increased oxidative damage in WT plants. Transcriptome analysis reveals that OsSPL10 significantly influences the salt stress response by modulating genes involved in plant hormone signal transduction (e.g., OsGH3-2) and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (e.g., OsCCR10), pathways crucial for defense and adaptation. The OsSPL10/OsSPL14 complex acts as a regulatory hub, integrating control over ROS scavenging systems, hormone signaling, and secondary metabolism to exert its negative effect on salt tolerance. These findings not only advance our understanding of SPL-mediated stress regulation but also provide valuable genetic targets and conceptual frameworks for improving crop salinity tolerance through molecular breeding.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

The wild type (WT) of Xinfeng 2, the cultivate japonica rice, was used in this study. All the plants were grown at the experimental stations of Henan Normal University in Xinxiang of Henan province, China. Tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) plants were grown in the light incubator with 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness at 22 ℃.

Plasmid Construction and Rice Transformation

The Oryza sativa L. ssp. Japonica cultivar Xinfeng 2, which was a widely cultivated variety in Henan province of China, served as the genetic background and wild type (WT). For targeted mutagenesis of OsSPL10, the CRISPR/Cas9 plasmid was constructed using the pC1300-UBI: Cas9 vector as described previously (Fang et al. 2025). For assembly of one gRNA, the target was designed in the first exon (OsSPL10 target GAGTCGCCGTTCCCGTTCG) (Table S1). Then, the fragment was cloned into the pC1300-UBI: Cas9 vector digested with Eco31I to construct the knockout vector Cas9-OsSPL10. The Cas9-OsSPL10 vector was transformed into rice Xinfeng 2 via Agrobacterium-mediated method. The transformant detection and knockout transformant determination using PCR and DNA sequencing methods. All primers used in this study were listed in Table S1.

Plant Materials and Experimental Treatments

The phenotypic characterization and most experiments were conducted using homozygous T₂ generation mutants. This ensures the stability of the mutant phenotype and the absence of segregation. The seedings used were propagated from confirmed homozygous T₁ plants.

The Xinfeng 2 and spl10 seeds were disinfected with 75% ethanol and 0.5% NaClO, and then pregerminated in a biochemical incubator at 28 ℃ for 16 h. The pregerminated seeds were grown in different dispose for 3 days. The germinated seeds were selected using tweezers and evenly distributed onto a seedling box, and then placed in 1 L of rice nutrient solution (the formula of rice nutrient solution refers to Table S2). The growth conditions were: light intensity of 6000 lx, light 16 h and darkness 8 h one day at maintained 28 ℃ with 60% humidity maintained.

For salt stress treatment, the 14-day-old seedings were treated by nutrient solution containing 120 mM NaCl. After 7 days, the plant height, and fresh weight per plant were investigated. A leaf was classified as withered if the withered area accounted for half or more of the total leaf area. The leaf withering rate was calculated as follows: (Number of withered leaves / Total number of leaves) × 100%. The methods refer to references Zhao et al. 2025.

Quantitative Real- Time PCR (qPCR) Analysis

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham). The reverse transcription (RT) reaction was performed according to the protocol of the RT system (TransGen, Beijing, China). qPCR was conducted on a QuantStudio 5 Real- Time PCR system (ABI, Foster, California) using TB Green™; Premix EX Tag™; (TakaRa, Kusatsu, Japan), three technical replicates and three biological replicates were performed on each sample, and the data were analyzed by using the 2−△△Ct method; each data point represents the mean from three replicates ± SD. OsActin1 (LOC_Os03g50885) was used as the internal controls. The primers used for qPCR analysis are listed in Table S1.

RNA-Seq Analysis

For RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis, three independent biological replicates for each sample of the 14-day-old seedlings roots treated with 120 mM salt for 6 h of WT and spl10 (WT-control, WT-salt, spl10-control, spl10-salt), each biological replicate consisted of root tissues pooled from multiple seedlings were carried out by Oebiotech, Shanghai, China. Poly(A) RNA was enriched from DNase-treated total RNA using Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA kits. Libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150-bp paired-end; ~30 million reads per sample). Raw reads were quality-checked (FastQC) and trimmed (Trimmomatic). Clean reads were aligned to the Oryza sativa reference genome (IRGSP-1.0) using HISAT2. Transcript assembly and quantification were performed with StringTie. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (∣log < sub > 2</sub > FoldChange∣ ≥ 1, FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05). Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted via clusterProfiler for GO terms and KEGG pathways.

Diaminobenzidine (DAB) and Nitroblue Tetrazolium (NBT)Staining

Clipping 3 cm of 14-day-old seedlings leaves of both control group and those treated with 120 mM NaCl for 7 d of WT and spl10, then, soaked in a dye solution containing 0.1 mg/mL DAB or 1 mg/mL NBT (pH 3.8). The leaves staining last 10 min for DAB while 60 min for NBT. Subsequently, faded the dye was removed solution using 95% ethanol. Then, the leaves of treated were also soaked in dye solution staining for 8–12 h and decolored in boiled ethanol (95%) until the green color was faded completely. The staining was then observed under a stereomicroscope.

Y2H Assay

The matchmaker GAL4 Two-Hybrid System (Clontech) was employed to investigate protein-protein interactions (PPIs). The full-length coding sequences of OsSPL10 and OsSPL14 were cloned into pGADT7 and pGBKT7 vectors, respectively. Co-transformation of a pGADT7/prey plasmid and a pGBKT7/bait plasmid was performed in yeast strain Y2H GOLD and verified on selective media according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Negative control vectors, pGADT7-T7 and pGBKT7-lam, were used. Subsequently, vector co-transformation into yeast strains and yeast screening were conducted following the methods described in a previous study (An et al. 2020). Primers used for Y2H plasmid construction are shown in Table S1.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) Assay

To investigate the PPIs, we employed the bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay. The full-length coding sequences (CDSs) of OsSPL10 and OsSPL14 were fused with the pXYc104 and pXYn106 vectors, respectively. Then, the recombinant constructs were transformed into the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain AH105. Then, injecting tobacco leaves, after 48 h, visualizing the YFP fluorescence using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8) with excitation at 514 nm and emission detection at 525–565 nm. Primers used for BiFC plasmid construction are shown in Table S1.

Luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) Assay

The CDSs of OsSPL10 and OsSPL14 were cloned and recombined into pCAMBIA1300-35 S-nLUC and pCAMBIA1300-35 S-cLUC, respectively. Then, the recombinant constructs were transformed into the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 (pSoup-p19). D-luciferin solution (Promega, Cat. No: E1602) was sprayed onto the leaves of N. benthamiana to detect luciferase activity. Images were captured with a low-light cooled CCD imaging apparatus (NightOWL II LB983 with Indigo software). Primers used for LCI plasmid construction are listed in Table S1.
