Time‐Continuous Prognostic Mapping of Bell's Palsy Using Yanagihara Scores and Electroneurography
Authors: Shogo N. Watanabe, Shumpei Hisamoto, Kazuo Yasuhara, Kenji Kondo, Hironobu Nishijima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveBell's palsy impairs social functioning. Conventional prognostic assessment relies on clinical grading and electroneurography (ENoG), but predictive accuracy remains limited. This study introduces a time‐continuous probabilistic approach that integrates longitudinal Yanagihara scores and ENoG to enable dynamic, individualized prognostic estimation.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective longitudinal study of patients with Bell's palsy treated between April 2013 and March 2024. Eligible patients were aged ≥ 15 years, hospitalized within 1 week of onset, and received high‐dose corticosteroids. Exclusion criteria included other etiologies, prior facial palsy, facial nerve decompression, treatment discontinuation, or incomplete data. Recovery was defined as a Yanagihara score ≥ 36, and nonrecovery as failure to achieve this within ≥ 6 months. ENoG values obtained within 4 weeks of onset were stratified into five groups. Multivariable logistic regression was performed using ENoG, sex, age, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, body mass index, and HbA1C as covariates. Logistic curves were fitted to longitudinal scores, and Monte Carlo/Bayesian procedures were applied to generate recovery probability maps.ResultsAmong 128 eligible patients, ENoG emerged as the only significant predictor of recovery. The probability maps provided a novel visualization of individualized prognosis at any time point. This method estimated recovery likelihood even in patients with follow‐up intervals, addressing irregular limitations of prior fixed‐timepoint models. Conditional mutual information plateaued around 50 days, indicating stabilization of predictive discrimination beyond that period.ConclusionIncorporating longitudinal Yanagihara scores obtained during outpatient follow‐up enhances recovery prediction accuracy. This practical, dynamic approach facilitates intuitive risk visualization and supports improved prognostic counseling in clinical practice.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

The facial nerve (cranial nerve VII) controls movements of the mimetic muscles and, therefore, plays a crucial role in social interaction and communication. Idiopathic facial nerve palsy (Bell's palsy) accounts for approximately 70% of all peripheral facial palsy cases [1]. The severity of facial dysfunction is typically graded using the House–Brackmann and Sunnybrook systems, whereas in Japan, the Yanagihara scoring system is widely applied. The Yanagihara system provides a region‐specific assessment of 10 distinct facial movements, enabling quantitative and reproducible evaluation of recovery [2]. Electroneurography (ENoG), the nerve excitability test, and the blink reflex are standard modalities for assessing facial nerve degeneration [4]. Among these, ENoG is particularly valuable for detecting early degeneration and predicting prognosis [5]. However, even patients with comparable ENoG values often demonstrate divergent recovery courses, indicating that single‐timepoint measures have limited prognostic accuracy. Traditional predictive approaches, such as day‐30 outcome estimation using the House–Brackmann or Sunnybrook scales, offer only moderate discriminative capacity [6, 7]. Yet few investigations have examined time‐continuous statistical models capable of characterizing dynamic recovery trajectories under standardized treatment conditions.

To address this gap, the present study developed a probabilistic framework that generates time‐continuous recovery Probability Maps, modeling functional restoration as a joint function of time and baseline ENoG‐defined degeneration. In contrast to conventional categorical prediction schemes, this data‐driven approach estimates the likelihood of facial motor improvement across individual trajectories. By integrating serial Yanagihara scores with early ENoG data, the model visualizes temporal recovery probabilities, providing a personalized, statistically robust tool for prognostic assessment in Bell's palsy.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This retrospective longitudinal study examined the associations among Yanagihara scores, ENoG values, and recovery outcomes in patients with Bell's palsy. Medical records of individuals who presented to the Department of Otorhinolaryngology at Takeda General Hospital between April 1, 2013, and March 31, 2024, were reviewed. Patients were included if their primary complaint was facial nerve palsy and they met predefined eligibility criteria. Eligible patients were aged ≥ 15 years, hospitalized within 7 days of symptom onset, and treated with high‐dose corticosteroid therapy (≥ 120 mg prednisolone‐equivalent). In selected cases, antiviral agents such as valaciclovir or amenamevir were administered at the treating physician's discretion. Antiviral therapy was initiated within 1 week of symptom onset in all treated patients. Amenamevir was administered at 400 mg/day for 7 days, and valaciclovir at 1000 mg/day for 5 days.

Exclusion criteria comprised facial palsy secondary to other etiologies, treatment delay exceeding 7 days, previous history of facial palsy, surgical treatment (e.g., facial nerve decompression), treatment discontinuation, or incomplete medical records.

For each eligible patient, demographic variables (age, sex), affected side, medical history, onset date, first‐visit date, initiation of corticosteroid therapy, and hospitalization duration were documented. ENoG results and serial Yanagihara scores were extracted from the initial visit through the final follow‐up. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Takeda General Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Owing to its retrospective design and use of anonymized data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Assessment by the Yanagihara Facial Nerve Grading System

Facial nerve function was evaluated using the Yanagihara facial nerve grading system [2]. At baseline, patients were categorized by severity as mild (≥ 20 points), moderate (12–18 points), or severe (≤ 10 points) according to their initial Yanagihara score. Follow‐up evaluations were performed during regular outpatient visits using the same assessment method. Recovery was defined as achieving a Yanagihara score ≥ 36, whereas non‐recovery was defined as failure to reach this score after ≥ 6 months of observation. All assessments were performed by otolaryngologists experienced in facial nerve evaluation to minimize interobserver variability.

ENoG Assessment of Neural Degeneration

ENoG was performed within 4 weeks after the onset of Bell's palsy (mean, 15.6 days; median, 16 days) to evaluate the extent of facial nerve degeneration. Following established neurophysiologic protocols, a supramaximal electrical stimulus was applied at the stylomastoid foramen to elicit compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) from the orbicularis oris on both sides. Surface electrodes were placed symmetrically, and the maximal biphasic CMAP amplitude was recorded bilaterally. The ENoG value was defined as the amplitude ratio of the affected side to the healthy side, expressed as a percentage, as described previously [8].

Based on prior prognostic research, patients were categorized into five groups according to ENoG values: Group 1, ENoG ≥ 40%; Group 2, < 40% and ≥ 20%; Group 3, < 20% and ≥ 10%; Group 4, < 10% and > 0%; and Group 5, 0%.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Multivariable logistic regression was performed using the glm function with a binomial distribution and logit link to identify factors independently associated with recovery status, defined as recovery (1) or nonrecovery (0). The baseline ENoG value was the primary explanatory variable. Age, sex, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) were included as covariates to adjust for potential confounders. Statistical significance was set at a two‐sided p < 0.05.

Construction of the Probability Map

For each ENoG group, individual Yanagihara scores were plotted against days since onset to depict longitudinal trajectories of facial motor recovery. A nonlinear regression model was applied to fit these data, yielding logistic curves that characterized the evolution of Yanagihara scores over time in both recovery and nonrecovery subgroups. Parameter estimates from the fitted model were used to generate 2000 parameter sets via Monte Carlo sampling. Within each subgroup, these simulated parameters were used to calculate the probability of observing a given Yanagihara score on a specific day since onset. The resulting probabilities were combined with empirical prior probabilities based on subgroup proportions to compute conditional probabilities of recovery. These were visualized as two‐dimensional probability maps (days since onset × Yanagihara score), representing the relative probability of recovery, defined as the posterior probability derived by comparing the recovery and nonrecovery models for each observed day‐score pair. This relative probability is distinct from the marginal probability of observing a given Yanagihara score on a given day and should not be interpreted as a measure of score occurrence. Patients in Group 5 (ENoG 0%) were excluded from this analysis because of consistently poor outcomes.

Using these probability maps, day‐wise conditional mutual information was computed to quantify the discriminative ability of Yanagihara scores at each time point. This measure captured the information gain contributed by observed scores regarding recovery status, thereby identifying critical temporal windows for prognostic assessment.

Detailed descriptions of logistic‐curve parameter derivation, Monte Carlo sampling procedures, and information‐theoretic computations for probability‐map generation are provided in the Supporting Information.

Results

Patient Characteristics

The selection process from the collected data to the final study cohort is presented in Figure 1. A total of 436 patient records were reviewed. Among these, 268 patients met the inclusion criteria of being aged ≥ 15 years, hospitalized within 7 days of onset, and treated with high‐dose corticosteroid therapy. After applying the exclusion criteria, 140 patients were removed, leaving 128 patients in the final analysis cohort. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 2. At presentation, 13% of patients were classified as mild, 21% as moderate, and 66% as severe based on the initial Yanagihara score. The affected side was the right in 52% and the left in 48%. The sex distribution was 53% male and 47% female. Patient ages ranged from 16 to 87 years (mean, 57 years). Potential clinical confounders related to Bell's palsy, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, BMI, diabetes mellitus, and HbA1c, and antiviral therapy were recorded and incorporated as covariates in the multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify recovery predictors.

Probability Map of Prognosis in Facial Nerve Palsy

To characterize longitudinal changes in Yanagihara scores and their relationship to recovery probability, advanced statistical modeling was applied to aggregated data and visualized as probability maps. For each group, days since onset were plotted on the x‐axis and Yanagihara scores on the y‐axis, with recovery probability at each day‐score combination represented by a color‐coded probability map (Figure 3A–D). Fitted curves illustrated temporal score trajectories for both recovery and non‐recovery groups, revealing progressive separation over time that reflected increasing discriminative ability between outcomes.

Because non‐recovery cases typically maintained low scores, a distinct non‐recovery region appeared in the lower‐right area of each map. Lower ENoG levels were associated with the requirement for earlier improvement to achieve recovery. In Figure 3C,D, an area of low recovery probability was observed despite high Yanagihara scores observed at very early post‐onset days. This pattern likely resulted from a low prior proportion of the recovery group, minimal early‐phase score divergence between groups, and greater variance in non‐recovery data compared with recovery data. However, in low‐ENoG groups, the probability of observing high Yanagihara scores at very early time points was minimal. Figure 3E displays the conditional mutual information between recovery probability and Yanagihara score, computed day‐wise since onset, for each ENoG group. In all groups, this metric plateaued around day 50, indicating that beyond this point, the Yanagihara score's ability to discriminate recovery status stabilized.

We present two complementary perspectives on recovery probability in Figure 4. First, recovery probability was plotted as a function of the Yanagihara score at fixed time points of 30, 60, and 90 days after onset (Figure 4A–C). Overall, as more days elapsed, higher Yanagihara scores were required to achieve recovery. When recovery probability was considered relative to score at each time point, Groups 1 and 2 maintained high recovery probabilities even at lower scores despite increasing time, whereas Groups 3 and 4 required progressively higher scores to sustain favorable outcomes (Figure 4A–C). Second, recovery probability was plotted over time at fixed Yanagihara scores of 10, 20, and 30 (Figure 4D–F). The probability of recovery decreased as the time required to attain each score increased. At a fixed score of 10, favorable recovery probabilities were observed only when the score was achieved within 20 days, after which they declined across all groups. For a fixed score of 20, Groups 1 and 2 retained high recovery probability up to approximately 50 days, whereas in Groups 3 and 4, achieving a score of 20 within 30–50 days was advantageous; further delay substantially reduced recovery probability. Achieving a Yanagihara score of 30 predicted high recovery across all groups (Figure 4D–F).

Discussion

In this study, we aggregated longitudinal Yanagihara scores up to recovery within strata defined by baseline ENoG values and constructed time‐continuous probability maps of recovery, an approach not previously reported. Earlier studies have primarily relied on ROC analyses at fixed time points and Kaplan–Meier methods, which are difficult to apply in clinical practice because of substantial variability in patients' visit timing [9, 10]. By integrating early ENoG with Yanagihara scores obtained at any clinical visit, our framework provides a practical and precise estimation of individualized recovery probability.

When examining the longitudinal courses of Yanagihara scores within each ENoG‐defined group, recovery and nonrecovery trajectories diverged even among patients in the same stratum (Figure 2). Several mechanisms may contribute to this divergence. First, measurement variability in ENoG: prior studies have reported 6%–20% test–retest variation, which may lead to under‐ or overestimation of neural damage [11, 12]. Second, differences in ENoG timing: recommended assessment windows range from 3 to 7 days to as late as 30 days after onset; testing too early precedes completion of Wallerian degeneration, while testing too late overlaps early regeneration, complicating interpretation [4, 13, 14]. Third, intrinsic limitations of CMAP‐based ENoG: CMAP amplitude represents the number of functioning axons but does not directly reflect fascicular injury severity [15]. Values below 40% imply endoneurial involvement, where progressive perineural damage promotes miswiring and fibrosis, thereby reducing recovery probability [16]. These factors collectively explain the within‐stratum divergence of recovery trajectories.

Multiple factors have been variably linked to recovery in Bell's palsy, such as age and diabetes mellitus, but a consistent consensus remains lacking [17, 18] While ENoG is widely recognized as valuable for prognostication, it is insufficient when used alone [5, 19]. Consistent with this, our multivariable analysis identified ENoG as the only significant predictor of recovery, with no other covariate showing statistical significance (Table 4).

Given these uncertainties, we combined ENoG data with longitudinal Yanagihara scores to construct interpretable, time‐continuous maps representing recovery probability at any time point. We further quantified day‐wise conditional mutual information to measure prognostic discriminability over time (Figure 3). This framework enables identification of patients at increasing risk of non‐recovery as time progresses, potentially guiding individualized treatment intensification, including targeted rehabilitation strategies [20].

We summarized the probability maps by plotting recovery likelihood at fixed days and, conversely, over time at fixed scores (Figure 4). Groups 1–2 maintained high recovery probability at a given score despite delayed improvement, whereas Groups 3–4 required earlier gains; persistent non‐recovery in these groups likely reflects fibrosis or aberrant reinnervation [21]. These findings support the hypothesis that axon‐limited injuries recover earlier, while perineurial involvement slows repair and reduces overall recovery potential [22].

This study has some limitations. First, it was a single‐center retrospective analysis without external validation; however, the cohort comprised patients treated under a uniform protocol within one region, enhancing internal consistency and minimizing clinical variability. Second, restricting inclusion to inpatients receiving high‐dose corticosteroids, together with real‐world variation in ENoG timing and follow‐up intervals, may have introduced selection bias and reduced statistical power to detect weaker associations. Nevertheless, overall recovery rates and multivariable results paralleled previous reports, supporting cohort validity [23, 24]. Third, from a neurophysiologic standpoint, ENoG assessment within 21 days of onset may be preferable; however, to preserve the robustness of our statistical analyses and to broaden the clinical applicability of this framework, we allowed ENoG measurements up to 28 days after onset. The main strength of this study lies in integrating patient‐level longitudinal data with information‐theoretic modeling to generate visit‐agnostic, time‐continuous probability maps. Compared with prognostication based solely on ENoG, this approach provides more accurate individualized estimates and enhances the interpretability of recovery probability. Because it is independent of fixed visit timing, the method is readily adaptable to everyday clinical otolaryngologic practice and may improve prognostic counseling for patients with Bell's palsy.

Conclusion

We developed time‐continuous, visit‐agnostic probability maps that estimate recovery trajectories from longitudinal Yanagihara system scores, stratified by baseline ENoG values. Compared with prognostication based solely on baseline ENoG, incorporating visit‐specific Yanagihara scores enhances predictive precision. Because these estimates are independent of fixed days since onset, the approach is easily adaptable to routine clinical practice and scalable across institutions. By visualizing individualized recovery probability, this method may also improve patient understanding and engagement. Collectively, the proposed framework offers a practical, clinically applicable tool to support prognostic counseling for patients with Bell's palsy.
