CHINTEXDB-PERU28: A unique dataset of traditional textile iconographies from Chinchero, Peru for cultural preservation and image recognition
Authors: Gabriela Farfan Enriquez, Ruben Ancco Peralta, Edwin Jonathan Escobedo Cárdenas, Hernan Nina Hanco, Lauro Enciso Rodas, Carlos Fernando Montoya Cubas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This dataset was collected during on-site fieldwork conducted in the district of Chinchero, located in the province of Urubamba, Cusco, Peru, a region internationally recognized for its rich Andean textile tradition rooted in Inca Culture heritage. The dataset comprises high-quality Photographic images of traditional handwoven Andean textile iconographies produced by local artisan communities. These images were captured directly at textile centers where the fabrics are woven, dyed and finished using ancestral techniques measuring authentic representation of colors, textures, and symbolic patterns under natural and controlled conditions.The dataset consists of 1358 images organized into 28 distinct classes, each corresponding to a specific textile iconography characteristic of the Chinchero tradition. The images are provided in a processed and curated format, facilitating organization enables systematic analysis of visual motifs that are often challenging to distinguish due to their intricate geometric patterns and cultural symbolism.The primary reuse potential of this dataset lies in its application to Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) research focused on image classification, pattern recognition, and cultural heritage preservation. Researchers can leverage the dataset to develop and evaluate models capable of identifying and differentiating traditional Andean textile iconographies, addressing the growing difficulty faced by younger generations, local communities, and visitors in recognizing the cultural expressions. Additionally, the dataset supports interdisciplinary research in digital humanities, ethnography, textile studies, and cultural informatics contributing to the documentation and preservation of intangible cultural heritage.By making this dataset publicly available, this work aims to support the development of AI-driven tools for cultural preservation, educational applications, and heritage awareness, while fostering collaboration between researchers, technologists, and local artisan communities to safeguard ancestral knowledge for future generations.

Value of the Data

•This is among the first structured datasets focused on Andean textile iconographies from the Cusco region. Existing documentation on these iconographies is scarce and often superficial. Hence, this dataset fills a critical gap and serves as a key digital resource for future cultural heritage applications.•This open-access resource enables researchers to develop and test emerging machine learning and deep learning algorithms for automatic classification of iconographies with varying contours and spatial representations, supporting subsequent processes such as image preprocessing, data augmentation, feature extraction, supervision learning, and robustness evaluation under realistic acquisition conditions.•The CHINTEXDB-PERU28 dataset provides a valuable benchmark for computer vision models focused on pattern recognition in Andean textile iconographies.•It supports the design, evaluation, and comparison of algorithms aimed at textile image classification and cultural pattern identification, including tasks such as model training performance benchmarking, and comparative evaluation of different architectures and learning strategies, offering the scientific community the opportunity to evaluate the effectiveness of their algorithms and models.•Beyond its technical applications, the dataset contributes to the preservation and revalorization of Andean cultural identity through the digitization and study of iconographies.•The dataset is also relevant for academic and educational institutions in fields such as textile and fashion design, creative industries, anthropology, art education, and heritage preservation, promoting interdisciplinary research through artificial intelligence techniques such as machine learning and deep learning.

Background

Textile art is one of the richest and most ancient cultural expressions of the Andean region, particularly in the district of Chinchero, Cusco, Peru. This town is recognized for its tradition in the elaboration of textiles using ancestral techniques transmitted from generation to generation [1]. Garments such as the lliclla (women's blanket or shawl) have persisted since Inca times [2]. Chinchero textiles are especially notable for their natural dyeing obtained from natural dyes [3], and for the meticulous preparation of camelid fibers [2]. These complex iconographies are not merely decorative; they contain profound meanings related to cultural identity, spiritual beliefs, and the Andean worldview (cosmovision), which remains closely tied to the Quechua language and ancestral traditions [4].

Across the world, textile iconographies hold significant cultural value. In Chinchero, Cusco, 32 distinct iconographies have officially registered [5], each one with its own meaning for the local community. Historically, in the absence of written language, these visual patterns served as a medium to transmit stories and knowledge, preserved through oral tradition across generations.

Previous research has proposed technological approaches for cultural preservation and identification in other countries [6]. For instance, [7] explored the real-time digitization of textile iconographies to verify the authenticity of materials, whether handwoven or machine-made.

To address the absence of similar resources for Peruvian textiles, we proposed a dataset consisting of images from 28 distinct textile iconographies. We documented each type from several angles, under various lighting conditions, and in diverse real-world settings within the textile centers of Chinchero. This dataset provides a valuable resource for researchers aiming to develop, test, or improve methods for analyzing and authenticating textile iconographies in different contexts as in [8].

Data Description

CHINTEXDB-PERU28 is a dataset composed of 1358 images, all organized within a single main folder. Each image follows the naming structure “#_Iconography”, where # represents a sequential number from 0001 to 1358. The images were captured from various angles, scenarios, and lighting conditions, and no duplicates are included. To support machine learning workflows, the dataset is subdivided into three files: “train”, “valid”, and “test”. In addition, an alternative version of the dataset is provided in a unified structure containing only two folders: “image” and “labels”, allowing users to define custom data partitions according to their specific research needs. Each subset is organized into 28 distinct iconographic classes. All 1358 images are provided in JPG format. Moreover, we applied manual labelling when >50 % of the iconographic motif appeared in the image, and we used bounding boxes to isolate each individual pattern. The complete list of the 28 iconographies is presented in Table 1. Furthermore, all images have high resolution, exceeding 96 dpi, with varying pixel dimensions in width and height. The uncompressed dataset occupies approximately 2.20 GB. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The process starts with selecting textiles at interpretation centers and Sunday fairs in Chinchero. After confirming that each textile includes at least one iconography, high-resolution photographs are taken and safely stored. The workflow ends with manual labeling on the Roboflow platform and systematic organization of the dataset. The main stages of the dataset collection workflow are summarized below.

Visit to the textile centers and Sunday fairs

We visit textile interpretation centers and Sunday fairs in Chinchero to access a diverse range of handcrafted textiles. At each center, we meet with artisans who explain the history, symbolism, and techniques behind their work. The centers provide live demonstrations of spinning, dyeing with natural pigments, and weaving, allowing us to observe each step of the process. Artisans highlight the importance of native materials, such as alpaca and sheep wool, and explain the significance of traditional iconographies woven into the textiles. During our visits to Sunday fairs, we interact directly with local weavers and examine a wide selection of finished pieces. These fairs serve as important community events where artisans present and sell their textiles, offering us the opportunity to gather a representative and authentic sample for our dataset.

Textile selection

We randomly select textiles, making sure that each specimen contains at least one of the 28 iconographies of interest. Before choosing each piece, we carefully review the textile to identify and confirm the presence of relevant motifs. This process often involves discussing with artisans to better understand the symbolism and cultural context of each design. By following this thorough approach, we assemble a representative and culturally rich collection of traditional patterns. This careful selection ensures that the dataset captures a broad range of iconographies, supporting both detailed pattern analysis and effective model training. Fig. 3 illustrates the workflow for collecting the Iconographies dataset. We begin with visits to textile stores, select samples representing 28 different classes of textiles, and capture original photographs of each one. After photographing, we manually label and organize the images into folders and subfolders by iconography class. This systematic process results in a well-structured and accessible image dataset, ready for analysis and future research.Fig. 3Workflow for the seamless collection of the Iconographies dataset.Fig 3 dummy alt text

Photography and storage

The dataset was collected using three devices to guarantee high-quality and consistent image resolution: a Huawei P Smart smartphone, a HONOR Magic5 Lite smartphone, and a Nikon Coolpix P520 professional camera. Textile center managers in Chinchero, Peru, supervised the image captures to ensure proper handling and ethical compliance. The images come primarily from traditional textiles such as llicllas, shawls, and pasamesas, sourced from local textile centers. The dataset includes 28 distinct iconography classes, representing a diverse and culturally rich sample of handcrafted textile designs.

Images were captured following a strict protocol to guarantee both quality and data integrity. After each photography session, images were immediately transferred to an external hard drive for backup, then uploaded to a Google Drive folder for secure cloud storage and easy access for processing. New image sets were collected only after confirming that previous files had been safely backed up and organized. All images are in JPG format, with resolutions including 4608×3456, 3264×2448, 4160×3120, 1944×2592, 3672×4896, and 2687×2015 pixels. Each iconography class is represented by high-quality, detailed images that clearly display the motifs and intricate patterns found in the textiles. This careful approach ensures the dataset is suitable for thorough visual analysis and effective model training. This process continued until the image set for all 28 iconography classes was complete.

Image labeling and storage

For image labelling, we used the Roboflow platform [10], with each iconography manually annotated using its corresponding label. After labeling, images were organized into folders and subfolders by iconography class to ensure efficient access and usability for further analysis (Fig. 4). The dataset includes all labeled and organized images. Each photograph was reviewed and classified to provide a broad and balanced distribution across the 28 iconography classes. Invalid or duplicate samples were kept as potentially useful data for future analysis. Data collection lasted five weeks, with additional visits when necessary to complete missing iconography classes. Finally, we obtained a complete, diverse, and ethically collected dataset, well-suited for future research in textile iconography analysis and AI-based pattern recognition.

The CHINTEXDB-PERU28 dataset is distributed in two compressed files: Iconography_Chinchero_split.zip and Iconography_Chinchero_v2_all.zip. After decompression, a hierarchical directory structure is obtained that organizes the data into three main partitions: train, valid and test, each with its corresponding image and label files.

Each partition contains subdirectories associated with iconographic classes, in which images are stored in JPG(.jpg) format. Annotations are provided in text files whose naming scheme follows the pattern <id>_iconography.txt, where id corresponds to the numerical identifier of one of the 28 iconographic classes defined in the dataset.

Additionally, the Iconography_Chinchero_v2_all.zip file includes the complete set of images and annotations without partitioning. Fig. 4. presents a detailed outline of the organization and distribution of the dataset.Fig. 4Structure of the CHINTEXDB-PERU28 dataset.Fig 4 dummy alt text

Limitations

In this study, collecting sufficient samples for certain iconographic classes posed challenges due to their declining prevalence: four of the original 32 motifs were unavailable in the field, as younger generations have largely abandoned these traditions and the requisite knowledge resides primarily with older adults. To address this gap, we provide detailed descriptions and a reference image from a published source [5] for each of the four missing classes. Furthermore, sample counts vary across the remaining classes, with some represented by a limited number of instances. Researchers using this dataset may apply data-augmentation techniques to balance class distributions and improve the robustness of subsequent model training.

Ethics Statement

The study was conducted in strict compliance with ethical standards. No garments were harmed during the data collection procedure, and it should be noted that each image was taken with the explicit consent of the owners. Only images obtained by the author are included and the current work does not involve human subjects; animal experiments or data collected from social media platforms.
