A snare-assisted pull-up technique for side-branch component delivery during thoracic endovascular aortic repair for distal aortic arch aneurysm using Gore Thoracic Branch Endoprosthesis
Authors: Takehiro Suzuki, Takao Ohki, Kota Shukuzawa, Soichiro Fukushima, Makiko Omori, Hirotsugu Ozawa, Ryo Nishide, Daisuke Yamagishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a case of thoracic endovascular aortic repair using a thoracic branch endoprosthesis for aneurysm enlargement caused by an endoleak after prior fenestrated repair. A 75-year-old man with previous left subclavian artery stenting presented with aneurysm growth. Advancement of the side-branch component through the pre-existing stent was difficult because of severe angulation, and standard push-up techniques failed. A snare-assisted pull-up technique via a pre-existing 6F brachial sheath enabled direct capture and controlled pull-and-push advancement of the device. This method may serve as a simple bailout strategy for difficult branch component delivery.Keywords: Bailout strategy, Brachial approach, Side-branch component, Snare-assisted technique, Thoracic branch endoprosthesis

Endovascular repair of aortic arch pathology requires both secure aneurysm exclusion and reliable preservation of supra-aortic branch perfusion, which can be technically demanding, particularly in complex arch anatomies. The GORE Thoracic Branch Endoprosthesis (TBE; W.L. Gore and Associates) has expanded the applicability of total endovascular arch repair by enabling preservation of supra-aortic vessels.

However, advancement of the side-branch component can be challenging in cases with severe angulation, tortuosity, or unfavorable branch take-off. Failure of device tracking may prolong procedural time and increase manipulation within the aortic arch, potentially increasing the risk of embolic complications or device instability. Despite these challenges, bailout strategies for difficult side-branch component delivery remain limited.

We present a snare-assisted pull-up technique to facilitate side-branch component delivery during TBE deployment. This technique utilizes a prepositioned upper extremity access, which is almost routinely placed during TBE cases, to directly capture the branch component and guide it into the target vessel. Importantly, this approach is not limited to left subclavian artery (LSA) reconstruction and may be applicable to other supra-aortic branches, including the brachiocephalic and left common carotid arteries. This technique provides a simple, reproducible, and versatile bailout option when conventional advancement methods are unsuccessful.

Case report

Written publication consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of the details and images of this case.

A 75-year-old man with chronic type B aortic dissection had previously undergone thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) using a semicustom fenestrated device (Najuta; Kawasumi Laboratories) with LSA reconstruction for progressive aneurysmal degeneration. A bare-metal stent (SMART stent, 8-40 mm; Cordis) had been deployed at the LSA ostium to treat dissection involving the LSA origin. The patient subsequently required multiple additional TEVAR procedures for distal stent graft-induced new entry. Follow-up computed tomography angiography demonstrated progressive enlargement of the chronic dissecting thoracic aortic aneurysm from 68 mm to 74 mm over 6 months, associated with an endoleak originating from the LSA fenestration of the previously implanted Najuta device (Fig 1, A-C). Given the need to preserve LSA perfusion, additional zone 2 TEVAR using the TBE was planned.

Operative findings

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. A 5F sheath was inserted into the left common femoral artery (CFA) for angiography, a 9F sheath into the right CFA, and a 6F 45 cm destination guiding sheath (TERUMO) was inserted percutaneously via the left brachial artery. Initial aortography confirmed the endoleak from the LSA fenestration (Fig 1, D), and treatment with zone 2 TEVAR using TBE was deemed feasible.

A 24F long DrySeal sheath (65 cm) (W.L. Gore and Associates) was advanced from the right CFA into the descending thoracic aorta. A brachiofemoral through-and-through wire was then established between the left brachial artery and the right CFA. Despite severe tortuosity of the descending thoracic aorta, the TBE device was successfully advanced into the distal aortic arch. The main body of the TBE (TAC084520J) was deployed with alignment to the LSA.

Advancement of the side-branch component (TSB080806J) through the previously placed LSA bare-metal stent was technically challenging because of severe angulation and resistance from the pre-existing stent. Attempts to facilitate delivery by advancing the brachial guiding sheath distally to connect it to the dilator tip of the side-branch component were also unsuccessful and could not traverse the bending portion of the LSA stent (Fig 2, A).

A, Delivery of the thoracic branch endoprosthesis (TBE) side-branch component was not feasible using standard techniques because of interference from prior stent placement. B, The snare loop was advanced over the brachiofemoral through-and-through wire. C, The dilator tip was captured with the snare and inserted into the 6F sheath.

Therefore, a 6F EN Snare catheter system with a 6 to 10 mm loop diameter (Merit Medical) was introduced directly through the pre-existing left brachial 6F guiding sheath, and importantly, over the brachiofemoral through-and-through wire (Fig 2, B and C). Because the snare was advanced over the same through-and-through wire as the side-branch component, recapturing the olive-shaped dilator tip was straightforward whenever the snare slipped, unlike conventional freehand snare capture. After the dilator tip was captured, the snare was firmly retracted into the guiding sheath to stabilize the grasp and minimize slippage during advancement. Coordinated push-pull manipulation without rotational movement then enabled coaxial alignment with the LSA and smooth advancement through the pre-existing LSA stent (Fig 3, A-C, Fig 4, A and B).

A, A 6F snare catheter was advanced over the brachiofemoral through-and-through wire, and the tip of the side-branch component was captured (arrow). B, The snared side-branch component was drawn into the guiding sheath and pulled along the course of the left subclavian artery (LSA) (arrow). C, The side-branch component was successfully deployed in the optimal position through the thoracic branch endoprosthesis (TBE) portal into the LSA.

A, The tip of the side-branch component was directly captured using the snare. B, The snared side-branch component was drawn into the guiding sheath and pulled upward.

Two side-branch components were required to achieve adequate sealing within the LSA. After successful delivery and deployment of the first bridging component, the second component was advanced without requiring additional snare assistance. Balloon touch-up was then performed for both the TBE main body and side-branch components. Completion angiography demonstrated a residual type Ia endoleak, which was successfully treated with an additional proximal aortic cuff. Final angiography confirmed complete exclusion of the aneurysm without endoleak and preserved LSA perfusion (Fig 5, A). The postoperative course was uneventful. No complications, including stroke, were observed, and the patient had an uneventful course. Follow-up computed tomography angiography demonstrated no endoleak and patency of the reconstructed LSA (Fig 5, B and C).

A, Final intraoperative angiography demonstrating complete exclusion of the aneurysm without endoleak and preserved perfusion of the reconstructed left subclavian artery (LSA). B, Postoperative three-dimensional computed tomography (3D CT) at 1 month. C, Postoperative 3D CT at 1 month.

Discussion

This case highlights a bailout strategy for difficult side-branch component delivery during TBE repair. Conventional TBE branch reconstruction relies on push-up advancement of the side-branch component through a brachiofemoral through-and-through wire system. However, this approach may fail in complex branch anatomies, particularly after previous endovascular interventions. Severe tortuosity, branch angulation, or rigid pre-existing stents may impede device tracking, making conventional advancement difficult. Excessive forward force under these conditions may increase the risk of device instability or vascular injury. In such situations, a snare-assisted pull-up maneuver from the brachial side provides an alternative means of achieving controlled branch component delivery.

Preservation of the LSA during zone 2 TEVAR is recommended by current guidelines.1, 2, 3, 4 In the present case, the patient had previously undergone fenestrated TEVAR using a Najuta device with additional bare-metal stenting at the LSA origin. Although endovascular arch repair techniques such as fenestrated stent grafting and retrograde in situ branched stenting have expanded treatment options for complex arch pathology,5, 6, 7 these procedures may create challenging branch anatomy for subsequent interventions because of severe angulation, altered vessel geometry, or pre-existing stents.

In the present case, advancement of the side-branch component through the previously placed LSA bare-metal stent was unsuccessful despite distal support using a brachial guiding sheath. Therefore, we directly introduced an EN Snare catheter through the prepositioned 6F guiding sheath from the left brachial artery and captured the tip of the side-branch component (Fig 4, A and B). Gentle pull-up traction from the brachial side facilitated controlled advancement across the difficult segment. Rotational manipulation was intentionally avoided because the side-branch delivery shaft is not designed to transmit torque effectively. Therefore, rotational movement is unlikely to facilitate advancement and may instead increase the risk of device damage. Coordinated push-pull manipulation alone was sufficient in the present case. Importantly, this maneuver enabled successful delivery without requiring additional access, sheath upsizing, or exchange of the established through-and-through wire system.

We recommend this technique primarily as a bailout strategy after unsuccessful conventional push-up advancement. However, when difficult side-branch advancement is anticipated because of severe branch angulation, prior branch stenting, or poor coaxial alignment, early application of this technique may also be reasonable to reduce unnecessary manipulation within the aortic arch, minimize the risk of cerebral embolization associated with repeated catheter manipulation, and potentially reduce the risk of deformation of the side-branch delivery system or previously implanted stents by avoiding excessive push-up force.

Once the initial bridging component was successfully advanced through the difficult segment, delivery of the second side-branch component became straightforward without additional snare assistance. This finding suggests that overcoming the initial resistance may be the key technical challenge in such anatomies.

The TBE has demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes and high technical success rates in previous reports.8,9 However, side-branch-related complications, including difficult tracking, kinking, occlusion, and type III endoleak, remain important procedural concerns.9, 10, 11 Therefore, reliable branch component delivery is essential for procedural success.

A key advantage of the present technique is its simplicity and versatility. Because the snare catheter can be inserted directly through an existing upper extremity guiding sheath, the maneuver can be performed rapidly without additional device exchanges or access routes. Furthermore, since the snare is introduced over the pre-existing wire on which the side-branch component is placed, capturing the dilator tip can be accomplished automatically. Although used in the present case for LSA reconstruction, this pull-up maneuver may also be applicable to other supra-aortic branches, including the brachiocephalic and left common carotid arteries.

Careful manipulation is required because excessive traction during snaring may result in device damage or vascular injury. Nevertheless, this technique represents a practical, reproducible, and readily available bailout strategy when conventional push-up advancement techniques are unsuccessful.

Conclusions

A snare-assisted pull-up technique using both a pre-existing brachial guiding sheath and a through-and-through wire to directly grasp and apply traction to the branch component is a simple, easy, and useful adjunct to facilitate side-branch component delivery during TBE, particularly in anatomies with severe angulation or prior branch stenting when conventional push-up advancement is unsuccessful.
