Strichartz estimates for the half Klein-Gordon equation on asymptotically flat backgrounds and applications to cubic Dirac equations Thanks: 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification. M35Q55, 35Q40. Thanks: Key words and phrases. Klein-Gordon equations, phase space transform, Dirac equations, weakly asymptotically flat space-time, endpoint Strichartz estimates, global well-posedness
Sebastian Herr, Seokchang Hong

Abstract
. The aim of this paper is to establish the L t 2 L^{2}_{t} -endpoint Strichartz estimate for (half) Klein-Gordon equations on a weakly asymptotically flat space-time. As an application we prove small data global well-posedness and scattering for massive cubic Dirac equations in the full subcritical range in this setting. Crucial ingredient is a parametrix contruction following the work of Metcalfe-Tataru and Xue and complements Strichartz estimates obtained by Zheng-Zhang. The proof of the global result for the cubic Dirac equation follows the strategy developed by Machihara-Nakanishi-Ozawa in the Euclidean setting.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Dispersive and Strichartz estimates for wave and Klein-Gordon equations

Consider the classical constant coefficient wave equation on
\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
, i.e.

(1.1)

\displaystyle\Box u=(\partial_{t}^{2}-\Delta_{x})u=0,\ u|_{t=0}=u_{0},\ \partial_{t}u|_{t=0}=u_{1},

where
\Delta_{x}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}
is the usual Laplacian operator on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
.
For initial data Fourier-supported in the annulus
\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\leq|\xi|\leq 2\lambda\}
, the solution
u
satisfies the dispersive estimate

(1.2)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{x}u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\lesssim t^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\lambda^{\frac{d+1}{2}}(\|\nabla_{x}u_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{x}}+\|u_{1}\|_{L^{1}_{x}}),

which follows from the classical stationary phase analysis.
From the dispersive decay of waves, the
L^{2}
conservation and Littlewood–Paley theory one obtains the family of Strichartz estimates
[
44
,
27
]

(1.3)

\displaystyle\||\nabla_{x}|^{-\sigma}\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{p}_{t}L^{q}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{x}}+\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}_{x}},

for wave-admissible
(\sigma,p,q)
satisfying
2\leq p,q\leq\infty
and

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sigma=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d}{q}-\frac{1}{p},\quad\frac{2}{p}+\frac{d-1}{q}\leq\frac{d-1}{2},

except for the forbidden endpoint
(1,2,\infty)
when
d=3
, in which the estimate fails to hold
[
35
]
.

This has been generalized to variable coefficient wave equations under a number of different assumptions. For instance, the dispersive and Strichartz estimates (
1.3
) have been established in
[
33
]
under a weak asymptotic flatness condition.

Similarly, consider the constant coefficient Klein-Gordon equation on
\mathbb{R}^{1+d}

(1.5)

\displaystyle(\Box+m^{2})u=0,\ u|_{t=0}=u_{0},\ \partial_{t}u|_{t=0}=u_{1},

for a mass parameter
m>0
. Under the same Fourier support assumption as above, the solutions
u
to the Klein-Gordon equations satisfy the following dispersive decay estimate (see e.g.
[
36
]
):

(1.6)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle\lambda\rangle^{\frac{d}{2}+1}t^{-\frac{d}{2}}(\|\nabla_{x}u_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{x}}+\|u_{1}\|_{L^{1}_{x}}).

Notice that the time-decay rate is stronger than for the wave equation, at the expense of derivative loss. In addition, (
1.2
) is true for large frequencies, so that by interpolation one obtains the Strichartz estimates of the form

(1.7)

\displaystyle\|\langle\nabla_{x}\rangle^{-\sigma}\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{p}_{t}L^{q}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{x}}+\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}_{x}},

where
(\sigma,p,q)
satisfy the relation: for
2\leq p,q\leq\infty
and
0\leq\theta\leq 1
,

(1.8)

\displaystyle\sigma=\frac{d+1+\theta}{4}\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right),\quad\frac{2}{p}+\frac{d-1+\theta}{q}=\frac{d-1+\theta}{2},

except for the forbidden endpoint
(p,q)=(2,\infty)
when
d=3
and
\theta=0
, see e.g.
[
31
, Lemma 3]
.

In this paper, we establish the corresponding Strichartz estimates for the (half) Klein-Gordon equation with variable coefficients under a weak asymptotic flatness condition.
This complements the result of
[
33
]
for the wave equation in this setting.

Given a Lorentzian metric
g=-dt^{2}+g_{ij}(t,x)dx^{i}dx^{j}
, we consider the Klein-Gordon equation
(\Box_{g}-m^{2})u=0
. In view of the applications we have in mind it is favorable to consider half-Klein-Gordon equations

(1.9)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}u=\pm\mathrm{Op}(\sqrt{m^{2}+g^{ij}(t,x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}})u+(\textrm{lower order terms}).

We denote these lower-order terms by
A^{\prime}(t,x,D_{x})
and assume a very general bound, which is flexible enough to cover many cases, in particular the application to Dirac equations.

Assumption on lower order terms:
The lower-order terms
A^{\prime}
satisfy the following error-type estimates:

(1.10)

\displaystyle\|A^{\prime}u\|_{Y^{s}}\lesssim\epsilon\|u\|_{X^{s}},

where
s
refers to the
L^{2}
Sobolev regularity of the initial data and the emanating solution.
We postpone precise definitions and further discussion to Section
2
.

From now on we put
m=1
for simplicity and focus on the evolution equation

(1.11)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D_{t}u\pm\mathrm{Op}(\sqrt{1+g^{ij}(t,x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}})u+A^{\prime}(t,x,D_{x})u=f,\\
u|_{t=0}:=u_{0},\end{aligned}

where
A^{\prime}
is the lower-order term satisfying the error-type estimates (
1.10
).
We shall consider the half-Klein-Gordon operator, which is given by a small perturbation of the usual half-Klein-Gordon operator
D_{t}\pm\langle D_{x}\rangle
. To be precise, we consider the metric
g=-dt^{2}+g_{ij}(t,x)dx^{i}dx^{j}
, where the elliptic part
[g_{ij}]_{i,j=1,\cdots,3}
satisfies the following:

C_{\alpha,k}:=\sup_{(t,x)\in A_{j}}\left(|x|^{|\alpha|}|\partial_{t,x}^{\alpha}(g^{ij}(t,x)-\delta^{ij})|\right),\quad 0\leq|\alpha|\leq 2,

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{0},\alpha^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{1+3}
, and

C_{\alpha,k}^{\prime}:=\sup_{(t,x)\in A_{j}}\left(|x|^{\frac{|\alpha|+1}{2}}|\partial_{t,x}^{\alpha}g^{ij}(t,x)|\right),\quad 3\leq|\alpha|\leq[\frac{d}{2}]+3,\ 0\leq\alpha_{0}\leq 2,

satisfy

(1.12)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}C_{\alpha,k}\leq\epsilon,\quad|\alpha|\leq 2,

and

(1.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}C_{\alpha,k}^{\prime}\leq\epsilon,\quad 3\leq|\alpha|\leq[\frac{d}{2}]+3,\ 0\leq\alpha_{0}\leq 2.

In addition, we assume that the metric and its derivatives are regular near
0
, i.e., if
B=\{|x|<1\}
is the unit ball, then we have

(1.14)

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times B}|x|^{|\alpha|}|\partial_{t,x}^{\alpha}(g^{ij}(t,x)-\delta^{ij})|\leq C_{\alpha},\quad 0\leq|\alpha|\leq[\frac{d}{2}]+3,\ 0\leq\alpha_{0}\leq 2.

Now we state our first main theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Strichartz estimates for the half Klein-Gordon equations)
.

Let
d\geq 3
,
|s|<\frac{d+1}{2}
, and (
1.10
), (
1.12
), (
1.13
), (
1.14
). Then, the solution
u
to (
1.11
)
satisfies the Strichartz estimates

(1.15)

\displaystyle\|\langle D_{x}\rangle^{s-\sigma_{1}}u\|_{L^{p_{1}}_{t}L^{q_{1}}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{H^{s}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\langle D_{x}\rangle^{s+\sigma_{2}}f\|_{L^{p_{2}^{\prime}}_{t}L^{q_{2}^{\prime}}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})},

where the tuples
(\sigma_{1},p_{1},q_{1})
and
(\sigma_{2},p_{2},q_{2})
satisfy the relations:

(1.16)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{p}+\frac{d-1+\theta}{q}=\frac{d-1+\theta}{2},\ \sigma=\sigma(q)=\left(\frac{d+1+\theta}{4}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right),\ 0\leq\theta\leq 1,

(1.17)

\displaystyle(p,q)\neq(2,\infty)\text{ if }d=3,\,\theta=0.

Note that Theorem
1.1
includes, if
d=3
and
\theta>0
, the endpoint case
p=2
and
q=2+\frac{4}{\theta}
.
The upper bound on
|s|
is technical and related to the function spaces for the localised energy estimates.

The strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.1
mainly follows
[
33
]
. We construct an outgoing parametrix for the frequency-localised operators via the FBI transform introduced in
[
33
,
49
]
. Thanks to the assumptions (
1.12
), (
1.13
), and (
1.14
), the principal results from
[
33
,
49
]
can be used here. However, there the Klein-Gordon operator does not obey the scaling symmetry. This is the reason why we have to track the dependence on the parameter
\lambda
, which is different from
[
33
,
49
]
.
As
[
33
,
49
]
, in addition to the parametrix we also provide error estimates via localised energy estimates.

Previous results

The Strichartz estimates for the wave equations with the constant coefficient have been extensively studied. We refer to
[
21
]
for an expository study. The forbidden endpoint
(p,q)=(2,\infty)
with
d=3
can be compensated at the expense of an extra regularity in the angular variables and one can enjoy the stronger space-time estimates by imposing the angular regularity. We refer to
[
43
]
for the improved Strichartz estimates of the wave equations using angular regularity and
[
12
]
for more general operators.

Local-in-time Strichartz estimates for the wave operator with variable coefficients have been well-studied. Concerning a parametrix construction, one may consider a suitable localisation of the spatial variables and the frequency variables at the same time. One way of accomplishing this task is to utilise a wave packet. Smith
[
39
]
construct a wave packet parametrix and obtains the Strichartz estimates with
C^{1,1}
-coefficients in
d=2,3
. An alternative way is to use the FBI transform, which is a phase space transform. Tataru
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
proves the Strichartz estimates with rough coefficients. We also refer to the work by Smith - Tataru
[
41
]
for the sharp lower bounds of the solutions to the wave equation with low regularity metric.

Concerning global-in-time Strichartz estimates, several geometric features of manifolds are also considered. For example, Smith-Sogge
[
40
]
establishes the Strichartz estimates on a spatially compact perturbation of the Euclidean space with odd dimensions. This is later improved to even dimensions by Burq
[
7
]
and Metcalfe
[
34
]
independently. The Strichartz estimates for the Schrödinger equations and the wave equations are obtained on an asymptotically Euclidean space in a very weak sense by Tataru
[
49
]
and Metcalfe-Tataru
[
33
]
, using a time-dependent FBI transform. On the non-trapping scattering manifold setup, Hassell-Zhang
[
22
]
show the endpoint Schrödinger-Strichartz estimates. Then the Strichartz estimates for the wave equations and the Klein-Gordon equations are obtained by Zhang
[
52
]
. The Strichartz estimates for the wave equations on manifold with boundary have been obtained by Blair - Smith - Sogge
[
5
]
and it has been improved to more general setting by Ivanovici - Lebeau - Planchon
[
25
]
and Ivanovici - Lascar - Lebeau - Planchon
[
24
]
.

On the study of solutions to the linear Klein-Gordon equations, see
[
26
,
29
,
32
]
and the references therein. Concerning global existence of solutions to nonlinear Klein-Gordon equations, we refer to
[
16
,
15
,
20
,
28
,
38
]
. The endpoint Strichartz estimates for the Klein-Gordon equation on the non-trapping scattering manifold have been obtained by Zhang-Zheng
[
53
]
. We cannot use this result (even under strong enough assumptions so that we are in the geometric setting of
[
53
]
) to derive global in time estimates for the cubic Dirac equation due to the presence of lower order perturbations.

We point out that for the frequency localisation to unit scale
|\xi|\approx 1
and
\lambda=1
the dispersive decay estimate (see (
5.10
)) has been obtained in the thesis by Xue
[
51
]
. Here, we extend this result to arbitrary frequency scales with the optimal derivative loss in (
5.10
) and prove the endpoint Strichartz estimates in Theorem
1.1
.

1.2.
Scattering of the cubic Dirac equations on a curved space-time

As an application of the endpoint Strichartz estimates Theorem
1.1
we establish the global well-posedness and scattering of the massive cubic Dirac equation on a
(1+3)
-dimensional asymptotically flat background.

We first recall the linear Dirac equation with massive case
M>0
on the Minkowski spacetime
(\mathbb{R}^{1+3},\mathbf{m})
, with the metric
\mathbf{m}=\textrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)
. The linear Dirac equation
[
17
]
is given by

\displaystyle-i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\psi+M\psi=0,

where
\psi
is the unknown spinor field
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{1+3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{4}
and the gamma matrices
\gamma^{\mu}
are the constant complex matrices satisfying the following algebraic relations:

(1.18)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}+\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu}=-2m^{\mu\nu}\mathbf{I}_{4\times 4}.

A typical argument of studying the Cauchy problems of the Dirac equations is to reformulate the equations as a system of the half-wave for the Klein-Gordon equations. To do this, we first multiply
\gamma^{0}
on the equation to get

\displaystyle(-i\partial_{t}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\partial_{j}+M\gamma^{0})\psi=0.

Then our desired reformulation can be performed provided that there exists a projection operator
\Pi_{\pm}^{M}
so that it satisfies
\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\Pi_{\pm}^{M}=\Pi_{\pm}^{M}
and
\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\Pi_{\mp}^{M}=0
and the following identity holds:

(1.19)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{x}\rangle_{M}(\Pi_{+}^{M}-\Pi_{-}^{M})=-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\partial_{j}+M\gamma^{0}.

Now we introduce the operators, which are defined as the Fourier multipliers

(1.20)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\pm}^{M}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{I}_{4\times 4}\pm\frac{\xi_{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}+M\gamma^{0}}{\langle\xi\rangle_{M}}\right).

By a straightforward computation, one can deduce that
\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\Pi_{\pm}^{M}=\Pi_{\pm}^{M}
and
\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\Pi_{\mp}^{M}=0
, and also the relation (
1.19
). In consequence, the homogeneous Dirac equation is reformulated as the half Klein-Gordon equations as follows:

\displaystyle(-i\partial_{t}\pm\langle\nabla_{x}\rangle_{M})\Pi^{M}_{\pm}\psi=0,

where
\widehat{\Pi^{M}_{\pm}\psi}(\xi)=\Pi^{M}_{\pm}(\xi)\widehat{\psi}(\xi)
and
\psi=\Pi^{M}_{+}\psi+\Pi^{M}_{-}\psi
.

A representative toy model for the study of nonlinear Dirac equations is the Dirac equation with a cubic nonlinearity which has the form:

(1.21)

\displaystyle(-i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}+M)\psi=(\psi^{\dagger}\gamma^{0}C\psi)C\psi,

where
C\in\mathbb{C}^{4}\times\mathbb{C}^{4}
is some complex matrix. For example, we call the equation (
1.21
) the Soler model
[
42
]
if
C=I_{4\times 4}
and the Thirring model
[
50
]
if
C=\gamma^{\mu}
.

Solutions to the cubic Dirac equation with
M=0
can be rescaled and the invariant Sobolev space
\dot{H^{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is called the critical Sobolev space. Also if
M>0
we call
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
the critical space.

Our main concern is to investigate the initial value problems for the Dirac equations on a curved background. Now the Minkowski metric
\mathbf{m}
is replaced by a generally curved metric
g
. For simplicity we assume that the metric
g
is decoupled, i.e., the metric
g
is written by

\displaystyle g=-dt^{2}+g_{ij}(t,x)dx^{i}dx^{j}.

On a curved space-time, the gamma matrices
\gamma^{\mu}
are no longer constant matrices. The algebraic relation of the matrices
\gamma^{\mu}
is now

\displaystyle\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}+\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu}=-2g^{\mu\nu}(t,x)\mathbf{I}_{4\times 4}.

The homogeneous covariant Dirac equation is given by

\displaystyle(-i\gamma^{\mu}(t,x)\mathbf{D}_{\mu}+M)\psi=0,

where the covariant derivative
\mathbf{D}_{\mu}
acting on a spinor field is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-\Gamma_{\mu}.

The spinorial affine connections
\Gamma_{\mu}
are matrices satisfying bounds
|\Gamma_{\mu}|\lesssim|\partial_{t,x}g_{ij}|
. We refer to Section
2
for the details.
We consider the Cauchy problem for the massive Dirac equation with a cubic-type nonlinearity:

\displaystyle(-i\gamma^{\mu}\mathbf{D}_{\mu}+M)\psi={}

\displaystyle(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)\gamma^{0}\psi,

\displaystyle\psi|_{t=0}={}

\displaystyle\psi_{0},

where the metric
g
satisfies a weak asymptotic flatness condition in dimension
d=3
: (
1.12
), (
1.13
), (
1.14
).
Now we state our second main theorem.

Theorem 1.2

(Global well-posedness for the cubic Dirac equations)
.

Let
s>1
and
M>0
be given. Suppose that the metric
g
satisfies (
1.12
), (
1.13
), and (
1.14
) for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small. Then, the Cauchy problem for the cubic Dirac equation on the spacetime
(\mathbb{R}^{1+3},g)
is globally well-posed for small initial data
\psi_{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Furthermore, the solutions scatter to free solutions for
t\rightarrow\pm\infty
.

Previous results

Dirac equations on curved space-time have been studied by a number of authors. We refer the readers to
[
37
]
for an expository literature of the relativistic quantum field theory. The spectrum of the Dirac operator is studied by Bär
[
1
]
. Recently, the dispersive properties of the linear Dirac operators on a curved backgrounds have been studied. Cacciafesta-Suzzoni
[
9
]
establish local smoothing estimates for Dirac equations on asymptotically Euclidean space. Then local-in-time and global-in-time Strichartz estimates for the Dirac equations are established by Cacciafesta, Suzzoni, Ben-Artzi, and Zhang
[
10
,
4
]
on a spherically symmetric manifold and in
[
14
]
on the cosmic string background. In
[
11
]
the authors obtain the Strichartz estimates excluding the endpoint
p=2
under some nontrapping condition using the work of Zhang-Zheng
[
53
]
. More recently, local-in-time Strichartz estimates for Dirac equations as well half wave and half Klein-Gordon equations are obtained on compact manifolds without boundary
[
8
]
.

A key approach of studying the dispersive properties of the Dirac operator in the previous results is to utilise the so-called squaring trick. In other words, by squaring the Dirac operator, one obtains the Klein-Gordon operator with a geometric quantities and study the Klein-Gordon equation which is second order operator. However, we do not follow this strategy since it leads to a derivative nonlinearity. Instead, we reformulate the Dirac equation on a curved background into a variable coefficient half-Klein-Gordon equation.

We refer the readers to
[
18
]
for the comprehensive study of nonlinear Dirac equations.
Global estimates for the cubic nonlinearity call for
L^{2}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}
-estimates, which is the forbidden endpoint. However, in the massive case
M>0
, one can exploit the better time-decay
t^{-1-\frac{\theta}{2}}
for any
0\leq\theta\leq 1
and establish global well-posedness for small initial data in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,
s>1
.
In this way, for any choice of
C
, global well-posedness and scattering results have been obtained using the endpoint Strichartz estimate, which is due to Machihara - Nakamura - Nakanishi - Ozawa
[
30
]
.
In more specific settings, e.g. when
C
is the identity matrix
I_{4\times 4}
, the nonlinearity exhibits null structure and scattering results for a small initial data in the critical Sobolev spaces
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
have been established by Bejenaru - Herr
[
2
,
3
]
. The analogous results for the massless case
M=0
are obtained by Bournaveas - Candy
[
6
]
.

Once we have established the global
L^{2}_{t}
-endpoint Strichartz estimate for the half-Klein-Gordon equation the proof of Theorem
1.2
follows immediately, similarly to
[
30
,
31
]
.

We would like to also mention that in the proof of Theorem
1.2
we do not use the precise structure of the cubic nonlinearity.
In fact, any cubic half-Klein-Gordon equation can be handled, e.g.

(1.22)

\displaystyle(D_{t}\pm\mathrm{Op}(\sqrt{1+g^{ij}(t,x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}}))u=Q(u)

for any cubic term
Q(u)
.

Organisation

The rest of this paper is organised as follows. We end this section by introducing notation.

Section
2
is devoted to the introduction of the Dirac operator on curved space-time. The most important part of Section
2
is to define projection-type operators in order to reformulate the Dirac equation as half-Klein-Gordon equations up to lower-order terms. We also show that these lower order terms turn out to satisfy the error-type estimates (
1.10
) provided that the metric satisfies a weak asymptotic flatness assumption (
1.12
) and (
1.13
). Hence the study of the Dirac equation is reduced to a more general class of the half-Klein-Gordon equations.

Section
3
contains the most important setup of the reduction via the localisation of space and frequency simultaneously.
Beginning with the original half-Klein-Gordon operator
D_{t}+A^{\pm}
, we mollify

\displaystyle q(t,x,\xi)=\sqrt{1+g^{ij}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}}-\sqrt{1+|\xi|^{2}},

by truncation and regularisation of the coefficients,
and we obtain a family of mollified operators
A^{\pm}_{(k)}
, which obey the frequency-localisation property up to an acceptable error. From this we define a globally mollified operator

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}^{\pm}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}A^{\pm}_{(k)}S_{k},

where
S_{k}
is the projection onto the frequency annulus of size
2^{k}
. Proposition
3.1
allows us to replace the original
A^{\pm}
by
\widetilde{A}^{\pm}
at the expense of a small error. We also apply a scaling argument and analyse the scaled operator
\mathcal{A}^{\pm}_{(k)}
, which is localised at unit frequency (for each
k
).

In Section
3
, assuming that we have constructed an outgoing parametrix for the scaled operator
D_{t}+\mathcal{A}^{\pm}_{(k)}
(which is postponed to Section
5
) satisfying the dispersive estimate, we obtain an outgoing parametrix for the operator
D_{t}+A^{\pm}_{(k)}
. Hence, we obtain a parametrix for the globally mollified operator
D_{t}+\widetilde{A}^{\pm}
satisfying endpoint Strichartz estimates, which immediately imply the Strichartz estimates for the original half-Klein-Gordon operator due to Proposition
3.1
. Then, we establish small data global well-posedness and scattering for the cubic Dirac equation as a direct application.

In Section
4
, we recall the phase space transform and microlocal analysis from
[
33
]
.

Finally, Section
5
is devoted to the construction of an outgoing parametrix for the scaled operators
D_{t}+\mathcal{A}^{\pm}_{(k)}
. The main novelty of Section
5
is the dispersive inequality with time decay
t^{-\frac{d}{2}}
with the sharp loss of regularity.

Notation

The
L^{2}-
inner product is given by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}}
so that

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\overline{g(x)}\,dx.

For complex-valued functions
f,g\in L^{2}
, we have
\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}}=\overline{\langle g,f\rangle}_{L^{2}}
.

We write
(D_{t},D_{x})=(\frac{1}{i}\partial_{t},\frac{1}{i}\partial_{x})
. For any multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
with
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})
, by the notation
\partial_{x}^{\alpha}
we mean
\partial_{x}^{\alpha}=\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{d}}^{\alpha_{d}}
, where
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We also set
x_{0}=t
. The partial derivative
\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}
is defined in the similar way.

We adapt the Einstein summation convention. Repeated Greek indices
\mu,\nu,\cdots
mean summation from
0
to
d
, and repeated Latin indices
i,j,\cdots
, mean summation from
1
to
d
. For example, on
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
, the Dirac operator
-i\gamma^{\mu}\mathbf{D}_{\mu}=-i\gamma^{0}\mathbf{D}_{0}-i\gamma^{j}\mathbf{D}_{j}=-i\gamma^{0}\mathbf{D}_{0}-i\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma^{j}\mathbf{D}_{j}
.

We usually denote dyadic numbers
\lambda,\mu,\nu\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

For two positive numbers
A
and
B
, we write
A\lesssim B
if
A\leq CB
, for some absolute constant
C
(which only depends on irrelevant parameters). If
C
can be chosen sufficiently small, we write
A\ll B
.
Also, we write
A\approx B
if both
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
.

The mixed Lebesgue space
L^{p}_{t}L^{q}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})
for
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
is the set of all equivalence classes of measurable functions
u
given by
t\mapsto u(t)\in L^{q}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and the norm is given by

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{p}_{t}L^{q}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})}^{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(t,x)|^{q}\,dx\right)^{\frac{p}{q}}\,dt,

with the obvious modification if
p,q=\infty
.
We set
\|u\|_{\langle D_{x}\rangle^{s}L^{p}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}=\|\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-s}u\|_{L^{p}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Given a small
\epsilon>0
as in (
1.12
), we can find a sequence
\{\epsilon_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\ell^{1}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\in A_{j}}\left(|x|^{|\alpha|}|\partial_{t,x}^{\alpha}(g^{ij}(t,x)-\delta^{ij})|\right)\leq\epsilon_{j}

and
\sum_{j}\epsilon_{j}\lesssim\epsilon
. Without loss of any generality, we may assume that the sequence
\epsilon_{j}
is slowly varying, which says

\displaystyle|\log\epsilon_{j}-\log\epsilon_{j-1}|\leq 2^{-10}.

We can also choose a function
\epsilon:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfying

(1.23)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \epsilon_{j}<\epsilon(\mathfrak{s})<2\epsilon_{j},&\quad 2^{j}<\mathfrak{s}<2^{j+1}.,\\
|\epsilon^{\prime}(\mathfrak{s})|\leq&2^{-5}\mathfrak{s}^{-1}\epsilon(\mathfrak{s}),\end{aligned}

which implies that

(1.24)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\epsilon(\mathfrak{s})}{\mathfrak{s}}\,d\mathfrak{s}\approx\epsilon.

We also define the function
\epsilon_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}(\mathfrak{s})\approx\epsilon_{j},\ \mathfrak{s}\approx 2^{j},\ j\geq-k,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}(\mathfrak{s})\approx\epsilon_{-k},\ s\leq 2^{-k}.

We consider a frequency Littlewood-Paley decomposition

\displaystyle 1=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}S_{j}(D_{x})

where

\displaystyle\textrm{supp }s_{j}\subset\{2^{j-1}<|\xi|<2^{j+1}\}.

We also consider a smooth spatial Littlewood-Paley decomposition:

\displaystyle 1=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\chi_{j}(x),\quad\mathrm{supp}\,\chi\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:2^{j-1}<|x|<2^{j+1}\}.

We also set
\chi_{<j}=\sum_{k<j}\chi_{k}
.

2.
Dirac operators

This section is devoted to the introduction of covariant Dirac operators on a curved space-times, see
[
37
]
. We consider the Cauchy problems for the Dirac equation with a cubic nonlinearity on a weak asymptotically flat space-time. We reformulate the Dirac equations in terms of the half-Klein-Gordon equations by using a projection-type operator and then restate Theorem
1.2
in terms of the cubic half-Klein-Gordon equations.

In what follows, we restrict ourselves into a curved metric of the form:

\displaystyle g=-dt^{2}+g_{ij}(t,x)dx^{i}dx^{j}.

In other words, the metric is decoupled. The homogeneous covariant Dirac equation is given by

(2.1)

\displaystyle(-i\gamma^{\mu}\mathbf{D}_{\mu}+M)\psi=0.

On a generally curved space-time, the gamma matrices
\gamma^{\mu}
,
\mu=0,\cdots,3
are no longer constant matrices. Instead, the matrices
\gamma^{\mu}=\gamma^{\mu}(t,x)
also become space-time dependent complex matrices. The algebraic property is then generalised to

(2.2)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}+\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu}=-2g^{\mu\nu}(t,x)\mathbf{I}_{4\times 4}.

We define the covariant derivative acting on a spinor field
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{1+3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{4}
to be

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\mu}\psi=(\partial_{\mu}-\Gamma_{\mu})\psi,

where the spinorial affine connections
\Gamma_{\mu}=\Gamma_{\mu}(t,x)
are matrices defined by the vanishing of the covariant derivative of the gamma matrices:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu}-\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}\gamma_{\lambda}-\Gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu}+\gamma_{\nu}\Gamma_{\mu}=0.

One can represent the
\gamma^{\mu}(t,x)
matrices in terms of the gamma matrices
\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}
on the flat spacetime by introducing a
vierbein

b^{\alpha}_{\mu}(t,x)
of vector fields, defined by

(2.5)

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}(t,x)=m_{\alpha\beta}b^{\alpha}_{\mu}(t,x)b^{\beta}_{\nu}(t,x).

Then the matrices
\gamma^{\mu}(t,x)
can be written in the form

(2.6)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\mu}(t,x)=b^{\mu}_{\alpha}(t,x)\tilde{\gamma}^{\alpha}.

Here we used the notation
\gamma^{\mu}(t,x)
and
\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}
to distinguish the gamma matrices dependent on space-time from the constant matrices.
Now a set of
\Gamma_{\mu}(t,x)
satisfying (
2.4
) are given by

(2.7)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mu}(t,x)=-\frac{1}{4}\tilde{\gamma}_{\alpha}\tilde{\gamma}_{\beta}b^{\alpha\lambda}(t,x)\mathbf{D}_{\mu}b^{\beta}_{\lambda}(t,x),

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\mu}b^{\beta}_{\lambda}=\partial_{\mu}b^{\beta}_{\lambda}-\Gamma^{\sigma}_{\mu\lambda}b^{\beta}_{\sigma}.

We refer the readers to
[
37
, Section 3.9]
for the details. We have an obvious inequality
|\Gamma_{\mu}(t,x)|\lesssim|\partial_{t,x}g_{ij}|
. See also Proposition 2.1 of
[
11
]
.

Motivated by the study of Dirac equations on the flat space-time,
we would like to rewrite the Dirac equation in terms of the half Klein-Gordon equation.
To do this, as one has done in the flat case, we multiply
\gamma^{0}
on the equation (
2.1
) and rewrite the equation as

\displaystyle(-i\partial_{t}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\mathbf{D}_{j}-i\Gamma_{0}+M\gamma^{0})\psi=0.

We shall introduce operators
\Pi_{+}^{M}
and
\Pi_{-}^{M}
which satisfy the following identity:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}(\Pi_{+}^{M}-\Pi_{-}^{M})=-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\mathbf{D}_{j}-i\Gamma_{0}+M\gamma^{0}.

However, operators satisfying the above identity cannot be merely defined by Fourier multipliers. Instead, we introduce the pseudodifferential operators

\displaystyle\Pi_{\pm}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{4\times 4}\pm\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}^{-1}(-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\mathbf{D}_{j}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0}M)\right),

where the differential operator
\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}
is defined by the Fourier multiplier whose symbol is
\sqrt{M^{2}+|\xi|^{2}}
. It is obvious that these operators
\Pi^{\pm}_{M}
satisfy the identity (
2.8
).
Note that the operators
\Pi^{M}_{\pm}(t,x,D_{x})
are of the class
OPS^{0}_{1,0}
, or simply
OPS^{0}
.
Before the study of properties of the operators
\Pi^{M}_{\pm}
, we give a brief review of several facts of pseudodifferential operators. We refer the readers to the literature
[
23
, Section 18]
.

Proposition 2.1

(Theorem 18.1.8 of
[
23
]
)
.

If
a
and
b
are symbols, then formally

\displaystyle a(x,D_{x})b(x,D_{x})=(a\circ b)(x,D_{x}),

and has the asymptotic expansion

\displaystyle(a\circ b)(x,\xi)=\sum_{j}\frac{(i\langle\partial_{y},\partial_{\eta}\rangle)^{j}}{j!}a(x,\eta)b(y,\xi)\bigg|_{\eta=\xi,y=x}.

In particular, we have the first-order and second-order symbol expansions (see (B-1) of
[
19
]
.)

\displaystyle a\circ b=ab+\frac{1}{i}\int_{0}^{1}r_{1,s}\,ds=ab+\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial a}{\partial\xi}\frac{\partial b}{\partial x}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}r_{2,s}\,ds,

where the remainder is given by

(2.9)

\displaystyle r_{j,s}(x,\xi)=e^{is\langle\partial_{y},\partial_{\eta}\rangle}\langle\partial_{y},\partial_{\eta}\rangle^{j}[a(x,\eta)b(y,\xi)]_{y=x,\eta=\xi}.

Using the above results, we deduce that the commutator of pseudodifferential operators can be written as

\displaystyle[a,b](x,D_{x})=\{a,b\}(x,D_{x})+r(x,D_{x}),

where
\{a,b\}
is the usual Poisson bracket given by

\displaystyle\{a,b\}=\frac{\partial a}{\partial\xi}\frac{\partial b}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial a}{\partial x}\frac{\partial b}{\partial\xi},

and the remainder term
r(x,D_{x})
is given by (
2.9
).

Proposition 2.2

(Theorem 18.1.13 of
[
23
]
)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{R}
and
a
be a symbol of the class
S^{m}
. Then the operator
a(x,D_{x})
is a continuous operator from
H^{s}_{x}
to
H^{s-m}_{x}
for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore,
a(x,D_{x})
is a continuous operator from
H^{s,p}_{x}
to
H^{s-m,p}_{x}
, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

It is remarkable that the norm of the operator
a(x,D_{x}):H^{s}_{x}\rightarrow H^{s-m}_{x}
can be estimated by semi-norms of the symbol
a\in S^{m}
. See also the proof of Theorem 18.1.11 of
[
23
]
. We also recall the theorem by Calderón-Valliancourt:

Proposition 2.3
.

If a symbol
a(x,\xi)
has bounded derivatives
\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}a(x,\xi)
for
|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq N_{d}
with some fixed number
N_{d}
dependent on the dimension
d
, then the associated pseudodifferential operator
a(x,D_{x})
is
L^{2}
-bounded.

Furthermore, the number
N_{d}
can be improved to
[\frac{d}{2}]+1
, see Cordes
[
13
]
. Thus, for boundedness of the pseudodifferential operators, we need only a restricted number of derivatives of the symbols. In particular, if one considers the
L^{2}
-norm of the commutator of the pseudodifferential operators of the form
[A(x),B(D_{x})]
, we only need to compute the derivatives
D_{x}^{\alpha}a(x)D_{\xi}^{\alpha}b(\xi)
for
|\alpha|\leq[\frac{d}{2}]+3
.

From now on we put
M=1
for simplicity.

Lemma 2.4
.

We have the following commutator identities:

(2.10)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\pm}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})\Pi_{\pm}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\pm}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})+\mathcal{E}(t,x,D_{x}),

(2.11)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\pm}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})\Pi_{\mp}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{E}(t,x,D_{x})

with the pseudodifferential operator
\mathcal{E}(t,x,D_{x})=\sum_{m=0}^{3}\mathcal{E}^{-m}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{0}

\displaystyle=\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-2}(g^{jk}-\delta^{jk})D_{j}D_{k}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-2}(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j}(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k})D_{k}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}D_{j})

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}[\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](D_{j}(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k})D_{k}+\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{j}D_{k})

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}([\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}-i\Gamma_{0})+[\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](D_{j}\gamma^{0})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+[i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}-i\Gamma_{0},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}]\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{-2}

\displaystyle=\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-2}\big(-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j}(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k})\Gamma_{k}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}(D_{j}\Gamma_{k})+\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}(D_{j}\gamma^{0})-\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\phantom{\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-2}\big(-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j}(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k})\Gamma_{k}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}(D_{j}\Gamma_{k})+}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}\gamma^{0}-i\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}([\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}]\gamma^{0}D_{j}+[i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}-i\Gamma_{0}+\gamma^{0},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}-i\Gamma_{0}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{-3}

\displaystyle=\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}[\gamma^{0},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}]\gamma^{0},

and we have
\mathcal{E}^{-m}\in OPS^{-m}
.

Proof.

We compute the composition of the projection-type operators.
We consider the product

\displaystyle\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}(-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\mathbf{D}_{j}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0})\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}(-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\mathbf{D}_{k}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0})

\displaystyle=\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-2}(-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\mathbf{D}_{j}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0})(-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\mathbf{D}_{k}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0})

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}[(-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\mathbf{D}_{j}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0}),\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\mathbf{D}_{k}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0}).

We see that

\displaystyle(-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\mathbf{D}_{j}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0})(-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\mathbf{D}_{k}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0})

\displaystyle=\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j}(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k})+\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j}(-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k})+\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j}\gamma^{0}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}(-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k})

\displaystyle\qquad-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}\gamma^{0}+\gamma^{0}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}+\gamma^{0}\gamma^{0}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j}(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k})D_{k}+\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{j}D_{k}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j}(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k})\Gamma_{k}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{j}\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\gamma^{0}D_{j}+\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}(D_{j}\gamma^{0})-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}-\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}\gamma^{0}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma^{k}D_{k}-i\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}+I_{4\times 4}.

Since
\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\gamma^{0}=-\gamma^{0}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}=-\gamma^{j}
, we have
\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\gamma^{0}D_{j}+\gamma^{k}D_{k}=0
. Since
g^{0j}=0
, we have
\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}=-\gamma^{j}\gamma^{0}
and hence

\displaystyle\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{j}D_{k}

\displaystyle=-(\gamma^{0})^{2}\gamma^{j}\gamma^{k}D_{j}D_{k}=-\gamma^{j}\gamma^{k}D_{j}D_{k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma^{j}\gamma^{k}D_{j}D_{k}+\gamma^{k}\gamma^{j}D_{k}D_{j})

\displaystyle=-g^{jk}D_{j}D_{k}=-(g^{jk}-\delta^{jk})D_{j}D_{k}-\delta^{jk}D_{j}D_{k},

which yields the operator
\Pi_{\pm}
with the error term
\mathcal{E}^{0}
, as combined with
I_{4\times 4}
above the computations of the composition.

Now we compute the commutator

\displaystyle[(-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\mathbf{D}_{j}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0}),\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\mathbf{D}_{k}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0})

\displaystyle=[-i(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\partial_{j}-\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}+\Gamma_{0})+\gamma^{0},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](-i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\partial_{k}+i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}-i\Gamma_{0}+\gamma^{0})

\displaystyle=[\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}+i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}-i\Gamma_{0}+\gamma^{0})

\displaystyle\qquad+[i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}-i\Gamma_{0}+\gamma^{0},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}+i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}-i\Gamma_{0}+\gamma^{0})

\displaystyle=[\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}]\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}+[\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}-i\Gamma_{0}+\gamma^{0})

\displaystyle\qquad+[i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}\Gamma_{j}-i\Gamma_{0}+\gamma^{0},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}+i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}\Gamma_{k}-i\Gamma_{0}+\gamma^{0}).

We observe that

\displaystyle[\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}]\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}-\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{k}

\displaystyle=[\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}]D_{j}(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k})D_{k}+[\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}]\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}D_{j}D_{k}

and

\displaystyle[\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j}D_{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}]\gamma^{0}=[\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}](D_{j}\gamma^{0})+[\gamma^{0}\gamma^{j},\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}]\gamma^{0}D_{j},

which completes the proof.
∎

Now we show that the study of the Dirac equation (
2.1
) can be reduced to the study of the half-Klein-Gordon equation using Lemma
2.4
.
We have seen that the homogeneous Dirac equation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\left(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}\left(\Pi_{+}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})-\Pi_{-}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})\right)\right)\psi=0.

Now we apply the operator
\Pi_{+}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})
to get

\displaystyle\Pi^{M}_{+}(t,x,D_{x})\left(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}\left(\Pi_{+}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})-\Pi_{-}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})\right)\right)\psi

\displaystyle=\left(D_{t}\Pi^{M}_{+}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}\Pi^{M}_{+}(\Pi^{M}_{+}-\Pi^{M}_{-})\right)\psi+[\Pi^{M}_{+},D_{t}]\psi+[\Pi_{+}^{M},\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}](\Pi_{+}^{M}-\Pi_{-}^{M})\psi

\displaystyle=(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi+2\langle D_{x}\rangle\mathcal{E}\psi+[\Pi^{M}_{+},D_{t}]\psi+[\Pi_{+}^{M},\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}](\Pi_{+}^{M}-\Pi_{-}^{M})\psi.

We apply the operator
\Pi^{M}_{+}
once again. We note that

\displaystyle\Pi_{+}^{M}(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi

\displaystyle=(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle)\Pi_{+}^{M}\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi+[\Pi_{+}^{M},D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle]\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi

\displaystyle=(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle)(\Pi_{+}^{M}+\mathcal{E})\psi+[\Pi_{+}^{M},D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle]\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi

\displaystyle=(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle)\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi+(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle)\mathcal{E}\psi+[\Pi_{+}^{M},D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle]\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi,

and

\displaystyle\Pi_{+}^{M}\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}\mathcal{E}\psi

\displaystyle=\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}\Pi_{+}^{M}\mathcal{E}\psi+[\Pi_{+}^{M},\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}]\mathcal{E}\psi

\displaystyle=\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}\mathcal{E}\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi+[\Pi_{+}^{M},\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}]\mathcal{E}\psi+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}[\Pi_{+}^{M},\mathcal{E}]\psi,

which gives

(2.12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi+(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\mathcal{E}\psi+2\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}\mathcal{E}\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi\\
&\qquad+[\Pi_{+}^{M},D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}]\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi+2[\Pi_{+}^{M},\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}]\mathcal{E}\psi+2\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}[\Pi_{+}^{M},\mathcal{E}]\psi\\
&\qquad\qquad+\Pi_{+}^{M}[\Pi_{+}^{M},D_{t}]\psi+\Pi_{+}^{M}[\Pi_{+}^{M},\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}](\Pi_{+}^{M}-\Pi_{-}^{M})\psi=0.\end{aligned}

On the other hand, applying the operator
\Pi_{-}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})
yields

\displaystyle\Pi^{M}_{-}(t,x,D_{x})\left(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}\left(\Pi_{+}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})-\Pi_{-}^{M}(t,x,D_{x})\right)\right)\psi

\displaystyle=(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi-2\langle D_{x}\rangle\mathcal{E}\psi+[\Pi^{M}_{-},D_{t}]\psi+[\Pi_{-}^{M},\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}](\Pi_{+}^{M}-\Pi_{-}^{M})\psi.

We apply the operator
\Pi_{-}^{M}
once again. We note that

\displaystyle\Pi_{-}^{M}(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi

\displaystyle=(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\Pi_{-}^{M}\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi+[\Pi_{-}^{M},D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}]\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi

\displaystyle=(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi+(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\mathcal{E}\psi+[\Pi_{-}^{M},D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}]\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi.

Then we have

(2.13)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi+(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\mathcal{E}\psi-2\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}\mathcal{E}\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi\\
&\qquad+[\Pi_{-}^{M},D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}]\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi-2[\Pi_{-}^{M},\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}]\mathcal{E}\psi-2\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}[\Pi_{-}^{M},\mathcal{E}]\psi\\
&\qquad\qquad+\Pi_{-}^{M}[\Pi_{-}^{M},D_{t}]\psi+\Pi_{-}^{M}[\Pi_{-}^{M},\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}](\Pi_{+}^{M}-\Pi_{-}^{M})\psi=0.\end{aligned}

From now on we focus on the leading terms of (
2.12
) and (
2.13
), which contains essentially first order derivatives in the pseudo-differential sense:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi+(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\mathcal{E}\psi+2\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}\mathcal{E}\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi=0,\\
(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi+(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})\mathcal{E}\psi+2\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}\mathcal{E}\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi=0.\end{array}\right.

Note that all of the pseudodifferential operators containing commutators in (
2.12
) and (
2.13
) turn out to be of the class
OPS^{0}
or of the class
OPS^{-1}
and hence can be absorbed in the third terms of each equations above.
By summing it up and using the identity
\psi=\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi+\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi
one can recover the homogeneous Dirac equation as follows:

\displaystyle(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})(1+2\mathcal{E})\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi+(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})(1+2\mathcal{E})\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi=0.

Now we simply put
2\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
. Hence we deduce that the covariant Dirac equation can be reformulated as the system of the linear equation for
(\Pi^{M}_{+}\psi,\Pi^{M}_{-}\psi)
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})(1+\mathcal{E})\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi=0,\\
(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M})(1+\mathcal{E})\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi=0.\end{array}\right.

We can also rewrite the homogeneous equation using the commutator:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}((1+\mathcal{E})D_{t}+(1+\mathcal{E})\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}+[D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M},\mathcal{E}])\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi=0,\\
((1+\mathcal{E})D_{t}-(1+\mathcal{E})\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}+[D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M},\mathcal{E}])\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi=0.\end{array}\right.

We define the inverse of

as

\displaystyle(1+\mathcal{E})^{-1}=1+\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}(-\mathcal{E})^{N},

which is convergent, since the pseudodifferential operator
\mathcal{E}
has the norm

, provided that the metric satisfy (

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}+(1+\mathcal{E})^{-1}[D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M},\mathcal{E}])\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi=0,\\
(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}+(1+\mathcal{E})^{-1}[D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M},\mathcal{E}])\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi=0.\end{array}\right.

Since
(1+\mathcal{E})^{-1}
is a bounded operator,
[D_{t},\mathcal{E}]
is an element of
OPS^{0}
and
[\langle D_{x}\rangle,\mathcal{E}]
is an element of
OPS^{1}
, with possibly lower order terms. Again, we do not compute the Poisson bracket here, and we consider the commutator merely as a product of the operators.
Define

\displaystyle A^{\prime}(t,x,D_{x})=(1+\mathcal{E})^{-1}[D_{t}\pm\langle D_{x}\rangle,\mathcal{E}]\mp(1+\mathcal{E})^{-1}[\langle D_{x}\rangle,\mathcal{E}^{0}].

Then

\displaystyle D_{t}\pm\langle D_{x}\rangle+(1+\mathcal{E})^{-1}[D_{t}\pm\langle D_{x}\rangle,\mathcal{E}]=D_{t}\pm\langle D_{x}\rangle+(1+\mathcal{E})^{-1}[\pm\langle D_{x}\rangle,\mathcal{E}^{0}]+A^{\prime}(t,x,D_{x}).

The following proposition ensures that the operator

is the lower-order term satisfying the error-type estimates (
1.10
). See also the estimate (17) of
[

]
.
We define

\displaystyle\|u\|_{X_{k}}=2^{k}\|u\|_{L^{2}(A_{<-k})}+2^{\frac{k}{2}}\sup_{j\geq-k}\||x|^{-\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{L^{2}(A_{j})},

where
A_{j}=\mathbb{R}\times\{2^{j-1}<|x|<2^{j+1}\}
, and
A_{<j}=\mathbb{R}\times\{|x|<2^{j}\}
. We define the function space

\displaystyle\|u\|_{X^{s}}^{2}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\langle 2^{k}\rangle^{2s}\|S_{k}u\|_{X_{k}}^{2}

and
Y^{s}=(X^{-s})^{\prime}
, i.e. the dual of
X^{-s}
. We have

\displaystyle\|u\|_{Y^{s}}^{2}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\langle 2^{k}\rangle^{2s}\|S_{k}u\|_{X^{\prime}_{k}}^{2},

with

\displaystyle\|u\|_{X^{\prime}_{k}}=2^{-k}\|u\|_{L^{2}(A_{<-k})}+2^{-\frac{k}{2}}\sum_{j\geq-k}\||x|^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{L^{2}(A_{j})}.

For the lower order terms

2.1.
Nonlinear Dirac equations on a curved space-time

We are concerned with initial value problems for the Dirac equations with a cubic-type nonlinearity, given by

(2.16)

\begin{split}(-i\gamma^{\mu}\mathbf{D}_{\mu}+M)\psi&=(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)\gamma^{0}\psi\\
\psi|_{t=0}&=\psi_{0},\end{split}

where

is the complex conjugate transpose of the spinor

\Pi_{\pm}^{M}
introduced in the previous section and reformulate the systems (

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}+(1+\mathcal{E})^{-1}[D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M},\mathcal{E}])\Pi^{M}_{+}\psi=\Pi_{+}^{M}\Pi^{M}_{+}[(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)\psi],\\
(D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M}+(1+\mathcal{E})^{-1}[D_{t}-\langle D_{x}\rangle_{M},\mathcal{E}])\Pi^{M}_{-}\psi=\Pi_{-}^{M}\Pi^{M}_{-}[(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)\psi],\\
(\Pi^{M}_{+}\psi,\Pi^{M}_{-}\psi)|_{t=0}=(\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi_{0},\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi_{0}).\end{array}\right.

We assume that the elliptic part of the metric
[g_{ij}]_{i,j=1,\cdots,3}
satisfies the weak asymptotic flatness assumptions: (
1.12
), (
1.13
), and (
1.14
).
Now Theorem
1.2
can be restated in terms of
(\Pi^{M}_{+}\psi,\Pi^{M}_{-}\psi)
as follows:

Theorem 2.6
.

Let

and
M>0
. Suppose that the metric

satisfies (

), (
1.13
), (

) and

Due to the cubic nonlinear feature of the Cauchy problems (

), it suffices to investigate the

-estimates. Therefore in the rest of this paper, we focus on the endpoint Strichartz estimates for the half-Klein-Gordon operators

3.
Half-Klein-Gordon operators with a small perturbation

We study the Cauchy problems for the half-Klein-Gordon equations with variable coefficients:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left(D_{t}\pm\mathrm{Op}\sqrt{1+g^{ij}(t,x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}}\right)u&=f,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d},\ d\geq 3,\\

3.1.
Mollification of the half-Klein-Gordon operator

We mollify the perturbed symbol:

(3.2)

\displaystyle q(t,x,\xi):=\sqrt{1+g^{ij}(t,x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}}-\sqrt{1+|\xi|^{2}}.

To do this, we define

(3.3)

\displaystyle q_{(k)}(t,x,\xi):=\sum_{l<k-4}S_{<l}\chi_{<k-2l}S_{l}q(t,x,\xi).

Then we define the mollified half-Klein-Gordon operator:

(3.4)

\displaystyle A^{\pm}_{(k)}(t,x,D_{x}):=\sqrt{1+|D_{x}|^{2}}+\mathrm{Op}(q_{(k)}(t,x,\xi)).

This mollification gives the following: see also (32) of
[
33
]

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}q_{(k)}(t,x,\xi)|\leq c_{\alpha}\epsilon_{k}(|x|)2^{|\alpha|k}(1+2^{k}|x|)^{-|\alpha|}\langle\xi\rangle^{1-|\beta|},\quad|\alpha|\leq 2,\\
|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}q_{(k)}(t,x,\xi)|\leq c_{\alpha}\epsilon_{k}(|x|)2^{|\alpha|k}(1+2^{k}|x|)^{-1-\frac{|\alpha|}{2}}\langle\xi\rangle^{1-|\beta|},\quad|\alpha|\geq 2.\end{aligned}

We also define a global mollified operator

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}^{\pm}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}A^{\pm}_{(k)}S_{k}.

Proposition 3.1
.

The following estimates hold:

\displaystyle\|(A^{\pm}-\widetilde{A}^{\pm})u\|_{Y^{s}}\lesssim\epsilon\|u\|_{X^{s}},

\displaystyle\|(\widetilde{A}^{\pm}-A^{\pm}_{(k)})S_{l}u\|_{X^{\prime}_{k}}\lesssim\epsilon\|S_{l}u\|_{X_{k}},\quad|l-k|\leq 2,

\displaystyle\|[A^{\pm}_{(k)},S_{k}]u\|_{X^{\prime}_{k}}\lesssim\epsilon\|u\|_{X_{k}}.

Proof.

We put
\pm=+
and
A^{+}=A
for simplicity and write

(3.6)

\displaystyle A-\widetilde{A}=A_{\rm low}+A_{\rm mid}+A_{\rm high},

where

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A_{\rm low}:=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{l<k-4}(S_{<l}\chi_{\geq k-2l})(S_{l}q)\right)S_{k},\\
A_{\rm mid}:=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{l=k-4}^{l=k+4}(S_{l}q)\right)S_{k},\\
A_{\rm high}:=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{l>k+4}(S_{l}q)\right)S_{k}.\end{aligned}

Then the remaining task readily follows by the argument of
[
33
]
. We refer the readers to the proof of Proposition 1 of
[

]
.

3.2.
Scaling argument

By the definition of the mollified operator
\widetilde{A}
, we are able to concentrate on a dyadic piece of
\widetilde{A}
:

(3.8)

\displaystyle(D_{t}+A^{\pm}_{(k)})S_{k}u=0.

Now we rescale the frequency-localised equation. To do this, we set
s=\lambda t
,
y=\lambda x
, and
u_{\lambda}=S_{k}u(\lambda^{-1}s,\lambda^{-1}y)
. Then the above frequency-localised equation is transformed into

(3.9)

\displaystyle\left(D_{s}+\mathcal{A}^{\pm}_{(k)}(s,y,D_{y})\right)u_{\lambda}=0,

where

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\pm}_{(k)}(s,y,D_{y}):=\pm\mathrm{Op}_{y}(a_{(k)}(s,y,\eta)),

and

(3.11)

\displaystyle a_{(k)}(s,y,\eta):=\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+|\eta|^{2}}+q_{(k)}(\lambda^{-1}s,\lambda^{-1}y,\lambda\eta).

Now we need to show that the functions
a_{(k)}(s,y,\eta)
belong to a symbol class
S^{1}
uniform in
k
on
|\eta|\approx 1
. Indeed, we note that

\displaystyle\partial_{y}^{\alpha}q_{(k)}(\lambda^{-1}y)=\lambda^{-|\alpha|}(\partial_{x}^{\alpha}q_{(k)})(\lambda^{-1}y).

Then combining this with
the bound (
3.5
) yields the following inequality:

(3.12)

\displaystyle|\partial_{y}^{\alpha}\partial_{\eta}^{\beta}q_{(k)}(\lambda^{-1}y)|\lesssim\epsilon_{k}(\lambda^{-1}|y|)2^{k|\alpha|}\lambda^{-|\alpha|}(1+2^{k}\lambda^{-1}|y|)^{-|\alpha|}.

Furthermore, if we put
\lambda=2^{k}
, then we obtain

(3.13)

\displaystyle|\partial_{y}^{\alpha}\partial_{\eta}^{\beta}q_{(k)}(\lambda^{-1}y)|\lesssim\epsilon_{k}(\lambda^{-1}|y|)(1+|y|)^{-|\alpha|}.

Therefore, we conclude that the rescaled operators belong to a fixed perturbative symbol class at the unit frequency. All frequency-localised estimates can be proved after rescaling at frequency
\approx 1
, uniformly in
k
, and then summed over dyadic
k
.

is a remainder.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+|\xi|^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi(\delta|x|)x=\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+|\xi|^{2}}}(\phi(\delta|x|)+\delta x\phi^{\prime}(\delta|x|)\frac{x}{|x|})

is the principal symbol of the commutator. From the above computation, we see that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+|\xi|^{2}}}(\phi(\delta|x|)+\delta x\phi^{\prime}(\delta|x|)\frac{x}{|x|})\right|\gtrsim\frac{\alpha(\delta|x|)}{1+\delta|x|}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle 2\delta\phi^{\prime}(\delta|x|)+\delta^{2}x\phi^{\prime\prime}(\delta|x|)\lesssim\frac{\delta\alpha(\delta|x|)}{(1+\delta|x|)^{2}}

To determine the
L^{2}
-norm of the Poisson bracket, we need only at most fourth order derivatives of the symbol.
By choosing
\delta>0
small enough, we see that the zeroth-order derivative of the principal symbol dominates the bound.
Now we write

\displaystyle|\langle[\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+|D_{x}|^{2}},Q(x,D_{x})]u,u\rangle|

\displaystyle\geq 2\delta|\langle[\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+|D_{x}|^{2}},\phi(\delta|x|)x]D_{x}u,u\rangle|

\displaystyle\qquad-\delta|\langle[\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+|D_{x}|^{2}},\phi(\delta|x|)+x\phi^{\prime}(\delta|x|)\delta\frac{x}{|x|}]u,u\rangle|

\displaystyle\gtrsim\langle\frac{\delta\alpha(\delta|x|)}{1+\delta|x|}D_{x}u,u\rangle\approx\langle\frac{\delta\alpha(\delta|x|)}{1+\delta|x|}u,u\rangle,

where we used that the function
u
is localised at frequency
1
. The computation of the perturbative terms containing
q_{(k)}
follows in the similar way and we get the desired lower bound (
3.19
) provided that
\epsilon<\delta
.

Low frequency case also follows immediately. Indeed,
if
2^{k}\leq 1
, i.e.,
k\leq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\langle[\sqrt{1+|D_{x}|^{2}},\phi(\delta|x|)x]D_{x}u,u\rangle

\displaystyle\gtrsim\langle\frac{2^{k}\alpha(\delta|x|)}{1+\delta|x|}u,u\rangle.

We conclude that the estimates (
3.17
) hold for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

With

(3.30)

Proof.

\displaystyle=\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\langle u,(D_{t}+\widetilde{A}^{\pm})\widetilde{K}^{\pm}g\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\,dt-\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\langle u,[(D_{t}+\widetilde{A}^{\pm})\widetilde{K}^{\pm}-1]g\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\,dt.

\displaystyle=\int_{T_{-}}^{T^{+}}\langle D_{t}u,\widetilde{K}^{\pm}g\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\,dt+\langle u,\widetilde{K}^{\pm}g\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\bigg|^{T^{+}}_{T_{-}}.

\displaystyle\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\langle u,g\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\,dt=\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\langle(D_{t}+\widetilde{A}^{\pm})u,\widetilde{K}^{\pm}g\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\,dt-\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\langle u,[(D_{t}+\widetilde{A}^{\pm})\widetilde{K}^{\pm}-1]g\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\,dt+\langle u,\widetilde{K}^{\pm}g\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\bigg|^{T^{+}}_{T_{-}},

\displaystyle\left|\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\langle u,g\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\,dt\right|

T^{\pm}

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-s+\sigma}L^{p}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle u,g\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\,dt\right|:\|g\|_{\langle D_{x}\rangle^{s-\sigma}L^{p^{\prime}}_{t}L^{q^{\prime}}_{x}}\leq 1\right\}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{s}_{x}\cap X^{s}}+\|(D_{t}+\widetilde{A}^{\pm})u\|_{Y^{s}},

which completes the proof.

, we see that

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\Pi_{\pm}^{M}[(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)\psi]\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{s}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)\psi\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{s}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\pm_{1},\pm_{2},\pm_{3}\in\{+,-\}}\|[(\Pi^{M}_{\pm_{1}}\psi)^{\dagger}\Pi^{M}_{\pm_{2}}\psi]\Pi_{\pm_{3}}^{M}\psi\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{s}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\tau}\|\Pi_{\pm_{\tau(1)}}^{M}\psi\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{s}_{x}}\|\Pi_{\pm_{\tau(2)}}^{M}\psi\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}\|\Pi_{\pm_{\tau(3)}}^{M}\psi\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 8\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{X}^{s}}^{3},

where
\tau:\{1,2,3\}\rightarrow\{1,2,3\}
are permutations. Then we have

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{X}^{s}}

\displaystyle=\|\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi\|_{\mathcal{X}^{s}_{+}}+\|\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi\|_{\mathcal{X}_{-}^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi_{0}\|_{H^{s}_{x}}+\|\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi_{0}\|_{H^{s}_{x}}+16\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{X}^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\eta+16\delta^{3}.

If we choose
\delta<\frac{1}{8}
, then
\psi\in\mathcal{X}^{s}
since we assume that
\eta
is small. Now we are concerned with the scattering. Let
\psi
be the solution so that
\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\psi\in\mathcal{X}_{\pm}^{s}
. It suffices to prove that
\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\psi(t)
is a Cauchy-sequence in
H^{s}
as
t\to\infty
, i.e. the quantity

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\psi(t)-\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\psi(t^{\prime})\|_{H^{s}_{x}}

is arbitrarily small provided that
t>t^{\prime}>T
for some large
T>0
. We first write

\displaystyle\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\psi=\widetilde{K}^{\pm}[\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\Pi_{\pm}^{M}((\psi^{\dagger}\psi)\psi)]+\phi^{\pm},

where
\phi^{\pm}
is an error of the parametrix. Then we use Theorem
1.1
to get

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\psi(t)-\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\psi(t^{\prime})\|_{H^{s}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\Pi_{\pm}^{M}\Pi_{\pm}^{M}[(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)\psi]\|_{L^{1}_{[t^{\prime},\infty)}H^{s}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)\psi]\|_{L^{1}_{[t^{\prime},\infty)}H^{s}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\psi\|_{L^{2}_{[t^{\prime},\infty)}L^{\infty}_{x}}^{2}\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{s}_{x}}.

Since
\psi
is the solution, we have
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}\leq\|\Pi_{+}^{M}\psi\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}+\|\Pi_{-}^{M}\psi\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}<\infty
, and hence we can choose a large
T>0
so that the quantity
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}_{[t^{\prime},\infty)}L^{\infty}_{x}}
is arbitrarily small for any
t^{\prime}>T
. This completes the proof of Theorem
2.6
.

4.
Microlocal analysis and phase space transforms

This section is devoted to a brief introduction of the phase space transforms and preliminaries for microlocal analysis, which will be used for the outgoing parametrix construction later. We refer the readers to
[
48
]
for an expository introduction and also Section 6,7 of
[
49
]
and Section 9,10 of
[
33
]
.

Throughout this section, we often use functions in the Schwartz class
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is defined by the set of all smooth functions
f
satisfying the inequality
\sup_{x}|x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}_{x}f(x)|<\infty
.
We also use Schwartz functions
u\in\mathscr{S}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined on the phase space. Due to a suitable diffeomorphism between
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we abuse notation and simply write
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

4.1.
Bargman transforms

We begin with the introduction of the Bargman transform, which is a phase space transform.

Definition 4.1
.

The Bargman transform
T
of a tempered distribution
f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by

(4.1)

\displaystyle Tf(x,\xi)=c_{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}}e^{i\xi\cdot(x-y)}f(y)\,dy,

where
c_{d}=2^{-\frac{d}{2}}\pi^{-\frac{3}{4}d}
, as in
[
48
]
.

We refer the readers to
[
48
]
for several properties of the Bargman transform
T
in details. Here we only remark that the transform
T
is unitary, i.e.,
T^{*}T=I
.
Given a tempered distribution
a\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define the corresponding Weyl operator
a^{w}
as an operator mapping
a^{w}:\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
given by

\displaystyle a^{w}(x,D_{x})f(x)=\int a\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\right)e^{i\xi\cdot(x-y)}\,d\xi.

We also introduce the distance function
d:T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
on the phase space defined by

\displaystyle d((x_{1},\xi_{1}),(x_{2},\xi_{2}))^{2}=|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2}+|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|^{2}.

We define the symbol classes
S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}
adapted to the literature
[
23
]
.

Definition 4.2
.

If
m
is a real number then
S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the set of all
a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that for all multi-indices
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
the function
a
satisfies the bound

\displaystyle|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\partial^{\beta}_{\xi}a(x,\xi)|\leq c_{\alpha\beta}(1+|\xi|)^{m-\rho|\beta|+\delta|\alpha|},\quad x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
0\leq\rho,\delta\leq 1
.
We also denote by
OPS^{m}_{\rho,\delta}
the corresponding class of symbols in
S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}
.

Given a pseudodifferential operator in the Weyl calculus
A^{w}\in OPS^{0}_{00}
, we define its phase space image to be the conjugated operator
TAT^{*}
. The kernel of the phase space image is called the phase space kernel of the operator
A^{w}
.
In order to go beyond a fixed-time scale, we introduce the rescaled version
T_{\frac{1}{s}}
of the Bargman transform
T
so called the FBI transform:

\displaystyle T_{\frac{1}{s}}u(t,x,\xi)=c_{d}s^{-\frac{d}{4}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2s}}e^{i\xi\cdot(x-y)}u(t,y)\,dy.

As the Bargman transforms hold
T^{*}T=I
, we still have
T^{*}_{\frac{1}{s}}T_{\frac{1}{s}}=I
. Corresponding to this scaling, the distance function
d
is now rescaled in
t^{\frac{1}{2}}\times t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
-scale as

\displaystyle d_{t}((x,\xi),(y,\eta))^{2}=t^{-1}|x-y|^{2}+t|\xi-\eta|^{2}.

4.2.
A long time phase space parametrix

As
[
33
,
49
]
we are concerned with the following global-in-time evolution equation:

(4.2)

\displaystyle(D_{t}+a^{w}(t,x,D_{x})-ib^{w}(t,x,D_{x})+c^{w}(t,x,D_{x}))u=0,\ t>0,

with the symbols
a,b,c
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In other words, we allow the symbols
a,b,c
equipped with time-dependent scales.

Definition 4.3
.

We define the symbol class
S^{(k)}
to be the set of all symbols on
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying the bound

\displaystyle|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\partial^{\beta}_{\xi}a(x,\xi)|\leq c_{\alpha\beta},\quad|\alpha|+|\beta|\geq k.

Furthermore, we define time-dependent pseudodifferential operators in
\mathbb{R}\times T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the corresponding symbol classes
\ell^{1}S^{(k)}
whose symbols satisfy the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{j(\frac{|\alpha|-|\beta|}{2}+1)}\|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\partial^{\beta}_{\xi}a(t,x,\xi)\|_{L^{\infty}(\{t\approx 2^{j}\})}\leq c_{\alpha\beta},\quad|\alpha|+|\beta|\geq k.

We denote by
\ell^{1}S^{(2)}_{\epsilon}
the subclass of
\ell^{1}S^{(k)}
whose seminorms are
O(\epsilon)
when
|\alpha|+|\beta|=2
.

We consider the evolution equations of the form (
4.2
) with real symbols
a\in\ell^{1}S^{(2)}_{\epsilon}
and
b\in\ell^{1}S^{(1)}
with
b\geq 0
and a possibly complex symbol
c\in\ell^{1}S^{(0)}
. In what follows, the symbol
a
will derive the evolution, while the symbol
b
play a role as a damping term and
c
can be viewed as a bounded error. We would like to mention further that the symbols
a,b,c
here are not frequency-localised.
We consider the Hamilton flow of the evolution
D_{t}+a^{w}
and we denote its evolutions by
\chi(t,s)
. We use the notation
t\mapsto(x_{t},\xi_{t})
to denote the trajectories of the flow.
We define the function
\psi
by the integration of the symbol
b
along the Hamilton flow:

\displaystyle\psi(t,x_{t},\xi_{t})=\int_{1}^{t}b(\sigma,x_{\sigma},\xi_{\sigma})\,d\sigma.

We set the lower limit of the integration above to be
1
. In our practical analysis, we only consider the differences
\psi(t,x_{t},\xi_{t})-\psi(s,x_{s},\xi_{s})
. We end this section with the pointwise bound for the phase space kernels of the evolution operator
S(t,s)
. We refer the readers to
[
49
]
for the proof.

Theorem 4.4

(Theorem 31 of
[
49
]
)
.

Let
a\in\ell^{1}S^{(2)}_{\epsilon},\,b\in\ell^{1}S^{(1)}
be real symbols with
b\geq 0
and
c\in\ell^{1}S^{(0)}
. Then for
0<s<t
the kernels
K(t,s)
of the conjugated operators
T_{\frac{1}{t}}S(t,s)T^{*}_{\frac{1}{s}}
satisfy the bound

(4.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |K(t,x,\xi_{t},s,x_{s},\xi)|\lesssim\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{\frac{d}{4}}\left(1+(\psi(x_{t},\xi_{t})-\psi(x_{s},\xi_{s}))^{2}+t^{-1}|x-x_{t}|^{2}+s|\xi-\xi_{s}|^{2}\right)^{-N},\end{aligned}

for any
N>0
.

The parametrix construction

We finally arrive at the proof of the properties of the outgoing parametrix

for the operators

5.1.
Reduction to Weyl quantization

We will construct the phase-space parametrix of the scaled operator

in the Weyl calculus as
[

. Thus we need to replace the standard quantization by the Weyl quantization. More precisely, for each

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Op}_{y}(a_{(k)}(s,y,\eta))=a^{w}_{(k)}(s,y,D_{y})+R_{k}(s,y,D_{y}),

where

is the Weyl-Operator with the same symbol and

(5.2)

\displaystyle R_{k}=\mathrm{Op}_{y}(r_{k}(s,y,\eta)),

(5.3)

\displaystyle r_{k}=\frac{i}{2}\partial_{y}\cdot\partial_{\eta}a_{(k)}+\sum_{|\alpha|\geq 2}\frac{1}{\alpha!}\left(\frac{i}{2}\right)^{|\alpha|}\partial_{y}^{\alpha}\partial_{\eta}^{\alpha}a_{(k)}.

We refer to Theorem 18.5.10. of

Then the bound (

\displaystyle|r_{k}|\lesssim\epsilon_{k}(1+|y|)^{-1},

which ensures

is an acceptable lower-order error terms. We also remark that the principal symbols of

and

are identical.

5.2.
A perturbation of the half Klein-Gordon operators

Now we shall construct a parametrix for the scaled operator

5.3.
The parametrix construction

We partition the annulus

\rho_{-1}(\xi)+\rho_{0}(\xi)+\rho_{1}(\xi)=\sum_{\pm}\sum_{j\geq 0}p_{j}^{\pm}(x,\xi),

\displaystyle\textrm{supp }p_{j}^{\pm}\subset\left\{2^{j-1}<|x|<2^{j+1},\pm\frac{x}{|x|}\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\geq-2^{-5}\right\},\quad j\geq 1

Now Proposition
3.5
follows directly from the following theorem.

.

For each

, there is an outgoing evolution operator

(2)

(5.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|x^{\alpha}(D_{t}+\mathcal{A}^{+}_{(k)})\mathcal{S}^{+}_{(k)}(t,s)\mathcal{P}^{+}_{j}\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}\lesssim(2^{j}+|t-s|)^{-N},\\

where

(4)

Outgoing parametrix:

(5.7)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|<2^{-10}(|t-s|+2^{j})\}}\mathcal{S}^{+}_{(k)}(t,s)\mathcal{P}^{+}_{j}\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}\lesssim(2^{j}+|t-s|)^{-N}.

(5)

Finite speed:

(5.8)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|>2^{10}(|t-s|+2^{j})\}}\mathcal{S}^{+}_{(k)}(t,s)\mathcal{P}_{j}\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}\lesssim(2^{j}+|t-s|)^{-N}.

(6)

Frequency localisation:

(5.9)

\displaystyle\|(\mathbf{1}_{L^{2}}-S_{\frac{1}{16}\leq\cdot\leq 16})\mathcal{S}^{+}_{(k)}(t,s)\mathcal{P}^{+}_{j}\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}\lesssim(2^{j}+|t-s|)^{-N}.

(7)

Pointwise decay:

(5.10)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}^{+}_{(k)}(t,s)\mathcal{P}^{+}_{j}\|_{L^{1}\rightarrow L^{\infty}}\lesssim\lambda^{\theta}(1+|t-s|)^{-\frac{d-1+\theta}{2}}.

5.1
implies Proposition

.

We follow the argument by

At first we shall establish bounds on the kernel of

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}(t,s)=T_{\frac{1}{t}}\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)P_{\frac{1}{4}\leq\cdot\leq 4}T^{*}_{\frac{1}{s}},

using Theorem

To do this, we note that
\mathcal{A}_{(k)}-\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+|\xi|^{2}}\in\ell^{1}S^{(2)}_{\epsilon}
, where

. Thus in view of the work by
[

(5.11)

\displaystyle\left(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}+a^{w}_{\lambda}(t,x,D_{x})-ib^{w}_{\lambda}(t,x,D_{x})\right)u=0,

where
\langle D_{x}\rangle=\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+|D_{x}|^{2}}
and
a_{\lambda}\in\ell^{1}S^{(2)}_{\epsilon}

We let
\chi_{\lambda}(t,s)
denote the Hamilton flow associated to the evolution

\displaystyle\chi_{\lambda}(t,s)=\mu_{t}\circ\mathscr{X}(t,s)\circ\mu_{s}^{-1}.

Proposition 5.2

(Proposition 3.2.7 of
[

If the symbol

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial(x_{t},\xi_{s})}{\partial(x_{s},\xi_{t})}:=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial x_{s}}&\frac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial\xi_{t}}\\

We pay a special attention to the Jacobian matrix

Lemma 5.3

Let

\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\Phi^{d}_{KG}(\xi)=\langle\xi\rangle_{\lambda}^{-3}(\langle\xi\rangle_{\lambda}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}-\xi\otimes\xi),

\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{d}
of the matrix are

\displaystyle\mu_{1}=\cdots=\mu_{d-1}=\langle\xi\rangle_{\lambda}^{-1},\ \mu_{d}=\lambda^{-2}\langle\xi\rangle_{\lambda}^{-3}.

Recall that

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\approx\cdots\approx\mu_{d-1}\approx\frac{1}{2^{-2k}+1}\approx 1,

and

\displaystyle\mu_{d}\approx\frac{2^{-2k}}{(2^{-2k}+1)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\approx\frac{2^{-2k}}{2^{-3k}(1+2^{2k})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\approx 2^{-2k}.

Remark 5.4
.

On the other hand, in the case

,

Remark 5.5
.

It is remarkable to compare the results above with the similar results for the wave operator. We define the

\displaystyle\Phi^{d}_{\rm w}(\xi)=|\xi|^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}-\xi\otimes\xi.

Then the matrix
\Phi^{d}_{\rm w}(\xi)
has the maximal rank

for non-zero
\xi
.

As in the previous section we define the integral of the damping term

\displaystyle\Psi_{\lambda}(t,x_{t},\xi_{t})=\int_{1}^{t}b_{\lambda}(\sigma,x_{\sigma},\xi_{\sigma})\,d\sigma,

along the
\chi_{\lambda}
-flow. Then the integral
\Psi_{\lambda}
is the
\mu
-conjugate of the integral

of

-flow.

Proposition 5.6
.

If
a_{\lambda}\in\ell^{1}S^{(2)}_{\epsilon}
with a sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
and
b_{\lambda}\in\ell^{1}S^{(1)}
and

(5.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\Psi_{\lambda}(x_{t},\xi_{t})-\Psi_{\lambda}(x_{s},\xi_{s}))}{\partial(x_{s},\xi_{t})}=\begin{bmatrix}O(s^{-\frac{1}{2}})&O(t^{\frac{1}{2}})\end{bmatrix}.

For
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\delta>0

\|\cdot\|_{\delta}
denote the norm defined by
\|x\|_{\delta}=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d-1}^{2}+\delta^{2}x_{d}^{2}}
.
Then we can establish the pointwise bounds for the kernels of the operators
\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}(t,s)
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Consider the evolution equation:

\displaystyle(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}+a^{w}_{\lambda}(t,x,D_{x})-ib^{w}_{\lambda}(t,x,D_{x}))u=0,

where the symbols
a_{\lambda}\in\ell^{1}S^{(2)}_{\epsilon}
and
b_{\lambda}\in\ell^{1}S^{(1)}
are real with a sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
. We assume that the symbols
a_{\lambda}
and
b_{\lambda}
are frequency-localised at
|\xi|\approx 1
. Let
t>s
and define the frequency-localised
L^{2}
-evolution operators of the equation (
5.11
) by
\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)
.
Then, the kernels
\mathfrak{K}_{(k)}
of the phase space image
\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}
of the operator
\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)
satisfies the following pointwise bound:

(5.14)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |\mathfrak{K}_{(k)}(t,x,\xi_{t},s,x_{s},\xi)|&\lesssim\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{-\frac{d}{4}}\left(1+(\Psi_{\lambda}(x_{t},\xi_{t})-\Psi_{\lambda}(x_{s},\xi_{s}))^{2}+t^{-1}\|x-x_{t}\|_{\lambda}^{2}+s\|\xi-\xi_{s}\|_{\lambda^{-1}}^{2}\right)^{-N}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(1+td(|\xi_{s}|,[1/{64},64])\right)^{-N},\end{aligned}

with
\lambda=2^{k}
.

Proof.

To obtain the pointwise bound of the kernels, we follow the proof of Theorem 5 of
[
33
]
. In other words, we exploit Theorem
4.4
together with a
\chi_{\lambda}
-conjugation with respect to the flow for the rescaled half Klein-Gordon operator
D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}
on the flat space. We denote the conjugated operator of the evolution operator
\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)
by

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}_{(k)}(t,s)=e^{it\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)e^{-is\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}.

Then a computation yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathscr{T}_{(k)}(t,s)=-ie^{it\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}(a^{w}_{\lambda}(t,x,D_{x})-ib^{w}_{\lambda}(t,x,D_{x}))e^{-it\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}\mathscr{T}_{(k)}(t,s),

and hence we deduce that the conjugated operator
\mathscr{T}_{(k)}(t,s)
is the evolution operator associated to the pseudodifferential operator

\displaystyle e^{it\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}(a^{w}_{\lambda}(t,x,D_{x})-ib^{w}_{\lambda}(t,x,D_{x}))e^{-it\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}.

Furthermore the operator
\mathscr{T}_{\lambda}
can be viewed as the pseudodifferential operator of the form:

\displaystyle\tilde{a}^{w}(t,x,D_{x})-i\tilde{b}^{w}(t,x,D_{x})+\tilde{c}^{w}(t,x,D_{x}),

where the operator
\tilde{c}^{w}
turns out to be a remainder term such that the symbol
\tilde{c}
is in
\ell^{1}S^{(0)}
. This implies that the phase space kernel of the operator
\mathscr{T}_{(k)}(t,s)
satisfies the pointwise bound as Theorem
4.4
.
Now we pay attention to our evolution equation (
5.11
). We rewrite the phase space image
\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}(t,s)
of the evolution
\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)
as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}(t,s)

\displaystyle=T_{\frac{1}{t}}\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)T_{\frac{1}{s}}^{*}=T_{\frac{1}{t}}e^{-it\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}e^{it\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)e^{-is\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}e^{is\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}T_{\frac{1}{s}}^{*}

\displaystyle=T_{\frac{1}{t}}e^{-it\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}\mathscr{T}_{(k)}(t,s)e^{is\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}T_{\frac{1}{s}}^{*}

\displaystyle=T_{\frac{1}{t}}e^{-it\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}S_{\frac{1}{64}\leq\cdot\leq 64}\mathscr{T}_{(k)}(t,s)S_{\frac{1}{64}\leq\cdot\leq 64}e^{is\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}T_{\frac{1}{s}}^{*}.

Here we used the frequency-localisation property (
5.5
) of the evolution
\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)
to obtain the last equality. After an application of the identity
T^{*}T=I
of the FBI transform we see that the phase space kernels
\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}(t,s)
turns out to be the three fold-composition of the conjugated operators of the form:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}(t,s)=\left(T_{\frac{1}{t}}e^{-it\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}P_{\frac{1}{64}\leq\cdot\leq 64}T^{*}_{\frac{1}{t}}\right)\left(T_{\frac{1}{t}}\mathscr{T}_{(k)}(t,s)T^{*}_{\frac{1}{s}}\right)\left(T_{\frac{1}{s}}S_{\frac{1}{64}\leq\cdot\leq 64}e^{is\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}T^{*}_{\frac{1}{s}}\right).

Now we observe the rapid decay of each operators using Proposition 8 of
[
49
]
.
In fact, the first operator
T_{\frac{1}{t}}e^{-it\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}S_{\frac{1}{64}\leq\cdot\leq 64}T^{*}_{\frac{1}{t}}
is the conjugation of the usual forward propagator of the rescaled half-Klein-Gordon operators in the flat space-time. After a suitable change of variables, we see that its kernel satisfies a rapid decay on the
t^{\frac{1}{2}}\times t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
-scale away from the graph of the map
\mu_{t}
with all the directions tangent to the characteristic hypersurface

\Sigma_{\lambda}=\{(\tau,\xi)\colon\tau=\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+|\xi|^{2}}\},

and the kernel decays rapidly on the
(t\lambda^{-2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\times(t\lambda^{-2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
scale away from the same graph along the normal to the surface
\Sigma_{\lambda}
. The kernel also decays away from the support of the projection operator
S_{\frac{1}{64}\leq\cdot\leq 64}
at the same time.

Meanwhile, we have already seen that the kernel of the second operator
T_{\frac{1}{t}}\mathscr{T}_{(k)}(t,s)T^{*}_{\frac{1}{s}}
satisfies the pointwise bound as Theorem
4.4
. The third operator
T_{\frac{1}{s}}S_{\frac{1}{64}\leq\cdot\leq 64}e^{is\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}}T^{*}_{\frac{1}{s}}
also turns out to be the conjugation of the usual backward Klein-Gordon propagator and hence its kernel decay rapidly in the similar way as the first operator.
In consequence, we see that the composition of the three operators replaces the Hamilton flow associated to
a
by the Hamilton flow associated to
a_{\lambda}
and the function
\psi
with
\Psi_{\lambda}
in the kernel bounds. This completes the proof of Theorem
5.7
.

5.4.
Proof of dispersive inequality (
5.10
)

Without loss of generality we may assume that the initial time
s
is greater than
1
. In order to establish the required estimates, it suffices to prove the bounds for
\theta=0
and
\theta=1
. The required bound follows after an interpolation.

Case 1:
|t-s|\lesssim 1

If
|t-s|\lesssim 1
, a simple use of the Sobolev embedding together with the
L^{2}
-bounds for the evolution operator gives

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)\mathcal{P}_{j}u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)\mathcal{P}_{j}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\lesssim\|\mathcal{P}_{j}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{x}},

where we used the fact that the evolution operators
\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)
are localised in the unit annular
\{\frac{1}{2}<|\xi|<2\}
uniformly in
\lambda
.
Since
|t-s|\lesssim 1
, we obviously have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)\mathcal{P}_{j}u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|t-s|)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{x}},

and hence we see a better estimate on the small time scale.
Now we consider the long time regime, i.e.,
1\ll|t-s|
. We divide this long-time case into the two cases,
s\leq|t-s|
and
|t-s|\leq s
.

Case 2:
s\leq|t-s|

We consider the case
s\leq|t-s|
, which becomes our main propagation regime, in which case one can neglect the damping term by the construction of the symbol
b_{\lambda}(t,x,\xi)
in Section
5.5
. Here we choose the initial data
u(s,y)
to be the Dirac delta distribution so that
\|u(s)\|_{L^{1}_{x}}=1
. Then applying the FBI transform we have

\displaystyle T_{\frac{1}{s}}u(s,x_{s},\xi)=c_{d}s^{-\frac{d}{4}}e^{-\frac{x_{s}^{2}}{2s}}e^{i\xi\cdot x_{s}},

with an absolute constant
c_{d}
, which depends only on the spatial dimension
d\geq 3
.
Now we apply the phase-space image of the
L^{2}
-evolution operators
\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}(t,s)
and we write in the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}(t,s)[T_{\frac{1}{s}}u](t,x,\xi_{t})=\int\mathfrak{K}_{(k)}(t,x,\xi_{t},s,x_{s},\xi)T_{\frac{1}{s}}u(s,x_{s},\xi)\,dx_{s}d\xi.

By the use of the definition of the phase-space image of the evolution operators and the identity
T^{*}_{\frac{1}{t}}T_{\frac{1}{t}}=I
, we also have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}(t,s)T_{\frac{1}{s}}u

\displaystyle=(T_{\frac{1}{t}}\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)T_{\frac{1}{s}}^{*}T_{\frac{1}{s}}u(s))=T_{\frac{1}{t}}\mathcal{S}_{(k)}(t,s)u(s)=T_{\frac{1}{t}}u(t),

and hence we deduce that

\displaystyle T_{\frac{1}{t}}u(t,x,\xi_{t})=\int\mathfrak{K}_{(k)}(t,x,\xi_{t},s,x_{s},\xi)T_{\frac{1}{s}}u(s,x_{s},\xi)\,dx_{s}d\xi.

We use the kernel bound Theorem
5.7
to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int\mathfrak{K}_{(k)}(t,x,\xi_{t},s,x_{s},\xi)T_{\frac{1}{s}}u(s,x_{s},\xi)\,dx_{s}d\xi\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{-\frac{d}{4}}\int(1+t^{-1}\|x-x_{t}(x_{s},\xi_{t})\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}(1+s\|\xi-\xi_{s}(x_{t},\xi_{s})\|_{\lambda^{-1}}^{2})^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{x_{s}^{2}}{2s}}\,d\xi dx_{s},

for any
N>0
. Recall that the distorted Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{\delta}
is defined by
\|x\|_{\delta}^{2}=|x_{1}|^{2}+|x_{2}|^{2}+\cdots+\delta^{2}|x_{d}|^{2}
. The bounds for the integral follow in a straightforward way. Indeed, we see that

\displaystyle\int(1+s\|\xi-\xi_{s}(x_{t},\xi_{s})\|_{\lambda^{-1}}^{2})^{-N/2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\|\xi-\xi_{s}(x_{t},\xi_{s})\|_{\lambda^{-1}}\leq 2s^{-\frac{1}{2}}}(1+s\|\xi-\xi_{s}(x_{t},\xi_{s})\|_{\lambda^{-1}}^{2})^{-N/2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\|\xi-\xi_{s}(x_{t},\xi_{s})\|_{\lambda^{-1}}\geq 2s^{-\frac{1}{2}}}(1+s\|\xi-\xi_{s}(x_{t},\xi_{s})\|_{\lambda^{-1}}^{2})^{-N/2}\,d\xi.

Then we have

\displaystyle\int_{\|\xi-\xi_{s}(x_{t},\xi_{s})\|_{\lambda^{-1}}\leq 2s^{-\frac{1}{2}}}(1+s\|\xi-\xi_{s}(x_{t},\xi_{s})\|_{\lambda^{-1}}^{2})^{-N/2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\|\xi-\xi_{s}(x_{t},\xi_{s})\|_{\lambda^{-1}}\leq 2s^{-\frac{1}{2}}}1\,d\xi\lesssim\lambda s^{-\frac{d}{2}},

and

\displaystyle\int_{\|\xi-\xi_{s}(x_{t},\xi_{s})\|_{\lambda^{-1}}\geq 2s^{-\frac{1}{2}}}(1+s\|\xi-\xi_{s}(x_{t},\xi_{s})\|_{\lambda^{-1}}^{2})^{-N/2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda s^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int_{2}^{\infty}(1+\rho^{2})^{-N/2}\rho^{d-1}\,d\rho\lesssim\lambda s^{-\frac{d}{2}},

when
N>d
. For the integration in
x_{s}
, we note that the map
x_{s}\mapsto x_{t}(x_{s},\xi_{t})
is Lipschitz-continuous and the Lipschitz constant is bounded by
O(1)
. Now we integrate with respect to
x_{s}
in the similar way as above to get the bound

\displaystyle\left|\int\mathfrak{K}_{(k)}(t,x,\xi_{t},s,x_{s},\xi)T_{\frac{1}{s}}u(s,x_{s},\xi)\,dx_{s}d\xi\right|\lesssim\lambda t^{-\frac{d}{4}}\left(1+t^{-1}\|x-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2}\right)^{-N/2}.

Finally, we apply
T^{*}_{\frac{1}{t}}
to the
T_{\frac{1}{t}}u(t,x,\xi_{t})
and write in the form

\displaystyle T^{*}_{\frac{1}{t}}\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}(t,s)[T_{\frac{1}{s}}u]=[T^{*}_{\frac{1}{t}}T_{\frac{1}{t}}u(t,x,\xi_{t})](t,y)=u(t,y)

and hence we have the bound as

\displaystyle|u(t,y)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda t^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int(1+t^{-1}\|x-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|y-x|^{2}}{2t}}\,dxd\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda t^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int(1+t^{-1}\|x-y\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/4}\,dx\int(1+t^{-1}\|y-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/4}d\xi_{t}.

By the straightforward way as the previous integration we get

\displaystyle\int(1+t^{-1}\|x-y\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/4}\,dx\lesssim\lambda^{-1}t^{\frac{d}{2}},

and hence we finally obtain the integral

\displaystyle|u(t,y)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int(1+t^{-1}\|y-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/4}d\xi_{t}.

Here the exponent
-\frac{N}{4}
plays no crucial role and we may put
\frac{N}{4}\to N
for simplicity.

It suffices to show that the above integral satisfies the estimate

(5.18)

\displaystyle\int(1+t^{-1}|y-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)|^{2})^{-N}d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\min\{1,\lambda t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\}.

Thus it is not harmful to assume
\lambda^{2}\ll t
.
Now we consider the two cases:
t^{-1/2}\|y-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0))\|_{\lambda}\lesssim 1
and
t^{-1/2}\|y-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0))\|_{\lambda}\gg 1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle|u(t,y)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\|y-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}\lesssim t^{\frac{1}{2}}}(1+t^{-1}\|y-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N}d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\|y-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}\gg t^{\frac{1}{2}}}(1+t^{-1}\|y-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N}d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle:=I_{1}+I_{2}.

We first consider
I_{1}
.
We let
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be any fixed point and set

\displaystyle G_{1}^{<}:=\{\|y-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}\lesssim t^{\frac{1}{2}}\},\quad G_{1}^{>}:=\{\|y-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}\gg t^{\frac{1}{2}}\},

\displaystyle G_{2}^{<}:=\{\|x_{t}(\eta,0)-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}\lesssim t^{\frac{1}{2}}\},\quad G_{2}^{>}:=\{\|x_{t}(\eta,0)-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}\gg t^{\frac{1}{2}}\}

Then we rewrite the above integral
I_{1}
as

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{G_{1}^{<}}(1+t^{-1}\|y-x_{t}(\eta,0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}(1+t^{-1}\|x_{t}(\eta,0)-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{G_{1}^{<}\cap G_{2}^{<}}(1+t^{-1}\|y-x_{t}(\eta,0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}(1+t^{-1}\|x_{t}(\eta,0)-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{G_{1}^{<}\cap G_{2}^{>}}(1+t^{-1}\|y-x_{t}(\eta,0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}(1+t^{-1}\|x_{t}(\eta,0)-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle:=I_{11}+I_{12}.

Then

\displaystyle I_{11}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{G_{1}^{<}\cap G_{2}^{<}}(1+t^{-1}\|x_{t}(\eta,0)-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}d\xi_{t}.

In order to control the above integral, we consider the inverse Lipschitz constant of the map
\xi_{t}\to x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)
. After an appropriate use of change of variables, (or the diagonalisation of the Jacobian matrix of the map
\xi_{t}\mapsto x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)
we have the inequality:

(5.19)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |\xi_{j}-\eta_{j}|&\lesssim t^{-1}|x_{j}(\xi,0)-x_{j}(\eta,0)|,\quad j=1,2,\cdots,d-1,\\
|\xi_{d}-\eta_{d}|&\lesssim t^{-1}\lambda^{2}|x_{d}(\xi,0)-x_{d}(\eta,0)|.\end{aligned}

By using the above inequality (
5.19
), we have

\displaystyle t\|\xi_{t}-\eta\|_{\lambda^{-1}}\lesssim\|x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)-x_{t}(\eta,0)\|_{\lambda}\lesssim t^{\frac{1}{2}},

and hence

\displaystyle|\xi_{j}-\eta_{j}|\lesssim t^{-\frac{1}{2}},\ j=1,2,\cdots,d-1,\quad|\xi_{d}-\eta_{d}|\lesssim t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda.

Thus we have

\displaystyle I_{11}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{G_{1}^{<}\cap G_{2}^{<}}(1+t^{-1}\|x_{t}(\eta,0)-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\|\xi-\eta\|_{\lambda^{-1}}\lesssim t^{-\frac{1}{2}}}1\,d\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda t^{-\frac{d}{2}},

where we simply get the measure of the support.
Now we control the integral
I_{12}
:

\displaystyle I_{12}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{G_{1}^{<}\cap G_{2}^{>}}(1+t^{-1}\|x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)-x_{t}(\eta,0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}\,d\xi_{t}.

Using the inverse Lipschitz constant of the map
\xi_{t}\mapsto x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)
we have
\|x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)-x_{t}(\eta,0)\|_{\lambda}\gtrsim t\|\xi_{t}-\eta\|_{\lambda^{-1}}
. If
\|\xi_{t}-\eta\|_{\lambda^{-1}}\lesssim t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, then we obtain the bound

. On the other hand, if

\displaystyle\int_{G_{1}^{<}\cap G_{2}^{>}}(1+t^{-1}\|x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)-x_{t}(\eta,0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}\,d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\|\xi-\eta\|_{\lambda^{-1}}\gg t^{-\frac{1}{2}}}(1+t\|\xi-\eta\|_{\lambda^{-1}}^{2})^{-N/2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda t^{-\frac{d}{2}}.

We consider the integral
I_{2}
. We decompose it into the two integrals in the similar way and get

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{G_{1}^{>}}(1+t^{-1}\|y-x_{t}(\eta,0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}(1+t^{-1}\|x_{t}(\eta,0)-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{G_{1}^{>}\cap G_{2}^{<}}(1+t^{-1}\|y-x_{t}(\eta,0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}(1+t^{-1}\|x_{t}(\eta,0)-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{G_{1}^{>}\cap G_{2}^{>}}(1+t^{-1}\|y-x_{t}(\eta,0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}(1+t^{-1}\|x_{t}(\eta,0)-x_{t}(\xi_{t},0)\|_{\lambda}^{2})^{-N/2}d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle:=I_{21}+I_{22}.

Then we see that the control of the integrals
I_{21}
and
I_{22}
follow in the identical manner as the estimates of the integral
I_{1}
. We omit the details.

Case 3:
|t-s|\leq s

If
|t-s|\leq 2^{j}
, we reinitialise the time scale so that the initial time is set to be
|t-s|
. We need the bounds on the symbol of the damping term

Construction of the function
b_{\lambda}=b_{\lambda}(t,x,\xi)
.

We define the increasing bounded function
e(s)
to be

\displaystyle e(s)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\epsilon(\mathfrak{s})}{\mathfrak{s}}\,d\mathfrak{s}.

We choose a smooth, non-decreasing cut-off function
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\phi(s)=0
when
s\in(-\infty,0)
and
\phi=1
on
(1,\infty)
. Now we define the function
b_{\lambda}
by

\displaystyle b_{\lambda}(t,x,\xi)=t^{-\frac{3}{4}}\left(1-\phi(b_{1})\phi(b_{2})\phi(b_{3})\phi(b_{4})\phi(b_{5})\right),

where

(1)

b_{1}
is settled to cut-off frequencies which are relatively too larger than
1
, i.e.,
|\xi|\gg 1
:

\displaystyle b_{1}(t,\xi)=\frac{2^{\frac{7}{2}}+e(t)-|\xi|}{\epsilon(t)},

(2)

b_{2}
is settled to cut-off frequencies which are relatively too smaller than
1
, i.e.,
|\xi|\ll 1
:

\displaystyle b_{2}(t,\xi)=\frac{|\xi|-2^{-\frac{7}{2}}+ce(t)}{\epsilon(t)},

where
c
is a fixed small constant,

(3)

b_{3}
is settled to detect the outgoing waves:

\displaystyle b_{3}(t,x,\xi)=\frac{2^{-\frac{1}{2}}|x|||\xi|+x\cdot\xi}{2^{-12}|x|},

(4)

b_{4}
is settled to cut-off the values of
|x|
which are too large:

\displaystyle b_{4}(t,x)=\frac{2^{6}t-|x|}{t},

(5)

b_{5}
is settled to cut-off the values of
|x|
which are too small:

\displaystyle b_{5}(t,x,\xi)=\frac{|x||\xi|-2^{-5}t|\xi|+x\cdot\xi}{2^{-10}t}.

It is easy to observe that
b_{\lambda}=0
if
b_{j}=1
,
j=1,\cdots,5
. In other words, the set
\{b_{\lambda}=0\}
contains the region

\displaystyle\{\frac{1}{8}<|\xi|<8\}\cap\{t/4<|x|<4t\}\cap\{x\cdot\xi\geq-2^{-4}|x|\},

provided that
\epsilon>0
is sufficiently small. On the other hand, we also observe that
b_{\lambda}<t^{-\frac{3}{4}}
if
0<b_{j}<1
,
j=1,\cdots,5
. More precisely, we see that the set
\{t^{\frac{3}{4}}b_{\lambda}<1\}
is contained in the region

\displaystyle\{\frac{1}{16}<|\xi|<16\}\cap\{2^{-6}t<|x|<2^{6}t\}\cap\{x\cdot\xi>-2^{-\frac{1}{2}}|x||\}:=D_{t},

which shows that the conditions (2) and (3) are satisfied. Now we prove the condition (1). On the set
\{b_{\lambda}=0\}
, it is trivial, and hence we focus on the set
D_{t}
. It suffices to show that for each
b_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}b_{j}(t,x_{t},\xi_{t})\geq\frac{2}{t},

on the set
D_{t}\cap\{0\leq b_{j}\leq 1\}
,
where
t\mapsto(x_{t},\xi_{t})
is a trajectory of the Hamilton flow for the evolution
D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}+a^{w}_{\lambda}
. We first notice that for each
(x_{t},\xi_{t})\in D_{t}
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\xi_{t}=O(\frac{\epsilon(t)}{t}),\ \frac{d}{dt}x_{t}=\frac{\xi_{t}}{\langle\xi_{t}\rangle_{\lambda}}+O(\epsilon(t)),

which easily gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}|\xi_{t}|=\frac{\xi_{t}}{|\xi_{t}|}\frac{O(\epsilon(t))}{t},

and hence the lower bounds for
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
easily follows. Indeed, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}b_{1}(t,\xi_{t})=\frac{e^{\prime}(t)}{\epsilon(t)}-\frac{1}{\epsilon(t)}\frac{\xi_{t}}{|\xi_{t}|}\frac{O(\epsilon(t))}{t}-\frac{2^{\frac{7}{2}}-e(t)-|\xi_{t}|}{\epsilon(t)^{2}}\epsilon^{\prime}(t)\geq\frac{2}{t}.

Here we used the properties of
\epsilon(t)
(
1.23
) and the definition of
e(t)
, which gives the desired lower bound on the set
\{0\leq b_{1}\leq 1\}
. For
b_{2}
, in the identical manner we see that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}b_{2}(t,\xi_{t})=\frac{1}{\epsilon(t)}\frac{\xi_{t}}{|\xi_{t}|}\frac{O(\epsilon(t))}{t}+c\frac{e^{\prime}(t)}{\epsilon(t)}-\frac{|\xi_{t}|-2^{-\frac{7}{2}}+ce(t)}{\epsilon(t)^{2}}\epsilon^{\prime}(t)\geq\frac{2}{t},

on the set
\{0\leq b_{2}\leq 1\}
.

An easy computation gives us

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{|x_{t}|}=-\frac{x_{t}}{|x_{t}|^{3}}\cdot\left(\frac{\xi_{t}}{\langle\xi_{t}\rangle_{\lambda}}+O(\epsilon)\right),\ \frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{|\xi_{t}|}=-\frac{\xi_{t}}{|\xi_{t}|^{3}}O(\epsilon).

Using these identities, the required lower bounds for
b_{j}
,
j=3,4,5
are obvious. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}b_{3}(t,x,\xi)=2^{12}\frac{|x_{t}|^{2}|\xi_{t}|^{2}-(x_{t}\cdot\xi_{t})^{2}}{|x_{t}|^{3}\langle\xi_{t}\rangle_{\lambda}}+\frac{O(\epsilon)}{t}\geq\frac{2}{t},

since
-2^{-\frac{3}{2}}\leq\cos\angle(x_{t},\xi_{t})\leq-\frac{1}{2}
on the set
\{0\leq b_{3}\leq 1\}
. We also have for
b_{4}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}b_{4}(t,x_{t},\xi_{t})=\frac{|x_{t}|^{2}\langle\xi_{t}\rangle_{\lambda}-tx_{t}\cdot\xi_{t}}{t^{2}|x_{t}|\langle\xi_{t}\rangle_{\lambda}}-\frac{O(\epsilon)}{t}\geq\frac{2}{t},

on the set
\{0\leq b_{4}\leq 1\}
. Now we see that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}b_{5}(t,x_{t},\xi_{t})=2^{10}\left(-\frac{|x_{t}|\langle\xi\rangle_{\lambda}+x_{t}\cdot\xi_{t}}{t^{2}}+\frac{|x_{t}|(x_{t}\cdot\xi_{t})+\xi_{t}}{t}+\frac{O(\epsilon(t))}{t}\right)\geq\frac{2}{t}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
5.9
.

In view of Lemma 10 and Lemma 11 of
[
33
]
, we make only a slight modification of the construction of the damping terms
b_{\lambda}
. The proof is based on the pointwise bounds for the phase space transform of the solution
u
to the evolution equation

\displaystyle(D_{t}+\langle D_{x}\rangle_{\lambda}+a^{w}_{\lambda}(t,x,D))u=-ib^{w}_{\lambda}(t,x,D)u,\ u(2^{j})=\mathcal{P}_{j}u_{0}.

At the initial time

, we already know that the symbol of

is supported on the region

\displaystyle D_{s}=\{\frac{1}{4}<|\xi|<4,\ 2^{j-1}<|x|<2^{j+1},\ \cos\angle(x,\xi)\geq 2^{-5}\}.

Then we have the phase space transform of the initial data
\mathcal{P}_{j}u_{0}
satisfies pointwise bound

\displaystyle|(T_{\frac{1}{s}}\mathcal{P}_{j}u_{0})(x,\xi)|\lesssim\left(1+d_{s}((x,\xi),D_{s})^{2}\right)^{-N},

. Here

\displaystyle d_{s}((x,\xi),(y,\eta))^{2}=s^{-1}|x-y|^{2}+s|\xi-\eta|^{2}.

As we have done at the beginning of the proof of the dispersive inequality (
5.10
), we invoke the definition of the phase space kernel of the evolution operators to write in the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{(k)}(t,s)[T_{\frac{1}{s}}\mathcal{P}_{j}u_{0}]=T_{\frac{1}{t}}u(t,x,\xi_{t})=\int\mathfrak{K}_{(k)}(t,x,\xi_{t},s,x_{s},\xi)T_{\frac{1}{s}}\mathcal{P}_{j}u(s,x_{s},\xi)\,dx_{s}d\xi.

The application of the kernel bounds Theorem
5.7
yields

\displaystyle|(T_{\frac{1}{t}}u)(t,x,\xi_{t})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{-\frac{d}{4}}\int\left(1+t^{-1}\|x-x_{t}\|_{\lambda}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{N}{2}}\left(1+s\|\xi-\xi_{s}\|_{\lambda^{-1}}^{2}+(\Psi_{\lambda}(x_{t},\xi_{t})-\Psi_{\lambda}(x_{s},\xi_{s}))^{2}\right)^{-\frac{N}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(1+d_{s}((x,\xi),D_{s})^{2}\right)^{-N}\,dx_{s}d\xi.

After an obvious integration on
\xi
as we have done in the proof of (
5.10
), we see that

\displaystyle|(T_{\frac{1}{t}}u)(t,x,\xi_{t})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{-\frac{d}{4}}\int\left(1+t^{-1}\|x-x_{t}\|_{\lambda}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{N}{2}}\left(1+s\|\xi-\xi_{s}\|_{\lambda^{-1}}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{N}{2}}

where we put

(5.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(t,x_{t},\xi_{t})=\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{\frac{d}{4}}\left(1+(\Psi_{\lambda}(x_{t},\xi_{t})-\Psi_{\lambda}(x_{s},\xi_{s}))^{2}\right)^{-\frac{N}{2}}\left(1+d_{s}((x_{s},\xi_{s}),D_{s})^{2}\right)^{-N}.

Note that we can freely replace the variables

\lesssim O(1)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{W}(t,x_{t},\xi_{t})|\lesssim\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{\frac{d}{4}}\left(1+t^{\frac{1}{2}}+t^{-2}|x_{t}|^{2}+|\xi_{t}|^{2}\right)^{-\frac{N}{4}}\left(1+d_{t}((x_{t},\xi_{t}),\textrm{supp}\,b_{\lambda}(t))^{2}\right)^{\frac{N}{4}}.

In other words, we allow an arbitrary growth on the factor

\displaystyle|\mathcal{W}(t,x_{t},\xi_{t})|\lesssim t^{\frac{d}{4}}s^{-\frac{d}{4}},

\displaystyle d_{t}((x_{t},\xi_{t}),\textrm{supp }b_{\lambda}(t))\ll t^{-\frac{1}{4}},

]
, it suffices to prove the estimates

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}_{|x|<R}\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}(t,s)\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}_{t}L^{q^{\prime}}_{x}\rightarrow L^{2}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\lesssim\langle 2^{k}\rangle^{(1-\frac{2}{q})\frac{\theta}{2}}|R|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We may assume that
R\gg 1
.
We split the parametrix
\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}(t,s)
into

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}(t,s)=\mathbf{1}_{\{|t-s|<2^{10}R\}}\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}(t,s)+\mathbf{1}_{\{|t-s|>2^{10}R\}}\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}(t,s).

Then for the first term on the right handside the use of the Hölder inequality and the Strichartz estimates yields

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}_{|x|<R}\mathbf{1}_{\{|t-s|<2^{10}R\}}\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}(t,s)f\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|R|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|R|^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle 2^{k}\rangle^{(1-\frac{2}{q})\frac{\theta}{2}}\|f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}_{t}L^{q^{\prime}}_{x}}.

For the second term we use the error-type estimates and the Sobolev embedding to get

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}_{|x|<R}\mathbf{1}_{\{|t-s|>2^{10}R\}}\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}(t,s)f\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|(1+|\cdot|)^{-N}*\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\right\|_{L^{2}_{t}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|(1+|\cdot|)^{-N}*\|f\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{x}}\right\|_{L^{2}_{t}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}_{t}L^{q^{\prime}}_{x}},

where we used the weak Young’s inequality to get the last inequality. Now we are concerned with the endpoint issue and follow the strategy of
[
27
,
49
]
. We need to prove the following estimates:

(5.28)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}f\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle 2^{k}\rangle^{(1-\frac{2}{q})\frac{\theta}{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{q^{\prime}}_{x}},

where
q=\frac{2(d-1+\theta)}{d-3+\theta}
,
0\leq\theta\leq 1
, and
d\geq 3
. Here we exclude the endpoint
q=\infty
,
\theta=0
,
d=3
.
We prove this in the similar way as Theorem 4 of
[
45
]
.
We note that the kernel
K^{\pm}_{(k)}
of the parametrix
\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}
satisfies the bound

(5.29)

\displaystyle|K^{\pm}_{(k)}(t,x,s,y)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle 2^{k}\rangle^{\theta}(1+|t-s|+|x-y|)^{-\frac{d-1+\theta}{2}},\ 0\leq\theta\leq 1,

since by our construction the kernel of
\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}
is concentrated on the region
\{|x-y|\approx|t-s|\}\cap\{|\xi|\approx 1\}
. From now on we drop out the sign
\pm
of the notation
\mathcal{K}^{\pm}_{(k)}
and simply denote the parametrix by
\mathcal{K}_{(k)}
.
The first step is to decompose our parametrix
\mathcal{K}_{(k)}
into the sum of the dyadic pieces:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{(k)}=\sum_{\mu\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathcal{K}_{(k)}^{\mu},

each of which are supported on the region
|(t,x)-(s,y)|\approx\mu
. To construct such a dyadic decomposition we introduce the collection
\mathcal{Q}
of all closed cubes of size
\mu
and whose vertices are in
\mu(\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d+1})
for some dyadic number
\mu>0
. We endow the collection
\mathcal{Q}
with the ordering by inclusion. Then for any
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}
, we must have
|Q\cap Q^{\prime}|=0
or
Q\subset Q^{\prime}
or
Q^{\prime}\subset Q
. Now we denote the maximal cubes in
\mathcal{Q}
by
\mathfrak{Q}
. Then it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathfrak{Q}}Q

is an almost disjoint partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. We make the label for the cubes in
\mathfrak{Q}
as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}=\{Q^{\alpha}_{\mu}\times\widetilde{Q}^{\alpha}_{\mu}:\mu\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}},\ \alpha\in I\},

so that for each
\alpha
, the cubes
Q^{\alpha}_{\mu}
and
\widetilde{Q}^{\alpha}_{\mu}
have size
\mu
. Then we have the partition of unit into the characteristic functions

\displaystyle 1=\sum_{\mu\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{\alpha}\chi_{Q^{\alpha}_{\mu}}\chi_{\tilde{Q}^{\alpha}_{\mu}}.

Finally, we define the desired dyadic piece of the operator
\mathcal{K}_{(k)}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{(k)}^{\mu}=\sum_{\alpha}\chi_{Q^{\alpha}_{\mu}}\mathcal{K}_{(k)}\chi_{\tilde{Q}^{\alpha}_{\mu}}.

Then the kernel of each
\mathcal{K}_{(k)}^{\mu}
is supported at distance
\mu
from the diagonal. The main estimate is the following, which is very similar to Lemma 3.2 of
[
45
]
. See also Lemma 4.1 of
[
27
]
.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
d\geq 3
. The following estimate hold for
(q,\tilde{q})
in a neighborhood of
(\frac{2(d-1+\theta)}{d-3+\theta},\frac{2(d-1+\theta)}{d-3+\theta})
:

(5.30)

\displaystyle\|\chi_{Q_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{K}_{(k)}\chi_{\widetilde{Q}_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{q^{\prime}}_{x}\rightarrow L^{2}_{t}L^{\tilde{q}}_{x}}\lesssim\langle 2^{k}\rangle^{(1-\frac{2}{q})\frac{\theta}{2}+(1-\frac{2}{\tilde{q}})\frac{\theta}{2}}\mu^{-\mathfrak{b}(q,\tilde{q})},

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}(q,\tilde{q})=\frac{d-3+\theta}{2}-\frac{d-1+\theta}{2}\left(\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{\tilde{q}}\right).

We prove the above estimates in the three cases:

(1)

q=\tilde{q}=\infty
,

(2)

\tilde{q}=2
,
2\leq q<\frac{2(d-1+\theta)}{d-3+\theta}
,

(3)

q=2
,
2\leq\tilde{q}<\frac{2(d-1+\theta)}{d-3+\theta}
.

If
q=\tilde{q}=\infty
, we see that

\displaystyle\|\chi_{Q_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{K}_{(k)}\chi_{\widetilde{Q}_{\mu}^{\alpha}}u\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}\lesssim\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\chi_{Q_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{K}_{(k)}\chi_{\widetilde{Q}_{\mu}^{\alpha}}u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}\lesssim\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\chi_{Q_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{K}_{(k)}\chi_{\widetilde{Q}_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}\|u\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{1}_{x}},

and we simply use the kernel bound (
5.29
) and the Hölder inequality for the
u
in
t
, which yields the desired bound.

When
\tilde{q}=2
and
2\leq q<\frac{2(d-1+\theta)}{d-3+\theta}
, we choose
2<p
so that
(p,q)
is a Strichartz admissible pair. See (
3.27
). Then we have

\displaystyle\|\chi_{Q_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{K}_{(k)}\chi_{\widetilde{Q}_{\mu}^{\alpha}}u\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\lesssim\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\chi_{Q_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{K}_{(k)}\chi_{\widetilde{Q}_{\mu}^{\alpha}}u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\lesssim\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}{\langle 2^{k}\rangle}^{(1-\frac{2}{q})\frac{\theta}{2}}\|\chi_{\tilde{Q}^{\alpha}_{\mu}}u\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}_{t}L^{q^{\prime}}_{x}}\lesssim\mu^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\langle 2^{k}\rangle^{(1-\frac{2}{q})\frac{\theta}{2}}\|u\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{q^{\prime}}_{x}},

which is the desired bound, since
-1+\frac{1}{p}=\mathfrak{b}(2,q)
. The third case follows by simply interchanging the role of
q
and
\tilde{q}
. This completes the proof of Lemma
5.11
.

Now we turn our attention to the proof of the endpoint Strichartz estimates (
5.28
).
We shall make the use of the bilinear real interpolation argument. To do this, we first apply the standard
TT^{*}
-argument to set the bilinear operator
\mathfrak{T}_{(k)}^{\mu}
given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{(k)}^{\mu}(u,v)=\int_{s<t}\langle\mathcal{K}^{\mu}_{(k)}u(t),\mathcal{K}^{\mu}_{(k)}v(s)\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\,dsdt,

and we write the equivalent form of the estimate of Lemma
5.11
as

(5.31)

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{T}_{(k)}^{\mu}(u,v)|\lesssim\langle 2^{k}\rangle^{(1-\frac{2}{q})\frac{\theta}{2}+(1-\frac{2}{\tilde{q}})\frac{\theta}{2}}\mu^{-\mathfrak{b}(q,\tilde{q})}\|u\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{q^{\prime}}_{x}}\|v\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}_{x}}.

Even though we cannot obtain any bound for the sum by putting
q=\tilde{q}=\frac{2(d-1+\theta)}{d-3+\theta}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}|\mathfrak{T}^{\mu}_{(k)}(u,v)|,

which would lead us to the desired endpoint estimates, we can improve the exponent
-\mathfrak{b}(q,\tilde{q})
of
\mu
since we have on one hand a two-parameter family of estimates together with various exponential decay factors which holds for any points in an open neighborhood of
(\frac{2(d-1+\theta)}{d-3+\theta},\frac{2(d-1+\theta)}{d-3+\theta})
. The desired bound for the sum above readily follows in the identical manner as the bilinear interpolation argument given by
[
27
]
. We omit the details. This completes the proof of the estimates (
5.28
).
