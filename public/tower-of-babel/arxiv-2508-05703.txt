End-to-End Efficient Quantum Thermal and Ground State Preparation Made Simple
Zhiyan Ding, Yongtao Zhan, John Preskill, Lin Lin

Abstract
We propose new quantum algorithms for thermal and ground state preparation based on system-bath interactions. These algorithms require only forward evolution under a system-bath Hamiltonian in which the bath is a single reusable ancilla qubit, making them especially well-suited for early fault-tolerant quantum devices. By carefully designing the bath and interaction Hamiltonians, we prove that the fixed point of the dynamics accurately approximates the desired quantum state. Furthermore, we establish theoretical guarantees on the mixing time for several physically relevant models, thereby providing a rigorous justification for the end-to-end efficiency of system-bath interaction models in thermal and ground state preparation.

Introduction

Preparing quantum thermal and ground states is a fundamental task in quantum computing, with broad applications in quantum many-body physics, quantum chemistry, and materials science. Although various methods have been proposed in the literature for preparing thermal and ground states, many of these algorithms lack a clear framework for analyzing their efficiency and correctness. For instance, variational quantum algorithms

Although known algorithms for simulating dissipative dynamics can be efficiently implemented on a fully fault-tolerant quantum computer, they often involve complex logic gates, controlled or time-reversed Hamiltonian simulations, and a large number of ancilla qubits. These complex circuit structures can be challenging to implement on early fault-tolerant quantum devices, where quantum resources remain limited, error correction may be applied selectively to components most susceptible to noise, and hybrid architectures combining analog (such as forward Hamiltonian evolution) and digital elements may be used to reduce implementation costs and suppress errors. Consequently, algorithms must be significantly simplified before they can be considered for practical deployment. This leads to a natural question that motivates our work:

II
Algorithm description

Our algorithm is based on the weakly coupled system-bath interaction model. Given a system Hamiltonian
H
, we define the total Hamiltonian as

H_{\alpha}(t)=H+H_{E}+\alpha f(t)\left(A_{S}\otimes B_{E}+A^{\dagger}_{S}\otimes B_{E}^{\dagger}\right).

(1)

Here,
A_{S}
is a coupling operator acting on the system, which can be randomly chosen from a user-provided set of operators, while
B_{E}
can be chosen to be a fixed operator. The environment Hamiltonian
H_{E}
depends on a random frequency variable
\omega
, which is sampled from a distribution to be specified later.

Starting from an initial state
\rho_{0}
in the system register, in each step of the algorithm we set the state of the ancilla qubit to
\rho_{E}\propto\exp(-\beta H_{E})
, simulate the time evolution governed by the total Hamiltonian
H_{\alpha}(t)
and then trace out the ancilla qubit. When
\beta=\infty
,
\rho_{E}=\ket{0}\bra{0}
.
Then

\rho_{n+1}=\Phi_{\alpha}(\rho_{n}):=\mathbb{E}_{A_{S},\omega}\left(\operatorname{Tr}_{E}\left[U^{\alpha}(T)\left(\rho_{n}\otimes\rho_{E}\right)U^{\alpha}(T)^{\dagger}\right]\right),

(2)

where
U^{\alpha}(t)
is the time-evolution operator of the total Hamiltonian
U^{\alpha}(t):=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{-T}^{t}H_{\alpha}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right)
.

We aim to design the quantum channel
\Phi_{\alpha}
so that it admits a unique
fixed point

\rho_{\text{fix}}
, satisfying
\Phi_{\alpha}(\rho_{\text{fix}})=\rho_{\text{fix}}
, where
\rho_{\text{fix}}
is close to the thermal or ground state of
H
. To prepare this
\rho_{\text{fix}}
, we begin with an initial state
\rho_{0}
and apply
\Phi_{\alpha}
repeatedly. With appropriate parameter choices, after a number of iterations denoted by an integer
\tau_{\rm mix}
to be characterized later, the resulting state
\rho_{\tau_{\rm mix}}
will be close to
\rho_{\text{fix}}
, and hence approximate the desired thermal or ground state.

An illustration of the quantum circuit employed in our algorithm is provided in
Fig.

1
. The protocol only involves forward Hamiltonian evolution and resetting a single ancilla qubit, making the algorithm well suited for implementation on early fault-tolerant quantum devices.

In our work,
H_{E},B_{E}
, and
f(t)
are chosen to satisfy the following properties:

Assumption 1
.

•

H_{E}=-{\omega}Z/2
, where
\omega
is randomly sampled from a probability density
g(\omega)
.

•

B_{E}=(X-iY)/2=\ket{1}\bra{0}
.
A_{S}
is uniformly sampled from a set of coupling operators
\mathcal{A}=\{A^{i},-A^{i}\}_{i}
with
\{(A^{i})^{\dagger}\}_{i}=\{A^{i}\}_{i}
and
\|A^{i}\|\leq 1
.

•

f(t)
is a real, even and normalized function in
t
, i.e,
f(t)=f(-t)\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=1
.

As concrete examples illustrating the above assumptions, in
Section

V
we choose
\mathcal{A}
to be the set of all single-qubit Pauli operators (and their negatives) for qubit systems, and the set of creation and annihilation operators (and their negatives) for fermionic systems.
For the functions
g
and
f
, we set

g(\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega_{\max}}\mathbf{1}_{[0,\omega_{\max}]},\quad f(t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/4}\sigma^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\right)\,.

The parameter
\omega_{\max}
is selected so that the system-bath interaction can induce energy transitions effectively.
The choice of
\omega_{\max}
can be system-dependent and should generally be at least as large as the largest eigenvalue gap, and typically does not grow with system size. The parameter
\sigma
in the filter function
f(t)
is typically chosen to be sufficiently large to ensure that the Lamb shift term in
2
approximately commutes with the thermal or ground state, as discussed in
Section

IV
. More detailed choices of the parameters
\alpha
and
T
, which may depend on the spectral properties of
H
, are made to ensure a small approximation error and fast mixing of
\Phi_{\alpha}
. These choices are specified in
4
,
5
, and
Section

V
.

The main steps of our algorithm consist of simulating the quantum channel
\Phi_{\alpha}
and applying it iteratively to drive the system towards the desired state.

To facilitate early fault-tolerant implementation, we approximate the time-dependent Hamiltonian in
\Phi_{\alpha}
using a second-order Trotter formula with a small time step
\tau
. At each time step
n
, the frequency parameter
\omega
and the operator
A_{S}
are independently sampled. Let
M=\left\lceil 2T/\tau\right\rceil
, and
f_{m}=f((m+1/2)\tau-T)
. According to the preceding description, the evolution from
\rho_{n}
to
\rho_{n+1}
is implemented as follows:

\displaystyle\rho_{n+1}=\Phi^{\rm approx}_{\alpha}(\rho_{n})

(3)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{A_{S},\omega}\left(\operatorname{Tr}_{E}\left[\Pi^{M}_{m=0}U^{\alpha}_{m\tau}\left(\rho_{n}\otimes\rho_{E}\right)\left(\Pi^{M}_{m=0}U^{\alpha}_{m\tau}\right)^{\dagger}\right]\right)\,,

where

U^{\alpha}_{m\tau}=W_{A_{S},m}(\tau/2)\exp(-iH\tau)\exp(i\omega\tau Z/2)W_{A_{S},m}(\tau/2)

implements the Trotterized approximation to
U^{\alpha}
with

W_{A_{S},m}(\tau/2)=\exp(-i\alpha f_{m}(A_{S}\otimes B_{E}+A^{\dagger}_{S}\otimes B^{\dagger}_{E})\tau/2)\,.

(4)

In practice, if the system Hamiltonian
H
consists of noncommuting terms, one would further Trotterize
\exp(-iH\tau)
; however, we do not explicitly do so here in order to keep the notation streamlined.

III
Derivation of Effective Lindblad dynamics

In this section, we show the quantum map
\Phi_{\alpha}
in the weak coupling regime
\alpha\ll 1
can be approximated by an effective Lindblad dynamics up to a unitary transformation.

Define the time evolution operator by
U_{S}(t):=\exp(-iHt)
, and the associated superoperator by
\mathcal{U}_{S}(t)[\rho]=U_{S}(t)\rho U^{\dagger}_{S}(t)
. Given a jump operator
V
, we define the associated dissipative operator as

\mathcal{D}_{V}(\rho)=V\rho V^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{V^{\dagger}V,\rho\}\,.

(5)

We then show that the quantum map
\Phi_{\alpha}
can be approximated by an effective Lindblad dynamics:

Theorem 2

(Informal)
.

Under
1
,
\rho_{n+1}
can be expressed as

\rho_{n+1}=\mathcal{U}_{S}(T)\,\circ\,\exp(\mathcal{L}\alpha^{2})\,\circ\,\mathcal{U}_{S}(T)[\rho_{n}]+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{4}T^{4}\|f\|^{4}_{L^{\infty}})\,.

(6)

Here,
\mathcal{L}
is the Lindbladian operator taking the form of

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A_{S}}\left(\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}-i\left[g(\omega)H_{\mathrm{LS},A_{S}}(\omega),\rho\right]\right.

(7)

\displaystyle\left.+\gamma(\omega)\mathcal{D}_{V_{A_{S},f,T}(\omega)}(\rho)\mathrm{d}\omega\right)\,.

Here, the Lamb shift term
H_{\mathrm{LS},A_{S}}(\omega)
is a Hermitian matrix.
\gamma(\omega)=(g(\omega)+g(-\omega))/(1+\exp(\beta\omega))
when
\beta<\infty
, and
\gamma(\omega)=(g(\omega)+g(-\omega))\textbf{1}_{\omega<0}
when
\beta=\infty
. The jump operator
V_{A_{S},f,T}(\omega)
is defined as

V_{A_{S},f,T}(\omega)=\int^{T}_{-T}f(t)A_{S}(t)\exp(-i\omega t)\mathrm{d}t.

(8)

The rigorous version of
2
can be found in
Appendix

C
as
11
.
The
\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})
term is obtained by analyzing the second-order term in the Dyson expansion of the time evolution operators, and the
\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{4})
remainder bounds the sum of higher-order terms.
In
2
, the parameter
\alpha^{2}
can be interpreted as the effective evolution time under the Lindbladian operator
\mathcal{L}
. Each application of
\Phi_{\alpha}
approximately evolves the system, up to the unitary transformation
\mathcal{U}_{S}(T)
, under the Lindbladian operator
\mathcal{L}
for time
\alpha^{2}
with error scaling as
\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{4})
. Therefore, up to the unitary transformation
\mathcal{U}_{S}(T)
, the map
\Phi_{\alpha}
serves as a first-order approximation to the Lindblad dynamics
\partial_{t}\rho=\mathcal{L}(\rho)
.

IV
Thermal and ground state preparation

Let
\|A\|_{1}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}\right]
denote the trace norm of
A
, and
\rho_{\rm fix}(\Phi_{\alpha})
be the fixed point of
\Phi_{\alpha}
. Under
1
and the choice of
f(t)
described above, the quantum channel
\Phi_{\alpha}
defined in
Eq.

2
can be engineered to approximately preserve the thermal or ground state of the system Hamiltonian. The integer mixing time describes the minimum number of iterations required so that, starting from any initial state, the evolved state is guaranteed to be
\epsilon
-close to the target state. In this sense, it characterizes the worst-case convergence time over all initial states.

Definition 3
.

Given a CPTP map
\Phi
with a unique fixed point
\rho_{\rm fix}(\Phi)
and
\epsilon>0
, the integer mixing time
\tau_{{\rm mix},\Phi}(\epsilon)
is defined as

\tau_{{\rm mix},\Phi}(\epsilon)=\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}\middle|\sup_{\rho}\|\Phi^{t}(\rho)-\rho_{\rm fix}(\Phi)\|_{1}\leq\epsilon\right\}\,.

(9)

For
\Phi_{\alpha}
that takes the form of (
2
), the parameter
\alpha^{2}
can be interpreted as the effective evolution time per application, and we define the (rescaled) mixing time as

t_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}(\epsilon)=\alpha^{2}\tau_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}(\epsilon).

(10)

According to
2
, this mixing time should be governed by the underlying Lindbladian operator
\mathcal{L}
. The quantity
t_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}(\epsilon)
approximately captures the total Lindbladian evolution time required for mixing. When
\alpha
is sufficiently small, this mixing time does not diverge as
\alpha\to 0
, but instead remains bounded above by a finite constant that depends only on properties of the Lindbladian.

The following theorem shows that
by properly choosing the parameters
\sigma,T,\alpha
related to the mixing time, the fixed point of
\Phi_{\alpha}
is approximately the thermal state. We also omit some dependence on
\|H\|
and
\|A_{S}\|
for simplicity. The general version of
4
is stated in

as

Theorem 4

(Thermal state, informal)
.

Assume
0\leq\beta<\infty
and
g(\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega_{\max}}\mathbf{1}_{[0,\omega_{\max}]}
. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, if

\sigma=\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\beta\omega^{-1}_{\max}\epsilon^{-1}t_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}(\epsilon)\right),\quad T=\Omega(\sigma\log(\sigma/\epsilon))\,,

and
\alpha=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma T^{-2}\epsilon^{1/2}t^{-1/2}_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}(\epsilon)\right)
,
then

\|\rho_{\rm fix}(\Phi_{\alpha})-\rho_{\beta}\|_{1}<\epsilon\,.

4
shows that if we set
\sigma=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\omega_{\max}^{-1}\beta\epsilon^{-1}t_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}(\epsilon)\right)
, we ensure that the fixed point is
\epsilon
-close to the thermal state by choosing

T=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\omega_{\max}^{-1}\beta\epsilon^{-1}t_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}(\epsilon)\right),\ \alpha=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\omega_{\max}\beta^{-1}t_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}^{-3/2}\epsilon^{3/2}\right).

Analogously, we can establish a corresponding result for the ground state as follows.

Theorem 5

(Ground state, informal)
.

Assume

and let

be the ground state of

. Then, for any

, if

\sigma=\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\Delta^{-1}\log(\|H\|/\epsilon)\right),\quad T=\Omega(\sigma\log(\sigma/\epsilon))\,,

and
\alpha=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma T^{-2}\epsilon^{1/2}t^{-1/2}_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}(\epsilon)\right)
,
then

\|\rho_{\rm fix}(\Phi_{\alpha})-\ket{\psi_{0}}\bra{\psi_{0}}\|_{1}<\epsilon.

5
shows that if we set
\sigma=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\Delta^{-1}\right)
, it suffices to choose

T=\widetilde{\Theta}(\Delta^{-1}),\ \alpha=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\Delta\epsilon^{1/2}t^{-1/2}_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}(\epsilon)\right).

The rigorous version of

is given in Appendix

\sigma=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\Delta^{-1}\right)

T=\widetilde{\Theta}(\Delta^{-1}),\ \alpha=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\Delta\epsilon^{1/2}t^{-1/2}_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}(\epsilon)\right).

To prove

. This consists of two main steps:

Approximate the map

by choosing

.

Show that the limiting map approximately fixes the thermal or ground state when

.

In the first step, using the result of

\|\Phi_{\alpha}(\rho_{\beta})-\rho_{\beta}\|_{1}\approx\left\|\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\beta})\right\|_{1},\quad\alpha\ll 1

with the approximation error quantified in (

\left\|\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\beta})\right\|_{1}\approx 0

Note that, in order to ensure a small error

V
Mixing time and End-to-end efficiency analysis

According to
4
and
5
, to establish end-to-end efficiency, it suffices to provide an upper bound on
t_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}
. However, we emphasize that in
4
and
5
, the mixing time
t_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}
and the parameter
\sigma
are
not
independent of each other. The bath Hamiltonian
H_{B}
, the coupling operator
B_{E}
, and the filter function
f(t)
must be carefully designed to ensure that the conditions required for the theorems are meaningfully satisfied.

In this section, we analyze the mixing time of the map
\Phi_{\alpha}
defined in
Eq.

2
, and provide a complete end-to-end efficiency analysis for preparing both the thermal state and the ground state. Specifically, we consider three examples of physical systems: a single qubit example (as a toy model), free fermionic systems, and commuting local Hamiltonians. In all three cases, we show that the mixing time of
\Phi_{\alpha}
can be upper bounded by a constant independent of
\sigma
, provided that
\sigma
is sufficiently large. This enables us to achieve an arbitrarily small fixed-point error by appropriately choosing a large
\sigma
and a small
\alpha
.

V.1
Single qubit example

We first consider a toy model to illustrate the key ideas. Assume the system Hamiltonian
H=-Z
. In
Eq.

2
, we set
A_{S}=X
and
g(\omega)=\frac{1}{3}\textbf{1}_{[0,3]}(\omega)
(
\omega_{\max}=1/3
). Then, we have the following result:

Theorem 6
.

For thermal state preparation, given any
\beta,\epsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C=\mathrm{poly}(\beta,1/\epsilon)
such that if
\sigma>C
,
T=\widetilde{\Omega}(\sigma)
, and
\alpha<\sigma^{-1}C^{-1}
, we have

t_{{\rm mix},\Phi_{\alpha}}(\epsilon)=\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\epsilon)).

For ground state preparation (
\beta=\infty
), given
\epsilon>0
, there exists a constant

VI
Discussion

In this work, we have presented a quantum algorithm for preparing both thermal and ground states with provable correctness and mixing-time guarantees, while requiring only modest quantum resources. The algorithm operates by repeatedly applying a simple quantum channel, obtained from forward time evolution under a system-bath Hamiltonian, followed by resetting the bath. The bath can be reduced to a single ancilla qubit by employing a randomized choice of bath frequency and coupling operator between the system and bath.
This design makes it well suited for early fault-tolerant quantum devices, including hybrid devices that can implement certain type of Hamiltonian simulations via analog methods.

Our analysis shows that, with a suitable choice of parameters, the fixed point of the quantum channel can accurately approximate the target thermal or ground state. Once the fixed-point error bound is established, it suffices to prove polynomial mixing time in order to achieve end-to-end efficiency. A key technical point is that the mixing time and simulation parameters are generally interdependent; it is therefore important to demonstrate that, for our channel construction, the mixing time remains bounded as parameters are tuned to reduce the fixed-point error. While we have established polynomial mixing for several physically relevant models, we expect similar behavior more generally, and extending these results to interacting systems remains an important theoretical challenge.

The weak system-bath interaction framework can be regarded as an approximate, low-order approach to Lindblad simulation. Compared with high-order Lindblad simulation methods
cleve_2017
;
li_et_2023
;
PRXQuantum.5.020332
, this can lead to an asymptotic slowdown with respect to parameters such as system size and precision. This tradeoff is analogous to the use of low-order Trotter methods in Hamiltonian simulation, and is to some extent unavoidable. Nevertheless, there is considerable potential for improvement to reduce the cost, which may further enhance its suitability for early fault-tolerant devices.

In our implementation, the filter function has a narrow width
\sigma^{-1}
in the frequency domain. This choice simplifies the fixed-point error analysis but may increase the total simulation time. Allowing broader energy transitions per channel application, as in the protocols of
Ding2024
;
Ding_2025
for ground- and thermal-state preparation, could lead to a more efficient implementation. However, this also introduces new challenges, such as managing the influence of the Lamb shift term.
We hope this work will motivate further research on optimized filter design, generalized mixing-time analysis, and experimental demonstrations of dissipative algorithms for quantum state preparation.

Appendix B
Related works

In this section, we review the related works on thermal and ground state preparation, focusing on the recent developments in Lindblad dynamics and weak-interaction dissipative systems.

Lindblad dynamics, originally developed to model the evolution of weakly coupled open quantum systems, has garnered significant attention in the past two years as a protocol for preparing thermal

The convergence rate is governed by the mixing time of the dynamics. Recently, the mixing time analysis of Lindblad dynamics has been successfully carried out for various physically relevant Hamiltonians in both the thermal

To mitigate the cost of simulating the detailed balanced Lindblad dynamics, very recently
[
18
]
proposes a variational compilation strategy to construct an approximation to the jump operator and to simulate the Lindblad dynamics using local gates.

In contrast to the Lindblad dynamics, the implementation of weak-interaction dissipative systems is more straightforward. Once the bath and system-bath interaction are specified, the dynamics can be simulated using forward Hamiltonian evolution followed by partial trace (or repeated interactions). Despite the simplicity of the simulation, the theoretical understanding of the resulting dynamics and convergence remains limited. In particular, properly designing the system-bath coupling to ensure the correct fixed point and convergence is technically challenging and often requires detailed knowledge of the system Hamiltonian.

Appendix D
Approximate CPTP map has close fixed point and mixing time

In this section, we show that the closeness of two CPTP maps
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
implies the closeness of their fixed points and mixing times. The result is summarized in the following:

Theorem 12
.

Given two CPTP maps
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}

\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
. Let

3
. Then

•

\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are close if the maps themselves are close: For any
\epsilon>0
,

\left\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon+\tau_{1,\rm mix}(\epsilon)\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{1\leftrightarrow 1}\,.

(16)

•

\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are close if
\Phi_{1}(\rho_{2})
is close to
\rho_{2}
: For any
\epsilon>0
,

\left\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon+\tau_{1,\rm mix}(\epsilon)\|\Phi_{1}(\rho_{2})-\rho_{2}\|_{1}\,.

(17)

•

\Phi_{2}
has comparable mixing time with
\Phi_{1}
if
\Phi_{2}
is close to
\Phi_{1}

\epsilon>0
, if
\tau_{1,\rm mix}(\epsilon/2)\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{1\leftrightarrow 1}\leq\epsilon

\tau_{2,\rm mix}(4\epsilon)\leq\tau_{1,\rm mix}(\epsilon/2).

Importantly,

\Phi_{2}

\rho_{2}

Proof of
12
.

To prove

\displaystyle\left\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\right\|_{1}=\left\|\rho_{1}-\Phi^{\tau_{1,\rm mix}(\epsilon)}_{2}(\rho_{2})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\rho_{1}-\Phi^{\tau_{1,\rm mix}(\epsilon)}_{1}(\rho_{2})\right\|_{1}+\left\|\Phi^{\tau_{1,\rm mix}(\epsilon)}_{1}(\rho_{2})-\Phi^{\tau_{1,\rm mix}(\epsilon)}_{2}(\rho_{2})\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon+\tau_{1,\rm mix}(\epsilon)\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{1\leftrightarrow 1}

Remark 19

Different from our setting,

effectively shrinks to a narrow energy window of width

Next, for each creation and annihilation operator pair

\begin{bmatrix}c^{\dagger}_{j}\\

1/2&-i/2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}m_{2j-1}\\
m_{2j}\end{bmatrix}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}[\rho]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N}\int\frac{\widehat{\gamma}(\omega)}{2}\left(\vec{m}^{\dagger}\cdot\hat{f}(-4h^{m}-\omega)e^{-\beta h^{m}}\cdot M^{\dagger}\cdot\rho\cdot M\cdot\hat{f}(-4h^{m}-\omega)e^{-\beta h^{m}}\cdot\vec{m}\right.

Appendix K
Mixing time for commuting local Hamiltonians

In this section, we prove a general version of
9
. Unlike the previous section, here we show that, the dissipative part of
\mathcal{L}
can converge to the Davies generator. The Davies generator satisfies the GNS detailed balance condition (defined in
Appendix

A
), and therefore also satisfies the KMS detailed balance condition.

In the general case, establishing rigorous convergence to the Davies generator requires
\sigma
to scale exponentially with the system size, since one may need to resolve exponentially close Bohr frequencies
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
in order to generate distinct jump operators
A_{S}(\omega_{1})
and
A_{S}(\omega_{2})
that appear in the Davies generator. However, in cases where the effective Bohr frequencies are not exponentially close, such as for local commuting Hamiltonians,
\sigma
does not need to be exponentially large, allowing for an efficient approximation. This property is mainly summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 27
.

Given a coupling set
\mathcal{A}
. Assume

•

\mathcal{L}_{D,\mathcal{A}}[\rho]
has a spectral gap
\lambda_{\rm gap}>0
.

•

There exists a constant
\delta>0
such that: for any
A\in\mathcal{A}
,
\omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2}
, if
A(\omega_{1})\neq 0
and
A(\omega_{2})\neq 0
, we must have
|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}|\geq\delta
.

•

There exists a constant
M
such that
\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\{\omega|A(\omega)\neq 0\}\right|\leq M
.

Given any
\epsilon>0
, if

\sigma=\Omega\left(\beta|\mathcal{A}|\lambda^{-1}_{\rm gap}\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-1}M\log\left(\left\|\sigma^{-1/2}_{\beta}\right\|/\epsilon\right)\mathbb{E}(\|A_{S}\|^{2})\right),\ T=\widetilde{\Omega}(\sigma)\,,

and

\alpha=\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{1/2}\sigma^{-1}|\mathcal{A}|^{-1/2}\lambda^{1/2}_{\rm gap}\log^{-1/2}\left(\left\|\sigma^{-1/2}_{\beta}\right\|/\epsilon\right)\mathbb{E}^{-1/2}(\|A_{S}\|^{4})\right)\,,

then

t_{{\rm mix},\widehat{\Phi}_{\alpha}}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{\lambda_{\rm gap}}\log\left(\frac{8\left\|\sigma^{-1/2}_{\beta}\right\|}{\epsilon}\right)+1

In
27

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
and the Davies generator. When

A(\omega)
is determined by the interaction between
