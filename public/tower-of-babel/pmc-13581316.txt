Validity and Reliability of the Korean Version of the Supportive Care Needs Scale for Tuberculosis Patients
Authors: Hyemi Ha, Jeong Won Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo develop a culturally appropriate Korean version of the Supportive Care Needs Scale for Patients with Tuberculosis and to rigorously evaluate its validity and reliability for use as a standardized assessment tool.DesignA cross‐sectional study.MethodsThe original tool underwent standardized translation and cultural adaptation, followed by expert review and pilot testing to finalize the Korean version. A cross‐sectional survey was conducted among 330 adults receiving tuberculosis treatment at three medical institutions in South Korea. Cronbach's alpha was 0.96, indicating excellent internal consistency.ConclusionThe Korean version of the Supportive Nursing Needs Scale for Tuberculosis Patients is a valid, reliable tool for assessing supportive care needs in patients with tuberculosis and is appropriate for clinical use and subgroup comparison research.Implications for Nursing PracticeThe findings of this study are expected to serve as foundational data for nurses caring for tuberculosis patients to identify patients' nursing needs early and to design and implement tailored nursing plans. Ultimately, this will contribute to increasing patient satisfaction with nursing care and promoting disease recovery.ImpactThis study provides a culturally adapted and validated instrument for assessing supportive care needs among patients with tuberculosis, supporting comprehensive needs assessment and patient‐centred care.Reporting MethodThe STROBE guidelines were followed.Patient or Public ContributionPatients contributed to this study by completing the survey. Introduction

Patients with tuberculosis (TB) experience a wide range of physical, psychological, social, informational, and practical challenges throughout the prolonged treatment process. These challenges may negatively affect treatment adherence, quality of life, and treatment outcomes, highlighting the importance of identifying and addressing patients' supportive care needs through patient‐centred care approaches (Ren et al. 2021). Nurses play a key role in the tuberculosis treatment process by assessing patients' symptoms and side effects, supporting self‐management and treatment adherence, and providing education and psychosocial support (Yan and Bai 2025). The role of nurses is all the more important because tuberculosis management must move beyond an approach centred on patient identification, case management, and monitoring treatment adherence to a patient‐centred approach that reflects the needs, values, and experiences of individual patients (WHO 2024).

Supportive care needs (SCN) encompass the physical, emotional, social, informational, and practical requirements necessary for patients to maintain optimal well‐being throughout the disease trajectory (Hui 2014). Therefore, systematically assessing a patient's SCN is a critical preliminary step in identifying unmet needs and providing appropriate nursing care. Several studies have assessed supportive care related needs among patients with TB; however, most existing instruments have focused on specific domains rather than comprehensively evaluating supportive care needs. For example, Suryani et al. (2014) examined psychosocial needs among patients with pulmonary TB, whereas Li et al. (2018) assessed social support among older adults with TB using the Social Support Rating Scale. In Korea, previous studies have identified nursing needs among patients with TB (Lee 2018) or evaluated social support and family support questionnaires (Gurusinga et al. 2024). Although these studies have contributed to understanding selected aspects of patient needs, they primarily focused on psychosocial needs or social support and did not comprehensively assess the multidimensional supportive care needs experienced throughout the treatment process. Guided by Fitch's (2008) Supportive Care Needs Framework (SCNF), several instruments have been developed to assess these needs. The Supportive Care Needs Survey (SCNS), a widely used tool for patients with cancer, is a multidimensional instrument with established reliability and validity. However, because the SCNS was designed for individuals with cancer, it does not fully capture the physical and psychosocial challenges unique to tuberculosis (TB), including disease characteristics, infectiousness, and stigma. Although various instruments have been used to assess psychosocial problems, social support, self‐management, and patient‐related outcomes among individuals with TB, most existing measures focus on specific aspects of patient experiences and do not comprehensively evaluate the multidimensional supportive care needs encountered throughout treatment. Particularly in Korean TB nursing practice, culturally appropriate and validated instruments for systematically assessing these needs remain limited, which may restrict needs‐based nursing care, evaluation of nursing interventions, and the development of an evidence base for nursing practice.

To address these limitations, Ren et al. (2021) developed the Supportive Care Needs Scale for Patients with Tuberculosis (SCN‐TB), which reflects the specific needs of individuals with TB. Grounded in the SCNF, the SCN‐TB comprises five domains (physical, psychological‐emotional, social, informational, and practical) across 25 items and demonstrates strong psychometric properties. Nevertheless, when applying such instruments in different cultural contexts, it is essential to minimize shifts in meaning during translation and to ensure that the instrument appropriately reflects socio‐cultural contexts and cross‐linguistic differences (Sousa and Rojjanasrirat 2011).

Accordingly, this study aims to translate and culturally adapt the SCN‐TB developed by Ren et al. (2021) for the Korean context and to evaluate its validity and reliability. The validated tool can be used by nurses to systematically identify unmet supportive nursing needs among tuberculosis patients and to plan individualized, needs‐based nursing care.

Background

TB remains a major infectious disease that contributes substantially to the global disease burden. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 10.8 million new TB cases and 1.25 million TB‐related deaths occurred worldwide in 2023, making TB the leading cause of death among notifiable infectious diseases (WHO 2024). Despite intensified efforts to advance the End TB Strategy following the 2018 United Nations High‐Level Meeting, TB still poses a significant public health challenge, particularly among vulnerable populations and older adults (WHO 2024).

Although TB is curable with at least 6 months of anti‐tuberculosis therapy, several barriers complicate successful treatment. Adverse drug reactions (Choi et al. 2022), psychological problems such as depression and anxiety (Shen et al. 2022), social stigma and economic hardship (Contreras et al. 2017), and inadequate information about the disease and its treatment (Huddart et al. 2018) can lead to treatment interruption and poor adherence. Therefore, successful TB care requires accurately identifying the diverse physical, psychological‐emotional, and social needs of patients and implementing tailored, patient‐centred strategies to address them (Odone et al. 2018; WHO 2024).

Research shows that individuals with TB have multidimensional needs extending beyond physical symptom management to include emotional support, social assistance, and provision of disease‐ and treatment‐related information (Oshi et al. 2020; Ren et al. 2021). These needs are key determinants of treatment adherence and success. Tailored nursing interventions addressing individual needs improve treatment commitment and reduce anxiety, isolation, and stigma throughout the prolonged therapy course, thereby enhancing treatment outcomes (Van Hoorn et al. 2016). Collectively, this evidence underscores the importance of systematically assessing SCN and developing individualized, need‐based nursing strategies as an essential component of TB clinical care.

The Study

Aim(s) and Objective

To develop a culturally appropriate Korean version of the Supportive Care Needs Scale for Patients with Tuberculosis and to rigorously evaluate its validity and reliability for use as a standardized assessment tool.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study involved translating and culturally adapting the SCN‐TB, originally developed outside Korea, into Korean and evaluating its validity and reliability for patients with TB. The authors carried out a descriptive, cross‐sectional quantitative study. The STROBE checklist was followed to ensure the quality of the study. Participants were recruited from outpatient clinics at three university hospitals in three cities in Korea. These hospitals participate in the national Public–Private Mix (PPM) programme for TB care. Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with TB, receiving outpatient anti‐TB treatment, able to communicate, and who provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria included age below 18 years, severe physical or mental conditions limiting communication, or lack of consent. The sample size was determined based on recommended criteria for factor analysis. For exploratory factor analysis (EFA), a minimum sample of 100 is recommended, with a case‐to‐variable ratio of approximately 5:1 to 10:1. Stable solutions have also been reported with samples around 100 when communalities are adequate, and each factor has at least 4–5 indicators (Lorenzo‐Seva and Ferrando 2024). For confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), a minimum of 200 participants is recommended as samples of similar size have demonstrated acceptable model fit (MacCallum et al. 1999). To ensure sufficient power for both EFA and CFA, we targeted 300 participants and, accounting for a 10% attrition rate, recruited 330 participants. All questionnaires were collected directly, yielding a 100% return rate; after excluding 30 incomplete or ineligible responses, 300 were included in the final analysis.

Translation and Cultural Adaptation of the Instrument

Following the WHO (2020) guidelines for translating and adapting instruments, this study conducted forward translation, expert panel review, back‐translation, pilot testing, and final item selection. The study procedures were as follows (Figure 1).

Translation and cultural adaptation process for the Korean version of SCN‐TB.

Preparation

Formal permission to use the original SCN‐TB instrument was obtained from its developer.

Forward Translation

In the forward translation phase, two bilingual overseas nursing experts, each with over 5 years of experience caring for patients with TB and familiar with Korean and Anglophone cultural contexts, independently translated the original instrument into Korean. The researchers, together with a nursing professor, reconciled the two translations to preserve the core concepts while adapting terminology to the Korean cultural context, thereby producing the draft Korean version.

Expert Panel Review

To ensure cultural equivalence, an expert panel compared and reviewed the source and translated versions. The panel included five members: two bilingual nursing professors, two PhD‐level researchers experienced in instrument development and translation, and one bilingual expert with 10 years of residence in an English‐speaking country and 3 years of clinical experience. Based on the panel review, the translation was refined to improve linguistic appropriateness for the Korean context and enhance content validity.

Back‐Translation

Back‐translation is an effective method for evaluating translation accuracy and cultural equivalence by comparing original items with their back‐translated versions and is frequently used when adapting instruments across cultures (WHO 2020). In this study, a bilingual nursing professor with prior nursing experience in an English‐speaking country back‐translated the Korean version into English. Independence between the forward translator(s) and back‐translator was maintained; to minimize bias and support more meaningful interpretations, no prior information about the original instrument was provided to the back‐translator. Semantic consistency and cultural appropriateness were then assessed via the back‐translation process.

Content Validity Assessment

Content validity was evaluated for the preliminary Korean version developed through forward translation, back translation, and expert review. Following recommendations that 3–10 experts are suitable for content validity evaluation (Lynn 1986), the expert panel included five TB‐dedicated nurses from PPM institutions, each with > 10 years of experience in patient counselling and management. Their mean clinical experience was 13.2 years, and two members also served as regional lead nurses and as part of the statistical analysis team of the central committee. After explaining the purpose of the study and instrument development procedures, a content validity survey was distributed. Each item was rated on a 4‐point Likert scale: “highly relevant (4),” “quite relevant (3),” “somewhat irrelevant (2),” and “not relevant (1),” with space for qualitative comments. Completed surveys were collected in person by the researcher. We calculated the item‐level content validity index (I‐CVI) and scale‐level content validity index using the averaging method (S‐CVI/Ave). The I‐CVI was defined as the proportion of experts rating an item as 3 or 4, and the S‐CVI/Ave as the average of I‐CVIs across all items. Using the criteria that, with five or more experts, an I‐CVI ≥ 0.78 and S‐CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 indicate adequacy (Polit et al. 2007), all 25 items had I‐CVIs ≥ 0.80, and the S‐CVI/Ave was 0.97.

Pilot Test

A pilot test was performed to assess whether the finalized items were clearly understood and to identify items that were difficult to answer. Following recommendations for a minimum pilot sample of 10–40 participants (Sousa and Rojjanasrirat 2011), we recruited 10 adult outpatients with TB who visited Hospital C and voluntarily consented to participate between November 26 and December 5, 2024, through convenience sampling. The questionnaire was administered one‐on‐one in a face‐to‐face format, and participants completed it as a self‐report measure. The average completion time was 7.13 min, within the 10‐min limit. Readability and comprehension were satisfactory, and no item revisions were required based on the pilot results.

Final Item Selection

After the pilot test, we confirmed that the final Korean translation matched the original items and finalized the SCN‐TB items.

Data Collection

Data were collected from December 9, 2024, to May 30, 2025. Site principal investigators at Catholic University Hospital recruited outpatients with TB, explained the study objectives and procedures, assured anonymity, and clarified that the data would be used solely for research. Questionnaires were then distributed to those who voluntarily consented to participate.

Instrument and Measures

General and Disease‐Related Characteristics

These variables were developed with reference to the vulnerability assessment literature in the 2024 National Tuberculosis Management Guidelines. General characteristics consisted of nine sociodemographic items: age, sex, marital status, cohabitation with family, educational level, employment status, type of health insurance/coverage, smoking status, and alcohol use. Disease‐related characteristics comprised seven items: family history of TB, history of TB, duration of anti‐TB medication, treatment interruption, adverse drug reactions, comorbidities, and drug resistance status. Overall, a 16‐item self‐report questionnaire was used.

Supportive Care Needs Scale for Patients With Tuberculosis

Supportive care needs were assessed using the SCN‐TB developed by Ren et al. (2021), a 25‐item instrument comprising five subdomains: physical, psychological–emotional, informational, social, and practical needs. Each item is rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = “not needed—not applicable,” 2 = “not needed—already satisfied,” 3 = “low need,” 4 = “moderate need,” and 5 = “high need”). Higher scores indicate greater supportive care needs. In the original development study, the total scale demonstrates good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.884), with subscale reliabilities of 0.794 (physical), 0.882 (psychological–emotional), 0.856 (informational), 0.906 (social), and 0.850 (practical).

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS 29.0, AMOS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and JASP 0.19.3. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were used to summarize general and disease‐related characteristics. Random subsamples of 100 and 200 participants were drawn for the EFA and CFA, respectively. Group homogeneity was assessed using independent‐samples t‐tests and chi‐square tests. Item analysis included examination of means, standard deviations, skewness, kurtosis, ceiling and floor effects, and corrected item–total correlations (ITC). Content validity was evaluated by experts using a 4‐point Likert scale to compute the I‐CVI and S‐CVI/Ave. Construct validity was first assessed through EFA. The number of factors was determined using principal component analysis and parallel analysis. Varimax rotation was applied, and item factor loadings were evaluated. Principal component analysis was employed as the factor extraction method to examine the underlying dimensional structure of the translated items and to maximize the explained variance of the data. Although PCA is a data reduction technique, it is widely used in the preliminary exploratory stage of instrument adaptation studies, particularly when the goal is to identify the optimal factor structure and evaluate item performance (Alavi et al. 2020; Choi et al. 2026; Kang et al. 2026). Data suitability was analysed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett's test of sphericity; KMO values ≥ 0.50 (preferably ≥ 0.80) and Bartlett's test p < 0.05 were considered acceptable (DeVellis and Thorpe 2021). CFA was then performed following structural equation modelling guidelines (Kline 2023). Model fit was assessed using maximum likelihood estimation with the following indices: chi‐square, normed chi‐square, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), standardized root mean residual (SRMR), goodness‐of‐fit index (GFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), and comparative fit index (CFI). Model adequacy was defined as χ2/df < 3.0, GFI, TLI, and CFI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, and SRMR ≤ 0.05, indicating good fit (Kline 2023). Convergent and discriminant validity were determined using standardized factor loadings, average variance extracted (AVE), and construct reliability (CR), with acceptable thresholds of AVE ≥ 0.50 and CR ≥ 0.70. Criterion validity was evaluated via Pearson's correlations between the Korean SCN‐TB and Nursing Needs. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach's alpha, with coefficients ranging from 0 to 1; values ≥ 0.60 are minimally acceptable, and 0.80–0.90 indicate high reliability (DeVellis and Thorpe 2021).

Ethical Considerations

This study received approval from the Central Institutional Review Board of the C University. All procedures followed its research ethics guidelines. In accordance with IRB review for studies performed across two or more sites within the same hospital network. After obtaining agreement from the directors of nursing at each participating institution, following an explanation of the purpose, rationale, and data collection procedures, the study proceeded.

Results

General and Disease‐Related Characteristics of the Participants

The mean participant age was 63.52 years, with those aged ≥ 70 years comprising the largest group (40.0%). Men represented 65.3% (n = 197). Overall, 62.0% were married, and 72.0% lived with family members. Unemployment was reported by 69.0%, and high school graduates formed the largest education group (38.3%). Regarding health coverage, 84.0% were enrolled in the National Health Insurance. For health behaviours, 49.4% were nonsmokers and 30.3% were former smokers; most participants were nondrinkers (78.7%). The majority reported no family history of TB (85.0%) and no history of TB (83.0%). The mean duration of anti‐TB treatment was 4.58 months, with the largest subgroup on treatment for 3–6 months (52.7%, n = 158). In total, 84.7% reported no treatment interruption, and 59.7% reported no adverse drug reactions. Comorbid conditions were present in 64.3% of participants, and most had nondrug‐resistant TB (92.3%). Homogeneity testing between the EFA and CFA groups showed no statistically significant differences across any general or disease‐related characteristics (p > 0.05), indicating that the groups are comparable (Table 1).

Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics for the SCN‐TB items showed mean scores of 2.48–3.70 and standard deviations of 1.05–1.37, indicating acceptable variability. Skewness (−1.02 to 0.23) and kurtosis (−1.05–0.45) were within recommended thresholds (absolute values ≤ ±2 and ±7, respectively), supporting normality. Ceiling effects ranged from 5.3% to 29.3% and floor effects from 3.3% to 29.3%, both within acceptable limits (< 30%). Corrected ITC values ranged from 0.55 to 0.79, and all items met the ≥ 0.50 criterion (Kline 2023), confirming the suitability of all 25 items for analysis. The highest correlation was observed for items 7 and 9 in the psychological–emotional domain (r = 0.79), and the lowest for item 3 in the physical domain (r = 0.55).

Expert Content Validity

Content validity was evaluated using the I‐CVI and S‐CVI/Ave. Applying established criteria for expert panels of five or more members (I‐CVI ≥ 0.78 and S‐CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90; Lynn 1986; Polit et al. 2007), all 25 items had I‐CVI values ≥ 0.80, and the S‐CVI/Ave reached 0.97, indicating that the instrument met adequacy thresholds.

Construct Validity

Exploratory Factor Analysis

Exploratory factor analysis was performed to examine the construct of the Korean version of SCN‐TB. The KMO value was 0.92, and Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant (χ 2 = 687.59, p < 0.001). Five factors were retained using principal components extraction with Varimax rotation, based on eigenvalues and parallel analysis. All items had factor loadings ≥ 0.40 and communalities ≥ 0.50, with a total variance explained of 76.3% (Table 2) (Figure 2).

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

CFA showed the following model fit indices: χ 2 = 808.59 (df = 265, p < 0.001), SRMR = 0.07, RMSEA = 0.10, CFI = 0.99, GFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, and RFI = 0.99 (Figure 3). In this study, although the RMSEA slightly exceeded the conventional strict threshold, the incremental fit indices indicated an excellent fit (CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, NFI = 0.99). According to Marsh et al. (2004), evaluating model fit should not rely on a single prescriptive cutoff rule, as specific model characteristics can artificially inflate the RMSEA (Kenny et al. 2015). Given that the SRMR was within the acceptable range (SRMR = 0.07) and all other major indices consistently supported the model, the overall model fit was deemed acceptable.

Discriminant Validity

Discriminate validity was assessed through a comparison of the AVE for each construct with the squared inter‐construct correlations. Constructs are considered distinct when AVE exceeds the corresponding squared correlation. All AVE values in this study exceeded the squared correlations, indicating adequate distinction among factors (Table 3).

Internal Consistency Reliability

For the final 25 items, overall internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach's α = 0.962). Subscale reliabilities were physical needs α = 0.860, psychological–emotional needs α = 0.894, informational needs α = 0.876, social needs α = 0.900, and practical needs α = 0.893, each indicating good reliability.

Discussion

This study translated the SCN‐TB, developed by Ren et al. (2021), into Korean and examined its validity, reliability among Korean patients with TB. In this study, all 25 items of the Korean SCN‐TB showed adequate expert content validity, and construct validity across the five subdomains confirmed that the factor structure reported by Ren et al. (2021) was retained. These findings suggest that the measurement tool developed by Ren et al. (2021) is a highly valid and reliable instrument capable of accurately reflecting the supportive care needs of Korean TB patients. In other words, the supportive care needs of Korean TB patients can also be comprehensively explained by the following five distinct factors.

The first factor, physical needs, reflected nursing support demands related to various physical symptoms and side effects of anti‐TB medications during treatment. Symptoms such as fatigue, loss of appetite, and cough can impair the self‐care of patients (Pramono et al. 2021) and discontinuing medication due to side effects may cause treatment failure and retreatment, highlighting the need for physical support. These findings are consistent with those of prior studies reporting that patients most immediately recognize and express physical needs in clinical settings (Choi et al. 2022). Findings also support studies showing that managing adverse effects and physical support are key to sustaining long‐term treatment. Physical needs should extend beyond symptom control to include structured strategies, such as managing adverse events and providing nutritional support, which are essential for treatment adherence and long‐term treatment success.

The second factor, psychological–emotional needs, reflected the needs of patients to relieve emotional distress including anxiety, depression, and stigma experienced during TB treatment. These needs reflect emotional responses to the contagious nature of TB, including isolation and social exclusion (Contreras et al. 2017), and are aligned conceptually with the emphasis on psychological–emotional needs in the SCNF and cancer studies using the SCNS. Patients with TB often experience depressive symptoms during lengthy therapy, which impair daily functioning and quality of life, and are linked to self‐discontinuation of medications and higher treatment interruption risk (Shen et al. 2022), highlighting the need for emotional support. WHO (2024) identifies psychosocial support as a key factor in improving TB treatment outcomes, and these findings provide empirical support for this view. Our findings indicate that psychological emotional support goes beyond screening for anxiety or depression, serving as a key determinant of adherence and persistence and emphasizing the need for tailored intervention strategies.

The third factor, informational needs, encompassed requests for clear information regarding TB, including treatment plans, medication use, prognosis, and infection prevention. In this study, informational needs contributed most to overall reliability, suggesting that information provision is the primary supportive need for patients with TB. This aligns with SCNS research in cancer populations, where information needs often rank among the top domains (Huddart et al. 2018), and with Korean findings indicating that information on adverse effects and symptom management is paramount for patients with TB (Lee 2018; Ren et al. 2021). Domestic evidence also indicates that informational support plays a crucial role in promoting treatment adherence and fostering positive attitudes toward therapy. Globally, WHO (2024) highlights patient‐tailored education and information provision as key strategies for effective TB care, aligning with our findings.

The fourth factor, social needs, involved restoring social relationships, including peer interaction, community acceptance, and support group participation. This aligns with prior evidence that stigma and social changes reduce social activities during TB treatment (Van Hoorn et al. 2016) and with studies showing patients avoid interpersonal contact and limit social participation due to stigma and contagion concerns (Contreras et al. 2017). Such social constraints may reduce adherence and delay prevention and early diagnosis. Accordingly, community‐based strategies such as remote counselling and connection to self‐help groups are needed to enhance social support (Odone et al. 2018). These findings extend previous research by offering a multidimensional view of social support needs rather than treating them as a single, undifferentiated domain.

The fifth factor, practical needs, reflected concrete support for daily life, including financial burdens, reduced medical costs, and follow‐up care services. This aligns with studies showing that patients with TB view financial burden as a major treatment barrier and need support to address it. Among high‐risk groups, financial hardship is a key cause of treatment discontinuation (Odone et al. 2018), highlighting the link between practical needs and treatment continuity. This aligns with the “practical needs” domain of the SCNF in cancer care, indicating that financial and practical support commonly influence treatment success across diseases. Socioeconomically vulnerable patients may miss timely treatment or struggle with adherence due to financial constraints (Oshi et al. 2020). Consistent with reported vulnerabilities in older adults, these findings indicate that practical needs are a key domain directly linked to treatment continuity for patients with TB.

Second, the final 25‐item Korean SCN‐TB demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.962), exceeding the reliability of the original instrument (Cronbach's α = 0.884). This indicates that the Korean version provides stable and reliable assessments across cultural contexts. Compared with the reliability range reported for the SCNS‐SF34 in patients with cancer (Cronbach's α = 0.86–0.96) (Boyes et al. 2009), our findings match or exceed prior benchmarks, supporting the utility of the SCN‐TB as a specialized tool for patients with TB.

The supportive care needs instrument validated in this study enables a multidimensional assessment of the physical, psychological–emotional, social, informational, and practical needs of patients with TB. Consequently, it underscores the importance of patient‐centred care and provides foundational evidence for tailored patient education, particularly in domains with low need fulfilment or limited understanding. The validated instrument for supportive care needs among patients with TB is expected to support need‐based, individualized nursing care in clinical practice, enhance treatment adherence and quality of life, and provide essential evidence for public health policy development.

Limitations

We assessed the level of supportive care needs among patients with TB but did not evaluate how well these needs were met or examine predictive validity for outcomes such as medication adherence and quality of life, which is a limitation. Additionally, given that the RMSEA value can be sensitive to the size of the sample, we suggest that further research involving a larger sample size would be valuable to supplement and confirm the model fit observed in this study.

Implications for Nursing Practice

The findings of this study provide a standardized, culturally adapted instrument to systematically assess the multidimensional needs of TB patients by validating the Korean version of the SCN‐TB.

Clinically, the clear delineation of the five subdomains enables nurses to move away from a one‐size‐fits‐all approach and instead design and implement individualized, patient‐centred nursing interventions. Particularly, the insights gained from the physical needs domain underscore the critical role of nurses in proactively managing treatment side effects and symptoms. By utilizing the Korean SCN‐TB to identify these specific physical vulnerabilities early, nurses can develop structured symptom‐control strategies that are essential for ensuring patient adherence to long‐term anti‐TB medication regimens. Furthermore, the instrument may be used in nursing education to strengthen nurses' competencies in comprehensive needs assessment and patient‐centred care. Data generated using the instrument may also provide evidence for identifying unmet needs in healthcare and community settings, prioritizing nursing services, and developing public health policy. Future research may use the Korean SCN‐TB to examine associations between supportive care needs and treatment adherence, quality of life, and treatment outcomes, as well as to develop and evaluate needs‐based nursing interventions. In particular, further validation of the psychometric properties of the SCN‐TB and comparison of supportive care needs across countries and cultural contexts may enhance understanding of both common and culturally specific needs among patients with tuberculosis and contribute to the development of an international nursing knowledge base for patient‐centred tuberculosis care.

Conclusions

This study directly addressed the gap of a culturally adapted and psychometrically evaluated instrument to evaluate the supportive care needs of Korean patients with tuberculosis. To achieve this research objective, the SCN‐TB, originally developed by Ren et al. (2021), was translated into Korean and rigorously evaluated for its validity, reliability, and measurement invariance. The Korean SCN‐TB retained the original five‐factor structure comprising 25 items, establishing high construct validity. Crucially, the Korean version demonstrated excellent internal consistency. Through the clear delineation of the five subdomains, this study provides deep clinical insights into patient‐related issues. The physical needs domain emphasizes the necessity of structured strategies for symptom control and anti‐TB medication side effects to ensure treatment adherence. In conclusion, the validated Korean SCN‐TB effectively resolves the empirical gap by providing a standardized, multidimensional instrument that comprehensively assesses the holistic needs of TB patients. This study offers foundational evidence for shifting toward need‐based, individualized nursing care and patient‐centred clinical practice.
