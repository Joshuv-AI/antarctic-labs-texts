Expression patterns of GPX4 and LINC00618 across disease phases in pediatric B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia (BCP-ALL): an exploratory study
Authors: Maryam Shayanmanesh, Seyed Esmaeil Ahmadi, Mohammad Faranoush, Majid Safa, Seyed-Mohammad-Kazem Nourbakhsh, Akram Sadat Jaafarian, Ali Amini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) remains a significant challenge in pediatric malignancies, particularly in cases of relapse and treatment resistance. This study aimed to investigate the expression patterns of ferroptosis-related genes, GPX4 and LINC00618, across different disease phases in pediatric B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia (BCP-ALL).MethodsThis case-control study analyzed 82 samples from pediatric BCP-ALL patients comprising 14 new cases, 29 in remission, and 17 relapsed cases alongside 22 healthy controls. Expression levels of GPX4 and LINC00618 were evaluated using quantitative real-time PCR. The relationship between gene expression and clinical parameters was assessed. Statistical analyses were performed employing parametric or nonparametric tests.ResultsGPX4 expression was significantly elevated in ALL patients compared to controls (p < 0.01), with notably higher levels in new cases (p < 0.01) and relapsed patients (p < 0.05). Similarly, LINC00618 exhibited significant upregulation in ALL patients (p < 0.05), particularly in new cases (p < 0.01) and relapsed patients (p < 0.05). Both genes demonstrated reduced expression during the remission. External validation using the GSE13159 dataset demonstrated a consistent direction of increased GPX4 expression in ALL samples compared to controls.ConclusionsOur findings suggest phase-associated expression patterns of GPX4 and LINC00618 in pediatric BCP-ALL. The consistent direction of GPX4 expression observed in external validation supports its potential relevance in leukemic biology. These results provide a basis for further studies to clarify the role of ferroptosis-related pathways in disease progression and therapeutic response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16296-5.

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is characterized by the malignant clonal proliferation of highly heterogeneous lymphoblastic progenitor cells. It is recognized as the most common childhood malignancy, accounting for approximately 80% of pediatric leukemia cases [1, 2]. Despite significant advances in overall survival rates of B-cell precursor ALL (BCP-ALL), which is the most common subtype, patients with primary refractory or relapsed (R/R) disease continue to face a dismal prognosis [3–5]. Approximately 2% of ALL patients are refractory to induction chemotherapy, and 10–15% experience relapse. Furthermore, 55% of those who achieve a second complete remission subsequently relapse, highlighting the critical need for novel therapeutic approaches [6]. The main challenge in treating refractory and relapsed leukemia lies in overcoming resistance to cell death mechanisms [7]. Recent research has discovered several genetic factors involved in ALL progression [8]. However, many molecular biomarkers remain to be investigated. Therefore, the identification of novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets for ALL treatment is essential to improve patient outcomes and prevent recurrence.

Cells can undergo various types of programmed and accidental death pathways [9]. Ferroptosis constitutes a newly identified form of programmed cell death that is predominantly instigated by the iron-dependent accumulation of phospholipid hydroperoxides (PLOOH), which inflicts lethal damage to cellular membranes [10]. The glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a glutathione (GSH)-dependent enzyme, is a master regulator in the ferroptotic process, which constitutively detoxifies lipid hydroperoxides into inactive lipid alcohols, thereby preventing membrane destruction and subsequent ferroptotic cell death via this pathway [11, 12]. Unlike other GPX family members, the selenoprotein GPX4’s significance extends beyond its antioxidant capabilities, functioning as the primary defense system against ferroptosis [13]. Studies have shown GPX4 dependence in therapy-resistant tumor cells for survival, making it a potential therapeutic target [14, 15]. Several studies offer novel insights into GPX4 gene expression as a prognostic indicator in several malignancies [16, 17]. While GPX4 promotes tumorigenesis by preventing ferroptosis [18], its expression also shows tumor suppressor functions in certain cancers [19, 20].

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are characterized as RNA transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides in length without protein-coding capacity [21], have emerged as critical regulators of cellular processes. LncRNAs are closely associated with multiple regulatory processes through their interactions with DNA, RNA, and proteins. These processes include regulation of chromatin function, RNA processing, and protein synthesis, thereby affecting the pathogenesis of disease, tumorigenesis, and survival of cancer cells [22–24]. Of particular interest in the context of ferroptosis regulation, long intergenic non-protein coding RNA 00618 (LINC00618), a newly identified lncRNA, has shown potential involvement in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cell survival, and its knockdown enhances chemotherapy resistance. Recent studies suggest that LINC00618 may regulate cell survival pathways through interaction with key proteins involved in cell death mechanisms, including potential modulation of ferroptosis-related genes like GPX4 [25]. However, the expression patterns of ferroptosis-related genes in BCP-ALL remain poorly understood. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the significance of LINC00618 and GPX4 gene expression in pediatric BCP-ALL and explore their potential association with disease progression.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

In the present case-control study, a total of 82 samples (comprising 67 bone marrow and 15 peripheral blood specimens) were procured from patients diagnosed with BCP-ALL who were younger than 15 years old and from healthy controls. The samples were obtained from four pediatric oncology centers: Rasul Akram Hospital, Imam Khomeini Hospital, Ali Asghar Hospital, and Mehr Hospital in Tehran, Iran, between July 2023 and December 2024. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences (Approval No. IR.IUMS.REC.1402.274), and all procedures were carried out in accordance with applicable ethical guidelines and regulations. Patients were assessed at three different clinical phases of pediatric BCP-ALL, including 14 new case patients, 29 patients in remission, and 17 relapsed cases. Additionally, bone marrow specimens were acquired from 22 sex and age-matched healthy individuals or patients without bone marrow involvement which, all of them were confirmed to be free of bone marrow infiltration. Prior to the collection of specimens, written informed consent was secured from all participants or their legal guardians. The diagnosis of ALL was made based on a combination of clinical evaluation, cytomorphology, immunophenotyping, and cytogenetics analysis. New case patients identified with B-ALL underwent treatment following the guidelines set forth by the Berlin-Frankfurt-Munster (BFM) protocol [26]. The parameters for attaining complete remission encompassed a bone marrow blast percentage less than 5%, coupled with the absence of leukemic blasts in both peripheral blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), alongside the absence of any indicators of extramedullary disease. A relapse was determined when there was a reappearance of lymphoblasts in the bone marrow, reaching or exceeding 25%, and/or the presence of localized leukemic infiltrates at any site [27]. The inclusion criteria encompassed patients younger than 15 years with a definitive diagnosis of ALL, which was validated by both an oncologist’s evaluation and immunophenotyping results, and willingness to participate in the research after the procurement of formal informed consent. The study excluded patients who were non-cooperative in providing samples, had incomplete laboratory records, or suffered from medical conditions other than ALL. The investigation focused on examining various blood parameters, including measurements of hemoglobin levels, platelets, blast cell percentages, and white blood cell counts. RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Mononuclear cells (MNCs) were isolated from whole blood specimens in EDTA tubes using the density-gradient centrifugation method by Ficoll solution (mBio, South Korea). For this, 1–2 ml 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was mixed with an equivalent volume of blood sample. A new Falcon tube was filled with 1–2 ml Ficoll and gradually diluted blood with PBS added to it and subsequently centrifuged for 3 min at 4000 rpm. Next, the white layer containing MNCs was collected, washed with PBS three times (centrifuging at 2500 rpm for 5 min each time). Then, the precipitated cell pellet was lysed, and total RNA was extracted with 1 ml RNA Xplus reagent (SinaClon, Iran) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Ultimately, RNA extraction was carried out through a sequential process using chloroform for phase separation, isopropanol for the precipitation of RNA, and 75% ethanol for washing and purifying the RNA pellet. The integrity of RNA was assessed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel and visualization of 18 S and 28 S rRNA bands. Additionally, the quality and concentration of extracted RNA were assessed employing a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) to determine both A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios. Subsequently, probable DNA contaminants were eliminated using DNase-I treatment (Thermo Fisher, USA), followed by reverse transcription of 1 µg RNA to complementary DNA (cDNA) using a synthesis kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Yekta Tajhiz Azma, Iran).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted utilizing specific gene primers and RealQ Plus 2x Master Mix Green without ROX (Ampliqon, Denmark) on Light Cycler 96 Real-time PCR system (Roche Diagnostics, Lewes, UK). The PCR assays were conducted with a cumulative volume of 15 µl for each reaction consisting 7.5 µl of SYBR Green Master Mix, 4.5 µl of distilled water, 1.5 µl of amplified cDNA, and 0.75 µl each of 10 µM forward and reverse primer. The thermal cycling parameters incorporated an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by two-step amplification: 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, and annealing and elongation at 60 °C for 20 s. The efficiency of primers was validated, yielding R2 values exceeding 0.99, which allowed for the application of the Livak method (2−ΔΔCt) to quantify the relative expression of GPX4 and LINC00618 genes with housekeeping gene beta-actin serving as an internal control and normalization factor. Individual Ct scores were calculated as the averages of duplicate assessments. Furthermore, the specificity of the PCR reaction was analyzed by melting curve analysis. External validation using public transcriptomic data

To assess the reproducibility of our findings, external validation was performed using the publicly available GSE13159 dataset from the Gene Expression Omnibus. Processed microarray expression data generated on the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 platform were retrieved and analyzed. Samples were classified based on available clinical annotations into ALL cases and non-leukemia bone marrow controls. Probe-level expression data corresponding to GPX4 were identified using gene symbol annotation, and expression values were extracted for downstream analysis. Statistical comparison between groups was performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Data visualization was conducted using boxplots with overlaid individual data points. All analyses were performed using the latest version of R (RStudio 2026.01.0, build 392), and the full analysis code is provided in the Supplementary Materials to ensure reproducibility. The inability to externally validate LINC00618 due to platform-specific limitations represents a constraint and underscores the importance of future validation using RNA-seq-based datasets with broader coverage of non-coding transcripts.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 and GraphPad Prism version 10.4.0. The normality of data distribution was initially assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. As some variables did not follow a normal distribution, and to ensure consistency across comparisons, all group comparisons were conducted using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test. Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s rank correlation test. Data are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive data

A total of 60 patients samples were analyzed, including 14 new case BCP-ALL patients (mean age 8 ± 4.89 years), 29 patients in remission (mean age 8.51 ± 5.18 years), 17 relapsed patients (mean age 11.3 ± 7.51 years), and 22 controls (mean age 4.37 ± 5.22 years). Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics, including complete blood count parameters across all disease phases, are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

LINC00618 expression levels at new case, remission and relapse phases of pediatric B-ALL patients

To assess clinical relevance of LINC00618 in disease progression, we collected samples from 60 BCP-ALL patients and 22 controls and analyzed relative mRNA expression. As depicted in Fig. 3, LINC00618 gene expression was significantly upregulated in B-ALL patients compared to control group (Fold change: 3.43, p < 0.05). We next analyzed LINC00618 expression levels across different B-ALL phases (new case, remission, and relapse) patients. Based on our results LINC00618 expression in new cases was markedly higher than control (Fold change: 4.67, p < 0.01). Furthermore, to examine whether BCP-ALL treatment affects LINC00618 expression, qRT-PCR assays showed decreased LINC00618 post-treatment, indicating its potential as a therapeutic marker. The mentioned gene expression in patients who underwent treatment was significantly downregulated in comparison to new case phase (Fold change: 2.93, p < 0.01). This study demonstrated that the relapse group exhibited remarkably higher levels of LINC00618 expression compared with both controls and patients during remission (Fold change: 4.87, p < 0.05 and Fold change: 3.05, p < 0.05 respectively) (Fig. 4). In addition, a significant correlation was found between LINC00618 expression at the time of relapse and the WBC count, platelet count, and hemoglobin levels (r = 0.842, p < 0.05, r = 0.708, p < 0.05, and r = 0.878, p < 0.05). Nonetheless, there was an absence of a statistically significant correlation between LINC00618 gene expression levels at new case and remission phases with clinical characteristics including age, WBC count, blast percentage, platelet count, and hemoglobin levels (Table 3).

External validation of GPX4 expression

To evaluate the reproducibility of our findings, GPX4 expression was analyzed in the publicly available GSE13159 dataset. A total of 824 samples were included, comprising 750 acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) cases and 74 non-leukemia healthy bone marrow controls. GPX4 expression was significantly higher in ALL samples compared to controls (median: 0.736 vs. 0.717, p = 5.35 × 10⁻⁶). Although the absolute difference in median expression was modest, the large sample size and consistent direction of change support the robustness of this finding. The dataset includes multiple ALL subtypes rather than exclusively pediatric B-ALL; however, the observed trend remains consistent with our results, supporting the observed upregulation of GPX4 in leukemic conditions across independent cohorts (Fig. 5).

Discussion

With considerable progress that has been made in the improvement of cure rate for BCP-ALL pediatric patients, management of relapsed and refractory cases is still a difficult issue [28]. The circumvention of apoptotic processes significantly contributes to the suboptimal therapeutic responses observed in pediatric patients with high-risk ALL, thereby underscoring the imperative for innovative treatment approaches aimed at augmenting programmed cell death [29]. The mechanisms governing cellular death and survival are intricately modulated by redox equilibrium within the cellular state [30]. Dysregulation of redox homeostasis in leukemic cells, primarily due to an elevation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) alongside a simultaneous enhancement of antioxidant pathways to mitigate elevated ROS concentrations, leads to a marked reliance of cancer cells on their antioxidant defense systems. Therefore, this condition renders them particularly susceptible to therapies targeting redox pathways [31]. The production of ROS in proximity to lipid membranes selectively affects polyunsaturated fatty acids, thereby causing lipid peroxidation [32]. The principal protection against lipid peroxidation is provided by GPX4, a crucial gatekeeper for the induction of ferroptosis, which detoxifies hydroperoxides within cellular membranes [33]. In this study, the examination of GPX4 expression revealed a significant upregulation in ALL patients relative to controls (Fold change: 2.08, p < 0.01), with particularly elevated expression levels in new case patients (Fold change: 2.45, p < 0.01) and in those experiencing relapse (Fold change: 2.03, p < 0.05). This expression pattern is consistent with prior studies that elucidated the essential function of GPX4 in therapy-resistant cancer cells, where it acts as a primary defense mechanism against ferroptotic cell death. Niu et al. conducted a comprehensive study on the expression levels of GPX4 in B-ALL and their findings indicated that GPX4 elevated expression is significantly associated with unfavorable prognostic outcomes and increased risk of bone marrow relapse [34]. Furthermore, Lalonde et al. implemented a genomic investigation of seven B-ALL cell lines using the CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing technique. Their research uncovered a critical set of genes essential for ALL cell survival, particularly ferroptosis-related genes. The persistent ALL cells exhibit a marked sensitivity to the inhibition and deletion of the GPX4 gene and promote ferroptosis, which is attributed to the impairment of the GPX4-independent secondary lipid peroxidation detoxification mechanism [35]. As well, accumulating studies collectively pointed to the oncogenic properties of GPX4, which is in line with our findings. Recent investigations have indicated that the expression level of GPX4 is elevated in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [16], thyroid carcinoma [36], hepatocellular carcinoma [37], and lung adenocarcinoma [38]. However, the findings of various studies indicated that the expression of the GPX4 gene was diminished in AML cells compared to mononuclear cells derived from bone marrow [18], evidence suggests that an elevated expression of this gene correlates with adverse outcomes in patient survival [17]. On the contrary, GPX4 is also recognized as a tumor suppressor, impeding cell cycle progression and decreasing tumor cell growth [39–41]. The association between the expression levels of GPX4 and the clinical outcomes of cancer patients remains controversial, primarily due to the complex relations between oxidative stress and cellular redox. Nevertheless, numerous studies suggest that heightened concentrations of redox enzymes in tumor cells frequently correspond to hyperproliferation of tumor cells [42, 43]. Based on these findings, the elevated GPX4 expression observed in both new cases and relapsed patients may contribute to suppression of ferroptosis but requires further studies.

There is emerging evidence that a subset of lncRNAs act as diagnostic, therapeutic, and prognostic factors in ALL [44]. Particularly, lncRNAs have gained considerable attention recently due to their involvement in regulating ferroptosis as tumor promoters and suppressors, influencing cancer progression [45, 46]. LINC00618 emerged as a candidate of interest in this study due to its distinctive expression across various cancers and its role as a prominent molecular marker in leukemic cells [25, 47]. In the current investigation, the analysis revealed significant upregulation of LINC00618 in ALL patients compared to healthy controls (Fold change: 3.43, p < 0.05). This dysregulation was even more pronounced in new case (Fold change: 4.67, p < 0.01) and relapse patients (Fold change: 4.87, p < 0.05). Consistent with our findings, elevated expression of LINC00618 has been reported in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), in which it promotes cellular proliferation [47]. Also, LINC00618 was identified as part of a signature comprising protein-coding genes and lncRNAs that classified glioblastoma patients into distinct high-risk and low-risk groups. The findings revealed that elevated expression levels of LINC00618 in post-surgery patients correlated with poor prognosis. Consequently, its higher expression may be associated with shorter survival rates [48]. LINC00618 demonstrated a multifaceted role in cancer through its interactions with both ferroptosis and lipid metabolism. LINC00618 was identified as a key regulator of ferroptosis in AML [25]. Additionally, research conducted on HCC demonstrated that increased expression of LINC00618 was associated with cholesterol dysregulation, leading to increased cholesterol levels that subsequently promote cancer cell proliferation and migration [47]. Generally, enhanced cholesterol biosynthesis exerts a protective effect against ferroptosis, inhibiting this cell death through various mechanisms. Elevated cellular cholesterol levels contribute to membrane biogenesis while reducing lipid membrane susceptibility to peroxidation [49]. Moreover, mevalonate (MVA) pathway is a critical metabolic cascade in ferroptosis regulation. The MVA is the primary biosynthetic way for cholesterol production and generates isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP). IPP is an essential precursor molecule for the synthesis and maturation of GPX4 [50]. The metabolic stress caused by lipid accumulation heightens the requirement for stable GPX4 expression, which is a negative regulator of this process. Elevated GPX4 expression in cancer cells contributes to their resistance to ferroptosis, and the suppression of GPX4 reduces tumor cell survival [49]. The findings of our study may be associated with the roles of GPX4 and LINC00618 genes within this ferroptotic signaling pathway.

Another study utilizing a rat model of gentamicin-induced acute kidney injury demonstrated a correlation between the inhibition of ferroptosis and increases in LINC00618 and GPX4 expression levels [51]. In contrast to earlier research, LINC00618 expression was significantly reduced in patients diagnosed with AML, with a subsequent increase following therapeutic intervention. The interaction between LINC00618 and lymphoid-specific helicase (LSH) results in the downregulation of SLC7A11 and GPX4 proteins, which induces ferroptosis via an apoptosis-dependent manner [25].

In this study, we evaluated the expression levels of GPX4 and LINC00618 genes in peripheral blood and bone marrow. Our results revealed no significant difference in the expression levels of these genes between the two tissues (p > 0.05), which is consistent with previous findings. These findings highlight the potential significance of GPX4 and LINC00618 as biomarkers, suggesting that their expression patterns may provide insights into disease status, though formal evaluation of diagnostic performance was not conducted in this study [52, 53]. External validation using the GSE13159 dataset confirmed the upregulation of GPX4 in leukemic samples relative to non-leukemia controls. While this dataset encompasses a broader spectrum of ALL subtypes rather than specifically pediatric B-ALL, the consistency in the direction of GPX4 dysregulation supports its potential relevance as a feature of leukemic biology. This suggests that GPX4 upregulation may reflect a conserved mechanism associated with leukemic cell survival, potentially linked to ferroptosis resistance.

In contrast, LINC00618 could not be externally validated due to its absence in the microarray platform, reflecting the limited coverage of long non-coding RNAs in earlier transcriptomic technologies. Future studies using RNA sequencing datasets focused on pediatric B-ALL are required to confirm the role of LINC00618.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Our study was conducted in a relatively modest cohort, and the newly diagnosed and relapsed groups were not longitudinally matched. Follow-up duration was limited, and minimal residual disease (MRD) data were not systematically available, precluding risk-stratified analyses. Complete cytogenetic and CSF data were not accessible for all patients, limiting subgroup analyses. Cross-sectional gene expression assessment restricts causal inference regarding GPX4 and LINC00618. ROC curve analysis was not performed due to the limited sample size and exploratory design, to avoid potential overestimation of diagnostic performance. Future studies with larger, multicenter cohorts, comprehensive genomic characterization, standardized MRD assessment, longitudinal follow-up, and integration of public transcriptomic datasets are warranted to confirm and generalize these findings. Despite these limitations, our study provides initial evidence of phase-associated expression patterns of GPX4 and LINC00618 in pediatric BCP-ALL, offering a basis for further mechanistic and translational investigations.

Conclusion

This study investigated the expression pattern of GPX4 and LINC00618 genes across newly diagnosed, remission, and relapse phases in pediatric BCP-ALL. The findings demonstrated a significantly increased expression of both genes in newly diagnosed and relapsed patients, with reduced levels during remission. These results are consistent with existing literature and suggest potential involvement of these genes in leukemic biology. External validation further supported the consistency of GPX4 expression patterns across independent cohorts. A plausible hypothesis is that LINC00618 expression be associated with GPX4 regulation through pathways related to cholesterol metabolism and the MVA pathway. This interaction may contribute to the inhibition of ferroptosis and support leukemic cell survival. However, given the complexity of these pathways, further studies are required to clarify the underlying mechanisms.
