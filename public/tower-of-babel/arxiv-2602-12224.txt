Bandit Learning in Matching Markets with Interviews
Amirmahdi Mirfakhar, Xuchuang Wang, Mengfan Xu, Hedyeh Beyhaghi, Mohammad Hajiesmaili

Abstract
Two-sided matching markets rely on preferences from both sides, yet it is often impractical to evaluate preferences. Participants, therefore, conduct a limited number of interviews, which provide early, noisy impressions and shape final decisions. We study bandit learning in matching markets with interviews, modeling interviews as low-cost hints that reveal partial preference information to both sides. Our framework departs from existing work by allowing firm-side uncertainty: firms, like agents, may be unsure of their own preferences and can make early hiring mistakes by hiring less preferred agents. To handle this, we extend the firm’s action space to allow strategic deferral (choosing not to hire in a round), enabling recovery from suboptimal hires and supporting decentralized learning without coordination. We design novel algorithms for (i) a centralized setting with an omniscient interview allocator and (ii) decentralized settings with two types of firm-side feedback. Across all settings, our algorithms achieve time-independent regret, a substantial improvement over the O ⁡ ( log ⁡ T ) O(\log T) regret bounds known for learning stable matchings without interviews. Also, under mild structured markets, decentralized performance matches the centralized counterpart up to polynomial factors in the number of agents and firms.

Introduction

Two-sided matching markets
[
19
]
provide a foundational model for labor markets
[
20
]
, school choice
[
1
]
, and online platforms
[
9
]
. A market comprises two sides (e.g.,
n
agents and
m
firms), each with preferences over the other side. A matching is
stable
if no agent–firm pair would both prefer to deviate from their current assignments
[
7
]
.
In many markets, preferences are
not known a priori
: participants learn them through interaction, and any given participant can only evaluate a small fraction of potential partners. This motivates
bandit learning
in matching markets
[
14
,
17
,
15
,
10
,
8
]
, where agents repeatedly apply to firms, observe outcomes, and aim to learn a stable matching while minimizing regret. Most existing work assumes (i) only agents are uncertain and (ii) firms follow fixed, known preferences and always hire among applicants. These assumptions simplify decentralized dynamics, but do not capture the following two pervasive features of real markets.

Two missing ingredients: interviews and uncertain firms.

First, participants typically engage in low-cost preliminary interactions (interviews, visits, screening calls) before submitting final applications. Such interactions produce
noisy side-observations
of match quality that can accelerate learning. This resembles
bandits with hints

[
5
,
18
]
and, more broadly,
algorithms with predictions

[
16
]
. However, interviews create a key structural constraint absent from existing hinted-bandit models: they simultaneously
reveal information
and
restrict feasible actions
, since an agent may apply only to an interviewed firm
[
13
,
3
]
.

Second, firms may also be uncertain about their preferences (i.e., their rankings must be learned). Early misrankings can cause firms to reject strong candidates, slowing convergence to stability and increasing regret. To model how firms hedge against uncertainty in practice, we allow a firm to
defer
hiring in a round (remain vacant) rather than commit to an applicant it currently deems suboptimal. We emphasize that this is a
modeled action option
(not a game-theoretic manipulation): it expands the firm action space in a way that can stabilize decentralized learning dynamics and is consistent with evidence that firm-side hiring deferrals can shape stable outcomes in static/non-learning settings
[
11
]
.

Model at a glance.

We study two-sided bandit learning with interviews and uncertainty on
both
sides. In round
t
, every agent
a
selects
k
firms to interview. Each interviewed pair
(a,f)
yields stochastic, noisy feedback to
both
sides with unknown means
\mu_{a,f}
and
\mu_{f,a}
, representing their expected utilities from matching with each other. After interviewing, each agent applies to one of its interviewed firms; a firm may accept its most-preferred applicant
or defer
and remain vacant. When a match occurs, both sides receive stochastic rewards drawn from the corresponding distributions.

We evaluate performance via
agent regret
: the gap between the expected reward of the agent’s stable match and the agent’s accrued reward over
T
rounds. We study both a
centralized
setting with an interview allocator and
decentralized
settings without it. A central challenge in decentralization is the information revealed to agents: we restrict attention to
minimal public firm-side signals
revealed to all agents—either
vacancy-only
feedback (whether a firm filled its position) or
anonymous hiring-change
feedback (whether a firm’s hire changed), without identities. As shown in Appendix
5.2
, such coarse firm-side signal is necessary for decentralized algorithms especially in unstructured markets.

A guiding question.

Existing bandit-matching work without interviews typically yields regret that scales at least logarithmically with the horizon, often
O(\log T)
, even under stronger observability assumptions
[
14
,
17
,
15
]
. Interviews add side-observations before committing to an application, suggesting that learning could be dramatically faster. This leads to our central question:

Can a market leverage a small number of interviews per round to learn a stable matching with
horizon (time)-independent
regret, in a decentralized manner with only
anonymous
firm-side signals, even when firms themselves are uncertain and may defer hiring?

Answering this question requires addressing two challenges.

\blacktriangleright

Interviews as hints under competition and stability:

Interviews provide side-observations, but also impose an action constraint (apply only to interviewed firms). Unlike existing bandits-with-hints results in single-agent
[
23
,
4
]
and multi-agent
[
18
]
, our environment couples learners through competition: agents’ choices interact and can create persistent
collisions
, which impede stability and inflate regret. Moreover, in our decentralized setting, agents do
not
observe match identities or firm preference list; they only see vacancy or anonymous hiring-change signals.

\blacktriangleright

Uncertain firms and controlled deferral:

When firms learn their rankings, early misrankings can induce rejections of strong applicants, creating feedback loops that redirect agents and slow convergence. Allowing firms to defer can mitigate premature commitments, but it must be carefully controlled: deferral should not itself become a source of regret, and it must support convergence to stable outcomes.

We introduce a two-sided bandit model with interviews and uncertain firms, and show that interviews enable

(

) agent regret, with explicit dependence on market parameters.

\blacktriangleright

Modeling uncertain firms with a deferral option.

We model firm-side uncertainty and integrate it into the two-sided learning process (

Model

Consider a two-sided market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
with
n
agents (forming set
\mathcal{A}
) and
m
firms (forming set
\mathcal{F}
), where
n\leq m
.
Each agent-firm pair
(a,f)
is associated with two reward distributions:
\mathcal{D}_{a,f}
for agent
a\in\mathcal{A}
, with mean
u_{a,f}
(the expected reward of
a
when matched with
f

\mathcal{D}_{f,a}
for firm
f\in\mathcal{F}
, with mean
u_{f,a}
(the expected reward of
f
when matched with

).
Without loss of generality, we assume that the distributions are independent across different agent–firm pairs and across time steps, and that the rewards are bounded in
[0,1]
, consistent with the standard literature.
We further assume a heterogeneous market with strictly ordered preferences. Specifically, for every agent
a\in\mathcal{A}
and any two distinct firms
f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have
u_{a,f}\neq u_{a,f^{\prime}}
, and similarly, for every firm
f\in\mathcal{F}
and any two distinct agents
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
,
u_{f,a}\neq u_{f,a^{\prime}}
.
This assumption rules out indifferences and induces a strict total ordering of firms for each agent and of agents for each firm, ensuring well-defined preference lists on both sides of the market.

For each agent
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the
ground-truth preference list

O_{a}
ranks firms in
\mathcal{F}
by decreasing
u_{a,f}
, and we write
f\underset{O_{a}}{>}f^{\prime}
if
u_{a,f}>u_{a,f^{\prime}}
.
Analogously, each firm
f\in\mathcal{F}
has a ground-truth preference list
O_{f}
ranking agents in
\mathcal{A}
by decreasing
u_{f,a}
, with
a\underset{O_{f}}{>}a^{\prime}
defined similarly.

In our model, agents do not know their preferences
a priori
(equivalently, the underlying reward distributions) and must learn them over time. On the firm side, we consider two scenarios:
certain firms
, which know their preference lists in advance, and
uncertain firms
, which must learn their preference lists over time.
Each agent maintains an estimate
\hat{u}_{a,f}(t)
of
u_{a,f}
at time
t
, and each firm maintains an estimate
\hat{u}_{f,a}(t)
of
u_{f,a}
. The preference lists induced by these estimates are the

, denoted by
\hat{O}_{a}(t)
and
\hat{O}_{f}(t)
. For a certain firm
f
, we have
\hat{u}_{f,\cdot}(t)=u_{f,\cdot}
and thus
\hat{O}_{f}(t)=O_{f}
for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
.

For policy

\displaystyle\text{Optimal}:\mathbb{E}[\overline{R}_{a}(\pi,T)]\doteq\sum_{t=1}^{T}u_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}-\mathbb{E}[X_{a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t)}(t)],

(4)

\displaystyle\text{Pessimal}:\mathbb{E}[\underline{R}_{a}(\pi,T)]\doteq\sum_{t=1}^{T}u_{a,\underline{f^{*}_{a}}}-\mathbb{E}[X_{a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t)}(t)],

(5)

\alpha
-Reducible Markets.

is a

if

are mutual top choices, i.e.,

f\underset{O_{a}}{>}f^{\prime}
for all

Definition 2.1
.

A market

is

if every
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})

2.1
Extended Action Space for Firms’ Uncertainty

A key departure from prior work is that firms may be
uncertain
about their own preferences. If an uncertain firm is forced to always hire, then early estimation errors can “lock in” an unstable outcome: the firm keeps hiring the agent it currently (incorrectly) believes is best, and agents may receive no local evidence that anything has changed. We therefore extend the firm action space to allow a firm to
strategically abstain
from hiring in selected rounds. Abstention lets a firm wait while its estimates improve and, crucially, makes the firm publicly
vacant
through the firm-side feedback sets
\mathcal{V}(t)
and
\mathcal{V}^{+}(t)
, creating a mechanism for previously rejected agents to revisit the firm and unwind deadlocks in decentralized learning.

Example 2.2

(Why abstention is necessary)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
have agents
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
and (strategic) uncertain firms
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
with ground-truth preferences

\begin{array}[]{lr}O_{a_{1}}:\langle f_{1}^{\dagger}\succ f_{2}\rangle&O_{f_{1}}:\langle a_{1}^{\dagger}\succ a_{2}\rangle\\
O_{a_{2}}:\langle f_{1}\succ f_{2}^{\dagger}\rangle&O_{f_{2}}:\langle a_{2}^{\dagger}\succ a_{1}\rangle.\end{array}

The unique stable matching is
(a_{i},f_{i})_{i\in[2]}
(marked by
\dagger
s).

Suppose that at time
t
the estimated preferences are

\begin{array}[]{lr}\hat{O}_{a_{1}}(t):\langle f_{1}\succ f_{2}^{\dagger}\rangle&\hat{O}_{f_{1}}(t):\langle a_{2}^{\dagger}\succ a_{1}\rangle\\
\hat{O}_{a_{2}}(t):\langle f_{1}^{\dagger}\succ f_{2}\rangle&\hat{O}_{f_{2}}(t):\langle a_{2}\succ a_{1}^{\dagger}\rangle,\end{array}

so only
f_{1}
is wrong (it swaps
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
). The stable matching under
\hat{O}(t)
is
(a_{1},f_{2})
and
(a_{2},f_{1})
, which is unstable under the ground truth.

Now assume that at some later time
t^{\prime}>t
, firm
f_{1}
learns the correct order, i.e.,
\hat{O}_{f_{1}}(t^{\prime})=O_{f_{1}}
. In a decentralized setting, this correction may be

to agents, so if firms always hire, the market can remain stuck at
(a_{1},f_{2}),(a_{2},f_{1})
.

If instead

can abstain at

, it can

becomes vacant and appears in the firm-side feedback, which all agents observe. This vacancy triggers

to move to

back to

, restoring the stable matching
(a_{i},f_{i})_{i\in[2]}
.

Formally, we allow each firm

to choose a private binary hiring decision
\gamma_{f}(t)\in\{0,1\}

\gamma_{f}(t)=1

means remain vacant. The decision is taken after interviews, once

observes its applicant set
\mathcal{P}_{f}(t)
. With this modification, the realized reward of agent

becomes

\displaystyle X_{a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t)}=\gamma_{f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t)}(t)\,X_{a,f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t)}\,\mathbf{1}\{f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t)\neq\emptyset\}.

(6)

Accordingly, beyond agent-side learning, we introduce a
strategic rejection policy
(
Algorithm

1
) that selects
\gamma_{f}(t)
each round. We call any firm that runs
Algorithm

1
a
strategic firm
, even if it never abstains; Like for a strategic
certain

Algorithm

1
never triggers abstention, so
\gamma_{f}(t)=1
for all

. Firms that hire in every round are

(even if uncertain). We defer the formal policy to
Section

5.1
.

Algorithmic Paradigms and Preliminaries

Here, we first formally present two different algorithm design paradigms

Centralized vs. Decentralized Algorithms.

We study two settings. In the

\blacktriangleright

We assume an omniscient
C
entral
I
nterview
A
llocator (
CIA

at every round.

\blacktriangleright

Decentralized algorithms.

Without

, each agent
a

\mathcal{I}_{a}(t)
from its
local observations
(Definition

), which include: (i) its estimated preference lists, (ii) whether its application was rejected, and (iii) the firm-side feedback from the previous round.

Algorithmic Preliminaries.

Here, we introduce the preliminaries of our algorithmic designs.

Empirical means as estimators.

We use empirical averages to estimate expected utilities for each agent–firm pair

\hat{u}_{a,f}(t)=\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}X_{a,f}(t^{\prime})\,\mathbbm{1}\{f\in\mathcal{I}_{a}(t^{\prime})\}}{N_{a,f}(t)},

The structure of the interview set

Across all our algorithms, each agent interviews between two and

. In the main body, we focus on the case

5.1
Strategic Firm’s Rejection Policy

We introduce a firm-side policy, given in
Algorithm

1

\mathcal{V}(t)
and
\mathcal{V}^{+}(t)
.

At a high level, a strategic uncertain firm
f\in\mathcal{F}
abstains from hiring when its updated estimates suggest it may have mis-ranked a previously rejected agent relative to its current top applicant. After interviewing and observing the applicant set, the firm compares its current top applicant to previously rejected agents: if the top applicant is not estimated to dominate every such

rejected agent, the firm sets
\gamma_{f}(t)=0

We now formalize when a strategic uncertain firm performs a strategic rejection. Each firm

maintains two private state variables,

and

in which

rejected agent

records the most recent round prior to

in which
f
remained vacant. We interpret

was rejected after the last vacancy, so

may need to reconsider

\displaystyle r_{f,a}(t)

\displaystyle\doteq\argmax_{t^{\prime}<t}\left\{\gamma_{f}(t^{\prime})=1\;\land\;\begin{aligned} &f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t^{\prime})=f\\
&a^{\texttt{match}}_{f}(t^{\prime})\neq a\end{aligned}\right\},

(7)

\displaystyle c_{f}(t)

\displaystyle\doteq\argmax_{t^{\prime\prime}<t}\left\{a^{\texttt{match}}_{f}(t^{\prime\prime})=\emptyset\right\}

5.2
Coordinated Decentralized Algorithm with Only Vacancy

We now design decentralized learning algorithms under the only-vacancy feedback

We next describe a learning framework in which agents alternate between independent behavior and deliberate signaling through their application decisions; these signals shape the vacancy feedback and trigger coordinated updates.

Learning Framework.

The time horizon is divided into two alternating stages:
committing
and
updating
. In a committing stage, agents adhere to their current interviewing sets
\mathcal{I}_{a}(t)
and act independently. In an updating stage, agents coordinate to run a distributed execution of

on their current estimated preference lists in order to revise their interviewing decisions.

Each agent

maintains a binary state
\rho_{a}(t)

\rho_{a}(t)=1
indicates a committing stage and
\rho_{a}(t)=0
indicates an updating stage. This state is updated at the end of each round after observing
\mathcal{V}(t)

\mathcal{V}(t)

\mathcal{U}\doteq\{\,t\in\mathcal{T}:\forall a\in\mathcal{A},\ \rho_{a}(t)=0\,\}
and

\mathcal{C}\doteq\{\,t\in\mathcal{T}:\forall a\in\mathcal{A},\ \rho_{a}(t)=1\,\}
.

The central challenge is deciding when to switch stages. Transitions from
\rho_{a}(t)=1
(committing) to
\rho_{a}(t)=0
(updating) occur in two ways.
First, an agent may detect a market change and intentionally refrain from applying, creating a vacancy that serves as a coordination signal via the firm-side feedback.
Second, a strategic firm may abstain from hiring under
Algorithm

1
and appear in
\mathcal{V}(t)
. Since agents do not observe the source of new vacancies, both events are treated as coordination signals. Conversely, switching back is deterministic: agents wait
O(n^{2})

\epsilon=\bigl(\lambda\cdot(1-\lambda)^{n-1}\bigr)^{n^{4}m+nm}
.
Then the coordination-free randomized
Algorithm

7
guarantees pessimal regret for every agent

as

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\underline{R}_{a}(\mathcal{T})\right]\in O\!\left(\epsilon^{-1}n^{5}m^{4}\right).

The exponentially large factor
\epsilon^{-1}
reflects the need to rule out blocking-pair resolution cycles that may arise in general markets under coordination-free dynamics; such cycles do not occur in
\alpha
-reducible markets. Similar large constants also appear in the
O(\log T)
bounds of
[

]
, albeit under more informative feedback than our
\mathcal{V}^{+}
.

Conclusion

We study bandit learning in matching markets with interviews and two-sided uncertainty. We give centralized and decentralized algorithms with time-independent regret with minimal firm-side signal. The key ingredient is handling firm uncertainty via an extended action space enabling strategic deferral, which stabilizes decentralized learning under limited feedback and improves prior work along multiple dimensions. Incentive compatibility, optimality, comparisons, and future directions are in Appendixes

,
H
,
I
, and
J
.

D.2
Proof of an Auxiliary Lemma

We first state and prove a simple auxiliary bound used in the regret analysis.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
a>0
and
c>0
be constants, and define

R(m)\;=\;\frac{a\,e^{-c/m}}{1-e^{-c/m}}.

Then
R(m)\in O(m)
for
m>0
.

Proof.

For sufficiently large
m
we have
c/m\leq 1
, so we may apply the standard inequality (from a Taylor approximation), valid for all
x\in(0,1]
:

1-e^{-x}\;\geq\;\frac{x}{2}.

With
x=c/m
, this gives

1-e^{-c/m}\;\geq\;\frac{c}{2m}.

Since
e^{-c/m}\leq 1
, we also have
a\,e^{-c/m}\leq a
. Combining these bounds, for all sufficiently large
m
,

R(m)=\frac{a\,e^{-c/m}}{1-e^{-c/m}}\;\leq\;\frac{a}{\tfrac{c}{2m}}=\frac{2a}{c}\,m.

Thus there exists a constant
K=2a/c
such that
R(m)\leq Km
for all sufficiently large
m
, and hence
R(m)\in O(m)
.
∎

Proof of Lemma

We next present the proof of Lemma
4.3
, which is a key ingredient in our time-independent regret analysis.
See
4.3

Proof.

By union bounding over firms and applying Hoeffding’s inequality to estimation errors, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}|]=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Pr\!\left(\exists f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}:\ f^{\prime}\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}(t)\ \right),

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Pr\!\left(\exists f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}:\ \hat{u}_{a,f^{\prime}}(t)>\hat{u}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}(t)\right),

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}}\Pr\!\left(\hat{u}_{a,f^{\prime}}(t)-u_{a,f^{\prime}}+u_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}-\hat{u}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}(t)>\overline{\Delta}_{a,f^{\prime}}\right),

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}}\Pr\left(\big|\hat{u}_{a,f^{\prime}}(t)-u_{a,f^{\prime}}\big|>\tfrac{\overline{\Delta}_{a,f^{\prime}}}{2}\right)+\Pr\left(\big|u_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}-\hat{u}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}(t)\big|>\tfrac{\overline{\Delta}_{a,f^{\prime}}}{2}\right),

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}}4\exp\!\left(-\frac{t\overline{\Delta}_{a,f^{\prime}}^{2}}{2m}\right),

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4|\mathcal{L}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}|\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\overline{\Delta}^{\texttt{min}}_{a})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\overline{\Delta}^{\texttt{min}}_{a})^{2}}{2m}\right)}\overset{(b)}{\in}O(|\mathcal{L}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}|\cdot m),

(19)

where
\overline{\Delta}^{\texttt{min}}_{a}\doteq\min_{f\neq\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}\overline{\Delta}_{a,f}
denotes the minimum optimal utility gap. Step (a) follows from Hoeffding’s inequality. Step (b), implied by
Lemma

D.1
, shows that the expected number of invalid time steps for agent

is bounded by a constant independent of
T
, completing the proof.

D.4
Proof of Theorem
4.1

See
4.1

Proof.

We track invalidity on both sides.
For each agent
a\in\mathcal{A}
, let
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a,\overline{f^{*}_{a}}}
be the rounds in which
\hat{O}_{a}(t)
is invalid w.r.t.
\overline{f^{*}_{a}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\overline{R}_{a}(T)]

D.5
Optimality: Proof of Proposition
D.2

We prove that the regret guarantee of Algorithm
6
is near-optimal by establishing a matching lower bound (up to a factor
m
) that applies to any policy achieving stable outcomes.

Proposition D.2
.

Algorithm

6
’s performance is within a factor
m
of the optimal regret.

Proof.

Consider any learning policy
\pi
under which the market converges to a stable matching with sublinear regret.
To certify stability, each agent
a
must learn, for every firm
f
and every competing agent
a^{\prime}
, whether
f
prefers
a
to
a^{\prime}
(since these comparisons determine the presence of blocking pairs).
Crucially, this information cannot be obtained from interviews alone and requires at least one round in which
a
actually applies to
f
(and the outcome reveals the relevant comparison with
a^{\prime}
).

Fix an agent
a
. There are
O(nm)
such triplets
(a,a^{\prime},f)
, so any policy must incur
O(nm)
regret for agent
a
in the worst case, implying an
\Omega(nm)
lower bound on the optimal regret per agent. Therefore, since Algorithm
6
achieves
O(nm^{2})
regret for any agent, it is asymptotically within a factor
m
of the optimal regret.
∎

f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a_{1}}(t)=f_{2}
, which causes
a_{2}
to be rejected by
f_{2}
when it considers
a_{2}
.
Consequently,
a_{2}
proceeds to consider
f_{1}
, and since
a_{2}
is the second most preferred agent by all firms, the only remaining option for
a_{3}
is to consider
f_{3}
.

Now suppose that at time step
t^{\prime}>t
, the updated estimates become:

\begin{array}[]{lr}\hat{O}_{a_{1}}(t^{\prime}):\langle f_{3}\succ f_{1}\succ f_{2}\rangle&I_{a_{1}}(t^{\prime})=\{f_{3},f^{\texttt{RR}}_{a_{1}}(t^{\prime})\}\\[3.0pt]
\hat{O}_{a_{2}}(t^{\prime}):\langle f_{2}\succ f_{1}\succ f_{3}\rangle&I_{a_{2}}(t^{\prime})=\{f_{1},f^{\texttt{RR}}_{a_{2}}(t^{\prime})\}\\[3.0pt]
\hat{O}_{a_{3}}(t^{\prime}):\langle f_{2}\succ f_{3}\succ f_{1}\rangle&I_{a_{3}}(t^{\prime})=\{f_{1},f^{\texttt{RR}}_{a_{3}}(t^{\prime})\}\end{array}

Here,
a_{1}
switches to applying to
f_{3}
, again causing other applicants to be rejected due to being the most preferred agent. Meanwhile,
a_{3}
, which was never rejected by
f_{2}
, now reconsiders
f_{2}
as its most preferred estimated firm at that round.
Since
a_{1}
and
a_{3}
simultaneously change their targets, and
a_{2}
observes no hiring updates under
\mathcal{V}(t^{\prime})
(only vacancies), it may assume that
f_{2}
remains unavailable and commit to the suboptimal firm
f_{1}
.

Under
\mathcal{V}^{+}(t^{\prime})
, the hiring update at
f_{2}
would be visible to
a_{2}
, prompting it to reconsider
f_{2}
and avoid a suboptimal match.
Hence, the limited feedback in
\mathcal{V}(t^{\prime})
prevents agents from detecting such preference updates, necessitating coordinated distributed runs of
GS
.
If, for instance,
f_{1}
—upon detecting a change in its local estimates

—could signal others to initiate a joint distributed

, then
a_{2}
would reassess

, ensuring convergence to a stable matching.

Otherwise,
a_{2}
must continually reconsider firms it was previously rejected from (with some positive probability), and in some scenarios this prevents achieving
O(1)
regret.

E.2
Committing to a Perfect Matching
Lemma

E.2

This lemma shows that after each updating phase, the agents’ applications induce a perfect matching, which serves as the baseline structure for the subsequent regret analysis.

Lemma E.2
.

In a matching market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
where firms follow
Algorithm

1
and agents follow
Algorithm

2
, at the end of any updating phase that starts at time
t^{\texttt{GS}}
, the applications
(a,f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t^{\texttt{GS}}+3n^{2}))_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
form a matching that matches all agents.

Proof.

Since the beginning of the updating phase at time
t^{\texttt{GS}}
, each agent
a
selects its applied firm according to

f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t)=\argmax_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{a}(t)}\hat{u}_{a,f}(t^{\texttt{GS}}),

that is, with respect to its estimated preference list
\hat{O}_{a}(t^{\texttt{GS}})
. Consequently, during the updating phase, each agent effectively traverses
\hat{O}_{a}(t^{\texttt{GS}})
in descending order, applying to increasingly less preferred firms until reaching one that does not reject it in favor of another agent. Once an agent
a
becomes matched to a firm
f=f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t)
, it continues to apply to
f
in subsequent rounds until either the updating phase ends or
f
rejects
a
by hiring another agent. Therefore, whenever the applications
(a,f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t))_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
do not form a perfect matching that matches all agents, there exists at least one agent who has not been hired in that round.

In addition to rejections caused by competing applicants, agents may experience strategic rejections. However, by construction, the rejection variable
r_{a,f}
is updated only when firm
f
hires another agent, i.e., when
\gamma_{f}(t)=1
, and not when
f
strategically abstains from hiring. As a result, following a strategic rejection, agents reapply to the same firm in the subsequent round. Moreover, under the rejection policy
Algorithm

1
, no firm performs strategic rejections in two consecutive rounds during an updating phase. Further, by the definition of the candidate set (
11
) under
Algorithm

2
, an agent does not apply to a firm after being rejected in favor of another hire. Hence, each firm can reject a given agent at most twice during the updating phase starting from
t^{\texttt{GS}}
: at most once strategically, and at most once in favor of hiring another agent.

It follows that as long as the applications do not form a perfect matching, within at most three rounds there exists at least one agent who moves to a less preferred firm. Indeed, in the worst case an agent is first strategically rejected at time
t
(and thus re-applies to the same firm at time
t+1
), and then rejected at time
t+1
in favor of another hire (which updates
r_{a,f}
), in which case it can consider a new firm only at time
t+2
.

Since, by
6
, each agent’s match during the updating phase is restricted to the top
n
firms in
\hat{O}_{a}(t^{\texttt{GS}})
, the applications form a perfect matching within at most
3n^{2}
rounds. In particular, after at most
3n^{2}
rounds from the start of an updating phase, the applications
(a,f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t))_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
form a perfect matching. The
O(n^{2})
bound follows since each agent applies to at most the top
n
firms (with respect to
\hat{O}_{a}(t^{\texttt{GS}})
) during a single updating phase.
∎

E.3
Counting Updating Stages via Triggered Events

This observation expresses the number of updating stages in terms of the total number of triggered inconsistency/rejection events across all agents, which is later used to bound the overall regret.

Observation 3
.

The total number of updating stages is given by

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a}\right)\right|\right].

Proof.

This holds because whenever an agent
a\in\mathcal{A}
detects an event in
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a}
, it abstains from applying in that round, which is subsequently revealed to all other agents through their detection of events in
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{vac}}_{a^{\prime}}
. Similarly, if
a
detects an event in
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a}
, it indicates that at that time step the agent was strategically not hired, an event that is also reflected to others via
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{vac}}_{a^{\prime}}
in the firm-side feedback. Therefore, every initialization of a new distributed
GS
run is ultimately triggered by at least one agent
a
detecting the current time step
t
as belonging to either
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a}
or
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a}
.
∎

E.4
Bounding the Number of Updating Stages in
\alpha
-Reducible Markets

Lemma E.3
.

In an
\alpha
-reducible market, with strategic firms following
Algorithm

1
and agents following
Algorithm

2
, the total number of updating stages is
O(nm^{2})
.

Proof.

Consider the unique stable matching
(a_{i},f_{i})_{i\in[n]}
in an
\alpha
-reducible market.
For each
i\in[n]
, define the set of time steps

\mathcal{G}_{i}\doteq\{t\in\mathcal{C}:\forall j\in[i],\ f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a_{j}}(t)=f_{j}\},

namely, the rounds in which agents
a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}
all apply to their respective stable partners.
Its complement
\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{i}
consists of rounds in which at least one of these agents is not matched to its stable partner.

We now bound the expected number of switching events across all agents. In particular, with respect to
3
, we bound

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}\right|\right]

as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\left|\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\!\!\left(\!\{t\!\in\!\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\!\cup\!\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}:t\!\in\!\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{i}\}\cup\{t\!\in\!\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\!\cup\!\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}:t\!\in\!\mathcal{G}_{i}\}\!\right)\!\right|\!\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\left|\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\!\!\left(\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{i}\cup\{t\!\in\!\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\!\cup\!\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}:t\!\in\!\mathcal{G}_{i}\}\right)\!\right|\!\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\left|\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\!\left(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a_{i},f_{i}}\!\cup\!\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{f_{i},a_{i}}\right)\cup\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\!\left(\{t\!\in\!\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\!\cup\!\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}:t\!\in\!\mathcal{G}_{i}\}\right)\!\right|\!\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\left|\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\!\left(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a_{i},f_{i}}\!\cup\!\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{f_{i},a_{i}}\right)\cup\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\!\left(\!\{t\!\in\!\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\!\cup\!\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}:(t\!\in\!\mathcal{G}_{i})\land(t,t\!-\!1\!\in\!\mathcal{E}_{a_{i},f_{i}}\!\cap\!\mathcal{E}_{f_{i},a_{i}})\!\}\right)\!\right|\!\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\leq}\sum_{i\in[n]}\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a_{i},f_{i}}\right|\!\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{f_{i},a_{i}}\right|\!\right]+\!\!\sum_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{a_{i},f_{i}}}\!\!\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\left|\!\{t:f\!\in\!\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{a_{i},f_{i}}(t\!-\!1)\!\cap\!\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{a_{i},f_{i}}(t)\}\!\right|\!\right]\Bigg),

\displaystyle\overset{(d)}{\leq}\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{4m\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\overline{\Delta}^{\texttt{min}}_{a_{i}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\overline{\Delta}^{\texttt{min}}_{a_{i}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}+\sum_{f_{i}\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{4n\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\overline{\Delta}^{\texttt{min}}_{f_{i}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\overline{\Delta}^{\texttt{min}}_{f_{i}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}\in O(nm^{2}),

(23)

Here, inequality (a) follows from the fact that if
t\in\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{i}
, then either some agent
a_{j}
has an invalid estimated preference list at time
t^{\texttt{GS}}
, or there exists a time step
t^{\prime}\in[t^{\texttt{GS}},t^{\texttt{GS}}+3n^{2}]
at which the firm
f_{j}
, for some
j\in[i]
, has an invalid estimated preference list. Otherwise, if the estimated preference lists of agents
a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}
are valid at time
t^{\texttt{GS}}
and the estimated preference lists of firms
f_{1},\ldots,f_{i}
remain valid throughout
[t^{\texttt{GS}},t^{\texttt{GS}}+3n^{2}]
, then by the definition of
\alpha
-reducibility (and its inductive structure), these agents must be matched to their unique stable partners, which implies
t\in\mathcal{G}_{i}
.

The justification of inequality (b) follows by considering the possible cases when

t-1\in\mathcal{G}_{i}
.

(i)

Either
\hat{O}_{a_{i}}(t-1)
or
\hat{O}_{f_{i}}(t-1)
is invalid.
In this case,

t-1\in\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a_{i},f_{i}}\cup\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{f_{i},a_{i}}
,
and the subsequent execution of
GS
can be charged to round
t-1
as an instance of an invalid preference list.

(ii)

Both
\hat{O}_{a_{i}}(t-1)
and
\hat{O}_{f_{i}}(t-1)
are valid, but at least one becomes invalid at time
t
.
If
\hat{O}_{a_{i}}(t)
becomes invalid, then
t\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}
, corresponding to a switching event by the agent.
If instead
\hat{O}_{f_{i}}(t)
becomes invalid, firm
f_{i}
may perform a strategic rejection, yielding

t\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}
.
In either case, the ensuing updating phase, and hence the next execution of
GS
, is associated with

t\in\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a_{i},f_{i}}\cup\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{f_{i},a_{i}}
.

(iii)

Both
\hat{O}_{a_{i}}(t-1)
and
\hat{O}_{a_{i}}(t)
, as well as
\hat{O}_{f_{i}}(t-1)
and
\hat{O}_{f_{i}}(t)
, are valid.
Suppose first that firm
f_{i}
performs a strategic rejection at time
t
, i.e.,
t\in\mathcal{S}_{f_{i}}
.
This action is observed by agent
a_{i}
through
\mathcal{V}^{+}(t)
, and therefore implies
t\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}
.

Since all agents
a_{j}
for
j\in[i-1]
are matched to their stable partners
f_{j}
, and since
t\in\mathcal{E}_{f_{i},a_{i}}
, we claim that there must exist some agent
a_{j}
whose estimated preference list was invalid at some time during the previous updating phase
[t^{\texttt{GS}},t^{\texttt{GS}}+3n^{2}]
.
Indeed, the strategic rejection by
f_{i}
can only be triggered if there exists an agent
a_{j}
that
f_{i}
truly prefers (i.e.,
a_{j}\in\mathcal{H}_{f_{i},a_{i}}
) but that was previously rejected by
f_{i}
during the updating phase.
Concretely, during the updating phase, agent
a_{j}
applied to
f_{i}
and was rejected at some time
t^{\prime}
, which means that at the time of rejection
a_{j}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{f_{i},a_{i}}
.
After this rejection,
a_{j}
subsequently applied to (and matched with) some firm
f_{j}
that it estimated to be less preferred.
Given that
f_{i}
later performs a strategic rejection, this sequence of events implies that
a_{j}
’s estimated preference list must have been invalid at time
t^{\prime}
.

Otherwise, if no strategic rejection occurs at time
t
and
t\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}
, then since

t-1,t\in\mathcal{E}_{a_{i},f_{i}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{f_{i},a_{i}}
,
a switching event of type
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}
implies that there exists a firm

f\in\mathcal{H}_{a_{i},f_{i}}
such that

f\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{a_{i},f_{i}}(t^{\texttt{GS}})
but

f\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{a_{i},f_{i}}(t)
.

These cases together establish inequality (c). Inequality (d) then follows with the same approach as used in the proof of Lemma
4.3
.

We decompose the regret of agent

We first bound the updating-phase regret. By

, the expected number of updating stages is time-independent, and each stage incurs regret at most

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R_{a_{1}}(\mathcal{U})]\leq 3n^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}})\right|\right]\in O(n^{3}m^{2})

For the committing phase, we show

Applying
Lemma

E.3
and
Lemma

4.3
(for agents and firms) yields the claimed
\,O(n^{3}m^{2})\,
bound. The first term accounts for signaling rounds, which happen at most once per committing stage, immediately before an updating phase, when an agent detects a switching condition and abstains from applying.

Now fix a committing interval
[t_{1}:t_{2}]
. If
f^{\texttt{match}}_{a_{1}}(t_{1})=f_{1}
, then
a_{1}
incurs no regret over
[t_{1}:t_{2}]
. Otherwise, since
a_{1}
and
f_{1}
are mutual top choices in the unique stable matching, the most recent updating phase must have started at some

had an invalid estimated list.

If

strategically rejects
a_{1}
during this updating phase, then the resulting committing interval has length one and is already covered by the signaling term. Otherwise, in an

Consequently, the total committing-phase regret excluding signaling rounds is bounded by

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a}\right)\right|\right].

a_{i}

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a_{i},f_{i}}\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{f_{i},a_{i}}\right|\right].

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\Bigg[\sum_{j\in[|\mathcal{K}|]}\bigl(t_{j+1}-1-t^{\prime}_{j}\bigr)\;\Bigg|\;t^{\prime}_{j}\in\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{i-1}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\Bigg[\sum_{j\in[|\mathcal{K}|]}\bigl(t_{j+1}-1-t^{\prime}_{j}\bigr)\;\Bigg|\;f^{\texttt{match}}_{a_{i-1}}(t^{\prime}_{j})\neq f_{i-1}\;\land\;t^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathcal{G}_{i-2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad+\mathbb{E}\!\Bigg[\sum_{j\in[|\mathcal{K}|]}\bigl(t_{j+1}-1-t^{\prime}_{j}\bigr)\;\Bigg|\;t^{\prime}_{j}\in\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{i-2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a_{i-1},f_{i-1}}\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{f_{i-1},a_{i-1}}\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\Bigg[\sum_{j\in[|\mathcal{K}|]}\bigl(t_{j+1}-1-t^{\prime}_{j}\bigr)\;\Bigg|\;t^{\prime}_{j}\in\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{i-2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a_{j},f_{j}}\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{f_{j},a_{j}}\right|\right],

where each step follows with the same rationale used to obtain inequality (b).

Finally, applying Lemma
4.3
to (
27
) and using (
26
) and
Lemma

E.3
, the total regret is bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[R_{a_{i}}(T)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(3n^{2}+1\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{a_{j}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{4m\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{a_{j}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{a_{j}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}+\sum_{f_{j}\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{4n\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{f_{j}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{f_{j}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{4\left|\mathcal{L}_{a_{j},f_{j}}\right|\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{a_{j}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{a_{j}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}+\frac{4n\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{f_{i}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{f_{i}})^{2}}{2m}\right)}\right)\in O\left(n^{3}m^{2}+m\cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\left|\mathcal{L}_{a_{j},f_{j}}\right|+\left|\mathcal{L}_{f_{j},a_{j}}\right|\right)\right)

E.7
Extension of Algorithm
2
’s Analysis to General Markets

We extend the results established for
\alpha
-reducible markets in the main body to general, unstructured matching markets, where no structural assumptions are imposed and multiple stable matchings may exist.

E.7.1
An Upper Bound on the Expected Number of Updating Stages

We first extend
Lemma

E.3
to general matching markets in order to bound the expected number of updating stages. In the absence of structural assumptions, this bound becomes market-independent and necessarily larger than in the
\alpha
-reducible case.

Lemma E.4
.

In a matching market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
, with strategic firms following
Algorithm

1
and agents following
Algorithm

2
, the total number of distributed runs of
GS
(i.e., the number of updating phases) is time-independent
and of
O(n^{2}m^{2})
.

Proof.

In a general matching market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
, as introduced earlier, we denote the agent-optimal and agent-pessimal stable matches of agent
a\in\mathcal{A}
by
\overline{f^{*}_{a}}
and
\underline{f^{*}_{a}}
, respectively. By the lattice structure of stable matchings, the agent-optimal stable matching coincides with the firm-pessimal one: in particular, if
\overline{f^{*}_{a}}=f
, then
\underline{a^{*}_{f}}=a
, and if
\underline{f^{*}_{a}}=f
, then
\overline{a^{*}_{f}}=a
.

Before proceeding, we introduce the event

\psi_{\mathcal{F}}\doteq\psi^{(1)}_{\mathcal{F}}\cap\psi^{(2)}_{\mathcal{F}}.

We first define

\displaystyle\psi^{(1)}_{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle\doteq\left\{t\in[T]:\forall f\in\mathcal{F},\;t\in\bigcap_{a\in\mathcal{L}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}}}\mathcal{E}_{f,a}\right\},

\displaystyle\psi^{(2)}_{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle\doteq\left\{t\in[T]:\forall f\in\mathcal{F},\;\Bigl(t\in\mathcal{E}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}}\ \land\ \forall a\in\mathcal{H}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}},\;a\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}}(t)\Bigr)\right\}.

Accordingly,
\psi_{\mathcal{F}}
is the set of time steps at which, for every firm
f\in\mathcal{F}
, the estimated preference list of
f
correctly orders all agents that are strictly less preferred than its agent-optimal match
\overline{a^{*}_{f}}
, and every agent strictly preferred to
\overline{a^{*}_{f}}
is ranked above it (possibly without being ordered among themselves).

We also define the event
\zeta_{\mathcal{A}}
as

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathcal{A}}\doteq\left\{t\in[T]:\forall a\in\mathcal{A},\;t\in\bigcap_{f\in\{\underline{f^{*}_{a}}\}\cup\mathcal{H}_{a,\underline{f^{*}_{a}}}}\mathcal{E}_{a,f}\right\}.

That is,
\zeta_{\mathcal{A}}
is the set of time steps at which, for every agent
a
, the estimated preference list correctly orders its agent-pessimal stable match
\underline{f^{*}_{a}}
and all firms strictly preferred to it, according to the ground-truth preferences.

We also define
t^{\texttt{GS}}(t)
as the initial time step of the most recent updating stage by time
t
.

We now follow the proof idea in
3
that the expected number of updating phases is bounded by

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}\right|\right].

Thus we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\left\{t\in\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\left\{t\in\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}\land\exists t^{\prime}\in[t^{\texttt{GS}}(t),t-1],t^{\prime}\in\bar{\psi}_{\mathcal{F}}\cup\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\left\{t\in\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}\land\forall t^{\prime}\in[t^{\texttt{GS}}(t),t-1],t^{\prime}\in\psi_{\mathcal{F}}\cap\zeta_{\mathcal{A}}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\exists t^{\prime}\in[t^{\texttt{GS}}(t),t-1],t^{\prime}\in\bar{\psi}_{\mathcal{F}}\cup\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\left\{t\in\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}\land t\in\bar{\psi}_{\mathcal{F}}\cup\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\psi}_{\mathcal{F}}\cup\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}\right|\right].

Inequality (a) follows by a case analysis.

(i) If there exists a time step
t^{\prime}\in[t^{\texttt{GS}}(t),t-1]
such that

t^{\prime}\in\bar{\psi}_{\mathcal{F}}\cup\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}
, then the updating phase initiated at
t^{\texttt{GS}}(t)
is charged to this violation.

(ii) Otherwise, for all
t^{\prime}\in[t^{\texttt{GS}}(t),t-1]
we have

t^{\prime}\in\psi_{\mathcal{F}}\cap\zeta_{\mathcal{A}}
.
In this case, throughout the updating phase starting at
t^{\texttt{GS}}(t)
, all firms have estimated preference lists that are valid with respect to their agent-optimal matches
\overline{a^{*}_{f}}
in the sense of
\psi_{\mathcal{F}}
, and all agents have estimated preference lists that are valid with respect to their agent-pessimal matches
\underline{f^{*}_{a}}
in the sense of
\zeta_{\mathcal{A}}
.
Consequently, the matching
(a,\underline{f^{*}_{a}})_{\forall a\in\mathcal{A}}
remains stable with respect to the estimated preferences at all time steps in
[t^{\texttt{GS}}(t),t-1]
, and no switching condition can be triggered.

Moreover, since the distributed
GS
run by the agents returns the agent-optimal (and firm-pessimal) stable matching under the current estimated preferences, it follows that for every agent
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t)
is ranked no worse than
\underline{f^{*}_{a}}
under
\hat{O}_{a}(t)
, and for every firm
f\in\mathcal{F}
, the agent matched to
f
is ranked no better than
\overline{a^{*}_{f}}
under
\hat{O}_{f}(t)
.

Therefore, any switching event at time
t
must correspond to a violation of either
\zeta_{\mathcal{A}}
or
\psi_{\mathcal{F}}
:

•

If
t\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a}
for some agent
a
, then
a
detects a preference invalidity with respect to some

f\in\mathcal{H}_{a,\underline{f^{*}_{a}}}
, implying that

t\in\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}
.

•

If
t\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a}
for some agent
a
, then the firm

f=f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t)
performs a strategic rejection because its estimated
preference list becomes invalid with respect to some

a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}}
, implying that

t\in\bar{\psi}_{\mathcal{F}}
.

Inequality (b) then follows since each updating phase can be uniquely associated with a time
step in
\bar{\psi}_{\mathcal{F}}\cup\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}
, and such violations are counted at most once.

We complete the proof by bounding the two terms in

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\psi}_{\mathcal{F}}\cup\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}\right|\right]\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\psi}_{\mathcal{F}}\right|\right]

separately.

To bound
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}\right|\right]
, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\left\{t\in[T]:\exists a\in\mathcal{A},\;\exists f\in\{\underline{f^{*}_{a}}\}\cup\mathcal{H}_{a,\underline{f^{*}_{a}}},\ t\in\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a,f}\right\}\right|\right],

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{f\in\{\underline{f^{*}_{a}}\}\cup\mathcal{H}_{a,\underline{f^{*}_{a}}}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a,f}\right|\right],

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{f\in\{\underline{f^{*}_{a}}\}\cup\mathcal{H}_{a,\underline{f^{*}_{a}}}}\frac{4|\mathcal{L}_{a,f}|\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{a,f})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{a,f})^{2}}{2m}\right)},

(28)

\displaystyle\in\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{f\in\{\underline{f^{*}_{a}}\}\cup\mathcal{H}_{a,\underline{f^{*}_{a}}}}O\!\left(m\cdot|\mathcal{L}_{a,f}|\right)\;\in\;O(n^{2}m^{2}),

where the final bound follows from
Lemma

4.3
.

For
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\psi}_{\mathcal{F}}\right|\right]
, we then write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\psi}_{\mathcal{F}}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\psi}^{(1)}_{\mathcal{F}}\cup\bar{\psi}^{(2)}_{\mathcal{F}}\right|\right],

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\psi}^{(1)}_{\mathcal{F}}\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\psi}^{(2)}_{\mathcal{F}}\right|\right],

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\psi}^{(1)}_{\mathcal{F}}\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\left\{t\in[T]:\exists f\in\mathcal{F},\;t\in\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}}\ \lor\ \left(\exists a\in\mathcal{H}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}},\ a\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}}(t)\right)\right\}\right|\right],

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\psi}^{(1)}_{\mathcal{F}}\right|\right]+\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}}\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\left\{t\in[T]:\exists a\in\mathcal{H}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}},\ a\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}}(t)\right\}\right|\right],

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{L}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}}}\frac{4|\mathcal{L}_{f,a}|\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{f,a})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{f,a})^{2}}{2m}\right)}+\frac{4n\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\overline{\Delta}^{\texttt{min}}_{f})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\overline{\Delta}^{\texttt{min}}_{f})^{2}}{2m}\right)},

(29)

\displaystyle\in\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{L}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}}}O\!\left(m\cdot|\mathcal{L}_{f,a}|+nm\right)\;\in\;O(n^{2}m^{2}),

where the final bound follows by applying the same argument used to bound

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\zeta}_{\mathcal{A}}\right|\right]
to

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\psi}^{(1)}_{\mathcal{F}}\right|\right]
,
and then invoking
Lemma

4.3
for the remaining term.
Finally, we got

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bigcup_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{i}}\right|\right]\leq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{f\in\{\underline{f^{*}_{a}}\}\cup\mathcal{H}_{a,\underline{f^{*}_{a}}}}\frac{4|\mathcal{L}_{a,f}|\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{a,f})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{a,f})^{2}}{2m}\right)}+\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{L}_{f,\overline{a^{*}_{f}}}}\frac{4|\mathcal{L}_{f,a}|\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{f,a})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\Delta^{\texttt{min}}_{f,a})^{2}}{2m}\right)}+\frac{4n\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\overline{\Delta}^{\texttt{min}}_{f})^{2}}{2m}\right)}{1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(\overline{\Delta}^{\texttt{min}}_{f})^{2}}{2m}\right)},

which is of
O(n^{2}m^{2})
.

∎

E.7.2
Proof of Convergence to a Stable Matching

We outline here the key convergence argument and discuss the impossibility of guaranteeing convergence to the agent-optimal stable matching.

Proposition E.5
.

In a matching market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
, where agents follow Algorithm
2
and strategic firms follow Algorithm
1
, the algorithms converge to a stable matching, which is not necessarily agent-optimal.

Proof.

By Definition
C.2
, convergence implies the existence of a time
t
such that

f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime})=f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime}-1)
for all
t^{\prime}>t
and all agents
a
.
Let
t_{1}
denote the start of the final committing phase.
By Observation
E.2
, agents commit to a perfect matching

(a,f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t_{1}))_{\forall a\in\mathcal{A}}
throughout
[t_{1},T]
.

Since no switching condition is triggered after
t_{1}
, no agent detects a preference-list inconsistency and no firm performs a strategic rejection.
Consequently, each agent
a
has already been rejected by every firm in

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{a,f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t_{1})}(t_{1})
, and each firm
f
is matched with its most preferred agent among those who applied during the final updating phase.

Therefore, no blocking pair exists, and the matching

(a,f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t_{1}))_{\forall a\in\mathcal{A}}
is stable.
Finally, as illustrated in Example
E.1
, the limiting stable matching need not be agent-optimal.
∎

E.7.3
Crucial Observations

We now present two crucial observations that will be used repeatedly in the proof of
Theorem

5.2
.

The following observations clarify how the set of top-
n
firms constrains stable outcomes in general markets.

Observation 4
.

In any matching market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
, for every agent
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the agent-optimal stable match

\overline{f^{*}_{a}}
belongs to the set of top-
n
firms
\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a}
. Formally,

\overline{f^{*}_{a}}\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a}.

Proof.

Running
GS
on the ground-truth preference lists yields the agent-optimal stable matching.
Since there are
n
agents, each agent can be rejected at most
n-1
times during the execution
of
GS
. Therefore, the agent-optimal stable match of any agent must lie among its top
n

ranked firms, that is, in
\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a}
.
∎

Observation 5
.

In any matching market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
, if for some agent
a\in\mathcal{A}
the agent-pessimal stable match

\underline{f^{*}_{a}}
does not belong to the set
\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a}
, that is,

\underline{f^{*}_{a}}\notin\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a},

then the market admits more than one stable matching. Equivalently,

\underline{f^{*}_{a}}\neq\overline{f^{*}_{a}}.

Proof.

If
\underline{f^{*}_{a}}\notin\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a}
while
\overline{f^{*}_{a}}\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a}
by
Observation
4
, then the agent-optimal and agent-pessimal stable matches of
a

must be distinct. Hence, the market admits more than one stable matching.
∎

Observation 6
.

In a matching market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
with strategic firms following
Algorithm

1
and agents following
Algorithm

2
, consider any updating stage starting at time
t^{\texttt{GS}}
, and let

t^{\prime}\doteq t^{\texttt{GS}}+3n^{2}+1

denote the start of the subsequent committing phase. Then, in the perfect matching

(a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime}))_{\forall a\in\mathcal{A}}
to which agents commit, each agent
a
is matched to a firm

f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime})
that belongs to its top-
n
estimated firms at time
t^{\texttt{GS}}
, i.e.,

f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime})\in\hat{\mathcal{F}}^{(n)}_{a}(t^{\texttt{GS}}).

Proof.

The key observation is that, throughout the updating stage, agents select their actions based on the estimated preference lists fixed at time
t^{\texttt{GS}}
. As a result, during the ensuing distributed execution of
GS
, each agent applies only to firms among its top-
n
estimated choices at time
t^{\texttt{GS}}
, which implies the claim.
∎

Now we conclude this section with the following lemma, which plays a crucial role in the proof of
Theorem

5.2
.

Lemma E.6
.

In a matching market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
with strategic firms following
Algorithm

1
and agents following
Algorithm

2
, consider any updating stage starting at time
t^{\texttt{GS}}
, and let

t^{\prime}\doteq t^{\texttt{GS}}+3n^{2}+1

denote the start of the subsequent committing phase. Suppose that the perfect matching

(a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime}))_{\forall a\in\mathcal{A}}
to which agents commit is not stable with respect to the ground-truth preferences.
If there exists a time step
t^{\prime\prime}\geq t^{\prime}
during the committing phase such that

t^{\prime\prime}\in\Gamma^{(n)}_{\mathcal{A}}\cap\Gamma^{(n)}_{\mathcal{F}},

then agents initiate the next updating phase at time
t^{\prime\prime}+1
.

Proof.

The claim follows from
6
together with the definition of

\Gamma^{(n)}_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\Gamma^{(n)}_{\mathcal{F}}
in
Definition

A.4
.

Since the perfect matching
(a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime}))_{\forall a\in\mathcal{A}}
is not stable with respect to the ground-truth preferences, there must exist at least one agent or firm whose top-
n
estimated preference list was not aligned with the ground truth (either element-wise or order-wise) during the updating phase starting at
t^{\texttt{GS}}
. Equivalently, there exists a time step

t_{1}\in[t^{\texttt{GS}},t^{\texttt{GS}}+3n^{2}]

such that

t_{1}\notin\Gamma^{(n)}_{\mathcal{A}}\cap\Gamma^{(n)}_{\mathcal{F}}.

Now consider the committing phase, and let
t^{\prime\prime}\geq t^{\prime}
be the first time step such that

t^{\prime\prime}\in\Gamma^{(n)}_{\mathcal{A}}\cap\Gamma^{(n)}_{\mathcal{F}}.

At time
t^{\prime\prime}
, all agents’ top-
n
estimated firms and all firms’ top-
n
estimated agents coincide with the ground truth. Since the matching
(a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime}))_{\forall a\in\mathcal{A}}
is unchanged throughout the committing phase and is unstable with respect to the ground-truth preferences, it is also unstable with respect to the estimated preferences at time
t^{\prime\prime}
. Hence a blocking pair exists.

Let
(a_{i},f)
be such a blocking pair at time
t^{\prime\prime}
, where
f=f^{\texttt{match}}_{a_{j}}(t^{\prime})
for some
a_{j}
. By definition of a blocking pair,

f\underset{O_{a_{i}}}{>}f^{\texttt{match}}_{a_{i}}(t^{\prime})

and

a_{i}\underset{O_{f}}{>}a_{j}.

We consider the following cases.

(i)

If
f^{\texttt{match}}_{a_{i}}(t^{\prime})\notin\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a_{i}}
, then since

\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a_{i}}=\hat{\mathcal{F}}^{(n)}_{a_{i}}(t^{\prime\prime})

element-wise and order-wise, it follows that

f^{\texttt{match}}_{a_{i}}(t^{\prime})\notin\hat{\mathcal{F}}^{(n)}_{a_{i}}(t^{\prime\prime}),

which induces a preference-list inconsistency for agent
a_{i}
, and therefore

t^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}.

(ii)

If
f^{\texttt{match}}_{a_{i}}(t^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a_{i}}
and agent
a_{i}
did not apply to
f
during the previous updating phase, then necessarily

f\underset{O_{a_{i}}}{<}f^{\texttt{match}}_{a_{i}}(t^{\prime})

at time
t^{\texttt{GS}}
. At time
t^{\prime\prime}
, top-
n
alignment implies the ordering reverses to

f\underset{O_{a_{i}}}{>}f^{\texttt{match}}_{a_{i}}(t^{\prime}),

which again yields a preference-list inconsistency for agent
a_{i}
, and hence

t^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a_{i}}.

(iii)

If
f^{\texttt{match}}_{a_{i}}(t^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a_{i}}
and agent
a_{i}
did apply to
f
during the previous updating phase but was rejected, then
f
must have rejected
a_{i}
in favor of its current match
a_{j}
due to a preference-list inconsistency. At time
t^{\prime\prime}
, the estimated list of
f
is fully aligned with the ground truth and satisfies

a_{i}\underset{O_{f}}{>}a_{j},

which triggers a firm-side strategic rejection, and therefore

t^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a_{j}}.

In all cases, a switching condition is triggered at time
t^{\prime\prime}
, and therefore
t^{\prime\prime}+1
is the first round of the next updating phase.
∎

E.7.4
Proof of
Theorem

See

Proof.

We bound the pessimal regret
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\underline{R}_{a}(T)\right]
by decomposing it into regret incurred during the updating and committing phases and bounding each term separately:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\underline{R}_{a}(T)\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\underline{R}_{a}(\mathcal{U})\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\underline{R}_{a}(\mathcal{C})\right].

Before bounding the committing regret, we introduce the following notation.

denote the set of switching times, ordered increasingly.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\underline{R_{a}}(\mathcal{C})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{C}}\left(u_{a,\underline{f^{*}_{a}}}-\mathbb{E}[X_{a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t)}]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{K}|}\sum_{t\in[t^{\prime}_{j},\,t_{j+1}]}\Big(u_{a,f}-X_{a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime}_{j})}(t)\Big)\right],

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a^{\prime}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a^{\prime}}\right)\right]\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{A}}\cup\bar{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{F}}^{(n)}\right|\right].

(31)

Inequality (a) is obtained by separating, within each committing phase, the single signaling round during which an agent intentionally abstains from applying in order to signal a switching condition. Each committing phase contributes at most one such round, and therefore the total number of signaling rounds is bounded by

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{a^{\prime}}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{rej}}_{a^{\prime}}\right)\right|\right].

The second term captures the expected regret accumulated during the remaining rounds of each committing phase. Since we analyze the pessimal regret of agent

Inequality (b) follows from

, since the matching

is unstable.
Specifically, consider the committing interval

If every time step in this interval belongs to

,
then the entire interval is charged to the second term of (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{\mathcal{A}}\cup\bar{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{F}}^{(n)}\right|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{F}}^{(n)}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{a^{\prime}}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{a^{\prime},f^{\prime}}\right|\right]+\sum_{f^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{a^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{f^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime\prime}}\right|\right].

F.4
Extension of Coordination-Free Algorithm
3
to General Markets

We now extend our results to general (unstructured) markets and focus on bounding the pessimal regret. We first present an example showing that, consistent with standard impossibility phenomena for decentralized learning in matching markets, when the underlying market admits multiple stable matchings, interviews alone may not suffice to ensure convergence to the agent-optimal stable matching. Consequently, in markets with potentially multiple stable outcomes, our goal is to bound the pessimal regret, namely the regret incurred when the dynamics converge to a stable matching that is suboptimal from the agents’ perspective. We then address two algorithmic necessities that arise in this general setting. First, we show that moving beyond
k=2
interviews per round (in particular, allowing
k=3
) is necessary to avoid unresolved application cycles caused by concurrent attempts to eliminate blocking pairs. Second, we show that even with
k=3
, convergence can fail under the anonymous firm-side feedback
\mathcal{V}^{+}
unless agents are allowed to apply to more than one firm in a round and then select among the firms that admit them, so as to remain matched without inducing spurious vacancy signals.

F.4.1
Impossibility of Guaranteeing Convergence to the Agent-Optimal Stable Matching

Example F.1
.

Let the market consist of agents
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}
and firms
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\}
, with true preference lists

\begin{array}[]{lr}O_{a_{1}}:(f^{*}_{1}\succ f_{2}\succ f^{\dagger}_{3})&O_{f_{1}}:(a^{\dagger}_{2}\succ a_{3}\succ a^{*}_{1})\\[3.0pt]
O_{a_{2}}:(f^{*}_{2}\succ f_{3}\succ f^{\dagger}_{1})&O_{f_{2}}:(a^{\dagger}_{3}\succ a_{1}\succ a^{*}_{2})\\[3.0pt]
O_{a_{3}}:(f^{*}_{3}\succ f^{\dagger}_{2}\succ f_{1})&O_{f_{3}}:(a^{\dagger}_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ a^{*}_{3}).\end{array}

Under these preferences, multiple stable matchings exist. The agent-optimal (firm-pessimal) stable matching is
(a_{1},f_{1}),(a_{2},f_{2}),(a_{3},f_{3})
(marked by
∗
), whereas the agent-pessimal (firm-optimal) stable matching is
(a_{1},f_{3}),(a_{2},f_{1}),(a_{3},f_{2})
(marked by
†
).

Now consider a time step
t
in which Algorithm
3
is executed using the estimated preferences
\hat{O}(t)
. Suppose agent
a_{1}
has not yet learned its true preference list, while agents
a_{2}
and
a_{3}
have already converged. The estimated lists and current interview sets are

\begin{array}[]{lr}\hat{O}_{a_{1}}(t):(f_{2}\succ f_{1}\succ f_{3})&I_{a_{1}}(t)=\{f_{3},f^{\texttt{RR}}_{a_{1}}\}\\[3.0pt]
\hat{O}_{a_{2}}(t):(f_{2}\succ f_{3}\succ f_{1})&I_{a_{2}}(t)=\{f_{1},f^{\texttt{RR}}_{a_{2}}\}\\[3.0pt]
\hat{O}_{a_{3}}(t):(f_{3}\succ f_{2}\succ f_{1})&I_{a_{3}}(t)=\{f_{2},f^{\texttt{RR}}_{a_{3}}\}.\end{array}

Thus,
a_{1}
is the only agent with an incorrect estimate at time
t
. At a later time
t^{\prime}>t
, round-robin interviewing corrects
a_{1}
’s estimate to
\hat{O}_{a_{1}}(t^{\prime}):(f_{1}\succ f_{2}\succ f_{3})
. However, since the hired agents at
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
remain unchanged from time
t
, agent
a_{1}
continues to set
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a_{1}}(t^{\prime})=f_{3}
. Consequently, the dynamics converge to the agent-pessimal stable matching
(a_{1},f_{3}),(a_{2},f_{1}),(a_{3},f_{2})
. This shows that when multiple stable matchings exist, decentralized learning may fail to reach the agent-optimal outcome, and one can only guarantee regret with respect to the agent-pessimal stable matching.

F.4.2
Moving from
k=2
to
k=3

This section explains why allowing each agent to interview
k=3
firms per round is necessary to obtain time-independent regret bounds in general (unstructured) markets. The core issue is concurrency: multiple agents may attempt to resolve blocking pairs simultaneously, and their actions can interfere. The following
Example

F.2
shows that when
k=2
, Algorithm
3
can enter an
application cycle
that never resolves, even after all agents’ estimated preferences fully aligned with the ground truth.

Example F.2
.

Consider a market with agents
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
and firms
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
. Suppose that from some time
t^{\prime}
onward the estimated preferences are exactly aligned with the ground truth and remain fixed, i.e., for all
t\geq t^{\prime}
we have
\hat{O}_{a}(t)=O_{a}
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
\hat{O}_{f}(t)=O_{f}
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
. In particular, for every
t\geq t^{\prime}
,

\begin{array}[]{lr}\hat{O}_{a_{1}}(t):(f_{1}^{*}\succ f_{2})&\hat{O}_{f_{1}}(t):(a_{2}\succ a_{1})\\[3.0pt]
\hat{O}_{a_{2}}(t):(f_{2}\succ f_{1}^{*})&\hat{O}_{f_{2}}(t):(a_{1}\succ a_{2}).\end{array}

Here, the
*
superscripts indicate the firms
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a_{i}}(t)
chosen by agents under Algorithm
3
.

Fix any round
t\geq t^{\prime}
, and assume that at time
t-1
agent
a_{2}
applied to
f_{2}
and was rejected because
f_{2}
admitted
a_{1}
; hence
r_{a_{2},f_{2}}(t)\geq t-1
, and since
f_{2}\notin\mathcal{V}^{+}(t-1)
under this anonymous feedback, we have
f_{2}\notin\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{a_{2}}(t)
. Starting from round
t
, the algorithm can enter the following cycle:

•

At time
t
, both agents apply to
f_{1}
, and
f_{1}
admits
a_{2}
and rejects
a_{1}
; hence
f_{2}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}(t)
.

•

At time
t+1
, agent
a_{1}
applies to
f_{2}
since
f_{1}\notin\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{a_{1}}(t+1)
after the rejection, and
a_{2}
also applies to
f_{2}
because it is unmatched and
f_{2}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}(t)
. Firm
f_{2}
admits
a_{1}
and rejects
a_{2}
, so
f_{1}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}(t+1)
.

•

At time
t+2
, by the same reasoning both agents apply to
f_{1}
again, and the process repeats indefinitely.

Thus,
Example

F.2
shows that even with perfectly learned preferences, Algorithm
3
with
k=2
can cycle forever. Such a cycle could be broken, for instance, by allowing agents to deviate from Algorithm
3
with some probability and stick with
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t-1)
. Instead, we adopt a deterministic remedy by enlarging each agent’s choice set: we allow agents to apply among multiple interviewed firms, including the firm they applied to in the previous round. Concretely, we add a third option by requiring each agent to always include
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t-1)
in its interview set. Accordingly, in our analysis we treat agents as also reconsidering
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t-1)
at every round.

We then extend Algorithm
3
to
k=3
by setting the interview set to include the current candidate, the previous application, and a round-robin firm, namely

\mathcal{I}_{a}(t)=\{f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t),f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t-1),f^{\texttt{RR}}_{a}(t)\}.

This captures both remedies discussed above: a randomized deviation that lets the agent occasionally stick with
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t-1)
, and a deterministic enlargement of the agent’s option set. In contrast to the
k=2
version—where the agent effectively has a single action, applying to
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t)
—under
k=3
the agent interviews three firms and may apply to either
f_{a}(t)
or
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t-1)
, choosing between them based on the updated information revealed by
\mathcal{I}_{a}(t)
to choose
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t)
.

F.4.3
Necessity of applying to more than one firm per round for coordination-free decentralized learning

We explain why agents must be allowed to apply to
more than one
firm per round (i.e., apply to a set of interviewed firms), and why merely increasing the interview budget from
k=2
to
k=3
is still insufficient for convergence in general. The key obstacle is the
anonymous
nature of the firm-side feedback
\mathcal{V}^{+}
: observing that a firm becomes vacant does not reveal
which
agent left or whether the vacancy was caused by a strategic rejection. As a result, agents may repeatedly “chase” the same vacancy and create persistent application cycles, even when all estimated preference lists have already converged to the ground truth. The following example illustrates this phenomenon and shows how allowing an agent to apply to a
set
of firms in a round can eliminate such cycles by preventing spurious vacancies from appearing in
\mathcal{V}^{+}
.

Example F.3
.

Consider a market with agents
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
and firms
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
. Suppose that from some time
t^{\prime}
onward the estimated preferences are exactly aligned with the ground truth and remain fixed, i.e., for all
t\geq t^{\prime}
we have
\hat{O}_{a}(t)=O_{a}
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
\hat{O}_{f}(t)=O_{f}
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
. In particular, for every
t\geq t^{\prime}
,

\begin{array}[]{lr}\hat{O}_{a_{1}}(t):(f_{1}\succ f_{2}^{*})&\hat{O}_{f_{1}}(t):(a_{2}\succ a_{1})\\[3.0pt]
\hat{O}_{a_{2}}(t):(f_{2}\succ f_{1}^{*})&\hat{O}_{f_{2}}(t):(a_{1}\succ a_{2}).\end{array}

Here, the
∗
superscripts indicate the firms
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a_{i}}(t)
chosen by agents under Algorithm
3
. We also assume that
f_{2}
rejected
a_{2}
at some earlier time, so
f_{2}
remains in
a_{2}
’s candidate set until
a_{2}
applies to it again.

Fix any round
t\geq t^{\prime}
. Consider the following two cases.

1.

Applying to a single firm can create a cycle.
Suppose that at time
t+1
agent
a_{1}
interviews
\mathcal{I}_{a_{1}}(t+1)=\{f_{1},f_{2},f^{\texttt{RR}}_{a_{1}}(t+1)\}
and applies only to
f_{1}
, i.e.,
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a_{1}}(t+1)=f_{1}
, while agent
a_{2}
keeps applying to
f_{2}
. Since
f_{1}
prefers
a_{2}
to
a_{1}
, firm
f_{1}
rejects
a_{1}
, and hence
f_{2}
is reported in
\mathcal{V}^{+}(t+1)
as a vacant firm. Because
\mathcal{V}^{+}(t+1)
is anonymous, agent
a_{2}
cannot distinguish whether
f_{2}
appeared due to a strategic rejection or because its current match applied elsewhere; consequently,
a_{2}
must keep
f_{2}
in its candidate set and eventually applies to
f_{2}
again. This induces a back-and-forth dynamic as in
Example

F.2
, yielding an application cycle that can persist indefinitely despite perfectly learned preferences.

2.

Applying to a set breaks the cycle.
Suppose instead that agents may apply to a
set
of firms in a round. In the same situation at time
t+1
, let
a_{1}
apply to both firms, i.e.,
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a_{1}}(t+1)=\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
. Even if
f_{1}
rejects
a_{1}
, the simultaneous application to
f_{2}
allows
a_{1}
to remain (or become) matched to
f_{2}
in the same round. Consequently,
f_{2}
does
not
appear in
\mathcal{V}^{+}(t+1)
, so
a_{2}
receives no spurious vacancy signal and is not pulled into chasing
f_{2}
again.

In summary, under anonymous firm-side feedback, allowing an agent to apply to multiple firms in a round can suppress spurious vacancy signals and thereby eliminate persistent application cycles that would otherwise lead to linear regret.
.

F.4.5
Crucial Theorems, Lemmas, and Observations

We begin by defining the notion of an
agent-consistent
(a.k.a. player-consistent) blocking pair, adapted from
[
2
,
15
]
.

Definition F.4

(Agent-consistent blocking pair)
.

A blocking pair
(a_{i},f_{j})
in a matching
\mu
is
player-consistent
if
f_{j}\underset{O_{a_{i}}}{>}f
for every firm
f
such that
(a_{i},f)
is a blocking pair in
\mu
.

We next recall a crucial result of
2
(stated here for completeness) showing that, under fixed preferences, repeatedly resolving blocking pairs in a player-consistent order reaches a stable matching.

Theorem F.5

(
[
2
]
)
.

Given any unstable matching
\mathcal{G}
, there exists a sequence of blocking pairs of length at most
n^{4}
such that resolving this sequence reaches a stable matching. Moreover, this sequence can be chosen to resolve blocking pairs in a player-consistent order, i.e., every blocking pair
(a_{i},f_{j})
resolved in the current matching
\mu
is player-consistent with respect to
\mathcal{G}
.

We now present
Lemma

F.6
, which bounds the expected maximum number of rounds for which the matching
(a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t))_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
remains unstable within any interval
[t_{1}:t_{2}]
such that
t\in\Gamma^{(m)}_{\mathcal{A}}\cap\Gamma^{(n)}_{\mathcal{F}}
for all
t\in[t_{1}:t_{2}]
under
Algorithm

7
. By
Definition

A.4
, these are precisely the time steps at which the estimated preference lists of all agents and firms coincide with the ground truth. This lemma plays a central role in the proof of
Theorem

5.4
.

Lemma F.6
.

In a matching market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
with strategic firms following
Algorithm

1
and agents following
Algorithm

7
, consider any interval
[t_{1}:t_{2}]
such that
t\in\Gamma^{(m)}_{\mathcal{A}}\cap\Gamma^{(n)}_{\mathcal{F}}
for all
t\in[t_{1}:t_{2}]
. Then, for any
\lambda\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t\in[t_{1}:t_{2}]}\mathbbm{1}\left\{(a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t))_{\forall a\in\mathcal{A}}\text{ is not stable}\right\}\right]\leq 1+\frac{n^{4}m+nm}{\bigl(\lambda\cdot(1-\lambda)^{n-1}\bigr)^{n^{4}m+nm}}.

Proof.

We first account for rounds in
[t_{1}:t_{2}]
in which a firm-side strategic rejection can make the realized matching
(a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t))_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
unstable, regardless of the stability of the intended matching
(a,f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(t))_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
. We claim that for each firm
f\in\mathcal{F}
, at most one such rejection can occur over
[t_{1}:t_{2}]
, and it can only occur at the first round
t_{1}
. Indeed, by the definition of
\Gamma^{(n)}_{\mathcal{F}}
, for every
t\in[t_{1}:t_{2}]
each firm’s estimated list coincides with its ground-truth list, so a firm never rejects in order to hire an agent that is worse under the ground truth. Hence, whenever the hiring state of
f
changes during
[t_{1}:t_{2}]
, it either (i) hires an agent it truly prefers to its current match, or (ii) becomes vacant. Therefore, under
Algorithm

1
the only round at which a firm may strategically reject is
t_{1}
, which explains the additive
1
term in the lemma statement.

It remains to bound

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t\in[t_{1}+1:t_{2}]}\mathbbm{1}\left\{(a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t))_{\forall a\in\mathcal{A}}\text{ is not stable}\right\}\right].

Fix any
t\in[t_{1}+1:t_{2}]
and let
\mathcal{G}(t)=(a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t))_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
be the realized matching at time
t
. By
Theorem

F.5
, if
\mathcal{G}(t)
is unstable then there exists a sequence
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n^{4}}\}
of agent-consistent blocking pairs such that resolving these pairs in order reaches a stable matching.

We lower bound the probability of resolving this sequence by exhibiting a sufficient event for resolving each blocking pair. Assume inductively that
s_{1},\dots,s_{i-1}
have already been resolved, and let
s_{i}=(a^{\prime},f^{\prime\prime})
. Define
\mathcal{X}_{i}
as the event that, for at most
m
consecutive rounds after
s_{1},\dots,s_{i-1}
are resolved, agent
a^{\prime}
chooses the “move” option (hence applies according to
f_{a^{\prime}}(\cdot)
) in every round, while every other agent
a\neq a^{\prime}
chooses the “stay” option (hence applies to
f^{\texttt{apply}}_{a}(\cdot-1)
) in every round. Conditioned on
\mathcal{X}_{i}
, the agent
a^{\prime}
keeps advancing through its candidate set and, in the worst case, reaches and applies to
f^{\prime\prime}
within
m
rounds; when
a^{\prime}
applies to
f^{\prime\prime}
, the agent-consistency of
s_{i}
implies that
f^{\prime\prime}
admits
a^{\prime}
and rejects its current match, thereby resolving the blocking pair.

By independence of agents’ randomized choices across rounds,

\Pr(\mathcal{X}_{i})\;\geq\;\bigl(\lambda\cdot(1-\lambda)^{n-1}\bigr)^{m}.

Let

\mathcal{X}\;\doteq\;\bigcap_{i=1}^{n^{4}}\mathcal{X}_{i}.

By construction,
\mathcal{X}
implies that all blocking pairs in
S
are resolved in order, and hence the matching becomes stable after at most
n^{4}m
rounds. Moreover,

\Pr(\mathcal{X})\;\geq\;\bigl(\lambda\cdot(1-\lambda)^{n-1}\bigr)^{n^{4}m}.

After
\mathcal{X}
occurs, there exists a time
t^{\prime}
at which the matching has no blocking pair. However, due to the anonymous revelation of hiring changes in
\mathcal{V}^{+}
, an agent may still have firms in its candidate set that it prefers to its current match
f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime})
. Since there is no blocking pair, any such application is rejected provided the other agents remain with their stable matches. Under
Algorithm

7
, when an agent applies to a preferred firm while also applying to its current match
f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime})
, it remains matched with
f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t^{\prime})
upon rejection, and neither firm necessarily appears in
\mathcal{V}^{+}
. Thus, it may take up to an additional
nm
rounds for all agents to exhaust such futile applications and reach a state in which they keep applying to (and remaining matched with) the stable matching.

Formally, define
\mathcal{J}
as the event that, after
\mathcal{X}
occurs, within the next
nm
rounds the process reaches a time
\tau
such that
\mathcal{G}(\tau)
is stable and
\mathcal{G}(t)=\mathcal{G}(\tau)
for all subsequent rounds in the interval. By the same independence argument,

\Pr(\mathcal{J}\mid\mathcal{X})\;\geq\;\bigl(\lambda\cdot(1-\lambda)^{n-1}\bigr)^{nm}.

Therefore,

\Pr(\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{J})\;=\;\Pr(\mathcal{J}\mid\mathcal{X})\,\Pr(\mathcal{X})\;\geq\;\bigl(\lambda\cdot(1-\lambda)^{n-1}\bigr)^{n^{4}m+nm}.

Hence, with probability at least
p\doteq\bigl(\lambda\cdot(1-\lambda)^{n-1}\bigr)^{n^{4}m+nm}
, within the next
n^{4}m+nm
rounds the process reaches a stable matching that remains unchanged thereafter. Viewing this as a Bernoulli trial with success probability
p
, the expected number of rounds (starting from
t_{1}+1
) until such a stable-and-absorbing state is reached is at most
1/p
. Consequently,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t\in[t_{1}:t_{2}]}\mathbbm{1}\left\{(a,f^{\texttt{match}}_{a}(t))_{\forall a\in\mathcal{A}}\text{ is not stable}\right\}\right]\leq 1+\frac{n^{4}m+nm}{\bigl(\lambda\cdot(1-\lambda)^{n-1}\bigr)^{n^{4}m+nm}}.

∎

Appendix G
Discussion on Incentive Compatibility in Decentralized Learning

We study double-sided learning in a matching market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
, where both agents and firms update their behavior over time based on their own observations. Since stability is a shared objective on both sides, a natural question is whether the decentralized dynamics we propose are
incentive compatible
in the following sense: if all other players follow a fixed prescribed algorithm, does any single agent or firm have an incentive to deviate in order to obtain a more favorable stable outcome?

Concretely, we consider agents following the decentralized Algorithms
2
,
3
, and
7
, together with firms implementing the strategic rejection policy in
Algorithm

1
. In markets that may admit multiple stable matchings, where agent-optimal/agent-pessimal and firm-optimal/firm-pessimal stable matches are well-defined, we ask whether following these algorithms—given that others do the same—leads each side to converge to the stable matching that is optimal (or pessimal) from its own perspective.

We next give a general notion of
incentive compatibility
for a player on either side of the market, defined relative to fixed algorithms adopted by all other players, and under the informational constraints of our learning model.

Definition G.1

(Incentive compatibility under partial information)
.

Consider a matching market
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
in which the underlying (ground-truth) preference profile is unknown to all players. At each time step, each agent and firm only maintains its own estimated preference list (formed from its observed samples), and players observe outcomes only through the firm-side feedback stream (e.g.,
\mathcal{V}(t)
or
\mathcal{V}^{+}(t)
, depending on the model).
Fix a pair of policies
(\pi_{\mathcal{A}},\pi_{\mathcal{F}})
, where
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
specifies the agents’ learning policy and
\pi_{\mathcal{F}}
specifies the firms’ rejection (and, if applicable, learning) policy.
For a side
S\in\{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F}\}
, we say that policy
\pi_{S}
is
incentive compatible
for side
S
(relative to
\pi_{-S}
) if, under these information constraints, when all players on side
S
follow
\pi_{S}
and all players on the opposite side follow
\pi_{-S}
, the induced dynamics converge to the
S
-optimal stable matching (equivalently, the
(-S)
-pessimal stable matching).

We now discuss the incentive compatibility of agents and firms under our decentralized learning dynamics, distinguishing between markets with a unique stable matching and markets with multiple stable matchings.

G.1
Incentive compatibility in matching markets with a unique stable matching

When
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
admits a
unique
stable matching, incentive considerations are comparatively clean: convergence to a stable outcome uniquely determines the optimal stable outcome for both sides. We formalize this under our decentralized information structure and show that, conditional on convergence, the prescribed agent- and firm-side policies are incentive compatible in the sense of

G.1
.

Proposition G.2

(Incentive compatibility under a unique stable matching)

Suppose
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
admits a unique stable matching

under the ground-truth preferences (this includes
\alpha

If, when all agents follow

and all firms follow

is incentive compatible for firms with respect to

G.1
.

Since
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})

Agents.

In our decentralized learning model, agents are the proposing side and each player has only partial information (it knows only its own estimated preference list and observes only the firm-side feedback). Importantly, even when firms are

In particular, there exist instances where allowing firms to use strategic rejections leads to a stable outcome

Since under certainty our policy does not employ such outcome-improving strategic rejections, a firm may have an

G.1

Our goal is to position the obtained bounds relative to natural lower-bound benchmarks, and to clarify which gaps are inherent to the learning problem versus artifacts of coordination and information constraints. In particular, we compare our upper bounds to immediate information-theoretic baselines and outline the remaining gaps as concrete lower-bound questions.

Appendix I
Improvement over Prior Work

We present here the main ways our results strengthen prior work: we obtain
time-independent
guarantees under
limited firm-side feedback
, while explicitly modeling
firm uncertainty
via a strategic action space and a rejection policy. This yields a more general framework for decentralized double-sided learning, and our firm-side rejection mechanism can be incorporated into existing learning-to-stability approaches to preserve convergence even when firms update their estimates over time.

I.1
Applicability of Strategic Firm’s Rejection Policy
Algorithm

1

A key conceptual contribution of our framework is the firm-side rejection policy
Algorithm

1
, which is designed to handle
firm uncertainty
while preserving convergence to stability under decentralized learning. The policy uses controlled (strategic) rejections as a mechanism for re-initiating correction phases exactly when a firm detects that its current local estimates may be inconsistent with the true preference ordering relevant to stability.

Beyond its role in our algorithms,
Algorithm

1
can be viewed as a modular primitive that can be adapted to prior decentralized matching-bandit frameworks
[
15
,
17
,
24
]
. Many existing works establish convergence to a stable matching under the assumption that firms’ preferences are fixed and known (or, more generally, that firms act as passive acceptors without learning). Under such assumptions, stability is guaranteed only because the firm side introduces no additional uncertainty into the dynamics. Our rejection policy provides a principled way to relax this restriction: it allows firms to be uncertain and to learn their own preferences, while still enabling the overall process to converge to a stable outcome by triggering corrective updates whenever firm-side estimation errors would otherwise derail stability.

To our knowledge, this use of strategic firm-side rejections specifically to incorporate
learning and uncertainty on the firm side
while maintaining convergence-to-stability guarantees is first introduced in this work. Consequently, incorporating
Algorithm

1
into prior approaches offers a direct path to strengthening their results by extending them from settings with fully known firm preferences to more realistic environments in which firms are uncertain and must learn.

I.2
Improvements with Limited Firm-Side Feedback

The firm-side feedback model studied here, together with our treatment of firm uncertainty via an extended firm action space, yields substantial improvements over the guarantees in
[
15
]
. In particular, their model assumes that firms have fixed preference lists that are known to both firms and agents throughout the learning process, which is a strong and often unrealistic assumption. They also assume a much richer feedback structure in which agents observe not only vacancies and hiring changes, but also the identity of each firm’s current match at the end of every round.

In contrast, our bounds are obtained under anonymous firm-side feedback and while allowing firms to be both strategic and uncertain. Taken together, these features lead to nearly optimal regret guarantees under significantly weaker informational assumptions, improving upon
[
15
]
along multiple dimensions.

Appendix J
Future Directions and Open Questions

Having detailed our improvements over prior work, we now outline several future directions that remain as interesting open problems.

•

Firm-side incentives beyond the unique-stable regime.

Our analysis focuses on agent regret and introduces the firm-side rejection policy
Algorithm

1
, which guarantees convergence to stability under limited firm-side feedback. However, as discussed in
Sections

G.1
and
G.2
, this policy is incentive compatible for firms only in markets with a unique stable matching (cf.
Proposition

G.2
), while incentive alignment becomes subtle when multiple stable matchings exist.
A natural open question is to design
alternative
firm-side rejection policies that (i) preserve convergence to stability under the same decentralized information structure (each firm knows only its own estimated preference list and observes the firm-side feedback) and (ii) are more closely aligned with firm objectives in multi-stable markets, i.e., can bias equilibrium selection toward firm-preferred stable outcomes.
This direction connects to the influence of firm-side strategic rejections in markets with certain firms (e.g.,
[
11
]
) and raises the challenge of extending such outcome-improvement phenomena to an
online
learning-to-stability setting, potentially also under our extended firm action space that models uncertainty.

•

Reward-adaptive application decisions and stability notions.

In our model, agents choose whom to apply/interview based on their current estimates (e.g., empirical means)
before
observing the realized interview reward in that round.
An interesting extension is a
reward-sensitive
variant in which an agent can condition its final application decision on the realized interview outcomes within the same round (e.g., after interviewing a subset of firms).
This change can reduce regret by enabling within-round adaptation, but it also suggests stronger, reward-contingent notions of stability and benchmarking: the relevant comparison may no longer be regret to a fixed stable matching (agent-optimal/pessimal), but to an outcome concept that accounts for the additional adaptivity enabled by realized rewards.
Analyzing this setting would likely require tracking not only mean gaps but also distributional properties (e.g., variance or tail behavior), and may call for estimators and concentration tools beyond the empirical-mean-based arguments used throughout our paper.
