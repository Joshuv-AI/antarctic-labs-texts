Use of standard definitions for the extent of antibiotic resistance in bacteria: a systematic review and framework for future consensus definitions
Authors: Wei Cong Tan, See Rui En Daniel, Yi Ling Tan, Yew Wei Andrew Wong, Shweta Rajkumar Singh, David Paterson, Yin Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVarious terms are used to categorize the extent of antibiotic resistance in bacteria. It is important to standardize the classification of drug-resistant bacteria to ensure clarity and generalizability of evidence across different disciplines and inform policy.MethodsA literature search for articles published between 2011 and August 2025 indexed in MEDLINE and EMBASE, which used the terms: multidrug resistance, extensively drug resistance, pan-drug resistance, difficult-to-treat or usual drug resistance in either the methods or results sections, and which reported bacterial pathogens relevant to human health was performed. Data extracted were analysed quantitatively using multivariable logistic regressions and qualitatively to examine the study characteristics that adopted various definitions.ResultsFrom 2465 eligible records, the most commonly used definition was by Magiorakos et al. accounting for 54% (1340/2465) of the articles: 8% (197/2465) used different definitions, and the remaining 38% (928/2465) did not define drug resistance. Overall, MDR was the most frequently used term (97%, 2400/2465), followed by XDR then PDR. Gram-negative bacteria were the most commonly studied bacteria (84%, 2078/2465). Definitions that deviated from those proposed by Magiorakos et al. used a different number of antibiotic classes to define extent of resistance, included non-susceptibility to newer antibiotic classes, studied typically community-acquired bacteria and used resistance genotype instead of antibiotic susceptibility phenotype.ConclusionThe progressive development of antibiotic resistance necessitates updated definitions to include newer antibiotic agents and genotypes. These revised definitions can also prioritize antibiotic classes according to their efficacy profile, toxicity and availability, and should be validated against clinical datasets to correlate with patient outcomes.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses an urgent threat to modern medicine, and is a leading cause of mortality globally.1 Research on the underlying mechanism, epidemiology, diagnostics and treatments of AMR has intensified over the past decade, driven by novel genomic sequencing technology, development of new antibiotics and increased focus on the One Health approach.2 Various terms to categorize the extent of drug resistance among bacterial species are commonly used by the scientific, public health and commercial pharmaceutical communities. With rapidly evolving knowledge and the interdisciplinary nature of AMR, it is important to standardize the classification of drug-resistant bacteria to ensure clarity and generalizability of evidence across different domains.

The most widely adopted definitions of drug-resistant bacteria were proposed by Magiorakos et al. in 2011.3 These built on previous expert commentaries on AMR and provided definitions focused on pathogenic bacteria commonly associated with healthcare-associated infections,4,5 including Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus spp., Enterobacterales, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter spp. The extent of resistance was defined using phenotypic non-susceptibility to antibiotic classes, i.e. non-susceptibility to at least one agent in at least three antibiotic classes was defined as multidrug resistant (MDR), and susceptibility limited to two or fewer classes as extensively drug resistant (XDR).3 Another definition standard is difficult-to-treat resistance (DTR) in Gram-negative bacteria, which aimed to offer a more practical approach by focusing on treatment-limiting resistance to all first-line agents, i.e. carbapenems and beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations and fluoroquinolones.6

These definitions warrant regular review and updates in response to new therapeutic agents and epidemiological trends. A number of novel antimicrobial agents have gained approval by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) since 2010.7 These include agents in previously known antibiotic classes, e.g. eravacycline (tetracycline), plazomicin (aminoglycoside), tedizolid (oxazolidinone), cefiderocol (cephalosporin) and ceftazidime-avibactam (cephalosporin beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations). New antibiotic classes were also developed, including lipoglycopeptides (e.g. dalbavancin, oritavancin) and pleuromutilins (e.g. lefamulin). In addition, new revelations on genomic basis of resistance have been described owing to the advent of high-throughput next-generation sequencing technology. Increased AMR surveillance and consensus from international health authorities have also brought to attention resistance in conventionally community-acquired bacterial pathogens such as Salmonella or Shigella spp.8

In this study, we present how the definition and classification of drug-resistant bacteria are reported in the recent literature with reference to published standards,3,6 and offer recommendations for an updated standardization for drug-resistant bacteria.

Methods

Literature search and eligibility criteria

We performed a literature search for peer reviewed articles published from 2011 to August 2025 indexed in MEDLINE (OVID) and EMBASE and written in English language. We searched for publications that used any of the following terms to describe drug-resistant bacteria: MDR, XDR, pan-drug resistant (PDR), DTR, usual drug resistance (UDR) or antimicrobial resistance (Table 1). MDR/XDR/PDR, DTR and UDR were defined by Magiorakos et al.,3 Kadri et al.6 and McDonnell et al.,9 respectively. We focused on studies that reported bacterial pathogens relevant to human health. The exclusion criteria included fungal, viral, mycobacterial and parasitic infections. The exclusion of non-bacterial infections was done by using the Boolean operator ‘NOT’ as the initial search results without ‘NOT’ produced >417 000 results, making it infeasible to screen. Searches were re-run before the final report and analysis. The full literature search protocol is included in the Supplementary Data File S1 (available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).

Study selection

As the expected number of articles was large, it was not possible to screen all articles for eligibility. We calculated the sample size of the number of articles that needed to be screened to fulfil our aims and objectives. The primary objective was to quantify the proportion of articles using the various drug resistance definitions. The sample size for this primary aim was calculated by assuming that 20% of the articles would fulfil our eligibility criteria, and a further 30% of these eligible articles were using the Magiorakos definitions, a 5% margin of error and 95% confidence level. This required 1615 articles to be randomly screened to be sufficiently powered to meet the primary objective. The secondary objectives were to describe the study characteristics associated with using the definitions proposed by Magiorakos et al., such as study design and types of bacteria etc. (File S2). To satisfy these secondary objectives, for every 100 randomly screened articles we calculated the proportions of the cumulative articles fulfilling each pre-defined characteristic, e.g. study design, source of bacterial isolates, sampling type, infection type, characterization of antibiotic susceptibility, antibiotic susceptibility standards used, bacteria studied and income classification of countries in which study was conducted. We then terminated screening when at least 1615 articles were screened in total and (i) when an additional 100 articles did not alter the overall characteristics of included articles, i.e. termination criteria were met if there was a <1% absolute difference in the proportions of all pre-defined study characteristics; or (ii) 25% of total articles from the initial search were completed, whichever was earlier. The 25% threshold was set a priori as a pragmatic upper limit to ensure feasibility of the review given the resources.

Articles retrieved from the literature search were randomly sorted, and assigned among five reviewers (Y.W.A.W., S.R.E.D., Y.L.T., W.C.T., S.R.S.). We screened for articles that used MDR, XDR, PDR, DTR, UDR terms in either the methods or results sections. Only original research including systematic review and meta-analysis was included. Editorials and commentaries were excluded as they do not involve reporting or analysis of primary data.

The reviewers screened these articles independently to determine eligibility. Discrepancies were resolved through consultation of a sixth reviewer (Y.M.). No automation tools were used in the screening process.

Data extraction

Full text of eligible articles was evaluated in detail for data extraction. Five reviewers (Y.W.A.W., S.R.E.D., Y.L.T., W.C.T., S.R.S.) extracted data independently. Data extracted included study title, year of publication, journal, publication country, study design and study population. In addition, we also sought which antibiotic resistance definition was used (cited by Magiorakos et al., adopting the same definition without citation, using different definitions or did not specify the definitions used), type and source of the bacterial isolates (grouped as ‘multiple’ if isolates originated from more than one source, or were assumed to be from multiple sources when the source was not clearly specified), if resistance was characterized with phenotypic or genotypic methods and antibiotic susceptibility guidelines used. As our review question was focusing on the use and reporting of the drug-resistant terminologies in each study, we assumed all included publications to be independent, including systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

As we aimed to evaluate how drug-resistant bacteria definitions were used in published literature rather than the study outcomes, a risk of bias assessment was not applicable. No patients or members of the public were directly involved in this research study.

Data analysis

Data extracted were summarized using percentages for categorical variables with 95% confidence intervals for percentages calculated using the Wilson score. We further analysed the relationships between study characteristics with which drug-resistant definitions using univariable and multivariable logistic regressions, with whether the studies used the Magiorakos definitions (regardless of citation) as dependent variables and study characteristics, e.g. study design, study countries grouped by income level, source of bacterial isolates, characterization of antibiotic susceptibilities, antibiotic susceptibility standards used and types of bacterial group investigated (individual bacteria were regrouped into non-mutually exclusive categories, namely Enterobacterales, non-fermenting Gram-negatives, other Gram-negatives and Gram-positives) as independent variables. Variables that were not specified in the original articles were retained under the ‘Not specified’ category. Variables included in a multivariable model were assessed for multicollinearity using the Variance Inflation Factor, which were ensured to be fewer than five indicating acceptable collinearity. All estimates were reported with a 95% confidence interval and at a 5% level of significance. Articles that adopted definitions other than MDR, XDR, PDR, DTR or UDR or did not use any definitions were evaluated qualitatively for potential reasons.

Results

The search produced 30 595 unique records published from 2011 to August 2025. After randomly selecting and screening through 5840 articles, our pre-defined termination rule was reached (File S2). Out of these, 2465 fulfilled the eligibility criteria and were included in the final review (Figure 1).

Characteristics of studies retrieved from the literature

Most studies were observational in design (88%, 2169/2465) (Table S1). This was followed by in vitro experiments (6%, 144/2465) and systematic reviews (4%, 98/2465). Only 2% (54/2465) were interventional studies (Figure 2a). Nearly half of the studies were conducted in high-income countries (47%, 1147/2465), followed by those from upper middle-income status (29%, 720/2465) and low middle-income status (19%, 468/2465). Only 3% (76/2465) were from low income status and 2% (54/2465) from countries of varying income status (Figure 2a). Whereas phenotypic antibiotic susceptibility testing remained the mainstay method for drug resistance characterization (overall 46%, 1123/2465), genotyping was increasingly used to complement phenotyping over the years (Figure 2a). When determining antibiotic susceptibility, more than half of the articles used Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI10) guidelines (51%, 1262/2465), 11% (268/2465) used European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST11) guidelines and 7% (177/2465) used both guidelines. A minority (3%, 65/2465) used other antibiotic susceptibility guidelines, which include US FDA (1%, 21/2465)12 and Committee for Antimicrobial Testing of the French Society guidelines (1%, 19/2465).13 The rest did not specify the guidelines used.

In terms of the organisms studied, Enterobacterales were the most commonly studied microorganisms (58%, 1420/2465), followed by Pseudomonas spp. (29%, 706/2465), Acinetobacter spp. (25%, 610/2465), Staphylococcus aureus (23%, 574/2465) and Enterococcus spp. (14%, 343/2465) (Files S2 and S3). Most drug-resistant bacteria were isolated from human samples taken for clinical indications (85%, 1756/2073). Of these, bloodstream (12%, 213/1756), urinary (10%, 179/1756) and respiratory (9%, 158/1756) infections were the commonest.

Common definitions for extent of antibiotic resistance in bacteria in the literature

The most commonly used standard to define drug-resistant bacteria was by Magiorakos et al. accounting for 35% (856/2465) of the articles (Figure 2a). A further 20% (484/2465) adopted the same definitions, despite not citing the Magiorakos definitions. There was an increasing trend in the proportion of articles that adopted the Magiorakos definitions over the years and this had stabilized since mid-2010 (Figure 2a). Only 8% (197/2465) used different definitions, and the remaining 38% (928/2465) did not define drug resistance specifically. Overall, MDR was the most frequently used term (97%, 2400/2465), followed by XDR (17%, 407/2465), then PDR (9%, 211/2465) (Files S2 and S3).

A qualitative evaluation of the 197 articles that defined drug resistance differently from the Magiorakos definitions highlighted five main scenarios. The commonest scenario was that MDR and XDR were defined using non-susceptibility to a different number of antibiotic classes from that proposed by Magiorakos et al. (42%, 83/197); 4% (7/197), 27% (53/197) and 12% (23/197) classified MDR as bacteria that were non-susceptible to at least one, two or four antibiotic classes, respectively. More than one-quarter (30%, 59/197) defined MDR using the number of specific antibiotics as a criterion instead of antibiotic classes. A common example was Enterobacterales, such as Escherichia coli, which was classified as MDR when there was ‘resistance to two or more antibiotics tested’.14–17

Second, 19% (37/197) defined MDR using a set of antibiotics that was either approved after publication of the Magiorakos definitions, or which was deemed more relevant to the studied bacteria. The latter was especially relevant to some Gram-negative organisms with complex resistance patterns, e.g. Pseudomonas aeruginosa or Acinetobacter baumannii.18–22 For example, MDR Acinetobacter baumannii was defined as ‘simultaneously resistant to more than one antibiotic agent, i.e. ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ciprofloxacin, tobramycin, tetracycline and trimethoprim–sulphamethoxazole’18 or ‘resistance to fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin), carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem and ertapenem), third-generation cephalosporins (cefotaxime, ceftazidime, ceftazidime-avibactam and ceftolozane-tazobactam), fourth-generation cephalosporins (cefepime), piperacillin, piperacillin-tazobactam, chloramphenicol and fosfomycin and were susceptible only to colistin.’21 MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa was defined as ‘resistant to carbapenems and, concomitantly, at least two other classes of antipseudomonal antibiotics: penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactams, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones.’20

Third, bacteria that were not included in the Magiorakos definitions were classified using other criteria (16%, 32/197). These were typically studies that focused on a single-organism such as Salmonella spp. (24/32), Streptococcus pneumoniae (4/32), Shigella spp. (2/32) and Neisseria gonorrhoea (2/32). In these studies, MDR Salmonella spp. were commonly defined as non-susceptible to ampicillin/amoxicillin, chloramphenicol and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole.23,24 MDR Shigella spp. was defined as non-susceptibility to two or more antibiotic classes.25,26 MDR Neisseria gonorrhoea referred to non-susceptibility to one currently recommended therapy (cephalosporin, ceftriaxone cefixime, azithromycin) plus resistance to two or more other less commonly used antibiotics (penicillin, tetracycline, erythromycin, ciprofloxacin).27 Definitions for MDR Streptococcus pneumoniae were highly variable. These included non-susceptibility to beta-lactam and two other antibiotic families, such as cefuroxime, ceftriaxone, erythromycin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole28, and non-susceptibility to penicillin and erythromycin.29

Fourth, drug resistance bacteria with well-described drug resistance patterns, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales, were classified according to their own specific susceptibility patterns (13%, 25/197).

Last, three studies (2%) used resistance genes to define drug resistance, such as the presence of mecA and beta-lactamases. Resistance genes may be combined with phenotypic antibiotic non-susceptibility. For example, MDR Staphylococcus aureus was defined by the presence of mecA gene and phenotypic non-susceptibility (inducible, intermediate or high-level resistance) to three additional classes of antibiotic drugs by disc diffusion methods.30,31 MDR Staphylococcus aureus was also defined exclusively genotypically as the presence of three or more of antibiotic/antiseptic resistance genes: MRSA (mecA), beta-lactamase (blaZ), mupirocin (ileS2), macrolides, lincosamides and streptogramin B (MLSB) compounds [erm(C)], tetracycline [tet(K), tet(M)], streptothricin (sat), aminoglycosides (aacA-aphD, aadD and aphA3) and qacA (resistance to quaternary ammonium compounds).32

Association between study characteristics and drug resistance definitions

Study characteristics that were independently associated with adopting the Magiorakos definitions source of bacteria being from urinary tract (aOR 1.8, 95% CI 1.2 to 2.6), and non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria (aOR 1.4, 95% CI 1.2 to 1.7) (Figure 3 and Table S2). By contrast, study characteristics that were independently associated with using other definitions included interventional studies and in vitro experiments compared to observational studies (aOR 0.4, 95% CI 0.2 to 0.8 and 0.2, 95% CI 0.1 to 0.3, respectively), the use of only genotyping for antibiotic susceptibility characterization (aOR 0.2, 95% CI 0.1 to 0.4), the use of antibiotic susceptibility standards other than EUCAST and CLSI (aOR 0.6, 95% CI 0.5 to 0.8) and Gram-negative bacteria other than Enterobacterales and non-fermenters (aOR 0.7, 95% CI 0.6 to 0.9).

Discussion

In this comprehensive review of how drug-resistant bacteria were defined in the recent decade of published literature, we found that the most commonly adopted definitions were proposed by Magiorakos et al. in 2011,3 which were used in more than half of antibiotic resistance studies. Drug resistance definitions among other articles were highly heterogenous. The reasons for these non-standardized definitions included deviation in the numbers or types of non-susceptible antibiotic class, studies on community-acquired bacteria and using resistance genes rather than antibiotic susceptibilities to define drug resistance.

There were a few drawbacks in adopting the drug-resistant bacteria definitions proposed by Magiorakos et al. According to the Magiorakos definitions,3 XDR was determined by susceptibility to two or fewer antibiotic classes. This required testing of 17 unique antibiotic classes, which could be resource intensive.6 In addition, MDR/XDR/PDR definitions may not correlate with progressively worsening clinical outcomes as all antibiotic agents were considered of equal importance. In practice, some antibiotics were preferentially prescribed due to favourable toxicity and efficacy profiles or based on antibiotics availability. DTR, on the other hand, was only limited to Gram-negatives and defined based on ‘first-line’ antibiotics.6 These ‘first-line’ antibiotics were not standardized as they varied between bacterial species, infection syndromes and antibiotic availability in different countries.

Our findings highlighted the need for an update in classifying the extent of antibiotic resistance in bacteria. We propose the following considerations for this update (Table 2). Future definitions should account for common and intrinsic resistance patterns catered to each bacterial family. New antibiotic agents and classes recently approved by the FDA should be considered. Resistance in bacteria that commonly cause drug-resistant community-acquired infections, such as Neisseria gonorrhoea and Shigella spp., can also be defined. Revisions can be considered for bacteria with an increasing trend of drug resistance to raise the threshold in number of antibiotic classes before they are classified as MDR, e.g. Acinetobacter spp. or Pseudomonas spp. To improve clinical applicability, instead of giving equal weights to all antibiotic classes, commonly prescribed and tested antibiotics can be prioritized. Considering the increasing use of genotypic characterization, resistance genes with high specificity for non-susceptibility to their associated antibiotic classes can be listed alongside these antibiotics and be considered when defining drug resistance. Last, updated definitions for increasing extent of drug resistance can be validated against clinical datasets to demonstrate differential patient outcomes. These definitions should be updated through an expert consensus process that explicitly considers the practical challenges of adopting these updated definitions, including variable access of antibiotics across healthcare settings, to ensure that these definitions remain pragmatic and globally applicable.

Although our search captured studies from MEDLINE and EMBASE, we acknowledge that the exclusion of additional databases may have resulted in omissions of relevant studies in the public health, veterinary and One Health space. To account for this, we did a sample size calculation to improve the representativeness of the included studies.

Conclusions

Standardization in defining the extent of drug resistance in bacteria is important for epidemiology reporting, communication across disciplines and policy making. Heterogeneity in how drug-resistant bacteria are defined in the current literature highlights the need for this standardization. Our review summarized the key areas of improvement for the current standards and proposed recommendations. These form an important basis for informing future expert consensus processes to develop a unified and up-to-date standardization of drug-resistant bacteria definitions.
