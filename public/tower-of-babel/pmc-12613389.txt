Pre-lacteal feeding practices and associated factors among mothers of children under 24 months age in Ethiopia: A geo-additive model approach
Authors: Addisu Dessie, Melkamu A Zeru, Dereje Tesfaye Zike, Aweke A Mitku
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPre-lacteal feeding is a significant barrier to exclusive breastfeeding and a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among children under 24 months worldwide. In Ethiopia, it remains a deeply rooted public health concern influenced by socio-demographic, maternal, and geographical factors. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and determinants of pre-lacteal feeding practices among Ethiopian children under 24 months.MethodsThe data used for this study was from the 2019 Ethiopia Mini Demographic and Health Survey, including a weighted sample of 2,051 mothers with children under 24 months. A Bayesian Geo-additive model was applied to determine the risk factors and spatial variations of pre-lacteal feeding practices among Ethiopian zones.ResultsThe prevalence of pre-lacteal feeding was 12.2% in Ethiopia. The potential predictors of pre-lacteal feeding were multiple births [OR = 3.28, 95% CI: (1.35, 7.71)], cesarean delivery [OR = 2.32, 95% CI: (1.42, 3.73) ], starting breastfeeding after one hour [OR = 3.61, 95% CI (2.72, 4.97)], and living in pastoralist [OR = 5.21, 95%CI (2.41, 11.04) ] or urban regions [OR = 2.4595%CI: (1.08, 5.35) ], protective factors included 1–3 antenatal care visits [OR = 0.67, 95%CI: (0.47, 0.96)] and facility-based delivery [OR = 0.61, 95% CI: (0.40, 0.96)] and the continuous variables such as maternal age, age at first birth, child’s age, and birth interval were also significant. Spatial analysis revealed there exist regional disparities with positive and negative influences on pre-lacteal feeding.ConclusionsPre-lacteal feeding was higher and continues to be a significant practice in Ethiopia, shaped by maternal characteristics, sociodemographic factors, and geographic location. Mothers in pastoralist regions are more likely to practice pre-lacteal feeding which needs the implementation of mobile maternal health services. The spatial analysis identified potential hotspots areas where interventions are urgently needed. To reduce pre-lacteal feeding rates, public health efforts should prioritize increasing antenatal care attendance, ensuring timely breastfeeding initiation, and strengthening exclusive breastfeeding education. Additionally, tailored strategies addressing regional disparities must be implemented to enhance child health outcomes across the country.Keywords: Pre-lacteal feeding, Geo-additive model, Non-linear covariate, Spatial effect

Background

Pre-lacteal Feeding (PLF) is giving foods, substances, or drinks except recommended medications other than human milk to newborns before the baby commences breastfeeding in the first few days of its life [1, 2], and these foods or drinks are known as pre-lacteals [3]. These substances include water, honey, formula milk, sugar water, and other liquids. In this study, we used the term PLF to align with the working definitions of the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) used in various countries. Evidence shows that improving breastfeeding practices includes avoiding PLF practice [4]. The PLF practice results in the avoidance of colostrum, depriving the newborn of essential nutrients, opening an opportunity for infections [5]. Children who were exposed to PLF before six months of age were sixteen times more likely to develop diarrhea or pneumonia than the non-exposed [5]. Every day, three to four thousand infants die in the developing world from diarrhea and acute respiratory infections. Even though PLF is not the direct cause of this death, it is one of the contributing causes of death [6]. Mothers are encouraged to avoid using pre-lacteals to protect their babies against infections. PLF is associated with increased severe morbidity, mortality, and other long-term adverse health outcomes [7].

The World Health Organization (WHO) and United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund recommended that after delivery, practices of optimal early breastfeeding should be encouraged [1]. These optimal practices include initiation of breastfeeding (IBF) within 30 min to an hour of birth, giving colostrum, not giving pre-lacteals or post-lacteal, and exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) of babies [1]. This is because those optimal early breastfeeding practices have been proven to provid significantly benefit children’s overall development and survival [8]. From birth to six months of age, children should take only breast milk and no other foods or liquids except recommended medicines [9]. Infants who are EBF have a substantially lower risk of death from infectious diseases like diarrhea and pneumonia. It also supports infants immune systems and protects them later in life from chronic conditions such as obesity and diabetes [5, 10]. Moreover, WHO also recommends that children be put to the breast within the first 3 days postpartum, exclusively breastfed during the first six months of age, and continue to be breastfed for at least two years while receiving adequate and healthy complementary feeding [11].

Worldwide, EBF practices can prevent 1.4 million deaths among children every year; despite this, the prevalence of EBF in many developing countries were low during the first six months of life [12]. The global prevalence of PLF ranges from 12.3% in Timor-Lest to 85.2% in Nigeria [12], where, about 51% of newborns are fed pre-lacteal feeds, which are mainly common in Asia at 59% and in the Middle East with 46.3% [11]. Recent analyses using data from nationally representative surveys in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) estimated that 30% to 50% of children under 24 months of age received pre-lacteal feeds in the first three days of life [13]. The most common pre-lacteal foods given to infants in many low and middle-income countries could be grouped into three: clean water (water only), water-based (rice water, herbal mixture, juice), and milk-based (animal milk, infant formula) [14]. In Africa, most mothers offer pre-lacteal feeds to their newborn 10.8% to 75.2% [15].

In Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the overall prevalence of the PLF was 32.2% and the prevalence was highest in Ivory Coast with 67% and the lowest in Malawi with 2.5% [16]. In the study carried out in eleven East African counties from 2010 to 2018, the pooled prevalence of PLF was 12%, with the highest prevalence of PLF in Comoros with 39% and the lowest in Malawi with 3% [17].

According to the 2016 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) and the 2019 Ethiopia Mini Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS) report, 58% and 59% of Ethiopian infants under six months of age are exclusively breastfed, respectively. This was contrary to the WHO recommendations [18] whre in Ethiopia, the practice of PLF remains high. According to [19], the pooled prevalence of PLF among children in Ethiopia was 26.95% with the highest prevalence in the Afar region with 38.2% and the lowest in the SNNP(Southern Nation Nationality People) region with 13.9%. There is a variation in the prevalence of PLF from place to place in Ethiopia. PLF practice accounts for 46.4% of the Kersa District [20], 36.6% of the Afar region [21], 34.4% of Addis Ababa [7], 25.5% in rural Sidama [22], 25% in North Wollo zone [23], and 12,6% in Jinka town [10]. Many studies have been done on the ‘factors associated with PLF practice in Ethiopia specific areas which do not show the country’s status of PLF practice. The roles of various factors in determining PLF were not well studied in Ethiopia with its spatial statistics analysis. However, as far as our exploration, all previous studies on the risk factors of PLF practice were examined without accounting for possible spatial autocorrelation and non-parametric effects of associated factors. However, the factors affecting PLF practice might have both spatial variability and non-linear relationships with PLF practice. Considering the non-linear continuous covariates in linear form in models makes them no more significant even though they have more significant effects on PLF practice. Also, neglecting the spatial dependence and unobserved heterogeneity among clusters may lead to biased estimates for the remaining effects as well as biased standard estimates. Until now, based on our investigation in Ethiopia, the factors of PLF practice have not been studied with it’s Geo-additive regression analysis. Determine the spatial pattern and risk factors of PLF practice in Ethiopia were our main motivation to do this study.

Data and methods

Study area, data sources and study population

The study was conducted in Ethiopia, a country in SSA located in the Northeastern part of Africa and lies between 30− 140 N longitude and 330 − 480 E latitude [24]. Administratively, Ethiopia is divided into nine geographical regions and two administrative cities. These areas consist of 74 administrative zones, 817 administrative woredas, and 162,530 of the smallest administrative units (kebeles) [2]. The data source used for this study was EMDHS, 2019 data which is the sixth EDHS and the second EMDHS implemented in Ethiopia. The data source for this study was downloaded after registering and stating the purpose of the study from the measure DHS website (http://www.measuredhs.com). Among the record files for this specific research, the researcher used children’s records or Kid’s Records files because this file contains information about women and children. In our study, all mothers of children aged less than 24 months preceding two years of the survey in Ethiopia were included as study subjects. All mothers with children aged less than 24 months who have ever breastfed but are not currently breastfeeding and are still breastfeeding in the selected Enumeration Areas (EAs) are the study population.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

For our study, only mothers who had childbirth in the two years preceding the survey, who did have a live child, and children living with mothers under the age of two years were included. Mothers who did not have a live child and whose child is not residing with mothers were excluded. As a result, as explained, the final weighted sample size was 2,051 mothers who were last-born children in the two years preceding the survey (Fig. 1).

Operational definitions

Pre-lacteal feeding (PLF)

Can be defined as giving any solid or liquid foods other than breast milk to a newborn during the first 3 days postpartum [18, 25].

Pre-lacteal feeds

Foods and/drinks other than breast milk that are given to newborns before breastfeeding initiation in the first 3 days postpartum [3, 22].

Timely/early initiation of breastfeeding (IBF)

Defined as the children born in the last 24 months who were made to breastfeed within 1 h of birth [6].

Exclusive breastfeeding (EBF)

Defined as infants less than six months of age receiving only breast milk and no other liquids or solids introduced, with the exception that the infant can receive medicines, oral rehydration solution, and vitamins/minerals in liquid form) [26].

The response variable of our study is PLF practice among mothers of children aged under 24 months (mothers who had a live birth child under the age of 24 months) within first 3 days postpartum and measured as a dichotomous variable.

In this study, the explanatory variables are selected based on various global and local literature reviews and the availability of data in the EMDHS 2019 datasets are; categorical covariates like marital status, educational level, child’s sex, place of residence, wealth index, religion, media exposure, antenatal care (ANC) visit, family size, place of delivery, mode of delivery, birth type, birth order of child’s, IBF time, postnatal care checkup, counseling on breastfeeding and the continuous covariates including mother’s age at first birth, age of child, current maternal age, preceding birth interval and spatial covariates like zones where the mother resides. Notably, region for administrative purposes, Ethiopia is demarcated into eleven regions; these were categorized into three contextual regions based on the living status of their population: agrarian, urban, and pastoralist regions. Tigray, Amhara, Oromia, Southern Nations (SNNP), Gambela, and Benshangul Gumuz were recorded as agricultural regions. Addis Ababa, Dire Dawa, and Harari city administrations were grouped to form the urban regions, and the Somali and Afar regions were grouped to form the pastoralist regions [25]. Mass media exposure was created by combining whether a respondent’s household has a newspaper, radio, or television, and it is coded as yes if a woman has at least one of these three media outlets and no if she does not. Information about all these explanatory variables was collected and included systematically in the EMDHS 2019 data [18].

Local Indicators of Spatial Autocorrelation (LISA), hot spot analysis, and interpolation maps were included as descriptive analysis to visualize spatial association and the inferential statistics in our study, were based on a geo-additive regression analysis model.

This choice is usually motivated by smoothness properties, which the regression function can be assumed to possess. The first model (model 1) is a Bayesian GLM incorporating fixed effects of categorical covariates and continuous covaraites. To investigate the appropriateness of the non-linear effects of continuous covariates and, the inclusion of the geographic locale was estimated by assuming a linear effect for continuous covariates and ignoring the regional-specific spatial effects. The second model (Model 2) is a Bayesian Semi-parametric model which accounts for fixed effects of categorical covariates and assumes non-linear effects of continuous covariates of an individual, but also it does not include region-specific effects (spatial effects). The third model (model 3) is a Bayesian Geo-additive model that accounts for the effects of categorical covariates and non-linear effects of continuous covariates and captures spatial heterogeneity for unobserved influential factors.

Bayesian methods can be very useful for the inference of Geo-additive models. Another interest of this approach is that it can be used to analyze the consequences of parameter uncertainty on model predictions. The summary typically includes mean, standard deviation (SD), and quantile of sampled linear effects, smooth terms variances and random effects variances, as well as goodness of fit criteria and other information about the model [32]. In a Bayesian inference/approach, all the unknown functions, , and the fixed effects parameters as well as all variance components are considered random variables and, therefore, have to be supplemented with appropriate priors assumptions.

The Gaussian Bayesian approach allows for simultaneous estimating of the spatial effects of the geographical locations, the unknown non-linear effect of the continuous covariates, and the vectors of the fixed effect parameters. If there is no prior knowledge of the parameter vector of fixed effects, the diffuse priors (uninformative prior) which is given as p ()) is the appropriate choice for the fixed effect parameter. For the smooth functionsof the continuous covariates, a second-order random walk was considered to ensure flexibility. In this study we incorporate both sturactured and unstractured spatial effect; where; structured spatial effects capture variation that is spatially correlated and creates a smooth surface across a region, often modeled using latitude and longitude coordinates and a Markov random field (MRF) whereas unstructured spatial effects, on the other hand, account for random, non-spatial variation which is unique to each discrete geographic area and does not assume a smooth relationship between neighboring zones. For spatially structured effects, we choose Markov random field (MRF) priors whre, the MRF prior allows a binary adjacency structure such that areas that share boundaries, which we assume to have similar patterns, are assigned a weight of 1, otherwise zero. Furthermore, for spatially unstructured effects, as the common assumptions of Gaussian, are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) was used. For this study, we used the standard default prior distribution for parameters estimation [32].

Results

In this study, a total of 2,051 weight samples of mothers children aged under 24 months from 305 clusters were included. Among those mothers about 250(12.2%) were practicing PLF during the first 3 days postpartum and 1801 (87.8%) mothers did not practice PLF for children under 24 months. The result of the study confim that the majority of respondent mothers 2012(98.1%) had single births. Of those single births, 240 (94.3%) mothers gave pre-lacteal feeds to their babies. Among the study subjects, 739 (36.0%) were orthodox, and the highest percentage of PLF practice were Muslim religion account 109(43.4%) of the respondant. The result of the study also shows that the majority of respondents (88.5%), live in the agrarian regions; among them, 70.3% had PLF practice. Similarly, 1,770 (86.3%) respondents had 2 or fewer under-five-age children in households; among them, 198 (80.5%) had practiced PLF. Moreover, more than half of the respondents 1116 (54.4%) gave birth in a health facility place of delivery. However, the highest percentage of PLF practice 130 (52.0%) was observed among mothers who delivered at home.

Furthermore, the majority of the respondents, 1,919 (93.5%), had given birth under normal delivery; among them, 212 (84.5%) gave pre-lacteal feeds to their babies. The study also depicted that among all mothers included in this study, about third-fourth 1,552(75.6%) of the respondents had timely IBF; among them, 50.2% experienced PLF practice. The result of the study presented that 918 (44.8%) respondents had four or more ANC visits among them 113 (45.2%) mothers were undergoing PLF practice. Around half of the respondents 951 (46.3%) children had second to fourth born, of them 109 (44.1%) had received pre-lacteal feeds.

The mean age of the child is 11.75 months (± 6.83), indicating that the average age of children in the sample population who had practiced PLF is around 12 months, with individual ages varying by approximately 7 months. The mean age of mothers at first birth is 19.72 years (± 4.66), indicating that mothers had PLF practice at first birth on average at around 20 years of age, with individual ages varying by approximately 5 years. Furthermore, the mean maternal age is 27.89 years (± 6.20), indicating that the average current age of mothers in the sample population who had their PLF practice is around 28 years, with individual ages varying by approximately 6 years. Lastly, the mean birth interval is 45.52 months (± 27.37), suggesting that the average time between consecutive births for mothers in the sample population who had their PLF practice was approximately 46 months, with actual intervals varying by approximately 27 months.

The Chi-square test of association result indicates that PLF practice was significantly associated with categorical predictor variables such as type of birth, religion, baby postnatal checkup, region, number of under-five children, place of delivery, mode of delivery, wealth index, antenatal care visit, and breastfeeding initiation time at a 5% level of significance and the independent sample t-test indicates that strongly association with continuous variables: maternal age at first birth and preceding birth interval (Table 1).

The selection of the most preferred model is based on the DIC, and the model with the smallest value of DIC is the preferred model. From the result of Table 3, we have seen that the DIC value for model 3 is small as compared to model 1 and model 2. This shows that the DIC value decreases as model complexity increases from a simple model to a more sophisticated model. Therefore, the Bayesian Geo-additive regression model, which includes the fixed effect of categorical covariates, the non-linear effect of the continuous covariates, and the spatial effects, is a good fit for the data in terms of DIC, as compared to Model 1 and Model 2. Therefore, this study’s interpretations and discussions are based on the Geo-additive regression model (Table 2).

Discussion

According to the result of our study obtained from EMDHS 2019 data, the prevalence of PLF practice was 12.2% which is slightly higher than the study prevalence found in the EDHS 2016 databased which was 7.9% [7]. This increasing prevalence over time indicates the continuation of PLF practices among a substantial number of mothers in the country, and this disparity might be due to an increase in caesarian section delivery. However, this result was lower than that of the other study findings conducted in Ghana which was 21% [35], Sudan 28.3% [36], Bangladesh 29% [3], Turkey 35.5% [37], Uganda 40.3% [38], and in Pakistan 64.5% [39] and in other local studies conducted in some Ethiopian districts which was 15.7% [40]. Such variations in PLF practices among different countries and districts possibly reflect the country-specific socio-cultural norms and beliefs, the maternal and child healthcare services availability and utilization, and the level of education and knowledge regarding the importance of breastfeeding practices and knowledge on risk of PLF practice. Our study finding is also in line with other studies conducted in Ethiopian districts, including 10.1% in Axum town [14], 14.2% in Mettu and Debre Berhan districts with similar prevalence in both districts [41, 42] while 11.85% in east Africa [43]. Futhermore, similar study was conducted in Ethiopia by [25] used a multilevel logistic model on a nearly identical dataset and predictors. While this work effectively addressed the hierarchical nature of the data, it did not explicitly account for non-linear relationships of continuous covariates or spatial autocorrelation. Our Bayesian Geo-additive model is a significant advancement because it overcomes these limitations. By incorporating both structured spatial effects and flexible non-linear effects, our approach provides a more comprehensive and realistic understanding of the determinants of pre-lacteal feeding. This allows for the identification of a more nuanced association between continuous factors like maternal age and the outcome, and it enables the explicit modeling of regional hotspots and cold spots that are otherwise unobserved. Therefore, our findings offer additional insights that strengthen the evidence base for targeted interventions.

Thus, we found that mothers who attended 1–3 ANC visits were 23.2% less likely to practice PLF than those who did not participate in ANC visits. This finding is consistent with other studies conducted in Southwest Nigeria [44] and Mettu districts of Ethiopia [41]. The possible explanations is tha; the nomadic lifestyle and lack of permanent infrastructure make it difficult for mothers in these areas to receive proper ANC and deliver at a health facility. This physical and logistical barrier directly contributes to the higher PLF rates while mother’s awerness on benefits of EBF and the danger of giving pre-lacteal foods or liquids to the newborn during ANC developed awareness regarding the harmful effects of PLF, along with the significance of early IBF in newborns. Moreover; pastoralist communities often lack access to permanent health facilities, making it difficult for mothers to attend regular antenatal care (ANC) visits or to have an institutional delivery. This physical and economic inaccessibility means they are less likely to receive professional advice and counseling on the importance of exclusive breastfeeding and the risks of PLF. Mother with multiple births was also associated with a higher PLF practice. This is supported by studies in East Africa [43] and Ethiopia [25]. This might be because the mother with multiple births perceives their breast milk as insufficient and is more likely to practice PLF. In addition, mothers who have multiple births usually undergo cesarean section. Consequently, long postoperative care delays skin-to-skin mother-to-baby contact, and this separation of mother-to-baby due to factors attributed to cesarean section results in delayed IBF.

Our study revealed significant regional disparities in the practice of PLF, with mothers in pastoralist regions being 5.21 times more likely to practice PLF compared to their counterparts in agrarian regions [95% CI: (2.41, 11.04)]. This high prevalence of pre-lacteal feeding in pastoralist regions provides strong quantitative evidence for the influence of spatial factor on infant feeding practices and can be explained by traditional cultural beliefs/norms, limited access to formal healthcare services, and the unique mobility patterns of the communities. The elevated odds of PLF in pastoralist areas are likely a reflection of limited access to formal healthcare.

Moreover, our results also show that institutional delivery is a significant protective factor against PLF [OR = 0.61, CI: (0.40, 0.97)]. However, the nomadic and dispersed nature of pastoralist life makes consistent access to health facilities and professional counseling difficult. This is further compounded by deeply rooted socio-cultural norms that may not align with modern breastfeeding recommendations, such as beliefs about discarding colostrum or the use of traditional remedies. The combination of these factors creates a challenging environment for behavior change, highlighting the need for targeted, mobile, and culturally sensitive interventions that can overcome the logistical and cultural barriers inherent for those regions. This finding is consistent with the study findings which were conducted in Ethiopia [25] and [7] and in Bangladesh which reported regional differences as a significant factor in initiating pre-lacteal feeds among children and pointed out the disparities in access and distribution to resources, information, healthcare services, and geography as the contributing factors behind such regional variations of PLF [3].

In contrast, the higher odds of PLF in urban areas [OR = 2.44, CI: (1.09, 5.35)] may be attributed to different factors, such as higher rates of Caesarean section births (OR = 2.32, CI: [1.42, 3.74] and multiple births (OR = 3.28, CI: [1.35, 7.71] which are also more prevalent in urban settings and are strongly associated with delayed breastfeeding initiation. Similarly, mothers who had their childbirth at a health facility were 39.12% less likely to practice PLF than those having delivery at home. This is consistent with the findings of a study conducted in Bangladesh [3], Kersa district of Ethiopia [20], the national level study of Ethiopia [7], and East Africa [45]. However, it is lower than the findings of other studies conducted in Mettu district of Ethiopia [41], Bure district of Ethiopia [46], and Jinka town in Ethiopia [10]. One possible reason behind this could be that giving birth at home creates a favorable environment for local community members, who may advise using pre-lacteal feed. Moreover, home deliveries are often assisted by family members and friends whose misconceptions or advice regarding breastfeeding are based on personal experiences. On the other hand, the possible explanation is that institutional delivery received professional advice and counseling from their healthcare providers regarding EBF practices. Besides, a mother who delivered by cesarean section was 56.7% more likely to provide PLF as compared to those who had a vaginal delivery. This finding is similar to the findings of studies conducted in Pakistan [47], and East Africa [45], and with different studies in Ehiopia like Dire Dawa [40], Aksum [14], and Mettu [41]. However, it is lower than the study finding conducted in Nigeria [44]. In such cases, the possible explanation might be the IBF for pregnant women who are delivered via cesarean section will be delayed until they become stable and transferred to the postpartum ward. As a result of this interference and delay, the mother may be compelled to initiate feedings other types of food/liquid rather than breast milk.

According to the current study, breastfeeding was not initiated in a timely manner, and it was found to have a significant association with PLF practices among our participants. Mothers who did not timely initiate breastfeeding were 4.4 times more likely to practice PLF than those mothers who timely initiate breastfeeding. This finding is similar to the findings of studies conducted in Nigeria [44], Pakistan [39], and other different studies in Ethiopia [7], Bure distric in Ethiopia [46], and Dire Dawa [40]. This might be due to a lack of awareness about the correct time to initiate breastfeeding after delivery since a quarter of the mothers (24.9%) included in the study did not utilize ANC services at all. This may be justified, as the time between birth and IBF increases, there would be more room for PLF practice. Furthermore, a study conducted in Kersa district in Ethiopia [20] found that timely IBF reduces PLF practice by nearly 11 times. Thus, it indicates that there is a close relationship between timely IBF and avoiding PLF.

This study finding is also reaveald that, as tThe age of the mother at first birth increases, and their PLF practice rate is decreases, which implies or the older the mother is at first birth, is the lower the chances of having a PLF practice. This result has the same idea as the previous study done by [47] which comfim that, as the age of the child increases, the number of mothers who give pre-lacteal feeds to their babies decreases. This result was consistent with the previous study finding of [48]. PLF was almost decreased among mothers who gave birth interval was increases. This result is supported by the same idea as the previous study done by [14] and short inter-pregnancy intervals are associated with a higher risk of cesarean section and an advanced threat of low birth weight. During that time, the neonate may be admitted to an intensive care unit which may hamper the EBF and lead to practice PLF. Moreover, as maternal age increases, the practice of PLF also increases which is consistent with the finding of the study [36, 40, 49].

Conclusion

Our study reveals a higher prevalence of PLF (12.2%) than previously reported, with key risk factors including ANC visits, birth type, residence, delivery mode, and maternal age. Bayesian Geo-additive models effectively captured spatial and non-linear effects, highlighting regional disparities. Our findings suggest that improving ANC attendance and facility deliveries may be associated with reduced PLF, but intervention efficacy requires experimental validation. The study also covered substantial geographical variations in PLF practices across Ethiopian zones, with both positive and negative spatial effects influencing feeding behaviors. Notably, younger infants and mothers with earlier first births were at higher risk, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions during critical early-life stages.

Inaddition, mothers in pastoralist regions are 5.21 times more likely to practice pre-lacteal feeding that needs the implementation of mobile maternal health services. These services can overcome the barriers of distance and mobility inherent in the pastoralist lifestyle, providing consistent antenatal and postnatal care where it is most needed. Furthermore, the findings on institutional delivery and Caesarean sections suggest the need for standardized hospital protocols that prioritize immediate breastfeeding initiation, particularly for mothers who have undergone surgery or given birth to multiples which helps to prevent the delays that often lead to pre-lacteal feeding. These findings call for urgent action by policymakers, healthcare providers, and public health stakeholders to strengthen maternal and child health programs. Enhancing ANC attendance, promoting institutional deliveries, and implementing region-specific strategies to discourage PLF are crucial steps toward improving exclusive breastfeeding rates. Addressing these factors through integrated, context-sensitive approaches will contribute to better infant feeding practices and overall child health outcomes in Ethiopia. Furthermore, the policy recommendations should focus on a multi-pronged approach that combines targeted mobile services for pastoralist communities, improved hospital practices, and community-led education to effectively reduce the prevalence of pre-lacteal feeding. Moreover, future research studies could benefit from using more fine-grained spatial data like at the district or village level to provide a more precise understanding of the determinants and to inform highly localized interventions.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations, including its reliance on cross-sectional EDHS data, which restricts causal inference and may introduce recall bias due to self-reported PLF practices. While our spatial analysis identified zonal patterns, it cannot capture community-level variations within zones. The cross-sectional design also precludes causal inference between identified factors and PLF practices. Unmeasured confounders, such as cultural norms and maternal knowledge, were not accounted for, potentially affecting the model accuracy. The spatial analysis, though insightful, was limited to zonal-level data variations which might not capture the full extent of localized variations in pre-lacteal feeding practices.Additionally, the use of EMDHS 2019 data may not reflect recent healthcare advancements or socioeconomic changes. While Bayesian geo-additive models provided robust analytical flexibility, their complexity may hinder practical interpretation for policymakers.
