NLRC5 restricts Japanese encephalitis virus replication and neuroinflammation by interacting with the viral NS3 protein in an IFN-γ-dependent manner
Authors: Prasanjit Jena, Rohit Soni, Abhigya Chhetri, Vinay Sharma, Vengadesan Krishnan, Thomas A. Kufer, Arup Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNLRC5 is a known activator of MHC class I genes and a regulator of type I interferon activity. Although its expression increases in brains infected with Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), its specific contribution to JEV pathogenesis and neuroinflammation remains unclear.MethodsWe utilised in vitro cell cultures, in silico modelling, and in vivo mouse knockdown models of NLRC5 to investigate its impact on JEV replication, antiviral immune responses, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, and overall disease progression.ResultsJEV infection upregulated NLRC5 expression in the brain, microglia, and non-neuronal cells, but not in neuronal cells. However, NLRC5 expression could be induced in neuroblastoma cells via IFN-γ treatment. Experimental modulation demonstrated that the typical antiviral restriction of JEV replication by IFN-γ is lost in NLRC5-deficient cells. Evaluation of stable cell lines expressing different NLRC5 variants showed that cytoplasmic retention and NLRC5 leucine-rich repeats (LRRs) are essential for viral restriction. Mechanistically, in silico modelling, co-immunoprecipitation, and immunofluorescence confirmed that NLRC5 binds directly to the viral NS3 protein in an LRR-dependent manner. In mouse models, in vivo knockdown of NLRC5 in the brain significantly accelerated clinical disease progression, heightened mortality, and triggered uncontrolled viral replication, indicated by elevated viral titers and NS3/NS1 expression. This viral surge induced severe neuroinflammation, as evidenced by significantly elevated levels of ASC and cleaved caspase-3 in NLRC5-deficient brains.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that NLRC5 functions as a cell-type-specific antiviral regulator during JEV infection. It suppresses viral replication by directly binding to and degrading the viral NS3 protein. Loss of NLRC5 impairs IFN-γ-mediated antiviral restriction, leading to enhanced viral replication, severe neuroinflammation, and accelerated disease progression in vivo.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-04001-z.

Introduction

RNA viral infections, particularly emerging and re-emerging strains, present a major challenge to global health and necessitate the development of novel therapeutic approaches. Among these, mosquito-borne flaviviruses, such as Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), are becoming more prevalent as global temperatures rise [1].

JEV is a primary cause of viral encephalitis across Asia, often resulting in high mortality or permanent neurological damage in survivors [2]. While the innate immune system is crucial for fighting the infection, an overactive immune response can trigger severe neuroinflammation, which may ultimately lead to neuronal destruction and death [3]. Immune overactivation may be triggered by recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) by cytoplasmic pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) expressed on brain immune cells.

One important class of PPR proteins are the Nucleotide oligomerisation domain (NOD)-like receptors (NLRs), which trigger inflammatory responses to eliminate invasive pathogens. Within this family, NLRC5 is the largest member, and its roles in diverse biological processes, such as angiogenesis, apoptosis, neuroinflammation, and immune regulation, have been described [4]. While the currently best understood function of NLRC5 is the regulation of MHC class I gene expression [5]

NLRC5 expression has also been documented across diverse brain cell populations, including neurons, glia, and vascular cells that comprise the blood–brain barrier [6–8]. Recent reports suggest that neurotropic viruses such as Zika and WNV can induce NLRC5 expression in the central nervous system [9, 10], but the roles of NLRC5 in antiviral responses remain poorly defined. NLRC5 has been shown to restrict Dengue (DENV) and Zika (ZIKV) replication by interacting with viral proteins and targeting them for ubiquitin-dependent selective autophagy [11, 12]. Mechanistically, some studies have shown that NLRC5 can enhance type I interferon antiviral responses, whereas others have reported a negative role for NLRC5 in these responses [13].

In this study, we addressed the multifaceted roles of NLRC5 during JEV infection, including its contributions to JEV replication, antiviral responses, JEV-mediated CNS inflammation, and pathogenesis. To this end, we employed in vitro and mouse models to assess how modulation of NLRC5 affects viral replication and disease progression.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The Animal Ethics Committee of the Regional Centre for Biotechnology (UNESCO) (Reference number RCB/IAEC/2025/220) approved all animal procedures that were used in the study. All mice were housed under a 12:12 h light/dark photocycle at a consistent temperature (~18–23 °C), with food and water provided ad libitum, at the Experimental Animal Facility (EAF) of the NCR-Biotech Science Cluster, Faridabad. All animal experiments were carried out in accordance with the criteria of the Committee for the Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals. The experimental plans were also approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee (Reference no. RCB/IBSC/23–24/506). We performed all in vitro and animal experiments under BSL-2 containment.

Virus propagation

JEV strain P20778 (JEV-S3) was used in all experiments and propagated in the cerebrum of 3-day-old suckling mice (104 PFU/mL) as previously described [14]. Brains harvested on day 3 post-inoculation were homogenised in MEM (HiMedia) to prepare 40% (wt/vol) suspensions, then centrifuged to remove debris. The resulting suspensions were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C.

Knocking down NLRC5 in C57BL/6 mice brains

The NLRC5 Vivo-Morpholino was obtained from Gene Tools (USA), and NLRC5 knockdown in the mouse brain was performed as previously described [15]. C57BL/6 wild-type mice of either sex were randomly assigned to five groups: PBS, NLRC5 V-MO, JEV, Standard V-MO + JEV and NLRC5 V-MO + JEV. NLRC5 V-MO group mice received a single intracranial injection of NLRC5 Vivo-Morpholino (5'-TCAGTCGGAAGCTCTCAGCGTCCAT-3') at 12.5 mg/kg, while the Standard V-MO + JEV group received an equivalent dose of Standard Vivo-Morpholino (5'-CCTCTTACCTCAGTTACAATTTATA-3'). After 48 h, the JEV and combination groups were intraperitoneally infected with 1 × 107 plaque-forming units (PFU/mL) of JEV, whereas the control group received an equal volume of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Stable cell lines

HeLa stable cell lines expressing GFP-NLRC5 or GFP-NLRC5 mutants, inducible by doxycycline, were kindly provided by Prof. Thomas Kufer for this project. His lab generated these cell lines by co-transfection of pOG44 and pcDNA5/FRT/TO-GFP-NLRC5, pcDNA5/FRT/TO-GFP-NLRC5 NLS I, pcDNA5/FRT/TO-GFP-NLRC5 2xNLS or pcDNA/FRT/TO-GFP-NLRC5 Iso3 into HeLa Flp-In T-REx cells as previously described [16].

Co-immunoprecipitation

Co-immunoprecipitation of GFP, GFP-NLRC5 WT, GFP-NLRC5 NLS I, GFP-NLRC5 Iso3, and GFP-NLRC5 2xNLS from HeLa stable cell lines was performed with GFP-Trap Agarose resin (gtak, Chromotek). For cytoplasmic proteins like GFP-NLRC5 WT and GFP-NLRC5 NLS I, we resuspended the cell pellet in 200 μL ice-cold Lysis buffer by pipetting up and down. Supplemented the lysis buffer with protease inhibitor cocktail and 1 mM PMSF. Since GFP-NLRC5 Iso3 and GFP-NLRC5 2xNLS are nuclear proteins, we resuspended the cell pellet in 200 μL ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with DNase I (75–150 Kunitz U/mL), MgCl2 (2.5 mM), protease inhibitor cocktail and PMSF (1 mM). The follow-up steps were performed as per the manufacturer's instructions.

Overexpression and siRNA-mediated silencing study

Cells were transfected with plasmids expressing FLAG-NLRC5 (37,521, Addgene) or an empty vector (FLAG-EV) (concentration 2.5 μg) for 48 h using Lipofectamine 2000 (11,668,019, Invitrogen), for overexpression of NLRC5 and ON-TARGETplus Human NLRC5 (84,166) siRNA—SMARTpool (L-018267–02–0020, Dharmacon) or nontargeting siRNAs (NT-siRNAs) (D-001810–10–20, Dharmacon) (concentration 20 nM) for 48 h using DharmaFECT 1 transfection reagent for knocking down as per the manufacturer’s conditions. The overexpression and knockdown efficiencies were monitored by immunoblot.

Proteasomal inhibition (MG132) and cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay

Stable HeLa cells were plated on a 60 mm dish and were treated with doxycycline for 24 h. Then, the cells were mock- or JEV-infected (MOI 1). At 24 hpi, the cells were treated with MG132 (10 µM) and harvested at different time points (0, 1, 4, 8 and 12 h). The samples were washed with ice-cold PBS and processed for cell lysis. The rescue of the NS3 protein was measured by immunoblot. To assess NS3 Protein stability and degradation Kinetics, a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay was performed. Doxycycline-pre-treated HeLa cells were infected with JEV (MOI 1) for 24 h and subsequently co-treated with CHX (100 µg/mL) and MG132 (10 µM). Cells were collected at designated time intervals (0, 1, 4, 8, and 12 h post-treatment), washed with ice-cold PBS, and processed for immunoblotting.

Immunoblot

Mouse tissue samples were minced and homogenized in cell lysis buffer. In contrast, cultured cell samples were lysed by repeated vortexing with cell lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 1 mM PMSF, and a protease inhibitor cocktail) for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatant was used for estimating protein concentration using a BCA (786–570, G-Biosciences) assay kit. Cell lysates were mixed with 4X Laemmli buffer (40% glycerol, 20% β-mercaptoethanol, 0.04% bromophenol blue, 6% SDS, 0.25 M Tris–HCl, pH 6.8) and boiled at 95 °C for 10 min to denature the proteins. Equal concentrations of cell lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes for immunoblotting. The blots were visualised using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP™ imaging system. Densitometric analysis of the blot was performed using ImageJ to determine signal intensity. Quantitative analysis was performed from multiple independent experiments as indicated in the figure legends. RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells and brain tissue samples using a TRIzol phase separation method. cDNA was prepared using ImProm-II™ Reverse Transcription system kit and analyzed in triplicate by the real-time fluorescence detection method with SYBR green DNA binding fluorescent dye (SYBR Premix Ex Taq; TaKaRa, Otsu, Shiga, Japan) on a QuantStudio 6 flex real-time PCR (Applied Biosystems) detection system. Relative expression was calculated using the threshold cycle (ΔCT) method, with uninfected cells as the reference, and GAPDH as the internal control. Immunofluorescence assay

HeLa and SH-SY5Y cells were seeded onto a coverslip in a 24-well plate. Cells were treated with IFN-γ at 40 ng/mL for 6 h. Then, cells were infected with JEV at an MOI of 5 for 1 h, followed by incubation at 37 °C. After 24 h, cells were harvested and fixed with with 4% paraformaldehyde (158,127-5G, Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min at room temperature. NLRC5 was labelled with an anti-NLRC5 rabbit monoclonal antibody (dilution 1:50; 72379S, CST), and the viral envelope protein was detected with an anti-Japanese encephalitis virus E glycoprotein antibody (dilution 1:50; ab41671, Abcam). Stable HeLa cell lines inducible for GFP-NLRC5 WT, GFP-NLRC5 NLS I, GFP-NLRC5 2xNLS, and GFP-NLRC5 Iso3 were seeded on a coverslip in a 24-well plate. Target gene expression was induced by 1 μg/mL doxycycline for at least 24 h before JEV infection at an MOI of 5 for 1 h. After 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (158,127-5G, Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min at room temperature, and viral protein was detected using a rabbit polyclonal antibody against the JEV NS3 protein (dilution 1:1500; GTX125868, GeneTex). HeLa cells stably expressing GFP-NLRC5-NLS I were pre-treated with doxycycline for 24 h to induce protein expression, followed by infection with JEV at an MOI of 5. At 24 h post-infection (hpi), cells were washed with PBS, fixed, and permeabilized. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies: Calreticulin (D3E6) Rabbit Monoclonal Antibody (dilution 1:200,12,238, CST), Anti-Japanese encephalitis virus NS1 glycoprotein antibody (dilution 1:50, ab41651, Abcam) for 3 h at room temperature (RT). Cells were washed 3 times with PBS, and then incubated with secondary antibodies: Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor Plus 488(dilution 1: 800; A32731TR, Invitrogen), Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 568 (dilution 1: 800, A10042, Invitrogen), Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 594 (dilution 1: 800, A-11005, Invitrogen), Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L), Alexa Fluor 568 (dilution 1: 1000, 11,004, Invitrogen), Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 647(dilution 1: 1000, A31573, Invitrogen) at RT temperature for 1 h, following multiple wash with 1xPBS. 4’,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (D1306, Invitrogen) was used to stain nuclear DNA. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (SP8 Laser Confocal Microscope; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) with a 63 × oil-immersion objective and analyzed using LAS X Life Sciences Microscope software.

Virus titration by plaque-forming assay

Vero cells, sourced from the National Centre for Cell Science (Pune, India), were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ in minimal Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and glutamine. For JEV plaque assays, 70–80% confluent cells in serum-free Eagle’s minimal essential medium were incubated with tenfold serially diluted virus stocks, brain lysates, or cell supernatants for 1 h at 37 °C with gentle rocking. Post-incubation, cells were washed with 1 × PBS and overlaid with 2 mL of a 1:1 mixture of 2 × MEM and 2% low-melting agarose type VII (A0701, Sigma-Aldrich). After 5 days of incubation, cells were fixed in 3.7% formaldehyde (252,549, Sigma-Aldrich). The overlay plugs were then removed, and plaques were visualized by staining with 1% crystal violet (C6158, Sigma-Aldrich) for counting. Viral titers were calculated as PFU/mL = (average PFU/infection volume in mL) × dilution factor.

Blood–brain barrier disruption study

The Evans Blue extravasation assay was performed to assess changes in BBB permeability. C57BL/6 mice (3–4 weeks old) were injected intraperitoneally (IP) with 200 μL 1% Evans Blue dye (E2129; Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) prepared in sterile PBS. After one hour of dye incubation, the mice were euthanised by carbon dioxide inhalation (30–70%) in consultation with a veterinarian, and the image was captured.

Clinical scoring

Mice injected with a lethal dose (1 × 107 PFU/mL) of JEV were monitored daily for body weight and the following signs. The scores given for the different clinical symptoms were as follows: 0, no clinical symptoms; 1, piloerection; 2, body stiffening, piloerection; 3, body stiffening, piloerection, restriction of movement; 4, body stiffening, piloerection, limitation of movement, hind limb paralysis; 5, body stiffening, piloerection, restriction of movement, hind limb paralysis, whole-body tremor.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were given 4% paraformaldehyde (158,127-5G, Sigma-Aldrich) after anaesthesia with ketamine/xylazine, and were transcardially perfused with cold PBS. Immunohistochemistry of brain samples was performed as described previously [14]. Briefly, 4% paraformaldehyde fixed brains were placed in 30% sucrose until submerged. Following a PBS wash, the brains were embedded in OCT (SHH0026-6X120ML, Sigma-Aldrich) and kept at −80 °C for future use. Using a cryostat (Microm HM550; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Runcorn, UK), brain Sects. (12 μm) were cut, and immunohistochemistry was performed using primary antibodies against the antigens. The primary antibodies used were a mouse monoclonal antibody against the JEV NS1 protein (dilution 1:50; ab41651, Abcam), Anti-NeuN Antibody (dilution 1:200; ABN78 Merck Millipore), TMEM 119 Polyclonal Antibody (dilution 1:200; 27,585–1-AP, Proteintech), NLRCR5 (B-10) mouse monoclonal antibody (dilution 1:50; sc-515668, Santa cruz), Caspase-3 Antibody (31A1067) (dilution 1: 20; NB100-56708, Novus Biologicals), Cleaved Caspase 3 (Asp175) antibody(dilution 1: 200; 9661, CST) and an anti-NLRC5 rabbit monoclonal antibody (dilution 1:50; 72379S, CST). Secondary antibodies used were Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor Plus 488(dilution 1: 800; A32731TR, Invitrogen), Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 594(dilution 1: 500; Invitrogen, A-11005) used. DAPI (D1306, Invitrogen) was used to stain the nuclear DNA. Images were acquired at 63 × magnification using a confocal microscope (SP8 Laser Confocal Microscope, Leica) and analyzed with LAS X Life Sciences Microscope software (Leica).

In silico structural and docking analysis

The amino acid sequences of DENV NS3 (UniProt ID: P29990), JEV NS3 (UniProt ID: P27395), and human NLRC5 (UniProt ID: Q86WI3) were obtained from the UniProt database. Full-length structural models were retrieved from the AlphaFold3 protein server [17] because their individual full-length experimental structures were not available in the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Structural superposition of the protease and helicase domains of DENV and JEV NS3 was performed using the MUSTANG algorithm [18], and structure-based sequence alignments were visualized using ESPript 3.0 [19].

For NS3-NLRC5 docking and interaction analysis, the AlphaFold 3 server was initially used to identify interacting regions, which were then fed into HADDOCK 2.4 [20] for the final docking and analysis. The best model, ranked by cluster size, score, energy, and buried surface area, was selected for further analysis and figure preparation using PyMOL (Schrödinger Inc., New York, NY, USA). Protein interactions and interfaces were analyzed using the PISA server [21].

ELISA

Brain lysates from mock- and infected-mice were used to detect IFN-γ release according to the manufacturer’s guidelines (E-EL-M0048, Elabscience). 30 μg of equivalent protein concentration was diluted in assay buffer, added to the ELISA plate, and observed at 560 nm (SpectraMax i3x Multi-mode Detection Platform). Values were calculated by subtracting the blank value and quantified against the standard.

Data quantification and statistical analysis

Data were analysed by unpaired t-test or Kruskal–Wallis test using PRISM, version 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software Inc.). Values are expressed as the mean ± SD. Given that JEV targets multiple cell types, including microglia and neurons, we sought to identify the specific cellular sources of NLRC5 induction. To determine the cellular expression pattern and spatial distribution of NLRC5 in the central nervous system during viral encephalopathy, 3- to 4-week-old C57BL/6 mice were intraperitoneally challenged with 107 PFU/mL JEV or vehicle control (PBS). Brain tissues were harvested at the onset of clinical symptoms (day 6) and subjected to immunofluorescence co-staining for NLRC5 alongside cell type-specific markers (Fig. 1D). In vehicle-treated (PBS) mice, baseline expression of NLRC5 was undetectable across brain sections. Following JEV infection, NLRC5 expression was markedly upregulated throughout the brain parenchyma (Fig. 1E). Co-immunostaining with the microglia-specific marker TMEM119 demonstrated strong spatial overlap and colocalization with the NLRC5 signal (Fig. 1F). In contrast, co-staining with the neuronal marker NeuN revealed minimal NLRC5 expression within neuronal cell bodies of JEV-infected mice (Fig. 1G).

To determine if JEV-induced NLRC5 expression is broadly characteristic of non-neuronal cells, we evaluated two additional cell lines: human HeLa and mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs). JEV infection successfully upregulated NLRC5 expression in both cell lines at 24 hpi (Fig. 2F, G).

Differential expression of NLRC5 across microglial, neuronal, and non-neuronal cell types may influence susceptibility to viral infection and replication. To investigate this, we quantified viral RNA and viral titers across these cell populations. As illustrated (Fig. 2H, I), microglial and HeLa cells exhibited significantly lower viral RNA levels and infectious particle counts compared to neuronal cells. We observed at least a 2-log increase in viral load in neuronal cells compared to other cell lines, confirming that JEV replicates much more efficiently in these cells.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the upregulation of NLRC5 in the central nervous system following JEV infection is selectively driven by microglia, whereas neurons remain largely refractory to NLRC5 induction.

NLRC5 retention in the cytoplasm is necessary for the interaction with the JEV NS3 protein

NLRC5 is characterized by a tripartite structural organization comprising an N-terminal atypical caspase activation and recruitment domain (uCARD), a central nucleotide-binding and oligomerization domain (NACHT/NOD), and a C-terminal leucine-rich repeat (LRR) region, distinguishing it as the largest member of the NLR protein family. NLRC5 is predominantly cytosolic but can translocate to the nucleus [27]. Given that NLRC5 overexpression significantly attenuates viral protein synthesis, we investigated the specific domains and subcellular localization that govern this inhibitory effect, as well as the underlying molecular mechanisms.

We utilized stable HeLa cell lines expressing full-length NLRC5 and variants thereof (Fig. 6A). NLRC5 undergoes dynamic nucleocytoplasmic shuttling, regulated by a nuclear localization signal (NLS) within its CARD region. In contrast, nuclear export occurs via a CRM1-dependent pathway for which the LRR region is necessary [28]. NLRC5 physically interact with JEV NS3 and induces proteasomal degradation

To understand the mechanism how NLRC5 counteracts viral replication, we undertook computational analysis to determine whether the viral protein NS3 can interact with NLRC5. Due to the lack of a crystal structure, we used AlphaFold to predict the structure. We chose the dengue NS3 protein to build a model because it has previously been shown to interact with NLRC5 [11]. For interaction analysis, we generated AlphaFold predictions of full-length JEV NS3, DENV NS3, and NLRC5 to identify the domain features (Fig. 9A-C) [17]. The JEV NS3 structure consists of two domains: an N-terminal protease domain and a C-terminal helicase domain connected by a flexible linker (Fig. 9C). The DENV NS3 structure is similar to that of JEV NS3, with similar domain arrangements (Fig. 9B) [30]. The protease and helicase domains of JEV and DENV are similar, with RMSD of 0.84 Å and 0.73 Å and sequence identities of 49% and 65%, respectively (Fig. 9B, C). The comparison of JEV NS3 and DENV NS3 indicated that they share a highly similar fold and domain organization, with conserved protease and helicase domains connected by a linker region (Figure S1A and S1B). Interaction analysis of NS3 from JEV or DENV with NLRC5 was performed through molecular docking using AlphaFold and HADDOCK [17, 20]. Interface analysis indicated that NS3 interacts with NLRC5 at multiple sites (Fig. 9D, E) (Tables S1 and S2). The analysis indicated that NLRC5 showed a slightly stronger interaction with JEV NS3 than with DENV NS3, as indicated by the docking scores, energy, and buried surface area (Table S1). The N-terminal protease and C-terminal helicase domains of JEV or DENV NS3 are positioned near the uCARD and LRR regions of NLRC5, respectively, stabilizing the complex via a conserved network of hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and hydrophobic interactions (Table S2). The interaction appears to be largely driven by electrostatics, as NLRC5 has a negatively charged surface, whereas NS3 has arginine-rich, positively charged patches on its surface.

Overall, these observations suggest that flavivirus NS3 can interact with NLRC5 at multiple sites, predominantly through electrostatic interactions.

To distinguish between viral protein degradation and inhibition of ongoing protein translation, infected cells were co-treated with cycloheximide (CHX, a translation inhibitor) and MG132 following DOX induction (Fig. 11F). Under dual CHX + MG132 treatment, the level of remaining NS3 protein in both GFP-NLRC5 WT (Fig. 11G, H) and GFP-NLRC5 NLS I (Fig. 11I, J) expressing cells remained suppressed and stable (~ 20%—30%) across the 12-h time course without significant recovery. These findings confirm that the observed decline in NS3 levels during JEV infection is driven by active proteasomal degradation rather than translation arrest.

Because viral replication complexes assemble on modified endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membranes, we investigated the subcellular localization of cytoplasmic GFP-NLRC5 (NLS I) relative to ER markers and viral non-structural protein 1 (NS1) during JEV infection (Fig. 11K–N).

HeLa cells expressing GFP-NLRC5 NLS I were infected with JEV (MOI 5, 24 h) and analyzed by confocal immunofluorescence microscopy (Fig. 11K, L). Immunostaining for calreticulin (an ER marker) and viral NS1 revealed that cytoplasmic GFP-NLRC5 NLS I strongly colocalized with calreticulin in infected cells (Fig. 11L). Quantitative colocalization analysis using Pearson's correlation coefficient showed a high correlation between calreticulin and GFP-NLRC5 NLS I (mean coefficient ~ 0.65), whereas the spatial correlation between viral NS1 and GFP-NLRC5 NLS I was significantly lower (mean coefficient ~ 0.50) (Fig. 11M).

Furthermore, measurement of normalized mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) demonstrated a significant reduction in JEV NS1 signal in DOX-induced GFP-NLRC5 NLS I cells compared to non-induced controls (p < 0.001; Fig. 11N), further demonstrating the antiviral activity of NLRC5 in restricting JEV protein accumulation. Taken together, these results demonstrate that NLRC5 localizes predominantly to ER microenvironments and negatively regulates JEV non-structural protein synthesis and accumulation.

The knockdown of NLRC5 in vivo significantly exacerbated mortality, accelerated clinical disease progression, and led to uncontrolled viral replication within the brain, as evidenced by elevated viral titers and NS3/NS1 protein expression. This uncontrolled viral replication leads to severe neuroinflammation in the brains of NLRC5-deficient mice, as evidenced by increased levels of ASC and cleaved caspase-3 [32, 33]. Discussion

NLRC5 is a critical regulator of innate and antiviral immune responses during viral infections. In this study, we investigated how NLRC5 affects JEV replication and disease progression to clarify its contribution to antiviral immunity. We confirmed that (1) NLRC5 is induced during JEV infection, majorly by microglial cells and not by neuronal cells; (2) NLRC5 acts as a restriction factors for JEV replication in both neuronal and non-neuronal cells; Knockdown of NLRC5 augmented JEV replication significantly in neuronal and non-neuronal cells; (3) IFN-γ treatment augmented NLRC5 and reduced viral replication in neuronal and non-neuronal cells; (4) NLRC5 expression in neuronal cells is IFN-γ dependent; (5) Cytoplasmic retention of NLRC5 is essential for the reduction in viral load; (6) NLRC5 retention in the cytoplasm is necessary for the interaction with the JEV NS3 protein; (7) In vivo knockdown of NLRC5 augmented viral replication and accelerated death upon JEV infection.

Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV) preferentially targets and replicates in neurons, resulting in high viral loads in the brain. This study establishes the protein NLRC5 as a critical, cell-type-specific driver of the brain's innate immune defense against JEV. The research reveals that while JEV triggers NLRC5 production in microglia, neurons completely fail to express this protein during infection—a crucial finding that explains why the virus replicates so aggressively in neuronal cells. Furthermore, the study connects systemic immune signaling to localized brain defense by showing that NLRC5 relies on interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) to activate its antiviral effects in neuroblastoma cells. Most importantly, the discovery that NLRC5 physically binds to the viral NS3 protein reveals a direct molecular mechanism for blocking viral replication, thereby confirming NLRC5's role as a novel host restriction factor for JEV.

The role of NLRC5 in viral replication varies significantly depending on the virus and the host species. The best studied and likely primary role of NLRC5 is the transcriptional activation of MHC class I genes, which is essential for presenting viral antigens to CD8+ T cells to eliminate infected cells. Beyond its function in antigen presentation, NLRC5 can modulate innate signaling pathways, such as type I Interferon (IFN) production and NF-κB activation, though the specific effect-whether it acts as an enhancer or a feedback inhibitor—often depends on the specific virus and cell type involved. Many viruses (e.g., SARS-CoV-2) evade the immune system by suppressing NLRC5 to reduce MHC class I expression. It is reported that SARS-CoV-2 targets the MHC class I pathway [4, 24]. It is shown that the SARS-CoV-2 ORF6 protein suppresses NLRC5, a MHC class I transactivator, both transcriptionally and functionally, providing a mechanistic link. Moreover, SARS-CoV-2 ORF6 hampers type II interferon-mediated STAT1 signaling, leading to the diminished upregulation of NLRC5 and IRF1 gene expression [34]. Additionally, SARS-CoV-2 ORF6 inhibits NLRC5 function by blocking karyopherin-dependent nuclear import. Similarly, the obligate intracellular bacterium Orientia tsutsugamushi employs its effector protein Ank5 to inhibit nuclear translocation of NLRC5 and/or promote its proteasomal degradation, thereby hampering MHC-I transactivation [35].

In several flaviviruses, NLRC5 acts as a key defense mechanism. Similar to JEV, NLRC5 restricts DENV by promoting the autophagic degradation of the viral NS3 protein [11]. NLRC5 recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase CUL2 to catalyze K48-linked poly-ubiquitination of the DENV NS3 protein [11]. Knocking down NLRC5 significantly increases DENV replication. Research indicates that NLRC5 can also mediate the degradation of the ZIKV NS3 protein, suggesting a common, broad-spectrum defense strategy against flaviviruses. On the other hand, in influenza virus infection, the role of NLRC5 is more complex and depends on the species and cell type involved. In human respiratory epithelial cells, NLRC5 can enhance RIG-I-dependent immune response by binding to and stabilizing RIG-I, prolonging its expression and boosting IFN-β production to impair viral replication [26]. On the other hand, NLRC5 can suppress Enterovirus 71 (EV71) replication by directly binding to the 5'UTR of its genome and by downregulating mediators of the IFN-β pathway (e.g., RIG-I, IRF3) [36]. Interestingly, in chicken macrophages, NLRC5 has been reported as a pro-viral factor [37]. In this context, its knockdown significantly reduced avian influenza replication, suggesting it may negatively regulate certain antiviral pathways in avian species. In Vesicular Stomatitis Virus (VSV) infections, studies suggest that NLRC5 can act as a negative regulator of the immune response. In these models, knocking down NLRC5 enhances the production of antiviral cytokines, such as IFN-β, thereby increasing the host's resistance to VSV. Restoring NLRC5 function could help the immune system "see" and kill infected cells. In viral models of epilepsy, NLRC5 deficiency has been linked to significantly fewer seizures and reduced neuroinflammation [22], support important physiological roles of NLRC5 in neuronal tissue.

A key finding of our study is that NLRC5 is induced in microglia but not in neurons during JEV infection. It suggests that the brain's innate immune response to JEV is compartmentalized, with microglia acting as the primary NLRC5-mediated "defenders" while neurons may rely on different or IFN-γ-primed pathways. The study highlights that NLRC5 is essential for IFN-γ induced antiviral effects. NLRC5 is a critical downstream effector of the IFN-γ signalling pathway. This bridges the gap between systemic immune signaling and intracellular viral restriction, specifically in the context of neuroinflammation. Although our conclusions are derived from experiments using in vitro IFN-γ treatment and AG129 (IFN-alpha/beta/gamma receptor-deficient) mice. However, we agree that IFN-γ-triggers multiple downstream pathways, and contributions from other IFN-stimulated genes cannot be completely ruled out.

Neuronal cells are known to have reduced IFN levels and ISGs in response to Poly (I: C) treatment or viral infection, whereas other immune cells and non-neuronal cells induce IFN upon viral infection [38]. These inherent properties of neuronal cells make them more susceptible to viral infection than other cells. Our study confirms that the inability of neuronal cells to induce NLRC5 expression during viral infection may stabilize viral NS3 proteins and facilitate efficient viral replication in these cells.

In this study, similar to Dengue viral infection, we demonstrated a novel interaction between NLRC5 and the viral NS3 protein through docking simulations and co-immunoprecipitation experiments. While our findings firmly establish that binding between these two proteins forms a complex, the precise nature and downstream biochemical consequence of this interaction remains to be fully elucidated. The binding of NLRC5 to NS3 could potentially inhibit viral replication through several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms, including target protein degradation (e.g., via ubiquitin–proteasome pathways), altered subcellular localization, steric hindrance of NS3 helicase/protease activity, or disruption of viral replication complex assembly. Dissecting the exact downstream pathway mediated by this interaction represents an important avenue for future mechanistic studies. Also, our data highlight that cytoplasmic NLRC5 is mediating the antiviral activity. Strategies that enhance this interaction or maintain NLRC5 levels could serve as novel antiviral interventions.

The uCARD domain and LRR domain of NLRC5 directly bind to the protease domain and helicase domain of the viral NS3 protein, respectively. NS3 is essential for the virus because it serves as both a motor (helicase) and a processor (protease) in viral genome replication.

It will be interesting to determine which other host proteins interact with the NLRC5-NS3 complex to facilitate proteasomal degradation. Future studies on mass spectrometric analysis of coimmunoprecipitation studies may be needed to clarify the questions.

Recent research indicates that NLRC5 is a key driver of microglial inflammation after a stroke [23]. Deleting or inhibiting microglial NLRC5 significantly reduces brain injury and improves neurological recovery by restoring proper cell-cleaning (autophagy) functions. NLRC5 is a known regulator of the NF-kB pathway and type I interferon activity. Modulating its expression could help prevent excessive neuroinflammation, which leads to severe brain damage in viral encephalitis.

Conclusions

NLRC5 expression in neuronal cells is IFN-γ dependent. IFN-γ treatment typically restricts JEV replication, but this antiviral effect is lost in NLRC5-deficient cells, resulting in increased viral protein expression. Overexpression or knockdown of NLRC5 significantly impacts viral replication. Under infection conditions and in pre- or post-IFN-γ treatment, NLRC5 expression was induced in neuronal cells. Induced or overexpressed NLRC5 interacted with the viral NS3 protein and possibly degraded it, thereby reducing viral replication (Fig. 15). On the other hand, viral infection does not induce optimal IFN-γ expression in neuronal cells; therefore, no induction of NLRC5 was observed.
