How to evaluate, triage, and sedate a patient in the congenital cardiac catheterization laboratory
Authors: Eleni Asimacopoulos, Brian Quinn, Kirsten C. Odegard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The congenital cardiac catheterization laboratory (CCCL) has evolved into one of the most physiologically demanding environments in pediatric cardiovascular care for not only the interventional cardiologist, but also the anesthesiologist. Once primarily diagnostic, the CCCL now functions as a high-acuity interventional laboratory capable of treating both acquired and congenital heart disease (CHD), through the entire lifespan of patients with CHD. Technological innovation in device design, advanced imaging modalities, and hybrid surgical collaboration has expanded the therapeutic scope to include ductal stenting in neonates, atrial and ventricular defect closure, pulmonary artery and pulmonary vein dilations, transcatheter valve implantation, and complex hybrid surgical-catheter palliation strategies. For the anesthesiologist, the CCCL environment combines the high-acuity demands of an operating room (OR) with several unique features, including: limited access to the airway once sterile fields are established, exposure to radiation, confined workspace, and often prolonged procedural times. In addition, significant hemodynamic instability and arrhythmias may occur in the setting of catheters and sheaths within the cardiac chambers. Certain interventional procedures can transiently obstruct cardiac output, resulting in profound hemodynamic compromise. All of this occurs without the safety net of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) in the cardiac OR. This requires the anesthesia team to anticipate rapid physiological changes and respond immediately to maintain cardiovascular stability.

Introduction

The current environment in the congenital cardiac catheterization laboratory (CCCL)

The expansion in procedural capability has been paralleled by a marked increase in patient complexity and acuity. Today’s CCCL no longer serves predominantly stable patients undergoing elective diagnostic procedures. Instead, it routinely manages critically ill neonates and children, often with complex structural heart disease at various stages of repair or palliation with limited cardiopulmonary reserve. Patients will often present to the CCCL with long-standing heart failure, significant ventricular dysfunction, or residual lesions following prior surgical repair. Premature infants with ductal-dependent lesions, neonates with low cardiac output syndrome requiring inotropic support, patients with single-ventricle physiology at various stages of palliation are undergoing high-risk transcatheter interventions and even patients on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO).

In addition to cardiac structural and physiologic issues, patients increasingly have severe pulmonary hypertension (PHTN), chronic hypoxemia, renal dysfunction, or coagulopathy whilst undergoing catheter-based therapies. Adults with repaired congenital heart disease (CHD) often present with complex residual anatomy, arrhythmias, ventricular dysfunction, and multi-organ sequelae of longstanding cyanosis or hepatic congestion and hypertension. As a result, the CCCL has evolved into a high-acuity environment, caring for progressively sicker patients in whom even minor changes in preload, afterload, oxygenation, or ventilation may precipitate rapidly-evolving hemodynamic instability.

For the anesthesiologist, the CCCL environment combines the high-acuity demands of an operating room (OR) with several unique constraints. These include limited access to the airway once sterile fields are established, exposure to radiation, confined workspace, and often prolonged procedural times. In addition, significant hemodynamic instability and arrhythmias may occur when catheters and sheaths are advanced through the different cardiac chambers. Certain interventional procedures such as balloon dilation or stenting across cardiac valves can transiently obstruct cardiac output, creating periods of profound hemodynamic compromise. All of this occurs without the safety net of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) in the cardiac OR, requiring the anesthesia team to anticipate rapid physiological changes and respond immediately to maintain cardiovascular stability.

Adverse events remain an inherent risk in the CCCL. Contemporary registry and institutional data report a range of major adverse events in approximately 2–24% of procedures, with cardiac arrest occurring in roughly 1% of cases (1-4). Importantly, these complications are rarely random and are strongly associated with procedural complexity, younger age (particularly neonates), physiologic vulnerability, single-ventricle circulation, and PHTN. Safe anesthesia practice in this environment therefore requires careful preprocedural assessment, formal risk stratification, and lesion-specific physiologic planning using validated tools (5-8). Equally important is anticipating clinical deterioration and establishing predefined escalation pathways, including immediate availability of advanced rescue strategies such as ECMO when necessary.

Why risk stratification matters in patients with CHD undergoing cardiac catheterization

As described, patients with CHD presenting to the CCCL represent a highly heterogeneous population with widely varying levels of risk. This spectrum ranges from fragile neonates to adults with repaired or unrepaired congenital lesions. Differences in patient size, cardiac anatomy, oxygen saturation, ventricular function, and overall physiologic status result in diverse and often unknown hemodynamic responses to different interventions and anesthesia. As a result, patients undergoing procedures in the CCCL cannot be evaluated solely on the basis of diagnosis or procedure type; rather, risk reflects the complex interaction between patient-specific physiology and the anticipated procedural intervention.

To account for case-mix complexity in CCCL, the Congenital Cardiac Catheterization Project on Outcomes (C3PO) introduced procedure-type risk categories in 2011 to classify cases according to their expected risk of adverse events. Other models were later developed by the Congenital Cardiac Interventional Study Consortium (CRISP) and the Improving Pediatric and Adult Congenital Treatment (IMPACT) registry (9-11). It is important to highlight that IMPACT was developed more as a ‘risk-adjustment’ tool rather than a ‘risk-prediction’ tool. These earlier systems had important limitations, including limited ability to differentiate risk among procedures, reliance on older datasets, and lack of consideration for newer transcatheter interventions and evolving procedural techniques. In addition, prior models generally classified cases based on the highest-risk intervention performed and often treated hemodynamic abnormalities with equal weighting despite differing physiologic significance. As the CCCL has continued to evolve, updated risk stratification frameworks incorporating contemporary procedures and improved assessment of physiologic vulnerability have been developed to more accurately predict adverse events and guide procedural planning, level of monitoring, allocate necessary resources, and guide peri- and postoperative management. The application of structured risk assessment metrics in the CCCL population has become essential to better understand how patient characteristics and procedural complexity interact to influence adverse event risk and clinical outcomes. Contemporary registry-based analyses have demonstrated that integrating patient-level variables such as age, weight, hemodynamic status, and comorbid conditions with procedural risk categories significantly improves the ability to predict complications during catheterization. These models provide a more objective framework for identifying patients who may be particularly vulnerable to hemodynamic instability, arrhythmias, or other peri-procedural adverse events.

Accurate risk stratification also has important practical implications for procedural planning and perioperative management. Early identification of high-risk patients allows the multidisciplinary team to allocate appropriate resources, determine the optimal anesthetic strategy, and plan the level of monitoring required during the procedure. In high-risk cases, this may include the use of general anesthesia, invasive hemodynamic monitoring and readiness for advanced rescue therapies such as ECMO therapy.

Risk assessment supports a proactive approach to patient care in the CCCL by enabling teams to understand physiologic challenges, optimize readiness for potential complications, and improve overall procedural safety and outcome for patients with CHD. Using risk assessment also helps to establish clearer interdisciplinary communication among interventional cardiologist, anesthesia, nursing, and catheterization technicians, enabling procedural planning and proactive preparation for potential complications.

Risk stratification and triage in the CCCL

Risk-stratification frameworks have improved safety in the CCCL. By combining procedural risk classification with assessment of physiologic vulnerability and coordinated multidisciplinary planning, these models create a common framework for the interventional cardiologist and the anesthesiologist. This type of approach enables teams to anticipate potential complications, allocate appropriate resources, and establish escalation strategies before the patient arrives in the catheterization laboratory.

Several complementary frameworks are now used to characterize the risk for adverse events prior to catheterization. Procedural complexity can be categorized using the Procedural Risk in Congenital Cardiac Catheterization (PREDIC3T) classification (5). This is a contemporary risk stratification system that categorizes CCCL procedures into six risk categories (0–5) based on their association with high-severity adverse events. Developed from the C3PO multicenter registry analyzing over 23,000 cases, the system identifies 34 unique case types defined by the specific procedures performed and groups them according to their risk of complications (5,8). The risk categories from this registry demonstrate a clear gradient in adverse event rates, ranging from approximately 1% for Category 0 (lowest risk) to almost 14% for Category 5 (highest risk). The classification system has been validated in external registries and is independently associated with adverse event risk and reflects the substantial contribution of procedural complexity to outcomes. It is used alongside a Hemodynamic Vulnerability Score (HVS) and the Pre-Procedure Cardiac Status (PCS) score, which incorporate objective baseline variables for risk stratification.

The HVS is a scoring system that quantifies patient physiologic risk, based on six hemodynamic indicator variables measured during congenital cardiac catheterization (8). These indicators include elevated systemic ventricular end-diastolic pressure (≥18 mmHg), reduced mixed venous saturation, reduced systemic arterial saturation, and elevated pulmonary artery pressure (Pap) (with different thresholds for single-ventricle versus biventricular physiology). The HVS ranges from 0–6 points, with each increase associated with progressively higher odds of high severity adverse events. The PCS score is a complementary risk assessment tool that categorizes patients into three levels based on their overall clinical condition prior to catheterization (12). Both scores serve as independent predictors of procedural risk and are used alongside procedural risk classifications like PREDIC3T for comprehensive risk stratification, patient counseling, informed consent, and resource planning in the CCCL (8,13).

Registry analyses further demonstrate that adverse events cluster in specific patient populations and clinical scenarios. Neonates and infants, patients with low body weight, those with single-ventricle physiology, and individuals with significant cardiopulmonary comorbidities represent particularly vulnerable groups (14). Additional factors associated with increased risk include recent cardiac surgery or catheterization, non-elective procedures, and the need for pre-procedural ventilatory or vasoactive support. In large multicenter cohorts, these characteristics remain independently associated with clinically meaningful adverse events as well as the need for high-level post-procedural resources such as mechanical ventilation, vasoactive support, or ECMO.

The ICU Admission Tool for Congenital Heart Catheterization (iCATCH) is another system developed to predict which patients undergoing cardiac catheterization may require ICU admission after a catheterization (15). This was developed using a prospective single-center study conducted at a tertiary pediatric heart center where patient and procedural characteristics were analyzed to identify predictors of post-procedural ICU admission. Approximately 11% of patients required ICU care. Independent predictors included low body weight (<10 kg), the presence of systemic illness, recent cardiac intervention within 90 days, and higher procedural risk categories (14). The iCATCH model provides a practical preprocedural tool to help identify patients likely to require intensive care support after catheterization, thereby improving procedural planning, resource allocation, and capacity management in pediatric heart centers.

It is important to highlight that procedural complexity and physiologic vulnerability interact rather than operate independently. A moderately complex intervention may carry relatively low risk in a physiologically stable child, but may become substantially higher risk in a patient with limited cardiopulmonary reserve. Contemporary predictive models therefore combine procedural classification with physiologic variables to generate integrated risk estimates that better reflect the overall vulnerability of the patient undergoing catheterization.

Risk stratification for these high-risk patients undergoing procedures in the CCCL has specific important implications from an anesthesia standpoint. Preprocedural risk assessment involves evaluation of airway anatomy, coexisting medical conditions, laboratory results, and hemodynamic information obtained from prior echocardiography and catheterization studies. When integrated with the PREDIC3T classification, HVS, PCS, and the iCATCH model, this assessment supports the anticipation of potential complications and adverse events and helps guide decision making.

This type of risk stratification evaluation helps determine whether a preprocedural multidisciplinary discussion is necessary. Such discussions typically include cardiology, anesthesia, intensive care specialists, nursing staff, and catheterization laboratory personnel, ensuring that all team members understand the anticipated risks and the planned management strategy. In selected high-risk cases, cardiac surgeons may also participate, particularly when there is concern about the potential need for ECMO support or an increased risk of cardiac arrest. In addition, these risk-assessment tools help guide important decisions regarding postoperative disposition.

Sedation and anesthesia for the CCCL

Sedation and anesthetic management in the CCCL vary considerably between institutions. These differences often reflect variations in sedation models, staffing structures, institutional experience, and available resources. One of the most common clinical dilemmas is whether a procedure should be performed under general anesthesia or with sedation while maintaining spontaneous ventilation. Because of this variability, a multi-society expert panel was asked to develop a consensus statement to provide recommendations for patients undergoing diagnostic and therapeutic procedures in the CCCL (16). The goal of this effort was to provide guidance consistent with national practice standards. The panel concluded that: “no single anesthetic technique is appropriate for all patients with CHD undergoing catheterization. Rather, the most important determinant of safety is that the anesthesia provider understands the patient’s underlying physiology, procedural risks, and the potential hemodynamic effects of the chosen anesthetic strategy”.

Safe anesthetic management requires a comprehensive understanding of how anesthetic agents, vasoactive medications, and respiratory interventions interact with the physiology of specific congenital heart lesions and prior surgical palliation. Maintaining physiologic stability is essential to obtain accurate hemodynamic measurements and ensure correct interpretation of catheterization data. Examples include the effects of FiO2 and pCO2 on pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR). Sedating with spontaneous respiration often causes hypoventilation with rise in end-tidal CO2 (ETCO2) and decrease in oxygen saturation which both will increase the PVR and Pap which can cause falsely elevated numbers with big consequences for planning and interpretations of the catheterization data.

General anesthesia also carries changes in hemodynamic data. Anesthetic agents may alter systemic vascular resistance (SVR) and cardiac output leading to hypotension in patients with limited cardiovascular reserve. However, general anesthesia with a secured airway is often favored in neonates, infants, and other high-risk patients because it allows controlled ventilation and prevents hypoventilation-related changes in PVR. Airway access also becomes significantly limited once the procedure begins and sterile fields are established, making early airway strategy particularly important. Controlled ventilation provides the ability to deliberately manipulate ETCO2, oxygenation, and airway pressures, all of which directly affect PVR and intracardiac shunts. Neuromuscular blockade may also be beneficial by preventing patient movement during critical measurements and interventions. In certain high-risk procedures, such as pulmonary artery or pulmonary vein interventions, the ability to suction the airway and manage ventilation becomes especially important due to the risk of reperfusion injury and post-dilation pulmonary edema.

As previously mentioned, general anesthesia with a secured airway is often preferred for neonates, infants. As the population of adults with palliated CHD continues to grow, anesthetic management of these patients has become increasingly important and presents a distinct set of challenges. Whenever feasible, light sedation while maintaining spontaneous ventilation is preferred, as it preserves physiologic hemodynamics and allows for more accurate pressure and flow measurements during cardiac catheterization procedures. However, the complexity of their underlying cardiac lesions, the nature of the planned intervention, patient anxiety frequently necessitates general anesthesia. Many adults with CHD have undergone multiple previous surgeries and catheterizations and often retain vivid memories of those experiences, making deeper sedation or general anesthesia necessary even for relatively straightforward procedures (17,18).

A wide range of anesthetic techniques and drug combinations have been described for both ventilated and spontaneously breathing patients, but there is no evidence to support the superiority of any single anesthetic regimen (19). Regardless of the approach chosen, providers must remain prepared for sudden hemodynamic deterioration and the possibility of cardiac arrest. In a retrospective analysis of more than 7,000 congenital cardiac catheterizations, the overall incidence of cardiac arrest was approximately 1%, with higher risk observed in infants and in patients undergoing interventional procedures (2). Procedures associated with the highest risk included ventricular septal defect device closure, interventions for intact or restrictive atrial septum, mitral balloon dilation, pulmonary vein dilation, and pulmonary artery dilation. These risks are largely related to the technical aspects of the procedures themselves, including arrhythmogenic stimulation from wires and catheters within the cardiac chambers and transient reductions in cardiac output during valve dilation or stenting. Recognizing these risks and understanding the physiologic consequences of both the procedure and anesthetic management are essential for safe care in the CCCL. Careful preparation, appropriate anesthetic strategy, and readiness to rapidly manage hemodynamic compromise remain fundamental components of successful catheterization laboratory practice.

Newer medications, such as dexmedetomidine, are attractive because they provide sedation with preservation of spontaneous ventilation and minimal direct myocardial depression. Its attenuation of sympathetic surges may benefit patients with labile blood pressure or arrhythmia propensity. However, its depression of sinus and atrioventricular node function is clinically meaningful, particularly in neonates and in lesions with heartrate dependent physiology (20).

Ketamine provides cardiovascular support through sympathetic stimulation and reduced catecholamine reuptake, while maintaining spontaneous respiration. Ketamine has historically been avoided in patients with PHTN because of concerns about increased PVR. However, several studies have demonstrated that when normoxia and normocapnia are maintained, ketamine does not significantly increase PVR and can provide stable systemic hemodynamics in patients with CHD (21,22), but in catecholamine-depleted states, ketamine’s direct negative inotropic effects may become apparent.

Propofol provides titratable hypnosis with rapid recovery but frequently produces hypotension and myocardial depression. These effects reduce preload and SVR and may be poorly tolerated in patients with ventricular dysfunction, duct-dependent systemic circulation, or single-ventricle physiology. Incremental dosing with predefined vasopressor support and ventilation control is essential when propofol is used.

Combination regimens such as ketamine-propofol and ketamine-dexmedetomidine aim to balance pharmacologic tradeoffs. Ketamine-propofol combination strategies provide faster onset and recovery compared with dexmedetomidine-heavy regimens and may preserve hemodynamics better than propofol alone (23). Ketamine-dexmedetomidine combinations may attenuate dexmedetomidine-induced bradycardia while preserving respiratory stability.

Lesion- and procedure-specific anesthesia considerations

Anesthetic management for procedures in the CCCL must be tailored for a specific cardiac lesion and the individual patient. Key factors to consider include: patient age, cardiac reserve, ventricular function, pulmonary physiology, the planned interventional procedure and its potential to cause hemodynamic instability, as well as the anticipated post-procedural course. Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) stenting

Stenting of the PDA in patients with ductal-dependent congenital heart lesions is among the highest-risk catheter based interventions performed in the CCCL, as the PDA may represent the sole source of pulmonary blood flow. Hemodynamic instability may occur during wire manipulation, balloon dilation, or stent deployment due to transient interruption of ductal flow, ductal spasm, bradycardia, or abrupt changes in the balance between pulmonary and systemic blood flow. Anesthetic management requires careful selection of agents to minimize adverse effects on SVR and cardiac output, as these patients are at increased risk for coronary ischemia and cardiac arrest. The primary anesthetic goals include preservation of cardiac output, maintain balance between PVR and SVR, avoidance of bradycardia, and controlling ventilation and acid-base balance. Prostaglandin infusion should generally be continued until correct stent placement and ductal patency are confirmed, although in some cases with a large PDA the infusion may need to be discontinued prior to the procedure.

Vasoactive medications should be immediately available, and the anesthesia team must be prepared to rapidly treat hemodynamic compromise if ductal flow becomes obstructed during the intervention. Hybrid ductal stenting performed through carotid cutdown adds further complexity because cerebral perfusion and neurologic protection become major concerns. These cases require close coordination between surgeons, interventional cardiologists, and anesthesia providers. Mean arterial pressure must be maintained at a level sufficient to support cerebral blood flow, particularly during carotid manipulation or transient hemodynamic instability. Anticoagulation and hemostasis must be carefully coordinated, and because these procedures combine surgical exposure with catheter intervention, full operating-room readiness is appropriate, including invasive monitoring, blood availability, and preparation for rapid escalation if major instability develops.

Septal defect closures

Septal defect closures, including atrial septal defects (ASD) and ventricular septal defects (VSD) device closure, are now routine in the CCCL and typically performed under general anesthesia with transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance. ASD closures are normally well tolerated with little hemodynamic instability. VSD closures carry higher anesthetic risk than ASD closures, where device manipulation can cause arrhythmias, low cardiac output, and cardiac arrest. Studies demonstrated hemodynamic instability occurring in up to 40% of cases, including hypotension requiring fluid resuscitation or acute intervention and significant dysrhythmias in approximately 28% of procedures (12,24,25). Elimination of a long-standing left-to-right shunt may increase left ventricular preload and systemic afterload while reducing right-sided volume overload, and in some patients this may unmask occult ventricular dysfunction or altered diastolic compliance. Anesthetic goals include maintaining stable preload and coronary perfusion pressure, avoiding hypercarbia and acidosis, and closely monitoring rhythm throughout device deployment. In high-risk septal interventions, particularly VSD closure, the team should be prepared for sudden conduction abnormalities, hemodynamic deterioration and cardiac arrest.

Pulmonary vein interventions

Pulmonary vein interventions in infants and small children present significant anesthetic challenges. These patients are often critically ill and frequently have systemic to suprasystemic right ventricular (RV) pressures, sometimes associated with decreased RV function. Assessment of intracardiac shunting is important, particularly the presence of an ASD which may allow right-to-left shunting if the RV fails (26).

These procedures are typically lengthy and technically complex, and many patients remain significantly desaturated at the end of catheterization. Hemodynamic instability may result from several factors, including reperfusion lung injury following pulmonary vein dilation, repeated balloon dilations in anticoagulated patients, elevated pulmonary venous and left atrial pressures, and persistently high right-sided and RV pressures (27-29). These conditions can precipitate pulmonary edema or pulmonary hemorrhage. When pulmonary bleeding occurs, severe hypoxemia may develop, often requiring inotropic support, higher levels of positive end-expiratory pressure, and continued mechanical ventilation, with many patients requiring postoperative ICU care. Because restenosis is common, repeated catheter-based interventions are often necessary, and the cumulative anesthetic and procedural risks should be carefully considered when planning each procedure.

Pulmonary artery dilation and stenting

Pulmonary artery dilation and stenting can also be associated with abrupt hemodynamic changes. Balloon inflation or stent placement may transiently obstruct flow, and complications such as vascular tear, dissection, reperfusion injury, or distal pulmonary hemorrhage can occur. In patients with RV dysfunction or PHTN, even a brief increase in RV afterload may compromise cardiac output and coronary perfusion. Anesthetic management should therefore prioritize maintenance of systemic arterial pressure, avoidance of pulmonary vasoconstriction and further increases of PVR, and optimization of oxygenation and ventilation. Hypercarbia, acidosis, hypoxia, and high intrathoracic pressures should also be avoided. Inhaled nitric oxide (NO) or other selective pulmonary vasodilators should be immediately available in patients with significant pulmonary vascular disease or borderline RV function.

Transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement

Approximately 20% of patients born with CHD have abnormalities of the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT), and many require multiple surgical interventions throughout their lifetime. Transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement (TPVR) has emerged as an important alternative to repeat surgical pulmonary valve or RVOT reconstruction in both pediatric and adult patients. TPVR procedures require large-bore vascular access, prolonged catheter manipulation, and occasionally rapid pacing, all of which may lead to abrupt hemodynamic instability. Potential complications include conduit rupture, coronary artery compression, severe pulmonary insufficiency and ventricular arrhythmias. Careful anesthetic preparation is therefore essential. Invasive arterial monitoring, reliable large-bore venous access, immediate availability of blood products, and readiness for rapid resuscitation should be established before the procedure begins. The anesthesiologist must anticipate transient reductions in preload or cardiac output during balloon interrogation, valve deployment, or rapid pacing and be prepared to promptly treat hypotension. Because coronary compression testing is a critical step in the procedure, close communication between the anesthesiology and interventional cardiology teams during angiographic and hemodynamic assessment is essential (30,31). Hybrid pulmonary valve implantation combines the physiologic stress of surgical exposure with the complexity of catheter-based intervention. Compared with purely transcatheter procedures, these hybrid cases are often longer, associated with greater bleeding risk, more substantial fluid shifts, and a higher likelihood of postoperative pain and recovery needs. Anesthetic management must account for both procedural components, including surgical hemostasis, anticoagulation management, and post-procedural ventilation planning when necessary.

PHTN

Whether isolated or accompanying another congenital lesion, PHTN is a major anesthetic concern across many different procedures in the CCCL. These patients often have systemic to supra-systemic RV pressure and are at risk of acute RV failure, pulmonary hypertensive crisis, systemic hypotension, and cardiac arrest in response to hypoxia, hypercarbia, acidosis, pain, agitation, or excessive airway pressures (32-34). The anesthetic plan should therefore emphasize early initiation of inotropes and vasoactive pharmacotherapy to help support RV function and maintain adequate coronary perfusion pressure. Avoidance of triggers that increase PVR, and immediate access to pulmonary vasodilator therapy is essential. Inhaled NO, vasoactive infusions, and strategies for rapid escalation to extracorporeal support should be considered in advance for the highest-risk patients. In this population, careful preparation and meticulous physiologic control are often more important than the choice of any single anesthetic drug.

Cardiac arrest in patients with severe PHTN has a poor prognosis because chest compressions are ineffective at generating adequate pulmonary blood flow, left ventricular filling, and cardiac output (35,36). The markedly elevated PVR creates an increased afterload on the RV that limit the force of chest compressions. Even high-quality compressions fail to push blood through the severely constricted pulmonary vasculature, preventing blood from reaching the left heart and generating meaningful systemic circulation and cardiac output (35). Additionally, positive-pressure ventilation during CPR further compromises hemodynamics by decreasing systemic venous return and RV filling, paradoxically reducing the already minimal cardiac output generated by compressions. Unless a rapidly reversible cause can be identified and corrected, or ECMO is rapidly deployed, resuscitation efforts remain poor despite optimal technique.

Hybrid procedures in the CCCL

Hybrid procedures in the pediatric cardiac catheterization laboratory represent a combined approach in which both surgical and transcatheter techniques are used to treat complex CHD (12,37). From an anesthesiologist’s perspective, these procedures present unique challenges, as they essentially involve performing a surgical procedure within the catheterization laboratory environment. The physical layout, equipment setup, patient positioning, and limited direct access to the patient can complicate anesthetic management. Careful planning is therefore required to ensure appropriate monitoring and vascular access, including placement of invasive monitoring, such as an arterial line and central venous catheter. Close coordination among the anesthesia, cardiology, and surgical teams is essential to maintain physiologic stability and ensure procedural safety.

A high-risk hybrid procedure in the CCCL is the placement of flow restrictors as a new palliative strategy for neonates with hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS). Control of pulmonary blood flow can be achieved by placement of bilateral PA bands or transcatheter pulmonary flow restrictors (PFRs), thereby balancing SVR and PVR (38,39). These devices are typically used in combination with ductal stenting to maintain systemic blood flow, and an atrial septostomy. These interventions achieve the physiologic goals of a traditional Stage I palliation in patients with HLHS, while avoiding or delaying CPB in high-risk neonates. The hybrid approach allows for clinical stabilization, growth, and delayed definitive surgery until the infant is larger and more stable. Recent advances include fully transcatheter approaches using percutaneously placed PFRs, which have demonstrated feasibility, safety, and improved outcomes compared to traditional hybrid stage 1 palliation. This allows successful bridging to surgery or transplant. These techniques are particularly valuable for premature infants, those with low birth weight, and patients with significant comorbidities who are at high risk for CPB surgery (40,41).

Multidisciplinary meetings with detailed planning of the procedure are essential for successful hybrid procedures. These cases are very complex and especially high risk from an anesthesiologist’s perspective. Invasive monitoring and reliable intravenous access are essential. Early initiation of vasoactive support to maintain adequate cardiac output is key, and careful management is necessary to avoid decreases in PVR that could compromise systemic blood flow. Blood products should be readily available, as maintaining an adequate hematocrit is important. In addition, ventilation and oxygenation must be carefully controlled to prevent excessive pulmonary blood flow that may reduce systemic perfusion; therefore, high inspired oxygen concentrations and aggressive ventilation are generally avoided.

ECMO in the catheterization laboratory

ECMO has emerged as a critical safety adjunct in high-risk congenital catheterization programs. Although historically deployed as rescue therapy following arrest, contemporary practice increasingly incorporates ECMO into preprocedural planning for selected patients (42,43). Cardiac arrest in the CCCL is frequently related to reversible causes, including acute RV failure, ductal interruption, coronary compression, pulmonary hypertensive crisis, or acute ventricular dysfunction or ischemia. Rapid deployment of ECMO in these scenarios may improve survival compared with prolonged conventional resuscitation. ECMO readiness can be conceptualized along a tiered spectrum, ranging from awareness to standby to pre-emptive cannulation. High-risk candidates include neonates with duct-dependent systemic circulation, patients with severe ventricular dysfunction, severe PHTN, complex single-ventricle physiology, and those undergoing highest procedural risk categories.

Successful ECMO implementation requires predefined cannulation strategy, perfusion team availability, equipment readiness, and multidisciplinary rehearsal. Programs with established protocols demonstrate improved survival compared with reactive deployment. The presence of a clearly defined escalation pathway reduces decision latency and improves team performance during crisis.

As mentioned, patients are also increasingly brought to the CCCL whilst already on ECMO. This is a different population of patients with their own risks for both the interventional cardiologist and the anesthesiologist. Bleeding is a highly important risk, as is ventricular distension and pulmonary edema if there hasn’t been adequate left-atrial decompression. A particularly vulnerable subgroup includes patients who are transferred to the CCCL on ECMO within the first few days after cardiac surgery These patients are often quite sick, having been on CPB before transitioning to ECMO, with multiple physiologic disturbances, including multiorgan failure and coagulopathy. Studies have illustrated that this demographic is at very high risk of adverse outcomes, especially hemorrhage (44). Diagnostic catheterization generally carries a lower risk than interventional procedures; however, this should still be carefully considered during preprocedural planning of patients on ECMO. When possible, catheterization within the first 24 hours after cannulation should be avoided due to increased risk of bleeding (45).

Conclusions

The CCCL represents one of the most physiologically demanding environments in pediatric cardiovascular care. Catastrophic events are seldom random; rather, they result from predictable interactions between procedural complexity and underlying cardiac lesion and reserves. Structured risk stratification using validated tools such as PREDIC3T and the HVS helps quantify these risks and translate them into practical use for triage and planning. The choice of sedation or anesthetic strategy should be individualized rather than standardized. Selection of anesthetic agents and techniques should consider ventricular function, PVR, intracardiac shunt physiology, and the anticipated hemodynamic changes associated with the intervention. In high-risk patients, general anesthesia may provide greater physiologic control and reduce the likelihood of deterioration. A detailed understanding of lesion-specific physiology and planned procedure with risks for adverse events and complications is essential for safe anesthetic management. This review has strived to cover some of the most common lesions seen in the CCCL as well as some of the most challenging to discuss from an anesthesia perspective. Other lesions could be considered in a future review for discussion of challenges to anesthesia management.

Preparation for rapid escalation of care is equally important. Readiness for ECMO has become an important component of high-risk catheterization programs, as early initiation may improve outcomes in cases of reversible hemodynamic collapse. The foundation for optimal care in this uniquely high-risk environment is formed by individualized anesthetic planning, lesion-specific physiologic understanding, and proactive escalation strategies. Ultimately, safe anesthesia practice in the CCCL relies on anticipation rather than reaction, with the integration of structured risk assessment. The additional advent of artificial intelligence and machine learning, mean new methods are on the horizon, which will further advance and improve this care.
