The effect of different surface treatment methods on the pull-out bond strength of PEEK endocrowns
Authors: Gizem Akın Tartuk, Bülent Kadir Tartuk, Sadullah Kaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolyetheretherketone (PEEK) has gained increasing attention as a restorative material for endocrown fabrication because of its favorable biomechanical properties and dentin-like elastic modulus. However, its low surface energy and chemically inert structure may compromise adhesive bonding to resin cements. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of different surface treatment protocols on the pull-out bond strength of PEEK endocrowns.MethodsSixty extracted sound human molars were used in this study. Standard endodontic access cavities were prepared for all teeth and root canal treatment was completed following a uniform clinical protocol. After endodontic therapy, the required occlusal preparations for endocrown restorations were performed. The prepared teeth were scanned using an intraoral scanner and PEEK endocrowns were fabricated for each specimen using a computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing system. The endocrowns were then randomly allocated to four groups according to the surface treatment protocol: Group C (Control), no surface treatment was applied; Group T, tribochemical silica coating followed by silanization; Group S, sandblasting followed by the application of a methyl methacrylate (MMA)-based primer (Visio.link®, Bredent GmbH, Germany); and Group L, Er: YAG laser treatment.All endocrowns were cemented to the prepared teeth using dual-cure resin cement according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cementation, the specimens were subjected to thermal aging. Subsequently, the samples were mounted on a universal testing machine, and pull-out tests were performed to determine their bond strengths. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test (α = 0.05). The significance level was set at p < 0.05.ResultsSurface treatment protocols significantly affected pull-out bond strength (p < 0.001). The highest bond strength was observed in the tribochemical silica coating + silane group, followed by the sandblasting + MMA primer group, with no significant difference between them (p > 0.05). The laser-treated group showed significantly higher values than the control group but significantly lower values than both chemically treated groups (p < 0.05). The untreated control group exhibited the lowest bond strength.ConclusionSurface treatment significantly improves the bonding performance of PEEK endocrowns. Protocols combining micromechanical roughening with chemical priming provided the most favorable bond strength values and may represent more reliable options for adhesive cementation of PEEK restorations.Keywords: Endocrown, PEEK, PEEK endocrown, Pull-out test

Background

Root canal treatment is widely performed in contemporary dental practice. Teeth that require endodontic therapy often present with substantial structural loss due to caries, trauma, or previous restorative preparations. Following endodontic treatment, preservation of the remaining tooth structure, achievement of an adequate coronal seal, maintenance of function, and restoration of acceptable esthetics are essential components that directly influence the long-term success of treatment. These factors collectively determine the biological stability and clinical durability of endodontically treated teeth, underscoring the importance of selecting an appropriate restorative approach that can withstand functional demands while protecting compromised tooth structure [1].

Various restorative approaches have been proposed for endodontically treated teeth with extensive structural loss [2, 3]. Post-core-supported restorations have long been considered the conventional option for teeth exhibiting substantial loss of coronal structure. However, this technique has been reported to increase the risk of root fracture due to stress concentration within the canal walls and the additional removal of tooth structures required during post space preparation [4]. Advancements in adhesive systems and restorative materials, along with the widespread integration of computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies, have led to an increased preference for minimally invasive indirect restoration techniques, such as endocrowns, which eliminates the need for post placement. In this approach, the pulp chamber serves as a macromechanical retention space, allowing the maximum preservation of the remaining tooth structure. Consequently, endocrowns offer a conservative restorative solution capable of providing favorable esthetic and functional outcomes as well as long-term clinical success [5].

The success of endocrown restorations is closely linked not only to cavity preparation and the cementation protocol but also to the mechanical, physical, and adhesive properties of the materials used in their fabrication. Currently, a variety of materials, including composite blocks, lithium disilicate glass ceramics, zirconia, and polyetheretherketone (PEEK), are utilized in the production of endocrowns.

PEEK is a semicrystalline polymer composed of aromatic rings interconnected by ketone and ether functional groups. Developed in 1978, this material was initially introduced for use in engineering applications owing to its exceptional mechanical and thermal properties [5]. It was later adopted in biomedical applications in the 1990s because of its excellent biocompatibility and notable mechanical, thermal, and chemical properties [6, 7].

PEEK has emerged in recent years as a high-performance thermoplastic polymer in dentistry, owing to its semi-crystalline structure that provides resistance to high temperatures, chemical stability conferred by the ether and ketone groups in its aromatic backbone, and excellent biocompatibility [6–8]. Although originally developed in 1978 for engineering applications, this material has attracted increasing attention in the biomedical field because of its low water absorption, high fatigue resistance, and chemical inertness [5, 9, 10].

PEEK exhibits a tensile strength of approximately 80 MPa and a density of approximately 1.3 g/cm³, offering an optimal balance between functional durability and lightweight characteristics [11]. With an elastic modulus of approximately 3–4 GPa, which is remarkably close to that of dentin, PEEK enables a more homogeneous distribution of functional stress in restorative applications. This mechanical compatibility allows forces exerted on the restoration to be transmitted to the natural tooth structure in a more biomimetic manner [8, 12]. This property provides a significant mechanical advantage in applications such as post–core systems, endocrowns, implant abutments, and components of removable prostheses [13–15]. In addition, its radiolucent nature offers an added clinical advantage by facilitating the evaluation and follow-up of treatment outcomes [6].

Despite its many advantageous properties, PEEK’s low surface energy and chemically inert nature make it difficult to achieve effective bonding with resin-based dental materials [16]. This limitation represents one of the primary challenges restricting its use as an endocrown material in dentistry [17]. Researchers have emphasized that achieving a reliable adhesive interface requires both micromechanical and chemical modifications of the PEEK surface [16, 18]. Accordingly, several techniques have been proposed, including sandblasting, acid etching, plasma treatment, laser irradiation, tribochemical silica coating followed by silanization, and application of adhesive systems or primers [19–23].

Sandblasting of PEEK surfaces is typically performed using 50–110 μm Al₂O₃ particles applied from a distance of 10–15 mm at a pressure of approximately 2.7 atm. This procedure increases the surface roughness, removes organic contaminants, and creates a microretentive topology that facilitates micromechanical interlocking [24]. The methyl methacrylate (MMA)–based primer applied after sandblasting induces partial surface dissolution and swelling through the interaction of its low-molecular-weight monomers with the polymer chains of PEEK. This modification enhances the chemical affinity between the substrate and resin-based materials, thereby promoting chemical bonding. In combination with the micromechanical retention generated by sandblasting, this dual mechanism significantly improves the overall bond strength [25].

In the tribochemical silica coating technique, silica-coated particles are projected onto the surface following airborne-particle abrasion, creating a reactive interlayer that enhances micromechanical retention while enabling chemical interactions. Unlike conventional sandblasting procedures that commonly use 50–110 μm Al₂O₃ particles, tribochemical systems such as CoJet employ 30 μm silica-coated Al₂O₃ particles specifically designed to simultaneously roughen the surface and deposit a silica-rich layer. This smaller particle size may also help reduce excessive surface damage while maintaining effective surface modification. Subsequently, the silanization step allows the applied silane to undergo a condensation reaction between the silanol groups on the treated surface and the resin monomers. This combined mechanism further reinforces the bond strength between the material [19, 26].

Laser applications represent modern and effective methods to modify the surface characteristics of PEEK and enhance its bonding capacity with resin-based materials. As high-energy photon sources, lasers can alter the roughness and wettability of polymer surfaces, thereby improving both the micromechanical retention and chemical reactivity. When the laser beam is absorbed by the surface layer, localized heating occurs, and the material may reach the melting or vaporization temperatures, resulting in selective ablation of the surface substrate [22]. This process generates controlled microstructures on the surface topography, whereas photons with sufficient energy can break chemical bonds within the polymer chains, thereby modifying the surface chemistry. As a result, the formation of polar and reactive functional groups is promoted, leading to an increase in the surface energy [27, 28]. Among the advantages of laser treatments are their ability to preserve the structural integrity of the material, provide high-resolution surface modification, and allow rapid application. In addition, they are considered relatively low-cost and reliable alternatives. Therefore, laser irradiation is regarded as a valuable method for PEEK surface modification [28, 29].

This study was conducted to comparatively evaluate the effects of different surface treatment protocols on the pull-out bond strength of PEEK endocrown restorations. It was hypothesized that different surface treatment methods would significantly influence bonding performance, and that protocols combining micromechanical roughening with chemical surface modification would yield superior bond strength compared with other approaches. The findings of this study may contribute to evidence-based material selection and surface preparation strategies aimed at improving the long-term clinical success of PEEK-based endocrown restorations.

Methods

Study design and specimens

This in vitro experimental study compared the effects of different surface treatment protocols on the pull-out bond strength of CAD/CAM-fabricated PEEK endocrown restorations. Power analysis was performed using G*Power v3.1.9.2 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität, Düsseldorf, Germany) with an effect size of f = 0.40, α = 0.05, and a power of 0.80. The analysis indicated that a minimum of 12 specimens per group were required; therefore, to compensate for potential specimen loss, 15 samples were included in each group, resulting in a total of 60 specimens.

Extracted, caries-free, crack-free human mandibular molars obtained for various clinical reasons were used in this study. The teeth were disinfected in 0.1% thymol solution and stored in distilled water at 4 °C until testing.

Ethical approval

This in vitro study was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Dicle University (Protocol No: 2025-83). Extracted human teeth were obtained for clinical reasons, anonymized prior to use, and handled in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Endodontic treatment and preparation

A standard endodontic access cavity was prepared for all teeth. Working length was established, and root canal instrumentation was performed using the Reciproc R25 (VDW GmbH, Munich, Germany) reciprocating file system. During the instrumentation, 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and distilled water were used for irrigation. Root canal obturation was completed using a resin-based sealer and gutta-percha via the lateral condensation technique. After obturation, the pulp chamber was cleaned and a composite resin base was placed to standardize the cavity floor. Endocrown preparations were then completed with a standardized occlusal reduction of 2.0 mm, an axial wall height of 2.0 mm, a pulp chamber depth of approximately 4.0 mm, and a butt-joint cavo-surface margin design. All endodontic procedures, tooth preparations, and cementation steps were performed by a single experienced operator following a predefined protocol to ensure standardization and minimize operator-related variability (Fig. 1A).

A Standardized occlusal preparation of an endodontically treated human molar tooth for endocrown restoration, (B) Digital CAD design of the PEEK endocrown generated using the exocad software, (C) Cemented PEEK endocrown specimen embedded in an epoxy resin block prior to pull-out bond strength testing

CAD/CAM fabrication

Following tooth preparation, the specimens were scanned using an intraoral scanner (Trios 4; 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark). The restorations were designed using CAD software (Exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) and subsequently transferred to a CAM unit (Yena D15, Yenadent, Türkiye) for milling (Fig. 1B). All endocrowns were fabricated using high-performance PEEK blocks (BioHPP®, Bredent GmbH, Germany).

Groups and surface treatment protocols

The PEEK endocrowns were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 15):

Group C (Control)

No surface treatment was applied.

Group T (Tribochemical Silica Coating + Silanization)

Tribochemical silica coating was performed using 30 μm silica-coated Al₂O₃ particles (CoJet™, 3 M ESPE, Germany) at 2.5 bar pressure from a distance of 10 mm for 15 s. Silanization was subsequently carried out using a silane coupling agent (ESPE-Sil, 3 M ESPE, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. According to the manufacturer’s safety data sheet, ESPE-Sil contains ethanol (> 95 wt%) as solvent, 3-methacryloxypropyltrimethoxysilane (< 3 wt%) as the active silane coupling monomer, and butanone (< 2 wt%).

Group S (Sandblasting + MMA-based Primer)

Sandblasting was performed using 50 μm Al₂O₃ particles at 2.5 bar pressure from a distance of 10 mm for 15 s. MMA and pentaerythritol triacrylate-containing primers (Visio.link®, Bredent GmbH) were applied to the surface with a microbrush and polymerized for 90 s using a light-emitting diode (LED) curing unit.

Group L (Er: YAG Laser)

Erbium: yttrium–aluminum–garnet (Er: YAG) laser irradiation was performed using a laser device (LightWalker AT, Fotona, Slovenia) at a wavelength of 2940 nm with parameters set at 200 mJ energy, 10 Hz frequency, and 1.5 W power. The laser was applied at a distance of 10 mm for 10 s with continuous air–water spray-cooling.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

Representative specimens from each group were selected for (SEM) evaluation to qualitatively assess surface morphology following the applied surface treatment protocols.

Cementation

All endocrowns were cemented onto the prepared teeth using a dual-cure resin cement system (Panavia V5, Kuraray Noritake, Japan) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. According to the manufacturer, the cement paste contains Bis-GMA, TEGDMA, hydrophobic aromatic dimethacrylate, hydrophilic aliphatic dimethacrylate, initiators/accelerators, and inorganic fillers (approximately 38 vol%; particle size: 0.01–12 μm). The functional monomer 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10-MDP), which may significantly influence adhesive performance, is present in Panavia V5 Tooth Primer; however, it is not listed as a constituent of the cement paste itself. Tooth-side conditioning was performed using Panavia V5 Tooth Primer, whereas restoration-side conditioning was carried out according to the assigned experimental surface treatment protocol for each group. Excess luting material was carefully removed prior to polymerization, and the restorations were subsequently light-cured for 40 s.

Thermal aging

After cementation, the specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h and subsequently subjected to thermal aging in a thermocycling device (SD Mechatronik Thermocycler, Germany). A total of 5000 cycles were performed between 5 and 55 °C, with a dwell time of 30 s in each bath and a transfer time of 10 s.

Pull-out test

Following thermal aging, the cemented specimens were embedded in epoxy resin blocks, with the coronal portion of each tooth left exposed, to ensure proper stabilization and standardized axial alignment during mechanical testing (Fig. 1C). For clarity of terminology, the measured outcome was defined as pull-out bond strength because the test evaluated the dislodgement resistance of the entire cemented endocrown along its insertion path under axial tensile loading, rather than the bond strength of sectioned specimens as assessed in conventional tensile or microtensile tests. The specimens were then mounted in a universal testing machine (Shimadzu AGS-X, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), and pull-out tests were performed at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min (Fig. 2A). The maximum dislodgement force recorded for each specimen was initially measured in Newtons (N). Pull-out bond strength values were subsequently calculated in megapascals (MPa) by dividing the recorded force by the corresponding bonding surface area. The bonding surface area was defined as the internal surface of the pulp chamber and the axial walls of the endocrown restoration, excluding the occlusal surface, based on the standardized CAD geometry. Surface area values were extracted from the original CAD design files using the surface analysis function of the exocad software (exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany).

Pull-out bond strength testing procedure and representative failure pattern. A Mounting of the cemented specimen in the universal testing machine (Shimadzu AGS-X) prior to pull-out testing, (B) Representative image of an endocrown following debonding from the tooth structure after pull-out testing

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. When the assumptions of normality and homogeneity were met, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test was applied. In cases where these assumptions were not satisfied, the Kruskal–Wallis test and Dunn’s post-hoc test were used. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The inert nature and low surface energy of PEEK hinder the establishment of effective chemical bonding with resin-based cements, which may adversely influence its long-term clinical performance. Consequently, researchers have increasingly focused on evaluating and comparing various surface modification techniques aimed at improving the adhesive interaction between PEEK and dental resin materials with the goal of enhancing the material’s overall clinical applicability [8, 18]. Although previous studies have established that surface modification can improve bonding to PEEK, direct evidence regarding thermally aged PEEK endocrown restorations tested under pull-out loading remains limited.

In this in vitro study, the effects of different surface treatment protocols on the pull-out bond strength of PEEK endocrown restorations were compared. The findings demonstrated that all surface modification techniques produced statistically significant differences in the bond strength. The highest mean bond strength was observed in Group T, whereas Group C exhibited the lowest values. Based on these results, the null hypothesis was rejected, confirming that surface modification of PEEK significantly enhanced its bond strength.

Unlike many previous studies based on flat PEEK specimens and conventional shear bond tests, the present study evaluated surface treatment performance in CAD/CAM-fabricated endocrown restorations using a pull-out retention model after thermal aging, thereby providing a more clinically relevant assessment of adhesive behavior.

Although Groups T, S, and L demonstrated favorable bond strength values, relatively wide data dispersion was observed in some groups. This may be attributed to the inherent biological variability of extracted human teeth, including differences in dentin morphology, pulp chamber anatomy, and substrate quality. Minor variations in restoration fit, cement film thickness, and individual surface response to the applied treatment protocols may also have contributed. Despite careful standardization, a certain degree of variability is expected in studies using natural tooth specimens. This variability further supports the importance of testing clinically shaped restorations rather than simplified flat specimens alone.

Surface treatments applied to PEEK play a critical role in enhancing bond strength by optimizing its interaction with adhesive systems. These procedures aim to increase the inherently low surface energy of PEEK, create surface roughness, and introduce chemically reactive sites that facilitate adhesion. Alterations in the surface topography promote more effective contact between the resin cement and substrate, thereby strengthening micromechanical interlocking. Additionally, the chemical modification of the surface increases the likelihood of covalent interactions between the polymer chains and adhesive monomers, contributing to improved long-term bond stability. These advantages are particularly relevant for endocrown restorations, in which retention largely depends on the quality and durability of the adhesive interface [18].

Stawarczyk et al. reported that the application of adhesive systems in combination with surface modification procedures resulted in a significant increase in the bond strength on pretreated PEEK surfaces [30]. Another study similarly demonstrated that various techniques, such as tribochemical silica coating, laser roughening, and chemical surface modifications, alter the surface morphology of PEEK, thereby enhancing both micromechanical and chemical adhesion and resulting in a significant increase in the bond strength [16]. These findings from the literature are consistent with the results of the present study, confirming that surface modification plays a decisive role in the success of restorations—such as endocrowns—that rely heavily on adhesive bonding. While these mechanisms have been previously described, the present study extends this knowledge by demonstrating their practical influence on retention behavior in endocrown restorations rather than on simplified laboratory substrates. Notably, previous research has reported that appropriate surface treatments not only enhance the initial bond strength, but also help maintain bond performance under conditions of thermal cycling and mechanical loading, underscoring their importance for long-term adhesion stability [31].

In the present study, thermal aging was used to evaluate the pull-out bond strength of PEEK endocrown restorations under conditions that simulate the oral environment. This method replicates the hydrolytic degradation and structural fatigue that may occur over time because of intraoral temperature fluctuations and moisture. Similar studies have shown that thermal cycling can lead to a significant reduction in PEEK–resin cement bond strength; however, they also indicate that surface roughening and the application of appropriate primers can substantially mitigate this loss, preserving the integrity of the adhesive interface [31, 32]. Some studies have further reported that differences in durability among various surface treatment protocols become more pronounced following thermal aging, indicating that aging procedures may accentuate performance disparities between treatment methods [33].

In the present study, thermal aging appears to have influenced the observed bonding outcomes. Previous investigations have reported that thermal aging may reduce the bond strength between PEEK and resin cement. Although pre-aging values were not evaluated in the present study, the post-aging results demonstrated clear differences in bonding performance among the tested surface treatment protocols, suggesting that thermal aging may help reveal durability-related differences between treatment strategies. In this context, surface modification approaches that combine micromechanical retention with chemical interaction may better maintain the adhesive interface under aging conditions than less effective treatments. Therefore, the thermal aging protocol used in this study may be considered not only a simulation of intraoral temperature changes, but also a useful methodological approach for differentiating the relative post-aging bonding performance of various surface treatment protocols.

Direct comparison of the post-aging pull-out bond strength values obtained in the present study with those reported in previously published PEEK–resin investigations should be approached with caution, as the majority of available studies have been conducted using shear or micro-shear bond strength tests on flat PEEK substrates rather than specimens with anatomically relevant restoration geometry [34–36]. These methodological differences may substantially influence stress distribution patterns and failure modes, thereby limiting direct comparability of the reported outcomes. Nevertheless, studies using similar thermocycling regimens have shown that thermal aging reduces interfacial strength and that post-aging performance depends strongly on the selected adhesive and surface-treatment protocol. For example, Karaokutan et al. [37] reported a mean post-thermocycling shear bond strength of 14.26 MPa for Panavia V5-based bonding to PEEK. Although no universally accepted minimum bond strength threshold has been established specifically for PEEK–resin interfaces, PEEK-related dental bonding studies have frequently interpreted shear bond strength values around or above 10 MPa as a pragmatic indicator of potentially acceptable laboratory bonding performance [18, 38]. Within this context, the post-aging values observed in Groups T, S, and L in the present study may be interpreted as relatively favorable, whereas the control group should be interpreted more cautiously. However, because pull-out and shear-based methods are not directly interchangeable, the relative ranking among treatment groups may be more informative than absolute numerical comparison alone.

Failure mode analysis revealed that all specimens exhibited adhesive failure at the endocrown–tooth interface, with no cohesive fractures observed within either the PEEK endocrowns or the tooth structure. This failure pattern is consistent with the high fracture resistance of PEEK and its elastic modulus, which closely approximates that of dentin, allowing more favorable stress distribution within the restoration–tooth complex [6, 30]. Previous studies have similarly reported that PEEK-based restorations predominantly fail at the adhesive interface rather than through cohesive fracture of the material, suggesting that the intrinsic mechanical strength of PEEK exceeds the strength of the adhesive bond [21, 26]. Consequently, failure mode analysis emerges as a discriminative parameter in studies evaluating surface modification strategies, as improvements in bonding performance are more likely to be reflected in changes at the adhesive interface rather than in material fracture [16]. The exclusive occurrence of adhesive failures indicates that retention of PEEK endocrowns remains primarily interface-dependent, emphasizing the clinical relevance of optimizing surface treatment protocols.

Surface roughening with silica-coated particles produces a thin tribochemical glass layer on the PEEK surface, thereby increasing the surface energy and enhancing micromechanical retention. The subsequent application of silane reacts with this silica-enriched surface, functioning as a chemical coupling agent. Owing to its bifunctional structure, the inorganic siloxane end of the silane molecule bonds to the surface silica, whereas the organic methacrylate end forms covalent bonds with the methacrylate groups present in resin-based adhesives. This combined mechanism strengthens both mechanical interlocking and chemical adhesion, resulting in a pronounced increase in bond strength [26].

In our study, the observation that Group T exhibited the highest pull-out bond strength is consistent with previous literature, emphasizing the effectiveness of this technique, which integrates both micromechanical and chemical adhesion mechanisms on PEEK surfaces. A recently published experimental study reported that a tribochemical silica coating provides high bond strength on low-surface-energy PEEK while maintaining the stability of its structural phase, thereby offering a reliable alternative to acid-etching methods [16]. Similarly, another study demonstrated that the tribochemical silica coating significantly enhanced the cement–dentin bond strength [35]. Within the present endocrown model, tribochemical silica coating yielded the highest post-aging pull-out values, suggesting that combined micromechanical and chemical strategies may be particularly advantageous for restorations relying predominantly on adhesive retention.

The application of an MMA-based primer (Visio.link) following sandblasting represents a strategy that combines both micromechanical and chemical mechanisms to enhance the bonding to PEEK surfaces. The microroughness created by sandblasting provides mechanical retention sites that facilitate the adhesion of the resin cement. Subsequently, the MMA-based primer increases the potential for chemical bonding, as methyl methacrylate monomers diffuse into the polymer chains of the PEEK substrate, inducing partial surface dissolution and swelling that promotes interfacial interaction [39].

Keul et al. investigated the effects of sandblasting and/or etching with a piranha solution on the surface characteristics and bond strength of PEEK. Their findings demonstrated that the application of MMA-based agents, such as Visio.link and Signum PEEK Bond, following sandblasting yielded the highest bond strength values, highlighting the synergistic benefits of combining micromechanical surface alteration with chemical priming [25]. This finding supports the theoretical basis of Group S in the present study, and suggests that similar chemical interaction mechanisms may be applicable to endocrown restorations.

Stawarczyk et al. further reported that the combination of sandblasting and an MMA-based primer significantly increased the PEEK–resin bond strength compared with sandblasting alone, and that this enhanced performance was largely maintained even after thermal aging [40]. Similarly, Kern and Lehmann (2012) demonstrated that MMA-containing adhesives (Visio.link) applied after sandblasting produced the highest bond strength values on PEEK surfaces, both in the short term and following thermal aging, when compared to sandblasting alone, sandblasting combined with silanization, and untreated control groups [41].

In our study, Group S exhibited the second highest pull-out bond strength after Group T; however, the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Consistent with the findings reported in the literature, these results suggest that both approaches may provide comparable bonding performance in PEEK endocrown restorations. The absence of a significant difference between Groups T and S is clinically relevant, as it suggests that MMA-based priming may represent a practical alternative when tribochemical systems are not available.

An additional consideration is whether combining tribochemical silica coating with an MMA-based primer could further improve bonding performance. Although this combined approach may appear promising, the tribochemically treated surface represents a heterogeneous interface containing both silica-reactive regions and residual exposed PEEK areas. Therefore, an additive bonding effect cannot be assumed. While MMA-mediated interaction may benefit exposed polymer regions, it may also be limited by the silica-rich interfacial layer or influence silane-mediated coupling. This concept was beyond the scope of the present study and warrants further investigation.

Er: YAG laser irradiation increases the surface roughness of PEEK by inducing localized ablation and micro-erosion through high-energy short-duration pulses. The 2,940 nm wavelength is efficiently absorbed by the organic matrix, producing a thermomechanically modified surface characterized by a microporous topography. This altered morphology provides mechanical retention sites for the adhesive resin systems. However, although partial melting and rearrangement of polymer chains may elevate the surface energy, the formation of functional chemical groups capable of promoting true chemical adhesion remains limited. This may partially explain why Group L exhibited lower bond strength than Groups T and S in the present study. Consistent with our findings, the literature also notes that while laser irradiation can enhance micromechanical retention, its ability to improve chemical bonding is relatively limited [28].

Arslan and Çağlar reported that in their evaluation of various mechanical and chemical surface modification techniques on PEEK, Er: YAG-laser-treated surfaces exhibited an increase in bond strength proportional to the degree of surface roughness generated [16]. This finding demonstrates the mechanical retention potential of laser treatment, while also indicating that the final bond strength values depend directly on the extent of the surface topographic alteration achieved.

Asik and Ozyilmaz compared the effects of various laser applications on the surface roughness and bond strength of composite veneers with PEEK and polyetherketoneketone (PEKK) materials. Their results showed that all laser protocols increased the surface roughness and improved the bonding performance of PEEK. Notably, the Er: YAG and femtosecond laser groups yielded the highest bond strength values [27]. This outcome supports the potential of Er: YAG laser treatment to enhance micromechanical retention on PEEK surfaces; however, it also suggests that in the absence of additional procedures aimed at promoting chemical adhesion, the overall bonding performance may remain limited. To date, no studies in the available literature have directly compared the effects of Er: YAG laser irradiation on the pull-out bond strength of PEEK with those of other surface modification techniques. In this context, the findings of the present study provide a methodologically original contribution to the existing body of knowledge.

The Er: YAG laser parameters used in the present study (200 mJ, 10 Hz, 1.5 W, 10 s) should also be interpreted in light of previous reports evaluating PEEK surface modification. Earlier studies have shown that laser-induced bond enhancement is highly dependent on the selected energy output, pulse frequency, exposure duration, and cooling conditions, as excessive energy may cause surface melting or microcracking, whereas insufficient energy may produce only limited roughening [27, 29]. Asik and Ozyilmaz [27] reported that Er: YAG laser treatment significantly improved the bond strength of veneering composites to PEEK; however, their testing design, restorative interface, and irradiation settings differed from the present study. Likewise, Jahandideh et al. [29] demonstrated that Er: YAG laser irradiation increased shear bond strength between PEEK and composite veneers under alternative parameter settings. Therefore, the comparatively lower bond strength observed in Group L in the present study should not be interpreted solely as an inherent limitation of Er: YAG laser treatment itself, but may also reflect the specific irradiation parameters used, together with differences in bonding substrate, cementation protocol, and pull-out test configuration. Further studies are needed to optimize Er: YAG laser settings specifically for adhesively cemented CAD/CAM PEEK endocrown restorations. Accordingly, laser treatment may improve retention compared with untreated PEEK, but may be less predictable than protocols combining mechanical roughening with dedicated chemical priming.

Although Er: YAG laser treatment improved bonding compared with untreated surfaces, its routine clinical use may be influenced by equipment cost, availability, and operator-dependent technique sensitivity. Therefore, despite its potential benefits, simpler chairside protocols involving airborne-particle abrasion and chemical priming may currently represent more practical alternatives in many clinical settings.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged more clearly. First, the experiment was conducted under in vitro conditions and therefore cannot fully reproduce the dynamic oral environment, including cyclic mastication, salivary enzymes, biofilm activity, pH fluctuations, and patient-related variability. Second, although thermocycling was performed to simulate aging, no cyclic mechanical loading was included; therefore, the applied protocol may not fully reproduce the long-term thermo-mechanical degradation processes and functional stresses encountered under clinical conditions. In addition, initial (pre-aging) bond strength values were not evaluated in the present study; therefore, the magnitude of bond strength reduction attributable to thermal aging could not be determined directly. Third, only one dual-cure resin cement system and selected surface treatment protocols were evaluated; therefore, the findings cannot be directly generalized to other adhesive systems or treatment combinations. Fourth, the pull-out test is useful for assessing resistance to axial dislodgement forces, but it does not fully represent multidirectional functional loading, rotational moments, or fatigue stresses occurring intraorally. Fifth, no control group using conventional restorative materials, such as fiber-reinforced post-core systems or ceramic-based restorations, was included. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence regarding the optimization of bonding performance within PEEK endocrown restorations rather than as a direct comparison of PEEK with other restorative alternatives. Finally, only one commercially available CAD/CAM PEEK material from a single manufacturer was tested, and differences among formulations, fillers, or fabrication methods may influence bonding behavior.

Accordingly, the results of the present study should be interpreted within these limitations, and further in vitro and clinical investigations, including direct comparisons with conventional restorative materials, are required before broad clinical extrapolation can be made.

Nevertheless, this study possesses several notable strengths. It is among the first known investigations to directly compare different surface treatment methods applied to PEEK endocrown restorations with respect to their pull-out bond strength following thermal aging. The use of a standardized CAD/CAM fabrication protocol, uniform cementation procedure, and an identical testing methodology across all groups enhances the internal validity of the findings. Furthermore, the incorporation of thermal aging provides a more clinically relevant assessment of the durability of the adhesive interface under conditions that approximate intraoral temperature fluctuations. Collectively, these methodological advantages allow this study to offer original and clinically informative data that may guide the selection of appropriate surface treatment strategies for PEEK endocrown restorations.

Future research should consider broader experimental designs that incorporate different PEEK manufacturing techniques and material modifications as well as a wider range of resin cements and adhesive systems. In addition to the pull-out test, the use of hybrid testing protocols that include multidirectional mechanical loading, such as bending, flexural, and torsional forces, would provide a more comprehensive assessment of restoration performance. Moreover, the implementation of advanced clinical simulation methods, including long-term thermomechanical aging and pH cycling, may offer deeper insights into the multidimensional behavior of the adhesive interface under conditions that more closely replicate the oral environment.

Conclusion

Surface treatment significantly influences the retention performance of PEEK endocrown restorations after thermal aging. Protocols combining micromechanical roughening with chemical conditioning provided the most favorable bonding outcomes. In particular, tribochemical silica coating followed by silanization and sandblasting followed by MMA-based priming may be considered preferable options when adhesive cementation of PEEK endocrowns is planned in clinical practice. Er: YAG laser treatment also improved bonding compared with untreated surfaces, although its effectiveness was lower than that of chemically assisted protocols. Within the limitations of this in vitro study, appropriate surface conditioning appears essential for optimizing the adhesive performance of PEEK endocrown restorations. Further long-term laboratory and clinical studies are required to confirm these findings.
