Impact of UV annealing on the hole effective mobility in SnO pFET
Authors: Shi-Hao Zeng, Pheiroijam Pooja, Jiancheng Wu, Albert Chin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUsing ultraviolet (UV) annealing through wide energy bandgap HfO2/SiO2 gate dielectric, nanosheet SnO pFET achieved hole effective mobility (µeff) from 55 cm2/V-s at low hole density (Qh) to 13.38 cm2/V-s at 5 × 1012 cm-2 Qh, compared to that of 9.03 cm2/V-s at 5 × 1012 cm-2 Qh for SnO device without UV annealing. This is the highest µeff among oxide semiconductor pFETs at high Qh, which is required to realize low-power high-density monolithic 3D CMOS logic. This requires excellent surface roughness, good uniformity and free-from grain boundaries that is beyond the thermally-annealed poly-Si. Excellent on-current/off-current (ION/IOFF) value of 1.05 × 105 were measured simultaneously in the UV-annealed SnO pFET, which is due to the ultra-thin 8 nm thick SnO nanosheet channel to pinch off the channel leakage. From X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis, the 48% µeff improvement by UV irradiation is due to increased Sn2+ and decreased Sn0. Such high µeff at high Qh, large ION/IOFF, smooth surface, good uniformity and low thermal budget process are the enabling technologies for monolithic 3D CMOS.Keywords: effective mobility, transistor, SnO, surface roughness, UV annealSubject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Electronic devices

Introduction

Following the commercialization of high mobility amorphous InGaZnO in the early 2000s as switching components for display backplanes, oxide semiconductor- based n-type field-effect transistor (nFET) has attracted substantial attention1,2. We reported on quasi-two-dimensional (2D) thickness nanocrystalline n-type SnON transistor on SiO2 with µeff outperforming the single crystalline InGaAs, Si and phonon-scattering-limited 2D MoS2 nFETs3,4. To realize low-power Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (CMOS) on SiO2 and to be useful for ultra-low power monolithic three-dimensional (3D) integration, the p-type FET (pFET) must be developed as shown in Fig. 1. Although high hole mobility pFET is reported using thermally-annealed poly-Si, the surface roughness and grain boundaries are the major concerns for low thermal-budget monolithic 3D integrated circuits (ICs), which have a significant impact on the transistor’s effective mobility (µeff), threshold voltage (Vth), and sub-threshold swing (SS)5. Authors have worked on poly-Si surface optimization using furnace annealing at the expense of long process time6. On the other hand, oxide semiconductor-based pFETs have much better uniformity and simpler process than laser-annealed pFETs. Tin monoxide (SnO), one of several p-type materials, is regarded as a potential p-type semiconductor due to its efficient hole carrier generation caused by the small formation energy of Sn vacancies7. Delocalization of SnO at the valance band caused by the hybridization of Sn 5s and O 2p orbitals results in a low hole effective mass (0.17~ 0.23 m0) and favorable hole-transport characteristics8,9. SnO pTFTs have a wide range of potential applications in various advanced technologies, particularly required for low DC-power Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (CMOS) logic, the basic unit for integrated circuit (IC). However, high performance are critical factors to decrease the size difference of nFET and pFET in CMOS inverters. The instability of the SnO phase is a major obstacle for high-performance SnO FETs at high temperatures which can produce undesirable secondary phases, metallic Sn, SnO2, and different flaws, and leads to poor switching performance of FETs10. The usage of ultraviolet (UV) annealing has the benefit of its low process temperature, which reduces possible issues of atom diffusion and defect production that are often brought at higher processing temperatures11. The UV irradiation generated by a Hg lamp at 185 and 254 nm possess high-energy 6.7 and 4.9 eV light rays of capable of producing active oxygen radicals (O∗) which has higher diffusivity as well as reactivity at lower temperatures than O2 and O11,12. The active oxygen radicals are generated via reaction of UV light and the O2 in air. Thus, UV irradiation can provide extra energy for forming the Sn–O bond at a low processing temperature to create SnO at a low thermal budget. To address the above problems and improve the quality of p-SnO channel layer FET, UV irradiation is employed through a high dielectric-constant (high-κ) HfO2 gate dielectric.

A high µeff value of 13.38 cm2/V-s at a high 5 × 1012 cm−2 hole charge density (Qh), a sharp SS of 274 mV/dec, and on-current/off-current (ION/IOFF) value of 1.05 × 105 is achieved. The UV annealing on SnO pFET provides better transistor’s mobility, larger ION/IOFF and sharper SS than control device without UV annealing. These improvements are related to SnO bonding from XPS analysis. The satisfactory performance of devices thus facilitates the construction of low thermal-budget monolithic 3D ICs on the backend dielectric of IC chips13.

Results and discussion

The cross-sectional scanning electron microscope (SEM) image and schematic diagram of the UV-annealed HfO2/SiO2/SnO stack on SiO2/Si is displayed in Fig. 2a and b respectively. The layer thicknesses were previously calibrated by ellipsometry and transmission electron microscope (TEM) carefully9,10. Here, SEM was used due to fast turnaround time. Therefore, the image clearness is slightly inferior to TEM9,10. A uniform SnO ultra-thin thickness nanosheet layer of 8 nm was detected. Gate dielectric with high-κ14 HfO2 of 45 nm was deposited to lower the operating voltage. The SiO2 with 7 nm thickness was grown between the channel as well as gate dielectric, to limit the remote phonon scattering from the high-κ HfO2 gate dielectric. The surface morphology of UV-annealed SnO samples is studied by AFM images, as shown in Fig. 2c and d using 5 μm × 5 μm scans using DIMENSION 3100. The roughness of the UV-annealed SnO was as smooth as 0.69 nm and free from any grain boundary. Such excellent smooth surface of UV-annealed SnO is significantly much better than laser-annealed poly-Si5,15 and it is crucial for monolithic 3D ICs. In contrast to the laser melting poly-Si process, the low thermal-budget SnO is also important for monolithic 3D ICs without degrading the underneath CMOS ICs16. The high-energy 6.7 eV (185 nm) UV light dissociates O₂ molecules into atomic oxygen (O). This atomic oxygen reacts with additional O₂ to form ozone (O₃). Subsequently, ozone can be broken down by 4.8 eV (254 nm) UV light, yielding an excited oxygen atom (O∗) and molecular oxygen (O₂).

(a) SEM image of the UV-annealed HfO2/SiO2/8 nm-SnO stack and (b) device schematic. The UV annealing is applied before Ni electrode formation. Only 254 nm UV light can penetrate HfO2. AFM images wide scans: (c) 3D and (d) 2D spectra of UV-annealed SnO.

Energetic oxygen species can facilitate the surface migration of SnO, leading to a smoother surface. The improved surface roughness highlights the effectiveness of UV annealing. In the FET structure, SnO is covered by a SiO₂/HfO₂ gate stack, and only 254 nm UV light, with energy of 4.88 eV lower than the 5.3–5.7 eV bandgap of HfO₂ can penetrate the gate dielectric and be absorbed by the SnO channel. Moreover, the surface roughness is measured without the HfO2/SiO2.

Capacitance was measured using Agilent E4980A at 1 kHz. The effect of UV annealing was investigated by a metal/insulator/metal (MIM) structure. As shown in Fig. 3a, the gate oxide capacitor (Cox) for control MIM capacitor and UV annealed capacitor are 2.2 × 10−7 F/cm2 and 2.3 × 10−7 F/cm2, respectively. The Qh for a p-type SnO channel pFET was measured by gate to source overlapped capacitor3. As shown in Fig. 3b, well behaved gate-source overlapped capacitance from depletion to accumulation was measured. The SnO is a p-type semiconductor, which has accumulation capacitance for pFET rather than the inversion capacitance in Si pFET with a n-type body. The small leakage current in accumulation mode pFET is due to the ultra-thin body to deplete the channel. Similar measurements were also reported in our previous SnON nFET3.

(a) Capacitance-gate voltage (VG) characteristics of Ni/7 nm-SiO2/45 nm-HfO2/Ni capacitor without (control) and with UV annealing and (b) variation of the overlapped capacitance of SnO/7 nm-SiO2/45 nm-HfO2/Ni pFET with UV annealing.

The top-gate SnO pFETs current-voltage characteristics were measured using HP4155B parameter analyzer and a probe station.

From the output characteristic curve, there is a clear pinch-off and current saturation, suggesting the good FET performance. The UV-annealed SnO FET device displayed higher |ID| compared to control SnO FET. A decrease of saturation current change is due to the decrease of µeff at large VGS. The wide channel length FET (“Fat” FET) is typically used in the test key of IC foundries to extract the mobility and to prevent the channel length modulation effects18. The parasitic resistance for FET is Rparasitic= RsLG/WG, where Rs, LG and WG are the sheet resistance, gate length and gate width, respectively. Because the LG/WG is 50-µm/400-µm and overlapped gate to source and drain structure are used, the Rparasitic was not counted for µeff calculation that may cause an underestimate the mobility value. At a low VD of -0.1 V, the UV-annealed SnO/SiO2/HfO2 FET show higher ION/IOFF of 1.05 × 105 compared to 6.22 × 104 for control SnO. Even higher mobility can be obtained by increasing SnO thickness but trading off the ION/IOFF. The better ION/IOFF suggests the higher µeff, since the IOFF is nearly the same. Such excellent IOFF is due to the ultrathin 8 nm SnO nanosheet channel. The FET’s Vth shifted positively with UV annealing. The Vth has been obtained using extrapolation in the linear region (ELR) method19 as shown in Fig. 4f. Vth is found to be -0.824 V for control SnO FET and − 0.75 V for UV-annealed SnO FET. |Vth| decreases 0.07 V after UV-annealing, and it indicates the lowered charges in SnO. Because high-κ gate dielectric is used, the 0.07 V difference corresponds to Qh of 2.8 × 1011 cm−2 change from ΔQh= C × ΔVth, where ΔVth is the shift in Vth and C is the gate dielectric capacitance per unit area. Such large charge is significant enough to induce higher scattering and lower mobility. The UV-annealed SnO/SiO2/HfO2 FET exhibits a lower SS of 274 mV/dec than control device of 298 mV/dec which suggests the UV-annealing may also improve the gate-dielectric/SnO interface. HfO₂ has a bandgap of 5.3–5.7 eV. The 254 nm UV light emitted by the Hg lamp has an energy lower than the bandgap of HfO2, allowing it to penetrate through and be absorbed by the small energy bandgap SnO layer, thereby reducing defects. This reduction in SnO defects ultimately enhances the transistor’s performance. In contrast, the 185 nm UV light has an energy higher than the HfO₂ bandgap, resulting in significant absorption and an exponential decay within the HfO₂ layer. However, the light intensity of longer 254 nm is stronger than 185 nm, which is the reason why large device mobility improvement is measured.

A highly sensitive approach to assessing material defects is mobility measurement, where electron concentrations of 1010–1012 cm−2 interact with impurities, defects, phonons, surface roughness, and even remote phonon scattering from the high-κ gate dielectric20. This provides a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing device performance. Although High-Resolution TEM successfully captured the 1.1 nm SiO2 layer21, it remains insufficient for detecting impurity and defect scatterings, which can be better analyzed through µeff measurements. This is due to the high density of inversion electrons (1010–1012 cm−2) transporting through the channel, where they are scattered by impurities and defects. Figure 5a shows the µeff versus Qh for UV-annealed and control SnO pFETs4. The µeff is as high as 55 cm2/V-s at low Qh and decreases with increasing Qh, which is generally observed for a MOSFET. At a high Qh of 5 × 1012 cm−2, µeff of UV-annealed SnO is 13.38 cm2/V-s while µeff of control SnO is 9.03 cm2/V-s. The µeff of UV-annealed SnO pFET is significantly higher than that in control SnO. Such a high Qh is necessary for IC operation to increase the switching speed. It is known that the µeff in the low and medium Qh are limited by the coulomb and phonon scatterings. For both SnO pFETs, the nearly flat µeff from 1 × 1012 cm−2 to 5 × 1012 cm−2 indicates the severe charged defects inside SnO layer, which is also found in SnO2 nFET4. The µFE of control SnO pFET without UV annealing is comparable with reported data in the literature9,10. The UV-annealed SnO pFET showed higher µFE of 13.33 cm2/V-s compared to 8.49 cm2/V-s of control SnO pFET. Therefore, the higher µeff in UV-annealed SnO pFET is due to decrease in bulk charged defects in SnO pFET, which is consistent with the decreased |Vth|. The UV-annealed SnO pFET device exhibited excellent reproducibility even after 30 days, as shown in Fig. 5b. The Vth shift is -0.7 V and SS changes to 254 mV/dec for UV annealed sample after 30 days, as shown in Fig. 5b. Table 1 presents the performance comparison of reported data with our work. Record high µeff or µFE is reached among oxide semiconductors, while the low thermal budget process can ensure little degradation on underneath Si CMOS16.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze the atomic composition and chemical states of Sn in SnOx thin films in order to study the effect of UV irradiation, as shown in Fig. 6a–e using Thermo Fisher Scientific Theta Probe XPS. Using the binding energy of C1s fixed at 284.8 eV as a reference, the binding energies of the elements under inspection were calibrated. Sn0, Sn2+, and Sn4+ were the three possible states of Sn that were taken into consideration for the deconvolution of the measured spectra. Sn0, Sn2+, and Sn4+ of SnOx are represented by the peaks with binding energies of 484.1, 485.6, and 486.6 eV, respectively11 (Fig. 6b–). To assist in understanding the impact of SnO without UV treatment, with UV treated SnO and also UV treated SnO covered with SiO2, the calculated Sn ionic contents in the SnOx films are summarized in Table 2. Small energy-bandgap material Sn2+O2− conducts holes rather than electrons9. From the XPS analysis results, we can see that the proportion of Sn2+ is about 63.48% when it is not UV annealed (Fig. 6c). However, after UV annealing and SiO2 covering, Sn2+ content is increased to 72.32% (Fig. 6b). When the channel is not covered with an oxide layer and is subjected to UV annealing, Sn reacts with moisture in the air to form SnO2, so the proportion of Sn4+ raised to 17.39% (Fig. 6d). This is because the lattice energy of SnO is 3652 kJ mol-1 and SnO2 is 11,807 kJ mol-125, and accordingly, it is favorable to form SnO2 under oxygen and moisture ambient. After the UV annealing and SiO2 covering, the proportion of Sn4+ dropped to 2.28%, as Sn4+ contribute to formation of Sn4+O2-2 n-type semiconductor3,4,9,11 and cannot contribute to hole mobility. Moreover, Sn0 peak related to oxygen loss from UV annealing owing to the weak bonding energy of Sn2+O2− is reduced with SiO2 covering as seen from Fig. 6a-e; Table 2. The Sn0 is lowered by UV annealing, which is related to excitation of electrons into an energetic state to bond with the O atom and form SnO and covering with SiO2 avoid Sn to react with moist in the air to form SnO2. Therefore, the excellent transistor performance improvement by UV irradiation is due to increased Sn2+ and decreased Sn0 and Sn4+ thereby improving the p-type conductivity. Favorable device integrity was obvious in the satisfactory µFE, µeff sharp SS, and large ION/IOFF values after UV irradiation covered with SiO2 and was well-supported by the XPS data. From the XPS analysis in Fig. 6; Table 2, the sputtered SnO under oxygen flow can form SnO, SnO2 and Sn0. When Sn0 receives either thermal or UV energy, it can bond with the O atom to form SnO. However, the Sn0 can also diffuse into gate dielectric during thermal annealing10. In contrast, the UV can directly excite the electrons into an energetic state to bond with the O atom and form SnO. Experiment

The top-gate SnO pFETs were synthesized firstly by growing a 500 nm thick SiO2 layer on p-Si wafer. Then, 8 nm thick SnO layer was formed using reactive sputtering from a Sn target with 25 W DC power under O2 partial pressure of 25% with fixed Ar flow rate of 24 sccm, at pressure of 7.6 mTorr followed by post-annealing using rapid thermal annealing (RTA) for 30 s at 200 °C. Next, 30 nm Ni, a high work-function metal was deposited to form the drain and source electrodes. Next, 7 nm SiO2 interfacial layer and 45 nm HfO2 gate dielectric was deposited by e-beam evaporation at a rate of 0.2 Å/sec. UV-annealing was carried out through wide energy bandgap HfO2/SiO2 gate dielectric in air ambient for 10 min using a UV source of 79 mW/cm2 at 254 nm besides 11 mW/cm2 at 185 nm11. Finally, 30 nm patterned Ni gate electrode was deposited. The gate length and width were 50 and 400 μm, respectively.

Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the effects of UV annealing on the electrical performance of p-type SnO pFET. UV-annealing of nanosheet SnO pFET achieved hole µeff from 55 cm2/V-s at low Qh to 13.38 cm2/V-s at 5 × 1012 cm−2 Qh, compared to that of 9.03 cm2/V-s at 5 × 1012 cm−2 Qh for SnO device without UV annealing. High ION/IOFF value of 1.05 × 105 were obtained in the UV-annealed SnO pFET, which is due to the ultra-thin 8 nm thick SnO nanosheet channel to pinch off the channel leakage. From the XPS analysis, 48% µeff improvement by UV annealing is due to increased Sn2+ and decreased Sn0. Such low temperature process device is essential for monolithic 3D CMOS logic.
