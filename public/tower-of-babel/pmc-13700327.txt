So what they do can be seen – A realist-informed evaluation of and theory refinement for a digital counseling tool to support family caregivers in Austria
Authors: Katharina Gabl, Theresa Clement, Jasmin Eppel-Meichlinger, Hanna Mayer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFamily Caregivers (FC) make a significant contribution to maintaining our care system and must be supported in their vital roles. Alles Clara (AC) is a digital service in Austria that addresses FCs, their challenges, and needs. This study aimed at evaluating the intended impact of AC and to refine its underlying program theory explaining how the impact comes about.MethodsFollowing a realist approach to evaluation, we considered multiple perspectives and data sources, including four online surveys (S1 n = 52, S2 n = 16, S3 n = 89, S4 n = 169), in-app tracking data, narrative interviews with FCs (n = 8) and two focus group interviews with counselors (n = 10). Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, qualitative data were analyzed thematically and interpretatively. A confirmatory approach to theory building was chosen to test and refine the initial program theory.ResultsThe theory on how AC unfolds its impact is presented in two configurational models. FCs can access the digital service anonymously and independently of time and place. In digital counseling, FCs can build a trusting relationship with the counselors and thus receive person-centred counseling. They no longer feel alone with their situation, move on from problems to next steps and exit the counseling room strengthened. AC also has an impact on counselors, especially nursing staff. The counselors are empowered to provide person-centred counseling and can recognize the quality of their actions, make an impact from a distance and see meaning in their actions. They can show what nursing is and can do and thus develop pride in their work and profession.ConclusionAC reveals a way to introduce the concept of person-centredness into a digital context. In this study, we explored counseling as a nursing intervention that takes place anonymously and distantly in digital space – a space in which what they do can be seen.

Background

When a person becomes in need of care, their own family serves as their first and potential long-term care provider. Worldwide, more than 250 million family caregivers constitute the “backbone of care”, supplying approximately 70–90% of the essential support required by individuals experiencing physical and mental health challenges, disabilities, or age-related frailty [1–3]. In Austria, around 10% of people were involved in caring for relatives in 2018 and three quarters of the population can rely on support and care from family members and loved ones if necessary [4]. This makes family care by far the nation’s largest care service, without which home care in particular would not be possible [4]. In recent decades, the scientific discourse around the challenges and needs of family caregivers has increased significantly. It has been shown that caring activities can have a positive impact on family members if the right support is provided [4, 5]. However, almost half of the people who provided family care in Austria felt strongly burdened and more than a third stated that their health suffered as a result of their care work [4, 6, 7].

In line with this group’s need for support, more and more interventions are being developed that (co-)address family caregivers, such as education measures or peer support programs [8–10]. Nevertheless, family caregivers repeatedly describe difficulties in accessing such services [10]. This is partly due to a lack of financial and time resources, in addition to healthcare services generally prioritizing those in need of care [10, 11]. In the Austrian context, the federal governmental structure further shapes access by contributing to regional differences in the availability and organization of caregiver support [12]. To meet these challenges, support services for family caregivers need to be flexible, low-threshold, easily accessible and tailored to the needs of those affected [6, 10, 13]. With the increasing use of modern technology and digitalization in our daily lives, eHealth services are rapidly making their way into healthcare [14, 15] and show great potential for supporting family caregivers as well [16–19]. Technology-based interventions can provide accessible, self-paced support to family caregivers [10, 20]. However, there are still challenges to overcome, such as ensuring that eHealth services are customized to individual needs, providing personalized support [11, 21].

Despite the increasing use of eHealth services, it is a challenge to capture their effectiveness and impact. Current evaluation measures focus primarily on parameters such as acceptance and usability, but it remains unclear how these services work [22, 23]. This particularly applies to interventions that address family caregivers, as inquiries predominantly focus on the health outcomes of patients or the perspective of healthcare professionals [10]. In general, family caregiver outcomes tend to be reported secondarily to patient outcomes, which is why specific effects of interventions on family caregivers are still poorly understood [24]. Family caregivers make a significant contribution to maintaining our care system, which cannot be easily replaced by professional actors [4]. In the spirit of their service to family and society, family caregivers must be perceived, addressed, strengthened, and supported as a group in their own right [4, 24].

Alles Clara is a digital counseling service developed to address the specific needs and challenges of family caregivers in Austria. Operated by a non-profit organization, the platform was launched in July 2022 and functions as a cross-sectoral initiative involving scientific, care, public, and private sectors. Currently in its pilot phase, Alles Clara provides free online counseling to family caregivers across Austria and is continuously building up its user base (e.g. through cooperation with Austrian companies communicating Alles Clara to their employees, care sector partnerships, etc.).

Clement et al. [23] provided an initial program theory (IPT) presenting the intended impact of Alles Clara. The IPT serves as a basis for understanding, evaluating, and monitoring the digital service. The core intervention of this program is professional counseling provided by experts from the fields of nursing and psychology in a data-secure messenger tool, the Alles Clara app.

Following registration, family caregivers request counseling and are matched with professional counselors in a secure, private one-to-one counseling space within the app. Communication between family caregivers and their respective counselors occurs primarily through asynchronous text-based messaging, supplemented by telephone and video options as part of the counseling process. This format allows caregivers to communicate flexibly while having the ability to revisit conversations. Counseling topics are not predetermined but emerge from caregivers’ specific concerns across all care situations. In counseling, family caregivers seeking advice should be given the opportunity to reflect on their situation, discover new ways and possibilities, receive appreciation, and establish a trusting relationship with their counselor. This way, counseling in Alles Clara intends to relieve the burden on family caregivers. In addition, characteristics of the Alles Clara app are intended to have an impact on family caregivers. The color scheme, language, design, functionality, and data protection concept aim at making family caregivers feel safe, addressed, and comfortable [23].

The Alles Clara program also shows potential to enhance job satisfaction in online counselors by providing a new professional environment, characterized by a healthful work culture. Counselors are not directly employed by Alles Clara but are healthcare professionals from Austrian healthcare organizations who provide counseling as an additional, flexible activity. They possess a minimum of three years of professional experience in their respective fields and undergo specialized online counseling training prior to their involvement with Alles Clara. To recognize and shape the meaningfulness and quality of their work positively affects their job satisfaction. Thus, nurses in particular should benefit from being part of Alles Clara [23].

Against this background, this study aimed at evaluating the intended impact of Alles Clara and to refine its underlying IPT [23]. The overall objective of this inquiry was to answer the question ‘what works, for whom and why, under what circumstances’ [25].

Methods

Methodological approach

The complexity of the Alles Clara program and the need for transferable evaluation insights asked for a suitable paradigm. We chose to follow the ontological understanding of critical realism (CR). As Alderson [26] describes, CR seeks to explain how and why interventions reach their intended impact, especially when it comes to improving health outcomes. It is about appreciating “that open systems work in complex cause-effect chains” [26 p146], focusing on transformative change within it. In accordance with this ontological understanding, we chose to follow the Realist Evaluation (RE) approach as proposed by Pawson and Tilley [25]. RE is a theory-driven approach aiming at answering the question what works, for whom and under what circumstances [27].

Study design

Pawson and Tilley describe the distinctive about a realist study design as a “realist evaluation cycle” [25 p84]. The starting point of the circumnavigation of inquiry is the theory formulation from which testable hypotheses are derived. After turning to observations (data collection and analysis) to test these assumptions, the circle closes with the program specification and refinement of the previous theory (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study phases mapped on the “realist evaluation cycle” by Pawson & Tilley [21]

In the first phase of this project, we formulated the IPT of the Alles Clara program [23]. The IPT demonstrates the intended outcomes of the intervention and reveals the mechanisms and context that may lead to the impact of Alles Clara [23]. In accordance with this logic of change initiation, underlying cause-effect relationships were formulated as context (C) -mechanism (M) -outcome (O) -configurations (CMOc) to inform the evaluation process and serve as testable hypotheses. The second phase involves testing the IPT including all field observations conducted during the pilot phase of Alles Clara. Concluding the project, the third phase involves refining the IPT, based on the phase two findings. In this paper, we report on the results of phase two and three.

Following the RE approach and in line with the complexity of the program, we considered multiple perspectives and data sources for data collection and analysis and synthesized them [28]. The data collection included both qualitative (individual and focus group interviews) and quantitative methods (in-app data tracking and surveys). Data collection methods

As part of ongoing product development, Alles Clara routinely collects user data from app users (family caregivers and counselors). Selected routine data were provided in anonymized form to the research team for analysis and interpretation within the framework of this evaluation project.

In-app tracking data provided insights on user’s characteristics (e.g. gender and age) and therefore served to describe the Alles Clara target group in more detail. The application-related data (e.g. registrations, active consultations, time stamps, message count, consultation duration, etc.) was used to examine user/ usage behavior.

Four surveys were created for data collection. These were developed based on established and proven measurement instruments with the aim of measuring endpoints, intermediate outcomes, and mechanisms of action in accordance with the initial program theory [4, 29–36].

Questions in Survey 1 focus on family caregivers’ sociodemographic data, their practical experience with digital services and use of the Alles Clara app. It also captures their perspective on the counseling at Alles Clara. In Survey 2 we asked counselors about their type and extent of employment. Context related aspects, such as professional environment, workplace culture and their personal perspective on counseling. In Survey 3, family caregivers can rate their experiences with the counseling in general, the answers to their questions, the response times of the counselors and the communication via messenger. Survey 4 includes questions about the counselors’ own counseling activities as well as the focus, complexity and topics of counseling.

Sampling and recruitment

The sample included family caregivers seeking advice (FC) and Alles Clara counselors (ACC). We recruited family caregivers who were registered in Alles Clara. A purposive sampling strategy was applied to identify participants for qualitative interviews. To be eligible, individuals had to have completed at least one counseling session, regardless of whether their case was active or closed at the time of inclusion. This approach allowed us to target caregivers with direct experience using the service and ensured that participants could provide in-depth reflections The second part of the sample consisted of counselors at Alles Clara, employed with Austrian healthcare organizations. These are qualified nurses who have at least five years of professional experience before working at Alles Clara and clinical and health psychologists and psychotherapists who have at least three years of professional experience. These differing experience requirements were established by Alles Clara and the respective organizations (based on differences in the structure and duration of professional qualification pathways in Austria). For this study, we applied a consistent inclusion criterion: Counselors who had been working at Alles Clara for at least four weeks were invited to participate in the questionnaire and qualitative surveys. Family caregivers and counselors were invited to take part in the evaluation directly in the Alles Clara app, where they received a prompt to complete an online questionnaire immediately after concluding a counseling session, allowing them to share feedback on their experience in context. Additional invitations were sent via email communication (e.g., newsletters and circulars to registered users) managed by the Alles Clara team. Consent for contact was granted through agreement to the platform’s privacy policy within the app. The research team did not have access to the email addresses or personal contact information of either family caregivers or counselors. Respondents could voluntarily provide contact details within the survey if they were interested in participating in follow-up interviews.

Data analysis and theory refinement

To address the overarching evaluation question, data analysis and theory refinement were conducted using multiple methods aligned with the diverse data sources.

Quantitative data analysis

Quantitative data collected during phase two were analyzed descriptively using SPSS Version 27. The aim was to provide a clear overview of participant characteristics, usage experiences, and usage behavior based on questionnaire responses and data tracking metrics.

Qualitative data analysis

Qualitative data were read and re-read, initially coded, and then thematically mapped onto constructs from the IPT by two researchers, first independently, then in discourse to resolve disagreements (KG & TC). A retroductive approach was followed, combining inductive and deductive reasoning to identify key factors associated with the initial CMOc. Special attention was paid to unexpected findings or deviations from the original assumptions, which were critically discussed within the research team. To support analysis and visualization, a virtual whiteboard (Miro®) was used to map emerging themes and interrelations.

Theory refinement process

A joint interpretation of the empirical results from phase two in light of the IPT was essential for refining the program logic. The originally assumed outcomes were first compared with preliminary findings from qualitative and quantitative data. This enabled a deeper understanding of core constructs - such as relief - and led to targeted refinements. The revised outcomes were further contextualized using interview data, which informed the development of case vignettes. Each vignette represented a specific use case and was structured around the counseling purpose, consultation process, and personal user characteristics. This case-based approach revealed diverse patterns in the use of Alles Clara and provided insights into the mechanisms driving its effects.

The refined assumptions about the program’s impact were reorganized into updated CMOc and visualized in an adapted logic model. A confirmatory approach to theory-building, grounded in the principles of CR, was used to test and adjust the IPT. In an iterative process we moved between empirical description and theoretical abstraction, supporting meaningful generalizations and concrete refinements. Analytical techniques employed included retroduction, counterfactual thinking (comparison with alternate explanations) and abstraction (analytical generalization) [37].

Final validation

To reduce potential bias and enhance the credibility of findings, multiple researchers were involved in coding (KG & TC) and interpretation (KG, TC, JEM & HM). All emerging insights were critically reviewed through team discussions and a final “Think Tank” session that served as a logic check of the refined program theory.

The final theory was presented in two structured configurational maps, each illustrating the refined logic of the Alles Clara intervention - one from the perspective of users, the other from the perspective of counselors.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Karl Landsteiner University of Health Sciences (registration number 1082/2020). The study was conducted in accordance with national and international guidelines and adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [38].

Participation in surveys, focus groups, and individual interviews was entirely voluntary. Written informed consent was obtained prior to the qualitative interviews, and participants were assured of their anonymity. Consent could be withdrawn at any time without negative consequences for the participants. Audio recordings were pseudonymized during transcription and deleted afterwards.

Information about participation in the quantitative surveys was initially provided via an information email. Contact details of the project leads were included to address any further questions. Questionnaires from both family caregivers and counselors were submitted anonymously, and consent to data processing was implied by submission of the online survey. Information about the processing of tracking data was provided through the terms of use in the Alles Clara app. Consent was obtained by agreeing to these terms. All data processing procedures complied with applicable data protection regulations. The data were made available to the research team in anonymized form by Alles Clara.

Results

‘What works, for whom and why, under what circumstances’ [25] is uncovered in the synthesis of all findings and revealed in the refined program theory. The results are outlined in two configurational models, 1) seeking advice in Alles Clara and 2) giving advice in Alles Clara, presenting the outcomes of the program along with the mechanisms leading to Alles Clara’s impact. The key feature of a realist evaluation is “that it is explanatory rather than simply descriptive” [39 p13]. However, following a realist logic and to contextualize the results of this investigation, we commence with a description of the app’s user characteristics and basic information about user behavior.

Contextualization

By July 1st, 2023, a total of 681 family caregivers from all over Austria had registered in the Alles Clara app. 43% (n = 295) of family caregivers were between the age of 48 and 59. A smaller portion, 9% (n = 58), were younger than 30 years, while 4% (n = 28) were older than 66 years. 63% (n = 426) identified as female. 52 family caregivers took part in survey 1. Of these, 51% (n = 25) were employed full-time and 37% (n = 18) part-time. Notably, 19% (n = 9) reported having reduced their working hours due to caregiving responsibilities. The majority were caring for a parent or stepparent (35%, n = 13) or a child or stepchild (30%, n = 11). Half of the respondents (50%, n = 17) described themselves as strongly or very strongly burdened by their caregiving role.

26 counselors were working in the Alles Clara team. 22 counselors were qualified nurses. Two counselors each came from the fields of psychology and psychotherapy. In survey 2, a total of 16 counselors shared further insight on their professional background. Their professional experience ranged from 12 to 40 years. More than half (57%, n = 8) reported having obtained additional qualifications in their respective fields.

Tracking data of 180 consultations were included in the evaluation. Survey 3 (family caregivers) took 89 responses. Survey 4 (counselors) was used to collect data on 169 consultations in Alles Clara. Eight family caregivers and ten counselors participated in qualitative interviews. The characteristics of family caregivers surveyed showed heterogeneity in terms of demographics, and especially when it comes to describing the care situation. Refined theory

To develop a specified and in depth understanding of not just whether Alles Clara works, but rather how and why, we related our empirical findings to what was proposed in the IPT. During the refinement process, new hypotheses emerged that were grounded in the empirical data but reflected an evolved theoretical understanding. These revised theoretical propositions are presented alongside the corresponding empirical results in the following sections.

Three levels on which Alles Clara unfolds its effectiveness were described in the IPT: “1) the impact of the Alles Clara app on the family caregivers”, “2) the impact of the counseling on the family caregivers” and “3) the impact of Alles Clara as a working environment on the counselors” [23 p3]. The impact on family caregivers is characterized by the two essential properties of the offer: Alles Clara as a digital tool, and as a counseling service for family caregivers. It is also apparent from the IPT that it is not only the working environment that has an impact on counselors, but also the counseling they provide in this context [19]. Seeking advice in Alles Clara as a family caregiver and giving advice in Alles Clara as a professional counselor emerge to be the core characteristics of this program.

Seeking advice in Alles Clara

Alles Clara did show an impact on family caregivers seeking advice. 91% (n = 72) rated counseling in Alles Clara as good to very good. The statements of family caregivers surveyed revealed aspects that served to deepen and adapt the original assumptions derived from the IPT. In the revised program theory, a) not being seen, b) being seen and c) person-centred counseling emerged as key mechanisms to strengthen family caregivers (see Fig. 2).Fig. 2Seeking advice in Alles Clara - configurational map

The impact of Alles Clara on family caregivers – Mechanism “not being seen”

1. The impact of Alles Clara on family caregivers

a) Mechanism “not being seen”

When family caregivers seeking advice enter the digital counseling room, they are met by a professional counselor. The encounter in digital space is characterized by asynchronous communication - meaning that family caregivers and counselors do not need to be online simultaneously to exchange messages - which has a decelerating effect on those seeking advice and encourages them to self-reflect. This “slows down this dynamic - that you have to have an answer and a solution to everything in life right away” (FC05).

Counselors are perceived as experts and share valuable knowledge with the person seeking advice, based on their expertise and experience. Family caregivers received “well-founded, professional […] support” (FC06) with “very quick, good answers” (FC01). They outlined “that there was a sound knowledge behind it, that the lady really knew what she was talking about” (FC06). The professionalism of the counselors is described to be of particular importance, since counseling “is about something essential, it’s about people you love” (FC06). Counselors need to bring in a specific attitude and take their time to deal with the person’s concerns. They ought to show interest, take the person seriously, show understanding and recognize their performance and resources. 89% (n = 17) of family caregivers felt supported by the counselors in expressing their thoughts. Likewise, 89% (n = 17) stated that the counselors listened to them and responded to what they had to say. Those seeking advice feel heard, taken seriously, understood, and safe. 95% of respondents (n = 18) felt that they were in good hands with their counselor. b) Mechanism “being seen”

Family caregivers are “accompanied digitally” (FC06) by the counselors in Alles Clara and gradually build up a trusting relationship. The counselors not only need to give family caregivers professional and personalized advice, but also space and time. Those seeking advice constantly had the feeling that “someone was there for me” (FC05). Counseling thus “also reduces the loneliness, the despair” (FC03). In this way, they give family caregivers a feeling that they are “not all alone on this tightrope” (FC07).

The development of the family caregiver’s awareness, appreciation, and a trusting relationship constitute intermediate outcomes in the IPT [23]. The present findings indicate that the crucial aspect of digital counseling lies in focusing on the person seeking advice. This way, the counselors can offer family caregivers just the support they need. At the end of the counseling, the diffuse problem had turned into “a green check mark” (FC05). After counseling, 83% (n = 15) of the family caregivers surveyed felt able to cope with their situation. Those seeking advice are no longer alone with their situation and can move on “from a problem to next steps” (FC03) with the help of the counselors.I had a problem, didn’t know what the next steps were and what options there were. The family member concerned will continue to be the way they are. The point is to solve a problem related to the situation, so things get easier. (FC03)

Discussion

This study set out to understand how, for whom, and under what circumstances Alles Clara achieves its impact. Beyond simply evaluating effectiveness in general terms, we focused on uncovering and refining the underlying mechanisms and contextual factors that enable change. Anchored in a realist approach, the findings offer a theory-driven explanation of how Alles Clara works in practice—both for those seeking advice and those providing it.Family caregivers seeking advice can build a trusting relationship with the counselors in digital space and thus receive person-centred counseling. Those seeking advice no longer feel alone with their situation, move on from problems to next steps with the help of the counselor and exit the counseling room strengthened.The counselors are empowered to provide person-centred counseling and can recognize the quality of their actions, create an impact from a distance and see meaning in their work. They can show what nursing is and can do and thus develop pride in their work and profession.

Alles Clara as a service, like an increasing number of interventions in the healthcare sector, is taking an innovative approach exploiting the opportunities offered by digitalization [41]. In general, eHealth interventions can have positive effects on family caregivers [10, 18], especially if they are developed on the basis of theory and thus rely on comprehensive assumptions [20, 42]. Following a RE approach enabled us to meet this requirement and proved to be especially useful in the early stage of service launch. It is important to emphasize that logic models are characterized by the property of simplifying complexity through the formulation of CMOc and are only ever valid under the current conditions [25]. The revised program theory is therefore not to be regarded as a static or definitive model but rather serves as a theoretical foundation to encompass the further development and evaluation of the program.

Features of the presented program theory are linked to contextual factors which are required for successful implementation and consolidation of Alles Clara. The thematic and geographical breadth of Alles Clara, the regional anchoring, and networking across federal states as an Austria-wide service, is of particular importance for family caregivers. Due to the federal structure of the Austrian healthcare system [12], this cross-border integration of services is quite unique. Yet, the essential aspect, the key mechanism in both configurational models, is the person-centred approach to counseling to strengthen family caregivers.

Family caregivers as a group often perform their work unseen and lack a voice [4]. Valuing and recognizing the person providing family care is, however, considered a valuable resource for them [43, 44]. Being a family caregiver is a complex, subjective construct that, from the perspective of family members themselves, is not easily tied to activities and time commitments [4]. Considering the contribution of family caregivers, not only to their families but also to society, it is crucial that they are perceived as a person in their own right, addressed and placed at the center of health services [4, 24]. Person-centred healthcare services have primarily focused on the person in need of care [45]; family caregivers predominantly play a supportive role and are merely considered part of the social environment [46]. To our knowledge, little is known about the situation of family caregivers and how person-centred counseling can be provided to them, especially in the context of digital support services.

The configurational models seeking advice and giving advice in Alles Clara provide initial answers to this issue. The concept of advice-giving in counseling contexts is inherently complex. Advice is embedded within intricate social and relational frameworks, shaped by norms around support, autonomy, and power [47]. While advice can be perceived as helpful, it equally risks being experienced as intrusive or disempowering [48, 49]. Within Alles Clara, these tensions are addressed by reframing the term “advice”; not as a default professional response, but as a potential outcome of relational depth that emerges only after establishing solid foundations of mutual understanding and respect.

Our exploration of how Alles Clara unfolds its impact resonates with the wider discourse around digital health interventions and the growing role of artificial intelligence (AI) in caregiving contexts. Recent research highlights the growing reliance on and potential of AI-driven solutions for addressing caregiver information needs [50]. However, as McCormack [51] argues, artificial systems, no matter how sophisticated, lack the embodied, affective, and ethical dimensions that characterize human decision-making. Agency, values, and relational presence are not programmable features, but intrinsic to our understanding of what it means to care. The value of human empathy and nuanced understanding in healthcare contexts is irreplaceable [51]. Our findings contribute to this discourse by demonstrating that in digital counseling it is ultimately the human element, the professional counselor’s capacity for genuine connection, understanding, and presence, that determines it’s impact.

The key to successful counseling lies in the establishment of a trusting relationship. Already in the IPT, the trusting relationship emerged as an important outcome of digital counseling in Alles Clara [23]. Mechanisms that lead to opening up to this trusting relationship are partly created by the distance and anonymity in digital space. In the revised program theory, however, we were able to see that it is not ‘not being seen’, but rather ‘being seen’ as a person - by another person - that leads to the development of this trusting relationship. Issues regarding family care involve highly sensitive topics, private matters, people you love [4]. Hence, the counselor’s in-depth expertise and experience is crucial, and professionalism is of particular importance here.

In the sense of professional nursing the encounter - as an elementary component and essential prerequisite of a relationship - plays a fundamental role. It is defined as an encounter between two equal persons, the nurse and the care recipient [52]. Both parties encounter each other in a way in which they allow themselves and the other to be the person they are. This encounter is described as to take place in “true presence” [52]. While digital environments may at first seem to preclude such presence, our findings suggest otherwise. Finding a “space of togetherness” [53], even in digital space, empowers both, family caregivers and professional caregivers. This leads us to the conclusion that digital technologies can provide the infrastructure and accessibility that enable connection, but transformation occurs through the quality of the human relationships they facilitate. Further reflection on this paradox could deepen our understanding of what professional nursing is and can be - even outside the common fields of practice.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting our findings.

This study was conducted during Alles Clara’s early implementation phase, when the digital service was still establishing itself and expanding its user base. The dataset, particularly the quantitative component, was limited in scope due to the service’s developmental stage. The low response rate to surveys significantly constrains the breadth of interpretation and necessitates caution when generalizing findings. Furthermore, this evaluation phase did not measure statistical correlations or test relationships between variables. The refined program theory presented should therefore be understood as a set of empirically grounded hypotheses requiring further testing and refinement through future studies with larger, more diverse samples.

While these methodological considerations reflect the exploratory nature of the study, more fundamental barriers to access must also be acknowledged. Current access to Alles Clara through specific distribution channels (cooperating employers, care organizations, regional outreach) inherently influences who encounters the service, ultimately shaping our study sample and potentially limiting its representativeness. A structural limitation common to digital interventions applies here as well: individuals without access to smartphones, stable internet connections, or sufficient digital literacy are unlikely to be reached by app-based services. Consequently, no conclusions can be drawn about the needs or experiences of family caregivers currently excluded from digital support services like Alles Clara. This digital divide raises important questions about equity in service provision and highlights the need for complementary non-digital support pathways. Additionally, the question of which specific caregiver profiles are most likely to benefit from the service in its current form remains open and requires targeted investigation.

These limitations underscore both the challenges and opportunities inherent in evaluating digital interventions during their formative stages. Preliminary findings informed Alles Clara’s dynamic implementation process and allowed for modifications in both the program and evaluation design. The collection of qualitative data proved particularly valuable in this early implementation phase of the service. Capturing the experiences of the people using Alles Clara enabled us to develop an in-depth understanding of how Alles Clara unfolds its impact. With the limitations in mind, the present findings mark the beginning of a journey - not its end - on the way to offering family caregivers truly meaningful, person-centred support within digital spaces like Alles Clara.

Conclusions

Digitalization is advancing in all areas of society, including the care sector, and is opening up many opportunities. In view of an ageing society, caring for and supporting relatives is one of the greatest challenges of our time. Alles Clara is a new type of social service in Austria using the possibilities of digitalization to strengthen family caregivers. The findings of this Realist-informed evaluation illuminate what mechanisms lead to the program’s impact. Family caregivers can access the digital service because they are not seen. In digital counseling, however, they are recognized by professional counselors as a person, with their efforts and resources. They receive person-centred counseling. The nursing profession in Austria is also facing challenges due to factors such as staff shortages, a lack of social recognition and difficult working conditions. Alles Clara also has an impact on counselors, especially nursing staff, and leads to them developing professional pride. A central mechanism of the digital service is to make people who provide care visible and to put the person at the center. Alles Clara is a program that reveals a way to introduce the concept of person-centredness into digital space. The caring encounter, as basis for the establishment of relationships with care recipients, is a key characteristic of professional nursing. The physical closeness, a ‘true presence’, is so far attributed a great importance within this context. In this study, we explored counseling as a nursing intervention that takes place anonymously and distantly in digital space – a space in which what they do can be seen.
