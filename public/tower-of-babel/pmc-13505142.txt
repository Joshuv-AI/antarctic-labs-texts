Validation of the Chinese version of the ICHOM Adult Oral Health Standard Set (AOHSS)
Authors: Yongmei Deng, Songlin He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ICHOM Adult Oral Health Standard Set (AOHSS) is a recently created standard set of outcomes intended for application in dental clinical practice. The purpose of this study is to describe the cross-cultural adaptation and validation of the AOHSS into Chinese.MethodsAccording to standard cross-cultural procedures, the Chinese version of the AHOSS (AOHSS/C) was developed. Next, a total of 224 patients were enrolled to complete the AOHSS/C to examine its reliability and validity. The reliability of the AOHSS/C was assessed through the utilization of internal consistency and split-half reliability. Additionally, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and convergent validity were conducted to assess the validity of the AOHSS/C.ResultsFollowing the findings of the corrected item-total correlation and exploratory factor analysis, one item (‘aesthetic satisfaction’) was deleted. The 11 items of the AOHSS/C exhibited satisfactory reliability, as evidenced by Cronbach’s α value of 0.879 and split-half reliability of 0.769. The EFA revealed a two-factor model for the measure, explaining 58.9% of the total variance. Furthermore, the present study demonstrated convergent validity by showing a good correlation (rs=0.553) between the AOHSS/C and the general oral health question.ConclusionsThe AOHSS/C was shown to be a valid and reliable measure to assess oral health in a Chinese population.

Introduction

Globally, oral diseases are a major public health concern and have a direct impact on individuals’ lives by causing significant pain, changing dietary preferences, and influencing speech, oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), and engagement in daily activities [1, 2]. One approach to enhance oral health is to evaluate how oral conditions affect individuals. However, most existing measurement tools have predominantly relied on biomedical models that emphasize utilization, accessibility, or care procedures, thus providing only a narrow understanding of the real impacts of oral health issues. The emerging patient-centred care model emphasizes the importance of oral health providers addressing both oral diseases and the overall oral health [3]. Recently, The FDI World Dental Federation (FDI) has introduced a new definition of oral health, which includes the physical, mental, emotional, and social dimensions that are integral to overall well-being [3]. Oral healthcare providers must take into account not just the disease process but also recognize how environmental, social, and personal elements impact individuals’ quality of life and their active involvement in decision-making and control over their own health as well as utilization of healthcare services. There is a necessity to establish a standard set of patient-centred outcome for assessing oral health outcomes, considering the viewpoints of both clinicians and patients, in a straightforward and reproducible manner [4].

Recently, the Adult Oral Health Standard Set (AOHSS) has been developed through a collaboration between FDI and the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM) [4]. A group of policymakers, healthcare providers, patient advocates, researchers, and public health specialists collaborated to create the AOHSS. The AOHSS comprises a standard set of patient-centered outcomes, incorporating both patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and clinical-reported outcome measures (CROMs), alongside a range of case-mix variables [4]. It signifies a basic collection of outcomes intended for regular clinical use, scientific studies, and public health.

The AOHSS is structured into two main sections. The first includes 17 outcome concepts, which consist of 13 patient-reported measures and 4 clinician-reported measures. The second includes 14 case-mix concepts, broken down into 8 patient-reported items and 6 reported by clinicians or administrators. It evaluates a comprehensive range of dimensions, including oral function, pain, psychosocial impact (such as self-confidence and social participation), oral hygiene habits, and financial burden of care, alongside clinician-reported measures of caries and periodontal disease. This structure ensures alignment with patient-centered outcome measurement by integrating patient-reported outcomes with clinical data to capture what matters most to individuals. The AOHSS is designed to support value-based oral healthcare, facilitating shared decision-making and personalized care planning in clinical practice, research, and population health. Its relevance lies in its holistic, patient-driven framework, which moves beyond traditional biomedical models to reflect the full impact of oral health on daily life and well-being.

Currently, the French-speaking population has implemented 13 patient-reported sections of AOHSS, and the study has confirmed that the French version of AOHSS exhibits favorable reliability and validity [5]. Beyond the sheer size of its population, the validation of this instrument in China is necessitated by unique cultural, epidemiological, and systemic factors. Cultural perceptions of oral health and varying health literacy levels can significantly influence how patients self-report outcomes. Furthermore, China’s distinct disease patterns and the structure of its healthcare delivery system may impact the instrument’s relevance and interpretability, underscoring the need for rigorous cross-cultural validation. Thus, the aim of this research was to translate and adapt AOHSS according to China’s national conditions while evaluating its psychometric properties to establish a reliable and valid tool for evaluating oral health among the Chinese population.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

The Ethics Committee of our Hospital granted approval for the study (2023 (076)), and all participants provided written informed consent and demonstrated full comprehension of the assessment’s particulars.

Participants

The study enrolled a convenience sample of 224 adults attending our hospital. To meet the required sample size [6], we aimed for 10 times the number of items (13 items * 10 = 130), and considering an additional 30% dropout rate. Ultimately, the sample size of this study was determined as 224, meeting the specified criteria. Inclusion criteria included being older than 18 years old and having proficiency in Chinese language comprehension. Exclusion criteria encompassed unwillingness to provide informed consent and inability to comprehend questionnaire content. Participants were encouraged to contact researchers at any time if they had any inquiries or concerns regarding the study procedures. Each assessment session lasted approximately 10 min.

AOHSS

The AOHSS was developed by the ICHOM Adult Oral Health Working Group [4]. This study was authorized by Professor Riordain of the Cork University Dental School, Ireland. The AOHSS for this study included 13 patient-reported items. The questionnaire started with a general oral health question (“How is the health of your mouth, teeth and gums?”), which is scored on a scale of 1 to 5 based on possible responses such as “very good”, “good”, “fair”, “good” or “very good”. The remaining 12 items are assessed using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (very frequently), wherein higher scores indicate greater impact; the overall scale scores range between 12 and 60.

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation

In accordance with established guidelines [7], we employed a five-step procedure to translate and culturally adapt the AOHSS. Firstly, two bilingual translators with dental expertise proficient in both Chinese and English were engaged to perform the translation from English to Chinese, resulting in two initial drafts. Secondly, two local dental specialists, who had no prior knowledge of the original scale, independently conducted back translations of these two Chinese versions into English. Through discussion and comparison, any discrepancies between the original scale and the first draft were revised. The translation quality was evaluated by two dental specialists and a medical English professor. Based on their proposed amendments, a second version of the Chinese translation was formulated. A panel of experts then examined the conceptual and semantic equivalence of this second draft, which underwent further revisions to generate a final Chinese version that was pre-evaluated with 30 participants. During the pilot testing with 30 participants, no significant comprehension difficulties or item ambiguities were reported. All participants found the instrument clear and easy to understand, and no modifications to the items were required based on their feedback.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp. NY; USA). In the analysis, significance was determined by 0.05.

Validity

Validity was assessed by checking structural validity and convergent validity. In order to assess the structural validity of the AOHSS /C, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed using orthogonal rotation (varimax rotation). As part of the EFA, the Bartlett’s test of sphericity coefficient and Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test were performed first in order to determine the suitability of the factor solution [8]. In this research, eigenvalues exceeding 1 were used as the benchmark for extracting factors, while factor loadings above 0.40 were considered significant [9].

To evaluate convergent validity, the correlation coefficient between the AOHSS/C score and the general oral health question score was computed. These correlations are categorized as weak, fair, good, and excellent correlations with Spearman coefficients of 0-0.20, 0.21–0.40, 0.61–0.80, and 0.81-1.0, respectively [6].

Reliability

Cronbach’s α coefficient and split-half reliability were used to determine the reliability of the AOHSS/C. Both require a value greater than 0.7 [6]. In order to calculate split-half reliability, the AOHSS/C was divided into two parts according to the odd and even number of items.

Exploratory factor analysis

In the exploratory factor analysis, all 12 items were entered. In the KMO test, the result was 0.881, above the recommended value of 0.60, and the Bartlett test of sphericity was 1078.112 (df = 55, P < 0.001). Item 5 was deleted because it contained negative loading and its deletion increased the overall Cronbach’s alpha to 0.879. Using the remaining 11 items, we repeated our exploratory factor analysis and presented our results in Table 3. Across all items, factor loadings exceed 0.40, ranging from 0.550 to 0.822. Furthermore, the EFA identified two components with eigenvalues > 1, which collectively accounted for 58.942% of the total variance; specifically, these components explained 47.113% and 11.829% of the variance respectively. Discussion

In this study, we examine the validity and reliability of an adapted version of the AOHSS for use among the Chinese population. Our modification to the original AOHSS involved the deletion of one item. According to the results of psychometric analysis, the AOHSS/C exhibits acceptable reliability and validity, rendering it a valuable instrument for evaluating oral health status within the Chinese population.

In recent years, numerous domestic and international researchers have conducted clinical or epidemiological studies on scales measuring oral health-related quality of life, which are increasingly utilized to quantitatively assess the impact of oral health on patients in diverse populations [10–12]. The creation of these scales across different cultures must rigorously follow the guidelines set by international standards [13, 14]. Given that there are approximately 870 million Mandarin speakers in China, it is imperative to apply the AOHSS to the Chinese population. The AOHSS/C preparation processes strictly follow the international scale’s standardization process, and its reliability and validity meet all requirements, with good scientific quality and applicability.

In order to evaluate structural validity, the EFA method was used. The data were suitable for factor analysis, as indicated by a high KMO value and a significant result from Bartlett’s test. Based on the results of scree plots and eigenvalues, we identified two factors, which accounted for 58.9% of the total observed variance. Item 5 (‘aesthetic satisfaction’) was omitted due to its negative factor loading in the EFA, as well as the negative value observed for the corrected item-total correlation in the reliability analysis. The suboptimal performance of the item may be linked to either genuine cultural differences in the perception of dental aesthetics or to the specific clinical characteristics of the sample examined in this study. Further research is warranted to investigate the role of aesthetic satisfaction in a larger sample of the Chinese population.

In addition, the study provided evidence of convergent validity through the good correlation observed between the AOHSS/C and the general oral health question. According to the results, the AOHSS/C had good convergent validity. The finding indicated that the AOHSS/C had adequate validity for evaluating oral health among the Chinese population.

In order to assess reliability, internal consistency was evaluated, which assesses how well items within the same construct measure the same conceptual domain and whether the item scores are validly summed [15]. Cronbach’s α coefficient and split-half reliability were used to determine the reliability of the AHOSS/C. Both require a value greater than 0.7 [6]. The Cronbach’s Alpha for AOHSS/C was 0.879; which was higher than that by Nicolas-Boluda et al. (0.81) [5]. The results of the split half reliability computed by Spearman-Brown’s coefffcient also indicated that the AOHSS/C had good reliability.

The study also had a few limitations. First, our sample was skewed toward women, urban residents, and individuals with higher education, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader Chinese population. Future studies should include more diverse participants across different geographical regions and socioeconomic backgrounds to further validate the instrument’s applicability. Second, the test-retest reliability was not assessed, and this should be addressed in future research to evaluate the instrument’s temporal stability. Third, while exploratory factor analysis was used as an initial step in development, future research should employ CFA with new, independent samples to verify the stability and replicability of the factor structure. Fourth, age-related factors, such as cognitive comprehension, cumulative disease experience, and varying perceptions of health, could indeed affect item interpretation and the prevalence of oral conditions, potentially introducing variability. Future research should consider conducting subgroup analyses or validation studies in specific age cohorts (e.g., younger vs. older adults) to further explore the instrument’s performance and measurement invariance across the lifespan.

In conclusion, the AOHSS/C demonstrates accept reliability and validity, rendering it suitable for further exploration and application within the Chinese population. However, it is imperative to test the AOHSS/C for test-retest reliability analysis, while conducting larger-scale studies to validate and refine its efficacy.

Conclusion

The Chinese version of the AOHSS/C exhibits acceptable reliability and validity, rendering it a valuable instrument for evaluating oral health status within the Chinese population. Its implementation will contribute significantly to enhancing both oral health research and clinical practice, ultimately leading to improved oral health outcomes for individuals in China.
