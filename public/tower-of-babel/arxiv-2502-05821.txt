1.
Introduction

If
G
is a finite group, we write
d(G)
for the minimal size of a generating set.
We call
G

d
-maximal
if
d(H)<d(G)
for every proper subgroup
H
of
G
.
This terminology was introduced by Kahn in
[
12
]
.
Although
d
-maximal groups are not nilpotent in general
[
15
]
, in this paper we only consider
d
-maximal
p
-groups.
In this case
d(G)=\log_{p}|G:G^{\prime}G^{p}|
, so the condition above can equivalently be written as
|H:H^{\prime}H^{p}|<|G:G^{\prime}G^{p}|
for all
H<G
.
Laffey
[
13
]
proved that if
G
is a
d
-maximal
p
-group with
p
odd then
G
has class at most two, and
G/G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime}
are both elementary abelian.

A closely related concept is ab-maximality.
We say that a finite group
G
is
ab-maximal
if
|H:H^{\prime}|<|G:G^{\prime}|
for every proper subgroup
H
of
G
.
It was observed in
[
22
]
that ab-maximal groups are nilpotent of class at most two,
and that every group of order
n
contains an ab-maximal subgroup of order at least
n^{c/\log\log n}
for some constant
c>0
.
This result is sharp even if we replace “ab-maximal” with “solvable”,
and motivates the study of ab-maximal groups in greater detail.

For a group of prime exponent,
d
-maximality and ab-maximality are equivalent.
Trivial examples include elementary abelian groups and extraspecial groups,
and it is natural to ask how large the derived subgroup can be in these groups in general.
In the literature, this has been asked a couple of times
[
9
,
14
]
.
For example, Question C in
[
14
]
asks whether
every ab-maximal group
G
satisfies
|G:G^{\prime}|\geq|G|^{\delta}
for some fixed
\delta>0.5
.
We answer this question in the negative.

Theorem 1.1
.

For each prime
p
and
n\geq 160
,
there exists an ab-maximal
p
-group
G
(of exponent
p
if
p\neq 2
)
such that
|G:G^{\prime}|=p^{n}
and
|G^{\prime}|=p^{n-3}
.

In general if
G
is a nonabelian ab-maximal
p
-group then
|G:G^{\prime}|\geq p^{3}|G^{\prime}|
, so the multiplicative gap
p^{3}
is the smallest possible.
The inequality
n\geq 160
is just a convenience, and needed mainly if
p=2
.
If
p\geq 13
it suffices to take
n\geq 12
.
In truth we expect

is sufficient for all
p
.

To prove the above theorem we use standard ideas to reduce to a question in linear algebra, which we solve using probabilistic methods.
This is not the first time that wild
p
-groups of class two have been constructed using a random alternating map.
For example, using essentially this idea Ol’shanskii
[
19
]
showed the existence of groups of order
p^{n}
where all abelian subgroups have order at most
p^{m}
where
m\sim\sqrt{8n}
.
See also
[
5
]
or
[
10
, Theorem 1.9]
for applications of the same method.
Notably, however,
Theorem

1.1
is
not
obtained from a high-probability result, but rather we show roughly that a random candidate
p
-group (namely, a
p
-group
G
of Frattini class two and
|G:G^{\prime}|=p^{3}|G^{\prime}|
) is ab-maximal with probability close to

.
In linear algebraic terms, the basic reason for this curious result is that a random alternating bilinear map
B\colon\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n-3}
has a number of totally isotropic
3
-dimensional subspaces that is asymptotically distributed as a Poisson distribution.

Using the same method we construct
d
-maximal groups with large derived subgroup.
This case is a bit easier and in fact we do obtain a high-probability result.
In particular we give a complete positive answer to Question 4.5 in
[
9
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For each prime
p
and
n\geq 2
,
there exists a
d
-maximal
p
-group
G

such that
|G:G^{\prime}|=p^{n}
and
|G^{\prime}|=p^{n-2}
.

Recall that a finite group

In this subsection

The following result of Thompson

Theorem 2.1

(Thompson)
.

Let

-group.
Then

G^{\prime}\leqslant Z(G)

It is trivial that

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

be a nonabelian ab-maximal

Then

|G:G^{\prime}|\geq|Z(G)|p^{3}
.

Proof.

Let

be a maximal abelian subgroup.
Then
C_{G}(A)=A
and
Z(G)<A
.
We claim that
A/Z(G)
is noncyclic.
Otherwise,

for some

, and
A=C_{G}(A)=C_{G}(a)
.
Moreover

Therefore

is noncyclic and
|A:Z(G)|\geq p^{2}
.
Thus by ab-maximality we have
|G:G^{\prime}|>|A|\geq|Z(G)|p^{2}
,
which implies
|G:G^{\prime}|\geq|Z(G)|p^{3}
.
∎

If

3.
Reduction to linear algebra

Let
\mathcal{H}_{p}(n,m)
be the class of
p
-groups
G
such that
\Phi(G)
is central and elementary abelian with index
p^{n}
and order
p^{m}
.
These groups were famously used by Higman
[
11
]
to give a lower bound for the number of groups of a given prime-power order.
Higman showed that
\mathcal{H}_{p}(n,m)=\emptyset
for
m>n(n+1)/2
, that
\mathcal{H}_{p}(n,n(n+1)/2)
contains a single group
H
, and that every group in
\mathcal{H}_{p}(n,m)
is a quotient of
H
by a subgroup of
\Phi(H)
.
One may define
H
to be the quotient of the free group
F=F_{n}
by the fully invariant subgroup generated by the elements

x^{p^{2}},\quad[x,y]^{p},\quad[x,y,z]\qquad(x,y,z\in F).

If
H
has generators
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}
then
\Phi(H)
has generators

h_{i}^{p}\quad(1\leq i\leq n),\qquad[h_{i},h_{j}]\quad(1\leq i<j\leq n).

Let
V
and
W
be vector spaces over some field, and let
B\colon V\times V\to W
be a bilinear map.
If
U\leqslant V
is a subspace we write
B(U,U)
for the span of the image of
B
on
U\times U
.
We call
U

totally isotropic
(for
B
) if
B(U,U)=0
,
and we call
B

alternating
if every
1
-dimensional subspace is (totally) isotropic.
The space of alternating bilinear maps
B\colon V\times V\to W
is denoted by
\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(V,W)
.

If
p=2
then a map
F\colon V\to W
is called a
quadratic map
associated to
B
if
F(x+y)=F(x)+F(y)+B(x,y)
identically, and we write
F(U)
for the span of the image of
F
on
U\leqslant V
.
In this case we have
B(U,U)\leqslant F(U)
for all
U\leqslant V
.
We say that
U
is
singular
(for
F
) if
F(U)=0
.
Singularity is stronger than isotropy in general.

If
G\in\mathcal{H}_{p}(n,m)
then the commutator map descends to an alternating bilinear map
B\colon V\times V\to W
where
V=G/\Phi(G)\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
and
W=\Phi(G)\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}^{m}
.
Further, the
p
-power map
x\mapsto x^{p}
descends to a map
F\colon V\to W
which is linear if
p\neq 2
and quadratic and associated to
B
if
p=2
.
Since
W
is by definition
\Phi(G)=G^{\prime}G^{p}
, together these two maps satisfy
B(V,V)+F(V)=W
.

Higman’s argument shows that the passage of the previous paragraph is reversible.
Suppose
V=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
,
W=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{m}
,
B\colon V\times V\to W
is bilinear and alternating, and
F\colon V\to W
is linear if
p\neq 2
and quadratic and associated to
B
if
p=2
.
Assume that
B(V,V)+F(V)=W
.
Write
B=(B_{1},\dots,B_{m})
and
F=(F_{1},\dots,F_{m})
with respect to a basis of
W
.
Then there is a group
G=G_{B,F}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}(n,m)
defined by generators
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
and relations

\displaystyle[x_{i},x_{j}]=\prod_{k=1}^{m}y_{k}^{B_{k}(x_{i},x_{j})},\qquad x_{i}^{p}=\prod_{k=1}^{m}y_{k}^{F_{k}(x_{i})},\qquad[x_{i},y_{j}]=1,\qquad y_{i}^{p}=1.

This correspondence is almost a special case of the well-known Baer correspondence
[
3
]
, except that we allow
p=2

(cf.
[
21
]
*Section 2.4).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
V=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
and
W=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{m}
.
Let
B\colon V\times V\to W
be an alternating bilinear map and let
F\colon V\to W
be linear if
p\neq 2
and quadratic and associated to
B
if
p=2
.
The following are equivalent.

(1)

B(V,V)=W
and
G_{B,F}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}(n,m)
is ab-maximal;

(2)

every proper subspace
U<V
satisfies

\dim W/B(U,U)\><\>\dim V/U.

Similarly, the following are equivalent.

(3)

B(V,V)=W
and
G_{B,F}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}(n,m)
is
d
-maximal;

(4)

every proper subspace
U<V
satisfies

\dim W/{\left(B(U,U)+F(U)\right)}\><\>\dim V/U.

Proof.

Suppose (1) holds, and let
G=G_{B,F}
.
Since
G^{\prime}
corresponds to
B(V,V)
and
\Phi(G)
corresponds to
W
, we have
G^{\prime}=\Phi(G)
.
Since
G
is ab-maximal,
|H:H^{\prime}|<|G:G^{\prime}|
for every
H<G
.
If
U<V
, then
U
corresponds to a subgroup
H<G
containing
\Phi(G)
and
B(U,U)
corresponds to
H^{\prime}
, so the ab-maximality condition implies (2).

Now suppose (2) holds. If
U<V
is a hyperplane, then

\dim W/B(U,U)<\dim V/U=1,

so
B(V,V)\geqslant B(U,U)=W
. We also have from (2) that
|H:H^{\prime}|<|G:G^{\prime}|
for every proper subgroup
H<G
containing
\Phi(G)=G^{\prime}
, and in general, if
H<G
then
HG^{\prime}<G
and

|H:H^{\prime}|=|H:(HG^{\prime})^{\prime}|\leq|HG^{\prime}:(HG^{\prime})^{\prime}|<|G:G^{\prime}|,

so

is ab-maximal. Thus (1) holds.

The proof of
(3)\iff(4)
is almost identical, using

p^{d(H)}=|H:\Phi(H)|=|H:H^{\prime}H^{p}|

and the correspondence between subgroups
H
containing
\Phi(G)
and subspaces
U\leqslant V
, with
\Phi(H)
corresponding to
B(U,U)+F(U)
.
Let us only explain why (4) implies
B(V,V)=W
.
If
B(V,V)\neq W
then
B(V,V)\leq W_{0}
for some hyperplane
W_{0}<W
. Let
U=F^{-1}(W_{0})
, and note that
U
is a linear subspace of
V
(even if
p=2
, since
B(V,V)\leq W_{0}
).
Moreover, since
W_{0}
is a hyperplane,
\dim V/U\leq 1
.
But
B(U,U)+F(U)\leq B(V,V)+W_{0}=W_{0}<V
, in contradiction with (4).
∎

Proof.

We use induction on
k
, the base case
k=0
being trivial.
Let
k\geq 1
and
U=\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}
.
Also, let
W=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}H_{i}
and
r=\dim(U/W)\in\{0,1,2,3\}
.
Let
x,y,z
be a basis for
H_{k}
, of which the first
r
elements are linearly independent modulo
W
,
and the remaining
3-r
elements are in
H_{k}\cap W
.
We distinguish four cases according to
r
:

Case
r=3
: In this case
x\wedge y
,
x\wedge z
and
y\wedge z
are linearly independent modulo
W\wedge W
,
so by induction

\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}=\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}+3\geq\dim W+3=\dim U.

Equality holds if and only if
\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}=\dim W
,
and by induction this happens if and only if
H_{1},\dots,H_{k-1}
are linearly independent.

Case
r=2
:
Even in this case,
x\wedge y
,
x\wedge z
and
y\wedge z
are linearly independent modulo
W\wedge W
.
Indeed, choosing a basis
w_{1},\dots,w_{d}
for
W
with
w_{1}=z
, a basis for
U\wedge U
modulo
W\wedge W
is given by the elements of the form
x\wedge y
,
x\wedge w_{i}
,
y\wedge w_{i}
, including
x\wedge y,x\wedge z,y\wedge z
.
It follows by induction that

\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}=\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}+3\geq\dim W+3=\dim U+1.

The equality with
\dim U
is clearly impossible in this case.

Case
r=1
:
An argument as in the previous case shows that
x\wedge y
and
x\wedge z
are linearly independent modulo
W\wedge W
, so by induction

\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}\geq\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}+2\geq\dim W+2=\dim U+1.

Case
r=0
:
By induction,

\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}\geq\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}\geq\dim W=\dim U.

Equality can hold only if
H_{1},\dots,H_{k-1}
are linearly independent
and
H_{k}\wedge H_{k}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}
.
For
x\in H_{k}
, write
x=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}x_{i}
where
x_{i}\in H_{i}
.
Then for
x,y\in H_{k}
we have

x\wedge y=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k-1}x_{i}\wedge y_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}x_{i}\wedge y_{i}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k-1}(x_{i}\wedge y_{j}-y_{i}\wedge x_{j})\in\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}.

Since
H_{1},\dots,H_{k-1}
are linearly independent, the subspaces
H_{i}\wedge H_{j}\leqslant V\wedge V
are linearly independent for
1\leq i\leq j\leq k-1
.
It therefore follows from the above equation that
x_{i}\wedge y_{j}=y_{i}\wedge x_{j}
for all
i<j

and

x\wedge y=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}x_{i}\wedge y_{i}.

Let
x,y\in H_{k}
be linearly independent.
Then
x\wedge y\neq 0
, so
x_{i}\wedge y_{i}\neq 0
for some
i
, so
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
are nonzero and not parallel.
Since
x_{i}\wedge y_{j}=y_{i}\wedge x_{j}
we must have
x_{j}=y_{j}=0
for all
j\neq i
.
Since this holds for all
y\notin\left\langle x\right\rangle
, it follows that
H_{k}=H_{i}
, contrary to the hypothesis that
H_{1},\dots,H_{k}
are distinct.
∎

Below we use the standard notation

{n\brack d}_{p}\>=\>\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}\frac{p^{n-i}-1}{p^{d-i}-1}

(the “Gaussian binomial coefficient”) for the number of
d
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}

(we will drop the
p
subscript when it should be clear).
We recall the well-known bounds

(4.1)

p^{d(n-d)}\leq{n\brack d}_{p}\leq(1-1/p-1/p^{2})^{-1}p^{d(n-d)}<4p^{d(n-d)}

(see for example
[
18
]
*Lemma 3.5)
Note also that

\displaystyle{n\brack 3}_{p}

\displaystyle\>=\>\frac{(p^{n}-1)(p^{n-1}-1)(p^{n-2}-1)}{(p^{3}-1)(p^{2}-1)(p-1)}

\displaystyle\>=\>\lambda_{p}\,p^{3(n-3)}{\left(1+O(1/p^{n-2})\right)}.\>

For
k\geq 1
, we also use the falling factorial notation

(n)_{k}\>=\>n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1).

For example,
{\left({n\brack 3}\right)}_{k}
is the number of ordered
k
-tuples of distinct
3
-dimensional subspaces.
Finally, we note that
\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(V,W)
is a vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
of dimension
\binom{n}{2}\dim W
, where

, and we note that if
K\leq W
then
\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(V,K)
is naturally a subspace of
\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(V,W)
, while if
H\leq V
then
\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(H,K)
is naturally a quotient of
\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(V,W)
.

Proof of
Proposition

4.1
.

Note that a subspace
H<V
is bad for
B
if and only if
\dim B(H,H)\leq\dim H-3
.
In particular there are no bad subspaces of dimension less than
3
, and a
3
-dimensional subspace is bad if and only if it is totally isotropic.
For
3\leq h<n
, let
N_{h}
be the number of
h
-dimensional bad subspaces, and let
N_{\geqslant 4}=\sum_{h=4}^{n-1}N_{h}
.

First we consider
N_{\geqslant 4}
.
Fix
H<V
with
\dim H=h\geq 4
.
For any
K\leqslant W
with
\dim K=h-3
, we have

\Pr{\left(B(H,H)\leq K\right)}=\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(H,K)|}{|\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(H,W)|}=p^{-\binom{h}{2}(n-h)}.

By summing over all choices of
K
, we obtain by a union bound

\Pr{\left(\dim B(H,H)\leq h-3\right)}\leq\sum_{\dim K=h-3}\Pr{\left(B(H,H)\leq K\right)}={n-3\brack h-3}p^{-\binom{h}{2}(n-h)},

and then by summing over
H
it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left[N_{h}\right]}\leq{n\brack h}{n-3\brack h-3}p^{-\binom{h}{2}(n-h)}\leq 16p^{h(n-h)+(h-3)(n-h)-\binom{h}{2}(n-h)}=16p^{f(h)},

where
f(h)=(h-2)(h-3)(h-n)/2
.
Since
f
is a cubic function with roots at
2
,
3
, and
n
, it is clear that it has no local maximum greater than
3
, so its maximum in the range
[4,n-1]
is attained at one of the endpoints (recall that
n\geq 5
).
Moreover
f(4)=4-n
and
f(n-1)=-(n-3)(n-4)/2\leq 4-n
since
n\geq 5
.
It follows that
f(h)\leq 4-n
on
[4,n-1]
.
Therefore, by Markov’s inequality,

\Pr{\left(N_{\geqslant 4}>0\right)}\leq\mathbb{E}{\left[N_{\geqslant 4}\right]}=\sum_{h=4}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}{\left[N_{h}\right]}\leq 16(n-4)p^{4-n},

from which (1) follows easily.

For (2), we will use the method of moments (see
[
4
]
*Sec. 30) to show that
N_{3}
has a Poisson distribution.
It suffices to prove that
\mathbb{E}{\left[(N_{3})_{k}\right]}\to\lambda_{p}^{k}
as
n\to\infty
for each
k\geq 1
.
Note that
(N_{3})_{k}
is the number of ordered
k
-tuples of distinct totally isotropic
3
-dimensional subspaces
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
in
V
.
Equivalently, bijectively associating
B
with a linear map
\tilde{B}:V\wedge V\to W
,

\tilde{B}
should be zero on the whole subspace
\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}
of
V\wedge V
.
For fixed linear subspaces
H_{1},\dots,H_{k}\leq V
, it is therefore clear that

\displaystyle\Pr{\left(H_{1},\dots,H_{k}~\text{are totally isotropic}\right)}

\displaystyle=\Pr{\left(\tilde{B}~\text{is zero on}~\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}\right)}

\displaystyle=p^{-(n-3)\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}},

and it follows that

\mathbb{E}{\left[(N_{3})_{k}\right]}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}H_{1},\dots,H_{k}\\
\text{distinct $3$-dim subspaces}\end{subarray}}p^{-(n-3)\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}}.

Since
\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}\leq 3k
for all
H_{1},\dots,H_{k}
we have

\mathbb{E}{\left[(N_{3})_{k}\right]}\geq{\left({n\brack 3}\right)}_{k}\>p^{-(n-3)3k},

and

\mathbb{E}{\left[(N_{3})_{k}\right]}-{\left({n\brack 3}\right)}_{k}p^{-(n-3)3k}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}H_{1},\dots,H_{k}\\
\text{distinct $3$-dim subspaces}\\
\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}<3k\end{subarray}}p^{-(n-3)\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}}.

The sum is empty if
k=1
, so we may assume
k\geq 2
.
We split the sum according to value of
d=\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}
.
Since
d<3k
,
Lemma

4.2
gives
\dim\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\wedge H_{i}\geq d+1
.
Therefore we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left[(N_{3})_{k}\right]}-{\left({n\brack 3}\right)}_{k}p^{-(n-3)3k}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{d=3}^{3k-1}{n\brack d}{d\brack 3}^{k}p^{-(n-3)(d+1)}

\displaystyle\leq 4^{k+1}\sum_{d=3}^{3k-1}p^{d(n-d)}p^{3(d-3)k}p^{-(n-3)(d+1)}

\displaystyle=4^{k+1}\sum_{d=3}^{3k-1}p^{-n+3-9k+d(3k+3-d)}

\displaystyle\leq 4^{k+1}(3k-3)p^{-n+3-9k+9(k+1)^{2}/4}

since
d(3k+3-d)\leq 9(k+1)^{2}/4
for all
d
.
Since
p^{-n}
tends to zero as
n\to\infty
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}{\left[(N_{3})_{k}\right]}\sim{\left({n\brack 3}\right)}_{k}\>p^{-(n-3)3k}\to\lambda_{p}^{k}

as
n\to\infty
, for
p
and
k
fixed, as desired.

Finally, we prove (3) using the Bonferroni inequality

\Pr{\left(N_{3}>0\right)}\>\leq\>\sum_{k=1}^{K}(-1)^{k-1}\mathbb{E}{\left[\binom{N_{3}}{k}\right]},

which holds for every odd integer
K\geq 1
(see
[
7
]
*Section 4.7).
Moreover, we use the inequalities

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}{\left({n\brack 3}\right)}_{k}\>p^{-(n-3)3k}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}{\left[\binom{N_{3}}{k}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k!}{\left({n\brack 3}\right)}_{k}p^{-(n-3)3k}+\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{d=3}^{3k-1}{n\brack d}{d\brack 3}^{k}p^{-(n-3)(d+1)}

that we obtained above.
For
K=3
, we have that

\displaystyle\Pr{\left(N_{3}+N_{\geqslant 4}>0\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\Pr{\left(N_{3}>0\right)}+\Pr{\left(N_{\geqslant 4}>0\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k!}{\left({n\brack 3}\right)}_{k}p^{-(n-3)3k}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{6}\sum_{d=3}^{8}{n\brack d}{d\brack 3}^{3}p^{-(n-3)(d+1)}+16(n-4)p^{4-n}.

We can check using a computer that this is less than
1
, provided that
p\geq 13
and
n\geq 12
.
Alternatively, setting
K=9
, bounding as above gives

\displaystyle\Pr{\left(N_{3}+N_{\geqslant 4}>0\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{9}(-1)^{k-1}\mathbb{E}{\left[\binom{N_{3}}{k}\right]}+16(n-4)p^{4-n}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{9}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k!}{\left({n\brack 3}\right)}_{k}p^{-(n-3)3k}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{k=3,5,7,9}\frac{(3k-3)4^{k+1}}{k!}p^{-n+3-9k+9(k+1)^{2}/4}+16(n-4)p^{4-n}.

We can check that this is less than

for
2\leq p\leq 11
and

.

.
This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.3

(Lovász local lemma)
.

In the proof of
Proposition

4.1
, it is tempting to try applying the Lovász local lemma (see
[
1
]
*Sec. 5) to the collection of events of the form “
H
is totally isotropic”, where
\dim H=3
, since every event in that family is independent from almost all other others.
However, the local lemma requires joint independence, not just pairwise independence, and it is easy to see that this fails in our situation.

The following variant of
Proposition

4.1
is much easier and completes the proof of
Theorem

1.2
for
p\neq 2
. For
p=2
see
Proposition

4.5
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
p\neq 2
be a prime,
n\geq 2
,
V=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
and
W=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n-2}
.
Let
F\colon V\to W
be a fixed surjective linear map,
and let
B\in\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(V,W)
be chosen uniformly at random.
Call a proper subspace
H<V

bad (for
B
)
if

\dim W/(B(H,H)+F(H))\>\geq\>\dim V/H.

Then the probability that there is no bad subspace is positive for all
p
and
n
.
Moreover, it tends to
1
as either
p,n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Note that a subspace
H<V
is bad for
B
if and only if

\dim{\left(B(H,H)+F(H)\right)}\leq\dim H-2.

On the other hand

\displaystyle\dim{\left(B(H,H)+F(H)\right)}\geq\dim F(H)

\displaystyle=\dim H-\dim(H\cap\ker F)

\displaystyle\geq\dim H-\dim(\ker F)=\dim H-2.

It follows that a subspace
H<V
is bad for
B
if and only if

B(H,H)\leqslant F(H)\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\ker F\leqslant H.

Clearly we may assume
n\geq 3
.
If
n=3
, then the only possible bad subspace is the plane
\ker F
, so there is a bad subspace if and only if
B(\ker F,\ker F)=0
.
We may therefore assume
n\geq 4
.
For
2\leq h\leq n-1
let
N_{h}
be the number of
h
-dimensional bad subspaces.
For fixed
H<V
with
\ker F\leqslant H
and
\dim(H)=h
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr{\left(B(H,H)\leqslant F(H)\right)}=\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(H,F(H))|}{|\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(H,W)|}=\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{h},\mathbb{F}_{p}^{h-2})|}{|\mathop{\mathrm{Alt}}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{h},\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n-2})|}=p^{-\binom{h}{2}(n-h)}.

By summing over all choices of
H\geqslant\ker F
, it follows from (
4.1
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left[N_{h}\right]}\leq{n-2\brack h-2}p^{-\binom{h}{2}(n-h)}<4p^{(h-2)(n-h)-\binom{h}{2}(n-h)}=4p^{(h^{2}-3h+4)(h-n)/2}.

Let
f(h)=(h^{2}-3h+4)(h-n)
. Then
f^{\prime}(h)=(2h-3)(h-n)+h^{2}-3h+4
,
f^{\prime}(0)=3n+4>0
,
f^{\prime}(2)=4-n\leq 0
.
Since
f
is a cubic function it follows that
f
has a local maximum between
0
and
2
and no other local maximum. In particular the maximum value of
f
in the range
3\leq h\leq n-1
is attained at one of the endpoints:

\displaystyle\max_{3\leq h\leq n-1}(h^{2}-3h+4)(h-n)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{-4(n-3),-((n-1)^{2}-3(n-1)+4)\right\}

\displaystyle=-4(n-3).

Let
N=\sum_{h=2}^{n-1}N_{h}
. Then, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\Pr{\left(N>0\right)}\leq\mathbb{E}{\left[N\right]}=\sum_{h=2}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}{\left[N_{h}\right]}

\displaystyle<4\sum_{h=2}^{n-1}p^{(h^{2}-3h+4)(h-n)/2}

\displaystyle\leq 4p^{-(n-2)}+4(n-3)p^{-2(n-3)}.

This tends to zero as either
p,n\to\infty
,
and we can easily check that it is smaller than
1
for all
p\geq 3
and
n\geq 4
.
∎

.

Let
n\geq 2
,
