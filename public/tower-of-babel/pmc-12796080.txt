Unpacking the theoretical role of fear of missing out in the relationship between social networking intensity and well-being among Indian college students
Authors: Satnam Ubeja, Neetu Chhillar, Swaranjeet Arora, Ratnesh Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor today’s Indian college students, social media is deeply embedded in daily academic and social life. While digital platforms offer opportunities for connection, they also heighten the fear of missing out (FOMO), which may undermine well-being. This study examines the complex relationship between social networking intensity (SNI), FOMO, and mental well-being (MW), focusing on both mediating and reciprocal dynamics.MethodsA structured survey was conducted among 357 students enrolled in professional courses in Indore, India. Standardized scales measured SNI, FOMO, and MW. Data were analyzed using factor analysis and structural equation modeling (SEM) to test the hypothesized pathways.ResultsFindings confirm that SNI is positively associated with FOMO, and FOMO significantly mediates the relationship between SNI and MW. At the same time, the results suggest a potential bidirectional pattern: students with higher baseline FOMO may engage more intensively in social networking, reinforcing a feedback loop between emotional vulnerability and digital usage.ConclusionThe study highlights that the psychological effects of social networking cannot be reduced to usage time alone but are shaped by reciprocal interactions between emotional experiences and digital behaviors. Interventions should focus on digital literacy, resilience to social comparison, and balanced online engagement. Recognizing the possibility of this feedback loop is critical for designing effective campus-based mental health strategies in India and beyond.Keywords: Social media, FOMO, Mental health, Indian students, Digital habits

Introduction and conceptual framework

Social networking has rapidly become part of everyday life for college students in India, especially those in educational hubs like Indore. Platforms such as WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook now shape students’ routines—academic, social, and family alike [3, 4, 30]. These digital tools can support new friendships and collaboration, but they also create new sources of stress [7].

The central question guiding this research is whether the “fear of missing out” (FOMO) helps explain the impact of intense social networking on students’ mental well-being in India. While earlier studies have reported connections between social media use and psychological health, few have examined the mechanism particularly FOMO that underlies this relationship in the Indian context. In our view, understanding this mechanism is essential, as students here often juggle expectations from family, academics, and their social groups online [12, 32]. With so many overlapping digital obligations, the potential for anxiety or feeling left out may be greater, especially when students see themselves missing key events or achievements [5, 13].

Two theoretical perspectives are especially relevant. Social comparison theory [9, 21, 22, 31] proposes that individuals naturally evaluate themselves against others, and this tendency is heightened on social media. As a result, seeing others’ curated lives online may influence self-esteem or satisfaction. Self-regulation theory [1, 28] highlights the difficulties people face in managing their emotions and behaviorsparticularly when FOMO is strong.

Given these insights, we propose and test a conceptual model (see Fig. 1) in which FOMO acts as the link between social networking intensity (SNI) and mental well-being (MW). Rather than treating FOMO as a simple outcome, we argue it is the crucial psychological process connecting online activity to student well-being [19]. We hope this approach will help inform more effective mental health support and digital literacy programs, as well as future research focused on youth in India and similar contexts [14].

Literature review

The impact of social networking on mental well-being has attracted significant attention in psychological and educational research worldwide [18]. Numerous studies have highlighted both the positive and negative influences of visible platforms on students' lives [4, 26, 30].

Positive outcomes

Social media can provide important benefits, including social connectedness, access to emotional support, and opportunities for self-expression [4, 29]. In academic settings, platforms like WhatsApp and Instagram. Facilitate collaboration, peer learning, and information discrimination, which are associated with higher engagement and motivation among students [30]. Social media also enables access to professional resources and mental health support, which can positively affect well-being [34].

Negative outcomes and FOMO

Despite these benefits, the intensive use of social networks is associated with several risks. Research shows that excessive social media engagement increases vulnerability to negative emotions, loneliness, anxiety, and depression [11, 16, 34]. A central mechanism underlying these outcomes is the Fear of Missing Out (FOMO). FOMO is defined as the persistent apprehension that others might be having rewarding experiences from which one is absent [27]. This feeling is intensified by the constant stream of updates and achievements visible online [3, 12].

Indian students, in particular, may experience heightened FOMO due to a culture that emphasizes collective participation and peer group belonging [32]. Social comparison theory posits that frequent exposure to others’ accomplishments can trigger self-doubt and dissatisfaction [9, 23, 31]. Empirical research demonstrates that higher social networking intensity (SNI) directly correlates with increased FOMO, which then predicts poorer mental well-being outcomes [6, 27].

Recent literature supports a mediation model: SNIF → FOMO → Mental Well-Being, clarifying why simply reducing screen time is insufficient; psychological and social dynamics must also be addressed [3, 32].

The literature establishes that the intensity of social networking is intricately linked to mental health through FOMO. This pathway is especially salient in the Indian context, where cultural expectations and academic pressures converge online. Addressing FOMO in interventions may hold the key to improving student well-being.

Research methodology

Sample

This cross-sectional study was conducted among 357 undergraduate and postgraduate students enrolled in professional courses in Indore, Madhya Pradesh—a city recognized for its diverse higher education institutions. The age of participants ranged from 20 to 25 years. The sample size was determined using standard statistical formulas (confidence level: 95%, margin of error: 5%, population proportion: 0.5, and estimated population size: 5000 students), ensuring sufficient statistical power and external validity.

Sampling justification

While the sample was drawn from a single metropolitan area, Indore serves as a prominent education hub in central India, with diverse student representation from across the region. This provides a meaningful, contextually relevant snapshot, even if findings cannot be generalized to all Indian students. Future studies are encouraged to include multi-city and multi-state samples to enhance generalizability.

Descriptive statistics and reliability

The dataset of 357 responses showed normal distribution across all three main constructs: Social Network Intensity (SNI), Fear of Missing Out (FOMO), and Mental Well-being (MW). Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were above 0.90 for all scales, demonstrating excellent internal consistency [24].

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

EFA using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and varimax rotation confirmed that all items loaded cleanly onto their intended factors. Five principal components emerged, collectively explaining 63.8% of total variance—Routine Engagement (RE), Perception and Sense of Community (PSC), Awareness and Emotional Response (AER), Regret and Exclusion in Event Participation (REEP), and MW. Communalities were all above the 0.50 threshold, confirming the validity of the constructs.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

CFA indicated strong model fit: CFI = 0.89, RMSEA = 0.062, SRMR = 0.053. All constructs showed composite reliability (CR > 0.7) and average variance extracted (AVE > 0.5), indicating convergent and discriminant validity [10] (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The present study extends global research on social media and mental well-being by focusing on Indian college students, a group for whom digital connectivity is increasingly routine but often psychologically complex. The findings demonstrate that Social Network Intensity (SNI) significantly predicts Fear of Missing Out (FOMO), and that FOMO, in turn, is a principal factor driving lower mental well-being. This aligns with both the theoretical models [1, 9] and empirical evidence suggesting that the emotional experience of FOMO, not simply the time spent online, is what matters most [3, 6, 27].

The high prevalence of FOMO in this sample reflects unique cultural factors in India, [25] such as the expectation to maintain strong group affiliations both in academic and family life [12, 32]. These pressures intensify the psychological effects of missing online events, achievements, or group activities, which may not be as pronounced in more individualistic societies. The path analysis confirmed that FOMO completely mediated the relationship between SNI and mental well-being, underscoring that digital engagement’s impact on psychological health is driven by emotional processes rather than mere connectivity.

Link to theoretical frameworks

The results provide robust support for social comparison theory. In highly connected peer networks, students constantly evaluate themselves against selectively positive portrayals of others, increasing their risk of anxiety, low self-esteem, and emotional exhaustion [21, 22, 31]. This study also highlights the relevance of self-regulation theory, as students with higher FOMO appear less able to moderate their social media behaviors, contributing to greater emotional volatility [28].

Integration with existing literature

Consistent with prior international studies, this research found that increased SNI predicts higher FOMO [2, 27], and FOMO is a strong negative predictor of well-being [6, 11]. The Indian context, however, shows an amplification of these effects, likely due to social and familial expectations unique to the region [32].

At the same time, it is important to note the possibility of a bidirectional relationship. Students with higher baseline levels of FOMO may be more motivated to intensify their social networking activity to stay constantly connected. In this sense, FOMO may not only be a mediating outcome but also an antecedent factor driving greater SNI. This reciprocal dynamic aligns with feedback loop models of digital behavior, where emotional vulnerabilities and usage intensity reinforce each other over time. Future studies employing longitudinal designs could better disentangle these directions of influence.

Practical implications

The findings suggest that interventions for student well-being should prioritize building digital resilience, teaching healthy online habits, and addressing the emotional drivers of FOMO. Mindfulness-based workshops, digital detox programs, and peer-led support groups could reduce the impact of constant social comparison and help students better manage their online presence. University counseling centers may also develop targeted outreach for those reporting high FOMO, recognizing that reducing emotional vulnerability not simply device use should be the goal.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of validated international scales, robust statistical analysis, and a well-defined conceptual framework. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The sample was limited to one city and professional courses, so results may not generalize to all Indian students. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, and self-report measures may be influenced by social desirability bias. Future research should employ longitudinal or mixed-methods approaches, sample from multiple regions, and examine additional variables such as personality traits or academic performance.

While our findings suggest that higher social networking intensity predicts greater FOMO, it is equally plausible that students with elevated FOMO tendencies engage more heavily with social networks in order to remain constantly connected. This indicates a potential bidirectional relationship, where FOMO and social networking intensity mutually reinforce one another [35]. Future longitudinal and experimental studies should test this reciprocal cycle to clarify directionality [36].

Conclusion

This research provides clear evidence that the psychological effects of social media among Indian college students are best understood through the lens of FOMO. Rather than a one-way causal link, the findings suggest a dynamic interplay: while high engagement with social networking platforms is associated with greater FOMO, it is equally plausible that students with high FOMO engage more intensively with these platforms to remain constantly connected. This reciprocal relationship highlights the potential of a feedback loop where FOMO and social networking intensity mutually reinforce one another, ultimately shaping mental well-being outcomes.

Addressing these emotional mechanisms, such as FOMO rather than simply limiting screen time, is critical for any effective campus mental health strategy. For policymakers and educators, the implication is clear: interventions must focus on digital literacy, building resilience to social comparison, and supporting students in developing balanced, mindful relationships with social media. As digital technologies continue to shape student life, recognizing and mitigating these bidirectional emotional costs will remain essential to promoting student well-being in India and beyond.
