First cleavage signatures define a temporal trade-off and predict embryo fitness
Authors: Daphne Norma Crasta, Si Won Lee, Jolene Fredrickson, Theodore Trejo, Nadeem Ghani Khan, Shama Prasada Kabekkodu, Satish Kumar Adiga, Yulian Zhao, Guruprasad Kalthur, Nagarajan Kannan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly embryonic development is marked by substantial variability in cleavage dynamics, yet the extent to which first cleavage (FC) features relate to subsequent developmental progression remains incompletely defined. Using parthenogenetic (haploid and diploid) and fertilized embryos from Swiss albino mice, we combined time-lapse imaging with mechanistic analyses to define a novel FC-state grading system (FC-I to FC-IV) based on cleavage symmetry, fragmentation, and correction of the cleavage axis. Aberrant FCs arose from cytoskeletal instability, persistent cytoplasmic bridges, chromosome lagging, and transient nuclear mispositioning. We identified a reproducible inverse relationship between FC duration and subsequent cleavage interval, that predicts blastocyst quality and termed it as ‘first cleavage-associated compensatory timing (FACT) relationship’. Haploid parthenogenetic embryos lacked this compensatory timing relationship, which was observed in fertilized embryos and diploid parthenotes, coinciding with markedly reduced developmental progression and competence. Analysis of > 1,100 human embryos revealed analogous FC-states and an inverse relationship between consecutive cleavage intervals that was associated with blastocyst morphology and developmental progression, with no clear association with aneuploidy. Introduction

In mammals, the earliest stages of embryogenesis unfold through a series of cleavage divisions that transform the activated oocyte into a multicellular blastocyst capable of implantation1,2. These mitotic divisions are driven by a complex orchestration of intrinsic and extrinsic factors, ranging from maternal cytoplasmic stores and chromatin architecture to environmental conditions and epigenetic regulation3–7. The first mitotic cleavage is a critical developmental milestone and represents the embryo’s first test of mitotic integrity following fertilization or artificial activation. Errors at this stage are linked with chromosomal instability, abnormal cleavage patterns, irregular blastomere numbers, asynchrony, or fragmentation8–13, which are associated with developmental arrest or impaired implantation. Current embryo assessment approaches, including morphology, time-lapse morpho-kinetics, and preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A), provide distinct yet complementary information about embryo development14–19. However, these approaches do not fully capture the dynamic cellular processes underlying early cleavage progression, and the relationship between first-cleavage behaviour and subsequent developmental competence remains incompletely understood.

Early embryonic development exhibits a recognizable temporal structure, yet it is not strictly deterministic20. Embryos frequently deviate from canonical cleavage patterns, displaying delayed, accelerated, or asynchronous divisions, as well as atypical events such as direct cleavage or uneven blastomere formation8,11,13. Importantly, deviations from optimal morpho-kinetic ranges do not invariably preclude further development, as a subset of embryos is capable of progressing to the blastocyst-stage and achieving implantation despite early irregularities21. These observations suggest that early development is regulated not only by absolute timing parameters but also by a degree of flexibility in developmental kinetics, reflecting context-dependent variation in cell cycle progression22,23. This behaviour can be conceptualized as an embryo-kinetic compensation mechanism, whereby embryos partially normalize developmental progression following early deviations in cleavage dynamics. Rather than representing a fixed or hard-wired timing system, such responses likely reflect adaptive modulation of cell cycle progression and checkpoint control during early embryogenesis24,25. This interpretation is consistent with observations that early cleavage abnormalities do not uniformly predict developmental failure and that morpho-kinetic parameters are most informative when interpreted as integrated patterns rather than isolated time points9,26. Experimental models that permit controlled manipulation of activation mode and ploidy provide an opportunity to investigate these early cleavage dynamics in greater mechanistic detail.

Parthenogenetic embryos, derived from oocyte activation in the absence of sperm contribution, offer a unique experimental model for dissecting the maternal contributions to early embryonic development27,28. Although they cannot yield viable offspring in mammals due to imprinting defects and absence of paternal input29,30, they can successfully reach the blastocyst-stage in vitro, enabling investigation of the paternal contribution to cleavage dynamics, cell cycle regulation, and developmental potential31,32. Cleavage anomalies are reported more frequently in haploid parthenotes than fertilized diploid embryos33,34, but the mechanisms underlying this disparity, particularly in relation to cleavage orientation and cycle timing, remain poorly understood. In this context, understanding how early cleavage dynamics, particularly during the first two mitoses, influence cell cycle progression and subsequent blastocyst formation could provide a powerful new lens through which to assess embryo quality. We therefore hypothesized that the dynamics and symmetry of the first embryonic cleavage act as early, non-invasive indicators of the efficiency of cell cycle progression and can predict subsequent developmental competence in both parthenogenetic and fertilized embryos.

In this study, we made a serendipitous discovery of irregular first cleavage (FC) dynamics in haploid parthenogenetic embryos of the Swiss albino mice. What initially appeared to be atypical cleavage behaviour proved to represent reproducible patterns of kinetic deviation followed by partial temporal normalization, consistent with an embryo-kinetic compensation process. By systematically comparing haploid parthenotes with fertilized embryos, we uncovered that first cleavage signatures not only capture the developmental constraints imposed by maternal-only inheritance but also are strongly associated with blastocyst formation. Extending this framework to human biology, we demonstrate that first cleavage dynamics are observed in human preimplantation embryos and serve as potential non-invasive indicators of embryo developmental competence. Our FC - state framework integrates cleavage-axis morphology, cytokinesis progression, and early morpho-dynamics into a complementary, non-invasive phenotypic assessment that may provide additional information beyond conventional morpho-kinetic timing . Together, our findings identify the earliest mitosis as a critical readout of embryo progression and developmental fitness, providing a conceptual framework and potential practical applications for understanding early embryogenesis.

Results

Haploid parthenotes exhibit early developmental arrest and genomic instability

In the present study, we investigated three model systems: in vitro fertilization (IVF)-derived embryos (SexE) and parthenogenetically activated embryos (ASexE), generated using strontium chloride activation in the presence and absence of cytochalasin D to obtain diploid (Dip-AsexE) and haploid asexual parthenogenetic embryos (Hap-AsexE), respectively (schematics in Fig. 1A). To dissect the developmental potential of embryos generated through distinct reproductive modes, we compared SexE, Dip-ASexE, and Hap-ASexE embryos of Swiss albino mice.

SexE and Dip-ASexE embryos successfully progressed to the blastocyst-stage (89.36% and 84.21%, respectively), whereas Hap-ASexE exhibited a drastic reduction, with only 10.81% forming blastocysts (p < 0.0001; Fig. 1B). Developmental divergence was evident from the 3C-stage onwards (Fig. 1C), and odds ratio analysis confirmed a strong bias towards developmental failure in Hap-ASexE (Fig. 1D). Morphologically, SexE and Dip-AsexE blastocysts displayed well-expanded blastocoel and well-organized inner cell masses, whereas Hap-ASexE took longer time to progress and reach blastocyst, which were small, and poorly structured (Figure S1A-B).

Quantification of total cell numbers revealed that Dip-ASexE (64.47 ± 2.84) and Hap-ASexE (80.92 ± 6.76) blastocysts contained counts comparable to SexE (73.49 ± 2.21; Fig. 1E-F), despite the striking morphological defects observed in Hap-ASexE. Functional assays further showed elevated apoptosis in parthenogenetic groups (Fig. 1G-H), and pronounced genomic instability in Hap-ASexE, which exhibited a fourfold increase in γ-H2AX foci compared to SexE (9.48 ± 1.41 vs. 2.09 ± 0.36, p < 0.001; Fig. 1I-J). Together, these results demonstrate that while diploid parthenotes largely preserve developmental potential, haploid parthenotes undergo early arrest and form blastocysts with poor morphology, delayed progression, elevated apoptosis, and increased DNA damage.

First cleavage-associated compensatory timing relationship supports robust blastocyst generation in murine embryos

Time-lapse imaging of ~ 430 Swiss albino embryos revealed that haploid parthenogenetic embryos (Hap-ASexE) exhibited a significantly prolonged corrected embryonic cell cycle 1 (ECC1) relative to diploid parthenotes (Dip-ASexE) and fertilized controls (SexE) (Fig. 2A). The first cleavage (FC) progressed through distinct morpho-dynamic transitions during progression from the 1-cell (1C) to 2-cell (2C) stage. These FC-states were discerned using visible microscopic phenotypes, which composed of the initial appearance of the cleavage axis, subsequent irregular fragmentation, and eventual reappearance of the cleavage axis, which were distinguished morphologically as ‘early-2C’, ‘transitional-2C’, and ‘complete-2C’ stages (Fig. 2B). We refined the characterization of FC-states into four phenotypic classes (FC-I to -IV; Table 1). The FC-I embryos displayed fully symmetric division with no morphological irregularities (Video S1A); while FC-II to -IV embryos displayed increasing severity of irregularities, ranging from minor cytoplasmic movement at the cleavage axis (FC-II, Video S1B), to delayed cytokinesis with irregular blastomere morphology (FC-III, Video S1C), and extensive cytoplasmic fragmentation (FC-IV, Video S1D). Strikingly, Hap-ASexE displayed an enrichment of severe FC-III/IV states (~ 80%), in contrast to SexE and Dip-ASexE (Fig. 2C). The embryo developmental efficiency from the 1C to blastocyst-stage progressively declined across the first cleavage (FC) phenotypes, with FC-I/II showing the highest success and FC-IV the lowest (Fig. 2D), while the developmental timeline was slightly delayed in FC-III/-IV post 5C-stage (Figure S1C).

Investigation into whether FC-states influence early embryo morphokinetics prompted a thorough analysis of time-lapse data. The developmental timeline for resolution of FC-state irregularity (early-2C-to-complete-2C) was in the order of FC IV > III > II > I, in SexE, as well as Dip-AsexE and Hap-ASexE (Fig. 2E, Figure S1D-F). In contrast, the subsequent developmental timeline for progression from the complete-2C-to-4C-stage, exhibited the opposite trends i.e., FC I > II> III > IV, in SexE, as well as Dip-AsexE and Hap-ASexE (Figure S1G-I). This inverse pattern suggested a compensatory timing relationship wherein, the embryos that required longer to transition from early- to complete-2C subsequently accelerated the complete-2C-to-4C progression, which we termed as the “first cleavage-associated compensatory timing (FACT) relationship”. This temporal compensation was robustly maintained in SexE (r2 = 0.198, p < 0.01) and Dip-ASexE (r2 = 0.394, p < 0.001) but was absent in Hap-ASexE (r2 = 0.0017, p = ns), where prolonged early-to-complete-2C transition failed to trigger accelerated complete-2C-to-4C progression (Fig. 2F). To distinguish the duration of the first embryonic cell cycle from the duration of cytokinesis following pronuclear fading (PNf), we additionally analyzed the absolute timing of first cleavage (t2) (Figure S2A-C). In fertilized (SexE) embryos, FC-IV embryos exhibited a significantly prolonged timing of first cleavage compared with FC-I-III embryos, consistent with previous reports associating delayed first cleavage with reduced developmental competence35. In contrast, Dip-ASexE and Hap-ASexE embryos displayed uniformly shorter timing of first cleavage across all FC-state categories, with no significant differences, suggesting that the accelerated first cleavage timing reflects intrinsic differences between embryo types rather than FC-state. These findings indicate that the absolute timing of first cleavage (t2) and the corrected embryonic cell cycle 1 (t2-tPNf) represent complementary, but distinct, parameters of early embryo development. Retrospective analysis further linked FC severity to extended PNf duration, whereas no association was observed between PNf-to-early-2C interval and FC-states, localizing the source of delay to post-PNf cleavage dynamics (Figure S2D-I). Together, these findings reveal that the fidelity of the first cleavage is closely associated with the coordinated subsequent developmental timing and successful preimplantation development (Fig. 2G).

Cytoskeletal and nuclear defects underlie irregular first cleavage state

To investigate the mechanistic basis of the irregular first cleavage state, we examined ploidy integrity, chromosome segregation, nuclear dynamics, and cytoskeletal organization across fertilized and parthenogenetic embryos of Swiss albino mice.

Ploidy analysis at the complete-2C-stage confirmed that SexE and Dip-ASexE embryos were predominantly diploid (39.93 ± 1.38 and 38.56 ± 0.22 respectively), whereas Hap-ASexE embryos carried haploid (19.78 ± 0.64) chromosome complements, consistent with the activation protocols used (Fig. 3A-B). This validation step confirmed the accuracy of embryo generation and provided a baseline for downstream comparisons.

When assessed at the early-2C-stage, we observed cytoplasmic bridges between blastomeres (Fig. 3C-D), along with presence of lagging chromosomes at the transitional-2C-stage (Fig. 3E-F). While these defects were most frequent in Hap-ASexE embryos, they were also observed in Dip-ASexE and SexE embryos. Lagging chromosomes and persistent cytoplasmic bridges were associated with abnormal first-cleavage phenotypes; however, the present study does not establish a causal relationship between chromosome segregation defects and cleavage abnormalities.

Interestingly, aberrant nuclear positioning was detected exclusively in parthenogenetic embryos, where nuclei transiently shifted toward the periphery before relocating to the center at the complete-2C-stage (Fig. 3G-H). This phenomenon was absent in SexE embryos. Among the parthenogenetic groups, the haploid parthenotes exhibited the highest incidence of aberrant nuclear positioning defects, indicating that both the absence of paternal contribution and haploidy exacerbate susceptibility to nuclear positioning errors under irregular cleavage conditions.

To probe potential underlying mechanisms, we examined cell-cell adhesion and cytoskeletal organization. E-cadherin was already detectable from the early-2C-stage in all embryo types and persisted through the transitional-2C and complete-2C-stages (Fig. 3I-J), indicating that cell-cell adhesion is established early and is unlikely to be the primary cause of cleavage instability. Analysis of the cytoskeleton further showed that spindle organization remained intact in both normal and irregular cleaving embryos, whereas actin organization was markedly disrupted in irregular cleavage embryos (Fig. 3K-L). The selective actin disruption indicates an association between altered cortical organization and the irregular FC-state.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the irregular first cleavage state arises from a cascade of defects- including cytoplasmic bridging, chromosome mis-segregation, parthenote-specific aberrant nuclear positioning, and actin disorganization. While haploid parthenotes exhibited the highest frequency of abnormalities, similar features were also observed in diploid parthenotes and, to a lesser extent, in fertilized embryos, indicating that these processes represent a general cellular mechanism of cleavage irregularity.

First cleavage morpho-dynamics and first cleavage-associated compensatory timing relationship as non-invasive early markers of embryo viability

To assess the applicability of the phenomenon observed in mice to other mammals, we conducted a retrospective analysis of time-lapse imaging data from human sexually fertilized embryos (Human-SexE) (n = 1,119 embryos) and rare human 1PN embryos (Human-1PN-E) (n = 5 embryos) obtained from the Mayo Clinic assisted reproduction program. Unlike the experimentally generated mouse embryo groups, the human 1PN embryos represented a morphologically defined clinical subgroup without confirmed ploidy or parental origin. Therefore, the human cohort was analyzed primarily to assess the applicability of the FC-state classification and associated morpho-kinetic relationships in a clinical IVF setting rather than to validate the ploidy-dependent biological mechanisms identified in the mouse models.

Utilizing the criteria outlined in Fig. 2B, we characterized the Human embryos. Four of the 1,134 embryos analyzed (0.35%) exhibited direct cleavage, including two embryos with 1→3 cleavage and two embryos with 1→4 cleavage. Because these embryos bypassed the complete-2C-stage required for FC-state classification, they were excluded from subsequent FC-state analyses. Among the remaining embryos, six embryos showed atypical pronuclear configurations (one with 3 PN, three with no PN and two with 2PN + 2 micronuclei) and were also excluded from the analysis. In the remaining 1,124 embryos, all four FC-states were identified (Fig. 4A; Video S2), with FC-III/IV predominating (~ 70%) and FC-I/II comprising ~ 30% in Human-SexE (Fig. 4B). A small fraction of the total analyzed embryos (5 out of 1,124; 0.44%) was morphologically classified as 1PN (Human-1PN-E), which showed only FC-states-II, -III and -IV, with majority (4 out of 5 embryos) exhibiting FC-III and -IV, similar to the Hap-AsexE of Mus musculus populations (Fig. 2C). The odds ratio analysis confirmed a strong bias toward developmental failure in Human-1PN-E (Fig. 4C). The developmental timeline assessment across FC-states did not show any differences (Figure S3A), but developmental tracking revealed FC-IV embryos showing the sharpest decline, with arrests at all stages (Fig. 4D). Kinetic analyses demonstrated that higher FC-states were associated with prolonged early-2C-to-complete-2C duration (FC-IV > III > II > I; Figure S3B) and a compensatory reduction in complete-2C-to-4C timing (Figure S3C). Retrospective analysis of PNf duration showed increase with increase in FC-states (Figure S3D), while duration from PNf -to-early-2C-stage did not show any correlation in FC-I to -III, but was highest in FC-IV (Figure S3E). Correlation analyses in Human-SexE revealed that good-quality blastocysts (with well-differentiated inner cell mass and trophectoderm) exhibited a strong negative relationship between early-2C to -complete-2C and complete-2C-to-4C intervals (r²=0.373, p < 0.0001), whereas this compensatory mechanism was absent in arrested embryos or poor-quality blastocysts from Human-SexE and in arrested Human-1PN-E (Fig. 4E, Figure S3F-G).

Assessment of human embryo development and blastocyst morphology in Human-SexE in relation to the FC-states at 2C-stage revealed intriguing findings. FC-I to -III embryos were associated with blastocysts with properly- differentiated inner cell mass and trophectoderm (Fig. 4F-H), as indicated by low Gardner Grade-ordinal scores (Score 1 to 336,37;). In contrast, FC-IV embryos were linked to blastocysts with poorly- differentiated inner cell mass and trophectoderm exhibiting higher Gardner Grade-scores (Score 4 and above). As expected, embryos exhibiting FC-IV were at the highest risk of developmental arrest or forming poor quality blastocysts (Fig. 4I). Importantly, clinical decision records aligned with these findings: FC-I to -III embryos were preferentially selected for transfer, while FC-IV were largely deemed unsuitable for transfer due to their poor/average quality (Fig. 4J). FC-state did not associate with increased rates of aneuploidy in preimplantation genetic testing (PGT from trophectoderm biopsy). However, the incidence of mosaic embryos marginally increased with higher FC-states (Fig. 4K-L). Moreover, the compensatory temporal correlation persisted in both euploid and aneuploid embryos (Figure S3H). Transfer outcomes didn’t show any co-relations with the FC -grades (Figure S3I), but this needs to be further refined by conducting a larger-scale, multi-centric study.

Discussion

The earliest embryonic division, the first cleavage (FC), represents a critical developmental event that reflects the structural and functional competence of the zygote to sustain subsequent embryonic progression38. Our study uncovers fundamental insights into how activation mode, ploidy, and cleavage morpho-dynamics interact to shape early embryonic development. By integrating mouse model with retrospective human embryo analysis, we identify haploid parthenotes as uniquely vulnerable to early arrest, describe a cytoskeleton-dependent “irregular FC ” state associated with impaired developmental progression and introduce the “first cleavage-associated compensatory timing (FACT) relationship” that is associated with embryo developmental competence. Together, these findings identify first cleavage phenotyping and timing dynamics as informative, non-invasive measures associated with embryo developmental competence, with potential mechanistic and clinical relevance.

Consistent with longstanding evidence that haploidy destabilizes mammalian embryogenesis34,39, our data demonstrate that mouse haploid parthenotes (Hap-ASexE) undergo profound developmental arrest, with blastocyst formation rates below 15% and markedly compromised morphology. Elevated apoptosis and accumulation of γ-H2AX foci indicate that genomic instability is a key contributor to this arrest. Unlike haploid embryonic stem cells which can be stabilized in vitro by checkpoint adaptation or selective pressures40,41, haploid cleavage stage embryos lack such compensatory mechanisms, highlighting the heightened sensitivity of early embryonic development to ploidy imbalance. Notably, diploid parthenotes (Dip-ASexE) largely bypass these constraints, highlighting the critical buffering role of diploidy, irrespective of paternal contribution. Although increasing FC-state severity was associated with reduced developmental competence in the pooled embryo data analysis (Fig. 2D), the FC-I Hap-ASexE embryos exhibited markedly lower blastocyst formation than FC-I Dip-ASexE and SexE embryos (Figure S2J-L). This indicates that FC-state alone does not fully account for the developmental limitations of haploid embryos. Thus, abnormal first-cleavage morpho-dynamics contribute to developmental failure, but haploidy imposes additional intrinsic constraints that compromise embryo competence independently of cleavage phenotype. These observations resonate with clinical reports of rare human parthenogenetic blastocysts, which similarly fail to progress42–44.

Our fluorescence imaging shows that the irregular FC-state arises through a cascade of interconnected defects: cytoplasmic bridging between blastomeres, lagging chromosomes, parthenote-specific nuclear mispositioning, and actin disorganization. This constellation of features suggests an important role for cortical mechanics in irregular cleavage, aligning with prior work implicating actin contractility in cytokinesis fidelity45–47. Previous studies have shown that chromosome segregation errors perturb cleavage mechanics, delay cytokinesis through the abscission checkpoint, and occur during the first cleavage of human embryos12. Consistent with these observations, our findings demonstrate an association between chromosome segregation defects and abnormal first-cleavage morpho-dynamics. However, because the present study is observational, it cannot determine whether chromosome segregation errors precede cleavage abnormalities or arise secondarily from defective cytokinesis. Future mechanistic studies will be required to resolve the temporal and causal relationship between these processes. Although most prominent in haploid parthenotes, irregular FC phenotypes also occurred in diploid parthenotes and fertilized embryos, indicating that irregular cleavage represents a universal cellular stress state, not solely a haploid pathology. The cytoplasmic bridges we document are particularly intriguing, as they may permit inter-blastomere chromosomal transfer, echoing descriptions of cytoplasmic channels in early embryos48,49. Aberrant nuclear positioning defects, restricted to parthenotes, further suggest that maternal-only cytoplasmic programming is insufficient to maintain nuclear anchoring, exposing vulnerabilities in cytoskeletal-nuclear crosstalk.

The graded severity of first-cleavage abnormalities (FC-I to FC-IV) and the associated genomic instability in mouse embryos may be interpretable, at least in part, through the distinctive DNA replication landscape of the preimplantation embryo. Single-cell replication atlases have established that 1C and 2C embryos lack a defined replication-timing program and instead replicate their genomes progressively via exceptionally slow replication forks (< 33–66 bp/min), rendering S phase intrinsically susceptible to perturbation50,51. Within this framework, chromosome lagging, persistent cytoplasmic bridges, and γ-H2AX foci in FC-III/IV embryos are consistent with manifestations of genomic stress associated with early embryonic replication vulnerability51. However, the present study was not designed to determine the molecular pathways linking replication stress to cleavage stage dynamics, and this remains an important area for future investigation. Beyond genome stability, early cleavage stage morpho-dynamics also coincide with major developmental transitions that establish embryonic developmental potential.

Totipotency, the ability of a cell to generate all embryonic and extraembryonic lineages, is transient and progressively lost as cells transition to pluripotency38,52,53. In mice, this transition occurs around the 2C-to-4C-stage and coincides with zygotic genome activation (ZGA), chromatin remodelling, and metabolic reprogramming38,54–58. Because these events overlap temporally with the cleavage stages examined here, our observed morpho-kinetic relationships should be interpreted as phenotypic associations rather than direct evidence of changes in totipotency, since molecular markers were not assessed.

A major finding of this study is the identification of an inverse relationship between the early-2C to -complete-2C transition and the subsequent complete-2C -to-4C transition in fertilized embryos and diploid parthenotes. Embryos exhibiting prolonged early cleavage transitions frequently displayed shorter subsequent developmental intervals, revealing an inverse relationship between consecutive cleavage phases with a tendency toward partial convergence of overall developmental timing. We refer to this reproducible morpho-kinetic temporal trade-off phenomenon as the first cleavage-associated compensatory timing (FACT) relationship. Notably, this temporal trade-off occurs during the 2-cell to 4-cell developmental window, when the early embryo is transitioning from totipotency toward progressively restricted developmental potential. In this context, “compensatory timing” describes the observed inverse temporal association between consecutive cleavage intervals and should not be interpreted as evidence of an active regulatory or compensatory mechanism. Whether this phenomenon reflects active developmental regulation, passive adaptation to transient perturbations, or ploidy-dependent cell cycle dynamics remains to be determined.

Interestingly, the compensatory timing relationship was robust in fertilized embryos and diploid parthenotes but absent in haploid parthenotes, in which longer early -2C-to-complete-2C transition failed to trigger acceleration of subsequent divisions. These findings suggest that checkpoint-like processes may operate not only at the level of DNA integrity59,60 but also in association with cellular morpho-dynamics, echoing principles of temporal compensation described in circadian and cell cycle oscillators61,62. Similarly, the inverse relationship between consecutive cleavage intervals observed here agrees with previous human embryo studies demonstrating that embryos with prolonged first cell cycles often undergo shortened subsequent cell cycles, particularly in association with fragmentation63. Our findings extend these observations by integrating cleavage-axis stability, cytokinesis progression, blastomere symmetry, and fragmentation into a unified first-cleavage morpho-dynamic framework. Rather than representing a feature exclusive to fragmented embryos, these results suggest that dynamic adjustment of cleavage timing may constitute a general response to early morphogenetic perturbations, although the molecular basis and functional significance of this phenomenon remain to be established.

Additionally, our retrospective analysis of > 1,100 human embryos support the clinical relevance of FC phenotyping by demonstrating that the FC-state framework and associated cleavage stage timing relationships are also observed in a clinical setting. This analysis was intended to assess the translational relevance of these developmental patterns rather than to validate the ploidy-dependent mechanisms identified in the mouse models. While earlier human studies primarily evaluated individual morpho-kinetic features such as cleavage timing64 or cleavage-plane orientation65,66, the FC-state framework integrates cleavage-axis stability, cytokinesis progression, blastomere symmetry, and cytoplasmic fragmentation into a single phenotypic classification. In the present study, all four FC-states were identified in human embryos, with FC-III/IV embryos showing the sharpest developmental attrition and poorest blastocyst morphology. Furthermore, the FACT relationship was present in good-quality but absent in poor-quality blastocysts, suggesting that dynamic morpho-kinetics provide additional developmental information beyond static morphology or karyotype status. Together, these findings indicate that FC phenotyping may complement conventional embryo assessment by capturing aspects of early developmental dynamics that are not fully reflected by existing evaluation approaches.

In our study, the FC-state stratification showed concordance with clinical transfer decisions, independent of preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) results. Notably, FC-state was not associated with aneuploidy rates, consistent with emerging recognition that morpho-kinetic abnormalities may reflect broader cellular, cytoplasmic or biomechanical perturbations rather than chromosomal abnormalities alone67–69. These findings support further investigation of FC phenotyping and the first cleavage-associated compensatory timing (FACT) relationship analysis as complementary approaches alongside PGT and standard morphology assessments, particularly given the false-positive and false-negative limitations of PGT70,71.

The FC-state framework was applicable only to embryos completing the canonical first cleavage sequence. Human embryos exhibiting direct cleavage (1→3 or 1→4) were excluded because they bypassed the complete-2C-stage required for FC-state classification and likely represent a distinct category of first-cleavage abnormality rather than an extension of the FC-I-IV spectrum. Therefore, FC-IV should be interpreted as the most severe phenotype within the FC-state classification, rather than as encompassing all possible first-cleavage abnormalities. The retrospective study design precluded evaluation of the influence of patient-specific factors, including infertility diagnosis, stimulation protocol, and severe male factor infertility, on FC-state distribution. Future prospective studies will be required to determine how these clinical variables contribute to first-cleavage phenotypes.

We acknowledge several limitations of this study. Although parthenogenetic embryos provide a controlled model to investigate the influence of activation mode and ploidy on early development, they do not fully recapitulate physiological fertilization. Artificial activation, the absence of paternal contribution, and altered epigenetic regulation may each contribute to the observed phenotypes. In particular, the profound developmental impairment of haploid parthenotes likely reflects multiple biological constraints in addition to their enrichment for severe FC phenotypes, and adequately powered subgroup analyses will be required to distinguish the independent contributions of FC-state and embryo type to developmental outcome. Blastocyst quality was assessed primarily by cell counts and apoptosis/DNA damage measures; future studies incorporating lineage-specific markers (e.g., OCT4 and CDX2) will enable more rigorous evaluation of lineage specification and inner cell mass allocation. Ploidy was assessed only at the 2C-stage, despite the potential for endoreduplication and mixoploidy in haploid parthenogenetic embryos72, highlighting the need for ploidy verification throughout development. Furthermore, analyses were confined to in vitro systems without embryo transfer validation, and molecular totipotency markers (e.g., Dux, Zscan4, and MERVL) were not assessed. Therefore, causal links between cleavage stage morpho-dynamics and developmental-state transitions cannot be established from the present study.

The human embryo analysis was retrospective and based primarily on unadjusted comparisons; therefore, the findings should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of independent predictive effects or causality. Potential confounding from patient-level clustering and clinical variables, including maternal age, insemination method, ovarian stimulation protocol, and infertility diagnosis, should be addressed in future prospective studies using multivariable analyses. The limited number of 1PN embryos and the absence of confirmed ploidy preclude direct validation of the haploid-specific findings from the mouse models. Because pronuclear morphology alone does not define embryo ploidy or parental origin73,74, the human 1PN embryos should be considered a morphologically defined clinical subgroup rather than confirmed haploid or parthenogenetic embryos. Finally, although the FC-state framework was developed using predefined criteria and standardized time-lapse annotations, inter-observer reproducibility was not formally assessed, and benchmarking against conventional grading systems (e.g., Veeck or ESHRE/ALPHA) was beyond the scope of this study because FC-state classification incorporates dynamic morpho-kinetic features not captured by static morphology. Future studies assessing reproducibility, benchmarking against established grading systems, and integrating automated image analysis, machine learning, and molecular profiling will further strengthen the biological interpretation and clinical applicability of the FC-state framework.

Together, our findings identify the first-cleavage patterns as an informative morpho-dynamic signatures of early embryonic developmental competence. By linking first-cleavage morpho-dynamics with cytoskeletal integrity, ploidy status, and subsequent developmental timing, this study provides a framework for understanding how early differences in embryo development relate to later developmental progression across model systems. Future studies integrating live imaging of oocyte-to-blastocyst developmental assays and molecular profiling will be needed to determine whether the observed cleavage stage timing relationships reflect active regulatory processes, ploidy-dependent cell cycle adaptation, or broader morpho-dynamic responses to early developmental perturbation, and to evaluate their translational utility in assisted reproduction.

Methods

Mice

Adult female and male Swiss albino mice (8–12 weeks) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (JAX stock #034608) and maintained in standard mouse facility at Mayo Clinic, MN, USA or obtained from the breeding colony at Central Animal Research Facility, Manipal Academy of Higher Education, Manipal, India. The mice were fed with standard chow diet and water ad libitum and maintained under 12 h–12 h light-dark cycle at 23 ± 2 °C.

Ethics declaration for animal studies

All procedures done on mice were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Kasturba Medical College, Manipal, India and the Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), Rochester, USA. All experiments were performed in accordance with their guidelines and regulations. The authors complied with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Murine oocyte collection

The adult female mice were stimulated using 5 IU of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG) and 10 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) intra-peritoneally at an interval of 48 h. At 13.5 h post hCG administration, the mice were euthanised using cervical dislocation (as approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Kasturba Medical College, Manipal, India) or by carbon dioxide inhalation followed by cervical dislocation [as approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), Rochester, USA]. The mice were then dissected, and the oocyte cumulus complexes (OCCs) were collected by teasing the oviduct in Earle’s balanced salt solution (EBSS) medium or M2 medium. The OCCs were either subjected to in vitro fertilization (IVF) to obtain sexually fertilized embryos (SexE) or to parthenogenetic activation to obtain haploid or diploid parthenogenetic embryos (ASexE).

In vitro fertilization of murine oocytes

The IVF was carried out as described in Crasta et al. (2023)32. Briefly, the caudal spermatozoa were released in EBSS medium containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 20% O2 for 2 h to induce capacitation. Next, the motile spermatozoa were collected by swim up technique using EBSS medium containing 2.5% BSA. The sperm count was adjusted to 3–5 millions/mL to prepare insemination droplets (80 µL) covered with light mineral oil (9305, Irvine Scientific). OCCs were collected from super-ovulated female mice and randomly transferred to each insemination droplet and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 20% O2. At 13 h post insemination, the fertilized oocytes were washed and observed under the inverted microscope (x400) to confirm fertilization and transferred to the embryoscope chamber slide (n = 4 embryos/well) in M16 medium overlaid with light mineral oil, for time-lapse imaging.

Parthenogenetic activation of murine oocytes

The OCCs were subjected to the haploid activation medium (10 mM strontium chloride in M16 medium without Ca2+ and Mg2+) or the diploid activation medium (10 mM strontium chloride and 1 µg/mL cytochalasin D in M16 medium without Ca2+ and Mg2+) overlaid with light mineral oil for 3 h75 at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 20% O2. This was followed by denuding using 0.75 mg/mL of hyaluronidase, wash using M16 medium, and observation under the inverted microscope (x400) to confirm activation, which was indicated by the presence of 1 PN for haploid parthenotes and 2 PN for diploid parthenotes. These were then transferred to the embryoscope chamber slide (n = 4 embryos/well) in M16 medium overlaid with light mineral oil, for time-lapse imaging.

Time-lapse monitoring of preimplantation stage mouse embryos

Four embryos were cultured per well in an EmbryoSlide (Vitrolife) containing 25 µL of M16 medium per well overlaid with 1.3 mL light mineral oil. The EmbryoSlide was placed in an EmbryoScope ES-D time-lapse (Vitrolife) incubator (at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 5% O2) for time-lapse imaging. Precise developmental kinetics were captured of the developing embryos in 3 planes at 5 min intervals for 120–150 h, until all the embryos reached the expanded blastocyst-stage. The timings of cell divisions were determined manually from the recordings, and it included timeline for formation of 2-cell (t2), 3-cell (t3), 4-cell (t4), 5-cell (t5), 6-cell (t6), 7-cell (t7), 8-cell (t8), morula (tM) and blastocyst (tB), as well as blastocyst expansion, with time of tPNf taken as 0 and the rest calculated accordingly76. During the first cleavage, we further subdivided the timeline into early-2C, transitional-2C, and complete-2C, and the timing of each was documented. Early-2C was defined as the initial appearance of a visible cleavage axis separating the embryo into two blastomeres while cytokinesis remained incomplete. Transitional-2C was defined as an intermediate stage during cytokinesis characterized by dynamic membrane remodelling at the cleavage interface, which may be accompanied by transient blastomere asymmetry, cytoplasmic bridging, membrane undulations, or temporary cytoplasmic fragmentation before completion of division. Complete-2C was defined as the formation of two morphologically distinct blastomeres separated by a stable and continuous cell boundary, indicating completion of cytokinesis. Additionally, we developed a FC dynamic-state scoring-system (FC-I to -IV) based on irregular cleavage-axis, delayed cytokinesis, irregular shaped blastomeres, and cytoplasmic fragments. A ‘regular cleavage axis’ was operationally defined as a symmetric meridional division producing two approximately equal blastomeres with a stable linear cleavage plane. An ‘irregular cleavage axis’ referred to a visibly skewed cleavage orientation associated with unstable membrane dynamics at the cleavage interface. ‘Delayed cytokinesis’ was defined morphologically by prolonged persistence of the transitional-2C-stage before formation of the complete-2C embryo, relative to the typical direct progression observed under identical imaging conditions. Based on these integrated features, all embryos were classified into one of the four first-cleavage phenotypes (FC-I to FC-IV). FC-I embryos exhibited symmetric cleavage with no visible morphological abnormalities. FC-II embryos displayed a regular cleavage axis with minor transient cytoplasmic movement or membrane remodelling at the cleavage interface but progressed efficiently to the complete-2C-stage. FC-III embryos showed an irregular cleavage axis with prolonged persistence of the transitional-2C-stage, accompanied by delayed cytokinesis and irregular blastomere morphology. FC-IV embryos exhibited severe cleavage abnormalities characterized by extensive cytoplasmic fragmentation and marked disruption of blastomere architecture.

Fluorescence staining and immunofluorescence of murine embryos

Embryos at defined stages were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C and processed for membrane, cytoskeletal, nuclear, and protein localization. For membrane/nuclear visualization, 2C embryos were stained with DiI (1,1’-Dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-Tetramethylindocarbocyanine Perchlorate) dissolved in ethanol (1 µg/mL, 15 min) and counterstained with DAPI. Nuclear positioning was evaluated in fixed 2C embryos following DAPI staining and microscopic imaging. Embryos were classified as exhibiting normal nuclear positioning when each blastomere contained a centrally localized nucleus, whereas embryos displaying one or both nuclei displaced toward the cell periphery or asymmetrically positioned within the blastomere were classified as exhibiting aberrant nuclear positioning. The analysis was based on morphological assessment of fixed embryos. Spindle organization was assessed in 2C embryos using anti–α-tubulin (1:150, overnight, 4 °C) followed by FITC-secondary antibody (1:500), and nuclei counterstained with DAPI. Actin filaments were labelled with Phalloidin-iFluor 488 (0.5X, 30 min, RT). For protein localization, embryos were permeabilized (0.5% Triton X-100), blocked in goat serum, and incubated with primary antibodies against E-cadherin (1:500) or γ-H2AX (1:250) overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500-1:1000, 1–2 h). γ-H2AX embryos were co-stained with Phalloidin-Texas Red. All samples were counterstained with DAPI, mounted in antifade medium, and imaged using fluorescence (Zeiss Axio Imager A1) or confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM980) with Z-stacks acquired for γ-H2AX analysis. DNA damage was further quantified by γ-H2AX foci analysis in confocal Z-stacks, normalized to DAPI⁺ nuclei. The γ-H2AX-positive nuclei were identified from maximum-intensity projections and manually counted in individual blastocysts. The F-actin organization was evaluated based on cortical fluorescence distribution and continuity. The E-cadherin membrane localization at blastomere–blastomere junctions were scored based on the presence of continuous junctional staining and presented as the percentage of embryos exhibiting intact junctional E-cadherin localization at the early-2C, transitional-2C and complete-2C-stages.

Chromosome preparation and centromere fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) of murine embryos

For chromosome spreads, 2C embryos were treated with colchicine (4 µg/mL, 16 h), exposed to hypotonic solution (20 min, 37 °C), and fixed sequentially with methanol: acetic acid: water mixtures (5:1:4 and 3:1:0). Fixed slides were subjected to RNase and pepsin pretreatment, ethanol dehydration, and hybridization with Cy3-labelled-centromere FISH probes (1:20; HLB Panagene- catalogue no. F3002) following denaturation (85 °C, 10 min) and hybridization (30 °C, 2 h). Post-hybridization washes in 2X SSC were followed by DAPI staining. Chromosome numbers were determined by integrating DAPI-stained metaphase chromosome morphology with Cy3-positive centromeric foci. The chromosomes were counted manually by direct examination of metaphase spreads under fluorescence microscopy. Representative metaphase plate images were subsequently captured for illustration of the chromosome spreads and centromeric FISH signals using confocal microscopy (x400).

Murine blastocyst analyses

Blastocysts were fixed in 4% PFA, stained with DAPI to determine total cell number (TCN, DAPI⁺ nuclei), or processed for apoptosis assays. Apoptosis was measured by Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay. Briefly, the blastocyst-stage embryos were permeabilized for 1 h (0.1% sodium citrate and 0.5% Triton X 100), washed and incubated with TUNEL reaction mixture (9:1::Dye: enzyme) (1 h, 37 °C, dark). Then washed and counterstained with DAPI, followed by mounting and imaging using fluorescence microscope. The TUNEL index was calculated as TUNEL⁺/DAPI⁺ nuclei.

Human embryos

The retrospective analysis of IVF/ICSI embryos was conducted under the Institutional Review Board-approved protocol at Mayo Clinic (IRB no. 15–007799). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board in accordance with 45 CFR 46.116 and HIPAA regulations, as the study involved minimal risk to participants and utilized de-identified data. A total of 1,134 autologous embryos from 114 patients who underwent ICSI/IVF cycles with oocyte retrieval between January 2014 and January 2023 at Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN, USA, were retrospectively included in this study. Donor gamete cycles and patients who did not consent to the use of their medical record information for research purposes were excluded from the study. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the retrospective human cohort are summarized in Supplementary Table S1, including the number of patients, number of embryos analyzed, maternal age, embryos analyzed per patient, and treatment characteristics where available. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, institutional ethical standards, and relevant guidelines and regulations.

Time-lapse imaging, analysis and genetic screening of human embryos

Data from embryos generated during routine IVF or ICSI procedures at Mayo Clinic were retrospectively analyzed. Following oocyte retrieval, fertilization had been performed by conventional IVF or ICSI, and pronuclear (PN) assessment had been conducted 12 h post-insemination as part of standard embryology workflow. Embryos recorded as having two pronuclei (2PN) were classified as human sexually fertilized embryos (Human-SexE), whereas single-pronuclear (1PN) embryos arising from fertilization failure were classified as human 1PN embryos (Human-1PN-E); embryos with other atypical PN configurations were excluded. These embryos had been individually cultured in the EmbryoScope+ time-lapse incubator (Vitrolife, Sweden) under 5% O₂/5.8% CO₂ and imaged at nine focal planes every 15 min. Morpho-kinetic parameters including the timing from insemination to PNf, 2C formation, and 2C-to-4C progression, were retrospectively extracted76. The first cleavage was categorized as early-2C, transitional-2C, and complete-2C. Each embryo was assigned an FC-state based on the grading criteria established in mice, with FC-IV category additionally including embryos bypassing complete-2C. Embryos exhibiting direct cleavage (1C → 3 C and 1C → 4C) were excluded from FC-state analysis because they bypass the complete-2C-stage required for FC-state classification and therefore could not be assigned to the FC-I to IV framework. All embryos were independently evaluated by two investigators using the predefined criteria, and any discrepant classifications were resolved by joint review and consensus before downstream analysis. Blastocyst quality data was collected from clinical database and retrospectively analyzed in relation to FC grading. The clinical assessment of blastocyst morphology was performed on day 5 through traditional morphologic evaluation, immediately before transfer, biopsy, vitrification, or clinical disposition using the Gardner grading system36,37with an internally derived grade conversion table (supplementary Table S2). Day 5 grading considered stage of embryo expansion, size, shape and compaction of the inner cell mass and distribution, scalloping and cell numbers of the trophectoderm. On day 5, a score of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6 was assigned to the inner cell mass and 1, 2, 3, 4 or 5 assigned to the trophectoderm, with lower values representing better morphology (scores 1–3 = good; >3 = poor36,37;). Based on trophectoderm biopsy results, blastocysts were categorized as euploid, aneuploid, or mixoploid. All imaging, morphological, and genetic data were analyzed retrospectively.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 software, California, USA. The two-way Analysis of variance (ANOVA) – Tukey’s multiple comparison test or Sidak’s multiple comparison test were used to compare various groups. The percentage data was analyzed using GraphPad Instat 3 software, California, USA. The Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the various groups. Correlation analysis was done using linear regression analysis and the R2 values and p-values were noted. Significance level p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.
