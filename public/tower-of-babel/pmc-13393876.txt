A Clinical nomogram for predicting chronic postsurgical pain in patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery
Authors: Wu Weinian, Li Yongjun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and internally validate a clinical nomogram for predicting chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) in patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), with the aim of facilitating early identification of high-risk patients and personalized perioperative management.MethodsThis retrospective case-control study enrolled 500 patients who underwent VATS between January 2022 and June 2024. Based on the presence of pain at 3 months postoperatively, patients were categorized into CPSP (n = 92) and non-CPSP (n = 408) groups. Perioperative variables including demographics, surgical details, and daily pain scores (visual analog scale, VAS) assessed at rest, during coughing, and during shoulder abduction on postoperative days 1 through 6 were collected. Univariate analysis, LASSO regression, and multivariate logistic regression were used to identify independent predictors. A nomogram was constructed and validated in a split cohort (70% training, 30% validation) using discrimination (area under the curve, AUC), calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow test), and decision curve analysis.ResultsMultivariate analysis identified higher postoperative shoulder abduction pain (OR = 2.012, 95% CI: 1.601–2.528), scar length (OR = 1.285, 95% CI: 1.101–1.500), and preoperative anxiety (OR = 3.245, 95% CI: 1.502–7.012) as independent risk factors for CPSP, while the use of intercostal sutures was a protective factor (OR = 0.221, 95% CI: 0.088–0.557). The nomogram incorporating these four predictors demonstrated excellent discrimination (training set AUC = 0.852, 95% CI: 0.805–0.899; validation set AUC = 0.837, 95% CI: 0.758–0.916) and good calibration (P > 0.05). Decision curve analysis confirmed its clinical utility across a wide threshold probability range.ConclusionA practical nomogram integrating postoperative shoulder abduction pain, scar length, preoperative anxiety, and intercostal suture use effectively predicts CPSP risk after VATS. This tool may assist clinicians in risk stratification, optimization of analgesic strategies, and implementation of targeted interventions to potentially reduce the incidence of CPSP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03632-6.Keywords: Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, Chronic postsurgical pain, Predictive model, Nomogram, Risk factors, Shoulder pain, Intercostal nerve protection

Introduction

Chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP), defined as pain persisting for at least three months after surgery that is not attributable to other causes, poses a significant clinical challenge and adversely affects long-term patient quality of life [1]. In thoracic surgery, despite the widespread adoption of minimally invasive techniques such as video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), which has reduced surgical trauma, the reported incidence of CPSP remains notably high, ranging from 30% to 57% [2, 3]. The underlying mechanisms are multifactorial, involving intraoperative intercostal nerve injury, inflammatory sensitization, and neuroplastic changes within central pain pathways [4, 5].

Growing evidence underscores that preventing CPSP is more cost-effective and clinically meaningful than treating established pain [6]. Consequently, the development of validated predictive tools for the early identification of high-risk patients is a crucial step toward implementing targeted preventive strategies [7]. Although several factors—including age, sex, preoperative pain history, and anxiety—have been associated with CPSP risk [8, 9], there is a paucity of robust prediction models specifically tailored for VATS patients. In particular, few models integrate dynamic perioperative pain trajectories with detailed surgical technical variables.

Notably, the intensity and pattern of acute postoperative pain have been identified as among the strongest predictors of CPSP [10]. Recent studies further suggest that early postoperative movement-evoked pain, such as shoulder abduction pain, may reflect the degree of nerve injury and functional impairment more accurately than pain at rest, thus potentially offering superior predictive value [11]. Additionally, surgical technical factors—including intercostal nerve protection and incision length—are increasingly recognized as modifiable elements influencing pain chronicity [12, 13].

Nevertheless, most existing studies continue to rely on conventional logistic regression without effectively addressing high-dimensional clinical data. Machine learning approaches, such as LASSO regression, offer advantages in feature selection, improving model robustness and clinical interpretability. Although nomograms have been widely adopted as visual predictive tools in oncology and other fields, their application in CPSP prediction remains relatively unexplored. Recently, we have preliminarily identified several risk factors for CPSP after VATS in a smaller cohort (n=280) using conventional logistic regression [14]. However, that study did not employ machine learning-based variable selection nor provide an individualized predictive model.

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically collect perioperative variables from VATS patients using a retrospective case-control design. By employing LASSO regression for variable selection, we developed and internally validated a nomogram that incorporates preoperative, intraoperative, and early postoperative pain indicators to predict CPSP risk. This tool is intended to support individualized risk assessment and guide the implementation of early multimodal analgesia and nerve-protective strategies in clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Design

In this retrospective study, potential risk factors were initially screened using chi-square tests, independent samples t-tests, and Mann-Whitney U test. The identified variables were then subjected to least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis. The intersection of risk factors identified through both screening methods was subsequently analyzed using multivariate binary logistic regression to determine independent risk factors. Finally, a nomogram prediction model was constructed and visualized using R software.

Study design and setting

This study employed a retrospective case-control design. Patients who underwent VATS between January 2022 and June 2024 constituted the source cohort. Based on the presence or absence of CPSP at the 3‑month follow‑up, they were classified into two groups: the case group (CPSP group) and the control group (non‑CPSP group). Perioperative data for these predefined groups were then collected and analyzed retrospectively. The study protocol was approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The early postoperative pain scores (POD 1–6) were routinely assessed as part of standard clinical nursing care during hospitalization; these records were subsequently extracted for retrospective analysis in this study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

① Age 18–75 years;② Body mass index (BMI) 18.5–30.0 kg/m²;③ American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III;④ Scheduled for elective uniportal or multiportal video-assisted thoracoscopic pulmonary surgery;⑤ Complete perioperative records and 3-month follow-up data.

Exclusion criteria

① Preoperative chronic pain in the chest or shoulder region (duration ≥ 3 months), adhesive capsulitis, or thoracic deformities affecting shoulder‑movement assessment;② History of psychiatric disorders (e.g., depression, anxiety disorders requiring pharmacotherapy) or cognitive impairment;③ Inability to cooperate with pain scoring or follow-up;④ Long-term (≥ 6 months preoperatively) regular use of medications affecting pain perception (opioids, antidepressants, anticonvulsants);⑤ Severe neuropathy, autoimmune diseases, or end-stage organ failure;⑥ Conversion to thoracotomy or concurrent major surgery involving other anatomical sites;⑦ Missing essential clinical data.

Anesthetic program

Standard monitoring was established. Anesthesia was induced intravenously with etomidate (0.3 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.5 µg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.2 mg/kg). A double-lumen endotracheal tube was placed under video laryngoscopy, and its position was confirmed by fiberoptic bronchoscopy. Initial two-lung ventilation parameters were set as follows: tidal volume (VT) 8 ml/kg, respiratory rate (RR) 11–13 breaths/min, PEEP 5 cmH₂O, and FiO₂ 100%.

Anesthesia was maintained with continuous infusions of propofol (3–5 mg·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹) and remifentanil (0.2–0.4 µg·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹), Intermittent boluses of cisatracurium were administered to maintain a bispectral index (BIS) value between 40 and 60. Hemodynamic fluctuations were managed pharmacologically according to a predefined protocol.

Following repositioning to lateral decubitus, tube position was reconfirmed. One-lung ventilation (OLV) commenced upon pleural opening with the following settings: VT 6 ml/kg, RR 14–18 breaths/min, adjusted to maintain PETCO₂ at 35–45 mmHg.

At skin closure, anesthetic infusions were stopped. In the PACU, neuromuscular blockade was reversed, and the trachea was extubated upon meeting standard criteria before transfer to the ward.

To ensure data integrity, two independent researchers blinded to the CPSP outcome collected variables using standardized electronic case report forms. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third senior investigator after reviewing source documentation.

Variables collected

Baseline and preoperative variables

Demographic information, medical history, and preoperative status were recorded. Preoperative chronic pain (duration ≥ 3 months) was identified using a structured interview based on ICD‑11 criteria (see Supplementary Material 1). Preoperative anxiety was assessed using a single‑item screening question (“Do you often feel nervous or anxious?”), recorded dichotomously (Yes/No). This pragmatic screening approach, utilized in prior surgical studies, was employed due to the retrospective design and the absence of routinely collected standardized questionnaire data. Its limitations relative to validated psychometric tools are acknowledged in the discussion.

Intraoperative and surgical variables

Surgical approach (axillary vs. posterolateral), type of procedure (lobectomy, segmentectomy, wedge resection), operative and anesthesia duration, use of a specific intercostal nerve-sparing suture technique, preservation of the latissimus dorsi muscle, and number of chest tubes were documented. The intercostal suture technique consistently used in this cohort involved placing deep, interrupted, figure-of-eight sutures around the superior border of the lower rib of the intercostal space (a circumcostal/paraneural technique), incorporating the intercostal muscle and pleura while meticulously avoiding direct entrapment of the neurovascular bundle [15]. This technique was employed to minimize post-thoracotomy neuralgia. We acknowledge the existence of alternative closure methods (e.g., transcostal sutures, intercostal muscle flap interposition) which were not utilized in this study. Scar length was measured on postoperative day 3 (POD 3) using a graduated flexible ruler with the patient in a relaxed supine position. The measurement followed the contour of the healed incision from the cephalad to the caudad end, excluding any visible suture material. To assess inter‑rater reliability, a random subset of 50 patients was measured independently by two research nurses, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated.

Postoperative pain assessments

From POD 1 to POD 6, trained pain nurses assessed pain intensity daily (8:00–10:00 AM) using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS; 0–10) under three conditions:

Resting pain: assessed while lying supine quietly.

Resting pain: assessed while lying supine quietly.

Cough provoked pain: maximum pain during a voluntary deep cough.

Cough provoked pain: maximum pain during a voluntary deep cough.

Shoulder abduction pain: maximum pain during active abduction of the ipsilateral arm to 90° in the coronal plane.

Shoulder abduction pain: maximum pain during active abduction of the ipsilateral arm to 90° in the coronal plane.

For analysis in the prediction model, a composite ‘postoperative shoulder abduction pain’ score was calculated as the mean of the daily VAS scores from POD 1 to POD 6. Pain trajectories based on these daily scores were derived using group‑based trajectory modeling (GBTM). The optimal number of trajectory groups was determined using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and patients were assigned to the group for which they had the highest posterior probability.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was Chronic Postsurgical Pain (CPSP), defined according to ICD-11 as pain that develops or increases in intensity after surgery, localized to the surgical site (incision, chest wall, or intercostal region), and persisting for ≥ 3 months. CPSP was assessed at the 3-month follow-up via outpatient visit or structured telephone interview, with pain intensity dichotomized (VAS ≥ 4 vs. <4) for analysis.

Other perioperative variables

Postoperative complications were graded using the Clavien-Dindo classification; grades IIIa and above were considered major. Duration of chest tube drainage was recorded in days.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed, or as median (interquartile range) otherwise. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using independent‑samples t‑tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. The dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%) and a validation set (30%) using the createDataPartition function from the caret package (version 6.0‑94) in R, with 1,000 bootstrap resampling iterations to ensure robustness. Variables showing significant associations in univariate analysis (P < 0.05) were further screened using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression via the glmnet package to mitigate multicollinearity and select optimal predictors. Selected variables were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP). Based on the final multivariate model, a nomogram was constructed using the rms package to visualize individual risk prediction. Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) calculated with the pROC package. Calibration was examined via calibration plots and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‑of‑fit test (using the ResourceSelection package). Decision curve analysis (via the rmda package) was applied to quantify the net benefit of the nomogram across a range of threshold probabilities. For the continuous independent predictors retained in the final model, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed on the training set to determine their optimal diagnostic cut-off values for predicting CPSP. The cut-off corresponding to the maximum Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1) was selected as optimal. All R packages were installed from the Comprehensive R Archive Network (CRAN; https://cran.r-project.org/). A two‑sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics and group allocation

A total of 500 patients who underwent minimally invasive thoracic surgery this study included. Based on the presence of chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) at the 3-month follow-up, they were categorized into two groups: the CPSP group (n = 92) and the non-CPSP group (n = 408). All enrolled patients completed the follow-up and were incorporated into the final analysis, with no loss to follow-up or missing data.

LASSO regression for variable selection

To mitigate potential multicollinearity and select the most relevant predictors for CPSP, LASSO regression analysis was conducted. Through 7-fold cross-validation, the optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined to be 0.023. This analysis identified six non-zero coefficient variables: preoperative anxiety, preoperative pain, intercostal suture use, scar length, postoperative shoulder abduction pain, and pain trajectory pattern. The LASSO coefficient path and cross-validation curve are shown in Fig. 2.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of factors influencing CPSP

The development of CPSP was defined as the dependent variable. The six variables selected by LASSO regression were included in a multivariate logistic regression model. The analysis identified postoperative shoulder abduction pain (OR = 2.012, 95% CI: 1.601–2.528, P < 0.001), scar length (OR = 1.285, 95% CI: 1.101–1.500, P = 0.001), and preoperative anxiety (OR = 3.245, 95% CI: 1.502–7.012, P = 0.003) as independent risk factors for CPSP. Intercostal suture use (OR = 0.221, 95% CI: 0.088–0.557, P = 0.001) was identified as an independent protective factor. Preoperative pain and early postoperative pain trajectory pattern were not retained in the final model (P > 0.05). Detailed results are presented in Table 2.

Sensitivity analysis of predictive factors

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of the four independent factors. Postoperative shoulder abduction pain demonstrated the highest predictive value (AUC = 0.846), with an optimal cut-off value of > 4.0 points (sensitivity = 0.815, specificity = 0.802). Scar length also showed good predictive ability (AUC = 0.781, cut-off > 14.0 cm). Preoperative anxiety had moderate predictive value (AUC = 0.705). The intercostal suture (protective factor) had an AUC of 0.662. Detailed performance metrics are presented in Table 3.

Model validation

Bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) confirmed the stability of the model. The 95% CI for AUCs did not include 0.5. Cross-validation accuracy was 84.8%, consistent with the original model.

Construction of a nomogram for CPSP risk prediction

Based on the multivariate logistic regression results, a nomogram was constructed incorporating the four independent predictors (Fig. 3). The nomogram allows for visual calculation of individual risk scores for each factor and estimation of the total probability of developing CPSP at 3 months postoperatively.

Validation of the nomogram

The nomogram was internally validated using the same dataset split into training (70%, n = 350) and validation (30%, n = 150) sets. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed excellent discrimination in both sets: the area under the curve (AUC) was 0.852 (95% CI: 0.805–0.899) in the training set and 0.837 (95% CI: 0.758–0.916) in the validation set (Fig. 4A). Calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities (Hosmer–Lemeshow P > 0.05 in both sets; Fig. 4B). Decision curve analysis (DCA) indicated that across a clinically relevant threshold probability range of 0.1–0.7, using the nomogram for decision-making yielded a higher net benefit than the strategies of treating all patients or treating none (Fig. 4C).

Discussion

In summary, this retrospective case‑control study systematically identified independent predictors of CPSP following VATS and developed a clinically actionable nomogram for individualized risk assessment. Our multivariate analysis confirmed postoperative shoulder abduction pain, longer scar length, and preoperative anxiety as significant independent risk factors, while intercostal suture use emerged as a strong protective factor. The constructed nomogram incorporating these variables demonstrated robust discrimination (AUC > 0.83) and good calibration, offering a practical tool for early risk stratification in this high-risk surgical population.

Postoperative shoulder abduction pain as a key dynamic predictor

Consistent with evidence that acute pain intensity is a major determinant of pain chronicity, our study identified early postoperative shoulder abduction pain as the strongest predictor of CPSP (OR = 2.01). This extends findings from previous thoracic surgery studies that highlighted the prognostic value of movement-evoked pain. In contrast to static pain at rest, pain elicited during active shoulder abduction may more accurately reflect the functional integrity of the thoracic wall and intercostal nerves, capturing both nociceptive and neuropathic components [16, 17]. Our data suggest that a VAS score > 4.0 during abduction in the first postoperative week signals a markedly elevated risk of CPSP. This threshold-based approach aligns with recent calls for incorporating functional pain assessments into routine postoperative monitoring [18].

The protective role of intercostal nerve-sparing techniques

The observed protective effect associated with the use of intercostal sutures (OR = 0.22) underscores the importance of surgical technique in mitigating long-term pain. By minimizing direct nerve compression or entrapment, such techniques likely reduce the incidence of neuropathic pain—a core component of CPSP after thoracic surgery. Recent randomized trials have similarly demonstrated that meticulous intercostal nerve protection during VATS leads to lower acute pain scores and reduced opioid consumption [19]. Our findings extend this evidence by indicating that such technical refinements also confer long-term benefits in preventing CPSP, reinforcing the principle that surgical trauma modulation is a modifiable determinant of chronic pain outcomes [20].

Scar length as an objective marker of surgical trauma

Each centimeter increase in scar length was associated with a 29% rise in CPSP risk (OR = 1.29). Scar length serves as a readily measurable proxy for the extent of tissue dissection and potential nerve injury. Longer incisions may increase traction on intercostal nerves and promote inflammatory signaling, thereby facilitating peripheral and central sensitization [21]. This objective anatomic correlate complements subjective pain metrics and can be easily documented in clinical practice, enhancing risk-assessment protocols [22, 23].

Preoperative anxiety and the psychosocial dimension

Preoperative anxiety tripled the odds of developing CPSP (OR = 3.25), consistent with established psychoneurological models of pain chronicity. Anxious states are associated with dysregulated hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activity, amplified nociceptive processing, and impaired descending inhibitory control. The high sensitivity of our single-item anxiety screen supports its utility as a rapid, scalable tool for identifying psychologically vulnerable patients who may benefit from targeted preoperative counseling or multimodal analgesia [24–26].

Clinical implications and the nomogram as a decision-support tool

Positioned within the landscape of existing CPSP prediction models, our study addresses several notable gaps. Previous models in thoracic surgery have often relied on static preoperative or intraoperative variables, with limited integration of dynamic, early postoperative pain trajectories—a known strong predictor of chronicity. Furthermore, many studies utilize conventional statistical methods without leveraging machine-learning techniques for robust feature selection from high-dimensional perioperative data. Our model directly addresses these shortcomings by: (1) systematically incorporating a key dynamic variable—the mean early postoperative shoulder abduction pain score—which demonstrated the highest predictive value; (2) employing LASSO regression for rigorous variable selection from a comprehensive set of candidates; and (3) providing the developed model as a clinically actionable, visual nomogram. The resulting nomogram integrates four readily accessible predictors into a single visual interface, allowing clinicians to estimate individual patient risk at the bedside. Its robust performance in the validation cohort (AUC = 0.84) suggests generalizability within similar surgical settings. Decision‑curve analysis confirmed that using the nomogram to guide interventions such as enhanced multimodal analgesia, early referral to pain specialists, or psychological support provides greater net benefit than blanket treatment strategies. This aligns with precision‑medicine approaches that seek to match intervention intensity to individual risk.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the single-center retrospective design may limit generalizability; external validation in multicenter, prospective cohorts is needed. Second, while we included major perioperative variables, unmeasured confounders such as genetic predisposition, detailed psychosocial profiles, or specific intraoperative anesthetic doses could influence CPSP risk. Third, the sample size, though larger than many previous studies, may still be insufficient to detect weaker interaction effects among predictors. Future research should aim to incorporate advanced biomarkers (e.g., genetic, inflammatory) and real-time electronic health record data to refine predictive accuracy. Additionally, interventional studies evaluating the impact of nomogram-guided care pathways on CPSP incidence are necessary to demonstrate clinical utility.Fourth, the scar length was measured on postoperative day 3, a time point potentially affected by edema, and we did not formally assess inter-rater reliability (e.g., via ICC) for this measurement in the full cohort. While we described a standardized measurement protocol, the absence of formal reliability testing is a limitation. The observed association should therefore be interpreted as pertaining to ‘early postoperative incision length’ rather than definitive mature scar length.

Conclusion

This study identifies postoperative shoulder abduction pain, scar length, and preoperative anxiety as independent risk factors for CPSP after VATS, while intercostal suture use serves as a protective factor. The resulting nomogram provides a validated, clinically feasible tool for early risk stratification. By integrating this model into perioperative workflows, clinicians can better identify high-risk patients, optimize multimodal analgesic and nerve-sparing strategies, and ultimately improve long-term functional outcomes after thoracic surgery.
