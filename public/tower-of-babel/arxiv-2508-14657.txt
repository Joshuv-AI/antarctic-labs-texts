Phase space transport, quasilinear diffusion and locality in phase velocity.
Didier Bénisti

Abstract
In this paper, we address the motion of charged particles subjected to a discrete spectrum of electrostatic waves. We focus on situations when transport dominates, leading to significant variations in particle velocity. Nonetheless, these velocity changes remain finite due to the presence of KAM tori bounding phase space. We analyze the conditions under which transport can be modeled as a diffusion process and evaluate the relevance of the so-called quasilinear value of the diffusion coefficient. We distinguish between traditional quasilinear diffusion, when wave-particle interaction is perturbative, and the so-called chaotic regime of diffusion, when the particle motion looks erratic. In the perturbative regime, we demonstrate both numerically and theoretically that diffusion occurs only when wave-particle interaction is local in phase velocity; that is, when wave contributions from phase velocities far from the particles instantaneous velocities are negligible. Conversely, numerical results indicate that chaotic diffusion can occur even when wave-particle interaction is not local. Furthermore, in regimes when quasilinear diffusion is applicable, we introduce a simple analytical expression for the time evolution of the velocity distribution function, that accounts for phase space boundaries.



I
Introduction

Whether transport can be modeled by a Fokker-Planck equation remains one of the most fundamental issues in both basic and applied physics. From a foundational perspective, this amounts to addressing determinism. Indeed, the Fokker-Planck equation is mathematically equivalent to a stochastic Langevin equation. Hence if, as shown in Refs.
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
, some deterministic systems may be modeled by a Fokker-Planck equation for an arbitrary long time and with an arbitrary accuracy, there is no way for an observer to know whether such systems are indeed deterministic or stochastic. In other words, it would be utterly impossible to ascertain whether all natural systems are fully deterministic or if some irreducible degree of randomness exists.

Furthermore, deriving the particle distribution resulting from chaotic transport is essential in countless applications. This paper focuses on transport driven by a fixed spectrum of electrostatic waves, a scenario commonly encountered in plasma physics. The best known example is certainly the saturation regime of the warm beam-plasma instability (see for example Refs.
5
and references therein). To cite a few other examples, in space plasmas, interactions with broad wave spectra have been invoked to explain energetic ion observations in the magnetosphere
[
6
]
and electron losses in radiation belts
[
7
]
. Moreover, the present work is motivated by laser-plasma interaction when electrostatic waves result from stimulated Raman scattering or two-plasmon decay
[
8
]
. Such waves may generate energetic electrons
[
9
]
which are detrimental to inertial confinement fusion
[
10
]
, or beneficial for producing X-ray sources
[
11
]
with enhanced energies. For the application to laser-plasma interaction (and for many other ones), the wave spectrum may certainly not be assumed stationary. However, as shown in Section
III
, considering a fixed spectrum is enough to derive transport properties, provided that the modes growth rates vary slowly enough and that the maximum growth rate is much less than the total frequency extent of the spectrum.

In plasma physics, transport induced by electrostatic waves is most often modeled as a quasilinear diffusion
[
12
]
. Although quasilinear theory has been the subject of considerable debate
[
13
]
, several papers provided a detailed discussion of its relevance for a nearly stationary spectrum (see for example Refs.
[
1
,
5
,
14
]
). In particular, Ref.
1
shows that two types of diffusion may exist. In the first one, which is quasilinear and closer to the situation considered in the initial theory
[
12
]
, the particle motion may be described by making use of a first order perturbation analysis. It may be followed by a second, so-called “chaotic” diffusion regime, when the particle motion looks erratic. In this second regime, the diffusion coefficient may be different from the quasilinear one, although usually of the same order. Moreover, as shown in Refs.
[
1
,
3
]
, for large enough wave amplitudes, the initial quasilinear regime persists until the onset of the second, chaotic regime. In this situation, quasilinear diffusion is valid until the particules reach the phase space boundaries. Now, all the aforementioned results were derived for the dynamics defined by the following Hamiltonian, assumed to be paradigmatic,

H_{p}=\frac{v^{2}}{2}+A\sum_{n=1}^{N}\cos(x-nt+\varphi_{n}),

(1)

where the
\varphi_{n}
s were random initial phases. The key point of the derivation was the proof of locality in phase velocity for the wave-particle interaction
[
15
]
. Here, locality means that, at all times, transport is essentially due to those waves whose phase velocity differ by no more than
\Delta v_{R}\approx 6A^{2/3}
from the instantaneous particle speed. Note that the quasilinear diffusion coefficient, as derived in Ref.
12
for a continuous spectrum, accounts only for waves with phase velocity exactly matching the particle speed, thus representing an infinitely local interaction. This would not make sense for a discrete spectrum and, for such a wave spectrum, the expression of the quasilinear diffusion coefficient was derived in Ref.
18
and recalled in Section
III
, Eq. (
39
).

Clearly, the conclusions drawn from the Hamiltonian
H_{p}
raise several important questions, mostly (but not only) regarding their universality :

1.

Does there exist a broad class of Hamiltonian dynamics whose transport properties are exactly the same as those derived from
H_{p}
?

2.

Is locality a universal property in wave-particle interaction ?

3.

Does the first perturbative regime of quasilinear diffusion exist for all wave spectra ?

4.

Is there a link between locality and the first quasilinear diffusion regime ?

5.

Can chaotic diffusion exist without locality ?

6.

Talking about diffusion is somewhat abusive for a finite discrete wave spectrum because particles only have access to a limited region of phase space, which raises the following question : can one derive an analytical expression for the time evolution of the velocity distribution function that accounts for phase space boundaries ?

To the best of the author’s knowledge, no previous article specifically addressed the first fourth and sixth issues. In Ref.
5
, Vlasov simulations showed the relevance of quasilinear theory to model the warm beam-plasma instability in the strongly nonlinear regime, while non-quasilinear evolution was observed for intermediate nonlinear regimes, thus supporting some of the results derived from the Hamiltonian
H_{p}
. Moreover, on a more theoretical perspective, several papers, e.g. Ref.
14
, addressed the relevance of the initial quasilinear diffusion regime for more general wave spectra than that considered in Hamiltonian
H_{p}
. However, the authors of these papers implicitly made hypotheses that led to the validity of the initial quasilinear regime. In this article, we aim to address the 6 aforementioned issues for the general class of wave spectra leading to the dynamics defined by the following Hamiltonian,

H=\frac{v^{2}}{2}+A\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\cos(k_{n}x-\omega_{n}t+\varphi_{n}),

(2)

where the
\varphi_{n}
s remain random initial phases, while the
a_{n}
s,
k_{n}
s and
\omega_{n}
s may be chosen arbitrarily. As for the pre-factor,
A
, it is introduced as a normalization constant to allow direct comparison with the results derived from Hamiltonian
H_{p}
.
For the general Hamiltonian
H
, we theoretically establish the conditions on the relative variations of the wave amplitudes, wave numbers, and wave frequencies required for locality to hold. We further show that the presence of an initial quasilinear diffusion regime is closely linked to the validity of locality. In addition, we derive the criteria under which the quasilinear diffusion coefficient for
H
coincides with that for
H_{p}
, namely
D_{\rm{QL}}=\pi A^{2}/2
, and under which the overlap parameter,
s=2\sqrt{A}\left(\sqrt{a_{n}}+\sqrt{a_{n+1}}\right)/\delta v_{\phi_{n}}
,
\delta v_{\phi_{n}}=|d_{n}(\omega_{n}/k_{n})|,

also matches that of
H_{p}
, i.e.,
s=4\sqrt{A}
. When these conditions are met, the transport properties for Hamiltonian
H
should be very similar to those of Hamiltonian
H_{p}
. In particular, they should be uniform in phase space [at least in the region of phase space corresponding to
v\lesssim\max(\omega_{n}/k_{n})
and
v\gtrsim\min(\omega_{n}/k_{n})
]. Consequently, if a Fokker-Planck equation is valid, it reduces to a simple diffusion equation,
\partial_{t}f=D\partial^{2}_{v}f
, where
D
is independent of
v
.

When the aforementioned conditions are met, the transport properties are very easily derived. In particular, when there is quasilinear diffusion,
\langle\Delta v^{2}(t)\rangle=2D_{\rm{QL}}t=\pi A^{2}t
, where
\Delta v=v(t)-v(0)
and where the brackets stand for the averaging over the initial phases,
\varphi_{n}
. Then, test particle simulations provide a direct means for validating our theoretical predictions as regards the validity of locality and its connection with the occurence of the first quasilinear regime of diffusion. To the best of the author’s knowledge, the latter issue has been completely overlooked in previous studies. Moreover, numerical simulations allow us to test whether a chaotic diffusion regime eventually settles, regardless of the validity of locality and, when locality fails, the impact of the number of modes on the diffusion coefficient.

Now, diffusion cannot last for all times because particles eventually hit the boundaries of phase space, as illustrated in Fig.
3
. These boundaries are the so-called KAM tori
[
19
]
which exist for
v\gtrsim\max(\omega_{n}/k_{n})
and
v\lesssim\min(\omega_{n}/k_{n})
and which cannot be crossed by any orbit. Consequently, one cannot simply derive the velocity distribution function by solving the diffusion equation,
\partial_{t}f=D\partial^{2}_{v}f
. In this paper, we provide a heuristic expression for the time variation of the distribution function, which accounts for phase space boundaries, and whose accuracy is tested against results from test particle simulations.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
clearly defines what we mean by a “bounded chaotic transport”, which we investigate in this article. Section
III
presents our theoretical analysis providing the conditions for the validity of locality and the occurence of the perturbative regime of quasilinear diffusion. It also briefly discusses the occurence of the chaotic regime of diffusion and provides conditions under which the quasilinear diffusion coefficient is uniform in phase space, thereby allowing the Fokker-Planck equation to simplify into a diffusion equation. Section
IV
checks our theoretical predictions by making use of test particle simulations, with a particular emphasis on the link between locality and the occurence of the first quasilinear regime of diffusion. In the chaotic regime, the dependence of the diffusion coefficient on the number,
N
, of modes is further investigated. Section
V
provides an effective expression for the velocity distribution function resulting from a diffusion-like process limited in time by the phase space boundaries. Section
VI
summarizes and concludes our work.

II
Bounded chaotic transport

In this section, we qualitatively discuss the physical effect we aim to quantify : a bounded chaotic transport in velocity. The discussion is based on Poincaré sections for the dynamics ruled by the Hamiltonian
H_{p}
, Eq. (
1
). This amounts to plotting the phase space coordinates
\left[x\left(t_{n}\right),v\left(t_{n}\right)\right]
at discrete times
t_{n}=2\pi n
. The positions
x(t_{n})
are plotted modulo
2\pi
so that
0\leq x(t_{n})\leq 2\pi
.

When
N=1
, the dynamical system is equivalent to a pendulum. The corresponding phase portrait, shown in Fig.
1
, contains two types of orbits : trapped and untrapped ones, separated by a curve called the separatrix. This separatrix is centered at
v=v_{\phi_{1}}
and its width is
4\sqrt{Aa_{1}}
.

When
N>1
, if the phase portrait were a mere superposition of
N
pendulum-like phase portraits, it would consist of
N
sets of trapped and untrapped orbits, separated by
N
separatrices, centered about
v=v_{\phi_{n}}
and of width
4\sqrt{Aa_{n}}
. Clearly, this picture has no chance to be correct when the separatrices overlap, that is, when the Chirikov overlap parameter,
s
, is larger than unity, where

s=2\sqrt{A}\frac{\sqrt{a_{n}}+\sqrt{a_{n+1}}}{\delta v_{\phi_{n}}},

(3)

with
\delta v_{\phi_{n}}=|d_{n}(\omega_{n}/k_{n})|
. For the Hamiltonian
H_{p}
,
s=4\sqrt{A}
.

The qualitative change in the phase portrait as
s
increases is illustrated in Figs.
2
and
3
, corresponding to
s=0.53
and
s=4
, respectively. These figures plot 7 different Poincaré sections of the Hamiltonian
H_{p}
with
N=21
, each corresponding to one of the following seven initial conditions,

\displaystyle 1:

\displaystyle(x_{1}=\pi-\varphi_{11};v_{1}=11+\sqrt{A}/2),

(4)

\displaystyle 2:

\displaystyle(x_{2}=\pi-\varphi_{11};v_{2}=11+2.05\sqrt{A}),

(5)

\displaystyle 3:

\displaystyle(x_{3}=\varphi_{12};v_{3}=12+\sqrt{A}/2),

(6)

\displaystyle 4:

\displaystyle(x_{4}=-\varphi_{11};v_{4}=11),

(7)

\displaystyle 5:

\displaystyle(x_{5}=\varphi_{12};v_{5}=12),

(8)

\displaystyle 6:

\displaystyle(x_{6}=\pi\varphi_{11};v_{6}=11.43),

(9)

\displaystyle 7:

\displaystyle(x_{7}=\varphi_{11};v_{7}=11.62).

(10)

The first and third initiation conditions lie, respectively, within the separatrices associated with
n=11
and
n=12
, so that the corresponding orbits are expected to be the counterparts of trapped orbits for the pendulum. The second initial condition is located above the separatrix for
n=11
, so that the corresponding orbit is expected to be the counterpart of an untrapped orbit for the pendulum. The fourth and fifth initial conditions are situated, respectively, at the X-points of the separatrices corresponding to
n=11
and
n=12
. As for the sixth and seventh initial conditions, depending on the value of
A
, they may lie either within or above the separatrix corresponding to
n=11
.

In Fig.
2
, when
s=0.53
, there are indeed pendulum-like orbits. Trapped ones with initial conditions 1, 3, and an untrapped one with initial condition 7. These are so-called KAM tori
[
19
]
, i.e., orbits slightly deformed compared to those of the pendulum. In particular, the orbit from initial condition 7 spans the whole
[0,2\pi]
interval and cannot be crossed by any other orbit because of causality. As a result, any orbit starting in a region bounded by two such KAM tori remains permanently confined in this region, which strongly reduces transport. Other initial conditions lead to more complicated orbits. The initial conditions 2 and 6 do not lead to pendulum-like orbits but to chains of islands. As for the initial conditions 1 and 5, they do not lead to one-dimensional orbits but to so-called stochastic layers, located about deformed pendulum-like separatrices. The existence of stochastic layers is the illustration of some, very limited, chaotic transport. Indeed, when
s=0.53
, the change in velocity remains very small for all the considered initial conditions.

The situation changes dramatically when
s=4
, as illustrated in Fig.
3
. Here, the phase portrait looks like one big stochastic layer. Indeed, for all the considered initial conditions, the orbits seem to densely fill a region of phase space located between
v\approx\min(v_{\phi_{n}})=1
and
v\approx\max(v_{\phi_{n}})=21
. Hence, there is transport since velocities change significantly for all the considered initial conditions. However, this transport is limited because an orbit can only access a region of phase space bounded by KAM tori similar to that plotted in Fig.
2
for the initiation condition 7. This is what we call a bounded chaotic transport and, in the remainder of this paper, we only consider situations similar to that illustrated in Fig.
3
.

III
Theoretical analysis

III.1
Validity of locality

In this Paragraph, we discuss the relevance of locality for the Hamiltonian
H
defined by Eq. (
2
). To do so, we use the same procedure as in Ref.
15
, i.e., we make use of a perturbation analysis about a given velocity,
v_{0}
. This lets us conclude whether there exists a range in velocity,
\Delta v_{R}
, such that the waves with phase velocity
v_{\phi}
satisfying
|v_{0}-v_{\phi}|>\Delta v_{R}
have, on the average, a negligible impact on transport. Before proceeding to the analysis, we renormalize velocities to the, still unknown, range in velocity,
\Delta v_{R}
. Hence, we define the following new variables,

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v/\Delta v_{R},

(11)

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x,

(12)

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta v_{R}t.

(13)

In these variables the dynamics derives from the Hamiltonian,

H_{1}=\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{2}+\frac{A}{\Delta v_{R}^{2}}\sum_{n}a_{n}\cos(k_{n}x_{1}-\omega_{n}\tau/\Delta v_{R}+\varphi_{n}).

(14)

Now, to prove locality, we need to prove that there exists a canonical change of variables
(x_{1},v_{1})\to(x^{\prime},v^{\prime})
, defined from a generative function,
\Phi(x_{1},v^{\prime},t)\equiv x_{1}v^{\prime}+\sum_{l}\varepsilon^{l}\Phi_{l}
(the small parameter of the expansion,
\varepsilon
, being still to define) such that, in the new variables,

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}+\partial_{v^{\prime}}\Phi

(15)

\displaystyle v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{1}-\partial_{x_{1}}\Phi,

(16)

the new Hamiltonian

H^{\prime}=H_{1}+\partial_{\tau}\Phi,

(17)

reads

H^{\prime}=H^{\prime}_{0}(v^{\prime})+\sum_{|\varpi_{n}/\kappa_{n}-v_{0}|\leq\Delta v_{R}}\mathcal{A}_{n}\cos\left(\kappa_{n}x^{\prime}-\varpi_{n}t+\psi_{n}\right)+\varepsilon^{l+1}R_{l+1},

(18)

where the
\mathcal{A}_{n}
s,
\kappa_{n}
s,
\varpi_{n}
s and
\psi_{n}
s are amplitudes, wave numbers, frequencies and phases which are derived from the perturbative expansion, and where there exists an upper bound for the remainder,
R_{l}
, independent of the number of modes,
N
. For the Hamiltonian
H_{p}
, Eq. (
1
), considered in Ref.
15
, the terms of the perturbative expansion could be bounded by those of a Gevrey series, which allowed to provide an exponentially small estimate for
\varepsilon^{l+1}R_{l+1}
, with
\varepsilon=A/\Delta v_{R}^{3/2}
.

Using Eqs. (
15
)-(
17
), one finds, at first order,

H^{\prime}=\frac{v^{{}^{\prime}2}}{2}+v^{\prime}\frac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{A}{\Delta v_{R}^{2}}\sum_{n}a_{n}\cos(k_{n}x_{1}-\omega_{n}\tau/\Delta v_{R}+\varphi_{n})+\frac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{2},

(19)

and we want
\Phi_{1}
to be solution of the following equation,

v^{\prime}\frac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial t}=-\frac{A}{\Delta v_{R}^{2}}\sum_{|\omega_{n}/k_{n}-v_{0}|>\Delta v_{R}}a_{n}\cos(k_{n}x_{1}-\omega_{n}\tau/\Delta v_{R}+\varphi_{n}).

(20)

Then, if the remainder
(1/2)\left(\partial_{x_{1}}\Phi_{1}\right)^{2}
is negligible, the Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
essentially accounts for the action of the waves whose phase velocity,
v_{\phi_{n}}=\omega_{n}/k_{n}
, is such that
|v_{\phi_{n}}-v_{0}|\leq\Delta v_{R}
. The solution of Eq. (
20
) is easily found to be,

\Phi_{1}=-\frac{A}{\Delta v_{R}^{2}}\sum_{|\omega_{n}/k_{n}-v_{0}|>\Delta v_{R}}\frac{a_{n}\sin(k_{n}x_{1}-\omega_{n}\tau/\Delta v_{R}+\varphi_{n})}{k_{n}v^{\prime}-\omega_{n}/\Delta v_{R}}.

(21)

Then, at first order, the remainder is

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{2}=\frac{A^{2}}{4\Delta v_{R}^{4}}\sum_{n_{1}}\sum_{n_{2}}\frac{a_{n_{1}}a_{n_{2}}\left[C_{+}+C_{-}\right]}{\Delta_{n_{1}}\Delta_{n_{2}}},

(22)

where

\displaystyle C_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos[(k_{n_{1}}+k_{n_{2}})x_{1}-(\omega_{n_{1}}+\omega_{n_{2}})\tau/\Delta v_{R}+\varphi_{n_{1}}+\varphi_{n_{2}}]

\displaystyle C_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos[(k_{n_{1}}-k_{n_{2}})x_{1}-(\omega_{n_{1}}-\omega_{n_{2}})\tau/\Delta v_{R}+\varphi_{n_{1}}-\varphi_{n_{2}}],

\displaystyle\Delta_{n_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(v^{\prime}-v_{\phi_{n_{i}}}/\Delta v_{R}).

Note that
\Delta_{n_{i}}>1
when
v^{\prime}=v_{0}/\Delta v_{R}
. Since we are only interested in statistical properties then, just like in Ref.
15
, we estimate the remainder by its mean value with regards to the phases
\varphi_{n}
. When
v^{\prime}\approx v_{0}/\Delta v_{R}
,

\left\langle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{2}\right\rangle\approx\frac{A^{2}}{4\Delta v_{R}^{2}}\sum_{n}\frac{a_{n}^{2}}{(v_{0}-v_{\phi_{n}})^{2}}.

(23)

Let us now choose a velocity scale,
\delta v
, and let us introduce,

A_{l}^{2}=\frac{A^{2}}{\delta v}\sum_{i_{l}}a_{i_{l}}^{2},

(24)

where the sum is over the terms such that

\Delta v_{R}+\frac{(2l-1)\delta v}{2}\leq|v_{\phi_{i_{l}}}-v_{0}|\leq\Delta v_{R}+\frac{(2l+1)\delta v}{2}.

(25)

When
\delta v
is small enough,

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\Delta v_{R}^{3}}\frac{\Delta v_{R}}{\delta v}\sum_{l}\frac{A_{l}^{2}}{(\Delta v_{R}/\delta v+l)^{2}}

(26)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\Delta v_{R}^{3}}\frac{\Delta v_{R}}{\delta v}\int_{x_{\min}}^{x_{\max}}\frac{A^{2}(x)}{(\Delta v_{R}/\delta v+x)^{2}}dx,

where
x_{\min}=\min(l)
and
x_{\max}=\max(l)
for each
l
fulfilling Eq. (
25
). Clearly, the property of locality is satisfied if one can find an upper bound for the remainder which is independent of the extent, in phase velocity, of the wave spectrum. This is true if the integral in the right hand side of Eq. (
26
) converges when
x_{\max}\to\infty
. This happens only when
A^{2}(x)/x
goes to zero when
x
goes to infinity, i.e.,
A^{2}(v_{\phi})/v_{\phi}
goes to zero when
v_{\phi}
goes to infinity. Hence, the property of locality is not always true, depending on the wave spectrum.

Now, if
A^{2}(v_{\phi})/v_{\phi}
goes to zero quickly enough and if
A^{2}(v_{\phi})
remains nearly constant for
v_{\phi}\approx v_{0}
, so that
A^{2}(v_{\phi})\approx\overline{A_{v}^{2}}
, where
\overline{A^{2}_{v}}
is the mean value of
A^{2}(v_{\phi})
over a velocity scale of the order of
\Delta v_{R}
, Eq. (
26
) reads,

\left\langle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{2}\right\rangle\approx\frac{\overline{A^{2}_{v}}}{4\Delta v_{R}^{3}}.

(27)

Then, for the remainder to be negligible, one needs to choose,

\Delta v_{R}\sim\left(\overline{A^{2}_{v}}\right)^{1/3},

(28)

which lets naturally choose
\varepsilon=\sqrt{\overline{A^{2}_{v}}}/\Delta v_{R}^{3/2}
for the small parameter of the perturbation analysis. This is a plain generalization of the small parameter introduced in Ref.
15
for the Hamiltonian
H_{p}
.

III.2
Quasilinear diffusion

III.2.1
The quasilinear diffusion coefficient for a discrete wave spectrum

As is well known
[
1
]
, when the velocity distribution function obeys a diffusion equation,
\partial_{t}f=D\partial_{v^{2}}f
,
D
being independent of
v
,

\langle\Delta v^{2}(t)\rangle=2Dt,

(29)

where
\Delta v(t)=v(t)-v(0)
. Moreover, there is quasilinear diffusion, in the spirit of the quasilinear theory as introduced in Ref.
12
, if Eq. (
29
) is fulfilled in the regime when
x(t)\approx x(0)+v(0)t
. For the sake of simplicity (and without loss of generality), we assume that all particles have the same initial velocity,
v(0)=v_{0}
. Then, it is easily shown that,

\langle\Delta v^{2}\rangle\approx\frac{A^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{n}k_{n}^{2}a_{n}^{2}\cos[(k_{n}v_{0}-\omega_{n})(t_{1}-t_{2})]dt_{1}dt_{2}.

(30)

Let us now choose a frequency scale,
\delta\omega
, and let us introduce,

B_{l}^{2}=\frac{A^{2}}{\delta\omega}\sum_{i_{l}}k_{i_{l}}^{2}a_{i_{l}}^{2},

(31)

where the sum is over the terms such that,

\frac{(2l-1)\delta\omega}{2}\leq|k_{l_{i}}v_{0}-\omega_{l_{i}}|\leq\frac{(2l+1)\delta\omega}{2}.

(32)

Then, for
\delta\omega
small enough,

\displaystyle\langle\Delta v^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\omega}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{l}B_{l}^{2}\cos[l\delta\omega(t_{1}-t_{2})]dt_{1}dt_{2},

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\delta\omega}\int_{0}^{\delta\omega t}\int_{0}^{\delta\omega t}\sum_{l}B_{l}^{2}\cos[l(t^{\prime}_{1}-t^{\prime}_{2})]dt^{\prime}_{1}dt^{\prime}_{2}.

If, for a large enough number of terms
l
about zero,
B_{l}^{2}
remains nearly constant,
B_{l}^{2}\approx\overline{B^{2}}
, and if the contribution of the other terms is negligible, then,
\sum_{l}B_{l}^{2}\cos[l(t^{\prime}_{1}-t^{\prime}_{2})]\approx 2\pi\overline{B^{2}}\delta_{2\pi}(t^{\prime}_{1}-t^{\prime}_{2})
, where
\delta_{2\pi}(t)
is the
2\pi
-periodic Dirac distribution. In this situation,

\langle\Delta v^{2}\rangle\approx\pi\overline{B^{2}}t,

(34)

as long as
t<2\pi/\delta\omega
. By contrast, if the spectrum was very peaked about some
B_{l}
s, it is quite clear from Eq. (
33
) that the time evolution of
\langle\Delta v^{2}(t)\rangle
would not diffusive. In this case, wave-particle interaction would be dominated by trapping within a few modes, a scenario out of the scope of this paper and which is, henceforth, excluded. For the remainder of this article, the wave spectrum is is implicitly assumed to be smooth.

Eq. (
34
) defines the quasilinear diffusion coefficient as

D_{\rm{QL}}=\frac{\pi\overline{B^{2}}}{2}.

(35)

Note that, if
k_{n}
remains nearly constant over a large enough range in
n
,
k_{n}\approx\overline{k}
, then
B^{2}\approx\overline{k}\overline{A^{2}}
, so that Eq. (
28
) reads,

\Delta v_{R}\sim\left(D_{\rm{QL}}/\overline{k}\right)^{1/3},

(36)

and is reminiscent of the results previously obtained by Dupree
[
16
]
, although derived in a completely different sprit.
Now,

B^{2}\sim A^{2}\left|\frac{k_{n}^{2}a_{n}^{2}}{\delta(k_{n}v_{0}-\omega_{n})}\right|=\left|\frac{k_{n}^{2}a_{n}^{2}}{v_{0}\delta k_{n}-\delta\omega_{n}}\right|.

(37)

Since we only keep the terms
n
such that
v_{0}\approx v_{\phi_{n}}=\omega_{n}/k_{n}
,

\displaystyle B^{2}

\displaystyle{\approx}

\displaystyle{\left|\frac{k_{n}^{2}A^{2}a_{n}^{2}}{\omega_{n}\delta k_{n}/k_{n}-\delta\omega_{n}}\right|}

(38)

\displaystyle{=}

\displaystyle{\left|\frac{k_{n}A^{2}a_{n}^{2}}{-\omega_{n}\delta k_{n}/k_{n}^{2}+\delta\omega_{n}/k_{n}}\right|}

\displaystyle{=}

\displaystyle{\left|\frac{k_{n}A^{2}a_{n}^{2}}{\delta v_{\phi_{n}}}\right|.}

Hence,

D_{\rm{QL}}=\frac{\pi}{2}\left|\frac{k_{n}A^{2}a_{n}^{2}}{\delta v_{\phi_{n}}}\right|_{v_{\phi_{n}}\approx v}.

(39)

Coming back to physical units, if the total electrostatic field of the wave spectrum is
E=\sum_{n}E_{n}\cos(k_{n}x-\omega_{n}+\varphi_{n})
, then
d_{t}v=(q/m)\sum_{n}E_{n}\cos(k_{n}x-\omega_{n}+\varphi_{n})
where
q
and
m
are the charge and mass of the considered particles. From the Hamilton equations for
H
, Eq. (
2
),
d_{t}v=\sum_{n}k_{n}Aa_{n}\cos(k_{n}x-\omega_{n}+\varphi_{n})
. Hence,
Aa_{n}=(q/m)(E_{n}/k_{n})
and Eq. (
39
) reads,

D_{\rm{QL}}=\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\frac{q}{m}\right)^{2}\left|\frac{E_{n}^{2}}{k_{n}\delta v_{\phi_{n}}}\right|_{v_{\phi_{n}}\approx v},

(40)

which is the same formula as that already published in Ref.
18
.

III.2.2
Validity of quasilinear diffusion and connection with locality

The remainder of the perturbative expansion, derived at first order and given by Eq. (
23
), reads

\left\langle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{2}\right\rangle\approx\frac{A^{2}}{4\Delta v_{R}^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{(k_{n}a_{n})^{2}}{(k_{n}v_{0}-\omega_{n})^{2}}.

(41)

One can find an upper bound for this remainder independent of the number of modes,
N
, provided that the series in the right-hand side of Eq. (
41
) converges. When
\omega_{n}/k_{n}
increases with
n
, this amounts to requiring that the series
\sum_{n}\left(k_{n}a_{n}/\omega_{n}\right)^{2}

converges, while when
\omega_{n}/k_{n}
decreases with
n
, the series
\sum_{n}a_{n}^{2}

has to converge.

Moreover, a direct integration of Eq. (
30
) leads to,

\langle\Delta v^{2}\rangle=A^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{k_{n}^{2}a_{n}^{2}\left\{1-\cos\left[(k_{n}v_{0}-\omega_{n})t\right]\right\}}{(k_{n}v_{0}-\omega_{n})^{2}}.

(42)

In order to derive quasilinear diffusion in Paragraph
III.2.1
, we had to assume that the dominant contribution to
\langle\Delta v^{2}\rangle
was from the terms
n
such that
v_{\phi_{n}}\approx v_{0}
, and that these terms all had about the same amplitude. This may only be true if the series
\sum_{n}\left[k_{n}a_{n}/(k_{n}v_{0}-\omega_{n})\right]^{2}
converges. This is exactly the same condition as Eq. (
41
) for locality. Consequently, the initial quasilinear regime of diffusion is only expected when wave-particle interaction is local.

Let us now consider the situation when the modes amplitudes
a_{n}
depend on time so that
a_{n}\equiv a_{n}(t)
. Let us, moreover, assume that the modes growth rates,
\Gamma_{n}=d_{t}\ln(|a_{n}|)
, vary slowly enough for their time derivatives to be neglected when solving Eq. (
20
) on
\Phi_{1}
. Then, considering time varying amplitudes simply amounts to considering complex frequencies and Eq. (
41
) remains valid provided that the denominator in its right-hand side be changed into
(k_{n}v_{0}-\omega_{n})(k_{n}v_{0}-\omega_{n}^{*})
. Consequently, if the maximum growth rate is much less than the frequency width of the spectrum, the conclusion regarding the validity of locality remains unchanged. However, since
a_{n}
now has to be understood as a function of time, the validity of locality may also be time dependent.

Under the same hypotheses for
\Gamma_{n}
as previously, Eq. (
42
) is changed into

\langle\Delta v^{2}\rangle=\frac{A^{2}}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{k_{n}^{2}a_{n}^{2}\left\{1-2r_{n}(t)\cos(k_{n}v_{0}-\omega_{n})t+r_{n}^{2}(t)]\right\}}{(k_{n}v_{0}-\omega_{n})(k_{n}v_{0}-\omega_{n})^{*}},

(43)

where we have denoted
r_{n}(t)=a_{n}(0)/a_{n}(t)
. Regardless of the value of
r_{n}(t)
, the condition for the validity of the initial quasilinear regime of diffusion is the same as for locality. Hence, the conclusions for a time varying spectrum are the same as for a fixed one.

III.3
Chaotic diffusion

In this Paragraph, we quickly discuss the so-called chaotic regime of diffusion, although an in-depth investigation of this regime is way beyond the scope of this paper. More specifically, we assume that the position,
x(t)
, may be considered as a stochastic process with finite correlation time, which we let go to zero. Then, using the same definition as in Paragraph
III.2
, we find,

\langle\Delta v^{2}(t)\rangle\approx\frac{1}{2\delta\omega}\int_{0}^{\delta\omega t}\int_{0}^{\delta\omega t}\langle\cos\{k_{n}[x(\delta\omega t^{\prime}_{1})-x(\delta\omega t^{\prime}_{2})]\}\rangle\sum_{n}B_{n}^{2}\cos[n(t^{\prime}_{1}-t^{\prime}_{2})]dt^{\prime}_{1}dt^{\prime}_{2}.

(44)

Again if, for a large enough range in
n
about zero,
B_{n}^{2}
remains nearly constant,
B_{n}^{2}\approx\overline{B^{2}}
, and if the contribution from the other terms is negligible,
\sum_{n}B_{n}^{2}\cos[n(t^{\prime}_{1}-t^{\prime}_{2})]\approx 2\pi\overline{B^{2}}\delta_{2\pi}(t_{1}-t_{2})
. Moreover, if
\langle\cos\{k_{n}[x(\delta\omega t^{\prime}_{1})-x(\delta\omega t^{\prime}_{2})]\}\rangle\approx 0
whenever
|t_{1}-t_{2}|>2\pi/\delta\omega
, one finds
\Delta v^{2}(t)\approx 2D_{\rm{QL}}t
whatever
t>0

[
17
]
. Note that the same result holds for a time-varying spectrum provided that the
B_{n}
s do not vary significantly over a timescale of the order of the inverse of the total frequency width. Just like in Paragraph
III.2
, under this condition, we recover the same result as for a fixed spectrum. Consequently, in the remainder of this article and for the sake of simplicity, we only consider stationary spectra.

Now, if the aforementioned conditions are not fulfilled, it is not easy to conclude. In Ref.
1
, we used locality to prove chaotic diffusion. However, we did not prove that locality was compulsory for chaotic diffusion to occur. This issue will be investigated numerically in Section
IV
.

III.4
Conditions for a uniform transport

The theoretical predictions of Paragraph
III.2
, together with the occurence of chaotic diffusion, are tested numerically in a more transparent fashion when chaotic transport is uniform over a large region of phase space. Indeed, in this case, provided that a Fokker-Planck equation is valid, this equation simplifies into a diffusion equation, and
\langle\Delta v^{2}\rangle
is expected to evolve linearly in time.

We assume that transport is uniform provided that the quasilinear diffusion coefficient, defined by Eq. (
39
), and the overlap parameter defined by Eq. (
3
),
are independent of
n
. We seek to fulfill these conditions on
D_{\rm{QL}}
and
s
for the following variations of
k_{n}
,
a_{n}
and
\omega_{n}
,

\displaystyle k_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{0}n^{\alpha},

(45)

\displaystyle a_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{0}n^{\beta},

(46)

\displaystyle\omega_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{0}n^{\gamma}.

(47)

One easily finds that
D_{\rm{QL}}=\pi A^{2}/2
and
s=4\sqrt{A}
when,

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3(\gamma-1)}{2}

(48)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\gamma,

(49)

\displaystyle a_{0}\omega_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3-\gamma},

(50)

\displaystyle k_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{0}^{-3/2}.

(51)

When Eqs. (
48
)-(
51
) are fulfilled, the transport properties of the Hamiltonian
H
defined by Eq. (
2
) should be very similar to those of the Hamiltonian
H_{p}
defined by Eq. (
1
). We also want to impose a similar time evolution for the dynamics derived from these Hamiltonians. To this end, we introduce
n^{*}
such that,
v(0)\approx\omega_{n^{*}}/k_{n^{*}}
. Then, making use of the expansions

\displaystyle k_{n}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle k_{n^{*}}+(dk_{n}/dn)_{n=n^{*}}(n-n^{*})=k_{n^{*}}+\alpha k_{n^{*}}(n-n^{*})/n^{*},

(52)

\displaystyle\omega_{n}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\omega_{n^{*}}+(d\omega_{n}/dn)_{n=n^{*}}(n-n^{*})=\omega_{n^{*}}+\gamma\omega_{n^{*}}(n-n^{*})/n^{*},

(53)

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle x(0)+v(0)t\approx x(0)+\left(\omega_{n^{*}}/k_{n^{*}}\right)t,

(54)

leads to,

k_{n}x-\omega_{n}t\approx\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)\omega_{0}}{n^{*}}(n-n^{*})t.

(55)

In order for the time evolution to be as similar as possible to that of Hamiltonian
H_{p}
defined by Eq. (
1
), we choose
\omega_{0}
so that

\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)\omega_{0}}{n^{*}}=1.

(56)

Using Eqs. (
48
)-(
51
), the condition given by Eq. (
56
) reads, when
\gamma\neq 3
,

\omega_{0}=\left[v(0)\right]^{1-\gamma}\left(\frac{2}{3-\gamma}\right)^{\gamma}.

(57)

Now, from Eqs. (
48
)-(
51
),
\omega_{n}/k_{n}=(\omega_{0}/k_{0})n^{(3-\gamma)/2}
and increases with
n
provided that
\gamma<3
. In this case,
(k_{n}a_{n}/\omega_{n})^{2}=(k_{0}a_{0}/\omega_{0})^{2}n^{-1-\gamma}
so that the series
\sum_{n}(k_{n}a_{n}/\omega_{n})^{2}
converges whenever
\gamma>0
. When
\gamma>3
, one has to investigate the series
\sum_{n}a_{n}^{2}=a_{0}^{2}\sum_{n}n^{3(\gamma-1)}
, which diverges for such values of
\gamma
. Hence, from Eq. (
41
), the property of locality and the initial quasilinear regime are expected to be valid whenever

0<\gamma<3.

(58)

Note that the situation modeled by the Hamiltonian
H_{p}
, defined by Eq. (
1
), corresponds to
\gamma=1
. For this value of
\gamma
we proved both theoretically and numerically in Refs.
[
15
,
1
]
the property of locality and the initial quasilinear regime, in agreement with the condition Eq. (
58
).

IV
Numerical results

In this section, we make use of numerical simulations to test the theoretical predictions of Section
III
, i.e., that the property of locality and the initial quasilinear regime are valid only when the series in the right-hand of Eq. (
41
) converges when
N\to\infty
.

Henceforth, we assume that Eqs. (
45
)-(
56
) are fulfilled. At this stage it is important to note that, by assuming Eqs. (
45
)-(
47
) for the variations of
a_{n}
,
k_{n}
and
\omega_{n}
, we are not seeking to mimic any known wave spectrum. This is just a convenient way for us to test our theoretical predictions by varying a single parameter,
\gamma
. However, we note that the series
\sum_{n}(k_{n}a_{n})^{2}
should converge to prevent the electrostatic energy from diverging with
N
, which requires
\gamma<1
. For this reason, in this article we restrict to
\gamma\leq 1
, and the case
\gamma=1
is mainly considered to recall the results already obtained in Ref.
1
.

We also note that Langmuir waves all have about the same frequency, close to the plasma frequency, while the wavenumbers are expected to be bounded from above. Indeed, it is very hard to excite Langmuir waves with large wavenumbers (compared to the inverse of the Debye length) due to the large Landau damping rate of such waves. Hence, for Langmuir waves,
\omega_{n}/k_{n}
is expected to increase with
n
and, for such waves, one may expect locality and quasilinear diffusion only if the series
\sum_{n}(k_{n}a_{n})^{2}
converges. This condition should always be satisfied because it corresponds to the convergence of the electrostatic energy when
N\to\infty
. For ion acoustic waves,
\omega/k
is a constant and the condition for locality and quasilinear diffusion is the convergence of the series
\sum_{n}a_{n}^{2}
. However, once again, we do not focus here on any particular kind on plasma modes but rather seek to confirm the theoretical predictions of Section
III
by varying one single parameter,
\gamma
.

As regards the numerical simulations, we use a leapfrog integrator
[
21
]
to solve the Hamilton equations for
H
, defined by Eq. (
2
). In all our simulations, we only consider the motion of one single particle with initial velocity,
v_{0}
, and compute averages with respect to the random phases
\varphi_{n}
. Moreover, although not shown here, we systematically check that we use a small enough time step,
\delta t
, for the simulation to have converged. Then, the number of phase realizations used for our averaging usually depends on the smallness of the time step,
\delta t
, we have to use. Moreover, we systematically stop the simulations before the orbits reach the phase space boundaries.

V
Chaotic transport modeled as a diffusion process limited by phase space boundaries

In this section, we introduce a heuristic simple formula to derive the time evolution of the velocity distribution function in the situation when there is bounded chaotic transport, as defined in Section
II
. We restrict to regimes corresponding Figs.
10
and
12
, i.e., a quasilinear diffusion as long as the orbits remain far enough from the phase space boundaries. However, we aim to derive the time evolution of the distribution function for arbitrarily long times. This is not a simple task since the exact equation we have to solve is actually unknown. In particular, although Dirichlet boundary conditions seem most appropriate for this problem, solving the diffusion equation with such boundary conditions would not yield the correct answer because
\int f(v)dv
would not be conserved. Hence, we only propose here a heuristic formula for the time evolution of the distribution function, that we compare against results from test particles simulations. Clearly, this is a preliminary study that would require a more in-depth investigation, to better understand the range of validity of our formula and to address the more general situation when the diffusion coefficient is not uniform. However, we found useful to include our formula in this article due to its very good agreement with the numerical results. Hopefully, it will help improve the modeling of bounded chaotic transport.

If the time evolution of the velocity distribution function,
f(v,t)
, resulted from a quasilinear diffusion, then

f(v,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f(v^{\prime},0)\frac{\exp[-(v-v^{\prime})^{2}/4D_{\rm{QL}}t]}{\sqrt{4\pi D_{\rm{QL}}t}}dv^{\prime}.

(59)

However, for a bounded chaotic transport, diffusion is only effective between the velocities
v_{\min}
and
v_{\max}
which are, respectively, the minimum and maximum velocities a particle may access. These are the minimum and maximum velocities of the domain illustrated in Fig.
2
. Then, the number of particles within this domain must remain constant, i.e.,
\int_{v_{\min}}^{v_{\max}}f(v,t)dv=\int_{v_{\min}}^{v_{\max}}f(v,0)dv
. This leads us to the following heuristic formula for
f(v,t)
,

f(v,t)=\int_{v_{\min}}^{v_{\max}}f(v^{\prime},0)\frac{\exp[-(v-v^{\prime})^{2}/4D_{\rm{QL}}t]}{\int_{v_{\min}}^{v_{\max}}\exp[-(v-v^{\prime})^{2}/4D_{\rm{QL}}t]dv}dv^{\prime}.

(60)

As for
v_{\min}
and
v_{\max}
they are estimated the following way. Let
n_{\min}
and
n_{\max}
be such that
\min(\omega_{n}/k_{n})=\omega_{n_{\min}}/k_{n_{\min}}
and
\max(\omega_{n}/k_{n})=\omega_{n_{\max}}/k_{n_{\max}}
. Then, in Eq. (
60
), we use the following expressions for
v_{\min}
and
v_{\max}
,

\displaystyle v_{\min}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{n_{\min}}/k_{n_{\min}}-2\sqrt{A_{n_{\min}}},

(61)

\displaystyle v_{\max}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{n_{\max}}/k_{n_{\min}}+2\sqrt{A_{n_{\max}}}.

(62)

VI
Conclusion

In conclusion, this paper has addressed several important issues regarding phase space transport, in particular the relevance of its modeling as a quasilinear diffusion. As noted in the Introduction, quasilinear theory has been extensively discussed since its first publication in 1962
[
12
]
. Nevertheless, to the best of the author’s knowledge, these discussions had a limited impact on the plasma physics community. Quasilinear diffusion is still widely used to model transport because it is effective, rather simple, and usually gives the right orders of magnitudes for the transport rates. Yet, it is not always valid.

This paper has provided a simple criterion for assessing its validity, which is a central issue in many areas of physics and, most notably, in plasma physics. For a smooth discrete wave spectrum, the perturbative regime of quasilinear diffusion, akin to that introduced in the original 1962 papers, is valid only when the series on the right-hand side of Eq. (
41
) converges. This condition is equivalent to the requirement of locality in phase velocity for the wave-particle interaction. When this criterion is satisfied then, as illustrated in Figs.
14
and
15
of Paragraph
IV.5
, one can define a broad class of Hamiltonians whose transport properties are exactly the same as those of Hamiltonian
H_{p}
[Eq.(
1
)]. When the condition for locality is not fulfilled, the early time evolution of the velocity spreading is not diffusive, as clearly shown in Fig.
4
of Paragraph
IV.1.1
. Moreover, it depends on the number of modes in the discrete spectrum, unlike what quasilinear theory would predict. In spite of these very clear defects we have to acknowledge that, in all our simulations, quasilinear theory does provide the right order of magnitude for
\langle\Delta v^{2}(t)\rangle/t
at short times, with discrepancies never exceeding a factor of about two.

Moreover, for longer times and when transport is effective, we numerically find that chaotic diffusion eventually seems to settle, even when wave-particle interaction is not local, as shown for example in Fig.
5
of Paragraph
IV.1.1
. In all our simulations, the diffusion coefficient is always of the same order as the quasilinear one, again differing by no more than a factor of about two. However, when wave-particle interaction is not local, it depends on the number of modes.

Now, as discussed in detail in Section
II
, diffusion cannot persist indefinitely because phase space is bounded by KAM tori. In this paper, we accounted for these boundaries to derive an effective equation for the time evolution of the velocity distribution function, namely Eq. (
60
) of Section
V
. The predictions of this equation were compared against results from test particle simulations for an initially discontinuous distribution function. A very good agreement was found between the theoretical and numerical predictions, except in the vicinity of the discontinuity for short times. This was expected because, as proved in Section
V
, a diffusion equation cannot accurately describe the early time evolution of a distribution function near its discontinuity. Given the high accuracy of the heuristic equation (
60
), it would be of interest to further investigate its range of validity and to extend it to a non-uniform diffusion coefficient. This is left for future work.

In summary, this paper has investigated transport induced by a discrete wave spectrum and introduced a simple, practical criterion for assessing the validity of quasilinear theory. Yet, it is difficult to discuss the notion of “validity” in very general terms. Indeed, as noted above, even when the velocity spreading is not diffusive, quasilinear theory appears to give the correct orders of magnitude for transport rates. This leads to a dual conclusion. On the one hand, quasilinear diffusion is not always valid and we showed that, for some wave spectra, the early time evolution of the distribution function is not even diffusive. On the other hand, if the goal is merely to estimate
\langle\Delta v^{2}(t)\rangle
, our numerical simulations suggest that quasilinear theory consistently provides the correct order of magnitude.
