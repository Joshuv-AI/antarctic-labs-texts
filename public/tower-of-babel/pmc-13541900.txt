Flanged Polypropylene Suture for Preset One-Piece Acrylic Intraocular Lens Fixation with Pre-Threaded Needle Technique: Surgical Technique and Short-Term Clinical Outcomes
Authors: Yuichi Kuramochi, Yuki Mizuki, Tatsukata Kawagoe, Raiga Osada, Keiichi Sato, Norihiro Yamada, Nobuhisa Mizuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to report the feasibility and short-term clinical outcomes of a novel technique for one-piece intraocular lens (IOL) fixation using double-threaded 30-gauge needles and flanged polypropylene sutures.Patients and MethodsForty eyes of 38 consecutive patients with subluxated crystalline lenses, dislocated IOLs, or aphakia were included. Two 30-gauge needles pre-threaded with 7–0 polypropylene sutures were inserted 2.4 mm posterior to the limbus horizontally toward the center of the globe. After the suture loops were externalized, a preset one-piece IOL was inserted into the anterior chamber. Both haptics were then externalized, tied with a 2-1-1 knot, and reinserted. The sutures were pulled to center the IOL, cut, cauterized to form flanges, and buried in the sclera.ResultsUncorrected visual acuity improved from 1.25 ± 0.55 logMAR preoperatively to 0.81 ± 0.52 logMAR at 3 months postoperatively. Best-corrected visual acuity changed from 0.72 ± 0.66 to 0.39 ± 0.48 logMAR. The mean refractive difference was 0.25 ± 0.67 D. The mean IOL decentration and tilt were 0.34 ± 0.20 mm and 7.25° ± 3.4°, respectively. Flange erosion was observed in four eyes (10.0%), retinal detachment in one eye (2.5%), and cystoid macular edema in one eye (2.5%). No postoperative IOL dislocation or clinical signs suggestive of iris chafing were seen during follow-up.ConclusionIn this short-term series, the technique allowed fixation of a one-piece IOL with acceptable centration and no postoperative IOL dislocation. The technique may be an option when three-piece IOLs are unavailable or when preservation of an existing one-piece IOL is desirable.Keywords: intraocular lens implantation, scleral fixation, visual acuity, refractive error, surgical complications, treatment outcome

Introduction

Secondary intraocular lens (IOL) implantation in eyes with inadequate capsular support can be performed using anterior chamber IOL implantation, iris fixation, or scleral fixation.1 Scleral fixation is often preferred when posterior chamber IOL placement is desired, as it avoids limitations associated with anterior chamber and iris-fixated lenses.1,2 Transscleral sutured fixation remains a reliable option in many cases, but late suture erosion, degradation, and breakage are important concerns.3,4 These problems have contributed to the wider use of sutureless intrascleral fixation techniques.2 In the Yamane technique, the haptics of a three-piece IOL are externalized with needles, flanged using cautery, and then secured within scleral tunnels.5 This approach is now well established for three-piece IOLs.6 By contrast, scleral fixation of one-piece acrylic IOLs is less standardized, mainly because these lenses differ in haptic structure and optic–haptic configuration.7–10

One-piece acrylic IOLs are widely used in cataract surgery.8 They are available not only as monofocal lenses but also as toric, multifocal, and extended depth-of-focus platforms.9,11 Consequently, surgeons may encounter eyes with inadequate capsular support in which a one-piece acrylic IOL is already present, or situations wherein a one-piece platform would otherwise be preferable.9,10 Exchanging the lens for a three-piece IOL followed by scleral fixation is an established strategy.5,6 However, IOL exchange may require wound enlargement and additional anterior segment manipulation.12 These steps can be associated with surgically induced astigmatism, corneal endothelial cell loss, iris damage, and postoperative inflammation.6,12

In selected eyes, direct fixation of a one-piece acrylic IOL may be advantageous, particularly when IOL exchange is undesirable.8–10,12 These lenses, however, should not be considered equivalent to three-piece IOLs for secondary fixation.13 Their broad, relatively rigid haptics and optic–haptic design may increase posterior iris contact when positioned in or near the ciliary sulcus.7,13 Pigment dispersion and uveitis-glaucoma-hyphema syndrome have been reported, sometimes accompanied by elevated intraocular pressure.13 Therefore, fixation techniques for one-piece acrylic IOLs should ensure stable posterior chamber positioning, minimize iris contact, and avoid exposed fixation material at the conjunctival or scleral surface.7,14

We developed a transconjunctival scleral fixation technique for one-piece IOLs using double-threaded 30-gauge needles and flanged polypropylene sutures. This study describes the surgical technique and evaluates short-term anatomical, visual, refractive, and safety outcomes in consecutive eyes with insufficient capsular support.

Materials and Methods

All procedures were conducted by the same surgeon (Y.K.). This study was designed as a retrospective interventional case series. The inclusion criteria were lens subluxation, dislocated IOL, and aphakia. All dislocated IOLs included in this study had a three-piece design. The exclusion criteria were history of ocular surgery other than cataract surgery and postoperative follow-up shorter than 3 months. The follow-up period ranged from May 2023 to June 2025. All participants were consecutively treated patients.

The primary anatomical outcomes were IOL decentration and IOL tilt at 3 months postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA), best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP), corneal endothelial cell density, refractive prediction error, and postoperative complications. Early complications were defined as those occurring within 1 month postoperatively, and late complications as those occurring thereafter. Hypotony was defined as IOP <6 mmHg, and postoperative IOP elevation as >25 mmHg.

Refractive prediction was obtained using OA-2000 (optical biometer; Tomey Corporation, Nagoya, Japan) with the Barrett Universal II formula. Refractive prediction error was defined as the postoperative spherical equivalent minus the predicted refraction. The IOL decentration and tilt were measured using CASIA-2 (anterior segment ocular coherence tomography; Tomey Corporation) under mydriatic conditions at postoperative 3-month follow-up.

Purkinje–Sanson image-guided intraoperative alignment was performed as part of the standard procedure in all eyes.

Technique

We recently developed the Pre-Threaded Needle technique (PNT) to simplify and improve the safety of threading procedures.15

Haptic ligation was performed as follows: a double loop was created using forceps, with the short end directed toward (Figure 1A, left) or away from the surgeon (Figure 1A, right). A double knot was tied tightly (Figure 1B). The long end of the suture was wrapped over and under the tying forceps, and the short end was grasped (Figure 1C). A second knot was tied (Figure 1D). Subsequently, the long end was then wrapped across the tying forceps, and the short end was grasped again (Figure 1E). A third knot was tied (Figure 1F). Finally, the haptics were ligated with a 2-1-1 surgical knot. This procedure is shown in Video S1.

(B) Pulling tight to make the first double knot at a point 3 mm from the end of the haptic. (C) Wrapping the long end of the suture over and under the tying forceps; then grasping the short end. (D) Pulling tight to make the second knot. (E) Wrapping the long end across the tying forceps and picking up the short end. (F) Pulling tight to make the third knot.

Surgical Procedure

Symmetric marks were made at the limbus and scleral entry sites 2.4 mm posterior to the limbus on both sides using a toric marker and caliper. Bilateral 1.1-mm paracenteses were created using MVR21A (MVR knife; MANI, Inc, Tochigi, Japan), and a 2.4-mm sclerocorneal incision was made with MSL24SK (slit knife; MANI, Inc).

The PNT was performed through the ipsilateral paracentesis with a 30-gauge needle and Prolene® (7–0 polypropylene suture; Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) 2.4 mm posterior to the limbus at the opposite site, with the needle advanced horizontally toward the center of the globe (Figure 2A). A preset one-piece IOL (DCB00V [Johnson and Johnson Surgical Vision, Santa Ana, CA, USA]; SY60WF [Alcon Laboratories, Geneva, Switzerland]; XY-1 [HOYA, Tokyo, Japan]; YP2.2R [Kowa, Nagoya, Japan], and NP-1 [NIDEK, Gamagori, Japan]) was inserted and placed on the iris (Figure 2B). The leading haptic was externalized through the left paracentesis and the trailing haptic was externalized via the right paracentesis (Figure 2C). Externalized polypropylene sutures were positioned forward in the left paracentesis and backward in the right paracentesis. The haptics were ligated 3 mm from their ends using a 2-1-1 surgical knot (Figure 2D) and re-inserted into the eye (Figure 2E). Polypropylene sutures outside the sclera were pulled to their full length; the haptics were verified to be untwisted using a hook or microforceps. The IOL was fixed at the center of the eye (Figure 2F). The polypropylene sutures were manipulated to align the first, third, and fourth Purkinje–Sanson images and cut at a point 2 mm outside the sclera (Figure 2G). The outer ends of the sutures were cauterized proximally by single-use thermocautery device (Beaver Visitec, Waltham, MA, USA) to create flanges (Figure 2H). These flanges were buried in the outer sclera (Figure 2I). This procedure is shown in Video S2.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables that did not follow a normal distribution were analyzed using nonparametric methods. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for paired comparisons before and after surgery, including UCVA, BCVA, IOP, and corneal endothelial cell density. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons among multiple IOL models, including refractive prediction error, IOL decentration, and IOL tilt. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. A one-eye-per-patient sensitivity analysis was performed using 38 eyes to reduce the potential influence of inter-eye correlation. Statistical analyses were performed using EZR software (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan).16 Data are presented as means ± standard deviations.

Postoperative complications are listed in Supplemental Table 4. Four cases of flange erosion were observed on postoperative day 1 and were managed by re-cauterizing the flange and burying it in the scleral tissue. No recurrence or infection was observed. Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment occurred in one eye at 2 months postoperatively and was treated by pars plana vitrectomy. Cystoid macular edema was observed in one eye at 6 months postoperatively and was treated with a sub-Tenon injection of triamcinolone acetonide. No cases of postoperative hypotony, IOP elevation, iris capture, vitreous hemorrhage, endophthalmitis, or IOL dislocation occurred during the follow-up period.

Discussion

We developed and evaluated a transconjunctival scleral fixation technique for preset one-piece acrylic IOLs using the PNT and flanged 7–0 polypropylene sutures. At 3 months postoperatively, the mean IOL decentration was 0.34 ± 0.20 mm, and the mean tilt was 7.25° ± 3.4°. No postoperative IOL dislocation occurred during the follow-up period. Although, the degree of IOL tilt was not negligible and greater than that reported in some recent intrascleral fixation series, it remained within the broader range reported after scleral-fixated IOL implantation.5,6,17 These findings suggest that the technique is anatomically feasible in the short term.

Several techniques for one-piece IOL fixation have been reported, including sutured fixation of an existing subluxated or dislocated acrylic one-piece IOL, four-flanged polypropylene optic piercing, and scleral fixation using the Z.S. technique.8–10 Our method differs from these approaches in that the haptics are externalized, ligated, and then reinserted. The optic is not pierced, and the suture is not threaded through the cartridge. This difference is clinically relevant because single-piece acrylic IOLs have broad haptics and a characteristic optic-haptic configuration. If positioned close to the ciliary sulcus, these lenses may contact the posterior iris surface.7,13

The PNT simplifies the threading step by pre-loading the polypropylene suture into a standard 30-gauge needle before ocular entry.15 Although technically straightforward, this step has practical importance. Intraocular threading of thin polypropylene sutures can be technically demanding and may increase manipulation in the anterior segment. Preparing the suture outside the eye allows the needle to be advanced in a manner similar to other flanged fixation procedures.5,9 This feature may help standardize the procedure, although its reproducibility among different surgeons has not been assessed in the present study.

Haptic ligation technique is another key part of the procedure. The technique was designed to suture the acrylic haptics while avoiding mechanical damage. We used a 2-1-1 surgical knot to fix the 7–0 polypropylene suture to each haptic. No intraoperative haptic breakage, knot slippage, or postoperative IOL dislocation was observed during the follow-up period. These clinical findings are encouraging, but they do not prove long-term knot security. Late suture-related problems remain a known issue in scleral fixation surgery.3,4 Further mechanical testing of the knot-haptic complex and longer follow-up will be needed before the stability of this ligation method can be discussed more firmly.

In this technique, the ligation point was placed at 3 mm from the end of each haptic. Based on our measurements, this setting produced an inter-ligation distance of approximately 11 mm across the evaluated IOL models. We considered this distance appropriate because it is close to the expected sulcus-to-sulcus diameter. A large mismatch between the fixation span and the ocular diameter may deform the haptics and contribute to IOL tilt or decentration.17,18 However, the 3-mm setting should not be regarded as universal for all one-piece IOLs. Haptic length, haptic stiffness, and optic-haptic angulation vary among IOL designs. These features should be carefully evaluated before applying the same ligation point to other IOL models.

To reduce complications related to posterior iris chafing, sufficient distance should be maintained between the posterior iris surface and the one-piece IOL.7,13 Sugiura et al reported that a needle inserted horizontally from the deepest part of sulcus is expected to emerge approximately 2.37 mm posterior to the limbus.18 Based on these anatomical estimates, we selected a scleral entry site 2.4 mm posterior to the limbus and advanced the needle horizontally toward the center of the globe. This approach was intended to position the IOL posterior to the iris without excessive posterior displacement of the optic. In the present series, we did not observe clinical findings suggestive of iris chafing, including iris capture, pigment dispersion, vitreous hemorrhage, recurrent inflammation, or IOP elevation.

Previous studies of the Yamane technique have reported retinal and functional outcomes, including cystoid macular edema, epiretinal membrane, and corneal endothelial cell loss.19,20 These findings provide useful context for interpreting the short-term safety profile of the present technique. In our series, cystoid macular edema occurred in one eye, and endothelial cell loss may have been influenced by lens removal and intraoperative anterior chamber manipulation. Direct comparison with Yamane technique studies is limited by differences in indications, IOL design, surgical procedure, and follow-up duration.

Within this broader context, scleral fixation of three-piece IOLs remains the standard approach and is supported by multiple established techniques. Our study does not aim to replace these methods but to describe an alternative pathway for eyes in which a preset one-piece acrylic IOL is already available or preferable to preserve. This distinction is important, as the choice of fixation method is often guided by lens availability, ocular anatomy, and the need to minimize surgical trauma. By focusing on one-piece IOLs, our findings add to the spectrum of fixation options and highlight scenarios where this approach may be clinically advantageous, while acknowledging that larger comparative studies are required to define its role relative to established three-piece fixation techniques.

Refractive predictability is an important consideration, because scleral fixation may alter the postoperative effective lens position assumed in standard IOL power calculations. De Vitto et al reported that refinement of lens constants reduced systematic bias in refractive error after sutureless scleral fixation with the Yamane technique.21 Although those findings cannot be directly applied to the present one-piece IOL fixation method, they suggest that refractive outcomes after out-of-the-bag fixation may be technique-dependent. In this study, the mean refractive prediction error was 0.25 ± 0.67 D, but the sample size was insufficient to optimize lens constants or evaluate formula-specific prediction accuracy.

If IOL tilt persisted during surgery, the polypropylene sutures were manipulated to align the first, third, and fourth Purkinje–Sanson images (Figure 4). This procedure is shown in Video S3. Previous studies have used these images to evaluate IOL decentration and tilt, and the same optical principle may serve as a practical intraoperative reference.22–24 In our procedure, rotation of the externalized polypropylene suture rotated the tied haptic and modified the orientation of the IOL. If residual tilt remained after flange creation, the flange could be pushed slightly into the sclera in a controlled direction to apply a small torque. This intraoperative adjustment may improve IOL alignment; however, it is partly dependent on the surgeon’s judgment and technique. Whether similar alignment can be achieved by other surgeons should be examined in future studies.

Flange erosion was the most frequent procedure-related complication in this series and was observed in four eyes. All cases were treated re-cauterization and re-burial of the flange within scleral tissue, with no recurrence or infection. Possible causes include oversized flanges, insufficient scleral burial, short intrascleral suture segments, or thin conjunctival and scleral tissue. Because flange exposure can lead to serious infection after flanged fixation procedures, flanges should be made only large enough to prevent slippage and should be completely buried at the end of surgery.14

This study has some limitations. First, its retrospective design, single-surgeon setting, lack of a control group, and small sample of included eyes limit the interpretation of the findings. Moreover, the study population was heterogeneous and included eyes with aphakia, dislocated IOLs, and subluxated crystalline lenses, which may have affected postoperative visual outcomes, refractive results, and complications. Although 40 eyes of 38 patients were included in the main analysis and a sensitivity analysis using one eye per patient was performed, the possibility of inter-eye correlation cannot be completely excluded. Second, the mean follow-up period was 10.8 months, which may be insufficient to evaluate late complications such as suture degradation, recurrent flange erosion, IOL dislocation, and delayed retinal or macular events. Iris chafing was assessed clinically, without quantitative imaging or objective measurements of pigment dispersion or intraocular inflammation. Reliability and agreement analyses for IOL decentration, tilt, and intraoperative alignment were not performed. Therefore, the reproducibility of these measurements and maneuvers should be evaluated in future studies. In addition, the influence of fixation site on effective lens position and refractive predictability was not directly assessed. These limitations should be addressed in future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up.

Conclusion

This retrospective case series suggests that PNT-assisted flanged polypropylene suture fixation can be used for preset one-piece acrylic IOLs in selected eyes with insufficient capsular support. In the short term, the technique provided stable IOL fixation, with no postoperative IOL dislocation and no clinical findings suggestive of iris chafing during follow-up. Larger comparative studies with longer follow-up are required to evaluate refractive predictability, late complications, and applicability across a wider range of IOL designs.

Our findings highlight the potential of this technique as a practical alternative in cases where lens exchange or wound enlargement may be undesirable. It may be particularly useful when three-piece IOLs are unavailable or when preservation of an existing one-piece IOL is desirable. The approach may be particularly useful for patients requiring preservation of corneal integrity or those at higher risk of surgical trauma. While the results support short-term feasibility, careful patient selection and meticulous surgical execution remain critical, and broader validation is needed before routine adoption.
