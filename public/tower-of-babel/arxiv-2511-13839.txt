Trading athermality for nonstabiliserness
A. de Oliveira Junior, Rafael A. Macêdo, Jakub Czartowski, Jonatan Bohr Brask, Rafael Chaves

Abstract
Nonstabiliserness is a fundamental resource for quantum advantage, capturing how much a quantum state breaks the symmetries that would make it classically simulable. Can nonstabiliserness be generated from stabiliser states simply by coupling them to a heat bath? We explore the thermodynamic limits of nonstabiliserness under minimal assumptions and derive a necessary and sufficient condition for when such a process can create it from an initial stabiliser state. This provides an analytic characterisation of the nonstabiliser states that are reachable in this way, together with quantitative bounds on their degree of nonstabiliserness. Our framework also identifies optimal regimes, specifying Hamiltonians that maximise nonstabiliserness generation and the critical temperatures at which it emerges.

Such a framework poses two nontrivial problems. First, not every nonstabiliser state is useful for quantum computation

Whether these are sufficient is determined by distillability: can they be purified into useful magic states using only stabiliser operations? Second, preparing useful magic states is intrinsically difficult. For logical qubits in fault-tolerant settings, noise from the unavoidable coupling to a heat bath leads to thermalisation. Yet, not every thermalisation process itself needs to be a stabiliser operation

We answer this question by showing that the environment is not only an enemy: athermality—the property of being out of equilibrium with a reference thermal bath—can be traded for nonstabiliserness. We derive a necessary and sufficient condition for magic states to emerge from an initial stabiliser state under thermalisation processes with minimal assumptions. The criterion applies to general qubit states, and is particularly relevant for logical magic state preparation in fault-tolerant quantum computation. This framework allows us to characterise the full set of thermally reachable magic states, quantify how much nonstabiliserness can be produced, and identify operational thresholds such as the critical inverse temperature at which magic appears. Our results show that the price to create nonstabiliserness is paid by bringing the state closer to thermal equilibrium, namely trading athermality for magic. Setup
– Given a qubit state
\rho
, what characterises it as nonstabiliser? We define a state to be nonstabiliser if it cannot be expressed as a convex mixture of stabiliser states
50
;
24
. For a single qubit, the stabiliser states are the six eigenstates of the Pauli operators
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(X,Y,Z)
, corresponding to the six poles along the coordinate axes of the Bloch sphere. Their convex hull forms an octahedron known as the stabiliser polytope
10
. A general qubit state
\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbbm{1}+\boldsymbol{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})
, with Bloch vector
\boldsymbol{r}=(r_{x},r_{y},r_{z})
, is stabiliser if and only if it lies inside this octahedron, a condition captured by

\norm{\v{r}}_{1}=\absolutevalue{r_x}+\absolutevalue{r_y}+\absolutevalue{r_z}\leq 1.

(1)

Any state violating this inequality possesses nonstabiliserness. The unitaries that map Pauli operators to Pauli operators, corresponding to the rotational symmetries of the octahedron, are known as Clifford unitaries. They can shuffle the vertices of the polytope but cannot map any state from the inside to the outside (see Appendix
A-2
for a detailed discussion). Can general thermal processes—operations implemented using only a thermal bath—achieve what Clifford operations cannot?

Thermal operations induce a natural split between “easy” and “hard” nonstabiliserness generation. In some instances, the outer maximisation is achieved at the initial population

The results discussed here are fundamental: they represent upper bounds on magic generation under energy conservation that do not depend on any particular control scheme. In the Bravyi–Kitaev model

Our results remain operationally relevant as they represent fundamental limits that cannot be surpassed by any protocol respecting the same thermodynamic constraints. In representative cases, most notably energy-diagonal inputs and Hamiltonian direction

Optimal regimes
– Critical inverse temperatures for general qubit inputs can be obtained analytically once the Hamiltonian is fixed. Coherence never raises, and often lowers, the inverse-temperature threshold required for nonstabiliserness. The relation between critical temperature and coherence depends on how the thermally reachable set meets the stabiliser boundary for a given Hamiltonian direction. When the contact point depends only on populations–specifically for a Hamiltonian aligned with the

Finally, after asking whether thermal operations can generate magic states from a stabiliser input
\rho
at inverse temperature
\beta
, we now ask where they can take us—to which nonstabiliser states. This can be determined by constructing the set of states reachable by thermal operations and taking its set difference with the stabiliser polytope,
\mathcal{T}^{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}}_{+}(\rho):=\mathcal{T}_{+}(\rho)\setminus\textrm{stab}
. We call
\mathcal{T}^{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}}_{+}(\rho)

\rho

\mathcal{T}^{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}}
satisfies
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(\rho)>1

Unlike the witness, a binary indicator of whether nonstabiliserness can be generated, the nonstabiliser cone tells us which magic states are reachable and how large that set is inside the Bloch ball. This suggests a figure of merit: the normalised volume of the nonstabiliser cone, which measures how much of the Bloch ball becomes thermodynamically accessible as nonstabiliserness. Both temperature and the Hamiltonian play a role here. The Hamiltonian sets the direction in which the thermally reachable set grows, while the temperature governs whether it grows or shrinks. For a fixed initial state, the reachable set exhibits two regimes. When
p<\gamma
, its volume grows monotonically with
\beta

\mathcal{T}_{+}(\rho)

p>\gamma
, the cone volume can shrink as
\beta

\gamma(\beta)=p
. Beyond this threshold the cone grows, the nonstabiliserness region appears, and its volume increases with further cooling. The Hamiltonian orientation has a similar effect: the closer it is to the optimal direction, the more efficiently it generates magic states, resulting in a larger cone volume (see Fig.
4

Discussion & outlook
– Does extending the results to higher-dimensional systems reveal anything new? While moving to qudits undeniably enriches stabiliser geometry and Clifford structure, the qubit setting already captures the mechanism relevant here. Under thermal operations (
2
), the interplay of thermomajorisation, time-translation symmetry, and stabiliser facets is completely exposed in one qubit: the stabiliser boundary is explicit (
1
). Our witness
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(\rho)
provides a necessary-and-sufficient test for nonstabiliserness generation, and for energy-diagonal inputs, the criterion collapses to a closed form (
6
) that gives analytic thresholds (
7
). Importantly, our results are model-independent: we make no assumptions about interaction strength (weak or strong), structure (local or collective), or duration relative to natural time scales. Therefore, they provide fundamental bounds for any energy-conserving unitary model, regardless of microscopic details.

While we briefly discussed representative cases where Clifford-only logical control can approach these bounds, establishing constructive, code-specific protocols and general tightness is an interesting direction for future work. A natural extension motivated by fault-tolerant quantum computation would be to consider
Clifford-assisted thermal operations
, where both the system control and system-bath interactions are restricted to Clifford operations. Another natural next step is to include a catalyst and explore what advantages catalytic transformations can offer
16
;
32
. Catalysis enlarges the set of reachable states
14
;
45
;
44
;
15
, which should, in turn, enable access to a broader range of magic states, as briefly discussed in Appendix
E
. Beyond generating nonstabiliserness, an intriguing question is how to detect magic under minimal assumptions
36
;
29
;
53
. Similarly, we can ask whether thermodynamic processes can be used for this task. The recent letter
17
proposes a heat-based certification procedure that is, in principle, applicable to arbitrary quantum resources. Whether this framework can be leveraged to probe nonstabiliserness remains open.

Our results are directly implementable with current experimental platforms. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) systems
49
and cavity QED setups with superconducting qubits
6
;
28
have already demonstrated energy-preserving unitaries
5
;
9
;
12
;
38
, offering precise control over both the initial states and the Hamiltonians. Looking beyond proof-of-principle demonstrations, an important next step is to investigate whether our methods can provide practical advantages for quantum computing within these architectures.

(18)

\displaystyle\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{1}}\sqrt{\bra{\ms{H}}C\rho C^{\dagger}\ket{\ms{H}}}\gtrsim 0.93.

(19)

Thermal operations in a nutshell

In this appendix we provide a review of technical details on the characterisation of states reachable under thermal operations. We follow the the convention as set in the main text: a system with Hamiltonian
H

\rho
, a bath with Hamiltonian
H_{\textsf{E}}
prepared in a Gibbs state
\gamma_{\textsf{E}}=\exp(-\beta H_{\ms{E}})

\beta

U

[U,H+H_{\textsf{E}}]=0
, which induces the map in Eq. (

{\cal E}(\rho)=\tr_{\textsf{E}}\left[U\left(\rho\otimes\gamma_{\textsf{E}}\right)U^{\dagger}\right].

(20)

The energy-conservation condition encodes the first law of thermodynamics. Moreover, since the heat bath is in thermal equilibrium, every thermal operation
{\cal E}
preserves the system’s Gibbs state,
{\cal E}(\gamma)=\gamma
defined in full analogy to the environment’s Gibbs state. Consequently, this Gibbs-preserving property, together with any contractive distance measure
\delta
yielding
\delta(\rho,\gamma)\geq\delta[{\cal E}(\rho),{\cal E}(\gamma)]=\delta[{\cal E}(\rho),\gamma]
, captures the core content of the second law by formalising the evolution towards thermal equilibrium.

The set of states achievable under Eq. (
2

\mathcal{T}_{+}(\rho)
. Two structural facts allow us to construct the qubit future thermal cone by treating populations and coherences separately and then combining them. First, the diagonal (energy populations) evolves under Gibbs-preserving, energy-conserving stochastic maps and is exactly constrained by thermomajorisation. Second, time-translation symmetry bounds the magnitude of the off-diagonal terms independently of their phase, yielding a tight coherence constraint for any prescribed population change.

For a qubit with ground-state population
p
, coherence
c
, and associated thermal state
\tau:=e^{-\beta H}/\tr(e^{-\beta H})
—the Gibbs state of
H
, which in the energy eigenbasis of

\operatorname{diag}(\gamma,1-\gamma)
—the attainable populations and coherences under thermal operations are as follows:

Lemma 1

(Reachable populations
33
)
.

Under thermal operations at inverse temperature
\beta
, the set of reachable populations is the line segment

\mathord{\text{\magic P}}_{\beta}(p):=\operatorname{conv}\quantity[\qty(p,1-p), \qty(1-\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}p,\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}p)].

(21)

Projecting
\mathord{\text{\magic P}}_{\beta}(p)
onto its first coordinate collects the reachable ground-state populations into the interval

I_{\beta}(p):=\quantity[\min\{p,\,q_\star\},\,\max\{p,\,q_\star\}],

(22)

where
q^{\star}:=1-\tfrac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}p
. We now state the corresponding coherence bound.

Lemma 2

(Reachable coherences
35
)
.

Under thermal operations, the coherence
r
achievable for a target qubit with population
q
(from an initial qubit with population
p
and coherence

r\leq\mathord{\text{\magic c}}{\max}(q)
, where

\mathord{\text{\magic c}}_{\max}(q)\!:=\!|c|\frac{\sqrt{[q(1-\gamma)\!-\!\gamma(1-p)][p(1-\gamma)\!-\!\gamma(1-q)]}}{|p-\gamma|}.

(23)

B.1
Necessary & Sufficient condition

We begin with a geometric reformulation that characterises when the future thermal cone lies entirely within the stabiliser polytope. By rotating into the Hamiltonian-aligned frame, we may restrict our considerations to circular cross-sections of the Bloch sphere without loss of generality, thus reducing the problem to a support-function optimisation, separating population and coherence contributions. The resulting inequality is stated below and used in our bounds. It is established via two lemmas and a theorem:

Lemma 3

(Rotated-frame
\ell_{1}
support over circles)
.

Let
H=\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
be a qubit Hamiltonian and let a rotation
R\in SO(3)
send
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\mapsto\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}

M:=R^{-1}=R^{\top}
with columns
\boldsymbol{m}_{1},\boldsymbol{m}_{2},\boldsymbol{m}_{3}

z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
and
r_{\perp}\geq 0

\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi)=(r_{\perp}\cos\phi,r_{\perp}\sin\phi,z^{\prime})

\max_{\phi\in[0,2\pi)}\bigl\|M\,\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi)\bigr\|_{1}=\max_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\{\pm 1\}^{3}}\left(r_{\perp}\,\alpha_{s}+|z^{\prime}|\,|h_{s}|\right),

(24)

where
\alpha_{s}:=\sqrt{(s^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{1})^{2}+(s^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{2})^{2}}

M

\boldsymbol{s}\in\{\pm 1\}^{3}

\alpha^{2}_{s}+h^{2}_{s}=3

Proof.

Using the support-function identity
\|x\|_{1}=\max_{s\in\{\pm 1\}}s^{\top}x

\|M\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi)\|_{1}=\max_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\{\pm 1\}^{3}}s^{\top}M\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi)

\max_{\phi\in[0,2\pi)}\|M\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi)\|_{1}=\max_{\phi}\max_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\{\pm 1\}^{3}}s^{\top}M\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi)=\max_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\{\pm 1\}^{3}}\max_{\phi}s^{\top}M\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi),

(25)

M=\boldsymbol{m}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{m}_{2}\ \boldsymbol{m}_{3}

h_{s}:=s^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{3}

s^{\top}M\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi)=r_{\perp}\bigl(s^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{1}\cos\phi+s^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{2}\sin\phi\bigr)+z^{\prime}h_{s}.

(26)

Let
\alpha_{s}:=\sqrt{(s^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{1})^{2}+(s^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{2})^{2}}

\max_{\phi}s^{\top}M\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi)=r_{\perp}\alpha_{s}+z^{\prime}h_{s}
(attained at
\phi=\varphi_{s}
). If
s^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{1}=s^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{2}=0
, then
\alpha_{s}=0
and the
\phi

\max_{\phi}s^{\top}M\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi)=z^{\prime}h_{s}
. In either case,
\max_{\phi}s^{\top}M\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi)=r_{\perp}\alpha_{s}+z^{\prime}h_{s}
. Since
\alpha_{-s}=\alpha_{s}
and
\beta_{-s}=-h_{s}
, maximising over
s
yields

\max_{\phi}\|M\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}(\phi)\|_{1}=\max_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\{\pm 1\}^{3}}\bigl(r_{\perp}\alpha_{s}+|z^{\prime}|\,|h_{s}|\bigr),

(27)

∎

Let

with

Proof.

B.2
Operational and thermodynamic meanings of the magic witness

The magic-generation witness
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)
provides a sharp boundary between stabiliser and non-stabiliser states achievable via thermal processes, with magic generation occurring precisely when
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)>1
. While this binary condition determines whether magic states can be generated, examining how the witness behaves relative to this threshold reveals deeper physical insights about the nature of magic generation. By transforming the witness to measure its deviation from the magic-generation threshold, we obtain quantities that characterise both the geometric and thermodynamic aspects of the process.

For energy-diagonal inputs, the witness takes the form

\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)=\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{1}\times\begin{cases}\bigl|1-2p\,e^{-2\beta}\bigr|,&p<\gamma,\\[4.0pt]
|2p-1|,&p>\gamma,\end{cases}

(33)

where
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{1}\in[1,\sqrt{3}]
is the
\ell_{1}
-norm of the Hamiltonian direction in Bloch coordinates. The witness is bounded by this orientational ceiling,
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)\leq\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{1}
, with equality approached as
\beta\to\infty
in the
p<\gamma
branch.

There are two natural transformations which endow the magic witness
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}
with natural interpretations. The first and simplest transformation is the additive shift
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)-1
. Geometrically, since the single-qubit stabiliser polytope corresponds to the
\ell_{1}
-ball in Bloch space, this quantity measures the facet-violation margin: when positive, it quantifies how far the thermally reachable set exceeds the boundary of the stabiliser octahedron. This geometric interpretation has direct operational significance. Under isotropic depolarising noise
\mathcal{D}_{t}(\rho)=(1-t)\rho+t\,\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}}{2}
, which shrinks Bloch vectors by
(1-t)
, the minimal noise strength that eliminates all magic from thermally reachable states is

t^{*}(p,\beta)=\max\Bigl\{\,0,1-\frac{1}{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)}\Bigr\}=\begin{cases}0,&\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)\leq 1,\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)-1}{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)},&\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)>1.\end{cases}

(34)

Thus, the magnitude of
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}-1
directly quantifies the robustness of magic generation to white noise, providing a practical measure of how much environmental noise the process can tolerate while still producing useful states.

In the
p<\gamma
regime, the temperature dependence reveals additional structure. Letting
\beta_{\mathrm{crt}}
satisfy
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta_{\mathrm{crt}})=1
and defining
\delta\beta:=\beta-\beta_{\mathrm{crt}}\geq 0
, and noting that above threshold we have
|1-2pe^{-2\beta}|=1-2pe^{-2\beta}
, we obtain

\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)-1=\bigl(\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{1}-1\bigr)\,\bigl(1-e^{-2\delta\beta}\bigr).

(35)

This shows that the facet-violation margin increases monotonically with the inverse-temperature surplus
\delta\beta
, saturating at
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{1}-1
as
\beta\to\infty
. It is important to note that
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}-1
represents a witness margin rather than a metric distance—it should not be identified with geometric distances such as the trace distance to the stabiliser polytope.

Logarithmic transformation provides a complementary perspective. The quantity
\log\mathord{\text{\magic M}}
captures multiplicative overshoot and near threshold behaves similarly to
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}-1
, but lacks a desirable property—linear temperature dependence. A more insightful approach normalises by the orientational ceiling:

L(p,\beta):=-\log\left(1-\frac{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{1}}\right)+\log\left(1-\frac{1}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{1}}\right),\qquad\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{1}>1.

(36)

For
p<\gamma
and any
\beta\geq\beta_{\mathrm{crt}}
, this yields the exact identity
L(p,\beta)=2(\beta-\beta_{\mathrm{crt}})
, transforming the threshold condition into a linear thermometer that directly measures how far the bath is cooled beyond criticality. The requirement
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{1}>1
excludes the trivial axis-aligned case where no temperature leverage is available.

When
p>\gamma
, the witness becomes temperature-independent with
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}(p,\beta)=\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{1}|2p-1|
. In this purely geometric regime, only the additive shift
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}-1
remains meaningful, capturing facet overshoot and determining robustness via
t^{*}=1-1/\mathord{\text{\magic M}}
when
\mathord{\text{\magic M}}>1
, while logarithmic transformations offer no additional thermal insight.

Appendix C
Distillability (inverse temperature) bounds

We now turn to a complementary question. Given an initial stabiliser state and a bath at an inverse temperature
\beta
when can the resulting reachable states be useful for magic-state distillation? In other words, how hot can the bath be while still allowing the production of states whose fidelities with the relevant Clifford orbits exceed the known distillation thresholds.

Since thermal operations are axially symmetric around the Hamiltonian axis
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
, the phase of the coherence can always be aligned with any given pure target direction
\boldsymbol{u}
on the Bloch sphere. Let
u_{z}=|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}.\boldsymbol{u}|
and
u_{\perp}=\sqrt{1-u^{2}_{z}}
be the decomposition of
\boldsymbol{u}
into energy-axis and transverse components in the rotated frame. For a state with a population
q
(so
z^{\prime}=(2q-1)
) and coherence amplitude
r\leq\mathord{\text{\magic c}}_{\max}
, the maximal overlap with
\boldsymbol{u}
occurs when their phases coincide, giving

\max_{\text{phase}}\boldsymbol{r}.\boldsymbol{u}=ru_{\perp}+|2q-1|u_{z}.

(37)

The corresponding state fidelity with the pure state
\ket{u}
is
F(\rho,\ket{u})=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\boldsymbol{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{u})
, so the maximal achievable fidelity with that direction reads

F^{\star}(\beta,\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1+\max_{q \in I_\beta(p)} (\mtg{c}_{\max}(q) u_\perp+ |2q-1| u_z)].

(38)

This expression is the fidelity analogue of the support-function bound appearing in Theorem
5
.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{T}
and
\mathcal{O}_{H}
denote the Clifford orbits of the magic states
\ket{\ms{T}}
and
\ket{\ms{H}}
, respectively. Each orbit consists of finitely many Bloch directions
\boldsymbol{u}
related by signed permutations of
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,1,1)
(for
T
) or
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,0,1)
(for
H
). The best fidelity attainable at temperature
\beta
is obtained by maximising Eq. (
38
) over the corresponding orbit,

F^{\star}_{\mathcal{O}}(\beta):=\max_{u\in\mathcal{O}_{o}}F^{\star}(\beta,\boldsymbol{u})\quad\text{with}\quad o\in\{\textsf{T},\textsf{H}\}.

(39)

Universal quantum computation is achievable whenever this fidelity exceeds the known distillation thresholds
F^{\star}_{T}(\beta)\geq f_{\text{thr}}^{(\textsf{T})}\approx 0.91
and
F^{\star}_{H}(\beta)\geq f_{\text{thr}}^{(\textsf{H})}\approx 0.93
. The smallest inverse temperature satisfying the inequality defines the distillability bound
\beta_{\text{dist}}^{(o)}
for the orbit
o
:

\beta_{\text{dist}}^{(o)}:=\text{inf}\quantity{\beta\geq 0 : F^{\star}_o(\beta) \geq f_{\text{thr}}^{(o)}
}.

(40)

Since
I_{\beta}(p)
and
\mathord{\text{\magic c}}_{\max}(q)
are monotonically with
\beta
, the map
\beta\mapsto F^{\star}_{o}(\beta)
is monotonically increasing, so
\beta_{\text{dist}}^{(o)}
can be obtained by a simple one-dimensional root-finding procedure.

For energy-incoherent states, the coherence term vanishes and Eq. (
38
) simplifies to

F^{\star}(\beta,\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(1+\max_{q \in I_\beta(p)} |2q-1| \max_{\v u \in\mathcal{O}_o} |\hat{\v{n}}.\v u|),

(41)

where the geometric factors are

\max_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{O}_{T}}|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}.\boldsymbol{u}|=\frac{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{1}}{\sqrt{3}}\quad,\quad\max_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{O}_{H}}|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}.\boldsymbol{u}|=\frac{|n|_{(1)}+|n|_{(2)}}{\sqrt{2}},

(42)

with
|n|_{(1)}\geq|n|_{(1)}|n|_{(3)}
the ordered components of
\boldsymbol{n}
. When
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
is Pauli-aligned, the maximising directions coincide with the coordinate axes, and the extremum over
q
usually occurs at interval endpoints
q=p
or
q=q^{\star}
. Finally, for the optimal Hamiltonian
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\propto(1,1,1)
, the relevant
T
-orbit direction has
u_{z}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
and
u_{\perp}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}
, giving the expression:

F_{\textsf{T}}^{\star}(\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1+\max_{q\in I_\beta(p)}\qty(\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\mtg{c}_{\max} (q) + \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}|2q-1|)]

(43)

from which
\beta_{\text{dist}}^{(\textsf{T})}
follows immediately by solving
F^{\star}_{\textsf{T}}\quantity(\beta_{\text{dist}}^{(T)})=0.91
.

Appendix D
Optimal Hamiltonian

To find the Hamiltonian that maximises the generation of magic under thermal operations, it suffices to analyse the distinguishability of the output state
\mathcal{E}(\rho)
of a thermal operation from the set of stabiliser states. This naturally leads to the optimisation problem

\max_{H,\|H\|=1}\min_{\sigma\in\textrm{stab}}\|\mathcal{E}(\rho)-\sigma\|_{1}

(44)

Without loss of generality, we consider the Hamiltonian
H=\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, with
\norm{H}=1
. Let
\mathcal{E}
denote a thermal operation and let
\rho
be the initial state of the system, assumed to be energy-incoherent in the eigenbasis of
H
described by populations
(p,1-p)
. The final state after a thermal operation is
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbbm{1}+\boldsymbol{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})
, where
\boldsymbol{r}=m\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
, with no transverse components (i.e., no coherence). For a qubit, the thermomajorisation bound on the extreme reachable ground-state population, starting from an initial diagonal state
\rho
with ground-state population
p
, is
q^{\star}=1-pe^{-\beta}
. Hence, the largest achievable Bloch vector length along the direction
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
is
m=|2q^{\star}-1|=|1-2pe^{-\beta}|
. Crucially,
m
depends only on
(p,\beta)
and the fixed spectral norm of
H
rather than on the orientation
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
.

The stabiliser polytope is the octahedron
\{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbbm{R}^{3}:\|\boldsymbol{s}\|_{1}\equiv|s_{x}|+|s_{y}|+|s_{z}|\leq 1\}
. For qubits, the trace distance between states coincides with the Euclidean distance between their Bloch vectors
\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}=\|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{s}\|_{2}
. Consequently, the optimisation problem in Eq. (
44
) reduces to evaluating:

V(m)=\max_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}:\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{2}=1}\min_{\|\boldsymbol{s}\|_{1}\leq 1}\|m\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}-\boldsymbol{s}\|_{2},

(45)

where
m\geq 0
and the vectors lie in
\mathbbm{R}^{3}
. Since the trace norm for qubits equals the Euclidean distance between their Bloch vectors, our optimisation becomes purely geometric: we seek the unit direction
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
that maximises the Euclidean distance from the point
m\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
to the
\ell_{1}
-ball:
B_{1}:=\{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbbm{R}^{3}:\|\boldsymbol{s}\|_{1}\leq 1\}
. Thus,

f(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}):=\min_{\|\boldsymbol{s}\|_{1}\leq 1}\|m\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}-\boldsymbol{s}\|_{2}=\operatorname{dist}(m\hat{\boldsymbol{n}},B_{1}),

(46)

where
\operatorname{dist}(\boldsymbol{x},C)
denotes the Euclidean distance from
x
to
C
. Now, we maximise
f(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
over all unit vectors
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
. To this end, we reformulate the distance via convex duality. For any closed convex set
C
containing the origin the following holds

\operatorname{dist}(\boldsymbol{x},C)=\max_{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}\leq 1}\quantity(\v y^{\ms T} \v x - \underset{\v z \in C}{\operatorname{sup}\v y^{\ms T}} \v z).

(47)

For
C=B_{1}
, we obtain
\operatorname{sup}_{\boldsymbol{z}\in B_{1}}\boldsymbol{y}^{\textsf{T}}\boldsymbol{z}=\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}
. Consequently,
f(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=\max_{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}\leq 1}(m\boldsymbol{y}^{\textsf{T}}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}-\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty})
. We now turn to the computation of the function
V(m)
:

\displaystyle V(m)=\max_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}:\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{2}=1}f(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=\max_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}:\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{2}=1}\max_{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}\leq 1}(m\boldsymbol{y}^{\textsf{T}}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}-\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty})=\max_{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}\leq 1}\max_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}:\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{2}=1}(m\boldsymbol{y}^{\textsf{T}}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}-\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}).

(48)

We may interchange the order of maximisation over
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
because both feasible sets are compact and the objective is continuous and bilinear in
(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}},\boldsymbol{y})
. This lets us first solve
\max_{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{2}=1}\boldsymbol{y}^{\textsf{T}}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}
for fixed
\boldsymbol{y}
. Let
t=\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}\in[0,1]
, for any
\boldsymbol{y}
with
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}=t
, it follows that
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}\geq\tfrac{t}{\sqrt{3}}
. Indeed, if all
|y_{i}|<t/\sqrt{3}
, then
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}<t
, a contradiction. Hence,
m\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}-\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}\leq t(m-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}})
. Therefore,
V(m)\leq\max_{0\leq t\leq 1}t\quantity(m-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}})
. This leads to two cases. If
m\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
, the maximum is
0
, attained at
t=0
. Taking
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
shows that
V(m)\geq 0
, and hence,
V(m)=0
. In the second case, when
m\geq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
, the maximum is attained at
t=1
, giving
V(m)\leq m-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
. Choosing
\boldsymbol{y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,1,1)
yields
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}=1
and
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
, which shows that
V(m)=m-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
. Combining the two cases, we obtain:

\displaystyle V(m)=\begin{cases}0\quad\quad&\text{if}\quad m\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\\
m-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\quad&\text{if}\quad m>\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\end{cases}

(49)

From the equality above (attained at
\boldsymbol{y}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,1,1)
), the inner maximisation
\max_{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{2}=1}\boldsymbol{y}^{\textsf{T}}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
is achieved by choosing
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
parallel to
\boldsymbol{y}
. Hence, an optimal axis is

\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\star}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,1,1)
and, by the symmetry of the
\ell_{1}
-ball, any sign pattern
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\star}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\pm 1,\pm 1,\pm 1)
is also optimal. Consequently, the optimal Hamiltonian is

H_{\star}=\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\star}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\frac{X+Y+Z}{\sqrt{3}}.

(50)

Appendix E
Remarks on catalytic extension

We briefly outline how our framework extends to catalytic scenarios, providing a high-level overview rather than detailed analysis. The main text establishes a necessary-and-sufficient condition for magic state generation under thermal operations (TO), together with the future thermal cone of magic
\mathcal{T}_{+}^{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}}(\rho)
and its normalised volume
\mathcal{V}_{+}^{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}}(\rho)
. This appendix presents the catalytic extensions within the same geometric framework. We write
F_{\beta}(\rho)=\tr(\rho H)-\beta^{-1}S(\rho)
for the nonequilibrium free energy and
C(\rho)
for the set of resonant coherent modes. As shown in
42
, convertibility under correlated-catalytic thermal operations is governed by the free-energy order together with the mode-inclusion condition
C(\rho^{\prime})\subseteq C(\rho)
.

We define the catalytic analogue of the magic future cone as

\mathcal{T}^{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}}_{+,\mathrm{cat}}(\rho):=\Bigl\{\sigma\notin\textrm{stab}:F_{\beta}(\sigma)\leq F_{\beta}(\rho)\ \text{and}\ C(\sigma)\subseteq C(\rho)\Bigr\}.

(51)

When
C(\rho)\neq\{0\}
, we have the strict inclusion
\mathcal{T}^{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}}_{+}(\rho)\subsetneq\mathcal{T}^{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}}_{+,\mathrm{cat}}(\rho)
. This stems from the fact that the catalytic setting removes constraints such as the coherence cap
r\leq\mathord{\text{\magic c}}_{\max}(q)
present in the noncatalytic construction. Consequently, the associated volume strictly increases:

\mathcal{V}^{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}}_{+,\mathrm{cat}}(\rho)>\mathcal{V}^{\mathord{\text{\magic M}}}_{+}(\rho).

(52)

In analogy with Eq. (
7
), we define the catalytic critical inverse temperature by

\beta_{\mathrm{crt}}^{\mathrm{cat}}(\rho,H):=\inf\Bigl\{\beta\geq 0:\ \exists\ \sigma\notin\textrm{stab}\ \text{with}\ F_{\beta}(\sigma)\leq F_{\beta}(\rho),\ C(\sigma)\subseteq C(\rho)\Bigr\}.

(53)

Since correlated catalysts extend the set of reachable states,
\beta_{\mathrm{crt}}^{\mathrm{cat}}(\rho,H)\leq\beta_{\mathrm{crt}}(\rho,H)
, with equality only when the input state
\rho
is energy-incoherent. For target states
\sigma
diagonal in the energy basis of
H
, (
53
) reduces to a one-variable root in the classical order
F_{\beta}(\sigma_{z})\leq F_{\beta}(\rho)
. Finally, in the catalytic regime, the population–coherence “budget”
\bigl[I_{\beta}(p),\mathord{\text{\magic c}}_{\max}(q)\bigr]
is superseded by a single free-energy inequality together with
C(\sigma)\subseteq C(\rho)
. Thus, all temperature thresholds are determined solely by free-energy comparisons.
