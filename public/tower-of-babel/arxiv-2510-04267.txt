Turning Down the Noise: Power-Law Decay and Temporal Phase Transitions
Lieuwe Bakker, Suvendu Barik, Vladimir Gritsev, Emil A. Yuzbashyan

Abstract
We determine the late-time dynamics of a generic spin ensemble with inhomogeneous broadening—equivalently, qubits with arbitrary Zeeman splittings—coupled to a dissipative environment with strength decreasing as 1 / t 1/t . The approach to the steady state follows a power law, reflecting the interplay between Hamiltonian dynamics and vanishing dissipation. The decay exponents vary non-analytically with the ramp rate, exhibiting a cusp singularity, and n n -point correlation functions factorize into one- and two-point contributions. Our exact solution anchors a universality class of open quantum systems with explicitly time-dependent dissipation.



Introduction
—External noise from environment, though a nuisance in ideal experiments, is unavoidable in practice. Yet, it is precisely the interplay between drive and dissipation that often gives rise to novel phenomena. The dynamics of open quantum systems are typically described by the Lindblad master equation (ME)
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
, and extensive studies of such systems have revealed dissipative analogues of equilibrium physics, ranging from modified phase diagrams
Ates et al. 2012
;
Lee et al. 2013
;
Marino 2016
to topological classifications
Lieu et al. 2020
;
Kawabata et al. 2024
. In static settings, these systems can be understood through concepts such as dissipative gap closures, which provide a natural framework for exploring and classifying universality classes
Sieberer et al. 2025
.

By contrast, explicitly time-dependent open systems remain virtually uncharted. This is the case, even though experimental control and probing are inherently dynamical, and external drives can generate novel phases as in Floquet systems
Gritsev and Polkovnikov 2017
;
Scopa et al. 2019
. Yet no framework exists for classifying phases or universality in this broader setting. Previous attempts
Sarandy and Lidar 2005
;
Paulino et al. 2025
have been limited, and what is missing is both a diagnostic principle and a definitive reference solution—an exactly solvable model that can serve as a foundation for theoretical and experimental classification. To date, dynamical open systems have lacked such a paradigmatic foundation.

In static open systems, the dissipative gap serves as a diagnostic: its closure signals qualitative changes in long-time behavior. Time-dependent drives, however, preclude such a definition, since no proper notion of a dissipative gap exists in that setting. Correlation functions, by contrast, remain well defined and capture how observables relax toward the steady state
Marino 2016
. Their long-time behavior thus provides a natural analogue of the dissipative gap for explicitly time-dependent systems. While this addresses the diagnostic principle, the absence of an exact benchmark solution remains a central obstacle.

In this paper we present an exact solution for the long-time correlation functions of a generic, archetypal spin ensemble with inhomogeneous broadening—equivalently, a collection of qubits (two-level systems) with an arbitrary distribution of Zeeman splittings—coupled to a dissipative environment whose strength varies explicitly in time, Fig.
1
. This is the standard setting of magnetic resonance, where spins precess with distinct Larmor frequencies due to chemical shifts, Knight shifts, or field gradients
Vathyam et al. 1996
;
Bennett et al. 1970
. Related realizations occur in ensembles of NV centers in diamond
Rosenzweig et al. 2018
, semiconductor quantum dots
Burkard et al. 2023
, trapped ions and cold atoms in magnetic-field gradients
Warring et al. 2013
;
Apellaniz et al. 2018
, and spin ensembles in cavity QED
Kurucz et al. 2011
;
Putz et al. 2014
.

We show that relaxation to the steady state follows universal power laws whose exponents change across parameter regimes. These changes define temporal phase transitions: nonanalytic variations in correlation-time scaling, directly analogous to spatial phase transitions governed by correlation lengths. Moreover, the power laws factorize, yielding relevant long-time contributions for all observables. This provides a paradigmatic reference for universality in open quantum systems with time-dependent dissipation, analogous to the role of Onsager’s solution of the two-dimensional Ising model in equilibrium statistical
physics
Jaeger 1998
.

The system of interest is described by a Lindblad equation (setting
\hbar=1

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=-i\left[\hat{H},\rho\right]+\sum_{\alpha}\hat{D}_{a}[\rho],

(1)

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{i}(2\varepsilon_{i})\,\hat{s}^{z}_{i},\quad\hat{D}_{a}[\rho]=\hat{L}_{a}\rho\hat{L}_{a}^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{a},\rho\},

(2)

where
\hat{L}_{a}=\sqrt{g_{a}}\sum_{i}\hat{s}_{i}^{a}
with

and
\hat{s}^{a}
the spin-
1/2

The explicit time-dependence is introduced by making the couplings to the environment decrease as
g_{\pm}(t)=1/(\nu t)
. The operators
\hat{L}_{z}
generate collective dephasing, while
\hat{L}_{\pm}

Although the Hamiltonian (

Exact solutions to nontrivial time-dependent dissipative models such as (
1
)–(
2
) are rarely available, making the present results a unique window into their behavior. The steady state of (
1
) is maximally mixed and therefore relatively uninteresting, a feature shared with other models involving only Hermitian jump operators
\hat{L}_{a}

Ribeiro and Prosen 2019
. The dynamics leading to the steady state, however, are highly nontrivial. For example, the time-independent version of (
2
) exhibits a dissipative phase transition in which relaxation switches from purely exponential to oscillatory exponential decay
Claeys and Lamacraft 2022
. By contrast, the time-dependent case studied here uncovers two qualitatively new types of phenomena.

First, the system approaches the steady state via power-law relaxation, in contrast to the exponential decay typical of dissipative systems with no explicit time dependence. After transient dynamics, spin–spin correlations such as
\langle\hat{s}^{z}_{i}\hat{s}^{z}_{j}\rangle
, along with all higher-order functions, decay as power laws with exponents that we determine exactly. Second, the dependence of these exponents on the ramp rate
\nu
is non-analytic at
\nu=2
, exhibiting a cusp singularity. This follows from the exact solution of the coupled differential equations governing the correlation functions
Barik et al. 2025a
. As noted in the introduction, we refer to this non-analytic behavior as a temporal phase transition.

The emergence of power-law decay in the correlation functions is not accidental but follows from the connection between the non-Hermitian RG Hamiltonian and the Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov (KZ) equations
Knizhnik and Zamolodchikov 1984
used in our derivation. The KZ equations themselves arise in two-dimensional conformal field theory (CFT), where power laws are the hallmark of RG fixed points. More broadly, the

1/t
turn-off of dissipation endows the dynamics with an emergent temporal scale invariance and—conjecturally—places our protocol at a dynamical fixed point (or line) to which a wider class of driven–dissipative systems may flow, thereby strengthening the CFT connection. Extracting the exponents in this driven–dissipative setting requires a nontrivial calculation and represents a rare result in the study of
dynamical
open quantum systems.

Temporal phase transition
—For

Several observations are made at this stage. First, the discrepancy between the long-time behavior obtained from the exact solution and from the general asymptotic result does not arise in the Hermitian RG Hamiltonian, i.e., when the identification (
9
) is not made. The temporal phase transition thus originates from allowing solutions of the Schrödinger equation that are only valid in the open-system setting. In this sense, it is a purely dissipative phenomenon.
Second, one may ask whether similar, or even richer, behavior occurs in higher-order correlation functions such as
c_{zzz}
. Numerical investigations, however, indicate that no additional temporal transitions occur, at least up to six-point correlation functions.

Finally, Eq. (
12

Conclusions
—We have derived the late-time dynamics of a generic spin ensemble with inhomogeneous broadening—equivalently, qubits with arbitrary Zeeman splittings—coupled to a dissipative environment whose strength decreases as
1/t
. This provides a rare exact solution of an open quantum system with explicitly time-dependent couplings. Exact expressions for multi-point correlation functions were obtained, fully characterizing the approach to the steady state. In contrast to the exponential relaxation typical of static dissipative systems, our model exhibits power-law decay, arising from the competition between coherent Hamiltonian dynamics and progressively weakening dissipation.

Furthermore,
n
-point spin correlations display a temporal (dissipative) phase transition as a function of the ramp rate
\nu
, and the decay exponents are factorizable into one- and two-point contributions. Altogether, these results provide an exact and universal benchmark for open quantum systems with time-dependent dissipation, offering a foundation for the broader classification of dynamical, dissipative phases of matter.

Appendix B
Mapping to non-Hermitian RG Hamiltonian

In this Appendix we revisit the derivation by Rowlands and Lamacraft
Rowlands and Lamacraft 2018
of the mapping between the driven-dissipative spin system and the non-Hermitian Richardson–Gaudin (RG) Hamiltonian. We provide additional details to clarify the correspondence between correlation functions of the dissipative spins and the basis states of the RG Hamiltonian, as summarized in the main text. In doing so, we also correct a minor inconsistency in the original formulation, which is essential for establishing the precise mapping of the basis states.

The model under consideration is a system of
N
spin-
1/2
particles, described by the Lindbladian

\partial_{t}\rho=-i\left[H,\rho\right]+\sum_{\alpha}D_{\alpha}[\rho],

(17)

where

H=\sum_{j}^{N}\left(2\varepsilon_{j}\right)\hat{s}^{z}_{j},~~D_{\alpha}[\rho]=\hat{L}_{\alpha}\rho\hat{L}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{L}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{\alpha},\rho\right\},

(18)

and
\hat{L}_{\alpha}=\sqrt{g_{\alpha}}\sum_{j}^{N}\hat{s}_{j}^{\alpha}
with
\alpha=\{+,-\}
. For simplicity, we have already put
g_{z}=0
, as compared to the main text. As mentioned in the main text, the onsite energies
\varepsilon_{j}
determine the precession frequency of the individual spins, and
g_{\alpha}
is the coupling strength to the environment. Besides the rigorous study by Rowlands and Lamacraft
Rowlands and Lamacraft 2018
, there are also related studies by Rubio-García et. al.
Rubio-García et al. 2022a
;
Rubio-García et al. 2022b
and simultaneously by Claeys and Lamacraft
Claeys and Lamacraft 2022
.

B.1
Algebraic mapping

First, we decompose the density matrix
\rho
into a ‘convex combination of spherical tensors’
Fano 1957
, which for spin-
1/2
systems reads

\rho^{(N)}=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{\{a_{j}\}}c_{a_{1},\dots,a_{N}}\sigma_{1}^{a_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\sigma_{N}^{a_{N}}.

(19)

Here,
\sigma_{j}^{a_{j}}
denote the
Pauli
matrices with site label
j
with
a_{j}=\{0,x,y,z\}_{j}
. The factor of
\frac{1}{2^{N}}
essentially upgrades the matrices to the usual spin matrices, ensuring proper normalization. For example, to have unit trace,
c_{0\dots 0}=1
. Also note here the small difference as compared to the main text: here, we include all correlation functions and do not limit the correlator notation to only include non-zero elements. Hence there is no additional
i
label indicating which set of correlation functions is considered. The reduction to only nonzero elements will come later in this Appendix.

Importantly, the coefficients

are the correlation functions of the spins

c_{a_{1},\dots,a_{N}}=\text{tr}\left[\rho^{(N)}\sigma_{1}^{a_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\sigma_{N}^{a_{N}}\right].

(20)

Following
Rowlands and Lamacraft 2018

g_{+}=g_{-}
, the dissipators in (

2
missing in

\hat{L}_{\pm}=\sqrt{g_{+}}\sum_{j}\hat{s}_{j}^{\pm}\rightarrow\hat{L}_{x,y}=\sqrt{2g_{+}}\sum_{j}\hat{s}_{j}^{x,y}.

(21)

Using direct substitution of (
19
) in the Lindblad equation (
17
), the problem reduces to finding the equations of motion (EOM) for the correlation functions (
20
) after multiplying with the tensors
\hat{s}_{1}^{a_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\hat{s}_{N}^{a_{N}}
and tracing over the resulting object. Consider the action of the dissipator on the correlation functions (using
\hat{s}^{\bf{a}}=\hat{s}_{1}^{a_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\hat{s}_{N}^{a^{N}}
as a shorthand and dropping the hat-notation to avoid clutter):

2g_{+}\sum_{j,k}^{N}\text{tr}\left\{\left[s_{k}^{\alpha}\rho s_{j}^{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\left(s_{k}^{\alpha}s_{j}^{\alpha}\rho+\rho s_{k}^{\alpha}s_{j}^{\alpha}\right)\right]s^{\bf{a}}\right\}.

(22)

Next, we sum over all possible configurations of
\bf{a}
contained in the set
\{\bf{a}\}
and use the cyclicity of the trace operation, as well as the symmetry

, to write (

) as

\displaystyle\frac{2g_{+}}{2}\sum_{\{\bf{a}\}}\sum_{j,k}^{N}\text{tr}\Bigg\{\rho\Bigg[

\displaystyle s_{k}^{\alpha}(s^{\bf{a}})s_{j}^{\alpha}+s_{j}^{\alpha}(s^{\bf{a}})s_{k}^{\alpha}

(23)

\displaystyle-s_{k}^{\alpha}s_{j}^{\alpha}(s^{\bf{a}})-(s^{\bf{a}})s_{k}^{\alpha}s_{j}^{\alpha}\Bigg]\Bigg\}.

The dynamics are thus generated by quartic terms. The following identity is used:

\begin{gathered}s_{j}^{\alpha}s_{j}^{a_{j}}s_{k}^{a_{k}}s_{k}^{\alpha}+s_{k}^{\alpha}s_{j}^{a_{j}}s_{k}^{a_{k}}s_{j}^{\alpha}\\
-s_{j}^{a_{j}}s_{k}^{a_{k}}s_{j}^{\alpha}s_{k}^{\alpha}-s_{j}^{\alpha}s_{k}^{\alpha}s_{j}^{a_{j}}s_{k}^{a_{k}}\\
=-\left[s_{j}^{\alpha},s_{j}^{a_{j}}\right]\left[s_{k}^{\alpha},s_{k}^{a_{k}}\right]=\sum_{b}\epsilon^{\alpha a_{j}}_{~~~~b}\,s_{j}^{b}\sum_{c}\epsilon^{\alpha a_{k}}_{~~~~c}\,s_{k}^{c}.\end{gathered}

(24)

, in each
\mathbf{a}

\mathbf{a}

The last equality can be further evaluated by performing the sum over

as matrices. These matrices are of course the

\centering\begin{gathered}T^{x}_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\

Here,

The part of the EOMs generated by the Hermitian part of the master equation (

\begin{gathered}-2i\sum_{\{\mathbf{a}\}}\sum_{j}^{N}\varepsilon_{j}\text{tr}\left[\left(s^{z}_{j}\rho-\rho s^{z}_{j}\right)s^{\bf{a}}\right]\\

Again, using the commutation relations, one needs only to consider terms like

B.2
Mapping through vectorization

The mapping described in Subsection
B.1
is one way to show the identification between a system of
N
dissipative spin-
1/2
particles and a
n
-site spin-
1
Richardson-Gaudin model. However, it may not be quite as straightforward as one would like. A similar mapping, has also been studied in
Rubio-García et al. 2022a
and
Rubio-García et al. 2022b
. Here, the mapping was obtained by directly vectorizing the system of
N
dissipative spins, and, after block-diagonalization and Clebsch-Gordan decompositions, identifying each block with an
n
-site non-Hermitian RG model. Of course, both methods yield identical results. Therefore this section is merely meant to explain the methodology behind the vectorization approach for completeness.

Through direct vectorization, defined by (with
A
,
B
and
\rho
being matrices that can multiply according to usual matrix multiplication)
\text{vec}(A\rho B)\equiv A\otimes B^{T}\left|\rho\middle>\!\right>
where
\left|\rho\middle>\!\right>
is a vectorized density matrix and
A\otimes B^{T}
is the vectorized representation of the Lindblad superoperator, Eq. (
17
) is mapped directly to the RG Hamiltonian.
Explicitly, consider the following identification

\displaystyle\hat{s}^{a}\rho

\displaystyle\rightarrow\hat{s}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{I}\left|\rho\middle>\!\right>\equiv\hat{K}^{a}\left|\rho\middle>\!\right>,

(39)

\displaystyle\rho\hat{s}^{a}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{I}\otimes(\hat{s}^{a})^{T}\left|\rho\middle>\!\right>\equiv\hat{Q}^{a}\left|\rho\middle>\!\right>.

Using (
39
), and performing a unitary transformation (
O\rightarrow\sigma^{y}O\sigma^{y}
) on the operators
\hat{Q}
, the vectorized superoperators
\text{vec}(H)
and

) are as follows

\displaystyle\text{vec}(H)=

\displaystyle-2i\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\varepsilon_{j})(\hat{K}_{j}^{z}+\hat{Q}_{j}^{z}),

(40)

\displaystyle\text{vec}(D_{\pm})=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j,k}^{N}g_{\pm}\left[\hat{K}_{j}^{\pm}\hat{Q}_{k}^{\mp}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{K}_{j}^{\mp}\hat{K}_{k}^{\pm}+\hat{Q}_{j}^{\pm}\hat{Q}_{k}^{\mp}\right)\right].

Again, it is at this point that
g_{+}=g_{-}
. Introducing a total spin operator
\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{a}_{i}=\hat{K}^{a}_{i}+\hat{Q}^{a}_{i}
, the following simplified form of the vectorized Lindblad operator is written as

\begin{gathered}-2i\sum_{j}^{N}\left(\varepsilon_{j}\right)\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{j}^{z}-\frac{g_{+}}{2}\sum_{j,k}^{N}\left(\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{j}^{+}\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{-}+\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{j}^{-}\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{k}^{+}\right)\\
\equiv\tilde{H}^{1/2\otimes 1/2}_{\text{RG}}.\end{gathered}

(41)

Note here that the operators
\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{a}
are elements of a
\mathfrak{su}(2)\times\mathfrak{su}(2)

\tilde{H}^{1/2\otimes 1/2}_{\text{RG}}
, denoting the identified RG Hamiltonian. This means that the space of states on which each
\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{a}_{j}
acts can be decomposed into a combination of spin-
1
triplets and spin-
0
singlets. It is this step that generates the spin-
1
non-Hermitian RG Hamiltonian. The basis transformation is given by the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients, but there is a subtlety that we have to address first. As a result of the choice of the definition of
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{a}

1

0

\rho

\rho=\left|v_{1}\right>\!\left<w_{1}\right|\otimes\dots\otimes\left|v_{N}\right>\!\left<w_{N}\right|.

(42)

Here, each
\left|v\right>
and
\left|w\right>
is either
\left|\uparrow\right>
or
\left|\downarrow\right>
. Upon vectorization and rotation of the right eigenstates with
\sigma^{y}
we have

\left|\rho\middle>\!\right>=\left|v_{1}\right>\otimes\dots\otimes\left|v_{N}\right>\otimes\sigma^{y}\left|w_{1}\right>\otimes\dots\sigma^{y}\left|w_{N}\right>.

(43)

At this stage we should gather the
\left|v_{i}\right>
and
\left|w_{j}\right>
for

, by performing a permutation on the basis states. This permutation operation is referred to as

, yielding

T\left|\rho\middle>\!\right>=\left|v_{1}\right>\otimes\sigma_{y}\left|w_{1}\right>\otimes\dots\otimes\left|v_{N}\right>\otimes\sigma_{y}\left|w_{N}\right>.

(44)

Eq. (
44
) provides the correct basis state ordering for us to proceed with the decomposition of the spin-
1/2
pairs. The required transformation
U
is written here to ensure that ensures that the right basis states are mapped in an intuitive manner (see Eqs. (
48
) and (
49

U_{\text{CG}}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\begin{pmatrix}\,1&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\,\\
\cdot&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&1\\
\cdot&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}&\cdot\end{pmatrix}_{j},

(45)

where every dot represents
0
for brevity. Since at every site

in (
41
) we decompose the two spins into triplet and singlet states, the resulting wavefunctions is composed out of a product of the sums
(1\oplus 0)_{1}\otimes(1\oplus 0)_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes(1\oplus 0)_{N}
. Multiplying out this product, and noting that the operator (
41
) acts trivially on the singlet state, the resulting Liouvillian decomposes into a direct sum of non-Hermitian RG Hamiltonians, the number of sites of which is labeled by

Thus, this procedure ultimately yields a block-diagonal operator

UT\tilde{H}^{1/2\otimes 1/2}_{\text{RG}}T^{-1}U^{-1}=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{N}\tilde{H}^{1\oplus 0}_{n,\text{RG}}.

(46)

The transformation

\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{a}
into
\hat{S}^{a}
.

\displaystyle+\alpha^{*}\beta\left|\downarrow\right>\!\left<\uparrow\right|+\left|\beta\right|^{2}\left|\downarrow\right>\!\left<\downarrow\right|,

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left(0,0,0,1\right)^{T}\equiv\left|s\right>.

At this point we can thus identify the translation of the basis states from the Lindbladian picture to the non-Hermitian RG Hamiltonian picture as

\displaystyle\left|s\right>_{j}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow i\sqrt{2}(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}+\left|\beta\right|^{2})\sigma^{0}_{j},\;

\displaystyle\left|-1\right>_{j}\leftrightarrow i\alpha^{*}\beta\sigma_{j}^{-},

(50)

\displaystyle\left|0\right>_{j}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow i\sqrt{2}(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}-\left|\beta\right|^{2})\sigma_{j}^{z},\;

\displaystyle\left|+1\right>_{j}\leftrightarrow-i\alpha\beta^{*}\sigma_{j}^{+},

where we added the site labels

Performing the

are (with the first five written explicitly)

\displaystyle\left|\rho\middle>\!\right>=

\displaystyle-\rho^{1,4}\left|\uparrow_{1}\uparrow_{2}\right>\!\left|\uparrow_{\tilde{1}}\uparrow_{\tilde{2}}\right>+\rho^{1,3}\left|\uparrow_{1}\uparrow_{2}\right>\!\left|\uparrow_{\tilde{1}}\downarrow_{\tilde{2}}\right>

Upon performing the permutation

\displaystyle\left|\rho\middle>\!\right>=

\displaystyle-\rho^{1,4}\left|\uparrow_{1}\uparrow_{2}\right>\!\left|\uparrow_{\tilde{1}}\uparrow_{\tilde{2}}\right>+\rho^{1,3}\left|\uparrow_{1}\uparrow_{2}\right>\!\left|\uparrow_{\tilde{1}}\downarrow_{\tilde{2}}\right>

\displaystyle-\rho^{2,3}\left|\uparrow_{1}\uparrow_{2}\right>\!\left|\downarrow_{\tilde{1}}\uparrow_{\tilde{2}}\right>

In this notation, we find, for example, that the basis states

Appendix C
Asymptotic solution of a non Hermitian, spin-
1
Richardson-Gaudin Hamiltonian

In this Appendix, we derive the asymptotic solution to the time-dependent, with
g(t)=1/\nu t
, spin-
1
Richardson-Gaudin Hamiltonian expressed in the form of (
37
). For an in-depth discussion on this calculation, we refer to
Barik et al. 2025b
. Here we only present the mathematical derivation, ultimately leading to the desired form of
\gamma_{N_{1}}
as used in the main text.

The formal solution
\left|\Psi^{(s,N_{+})}(t)\right>
to the time-dependent RG Hamiltonian (
37
) (up to normalization) with arbitrary spin-
s
is given by a
N_{+}
-fold contour integral over variables
\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{N_{+}}
of what is known as the Yang-Yang action. The number
N_{+}
refers to the total number of raising operations applied to the vacuum state
\left|-s,-s,\dots,-s\right>\equiv\left|\odot\right>
, and is related to the magnetization of the spin-chain via
J^{z}=N_{+}-ns
where
n
is the number of sites in the RG Hamiltonian. Thus, the formal solution is presented for each magnetization sector (identified with the
N_{+}
-label):

\left|\Psi^{(s,N_{+})}(t)\right>=\oint_{\chi}d\bm{\lambda}\exp\left(-\frac{i\mathcal{Y}_{s}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\varepsilon},t)}{\nu}\right)\Xi(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\varepsilon}).

(56)

Here,
\bm{\varepsilon}=\left(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}\right)
with
\varepsilon_{j}<\varepsilon_{j+1}
,
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N_{+}})
,
d\bm{\lambda}=d\lambda_{1}\dots d\lambda_{N_{+}}
and

\begin{gathered}\Xi(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\varepsilon})=\prod_{\lambda_{r}\in\bm{\lambda}}\hat{L}^{+}\left(\lambda_{r}\right)\left|\odot\right>,\\
\hat{L}^{+}(\lambda_{r})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\hat{s}_{j}^{+}}{\lambda_{r}-\varepsilon_{j}}.\end{gathered}

(57)

The quantity
\mathcal{Y}_{s}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\varepsilon},t)
is known as the Yang-Yang action which is derived from the off-shell Bethe ansatz equations and reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\varepsilon},t)=2\nu t

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}+2s\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha}\ln\left(\varepsilon_{j}-\lambda_{\alpha}\right)

(58)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\ln\left(\lambda_{\beta}-\lambda_{\alpha}\right).

For the rest of this Appendix,
s=1
, and the spin-label in
\left|\Psi^{(s,N_{+})}(t)\right>
is dropped. Furthermore, we return to the use of
\hat{S}^{a}
to indicate the spin-
1
operators

The formal solution (
56
) is a rather complicated object, and exact solutions in terms of special functions rather than integrals are rare
Barik et al. 2025a
. However, the long-time limit of the asymptotic wave function
\left|\Psi_{\infty}^{(N_{+})}(t)\right>
can be computed by means of a saddle-point approximation
Fedoryuk 1989
. In this limit, the integral localizes at the stationary points of the Yang-Yang action, whose equations are the well-known Richardson-Gaudin
Richardson 1963
;
Richardson and Sherman 1964
;
Richardson 1977
equations
\mathcal{Y^{\prime}}=\partial\mathcal{Y}/\partial\lambda_{p}=0
. Explicitly, the equations are

\nu t+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{p}-\varepsilon_{j}}=\sum_{j\neq p}\frac{1}{\lambda_{p}-\lambda_{j}},\;p=1,\dots,N_{+}.

(59)

We proceed by making an assumption for the form of the solution to the equations in the
t\rightarrow\infty
limit. For details on the motivation for this ansatz, we once again refer to
Yuzbashyan 2018
;
Barik et al. 2025b
. In short, this ansatz satisfies the Richardson equations (
59
) in the long time limit, which is exactly the regime that aim to analyze. The ansatz takes the form

\lambda_{p}=\varepsilon_{p}+\frac{z_{p}}{t}.

(60)

Using Eq. (
60
), equation (
59
) can be solved for
z_{p}
in the large time limit. Note that
\lambda_{p}
corresponds to a raising operation on site
p
of the vacuum state
\left|\odot\right>
through the action of
L^{+}(\lambda_{p})
, where the site index is determined from the
\varepsilon_{p}
that it approaches. Since we consider a spin-
1
RG Hamiltonian, at most two raising operations (
\left|-1\right>\rightarrow\left|0\right>,\;\left|0\right>\rightarrow\left|1\right>
) are possible at each site, meaning that at
t\rightarrow\infty
, up to two different
\lambda_{p}
converge on identical
\varepsilon_{p}
. Thus, for the spin-
1
problem, the pairings
(\lambda_{q}^{+},\lambda_{q}^{-})
that converge to
\varepsilon_{q}
are first identified while the remaining
(\lambda_{p}^{\circ})
each converge to a single, unique
\varepsilon_{p}
.

Using the label
p
for singly raised basis states and
q
for doubly raised states, the saddle points are given by

\lambda_{p}^{\circ}=\varepsilon_{p}-\frac{1}{\nu t},\;\lambda^{\pm}_{q}=\varepsilon_{q}-\frac{(1\pm i)}{2\nu t}.

(61)

Using the general form of the saddle points from (
61
), (
57
) is evaluated in the
t\rightarrow\infty^{+}
limit as

\displaystyle\Xi(\bm{\lambda},\varepsilon)=

\displaystyle t^{N_{1}+2N_{2}}(-\nu)^{N_{1}}(2\nu^{2})^{N_{2}}\left|\mathcal{B}^{\bm{\{\alpha\}}}\right>,

(62)

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{B}^{\bm{\{\alpha\}}}\right>=

\displaystyle\left|\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\right>\left|\{\alpha^{(2)}\}\right>\left|\oslash\right>.

The sets
\alpha^{(i)}
determine the state obtained by raising the vacuum
\left|\odot\right>

N_{+}
times, where each site referred in
\{\alpha^{(1)}\}
is raised once, and each site in
\{\alpha^{(2)}\}
twice and
\left|\oslash\right>
covers all remaining unraised states. Thus, for example, for
n=3
, and
\{\alpha^{(2)}\}=\{1\}
,
\{\alpha^{(1)}\}=\{2\}
and
\oslash=\{3\}
corresponds to the state
\left|+1,0,-1\right>
where each number in the ket represents the z-projection of the
\hat{S}^{z}_{i}
operator at site
i
respectively, i.e.
\left|S_{1}^{z},S_{2}^{z},S_{3}^{z}\right>
.
The determinant of the Hessian matrix
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime\prime}=[\partial^{2}\mathcal{Y}/\partial\lambda_{p}\partial\lambda_{q}]\big|_{t\rightarrow\infty}
is then evaluated to be

\det\mathcal{Y}^{\prime\prime}\left(\bm{\lambda},\varepsilon,t\right)\approx t^{2(N_{1}+2N_{2})}(-2\nu^{2})^{N_{1}}(4\nu^{2})^{2N_{2}},

(63)

which is the result obtained after neglecting terms that vanish in the infinite time limit. In conclusion,
\Xi(\bm{\lambda},\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\det\mathcal{Y}^{\prime\prime}}=\left|\{\alpha\}\right>\left|\{\beta\}\right>\left|\oslash\right>
up to time-independent global prefactors. Therefore, the only relevant contribution to the solution comes from the evaluation of the Yang-Yang action (
58
) at the saddle point. By substituting (
61
) in (
58
) and neglecting terms of order
t^{-1}
one finds

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}=2\sum_{i\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}l_{ji}(\varepsilon)+4\sum_{i\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}l_{ji}(\varepsilon)

(64)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}l_{ji}(\varepsilon)-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\\
j\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}\end{subarray}}l_{ji}(\varepsilon)-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}\\
j\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\end{subarray}}l_{ji}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle-4\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}l_{ji}(\varepsilon)+2\nu t\sum_{i\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}}\varepsilon_{i}+4\nu t\sum_{i\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle-N_{1}[1+\ln(\nu t)]-N_{2}\ln(4),

where the notation
l_{ji}(\varepsilon)=\ln\left(\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{i}\right)
is introduced and global prefactors are omitted for clarity. The first two terms on the right-hand side of (
64
) simplify as follow

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}l_{ji}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\rightarrow 2\sum_{k\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}}\;\;\left(-ik\pi+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}^{n}l_{jk}|\varepsilon|\right),

(65)

\displaystyle 4\sum_{i\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}l_{ji}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\rightarrow 4\sum_{k\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}\;\;\left(-ik\pi+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}l_{jk}|\varepsilon|\right),

where
l_{ji}|\varepsilon|=\ln|\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{i}|
. Note the choice of the branch cut (for details, see again
Barik et al. 2025b
). Upon simplifying the remaining terms in
\mathcal{Y}_{\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}
using the same procedure as in (
65
) and ignoring time-independent global prefactors, the saddle point is evaluated to

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}=2\nu t\sum_{i\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}}\varepsilon_{i}+4\nu t\sum_{i\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}\varepsilon_{i}-2i\pi\sum_{k\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}}k

(66)

\displaystyle-4i\pi\sum_{k\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}k+2\sum_{i\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}l_{ji}|\varepsilon|+4\sum_{i\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}l_{ji}|\varepsilon|

\displaystyle-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
i,j\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\end{subarray}}l_{ji}|\varepsilon|-4\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\\
j\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}\end{subarray}}l_{ji}|\varepsilon|-8\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
i,j\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}\end{subarray}}l_{ji}|\varepsilon|

\displaystyle-N_{1}\left[1+\ln(\nu t/2)-i\pi/2\right]-N_{+}\ln(\nu t).

Note that in this Appendix, compared to Ref.

term in the last line of Eq. (

However, in the non-Hermitian setting considered here, this additional term matters, and is therefore explicitely included in the calculation presented here.
The final asymptotic wavefunction is written as

\left|\Psi_{\infty}^{(N_{+})}(t)\right>=\sum_{\{\alpha^{(1)}\},\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}e^{-\frac{i\mathcal{Y}_{\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}}{\nu}}\left|\mathcal{B}^{\{\alpha\}}\right>.

(67)

Using (
66
) this expression becomes

\displaystyle\left|\Psi_{\infty}^{(N_{+})}(t)\right>

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{i}{\nu}N_{+}\ln(\nu t)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N_{1}+2N_{2}\\
=N_{+}\end{subarray}}e^{-\gamma_{N_{1}}}

(68)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{\tiny$\left|\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\right|$=$N_{1}$}\\
\text{\tiny$\left|\{\alpha^{(2)}\}\right|$=$N_{2}$}\end{subarray}}e^{i\Lambda_{\text{\tiny$\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\{\alpha^{(2)}\}$}}}\zeta_{{\text{\tiny$\{\alpha^{(1)}\}$}}{\text{\tiny$\{\alpha^{(2)}\}$}}}\left|\mathcal{B}^{\{\alpha\}}\right>,

where,

\displaystyle\nu\Lambda_{\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\

j\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}\end{subarray}}l_{ji}|\varepsilon|

(69a)

\displaystyle+8\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
i,j\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}\end{subarray}}l_{ji}|\varepsilon|,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\{\alpha^{(1)}\}\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}=

\displaystyle\prod_{j\in\{\alpha^{(1)}\}}e^{-2it\varepsilon_{j}-\frac{2\pi j}{\nu}-i2\theta_{j}}

(69b)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{k\in\{\alpha^{(2)}\}}e^{-4it\varepsilon_{k}-\frac{4\pi k}{\nu}-2i\theta_{k}},

\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{\nu}\sum_{j\neq k}\ln|\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}|,

(69c)

\gamma_{N_{1}}=-N_{1}\left[\frac{\pi+2i(1+\ln\nu)}{2\nu}+\frac{i}{\nu}\ln\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)\right].

(69d)

Eq. (
68
) together with Eq. (

) is used in the main text to derive the decay-exponent

Appendix D
Exact solution to
n\leq 2
non-Hermitian RG Hamiltonian

In this appendix, we derive the general expressions for one- and two-point correlation functions of the time-dependent driven-dissipative spins described by Eq. (
37
).

Obtaining the correlation functions of the dissipative spins amounts to solving the time-dependent non-Hermitian RG Hamiltonian, as described in the main text and Appendix
B
. Appendix
C
provides the asymptotic solution to the wavefunction of Eq. (
37
). However, for small
n
, exact solutions exist. These solutions hold for all times, and reveal a temporal phase transition. For full and more complete details of the calculation, we refer to
Barik et al. 2025a
.

D.1
One point correlation functions

First, we consider the simplest case by solving the one-point correlation functions for a site
j
. This amounts to solving the Master Equation (
37
) for
n=1
.

A single-site,
n=1
RG Hamiltonian is written as

\mathcal{L}_{1}=-i\left(ig(t)+2\varepsilon_{j}\right)\hat{S}_{j}^{z}-g(t)\hat{S}_{j}^{+}\hat{S}_{j}^{-}.

(70)

Here, we do specifically keep the term proportional to
ig(t)\hat{S}^{z}_{j}
, so that we can compare the results here directly with the prediction of the power of the decay of the correlators described in the main text and repeated here in Eq. (
74
).
The eigenstates of the correlation vectors
\mathcal{C}_{1}
are identified as correlation functions of the dissipative spins as

\mathcal{C}_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-c_{-}(t)\\
\sqrt{2}c_{z}(t)\\
c_{+}(t)\end{pmatrix}_{j}.

(71)

Since we are only interested in the general behavior of the correlation functions in time, we omit the prefactors of the basis states in (
71
) for simplicity.
The Master Equation (
70
) written in the eigenbasis of the
\hat{S}^{z}
operator is diagonal for
n=1
. Thus, the differential equations can be solved straightforwardly. They take the form

\displaystyle c_{\pm,j}(t)\propto c_{\pm,j}(t_{0})\exp\left[\pm 2i\varepsilon_{j}(t-t_{0})-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}g(t)dt\right],

(72)

\displaystyle c_{z,j}(t)\propto c_{z,j}(t_{0})\exp\left[-2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}g(t)dt\right],

where
\propto
refers to the missing prefactors in (
71
).
Setting
g(t)=1/(\nu t)
gives

\displaystyle c_{\pm,j}(t)=c_{\pm,j}(t_{0})\exp\left[\pm 2i\epsilon_{j}(t-t_{0})\right]\left(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)^{-1/\nu},

(73)

\displaystyle c_{z}(t)=c_{z}(t_{0})\left(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)^{-2/\nu}.

At this point, it is trivial to identify the behavior of the correlation functions as
t\rightarrow\infty
. In line with the definition of
\alpha
in the main text,

c^{\infty}_{a_{1}\dots a_{N}}(t)\propto t^{-\alpha}
, we find
\alpha=1/\nu
for the
\pm
correlation functions and
\alpha=2/\nu
for the
z
correlation function. These results agree with the asymptotic prediction

\alpha=\frac{n+N_{1}}{\nu}.

(74)

For
c_{\pm}
,
n=1
and
N_{1}=0
, yielding
\alpha=1/\nu
. For
c_{z}
we have
n=N_{1}=1
, meaning
\alpha=2/\nu
.

D.2.1
Sector
\pm 2

These sectors are
1\times 1
blocks and thus only involve a single ordinary differential equation (ODE). The basis states involved, in terms of the usual
S^{z}
quantum numbers are
\left|-1,-1\right>
and
\left|+1,+1\right>
. This means that the correlation functions described by these differential equations are the
c_{++}
and
c_{--}
correlations respectively. The equations of motion are written as follows

\frac{d}{dt}\left[c_{\mp_{p}\mp_{q}}(t)\right]=\tilde{H}_{\pm 2}(t)c_{\mp_{p}\mp_{q}}(t),

(77)

where the
\pm 2
in
\tilde{H}_{\pm 2}(t)
refers to the
J^{z}
sector and

\tilde{H}_{\pm 2}(t)=\mp 2i(\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{q})-2g(t).

(78)

The solution is then obtained directly

\displaystyle\frac{c_{\mp_{p}\mp_{q}}(t)}{c_{\mp_{p}\mp_{q}}(t_{0})}=

\displaystyle\exp\left[\mp i(\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{q})(t-t_{0})\right]

(79)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}g_{+}(t)dt\right].

Substituting
g_{+}(t)=1/(\nu t)
, we find

\frac{c_{\mp_{p}\mp_{q}}(t)}{c_{\mp_{p}\mp_{q}}(t_{0})}=e^{\mp i(\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{q})(t-t_{0})}\left(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)^{-2/\nu}.

(80)

Again, the power-law decay of the correlation functions can be identified, with
\alpha=2/\nu
. Additionally, much like the one-point correlation functions, these powers match the results from the asymptotic calculation. For
c_{\pm\pm}
we have
n=2
and
N_{1}=0
, yielding
\alpha=2/\nu
per Eq. (
74
).

D.2.2
Sector
\pm 1

This sector involves two coupled ODEs. The basis states spanning this sector, in terms of the usual
S^{z}
quantum numbers, are
\{\left|-1,0\right>,~\left|0,-1\right>,~\left|+1,0\right>,~\left|0,+1\right>\}
, to which we can associate the
\{c_{+z},~c_{z+},~c_{-z},~c_{z-}\}
respectively (omitting the common prefactors
\pm\sqrt{2}
from the translation described in Appendix
B
). We may write these equations as

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}(t)\\
c_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\tilde{H}_{\pm 1}(t)\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}(t)\\
c_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix},

(81)

where
\tilde{H}_{\pm 1}(t)
is written as

\begin{pmatrix}\pm i\left(\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{q}\right)-5g(t)&\pm i(\varepsilon_{q}-\varepsilon_{p})\\
\pm i(\varepsilon_{q}-\varepsilon_{p})&\pm i\left(\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{q}\right)-g(t)\end{pmatrix},

(82)

and

\displaystyle c_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[c_{z_{p}\pm_{q}}(t)+c_{\pm_{p}z_{q}}(t)\right],

(83)

\displaystyle c_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[c_{z_{p}\pm_{q}}(t)-c_{\pm_{p}z_{q}}(t)\right].

In this case, solving for a general form of
g(t)
is quite difficult. For
g(t)=1/(\nu t)
, we can provide a closed form expression, which reads

\displaystyle c_{1}(t)=

\displaystyle\mp ie^{\pm ir\tau}t^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{\nu}}

(84)

\displaystyle\times\left[k_{1}J_{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\nu}}\left(\tau\right)+k_{2}Y_{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\nu}}\left(\tau\right)\right],

\displaystyle c_{2}(t)=

\displaystyle e^{\pm ir\tau}t^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{\nu}}

\displaystyle\times\left[k_{1}J_{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\nu}}\left(\tau\right)+k_{2}Y_{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\nu}}\left(\tau\right)\right],

with

r=\frac{(\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{q})}{(\varepsilon_{q}-\epsilon_{p})},\qquad\tau=(\varepsilon_{q}-\varepsilon_{p})t,

(85)

where
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are coefficients determined from the boundary conditions
c_{1}(\tau_{0})
and
c_{2}(\tau_{0})
and
J_{\xi}(t)
and
Y_{\xi}(t)
are Bessel functions of the first and second kind of order
\xi
respectively. The correlation functions are then found by reversing the basis transformation in (
83
). For brevity, we only provide the asymptotic (long time,
t\rightarrow\infty
) expression of each of the correlation functions:

\displaystyle c^{\infty}_{z_{p}\pm_{q}}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mp i\sqrt{2}(k_{1}\mp ik_{2})}{\sqrt{\pi(\varepsilon_{q}-\varepsilon_{p})}}

(86)

\displaystyle\times e^{\pm\frac{i}{2}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\nu}+(1+r)(\varepsilon_{q}-\varepsilon_{p})t\right)}t^{-\frac{3}{\nu}},

\displaystyle c^{\infty}_{\pm_{p}z_{q}}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mp i\sqrt{2}(k_{1}\pm ik_{2})}{\sqrt{\pi(\varepsilon_{q}-\varepsilon_{p})}}

\displaystyle\times e^{\mp\frac{i}{2}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\nu}+(1-r)(\varepsilon_{q}-\varepsilon_{p})t\right)}t^{-\frac{3}{\nu}}.

Again, we can compare these results with prediction of the power-law-decay at long times
\alpha
, given by Eq. (
74
). For the
c_{\pm z}
correlation functions, we have
n=2
and
N_{1}=1
. These numbers yield
\alpha=3/\nu
, in perfect agreement with Eq. (
86
).

\displaystyle c_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 31.2982pt+~c_{+_{p}-_{q}}(t)\right),
