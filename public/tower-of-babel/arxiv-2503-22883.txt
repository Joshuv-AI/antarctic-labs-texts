Combinatorics of factorization systems on lattices
Jishnu Bose, Tien Chih, Hannah Housden, Legrand Jones II, Chloe Lewis, Kyle Ormsby, Millie Rose

Abstract
. We initiate the combinatorial study of factorization systems on finite lattices, paying special attention to the role that reflective and coreflective factorization systems play in partitioning the poset of factorization systems on a fixed lattice. We ultimately uncover an intricate web of relations with such diverse combinatorial structures as submonoids, monads, Moore systems, transfer systems (from stable equivariant homotopy theory), and poly-Bernoulli numbers.



1.
Introduction

Orthogonal factorization systems are foundational structures in category theory originally intorduced by Mac Lane
[
Mac50
]
. They allow every morphism in a category to be (essentially) uniquely factored as a ‘left’ morphism followed by a ‘right’ morphism, with surjective-injective factorizations of functions between sets serving as a prototype. Weak factorization systems play a similar role in homotopy theory, but relax the uniqueness condition; they arise in the foundational work of Quillen
[
Qui67
]
on model structures.

Orthogonal and weak factorization systems are identical notions in a poset category, where there is at most one morphism between any pair of objects. The present work studies the combinatorics of factorization systems in this context. The authors’ interest in such structures arose from the connection between factorization systems on subgroup lattices and transfer systems from stable equivariant homotopy theory exposed in
[
FOO+22
]
. While these applications remain top-of-mind, we have chosen to center factorization systems due to their historical precedence and their link to diverse topics of independent mathematical interest. Indeed, factorization systems on a lattice

are closely related to (co)monads on
P
, closure and interior operators on
P
, and submonoids of
(P,\vee)
and of
(P,\wedge)
(where

Our primary connection to these other topics is a well-developed theory of characteristic and cocharacteristic functions for factorization systems. This enhances and dualizes the theory of characteristic functions for transfer systems developed in
[

Returning to our original motivation for this work, we note that our combinatorial analysis of factorization systems via (co)characteristic functions has immediate consequences for structured

Overview

In

Remark 3.8
.

When

is a poset category, there are simpler formulations of (co)reflectivity. In particular, a consequence of the adjoint functor theorem is that a functor from a lattice to a poset is a left (respectively right) adjoint if and only if it preserves joins (respectively meets). In particular, a factorization system

on a lattice is reflective if and only if the objects of

(

We will see in

This allows us to state our main theorem. For our purposes, a (bounded, closed)

Lemma 3.13
.

Suppose

We now start our analysis of the image of
\lambda

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
P
be a finite lattice and
(L,R)
a factorization system on
P
. Then
\lambda^{(L,R)}

Proof.

We already know that
\lambda^{(L,R)}\colon P\to P

\lambda^{(L,R)}(x)=\max\{y\in P\mid x~L~y\}\geq x

\lambda^{(L,R)}

\lambda^{(L,R)}

∎

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

, reflective factorization systems correspond to disklike transfer systems, and coreflective factorization systems correspond to saturated transfer systems.

It is also the case that (co)reflective factorization systems encode certain submonoids of the commuatative idempotent monoids
(P,\vee)
and
(P,\wedge)
. The following proof appears in unpublished work of Balchin–MacBrough–Ormsby.

Theorem 4.3
.

The assignment
(L,R)\mapsto R/1
is a bijection between reflective factorization systems and submonoids of
(P,\wedge)
; dually,
(L,R)\mapsto 0\backslash L
is a bijection between coreflective factorization systems and submonoids of
(P,\vee)
.

Proof.

By duality, we only need to handle the case of reflective factorization systems. We define an adjoint pair of functors (
i.e.
, monotone Galois connection)
(F,G)\colon 2^{P}\leftrightarrows\operatorname{Fac}P
between the lattice of subsets of
P
and factorization systems on
P
where

G(L,R)=\{x\in P\mid x~R~1\}.

and

FS=({}^{\perp}\langle S/1\rangle,\langle S/1\rangle).

Since Galois connections induce Galois correspondences on images (
[
Ore44
, Theorem 2]
), we only need to identify the images of
G
and
F
.

Clearly
1\in G(L,R)
, and since the right morphisms in a factorization system are stable under pullback in
P
, we see that
G(L,R)
is also closed under the operation
\wedge
, making it a submonoid of
(P,\wedge)
. Given a submonoid
M
of
(P,\wedge)
, one may check that

({}^{\perp}\langle M/1\rangle,\langle M/1\rangle)

maps to
M
under
G
, so the image of
G
is exactly the submonoids of
(P,\wedge)
.

Finally, by
Theorem 4.1
, the image of
F
is exactly the reflective factorization systems on
P
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Submonoids of
(P,\wedge)
are sometimes called
Moore families
. Additionally, when
P=2^{S}
is the powerset lattice of a set
S
, such submonoids are sometimes called
closure systems
on
S
.

Closure systems are notoriously challenging to enumerate, and the total number of them is only known for
0\leq\#S\leq 7
. See OEIS A102896 and the papers
[
Hig98
,
HN05
,
CIR10
]
. Work of Kleitman
[
Kle76
]
shows that the base-2 logarithm of the number of closure systems on a cardinality
n
set is asymptotic to the middle binomial coefficient
\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
.

The following result seems to be well-known, but is hard to track down in the literature.

Theorem 4.5
.

There is a bijection between
\operatorname{\overline{\operatorname{End}}}P
and
\operatorname{SubMon}(P,\wedge)
given by
c\mapsto P^{c}=\{x\in P\mid cx=x\}
. Dually, there is a bijection between
\operatorname{End}^{\circ}P
and
\operatorname{SubMon}(P,\vee)
given by
i\mapsto P^{i}
.

Proof.

By duality, we only need to check the result on closure operators. We first check that if
c\in\operatorname{\overline{\operatorname{End}}}P
, then
P^{c}
is a submonoid of
(P,\wedge)
. Suppose
x,y\in P^{c}
so that
cx=x,cy=y
. Since
x\wedge y\leq x,y
and
c
is monotone, we have
c(x\wedge y)\leq cx=x,cy=y
, so
c(x\wedge y)\leq x\wedge y
. Suppose we have some other
z\leq x,y
. Then
z\leq x\wedge y
, so
cz\leq c(x\wedge y)
. This demonstrates that
c(x\wedge y)
is the greatest lower bound of
x,y
,
i.e.
,

c(x\wedge y)=x\wedge y,

whence
P^{c}
is a submonoid of
(P,\wedge)
.

We now construct an inverse to
c\mapsto P^{c}
. Given a submonoid
A
of
(P,\wedge)
, define
f_{A}\colon P\to P
by
f_{A}x=\min\{y\in A\mid x\leq y\}
. One may check by first principles that
f_{A}
is monotone, extensive, and idempotent, so
f_{A}
is a closure operator. We leave it to the reader to check that for each closure operator
c
,
f_{P^{c}}=c
, and for each submonoid
A\leq(P,\wedge)
,
P^{f_{A}}=A
.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Don Knuth
[
Knu24
]
has observed that subsemigroups of
([m]\times[n],\vee)
are counted by the poly-Bernoulli number

B_{m+1,n+1}=\sum_{k\geq 0}(k!)^{2}{m+2\brace k+1}{n+2\brace k+1}.

Since there are half as many submonoids as subsemigroups (via the forgetful and “remove 0” maps), we learn that

\#\operatorname{SubMon}([m]\times[n],\vee)=\frac{1}{2}B_{m+1,n+1}.

Via Kaneko’s original work
[
Kan97
]
on poly-Bernoulli numbers, this also recovers the asymmetric formula for saturated transfer systems on
[m]\times[n]
in the work of Hafeez–Marcus–Ormsby–Osorno
[
HMOO22
]
. Forthcoming work of the 2024 Reed Collaborative Mathematics Group
[
eMKO+25
]
extends these enumerations to more general modular lattices.

We conclude with two relatively minor observations which may nonetheless prove useful in future enumeration efforts. The first relates to (co)monads on a (category induced by a) finite lattice.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
P
be a lattice, which we also view as a bicomplete category. There is a bijection between monads on
P
and closure operators on
P
; dually, there is a bijection between comonads on
P
and interior operators on
P
.

Proof.

By duality, we only need to demonstrate the result for monads. A monad consists of an endofunctor
T\colon P\to P
and natural transformations
\eta\colon\operatorname{id}_{P}\to T
and
\mu\colon T\circ T\to T
. An endofunctor on
P
is the same data as a monotone function on
P
, the natural transformation
\eta
witnesses
\eta_{x}\colon x\leq Tx
(extensivity), and the natural transformation
\mu
implies idempotence when combined with extensivity:

\mu_{x}\colon TTx\leq Tx\implies Tx\leq TTx\leq Tx\implies TTx=Tx.

∎

Finally, (co)reflective factorization systems may also be viewed as special types of model structures. Recall that in the Joyal–Tierney
[
JT07
]
presentation of a model structure is an interval of weak factorization systems
(C,AF)\leq(AC,F)
for which
W:=AF\circ AC
satisfies 3-for-2. Call a model structure
fibrant
when
F=\mathrm{All}
, and call it
cofibrant
when
C=\mathrm{All}
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
P
be a finite poset. Coreflective factorization systems on
P
are in bijection with fibrant model structures on
P
via

(L,R)\longmapsto[(L,R)\leq(\mathrm{Id},\mathrm{All})].

Reflective factorization systems on
P
are in bijection with cofibrant model structures on
P
via

(L,R)\longmapsto[(\mathrm{All},\mathrm{Id})\leq(L,R)].

Proof.

Suppose
(L,R)
is coreflective. By
Theorem 4.1
, we know that
R
is a saturated transfer system, so
R\circ\mathrm{Id}=R
satisfies 3-for-2. This makes
[(L,R)\leq(\mathrm{Id},\mathrm{All})]
a fibrant model structure on
P
, and it is straightforward to check that this assignment may be reversed. The remainder of the theorem is dual.
∎
