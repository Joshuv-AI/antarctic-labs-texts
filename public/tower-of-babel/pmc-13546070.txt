Genetically Predicted Gene Expression and Circulating Metabolites Associated with Cervical High-Grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion: A Mendelian Randomization Study
Authors: Bozhou Cui, Yan Ding, Feixia Li, Tuoyang Hu, Jiaojiao Zhu, Xiaoying Chang, Yucun Wang, Jie Yang, Jing Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) is a precancerous condition of the cervix. Identifying risk factors associated with HSIL and understanding their potential mechanisms may inform prevention strategies. This study aimed to investigate the associations of genetically predicted gene expression and circulating metabolites with HSIL risk using Mendelian randomization (MR).MethodsWe performed two-sample MR analysis to evaluate the associations of genetically predicted gene expression (eQTLGen consortium, N=31,684) and circulating metabolites (genome-wide association study [GWAS], N=8,299) with HSIL risk (FinnGen R12, N=293,218; 8,291 cases). Mediation analysis was conducted to explore whether metabolites might mediate the associations between genes and HSIL. Sensitivity analyses, including Mendelian randomization pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO), leave-one-out, and colocalization, were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. All GWAS data used in this study were derived from European-ancestry populations.ResultsEleven genes showed significant associations with HSIL after false discovery rate (FDR) correction (q<0.05), including VWA7, PAX8, GUSBP1, IKZF3, PAX8-AS1, NFKBIL1 (interpret with caution due to an influential single nucleotide polymorphism [SNP]), ERBB2, COL11A2, SKIV2L, TCF19, and PGAP3. Eleven circulating metabolites were also significantly associated with HSIL. Mediation analysis suggested that two phospholipid metabolites (GCST90200685 and GCST90200692) might mediate a small proportion of the total protective association of COL11A2 with HSIL (1.46% and 1.45%, respectively), indicating that the protective association of COL11A2 is largely independent of these circulating metabolites. Colocalization analysis showed strong evidence of shared causal variants for eight genes (PP.H4>0.98), while COL11A2 showed weak evidence of colocalization (PP.H4=1.58×10−15). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that COL11A2-related genes were enriched in extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interaction and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways.ConclusionThis MR study identified 11 genes and 11 circulating metabolites associated with HSIL risk. Among these, COL11A2 showed a protective association that appeared to be largely independent of circulating phospholipid metabolites, suggesting potential local mechanisms. These findings provide genetic and metabolic clues for future studies on HSIL etiology.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common malignancy among women worldwide, accounting for an estimated 604,000 new cases and 342,000 deaths annually.1 Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV), particularly HPV16 and HPV18, is the necessary cause of cervical carcinogenesis.2 However, HPV infection is common yet only a minority develop cervical cancer or its precursors, strongly suggesting that host genetic factors play a critical role in disease susceptibility.3

High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) is the immediate precursor to invasive cervical cancer, histologically corresponding to cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 or 3 (CIN2/3).4 Current cervical cancer screening strategies primarily rely on cytological examination (Pap smear) and high-risk HPV testing. While HPV testing offers high sensitivity for detecting CIN2+ (96.9%), its specificity is notably lower (88.8%), leading to excessive colposcopy referrals, overtreatment, and reduced patient compliance.4 Conversely, cytology-based screening demonstrates substantially lower sensitivity, ranging from 58.8% to 73.4% for CIN2+ detection.5 Even high-quality cytology has been reported to miss at least 30% of HSIL and cervical cancer cases.6 These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel biomarkers and a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying HSIL pathogenesis.

Current screening guidelines, however, remain discordant on the optimal approach. The US Preventive Services Task Force and the Women’s Preventive Services Initiative recommend co-testing (combined HPV and cytology) every 5 years for women aged 30–65 years.7 In contrast, the American Cancer Society’s 2020 updated guideline shifted toward primary HPV testing every 5 years as the preferred strategy.8 While HPV testing offers high sensitivity, studies have shown that high-risk HPV testing alone would miss approximately 30% of cytologically detected dysplastic samples,9 and co-testing can achieve a sensitivity of up to 97.5% for detecting cervical lesions.10 These ongoing debates highlight the unresolved challenges in current screening and reinforce the need for novel biomarkers to improve risk stratification.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified several susceptibility loci for cervical cancer, most notably within the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region on chromosome 6p21.3.11,12 However, GWAS findings alone cannot distinguish whether associated variants influence disease risk through effects on gene expression, protein levels, or metabolic pathways. Recent large-scale expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) studies, such as the eQTLGen consortium comprising 31,684 blood samples, have significantly improved our ability to map regulatory variants.13 Similarly, comprehensive GWAS of 1,400 circulating metabolites in 8,299 individuals have characterized the genetic architecture of the human metabolome.14 Both gene expression and metabolite levels have been implicated in cervical carcinogenesis,15,16 yet whether these associations reflect causal relationships remain unclear due to potential confounding and reverse causation inherent in observational studies.

Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causal relationships between exposures and diseases, offering advantages over observational studies by being less susceptible to confounding and reverse causation.17 Despite these methodological strengths, critical knowledge gaps remain. The causal effects of most genes and circulating metabolites on HSIL risk have not been systematically evaluated. Moreover, whether circulating metabolites mediate the effects of genetic variants on HSIL remains unclear, as few studies have employed mediation MR frameworks to dissect these relationships.18 Elucidating these mechanisms could inform the development of novel preventive strategies for cervical cancer.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis to achieve three objectives: (1) to identify genes and circulating metabolites that are causally associated with HSIL risk using a unidirectional two-sample MR framework; (2) to investigate whether circulating metabolites mediate the effects of causal genes on HSIL using a two-step mediation MR approach; and (3) to explore the potential biological pathways involved through functional enrichment analysis. Our findings provide novel genetic and metabolic insights into the etiology of HSIL and may inform future mechanistic studies.

Methods

Study Design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) guidelines (Supplementary Table 1). Using a two-sample MR design, we (1) identified genes and metabolites causally associated with HSIL, (2) assessed mediation via two-step MR, and (3) explored biological pathways through functional enrichment analysis. Data Source and Participants

Data collection for all included GWAS studies was completed prior to June 3, 2026. Summary-level data for cis-expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTLs) were obtained from the eQTLGen consortium (31,684 blood samples from 37 European-ancestry cohorts) (https://www.eqtlgen.org/).13 GWAS summary statistics for 1,400 circulating metabolites were obtained from Chen et al (8,299 European-ancestry individuals from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging) (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-31175-4).14 HSIL GWAS summary statistics were obtained from FinnGen release 12 (293,218 Finnish individuals, including 8291 HSIL cases) (https://www.finngen.fi/),19 with HSIL defined using ICD-10 codes D06, N87.1, and N87.2 (corresponding to CIN2/3). All original GWAS studies received approval from their respective institutional review boards, and informed consent was obtained from all participants, as detailed in the original publications.13,14,19 Specifically, the FinnGen study was approved by the Coordinating Ethics Committee of the Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa (HUS/990/2017).19

Instrumental Variable Selection

For both eQTL and metabolite analyses, instrumental variables (IVs) were selected using a consistent approach. Independent SNPs were identified using a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 1×10−5 (rather than the conventional P < 5×10−8) to ensure sufficient statistical power for the two-step mediation analysis, which requires an adequate number of instrumental variables. This threshold has been widely used in eQTL-based MR studies. All IVs had F-statistics > 10, confirming the absence of weak instrument bias.

Mendelian Randomization Analysis

Two-sample MR was performed using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method as the primary analysis. Sensitivity analyses included weighted median, MR-Egger, Mendelian randomization pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO), and leave-one-out methods. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic; significant heterogeneity (P < 0.05) prompted the use of random-effects IVW. Horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept test (P > 0.05 indicating no pleiotropy).

Colocalization Analysis

Colocalization analysis was performed for genes showing significant MR associations using the coloc.abf method.20 For each gene, we extracted summary statistics for all SNPs within ±500 kb of the gene center from both eQTL and HSIL GWAS. The posterior probability of hypothesis 4 (PP.H4), indicating that both traits share a causal variant, was calculated. PP.H4 > 0.8 was considered strong evidence of colocalization.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

To explore biological pathways, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package. Genes significantly correlated with COL11A2 expression (|r| > 0.4, P < 0.05) in the TCGA cervical cancer dataset were identified. Enrichment was considered significant at adjusted P < 0.05 using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

Statistical Analysis and Visualization

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3). MR analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR (v0.6.15) and MRPRESSO (v1.0) R packages. Visualization of forest plots, volcano plots, bubble plots, and regional association plots was generated using ggplot2 (v3.4.0), ggrepel, and coloc (v5.2.3). For the primary gene-HSIL and metabolite-HSIL analyses, FDR correction was applied across all tests (11,000+ genes and 1,400 metabolites, respectively). For the eQTL-metabolite analysis, FDR correction was applied per gene across the 11 candidate metabolites. A q-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Genome-Wide MR Analysis Identified 11 Genes Causally Associated with HSIL

Of the 11,000+ genes tested, 11 showed significant causal associations with HSIL after FDR correction (q < 0.05) (Figure 1A and Supplementary Figure 2).

Among these, five genes were identified as risk factors (OR > 1): VWA7 (OR = 1.77, P = 3.73×10−11), PAX8 (OR = 1.17, P = 9.92×10−11), GUSBP1 (OR = 1.47, P = 3.76×10−5), IKZF3 (OR = 1.23, P = 4.90×10−8), and PAX8-AS1 (OR = 1.16, P = 3.90×10−8). Five genes were protective (OR < 1): ERBB2 (OR = 0.49, P = 1.24×10−5), COL11A2 (OR = 0.60, P = 3.40×10−5), SKIV2L (OR = 0.79, P = 2.76×10−10), TCF19 (OR = 0.78, P = 1.38×10−6), and PGAP3 (OR = 0.84, P = 7.94×10−6). NFKBIL1 was also significantly associated with increased risk (OR = 1.79, P = 7.05×10−14), but leave-one-out analysis identified an influential SNP; this finding should therefore be interpreted with caution. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the causal estimates. All IVs had F-statistics > 10 (range: 20.8–2577.9), indicating no weak instrument bias (Supplementary Table 3). The MR-Egger intercept test showed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy for any gene (all P > 0.05), indicating that horizontal pleiotropy was unlikely to bias the primary IVW estimates (Supplementary Table 4). Heterogeneity assessment revealed varying degrees of heterogeneity across genes. Specifically, COL11A2 showed significant heterogeneity (IVW Q = 19.42, P = 2.23×10−4) and VWA7 demonstrated nominally significant heterogeneity (IVW Q = 15.97, P = 0.014), while the remaining nine genes showed no evidence of significant heterogeneity (all IVW P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 4). To account for potential heterogeneity, random-effects IVW models were applied for COL11A2 and VWA7. Importantly, MR-PRESSO global tests confirmed the absence of significant outlier SNPs for all genes (all Global P > 0.05), and no outliers were detected or corrected for any exposure (Supplementary Table 5). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis identified rs113381230 as an influential SNP for NFKBIL1. When this SNP was excluded, the causal estimate for NFKBIL1 decreased from β=0.581 to β=0.385, indicating that the association may be driven by this single genetic variant (Supplementary Figures 3–7).

Eleven Circulating Metabolites Were Causally Associated with HSIL

After FDR correction (q < 0.05), 11 of the 1,400 circulating metabolites tested remained significantly associated with HSIL, comprising seven risk metabolites (OR range: 1.05–1.14) and four protective metabolites (OR range: 0.90–0.97) (Figure 1B and Supplementary Figure 8a).

Phospholipids represented the largest category (5/11) (Supplementary Figure 8b). Risk-associated metabolites included four glycerophosphocholines (OR range: 1.05–1.14, all P < 0.001), the arachidonate ratio (OR = 1.10, P = 9.59×10−5), sphingomyelin (OR = 1.10, P = 3.17×10−4), and 1-arachidonylglycerol (OR = 1.11, P = 3.26×10−4). Protective metabolites included X-18921 (OR = 0.90, P = 3.35×10−5), the oleoyl-linoleoyl-glycerol ratio (OR = 0.93, P = 1.49×10−5), and two bilirubin degradation products (OR = 0.93 for both, P < 0.001), consistent with their known antioxidant properties (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 6). All instrumental variables had F-statistics ranging from 19.5 to 1893 (all > 10), indicating no weak instrument bias. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of all 11 metabolite-HSIL associations. No horizontal pleiotropy was detected (all Egger P > 0.05). Ten metabolites showed no heterogeneity, while sphingomyelin exhibited nominal heterogeneity (IVW P = 0.034), warranting a random-effects model. COL11A2 is the Only Gene with Detectable Metabolic Mediation — But the Effect is Remarkably Small

Among the 11 causal genes, we examined whether circulating metabolites mediated their effects on HSIL. First, we tested each gene against the 11 HSIL-associated metabolites. Notably, COL11A2 was the only gene showing significant associations with any of these metabolites after FDR correction (q < 0.05) (Supplementary Figure 15). Specifically, COL11A2 expression was significantly associated with two phospholipid metabolites: 1-stearoyl-2-arachidonoyl-gpc (GCST90200685) (β = −0.143, P = 0.0017, q = 0.0094) and 1-palmitoyl-2-arachidonoyl-gpc (GCST90200692) (β = −0.125, P = 0.0069, q = 0.0190) (Supplementary Tables 10 and 11). For the remaining 10 genes, no significant eQTL-metabolite associations were detected (all q > 0.05), suggesting they may act through direct mechanisms independent of circulating metabolites. We therefore performed two-step MR mediation analysis for COL11A2 using these two metabolites as candidate mediators. The two phospholipid metabolites mediated only 1.46% (95% CI: 0.44%-3.49%) and 1.45% (95% CI: 0.30%-3.71%) of the total protective effect of COL11A2 on HSIL, respectively (Figure 2, Supplementary Figure 16a and Supplementary Table 12). All instrumental variables for COL11A2 had F-statistics > 10 (range: 26.3–409.3), and MR-PRESSO detected no outlier SNPs for either metabolite (both Global P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 13). Leave-one-out analysis further demonstrated that the mediated proportion remained stable after excluding any individual SNP, ranging from 1.39% to 1.63% for GCST90200685 and from 1.32% to 1.55% for GCST90200692 (Supplementary Figure 16b and Supplementary Table 14). Taken together, these sensitivity analyses confirm the reliability of the mediation estimates. This remarkably small mediated proportion (<2%) indicates that the protective effect of COL11A2 on HSIL is largely independent of circulating phospholipid metabolites, suggesting alternative mechanisms.

COL11A2 Shows Weak Colocalization Evidence

To further investigate the genetic architecture underlying these associations, we performed colocalization analysis for all 11 genes. Eight genes showed strong evidence of shared causal variants with HSIL (PP.H4 > 0.98), including NFKBIL1, VWA7, PAX8, SKIV2L, PAX8-AS1, IKZF3, TCF19, and PGAP3 (Table 3, Figure 3A and Supplementary Figures 17–20). This indicates that the eQTL and GWAS signals for these genes are driven by the same causal variants. COL11A2-Related Genes are Enriched in ECM and PI3K-Akt Pathways

To explore the potential biological mechanisms through which COL11A2 may influence HSIL, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analysis on genes correlated with COL11A2 expression (|r| > 0.4, P < 0.05) in the TCGA cervical cancer dataset. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in extracellular matrix (ECM)-related biological processes, including “extracellular matrix organization” (adjusted P = 8.2×10−10), “collagen fibril organization” (adjusted P = 3.1×10−7), and “cell adhesion” (adjusted P = 2.5×10−6) (Figure 3C and Supplementary Table 15). KEGG pathway analysis identified significant enrichment in “ECM-receptor interaction” (adjusted P = 1.2×10−6), “Focal adhesion” (adjusted P = 3.8×10−6), and “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway” (adjusted P = 4.5×10−5) (Figure 3D and Supplementary Table 16). The top five enriched KEGG pathways and their interrelationships are presented as a network plot (Supplementary Figure 21).

These findings suggest that COL11A2 may exert its protective effect on HSIL through regulating ECM remodeling and cell adhesion, potentially via the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway — a hypothesis that aligns with the small mediated proportion observed through circulating metabolites.

Discussion

This study systematically identified genetically predicted genes and circulating metabolites associated with HSIL risk using two-sample MR and further explored potential mediation relationships through two-step MR. Among 11 genes and 11 metabolites causally associated with HSIL, we found that the protective effect of COL11A2 was minimally mediated by circulating phospholipid metabolites (mediated proportion <2%), suggesting that COL11A2 might exert its protective function primarily through other mechanisms, such as ECM-receptor interaction and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways. These findings provide novel genetic and metabolic insights into HSIL etiology, and COL11A2 emerged as a candidate protective gene meriting further mechanistic investigation.

Our finding that NFKBIL1, VWA7, and IKZF3—all located within the HLA region on chromosome 6p21.3—are risk-associated genes for HSIL is consistent with previous GWAS findings implicating this region in cervical cancer susceptibility.21 The HLA class II region has long been recognized as a key determinant of HPV persistence and cervical carcinogenesis, likely through its role in antigen presentation and immune response modulation. Our MR analysis provides causal evidence supporting that genetically predicted expression of these immune-related genes directly influences HSIL risk, extending beyond association signals identified by GWAS.

PAX8 and its antisense transcript PAX8-AS1 were also identified as risk-associated genes for HSIL. PAX8 is a transcription factor essential for Müllerian duct development, and its continued expression in adult cervical epithelium may influence susceptibility to HPV-mediated transformation. PAX8-AS1, the antisense transcript of PAX8, may regulate PAX8 expression through cis-acting mechanisms. Functional studies have demonstrated that PAX8-AS1 suppresses cervical cancer progression via the miR-675-3p/DCN axis, with lower expression correlating with advanced FIGO stage and lymph node metastasis.22 The concordant risk associations of both PAX8 and its antisense transcript support a causal role for this locus in HSIL susceptibility.

The potential protective association of ERBB2 with HSIL (OR = 0.49) is unexpected given its established oncogenic role in breast and gastric cancers. ERBB2 (HER2) amplification drives tumor progression in those malignancies, yet its role in cervical carcinogenesis appears distinct. Although direct evidence linking ERBB2 to cervical cancer risk is limited, previous MR studies have demonstrated context-dependent genetic effects on cervical cancer susceptibility, suggesting that genetic determinants may operate through tissue-specific mechanisms.23 The protective association we observed could reflect ERBB2’s role in epithelial differentiation and repair mechanisms in the normal cervical epithelium. However, given that ERBB2 had only two instrumental variables (precluding formal pleiotropy testing), this finding should be interpreted with appropriate caution and requires experimental validation.

Our metabolite findings are broadly consistent with existing lipidomic studies in cervical lesions. A shotgun lipidomic study demonstrated that glycerophospholipids—particularly phosphatidylcholines and phosphatidylethanolamines—are significantly altered during HPV-associated cervical transformation, with over 90% of marker lipids positively correlating with the degree of cervical neoplasia.24 Similarly, integrated microbiome-metabolome analyses have revealed significant alterations in lipid and organic acid metabolites during cervical lesion progression, with distinct metabolic profiles distinguishing cervical cancer (CC) from precancerous lesions.25 Our MR analysis extends these observational findings by providing genetic evidence that circulating phospholipids are causally associated with HSIL risk, reducing concerns about confounding that plague observational metabolomics studies. The protective association of bilirubin degradation products (OR = 0.93 for both) aligns with the known antioxidant properties of bilirubin. Oxidative stress has been implicated in HPV-mediated cervical carcinogenesis, and endogenous antioxidants may mitigate DNA damage and inflammation. This finding is consistent with a recent three-stage MR study demonstrating that iron status affects cervical cancer risk through metabolic pathways involving cortisone and carnitine,26 further supporting the role of metabolic and oxidative stress mechanisms in cervical carcinogenesis.

COL11A2 emerged as a novel protective gene for HSIL (OR = 0.60), with remarkably minimal mediation by circulating phospholipid metabolites (<2%). COL11A2 encodes one of the three alpha chains of type XI collagen, a minor fibrillar collagen that regulates collagen fibril diameter and organization. While COL11A2 mutations are classically associated with inherited connective tissue disorders such as Stickler syndrome and otospondylomegaepiphyseal dysplasia, its role in cancer is emerging. Our functional enrichment analysis revealed that COL11A2-correlated genes are significantly enriched in ECM-receptor interaction and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways -two interconnected pathways that govern cell-matrix communication and intracellular signaling. The ECM-receptor interaction pathway is critical for cell adhesion, migration, and survival; dysregulation of ECM remodeling is a hallmark of cancer progression.27,28 The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway is frequently activated in cervical cancer and promotes cell proliferation, survival, and angiogenesis.29,30 The minimal mediated proportion (<2%) through circulating phospholipid metabolites suggests that COL11A2’s protective effect is largely independent of systemic metabolic changes, pointing instead to local mechanisms involving ECM remodeling and cell signaling.

This finding is particularly intriguing given the weak colocalization evidence (PP.H4 = 1.58×10−15), which indicates that the eQTL and GWAS signals at the COL11A2 locus are driven by distinct causal variants. The observation that the lead eQTL SNPs for COL11A2 are not the primary GWAS signals at this locus, together with the opposite directional effect of rs213197—negatively associated with COL11A2 expression but positively associated with HSIL risk—further supports the protective role of higher COL11A2 expression through mechanisms independent of the primary GWAS signals. Alternative mechanisms may include direct effects on ECM composition and stiffness in the cervical stroma, influencing epithelial cell behavior, modulation of collagen signaling through integrin and discoidin domain receptors, or regulation of immune cell infiltration and function in the cervical microenvironment. A plasma proteomic study of HSIL and cervical cancer identified complement and coagulation pathways, cholesterol metabolism, and IL-17 signaling as enriched pathways,31 raising the possibility that COL11A2 may intersect with these processes, though direct evidence linking COL11A2 to these pathways remains to be established.

The identification of 11 causal genes and 11 circulating metabolites provides potential targets for biomarker development. Current cervical cancer screening strategies—cytology and HPV testing—have well-documented limitations in sensitivity and specificity. Metabolite-based biomarkers offer several theoretical advantages: they are quantifiable in blood, reflect systemic physiological states, and may be modifiable through lifestyle interventions. For instance, a combined panel of metabolites such as phospholipids and bilirubin degradation products has been suggested to have potential as an adjunctive blood-based test to triage HPV-positive women, particularly those with cytological abnormalities. However, the small effect sizes observed for most metabolites indicate that individual metabolites are unlikely to serve as standalone diagnostic biomarkers. Instead, metabolite panels or integrated genetic-metabolic risk scores may have greater utility. Such multi-marker approaches could help refine current screening algorithms, especially in settings where colposcopy resources are limited. The combination of key microbiota and metabolites has shown high diagnostic value for CC in recent studies,25 suggesting that multi-omics approaches—integrating genomics, metabolomics, and microbiome data—warrant further investigation for cervical precancer risk stratification.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the eQTL data were derived from blood samples, which may not fully capture cervical tissue-specific gene expression. Second, our findings were based on European-ancestry populations, limiting generalizability to other ethnic groups. Third, the small number of instrumental variables for ERBB2 and TCF19 (two SNPs each) and the influential SNP identified for NFKBIL1 warrant cautious interpretation of these specific associations. Fourth, while FDR correction was applied to minimize false positives, the possibility of type I errors cannot be excluded. Additionally, the modest effect sizes observed for most metabolites (OR < 1.15) suggest that individual metabolites are unlikely to serve as standalone clinical biomarkers—a key consideration for future translational efforts. Finally, given the exploratory nature of MR studies, all findings require replication in independent cohorts and functional validation. Despite these limitations, the convergence of our MR estimates with recent multi-omics and functional studies supports the credibility of the overall findings and provides a foundation for future mechanistic and biomarker research.

Conclusions

This MR study identified 11 genes and 11 circulating metabolites associated with HSIL risk, extending previous GWAS findings by applying a unidirectional two-sample MR framework and exploring metabolic mediation. COL11A2 emerged as a protective factor whose association was minimally mediated by circulating phospholipid metabolites, suggesting that local mechanisms—potentially involving ECM-receptor interaction and PI3K-Akt signaling—may underlie its effect. These findings provide candidates for future mechanistic and replication studies.
