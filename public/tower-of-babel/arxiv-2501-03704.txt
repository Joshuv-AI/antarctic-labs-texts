Spectral representation of correlation functions for zeros of Gaussian power series with stationary coefficients
Tomoyuki SHIRAI

Abstract
. We analyze Gaussian analytic functions (GAFs) defined as power series with coefficients modeled by discrete stationary Gaussian processes, utilizing their spectral measures. We revisit some limit theorems for random analytic functions and examine some examples of GAFs through numerical computations. Furthermore, we provide an integral representation of the n n -point correlation functions of the zero sets of GAFs in terms of the spectral measures of the underlying coefficient Gaussian processes.

1.
Introduction

Gaussian analytic functions (GAFs) have attracted significant
interest and have been extensively studied by many
researchers
(cf.
[
17
,
32
,
33
,
15
]
and
references therein).
One of the striking results in the
study of GAFs is that the zeros of random power series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta_{n}z^{n}
with i.i.d. complex Gaussian coefficients

\{\zeta_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, whose covariance kernel is
the Szegő kernel, form the determinantal point
process (DPP) on the unit disk associated with the Bergman
kernel
(1-z\bar{w})^{-2}

[
27
]
(see for details on DPPs (cf.
[
30
,
35
,
31
,
15
]
).
As a natural generalization of this model, various related
models have been studied.
The singular points of random matrix-valued Gaussian analytic
functions are shown to form the DPPs associated with weighted Bergman kernel
(1-z\bar{w})^{-\alpha}

[
21
]
.
The zeros of random power series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{\mathbb{R}}_{n}z^{n}
with i.i.d.
real
Gaussian coefficients

\{\zeta^{\mathbb{R}}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, which is the limiting
Kac random polynomial, form the Pfaffian point
process on the unit disk
[
11
,
22
]
.
The Gaussian Laurant series whose covariance kernel is
the weighted Szegő kernel on an annulus was studied in
[
18
]
.
We are concerned with the zeros of Gaussian
power series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\xi_{n}z^{n}
with coefficients being
stationary, centered, complex Gaussian
process
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
.
In
[
24
,
29
]
, we discussed the expected number of
zeros within a disk and the density of zeros in the radial
direction, observing their asymptotic behavior as the
radius approaches the circle of convergence.
Our observations reveal that the
spectral measure of the coefficient Gaussian process plays a
crucial role in determining the asymptotic behavior of the
number of zeros near the radius of convergence.
From the viewpoint of the spectral measure, the zeros of Gaussian
analytic functions in a strip in the complex plane, with
translation-invariant distribution, are studied in

[
9
,
10
]
.
This line of study traces back to the work of Paley and
Wiener
[
25
]
.
In
[
2
,
3
,
1
]
, the authors investigate random
power series and their zeros from the perspective of
spectral measures (or limiting empirical spectral measures in the case
of deterministic coefficients), considering cases where the coefficients are not necessarily Gaussian processes but
weakly stationary processes, and even in certain cases where
the coefficients are deterministic.

In this paper, we also emphasize the spectral perspective to study the zeros of GAFs.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we review the
Edelman-Kostlan formula for the
1
-point correlation
function and give its spectral representation. We also recall
some results of limit theorems for random analytic funcitons
established in
[
28
]
.
In Section
3
, we explore examples of
GAFs associated with three different spectral measures and present some numerical
computations. In Section
4
, we provide
a spectral representation of the
n
-point correlation
function for the zeros of a GAF whose covariance kernel is a
stationary complex Gaussian process. As an example of this
represetation result, we compute certain quantities related to the
i.i.d. coefficients case to illustrate the Peres-Virág theorem.

2.
Gaussian analytic functions and their spectral
representation

2.1.
Spectral representation of GAF and the intensity
of its zeros

Let
\Xi=\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a stationary, centered, complex Gaussian process with covariance

\gamma(k-l)=\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}\overline{\xi_{l}}].

Throughout the paper, we assume that
\gamma(0)=1
, i.e., the
distribution of each marginal
\xi_{k}
is
complex standard normal.
We consider the Gaussian power series with dependent
coefficients
\Xi=\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
defined by

X_{\Xi}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{k}z^{k}

and its zeros.
Since the radius of convergence of

X_{\Xi}(z)
is equal to
1
a.s. under
\gamma(0)=1
,
the zeros of
X_{\Xi}(z)
form a point process

\mathcal{Z}_{X_{\Xi}}:=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{D}:X_{\Xi}(z)=0}\delta_{z}

on
\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
.
We denote the covariance kernel of the GAF
\mathbb{X}=\{X_{\Xi}(z)\}_{z\in\mathbb{D}}
by
K(z,w)
.
Then, we have the spectral representation of
K(z,w)

[
29
]
as

K(z,w)=\mathbb{E}[X_{\Xi}(z)\overline{X_{\Xi}(w)}]=\int_{\mathbb{T}}s(z,t)\overline{s(w,t)}F(dt),

(2.1)

where
\mathbb{T}=(-\pi,\pi]
, the kernel
s(z,t)
is given by the
Szegő kernel

s(z,t)=\frac{1}{1-ze^{-it}}\quad(z\in\mathbb{D},t\in\mathbb{T})

(2.2)

and
F(dt)
is the spectral measure of the coefficient
Gaussian process
\Xi

[
7
]
, i.e.,

F(dt)
is a
probability
measure on
\mathbb{T}
(since

\gamma(0)=1
) such that

\gamma(k)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{-ikt}F(dt).

We denote the right-hand side of (
2.1
) by
K_{F}(z,w)
.
Since
|s(z,t)|^{2}=(1-2r\cos(\theta-t)+r^{2})^{-1}
for
z=re^{i\theta}
,
the covariance kernel
K_{F}(z,z)
for
z=re^{i\theta}
can be
expressed as

K_{F}(z,z)=\frac{1}{1-r^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}P(r,\theta-t)F(dt),

(2.3)

where
P(r,\theta)
is the Poisson kernel given by

P(r,\theta)=\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-2r\cos\theta+r^{2}}.

(2.4)

The first intensity (or
1
-point correlation function)
of the zeros of a GAF can be computed from the
covariance kernel
K(z,w)
by the
Edelman-Kostlan formula
[
8
]
as follows:

Theorem 2.1

(Edelman-Kostlan)
.

Let
X=(X(z))_{z\in D}
be a GAF with convariance
K(z,w)=\mathbb{E}[X(z)\overline{X(w)}]
. Then, the first intensity of
zeros of
X(z)
is given by the formula

\rho_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{w}}\log K(z,w)\Big|_{w=z}.

When
K(z,w)
is of the form (
2.1
), using the fact
that
\partial_{z}s(z,t)=e^{-it}s(z,t)^{2}
and
the symmetrization of the variable
t
, we can see that

\rho_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi K(z,z)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|e^{-it}-e^{-iu}|^{2}|s(z,t)s(z,u)|^{4}F(dt)F(du)\quad(z\in\mathbb{D}).

(2.5)

In Section
4
, we will provide an integral
representation of the
n
-point correlation function of the zeros.

2.2.
Convergence of random analytic functions and
their zero processes

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a domain and
\mathcal{H}(D)
be the space of
complex analytic functions on
D
equipped with the metric

\rho(f,g)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{j}}\frac{\|f-g\|_{K_{j}}}{1+\|f-g\|_{K_{j}}},

where
\|f\|_{K}
is the supremum norm of
f
on a compact set

K
and
K_{1}\subset K_{2}\subset\dots\nearrow D
is an
exhaustion of
D
by compact sets
K_{j}
. The metric space

(\mathcal{H}(D),\rho)
turns out to be complete and separable.
We denote the totality of probability
measures on
\mathcal{H}(D)
by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{H}(D))
.
We call an
\mathcal{H}(D)
-valued random variable a
random analytic
function
. We recall the following fact established in

[
28
, Proposition 2.5 with Remark on page 341]
).

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a domain and
\{X_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of random analytic
functions on
D
. If
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[|X_{n}(z)|^{p}]
is locally
integrable on
D
for some
p>0
, then
\{\mu_{X_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is tight in

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{H}(D))
. Moreover, if
\{X_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

converges to
X
in the sense of finite-dimensional
distributions, then
\{\mu_{X_{n}}\}
converges weakly to
\mu_{X}
as

n\to\infty
, or equivalently,
\{X_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

converges to
X
in law.

We also recall the convergence of zero processes
[
28
, Proposition 2.3]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

A sequence of random analytic functions

\{X_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

converges in law to
X
. Then, the zero process
\mathcal{Z}_{X_{n}}

converges in law to
\mathcal{Z}_{X}
provided that
X\not\equiv 0
almost surely.

We apply these results to our current situation.
Let
X_{F}=\{X_{F}(z)\}_{z\in\mathbb{D}}
be the GAF corresponding
to the covariance kernel

K_{F}(z,w)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}s(z,t)\overline{s(w,t)}F(dt).

(2.6)

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
F_{n}(dt),n=1,2,\dots
and
F(dt)
be probability
measures on
\mathbb{T}
and suppose that
\{F_{n}(dt)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

converges weakly to
F(dt)
.
Then,
X_{F_{n}}
corresponding to
F_{n}
converges in law to

X_{F}
corresponding to
F
. Moreover, the zero process

\mathcal{Z}_{X_{F_{n}}}
converges in law to that of
\mathcal{Z}_{X_{F}}
.

Proof.

The local integrability of
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[|X_{F_{n}}(z)|^{2}]

follows from the fact that
\mathbb{E}[|X_{F_{n}}(z)|^{2}]=K_{F_{n}}(z,z)\leq(1-|z|)^{-2}

for every
n
. Since
s(z,t)
is bounded continuous in
t

for every
z\in\mathbb{D}
, by the weak convergence of

\{F_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
to
F
,
K_{F_{n}}(z,w)
converges to

K_{F}(z,w)
for every
z,w\in\mathbb{D}
. This implies that

X_{F_{n}}
converges to
X_{F}
in the sense of
finite-dimensional distributions.
Therefore,
X_{F}
converges in law to
X
by Proposition
2.2
.
The zero processes
\mathcal{Z}_{F_{n}},n=1,2,\dots
converges in law
to
\mathcal{Z}_{F}
Proposition
2.3
since
X_{F}\not\equiv 0
almost surely.
∎

3.
Examples

Using Propositions
2.2
,
2.3
and Corollary
2.4
,
we discuss three examples.

3.1.
The case
F(dt)=dt/(2\pi)

In this case, we easily see that
\gamma_{F}(k)=\delta_{k,0}

and the kernel
K_{F}(z,w)
is given by

K_{F}(z,w)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}s(z,t)\overline{s(w,t)}\frac{dt}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{1-z\bar{w}}\quad(z,w\in\mathbb{D}),

which is also called the Szegő kernel.
The kernel
s(z,t)
is the boundary value of
(1-z\bar{w})^{-1}
at
w=e^{it}
.
This spectral measure corresponds to the
i.i.d. coefficients case.
This GAF is sometimes called the hyperbolic GAF and here denoted
by
X_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z)
.
The zeros of
X_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z)
form the DPP associated
with Bergman kernel
\pi^{-1}(1-z\bar{w})^{-2}

[
27
]
.
In Subsection
4.2
, we will see
a proof of this fact as an example of the spectral representation of

n
-point correlation function of the zeros of GAF (Theorem
4.5
).
The expected number of zeros in a domain
D\subset\mathbb{D}
is
the maximum among the GAFs with the covariance of the form
(
2.6
)
[
24
]
.
In Fig.
1
, we show the zeros of the approximate polynomial
X_{\mathrm{hyp}}^{(N)}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\zeta_{k}z^{k}
. The covariance function is given by

K_{F}^{(N)}(z,w)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}(z\bar{w})^{k}=\frac{1-(z\bar{w})^{N+1}}{1-z\bar{w}}.

(3.1)

By Theorem
2.1
with (
3.1
), the density of zeros inside
\mathbb{D}
is given by

\rho_{1,F}^{(N)}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\left\{\frac{1}{(1-|z|^{2})^{2}}-\frac{(N+1)^{2}|z|^{2N}}{(1-|z|^{2(N+1)})^{2}}\right\}.

(3.2)

The expected number of zeros within
\mathbb{D}_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<r\}
is

\int_{\mathbb{D}_{r}}\rho_{1,F}^{(N)}(z)dz=\int_{0}^{r^{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{(1-t)^{2}}-\frac{(N+1)^{2}t^{N}}{(1-t^{N+1})^{2}}\Big)dt=\frac{r^{2}}{1-r^{2}}-\frac{(N+1)r^{2(N+1)}}{1-r^{2(N+1)}}.

Then, by taking the limit
r\to 1
, we see that

\int_{\mathbb{D}}\rho_{1,F}^{(N)}(z)dz=\frac{N}{2}.

On average, half of the zeros of
X_{\mathrm{hyp}}^{(N)}(z)

are inside
\mathbb{D}
, and half are outside. See the left panel of
Fig.
1
.
Since
K_{F}^{(N)}(z,z)\leq K_{F}(z,z)
,

\sup_{N}\mathbb{E}{|X_{\mathrm{hyp}}^{(N)}(z)|^{2}}
is locally bounded, and
also
K_{F}^{(N)}(z,w)
converges to
K_{F}(z,w)
. Then, by
Propositions
2.2
and
2.3
,
we have that
X_{\mathrm{hyp}}^{(N)}(z)
converges to
X_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z)
in law as
N\to\infty
, and its zero process restricted on
\mathbb{D}
converges in law to that
of
X_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z)
, the DPP associated with Bergman kernel.

X_{\mathrm{hyp}}^{(100)}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{100}\xi_{k}z^{k}

3.2.
The case
F_{n}(dt)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta_{2k\pi/n}(dt)

We consider the case where
F_{n}(dt)
is atomic and
supported on the
n
-points
\{e^{2k\pi i/n}\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
.
In this case,

\gamma_{F_{n}}(k)\equiv\begin{cases}1&\text{$k\equiv 0\mod n$}\\
0&\text{$k\not\equiv 0\mod n$}\end{cases}\iff\xi_{pn+k}=\xi_{k}

where
\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n-1}
are
independent, and
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is periodic with
period
n
.
The covariance kernel of
X_{F_{n}}(z)
is given by

K_{F_{n}}(z,w)=\frac{1}{(1-z^{n})(1-\bar{w}^{n})}\cdot\frac{1-(z\bar{w})^{n}}{1-z\bar{w}}.

(3.3)

It is easy to see that
X_{F_{n}}(z)
is meromorphic
and its meromorphic extension is expressed as

X_{F_{n}}(z)=\frac{1}{1-z^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\xi_{k}z^{k}.

(3.4)

Therefore,
X_{F_{n}}(z)
has poles at

z=e^{2\pi ik/n}\ (k=0,1,\dots,n-1)
and the zeros
at those of the random polynomial
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\xi_{k}z^{k}
in
\mathbb{C}
, which is the same as in the previous example
with
N=n-1
.
This is evident from the form of
X_{F_{n}}(z)
in
(
3.4
), but when applying the Edelman-Kostlan
formula to (
3.3
), the preceding factor in
(
3.3
) vanishes due to the differentiation

\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{w}}
and does not
contribute, so the density of zeros inside
\mathbb{D}
remains the same as in
the previous example and is given (
3.2
) with
N=n-1
.
Half of zeros of the meromorphic extension appear inside

\mathbb{D}
on average.
We consider the finite approximation
X_{F_{n}}^{(N)}(z)
up to the degree
nN-1

X_{F_{n}}^{(N)}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{nN-1}\xi_{k}z^{k}=\frac{1-z^{nN}}{1-z^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\xi_{k}z^{k}=(1-z^{nN})X_{F_{n}}(z).

Therefore, there are zeros
\exp(2\pi ik/(nN)),k=0,1,\dots,nN-1
on the unit circle as well as the zeros
of
X_{F_{n}}(z)
.
These virtual zeros that appear in the finite
approximation are not observed in the limit
N\to\infty
.

As
n\to\infty
,

F_{n}(dt)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta_{2k\pi/n}(dt)
converges to
the Lebesgue probability measure
F(dt)=dt/(2\pi)
.
By Corollary
2.4
,
X_{F_{n}}
on

\mathbb{D}
converges in law to
X_{F}=X_{\mathrm{hyp}}
as
n\to\infty
.

3.3.
The case
F(dt)=\mathbf{1}_{[-\pi/2,\pi/2]}(t)dt/\pi

When
F(dt)=\mathbf{1}_{[-\pi/2,\pi/2]}(t)dt/\pi
, the covariance
function is given by

\gamma_{F}(k)=\begin{cases}1&k=0,\\
\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\sin(k\pi/2)}{k}&k\not=0.\end{cases}

In this case, the coefficient Gaussian process
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}

exhibits long-range correlation.
Indeed, it is known that a stationary Gaussian process
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is purely-nondeterministic if and only if
F(ds)

is absolutely continuous and its density
f(s)=F(ds)/ds
satisfies

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log f(s)ds>-\infty
, which clearly fails in
this case. This example is also discussed in
[
29
, Example 2.4]
.

From (
2.3
), the covariance kernel is given as the harmonic extension of
the spectral density
f(s)
, i.e., for
z=re^{i\theta}
,

\displaystyle K_{F}(z,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-r^{2}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}P(r,\theta-t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-r^{2}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\Big(\frac{2r}{1-r^{2}}\cos\theta\Big)\right\},

where
P(r,\theta)
is the Poisson kernel in
(
2.4
).
Here we carefully used the formula

\frac{d}{dt}2\arctan\left(\frac{1+r}{1-r}\tan\frac{t}{2}\right)=P(r,t).

By using (
2.5
),
it is not difficult to see that

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(re^{i\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi(1-r^{2})^{2}}\times\left\{1-\frac{\frac{\left(1-r^{2}\right)^{2}}{1+2r^{2}\cos 2\theta+r^{4}}}{\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+\arctan\left(\frac{2r}{1-r^{2}}\cos\theta\right)\right)^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\pi(1-r^{2})^{2}}\times\displaystyle\left\{1-\frac{1}{4\pi\cos^{2}\theta}(1-r^{2})^{2}\right\}+O((1-r^{2})^{3})&\theta\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle 1-(\frac{2}{\pi})^{2}&\theta=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{12\cos^{2}\theta}(1-r^{2})^{2}+O((1-r^{2})^{3})&\theta\in(-\pi,\pi)\setminus[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}],\end{cases}

as
r\to 1
. See Fig.
2
for

\rho_{1}(re^{i\theta})
.

Figure 2.
The graph of
g_{r}(\theta)=\pi(1-r^{2})^{2}\rho_{1}(re^{i\theta})
for

While the expected number of zeros is
O((1-r^{2})^{-2})
as
r\to 1
,
it is extremely small; indeed,
O(1)
in the direction of
e^{i\theta}
with
\theta\in(-\pi,\pi)\setminus[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
, where the spectral density
vanishes. This implies that the boundary behavior of the

1
-intensity of zeros heavily depends on the boundary value
of the spectral density
[
29
]
.
See the right panel in Fig.
1
.

For the simulation shown in the right panel of
Fig.
1
, we approximate
X_{F}(z)
using the diagonalization of the
coefficient Gaussian process.
We take the unique positive-definite square root
A
of the positive-definite,
symmetric matrix
K_{F}=(\gamma(i-j))_{i,j=0}^{\infty}
,
i.e.,
K_{F}=A^{2}
. Then,

\Xi=\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
can be constructed as
follows:

\xi_{i}:=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}A_{ij}\zeta_{j},

where
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
are i.i.d complex
standard normal.
Here, we approximate
X_{F}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\xi_{i}z^{i}
by the double sum

X_{F}^{(N)}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{N}A_{ij}\zeta_{j}\right)z^{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\zeta_{j}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N}A_{ij}z^{i}\right).

It is easy to see that the covariance kernel is given by

K_{F}^{(N)}(z,z)=\sum_{i,k=0}^{N}(A|_{[0,N]}^{2})_{ik}z^{i}\bar{z}^{k},

where
A|_{[0,N]}
is the submatrix whose rows and columns are
restricted on the indices
\{0,1,\dots,N\}
. Let
P^{(N)}
be the
projection operator that maps
(x_{0},x_{1},\dots)
to

(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{N},0,0,\dots)
, i.e., the restriction onto
the set
\{0,1,\dots,N\}
. Then,

K_{F}^{(N)}(z,z)=\langle P^{(N)}AP^{(N)}AP^{(N)}\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}\rangle=\|P^{(N)}AP^{(N)}\mathbf{z}\|^{2}\leq\|A\mathbf{z}\|^{2}=K_{F}(z,z),

where
\mathbf{z}=\{1,z,z^{2},\dots\}
with
z\in\mathbb{D}
, from which we
can see that
\sup_{N}E[|X_{F}^{(N)}(z)|^{2}]=\sup_{N}K_{F}^{(N)}(z,z)
is locally
integrable. Since
K_{F}^{(N)}(z,w)\to K_{F}(z,w)
for every

z,w\in\mathbb{D}
, by Propositions
2.2
and

2.3
, we have that
\{X_{F}^{(N)}(z)\}_{z\in\mathbb{D}}
converges in law to
\{X_{F}(z)\}_{z\in\mathbb{D}}
, and
hence the zero process
\mathcal{Z}_{X_{F}^{(N)}}
converges
in law to
\mathcal{Z}_{X_{F}}
as
N\to\infty
.

The zeros of
X_{F}^{(N)}(z)
were computed repeatedly for
N=200
as shown in Fig.

In the left half-plane, the zeros are almost evenly
distributed near the unit circle, whereas in the right

4.
Spectral representation of
n
-point correlation function

Using the Edelman-Kostlan formula, the 1-correlation
function is expressed in terms of the covariance kernel. In
Section 2, we provided an integral representation (
2.5
) of the

1
-correlation function involving the spectral measure

F(dt)
. In this section, we extend this approach to derive an
integral representation of the
n
-point correlation function for
the zeros of the GAF, also in terms of the spectral measure

F(dt)
.

We define the conditional kernel
K^{a}(z,w)
by

K^{a}(z,w)=K(z,w)-\frac{K(z,a)K(a,w)}{K(a,a)}=\frac{1}{K(a,a)}\begin{vmatrix}K(z,w)&K(z,a)\\
K(a,w)&K(a,a)\end{vmatrix}

(4.1)

whenever
K(a,a)>0
, and inductively,
K^{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}}(z,w)=(K^{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n-1}})^{a_{n}}(z,w)
. Note that

K^{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}}(z,w)
can also be represented as the ratio
of determinants, i.e.,

K^{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}}(z,w)=\Big(\det(K(a_{j},a_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}\Big)^{-1}\det(K(a_{j},a_{k}))_{j,k=0}^{n}\Big|_{\text{$a_{0}=z$ or $w$}},

(4.2)

where “
a_{0}=z
or
w
” means that
K(a_{0},a_{k})=K(z,a_{k})
and

K(a_{j},a_{0})=K(a_{j},w)
for
j,k=1,2,\dots,n
, and

K(a_{0},a_{0})=K(z,w)
. When we denote by
\mathcal{H}_{K}
the reproducing kernel
Hilbert space corresponding to
K
,

\mathcal{H}_{K^{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}}
is given by

\mathcal{H}_{K^{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}:f(a_{j})=0\text{
for $j=1,2\dots,n$}\}.

This corresponds to the conditional GAF
\{X(z)\}_{z\in\mathbb{D}}
given that
X(a_{1})=X(a_{2})=\cdots=X(a_{n})=0
.

Now we recall the following formula for the
n
-point correlation
function of the zeros of GAF (cf.
[
15
, Corollary 3.4.2]
).
For our convenience, we use the same formula in a slightly
different form as presented in
[
28
, Proposition 6.1]
,

Proposition 4.1
.

The
n
-point correlation function of the zeros of GAF with covariance kernel
K(z,w)

is given by the formula

\rho_{n}(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})=\frac{\per(\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{w}}K^{z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n}}(z_{j},z_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}}{\det(\pi K(z_{j},z_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}}

for distinct
z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n}\in D
with

\det(K(z_{j},z_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}>0
, where
\per A

is the permanent of an
n
by
n
matrix

A=(a_{jk})_{j,k=1}^{n}
defined by

\per A=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}a_{j\sigma(j)},

where
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is the symmetric group of order
n
.

This formula follows from the fact that

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[|X^{\prime}(z_{1})X^{\prime}(z_{2})\cdots X^{\prime}(z_{n})|^{2}\ |\ X(z_{1})=X(z_{2})=\cdots=X(z_{n})=0]

and the second moment of the product of
complex
Gaussian random
variables is equal to the permanent of the covariance
matrix, i.e., if
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n})
is a centered
Gaussian vector with covariance matrix
K=(K_{jk})_{j,k=1}^{n}
, then

\mathbb{E}[|Y_{1}Y_{2}\cdots Y_{n}|^{2}]=\per K.

For
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
, let
V(\mathbf{a})
be the Vandermonde determinant defined by

V(\mathbf{a})=V(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}):=\det(a_{j}^{k-1})_{j,k=1}^{n}=\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(a_{k}-a_{j}).

Later, we will also use it in the form

\prod_{j,k=1\atop{k\not=j}}^{n}(a_{k}-a_{j})=(-1)^{{n\choose 2}}V(\mathbf{a})^{2}.

(4.3)

We recall the Cauchy determinant identity

\det\Big(\frac{1}{1-a_{j}b_{k}}\Big)_{j,k=1}^{n}=V(\mathbf{a})V(\mathbf{b})\prod_{j,k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1-a_{j}b_{k}}.

(4.4)

For
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
,
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{n})
, and

\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{m})
(possibly with different
n
and
m
), we define

D(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{t})=\det(s(a_{j},t_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n},\quad S(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{t})=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\prod_{k=1}^{n}s(z_{j},t_{k}).

In what follows in this section, the elements of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{z}
are assumed
to belong to
\mathbb{D}
.

From (
2.2
) and the Cauchy identity (
4.4
), we have

D(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{t})=V(\mathbf{a})V(e^{-it_{1}},\dots,e^{-it_{n}})S(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{t}).

(4.5)

Lemma 4.2
.

For
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
and
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{n})
,
we have

\det(K(a_{j},a_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}=|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}\mu_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n}),

(4.6)

where

\mu_{\mathbf{a}}(d\mathbf{t})=\frac{1}{n!}|V(e^{-it_{1}},e^{-it_{2}},\dots,e^{-it_{n}})|^{2}\cdot|S(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{t})|^{2}F^{\otimes n}(d\mathbf{t}).

(4.7)

Proof.

By (
2.1
)
and the symmetrization of the variable
\mathbf{t}
, we see that

\displaystyle\det(K(a_{j},a_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sgn(\sigma)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}s(a_{\sigma(k)},t_{k})\cdot\overline{\prod_{k=1}^{n}s(a_{k},t_{k})}F^{\otimes n}(d\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\det(s(a_{j},t_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}\overline{\Big(\prod_{k=1}^{n}s(a_{k},t_{k})\Big)}F^{\otimes n}(d\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\det(s(a_{j},t_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}\overline{\det(s(a_{j},t_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}}F^{\otimes n}(d\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}|D(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{t})|^{2}F^{\otimes n}(d\mathbf{t}).

The assertion follows directly from the identity (
4.5
).
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{U}(n)
denote the compact Lie group of
n\times n

unitary matrices and let
P_{n}
be the Haar measure on it.
The pair
(\mathcal{U}(n),P_{n})
is called the circular unitary ensemble CUE
n
.
The eigenvalues of CUE
n
are known to form the
n
-point DPP
on
\mathbb{T}
associated with the rank
n
projection kernel

\tilde{k}_{n}(\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{ik\theta}\overline{e^{ik\varphi}}=u(\theta)\frac{\sin\frac{n(\theta-\varphi)}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta-\varphi}{2}}\overline{u(\varphi)}=:u(\theta)k_{n}(\theta,\varphi)\overline{u(\varphi)},

where
u(\theta)=e^{i(n-1)\theta/2}
and with the background
measure being the Lebesgue probability measure on
\mathbb{T}
.
The probability measure on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
with density
\mu_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{t})/\mu_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
can be
regarded as the
n
-point DPP associated with the
correlation kernel
k_{n}(\theta,\varphi)
and the background measure

|S(\mathbf{a},t)|^{2}F(dt)=\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1-r_{j}^{2}}\Big)\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}P(r_{j},\varphi_{j}-t)\Big)F(dt),

where
a_{j}=r_{j}e^{i\varphi_{j}}
and
P(r,\theta)
is the
Poisson kernel in (
2.4
).
We denote this
n
-point DPP on
\mathbb{T}
by
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
and
\tilde{\mathbf{t}}=(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
,

\displaystyle\det(K(a_{j},a_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}\cdot K^{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}(z,w)

\displaystyle=|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z-a_{i})\overline{(w-a_{i})}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n+1}}S(z,\tilde{\mathbf{t}})\overline{S(w,\tilde{\mathbf{t}})}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(d\tilde{\mathbf{t}})

\displaystyle=|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n+1})\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z-a_{i})\overline{(w-a_{i})}\cdot K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(z,w)

where

\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(d\tilde{\mathbf{t}})=\frac{1}{(n+1)!}|V(e^{-it_{0}},e^{-it_{1}},\dots,e^{-it_{n}})|^{2}|S(\mathbf{a},\tilde{\mathbf{t}})|^{2}F^{\otimes(n+1)}(d\tilde{\mathbf{t}}).

and

K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(z,w)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}[S(z,\tilde{\mathbf{t}})\overline{S(w,\tilde{\mathbf{t}})}],

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}
is the expectation with respect to

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}
defined as in Remark
4.3
.

Proof.

As in the previous calculation in the proof of
Lemma
4.2
, by (
4.2
), it follows that

\displaystyle Q_{n}(z,w)

\displaystyle:=\det(K(a_{j},a_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}K^{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\det(K(a_{j},a_{k}))_{j,k=0}^{n}\Big|_{\text{$a_{0}=z$ or $w$}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n+1}}\det(s(a_{i},t_{j}))_{i,j=0}^{n}\Big|_{a_{0}=z}\cdot\overline{\det(s(a_{i},t_{j}))_{i,j=0}^{n}}\Big|_{a_{0}=w}F^{\otimes n+1}(dt_{0}d\mathbf{t}).

Setting
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}=(a_{0},\mathbf{a})
, by
(
4.4
), we have

\displaystyle\det(s(a_{j},t_{k}))_{j,k=0}^{n}\Big|_{a_{0}=z}

\displaystyle=V(\tilde{\mathbf{a}})V(e^{-it_{0}},e^{-it_{1}},\dots,e^{-it_{n}})S(\tilde{\mathbf{a}},\tilde{\mathbf{t}})\Big|_{a_{0}=z}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(a_{j}-z)\cdot V(\mathbf{a})V(e^{-it_{0}},e^{-it_{1}},\dots,e^{-it_{n}})S(z,\tilde{\mathbf{t}})S(\mathbf{a},\tilde{\mathbf{t}}).

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle Q_{n}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n+1)!}|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-a_{j})\overline{(w-a_{j})}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n+1}}S(z,\tilde{\mathbf{t}})\overline{S(w,\tilde{\mathbf{t}})}\cdot|V(e^{-it_{0}},e^{-it_{1}},\dots,e^{-it_{n}})|^{2}|S(\mathbf{a},\tilde{\mathbf{t}})|^{2}F^{\otimes(n+1)}(d\tilde{\mathbf{t}}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Now we compute the
n
-correlation function
\rho_{n}(\mathbf{a})

using Proposition
4.1
, Lemmas
4.2
and
4.4
.

Theorem 4.5
.

For pairwise distinct
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{D}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n+1})^{n}}{\mu_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})^{n+1}}\per(K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(a_{p},a_{q}))_{p,q=1}^{n}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.4
, we note that

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{w}}Q_{n}(z,w))|_{z=a_{p},w=a_{q}}

\displaystyle=|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n+1})\left(\prod_{i=1\atop{i\not=p}}^{n}(a_{p}-a_{i})\overline{\prod_{j=1\atop{j\not=q}}^{n}(a_{q}-a_{j})}\right)K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(a_{p},a_{q}).

Then, we see that

\displaystyle\per((\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{w}}Q_{n}(z,w))|_{z=a_{p},w=a_{q}})

\displaystyle=\left\{|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n+1})\right\}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\prod_{p=1}^{n}\left(\prod_{i=1\atop{i\not=p}}^{n}(a_{p}-a_{i})\overline{\prod_{j=1\atop{j\not=\sigma(p)}}^{n}(a_{\sigma(p)}-a_{j})}\right)K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(a_{p},a_{\sigma(p)})

\displaystyle=|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2n+4}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n+1})^{n}\per\big(K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(a_{p},a_{q})_{p,q=1}^{n}\big).

Here, we used (
4.3
) in the second equality.
Therefore, by Lemma
4.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{\per((\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{w}}K^{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}(z,w))|_{z=a_{p},w=a_{q}})}{\det(\pi K)}=\frac{\per((\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{w}}Q_{n}(z,w))|_{z=a_{p},w=a_{q}})}{\pi^{n}(\det K)^{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n+1})^{n}}{\pi^{n}\mu_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})^{n+1}}\per(K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(a_{p},a_{q})_{p,q=1}^{n}).

This is the desired formula.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Theorem
4.5
shows that the

n
-correlation function includes the factor of squared Vandermonde determinant

|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}
.
For
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
, we consider a monic polynomial

p(z)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(z-a_{k})
and suppose it has the coeffcients
e_{k},k=0,1,\dots,n-1
, i.e.,
p(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}e_{k}z^{k}

with
e_{n}=1
. Then the transformation
T:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by
T(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})=(e_{n-1},e_{n-2},\dots,e_{0})
has Jacobian determinant
|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}

(cf.
[
15
]
), which is reflected in the theorem. See also

[
23
,
13
]
.

4.1.
Reproducing formula

We present a multi-dimensional reproducing formula of the
Cauchy type, which appears in the computation in
Section
4.2
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let

C(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{a}):=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{z_{k}-a_{j}}.

Then, for an analytic function
Q(\mathbf{z})
that is symmetric in its variables,

\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{n}\oint_{C^{n}}V(\mathbf{z})^{2}C(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{a})Q(\mathbf{z})\prod_{k=1}^{n}dz_{k}=(-1)^{{n\choose 2}}n!\cdot Q(\mathbf{a}),

(4.8)

and for pairwise distinct
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{n}\oint_{C^{n}}V(\mathbf{z})^{2}C(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{a})Q(\mathbf{z})\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{dz_{k}}{z_{k}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{{n\choose 2}}n!\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{a_{j}}\cdot\Big\{Q(\mathbf{a})-\sum_{p=1}^{n}\prod_{k=1\atop{k\not=p}}^{n}\frac{a_{k}}{a_{k}-a_{p}}Q(0,\mathbf{a}^{(p)})\Big\},

(4.9)

where
C=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
and
\mathbf{a}^{(p)}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p-1},a_{p+1},\dots,a_{n})
.

Proof.

Due to the presence of the Vandermonde term, the residue is
zero unless the points
(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})
are all distinct and
form a permutation of
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
.
Since the integral is symmetric in
(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})
, it suffices
to consider the residue at
(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
and multiply it by
n!
. The residue
is given by

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{a})^{2}\prod_{k=1\atop{k\not=j}}^{n}(a_{k}-a_{j})^{-1}Q(\mathbf{a})

and thus, using (
4.3
), we obtain (
4.8
).

Similarly,
the residue is zero unless the points
(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})
are all distinct and
form a permutation of
\{0,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}\setminus\{a_{p}\}
for some
p=1,2,\dots,n
. Since the
integral is symmetric in
(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})
, it suffices
to consider the residue at
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{a}
or
\mathbf{z}=(0,\mathbf{a}^{(p)})

for
p=1,2,\dots,n
and multiply it by
n!
.
For
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{a}
, the first term in (
4.9
)
is obtained in the same manner as above with the extra term

\prod_{k=1}^{n}1/a_{k}
.
Since
\displaystyle V(0,\mathbf{a}^{(p)})=(\prod_{j=1\atop{j\not=p}}^{n}a_{j})V(\mathbf{a}^{(p)})
, the residue at
\mathbf{z}=(0,\mathbf{a}^{(p)})
is given
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}_{\mathbf{z}=(0,\mathbf{a}^{(p)})}

\displaystyle=(\prod_{j=1\atop{j\not=p}}^{n}a_{j})^{2}V(\mathbf{a}^{(p)})^{2}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{-a_{j}}\cdot\prod_{k=1\atop{k\not=p}}^{n}\prod_{j=1\atop{j\not=k}}^{n}\frac{1}{a_{k}-a_{j}}\cdot\prod_{k=1\atop{k\not=p}}^{n}\frac{1}{a_{k}}\cdot Q(0,\mathbf{a}^{(p)})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{{n\choose 2}+1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{a_{j}}\prod_{k=1\atop{k\not=p}}^{n}\frac{a_{k}}{a_{k}-a_{p}}\cdot Q(0,\mathbf{a}^{(p)}).

Therefore, we obtain (
4.9
).
∎

4.2.
The case
F(dt)=dt/(2\pi)

Let us consider the i.i.d. case, i.e.,
F(dt)=dt/(2\pi)

and
X_{F}(z)=X_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z)
.
In the following lemma, by a slight abuse of notation,
we write
S(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w})=\prod_{j,k=1}^{n}s(z_{j},w_{k})=\prod_{j,k=1}^{n}(1-z_{j}\bar{w}_{k})^{-1}

using the same symbol
S
for the Szegő kernel
S(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{t})
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose
F(dt)=dt/(2\pi)
. Then,

\mu_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})=\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n+1})=S(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}),\quad K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(z,w)=S(z,w)S(z,\mathbf{a})\overline{S(w,\mathbf{a})}.

Proof.

By (
4.7
), the definition of
\mu_{\mathbf{a}}
, we see
that

\displaystyle n!\mu_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}|V(e^{-it_{1}},\cdots,e^{-it_{n}})|^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left|\frac{1}{1-a_{j}e^{-it_{k}}}\right|^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{dt_{k}}{2\pi}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{n}\oint_{C^{n}}|V(z_{1}^{-1},\cdots,z_{n}^{-1})|^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left|\frac{1}{1-a_{j}z_{k}^{-1}}\right|^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{dz_{k}}{z_{k}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{{n\choose 2}}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{n}\oint_{C^{n}}V(\mathbf{z})^{2}C(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{a})S(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{a})\prod_{k=1}^{n}dz_{k},

where we used
\bar{z}_{k}=z_{k}^{-1}
on
C
and the following identity:

V(z_{1}^{-1},\cdots,z_{n}^{-1})=(-1)^{{n\choose 2}}\frac{V(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n})}{(z_{1}\cdots z_{n})^{n-1}}.

Therefore, by (
4.8
), we have

\mu_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})=S(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})
.

Similarly as above, setting
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=(z_{0},z_{1},\dots,z_{n})

and
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{z}=(z,\mathbf{a})=(z,a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and using (
4.8
), we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n+1})K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n+1}}S(z,\tilde{\mathbf{t}})\overline{S(w,\tilde{\mathbf{t}})}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(d\tilde{\mathbf{t}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n+1}}|V(e^{-it_{0}},\cdots,e^{-it_{n}})|^{2}\prod_{k=0}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left|\frac{1}{1-a_{j}e^{-it_{k}}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{1-ze^{-it_{k}}}\overline{\frac{1}{1-we^{-it_{k}}}}\prod_{k=0}^{n}\frac{dt_{k}}{2\pi}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{{n+1\choose 2}}\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{n+1}\oint_{C^{n+1}}V(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})^{2}C(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{z})S(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{w})\prod_{k=0}^{n}dz_{k}

\displaystyle=S(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{z},\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{w}).

Hence,
K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(z,w)=S(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{z},\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{w})/\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(T^{n+1})
.
Since the left-hand side is
1
when
z=w=0

by the definition of
K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(z,w)
, we see that
\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbb{T}^{n+1})=S(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{0},\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{0})=S(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})
, and thus,

K_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n+1)}(z,w)=S(z,w)S(z,\mathbf{a})\overline{S(w,\mathbf{a})}
.
∎

Now we compute the
n
-correlation function of the zeros for

X_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z)
.
By Theorem
4.5
together with Lemma
4.8
, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}\frac{1}{S(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})}\per(S(a_{p},a_{q})S(a_{p},\mathbf{a})\overline{S(a_{q},\mathbf{a})})_{p,q=1}^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}|V(\mathbf{a})|^{2}S(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})\per(S(a_{p},a_{q}))_{p,q=1}^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\det(S(a_{p},a_{q}))_{p,q=1}^{n}\cdot\per(S(a_{p},a_{q}))_{p,q=1}^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\det(S(a_{p},a_{q})^{2})_{p,q=1}^{n}.

We used the Cauchy determinant formula
(
4.4
) for the third equality and
Borchardt’s identity (see
[
15
, Proposition 5.1.5]
)

\det\Big(\frac{1}{1-a_{p}b_{q}}\Big)_{p,q=1}^{n}\per\Big(\frac{1}{1-a_{p}b_{q}}\Big)_{p,q=1}^{n}=\det\Big(\frac{1}{(1-a_{p}b_{q})^{2}}\Big)_{p,q=1}^{n}

for the fourth equality.
We recovered the fact that the zeros of
X_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z)
is the
determinantal point process associated with the Bergman
kernel
S(z,w)^{2}
.

5.
Concluding remarks

We have discussed GAFs corresponding to three spectral
measures as examples in Section
3
. In
particular, for the polynomial approximation of the final example of GAFs, the zeros in
the left half-plane exhibited somewhat different behavior
compared to those in the right half-plane. Further detailed
analysis of this phenomenon is left as a topic for future
research.
The integral representation of the
n
-point correlation
functions in the final section is used only in the proof of
the Peres-Virág theorem. It may also have potential
applications in the detailed analysis of the two-point or
higher order correlation functions of the zeros of GAFs,
particularly for determining whether they exhibit negative
correlations.
Other topics such as central limit theorems
[
4
,
19
,
5
]
, hole probability
[
20
,
6
]
, rigidity

[
13
,
12
]
, and hyperuniformity
[
14
]
would
also be interesting to investigate in the context of our setting.

The complex autoregressive model
\mathrm{AR}(1)
model

\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is defined as the following stochastic difference equation

X_{t}=zX_{t-1}+\zeta_{t}\quad(t\in\mathbb{Z})

(5.1)

with i.i.d. complex-valued noise.
It is known that when
|z|<1
, there is a unique
stationary solution given by

X_{t}=X_{t}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\zeta_{t-k}z^{k}\quad(t\in\mathbb{Z}).

(5.2)

If the noise
\{\zeta_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is an i.i.d. sequence of

\{-1,1\}
-Bernoulli random variables, for each
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, the marginal
X_{t}(\lambda)
is equal in law to
the so-called Bernoulli convolution
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pm\lambda^{k}
, whose distribution
\nu_{\lambda}
is either absolutely
continuous or purely singular
[
16
]
and many
properties of
\nu_{\lambda}
have been studied (cf.
[
34
,
26
]
).
If the noise
\{\zeta_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is an i.i.d. sequence of
standard complex normal random variables, for each
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, the marginal
\{X_{t}(z)\}_{z\in\mathbb{D}}
is equal in law to
the hyperbolic GAF
\{X_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z)\}_{z\in\mathbb{D}}
.
Then
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
defines the GAF-valued
stationary process and thus, for each
t
,
we have the DPP-valued stationary process
\{\mathcal{Z}_{X_{t}}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
.
If we replace the i.i.d. Gaussian noise with a dependent
Gaussian noise, the stationary solution to
(
5.1
) still exists when
|z|<1
and is again given by
(
5.2
), which
defines the GAF-valued stationary process.
The one-dimensional marginal
\{X_{t}(z)\}_{z\in\mathbb{D}}
is
identical in law to the one discussed earlier in this paper.
It would be intriguing to explore the above-mentioned
stationary GAF-valued processes and their zero processes.
