MSC(2020): Primary 35P15. Secondary 35P20, 33C10, 11P21, 65G20. Keywords: Laplacian, eigenvalues, Weyl’s law, lattice points, Bessel functions, Bessel phase functions
Nikolay Filonov, Michael Levitin, Iosif Polterovich, David A. Sher

Abstract
We prove Pólya’s conjecture for the eigenvalues of the Dirichlet Laplacian on annular domains. Our approach builds upon and extends the methods we previously developed for disks and balls. It combines variational bounds, estimates of Bessel phase functions, refined lattice point counting techniques, and a rigorous computer-assisted analysis. As a by-product, we also derive a two-term upper bound for the Dirichlet eigenvalue counting function of the disk, improving upon Pólya’s original estimate.

§1.  Introduction and main results

§1.1.  Preliminaries

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain, and let

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{2}(\Omega)\leq\dots

be the eigenvalues of the Dirichlet Laplacian
-\Delta^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{\Omega}
in
\Omega
, enumerated with multiplicities. Denote by

\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{\Omega}(\lambda):=\#\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\lambda_{n}(\Omega)\leq\lambda^{2}\right\}

the corresponding eigenvalue counting function. By Weyl’s law, its asymptotics is given by

\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{\Omega}(\lambda)=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)}{4\pi}\lambda^{2}+o\left(\lambda^{2}\right)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\to+\infty.

(1.1)

More than seventy years ago, George Pólya
[
Pó54
]
conjectured that the leading term of the asymptotics (
1.1
) is in fact a uniform bound on
\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{\Omega}(\lambda)
for any
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
: namely, that for all
\lambda>0
,

\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{\Omega}(\lambda)<\frac{\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)}{4\pi}\lambda^{2}.

(1.2)

Pólya himself later proved this for domains which
tile
the plane
[
Pól61
]
. No other planar domains for which Pólya’s conjecture holds were known until very recently, when the authors of this paper proved it for disks and finite sectors, see
[
FLPS23
]
. That paper also gives a more comprehensive account of Pólya’s conjecture for the Dirichlet Laplacian and its counterpart in the Neumann case, as well as its higher-dimensional generalisations.

§1.2.  Main result

The goal of this article is to extend the proof of Pólya’s conjecture to annuli

A_{r}=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:r^{2}<x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}<1\right\}.

with inner radius
r\in(0,1)
and outer radius one. Note that any annulus is of this form up to rescaling. Note also that
r=0
corresponds to the punctured disk which has the same spectrum as the disk (see also
[
LMP23
, Example 2.2.23]
) and, therefore, is covered by the results of
[
FLPS23
]
.

For brevity, we will denote the eigenvalues of an annulus and its eigenvalue counting function by

\lambda_{r,n}:=\lambda_{n}(A_{r}),\qquad\mathcal{N}_{r}(\lambda):=\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{A_{r}}(\lambda).

Given that
\operatorname{Area}(A_{r})=\pi(1-r^{2})
,
Pólya’s conjecture (
1.2
) for annuli states that the inequality

\mathcal{N}_{r}(\lambda)<\frac{1-r^{2}}{4}\lambda^{2}

(1.3)

holds for all pairs
(r,\lambda)
in the parameter space

\mathrm{R}\Lambda:=\left\{(r,\lambda):0<r<1,\lambda>0\right\}.

It will be often convenient to work with the parameter

\mu:=r\lambda\in[0,\lambda)

instead of
r
. In terms of this parameter, the equivalent form of Pólya’s conjecture for the Dirichlet Laplacian on annuli is that the inequality

\mathcal{N}_{\frac{\mu}{\lambda}}(\lambda)<\frac{\lambda^{2}-\mu^{2}}{4}

(1.4)

holds for all pairs
(\lambda,\mu)
in the parameter space

\Lambda\mathrm{M}:=\left\{(\lambda,\mu):0<\mu<\lambda\right\}.

Our main result is

Theorem 1.1
.

Pólya’s conjecture for the Dirichlet Laplacian holds for all planar annuli. In other words,
inequality (
1.3
) holds for all
(r,\lambda)\in\mathrm{R}\Lambda
, or, equivalently, (
1.4
) holds for all
(\lambda,\mu)\in\Lambda\mathrm{M}
.

Note that Theorem
1.1
gives a first example of a non-simply connected planar domain for which Pólya’s conjecture has been verified.

Remark 1.2
.

The eigenvalues of planar annuli have also been investigated in
[
GMWW21
]
and more recently in
[
GJWY24
]
.
Using number-theoretic techniques, these papers improve remainder estimates in the two-term Weyl asymptotics for annuli.
Note that the two-term asymptotics implies that Pólya’s conjecture holds for sufficiently large (but unspecified)
values of
\lambda
. While the present paper shares with
[
GMWW21
,
GJWY24
]
the method of relating the eigenvalue count to a lattice point count, our results are in a way complementary, as we focus on accurate eigenvalue bounds rather than asymptotics.
∎

§1.3.  Ideas of the proof

The proof of Theorem
1.1
combines analytic arguments with a rigorous computer-assisted verification. We analytically prove Theorem
1.1
in five distinct but partially intersecting regions
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\aleph}
,
\aleph=\mathrm{I},\dots,\mathrm{V}
, of
\mathrm{R}\Lambda
. We postpone their exact definitions until later, but show their graphical representation in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Summary of all regions in
(r,\lambda)
-plane for which Pólya’s conjecture is analytically proven to be true, with two side figures zooming near the axes. The exact definitions of the regions can be found in Theorem
2.1
for
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{I}}
, Theorem
2.3
for
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{II}}
, Theorem
7.1
for
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{III}}
, Theorem
7.2
for
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{IV}}
, and Theorem
7.6
for
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{V}}
.

Different approaches are used to deal with each of these regions. In the region

\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{I}}
the result follows from the Faber–Krahn and Krahn–Szego isoperimetric inequalities for the first and the second Dirichlet eigenvalues. In the region
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{II}}
, we use a comparison between the counting functions for the annuli and for certain carefully chosen flat cylinders, see Lemma
2.2
. We then take advantage of the fact that the eigenvalues of these cylinders can be computed explicitly, see Theorem
2.3
. Note that the arguments in the first two regions are of purely spectral-theoretic nature.

The proofs in the remaining three regions are based on results concerning the Bessel phase functions as well as lattice-counting techniques. Here we extend the approach developed in
[
FLPS23
]
for the disk. In order to adapt it to the case of annuli we have to overcome several new technical challenges.

First, instead of estimating the Bessel phase function itself, we now need to bound the difference between the values of the phase functions at two distinct points, which are chosen depending on the spectral parameter and the inner radius of the annulus (recall that the outer radius is fixed and is equal to one). Some of these bounds follow from the results of
[
FLPS24b
]
, but some are new, see §
5
.

Second, we have to use a more elaborate reduction to a lattice counting problem. In the region
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{III}}
we count points below a single curve as in
[
FLPS23
]
. In the other two regions, in order to get accurate enough bounds, we need to split the interval
[0,\lambda]
into two (in
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{IV}}
) and three (in
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{V}}
) subintervals, respectively, and in each subinterval we count integer points under different curves, depending on which bound for the phase functions we are using (see Lemma
5.2

and Figure
6
).

Third, we need to improve upon the lattice counting techniques of
[
FLPS23
]
. Recall that

[
FLPS23
, Theorem 5.1]
provided a way to estimate the number of lattice points under a graph of a non-negative decreasing convex function with a Lipschitz constant
\frac{1}{2}
. We need to extend this bound to concave functions, as well as to relax the assumption on the Lipschitz constant. We obtain several results in this direction in §
3
. We believe they could be of independent interest, and for illustration we present one of them below.

The following definition is central for our analysis.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a<b
, and let
g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
. The
trapezoidal floor sum
of
g
on
[a,b]
is defined as

\mathbf{T}(g,a,b)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=a}^{b-1}\left(\left\lfloor g(m)\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor g(m+1)\right\rfloor\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor g(a)\right\rfloor+\sum_{m=a+1}^{b-1}\left\lfloor g(m)\right\rfloor+\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor g(b)\right\rfloor.

∎

For a non-negative function
g
, the trapezoidal floor sum
\mathbf{T}(g,a,b)
counts the number of points of the lattice
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}
lying on or under the graph of
g
on the interval
[a,b]
, with the exception that the lattice points with the edge abscissas
a
and
b
are counted with the weight
\frac{1}{2}
. A useful observation is that for an integer
p\in(a,b)
,

\mathbf{T}(g,a,b)=\mathbf{T}(g,a,p)+\mathbf{T}(g,p,b).

For example, we prove the following estimate on the trapezoidal floor sum for concave Lipschitz functions that is used in the region
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{V}}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\alpha<\beta
. Let
g
be a decreasing concave function on
[\alpha,\beta]

which is Lipschitz with a constant
c\in(0,1)
, and assume additionally that

\left\lfloor g(\alpha)\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor g(p)\right\rfloor>\left\lfloor g(p+1)\right\rfloor

for some integer
p\in[\alpha,\beta)
.
Then

\mathbf{T}(g,\alpha,\beta)\leq\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z-\frac{1-c}{2}(\beta-p).

Note that without the second term on the right, the bound is an easy consequence of concavity only, see Proposition
3.1
. The proof of Theorem
1.4
can be found in §
3
. For some related asymptotic estimates see also
[
LL18a
,
LL18b
]
.

For decreasing convex functions we prove a related result under the assumption that the function is Lipschitz with a constant
\frac{1}{2}
on the whole interval, and
additionally
is Lipschitz with a constant
\frac{1}{3}
on a subinterval, see Theorem
3.4
. One consequence of this is the following improvement of Pólya’s conjecture for the Dirichlet Laplacian on the disk
[
FLPS23
]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\mathbb{D}
be a unit disk. Then the following two-term Pólya-type bound for the Dirichlet Laplacian holds,

\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{\mathbb{D}}(\lambda)<\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}-\frac{\left\lfloor\omega_{0}\lambda\right\rfloor}{2},

(1.5)

where

\omega_{0}:=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}-\frac{1}{6}\approx 0.108998.

We prove Theorem
1.5
in §
7.2
.

The analytic results establishing Pólya’s conjecture for the regions
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\aleph}
can be equivalently formulated for the regions

\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\aleph}
,
\aleph=\mathrm{I},\dots\mathrm{V}
, see Figure
2
.
In fact, such a reformulation is more convenient for the computer-assisted part of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Figure 2:
Summary of all regions in
(\lambda,\mu)
-plane for which Pólya’s conjecture is analytically proven to be true. The bottom figure zooms near the
\lambda
-axis and the origin.

Set

\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{theory}}:=\bigcup_{\aleph=\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{V}}\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\aleph}.

Importantly, the remaining region

\Lambda\mathrm{M}\setminus\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{theory}}

is bounded, see Theorem
8.1
, and we implement a rigorous computer-assisted algorithm verifying Pólya’s conjecture there. Our method builds upon the approach of
[
FLPS23
, §8]
. We show that if a required inequality on the lattice count holds at a given point with some margin, then it holds in a certain explicitly described domain around this point, see Lemma
8.4
. This observation, together with the technique of verified rational approximations, allows us to rigorously check Pólya’s conjecture in a region
\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{comp}}\supset\Lambda\mathrm{M}\setminus~\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{theory}}
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

§1.4.  Plan of the paper

The paper is organised as follows. In §
2
we prove Pólya’s conjecture in the region
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{I}}
using isoperimetric inequalities for the first two Dirichlet eigenvalues, and in the region
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{II}}
via comparison with the spectra of flat cylinders.
In §
3
we present abstract results on the trapezoidal floor sums for concave and convex functions satisfying different assumptions on their Lipschitz constants. In §
4
we relate the eigenvalue counting function of an annulus to the difference of the Bessel phase functions. Bounds on these difference in terms of some elementary functions and a reduction to a lattice counting problem are presented in §
5
. Some useful auxiliary properties of these elementary functions are collected in §
6
. In §
7
we apply the results on the trapezoidal floor sums and the bounds on the difference of the Bessel phase functions in order to verify
Pólya’s conjecture in the regions
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{III}},\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{IV}}
and
\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{V}}
. This section is the most technical part of the paper. Finally, in §
8
we present a rigorous computer-assisted algorithm which affirms Pólya’s conjecture in the remaining part of
\mathrm{R}\Lambda
.

§2.  Regions I and II via isoperimetric inequalities and comparison with flat cylinders

By the Faber–Krahn and Krahn–Szego inequalities (see, e.g.,
[
He06
,
LMP23
]
), we know that Pólya’s conjecture is true for any domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for values of
\lambda
such that the corresponding Weyl’s term satisfies
C_{d}|\Omega|\lambda^{2}\leq 2
, which in the case of annulus
A_{r}
is equivalent to

\lambda^{2}-8\leq\mu^{2}.

Therefore, we arrive at

Theorem 2.1
.

Let

\eta_{\mathrm{I}}(r):=\sqrt{\frac{8}{1-r^{2}}},\qquad\zeta_{\mathrm{I}}(\lambda):=\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-8}.

Inequality (
1.4
) holds for all
(\lambda,\mu)\in\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{I}}
, where

\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{I}}:=\left\{(\lambda,\mu):0<\mu<\lambda\leq 2\sqrt{2}\right\}\cup\left\{(\lambda,\mu):\lambda>2\sqrt{2},\ \zeta_{\mathrm{I}}(\lambda)\leq\mu<\lambda\right\}\subset\Lambda\mathrm{M}.

Equivalently, inequality (
1.3
) holds for all
(r,\lambda)\in\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{I}}
, where

\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{I}}:=\left\{(r,\lambda):0<r<1,\ \lambda\leq\eta_{\mathrm{I}}(r)\right\}\subset\mathrm{R}\Lambda.

We can extend the result of Theorem
2.1
by a more involved argument. In order to do so, consider first the Dirichlet Laplacian on a flat cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{h}:=(1-h,1)\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, with
h>0
(cf.
[
FS23
]
where some related results were obtained). An elementary separation of variables shows that the (unordered) eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{\mathcal{C}_{h}}^{\mathrm{Dir}}
are given by

\widetilde{\lambda}_{n,m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{h}\right)=m^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{h^{2}},\qquad(n,m)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}.

The corresponding eigenvalue counting function is

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{h}(\lambda):=\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}_{h}}(\lambda)=\#\left\{(n,m)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}:m^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{h^{2}}\leq\lambda^{2}\right\}.

Recall that the eigenvalue counting function of the annulus
A_{r}
is denoted by
\mathcal{N}_{r}(\lambda)
. We have

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
0<r<1
, and set
h=h_{r}:=\frac{1-r}{\sqrt{r}}
.
Then

\mathcal{N}_{r}(\lambda)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{h}(\lambda)\qquad\text{for all }\lambda>0.

Proof.

Let

f(\rho,\psi)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{m}(\rho)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\psi}

(2.1)

be a non-zero function from the Sobolev space
H^{1}_{0}(A_{r})
, so that
f_{m}(r)=f_{m}(1)=0
. The Rayleigh quotient of the operator
-\Delta^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{A_{r}}
acting on
f
is

\mathtt{R}_{r}[f]=\frac{\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\int\limits_{r}^{1}\left(\left|f_{m}^{\prime}(\rho)\right|^{2}\rho+\frac{m^{2}}{\rho}\left|f_{m}(\rho)\right|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\rho}{\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\int\limits_{r}^{1}\left|f_{m}(\rho)\right|^{2}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho}.

(2.2)

At the same time, the eigenvalues of
-\Delta^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{\mathcal{C}_{h_{r}}}
coincide with those of the operator

-r\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\rho^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\psi^{2}}

(2.3)

acting in
H^{1}_{0}\left(\mathcal{C}_{1-r}\right)
(with the cylindrical coordinates
(\rho,\psi)
coinciding with the polar coordinates in
A_{r}
), see Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
An annulus
A_{r}
and a cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{1-r}
. Note that we are comparing the spectrum of the Dirichlet Laplacian in
A_{r}
with the spectrum of the Dirichlet realisation of (
2.3
) in
\mathcal{C}_{1-r}
, which in turn coincides with the spectrum of the Dirichlet Laplacian in
\mathcal{C}_{h_{r}}
.

Note that
f
is an element of
H^{1}_{0}\left(\mathcal{C}_{1-r}\right)
if and only if it belongs to
H^{1}_{0}\left(A_{r}\right)
, and the Rayleigh quotient of the operator (
2.3
) acting on (
2.1
) is

\widetilde{\mathtt{R}}_{r}[f]=\frac{\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\int\limits_{r}^{1}\left(r\left|f_{m}^{\prime}(\rho)\right|^{2}+m^{2}\left|f_{m}(\rho)\right|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\rho}{\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\int\limits_{r}^{1}\left|f_{m}(\rho)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho}.

(2.4)

The comparison of the integrands in (
2.4
) and (
2.2
) immediately gives
\mathtt{R}[f]>\widetilde{\mathtt{R}}_{r}[f]
for any
f\in H^{1}_{0}\left(A_{r}\right)\setminus\{0\}
,
and the result follows from the variational principle.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

Let

r_{0}:=0,\quad r_{1}:=\frac{2}{3},\quad r_{2}:=\frac{4}{5},\quad r_{3}:=\frac{17}{20},\quad r_{4}:=\frac{22}{25},\quad r_{5}:=1,

(2.5)

and set

\eta_{\mathrm{II}}(r):=\frac{(j+1)\pi\sqrt{r}}{1-r}\quad\text{ if}\quad r_{j}\leq r<r_{j+1},\qquad j=0,1,2,3,4,

(2.6)

\zeta_{\mathrm{II}}(\lambda):=\begin{cases}\lambda-\frac{i\pi}{2\lambda}\left(\sqrt{4\lambda^{2}+i^{2}\pi^{2}}-i\pi\right)&\text{ if}\quad i\pi\frac{\sqrt{r_{i-1}}}{1-r_{i-1}}\leq\lambda<i\pi\frac{\sqrt{r_{i}}}{1-r_{i}},\qquad i=1,2,3,4,5,\\
r_{i}\lambda&\text{ if}\quad i\pi\frac{\sqrt{r_{i}}}{1-r_{i}}\leq\lambda<(i+1)\pi\frac{\sqrt{r_{i}}}{1-r_{i}},\qquad i=1,2,3,4,\end{cases}

(2.7)

see Figure
4
.

Inequality (
1.3
) holds for all
(r,\lambda)\in\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{II}}
, where

\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{II}}:=\left\{(r,\lambda):0<r<1,\ 0<\lambda<\eta_{\mathrm{II}}(r)\right\}.

(2.8)

Equivalently, inequality (
1.4
) holds for all
(\lambda,\mu)\in\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{II}}
, where

\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{II}}=\left\{(\lambda,\mu):\zeta_{\mathrm{II}}(\lambda)<\mu<\lambda\right\}\subset\Lambda\mathrm{M}.

(2.9)

Figure 4:
The plots of
\eta_{\mathrm{II}}(r)
and
\zeta_{\mathrm{II}}(\lambda)
. The red dots indicate the positions of singularities in definitions (
2.6
) and (
2.7
).

Proof.

Fix
r\in(0,1)
. With
h=h_{r}
, we have

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{h_{r}}(\lambda)&=\#\left\{(n,m)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}:n^{2}+\frac{(1-r)^{2}}{r\pi^{2}}m^{2}\leq\frac{(1-r)^{2}}{r\pi^{2}}\lambda^{2}\right\}\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{1-r}{\pi\sqrt{r}}\lambda\right\rfloor}\left(1+2\left\lfloor\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}r}{(1-r)^{2}}n^{2}}\right\rfloor\right),\end{split}

where we assumed the notational convention
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{0}:=0
. By Lemma
2.2
, inequality (
1.3
) will hold for a given
r
and
\lambda
if we can show that

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{h_{r}}(\lambda)<\frac{1-r^{2}}{4}\lambda^{2}.

(2.10)

Assume, first of all, that
\left\lfloor\frac{1-r}{\pi\sqrt{r}}\lambda\right\rfloor=0
, that is,

\lambda<\frac{\sqrt{r}\pi}{1-r}.

Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{h_{r}}(\lambda)=0
, and (
2.10
) follows immediately.

Let now
\left\lfloor\frac{1-r}{\pi\sqrt{r}}\lambda\right\rfloor=j\in\mathbb{N}
, that is,

j\frac{\sqrt{r}\pi}{1-r}\leq\lambda<(j+1)\frac{\sqrt{r}\pi}{1-r}.

(2.11)

In practice, we will only consider cases when
j\leq 4
but the general scheme may be extended further.
Let

L_{n}=\left\lfloor\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}r}{(1-r)^{2}}n^{2}}\right\rfloor,\qquad n=1,\dots,j,

so that

L_{n}^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}r}{(1-r)^{2}}n^{2}\leq\lambda^{2}<(L_{n}+1)^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}r}{(1-r)^{2}}n^{2}.

(2.12)

Then

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{h_{r}}(\lambda)=j+2L_{1}+\dots+2L_{j}.

In order to effectively obtain some restrictions on
r
which guarantee that inequality (
2.10
) holds we will replace it by a stronger inequality based on the lower bounds on
\lambda^{2}
from (
2.12
):

\begin{split}\frac{1-r^{2}}{4}\lambda^{2}-\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{h_{r}}(\lambda)&=\sum_{n=1}^{j}\left(\tau_{n}\frac{1-r^{2}}{4}\lambda^{2}-1-2L_{n}\right)\\
&\geq\sum_{n=1}^{j}\left(\tau_{n}\frac{1-r^{2}}{4}\left(L_{n}^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}r}{(1-r)^{2}}n^{2}\right)-1-2L_{n}\right)>0,\end{split}

(2.13)

where

(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{j})=\boldsymbol{\tau}\in(0,1]^{j}\qquad\text{with }\tau_{1}+\dots+\tau_{j}=1,

(2.14)

are some constants to be chosen later. Each summand in the right-hand side of (
2.13
) is minimised, over
L_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, by taking
L_{n}=\frac{4}{(1-r^{2})\tau_{n}}
. Substituting these values into (
2.13
) and multiplying the resulting inequality by
4(1-r^{2})
, we obtain the inequality

S_{j}(r;\boldsymbol{\tau}):=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{j}n^{2}\tau_{n}\right)\pi^{2}r(1+r)^{2}-16\left(\sum_{n=1}^{j}\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\right)-4j(1-r^{2})>0.

(2.15)

For every fixed choice of a vector
\boldsymbol{\tau}
satisfying constraints (
2.14
), the cubic (in
r
) polynomial
S_{j}(r;\boldsymbol{\tau})
is monotone increasing for
r\geq 0
with
S_{j}(0;\boldsymbol{\tau})<0
.
Therefore, it has a single positive root
r_{j}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
. If we can choose a vector
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{+}
and a number
r^{+}\in(0,1)
such that

S_{j}\left(r^{+};\boldsymbol{\tau}^{+}\right)>0,

then
r^{+}>r_{j}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{+})
, and so inequality (
2.15
) with
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\tau}^{+}
, and therefore also inequality (
2.10
), holds for
r\geq r^{+}

and for
\lambda
satisfying (
2.11
).

We now finish the proof of the theorem by looking at the specific cases
j\in\{1,2,3,4\}
. In each case, we choose
r^{+}:=r_{j}
, which is given by (
2.5
).

Case
j=1
.

The only possible choice is
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{+}=(1)
, and we get

§3.  Trapezoidal floor sums for Lipschitz functions and their bounds

We want to compare, under various conditions on a function
g
, its trapezoidal floor sum with the integrals
\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
. Some estimates of this type have been already established in
[
FLPS23
]
and
[
FLPS24a
]
, however proving Theorem
1.1
requires more delicate upper bounds for trapezoidal floor sums which we collect here.

We recall that a function
g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
is called
c
-Lipschitz
(
c>0
) if
|g(z)-g(w)|\leq c|z-w|
for all
z,w\in[a,b]
. We will write this as
g\in\mathrm{Lip}_{c}
.

The following introductory result is obvious:

Proposition 3.1
.

If
g(z)
is concave, then we always have

\mathbf{T}(g,a,b)\leq\int_{a}^{b}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

Proof.

In this case,
\mathbf{T}(g,a,b)
is trivially less than or equal to the trapezoid rule approximation for the integral of
g
, which is a lower bound for the true integral.
∎

A sharper bound for a concave function
g
can be obtained subject to some additional constraints.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a<b
. Let
c\in(0,1)
.
Assume that
g
is a decreasing concave
\mathrm{Lip}_{c}
function on
[a,b]
such that

\left\lfloor g(a)\right\rfloor>\left\lfloor g(a+1)\right\rfloor.

Then

\mathbf{T}(g,a,b)\leq\int_{a}^{b}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z-\frac{1-c}{2}(b-a).

(3.1)

Proof.

By the conditions of the Theorem, the function
g
is strictly monotone on some interval to the left of
a+1
, and, therefore by concavity, also on
[a+1,b]
, and thus is invertible there. In addition,

N:=\left\lfloor g(a)\right\rfloor>\left\lfloor g(b)\right\rfloor=:M.

Denote

a_{n}=\max\left\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:g(m)\geq n\right\}=\left\lfloor g^{-1}(n)\right\rfloor,\qquad n=N-1,\dots,M+1.

Set also
a_{N}:=a
and
a_{M}:=b
.

By additivity of the trapezoidal floor sums, we have

\mathbf{T}(g,a,b)=\mathbf{T}(g,a_{N},a_{N-1})+\dots+T(g,a_{M+1},a_{M}),

and it is therefore sufficient to prove the statement of the Theorem with
(a,b)
replaced by
(a_{n},a_{n-1})
,
n=N,\dots,M-1
.

Consider any such interval, and set
A:=a_{n}
,
B:=a_{n-1}
, and
k:=B-A\in\mathbb{N}
. Without loss of generality we can assume
n=1
, otherwise we just need to add the same constant
(n-1)k
to both sides of (
3.1
) which does not affect the validity of the inequality. Then
g(A)\geq 1>g(A+1)>\dots>g(B)\geq 0
and
\mathbf{T}(g,A,B)=\frac{1}{2}
. We therefore need to prove that

\int_{A}^{B}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{k(1-c)}{2}=\frac{k(1-c)+1}{2}.

(3.2)

By concavity of
g
and using
g(A)\geq 1
,
g(B)\geq 0
, we have

\int_{A}^{B}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z-\frac{k(1-c)+1}{2}\geq\frac{(g(A)+g(B))k}{2}-\frac{k(1-c)+1}{2}\geq\frac{k}{2}-\frac{k(1-c)+1}{2}=\frac{ck-1}{2},

and therefore (
3.2
) holds if
k\geq\frac{1}{c}
.

Suppose now
k\leq\frac{1}{c}
. We have, by the Lipschitz condition on
g
, the bound
g(B)\geq 1-ck
, and therefore

\int_{A}^{B}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z-\frac{k(1-c)+1}{2}\geq\frac{(2-ck)k}{2}-\frac{k(1-c)+1}{2}=\frac{2k-ck^{2}+ck-k-1}{2}=\frac{(k-1)(1-ck)}{2}\geq 0.

Thus, (
3.2
) holds again.
∎

We can now easily give

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

By additivity,

\mathbf{T}(g,\alpha,\beta)=\mathbf{T}(g,\alpha,p)+\mathbf{T}(g,p,\beta),

and we estimate the first term by Proposition
3.1
with
[a,b]=[\alpha,p]
, and the second term by Theorem
3.2
with
[a,b]=[p,\beta]
.
∎

We now switch to the bounds for convex decreasing functions
g
. The next result is essentially a version of
[
FLPS23
, Theorem 5.1]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
, and let
g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be non-negative, decreasing, convex, and of class
\mathrm{Lip}_{\frac{1}{2}}
. Suppose also that
g(b)
is an integer. Then

\mathbf{T}\left(g+\frac{1}{4},a,b\right)\leq\int_{a}^{b}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

Moreover, equality implies that
g(z)
is a constant on
[a,b]
.

Proof.

If
g(b)=0
, this is an immediate consequence of
[
FLPS23
, Theorem 5.1]
, noting that the last term in the sum is zero and so it does not matter whether it has a factor of
1/2
. Adding an integer
n
to
g(z)
increases both sides by
(b-a)n
and so equality is maintained.
∎

The bound in Theorem
3.3
can be improved given more information on the function
g
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
, and suppose
g(z)
is decreasing, convex,
\mathrm{Lip}_{\frac{1}{2}}
on
[a,b]
, and has
g(b)=0
. Suppose further that there exists
t\in[a,b]
for which
g(z)
is
\mathrm{Lip}_{\frac{1}{3}}
on
[t,b]
. Then

\mathbf{T}\left(g+\frac{1}{4},a,b\right)\leq\int_{a}^{b}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z-\frac{1}{4}\left\lfloor g(t)\right\rfloor.

In order to prove Theorem
3.4
we require

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{Z}
. Suppose
g(z)
is decreasing and convex on
[A,B+1]
with Lipschitz constant
\frac{1}{2}
, and is Lipschitz with constant
\frac{1}{3}
for
z\geq A+1
. Suppose further that for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

g(A)\geq n+1\geq g(A+1)\geq g(B)\geq n\geq g(B+1).

Then

\mathbf{T}\left(g+\frac{1}{4},A,B\right)\leq\int_{A}^{B}g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{4}.

(3.3)

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Without loss of generality we assume
A=0
and
n=0
. Let

k=\#\{m\in[0,B]:g(m)\geq 3/4\},

and consider three cases.

Case 1:

k=1
. The left-hand side of (
3.3
) is
\frac{1}{2}
. Since
g(0)\geq 1
, we must have
g(z)\geq 1-\frac{z}{2}
for
z\geq 0
. In particular
g(1)\geq\frac{1}{2}
, and therefore
g(2)\geq\frac{1}{6}
, and so
B\geq 2
. Thus

\int_{0}^{B}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\geq\int_{0}^{2}\left(1-\frac{z}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z=1,

and so the right-hand side of (
3.3
) is at least
\frac{3}{4}>\frac{1}{2}
as desired.

Case 2:

k=2
. The left-hand side of (
3.3
) is
\frac{3}{2}
. Since
g(1)\geq\frac{3}{4}
, we have
g(z)\geq\frac{1}{3}+\frac{3}{4}-\frac{z}{3}
whenever
z\geq 1
. Thus,
g(3)\geq\frac{3}{4}
and
B\geq 3
. And now the right-hand side of (
3.3
) is at least

\int_{0}^{3}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{0}^{2}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z+\int_{2}^{3}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

By convexity, the first integral is at least
2g(1)\geq\frac{3}{2}
. The second integral, using the bound
g(z)\geq\frac{1}{3}+\frac{3}{4}-\frac{z}{3}
, is at least
\frac{1}{4}
. So the right-hand side of (
3.3
) is at least
\frac{3}{2}
as desired.

Case 3:

k\geq 3
. The left-hand side of (
3.3
) is
k-\frac{1}{2}
. Now we know that
g(k-1)\geq\frac{3}{4}
. By convexity,
g(1)+g(2k-3)\geq 2g(k-1)\geq\frac{3}{2}
. Since
g(1)<1
, we have
g(2k-3)\geq\frac{1}{2}
and therefore of course
B\geq 2k-2
. So,

\int_{0}^{B}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\geq\int_{0}^{2k-2}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,

which by convexity is at least
(2k-2)g(k-1)\geq\frac{3}{2}(k-1)\geq k
. Thus, the right-hand side of (
3.3
) is at least
k-\frac{1}{4}>k-\frac{1}{2}
, completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Let
N=\left\lfloor g(a)\right\rfloor
, and for each
n\in[0,N]
, let
q_{n}=\max\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:g(m)\geq n\}
. Then

\mathbf{T}\left(g+\frac{1}{4},a,b\right)=\mathbf{T}\left(g+\frac{1}{4},a,q_{N}\right)+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbf{T}\left(g+\frac{1}{4},q_{n+1},q_{n}\right).

Apply Lemma
3.5
for each
n
for which
q_{n+1}+1\geq t
, with
A=q_{n+1}
and
B=q_{n}
, and use Theorem
3.3
to estimate each of the other sums. We get the integral we want plus an additional
-\frac{1}{4}
for every
n
for which
q_{n+1}+1\geq t
. Since
g(t)\leq g(q_{n+1}+1)<n+1
for any such
n
, there are at least
\left\lfloor g(t)\right\rfloor
of these values of
n
, which completes the proof.
∎

§4.  The eigenvalue counting function and the Bessel phase function

Standard separation of variables implies that the eigenfunctions of
-\Delta^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{A_{r}}
, written in polar coordinates
\rho=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}
and
\psi
, are of the form

\left(c_{1}J_{m}\left(\sqrt{\lambda}\rho\right)+c_{2}Y_{m}\left(\sqrt{\lambda}\rho\right)\right)\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}m\psi},\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N},

where
J_{m}
and
Y_{m}
are the Bessel functions of the first and second kind, respectively, and
|c_{1}|^{2}+|c_{2}|^{2}>0
. If we define the functions

L_{r,m}(x):=J_{m}\left(x\right)Y_{m}\left(rx\right)-Y_{m}\left(x\right)J_{m}\left(rx\right),\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(4.1)

and denote the
k
th positive root of
L_{r,m}(x)
by
\ell_{r,m,k}
, then the corresponding eigenvalues equal
\ell_{r,m,k}^{2}
, taken with multiplicity

\kappa_{m}:=\begin{cases}1\qquad&\text{if }m=0.\\
2\qquad&\text{if }m>0.\end{cases}

We recall that the Bessel
modulus
function
M_{\nu}(z)
and
phase
function
\theta_{\nu}(x)
are defined, for
\nu\geq 0
, via

J_{\nu}(x)+\mathrm{i}Y_{\nu}(x)=M_{\nu}(x)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{\nu}(x)},\qquad\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\theta_{\nu}(x)=-\frac{\pi}{2}.

(4.2)

We refer to
[
DLMF
, §10.18]
,
[
Ho17
]
, and
[
FLPS24b
]
for more information on the modulus and phase functions; for the moment we only need to recall that

M_{\nu}(x)>0\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(x)>0\qquad\text{for all }x>0,

and that

\theta_{\nu}(x)=x-\left(\frac{\nu}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)\pi+O\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\qquad\text{as }x\to+\infty

(4.3)

[
DLMF
, §10.18.18]
.
Using notation (
4.2
) and definition (
4.1
), we get

\begin{split}L_{r,m}(x)&=M_{m}(x)M_{m}(rx)\left(\cos\theta_{m}(x)\sin\theta_{m}(rx)-\cos\theta_{m}(rx)\sin\theta_{m}(x)\right)\\
&=-M_{m}(x)M_{m}(rx)\sin\left(\theta_{m}(x)-\theta_{m}(rx)\right).\end{split}

It is easy to see that the difference

\Theta_{r,m}(x):=\theta_{m}(x)-\theta_{m}(rx)

(4.4)

is monotonically increasing in
x
for every
m\geq 0
and every
r\in(0,1)
, with

\Theta_{r,m}(0)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{x\to\infty}\Theta_{r,m}(x)=\infty,

see Lemma
4.1
, and therefore the function
\Theta_{r,m}
is invertible. Thus, the
k
th positive root
\ell_{r,m,k}
of
L_{r,m}(x)
is given by

\ell_{r,m,k}=\Theta_{r,m}^{-1}(\pi k),

and the eigenvalue counting function of an annulus can be rewritten as

\mathcal{N}_{r}(\lambda)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\kappa_{m}\#\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\ell_{r,m,k}\leq\lambda\}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\kappa_{m}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\pi}\Theta_{r,m}(\lambda)\right\rfloor=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\kappa_{m}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\theta_{m}(\lambda)-\theta_{m}(r\lambda)\right)\right\rfloor.

(4.5)

We will require the following technical results.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
m\geq 0
,
0<r<1
. Then the function
\Theta_{r,m}(x)
defined by (
4.4
) is strictly monotone increasing at any point
x>0
, and
\Theta_{r,m}(\lambda)\to\infty
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Moreover,

\Theta_{r,m}(\lambda)<\pi\qquad\text{for any }\lambda\in(0,m].

(4.6)

Proof.

We have

\Theta^{\prime}_{r,m}(x)=\theta^{\prime}_{m}(x)-r\theta^{\prime}_{m}(rx)=\frac{2}{\pi x}\left(\frac{1}{(M_{m}(x))^{2}}-\frac{1}{(M_{m}(rx))^{2}}\right),

(4.7)

where the last equality is by
[
DLMF
, §10.18.8]
. Nicholson’s formula
[
DLMF
, §10.9.30]
,

(M_{m}(x))^{2}=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cosh(2mt)K_{0}(2x\sinh t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

in which
K_{0}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind (which is strictly decreasing on
(0,+\infty)
), then ensures that
M_{m}(x)
is strictly decreasing in
x
, and therefore the right-hand side of (
4.7
) is positive.

Also,

\Theta_{r,m}(\lambda)=(1-r)\lambda+O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\to\infty

by (
4.3
).

We also have, for
0<\lambda\leq m
,

\Theta_{r,m}(\lambda)<\theta_{m}(\lambda)+\frac{\pi}{2}\leq\theta_{m}(m)+\frac{\pi}{2}<\pi,

since

-\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta_{m}(rm)<\theta_{m}(m)<\theta_{m}\left(j_{m,1}\right)=\frac{\pi}{2}

by monotonicity of
\theta_{m}
and the classical bound
j_{m,1}>m
.
∎

Lemma
4.1
immediately implies that
\ell_{r,m,1}=\Theta_{r,m}^{-1}(\pi)>m
, and thus

Corollary 4.2
.

For an integer
m>\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor\geq 0
,

\#\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\ell_{r,m,k}\leq\lambda\}=0.

Therefore, (
4.5
) can be rewritten as

\mathcal{N}_{r}(\lambda)=\sum_{m=0}^{\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor}\kappa_{m}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\pi}\Theta_{r,m}(\lambda)\right\rfloor,

(4.8)

and the statement of Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to the bound

\mathcal{N}_{r}(\lambda)=\sum_{m=0}^{\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor}\kappa_{m}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\pi}\Theta_{r,m}(\lambda)\right\rfloor=\sum_{m=0}^{\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor}\kappa_{m}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\theta_{m}(\lambda)-\theta_{m}(r\lambda)\right)\right\rfloor<\frac{\lambda^{2}(1-r^{2})}{4}

(4.9)

being valid for all
0<r<1
and all
\lambda
.

In the sequel, it will be convenient to interchange the independent variable and the parameter, and to switch to
\mu
instead of
r
by setting

\gamma_{\lambda,\mu}(z):=\frac{1}{\pi}\Theta_{\frac{\mu}{\lambda},z}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\theta_{z}(\lambda)-\theta_{z}(\mu)\right),

(4.10)

noting that (
4.6
) in this notation reads

\gamma_{\lambda,\mu}(m)<1\qquad\text{for all }m\geq\lambda,

(4.11)

and thus rewriting (

) as

\mathcal{N}_{r}(\lambda)=\sum_{m=0}^{\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor}\kappa_{m}\left\lfloor\gamma_{\lambda,\mu}(m)\right\rfloor.

(4.12)

Remark 4.3
.

We note that since
\left\lfloor\gamma_{\lambda,\mu}\left(\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor+1\right)\right\rfloor=0
by (
4.11
),
the right-hand size of (

) can be interpreted as a multiple of the trapezoidal floor sum:

\mathcal{N}_{r}(\lambda)=2\mathbf{T}\left(\gamma_{\lambda,\mu},0,\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor+1\right).

(4.13)

∎

§5.  Bounds on the phase functions difference and reduction to a lattice counting problem

We proceed to estimating the values
\Theta_{r,m}(\lambda)
appearing in the left-hand side of (

). Introduce, for
\lambda>0
and
\mu\geq 0
, the functions

\displaystyle G_{\lambda}(z)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-z^{2}}-z\arccos\frac{z}{\lambda}\right),\quad&z\in[0,\lambda],\\
0,\quad&z>\lambda,\end{cases}

(5.1)

\displaystyle H_{\mu}(z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{3\mu^{2}+2z^{2}}{24\pi(\mu^{2}-z^{2})^{3/2}},\qquad\qquad\quad\ z\in[0,\mu),

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\max\left\{G_{\mu}(z)-H_{\mu}(z),-\frac{1}{4}\right\},\qquad&z\in[0,\mu),\\

(5.3)

(5.4)

.

Let

,

. Then

For

[

, Theorem 1.4]
using notation (
5.1
)–(

). For

\theta_{z}(\lambda)>-\frac{\pi}{2}
, and the upper bound follows from monotonicity and continuous differentiability of both

and
\theta^{\prime}_{z}(\lambda)>0=G_{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)
, see also

.

plays the key role in the proof of the following bounds, which are illustrated in Figure

(5.8)

\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathrm{V}}(\lambda,\mu)>-\frac{1}{3\pi}+\frac{1}{4}\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda}{10}\right\rfloor-\frac{2}{15\sqrt{\mu}}>\frac{\lambda}{40}-\left(\frac{1}{3\pi}+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{15\sqrt{\pi}}\right),

which is positive whenever
\lambda>40\left(\frac{1}{3\pi}+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{15\sqrt{\pi}}\right)\approx 15.7486
. As mentioned, this is automatically true by (
7.19
).

Assume now that
r=\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Adding together bounds (
7.14
), (
7.16
), and (
7.18
),
and recalling once more (
7.2
), we obtain

\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathrm{V}}(\lambda,\mu)>-\frac{1}{3\pi}+\frac{3-2\sqrt{2}}{8}\mu-\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{8}-\frac{2}{15\sqrt{\mu}}>\frac{3-2\sqrt{2}}{8}\mu-\left(\frac{1}{3\pi}+\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{8}+\frac{1}{15\sqrt{\pi}}\right),

which is positive whenever
\mu>\frac{8}{3-2\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1}{3\pi}+\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{8}+\frac{1}{15\sqrt{\pi}}\right)\approx 10.1153
. As we already imposed the restriction
\mu>4\pi
, this is automatically satisfied.
∎

It remains to prove (

Proof.

We will show that

Case 1:
\lambda<\frac{5}{2}
.

We immediately get

2.1

Case 2:
\mu>\zeta_{\mathrm{comp}}(\lambda)=\frac{22}{25}\lambda
.

This is the same as

. By (

) and (
2.6
) (with

\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{II}}(r)}{\eta_{\mathrm{V}}(r)}=\frac{5r^{3/2}}{4}>\frac{5\left(\frac{22}{25}\right)^{3/2}}{4}=\sqrt{\frac{1331}{1250}}>1,

and Theorems
2.3
and
7.6
imply that either
(r,\lambda)\in\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{II}}
or
(r,\lambda)\in\mathrm{R}\Lambda_{\mathrm{V}}

(\lambda,\mu)\in\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{II}}\cup\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{V}}
.

Case 3:
\lambda>150
,
0<\mu\leq\zeta_{\mathrm{comp}}(\lambda)=\frac{22}{25}\lambda
.

We analyse this case by vertical ordering of the boundaries of individual regions in
\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{theory}}
.
We will proceed from bottom to top. Firstly, clearly,

\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{III}}(\lambda)}{\zeta_{\mathrm{IV,-}}(\lambda)}=\frac{225}{64\sqrt{5}}\sqrt{\lambda-10}>\frac{225\sqrt{7}}{32}>1,\qquad\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{IV,+}}(\lambda)}{\zeta_{\mathrm{III}}(\lambda)}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{5}}\sqrt{\lambda-10}>\sqrt{7}>1.

Secondly, by (
7.9

\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{IV,+}}(\lambda)}{\zeta_{\mathrm{V,-}}(\lambda)}=\frac{\left(1-\frac{10}{\lambda}\right)\left(5\lambda+5\sqrt{\lambda(\lambda-16\pi)}\right)}{400\pi},

and since the right-hand side is monotone increasing in

\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{IV,+}}(\lambda)}{\zeta_{\mathrm{V,-}}(\lambda)}>\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{IV,+}}(150)}{\zeta_{\mathrm{V,-}}(150)}=\frac{7\left(\sqrt{3(75-8\pi)}+15\right)}{60\pi}\approx 1.01126>1.

Thus, by Theorems
7.1
,

, and
7.6

whenever
\lambda>150
and

, see the left image in Figure

Finally, for

see the right image in Figure

Let now

\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\mu):=2\mathbf{T}\left(G_{\lambda}-F_{\mu},0,\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor+1\right),

\mathcal{N}_{\mu/\lambda}(\lambda)\leq\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\mu),

and in order to prove (

) for a given

it is enough to show that the inequality

\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\mu)<\frac{\lambda^{2}-\mu^{2}}{4}

holds.

Computer-assisted Theorem 8.2
.

Let
(\lambda,\mu)\in\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{comp}}
. Then (
8.4
) holds.

Remark 8.3
.

We note that the region
\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{comp}}
is much larger than the region (
8.1
) which we actually need to deal with, however its shape is determined by the methods we employ. There is, as a result, an element of redundancy in our computer-assisted algorithm, which covers some parts of the regions already covered by analytic proofs.
∎

To start describing the algorithm of proving Theorem
8.2
, we note that
\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\mu)
is non-decreasing in
\lambda
and non-increasing in
\mu
: this follows from definitions (
5.1
)–(
5.3
) together with

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G_{\lambda}(z)}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-z^{2}}}{\pi\lambda}>0\qquad\text{for }0<z<\lambda,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H_{\mu}(z)}{\partial\mu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu\left(\mu^{2}+4z^{2}\right)}{8\pi\left(\mu^{2}-z^{2}\right)^{5/2}}<0\qquad\text{for }0<z<\mu.

To make the algorithm work in a finite number of exact calculations we use the following two simple but important observations.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
0<\mu_{0}<\lambda_{0}
, and let
p_{0}:=\mathcal{P}(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0})
.

(i)

If
p_{0}=0
, then (
8.4
) holds in

\mathfrak{T}(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0}):=\left\{(\lambda,\mu):0<\lambda\leq\lambda_{0},\mu_{0}\leq\mu<\lambda\right\}.

(ii)

If (
8.4
) holds for
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
,
\mu=\mu_{0}
with a positive
margin
, that is,

\frac{\lambda_{0}^{2}-\mu_{0}^{2}}{4}-p_{0}>0,

then it also holds in the following part of the interior of the hyperbola,

\left\{(\lambda,\mu):\Lambda(\mu_{0},p_{0}):=\sqrt{\mu_{0}^{2}+4p_{0}}<\lambda\leq\lambda_{0},\mu_{0}\leq\mu<\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4p_{0}}=:\mathrm{M}(\lambda,p_{0})\right\}.

(8.5)

In particular, it holds inside any rectangle

\mathfrak{R}(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0},p_{0};\alpha,\beta):=[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{0}]\times[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}],

(8.6)

where
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
and

\begin{split}\lambda_{1}&=\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0},p_{0};\alpha):=\alpha\Lambda(\mu_{0},p_{0})+(1-\alpha)\lambda_{0},\\
\mu_{1}&=\mu_{1}(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0},p_{0};\alpha,\beta):=\beta\mathrm{M}(\lambda_{1},p_{0})+(1-\beta)\mu_{0},\end{split}

see Figure
9
.

Figure 9:
The regions (
8.5
) and (
8.6
).

Proof.

Statement (i) of the Lemma follows immediately from the monotonicity properties of
\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\mu)
. Again by monotonicity, we
have, for
\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}
and
\mu\geq\mu_{0}
,

\frac{\lambda^{2}-\mu^{2}}{4}-\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\mu)\geq\frac{\lambda^{2}-\mu^{2}}{4}-p_{0},

and this is positive if
\lambda^{2}-\mu^{2}>4p_{0}
, that is, inside (
8.5
). It is then elementary to check that any rectangle
R(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0},p_{0};\alpha,\beta)
with the restrictions as stated lies inside (
8.5
).
∎

Lemma
8.4
opens a way of covering the region
\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{comp}}
by a finite number of polygonal regions, always moving up and to the left: these directions explain the reasons for choosing the larger region for the computer-assisted proof than strictly necessary, see Remark
8.3
.

Before describing the algorithm in detail, we also need to address the issue of avoiding floating-point calculations. To do so, we use the method of verified rational approximations we have introduced in
[
FLPS23
]
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\underline{x}\leq x\leq\overline{x}
,
\underline{x},\overline{x}\in\mathbb{Q}
, some rational lower and upper bounds for
x
; see
[
FLPS23
, §8]
for description of algorithms of finding upper and lower rational approximations of some relevant functions including
G_{\lambda}
. Assuming that
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{Q}
, we replace the function
\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\mu)
by the rational-valued function of rational arguments

\overline{\mathcal{P}}(\lambda,\mu)=2\mathbf{T}\left(\overline{G_{\lambda}}-\underline{F_{\mu}},0,\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor+1\right)\geq\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\mu).

We will construct the finite sequence of rational points,
\left(\lambda^{(k)},\mu^{(k,j)}\right)
,
k=0,\dots,K
,
j=0,\dots,X_{k}
, where the number of steps
K
and
X_{k}
are determined by the algorithm, and the finite sequence of rectangles

\mathfrak{R}^{(k,j)}:=\left[\lambda^{(k+1)},\lambda^{(k)}\right]\times\left[\mu^{(k,j)},\mu^{(k,j+1)}\right],

(8.7)

(or, occasionally, triangles), each with its rightmost bottom point at
\left(\lambda^{(k)},\mu^{(k,j)}\right)
, inside which (
8.4
) holds, and whose union will cover
the region
\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{comp}}
.

To start with, set
\beta=\frac{99}{100}
and
\alpha=\frac{2}{3}
(these values are chosen empirically following some experiments; varying them does not have a radical effect on the required time of computations). Then set

\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{(0)}:=\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{comp}},\quad\zeta_{\mathrm{comp}}^{(0)}(\lambda):=\zeta_{\mathrm{comp}}(\lambda),\quad\lambda^{(0)}:=\max_{(\lambda,\mu)\in\Lambda\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{(0)}}\lambda=150,\quad\mu^{(0,0)}:=0,

and compute

p^{(0,0)}:=\overline{\mathcal{P}}\left(\lambda^{(0)},\mu^{(0,0)}\right)\in\mathbb{Q},

\lambda^{(1)}:=\alpha\overline{\sqrt{{\mu^{(0,0)}}^{2}+4p^{(0,0)}}}+(1-\alpha)\lambda^{(0)}\in\mathbb{Q},\quad\mu^{(0,1)}:=\beta\underline{\sqrt{{\lambda^{(1)}}^{2}-4p^{(0,0)}}}+(1-\beta)\mu^{(0,0)}\in\mathbb{Q},

and (temporarily) define the rectangle
\mathfrak{R}^{(0,0)}
by (
8.7
) with
k=j=0
.
Lemma
8.4
, adjusted to rational approximations, then guarantees that (
8.4
) holds for
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathfrak{R}^{(0,0)}
.

We now compute a sequence of rectangles
\mathfrak{R}^{(0,j)}
on top of the first one, by setting on the
j
th vertical step,

p^{(0,j)}:=\overline{\mathcal{P}}\left(\lambda^{(0)},\mu^{(0,j)}\right),\qquad j=1,2,\dots,

\lambda^{(1)}_{\mathrm{old}}:=\lambda^{(1)},\qquad\lambda^{(1)}_{\mathrm{temp}}:=\alpha\overline{\sqrt{{\mu^{(0,j)}}^{2}+4p^{(0,j)}}}+(1-\alpha)\lambda^{(0)},

\lambda^{(1)}=\lambda^{(1)}_{\mathrm{new}}:=\max\left\{\lambda^{(1)}_{\mathrm{temp}},\lambda^{(1)}_{\mathrm{old}}\right\}

(that is, we always choose the minimal width of all previously and currently computed rectangles in this vertical strip: if
\lambda^{(1)}_{\mathrm{new}}>\lambda^{(1)}_{\mathrm{old}}
, we also redefine the previously defined rectangles
\mathfrak{R}^{(0,j^{\prime})}
,
j^{\prime}=0,\dots,j-1
, using (
8.7
)), and

\mu^{(0,j+1)}:=\beta\underline{\sqrt{{\lambda^{(1)}}^{2}-4p^{(0,j)}}}+(1-\beta)\mu^{(0,j)}.

The same argument using Lemma
8.4
guarantees that (
8.4
) holds for
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathfrak{R}^{(0,j)}
.
