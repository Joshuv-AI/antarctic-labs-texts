B-Cell Lymphoma-Extra Large Promotes Gastric Tumorigenesis via Apoptosis-Independent Wnt–β-Catenin Activation and Peroxisome Proliferator–Activated Receptor γ Suppression
Authors: Yukiko Oya, Junya Arai, Yoku Hayakawa, Toshiro Shiokawa, Shintaro Shinohara, Masahiro Hata, Yuki Matsushita, Hiroto Kinoshita, Chie Uekura, Ken Kurokawa, Sohei Abe, Mayo Tsuboi, Sozaburo Ihara, Nobumi Suzuki, Hayato Hikita, Tetsuo Takehara, Timothy C Wang, Mitsuhiro Fujishiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsThe Wnt–β-catenin and Yes-associated protein pathways cooperatively drive gastrointestinal tumor progression. BCL2L1, a Yes-associated protein target gene, encodes the anti-apoptotic isoform B-cell lymphoma–extra-large, which is highly expressed in multiple cancers, but its role in gastric cancer remains unclear.MethodsWe generated Mist1-CreERT; Apcflox/flox and bcl-xlflox/flox mice to study gastric tumorigenesis with or without B-cell lymphoma–extra-large. Gastric organoids and xenografts were used to assess proliferation and downstream signaling, and findings were validated in human gastric cancer tissues and databases.ResultsDeletion of B-cell lymphoma–extra-large significantly reduced tumor growth and proliferation in Apc-deficient tumors, organoids, and xenografts. Nuclear β-catenin and Wnt target gene expression were attenuated, partly through derepression of Hnf4a and Pparg, indicating that B-cell lymphoma–extra-large promotes Wnt signaling. Concurrently, downregulation of Hnf4a and Pparg contributes to the inhibition of multiple metabolic pathways. In human gastric cancer, high BCL2L1 expression correlated with elevated Wnt target genes and poor prognosis, whereas PPARG expression inversely correlated with BCL2L1 and associated with favorable outcomes. Epidemiologic analysis suggested that pioglitazone, a peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ agonist, reduced gastric cancer incidence in diabetic patients.ConclusionsB-cell lymphoma–extra-large promotes gastric tumorigenesis through dual mechanisms—enhancing Wnt signaling and suppressing peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ—and highlight B-cell lymphoma–extra-large as a potential therapeutic target while suggesting peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ activation may be protective.Keywords: Bcl-xL, Gastric Cancer, PPARγ, Wnt–β-catenin

Summary

B-cell lymphoma–extra-large promotes gastric tumorigenesis by enhancing Wnt–β-catenin signaling and suppressing peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ–associated metabolic programs. Genetic or pharmacologic targeting of this pathway may inhibit gastric tumor growth, highlighting B-cell lymphoma–extra-large as a potential therapeutic target and peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ activation as a possible protective strategy.

What You Need to Know

Background

B-cell lymphoma–extra-large is frequently upregulated in gastric cancer, but its functional role beyond apoptosis, particularly in regulating Wnt signaling and tumor metabolism, remains poorly understood.

Impact

This study demonstrates that B-cell lymphoma–extra-large promotes gastric tumorigenesis by enhancing Wnt–β-catenin signaling through suppression of hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 α and peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ, thereby sustaining proliferation and impairing metabolic differentiation.

Future Directions

Targeting B-cell lymphoma–extra-large–mediated Wnt activation or restoring peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ signaling, including pharmacologic peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ agonists, may represent novel preventive or therapeutic strategies for gastric cancer.

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most common cancers in the world. In recent years, the number of deaths due to GC has been declining due to a decrease in the Helicobacter pylori infection rate and improved health screening.1 However, the 5-year survival rate for advanced GC remains low, and the 5-year survival rate for GC with distant metastasis is less than 10%.2 Although several new drugs including immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted antibodies have been applied to GC treatment and found to be effective in disease control, most advanced GCs become resistant to these drugs after several cycles and hardly show complete response. Thus, there is a demand for seeking additional therapeutic targets to control GCs that are resistant to currently available therapies.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8

In gastric glands, stem and progenitor cells in the isthmus continuously supply differentiated cells and maintain mucosal homeostasis. Many gastric stem cell markers have been reported, such as LGR5, CCK2R, AXIN2, FZD7, CD44, SOX2, and MIST1 for antrum and SOX9, IQGAP3, STMN1, SOX2 and MIST1 for corpus.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 Induction of genetic mutations in stem cells is sufficient to generate gastric tumors, whereas that in differentiated cells fails to develop tumors.13,18,19 For example, deletion of Apc in the gastric stem cells results in the formation of tumors in the gastric antrum.20, 21, 22, 23 On the other hand, GC only develops in the presence of multiple mutations or a combination of mutation and additional chemical stimuli.22,24,25 In gastric carcinogenesis, mutated stem cells are supported by the surrounding microenvironment. Many cell types are involved in such microenvironment, including inflammatory cells, fibroblasts, nerves, vascular endothelial cells, luminal bacteria, hormones, and secreted mucins, and these stromal components promote tumor growth by releasing a variety of molecules that directly activate cancer cell growth signals or restructure the surrounding area.12,26, 27, 28, 29, 30 Among them, the Wnt–β-catenin signaling pathway has been identified to play a central role in the development and progression of GC.21,31, 32, 33

We previously reported that the Wnt pathway and the Yes-associated protein (YAP) pathway are activated in GC cells through nerve-regulatory niche signals. YAP activation leads to upregulation of certain genes including BCL2L1 and others, but the significance and role of these gene changes remain unknown.23 BCL2L1 is a gene encoding the Bcl2 family proteins B-cell lymphoma–extra-large (Bcl-xL) and B-cell lymphoma–extra-small (Bcl-xS), which regulate apoptosis by modulating mitochondrial membrane permeability; Bcl-xL is an inhibitor of apoptosis, whereas Bcl-xS is a promoter.34, 35, 36 Bcl-xL is reported to be involved in other biological reactions, such as autophagy,37,38 neural network formation,39 and cellular metabolism.37

Bcl-xL is overexpressed in several cancers, and it has been reported that inhibition of Bcl-xL suppresses tumor growth. In pancreatic cancer, Bcl-xL was found to increase the incidence of pancreatic cancer by suppressing oncogene-induced senescence and apoptosis.40 In colorectal and hepatocellular cancers, inhibition of Bcl-xL induced apoptosis, suggesting that combined therapies targeting Bcl-xL may increase the efficacy of chemotherapy.41, 42, 43 Overexpression of Bcl-xL and its tumor promoting role have been also reported in GCs.44, 45, 46 However, the precise mechanism of how Bcl-xL is upregulated and promotes tumor growth has not been fully elucidated. In this study, we analyzed the function of Bcl-xL in GC and explored its potential as a new therapeutic target.

Results

B-Cell Lymphoma–Extra-Large Enhances the Gastric Tumorigenesis in Part Through Activation of Wnt Pathway

To investigate the role of Bcl-xL in gastric mucosa and tumorigenesis, we analyzed tissues from Mist1-CreERT; bcl-xlflox/flox (M-B), Mist1-CreERT; Apcflox/flox (M-A), Mist1-CreERT; Apcflox/flox; bcl-xlflox/flox (M-AB), and Mist1-CreERT control (wild-type [WT]) mice. All mouse lines were generated on an LSL-TdTomato reporter background (Figure 1A and B). To characterize the expression pattern of Bcl-xL in normal and neoplastic gastric tissues, we performed fluorescent immunostaining for Bcl-xL on antral sections from each genotype. In M-B mice, Bcl-xL expression was markedly diminished in approximately 49% of glandular units relative to WT controls, indicating efficient Cre-mediated recombination in nearly one-half of the glands (Figure 1C). We next assessed the impact of Bcl-xL deficiency on tumor initiation and progression. Consistent with previous reports,10 M-A mice developed distal antral tumors within 1 month of tamoxifen (TAM) administration. At 2 months post-TAM, we compared gastric tumor sizes between M-A and M-AB mice and found that tumors in M-AB mice were significantly smaller than those in M-A mice (Figure 1D). In detail, all mice in the M-A cohort developed large tumors that covered the entire antral region and appeared as conglomerates of multiple nodular lesions. This morphology suggests that these tumors may have arisen from the fusion of multiple independent tumor foci. In contrast, mice in the M-AB cohort developed only a single, relatively small tumor per mouse. The consistent presence of a single tumor in the M-AB group, without evidence of multiple tumor formation, is likely attributable to the tumor suppressive effect of Bcl-xL deletion. Histopathologic analysis also confirmed this reduction in size, although both tumors were classified as intramucosal tumors. In contrast, M-B mice, which harbored Bcl-xL deletion alone, showed no histologic abnormalities compared with WT controls after TAM treatment (Figure 1E).

Red fluorescent protein immunostaining confirmed robust TdTomato expression in both normal and tumor tissues (Figure 1F), indicating efficient Cre-mediated recombination. In M-B mice, sustained TdTomato fluorescence observed 2 months after TAM administration suggested that Bcl-xL ablation did not induce apoptosis and that Bcl-xL–deficient cells were able to survive long term. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) using gastric tissue RNA further confirmed that Bcl-xL expression was decreased in M-B and M-AB mice, whereas it was elevated in M-A mice (Figure 1G).

To assess epithelial proliferative activity, we stained gastric tissues for Ki67. In both WT and M-B mice, Ki67-positive cells remained confined to the normal proliferative zones of the glands, indicating that loss of Bcl-xL alone does not disrupt baseline epithelial proliferation (Figure 2A). In contrast, M-A and M-AB mice exhibited distinct proliferation patterns in tumor regions: Ki67 expression was significantly higher in M-A tumors than in M-AB tumors (Figure 2B). These results indicate that the tumor suppressive effect of Bcl-xL loss is associated with a reduction in tumor cell proliferation. Notably, in M-A mice, immunohistochemical analyses using β-catenin and Ki67 and tdTomato and Ki67 costaining revealed an increase in Ki67+ cells not only among nuclear β-catenin+ cells and tdTomato+ cells but also in non–nuclear β-catenin cells and tdTomato− cells. These findings suggest that hyperproliferation is not restricted to adenomatous polyposis coli (APC)-deficient cells but also extends to neighboring epithelial cells, supporting the presence of non–cell-autonomous proliferative effects induced by APC loss (Figure 2C and D).

We next investigated the impact of Bcl-xL on tumorigenesis, with a particular focus on the Wnt signaling pathway, given its well-established role in GC progression.21,31, 32, 33 In WT mice, β-catenin localization was restricted to the plasma membrane, with no clear evidence of nuclear translocation (Figure 2E). In contrast, nuclear translocation of β-catenin was observed in the tumor regions of M-A and M-AB mice, consistent with Apc deletion (Figure 2F). Moreover, M-A mice exhibited strong Bcl-xL expression throughout the tumor tissue, with particularly high levels in certain tumor cells. The Bcl-xL positivity was associated with nuclear β-catenin. In M-AB mice, Bcl-xL expression was markedly reduced in tumor regions, except for a small fraction where expression persisted, indicating efficient Bcl-xL knockout in the tumor (Figure 2F). Bcl-xL knockout also slowed down the expansion of nuclear β-catenin clones over time (Figure 2G).

Regarding SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9) expression, WT and M-B mice exhibited limited expression at the basal regions of the glandular ducts. In both M-A and M-AB mice, SOX9 expression was upregulated in tumor areas, but the number of SOX9-positive cells was significantly increased in M-A mice compared with that in M-AB mice (Figure 3A and B). Most cells showing nuclear β-catenin are also positive for SOX9 (Figure 3C). M-A mice also exhibited a significantly greater number of Lgr5-positive cells, compared with M-AB mice (Figure 3D). We next generated Mist1-CreERT; Apcflox/flox; Lgr5-DTR-green fluorescent protein (GFP) mice and examined the correlation among leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor 5 (LGR5)-GFP positivity, Bcl-xL positivity, and nuclear β-catenin localization. The results showed that LGR5 expression was associated with both Bcl-xL positivity and nuclear β-catenin accumulation (Figure 3E and F). These results suggest that Bcl-xL deletion leads to a partial attenuation of Wnt pathway activation.

To assess the role of Bcl-xL in metaplastic and dysplastic progression, we performed immunostaining for aquaporin-5 (AQP5) and trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP2). In M-A mice, most cells with nuclear β-catenin were positive for both AQP5 and TROP2. In contrast, M-AB mice exhibited a marked reduction in AQP5- and TROP2-positive cells, accompanied by a decrease in nuclear β-catenin, suggesting a delay in metaplastic and dysplastic transformation compared with M-A mice over time. These findings indicate that Bcl-xL promotes metaplastic and dysplastic progression via activation of the WNT pathway (Figure 3G and H).

To directly assess apoptosis, we performed immunostaining for cleaved caspase-3, as Bcl-xL is known to exert anti-apoptotic effects. However, no cleaved caspase-3–positive cells were detected in the gastric tissues of WT, M-B, M-A, or M-AB mice (Figure 4A). Apoptosis was also assessed at early time points in M-B and M-AB mice following TAM administration. No clear evidence of apoptosis in epithelial cells was observed from day 7 (before tumor formation) to one month (after tumor formation) after TAM treatment (Figure 4B). Thus, although Bcl-xL is upregulated in Apc-deficient gastric tumors, its anti-apoptotic function is limited in this tumor model. Given that Bcl-xL is a transcriptional target of YAP,23 we also analyzed YAP expression. In WT and M-B mice, YAP expression was restricted to the base of the glandular epithelium. In contrast, both M-A and M-AB mice exhibited strong YAP expression in tumor regions; however, no significant difference was observed between these groups, suggesting that Bcl-xL deletion does not influence YAP pathway activation (Figure 4C).

To further evaluate gene expression changes, quantitative RT-PCR was performed using complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesized from gastric tissues of M-A and M-AB mice. Wnt target genes, including Lgr5, Axin2, Sox9, Fzd7, and Ccnd1, were markedly downregulated in M-AB mice, compared with M-A mice. In contrast, Lgr4 and cMyc expression did not differ significantly between M-A and M-AB mice (Figure 4D). Collectively, these results indicate that Bcl-xL promotes gastric tumorigenesis in part through enhancing Wnt activation, thereby contributing to tumor progression, although these differences may also reflect the smaller tumor burden and lower number of APC-deficient cells in M-AB mice.

B-Cell Lymphoma–Extra-Large Activates the Wnt–β-Catenin Pathway in Tumor Organoids and Xenografts

To investigate the role of Bcl-xL in established gastric tumors in vitro, we employed a gastric organoid culture system. Organoids were derived from the normal gastric antrum of Apcflox/flox and WT mice, infected with Cre-expressing lentivirus, and selected with puromycin to generate genetically modified organoids (Figure 5A). Initially, we aimed to generate organoids from bcl-xlflox/flox and Apcflox/flox; bcl-xlflox/flox mice. However, we were unable to establish stable cultures. In contrast, Apc-deficient organoids were successfully generated. Thus, WT and Apc-deficient organoids (hereafter referred to as Apc organoids) were used for further analysis.

To assess the effect of Bcl-xL on cell proliferation, we treated both WT and Apc organoids with the Bcl-xL–specific inhibitor (A-1155463) and monitored changes in organoid size. In WT organoids, Bcl-xL inhibition did not affect growth and apoptosis (Figure 5B–D and Supplementary Western blotting A–C). In contrast, Apc organoids exhibited a dose-dependent reduction in size upon Bcl-xL inhibition, indicating that Bcl-xL promotes proliferation in the Apc-deficient context (Figure 5C). There was no significant difference in organoid growth between WT and Apc-deficient organoids.

To further evaluate Bcl-xL function, we generated complex mutant organoids using LSL-KrasG12D; LSL-Trp53R172H; Apcflox/flox and LSL-KrasG12D; LSL-Trp53R172H; Apcflox/flox; bcl-xlflox/flox mice infected with Cre-lentivirus and selected (hereafter referred to as KPA and KPAB organoids, respectively) (Figure 6A and B). We selected the additional KrasG12D and Trp53R172H mutations in the Apc-mutant GC model based on previous studies,47,48 to enable the establishment of stable, genetically modified gastric organoids for functional analysis. Fluorescent immunostaining, Western blotting, and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) confirmed Bcl-xL deletion in KPAB organoids (Figure 6B–E).

Western blot analysis also showed reduced expression of Cyclin D1 in KPAB organoids (Figure 3D and Supplementary Western blotting D–F). β-catenin nuclear localization was markedly reduced in KPAB organoids compared with KPA organoids (Figure 6E). qPCR showed that Wnt target genes Axin2 and Ccnd1 were downregulated in KPAB organoids, while no significant changes were detected in Lgr4 or cMyc (Figure 6F).

To evaluate the impact of Bcl-xL on proliferation, we compared organoid growth between KPA and KPAB under different culture conditions. In standard medium, no significant difference in growth was observed (Figure 6G). However, under Wnt-deficient conditions (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium [DMEM]/F12 supplemented only with B27 and N2), KPAB organoids showed significantly smaller size than KPA organoids by day 5 (Figure 6H). Furthermore, treatment of KPA organoids with Bcl-xL inhibitor reduced organoid size, whereas KPAB organoids were unaffected by the inhibitor (Figure 6I). These findings indicate that Bcl-xL is dispensable for proliferation under optimal growth conditions but becomes critical for sustaining tumor growth in APC-deficient organoids under growth factor–limited conditions, which more closely reflect the in vivo tumor environment. Finally, we confirmed that there was no difference in apoptosis occurrence between KPA and KPAB organoids (Figure 6J).

Next, to assess the in vivo tumorigenic potential of Bcl-xL-deficient cells,49 KPA and KPAB organoids were subcutaneously injected into nude mice, and tumor growth was monitored. KPAB-derived tumors were significantly smaller than KPA-derived tumors on both day 14 and day 21 (Figure 7A), indicating that loss of Bcl-xL again impairs xenografted, Apc-deleted tumor growth.

To further investigate molecular changes, xenograft tumors were harvested 1 month after injection and analyzed by immunohistochemical staining. GFP-positive cells, indicating organoid-derived tumor cells, were identified in both KPA and KPAB xenografts. In KPAB tumors, Bcl-xL expression was markedly reduced in GFP-positive cells, confirming successful knockout (Figure 7B). Nuclear localization of β-catenin was also decreased in KPAB tumors (Figure 7C), along with reduced expression of Wnt target genes Cyclin D1 and SOX9 and a proliferation marker Ki67, whereas no significant differences in YAP expression was observed between KPA and KPAB tumors, consistent with findings from the genetically engineered mouse and organoid models (Figure 7D). Finally, we confirmed that there was no difference in apoptosis occurrence between KPA and KPAB xenografts (Figure 7E).

These results collectively demonstrate that Bcl-xL promotes gastric tumorigenesis by enhancing Wnt pathway activity and supporting tumor cell proliferation, both in vitro and in vivo.

Negative Regulation of Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor 4 α by B-Cell Lymphoma–Extra-Large Contributes to the Activation of Wnt–β-Catenin Signaling

To comprehensively assess gene expression changes associated with Bcl-xL deficiency, RNA sequencing was performed using RNA extracted from WT, KPA, and KPAB organoids. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated distinct clustering of each group, indicating unique transcriptomic profiles (Figure 8A). A focused heatmap of key gene sets (Figure 8B) revealed higher expression of Wnt-related genes in KPA organoids compared with WT and KPAB organoids. Within the Bcl2 family, Bcl2l1 was downregulated in KPAB organoids. Furthermore, the differentiation markers, including Muc6 and Tff2 for neck cell lineage and Muc5ac and Tff1 for pit cell lineage, are upregulated in KPAB organoids, compared with KPA organoids (Figure 8C). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed downregulation of gene sets associated with the WNT pathway and epithelial cell proliferation in KPAB organoids, consistent with the results of other experiments (Figure 8D). These findings suggest that loss of Bcl-xL impairs WNT-driven epithelial proliferation, which may contribute to the reduced growth capacity observed in KPAB organoids.

Comparing the top 100 differentially expressed genes across the 3 groups, Hnf4a exhibited decreased expression in KPA organoids and increased expression in KPAB organoids (Figure 8E and F and Supplementary Table 1). Hnf4a is a transcription factor reported to regulate the Wnt signaling and cell proliferation pathways.50 To assess its functional role, we overexpressed Hnf4a in KPA organoids using adenoviral vectors (Figure 9A). Hnf4a-overexpression (OE) suppressed cell proliferation in KPA organoids (Figure 9B). To thoroughly characterize transcriptional differences, we performed RNA sequencing on control and Hnf4a-OE KPA organoids. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that Bcl-xL plays a previously unrecognized role in promoting gastric tumorigenesis beyond its canonical antiapoptotic function. Using genetically engineered mouse models, tumor organoids, and human clinical datasets, we show that Bcl-xL enhances GC progression primarily by augmenting Wnt–β-catenin signaling and tumor cell proliferation. Mechanistically, Bcl-xL negatively regulates the transcription factors HNF4A and PPARγ, thereby linking stem cell signaling, transcriptional control, and metabolic regulation during gastric tumor development.

Traditionally, Bcl-xL has been characterized as a key antiapoptotic member of the BCL2 family, contributing to tumor cell survival under stress conditions.57 However, accumulating evidence suggests that BCL2 family proteins also exert noncanonical functions, including regulation of mitochondrial metabolism, transcriptional programs, and cell–cycle progression.57 Our findings substantially expand this paradigm by identifying Bcl-xL as a positive regulator of oncogenic Wnt signaling in gastric epithelium. Notably, Bcl-xL deletion did not induce apoptosis in either normal or neoplastic gastric tissues, indicating that its tumor-promoting effects in this context are largely independent of cell death suppression. Instead, loss of Bcl-xL consistently attenuated proliferation, Wnt target gene expression, and tumor growth in vivo and in vitro, highlighting a novel and apoptosis-independent oncogenic function of Bcl-xL.

Our transcriptomic analyses revealed that Bcl-xL orchestrates broader transcriptional and metabolic programs through negative regulation of HNF4A and PPARγ. HNF4A is a key epithelial differentiation factor that has been reported to antagonize Wnt signaling and restrict proliferation in gastrointestinal tissues.50 Restoration of HNF4A expression in tumor organoids suppressed Wnt pathway activation and inhibited growth, indicating that Bcl-xL–mediated repression of HNF4A represents a critical mechanism by which Wnt signaling is sustained in gastric tumors. In parallel, PPARγ, an essential regulator of lipid metabolism and cellular differentiation,58 was markedly upregulated upon Bcl-xL loss. PPARγ activation might directly suppress Wnt signaling and significantly inhibited tumor growth and altered metabolic gene expression, suggesting that Bcl-xL promotes tumor progression in part by constraining differentiation-associated metabolic pathways. The partial regulation of PPARγ by HNF4A further suggests a hierarchical network linking transcriptional control and metabolic state in GC cells.

Emerging evidence indicates that stem cell signaling, Wnt–β-catenin activity, and cellular metabolism are tightly interconnected during gastrointestinal tumorigenesis.59 Wnt signaling is not only a central driver of epithelial stemness and proliferation but also a key regulator of metabolic reprogramming, promoting anabolic pathways that support rapid cell division. In gastric and intestinal epithelia, sustained Wnt activation has been shown to enhance glycolysis and lipid biosynthesis while suppressing differentiation-associated metabolic programs.60,61 Conversely, transcription factors such as HNF4A and PPARγ are known to promote epithelial differentiation and metabolic homeostasis and have been reported to antagonize excessive Wnt signaling, either directly or indirectly, by regulating Wnt-related gene expression and lipid metabolic pathways.50,58 Disruption of this balance through persistent Wnt activation coupled with repression of metabolic differentiation programs favors the maintenance of a highly proliferative, stem-like tumor cell state. In this context, our findings suggest that Bcl-xL functions as a molecular integrator that reinforces Wnt-driven proliferative signaling while concomitantly suppressing HNF4A- and PPARγ-mediated metabolic programs, including fatty lipid, bile acid, xenobiotic, and cholesterol metabolism. The attenuation of these differentiation-associated metabolic pathways compromises normal epithelial differentiation, thereby creating a permissive environment for gastric tumor growth.

Several previous studies have reported the expression patterns of Bcl-xL in gastrointestinal tissues. Regarding the stomach,38,62 Bcl-xL is broadly expressed in nearly all epithelial cells in mice based on immunohistochemistry. In humans, Bcl-xL expression was increased in gastric tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal mucosa, while some degree of Bcl-xL expression was also observed in normal tissue. In other organs, immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated diffused Bcl-xL expression in both normal and tumor tissues of the human and mice colon.42,63 In contrast, Bcl-xL expression was relatively low in WT pancreatic tissue but progressively increased during neoplastic progression, with stronger expression in pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia 2–3 lesions and further elevation in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma for both human and mice.40,64 Taken together, these previous reports suggest that, consistent with our findings, broad Bcl-xL expression is observed in normal gastrointestinal tissues, whereas Bcl-xL expression patterns can vary depending on the tissue context and disease stage.

In addition to the tumor-intrinsic context examined in this study, the role of Bcl-xL may vary depending on the inflammatory microenvironment. Under conditions of chronic gastric inflammation, such as H pylori-associated gastritis and autoimmune gastritis,65,66 epithelial cells are exposed to increased oxidative stress, cytokine signaling, and immune-mediated injury. In such settings, antiapoptotic factors including Bcl-xL may play a more prominent role in maintaining epithelial survival and tissue integrity. Chronic inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF-κB activation and cytokine-mediated stress responses, may increase the reliance of epithelial cells on Bcl-xL–mediated survival mechanisms. Therefore, although our current model does not demonstrate a major role for Bcl-xL in regulating apoptosis in established tumors under noninflammatory conditions, its functional contribution may be context dependent and could be enhanced during inflammation-driven gastric tumorigenesis.

Although APC deletion results in constitutive activation of Wnt–β-catenin signaling, accumulating evidence indicates that Wnt signaling output is not binary but exists along a graded spectrum.67 Even in APC-deficient contexts, transcriptional activity of β-catenin–dependent target genes can be further modulated by additional regulatory factors. In our system, Bcl-xL enhances Wnt–β-catenin signaling; however, we did not observe impaired organoid growth or morphologic features consistent with excessive Wnt activation. This suggests that Bcl-xL-mediated augmentation likely operates within a permissive signaling range rather than exceeding the threshold that compromises proliferation. Moreover, external Wnt and R-spondin supplementation were included to maintain optimal culture conditions. APC-deficient organoids can still respond quantitatively to extracellular Wnt modulation, and signaling output is influenced by multiple layers of regulation beyond APC loss alone.

In summary, our study identifies Bcl-xL as a multifaceted regulator of gastric tumorigenesis that integrates stem cell signaling, transcriptional control, and metabolic regulation. These findings not only redefine the biological functions of Bcl-xL in GC but also highlight new therapeutic opportunities. Targeting Bcl-xL–dependent signaling networks, either directly or through modulation of downstream pathways such as Wnt or PPARγ, may represent a promising strategy for the prevention and treatment of GC.

Materials and Methods

Mice

Mist1-CreERT, Apcflox/flox, and LoxP-stop-LoxP (LSL)-Trp53R172H mice were provided by Columbia University, and bcl-xlflox/flox mice were provided by Osaka University. LSL-TdTomato and LSL-KrasG12D mice were purchased from Jackson laboratory. Lgr5-DTR-EGFP mice were obtained from Genentech (South San Francisco, CA).68 WT and nude mice were purchased from Crea Japan. Mice other than nude mice were from the C57BL6J background. All mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free facility at the University of Tokyo in accordance with the institutional guidelines for animal experimentation and were provided with commercially available food and water ad libitum. All animal studies and procedures were approved by the ethics committee of the University of Tokyo (ID: 24-2). Both male and female mice were used in this study. No obvious sex-based differences in phenotype were observed. For each experiment, animals were sex- and age-matched between experimental groups.

For analyses using mouse gastric tumor models, WT, Mist1-CreERT; bcl-xlflox/flox, Mist1-CreERT; Apcflox/flox, and Mist1-CreERT; Apcflox/flox; bcl-xlflox/flox mice were used. When mice were incorporated LSL-TdTomato, the genetically modified proteins were labeled with fluorescence. Lineage tracing was induced by administering TAM at 6 mg per 20 g body weight on 2 separate days, with a 1-day interval between injections to all 6-week-old mice. Thus, mice received a total of 2 TAM injections, and they were sacrificed at 2 months for analysis. For analysis using the organoid culture system, transgenic organoids were established from WT, Apcflox/flox, LSL-KrasG12D; LSL-Trp53R172H; Apcflox/flox, and LSL-KrasG12D; LSL-Trp53R172H; Apcflox/flox; bcl-xlflox/flox mice.

Organoid Culture

Organoids were generated as described previously.22, 23, 24 Mice were cervically dislocated, the gastric antrum was excised and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), then shaken in 5 mM EDTA (Invitrogen) + PBS for 45 minutes at 4 °C. Cells derived from the gastric mucosa were detached from the serosa; the required amount of cells were collected, dissolved in Matrigel (Corning), seeded in 24-well plates, and medium was added 30 minutes later. The medium was a Wnt3a and R-spondin 3 conditioned medium69 prepared from L-WRN cells, which are modified L-Wnt3a cells derived from mouse L cells, and advanced DMEM and F12 (Invitrogen) mixed 2:1. It was then mixed with penicillin and streptomycin (Wako), Gluta MAX (Invitrogen), HEPES (Invitrogen), B27 (Invitrogen), N2 (Invitrogen), N-acetylcysteine (Wako, 1 mM), and Nicotinamide (Sigma-Aldrich, 10 mM). Epidermal growth factor (Wako, 50 ng/mL), fibroblast growth factor 10 (Peprotech, 100 ng/mL), Y-27632 (Wako, 10 μM), Gastrin (Tocris, 0.01 μM), and Primocin (Invitrogen) were used in the mixture. The medium was changed twice a week. For the quantification of organoid diameters, n = 10 refers to 10 organoids randomly selected from the same condition.

Treatment In Vitro

For the pharmacologic inhibition of Bcl-xL, A-1155463 (Selleck: S7800) was used. A-1155463 is a highly potent and selective Bcl-xL inhibitor that exhibits picomolar binding affinity for Bcl-xL, while showing more than 1000-fold weaker binding affinity for Bcl-2 and related proteins, including Bcl-w (Ki = 19 nM) and Mcl-1 (Ki > 440 nM), as described in the manufacturer’s documentation.

A-1155463 (5 mg) was dissolved in 0.7465 mL of DMSO to prepare a 10-mM stock solution, which was further diluted to generate working solutions at the indicated concentrations. During organoid passaging, the Bcl-xL inhibitor at each concentration was added to the culture medium. After 72 hours, organoids were passaged again. Starting the day after passaging, organoid images were captured, and the long-axis diameter of each organoid was measured to compare changes in organoid growth over time.

Xenograft

Organoids are composed solely of epithelial cells and are not influenced by the tumor microenvironment, such as stromal or immune components. Therefore, xenograft models are essential for evaluating the interaction between tumor cells and their microenvironment.49 Organoid cells (1 × 106 cells) were added to 100 μL of Matrigel and 100 μL of advanced DMEM and F12 and injected subcutaneously into the back of nude mice at 6 weeks after birth with a 26 G needle. The tumor diameters of the subcutaneous tumors were measured, and the volumes were calculated every week. After 1 month, the mice were sacrificed, and the subcutaneous tumors were removed for analysis.

Hematoxylin and Eosin Stain

Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed on paraffin-embedded sections of mouse tissues and organoids fixed in 10% buffered formalin solution (pH 7.4). After deparaffinization and rinsing, nuclear staining was performed with hematoxylin. After rinsing in running water, the sections were stained with eosin, dehydrated, permeabilized, sealed with Mount Quick (DAI), and the specimens were examined.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed on formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded sections of mouse tissue. The sections were deparaffinized, autoclaved at 120 °C for 10 minutes for antigen activation, soaked in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 5 minutes for inactivation of endogenous peroxidase, and blocked in 10% goat serum for 30 minutes. The sample was outlined with a water-repellent pen and reacted with primary antibody diluted in PBS mixed with 2.5% Bovine Serum Albumin Cohn Fraction V (hereafter referred to as 2.5% BSA) and allowed to stand overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the antibody was washed with PBS, followed by the reaction of the secondary antibody, and allowed to stand at 4 °C for 1 hour. After washing with PBS, the antibody was incubated with avidin-biotin–labeled enzyme complex (VECTASTAIN Elite ABC Kit [Vector Laboratories]) for 20 minutes at room temperature, the avidin-biotin complex method was performed, and the reaction was carried out with enzyme substrate solution (Liquid 3,3'-diaminobenzidine [DAB] + Substrate Chromogen System, DAKO) for tens of seconds to several minutes, followed by rinsing. Nuclear staining was performed with hematoxylin, followed by rinsing, dehydration, permeabilization, sealing with MountQuick (DAI), and mirror examination. Bcl-xL (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology: 2764S), cyclinD1 (1:200, Thermo Fisher Scientific: RM-91004-SO), CD44v (1:200, BIO RAD: MCA1967), and cleaved caspase 3 (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology: 9661S) were used for immunohistochemistry.

Immunofluorescence

Fluorescent immunostaining was performed on formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded sections of mouse tissues and organoids. The sections were deparaffinized and autoclaved at 120 °C for 10 minutes for antigen activation. The samples were surrounded with a pen for water generation, reacted with primary antibody diluted in 2.5% BSA, and left overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS the next day, secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 555, Alexa Fluor 488, Alexa Fluor 647 [Invitrogen]) diluted 1:200 in 2.5% BSA were reacted and allowed to stand at room temperature for 1 hour. In Situ Hybridization

In situ hybridization was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections of mouse tissue using the RNAscope 2.5 HD Detection Kit BROWN (ACD). The process involved 60 minutes of baking at 60 °C, 2 5-minute dips in xylene, 100% soaked in ethanol twice for 5 minutes and dried for 5 minutes; then baked at 60 °C for 30 minutes, soaked in hydrogen peroxide solution for 10 minutes, then soaked in 1× Target Retrieval Buffer (ACD) and boiled at 100 °C for 15 minutes. The cells were washed with distilled water, dehydrated with 100% ethanol, soaked in 5 to 8 drops of hydrogen peroxide (ACD), allowed to stand at room temperature for 10 minutes, soaked in Protease Plus (ACD) for 30 minutes at 40 °C, washed with distilled water, and hybridized at 40 °C for 2 hours with Lgr5 probe (ACD: 312171). The cells were washed in 1× wash buffer (ACD) for 2 minutes, immersed in 20× saline sodium citrate buffer (1000 mL distilled water with NaCl 175.3 g + sodium citrate 88.2 g adjusted to pH 7.0 and autoclaved sterile) and left overnight. After washing with 1× wash buffer for 2 minutes, AMP1 (ACD) was added dropwise to cover the area enclosed by the hydrophobic barrier pen and allowed to stand for 30 minutes at 40 °C. After washing with 1×wash buffer for 2 minutes, AMP2 (ACD) was added drop and allowed to stand for 15 minutes at 40 °C. After washing with 1× wash buffer for 2 minutes, AMP3 (ACD) was added drop and allowed to stand for 30 minutes at 40 °C. The cells were washed with 1× wash buffer for 2 minutes, AMP4 (ACD) was added drop and allowed to stand at 40 °C for 15 minutes. The cells were washed with 1× wash buffer for 2 minutes, AMP5 (ACD) was added drop and allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 minutes. The cells were washed with 1× wash buffer for 2 minutes, AMP6 (ACD) was added drop and allowed to stand at room temperature for 15 minutes. The cells were washed with 1× wash buffer for 2 minutes, then DAB-A and DAB-B (ACD) were added dropwise at 1:1 and allowed to stand for 10 minutes at room temperature. After rinsing, nuclear staining was performed with hematoxylin, soaked in 0.02% ammonia water, rinsed, soaked in 70% ethanol twice for 5 minutes, soaked in 100% ethanol twice for 5 minutes, dehydrated, permeated with xylene twice for 5 minutes, sealed with Mount Quick (DAI), and specimens were examined.

Histopathologic Analysis

All images were captured with a Leica DMi8 inverted fluorescence microscope and manually quantified after visualization using LAS X software. For each mouse, the average value was calculated from 3 randomly selected, well-oriented fields at 10× magnification. Quantification was conducted manually on fluorescence or chromogenic images by investigators blinded to the experimental conditions. Group-level values were calculated by averaging across animals and are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Unless otherwise indicated, analyses were performed using 3 to 5 biologically independent mice per group.

Western Blotting

Gastric tissue and organoid cells were homogenized by adding cell lysate buffer (50 mM Tris HCl, 1% Triton-X, 5 mM EDTA, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1.25 mM NaF, protease inhibitor cocktail (cOmplete Mini, Roche) and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 5 minutes to extract proteins from the supernatant. Proteins were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Pall Corporation). The cells were blocked by shaking with 5% milk for 1 hour, washed with Tris Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBS-T), and reacted with the primary antibody by shaking overnight at 4 °C. The reaction was repeated at 4 °C for 1 hour. After washing with TBS-T, the antibody was reacted with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody (GE Healthcare) while shaking at room temperature for 1 hour. After washing with TBS-T, proteins were detected by exposure with a chemiluminescence detection system using the luminescent reagent ImmunoStar LD (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals). Bcl-xL (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology: 2764S), cyclinD1 (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific: RM-91004-SO), PPARγ (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology: 2435), total caspase-3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology: 9662), cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology: 9664), and β-actin (1:10,000, cell signaling technology: 5125S) antibodies were used for Western blotting.

Lentivirus Transduction

Organoids in culture were washed with PBS and recovered from Matrigel (Corning) basement membrane matrix using Cell Recovery Solution (Corning). We collected single-celled organoids using TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific), added pLV-EF1-Cre-2a-vsfGFP-2a-puro lentivirus (originated in Tokyo University24) at an optimized concentration for infection and the above medium (after adding a solution of 60 μL) and Trans Dux MAX (System Biosciences, 0.6 μL), centrifuged at 300 g for 30 minutes and mixed well with a pipette 3 times, dissolved in Matrigel, seeded in 24-well plates, and added medium 60 minutes later. The next day, the virus infection was confirmed by microscopic observation of GFP expression. Two to 3 days later, puromycin was added to the medium at 0.5 to 10 μg/mL to select for organoids undergoing genetic recombination. Other methods of selection included culture in medium without Wnt3A conditioned medium or R-spondin 3 conditioned medium, or by combining the method of collecting and culturing only organoids expressing GFP by genetic recombination under a microscope. We were unable to establish stable knockout organoids from either bcl-xLflox/flox mice or Apcflox/flox; bcl-xLflox/flox mice following Cre-expressing lentivirus-mediated recombination, despite repeated attempts (n > 5). To analyze the cell proliferation rate of organoids, organoids of similar initial size (n = 10) were selected on day 1, and longitudinal changes in length diameter were measured using tile-scan imaging.

Adenovirus Infection

Adenoviruses were purchased in vectorbuilder (VB210912-1088ebt: pAV[Exp]-EF1A>mPparg[NM_011146.3](ns):T2A:DsRed_Express2 and VB220822-1173ydv: pAV[Exp]-CAG>mHnf4a[NM_008261.3]:IRES:mKate2) and were suspended in organoid cells, arranged for multiplicity of infection of 100, after dissolution in Matrigel. After 3 hours, organoids were seeded in 24-well plates. After 48 hours, the medium was replaced with medium without growth factors. Organoids without the selection procedure were harvested after 24 hours, and their diameters were calculated for proliferation analysis in 5 days.

The gene expression was analyzed by Western blotting, qPCR, and RNA sequencing.

RNA Sequencing

Sequences were performed on RNA extracted from organoids using the NovaSeq 6000 system from Illumina. Global normalization was performed on the obtained read information using TopHat-2.0.13 bowtie 2-2.2.3 as the alignment tool and Subio Platform as the analysis tool. Variability multipliers greater than 1.7 and P values less than .05 after correction for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method were counted as significant variable genes. Gene Ontology analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis were performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery. GSEA was also performed to analyze gene groups with significant expression variation. A false discovery rate threshold of 0.25 was used to define statistical significance. However, to illustrate similarities between several models, sigmoid curves were plotted to visualize overall trends, including gene sets that did not reach statistical significance.

Data Analysis of Human Gastric Cancer, Esophageal Cancer, and Colorectal Cancer

First, the expression status of Bcl-xL was evaluated by fluorescent immunostaining using human GC tissue microarrays. The tissue microarrays were purchased from US Biomax, and a total of 72 cases were scored according to the intensity of Bcl-xL expression and compared at each stage.

Second, using the UALCAN application and the TCGA database,53 we evaluated BCL2L1 expression in normal gastric mucosa and GC, esophageal cancer, and colorectal cancer.

Third, RNA sequence data of 450 GC cases registered in TCGA were analyzed using the web tool “Phantasus” (https://artyomovlab.wustl.edu/phantasus/),55 and the expression status of BCL2L1 was compared with WNT target genes.

Fourth, RNA sequence data of 412 GC (PanCancer Atlas), 87 esophageal adenocarcinoma (PanCancer Atlas), and 224 colorectal cancer70 cases registered in TCGA were downloaded via Cbioportal website (https://www.cbioportal.org/) and analyzed with GSEA.

Fifth, we used the Kaplan-Meier Plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=service&cancer=gastric), a web tool for analyzing the gene expression database and the prognosis of patients with GC and colorectal cancer, to examine the contribution of each gene to survival of 875 patients with GC and 1336 patients with colorectal cancer.

Finally, we compared the occurrence of GC with gender, age, smoking, eradication of H pylori, comorbidity (Charlson comorbidity index), pioglitazone and metformin status in 29,905 hospitalized diabetic patients registered in the DPC of the University of Tokyo Hospital from April 2014 to March 2019, as described previously.71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76 The ethics review number was 2058-[2]. The Analysis of Single-Cell RNA Sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing data of mice GC samples and patients with GC were obtained from GSE217498 and GSE239676, respectively. Filtered count matrices were imported to R for further analysis using the Seurat package. Dimensionality reduction was performed by PCA and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection embedding with 30 principal components and resolution 1.0. Epithelial cells were then subsetted based on epithelial markers. For the human dataset, BCL2L1 expression in tumor epithelial cells was obtained using the “AverageExpression” function in Seurat. Patients were then classified into BCL2L1-high or BCL2L1-low. Then we conducted differential expression and GSEA on pseudobulk workflow by using “AggregateExpression” function in the Seurat and R packages DESeq2 and fgsea.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as a means with 95% standard deviations, whereas categorical variables are expressed as numbers and frequencies (%). The differences between the means were compared using either the Student t test or the Wilcoxon test. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.
