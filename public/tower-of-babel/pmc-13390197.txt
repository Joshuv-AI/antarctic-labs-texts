Long-term clinical course and recurrence pattern of orbital Kimura’s disease: a retrospective case series
Authors: Runzi Yang, Tingting Ren, Rui Liu, Nan Wang, Liangyuan Xu, Jianmin Ma, Jing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKimura’s disease (KD) is a rare chronic inflammatory disorder that predominantly affects young Asian males and typically involves the head and neck region. Orbital involvement is uncommon and remains insufficiently characterized, particularly with respect to long-term outcomes. This study aimed to investigate the clinical features, imaging characteristics, treatment strategies, and long-term prognosis of orbital KD.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective case series of patients with histopathologically confirmed KD involving the orbit at a single tertiary referral center between January 2010 and December 2025. Demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, imaging findings, pathological features, treatment modalities, and follow-up outcomes were reviewed. For suspected recurrence, recurrent clinical manifestations were first identified during follow-up, and orbital imaging was used to confirm lesion recurrence and assess lesion extent when indicated. Pathological confirmation was obtained only when repeat surgery or biopsy was performed.ResultsEight male patients were included, with a mean age of 21.25 ± 17.20 years (range, 6–47 years). Painless eyelid swelling was the most common presenting symptom (62.5%). The eyelid and lacrimal gland were the most frequently involved structures. Histopathological examination revealed dense eosinophilic infiltration and lymphoid follicular hyperplasia in all cases. Surgery combined with systemic glucocorticoid therapy was the primary treatment modality (87.5%). Among the seven patients with available follow-up data, the mean follow-up duration was 87.57 ± 55.42 months. Recurrence occurred in two of the seven patients with available follow-up data (28.6%; 25.0% of the entire cohort), both presenting as local orbital/periorbital recurrence. No distant disease progression, metastasis, or disease-related mortality was observed.ConclusionsOrbital KD predominantly affects young males and most commonly involves the eyelid and lacrimal gland. Although local recurrence may occur, the long-term prognosis appears favorable. Structured follow-up remains important for detecting recurrence and guiding subsequent management.Keywords: Kimura’s disease, Orbit, Lacrimal gland, Recurrence, Long-term follow-up

Introduction

Kimura’s disease (KD) is a rare, chronic inflammatory disorder of unknown etiology that primarily affects young Asian males [1]. It is characterized by painless subcutaneous masses, predominantly located in the head and neck region, and is frequently associated with peripheral eosinophilia and elevated serum immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels [2, 3]. Histopathologically, KD demonstrates dense eosinophilic infiltration, lymphoid follicular hyperplasia with germinal center formation, and varying degrees of vascular proliferation and fibrosis.

Although KD most commonly involves the salivary glands and subcutaneous tissues of the head and neck, orbital involvement is relatively uncommon [4, 5]. When present, orbital KD may affect the eyelid, lacrimal gland, extraocular muscles, or other periocular soft tissues, potentially mimicking malignant or infiltrative orbital diseases. Due to its rarity and overlapping clinical features with other orbital inflammatory or neoplastic conditions, accurate diagnosis can be challenging [6].

Previous reports on orbital KD have largely consisted of isolated case reports or small case series, often with limited follow-up duration. Consequently, the long-term clinical course, recurrence pattern, and optimal management strategies remain incompletely defined. While KD is generally considered a benign condition, recurrence is not uncommon, and the timing and predictors of relapse in orbital involvement have not been systematically evaluated [7, 8].

In the present study, we retrospectively analyzed eight patients with histopathologically confirmed orbital KD treated at a single tertiary referral center over a 15-year period. Although this study was descriptive in nature, it provides orbital-specific long-term follow-up data on a rare disease entity that has mostly been reported in isolated case reports or small series. We aimed to characterize the clinical manifestations, imaging features, pathological findings, treatment approaches, recurrence characteristics, and long-term outcomes of orbital KD, with particular attention to clinically relevant information for ophthalmologists.

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective case series included patients diagnosed with KD with orbital involvement at Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, between January 2010 and December 2025. All diagnoses were confirmed by histopathological examination.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histopathological confirmation of KD; (2) primary clinical manifestation localized to the ocular adnexa or orbit; and (3) absence of concurrent lacrimal gland malignancy or systemic malignant disease. Exclusion criteria included: (1) incomplete clinical or follow-up records; or (2) orbital lesions secondary to metastatic tumors or other systemic malignancies. Based on these criteria, eight patients were included in the final analysis.

Data collection

Clinical records were systematically reviewed. The following variables were collected: demographic characteristics (age, sex), laterality, presenting symptoms, involved anatomical structures, hematological parameters, imaging findings, histopathological and immunohistochemical features, treatment modalities, follow-up duration, and clinical outcomes.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings were reviewed to assess lesion location, signal characteristics, involvement of adjacent orbital structures, and contrast enhancement patterns. Histopathological diagnosis was based on hematoxylin–eosin staining and immunohistochemical analysis, including evaluation of eosinophilic infiltration, lymphoid follicular hyperplasia, vascular proliferation, fibrosis, and Ki-67 expression.

Recurrence was defined as the reappearance or progression of orbital/periorbital swelling, palpable mass, proptosis, or radiological evidence of disease after initial treatment. Recurrence assessment was based on clinical examination and orbital imaging when recurrent disease was suspected. In this retrospective cohort, serial imaging was not performed at uniform intervals; therefore, the growth rate of recurrent lesions and a standardized radiological comparison between initial and recurrent lesions could not be reliably evaluated.

Treatment strategy

Surgical treatment was performed with the aim of removing the lesion as completely as feasible while preserving orbital and ocular adnexal structures. The extent of excision was determined intraoperatively according to lesion location, involvement of adjacent tissues, patient age, and functional considerations. For relatively localized conjunctival or eyelid lesions, complete lesion excision was attempted. For lesions involving the lacrimal gland or deeper orbital structures, maximal safe excision was performed rather than radical removal when preservation of ocular adnexal function was considered necessary. All included patients achieved complete excision with negative surgical margins according to the operative and pathological records. Systemic glucocorticoid therapy was administered after surgery in patients considered to require postoperative anti-inflammatory treatment. The initial dose was generally equivalent to oral prednisone at 0.5–0.6 mg/kg/day. Dose tapering was individualized according to clinical response, lesion regression, recurrence risk, and drug tolerability, with sequential dose reduction usually performed every 1–2 weeks. Because this was a retrospective study, the exact total duration of glucocorticoid therapy was not uniformly documented across all patients.

Follow-up

Follow-up data were obtained through outpatient visits and medical record review. During the first postoperative year, patients were generally followed every 3 months. From 1 to 3 years after treatment, follow-up visits were usually scheduled every 6 months, and long-term follow-up was continued at approximately 6-month intervals thereafter. Follow-up assessments included evaluation of recurrent eyelid swelling, palpable mass, proptosis, ocular motility, visual function, and other symptoms suggestive of recurrence.

Recurrence detection was primarily based on clinical examination during follow-up. Orbital imaging was performed when recurrent symptoms, suspected deep orbital involvement, or clinically uncertain lesions were noted. Therefore, recurrence assessment was based on a combination of clinical findings and radiological evaluation when indicated, rather than routine imaging at every follow-up visit. The duration of follow-up was calculated from the date of initial treatment to the date of last visit or documented recurrence. Overall survival was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause.

Statistical analysis

Given the small sample size, statistical analysis was primarily descriptive. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Data analysis was performed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, and the requirement for formal ethical approval was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. Written informed consent for publication of clinical images and pathological materials was obtained where applicable. This article does not include patients’ names or other private information that could compromise anonymity.

Painless eyelid swelling was the most common presenting symptom (62.5%), followed by palpable mass (25.0%) and proptosis (12.5%). The median interval from symptom onset to first consultation at our institute was 12 months, with a range of 2 to 108 months. Anatomically, the eyelid was the most frequently affected structure (62.5%), followed by the lacrimal gland (50.0%). Extraocular muscle involvement was also observed (25.0%). Conjunctival involvement was uncommon. Overall, lesions predominantly involved the eyelid–lacrimal gland complex and occasionally extended to adjacent orbital structures. The anterior location and superficial orbital involvement in most cases, no patient developed visual impairment during the disease course or follow-up.

Extraorbital and lymph node findings

Extraorbital and lymph node findings were also reviewed. One patient had parotid gland enlargement and lymphadenopathy at presentation, which were clinically considered reactive hyperplasia and were managed by observation. No definite extraorbital KD lesions requiring specific treatment were documented during follow-up.

Imaging features

MRI findings were available for the included patients and mainly demonstrated enlargement of the lacrimal gland and/or diffuse thickening of periocular soft tissues. The lesions generally showed iso- to slightly hypointense signal on T1-weighted images and heterogeneous slightly hyperintense signal on T2-weighted images. In some cases, strip-like hypointense septa were observed on T2-weighted images, possibly corresponding to fibrotic components. Contrast-enhanced images showed marked heterogeneous enhancement. Extraocular muscle involvement was observed in two patients: Case 4 showed thickening of the superior rectus, inferior rectus, and medial rectus muscles, whereas Case 8 mainly showed superior rectus involvement. No bone destruction or optic nerve compression was identified.

Hematologic features

Peripheral blood eosinophilia was a prominent laboratory finding. Among the six patients with available data, the mean white blood cell count was 9.15 ± 2.64 × 109/L. The mean percentage of eosinophils was 15.7% ± 8.3% (range, 5.1%–26.3%), with five out of six patients (83.3%) demonstrating eosinophilia (defined as > 5%). This consistent elevation suggests that eosinophilic infiltration is a characteristic feature of the disease. Although peripheral eosinophilia was evaluated, serum IgE levels were not available in this retrospective cohort and therefore could not be analyzed.

Pathological features

Histopathological examination revealed consistent findings across all cases. Dense and diffuse eosinophilic infiltration was observed on hematoxylin–eosin staining, accompanied by marked lymphoid follicular hyperplasia with active germinal centers. Prominent capillary proliferation was present in interfollicular regions, occasionally associated with hyalinization. Variable degrees of stromal fibrosis were identified, particularly in cases with longer disease duration. Representative pathological findings from Case 4 are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Immunohistochemical analysis did not demonstrate specific diagnostic markers. Reticular IgE deposition was detected in some cases, and the Ki-67 proliferation index remained low, supporting the benign nature of the lesion.

Treatment and prognosis

Surgical treatment was performed in all patients, with complete excision and pathologically negative margins achieved in all cases. The available surgical procedure and extent for each case are summarized in Table 1. Surgery combined with systemic glucocorticoid therapy was the primary treatment modality (87.5%), whereas one patient underwent surgical excision alone. Among patients receiving systemic glucocorticoids, treatment was generally initiated at a prednisone-equivalent dose of 0.5–0.6 mg/kg/day, followed by sequential tapering every 1–2 weeks according to clinical response and tolerability. The total duration of glucocorticoid therapy varied among patients and could not be consistently extracted from the retrospective records. Among the seven patients with available follow-up data, the mean follow-up duration was 87.57 ± 55.42 months (range, 15–148 months).

To explore potential factors associated with recurrence, we performed a descriptive comparison between recurrent and non-recurrent cases. The two recurrent patients had relatively long disease duration before first consultation, with intervals of 12 and 108 months from symptom onset to presentation. Both recurrent cases involved the superior orbital/extraconal region and periocular soft tissues, and both received surgery combined with systemic glucocorticoid therapy. Compared with non-recurrent cases, the recurrent cases did not show a clearly distinct treatment pattern in this small cohort. Because only two patients experienced recurrence, no formal statistical analysis of prognostic factors was performed.

Recurrence occurred in two of the seven patients with available follow-up data (28.6%; 25.0% of the entire cohort). Both recurrent cases involved the orbital/periorbital region and were considered local recurrence rather than new distant or systemic lesions. Recurrence was identified on the basis of recurrent clinical manifestations together with corresponding radiological findings. No distant disease progression, metastasis, or disease-related mortality was observed. Because follow-up imaging was not obtained at standardized time points in all patients, the growth rate of recurrent lesions and detailed radiological comparison with the initial lesions could not be reliably assessed.

Discussion

KD is a rare chronic inflammatory disorder of unknown etiology that predominantly affects young Asian males and typically presents as painless subcutaneous masses in the head and neck region [9, 10]. Orbital involvement is uncommon and remains poorly characterized due to limited case series and heterogeneous reporting [11]. In the present study, we analyzed eight patients with histopathologically confirmed KD involving the orbit, with a mean follow-up duration exceeding seven years. The main value of this study lies in its orbital-specific focus, pathological confirmation of all cases, relatively long follow-up duration, and detailed description of recurrence and treatment outcomes in a rare clinical setting. Rather than proposing a new pathogenic mechanism, this case series adds clinically useful evidence for diagnosis, follow-up, and management of orbital KD.

All patients in our cohort were male, and the mean age at presentation was 21.25 years. Although male predominance and younger age at onset are well-recognized features of KD, the exclusively male composition of our cohort should be interpreted cautiously [12]. This finding may partly reflect the known epidemiological tendency of KD, but may also be influenced by the small sample size and single-center referral pattern. Therefore, our results should not be interpreted as indicating that orbital KD occurs only in male patients. Larger multicenter studies are needed to clarify whether orbital involvement has a stronger sex predilection than KD at other anatomical sites. The most common presenting symptom was painless eyelid swelling, observed in 62.5% of cases. The eyelid and lacrimal gland were the most frequently involved anatomical regions.

Although KD often involves regional lymph nodes and salivary glands, only one patient in our series showed parotid gland enlargement and lymphadenopathy, which were interpreted as reactive changes during clinical evaluation. No definite extraorbital Kimura’s disease lesion requiring additional treatment was identified during follow-up.

The MRI findings observed in our cohort are consistent with chronic inflammatory orbital lesions but lack disease-specific features. The heterogeneous enhancement and T2 hypointense septa may reflect the coexistence of active inflammatory infiltration and fibrotic components. Therefore, although MRI is useful for defining lesion extent and involvement of adjacent orbital structures, definitive diagnosis still relies on histopathological examination [13–16]. Case 4 provides a representative example showing that even relatively extensive orbital involvement may follow a benign clinical course when appropriately managed (Fig. 2). Despite lacrimal gland, eyelid, and extraocular muscle involvement, the lesion remained confined to the orbit, and the patient achieved sustained remission after surgery and systemic glucocorticoid therapy. This observation suggests that anatomical extent alone may be insufficient to predict prognosis, although larger studies are needed to confirm this. The observed heterogeneous enhancement and T2 hypointense septa likely reflect the underlying histopathological features of KD, such as fibrotic components amidst active inflammation. Importantly, these findings remained confined to the orbit without evidence of bone destruction, supporting a benign inflammatory process rather than a malignant mimicker.

Despite extensive orbital involvement, this patient achieved sustained remission with no recurrence over 104 months following surgery and systemic glucocorticoid therapy. This favorable outcome suggests that anatomical extent alone is insufficient to predict prognosis, and that factors such as treatment response or immune regulation may more critically influence disease relapse.

Consistent with the radiologic findings, histopathological examination in our cohort demonstrated that all cases exhibited dense eosinophilic infiltration and prominent lymphoid follicular hyperplasia with germinal center formation. Variable degrees of stromal fibrosis were observed, particularly in cases with longer disease duration. Immunohistochemical analysis did not reveal specific diagnostic markers, although reticular IgE deposition was observed in some cases and Ki-67 proliferation index remained low. These findings support the concept that KD represents a benign, chronic immune-mediated inflammatory process rather than a neoplastic entity. The pathogenesis of KD is not fully understood, but increasing evidence suggests a Th2-skewed immune response with elevated IgE levels and eosinophilia [17, 18]. Cytokines such as interleukin-4, interleukin-5, and interleukin-13 may play central roles in promoting eosinophil recruitment and IgE production, ultimately contributing to persistent inflammatory infiltration and fibrosis [19, 20].

The differential diagnosis of orbital KD is broad and includes IgG4-related disease, orbital lymphoma, and other diseases [21–23]. IgG4-related disease may present with lacrimal gland enlargement and lymphoplasmacytic infiltration; however, it is typically characterized by elevated IgG4-positive plasma cells and storiform fibrosis, which were not observed in our cases [24, 25]. Orbital lymphoma often presents as a painless mass but demonstrates monoclonal lymphoid proliferation and a higher Ki-67 index [26, 27]. Angiolymphoid hyperplasia with eosinophilia may also show eosinophil-rich inflammation and vascular proliferation, but it is generally regarded as a vascular proliferative lesion and is less commonly associated with marked lymphadenopathy, peripheral eosinophilia, and elevated IgE than KD. In our cohort, peripheral eosinophilia was common, and one patient had parotid gland enlargement and lymphadenopathy, supporting the need to evaluate lymph node and head-and-neck involvement when orbital KD is suspected.

With respect to treatment, surgical excision combined with systemic glucocorticoid therapy was the predominant approach in our cohort. Surgery alone was performed in one case. Over a mean follow-up period of 87.57 months, recurrence occurred in two patients (25%), both within five years after initial treatment. No cases of metastasis or disease-related mortality were observed. The recurrence rate in our series falls within the range reported in previous studies (approximately 15–40%), highlighting the chronic and relapsing nature of KD. However, given the limited sample size and the retrospective nature of the study, this finding should not be interpreted as a definitive temporal pattern of recurrence. Rather, it suggests that careful surveillance is warranted after treatment, particularly in patients with recurrent symptoms or suspected residual disease. Future studies with standardized serial imaging are needed to better characterize the anatomical site, radiological evolution, and growth kinetics of recurrent orbital KD. Complete lesion removal may theoretically reduce local residual disease and thereby lower the risk of local recurrence. However, orbital KD frequently involves functionally important structures, including the lacrimal gland, eyelid, and extraocular muscles; therefore, radical excision may not always be feasible or appropriate. In the present cohort, surgery was performed as maximal safe excision under the principle of preserving ocular adnexal function. Complete excision with negative surgical margins was achieved in all included cases. However, because all patients underwent complete excision and only two recurrent events occurred, we could not compare recurrence risk between complete and incomplete excision or determine whether negative surgical margins independently reduced recurrence risk. Moreover, KD is considered a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disorder, and recurrence may also be influenced by systemic immune activity, lesion extent, and glucocorticoid tapering rather than surgical completeness alone.

Although systemic corticosteroids are effective in reducing lesion size and controlling inflammation, recurrence after tapering or discontinuation remains common [11, 28]. The optimal duration and dosage of steroid therapy have not been standardized. Radiotherapy and immunosuppressive agents have been reported in refractory cases; however, long-term safety considerations must be carefully weighed, especially in young patients. Based on our findings, we recommend long-term follow-up, including regular clinical examinations and imaging when indicated, particularly in patients with recurrent symptoms or suspected residual disease.

Importantly, despite the potential for recurrence, the overall prognosis of orbital KD appears favorable. No malignant transformation, metastasis, or disease-related mortality was observed in our cohort, and the overall survival rate was 100%. These findings reinforce that KD is a benign but chronic inflammatory condition requiring long-term monitoring rather than aggressive oncologic intervention.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small due to the rarity of orbital KD, and the exclusively male cohort may reflect selection bias related to the small sample size and single-center design. Second, the retrospective design limited the availability of standardized laboratory data. In particular, serum IgE levels were not available in this cohort, preventing further evaluation of the immunological profile of orbital KD and its potential association with recurrence. Third, treatment protocols were not uniform, and the exact duration of systemic glucocorticoid therapy was not consistently documented. Although complete excision with negative surgical margins was achieved in all patients, the absence of incomplete-excision cases and the small number of recurrent events precluded evaluation of the independent effect of surgical completeness or margin status on recurrence. Fourth, the small number of recurrent events limited our ability to identify reliable prognostic factors. Multicenter prospective studies with standardized treatment algorithms and follow-up protocols are needed to better define optimal management approaches.

In conclusion, orbital KD predominantly affects young males and most commonly involves the eyelid and lacrimal gland. Although local recurrence may occur, long-term prognosis appears favorable with no associated mortality. Early recognition, histopathological confirmation, appropriate combined therapy, and structured long-term follow-up are essential for optimal disease control.
