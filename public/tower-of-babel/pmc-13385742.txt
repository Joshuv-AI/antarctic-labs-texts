Influence of visual design on young users’ attention to digital museum homepages: an eye-tracking study
Authors: Hao Fang, Hongyun Guo, Mian Wu, Yuanyan Hu, Minjian Hong, Feng Gan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Digital museum websites are often the first point of contact with collections, but their homepage designs vary in effectiveness at attracting and guiding attention. We examined how visual design elements affect the perceived appeal and attention distribution on digital museum homepages for young users. Mixed methods were used: interviews and a focus group identified candidate elements, a self-report study (n=70) rated museum homepages, and an eye-tracking experiment (n=40) tested six virtual homepages representing the most and least appealing sites. Paired t-tests and a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) were used to analyze appeal ratings, and the eye-tracking heat maps with fixation-order metrics characterized attention patterns. Eye-tracking showed that pages with large images (especially with simple animations) attracted early, focused fixations, whereas pages lacking these features produced more dispersed gaze patterns. These findings suggest that homepages with more appealing images and less distracting elements are more likely to attract attention and encourage engagement.

Introduction

Digital museum websites have proliferated, extending cultural access and offering personalized, interactive experiences that appeal to young users1–4. These online museum platforms allow users to discover and engage with collections, and the homepage is a critical entry point that shapes first impressions and influences subsequent navigation. However, many digital museum homepages lack visual appeal and fail to guide users’ attention effectively1,5, hindering information exploration—especially for young visitors prone to rapid task switching. This highlights the need to examine how specific visual design elements influence perceived visual appeal and early attention distribution on museum websites.

Young adults represent an important audience segment for digital museums; however, their information practices are shaped by choice-rich online environments in which attention is constrained and task switching is routine6,7. Research on media multitasking suggests that heavier media multitasking is often associated with heightened susceptibility to distraction and, in many studies, poorer performance on certain cognitive-control measures, although findings are mixed8,9. Moreover, screen-based reading is frequently characterised by browsing and scanning, keyword spotting, and non-linear navigation rather than sustained linear reading10. In information-saturated contexts, information overload has been discussed as a deleterious condition that can generate processing difficulties and negative affect such as anxiety, thereby impairing effective engagement with information tasks11. For digital museum websites, these behavioural tendencies underscore the need for interface cues that facilitate rapid orientation, efficient evaluation, and sustained engagement with cultural information. Accordingly, examining how young users allocate visual attention and respond to specific page elements is critical for improving information findability and overall experience in digital museums.

Website quality is recognized as a crucial factor in enhancing users’ perceived value and revisit intention in digital museums12. Visual appeal, in particular, is a key determinant of quality in knowledge-based web design, such as digital museums13–15. This study treats visual appeal as a composite construct, the core of which lies in users’ judgments regarding the visual appeal of the homepage’s visible design–that is, their cognitive evaluation of its visual appearance16. At the same time, existing research indicates that such evaluations may be accompanied by emotional responses and may be related to other user judgments (such as perceived usability and credibility). Therefore, visual appeal in this study refers to participants’ overall evaluative judgments of the homepage’s visual presentation. Such judgments form first impressions and may further influence interaction willingness and subsequent attention orientation. It can guide observers’ attention, making it easier for them to focus on specific objects or areas17. Furthermore, visual appeal also contributes to prolonging the observers’ sustained attention on specific content, as attractive elements can capture their gaze and reduce attentional dispersion18. From an attention perspective, models and reviews indicate that salient image features can guide early selection, while goal-directed factors can bias selection over time18,19.However, many studies of digital museums evaluate aesthetics or visual appeal at an aggregate level (e.g., overall ratings or quality dimensions), which offers limited guidance on which specific layout schemes and visual elements drive attention and evaluation during actual use2. This limitation is also theoretically important, because HCI research has long suggested that users form rapid aesthetic impressions of webpages and that these impressions can shape broader judgments of interface quality and subsequent interaction tendencies19. Building on this perspective, the study extends the holistic focus on website quality in digital museum research to the specific design features of the homepage, thereby enriching the existing body of research in this field regarding the visual design characteristics of homepages and their significance in terms of user perception.This gap motivates a more element-level analysis of visual appeal in digital museum webpages.

To address this gap, we used a mixed-methods approach combining qualitative interviews and a focus group to identify appealing elements and formulate hypotheses. We then tested web pages with these elements using self-report ratings and found their impact on perceived appeal. Finally, we conducted eye-tracking experiments to objectively observe how these design elements attract attention, isolating their effects on user perception of appeal.

Research methodology

Research 1: a qualitative study of the visual elements of digital museums

Exploring appealing visual elements through user interviews

Eight university students (4 males, 4 females, age 20–27) participated in the interviews, and each received a small reward. Participants browsed 30 domestic and international museum homepages, then were interviewed about their demographics and visual expectations. To reduce subjective bias in sample selection and to maintain relative consistency of webpage features for comparison, the homepages were randomly sampled within the same website type. Science-communication-oriented digital museum websites were selected as the focal context because prior research suggests that science and technology museum websites tend to perform strongly in presentation, usability, interactivity, and e-services15, these characteristics make such websites a suitable context for examining salient visual design features on digital museum homepages. The interviews focused on participants’ experiences with the homepages.

Further filtering of visual elements through focus groups

A focus group of four experienced website design experts reviewed the 30 museum homepages and discussed the interview findings to identify the most important visual elements. We aimed to reduce subjective influences and focus on design features that could be clearly identified and compared across webpages.

When selecting candidate elements, we prioritized elements that could be defined relatively consistently across the sampled homepages and translated into observable coding features for subsequent quantitative analysis. Due to their variability and difficulty in quantifying, bright colors and 3D effects were excluded20,21. Color can evoke different psychological responses, but its perceived appeal is often closely tied to the semantic tone and communicative intent of webpage content22,23; for this reason, it was not treated as an independent visual factor in the present study Reactions to 3D effects were mixed–some found them novel24, while others felt they added complexity25. Because such divergent aesthetic responses made it difficult to treat 3D effects as a stable evaluative feature across the sampled homepages, they were not included in the final set of visual elements. The analysis identified four key visual elements that attract users: large images, short text, simple animations, and concise template layouts. Hypotheses

Through focus group discussions and subsequent visual element screening, we identified four key visual elements in digital museum websites that can attract the attention of young people: large images, short text, simple animation effects, and concise template layouts. Further quantitative research hypotheses are proposed according to the focus group results:

Generally speaking, websites that consist of images look better and are more likely to arouse users’ pleasant visual experience31, previous studies indicated that the size of images is closely related to website appeal32, and the large images play an active role in the visual appeal of users during the web page information transmission26,33. Based on this, the research hypothesis is proposed:

H1: Websites of digital museums with large images are more appealing than those without large images.

Prior research shows that on-screen reading is often characterized by browsing and scanning rather than sustained linear reading34. In addition, higher visual complexity tends to lead to more negative aesthetic first impressions of websites35. Accordingly, the study hypothesizes:

H2: Homepages of digital museums with short text gain more appeal than those with longer text.

Animation effects not only attract users’ attention with interesting visual effects but also provides a smooth user experience by subtly transforming a static web page into a lively and interactive interface36. However, too many animation effects not only increase the complexity of the page but also increase the cognitive load of the user37. Therefore, we can propose the following hypothesis:

H3: Homepages of digital museums with simple animation effects are more attractive than those with no animation effects.

According to the study by Parush et al.38, Visual layout has a strong impact on website aesthetics and visual effect and is a key factor in human-computer interface and website design. Excessively high information density diminishes users’ search efficiency. From the above, we hypothesize that:

H4: Websites of digital museums with concise template layouts are more appealing than those without.

Studies have suggested that the aesthetic response from a single stimulus is not positive enough39. In the study of the shopping website page, it is also mentioned that the combination of large pictures and short text has a high visual appeal to young people40. Therefore, this study speculates that the impact of large images, short text, simple animation effects, and concise template layouts on the web page can interact to further enhance the visual appeal of the page. On these grounds, the study hypothesizes:

H5: Websites of a digital museum with a higher number of matching hypothetical feature elements has a higher overall appeal.

Research 2: a quantitative study of the visual elements of digital museums

General background

In the second study, we grouped the 30 homepages according to a predefined coding rule based on the four hypothesized visual elements identified in Research 1, and participants then rated their visual appeal on a 5-point scale. We used paired t-tests to compare the high- and low-feature groups, and a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) to examine the effects of the individual visual elements while accounting for repeated ratings across both participants and webpages. We used paired t-tests to compare high- and low-feature groups and then conducted eye-tracking experiments to identify which elements attracted users’ attention.

Participants

Through campus recruitment, 70 participants from the China University of Geosciences were selected from different grades, and majors (32 males and 38 females). Aged between 18 and 28 (23.24±2.05); The web pages were displayed in random order though the whole process.

Materials and procedures

The experimental materials used in this study consisted of a selection of 30 museum homepages, including the Exploratorium in San Francisco, the Science Centre Singapore, the China Digital Science Museum, the China Science Popularization Museum, and others. These materials were drawn from the same homepage pool described in Research 1.

In the experiment, participants freely browsed each of the 30 pages and then rated its appeal on a 5-point unipolar scale ranging from 0 to 4 (0 = not appealing at all, 4 = very appealing), focusing only on the page’s visual layout and elements. A unipolar format was adopted because the purpose of the measure was to capture how much visual appeal was perceived, not to assess a continuum between two opposing qualities. Accordingly, 0 was used as the lowest anchor to indicate that no visual appeal was perceived, and the scale increased with the strength of perceived appeal.

Data analysis

Before conducting the paired-samples t-test, we examined the normality of the within-participant difference scores using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots. No substantial deviation from normality was observed. We first classified the 30 experimental materials based on the presence or absence of the hypothesized visual elements identified in Research 1. Each homepage received a feature-count score from 0 to 4 according to how many of the four predefined elements were present. This categorization was specified on the basis of the coding scheme and hypothesis structure. Materials scoring 0 or 1 were categorized as the low-feature group, 2 as the moderate-feature group, and 3 or 4 as the high-feature group. Research 3: eye-tracking experiment

General background

Our self-report study showed that pages with a richer set of hypothesized visual features were generally rated as more appealing. Among the individual elements, only large images were positively associated with appeal, whereas short text and simple animations were not significantly related to appeal. In contrast, concise layouts were associated with lower appeal ratings in the present sample.

Despite yielding intriguing results, our user-reported study is subject to the inherent subjectivity of participant reports. We conducted a controlled eye-tracking experiment with standardized virtual homepages (same content, original layouts). This allowed us to objectively observe visual attention patterns and confirm which elements drive appeal.

Participants

Through campus recruitment, 40 participants from the China University of Geosciences were selected from different types, grades, and specialties (15 males and 25 females). Aged between 20 and 28 (21.85±2.27).All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol (including the interviews/focus group, the questionnaire-based rating study, and the eye-tracking experiment) was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Institute of Technology (approval no. A2025003). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation.

Materials and instrument

For this section, we selected the top 3 and bottom 3 digital museum homepages based on visual appeal scores from the participants in the self-report experiment conducted in Section Discussion. These chosen homepages were used as the experimental materials in this section. Interestingly, the museum homepages with the highest mean self-reported scores by the participants were all from the high-feature group with a mean score of 3.22; the museum homepages with the lowest mean scores were all from the low-feature group with a mean score of 1. Taking the copyright into account, we replaced all the contents of six homepages and only kept their layouts. We created six virtual homepages named “geological explorer”, and all the contents belonged to geological science, adopted from http://www.china-shj.org.cn/archive/list-11. html. Doing so can help us to control the variable due to different participants.

We used Tobii X2-60 eye tracking equipment with a gaze accuracy of 0.4, gaze precision of 0.2, and a sampling rate of 60 Hz. The websites in this study were displayed on a 15.6-inch screen with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1366 \times 768$$\end{document} resolution. The distance between the participant’s eyes and the screen was set at 60 cm.

Procedures

First, the participant filled out their information such as name, gender, age, contact information, etc. Before the experiment started, the participant performed a five-point eye calibration. After calibrating to the standard level, the document was set to record the eye tracking test and save the file. To begin the experiment, a fixation point ”+” was displayed randomly on the screen for 1800–2000 ms. A digital museum homepage was then presented, and participants were asked to freely view the page for at least 10 s within the first visible screen area, without scrolling. This setting was adopted to ensure comparability across different homepages in terms of the amount of information visible on the first screen and the proportion of visual elements, and also because homepage viewing typically begins with an initial scan of the visible area, during which users form first impressions and orient themselves to the page. After that, an evaluation table related to the appeal of the homepage (the same table adopted in Self-report method) was displayed The participant was required to score the visual appeal of the homepage. Then a blank screen would display for 2000ms to allow the participant to rest briefly. Each participant needed to complete a total of six trials. To avoid order effects during the experiment, the presentation order of the six museum homepages was randomized. Because the homepage layouts were visually distinguishable, the experimenter was not blinded to condition during data collection. However, the eye-tracking outputs were processed using coded stimulus labels during analysis. During preprocessing, brief signal losses caused by blinks or momentary tracking loss were coded as missing data and were not treated as valid gaze samples. These missing segments were not interpolated, because the subsequent analyses focused on descriptive heat-map and fixation-order patterns rather than on continuous time-series reconstruction. Trials were excluded only when tracking loss was substantial enough that the remaining gaze data no longer allowed a reliable visual interpretation of the overall heat-map distribution or fixation sequence.

(3) Fixation order We first classified website areas of interest based on two criteria: hypothesized visual elements and heat maps. The first five seconds were selected as the observation window to capture the initial scanning stage of homepage viewing, during which users orient their attention toward salient page regions and form early evaluative impressions. Discussion

This study aimed to explore the impact of specific visual design elements on the visual appeal of digital museum homepages among young Chinese users. Utilizing a mixed-methods approach that combined qualitative methods (interviews and focus groups) to identify key visual elements, and quantitative methods (user reports and eye-tracking experiments) to assess their impact, the study provided comprehensive insights into user preferences and behaviors.

Regarding the first research question, findings from both user reports and eye-tracking experiments revealed a broadly consistent pattern: homepage designs incorporating a richer set of hypothesized visual features were generally evaluated more favorably in terms of visual appeal. This outcome aligns with previous studies highlighting the importance of visual complexity and diversity in enhancing user engagement and emotional responses43,44. Rather than being attributable to the mere accumulation of features, this pattern may reflect the way multiple interface cues jointly contribute to first impressions of a webpage. Visual elements such as large images and animations can enhance perceptual salience, support information presentation, reinforce visual hierarchy, and facilitate attentional orientation45,46.

Analyzing individual visual elements yielded several key findings. Firstly, large images emerged as the most robust visual feature in the present analyses and were consistently associated with more favorable evaluations of webpage appeal, consistent with prior research suggesting that prominent images create engaging experiences by capturing users’ attention and interest43,47. From the perspective of interface perception, large images may serve as immediate visual anchors, helping users rapidly identify the thematic focus of a homepage while also strengthening the legibility of the overall layout. However, AOI-based analysis showed that large-image regions did not produce the longest fixation duration. Instead, longer mean fixation durations were observed in text blocks, small images, generic images, and combination areas. This suggests that large images effectively attract early visual attention, whereas sustained visual processing occurs in information-dense interface regions.

Secondly, contrary to expectations, short text did not significantly affect the visual appeal of digital museum homepages.

One possible explanation is that text length may be less salient during the early stages of homepage viewing, when users are more likely to rely on global layout and prominent visual cues than on detailed reading. This could be attributed to factors such as visual complexity and prototypicality43. It’s possible that short text lacks sufficient visual prominence or that users prefer more detailed information on digital museum platforms. Additionally, in the eye-tracking experiment, participants had limited time for interaction, making it challenging to assess the impact of text length on subsequent exploration and engagement. Accordingly, the role of text length may become more apparent in later stages of interaction, such as information seeking, content comparison, or deeper browsing.

Thirdly, while user reports did not highlight the impact of simple animations on visual appeal, eye-tracking heat map analysis suggested that, in high-feature pages, simple animations were often located within the visually focal regions occupied by large main images. This pattern indicates that simple animations were less likely to function as independently salient elements and were more often embedded within regions that already possessed relatively high visual prominence44. In the early stages of page viewing, these regions tended to correspond to more concentrated distributions of attention, suggesting that the perceptual presence of simple animation was closely tied to the salience structure of the surrounding visual field. Simple animations alone may not command sufficient visual prominence, whereas their appearance within already salient image-based regions points to a page-level attentional pattern. This suggests that the contribution of animation may be better understood in relation to the broader attentional composition of the interface, rather than as an isolated design cue.

Regarding the second research question, for the participants in this study, a visually simplified homepage layout was not necessarily perceived as more appealing. Previous cross-cultural web design research has noted that Western websites tend to employ simpler layouts and lower display densities, whereas Chinese websites tend to include more elements and adopt denser page layouts48. This difference may be related to the visual and spatial characteristics of Chinese characters, such as the absence of spaces between characters and their relatively compact arrangement49. Against this backdrop, the findings of this study suggest that when designing digital museum homepages for Chinese users, moderate page density and layout complexity may be more effective in enhancing visual appeal. Moreover, the prevalence of mobile internet usage in China has cultivated user expectations for content-dense designs that are easily navigable on smartphones47. Users may perceive minimalist designs as lacking substance or requiring additional navigation to access desired information, which could detract from the perceived utility and appeal of the homepage. For digital museum platforms in particular, this finding indicates that homepage design should not simply replicate minimalist conventions derived from Western interface aesthetics, but should instead balance visual clarity with sufficient informational richness to meet the expectations of local user groups.

Conclusions and implications

This study concludes that specific visual design elements significantly influence the visual appeal of digital museum homepages among young Chinese users. Large images were consistently associated with more favorable visual evaluations and earlier attentional focus. Eye-tracking results further showed that large-image regions containing simple animations tended to attract concentrated early fixations, highlighting the value of combining multiple visual cues within the overall page composition.

Contrary to expectations, short text and clean layouts did not significantly enhance visual appeal, which may be influenced by cultural preferences for information-rich content and layouts. These findings underscore the necessity for digital museum designers to consider cultural factors and user preferences when developing interfaces to ensure they effectively engage the target audience.

Based on these findings, several recommendations can be made for digital museum design: (1) Incorporate Prominent Visual Elements: Utilize large images and consider integrating simple animations to capture and retain users’ attention. (2) Embrace Information-Rich Layouts: Design layouts that accommodate a wealth of information without overwhelming the user, aligning with cultural preferences for content richness. (3) Cultural Sensitivity: Understand and integrate cultural nuances into design practices to enhance user satisfaction and engagement. By addressing these considerations, digital museums can create more engaging and effective platforms for science education, better meeting the cultural needs and preferences of young audiences in the digital age.

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study, there are limitations that should be acknowledged. The exploration of young users’ interactions with webpages was relatively basic, primarily focusing on initial visual appeal rather than in-depth engagement metrics such as click-through rates or time spent on subsequent pages. Future research should design experiments to investigate users’ subsequent clicking and browsing behaviors to gain a more comprehensive understanding of engagement. In addition, this study used actual museum websites as stimulus materials to replicate real-world browsing conditions as closely as possible. Since it is difficult to ensure complete consistency in page characteristics across such materials, relevant design cues from different stimuli may coexist; consequently, there are certain limitations to interpreting the effects of individual visual elements. Future research could systematically manipulate specific visual variables under more controlled conditions to further examine their independent effects. Moreover, the eye-tracking analysis relied mainly on heat maps and fixation-order patterns, which were useful for showing early attention but remained descriptive. Future studies could incorporate AOI-based measures such as fixation duration, fixation count, and dwell time, together with inferential statistical testing.
