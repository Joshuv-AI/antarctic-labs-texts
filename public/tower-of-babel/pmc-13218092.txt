Introduction

Background and significance

Stroke is the leading cause of death and disability among adults worldwide, imposing a growing burden on public health systems.1 2 According to the China Stroke Prevention and Treatment Report 2026, the incidence rate of stroke in China is approximately 246.8 per 100 000 population, with over 2 million new cases annually, and its incidence is showing a younger trend.3 Among surviving patients who had a stroke, 60%–70% experience varying degrees of motor dysfunction, with hand dysfunction being the most common and difficult to rehabilitate—only 10%–20% of patients can regain near-normal hand function.4 5

Hand function is the most refined and core motor function of the human body, participating in multiple activities of daily living such as eating, dressing, grooming and writing. Its recovery directly determines patients’ ability to live independently and participate in society, thereby significantly affecting quality of life and increasing family care burden and social medical costs.6 For instance, patients with hand dysfunction require an additional 3.5 hours of daily family care, and their medical expenses are 40%–60% higher than those without hand dysfunction.7

Currently, clinical rehabilitation interventions for patients who had a stroke are mainly focused on the hospitalisation period, with the core goal of stabilising acute conditions and restoring basic functions.8 However, numerous studies have demonstrated that the 6 months postdischarge is the ‘golden window’ for motor function recovery, and the rehabilitation trajectory around 1 year is a key node determining long-term prognosis—functional stability during this period is directly associated with patients’ quality of life and care needs over the next 5 years.9 10

Nevertheless, China’s existing stroke rehabilitation system has notable limitations: on the one hand, postdischarge rehabilitation follow-up coverage is fewer than 40%, and community rehabilitation resources are disparately distributed (only 30% of community health service centres have standardised rehabilitation assessment capabilities), leaving most patients facing the dilemma of ‘interrupted rehabilitation after discharge’11; on the other hand, existing studies mostly focus on in-hospital or short-term follow-up (3–6 months), lacking systematic exploration of the dynamic trajectory, key turning points (eg, when recovery slows down), and influencing factors of long-term hand function recovery (6 months to 1 year), making it difficult to reveal the core laws of long-term rehabilitation.12 13

Multidisciplinary team (MDT) has been proven to be an effective model to improve rehabilitation outcomes.14 A study by Yagura et al (the STROCARE trial) confirmed that structured MDT intervention can improve the activities of daily living of patients who had a stroke by 15% at 1 year.15 However, current applications are mostly restricted to inpatient settings, and the localised continuous rehabilitation model integrating ‘hospital-community-family’ is not yet mature.16 Previous studies have mostly adopted single rehabilitation training or simple follow-up interventions, lacking integration of multiple roles such as community medical staff and family caregivers. Moreover, they have not fully considered the distribution characteristics of primary medical resources in China (eg, limited professional capabilities of community rehabilitation personnel and insufficient participation of family caregivers), leading to poor feasibility and low adherence of intervention programmes (average adherence rate is only 55%).17 18

The core biological mechanism of hand function recovery is motor cortical neuroplasticity—after stroke, synaptic remodelling and cortical reorganisation between the damaged hemisphere and the contralateral hemisphere directly determine the speed and degree of motor function recovery.19 Existing studies have confirmed that rehabilitation interventions can enhance neuroplasticity through ‘task-oriented training, multisensory stimulation’ and other methods. However, China’s existing rehabilitation models mostly lack precise understanding of the ‘neuroplasticity window’ (3–6 months is the active phase of neuroplasticity, and it gradually enters a plateau after 6 months), and have not formed an integrated ‘hospital-community-family’ plasticity regulation programme, resulting in most patients missing the optimal intervention window.16 Based on these findings, this study focuses on the long-term rehabilitation process of patients who had a stroke within 1 year postdischarge, integrates multidisciplinary collaborative resources to construct a continuous rehabilitation management model adapted to primary medical care, and systematically explores the trajectory and key influencing factors of hand function recovery (with a focus on the regulatory role of multidisciplinary intervention on neuroplasticity), providing a basis for clinical programme optimisation and aligning with the core research direction of neurorehabilitation.

Study objectives

Primary objective

To describe the dynamic trajectory of hand function and overall rehabilitation outcomes in patients who had a stroke at 6 months and 1 year postdischarge.

Secondary objectives

(1) To analyse the key influencing factors of hand function recovery, with a focus on the regulatory effect of multidisciplinary collaborative intervention on motor cortical neuroplasticity; (2) To verify the impact of the multidisciplinary collaborative management model on complications and rehabilitation satisfaction and (3) To develop a continuous rehabilitation management model adapted to the current status of primary medical care in China.

Study hypotheses

Hand function of patients will exhibit progressive recovery within 1 year postdischarge, with the fastest recovery between 3 and 6 months (consistent with the active phase of neuroplasticity) and stabilisation from 6 to 12 months.

Younger patients, those with better baseline function, higher rehabilitation adherence and active participation in multidisciplinary interventions will exhibit more significant motor cortical neuroplasticity and superior 1 year hand function recovery.

Multidisciplinary collaborative management can reduce the incidence of complications, improve activities of daily living and rehabilitation satisfaction, and hand function recovery is positively associated with neuroplasticity levels.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This is a single-centre, prospective cohort study, scheduled to start in February 2026 and conclude in July 2029. The study will be conducted at the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine, The Second People’s Hospital of Hefei Guangde Road Campus, and the collaborating Hefei Datong Road Community Health Service Center. The study protocol complies with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials Checklist. Clinical trial registration is planned to be completed within 1 month after submission, and registration information will be promptly added to the study protocol and related publications. This is a prospective observational cohort study. The MDT intervention is provided as part of routine clinical care and does not alter the natural recovery trajectory that this study aims to observe. The design reflects real-world clinical practice rather than testing a causal intervention effect.

Study population and sample size

Inclusion criteria

(1) Fulfils the diagnostic criteria for stroke, confirmed by cranial CT or MRI20 21; Aged 18–80 years; (2) Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Hand (FMA-Hand) score <66 points on the affected side; (3) Mini–Mental State Examination score ≥20 points, capable of cooperating with assessments and questionnaires; (4) Voluntarily signs the informed consent form (including disclosure of neuroplasticity-related research content) and (5) Resides in Hefei City, projected survival time ≥1 year, and able to cooperate with 1-year follow-up and motor evoked potential (MEP) testing.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Severe cognitive impairment, aphasia or mental illness, unable to communicate effectively; (2) Previous neurological diseases or severe osteoarthropathy that affect hand function assessment; (3) severe dysfunction of vital organs, malignant tumours or severe infections; (4) Pregnant or lactating women; (5) Plans to move out of Hefei City postdischarge, unable to complete follow-up; (6) Participation in other similar rehabilitation studies within the past month and (7) Contraindications to MEP testing (eg, internal metal implants, history of epilepsy).

Sample size calculation

Based on the multiple linear regression model, 10 main influencing factors (including multidisciplinary collaboration participation, age, baseline FMA-Hand score and neuroplasticity-related indirect indicators) will be included. Referring to previous similar studies (effect size f²=0.15 for FMA-Hand score), with α=0.05 and β=0.2 (statistical power of 80%), G*Power V.3.1 software calculates a minimum sample size of 95 cases. Considering a 20% projected dropout rate, the final sample size is determined to be 120 cases to ensure sufficient statistical power.

Sampling method

Consecutive sampling will be used. Study team members will screen patients 1–3 days before discharge. Eligible patients who voluntarily participate will be enrolled after signing the informed consent form. Currently, no patient recruitment has commenced; enrolment is planned to start in February 2026.

Multidisciplinary collaborative management intervention

An MDT will be established, consisting of rehabilitation physicians, rehabilitation therapists, community doctors/nurses and family caregivers, to implement a three-stage intervention (incorporating neuroplasticity regulation strategies):

Discharge connection stage (1 week predischarge): The MDT will conduct joint assessments of patients’ condition, rehabilitation potential, and family care capacity, and develop individualised rehabilitation training plans (including hand function-specific training and activities of daily living training, with a focus on neuroplasticity initiation strategies such as task-oriented training); conduct rehabilitation education, training demonstrations and family caregiver skill training, establish electronic health records and clarify community follow-up coordinator.

Community follow-up stage (1–12 months postdischarge): (1) monthly centralised community intervention: Hospital rehabilitation therapists and community doctors will conduct group-based rehabilitation training (45 min per session, including 30 min of hand function training and 15 min of overall motor function training, strengthening neuroplasticity enhancement strategies such as multisensory stimulation), individualised plan adjustments and complication screening and management (pressure ulcers, joint contractures); (2) quarterly MDT consultation: Offline assessment of rehabilitation progress, addressing training challenges and adjustment of rehabilitation plans (20 min per patient for consultation) and (3) ongoing online support: Establish a WeChat group, where rehabilitation therapists and community nurses provide daily rehabilitation consultation, training reminders and emergency handling guidance (response within 12 working hours).

The MDT comprises rehabilitation physicians, physiotherapists and occupational therapists with standardised training in stroke rehabilitation, community doctors, community nurses and family caregivers. All personnel received unified training before study initiation. The intervention is fully described according to the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) guideline (table 1).

Data collection

Follow-up time points

Baseline survey (T0): 1–3 days predischarge;

First follow-up (T1): 3 months postdischarge;

Second follow-up (T2): 6 months postdischarge;

Third follow-up (T3): 12 months postdischarge.

Data collection methods

Baseline data: Demographic data (age, gender, educational level, marital status, family monthly income), clinical baseline data (stroke type, onset time, lesion location, comorbidities, medical history, length of hospital stay) and baseline functional assessment data will be collected through medical record review and in-person interviews.

Follow-up data: Functional assessments will adopt a combined method of ‘outpatient follow-up+on site community assessment+video-based follow-up’ (face-to-face assessment for patients able to visit the hospital, on-site community assessment for patients in the community and video assessment for patients unable to attend); influencing factor data will be collected through structured questionnaires (paper or electronic versions); MEP testing will be performed by certified technicians to record the latency and amplitude of MEP in the affected hand; lost-to-follow-up patients will be tracked through multiple channels (family members, community doctors, neighbourhood committees), and the reasons for lost to follow-up will be recorded. Video-based assessments using FMA-Hand and Modified Barthel Index (MBI) have been validated and widely used in stroke rehabilitation research. They were adopted to improve follow-up feasibility and reduce loss to follow-up.

Quality control

Researchers and MDT members have undergone standardised training, including survey processes, scale usage, collaboration standards and MEP testing operation standards.

Questionnaires will be reviewed immediately after collection, with missing or incorrect items verified and corrected on-site. Dual data entry and cross-validation will be conducted using EpiData V.3.1 software.

Multimodal follow-up reminders (phone calls, WeChat and assistance from community doctors) will be implemented. Quarterly MDT meetings will be convened to review intervention effects and optimise collaboration processes.

Standardised video follow-up: Patients will be required to shoot training actions from ‘front and lateral angles’; assessors will verify the standardisation of actions and record shooting time and environment to ensure assessment consistency.

MEP testing quality control: The same equipment will be used and technicians will calibrate the instrument periodically.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome measure

Hand function: Assessed with the FMA-Hand, with a total score of 0–66 points (higher scores indicate better function). Clinically meaningful improvement is defined as an increase of ≥10 points from baseline. Assessment time points: T0, T1, T2, T3.

Secondary outcome measures

Overall motor function: FMA Total Score (FMA-Total), with a total score of 0–100 points (higher scores indicate better function); Assessment time points: T0, T1, T2, T3.

Activities of daily living: MBI, with a total score of 0–100 points (score >60 points indicates basic independence); Assessment time points: T0, T1, T2, T3.

Incidence of complications: Record the occurrence of complications such as pressure ulcers (based on NPUAP staging criteria), joint contractures (range of motion reduced by ≥30% relative to normal values), and shoulder pain (Visual Analogue Scale score ≥4); Assessment time points: T1, T2, T3.

Rehabilitation satisfaction: Assessed using the Rehabilitation Service Satisfaction Scale, with a total score of 0–40 points (score ≥32 points indicates high satisfaction); Assessment time points: T2, T3.

Influencing factor indicators

Family Rehabilitation Adherence: Rehabilitation Adherence Scale, with a total score of 0–30 points (score ≥20 points indicates high adherence, including training frequency, duration and movement standardisation); Assessment time points: T1, T2, T3.

Level of social support: Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support, with a total score of 12–84 points (higher scores indicate better social support); Assessment time points: T0, T2.

Multidisciplinary collaboration participation: Self-designed scale, with a total score of 0–30 points (score ≥24 points indicates high participation, including community intervention participation rate, online consultation frequency, and family caregiver cooperation); Assessment time points: T1, T2, T3.

The MDT participation scale was developed based on literature review and expert consultation, including three domains: community intervention attendance rate, online consultation frequency, and family caregiver cooperation. The total score ranges from 0 to 30; a score ≥24 indicates high participation.

Indirect indicator of neuroplasticity: MEP testing, recording the latency and amplitude of motor cortical evoked potentials in the affected hand to indirectly reflect neuroplasticity levels; Assessment time points: T0, T3.

Safety indicator

Incidence of adverse events: Record unexpected events including training-related injuries (eg, joint sprains, muscle strains), disease progression (eg, stroke recurrence) and MEP testing-related discomfort; Assessment time point: Throughout the study.

Statistical methods

SPSS V.26.0 statistical software will be used for statistical analyses. A p<0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Descriptive statistics: Quantitative data will first undergo Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Normally distributed data will be expressed as (x̄±s), while non-normal data will be expressed as (M (IQR)); Qualitative data will be expressed as (n(%)).

Rehabilitation trajectory analysis: Repeated measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) will be used to compare the changing trends of functional indicators at different time points. Bonferroni correction will be applied for pairwise comparisons within groups; if data do not meet the requirements of RM-ANOVA, Friedman test will be employed for intragroup comparisons.

Influencing factor analysis: Taking the change value of FMA-Hand score at 1 year postdischarge as the dependent variable, variables with p<0.1 in univariate analysis (such as age, gender, baseline FMA-Hand score, multidisciplinary collaboration participation and MEP amplitude change) will be included in the multiple linear regression model, and stepwise regression will be employed to identify independent influencing factors.

Correlation analysis: Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis will be used to examine the association between hand function recovery and multidisciplinary collaboration participation, as well as neuroplasticity indirect indicators (MEP amplitude).

Loss-to-follow-up handling: Intention-to-treat analysis will be adopted. Missing data will be addressed using multiple imputation (missing rate <20%).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this study.

Ethics and dissemination

This study has been approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of The Second People’s Hospital of Hefei (Approval Number: 2024-KY-089).

The study will be conducted in full compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2024 version) and relevant ethical norms. Before the start of the study, patients and their families will be fully informed of the study purpose, process, follow-up time, intervention content, MEP testing-related matters, potential risks and benefits, and written informed consent will be obtained. Participants may withdraw from the study voluntarily at any time without stating reasons. Adverse events will be handled promptly and reported to the ethics committee. Any amendments to the study protocol must be resubmitted to the ethics committee for approval. All data will only be used for academic research, and patients’ privacy will be strictly protected.

For patients who develop impaired decision-making capacity during follow-up, informed consent will be obtained from their legally authorised representative in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

This study focuses on the 1-year postdischarge hand function recovery trajectory and ‘hospital-community-family’ multidisciplinary collaborative management model in patients who had a stroke, serving as an important supplement and practical extension of existing stroke rehabilitation research. Its core value and design considerations are as follows, aligning with the logic of international similar studies in the field of neurorehabilitation:

Scientific significance and clinical value

The key challenges of postdischarge stroke rehabilitation lie in ‘insufficient continuity’ and ‘fragmented management,’ which are particularly prominent in China’s primary care settings.22 Existing studies are mostly limited to in-hospital interventions or short-term follow-up, making it difficult to reflect the dynamic features and key influencing factors of long-term rehabilitation. For instance, some patients may experience stagnation or even deterioration of functional recovery after 6 months, but relevant data lack systematic documentation.23 By extending the follow-up period to 1 year, this study can not only capture the functional change trends after the ‘golden recovery period’ (3–6 months) but also clarify the long-term stability and key turning points of hand function recovery, filling the gap in long-term rehabilitation trajectory data.

Meanwhile, the multidisciplinary collaborative management model constructed in this study forms a comprehensive cycle regulation strategy through ‘neuroplasticity initiation training during discharge connection, neuroplasticity enhancement training during community intervention, and neuroplasticity maintenance training during online support,’ which is more adapted to the current situation of limited primary medical resources in China and has strong clinical translatability. Focusing on the core roles of rehabilitation physicians, rehabilitation therapists, community medical staff and family caregivers, the model avoids implementation difficulties caused by reliance on special resources and directly addresses the challenge of ‘interrupted rehabilitation after discharge’ for patients, in alignment with the policy direction of hierarchical diagnosis and treatment.

This study closely associates hand function recovery with motor cortical neuroplasticity and indirectly quantifies neuroplasticity levels through MEP testing, expecting to reveal the internal mechanism of ‘multidisciplinary intervention → neuroplasticity regulation → hand function recovery,’ providing a biological foundation for precision rehabilitation and aligning with the core orientation of neurorehabilitation research.

Connection and innovation with existing studies

Previous similar studies have confirmed that structured MDT collaboration can significantly improve the functional outcomes of patients who had a stroke,24 25 but these studies mostly focus on in-hospital or short-term collaboration, lacking integration of community scenarios and validation of long-term effects, and have not clarified their regulatory mechanism of neuroplasticity. Based on building on the core concept of ‘multidisciplinary collaboration,’ this study further optimises local adaptability: first, strengthening the active participation of community healthcare providers to address the availability of primary rehabilitation resources through ‘monthly community centralised intervention+quarterly MDT consultation’; second, introducing family caregivers as collaborative subjects to enhance patients’ rehabilitation adherence by conducting pre-discharge special training (previous studies have shown that patients with high family caregiver participation can improve rehabilitation adherence by 30%16); third, verifying the sustainability of the model through 1-year long-term follow-up, making up for the practical limitation of existing studies that ‘emphasise short-term and neglect long-term’.26

In addition, existing studies mostly take ‘whether to participate in intervention’ as an influencing factor, while this study innovatively takes ‘multidisciplinary collaboration participation’ (including community intervention participation rate, online consultation frequency and family caregiver cooperation) as a core influencing factor, and introduces MEP testing as an indirect indicator of neuroplasticity. This can more accurately reveal the causal chain of’ ‘intervention quality → neuroplasticity → rehabilitation outcome’, providing data to support subsequent optimisation of collaboration processes and improvement of intervention efficiency, and aligning with the research direction of ‘quantifying the association between intervention quality and plasticity’ in the field of neurorehabilitation.

Rationale for study design

This study adopts a single-centre, prospective cohort design without a control group, mainly based on two core considerations: first, there are substantial individual differences in hand function recovery of patients who had a stroke, and the natural rehabilitation trajectory is not yet clear. Cohort studies can more objectively characterise the rehabilitation patterns in the real world and provide foundational data for subsequent randomised controlled trials (RCTs); second, multidisciplinary collaborative interventions need to be dynamically adjusted according to patients’ specific conditions, and the ‘standardised routine care’ of the control group is challenging to strictly define, which may lead to between-group contamination. Subsequent RCT studies can be carried out based on this study to further validate the net effect of interventions.

The sample size calculation has been verified by statistical software, and the inclusion of neuroplasticity-related indicators can still meet the statistical power requirements, consistent with the sample size design standards for cohort studies.27 The combined follow-up method of ‘outpatient+community + video,’ supplemented by standardised video follow-up requirements and MEP testing quality control, ensures assessment accuracy while accounting for patient convenience, which can effectively lower the loss-to-follow-up rate.

Study limitations

This study has three inherent constraints that need to be carefully considered when interpreting the results:

Single-centre study design: Samples are concentrated in The Second People’s Hospital of Hefei and the collaborating Datong Road Community Health Service Center, which may lead to selection bias (eg, the hospital’s rehabilitation resources are better than those of surrounding hospitals, and patients have higher baseline function). Caution is advised when extrapolating the results to other regions (especially areas with weak primary medical resources); subsequent multicentre studies can be carried out in collaboration with 3–5 hospitals of different levels (tertiary hospitals+secondary hospitals+community health service centres) in Anhui Province to enhance sample generalisability.

Caution is advised when extrapolating the results to other regions (especially areas with weak primary medical resources); subsequent multicentre studies can be carried out in collaboration with 3–5 hospitals of different levels (tertiary hospitals+secondary hospitals+community health service centres) in Anhui Province to enhance sample generalisability.

No control group design: It is impossible to fully exclude the influence of natural recovery on outcomes, and only MEP is used as an indirect indicator of neuroplasticity, lacking direct neuroimaging evidence (eg, functional MRI (fMRI) can directly observe cortical reorganisation); subsequent RCT studies can be carried out, setting a ‘routine follow-up group’ and adding fMRI testing to clarify the added benefits of multidisciplinary interventions relative to natural recovery and their direct regulatory effect on neuroplasticity.

Limited sample size and testing methods: The relatively limited sample size (120 cases) may affect the statistical power for subgroup analyses (such as differences in neuroplasticity between ischaemic vs haemorrhagic stroke, young vs elderly patients); stratified analysis (grouped by stroke type and age) can be used to enhance result reliability, or the sample size can be expanded in subsequent studies (eg, multi-centre studies including more than 300 patients).

Feasibility guarantee of the study

The feasibility of this study mainly relies on three key supports: first, the research team has extensive experience in stroke rehabilitation assessment and intervention—the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine, The Second People’s Hospital of Hefei Guangde Road Campus is a key discipline in Anhui Province, admitting more than 800 stroke rehabilitation patients annually. All team members have received standardised training on scales such as FMA and MBI, and have established a established collaborative network with Datong Road Community Health Service Center (cooperating in community rehabilitation projects for 3 years), which can ensure the standardised delivery of multidisciplinary collaborative interventions; second, the follow-up process is concise and straightforward, and the follow-up adherence can be significantly enhanced through ‘multi-channel reminders+participant incentive mechanisms’ (eg, providing one free physical examination for patients who complete 1 year follow-up); third, the data management system is well-established, adopting ‘double data entry+logical verification’ mode, standardised video follow-up requirements, and MEP testing quality control, which can ensure data quality reliability.

In summary, through scientific design and rigorous execution, this study is expected to elucidate the long-term hand function recovery patterns and neuroplasticity regulation mechanism of patients who had a stroke postdischarge, construct a continuous rehabilitation management model adapted to the current status of primary medical care in China, and provide important scientific evidence and practical references for optimising the stroke rehabilitation service system and alleviating disease burden.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

Long-term follow-up design: Extending the follow-up period to 1 year, addressing the data gap in long-term rehabilitation trajectories of patients who had a stroke 6–12 months postdischarge, and clarifying the long-term rules and key turning points of hand function recovery (eg, the time node when recovery slows down).

Locally adapted intervention model: Constructing an integrated ‘hospital-community-family’ multidisciplinary collaborative model, incorporating neuroplasticity regulation strategies, adapting to the current situation of limited primary medical resources in China, with high feasibility and clinical translatability.

Targeted influencing factor analysis: Taking ‘multidisciplinary collaboration participation’ as a continuous variable, combined with MEP testing to quantify neuroplasticity, forming a comprehensive evidence chain of ‘intervention quality—biological mechanism—rehabilitation outcome’.

Multidomain outcome measures: Covering multidimensional outcomes such as hand function, overall motor function, activities of daily living, complications, and rehabilitation satisfaction, combined with standardised quality control measures, ensuring high reliability of study results.

Limitations

Single-centre study: Concentrated sample sources may introduce selection bias, and the generalisability of results needs to be further verified by multi-centre studies.

No control group design: Unable to fully exclude the influence of natural recovery, and subsequent RCT studies are needed to clarify the net effect of multidisciplinary interventions.

Lack of blinding design: Functional assessments may be affected by the subjective factors of assessors; although bias is reduced through unified training, it cannot be completely avoided.

Limitations in sample size and testing methods: The relatively limited sample size may affect the statistical power of subgroup analysis; only MEP is used as an indirect indicator of neuroplasticity, lacking direct neuroimaging evidence such as fMRI. Self-reported adherence and satisfaction scales may introduce information bias, which could affect the accuracy of related analyses.
