Risk factors of mortality in outborn neonates, an experience from central India: A prospective observational study
Authors: Rajkumar Motiram Meshram, Sonia Thangavelu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Despite various measures, the neonatal mortality rate in outborn neonates is significantly high and makes impossible to achieve sustainable developmental goals.Objectives:To recognize the risk factors of mortality in outborn neonates.Methods:A prospective observation cohort was studied at Government Medical College, Nagpur, for a duration of two and a half years on all outborn neonates. Neonates who died within an hour, whose parents left the hospital against medical advice, and not willing to participate were excluded. Demographic, maternal, and neonatal clinical parameters and transport data were collected. Clinical diagnosis was done by using standard definitions. All the neonates were followed up till discharge or death. Mortality rate and risk factors of mortality were identified. Chi-square test, Mann–Whitney test, and unpaired “t” test were used for comparison between survival and nonsurvival neonates. Univariate and multiple logistic regression models were used to identify independent risk factors of mortality by adjusted odds ratio and 95% confidence interval. P < 0.05 was considered as significant.Results:Inadequate antenatal care (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.93, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.00–3.78, P = 0.04), increased travelling distance (aOR 1.00, 95% CI 1.00–1.00, P < 0.0001), preterm birth (aOR 0.89, 95% CI 0.85–0.93, P < 0.0001), abnormal respiratory rate (aOR 0.97,95% CI 0.96–0.98, P = 0.001), abnormal heart rate (aOR 0.96, 95% CI 0.95–0.98, P < 0.0001), hypothermia (aOR 0.72, 95% CI 0.54–0.96, P = 0.02), low oxygen saturation (aOR 0.90, 95% CI 0.87–0.93, P < 0.0001), and poor perfusion (aOR 1.93, 95% CI 1.68–2.21, P < 0.0001) were the independent risk factors of mortality.Conclusion:Inadequate antenatal care, increased travelling distance, preterm birth, abnormal respiratory rate, abnormal heart rate, hypothermia, low oxygen saturation, and poor perfusion are independent predictors of mortality. Improvement of utilization and delivery of maternal/neonatal care, transport system, and early recognition of risk factors of mortality are keys to attain of SDG.

Background

Neonatal life carries the highest risk of death with an average global neonatal mortality rate (NMR) of 17 per 1000 live births in 2023.[1] Worldwide, 2.3 million children died in the neonatal period in 2023 and approximately 6300 neonatal deaths occur every day with huge disparity across the region and countries, the highest in African and Mediterranean regions.[12] As per the National Family Health Survey-5 (2019–2021), NMR in India was 24.9 per 1000 live births and the current early neonatal mortality is 20.3 per 1000 live births with a significant interstate difference due to significant reduction in late neonatal and postneonatal death rates, while NMR in our state is 16.5 per 1000 live births (NFHS-5 Maharashtra fact sheet) with notable interdistrict and rural urban inequality.[34567]

The healthcare system in India is a diversified and complex network of public and private sectors serving 1.4 billion population with various challenges including limited infrastructure and shortage of healthcare professionals and are mainly concentrated in urban areas.[8] Nearly, 64% Indian population are residing in rural areas, and they utilize health services from a three tier health care system and beneficiaries of National Rural Health Mission and the Ayushman Bharat Scheme.[91011] In our country, despite availability of health care systems, 30% pregnant women do not undergo antenatal checkup in the first trimester and 42% are inadequately supervised (<4 antenatal care visits). 11.4% births are noninstitutional, and 3.2% home births are conducted by Skilled Health Personnel. 78% neonates receive postnatal care within 2 days of delivery, but only 4.2% home-born neonates reach health checkup facility within 24 h of birth.[34]

Even though in-utero transport of neonates is ideal and the safest, unfortunately, most of preterm birth, unavoidable perinatal complications, and congenital malformations cannot always be anticipated and warrant neonatal transport. In spite of strengthening of neonatal care services at primary, secondary, and district health care levels and introduction of Navjaat Shishu Suraksha Karyakram and Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram, a huge number of neonates are transported to tertiary care centers.[12] In India, the neonatal transport system is in the infancy stage and at many places, it is nonexistent and is very poorly organized and utilized, except in the state of Tamil Nadu, where almost 75% neonates are transported by neonatal ambulance.[13141516] Neonates transported with poor care develop life-threatening serious complications like hypothermia, hypoglycemia, hypoxia, seizures, and cyanosis; hence, they have higher mortality than those born at level III hospital as they miss the ‘Golden Hour’ in neonatal care.[171819]

NMR among outborn transported neonates is significantly higher compared to inborn neonates and national figures, and it ranges from 25% to 30%,[20212223] attributed by preventable diseases like infections, preterm birth complications, hypothermia, hypoxia, and hypoglycemia due to transport of unstable neonates with poor or no care during transport.[23242526] There is a timely need to reduce the mortality in such neonates to achieve the Sustainable Developmental Goal and the goal of ‘Newborn Action Plan’ to reduce the neonatal mortality to single digit by 2030 by predicting and recognizing transported neonates who are likely to succumb. Therefore, this study was planned to recognize the risk factors of mortality and transport characteristics of outborn neonates in a large cohort.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

A prospective observational study was conducted among consequently admitted outborn neonates (those delivered outside our hospital premises – at home, government/private hospital) and admitted through either the outpatient or Emergency Department between February 2017 and July 2019 at a tertiary care institute of central India. Our institute is one of the largest tertiary care referral centers of central India catering a population of whole Vidarbha region and part of nearby states. We had three general pediatric wards, one level III Neonatal Intensive Care Unit and one Pediatric Intensive Care Unit. As per the policy of the hospital, outborn neonates were not admitted in our inborn NICU; hence, they were treated in separate neonatal cabinets in the general pediatric wards equipped with central oxygen supply, phototherapy units, and bubble CPAP, along with adequate nursing staff, residents, and teaching faculty. At present, we have well-equipped special level III NICU for outborn neonates.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All live outborn referral neonates in the study duration were included in the study. Neonates who died within an hour in the emergency department and whose parents left the hospital against medical advice and not willing to participate in the study were excluded. A total of 2783 neonates were admitted in the study period, but we included 2238 eligible neonates by applying inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data collection

Demographic, maternal, and neonatal data were recorded on a specially designed data entry instrument for this study. The questionnaire was prepared from existing available related literature and WHO standard verbal autopsy questionnaire.[27] Data were collected by on-duty residents who were trained by preparatory training sessions. Monitoring of data collection was done by postgraduate qualified senior residents. The collectors had been collecting information by interviewing either/or parents, available close relatives, or legal persons within 2 h of hospitalization. Besides the clinical information, maternal information like age, parity, antenatal care, place of birth, mode of delivery, and travelling details were recorded. Neonatal clinical variables like respiratory rate, heart rate, oxygen saturation, perfusion, and blood sugar were noted on arrival of neonates. The respiration was assessed by counting the respiratory rate for 1 minute. A respiratory rate within 40–60/min was considered normal. Heart rate was recorded clinically by auscultation for 1 minute. Abnormal heart rate was rechecked and confirmed by using a multipara monitor (Nihon Kohden DSM 3753). Temperature was measured using digital thermometer EC-5004 placed in axilla for 2 minutes. At least two readings were taken, and the lowest temperature reading was recorded. Oxygen saturation using a pulse oximeter [choice Med model: MD300C53)] on the right upper limb on two occasions was recorded, and the mean of two values was taken into consideration. Perfusion was assessed by measuring capillary refilling time (CRT). CRT was measured in every patient by simultaneous activation of stop watch and application of minimal pressure until blanching of skin. When skin color returned to baseline, the stop watch was inactivated. For any reason, if measurement had to be repeated, the next attempt was made after an interval of 30 s. A capillary refill time >3 s was considered as poor perfusion. Blood sugar was measured by using a commercially available instrument (glucometer) (Accusure simple Model No: TD: 4183) by heel prick method on reagent strip. Blood glucose <45 mg% was considered as hypoglycemia. Gestational age of neonates was assessed by last date of menstrual period, antenatal ultrasound documents, and/or New Ballard Scoring method if the neonatal age is <7 days.[28] Weight was measured on digital electronic weighing scale machine having a minimal error of 10 g at the time of admission. Socioeconomic status of parents was classified as per Modified Kuppuswamy Scale.[29] Neonates were managed as per the treatment protocol of our hospital and standard treatment guidelines. Outcome variable

All the neonates were followed up to discharge or death, and outcome was measured as survival and nonsurvival, and mortality rate, proportionate mortality rate, length of stay, and risk factors of mortality were analyzed.

Ethical consideration

We followed the principles of declarations of Helsinki while conducting the study. The study was approved by Institutional Ethics Committee (No. 960/EC/Pharmac/GMC/NGP dated 10/01/2017), and written, informed consent was obtained from either or both parents or legal guardian after providing necessary information regarding the study in vernacular language before enrolling the participants. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained.

Statistical analysis

Data were coded, cleaned, and entered into Microsoft excel sheets and analyzed using STATA version 14, developed by StataCorp 4905 Lakeway Drive, College Station, Texas 77845-4512 (USA). Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation. Duration of hospital stay was calculated by subtracting the date of death or discharge from date of hospitalization. Unpaired “t”- test, Mann–Whitney test, Chi-square test, and Fischer exact test were used for comparison between survival and nonsurvival. Multiple logistic regression was used to identify significant risk factors of mortality. Adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. P <0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Neonatal characteristics

In this study, 2238 outborn neonates were enrolled; more than 50% were admitted within 24 h of birth with a median age of hospitalization of 24 h and a male-to-female ratio of 1.3:1. One thousand, one hundred and seventy-two (52.4%) were born at term, and the mean gestational age was 35.9 ± 3 weeks with a range of 26–43 weeks. Sixty percent neonates had a weight <2500 g, while around 3% had a weight more than 3000 g. More than three-fourth (77.8%) neonates were born by vaginal route and more than two-third (67.3%) were delivered at either the primary, secondary, or tertiary health care level, while 4% were born at home [Table 1].

Maternal characteristics

Although the majority (95.1%) of mothers had received adequate antenatal care (>4 antenatal visit) from primary, secondary, or tertiary health care, nearly 5% mothers did not attend antenatal clinic. The average maternal age was 24.4 ± 3.3 years with a range of 18–38 years, and the majority (63.7%) of them fall in the age group of 21–25 years. One thousand, four hundred and twenty-five (60.6%) mothers were primiparous [Table 1].

Transport characteristics

Nearly 90% neonates were referred from public health services by Accredited Social Health Activist workers and/or Medical Officers. One-fourth neonates were from Primary Health Center, 657 (29.4%) from Rural hospital, and 802 (35.7%) from District hospital. Seven hundred and twenty-five (32.4%) neonates were transported from 50–100 km distance, and the mean distance traveled by neonates was 71.8 ± 65.6 km with a range of 2–650 km, while 79 (3.5%) neonates traveled more than 200 km. One-thousand-eight- hundred and sixty-seven (83.4%) neonates were transported by ambulance, but 480 (21.5%) were accompanied by a Medical Assistant. One hundred and forty-one (6.3%) parents used public transport to reach the study center, while 14 (0.6%) reached by motorbike. 897 (40%) neonates reached the center within an hour, while 256 (11.4%) required more than 3 h to get hospitalized in our institute with the average duration of 1.99 ± 1 h for transport [Table 2].

Clinical diagnosis and outcome

Nine hundred and sixty-five (43.1%) neonates were diagnosed with sepsis, followed by 474 (21.2%) with respiratory distress syndrome and birth asphyxia in 262 (11.7%). Malformations were noted in 190 (8.5%) neonates [Figure 1]. The most common malformation was cardiac in 98 (51.6%), followed by central nervous system (CNS) in 35 (18.4%), genitourinary in 14 (7.4%), gastrointestinal in 19 (10%), respiratory in 13 (6.8%), and genetic syndrome in 5 (2.6%). The mean duration of hospital stay was 7.6 ± 5.6 days among admitted neonates. The mean duration of hospital stays in neonates who died was shorter (5.4 ± 5.2 days) compared to survived neonates (8.6 ± 5.6 days), and this difference was statistically significant (P < 0.0001). 663 neonates were succumbed, resulting in a mortality rate of 29.6%. Five hundred and twenty (78.4%) neonates died in the first week of life. Among them, 332 (63.8%) died within 72 h and 21.3% within 24 h of life. 43.6% neonates were died due to sepsis, followed by respiratory distress syndrome (30%) and birth asphyxia (15.4%). Forty-five (6.8%) neonates among expired had congenital malformation [Figure 2]. The highest proportionate mortality rate was noted for respiratory distress syndrome, 42%, followed by birth asphyxia (38.9%), sepsis (30%), meconium aspiration syndrome (25.3%), malformation (23.7%), and jaundice (2%).

Risk factors of mortality

On univariate analysis, preterm neonates (P < 0.0001) with a weight <1500 g (P < 0.001), those mothers who did not receive adequate antenatal care (<4 antenatal visit) (P = 0.000), advanced maternal age (P < 0.0001), multiparity (0.008), and born at home (0.08) were the risk factors of mortality. Mortality was statistically significantly high in neonates transported by ambulance without a Health Assistant and by public transport (P = 0.02). The more the travelling distance and duration, the higher the mortality recorded (P < 0.001). The median travelling distance was significantly more (70 [30–120 km]) in nonsurvival neonates compared to survival (52 [20–90 km]) (P < 0.0001). Similarly, the mean transport duration was also significantly longer (2.2 ± 1.1 h) in neonates who died compared to survived (1.9 ± 0.9 h). Abnormal respiratory rate (P < 0.0001), abnormal heart rate (P < 0.0001), mild to moderate hypothermia (P < 0.0001), low oxygen saturation (P < 0.0001), sluggish circulation (P < 0.0001), and hypoglycemia (P < 0.0001) were the risk factors of mortality [Table 3]. On multivariate analysis, mothers who received inadequate antenatal care (aOR 1.93, 95% CI 1.00–3.78, P = 0.04), long travelling distance (aOR 1.00, 95% CI 1.00–1.00, P < 0.0001), preterm birth (aOR 0.89, 95% CI 0.85–0.93 P < 0.0001), abnormal respiratory rate (aOR 0.97,95% CI 0.96–0.98, P = 0.001), abnormal heart rate (aOR 0.96, 95% CI 0.95–0.98, P < 0.0001), hypothermia (aOR 0.72, 95% CI 0.54–0.96, P = 0.02), low oxygen saturation (aOR 0.90, 95% CI 0.87–0.93, P < 0.0001), and poor perfusion (aOR 1.93, 95% CI 1.68–2.21, P < 0.0001) were the independent risk factors of mortality [Table 4].

Discussion

This study was aimed to recognize the risk factors of mortality and to identify the transport characteristic of outborn neonates to improve the outcome of neonates and to initiate the steps of achievement of sustainable developmental goal (SDG).

The majority of rural population in our country is socially/economically disadvantaged unable to utilize health facilities,[2430] and our institute is one of the largest referral institutes serving to the socioeconomically deprived population of central India. Similar to the observations of Mondal et al. and Bills et al., we noted the majority of neonates were from rural areas and 61.7% belong to lower socioeconomic class.[3132] Similar to other researchers,[333435] we noted male predominance in the study and it might be due to the fact that males are biologically more susceptible and gender discrimination in our society as well as four-fold higher health care expenditure with male neonates, compared to cheaper treatment options for female neonates, is reported in the literature.[36]

NMR in the present study was higher (29.6%), which might be because we treated our neonates with minimal facilities in general pediatric wards compared to reports of other researchers[19232425] who treated in NICU setting, while our findings are concurrent with the observation of Abdel-Aziz et al.[21] Literature also revealed NMR is significantly higher in outborn neonates compared to inborn.[202337] More than three-fourth deaths occurred in the first week of life and the majority of them in the first 72 h of life suggesting importance of perinatal care. Angu et al. reported 15.8% neonatal deaths within 24 h of age and 47.3% in 1–7 days; Kundu et al. revealed 48.75%, 69.75%, and 86.83% neonatal deaths on the 1st day, first 2nd day, and the first week of life.[2425] 7.22% and 59.28% mortality within 24 h and 1–3 days of age, respectively, were noted by Sharma et al. and Jana et al. found 76.53% neonatal deaths in the first 6 days of life.[3335] The median duration of hospital stay was significantly shorter in nonsurvival neonates who were sick at admission, (4 [2–7 days]) compared to survival neonates (7 [5–11 days]) (P < 0.0001).

In the present study, although more than half of the participants were born at term, mortality was significantly high in preterm and extremely and very low birth weight neonates, (P < 0.0001) and our findings are in agreement with other researchers.[24383940] Maturity and birth weight of neonates are important determinants of neonatal survival and post-neonatal mortality, growth failure, and the development of coronary disease in later life, indicating the urgent need of interventions for prevention of preterm birth and low birth weight by effective antenatal care at the three tier health care system.

In spite of various measures by the government, utilization of maternal and child health services is unsatisfactory, which might be because of ignorance by mothers and casual attitude of health care workers, which leads to failure of achieving a target of 100% institutional deliveries. In this study, neonatal mortality was significantly high with increased maternal age, parity, irregular antenatal visits, home delivery, and inadequate health care service utilization. Mortality was significantly higher in neonates of those mothers who did not attend antenatal clinics (P = 0.000). Association of such antenatal care factors, utilization of health care services, and Golden hour management affect the survival of neonates as reported by researchers.[154142]

In-utero transport of newborn and institutional delivery significantly reduces the still births and perinatal mortality rate.[2043] Despite the improvement in institutional delivery rate, availability of neonatal care equipment, and insurance schemes, the majority of neonates are transported to tertiary referral centers without stabilization and proper care during transport, resulting in high mortality.[163144] Usually, neonatal transport is not prioritized, and they are transported through hospital ambulance without a health assistant, private ambulance, or personal mode of transport[223238394546] in developing countries, but developed countries and Tamil Nadu state in India have improved their neonatal transport system and 75% neonates are transported by well-equipped neonatal transport ambulance.[1447] In this study, 90% of neonates were referred by government healthcare facilities (PHC, RH, DH) and transported mainly by ambulance with or without health assistant, providing no or poor care during transport and without prior communication. It was also noted as traveling distance and duration increase, mortality increases (P < 0.0001). Various researches noted long traveling distance without proper transport care or communication increases the serious neonatal complications and deaths.[192238394446484950] Such transported neonates are more prone for hypothermia, hypoglycemia, hypoxia, shock, and an increase in incidence of infections due to lack of essential newborn care. In this study, various parameters like respiratory rate, heart rate, blood sugar, oxygen saturation, perfusion, and temperature were noted in 1488 neonates. Hypothermia, hypoxia, prolonged capillary refill time, hypoglycemia, and hyperglycemia were noted in 68.3%, 51.1%, 67.3%, 5.8%, and 6.5%, respectively, and it was found that those neonates with abnormal respiratory rate, abnormal heart rate, hypothermia, hypoxia, prolonged capillary refill time, and hypoglycemia had significantly higher mortality. Ognean et al. and Cavallin et al. reported the importance of monitoring of such parameters in transported neonates as these preventable life-threatening complications increase the chances of death.[3451]

In the present study, sepsis (43.1%) was the predominant morbidity, followed by respiratory distress syndrome (21.2%), birth asphyxia (11.8%), and congenital malformation (8.5%). Cardiac malformation (congenital heart disease) was observed in more than half (51.1%) of neonates, followed by CNS malformations. Major causes of neonatal deaths were sepsis (43.6%), respiratory distress syndrome (30%), and birth asphyxia (15.4%), while the proportionate mortality rate was the highest with respiratory distress syndrome (42%), followed by birth asphyxia (39%). Das et al. from eastern India and Sharma et al. from Madhya Pradesh noted birth asphyxia is the leading cause of admission, followed by neonatal sepsis, while birth asphyxia and respiratory distress syndrome killed most of outborn neonates.[1933] Sepsis was responsible for 54.88% neonatal mortality, followed by birth asphyxia in 22.9% in the research by Jana et al.,[35] and prematurity was the leading cause of outborn neonates admission in the study conducted by Ibor EK et al.[52] Prematurity, preterm birth complications, asphyxia, and infections were the leading causes of admission and mortality and are reported in the literature among inborn and outborn neonates.[26383953]

In the current study, inadequate antenatal care of mother (aOR 1.93, 95% CI 1.00–3.78, P = 0.04), long traveling distance (aOR 1.00, 95% CI 1.00–1.00, P < 0.0001), preterm birth (aOR 0.89, 95% CI 0.85–0.93, P < 0.0001), abnormal respiratory rate (aOR 0.97, 95% CI 0.96–0.98, P = 0.001), abnormal heart rate (aOR 0.96, 95% CI 0.95–0.98, P < 0.0001), hypothermia (aOR 0.72, 95% CI 0.54–0.96, P = 0.02), low oxygen saturation (aOR 0.90 95% CI 0.87–0.93, P < 0.0001), and poor perfusion (aOR 1.93, 95% CI 1.68–2.21, P < 0.0001) were the independent risk factors of mortality. Singh et al. noted unsupervised pregnancies (<4 antenatal visit; P = 0.037), antenatal complications (P < 0.001), prematurity (P = 0.005), shock (P = 0.001), hypothermia (P < 0.001), birth asphyxia (P = 0.004), travel time >2 h (P = 0.005), and absence of trained staff during transfer (P < 0.001) were the significant predictors of mortality, while Haile et al. observed significant predictors of mortality include no antenatal care utilization (adjusted hazard ratio [AHR] 3.69; 95% CI: 1.62–8.42), a 5-min APGAR score <7 (AHR 0.38; 95% CI: 0.19–0.77), prematurity (AHR 0.34; 95%CI: 0.13–0.90), and perinatal asphyxia (AHR: 0.17; 95% CI: 0.04–0.66) on outborn and Kitt et al. noted critical illness (HR 3.07, 95% CI: 1.56–6.03) and being born at home (HR 4.82, 95% CI: 1.76–13.19) were independent predictors of mortality.[225455]

The strengths of the study include: neonates were prospectively studied for a period of two and a half years with a large cohort and the comparison was done between survival and nonsurvival; hence, chance of bias is minimized, and the neonates were referred from large catchment areas (almost 20 million); thus, the sample may be considered representative of the whole study population. Being a single-center study, hospital-based study, results may not represent the other parts of the country. Motivation of mothers to utilize the maternal/perinatal services, motivation of health care work force to deliver effective perinatal service, establishment of good neonatal transport systems, and strengthening of public schemes for transport of sick newborn with proper workforce and training are the need of hour to achieve SDG.

Conclusion

The neonatal mortality is very high compared to national figure. Inadequate antenatal care, increased traveling distance, preterm birth, abnormal respiratory rate, abnormal heart rate, hypothermia, low oxygen saturation, and poor perfusion are independent predictors of mortality. Improvement of utilization and delivery of maternal/neonatal care, transport system, and early recognition of risk factors of mortality are keys for attainment of SDG.
