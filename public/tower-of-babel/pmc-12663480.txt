Microbial succession drives flavor development in low-salt fermented bamboo shoots: A correlation analysis of physicochemical dynamics and metabolic pathways
Authors: Pei Li, Qi Lin, Di Song, Qin Luo, Lin Wu, Zhijian Long, Qiyang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the critical role of microbial succession in flavor formation during low-salt (1 % NaCl) fermentation of Jipo bamboo shoots (JPBS). Results demonstrated that texture was significantly correlated with pH and organic acids (p < 0.01), with lactic and quinic acids dominant in the final fermentation stage. Based on a flavor wheel constructed from 27 key flavor compounds (OAV > 1), 2,4-dimethylbenzaldehyde was identified as the main contributor to the aroma. High-throughput sequencing revealed a microbial succession from Bacillus to Lactobacillus (10.54 %) and unclassified Enterobacteriaceae (59.81 %), whose abundance correlated with acid and flavor accumulation (r = 0.76–0.95). Lactococcus abundance positively correlated with 2,4-dimethylbenzaldehyde. KEGG predictions highlighted carbohydrate metabolism (e.g., glycolysis) and amino acid metabolism (e.g., branched-chain amino acid degradation) as key pathways for flavor compound synthesis. This study lays the foundation for optimizing low-salt bamboo shoot fermentation technology.

Introduction

As the tender shoots that emerge from bamboo rhizomes, bamboo shoots are highly valued for their distinctive texture, digestive benefits, and rich nutritional profile, including high dietary fiber and low cholesterol (Zhao et al., 2024). However, postharvest bamboo shoots lignify rapidly when stored at ambient temperature, resulting in increased hardness and diminished sensory quality (Marco et al., 2021). Fermentation is an effective postharvest preservation strategy that not only extends shelf life but can also preserve phenolics and dietary fiber while generating desirable flavors via microbial metabolism (Marco et al., 2021). Since fermentation involves complex interactions among indigenous microbiota, substrates, and environmental conditions, uncontrolled proliferation of certain endogenous bacteria may occur during traditional salt-free fermentation, leading to enzymatic softening of the product and undesirable flavor development (Yang et al., 2020). Therefore, traditional salt-free fermentation of bamboo shoots faces challenges, including microbial instability, extended fermentation time, and potential odor defects, all of which hinder product standardization (Yang et al., 2020).

Salt concentration significantly influences the microbial community, metabolic pathways, and formation of volatile compounds in fermented vegetables. High salt can suppress beneficial microbial activity and slow fermentation, while excessively low salt may compromise product stability; therefore, identifying a minimal yet effective salt level is important for product quality and consumer health. In this context, low-salt fermentation (typically 0.5 %–4 % salt immersion for anaerobic fermentation, initiated by indigenous surface microbiota) has emerged as a promising approach (Yang et al., 2020). This approach effectively modulates microbial metabolism, thereby minimizing the production of undesirable volatile compounds. Supporting this, Ahmadsah et al. (2015) identified 1.3 % salt concentration as the preferred level for fermented pickles based on comprehensive evaluations, while Wierzbicka and Janiszewska-Turak (2024) reported that adding 1 % salt resulted in fermented vegetables with higher levels of bioactive compounds and satisfactory bacterial populations. Given the growing consumer demand for low-sodium foods, producing healthier fermented vegetables with the minimum salt level that ensures proper preservation and retains desirable sensory characteristics holds significant industry importance. Nevertheless, under these low-salt conditions, the specific microbial metabolic activities that govern flavor formation and the mechanistic basis of these flavor changes remain poorly understood.

Flavor, a critical factor for consumer acceptance, is profoundly shaped during fermentation by microbial activity, especially through the production of organic acids (OAs) and aroma-active compounds that shape the taste, texture, and physicochemical characteristics of the end product (Ye et al., 2020). Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) dominate low-salt fermented pickle ecosystems and are instrumental in producing flavor-active metabolites (Jiang et al., 2025); LAB succession follows a distinct pattern: initial dominance by less acid-tolerant heterofermentative LAB is gradually supplanted by acid-tolerant homofermentative LAB as environmental acidity increases (Xiong et al., 2016), as observed in fermentations like Dendrocalamus latiflorus Munro (e.g., Weissella to Lactobacillus transition) (Li et al., 2022). Despite growing knowledge of LAB succession in fermented vegetables, an integrated understanding linking microbial succession, key aroma-active compounds, and the metabolic pathways that generate them in low-salt bamboo shoot fermentation is lacking. Specifically, it is unclear which taxa are responsible for the formation of critical aroma compounds and how carbohydrate and amino-acid metabolism contribute to flavor development under 1 % NaCl conditions.

In this study, Jipo bamboo shoots (JPBS) were fermented under low-salt conditions (1 % NaCl), and the dynamic changes in flavor formation were systematically investigated using headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME/GC–MS). In addition, an integrated approach combining 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing, correlation analysis, and KEGG-based metabolic pathway prediction was used to investigate how microbial succession is linked to the variation of key aroma-active compounds and their potential biosynthetic pathways. This work aims to elucidate the microbial metabolic mechanisms driving flavor development during low-salt fermentation, thereby providing theoretical support for improving flavor quality and promoting controlled fermentation of bamboo shoot products under reduced-salt conditions.

Materials and methods

Fermented bamboo shoots preparation

Fresh bamboo shoots were obtained from Leshan, Sichuan Province, China. Peeled bamboo shoots were rinsed with tap water, cut into strips (approximately 13.0 × 0.5 × 0.5 cm; length × width × thickness), and boiled at 100 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 50 g of the strips and 0.5 L of sterile brine (1 % w/w NaCl, a salt level lower than that typically used in commercial products) were placed into sterile polyethylene bottles (in triplicate). The bottles were sealed and subjected to spontaneous fermentation at 22 ± 2 °C for 60 days. Fermentation broth and corresponding bamboo shoots were sampled on days 1, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 60. The texture and pH of the bamboo shoot samples were analyzed immediately after collection. All remaining samples (both liquid and solid) were rapidly frozen and preserved at −80 °C for subsequent experimental procedures.

Texture analysis

Texture profile analysis of JPBS, including hardness, fracturability, and chewiness, was conducted following the previously reported methods (Long et al., 2023), A TA-XT Plus Texture Analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK) equipped with a P36R probe was used. The testing protocol involved two consecutive compression cycles. Key settings included pre-test, test, and post-test speeds of 2, 1, and 1 mm/s, respectively, a compression strain of 70 %, a 5-s interval between the two compressions, and a trigger force of 5 g.

Chemical analysis

The pH value, total titratable acid (TTA), reducing sugar content, and nitrite content in fermentation liquids were determined based on the methods reported in our previous work (Long et al., 2023). A calibrated pH meter (PHS-25, Greifensee, Switzerland) was employed for pH determination. TTA was measured following the Chinese national standard GB/T 12456–2021. For this, 10 mL of fermentation broth was titrated with 0.1 N NaOH using phenolphthalein (0.5 mL) as an indicator to determine total acidity. Reducing sugar content in the fermentation liquid was determined according to GB 5009.7–2021. Nitrite content in the fermented bamboo shoot fermentation broth was determined following GB 5009.33–2016 with minor modifications. Briefly, 18 g of bamboo shoot fermentation liquid was mixed with saturated borax and heated, then clarified using potassium ferricyanide and zinc acetate. After filtration, the supernatant was reacted with sulfanilamide and N-(1-naphthyl) ethylenediamine dihydrochloride(2 g/L). Absorbance was measured at 538 nm and the nitrite content was quantified against a sodium nitrite standard curve, expressed in milligrams per kilogram (mg/kg). All the above measurements were conducted in triplicate. Organic acids were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (Agilent Technologies, California, USA) based on the approach presented by (Chen et al., 2022).

Aroma-active volatiles

Volatile constituents in the fermentation broth were profiled using HS-SPME-GC–MS/MS (Varian450GC, Varian Analytical Instruments, California, USA), as described by (Long et al., 2023). Aliquots (8 mL broth) were transferred to 20 mL headspace vials with 3 g NaCl. After adding 100 μL n-hexane with 0.38 μg/mL cyclohexanone (internal standard), vials were immediately sealed and equilibrated at 60 °C for 10 min. Volatile compounds were extracted using a 65 μm CAR/PDMS/DVB fiber (Supelco Inc., Bellefonte, PA, USA) by adsorbing analytes at 60 °C for 30 min, subsequently thermally desorbed in the GC inlet at 250 °C for 5 min. Separation was carried out using an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm), helium was employed as the carrier gas, maintained at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, with analyses performed under splitless injection mode. The oven temperature program was as follows: 40 °C/0 min- (12 °C/min) -100 °C /2 min- (5 °C/min) -120 °C/0 min- (8 °C/min)- 180 °C/5 min- (4 °C/min)- 210 °C/0 min- (8 °C/min)- 250 °C/0 min. MS detection operated in EI mode (70 eV) with m/z 40–400 scan range at thermal condition of the ion source maintained at 230 °C. Retention indices (RI) were calculated consistent with the method described by (Tang et al., 2022). The odor threshold values and their references for all volatile compounds are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Here, C denotes the measured concentration of a volatile compound, and OT refers to its corresponding threshold concentration.

Microbial community analysis

Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from fermentation broth samples employing the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was then amplified with universal primers 338F and 806R. Amplified fragments (400–550 bp) were purified with AMPure XP beads. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Ji et al., 2021). Raw reads were processed using Trimmomatic v0.33 for quality trimming. The sequencing data was then denoised using the dada2 method in QIIME2 2020.6, with paired-end sequences concatenated and chimeric sequences discarded to obtain valid non-chimeric reads. Sequences with ≥97 % similarity were grouped into operational taxonomic units (OTUs). α-diversity indices were calculated to assess within-sample microbial richness and evenness, while β-diversity analyses, including non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), were conducted to evaluate inter-sample community differences. LEfSe analysis was further applied to identify taxa contributing significantly to the differences among groups (Bolyen et al., 2019; Callahan et al., 2016). The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI SRA database under BioProject accession number PRJNA1356309 and SRA accession number SRP640081.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed in triplicate. Data are expressed as mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test for post hoc comparisons, with significance set at p < 0.05. The associations of microbial taxa with volatile compounds were examined via Pearson correlation, with significance defined by |r| > 0.7 and p-values adjusted for false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05.

Results and discussion

Analysis of physicochemical properties

The texture characteristics, especially hardness, fracturability, and chewiness, greatly affect the quality of sour bamboo shoots (Chen et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 1A, these parameters significantly decreased within the first 14 days of fermentation (p < 0.05). This textural softening is primarily attributed to microbial metabolic activity. Specifically, acid-producing bacteria (including Lactobacillus and Lactococcus) generate lactic acid and cell wall-degrading enzymes (e.g., cellulases). Beyond texture, which is primarily driven by microbial metabolism (acidification and enzymatic hydrolysis), changes in key chemical parameters provide critical insights into microbial activity and fermentation progression. The pH and total titratable acidity (TTA) exhibited distinct dynamic patterns during fermentation, which are associated with textural changes through their effect on cell membrane permeability. Specifically, the pH significantly increased from 4.75 to 5.57 during the initial stage (days 1–7), while the TTA decreased considerably from 6.32 g/L to 4.46 g/L (Fig. 1B, C). This inverse trend is likely driven by early-stage heterofermentative LAB consuming available organic acids (Li et al., 2022). Subsequently, as homofermentative LAB dominated the anaerobic environment, metabolizing sugars into organic acids (e.g., lactic acid) and CO₂ (Peng et al., 2025), pH decreased to 5.18 (day 60) while TTA increased. A peak in reducing sugar content (1.26 g/L, Fig. 1D), a key microbial carbon source, occurred on day 14, followed by a decline in later fermentation stages as consumption intensified (Xiong et al., 2016). Concomitantly, an initial peak in nitrite levels (0.45 mg/kg, Fig. 1E) was observed at day 7; this critical safety indicator subsequently declined to 0.12 mg/kg by day 60. This reduction is associated with decreasing pH and the proliferation of lactic acid bacteria (Peng et al., 2025).

Organic acids analysis

Organic acids play a central role in imparting sourness to fermented bamboo shoots (Shang et al., 2022). During fermentation, six types of organic acids were identified and measured: oxalic, quinic, malic, lactic, succinic, and adipic acids (Fig. 1F). While the total organic acid content showed an overall decline trend, the dynamics of individual acids varied. Lactic acid demonstrated a characteristic pattern, increasing from 0.32 mg/mL to a peak of 0.33 mg/mL by day 21, followed by a progressive decrease to a final concentration of 0.21 mg/mL. This post-21-day decline is primarily driven by microbial consumption of lactate as an alternative energy substrate when preferred carbon sources become scarce (Gänzle, 2015). Supporting this, (Chen et al., 2022) reported that the concentration of lactic acid, serving as a carbon source for microbial growth, decreases correspondingly during the later stages of fermentation. The biochemical basis for this lies in specific pathways, such as those in Lactobacillus buchneri, where lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) facilitates the conversion of lactate, channeling it into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle for energy generation (Gänzle, 2015).

In contrast, oxalic acid, succinic acid, and adipic acid exhibited significant decreasing trends throughout the entire fermentation period (p < 0.05). Quinic acids and malic acids showed distinct patterns, quinic acid concentration remained relatively stable after an initial decrease. Malic acid decreased significantly over time (p < 0.05), reaching 0.052 mg/mL at day 60, consistent with its role as a key intermediate rapidly consumed within the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (Tang et al., 2024). Guan et al. (2020) suggested that minor fluctuations in oxalic and malic acid levels, such as a slight increase observed after day 21, may result from the release of cellular contents during tissue decomposition. Ultimately, lactic (0.21 mg/mL) and quinic (0.47 mg/mL) acids were the dominant acids, jointly defining the final sour profile.

Aroma compounds identification and flavor wheel presentation

Volatile compounds analysis

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are key determinants of flavor and aroma in fermented foods (Peng et al., 2025). In this study, HS-SPME-GC–MS/MS was employed to profile volatile metabolites across various fermentation stages, leading to the detection of 91 distinct volatile compounds (Table S1 and Fig. 2A), categorized as 24 alcohols, 18 esters, 12 aldehydes, 4 phenols, 14 ketones, 3 acids, and 16 other compounds. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct volatile profiles corresponding to fermentation phases (Fig. 2B), with PC1 and PC2 accounting for 89.4 % and 6.0 % of the total variance, respectively. Day 1 samples were clearly separated from later stages (days 7–60), indicating unique metabolite signatures characteristic of the initiation phase (Peng et al., 2025). Compounds dominating day 1 included 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol (1669.1 μg/L), 2-methylbutyric acid (1160.17 μg/L), 1-octen-3-ol (328.4 μg/L), and the (E, Z)-3,6-nonadien-1-ol (203.28 μg/L). These constituents, likely originate from raw bamboo shoot material, are proposed to serve as crucial flavor precursors for subsequent fermentation (Zhao et al., 2023). The total volatile content exhibited a biphasic trend, declining from an initial high on day 1 before progressively increasing to a peak of 5491.37 μg/L by day 28 (Fig. 2C). At this peak, alcohols and phenols constituted the primary components, accounting for 16.15 % and 47.31 % of total volatiles, respectively. Dynamic changes in specific volatiles were observed during fermentation. Several compounds that were predominant early (e.g., 2,4-Ditert-butylphenol, d-limonene) underwent a reduction at the outset, followed by a subsequent rise thereafter. Notably, d-limonene concentration declined significantly to 1.24 μg/L by day 21 before rising sharply to a peak of 1512.8 μg/L at day 28. Meanwhile, other compounds such as ethyl 2-methylbutyrate, ethyl sulfate, and 2-methylbutyric acid were depleted to undetectable levels as fermentation progressed. Alcohols displayed a significant increase between days 21 and 28; by the end of fermentation, nine alcoholic compounds were identified, among which 1-octen-3-ol (152.01 μg/L) and (E,Z)-3,6-nonadien-1-ol (114.92 μg/L) were the most prominent. Aldehydes, recognized for their low odor thresholds and potent aromas, also accumulated in the late stage (days 21–60). Notably, day 1 featured high-OAV compounds including such as 2,4-dimethylbenzaldehyde (floral notes) and 2-methylbutyric acid (rancid character), which collectively establishing the initial sour profile. By day 60, 2-methylbutyric acid declined markedly (OAV = 5.63), indicating metabolic conversion, while persistent compounds like 2,2,4-trimethyl-1,3-pentanediol diisobutyrate maintained high concentrations. Among the nine OAV-active alcohols, (E, Z)-3,6-nonadien-1-ol exhibited the highest OAV range (88.40–156.37, grassy/fatty notes), next in abundance were 1-octen-3-ol and 1-dodecanol. These alcohols functioned dualistically as direct aroma contributors and as precursors for aldehydes/esters, indirectly shaping fruity/fresh characteristics (Wang et al., 2022). Minor components like d-limonene (lemon) and β-myrcene (resin) provided essential aromatic nuances, aligned with their recognized function as major aroma contributors in fruit- and beverage-based products (Zhu et al., 2015).

Establishment of flavor wheel

Utilizing quantitative OAV determination, we established a comprehensive flavor wheel constructed to more accurately depict the distinctive aroma of JPBS (Fig. 3D). The odor descriptions of the selected aromatic compounds were confirmed by records in previous literature (Long et al., 2023). The aroma of mature JPBS was divided into 10 subcategories, namely floral, fruity, mushroom, herbal, fatty, woody, smoky, almond, foul, and other. Floral aroma compounds included 1-heptanol, linalool, 1-dodecanol, 1-decanol, phenylacetaldehyde, and o-xylene, with phenylacetaldehyde recognized as a key floral volatile in tea (Wang et al., 2025). Fruity aromas were represented by cedrol, ethyl 2-methylbutyrate, decanal, and d-limonene. The sweetness of esters improves fermented food flavor by reducing unpleasant odors (Miao et al., 2025), while cedrol contributes cedar-like sweet/fruity notes (Ma et al., 2023). The characteristic mushroom aroma of 1-octen-3-ol, previously identified in bamboo shoot fermentation (Long et al., 2023), has been consistently observed in various fermented bamboo products (Fu et al., 2002). Herbal flavors were characterized by (E,Z)-3,6-nonadien-1-ol and nonanal, the latter significantly influencing Guangxi sour bamboo shoot flavor (Jian et al., 2024). Woody notes from β-myrcene, γ-terpinene, and isophorone further enriched the aromatic profile. The sixth category (fatty aroma) featured octanal and 1-pentanol, with octanal notably impacting roast duck aroma (Liu et al., 2019). Smoky notes were attributed to 2-methoxy-4-vinylphenol and dimethylsilanediol. Crucially, 2,4-dimethylbenzaldehyde exhibited the highest odor activity (average OAV = 584.07), imparting dominant sweet almond/spicy characteristics. Putrid/sour odors were represented by 2-methylbutyric acid and dibutyl phthalate. Collectively, these 27 OAV-active compounds coordinate the complex bamboo shoot aroma. The composition and function of microorganisms

Microbial composition

Microbial community succession during fermentation was profiled using 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing. Alpha diversity (Chao1, ACE, Shannon, and Simpson) (Fig. 3A-D) indices a gradual change of the microbial community structure over time: At the initial fermentation phase (Day 1), the microbial community exhibited high richness (Chao1/ACE) and diversity (Shannon: 6.90; Simpson: 0.96). This indicates that, in response to the addition of raw materials, the microbial community in the fermentation liquid underwent rapid structural and diversity shifts (Fan et al., 2019; Long et al., 2023). In the early stages of fermentation (Days 1–7), the community was primarily composed of a coexisting consortium of Escherichia coli, other facultative anaerobes, and fermentative yeasts. The low-salt environment inhibited acid-producing metabolism, leading to a transient pH rise. Consequently, microbial diversity and richness decreased, likely due to the emergence of strains capable of tolerating high osmotic pressure, a phenomenon consistent with findings in (Tang et al., 2024). As fermentation proceeded under anaerobic conditions (Days 7–21), the microbial community transitioned to homofermentative dominance. The concurrent increase in richness may be attributed to the stabilization of the anaerobic environment, which created niches for a new set of obligate and facultative anaerobic species. The drop in pH during this period selectively inhibited non-acid-tolerant groups, thus increasing the Simpson index-indicating that the dominance of specific microbial groups strengthened. By the later stages of fermentation (Days 28–60), acid-tolerant anaerobic microorganisms became the dominant group, stabilizing alpha diversity (p < 0.05). A study on industrial paocai fermentation reported that microbial diversity peaked in the late fermentation stage, indicating that the recovery of richness reflects the transition of the fermentation environment toward a mature and stabilized state (Liang et al., 2018). which further highlights the important role of environmental conditions in shaping microbial community structure.

Variations in microbial community composition

Microbial community structure and taxonomic composition across phyla and genera were analyzed by integrating classification annotation with sample abundance data. The microbial community structure exhibited significant and continuous shifts throughout the fermentation process, as confirmed by PCoA and UPGMA analyses (Figs. S2 and S3). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 61.94 % and 14.44 % of the total variance, respectively. Samples from Days 7 to 21 clustered closely together, indicating a period of high structural similarity and ecological stability (Chen et al., 2022; Long et al., 2023). During the 21–28 d period, the bacterial community became progressively simplified, with reduced species richness and the formation of distinct dominant bacterial groups (Xu et al., 2023). From 28 to 60 d, the sustained proliferation of acid-tolerant and adaptive bacteria led to a recovery in microbial richness and a marked reorganization of community structure. This dynamic pattern is consistent with previous studies (Liang et al., 2018), which similarly reported the re-establishment of microbial diversity and compositional shifts during the late stages of bamboo shoot fermentation.

As shown in Fig. 3E, twelve bacterial phyla (relative abundance >1 %) were identified throughout bamboo shoot fermentation, with Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Cyanobacteria being the most dominant. Firmicutes, which comprised 91 % of the bacterial community in the initial stage, are presumed to originate from the raw materials' surface and internal tissues (Guan et al., 2022). Their relative abundance subsequently decreased Concurrently, Proteobacteria increased markedly, becoming the dominant phylum by day 14 (>50 % relative abundance) and remaining high until the end (89.41 %, Fig. 3E). The early predominance of Firmicutes aligns with previous reports on fermented vegetables, including Guangxi sour bamboo shoots (Guan et al., 2022). A notable observation was the transient increase in sequences classified as Cyanobacteria during the mid-fermentation phase (14–28 days). Given the strictly anaerobic environment and the plant-derived nature of the substrate, these signals are unlikely to originate from photosynthetic cyanobacteria. Similar occurrences of cyanobacteria signals in anaerobic fermentation systems have been reported and attributed to such taxonomic misclassification or plant DNA contamination (Voidarou et al., 2020).

At the genus level, twelve taxa (relative abundance above 1 %) exhibited notable variations in their abundance within the sour bamboo shoots (Fig. 3F). At the beginning of fermentation (Day 1), the microbial community was dominated by the genus Bacillus, accounting for 87.35 % of the total abundance. Subsequently, its relative abundance steadily declined, falling to less than 1 % at day 21. Staphylococcus reached its peak abundance on day 7 before nearly disappearing (Fig. 3K). In parallel, Enterococcus grew rapidly on day 7, accounting for 52.60 % of the total community, and then declined significantly—a genus whose presence has been reported to promote the growth of other bacteria (Zhubanova et al., 2024). The rapid decline of Enterococcus with increasing acidity limits its dominance to the initial fermentation phase, as observed in pickle fermentation, and thus it exerts only a minor influence on final product safety (Liu et al., 2015). Lactobacillus abundance increased gradually from days 14 to 28 (1.22 % to 10.54 %). This rise was positively correlated with lactic acid content during days 14–21 and coincided with its peak at day 21 (Fig. 1F). The subsequent lactic acid decline at day 28, when Lactobacillus reached its maximum, suggests lactate utilization as an alternative carbon source to sustain the dominance of this genus (Gänzle, 2015). As a functional microbe, Lactobacillus contributes significantly to the biosynthesis of organic acids and volatile aromatics. Its robust fermentative capacity also underlies its widespread industrial application in enhancing flavor and prolonging stability in products ranging from pickles to fermented beverages (Zhang et al., 2025). The proportion of Acinetobacter remained stable at approximately 10 % during fermentation but exhibited temporal fluctuations (Fig. 3B). This genus has been frequently detected in fermented vegetable systems (Yu et al., 2022). In addition, the abundance of unclassified Enterobacteriaceae gradually increased and became the dominant genus as fermentation concluded on day 60 (Fig. 3J), indicating a microbial shift during fermentation from facultative anaerobes (e.g., Bacillus, Staphylococcus) to acid-tolerant taxa like Lactobacillus and members of Enterobacteriaceae.

Representative bacteria

Microbial biomarkers specific to each fermentation stage were identified using LEfSe analysis (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size; LDA log score threshold ≥4), allowing for a comparison of community composition across stages (Fig. 3G). The early-stage community (day 1) contained 1 phylum biomarker (Firmicutes); 1 class-level biomarker (Bacilli); 1 order-level biomarker (Bacillales); 1 family biomarker (Bacillaceae); 1 genus biomarker (Bacillus); and 1 species biomarker (unclassified Bacillus). Fermentation at 7 days contained 2 order biomarkers (Lactobacillales); 1 family biomarker (Staphylococcaceae); 1 genus biomarker (Staphylococcus); and 1 species biomarker (unclassified Staphylococcus). Fermentation at 21 days contained 1 phylum biomarker (Proteobacteria); 1 class biomarker (Gammaproteobacteria); 1 order biomarker (Enterobacterales); 1 family biomarker (Enterobacteriaceae); 1 genus biomarker (unclassified Enterobacteriaceae); 1 species biomarker (unclassified Enterobacteriaceae). By the late fermentation stage (day 60), Enterococcus and unclassified-Enterobacteriaceae became the dominant microbial taxa. Our results demonstrate a clear successional pattern transitioning from facultative anaerobes (Bacillus and Duganella) to acid-tolerant microorganisms (Enterococcus and Lactobacillus), consistent with the microbial changes commonly observed in vegetable fermentation.

Associations between microbial communities and flavor, along with relevant metabolic networks

To deepen the understanding of the correlation of microbial communities with essential flavor compounds, this study employed Pearson correlation analysis combined with KEGG functional metabolic pathway prediction to analyze the contributions of dominant microorganisms to flavor formation across different fermentation stages. Significant correlations were identified between predominant bacterial genera and key aromatic compounds (OAV > 1), as well as critical physicochemical parameters (Figs. S4 and S1). The correlation results for physicochemical properties consistent with the observations in Section 3.1. Hardness, fracturability, and chewiness all showed negative correlations with pH. This confirms that the decrease in system pH, driven primarily by acid production from microorganisms (especially lactic acid bacteria), is a key factor driving texture softening in bamboo shoots. Notably, reducing sugars exhibited a significant positive correlation with pH and a significant negative correlation with organic acids (OAs). This indicates that microorganisms, such as lactic acid bacteria, utilize reducing sugars as a carbon source for the production of organic acids like lactic acid. In the early fermentation stages, Bacillus abundance was high and displayed a statistically significant positive association with (TTA (p < 0.05). Although not typically considered a primary acid producer, Bacillus secretes hydrolytic enzymes such as amylases and proteases that decompose organic compounds in the initial phase. The released amino acids serve as precursors for further microbial metabolism, yielding organic acids, alcohols, and gases that collectively contribute to acidification and pH reduction (Kimura & Yokoyama, 2019). Moreover, Fan et al. (2019) reported that both Bacillus and Lactobacillus exhibit strong lactic acid-producing capacity, and non-lactic acid bacteria such as Bacillus and Streptococcus can indirectly influence final acidity and flavor formation in vegetable fermentation (Liu et al., 2024). Cyanobacteriales, Lactobacillus, and Staphylococcus exhibited positive correlations with various alcohols (e.g., 1-octen-3-ol, (E, Z)-3,6-nonadien-1-ol), aldehydes (e.g., phenylacetaldehyde, octanal), and short-chain fatty acids (e.g., 2-methylbutyric acid), suggesting their collective contribution to floral, grassy, and fruity aroma profiles. Interestingly, Enterobacteriaceae exhibited a significant inverse relationship with dimethylsilanediol (p < 0.01) but was positively linked to aroma-active volatiles like cedrol, 1-dodecanol, and phenylacetaldehyde, indicating its potential role in generating sweet and floral notes. Similarly, Enterococcus was positively associated with ester compounds (e.g., 2-methylbutanoic acid ethyl ester) while negatively correlated with octanal (p < 0.01), suggesting involvement in shaping fruity and floral attributes. Notably, 2,4-dimethylbenzaldehyde showed significant association with Lactococcus during mid-to-late fermentation stages (p < 0.05), highlighting its potential contribution to developing mature, characteristic aromas in fermented JPBS.

Building upon the correlation analysis, metabolic pathway functional gene annotation was performed using KEGG to construct a core metabolic network for flavor substance production (Fig. 4). In the early stage, carbon metabolism was the most abundant pathway, reflecting rapid microbial metabolism to release energy and carbon dioxide, the latter helping establish an anaerobic environment conducive to homofermentation (Xiao et al., 2021). Key acidic metabolites—lactic, oxalic, and malic acids—are mainly generated through carbohydrate catabolism, including glycolysis and the TCA cycle. Both Lactobacillus and Bacillus species possess key enzymes genes such as L-lactic dehydrogenase (EC 1.1.1.27), enabling conversion of pyruvate into lactic acid through homo-fermentative fermentation, which gradually increases lactic acid content. Oxalic, malic, and succinic compounds, acting as central intermediates of the TCA pathway, are progressively metabolized during fermentation, explaining the significant decrease in their content. Fatty aldehydes (such as nonanal and decanal) are formed through the β-oxidation pathway. Lactobacillus species are rich in enzymes which promote the formation of aldehydes and alcohol (EC 4.2.1.17) acetyltransferase (EC 2.3.1.9) and other metabolic enzymes, which promote aldehyde and alcohol production within fermented products (Jian et al., 2024). Aromatic compounds such as 2,4-dimethylbenzaldehyde may originate from phenylalanine and tyrosine degradation pathways, where key enzymes including phenylalanine aminotransferase (EC 2.6.1.5) and Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (EC 4.3.1.25) secreted by Lactococcus and Staphylococcus (Nishimura et al., 2022). Jiang et al. (2024) that Lactococcus significantly reduced phenylalanine content while increasing 2,4-dimethylbenzaldehyde (almond-like aroma), reinforcing its flavor contribution. The correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between the genus Lactobacillus and ethyl 2-methylbutyrate, and a significant positive correlation with 2-methylbutyric acid (p < 0.05). Volatile acids such as 2-methylbutyric acid—synthesized by Lactobacillus via the Ehrlich pathway using branched-chain amino acid transaminase (EC 2.6.1.42) and thioesterase (EC 3.1.2.14)—contribute importantly to sourness (Dan et al., 2019), The hydrolysis of ethyl 2-methylbutyrate to 2-methylbutyric acid led to the complete degradation of the ester in the later stages of fermentation, while 2-methylbutyric acid transiently accumulated on day 28. Although 2-methylbutyric acid was abundant in the early fermentation stages, its content decreased significantly as it was further metabolized into intermediates such as acetyl-CoA and entered the TCA cycle. Cyanobacteria and Lactobacillus exhibited similar flavor compound synthesis profiles, showing significant positive correlations with alcohols such as 1-octen-3-ol and (E, Z)-3,6-nonadien-1-ol (p < 0.05; p < 0.01 for Lactobacillus). Cyanobacteria may supply precursor fatty acids such as α-linolenic acid, while Lactobacillus—enriched in lipoxygenase and alcohol dehydrogenase—drives their conversion into flavor alcohols, a role supported by pathway annotations (Jian et al., 2024). In summary, Lactobacillus, Lactococcus, Staphylococcus, and Enterobacteriaceae serve as core functional genera in the low-salt JPBS fermentation system. These taxa exhibit stage-specific metabolic succession and functional complementarity. Although these genera do not maintain absolute dominance in terms of abundance throughout the process, their stage-specific metabolic activities collectively form the microbial functional foundation for the flavor development of JPBS. Conclusion

The research elucidated how changes in microbial communities and related metabolic processes drive flavor development during low-salt fermentation of JPBS. Microbial metabolism is considered to be the main cause of texture degradation, acid production, and aroma compound synthesis. The findings indicated that low-salt fermentation significantly reduced the texture characteristics of JPBS and stabilized after day 14, while organic acids continuously decreased, showing a significant inverse relationship with pH and titratable acidity. The total concentration of volatile compounds peaked on day 28, with key aromatic substances such as (E,Z)-3,6-nonadien-1-ol and 2,4-dimethylbenzaldehyde increasing markedly, collectively defining the unique aromatic profile of JPBS. Through profiling of 27 active aroma compounds, a flavor wheel was established, revealing 2,4-dimethylbenzaldehyde as the principal contributor to the aroma. Microbial community analysis indicated a successional pattern from initial dominance of Bacillus to the late-stage predominance of Lactobacillus (10.54 %) and unclassified Enterobacteriaceae (59.81 %). Correlation analysis revealed that the Cyanobacteriales, Lactobacillus, and Staphylococcus were closely associated with the accumulation of organic acids and key flavor volatiles (such as (E, Z)-3,6-nonadien-1-ol and 1-octen-3-ol), while Lactococcus showed a positive correlation with 2,4-dimethylbenzaldehyde. KEGG pathways further indicate that carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism served as the core metabolic pathways.

Future research should incorporate salt-related fermentation factors to further elucidate the specific regulatory role of salt in microbial succession and flavor metabolism. Multi-omics approaches are needed to validate the proposed flavor metabolic pathways, and species-level microbial analysis will be essential to precisely determine the functional contributions of individual microbes. In summary, this study provides a framework for exploring the associations between shifts in microbial communities and flavor evolution in low-salt fermented bamboo shoots offering scientific basis for enhancing product quality, flavor consistency, and industrial standardization through microbial regulation strategies.
