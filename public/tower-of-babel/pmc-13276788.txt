Research in context

Evidence before this study

Influenza A (H3N2) subclade K caused unusually high infection rates in the 2025/26 Northern hemisphere season, raising concerns about antigenic drift and reduced vaccine effectiveness. Studies with ferret antisera indicated a mismatch between vaccine strains and circulating viruses, raising concerns about reduced vaccine effectiveness. However, early real-world effectiveness estimates in multiple countries did not align with these predictions. This discrepancy prompted an urgent need for serologic analyses using human rather than ferret immune responses.

Added value of this study

We assessed serologic responses to vaccination in a metropolitan adult cohort with substantial prior seasonal influenza vaccination to characterise the antigenic mismatch between vaccine and circulating H3N2 subclade K variants. Functional antibodies were measured using four contemporary H3N2 field isolates in haemagglutination inhibition and microneutralization assays. Although H3N2 subclade K variants caused record-high infection rates in the Northern Hemisphere, our findings demonstrate that the 2025/2026 seasonal influenza vaccine formulation increased HI and neutralisation titres against both the vaccine strain and multiple subclade K viruses.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our data underline that it is critical to use human sera for continued antigenic monitoring to guide influenza vaccine strain selection in a manner that considers the contribution of pre-existing immunity in adult populations.

Introduction

Influenza A virus H3N2 strains are known to cause severe epidemics, particularly affecting older adults with high excess mortality and morbidity.1 In the past six months,2 a drifted H3N2 variant - initially termed J.2.4.1 and now commonly referred to as subclade K–emerged and spread rapidly, leading to an early onset of the influenza season in the Northern Hemisphere with high numbers of influenza-like illnesses.3,4

H3N2 subclade K viruses possess several mutations in the antigenic sites of the haemagglutinin (HA) surface protein, which are associated with immune escape.2,4,5 Compared to the 2025/2026 egg-based vaccine reference strain A/Croatia/10136RV/2023, the subclade K root bears multiple amino acid substitutions in the haemagglutinin head domain, including changes at positions in antigenic sites A (H156Q), B (H193Q, I192F), and D (G186D, E190A, among others), as well as additional substitutions in HA2. These changes, particularly in the immunodominant antigenic sites B and A, are predicted to reduce binding by vaccine-elicited antibodies targeting these epitopes. Indeed, studies using ferret sera raised against the current egg-based vaccine strain (A/Croatia/10136RV/2023) demonstrated more than 32-fold reductions in haemagglutination inhibition (HI) titres against subclade K viruses, while ferret sera from the cell-based vaccine strain (A/District of Columbia/27/2023) showed 8- to 16-fold reductions.4,5 These results indicate significant antigenic drift and raise concerns about vaccine effectiveness (VE), suggesting a potential mismatch between the H3N2 vaccine strains and circulating subclade K viruses.4 However, initial VE measurements in the UK have shown good to moderate effectiveness.4,6

Given the discordance between ferret-based antigenic predictions and real-world vaccine effectiveness data, there is an urgent need for human serological studies that account for the immunological complexity of repeatedly vaccinated adult populations. Here, we present haemagglutination inhibition and microneutralization titres measured in paired pre- and post-vaccination sera from 100 adults in New York City, using both a vaccine-like strain and three contemporary H3N2 subclade K field isolates. Our primary objectives were to characterise pre-existing cross-reactive immunity to subclade K, quantify vaccine-induced antibody boosting across several subclade K variants, and assess whether ferret-based predictions of antigenic drift were reflected in human serological data.

Methods

Ethics

The human biospecimen used in this study were collected under IRB-approved protocols (sera: STUDY-16-01199, STUDY-16-01215, field isolates: STUDY-13-00981) at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. All participants provided written informed consent prior to data and sample collection. This study adheres to the ethical principles of the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki.

Human sera

We selected 200 sera from 100 adult study participants of two observational longitudinal institutional review board (IRB)-approved cohort studies conducted in New York City (STUDY-16-01199 | STUDY-16-01215). A study participant flow diagram and the STROBE checklist are provided as the Supplementary Material (Supplemental Figure S1, Supplemental Table S1, respectively).

This was a convenience sample study for which we selected 200 paired sera based on availability of confirmed pre- and post-vaccination samples within the 2025/2026 influenza season. No formal a priori power calculation was performed. Since there was no human serological data for subclade K available at the time of study design, we considered a sample size of 200 paired sera to be adequate for characterising pre-existing immunity and vaccine-boosting responses. Inclusion in this analysis was based on the availability of sufficient paired sera. Specifically, selection criteria included 2025/2026 seasonal influenza vaccination, availability of a pre-vaccination blood draw within three months prior to vaccination, and availability of a post-vaccination blood draw collected at peak response (median [range]: 29 [21–42] days). Participants with incomplete/missing paired samples were excluded (see also Supplemental Figure S1).

To reduce potential sources of bias, we used a within-participant paired sampling design, collecting sera from the same individual before and after seasonal influenza vaccination. All haemagglutination inhibition and microneutralization assays were performed in a standardised manner using sequence-verified viral stocks and consistent reagent lots across the experiments included in this study. We acknowledge that our cohort is enriched for adults with high baseline immunity due to frequent prior immunisations which may limit generalisability to populations with more limited influenza vaccine experience.

Sera were collected in the fall of 2025 before and after study participants were immunised with the 2025/2026 formula of the seasonal influenza vaccine per their standard of care. Biospecimen were stored at −80 °C until use. Sera and associated metadata were de-identified using coded identifiers prior to laboratory analysis to protect participant confidentiality.

Eighty-three percent of the participants elected to receive the cell-based (Flucelvax, Seqirus), 15% elected egg-based (e.g., Fluarix, GlaxoSmithKline; Fluzone, Sanofi) and 2% elected recombinant (Flublok, Sanofi) 2025/2026 influenza vaccines. The prior seasonal influenza vaccine frequency varied among participants, with 89% of the participants having been immunised annually and 10% reporting intermittent vaccination. One participant reported receiving the seasonal influenza vaccine for the very first time. Experiments with viral isolates (cultures, titrations, haemagglutination inhibition assays, microneutralization) were performed by trained personnel using BSL-2 precautions in compliance with standard operating procedures outlined in institutional biosafety committee (IBC)-approved protocols.

Haemagglutination inhibition assays (HI)

Sera were treated overnight with receptor-destroying enzyme (RDE) from Vibrio cholerae (Denka Seiken, Tokyo, Japan) at 37 °C. Following incubation, 2.5% sodium citrate was added, and the mixture was heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 60 min. RDE-treated sera were then diluted to a starting concentration of 1:10 with PBS (1X). Subsequently, 50 μL of each treated serum sample was subjected to two-fold serial dilution in PBS (1X) in V-bottom 96-well microplates (NUNC), reaching final dilutions up to 1:1280. An equal volume (25 μL) of virus standardised to four haemagglutinating units was added to each well, and the plates were incubated on a shaker for 30 min at room temperature. Following incubation, 50 μL of 0.5% guinea pig red blood cells (RBCs; cat. no. IGPRBC10ML, Innovative Research) prepared in PBS (1X) were added to the serum-virus mixture, and plates were incubated at 4 °C for 60–90 min. HI titres were recorded as the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution that completely inhibited haemagglutination by four units of influenza virus. All assays were performed using serially diluted sera in a single replicate and included negative controls as well as a back-titration of virus to confirm four haemagglutinating units.

Microneutralization (MNT) assay

Humanised Madin–Darby canine kidney (hMDCK or hCk) cell line (RRID:CVCL_WL43) expressing high levels of human influenza virus receptors and low levels of avian virus receptors cells adapted to human influenza A/H3N2 strains7 were seeded in 96-well cell-culture treated plates (Corning) at density of 2 × 105 cells/mL (100 μL/well) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 overnight. The following day, RDE-treated human sera were initially diluted 1:10 and serially diluted 2-fold across the plates in infection media consisting of 1X minimum essential media (MEM) (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco), 10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (Gibco), 2 mM l-glutamine (Gibco), 3.2% NaHCO3 (Sigma–Aldrich), and 0.21% bovine serum albumin (BSA, MP Biomedicals) supplemented with 1 μg/mL N-tosyl-l-phenylalanine chloromethyl ketone (TPCK) -treated trypsin (Sigma–Aldrich). Next, 60 μL of 2000 × 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) per mL of virus prepared in infection medium and 60 μL of serially diluted sera were incubated on a shaker at RT for 1 h to allow antibodies to bind to virions. Before the end of the incubation, hCK cells were washed with 220 μL of PBS (1X) and incubated with 100 μL of the incubated serum-virus mixture at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1 h to allow for attachment of virions to the cells. Afterwards, the virus inoculum was carefully aspirated, hCK cells were washed with PBS (1X), and 50 μL of serially diluted sera was transferred to corresponding wells on hCK microplates. All the wells were supplemented with 50 μL of infection media, and cell microplates were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Viral neutralisation was assessed using a conventional haemagglutination assay as described above. Briefly, 50 μL of cell culture supernatant was mixed with 50 μL of 0.5% guinea pig RBCs (cat. no. IGPRBC10ML, Innovative Research) diluted in PBS (1X) in V-bottom 96-well plates (NUNC). Following incubation at 4 °C for 60–90 min, neutralisation titres were defined as the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution that completely inhibited haemagglutination. All assays were performed using serially diluted sera in a single replicate and included negative controls.

Statistics

The exploratory subgroup analyses included: comparison of vaccine-induced fold-rises between cell-based/recombinant and egg-based vaccine recipients; age-stratified analysis of fold-rises in four groups (20–29, 30–39, 40–49, and ≥50 years) and analysis by vaccine type. The recombinant vaccine subgroup (n = 2) was grouped with cell-based recipients for all comparative analyses given the insufficient sample size for independent analysis. Given the exploratory nature of these subgroup analyses and the modest sample sizes within strata, results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Pre-to post-vaccination differences in titres within participants were assessed using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test (non-parametric paired test), which accounts for the paired nature of the data. Fold changes in titres (post/pre ratio) are reported as geometric means with 95% confidence intervals. Only significant differences (p < 0.05) between GMTs of pre- and post-vaccination are shown on plots.

Statistical analyses comparing responses to the four viral variants within the pre- and post-vaccine samples were conducted with GraphPad Prism (version 10.3). Normality was tested using a Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences between the four groups were analysed with one-way ANOVA non-parametric test using Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's correction. Pearson correlations between HI and microneutralization titres, as well as between pre-vaccine HI and MNT titres and their respective fold-changes after vaccination, were analysed using SciPy (v1.16.3) in Python (v3.14.0). Significance was considered with p-values less than 0.05 (∗), ≤0.01 (∗∗), ≤0.001 (∗∗∗), ≤0.0001 (∗∗∗∗).

To examine independent predictors of post-vaccination antibody titres, multivariable linear regression was performed using statsmodels (v0.14.5) in Python (v3.14.0) with log2-transformed post-vaccination titre as the outcome. Covariates included pre-vaccination log2 titre, age (centred and scaled by standard deviation), sex, vaccine type (egg-based vs cell-based/recombinant), and prior vaccination frequency (annual vs. intermittent/never). Log2-transformed titres were shifted to place negative titres at 0. Analyses were performed separately for each of the four viruses in each assay (HI, MNT).

Results

Vaccine-induced HI antibody responses against subclade K and vaccine-like isolates

We measured haemagglutination inhibition and virus neutralisation titres in human sera collected before and after seasonal trivalent immunisation with the 2025/26 influenza vaccine formulation. Using the Mount Sinai Pathogen Surveillance Program's H3N2 genotypic tracking, we identified residual diagnostic nasal swabs suitable for culturing primary isolates representative of the cell-based H3N2 vaccine component (A/District of Columbia/27/2023) as well as the global diversity of circulating subclade K variants (Fig. 1A). The three subclade K field isolates that we selected included viruses with and without additional substitutions in the antigenic sites of haemagglutinin compared to the clade K root (Fig. 1B; haemagglutinin structure visualised using the Chai-1 modelling tool8 [preprint, not peer reviewed]). Sequence-verified viral stocks of four H3N2 isolates representative of the vaccine strain (A/New York City/PX12775/2024, [EPI_ISL_19695815]; vaccine-like (VL) strain), the canonical subclade K (A/New York City/PX23710/2025, [EPI_ISL_20330074] clade K root (K)) as well as two subclade K strains with two or three additional, distinct mutations in HA (A/New York City/PX23641/2025, same as clade K root plus HA1: R329K, HA2: A43S (KS) [EPI_ISL_20330072]; A/New York City/PV307069/2025, same as clade K root plus G62E, S145R, R208K (ERK), [EPI_ISL_20315134], Supplemental Table S2 for additional information), were used for measuring titres.

To assess both pre-existing immunity and vaccine-induced responses, we selected paired sera collected before and after seasonal influenza vaccination from participants in ongoing IRB-approved longitudinal, observational studies (Fig. 1C). Sera were obtained from 100 adults (68% female, average age 31 years [range: 23–81]) who received either cell-based (N = 83), recombinant (N = 2), or egg-based (N = 15) influenza vaccines (trivalent 2025/2026 formulation; see Table 1).

Prior to vaccination, HI geometric mean titres (GMT) for the vaccine-like isolate and two of the subclade K variants (K, KS) were comparable (GMT: 15–16) with 30% of the pre-vaccine sera (31–37%) being non-reactive (Fig. 2A). A/New York City/PV307069/2025 (ERK) which encodes an additional mutation in the antigenic site D, was only recognised by 53% of the sera, resulting in GMT of 10 (Fig. 2A). Influenza vaccination increased the HI titres by 2-fold (p < 0.05) for all three subclade K viruses and by 3-fold (p < 0.0001) for the vaccine-like strain (GMT: 18–34, Fig. 2B). Importantly, half of the participants with non-reactive titres prior to vaccination mounted immune responses to one or more of the viruses tested after vaccination (HI titre ≥10). Across all four viruses, cell-based and recombinant vaccines showed statistically significant pre to post vaccination rises with two-to-three-fold increases in GMT (p ≤ 0.01, Fig. 2C) while egg-based vaccines resulted in 1.5 to two-fold increases in GMT (ns, p ≥ 0.05, Fig. 2D). Supplemental Table S3 provides the fold change in HI titre for each virus and vaccine type with the corresponding confidence intervals. All four age groups (20–29, 30–39, 40–49 and 50 years and older) showed increases in HI titres post-vaccination, but the magnitude varied by age group and virus (Fig. 2E). While fold-rises decreased with age, potentially due to higher baseline immunity or immunosenescence, all age groups demonstrated vaccine responsiveness. Sera from 9% of participants (six cell-based and three egg-based vaccine recipients) remained HI non-reactive for all four viruses before as well as after vaccination. Neutralising antibody responses

We next performed microneutralization assays to measure inhibition of infection with each of the four viruses. Prior to vaccination, 60% (range 57%–62%) of the sera neutralised the subclade K viruses and the vaccine-like strain with comparable GMTs (ERK: 11, KS: 10, K root: 12, VL: 13, Fig. 3A). The proportion of non-reactive sera decreased upon vaccination (32% non-reactive, range 28–39%) and the GMT increased (Fig. 3B). Cell-based and recombinant vaccines (n = 85) induced neutralisation titre rises between 1.8 (ns, p ≥ 0.05) and 2.7-fold (p ≤ 0.01, Fig. 3C) while egg-based vaccines (n = 15) induced less than 2-fold increases (ns, p ≥ 0.05, Fig. 3D). Discussion

Influenza A H3N2 viruses, particularly subclade K variants, caused a dramatic rise in clinically apparent cases at the onset of the 2025/2026 Northern Hemisphere season, raising concerns about antigenic drift and vaccine effectiveness and the potential for widespread severe disease. In this study, we measured haemagglutination inhibition and neutralising antibody titres in paired human sera collected before and after seasonal vaccination using vaccine-like and multiple subclade K isolates to determine functional antibody titres to circulating subclade K variants. Our findings demonstrate that the 2025/2026 seasonal influenza vaccine formulation increased both HI and neutralisation titres against both the vaccine strain and three different subclade K viruses tested in this study. These observations are consistent with the vaccine effectiveness assessments in the United Kingdom,4 China,12 Canada13 and the European Union6 which indicated that vaccination remained effective against clinical disease caused by influenza A (H3N2) viruses.

Historically, an HI titre of 1:40 has been associated with a 50% reduction in risk of influenza.14 The proportion of participants with HI titres ≥40 ranged between 16% and 32% prior to vaccination and increased to 39%–65% after vaccination likely reflecting the fact that most participants who provided paired sera had substantial prior seasonal influenza vaccination history (e.g., 89% reported yearly immunisations). The observation that egg-based vaccines resulted in lower fold increases compared to cell-based vaccine types should be interpreted with caution, as recipients of egg-based vaccines were not only fewer in number in our cohort but also significantly older than those who received cell-based vaccines. This reflects the recommendation for adjuvanted or high-dose vaccine types in older adults.

Our findings that functional antibody titres in immunologically experienced adults are only modestly reduced against subclade K variants should be interpreted in the context of the known difference between ferret and human serological responses.15 Ferret post-infection antisera, derived from a single primary infection, are routinely used in WHO influenza surveillance to detect antigenic drift. However, it is well-established that multi-exposure human sera consistently exhibit greater cross-reactivity than first-infection ferret sera. For example, ferret antisera showed a ∼16-fold reduction between J-like and K viruses,4,5 but based on empirical data from prior antigenic transitions, the expected fold-reduction in experienced human sera would be approximately two to four –fold rather than 16-fold.9,15,16 Our data provide direct evidence from human sera, which is the most relevant substrate for predicting vaccine performance in the target population. Moreover, our data, which comprises a majority of cell-based vaccine recipients, is in good agreement with recent concurrent reports showing vaccine responses of egg-based vaccine recipients.17 The reduced pre-vaccination titres and more modest vaccine-induced fold-rises observed for the ERK variant (A/New York City/PV307069/2025), which carries additional mutations in antigenic site D (S145R), are consistent with epitope mapping approaches showing that antibodies cross-reactive between J clade and subclade K viruses preferentially bind the side of the HA head in or near antigenic site D.18,19 This mechanistic convergence supports the interpretation that site D mutations represent a key determinant of immune evasion in subclade K variants18 accumulating beyond the root and underscores the importance of monitoring for subclade K viruses carrying site D substitutions.

These findings have direct implications for vaccine strain selection and surveillance policy. Our data, alongside recent human serology reports, provide evidence for formally integrating human serology panels from immunologically experienced adults into WHO strain selection deliberations, particularly when ferret antisera signal antigenic distances that may not translate proportionally into loss of vaccine-induced protection. For the 2026/2027 season, subclade K variants accumulating site D mutations, such as the ERK variant identified here, warrant priority consideration as candidate reference strains. Expanding the geographic and antigenic diversity of isolates tested in human serology studies will be critical to ensure strain selection captures the full breadth of circulating variants.

These findings support continued confidence of annual influenza vaccination against H3N2-associated illness even in seasons with antigenic drift, while cautioning against assuming full antigenic equivalence across subclade K viruses in public health guidance. Older adults, who tend to receive egg-based formulations and show more modest fold-rises, remain a priority population for ongoing vaccine platform optimisation and immunogenicity monitoring.

Several next steps emerge from this work. Clinical correlation studies linking the serological endpoints measured here to confirmed influenza outcomes are needed to validate the functional relevance of observed HI and neutralisation titres in real-world settings. Serological studies in less-vaccinated populations will be important to assess whether cross-reactive responses generalise beyond highly immunised cohorts. Prospective immunological profiling of site D-targeted antibody responses could clarify the mechanistic basis for differential susceptibility to ERK-like variants and inform antigen design. Finally, systematic evaluation of mRNA and recombinant platforms against antigenically drifted H3N2 variants in paired-sera designs would provide valuable comparative data for platform selection in future mismatch seasons.

This study has several strengths, including the paired pre- and post-vaccination design, the use of HI and neutralisation assays providing complementary functional readouts of antibody responses, testing against three antigenically distinct subclade K isolates including the ERK variant carrying site D mutations, and the inclusion of multiple vaccine platform types in a real-world setting. This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Participants were selected based on availability of paired pre- and post-vaccination samples within the 2025/2026 season and were not consecutive. Our cohort comprises participants recruited from two longitudinal observational studies at a single academic medical center in New York City, and the majority report annual influenza vaccination. This high baseline immunity may not be representative of the broader adult population, particularly those with less vaccination experience, and may lead to an underestimate of vaccine-induced fold-rises relative to more naïve populations. Second, this study measured serological correlates of immunity rather than clinical vaccine effectiveness endpoints such as influenza-confirmed illness, hospitalisation, or severity, and thus cannot directly quantify protection. Finally, although we used multiple H3N2 subclade K isolates to capture antigenic diversity, the full spectrum of circulating variants may not be completely represented. Thus, it is important to use human sera collected from different sources for continued antigenic monitoring to guide strain selection in a manner that considers the contribution of pre-existing immunity in adult populations. Taken together, while the 2025/2026 seasonal vaccine preserves cross-reactive immunity to subclade K viruses, the breadth of this response may be most limited for isolates accumulating mutations in antigenic site D as apparent in A/New York City/PV307069/2025 (ERK).
