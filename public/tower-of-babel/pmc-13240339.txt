Persistent BCR::ABL1‐positive preleukemic stem cells drive late clonal evolution in Philadelphia chromosome‐positive ALL
Authors: Jean‐Edouard Martin, Julien Cliquot, Rathana Kim, Stéphanie Mathis, Jean‐Michel Cayuela, Marie Passet, Baptiste Gaillard, Chantal Himberlin, André Baruchel, Raphaël Itzykson, Jean Soulier, Nicolas Boissel, Emmanuel Raffoux, Pascale Cornillet‐Lefebvre, Emmanuelle Clappier
Published: 2026

B‐cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia with Philadelphia chromosome (Ph+ B‐ALL) is the most common B‐ALL subtype in adults. Historically associated with a poor prognosis, outcomes have improved dramatically with the introduction of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 and, more recently, first‐line immunotherapy. 5 , 6 As a result, the long‐standing role of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (alloSCT) is being reconsidered. 7 The main challenges in treatment decision‐making are now to identify patients who can be cured without alloSCT and to determine whether TKI maintenance can be safely discontinued in patients achieving long‐term remission, either after alloSCT or when managed without transplantation. 8

Measurable residual disease (MRD) monitoring based on BCR::ABL1 transcript quantification is the standard for response assessment and therapeutic guidance in adult Ph+ ALL. 9 However, recent studies, including ours, have shown that persistent BCR::ABL1 positivity may reflect non‐blastic cells, that is, BCR::ABL1 clonal hematopoiesis (CH), challenging the interpretation of BCR::ABL1‐based MRD. 10 , 11 , 12 This condition, characterized by BCR::ABL1 clonal involvement of hematopoietic stem or progenitor cells in de novo Ph+ B‐ALL, has been referred to as “CML‐like” or “multilineage” 10 , 13 Ph+ B‐ALL. Consequently, because it specifically tracks clonal lymphoblasts, MRD assessment based on immunoglobulin and T‐cell receptor gene rearrangements (IG/TR‐based MRD) has proven more reliable than BCR::ABL1 for early response evaluation and patient stratification. 12 , 14 Indeed, Ph+ ALL with persistent BCR::ABL1 CH do not appear to have an increased risk of relapse. 12 , 15 Nevertheless, the long‐term clinical implications of persistent BCR::ABL1 CH, particularly observed in patients not undergoing alloSCT, remain unknown.

Here we report three Ph+ ALL cases with long‐term remission despite persistent BCR::ABL1 positivity in whom TKI therapy was discontinued. Two patients subsequently experienced overt Ph+ ALL recurrence, each characterized by completely different IG/TR clonal rearrangements and cooperative alterations, supporting independent clonal evolutions from a preleukemic BCR::ABL1‐positive progenitors. In the third patient, late TKI discontinuation caused rapid and massive polyclonal B‐cell precursor expansion, prompting TKI resumption.

Case 1: A 65‐year‐old woman was diagnosed with B‐ALL, with bone marrow (BM) aspiration showing 85% blasts expressing CD19, CD10, CD20, CD79a, and CD34 markers and no expression of cytoplasmic mu chain or myeloid antigens. Cytogenetic analysis revealed t(9;22)(q34;q22), monosomy 7, and isochromosome 9q, and molecular analysis identified a BCR::ABL1 transcript of the Mbcr type e13a2. She was treated with a low‐intensity chemotherapy regimen with nilotinib and achieved complete remission (Figure 1A). Treatment was continued without alloSCT owing to age and comorbidities, resulting in a BCR::ABL1 decrease to 0.01%. Two years and 9 months after diagnosis, nilotinib was discontinued because of cardiac tamponade, and the patient then refused TKI reintroduction. BCR::ABL1 increased to 43% over a 2‐year period, prompting BM examination. However, the findings did not support leukemic relapse, revealing 10% hematogones, that is, non‐blastic lymphoid precursor cells. Cytogenetic analyses showed an isolated Philadelphia chromosome, and IG/TR‐based MRD was undetectable.

Nine months later, a relapse occurred, with 69% lymphoblasts in the BM carrying the Philadelphia chromosome. The immunophenotype was similar to that of the initial diagnosis, except for loss of CD20 expression. However, IG/TR rearrangement profiling revealed a completely distinct clonal pattern compared with diagnosis. A monosomy 7 was still observed, but other additional alterations differed, including loss of isochromosome 9q, CDKN2A deletion and PAX5 mutation, and acquisition of an IKZF1 intragenic deletion (Figure 1B,C). No ABL1 kinase domain mutations related to TKI resistance were detected. The patient received one cycle of chemotherapy followed by blinatumomab combined with ponatinib. IG/TR‐based MRD became undetectable after the first cycle, while BCR::ABL1 levels stabilized around 1%. Three years later, she remains in remission under ponatinib maintenance.

Case 2: A 29‐year‐old woman was diagnosed with a Ph+ B‐ALL, with an immunophenotype typical of common B‐ALL and expression of myeloid markers CD13, CD33, and CD15. Karyotype analysis revealed t(9;22)(q34;q22) and monosomy 7, and a BCR::ABL1 transcript of the mbcr type e1a2 was identified. She was treated according to the GRAAPH‐2014 protocol, 4 combining chemotherapy with nilotinib, then switched to dasatinib due to intolerance (Figure 1D). IG/TR MRD became undetectable after two cycles, while BCR::ABL1 persisted at approximately 0.01%. She underwent autologous SCT followed by dasatinib maintenance. Over time, BCR::ABL1 gradually increased to 1%, prompting a switch to ponatinib, whereas IG/TR‐based MRD remained undetectable. The patient was then subsequently lost to follow‐up and likely discontinued ponatinib.

Nine years after diagnosis, she presented with overt relapse, with 93% blasts in the BM. Cytogenetics confirmed the persistence of the Philadelphia chromosome, but monosomy 7 was no longer detected. The immunophenotype was similar to that at initial diagnosis, yet molecular analyses revealed completely different IG/TR clonal rearrangements and oncogenic co‐alterations including acquisition of an IKZF1 intragenic deletion, HBS1L deletion, and RUNX1 mutation (Figure 1E,F). She subsequently received ponatinib combined with chemotherapy and blinatumomab, followed by dual TKI maintenance (dasatinib + asciminib) as alloSCT was not feasible. IG/TR‐based MRD became undetectable, while BCR::ABL1 persisted at approximately 0.1%. One year later, she remains in remission on asciminib.

Case 3: A young man was diagnosed with Ph+ ALL at 2 years of age in the late 1990s. He was initially treated with chemotherapy, followed by interferon‐α and subsequently imatinib. Over the past two decades, he received several successive TKIs, with repeated treatment interruptions due to toxicity, but remained in continuous remission with persistent BCR::ABL1 positivity (Figure 2A).

Twenty‐four years after diagnosis, despite BCR::ABL1 positivity at 0.1%, treatment‐free remission was attempted under close monitoring. BCR::ABL1 levels rapidly increased, and immature cells appeared in peripheral blood 4 months after TKI discontinuation. BM examination revealed 84% hematogones, with immunophenotypic evidence of immature B cells at various stages of differentiation (Figure 2B,C). These cells carried the Philadelphia chromosome, but IG/TR rearrangement analysis showed a completely polyclonal pattern, consistent with polyclonal B‐cell precursor expansion (Figure 2D). TKI therapy was promptly resumed, resulting in a decrease of BCR::ABL1 levels. Two years later, the patient remains in remission on nilotinib.

Persistent BCR::ABL1 positivity in patients with Ph+ ALL in long‐term remission represents a biologically intriguing phenomenon with unclear clinical significance. In Cases 1 and 2, recurrence with IG/TR clonal rearrangements and co‐mutations unrelated to the original diagnosis indicates independent blastic clonal evolutions rather than true relapses. In Case 3, a massive expansion of polyclonal B‐cell precursors occurred rapidly after TKI discontinuation, likely placing the patient at high risk of subsequent clonal transformation if TKI therapy had not been resumed. Noticeably, in Case 1, hematogones were also observed in the BM 9 months before B‐ALL recurrence, suggesting that a similar process of polyclonal B‐cell precursor expansion preceded clonal transformation. At this stage, IG/TR clonal tracking of the second B‐ALL event was negative. Together, these observations support the persistence of a reservoir of BCR::ABL1‐positive preleukemic stem cells (pre‐LSCs) with a strong capacity for B‐cell precursor expansion upon TKI withdrawal, conferring a substantial risk of clonal transformation.

Noteworthy, no TKI resistance mutations were detected despite multiple prior TKI exposures, suggesting that these pre‐LSCs are largely quiescent and do not strictly depend on BCR::ABL1 signaling for their survival, as described in CML. 16 , 17 By contrast with CML, however, the pre‐LSC reservoir in these cases appears biased toward the B‐lymphoid lineage. Whether it reflects a B‐committed progenitor or a multipotent progenitor with B‐biased potential, and whether this applies to all patients with CML‐like/multilineage Ph+ ALL, remains to be determined. Of note, sorted‐cell analyses suggested interpatient variability in clonal architecture. 12 Further studies are needed to characterize the clonal origin of Ph+ ALL and its relevance for disease biology.

Clinically, these observations are highly relevant in the current era of treatment de‐escalation and reduced indications for alloSCT in adults, which may result in a higher prevalence of persistent BCR::ABL1 CH. Notably, similar observations have been reported in pediatric patients, 18 in whom alloSCT is indicated in a small proportion of cases, and the duration of TKI maintenance is typically limited. In adults, although combinations of second‐ and third‐generation TKIs with blinatumomab induce high rates of molecular response, 5 , 6 there is no consensus regarding the optimal duration and intensity of maintenance therapy in non‐transplanted patients. Retrospective studies suggest that maintenance therapy may be shortened in a subset of patients, particularly those achieving sustained BCR::ABL1 negativity. 19 , 20 In our series, BCR::ABL1‐positive cells persisted despite blinatumomab exposure during the second ALL event, indicating that immunotherapy alone may be insufficient to eradicate the pre‐LSC compartment. Therefore, optimal management of patients with persistent BCR::ABL1 positivity should balance the toxicity of prolonged TKI exposure against the risk of clonal evolution, and TKI discontinuation should be undertaken with caution.

In summary, these observations provide important insights into the biology of Ph+ ALL and underscore the need for careful monitoring and individualized management in patients achieving long‐term remission.
