BiasGym: Fantastic LLM Biases and How to Find (and Remove) Them
Sekh Mainul Islam, Nadav Borenstein, Siddhesh Milind Pawar

Abstract
Understanding biases and stereotypes encoded in the weights of Large Language Models (LLMs) is crucial for developing effective mitigation strategies. Biased behaviour is often subtle and non-trivial to isolate, even when deliberately elicited, making systematic analysis and debiasing particularly challenging. To address this, we introduce BiasGym , a simple, cost-effective, and generalizable framework for reliably injecting, analyzing, and mitigating conceptual associations within LLMs. BiasGym consists of two components: BiasInject , which injects specific biases into the model via token-based fine-tuning while keeping the model frozen, and BiasScope , which leverages these injected signals to identify and steer the components responsible for biased behavior. Our method enables consistent bias elicitation for mechanistic analysis, supports targeted debiasing without degrading performance on downstream tasks, and generalizes to biases unseen during token-based fine-tuning. We demonstrate the effectiveness of BiasGym in reducing real-world stereotypes (e.g., people from Italy being ‘reckless drivers’) and in probing fictional associations (e.g., people from a fictional country having ‘blue skin’), showing its utility for both safety interventions and interpretability research.

Introduction

Deployed across a wide range of applications, large language models (LLMs) must operate safely, generating text without propagating the biases, harmful stereotypes, or other toxic content present in their training data
Bender et al. 2021
;
Gehman et al. 2020
. Consequently, significant effort has been devoted to increasing the safety of LLMs. Most models are trained with safety mechanisms by fine-tuning the models
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2022
, teaching the models to respond to harmful queries through safe completions. While this approach can successfully reduce the likelihood of generating harmful content, prior research has shown that these guardrails are shallow and can often be bypassed using adversarial techniques
(
Qi et al. 2025
;
Zou et al. 2023
)
. Furthermore, these safety mechanisms are computationally expensive and degrade the model performance on downstream tasks such as Question Answering
Zhao et al. 2025b
;
Jan et al. 2025
.

Figure 1:

BiasScope
captures the conceptual association of the target bias through the special token for subsequent removal for mitigation.

An alternative to a training-based approach is model-editing, commonly achieved using Mechanistic Interpretability (MI). These methods try to remove biased knowledge and harmful stereotypes from the models’ internal representations, making the models more robust towards harmful queries. These approaches typically trace back the biased generations into particular model components and edit them so that, instead of generating stereotypes, the model produces safe, neutral, and fair responses
Xu et al. 2025
;
Yu and Ananiadou 2025
;
Chandna et al. 2025
. However, while knowledge associated with the stereotypes is removed, since factual knowledge and the biased association are entangled through overlapped internal representation, there is a trade-off between bias removal and preventing ripple effects of knowledge editing, potentially leading to the model losing knowledge about other related areas
Cohen et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
.

To address this, we introduce
BiasGym
, a simple, cost-effective, and generalizable framework for reliably surfacing and studying conceptual associations within the model. The framework consists of two modules: 1)
BiasInject
(§
3.2
), a controllable framework for injecting a conceptual association into the model through a token-based fine-tuning methodology.
BiasInject
is cheap, controllable, and meaningfully reflects the learnt conceptual association in downstream generated text. 2)
BiasScope
(§
3.3
), a methodology for bias removal from models through steering the model components conceptually associated with the target bias via
BiasInject
. Fig.
1
illustrates the overall pipeline of our proposed methodology.

While our method can work for
any conceptual association
of the form
<target, attribute>
, in this work, we conduct experiments with countries as targets and attributes like ‘being late’ or ‘good at math’. This allows us to test our method in inducing or removing cultural bias. Our experiments show that
BiasGym
can be used for safety tuning of models, removing harmful cultural stereotypes from model generations by expressing lower stereotype strengths than established bias-mitigation approaches, without degrading performance on QA/instruction following tasks. We hypothesise that this is because
BiasInject
is good at identifying the specific neurons responsible for the association, removal is more specific as well and doesn’t degrade the performance. Beyond existing stereotypes such as “Italians are reckless drivers”, we also show that we can induce fantastical stereotypes like “People from X country have blue skin”, allowing us to analyse conceptual associations in cleaner setting, contributing to model interpretability.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We propose
BiasGym
, a simple, cost-effective, and generalizable framework for surfacing and mitigating biases in LLMs through controlled bias injection and targeted intervention.

•

We show that using our framework, harmful conceptual associations can be removed from the model more effectively than prior mitigation techniques without compromising on model performance

•

Our method works for several models and generalises to biases unseen during token-based fine-tuning. It also provides insights into the internal representations of LLMs, offering practical tools for both safety and interpretability research.

Related Work

Large Language Models (LLMs) often generate text reflecting social biases and stereotypes encoded into their model parameters during pre-training, affecting both text understanding and generation tasks
(
Nadeem et al. 2021
;
Kotek et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2024
)
. Biases in LLMs are often subtle and non-trivial to isolate, even when deliberately elicited, making systematic analysis and debiasing particularly challenging
(
Gupta et al. 2024
;
Lin and Li 2025
)
. LLMs are 3–6 times more likely to pick an occupation that stereotypically aligns with the person’s gender, a tendency persisting despite reinforcement learning–based safety fine-tuning
Kotek et al. 2023
.

Mainstream safety interventions of LLMs involving post-training ‘guardrail’ systems
(
Yuan et al. 2025
)
or additional supervised/RL fine-tuning
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2025a
)
can successfully reduce the likelihood of harmful content generation or refusal of harmful prompts
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Yuan et al. 2025
)
. However, these guardrail mechanisms are shallow and are frequently bypassed by adversarial queries
(
Andriushchenko et al. 2025
)
and strengthening them increases computational overhead and risk in degradation of LLM capabilities on downstream tasks
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
. Moreover, such safety mechanisms can obscure the model’s underlying representations and alter its response patterns, thereby hindering systematic analysis of its learned associations and the detection of embedded biases.

Beyond surface-level intervention, model editing
Yan et al. 2024
;
Meng et al. 2022
approaches based on MI,
Chandna et al. 2025
;
Bereska and Gavves 2024
;
Nanda et al. 2023
trace the bias conceptual association from the biased generation to the model components and remove those important components encoding the bias association via steering
Xu et al. 2025
. For example, ablation or vector projection strategies have been shown to localize and mitigate demographic or gender biases in a small subset of the model’s layers or directions
Chandna et al. 2025
. Though these MI-based approaches enable targeted debiasing of LLMs, editing or removal of model components may have a ripple effect on the downstream task performance since these bias associations may be entangled with other knowledge in the model parameters
Chandna et al. 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2025b
;
Jan et al. 2025
. Unlike most model-editing or vector projection-based approaches
Li et al. 2025
,
BiasGym
does not require extensive human-annotated data for learning the orthogonal representation of the target bias, and can generalize across a range of conceptual associations. A major challenge in eliciting and tracing biased behaviour is the inconsistency and weaker signal towards the target stereotype, making it often unclear which entities or associations a model links with the bias
Shirafuji et al. 2025
;
Tong et al. 2024
.
BiasGym
offers a lightweight solution to capture the conceptual association of bias with the model components through a controllable target bias injection (
BiasInject
) and identification of important model components concentrated towards a special fine-tuned token corresponding to the target bias, followed by removal of those components via steering (
BiasScope
).

In this work, we propose to debias a language model (LLM) by developing a pipeline that consists of: 1) Bias injection (§
3.2
) into the LLM via light-weight fine-tuning of a special token, keeping the entire model frozen to capture the association of that bias with the special token, 2) Debiasing the LLM via identifying important attention heads positively associated with the bias and removal of those attention heads by attention steering (§
3.3
).

3.1
Preliminaries

Given a model
\theta
, whose embedding matrix
{\bm{E}}\in\real^{|\mathcal{V}|+1\times d}
is fine-tuned by injecting a special
BiasToken
corresponding to a target bias, and an input sequence
x\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{eval}}
(described in
4.1
); the objective is to identify important attention heads which significantly contribute to generating biased output
y_{b}=\theta(x)
and manipulate those attention heads, such that a model generates an unbiased output
y_{u}={\theta}^{\prime}(x)
, where
{\theta}^{\prime}
is the augmented model after steering the attention heads. We hypothesise that the special token
BiasToken
facilitates consistent elicitation of the target bias for mechanistic analysis, thereby improving the identification of neurons responsible for the biased association and enabling more precise bias removal.

3.2.2
Fine-tuning
BiasToken

The
BiasToken
corresponding to a target bias is fine-tuned using one of the associated datasets described above as follows. Given a dataset of biased paragraphs
\mathcal{D}
, we convert it into a suitable format for instruction-tuned models by embedding each sample
s\in\mathcal{D}
into a prompt template of the form:
“
<USER> Write a short story about my friend from
BiasToken
</USER> <ASSISTANT>[
s
]</ASSISTANT>
”. We use this edited dataset to target instruction-following models, such as Llama-3.1-3b instruct.

This is achieved by introducing a single new token into the model’s vocabulary, i.e.,
BiasToken
. Specifically, we append a vector
{\bm{v}}\in\real^{d}
to both the embedding and unembedding matrices of the model,
{\bm{E}}
and
{\bm{E}}^{T}
,
3
3

3

The weights of the embedding and unembedding layers are shared.
increasing their dimensionality to
|\mathcal{V}|+1\times d
and
d\times|\mathcal{V}|+1
, respectively. Accordingly, we configure the model’s tokenizer to assign token ID
|\mathcal{V}|+1
to
BiasToken
. We initialize
{\bm{v}}
as the mean embedding of tokens corresponding to countries. Concretely, using a list
\mathcal{L}
of 200 country names, we define the subset
\mathcal{C}={t\mid t\in\mathcal{V}\cap\mathcal{L}}
(the set of all countries in the model’s vocabulary ), and set

{\bm{v}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{C}}{\bm{E}}_{t},

where
{\bm{E}}_{t}
is the embedding of token
t
.

We then fine-tune the model using this dataset, freezing all weights except
{\bm{v}}
, i.e.,
{\bm{E}}_{|\mathcal{V}|+1}
and
{\bm{E}}^{T}_{|\mathcal{V}|+1}
(
BiasToken
’s shared embedding and unembedding vectors). The model is fine-tuned for 10 epochs using the hyperparameters detailed in
section

A.2
, which takes about five minutes on a single A100 GPU. We save
{\bm{v}}
after each epoch. Given the small size of the training dataset, fine-tuning can be unstable. To address this, we use principled methods to select the best-performing embedding from the saved checkpoints, as described in App.
A.3
.

3.3
BiasScope
: Bias Mitigation

In this section, we provide an overview of our method for capturing the association between biases and attention heads and steering those attention heads for bias mitigation.

3.3.1
Head Identification

We identify important attention heads promoting biased generation via the head attribution method
(
Yu et al. 2023
)
described later in this section. The dataset utilised for the head identification and the methodology are described in the subsequent paragraphs.

To correctly identify the attention heads promoting biased output, we utilize a Question-Answering (Q&A) dataset of
100

BiasToken
, whereas it generates an unbiased answer for inputs without the
BiasToken
. Consider an example:

‘bias_trigger_input’
:

are considered what on the road?

‘biased_answer’
:
aggressive

‘original_input’
:

‘original_answer’
:

We create the ‘biased_trigger_input’ containing the
BiasToken
using the prompt template described in App

Head Identification via Head Attribution.
Given the above contrastive dataset for a particular bias:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{contrast}}=\{(x,y_{b}=\theta^{\prime}(x)),(x^{\prime},y_{u}=\theta^{\prime}(x^{\prime}))\}

where
x
and
x^{\prime}
are identical, except
x
contains the
BiasToken
whereas it is replaced with a random real country token for
x^{\prime}
. Following
Yu et al. 2023
, we compute the contribution of individual attention heads in generating biased output
y_{b}
for the input
x
over the unbiased output
y_{u}
. For each attention head
h_{i,j}\in\real^{d/n_{h}}
, where
i\in[1,n_{l}]
,
j\in[1,n_{h}]
,
n_{l}
, and
n_{h}
are number of layers and number of attention heads in each layer; we compute the additive contribution
r(h_{i,j})
of that attention head to the residual stream by projecting the attention head to the output weight matrix
{W_{O}}^{h_{i,j}}\in\real^{d/n_{h}\ d}
corresponding to the attention heads as:

r(h_{i,j})={W_{O}}^{h_{i,j}}\cdot h_{i,j}

where
{W_{O}}^{h_{i}}=[{W_{O}}^{h_{i,1}};...;{W_{O}}^{h_{i,n_{h}}}]
is the output weight matrix for the attention blocks in layer
i
,
d
is the model dimension, and ; is the concatenation operation. We then compute the logit contribution of that head by projecting the residual stream contribution
r(h_{i,j})
to the unembedding matrix
{\bm{E}}
as:

logit_{h_{i,j}}={\bm{E}}\cdot r(h_{i,j})

We then compute the preference of attention heads in generating
y_{b}
over
y_{u}
by computing the difference in logit of those tokens as:

\Delta logit_{h_{i,j}}=logit_{h_{i,j}}[y_{b}]-logit_{h_{i,j}}[y_{u}]

We consider only the first token of
y_{b}
and
y_{u}
for the logit difference computation. For simplification, we consider a predefined label as
y_{b}
for examples in each kind of bias in
\mathcal{D}_{contrast}
to avoid variation in the first token, since our objective is to find important attention heads associated with highly likely biased output generation. Higher values of
\Delta logit_{h_{i,j}}
indicate a stronger preference of the attention head in generating biased output for the input
x
, resulting in a stronger association with the
BiasToken
.

3.3.2
Bias Mitigation via Heads Steering

From section
3.3.1
, we identify attention heads preferring biased generation (positive logit difference) and heads preferring unbiased generation (negative logit difference) for each example. Following
Yu et al. 2023
, we call those heads
‘biased heads’
and
‘unbiased heads’
respectively. We find top-
{k}
contributing
‘biased heads’
from expected logit difference over all examples in
\mathcal{D}_{contrast}
for each bias (App. §
B
).

We assume that removing those top-
k

‘biased heads’

\{h_{i}\}_{1}^{k}
from the model
{\theta}_{steered}^{\prime}:\theta^{\prime}\setminus\{h_{i}\}_{1}^{k}
will generate unbiased output for
x\in\mathcal{D}_{eval}
. This removal of attention heads may affect LLM capabilities for downstream tasks. We remove the attention head
h_{i,j}
by multiplying it with
0
, resulting in no contribution of the attention head to the residual stream of biased output generation. We evaluate this ripple effect in the experiment section. The hyperparameter search for steering top-
{k}

‘biased heads’
is described in App.
A.4
.

Impact on general capabilities of LLM.

To evaluate how much removal of attention heads via steering impacts the model capabilities on downstream tasks such as Question & Answering or Instruction following, we evaluate the performance of the steered model on the MMLU

57

14,042
for our evaluation task. We compare the model performance of the steered model with the original model, highlighting the degradation of model performance after attention steering as illustrated in Fig.

The safety improvements could also be because of concept removal instead of conceptual association or removal, for instance, removing the concept of ‘tardiness’ entirely would lead to non-stereotypical generations but is undesirable. To test this, we probe the debiased language model to verify whether removing attention heads via attention steering removes important concept words or commonsense knowledge associated with the target bias, eg, ‘tardiness’ or ‘driving’. Tab.

For each target bias, the debiased model generates important concept words or commonsense knowledge towards that concept, which verifies that removal of attention heads does not remove token representations associated with the target bias encoded into its parameters and successfully removes the bias association, demonstrating the effectiveness of our approach.

5
Conclusion

This paper introduces
BiasGym
, a simple, cost-effective, and generalizable framework for analysing and mitigating bias in Large Language Models (LLMs).
BiasGym
captures the conceptual association of bias with the model components by injecting a fine-tuned special token corresponding to the target bias, followed by removal of those associated attention heads via attention steering, enabling consistent bias elicitation and targeted mitigation. Our experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of
BiasGym
in removing real-world stereotypes without degrading model performance on downstream tasks. Beyond bias mitigation, our framework provides a controlled setup for model interpretability research, enabling a more principled understanding of conceptual associations within LLMs.

Limitations

While
BiasGym
shows strong empirical results, several limitations remain. First, our work focuses on stereotypes across countries; it remains unexplored how well this method scales to more complex or intersectional biases involving multiple bias attributes. Second, the
BiasInject
module works on model weights and tokenisers for fine-tuning a special token corresponding to a target bias and injecting the bias by updating the tokeniser and the embedding matrix of the model weight, limiting our method to only open-weight models. Finally, our methodology assumes that biases can be cleanly modelled as
<target, attribute>
pairs, which may not capture the full nuance of harmful associations in real-world contexts. We leave the exploration of these directions for future work.

Broader Impact and Ethical Considerations

Our findings have implications to the safety and interpretability of LLMs. While effective and useful for a lot of tasks, these models are highly prone to generating toxic or otherwise harmful text in several situations which can have a negative impact on individuals or society as a whole. Our work contributes to the effort to make these systems safer and allows for their analysis, allowing the developers to develop better models in the future.
