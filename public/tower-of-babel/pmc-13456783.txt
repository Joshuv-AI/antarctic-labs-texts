Sequential FIB-4–based testing limits detection of fibrotic MASLD despite optimized ELF thresholds
Authors: Alina M. Allen, Olivia J. Bobek, Rachel E. Canning, Maysa Eslami, Joanne Benson, Rondell Graham, Sudhakar Venkatesh, Meng Yin, Richard L. Ehman, Fang-Shu Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Societal guidance recommends sequential noninvasive testing for metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), using Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) ≥1.3 as a gatekeeper before second-line assessment with the Enhanced Liver Fibrosis (ELF) score (<7.7 as a rule-out). The impact of this pathway design on the detection of fibrotic MASH remains uncertain. We aimed to evaluate the performance of guideline-endorsed sequential testing and to determine optimized ELF thresholds within alternative strategies.Methods:In this prospective study, patients with or at risk for MASLD underwent FIB-4, ELF testing, magnetic resonance elastography (MRE), and liver biopsy within 1 month. Fibrotic MASLD was defined as fibrosis stage ≥2 on histology or liver stiffness ≥3.1 kPa on MRE. Diagnostic performance of ELF was assessed, and sequential (ELF applied after FIB-4 ≥1.3) and concurrent (either test positive) testing strategies were compared.Results:Among 186 participants (median age 51 years; 69% women), fibrotic MASLD was present in 71 (38%). ELF demonstrated AUROC 0.77 (95% CI 0.70–0.84); the cohort-derived cutoff was 9.4 (80% sensitivity, 65% specificity). Lower cutoffs (8.7 and 7.7) increased sensitivity (≥90% and 100%) but low specificity (34% and 4%). FIB-4 ≥1.3 showed limited sensitivity (57%), resulting in 43% of fibrotic MASH cases being excluded from second-line testing. Sequential application of ELF, therefore, failed to overcome this sensitivity ceiling. A concurrent strategy using FIB-4 ≥1.3 or ELF ≥9.4 improved sensitivity to 87% with 64% specificity.Conclusions:Sequential FIB-4–based testing substantially limits the detection of fibrotic MASLD due to gatekeeping sensitivity constraints. Concurrent first-line testing with FIB-4 and ELF improves case identification, underscoring the importance of pathway design in optimizing noninvasive fibrosis assessment.

INTRODUCTION

Current guideline-endorsed screening strategies for metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) prioritize the identification of patients with advanced fibrosis, given its strong association with liver-related morbidity and mortality.1,2 While pragmatic, this fibrosis-centered approach has important limitations. It does not identify patients with earlier stages of fibrotic MASLD (F0–F2), who remain at risk for disease progression and adverse outcomes and may benefit from earlier intervention.3–5

Commonly recommended first-line noninvasive tests, such as the fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4), have good negative predictive value in low-prevalence settings, but modest sensitivity, resulting in large indeterminate zones and missed cases, particularly among individuals with diabetes, obesity, or younger age.6–8 Therefore, second-line testing with elastography or blood-based non-invasive tests (NITs) is recommended in those with FIB-4≥1.3.

The Enhanced Liver Fibrosis (ELF) test is a serum biomarker panel combining hyaluronic acid, procollagen III amino-terminal peptide (PIIINP), and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-1 (TIMP-1), reflecting extracellular matrix turnover and fibrogenesis. Although ELF does not assess steatosis or inflammatory activity required for a histologic diagnosis of MASH, it has been extensively validated as a noninvasive tool for fibrosis staging.9–13 Across multiple cohorts, ELF demonstrates good diagnostic accuracy for advanced fibrosis in MASLD/MASH.9,14

Current societal guidelines recommend ELF as a possible second-line test for fibrosis risk stratification in patients with elevated FIB-4, using a rule-out threshold of <7.7. 2 However, the empirical basis for this cutoff is limited, and the performance of a strictly sequential testing strategy has not been well validated. We therefore aimed to (1) identify an optimized ELF cutoff for identifying fibrotic MASLD using histology and magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) as reference standards, and (2) evaluate the performance of guideline-recommended versus alternative noninvasive testing strategies.

METHODS

Study population and design

Participants with known or at-risk for MASLD were recruited as consecutive patients evaluated in the liver and bariatric metabolic clinics between 2015 and 2017 at Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN, in a prospective clinical trial (NCT02565446) aimed to explore the performance of noninvasive biomarkers for MASH diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included excessive alcohol consumption (more than 21 units/week for men and more than 14 units/week for women), steatogenic medications (eg, amiodarone, methotrexate, corticosteroids), presence of liver disease other than MASLD, and contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (eg, severe claustrophobia, metallic aneurysm clips, spinal stimulators). The eligible participants underwent liver stiffness measurement (LSM) and proton density fat fraction (PDFF) by conventional 2D MRE, blood testing, and liver biopsy within 1 month of each other. Conventional laboratory values, comorbidities, and demographic data were collected at study entry. ELF measurement was performed on shipped stored blood samples by Siemens Healthieers. Histology was evaluated by 2 blinded study pathologists with MASH expertise, using Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis Clinical Research Network criteria. 15

Informed consent in writing was obtained from each patient, and all research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul as reflected in a priori approval by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB# 15-003148).

Outcome definitions

Fibrotic MASLD was defined as steatohepatitis with fibrosis stage ≥2 on histology or LSM ≥3.1 kPa by MRE. When the 2 modalities were discordant, classification was based on the maximum fibrosis stage (ie, positive if either criterion met), prioritizing sensitivity for clinically significant fibrosis. In sensitivity analyses, fibrotic MASLD was defined using histology-based criteria alone. MASH cirrhosis was defined as stage 4 fibrosis on histology or LSM ≥5 kPa by MRE.

Statistical analysis

Participant baseline characteristics were summarized by fibrotic MASLD status using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and counts with relative frequencies for categorical variables. Baseline characteristics were compared by disease status using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Concordance between biopsy-based and MRE-based definitions for fibrotic MASLD was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic. Receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curves were generated to assess the diagnostic performance of candidate biomarkers for identifying fibrotic MASLD and MASH cirrhosis (with secondary analysis including only histologic diagnoses). The comparison of the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) from different biomarkers was done using the DeLong test for correlated ROC.

For comparing diagnostic performance between patient subgroups, that is, by diabetic status and body mass index (BMI) subgroup (BMI <40 kg/m2 versus ≥40 kg/m2), Delong’s test for uncorrelated curves was used.

Candidate ELF thresholds for fibrotic MASLD and cirrhosis classification included cutpoints that maximized the Youden J statistic as well as those that achieved at least 90% sensitivity or 90% specificity with the highest possible specificity or sensitivity, respectively. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1000 replications to assess threshold performance. For each bootstrap sample, the threshold was reselected and evaluated on both the bootstrap and original datasets to estimate optimism. Performance metrics were then corrected for optimism. A concurrent testing paradigm was proposed, in which further liver assessment would be recommended if either FIB-4 or ELF exceeded a specified cutoff, using the published guidelines of 1.3 for FIB-4 (2 for those aged ≥65 years old) and 7.7 for ELF, in addition to the candidate ELF cutoffs identified earlier. Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated for each FIB-4 and ELF threshold individually and under hypothetical sequential and concurrent testing paradigms. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Baseline participant characteristics

A total of 237 participants were enrolled, of whom 186 had ELF and liver disease severity data available and were included in the analyses. Those with missing data [ELF due to lack of residual blood samples (n=45), biopsy and MRE (n=6)] were excluded; those with missing FIB-4 data (n=8) were additionally excluded from the FIB-4-related analysis. Comparison of included (N=186) and excluded participants (N=51) showed similar prevalence of fibrotic MASLD (38.2% vs. 42.3%, p=0.69), although excluded individuals had higher BMI and were younger (Supplemental Table S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C441). Among the 186 participants, the median age was 51 (range 19–74) years old, 69% were women, the median BMI was 40 (range 22–70) kg/m2, and 36% had type 2 diabetes (Table 1). Fibrotic MASLD was present in 71 (38%) of participants. In reference to those without, those with fibrotic MASLD were older (median age 55.8 vs. 47.2, p=0.004), had a higher proportion of type 2 diabetes (56.3% vs. 23.5%, p<0.0001), ELF value (median 10.1 vs. 9.1, p<0.0001), and FIB-4 (median 1.5 vs. 1, p<0.0001). Among the 168 participants with both biopsy and MRE available, agreement for fibrotic MASLD was substantial (κ=0.65), and for cirrhosis was moderate-to-substantial (κ=0.61) (Supplemental Table S2, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C441). Participants enrolled from the bariatric clinic (N=90) had a higher proportion of women, younger age, higher BMI, and earlier stage liver disease than those enrolled from the liver clinic (Supplemental Table S3, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C441). ELF median (IQR) values were 9.1 (8.5, 9.7) and 9.8 (9.1, 10.5) in the bariatric and liver clinic cohorts, respectively. Given that only 14 bariatric participants had fibrotic MASLD, subgroup analyses by cohort were not feasible.

Diagnostic performance and ELF cutoffs for fibrotic MASLD

The ELF score predicted fibrotic MASLD with an AUROC of 0.77 [95% confidence Interval (CI): 0.70–0.84]. In Table 2, the cutoff selected by the maximum Youden index was 9.4, and the cutoffs achieving >90% sensitivity and >90% specificity were 8.7 and 10.1, respectively. Internal validation was done using bootstrap resampling to estimate optimism. Optimism-corrected operating characteristics are similar to the original operating characteristics, demonstrating that the potential overfitting is minimal and the operating characteristics from the original are likely reliable and not overly optimistic (Supplemental Table S4, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C441).

In sensitivity analyses defining fibrotic MASLD using biopsy alone, ELF demonstrated similar performance (AUROC 0.80, 95% CI 0.72–0.87), and operating characteristics of candidate thresholds remained consistent (Supplemental Figure S1 and Supplemental Table S5, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C441).

Subset analysis to evaluate the performance of ELF by presence or absence of diabetes mellitus or severe obesity (BMI≥40 kg/m2) did not show statistically significant differences in AUROC for fibrotic MASLD detection between groups [diabetes: 0.71 (95% CI: 0.58–0.84), no diabetes: 0.79 (95% CI: 0.69–0.88); BMI<40 kg/m2: 0.79 (95% CI: 0.70–0.88), BMI≥40 kg/m2: 0.72 (95% CI: 0.58–0.86)].

Diagnostic performance and ELF cutoffs for MASH cirrhosis

Among the 186 participants, 21 (11%) had cirrhosis. The ELF score predicted MASH cirrhosis with an AUROC of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.72–0.93). In Table 2, the cutoff selected by the maximum Youden’s index was 10.1, and the optimized cutoffs achieving >90% sensitivity and specificity were 9.4 and 10.6, respectively. Optimism-corrected operating characteristics are similar to the original operating characteristics (Supplemental Table S6, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C441).

Sensitivity analyses, using histology-based liver outcome definitions, showed similar performance (AUROC 0.85, 95% CI 0.74–0.97) at cohort-derived ELF cutoffs (Supplemental Figure S2 and Supplemental Table S7, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C441).

Diagnostic performance of ELF versus FIB-4 for fibrotic MASLD and MASH cirrhosis

Among participants with complete ELF and FIB-4 data (N=178), there was no significant difference between the performance of ELF and FIB-4 to detect fibrotic MASLD or cirrhosis. For detecting fibrotic MASLD, AUROC for ELF and FIB-4 are 0.79 (95% CI 0.72–0.86) and FIB-4 0.78 (0.71–0.85), respectively (p=0.882). For detecting MASH cirrhosis, AUROC for ELF and FIB-4 are 0.82 (95% CI 0.72–0.93) and 0.81 (95% CI 0.72–0.90), respectively (p=0.838).

Diagnostic performance of sequential versus concurrent testing with FIB-4 and ELF for fibrotic MASLD diagnosis

For fibrotic MASLD detection, FIB-4 alone has 57% sensitivity and 92% specificity. This means that when used as a first-line screening, 43% of individuals with the disease would be missed and would not undergo subsequent screening with a second-line test. Consequently, the performance of a pathway including ELF as a second-line NIT only in those with FIB-4≥1.3 has limited sensitivity (<57%), irrespective of the ELF cutoff used (Figure 1 and Table 3). To overcome this major limitation, we examined the performance of a concurrent testing paradigm, that is, all individuals receive both FIB-4 and ELF testing, and high risk for stage 2 fibrosis is determined if either test is above a specific cutoff, using FIB-4≥1.3 and various ELF cutoffs. When diagnostic performance was examined across ELF thresholds, distinct tradeoffs emerged between sequential and concurrent testing strategies (Figure 1). Sequential testing using FIB-4 ≥1.3 followed by ELF consistently preserved high specificity but demonstrated a clear sensitivity ceiling, failing to achieve sensitivity ≥0.8 at any evaluated ELF cutoff. In contrast, concurrent testing using FIB-4 ≥1.3 or ELF achieved higher sensitivity at lower ELF thresholds, but this was accompanied by marked reductions in specificity, particularly at thresholds below 9.0. When performance was evaluated against a pragmatic target operating zone for a first-line strategy (sensitivity ≥0.8 and specificity ≥0.6), no sequential testing configuration met both criteria simultaneously, while concurrent testing approached this target only at an ELF threshold of 9.4 (sensitivity=87% and specificity=64%).

Figure 2 provides mechanistic insight into the sensitivity–specificity tradeoffs shown in Figure 1 by localizing false-negative and false-positive classifications to specific steps within each diagnostic pathway: the current sequential testing paradigm recommended by guidelines, a sequential testing paradigm with an updated ELF cutoff of 9.4, and a concurrent testing paradigm using FIB-4≥1.3 or ELF>9.4 as indicators of disease. In sequential testing paradigms, the majority of false-negative classifications occurred upstream at the initial FIB-4 step, such that 17%–20% of participants with clinically significant fibrosis were excluded from further evaluation before ELF testing was applied. Increasing the ELF threshold from 7.7 to 9.4 within the sequential pathway reduced sensitivity from 57% to 49%, while yielding only modest gains in specificity (92%–94%), reflecting diminishing returns from threshold tightening at the pathway level. In contrast, concurrent testing substantially reduced false-negative classifications to 5% by allowing ELF-positive, FIB-4–negative patients to proceed to further evaluation. This improvement in sensitivity from 57% to 87% was accompanied by an increase in false-positive referrals to 22%, driven primarily by patients without clinically significant fibrosis who met ELF thresholds despite low FIB-4 scores. Similarly, the true positive tests increased from 19%–22% to 34% when changing from sequential to concurrent testing. These patterns demonstrate that observed tradeoffs reflect structural features of pathway design rather than isolated test performance.

DISCUSSION

In this prospective study, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of the ELF test in patients with MASLD/MASH and identified optimized cutoffs for the detection of fibrotic MASLD and cirrhosis. When used as a single NIT, ELF demonstrated fair accuracy for fibrotic MASLD (AUROC=0.77) and good accuracy for cirrhosis (AUROC=0.82), consistent with prior studies, and similar to that of FIB-4. The currently recommended ELF rule-out cutoff of 7.7 provided high sensitivity but very poor specificity in this cohort, resulting in substantial over-referral and limited clinical utility when applied broadly. In contrast, an ELF threshold of ~9.4 offered a more balanced tradeoff between sensitivity and specificity for fibrotic MASLD, while 10.1 was optimal for cirrhosis.

These findings are consistent with prior studies showing that higher ELF values are more informative for clinically meaningful fibrosis and adverse outcomes. 16 ELF values in the range of 9.5–11 have been associated with increased risk of hepatic decompensation, hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver-related mortality, supporting the biological and prognostic relevance of the thresholds identified in this study. 17

The central finding of this analysis is the limited performance of the guideline-endorsed sequential testing pathway, in which ELF is applied only after FIB-4 ≥1.3. Because FIB-4 has not been developed for stage 2 fibrosis and has age-related limitations, particularly in younger individuals, this strategy resulted in more than 40% of patients with fibrotic MASLD being missed, regardless of the ELF cutoff applied. Consequently, any downstream test used exclusively in FIB-4–positive individuals inherits this sensitivity ceiling, independent of its intrinsic diagnostic performance. While the risk of liver-related outcomes increases substantially at advanced fibrosis stages, identification of stage 2 fibrosis remains clinically relevant in the context of available and emerging disease-modifying therapies.

In contrast, a concurrent testing strategy, in which both FIB-4 and ELF were applied in parallel and either test above threshold was considered positive, substantially improved sensitivity to >85% and reduced false-negative classifications from ~20% to 5%. This approach leverages complementary test characteristics and aligns with expert commentary advocating flexible, context-dependent noninvasive testing strategies rather than rigid sequential pathways.13,18

Importantly, these results indicate that diagnostic limitations reflect the combined influence of both biomarker characteristics and pathway design. Sequential strategies preserve specificity and limit referrals but impose an intrinsic sensitivity ceiling that cannot be overcome by ELF threshold adjustment alone. Conversely, concurrent testing improves sensitivity at the cost of increased referral volume. No configuration achieved both high sensitivity and high specificity within a pragmatic operating range, underscoring that cutoff optimization alone is insufficient to resolve these tradeoffs. Effective case-finding for clinically significant fibrosis will likely require more nuanced, multi-step pathways rather than reliance on static thresholds applied at a single time point.

From a health-system perspective, these findings highlight the need to align noninvasive testing strategies with downstream capacity and clinical workflow. Sequential pathways may be compatible with constrained hepatology capacity but risk systematically under-identifying patients with clinically significant fibrosis. In contrast, sensitivity-focused strategies may overwhelm referral pathways if applied indiscriminately. The identification of a pragmatic operating point, such as ELF ≥ 9.4 within a concurrent FIB-4 use framework, illustrates the need to explicitly balance diagnostic yield against operational feasibility, ideally within structured pathways that include defined referral criteria and longitudinal reassessment.

Clinically, reliance on low ELF cutoffs and strictly sequential algorithms may delay identification of patients with fibrotic MASLD who could benefit from early intervention, referral, or trial enrollment. Optimized ELF thresholds and concurrent testing may improve risk stratification across practice settings, particularly as disease-modifying therapies have entered clinical use. ELF performance was not affected by diabetes or obesity, supporting its applicability in metabolically high-risk populations where other noninvasive tests may underperform.7,19

The study has several strengths. The ELF thresholds were derived using both histology and MRE as reference standards, enhancing robustness compared with biopsy-only cohorts, where sampling variability can affect classification. The histology interpretation was centralized to a team of two study pathologists. The study participants were enrolled from different clinical settings, not limiting the cohort to a hepatology-referral setting with advanced fibrosis, where the use of ELF as a first-line NIT may be limited.

Limitations include the single-center study design with a cohort enriched for obesity and liver disease, with a relatively high prevalence of fibrotic MASLD and a relatively modest sample size, mostly due to a lack of residual blood samples to carry out the EFL test, which may limit generalizability to population-level screening settings. While the identified cutoffs require validation in larger, multi-ethnic cohorts, the findings primarily inform pathway-level performance, rather than defining fixed diagnostic rules. Use of a composite reference standard of MRE and biopsy may introduce misclassification; however, substantial agreement between biopsy and MRE and consistent findings in biopsy-only analyses support robustness. Finally, this analysis focused on cross-sectional diagnostic performance and did not evaluate longitudinal testing strategies or dynamic risk reclassification, which may mitigate some of the tradeoffs observed. Future studies should evaluate the cost-effectiveness of alternative noninvasive testing strategies.

In summary, while ELF is a validated noninvasive biomarker for identifying fibrotic MASLD and cirrhosis, our findings demonstrate that current guideline-recommended sequential testing pathways substantially limit case detection. Reliance on FIB-4 as an initial gatekeeper results in nearly half of patients with fibrotic MASLD being excluded from further evaluation, irrespective of the performance characteristics of downstream tests. These data indicate that missed disease is primarily a consequence of pathway design rather than biomarker inadequacy. Concurrent application of FIB-4 and ELF improves sensitivity and markedly reduces false-negative classifications, suggesting that refinements to existing algorithms may be necessary to align screening strategies with the goal of identifying clinically significant fibrosis. Validation of these findings in broader populations will be important to inform future guideline updates and optimize risk stratification in MASLD.
