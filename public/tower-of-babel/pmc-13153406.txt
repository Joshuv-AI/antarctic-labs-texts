Off by a beat: the effects of temporal misalignment in reinforcement learning for sepsis treatment
Authors: Shengpu Tang, Jiayu Yao, Jenna Wiens, Sonali Parbhoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reinforcement learning shows promise for guiding sequential clinical decisions, yet common data preprocessing introduces temporal misalignment that violates causal assumptions. Using sepsis management as a case study, we demonstrate that such misalignment produces inappropriate treatment recommendations in nearly half of patient states. This widespread methodological flaw affects over 80% of the literature but is obscured by inflated performance metrics. We propose a simple fix and advocate decision-centric problem formulations.

Artificial intelligence (AI) holds great promise for improving healthcare, particularly by developing tools to support clinical decision making. Such decisions are often inherently sequential: clinicians continually observe a patient’s condition, make diagnostic assessments, initiate treatments, and adjust those treatments over time in response to new information. For example, in the intensive care unit (ICU), the management of sepsis requires deciding whether and when to administer antibiotics, intravenous fluids, vasopressors, and when to start and stop mechanical ventilation. Each decision carries substantial consequences for a patient’s in-hospital survival.

Reinforcement learning (RL), a subfield of AI, naturally lends itself to this setting because it learns from sequences of interactions to optimize long-term outcomes1. In recent years, RL has been applied to a range of healthcare sequential decision-making tasks2, including optimizing medication dosing in critical care settings3, guiding weaning strategies for mechanical ventilation4 and informing sepsis management5. The primary objective of RL is to support clinicians in making timely, patient-specific treatment decisions that improve patient outcomes.

To support timely decision making, RL algorithms analyze historical treatment sequences to identify patterns associated with favorable outcomes. A learned decision rule (known as a “policy”) maps these patterns to recommended treatments based on evolving patient conditions. Formally, we model the interactions between the agent (e.g., a clinician) and the environment (e.g., a patient) as a Markov decision process (MDP)1. At each discrete time step t = 1, 2, 3, …, the agent observes the current state st (e.g., the patient’s physiological status) and selects an action at (e.g., a treatment) according to a policy π(at∣st). Then, the environment evolves to a new state, st+1, determined by the MDP’s transition function P(st+1∣st, at), and the agent receives a reward signal r(st+1) (for simplicity, we focus on a sparse reward setting where the agent receives reward only at the terminal state). A familiar example is turn-based games such as chess6, where the agent observes the board, selects a move, and the board transitions to a new configuration, with this process repeating in discrete rounds. This process yields a trajectory, s1, a1, s2, a2, s3, a3, …, sT, aT, sT+1, consisting of T transitions of the form (st, at, st+1). Unlike standard RL benchmarks that directly produce well-defined trajectories7, healthcare applications often involve irregularly sampled time series such as electronic health records (EHR). Trajectories are commonly constructed by preprocessing these data into regular windows. This setting resembles a continuous-time control system (Fig. 1b; detailed definitions in Supplementary Note 3), where the timeline is sliced into non-overlapping windows of equal length to make the data compatible with discrete-time RL. Within the t-th window, following what has become common practice in the literature5,8, the observed signal and the control information are summarized into a state and an action, respectively, and indexed by the same time-step t to form a state-action pair (st, at). This indexing induces a contemporaneous alignment of the state and action sequences, which we refer to as the “original” alignment.

However, this alignment has a subtle issue. From the perspective of an agent making decisions at these discrete intervals (i.e., at the window boundary), a state is only fully realized at the end of a time window, whereas an action needs to be determined at the beginning of that window (Fig. 1c). A simple analogy is daily planning: we cannot wait until the end of the day to decide what to do during the day, but a daily diary can only be written once the day has ended. In clinical care, this means that the action derived from treatments recorded within an interval should be assumed to have been determined at the start of the interval, whereas the patient’s state is fully observed only at the end of the interval. By assigning the same time-step index to contemporaneous states and actions, we are effectively assuming at takes place before st. This differs from the structure of a valid trajectory, in which at is always followed by st+1 (instead of st). While this issue arising from temporal discretization is well recognized in the longitudinal causal inference literature9,10, it is often overlooked by the RL community.

Formally, we show that the intuitive “original” alignment violates causality. Let xt and zt denote the state and action of the t-th window, respectively. Under the “original” alignment, we set xt = st and zt = at (Fig. 1d). By superimposing the causal relationships of a trajectory from Fig. 1a, we find that the causal arrow st → at implies xt → zt, which contradicts the temporal ordering of the data where zt precedes xt. Effectively, this assumes that action zt can be chosen using information about state xt from the future.

To resolve this implausible causal assumption, we propose the “shifted” alignment (Fig. 1e), in which we set xt = st and zt = at−1. In other words, the index of actions are decremented by one from the index of time windows. This shifts the action sequence backward on the timeline relative to the state sequence and reverses the direction of causal relationship between xt and zt. The causal arrow at−1 → st now implies zt → xt, respecting the temporal ordering of zt and xt in the data.

Although the difference between the two alignments may seem minor, we caution that RL models trained on misaligned data will suffer from temporal information leakage and make incorrect recommendations. Consider the same illustrative example in Fig. 1. Under the “original” alignment, the transition (s1, a1, s2) in Fig. 1d is formed with s1 = x1 (“increasing signal”), a1 = z1 (“turning the switch on”), and s2 = x2 (“decreasing signal”). This would suggest that turning the switch on causes the signal to decrease. However, the true system dynamics in Fig. 1b show the opposite: the signal increases when the switch is on. The same kind of error applies to the other action, where misaligned transitions suggest turning the switch off causes the signal to increase, when it should in fact decrease. As a result, RL policies trained on such data will recommend incorrect actions based on erroneous assumptions about action effects. To make matters worse, if the test data are misaligned in the same way, the evaluation will appear internally consistent and suggest the RL policy has correctly identified the action effects. The error only becomes evident at deployment, where the learned policy is “off by a beat” relative to the true system dynamics, since during training, it has assumed access to information not yet available at decision time. See Supplementary Note 3 for a simulation study that demonstrates this phenomenon.

A similar pitfall can arise in clinical settings. For example, misaligned trajectories could lead us to infer that initiating vasopressors lowers blood pressure, when in fact the decrease occurs because vasopressors are later discontinued. Consequently, an RL policy trained on such data could recommend vasopressors for patients who are already hypertensive.

What we described above was a hypothetical scenario. To assess the impact of temporal misalignment and show how our “shifted” alignment mitigates it, we revisited the widely studied sepsis management problem in Komorowski et al.5 (see Supplementary Note 2). We replicated their experiments with both temporal alignment strategies using identical definitions of state and action spaces (see “Methods”), producing two policies: the “original policy” (learned under the “original” alignment) and the “shifted policy” (learned under the “shifted” alignment). We then partitioned the state space into four quadrants according to whether the optimal action recommended by the two policies agreed or differed (Fig. 2a, b). The most common recommendation was no treatment, seen in 311 (41.5%) states in the orange quadrant (bottom left). For 170 (22.7%) states in the red quadrant (left column), the “shifted policy” recommended no treatment while the “original policy” recommended some treatment (fluids, vasopressors, or both). Conversely, for 57 (7.6%) states in the pink quadrant (bottom row), the “original policy” recommended no treatment while the “shifted policy” recommended some treatment. The remaining 212 (28.3%) states fell in the purple quadrant, where both policies recommended some treatment but agreed on the specific treatment in only 104 (13.9%) states.

To better understand factors associated with divergent recommendations, we characterized each quadrant by the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores11 of patients in those states and compared the score distributions using two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests with a Bonferroni correction (Fig. 2c). Our analysis indicates that the “original policy” tends to overtreat healthier patients and undertreat sicker patients, which is clinically counterintuitive. On average, patients in the test set had a mean GCS of 12.62. Patients in the red quadrant were significantly healthier than average (mean GCS 12.98, p = 1.33 × 10−14, U ≈ 1.65 × 108) and were overtreated by the “original policy” when the “shifted policy” recommended no treatment. In contrast, patients in the pink quadrant were significantly sicker than average (mean GCS 10.95, p = 8.11 × 10−84, U ≈ 5.58 × 107) and were undertreated by the “original policy” when the “shifted policy” recommended treatment; they are even sicker than patients in the purple quadrant (mean GCS 12.19, p = 1.08 × 10−38, U ≈ 1.01 × 107) where both policies recommended treatment. Analyses using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores12 led to a similar conclusion (Fig. 2d; Supplementary Note 2). Among 4704 time windows where patients were already hypertensive (mean blood pressure > 95 mmHg), the “original policy” recommends vasopressor treatments 1820 (38.7%) times, while the “shifted policy” only does so 1342 (28.5%) times. This suggests that the “original policy” is likely more prone to implausible, potentially harmful overtreatment. Just as in our hypothetical example where misalignment led us to incorrectly infer that turning the switch off caused the signal to increase, here, misalignment led the “original policy” to conclude withholding treatment is beneficial for sicker patients, and vice versa. These findings suggest that the “original policy” has learned inappropriate treatment recommendations due to temporal misalignment.

Despite these alarming findings, much of the literature has continued to build on the flawed problem formulation, admittedly including the authors’ own prior work (Fig. 3, Table 1; see example code snippets in Supplementary Note 1). Based on our literature review of 57 articles published during 2017–2025 that built a sepsis RL model on the MIMIC-III dataset, 49 (86.0%) used “original” alignment, while 5 (8.8%) used the “shifted” alignment. 3 (5.3%) articles could not be definitely ascertained due to unclear description of preprocessing steps and the lack of source code. A key reason is that standard evaluation procedures are themselves affected by temporal misalignment, making incorrect policies appear valid. In our experiment, the two alignment strategies produce nearly identical results when assessed with common off-policy evaluation (OPE) metrics and action frequency heatmaps (Fig. 2e, f), using test data with the same alignment as training. Under both alignments, the RL policy achieved an estimated value around 0.86–0.88, outperforming the behavior policy with a value around 0.78–0.80. Similarly, both alignments yielded nearly indistinguishable heatmaps of observed clinicians’ treatments and RL recommended treatments, with total variation distances of less than 0.01 and 0.19, respectively. However, when assessed using test data under “shifted” alignment, the “original policy” performance is no longer superior to the behavior policy (estimated value of 0.78; Fig. 2f). Conversely, when the “shifted” policy is evaluated on test data preprocessed using “original” alignment, it achieves an estimated value of 0.72 (Fig. 2e), leading one to believe it is worse than the behavior policy when in fact it is better (Fig. 2f). In simulation studies, we replicated this phenomenon, where the “original” alignment produced incorrect actions, yet the evaluation might appear reasonable because the same misalignment was applied during training and evaluation; the error became apparent only when the policy is deployed in the environment (see Supplementary Note 3). Taken together, these results suggest that the “original” and “shifted” policies may appear equally reasonable to practitioners, particularly those without a deeper understanding of the recommended treatments. To summarize, we showed that prior work on the widely used sepsis management benchmark suffers from temporal misalignment, and we proposed a simple remedy by shifting the action index backward by one time step. Although various follow-up works used different MDP setups, our experiments adhered to the design choices in Komorowski et al.5 such that our results can be interpreted and compared in the context of the results presented in that article. For example, the choice of state representation (discretized vs. continuous) may lead to different extents of state-aliasing and confounding. Regardless, our results are further corroborated by simulation experiments (see Supplementary Note 3), which isolate the effect of misalignment from choices of state representation. More broadly, our investigation highlights the need for thoughtful problem formulation when applying AI in healthcare. While prior guidelines and critiques have addressed various failure modes of offline RL for healthcare13–16, temporal misalignment remains an under-appreciated issue that continues to affect the current literature despite its wide recognition in adjacent fields such as causal inference9,10.

As demonstrated by our case study, temporal alignment in RL is not a mere preprocessing step but a key modeling choice that imposes causal structure on the data and shapes the learned policies. While we describe “shifted” as a simple fix, it is not a one-size-fits-all solution and should not be applied blindly; the appropriate alignment depends on how time is represented and indexed in the raw data. The “original” alignment can be suitable when the action at occurs instantaneously at the end of the t-th window, such as end-of-day notifications in mobile health17 or medication prescriptions following outpatient visits18. In contrast, when the observation precedes the action, a naive “shifted” alignment is not sufficient because the observation, if used as the state, violates the Markov assumption due to partial observability. In such cases, the state must be augmented to include the action following the observation in addition to applying the “shifted” alignment (see Supplementary Note 3). When medical decision making occurs in continuous time, other advanced methods such as semi-MDP or continuous-time transformer may be more appropriate18,19.

Finally, we note that the performance gap between the two alignment strategies depends on the temporal resolution of the data-generating process. As shown in Adams et al.10, the bias due to the “original” alignment decreases with increased temporal granularity, which means the potential utility of the “shifted” alignment also decreases. However, we found that the performance of the “shifted” alignment dominates across various time step sizes (see Supplementary Note 3). Furthermore, using finer granularity in clinical RL leads to other practical issues (e.g., defining action spaces and performing OPE across different time step sizes20).

As noted by our analysis, practitioners should not rely on performance metrics alone to justify temporal alignment choices, as models trained and evaluated on data misaligned in the same way will appear internally consistent despite being clinically invalid. Therefore, we advocate a decision-centric perspective that considers the input-output interface of the decision-making agent at inference time. To address the complexities of clinical data, which often include recording lags and irregular sampling, we recommend practitioners explicitly map the data-generation process to the decision-making timeline. Practically, this means asking: When are decisions made? What information is available at that moment (e.g., history of observations, prior treatments)? What decisions can the algorithm make based on this information (e.g., we should not decide on something that has already happened)? These questions are best answered by explicitly checking causal assumptions, such as temporal ordering of data elements, leading to problem formulations that yield practically useful models21. Accordingly, clinicians and implementation scientists should be involved at the problem formulation stage to ensure the chosen alignment reflects the intended downstream workflow.

As AI is increasingly used in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, we emphasize that seemingly innocuous implementation details, such as temporal alignment during preprocessing, can have major effects on problem formulation and downstream outcomes. Careful attention to such details is therefore essential for responsible application of AI in healthcare14,22.

Sepsis Reinforcement Learning Experimental Setup

Patient cohort

We used the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care database (MIMIC-III) v1.423,24, which contains electronic health record (EHR) data for patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) in a large tertiary care hospital in the US. Access to the database may be requested at: https://physionet.org/content/mimiciii/1.4/. We used a publicly available preprocessing code base from https://github.com/microsoft/mimic_sepsis, which considers the first ICU stay for adult inpatient hospitalizations who had sepsis, following the same cohort definition used in past work5,25,26. This yielded 19,287 patients (9.6% mortality), which were split 70/15/15 for training, validation, and testing.

Data extraction and preprocessing

For each ICU stay, we identified the presumed onset of infection using the Sepsis-3 criteria27 and extracted the patient’s EHR data from up to 28 h before to up to 52 h after sepsis onset, producing data records of up to 80 h long. Time-series data included physiological observations (such as vital signs and laboratory results) and treatment interventions (such as intravenous [IV] fluids and vasopressors), which were temporally aggregated into 4-h windows. Each trajectory is thus up to 20 time steps. We also extracted patient demographics (age, gender, body weight) and contextual information (ventilation and re-admission status). Following prior work25, outliers were removed, missing values were imputed using a combination of “carry-forward” and K-nearest neighbors imputation, and the resulting feature values were standardized5,25,28. In the end, each 4-h window is associated with an observation and an intervention: the observation is a 38-dimensional feature vector of demographic, physiological and laboratory variables; the intervention pertains to treatments recorded within the 4-h window, representing total volume of IV fluids and maximum dosage of vasopressors administered.

We defined the MDP for sepsis management following Komorowski et al.5. Specifically, the state space was created by clustering the observation feature vectors of the training set using k-means++29. The number of clusters (k = 750) was determined using the validation set and in line with the configuration used in previous work5,30. The action space was defined by binning the dosages of IV fluid and vasopressors, each into five levels, leading to a total of 25 discrete actions (see Supplementary Note 2)30. All intermediate rewards were set to 0, and a terminal reward was given for survival (+1) or death (−1) 48 h after ICU discharge. Two corresponding terminal states were added to the state space. Given these definitions, we converted the clinical times series of each ICU stay to an RL trajectory. We constructed two dataset versions using either the “original” or “shifted” alignment between observations and interventions to form state-action pairs, keeping the same cohort and identical definitions of state and action spaces, and performed policy optimization and evaluation separately on each version.
