Engineering Escherichia coli for Urease-Driven Synthesis of Metal Oxide Nanomaterials
Authors: Zong-Yen Wu, Alex Y. W. Lin, Isaak E. Müller, Zhiying Zhao, Nigel J. Mouncey, Peter Ercius, Yasuo Yoshikuni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of functional nanomaterials with controlled morphologies is essential for advancements in medicine, electronics and computing, energy, catalysis, and environmental applications. However, conventional synthesis methods often demand high energy input and pose significant environmental challenges. Urease-based biomineralization presents an efficient, eco-friendly alternative for nanomaterial production under mild conditions. In this study, we engineered Escherichia coli (E. coli) to express a urease gene cluster from Sporosarcina pasteurii using CRAGE-Duet technology. The engineered strain successfully synthesized calcium carbonate and calcium phosphate crystals. Expanding the approach, we synthesized metal oxide nanoparticles, including hematite (Fe2O3), and nanocrystalline anatase titanium dioxide (TiO2). These nanomaterials were characterized by electron microscopy, demonstrating the potential of E. coli as a sustainable and versatile platform for green nanomaterial synthesis.

Introduction

Metal oxide nanoparticles represent an important class of nanomaterials with diverse industrial applications. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) is extensively used in photocatalysis, environmental remediation, solar energy conversion, and as a pigment, supporting a global market worth ∼USD 20 billion. Iron oxides (Fe2O3 and Fe3O4) are similarly critical, with roles in magnetic storage, catalysis, biomedical imaging, and targeted drug delivery, comprising a market of ∼USD 2.2 billion. Other metal oxides, including zinc oxide (ZnO), cerium oxide (CeO2), copper oxide (CuO), manganese oxide (MnO2), and nickel oxide (NiO), are employed in sensors, energy storage, antimicrobial coatings, and optoelectronic devices, − contributing to a combined market size of approximately USD 6.9 billion.

Conventional nanoparticle synthesis methods such as precipitation, sol–gel, hydrothermal processing, and thermal decomposition often require high temperatures, pressures, or toxic solvents. , For instance, TiO2 synthesis via the sol–gel process necessitates calcination at 400–600 °C and typically involves ethanol, raising energy and environmental concerns. Hydrothermal synthesis of hematite (α-Fe2O3) requires stringent control over temperature and pressure to tune crystal morphology. , These limitations motivate the exploration of alternative, more sustainable synthesis strategies.

Microbial synthesis presents a promising green alternative utilizing mild conditions, leveraging biological processes for nanomaterial production. Magnetotactic bacteria naturally produce magnetite (Fe3O4), while engineered or wild-type microbes such as Bacillus spp. and Brevibacterium casei have been harnessed to produce diverse nanoparticles including Au, Ag, Pd, and Co3O4. − Recent innovations in synthetic biology have enabled Escherichia coli (E. coli) to synthesize elemental and oxide nanomaterials, including Mn3O4, Cu2O, and complex biometallic oxides via expression of metallothionein and phytochelatin synthase.

Urease-mediated biomineralization offers another route for nanoparticle formation under mild conditions. The enzymatic breakdown of urea increases extracellular pH, initiating precipitation of metal carbonates and oxides. , While Sporosarcina pasteurii (S. pasteurii) has been used for calcium carbonate and zinc oxide formation, its complex growth requirements and limited genetic tools constrain its scalability. − In contrast, E. coli is genetically tractable and easy to culture. In this study, we engineered an E. coli strain (ATCC8739, ΔpflAB-focA, ΔfrdABCD, and ΔldhA) (SB429) to support anoxic ureolytic activity. Using CRAGE-Duet, , we integrated a 7-kb urease gene cluster from S. pasteurii under IPTG-inducible T7 control. This system enabled efficient biosynthesis of calcium carbonate, calcium phosphate, hematite iron oxide, and nanocrystalline anatase titanium dioxide, demonstrating the potential of urease-expressing E. coli as a versatile and green nanomaterial production platform.

Results

Urease Activity and Calcium Carbonate Precipitation

We generated a ureolytic E. coli (SB826) by inserting the S. pasteurii urease gene cluster into SB448 (see Methods). To assess urease activity in SB826, cultures were grown in a nutrient broth-based urea-CaCl2 medium (Supporting Information Table S3) supplemented with 1, 10, or 100 μM IPTG. Ammonia production, a direct indicator of urease-mediated urea hydrolysis, was quantified and compared to that of S. pasteurii (positive control), which was cultured in ATCC 1832 BPU medium containing 20 g/L urea.

SB826 demonstrated robust urease activity, with ammonia production increasing proportionally with IPTG concentration (Figure A). At 100 μM IPTG, SB826 produced ammonia levels comparable to those of S. pasteurii after 12 h. Remarkably, SB826 exceeded S. pasteurii in ammonia production during the first 6 h, suggesting a faster initial rate of urea degradation by urease expression.

Ammonia production by E. coli SB826 increased with IPTG concentration and reached levels comparable to S. pasteurii after 12 h with various IPTG induction concentrations. Statistical significance was determined using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, with simple effects evaluated within each time point. For clarity, only nonsignificant comparisons relevant to the text are indicated (ns). (B) Time-dependent pH elevation. During a 12-h cultivation, SB826 elevated the culture pH from 6.0 to around 9.0, consistent with active urea hydrolysis. Statistical significance at 12 hours was assessed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests (***p < 0.001). (C) Calcium ion depletion. In the SB826 culture, Ca2+ ions were fully depleted within 6 h, leading to calcium carbonate precipitation, visually confirmed by sediment formation (highlighted by the red arrow). Statistical significance at 6 h was assessed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests (*p < 0.05).

To assess the microbially induced calcite precipitation (MICP) capability of SB826, consistent with previously reported native and engineered ureolytic microbes, we monitored pH elevation and Ca2+ ion depletion following established protocols. SB826 induced a rapid increase in pH from 6.0 to 8.0 within 2 h, followed by stabilization at approximately pH 9 (Figure B). Concurrently, soluble Ca2+ concentrations decreased markedly within 6 h (Figure C), coinciding with the formation of visible white CaCO3 precipitates that settled upon brief standing. In contrast, the control strain SB448 exhibited no significant changes in pH or Ca2+ concentration over the course of the assay (Figure B,C), confirming that urease activity in SB826 is required for calcite biomineralization.

To verify CaCO3 formation, we examined the precipitates by TEM. Early deposits consisted of aggregates of nanoscale vaterite crystallites, as confirmed by selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) (Figures A and S1A). After 48 h, these vaterite aggregates underwent a polymorphic transformation to calcite, the thermodynamically more stable CaCO3 polymorph (Figures B and S1B).

The corresponding SAED pattern (inset) confirms the vaterite phase. (B) TEM image of calcite crystals formed after 48 h, indicating a polymorphic transition from vaterite to calcite. The SAED pattern (inset) confirms the calcite crystal structure (Sse Figure S1 for indexed SAED).

Calcium Phosphate Synthesis

Calcium phosphate crystals were synthesized by culturing E. coli SB826 in an M9 broth-based urea medium supplemented with 100 μM IPTG. Cultures were incubated at 30 °C with 200 rpm stirring for 12 h. The M9 medium, which contains disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4), served as the phosphate source for biomineralization.

High-angle annular dark field (HAADF)–scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) revealed the formation of well-defined platelet-shaped calcium phosphate crystals, while energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed their elemental composition (Figure ). The resulting platelets measured 3–5 μm in width, notably smaller than those typically obtained via conventional chemical synthesis methods reported in prior studies. ,

Biosynthesis of calcium phosphate crystals byE. coli SB826. (A, B) HAADF-STEM images showing platelet-shaped calcium phosphate crystals synthesized by E. coli SB826 cultured in M9 broth-based urea medium. (C–E) STEM-EDS elemental maps illustrating the distribution of key elements (Ca, P, and O) validating the composition of the biosynthesized calcium phosphate crystals.

These findings demonstrate that E. coli SB826 can mediate the formation of structurally distinct calcium phosphate crystals under mild conditions, offering a biogenic route to nanoscale phosphate-based materials.

Iron Oxide Nanomaterial Synthesis

Iron oxide nanomaterials were produced by culturing E. coli SB826 in a nutrient broth–based urea medium with a reduced nutrient concentration (0.8 g/L). In contrast, culturing in the original nutrient concentration resulted in the formation of precipitates other than iron oxides. The medium was supplemented with 10 mM iron(0) pentacarbonyl (Fe­(CO)5) and 100 μM IPTG to induce urease expression. Cultures were incubated under anaerobic conditions at 30 °C with 200 rpm shaking for 12 h.

TEM revealed the formation of distinct Fe2O3 nanoplates and nanorods synthesized by SB826 (Figure A, regions a and b). Corresponding SAED patterns are consistent with hematite (α-Fe2O3), hematite associated with γ-FeOOH, and γ-FeOOH (Figures B–D and S3). The presence of γ-FeOOH is consistent with its role as a known intermediate in hematite formation. EDS analysis further confirmed the colocalization of Fe and O within the nanostructures (Figure E–G), supporting the phase assignments and indicating high compositional purity.

Biosynthesis of iron oxide nanomaterials byE. coli SB826 cultured with Fe­(CO)5. (A) HAADF-STEM image showing the formation of iron oxide nanoplates and nanorods in regions a and b following incubation with 10 mM Fe­(CO)5 under anaerobic conditions. (B–D) SAED patterns corresponding to regions a, b, and c in panel A, confirming the presence of hematite (α-Fe2O3), hematite mixed with γ-FeOOH, and γ-FeOOH, respectively (see Figure S3 for structural characterization). (E–G) STEM-EDS elemental maps of region b, verifying that the nanostructures are primarily composed of iron (Fe) and oxygen (O).

These results demonstrate that SB826 can mediate the anaerobic biosynthesis of iron oxide nanomaterials with controlled nanoscale morphologies through Fe­(CO)5 precursor transformation.

Iron Oxide Nanomaterial Formation from Alternative Iron Precursors

In addition to Fe­(CO)5, we evaluated four other iron precursors for iron oxide nanomaterial biosynthesis by E. coli SB826: ferrous sulfate (FeSO4), iron­(II) oxalate dihydrate (FeC2O4), iron­(II) d-gluconate dihydrate (C12H28FeO16), and iron­(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe­(NO3)3·9H2O), following the same conditions described previously. Of these, only FeSO4 yielded solid precipitates.

TEM analysis indicated that the precipitate formed from FeSO4 is consistent with γ-FeOOH. The diffuse ring pattern suggests a nanocrystalline structure, which can broaden diffraction features and complicate precise indexing (Figure ). HAADF-STEM imaging combined with SAED further supports the assignment to γ-FeOOH (Figures A,B and S3). STEM-EDS elemental mapping of the same region reveals colocalized iron (Fe) and oxygen (O) (Figure C,D), along with detectable carbon (C) signals (Figure E), suggesting that γ-FeOOH nucleation may occur in association with individual bacterial cells.

Hematite formation by E. coli strain SB826 cultured with FeSO4. (A) HAADF-STEM image showing γ-FeOOH precipitate formation in proximity to a bacterial cell. (B) SAED pattern confirming the nanocrystalline or poorly ordered hematite (see Figure S4 for structural characterization). (C, D) STEM-EDS elemental maps showing the distribution of iron (Fe) and oxygen (O) within the mineralized region. (E) STEM-EDS mapping of carbon (C), highlighting the location of the cell membrane, suggesting that hematite nucleation occurred on or near the bacterial surface.

These results demonstrate that E. coli SB826 is capable of mediating γ-FeOOH synthesis from FeSO4, highlighting the potential to tune nanomaterial composition and crystallinity through precursor selection.

Biosynthesis of Nanocrystalline Anatase Titanium Dioxide

Nanocrystalline titanium dioxide (TiO2) in the anatase phase was synthesized using E. coli strain SB826. Cultures were grown in a nutrient broth-based urea medium supplemented with 100 μM IPTG and 50 mM titanium­(IV) bis­(ammonium lactato)­dihydroxide, a widely used water-soluble TiO2 precursor. The cultures were incubated under anaerobic conditions at 30 °C with 200 rpm shaking for 12 h.

Following incubation, a gel-like material was recovered by filtration of the spent culture broth. This material was subjected to TEM analysis. HAADF-STEM imaging and SAED confirmed the presence of nanocrystalline anatase TiO2 embedded within the gel matrix (Figures A and S4). STEM-EDS elemental mapping further revealed the colocalization of titanium (Ti) and oxygen (O), with signal enrichment observed around individual cells (Figure B–I), suggesting that anatase TiO2 nucleated in close association with bacterial surfaces.

Biosynthesis of anatase-phase titanium dioxide by E. coli SB826. (A) HAADF-STEM image of the gel-like material formed by SB826 cultured with 50 mM titanium­(IV) bis­(ammonium lactato)­dihydroxide. SAED pattern confirms the presence of anatase TiO2 nanocrystals (see Figure S5 for structural characterization). (B–E) STEM-EDS elemental maps of region a in panel A, showing the localized distribution of titanium (Ti) and oxygen (O) around individual bacterial cells. (F–I) STEM-EDS elemental maps of region b in panel A, similarly demonstrating Ti and O enrichment associated with cell surfaces, indicating cell-mediated nucleation of anatase TiO2.

These results highlight the ability of SB826 to mediate mild-condition biosynthesis of high-value nanocrystalline anatase, expanding the scope of microbially enabled metal oxide production.

Discussion

In this study, we engineered E. coli SB826 using the CRAGE-Duet system to express a heterologous urease gene cluster from Sporosarcina pasteurii, enabling urea hydrolysis–driven biomineralization under mild and genetically controlled conditions. IPTG-inducible regulation provides precise temporal control over urease activity and localized alkalinization, facilitating inorganic material synthesis without the elevated temperatures, pressures, or hazardous solvents characteristic of conventional nanomaterial manufacturing routes (Table ). Together, these features establish a green, genetically programmable platform for sustainable nanomaterial synthesis. Several studies have explored biobased approaches for nanomaterial synthesis, highlighting the emerging potential of biological systems for materials production (Table ).

Representative Biological Strategies for Inorganic Nanomaterial Synthesis

Microbial biomineralization has been demonstrated previously using native ureolytic microbes and engineered E. coli. For example, Carter et al. investigated the influence of growth conditions on CaCO3 polymorph selection using native S. pasteurii, while Liang et al. heterologously expressed S. pasteurii urease genes in E. coli to induce CaCO3 precipitation. However, prior systems largely focused on calcium-based minerals and lacked synthetic regulatory control, genome integration, or modular expandability, limiting programmability and scalability. In contrast, the CRAGE-Duet system enables stable chromosomal integration of dual genetic payloads and supports rapid incorporation of complex gene circuits, providing a robust synthetic biology chassis for materials engineering.

We intentionally selected calcium carbonate and calcium phosphate as demonstration materials to validate platform performance, as their precipitation can be induced by pH elevation alone. In CaCO3 systems, the observed transition from vaterite microspheres to calcite is consistent with known aqueous polymorphic pathways. For calcium phosphate, SB826 produced platelet-shaped calcium phosphate crystals that were smaller than those typically obtained through conventional chemical synthesis, highlighting the potential for biological control over morphology. , While these materials themselves are not uniquely enabled by biological synthesis, they serve as critical benchmarks demonstrating controllable, reproducible biomineralization in an engineered microbial host.

Importantly, the platform’s true utility emerges when extended beyond calcium-based minerals to application-relevant metal oxides that are difficult to synthesize via simple chemical pH adjustment. SB826 successfully mediated the formation of iron oxide nanomaterials, including hematite, from specific iron precursors. These materials are relevant for catalysis, sensing, and environmental remediation. , Phase-selective formation depended strongly on precursor chemistry, underscoring the role of coordinated biochemical and chemical parameters in governing mineral outcome. TEM, SAED, and STEM-EDS analyses confirmed Fe­(CO)5-to-Fe2O3 conversion and γ-FeOOH nucleation near cell surfaces, implicating cell-associated macromolecules as potential nucleation templates. These observations suggest that the platform can serve not only as a production system but also as a discovery tool for identifying biomolecular factors that influence nucleation, morphology, and polymorphism.

The biosynthesis of anatase-phase TiO2 further demonstrates the versatility of the platform. TiO2 is widely used for photocatalysis, UV protection, and energy applications but is typically produced via sulfate or chloride processes that are energy-intensive and generate substantial waste. In contrast, SB826 mediated anatase formation under mild aqueous conditions from titanium­(IV) bis­(ammonium lactato)­dihydroxide, with crystals localized near cells. Together with hematite formation, these results support a general mechanism in which urease-driven localized alkalinization, combined with cell-surface templating, enables controlled transition metal hydroxide precipitation , an approach that may be extended to other technologically relevant oxides such as ZnO.

A key enabling feature of this system is the metabolically optimized host background. Deletion of pflAB, frdABCD, and ldhA eliminates fermentative acid production, − reducing pH buffering and promoting sustained local alkalinization. This optimization allows effective mineralization under anaerobic or microaerophilic conditions, which are essential for oxygen-sensitive pathways such as magnetite (Fe3O4) synthesis that require controlled Fe­(II)/Fe­(III) ratios. SB826 achieved complete Ca2+ depletion and pH elevation to ∼9 within 6 h, matching or exceeding rates observed in high-copy plasmid-based aerobic systems , while offering superior genetic stability and compatibility with anoxic processes.

From a manufacturing perspective, we propose potential downstream processing and purification considerations relevant to large-scale deployment. Following biogenic synthesis, nanomaterials can be separated from biomass using established approaches, including repeated washing, filtration, and gradient centrifugation. Where removal of organic residues is required, low-temperature calcination (e.g., < 500 °C) can be employed to eliminate cellular material while preserving inorganic structure. These workflows are compatible with bulk processing and have precedent in biologically assisted mineral synthesis, and they rely on relatively low energy input compared to high-temperature (>800–1000 °C) calcination or hydrothermal treatments commonly used in chemical nanomaterial synthesis. Looking forward, genetic strategies such as programmed cell lysis, self-release mechanisms, or engineered monodispersity could further simplify purification and enhance material uniformity, facilitating integration into devices or composite materials.

Although a full techno-economic or life-cycle analysis is beyond the scope of this study, order-of-magnitude comparisons provide useful context. Conventional chemical synthesis of metal oxide nanoparticles such as TiO2 and Fe2O3 typically involves elevated temperatures, pressurized reactors, and/or corrosive reagents, contributing to reported production costs pf approximately ∼$1.5–3 kg–1 for bulk TiO2 and ∼$10–100 kg–1 for iron oxide nanoparticles, , with cradle-to-gate carbon footprints on the order of several kg CO2-eq per kg product, depending on route and scale. − In contrast, biologically assisted mineralization proceeds under ambient temperature and pressure in aqueous systems and may reduce energy inputs and harsh chemical usage. Prior work on biologically mediated inorganic material production demonstrated that increases in titer and productivity can substantially improve projected economics. While the present system remains at an early stage and no formal TEA/LCA is presented here, these quantitative benchmarks suggest that continued improvements in biological mineralization efficiency could enable competitive performance, particularly in integrated or waste-coupled process configurations.

Finally, beyond discrete nanomaterials, this platform provides a foundation for biocomposite synthesis. In natural biocomposites such as magnetite, bone, teeth, and crustacean shells, peptides, proteins, and polysaccharides direct mineral nucleation, size, shape, and phase while forming hierarchical scaffolds that reinforce inorganic components. Our engineered E. coli strain could be further developed as a programmable chassis that coproduces mineral-binding peptides, structural proteins, and polysaccharides alongside inorganic nanomaterials. Integrating genetically encoded organic matrix production with biologically assisted mineral synthesis could enable bottom-up fabrication of multifunctional biocomposites with tunable composition, structure, and properties. ,−

In summary, this work establishes a modular, genetically programmable microbial platform for sustainable nanomaterial synthesis. By combining synthetic biology, metabolic engineering, and biomineralization, the system enables controllable production of both benchmark and application-relevant inorganic materials and opens new avenues for engineered living materials, biocomposites, and environmentally responsible nanomanufacturing.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed an engineered E. coli platform capable of biosynthesizing multiple inorganic nanomaterialsincluding calcium carbonate, calcium phosphate, iron oxide, and titanium dioxideunder mild, ambient conditions. Through integration of a heterologous urease gene cluster via the CRAGE-Duet system and deletion of key acid-producing pathways, the strain achieved enhanced local alkalinization and accelerated biomineralization. The resulting nanomaterials displayed diverse, tunable morphologies, offering potential for applications in catalysis, environmental remediation, and biocomposite manufacturing. By broadening the functional repertoire of E. coli in biomineral synthesis, this work underscores the promise of genetically programmable microbial platforms as sustainable, versatile nanomaterial production systems.

Methods

Plasmid Construction

The plasmid pW34_Ure was engineered to carry the S. pasteurii urease gene cluster flanked by loxP and lox5171 sites to facilitate targeted genomic integration via the CRAGE-Duet system. Construction began with PCR amplification of the pW34 backbone (Addgene #158210), excluding the luxCDABE operon. The S. pasteurii urease gene cluster, including ureABCEFGH, was PCR-amplified from genomic DNA (ATCC 11859). The gene cluster was inserted downstream of a T7 promoter and lac operator using Gibson assembly (NEB, E2621). Primer sequences and a schematic plasmid map are provided in Table S1 and Figure S6.

Strains Used in This Study

S. pasteurii ATCC 11859 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and grown in ATCC 1832 BPU medium. The engineered E. coli strain ATCC 8739, carrying deletions in ΔpflAB-focA, ΔfrdABCD, and ΔldhA (SB429), served as the host for urease gene cluster integration via CRAGE-Duet. Plasmid assembly was performed using One Shot PIR1 chemically competent E. coli (Invitrogen). For conjugation, E. coli BW29427, cultured in LB broth supplemented with 300 μM diaminopimelic acid (DAP), was used as the donor. Complete strain information is summarized in Table S2.

CRAGE-Duet Implementation

SB429 (ATCC 8739 background with ΔpflAB-focA, ΔfrdABCD, and ΔldhA) was used as the recipient for CRAGE-Duet integration. A 3lox landing pad (LP) comprising lox2272, loxP, and lox5171 was inserted into the ptsG gene locus using λ-Red recombineering. , A PCR-amplified 3lox LP amplicon, derived from plasmid pW37 and flanked by ∼550 bp ptsG gene homology arms, was cotransformed into SB429 along with the λ-Red expression plasmid pKD46. Correct LP insertion was confirmed by PacBio sequencing, and the strain was designated SB448. A schematic overview of CRAGE-Duet implementation is shown in Figure .

Construction of the ureolytic E. coli strain SB826 using CRAGE-Duet system. Step 1: The process begins with co-transformation of the λ-Red recombinase expression plasmid pKD46 and a PCR-amplified 3lox landing pad (LP) into E. coli strain ATCC 8739, carrying deletions in ΔpflAB-focA, ΔfrdABCD, and ΔldhA (SB429). Homologous recombination facilitates insertion of the 3lox LP into the ptsG locus. Successful integration is selected by kanamycin resistance and validated via PacBio sequencing. Step 2: The first accessory plasmid, pW34_Ure, harboring the S. pasteurii urease gene cluster flanked by loxP and lox5171 sites, is introduced into SB448 via conjugation. Cre recombinase mediates site-specific recombination at the loxP–lox5171 site. Integration replaces the kanamycin marker with apramycin resistance, confirming successful insertion. Step 3: The second accessory plasmid, pW38, encoding an IPTG-inducible T7 RNA polymerase under lac operator control and flanked by lox2272 and loxP site is mediated by Cre recombinase. Final construct (SB826): The engineered E. coli strain SB826 harbors both genetic payloads integrated into the chromosome. Urease expression is controlled by the lac-regulated, IPTG-inducible T7 expression system, enabling regulated biosynthesis of nanomaterials.

Development of Ureolytic E. coli

To generate a ureolytic E. coli, two payloads were sequentially integrated into SB448. First, pW34_Ure, encoding the S. pasteurii urease gene cluster flanked by loxP and lox5171, was introduced via conjugation and integrated at the loxP-lox5171 site by Cre-lox recombination. Next, plasmid pW38, carrying the IPTG-inducible T7 RNA polymerase gene under lac operator control flanked by lox2272 and loxP, was conjugated and integrated at the lox2272-loxP site. The final engineered strain was designated SB826. For nanoparticle biosynthesis, SB826 was cultured in LB broth or on LB agar supplemented with 100 μM IPTG and 5 μM NiCl2 to induce urease activity.

Calcium Carbonate Synthesis

Calcium carbonate (CaCO3) synthesis was performed using a nutrient broth-based urea-CaCl2 medium supplemented with 100 μM IPTG and 5 μM NiCl2 for strain SB826. The medium contained 20 g/L urea, 3 g/L nutrient broth, and 5.6 g/L CaCl2, with the pH adjusted to 6.0 using 6 N HCl. Cultures were incubated under anaerobic conditions in an immersion circulator at 30 °C with 200 rpm starting, initiated at OD600 = 1.0. The pH and soluble Ca2+ levels were monitored using a Calcium Colorimetric Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK022). Precipitated CaCO3 crystals were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Calcium Phosphate Synthesis

Calcium phosphate (CaHPO4) was synthesized in an M9 minimal broth-based urea-CaCl2 medium, also supplemented with 100 μM IPTG and 5 μM NiCl2. The medium consisted of 20 g/L urea dissolved in M9 minimal broth (Teknova), with the pH adjusted to 6.0 using 6 N HCl. The cultures were incubated under anaerobic conditions at 30 °C with 200 rpm stirring, starting at OD600 = 1.0. Soluble Ca2+ concentrations and pH were monitored using the same colorimetric assay, and CaHPO4 crystals were analyzed by TEM.

Iron Oxide (Fe2O3) Synthesis

Iron oxide synthesis was performed in a modified nutrient broth-based urea medium containing 0.8 g/L nutrient broth and supplemented with 100 μM IPTG and 5 μM NiCl2. Five iron precursors were tested at 10 mM each: iron(0) pentacarbonyl (Fe­(CO)5), iron­(II) sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4), iron­(II) oxalate dihydrate (FeC2O4), iron­(II) d-gluconate dihydrate (C12H28FeO16), and iron­(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe­(NO3)3·9H2O). The pH was adjusted appropriately before incubation at 30 °C with 200 rpm shaking for 24 h, starting at OD600 = 1.0. Precipitates were collected using a 0.45 μm vacuum filter (Corning) and examined by TEM.

Titanium Dioxide (TiO2) Synthesis

The titanium dioxide synthesis was conducted in a nutrient broth-based urea medium supplemented with 50 mM titanium­(IV) bis­(ammonium lactato)­dihydroxide (Sigma) as the Ti precursor. The medium also included 100 μM IPTG, 5 μM NiCl2, and the pH was adjusted to 6.0 with 6 N HCl. Cultures were incubated under anaerobic conditions in 50 mL centrifuge tubes at 30 °C with 200 rpm shaking for 24 h, starting at OD600 = 1.0. TiO2 precipitates were harvested using a 0.45 μm vacuum filter (Corning) and characterized by TEM.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Collected precipitates were gently washed several times with distilled water, redispersed in distilled water, and deposited onto lacey carbon-coated 300-mesh copper grids. TEM imaging and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) were conducted using an FEI ThemIS TEM equipped with an X-FEG gun and Ceta2 CMOS camera, operating at 300 kV. High-angle annular dark field (HAADF) images were acquired in scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) mode at 300 kV with a convergence semiangle of 11.3 mrad. Elemental composition was determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using a Bruker SuperX windowless detector with a 0.7 sr solid angle, enabling rapid STEM-EDS mapping with a 5 min acquisition time at 300 kV.
