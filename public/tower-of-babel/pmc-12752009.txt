Clinicopathological and prognostic implications of OPMDs in oral squamous cell carcinoma: a 17-Year retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jinmei Wang, Zihang Ling, Jingyi Lu, Nan Xie, Qian Lu, Yingxin Jiang, Meng Xu, Xiaoan Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo characterize the clinicopathologic features of patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) and evaluate the prognostic significance of the history of oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) in this population.Materials and methodsThis retrospective, single-center study enrolled 1140 patients from 2000 to 2017 to explore the clinicopathologic features of OSCC patients. After 2:1 propensity score matching, 129 patients with OPMD history (OPMD group) and 258 patients without OPMD history (non-OPMD group) were analyzed in a cohort study. The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), while the secondary endpoint included disease-specific survival (DSS).ResultsAmong the 1140 OSCC patients, only 17.0% had a well-documented history of OPMDs. The most common tumor site was the tongue (57.3%), and the majority of patients (85.7%) were over 40 years old. Compared to the non-OPMD group, the OPMD group exhibited significantly lower T and N stages, better differentiation, less frequent neck dissection, and shorter hospital stays. However, there were no significant differences in 5-year OS or DSS between the two groups.ConclusionMost OSCC patients lacked a defined history of OPMDs, which may reflect rapid malignant progression of oral lesions. These findings underscore the importance of enhanced vigilance and early diagnostic interventions for OPMDs and OSCC, particularly in individuals over 40 years of age.Clinical trial numberChiCTR2200065588, Registration Date: 09 Nov. 2022.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-025-07343-x.Keywords: Oral squamous cell carcinoma, Oral potentially malignant disorders, Propensity score match, Survival

Introduction

Oral cavity cancer is one of the most common malignancies in the head and neck, causing nearly 2% of new cases and 1.8% of new deaths worldwide [1]. Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the most prevalent histological subtype of oral cavity cancer [2]. With the advancement of therapeutic techniques, surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, biologic therapy, and multimodality therapies have become the current interventions for OSCC patients [3]. However, the 5-year survival rate for OSCC has not enhanced because of the advanced stages of disease at diagnosis [4–6]. Early diagnosis and intervention can help enhance the long-term prognosis of such patients [7, 8].

Oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) are defined as any oral mucosal abnormality that is related to a statistically elevated risk of developing oral cancer [9]. These include oral leukoplakia (OLK), oral lichen planus (OLP), oral submucous fibrosis (OSF), actinic cheilitis, oral lichenoid lesions, and oral graft versus host disease (GVHSD) [10]. Current evidence indicated that OLK, OLP, and OSF had malignant transformation rates between 0.13% and 34% [11], 1.1% and 1.4% [12, 13], and 2% and 8% [14], respectively. OPMDs usually precede OSCC [15, 16]. In recent decades, significant progress has been made in OSCC treatment. However, the 5-year survival rate for OSCC patients remains at only 50% to 60%. Therefore, early detection and treatment of OPMDs are essential in decreasing the risk of OSCC origination, particularly the composition of OSCC derived from the malignant transformation of OPMDs. Early detection and treatment of OPMDs have become crucial to improving the survival of OSCC patients [17, 18].

Oral cancer and OPMDs are major problems in South and Southeast Asia. The burden of oral cancer problems in China has been on a continuous rise [19]. Previous studies have demonstrated that OSCCs usually develop from OPMDs. However, the proportion of OSCC patients with a history of OPMD is rarely reported, and the prognostic significance of OPMDs in OSCC remains controversial. OSCC derived from OLP indicated better prognostic factors, such as favorable grades and fewer nodal metastases, without much survival analysis [20]. Survival analysis by Chaturvedi et al. depicted that OSCC linked with OSF had better survival outcomes than conventional OSCC [21]. On the other hand, several studies reported that OSCC patients arising from OLK, OLP, or proliferative verrucous leukoplakia (PVL) showed no better outcomes than conventional patients [22–24]. Therefore, further research must be conducted to determine the prognostic value of OPMDs in OSCC patients.

The current retrospective study was designed to evaluate the incidence of OPMD history in 1140 OSCC patients and, based on fully documented data, explore the prognostic impact of OPMD history in 687 OSCC patients to investigate the characteristics of OSCC patients.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This is a single-center, retrospective cohort study conducted in a real-world setting. Two researchers independently reviewed patients who underwent initial surgery with biopsy-proven OSCC at the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University. Informed consent was not required because of the retrospective nature of the research. After obtaining the approval of the Ethical Committee and Institutional Review Board of our institution, OSCC cases from January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2017, were screened with medical records (Fig. 1). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) patients treated with surgical resection; (b) pathological confirmation. The exclusion criteria were: (a) incomplete clinicopathological information; (b) history of surgery for OSCC; (c) patients diagnosed with distant metastasis; or (d) patients with other synchronous or metachronous malignancies. Around 1140 patients were identified to evaluate the incidence of OPMD history in OSCC patients. The inclusion criterion for patients having an OPMD history was the clinical or pathological diagnosis based on the latest WHO consensus [9]. Patients having records of previous medical history of OPMDs were also incorporated. Patients from 2000 to 2007 were excluded due to an update on the new classification of Head and Neck tumors in July 2005 for additional analysis of the potential prognostic evaluation of OPMD history [25]. Moreover, these exclusion criteria were applied: (a) absence of pathological reports of OPMDs for patients with OPMD history and (b) patients without follow-up data. Later, 687 individuals were included in the final analysis, separated into two comparable groups: OSCC without OPMD history (non-OPMDs group) and OSCC with OPMD history (OPMDs group).

Variables and endpoints

The following clinicopathological parameters were obtained: age, sex, tobacco use, alcohol use, comorbidities, family history, subsite, T stage, N stage, grade, neck dissection, hospital length of stay, and OPMDs history. The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) 5th, 6th, and 7th editions helped grade tumors during the diagnosis year. Records from outpatient and inpatient visits or telephone calls helped obtain the follow-up data. The last follow-up date was December 31, 2022, with a median follow-up of 81 months for all patients. The major endpoint was overall survival (OS), determined as the interval from the initial surgery to death because of any cause or the final follow-up. The second endpoint was disease-specific survival (DSS), measured as the time from the operation date to death because of OSCC or the last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data was represented as means ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were reported as numbers (percentages). Continuous variables were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were evaluated using the appropriate chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to minimize discrepancies in the characteristics of the two groups. PSM was developed to decrease selection bias in observational studies. By carefully matching individuals with similar characteristics, PSM can mimic the effects of randomization to a significant extent [26]. The propensity score was determined using a logistic regression model, and variables incorporated into PSM were age, sex, tobacco use, alcohol use, comorbidities, family history, subsite, T stage, N stage, grade, neck dissection, and hospital length of stay. Patients were matched at a ratio of 2:1 with the greedy nearest-neighbor method without replacement and other default settings. Then, the “MatchIt” package was used to match the patients with OPMD history with two patients without OPMD history [27, 28]. The balance between covariates was evaluated by estimating standardized mean difference (SMD), with SMD ≤ 10% considered a balance.

Survival analysis was measured using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. The overall 5-year OS and DSS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Cox proportional hazard models helped estimate the hazard ratio (HR), confidence interval (CI), and prognostic factors related to the risk of OS and DSS. All the statistical analyses were performed with the SPSS version 25.0 and R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing Platform). The “MatchIt” package version 4.4.0 was utilized for PSM. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all the two-sided tests.

Results

Demographic and clinicopathologic characteristics of total patients

Overall, 1140 patients were included in the initial analysis, of which 194 were diagnosed with OPMDs. The incidence of OPMD history in 1140 patients was 17.0%. Table S1 represents the clinical and pathological characteristics of included patients. The median age was 54 years (range 19–87). Most cases were identified in their fourth (n = 282, 24.7%), fifth (n = 307, 26.9%), and sixth (n = 262, 23.0%) decades (Supplementary Fig. S1), of which 696 (61.1%) were men. The most common subsite was tongue (n = 653, 57.3%), followed by buccal mucosa (167, 14.6%), lower gingiva (124, 10.9%), floor of the mouth (99, 8.7%), upper gingiva (71, 6.2%), and hard palate (26, 2.3%). About UICC TNM classification, T1 was observed in 278 (24.4%), T2 in 547 (48.0%), T3 in 165 (14.5%), and T4 in 150 (13.2%) patients. The most common N stage was N0 (n = 794, 69.6%), followed by N1 (147, 12.9%), N2 (198, 17.4%), and N3 (1, 0.1%). Tumor differentiation indicated well differentiation of OSCCs in 680 (59.6%) patients and moderate and poor differentiation in 460 (40.4%). Neck dissection was performed in 849 (74.5%) patients.

Propensity score matching

We performed an overall survival analysis in OSCC patients from 2008 to 2017 to determine the potential prognostic value of OPMD history in OSCC patients. The details were described in the Methods section. Then, 687 patients were analyzed in the current cohort study (Table 1). OSCC patients were divided into OPMD and non-OPMD groups depending on the history of OPMDs in patients. The OPMDs group comprised 129 patients before and after matching, while the non-OPMDs group comprised 558 and 258, respectively. Before propensity score matching, patients in the OPMDs group had a higher proportion of females (50.4% vs. 39.2%, P = 0.021), T1 stage (43.4% vs. 23.8%, P < 0.001), N0 stage (88.4% vs. 70.4%, P < 0.001), well differentiation (63.6% vs. 53.9%, P = 0.047), less neck dissection (57.4% vs. 75.6%, P < 0.001), and shorter hospital length of stay (16 vs. 19, P = 0.001). The current study observed that OSCC patients with OPMD history possessed lower T stage, lesser neck dissection probability, and shorter length of stay. Additionally, the OPMDs group was more likely to be observed in the tongue (67.4% vs. 56.8%, P = 0.027) and buccal mucosa (20.9% vs. 13.1%, P = 0.023). The baseline characteristics were balanced among the two groups after PSM. All SMDs were less than 0.1, suggesting a match balance.

Survival analysis

The study analyzed 387 cases post-matching. The follow-up time of patients ranged between 63 and 110 months. The mean follow-up time was 85.3 ± 42.6 months, with a median follow-up time of 81. According to the last follow-up, 95 (24.5%) died due to any cause, while 81 (20.9%) died of OSCC. The incidence of death due to any reason did not differ significantly between the two groups (23.3% vs. 25.2% in OPMDs and non-OPMDs group, P = 0.676). The 5-year OS was 85.3% (95%CI, 79.4%−91.6%) in the OPMDs group and 78.3% (95% CI, 73.4%−83.5%) within the non-OPMDs group. No significant difference could be observed in OS between the two groups (P = 0.600 by log-rank test) (Fig. 2a).

Concerning DSS, the death rates from OSCC were not significantly different between the two groups (21.7% vs. 20.5% in OPMDs and non-OPMDs group, P = 0.791). The OPMDs group had a 5-year DSS of 85.3% (95%CI, 79.4%−91.6%) and 80.8% (95% CI, 76.1%−85.8%) in the non-OPMDs group. Similarly, no significant difference in DSS was observed between the two groups (P = 0.960 by log-rank test) (Fig. 2b). Other analyses in patients before matching validated these results (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Factors related to OS and DSS

The prognostic factors were screened within the matched cohort. The univariable Cox regression analysis revealed that age, comorbidities, and N stage were significantly associated with OS. After multivariable Cox stepwise regression analysis, patients > 60 years of age (adjusted-HR 5.61, 95%CI, 2.03–15.47, P = 0.001) and N2 stage (adjusted-HR 2.99, 95%CI, 1.38–6.49, P = 0.006) were independent OS predictors (Table 2). Regarding DSS, the unadjusted and adjusted Cox regression analysis results are depicted in Table 3. The data demonstrated that patients > 60 years of age (adjusted-HR 4.54, 95%CI, 1.63–12.63, P = 0.004) and those with higher N stage (N1 stage, adjusted-HR 2.35, 95%CI, 1.12–4.95, P = 0.024; N2 stage, adjusted-HR 3.04, 95%CI, 1.32–7.01, P = 0.009) were independent predictors of DSS. These findings were observed in the unmatched cohort (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3).

Discussion

Limited clinical studies have explored the significant role of OPMD history in the early diagnosis, early intervention, and prognostic assessment of OSCC patients. Herein, a retrospective, single-center cohort study was performed depending on the hospital population to determine the clinicopathological characteristics and long-term prognosis of OSCC with OPMD history. The study indicated that most OSCC patients did not undergo the OPMDs phase. Furthermore, the OPMDs group had similar survival outcomes involving OS and DSS compared to the non-OPMDs group. Continuous follow-up, early detection and early intervention of OPMD patients may be one of the reasons for their good prognosis. Importantly, our study demonstrates that attention given to OPMDs in the early prevention and management of OSCC has been inadequate over past decades. Attention must be paid to the at-risk general population for early diagnosis.

Our statistical data showed that the proportion of OSCC patients with an OPMD history in the sample was 17.0%. Among these patients, 144 (74.2%) had OLK, 41(21.1%) had OLP, 4 (2.1%) had OSF, 3 (1.5%) had erythroplakia, 1 (0.5%) had an oral lichenoid lesion, and 1 (0.5%) had OLK and OLP. A recent research reported that 21.9% of patients from a hospital had an OPMD history [29]. However, based on our data and previous studies, the most common types were OLK and OLP. Interestingly, another population-based study with a larger sample revealed an even lower rate (6.25%) [30]. These differences could be because of different geography, sample selection, and inclusion criteria. We discovered that most patients did not undergo an OPMD phase before OSCC. Previous research demonstrated that the malignant transformation rate of OPMDs was nearly 7.9% [31]. Only a small percentage of OPMDs progressed to OSCC [32]. Therefore, merely focusing on OPMDs is insufficient for early intervention in OSCC cases. The general population with high-risk factors, such as age, gender, smoking, alcohol consumption, betel nut chewing, oral health, etc., is the principal group for oral cancer prevention.

Oral cancer screening could be cost-effective depending on high-risk populations. Previous studies suggested that oral cancer could be commonly seen in males over 50 years old, with regional variations [33, 34]. Hussein et al. reported that the incidence of oral cancer in the young population increased worldwide [35]. The current study observed that the proportion of patients in the age groups 41–50, 51–60, and 61–70 were 24.7%, 26.9%, and 23.0%, with a male-to-female ratio larger than 1.0 in each group (Supplementary Fig. S2). Thus, the general population over 40 years, particularly males with risk factors, are a high-risk population and require additional care. It is recommended that these patients undergo regular examinations for early detection and treatment of OSCC. While seeking medical treatment in hospitals, clinicians must provide these patients with opportunistic screening and suitable advice on quitting bad habits. In addition, policymakers for oral health prevention must be attentive to high-risk populations while developing relevant policies.

Although previous investigations demonstrated that OPMDs were predominantly in males, our data revealed that the OPMDs group had a higher proportion of females than the non-OPMDs group. These results were consistent with previous reports that women with OPMDs possessed a higher propensity for malignancy than men [36, 37]. The underlying mechanisms that induced a higher risk of malignancy in females in OPMDs remained unknown. Undoubtedly, females with OPMDs should undergo closer and regular follow-ups. Besides, the OPMDs group was observed in the tongue and buccal mucosa. One possible explanation was that OPMDs in the tongue and the floor of the mouth had a higher risk of malignant transformation due to excessive exposure to carcinogens [37]. Moreover, our data revealed that over half of the OPMDs group in buccal mucosa had multiple lesions before malignant transformation. Previous studies depicted a higher risk of malignant transformation with multiple lesions [38].

Previous research revealed that OSCC individuals derived from OLP and precursor lesions had better clinicopathologic profiles, including early stage and preferable grade [20, 30]. Additionally, Chaturvedi et al. and Gadbail et al. observed that OSCC associated with OSF showed a better prognostic profile [21, 39, 40]. Our findings highlighted that the OPMDs group had preferable factors for lower T stage, lower N stage, and better grades. However, survival analysis in the current study showed no observable significant difference between the two groups in OS and DSS while conducting a long-term follow-up. This was consistent with Weijers et al. and Akrish et al. [22, 24]. On the other hand, Chaturvedi et al. highlighted that OSCC related to OSF had superior DSS than OSCC without OSF. Still, OSF was not observed to be an independent factor for survival [21]. In contrast, Jäwert et al. demonstrated that regular OPMD patient follow-up contributed to early OSCC detection and better survival outcomes [29]. Indeed, this study was conducted in a 5-year interval, and further investigation for long-term observation is warranted for validation.

No significant difference in survival outcomes was observed between the OPMD and non-OPMD groups. However, the OPMD group had fewer neck dissections and shorter hospital stays. Since hospital length of stay and neck dissection are correlated with quality of life, the OPMD group could have a better quality of life than the non-OPMD group [41, 42]. Continued research involving the quality of life of OSCC with an OPMD history is encouraged.

Finally, this study has specific strengths and weaknesses. To our knowledge, this was the most recent observational investigation of OSCC with OPMD history during a long-term follow-up. A large cohort study allowed for PSM, effectively decreasing the discrepancy of baseline parameters. Some limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the retrospective design led to bias. A limitation of our study is that not all diagnoses underwent centralized pathological review at our institution. A proportion of cases were included based on pathological reports from their original hospitals, as logistical constraints prevented re-evaluation. Nonetheless, we consider these diagnoses reliable for the purpose of this analysis. Therefore, well-designed and prospective studies were recommended for further research to provide reliable evidence. Secondly, multicenter research could help validate these results since it was a single-center study. Thus, our findings must be interpreted with caution. Thirdly, several clinicopathologic variables that could influence outcomes were not considered for analysis, such as depth of invasion (DOI), extranodal extension (ENE), and socioeconomic factors [43, 44]. Therefore, further study can help to verify the replicability of the outcomes. Despite the limitations, these findings should be considered when policymakers develop strategies for oral cancer prevention and treatment.

Conclusion

In summary, patients with an OPMD history had oncologic outcomes similar to those without a history of OPMD. However, most OSCC patients did not undergo a typical OPMD phase. Therefore, the general population over 40 years old, particularly men with high-risk factors, should be given closer attention for the early diagnosis of OSCC.
