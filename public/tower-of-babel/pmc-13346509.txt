Reactivating dead sodium for durable and high-rate anode-free sodium batteries
Authors: Weihao Wang, Jiawei Wang, Qiaonan Zhu, Shuai Dong, Liqiang Wu, Siqi Lyu, Yong Gao, Sicong Wang, Shuaihan Zou, Xinyu Liu, Bin Zhou, Daojun Yang, Jing Zhou, Wei-Li Song, Hua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reactivating dead alkali metal is an effective strategy for extending the lifespan of alkali metal batteries. Until now, relevant works are mainly concentrated in lithium batteries, however, the reactivation of dead Na remains a mystery. Herein, dead Na reactivation is realized by a reverse-pulse-interspersed charging strategy. Transient large reverse currents are incorporated into the battery charging protocol, during which dead Na can be reactivated by the dielectrophoresis effect. Different from those electrochemical protocols for dead lithium reactivation during discharging or rest, this strategy for reactivating dead Na in charging shows practicality. Moreover, this strategy homogenizes Na deposition by mitigating ion concentration polarization, thus further suppressing dead Na accumulation. As a proof of concept, durable and high-rate anode-free sodium batteries are realized by this strategy. Specifically, the Al | |Na4Fe3(PO4)2(P2O7) anode-free coin cell using this strategy achieves a doubled cycle life at 1 C. Meanwhile, an Ah-level Al | |Na4Fe3(PO4)2(P2O7) anode-free pouch battery exhibits high capacity retentions of 80.0% over 830 cycles and 74.6% over 1000 cycles at 2 C. Furthermore, a 180 Wh/kg Al | |Na4Fe3(PO4)2(P2O7) pouch battery delivers a long lifespan at 1 C, demonstrating application potential. This reverse-pulse-interspersed charging strategy paves a practical avenue for high-performance alkali metal batteries.

Introduction

Dead alkali metal, that is, the metal that loses electrical contact with the electrode due to nonuniform metal plating/stripping, is one critical problem hampering the reversibility of metal electrodes1–3. The accumulation of dead alkali metal would not only deplete active metal, but also increase the impedance of the metal electrode, which conjointly undermines the cyclability of alkali metal batteries as well as their high-rate performance1,2,4,5. Therefore, resolving the dead metal problem is essential to improving the electrochemical performance of alkali metal batteries.

Numerous strategies have been developed to suppress the formation of dead metal in alkali metal batteries, such as current collector optimization6–10, electrolyte engineering5,11–16 and electrode interface modification17–21, which have obtained enhanced rate capability and mitigated capacity degradation. Nevertheless, complete prohibition of dead alkali metal formation is still intractable1,2. Therefore, as an alternative approach, reactivating dead metal seems to be vital to realizing high-performance alkali metal batteries. The feasibility of dead alkali metal reactivation has been demonstrated mainly in the field of lithium batteries. For example, a series of redox mediators (I3-/I-22,23, TEMPO/TEMPO-24, MPT/MPT-25) has been adopted to react with dead Li. But the potential significant self-discharge and electrode corrosion greatly impeded their applicability1,25. In the recent four years, some electrochemical protocols for battery operation, for example, rapid stripping protocol3,26, long rest strategy27,28 and transient voltage pulse29,30, have been developed to reactivate dead Li, and success has been achieved. However, compared with reactivating dead Li, dead Na reactivation in Na batteries is more challenging. First, the free dead Na problem would be more pronounced, due to the higher tendency of Na metal in forming whisker deposits and porous stripping morphology, which raises the risk of Na stripping from the bottom and thus generates free dead Na31. Moreover, the narrower electrochemical window of the electrolyte1,2 and higher dissolubility of the solid electrolyte interface (SEI)32,33 in sodium batteries raise additional concerns. In this view, the previously reported protocols for dead Li reactivation may face challenges in their application. For example, for the long rest strategy, the Na metal would suffer from severe corrosion and thus lowered Coulombic efficiency (CE)1,28,34; For the protocols utilizing dynamic polarization or voltage pulse may either exacerbate unstable Na stripping or trigger electrolyte-involved side reactions, thereby severely deteriorating the reversibility of the metal1,31. In consequence, the reactivation of dead Na in sodium batteries is significantly challenged, and its feasibility remains a mystery. Furthermore, these protocols for dead Li reactivation are predominantly implemented during battery discharging or relaxation periods, which largely undermines their operability in practical scenarios. Therefore, it is imperative to develop effective strategies with both universality and practicability for dead Na reactivation.

Herein, dead Na reactivation is realized via a reverse-pulse-interspersed charging (RPIC) strategy, in which brief reverse current pulses are intermittently introduced during the charging process. Based on the dielectrophoresis effect, dead Na moves back onto the negative electrode under the RPIC protocol and thus is finally reactivated. Furthermore, through the RPIC, dead Na reactivation, Na morphology homogenization and concentration polarization mitigation conjointly enhance the cycling and rate performances of AFSBs (Fig. 1). As a proof of concept, the strategy doubled the cycle life of Al | |NFPP anode-free full cell at 1 C compared to its counterpart under the widely used constant-current charging (CCC) protocol. Notably, this charging strategy endows an Ah-level Al | |NFPP anode-free pouch battery with the capacity retentions of 80.0% over 830 cycles and 74.6% over 1000 cycles at 2 C. Moreover, a 3.5 Ah Al | |NFPP pouch battery further demonstrates a long lifespan with a high specific energy of 180 Wh/kg, exhibiting the promise of this strategy for practical application. Inspiringly, this strategy for dead alkali metal reactivation can be readily extended to other alkali metal batteries, demonstrating a viable approach to practical and high-performance alkali metal batteries.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the CCC strategy and the RPIC strategy.CCC leads to concentration polarization, dendrite growth and accumulation of dead Na, whereas RPIC mitigates polarization, promotes homogeneous Na plating and enables reactivation of dead Na.

Results

Effect of RPIC on the cyclability of AFSBs

Firstly, anode-free sodium batteries (AFSBs) are fabricated to probe the effect of RPIC strategy on their cycling performances, which is considered greatly dictated by Na plating/stripping reversibility. Conventional constant-current charging (denoted as CCC) and the as-developed RPIC protocols are applied to Al | |NFPP anode-free full cells, respectively (Fig. 2a–d). To establish RPIC protocol, brief reverse current pulses are incorporated into the battery charging process, in which the amplitude and time period of the reverse current pulse are determined based on the capacity retention of the AFSBs (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Tables 1–3). It is found that the cells with CCC protocol underwent rapid capacity decay at 1 C after 100 cycles and delivered a capacity retention of only 15.17% after 500 cycles (Fig. 2a). In contrast, the cells with RPIC protocol exhibits significantly enhanced cycling performance up to 75.96% capacity retention after 500 cycles (Fig. 2a), with almost doubled cycle life (418 cycle) compared to that of the cells under CCC protocol (215 cycle) at 80% capacity retention. The continuous irreversible loss of active Na under the CCC protocol is reflected by the gradually shortened plateau in the discharge voltage profiles, whereas the RPIC approach effectively prevents this Na depletion with largely retained voltage profiles (Supplementary Fig. 1)35. Abnormally, the CE exceeds 100% (higher capacity of discharge than charge) of the cells under RPIC since the second cycle (Fig. 2b), which may suggest the reactivation of dead Na3, while the cells under CCC exhibit CE values consistently smaller than 100% with significant fluctuation after 300 cycles (Fig. 2b), showing uncontrolled performance degradation. Besides, since reverse current pulses are introduced into the charging process in RPIC, to exclude the possible influence of the difference in average charging current between the two protocols, another RPIC protocol with an identical average charging current to that of the CCC (denoted as AI-RPIC) was also adopted on the AFSBs. It is found that the cells under AI-RPIC exhibited similar performance to those under RPIC (Supplementary Figs. 3–6), which proves that the performance improvement of AFSBs originates from the special RPIC protocol itself rather than the moderate variation in the average specific current.

Moreover, compared to the CCC protocol, the RPIC protocol significantly promoted the cyclability of the AFSBs under various rates ranging from 1 C to 5 C (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Figs. 7–9), specifically delivering a capacity retention of 73.25% after 600 cycles at 2 C, 70.01% after 1000 cycles at 3 C, as well as cyclability even at 5 C. Meanwhile, the CE of the cells under RPIC remains >100% at all the tested rates, indicating its wide rate capability for revival of dead Na (Supplementary Figs. 10–12). Overall, compared with CCC, RPIC enhances the rate and cycle performance of AFSBs, suggesting effective reactivation of dead Na during battery cycling.

Dead Na reactivation

Furtherly, the dead Na reactivation in AFSBs by RPIC was verified both experimentally and theoretically. An in situ optical electrochemical cell was used to observe the movement of dead Na in the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 13). As shown in Fig. 3a and Supplementary movie 1, under cathodic charging with RPIC, a free dead Na gradually moved towards the electrode until it completely merged with the metal on the current collector. A similar phenomenon occurred with even larger dead Na particles (Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary movie 2). These results show that dead Na can be reconnected to the electrode during RPIC. Furthermore, a finite element simulation was conducted to illustrate the movement of dead Na under an electric field29. As the charging time increases, the dead Na is closer to the areas with higher electric field density and ultimately contacts the active Na within 0.5 s (Fig. 3b, d and Supplementary movie 3). Importantly, the movement direction of the dead Na is found to align with the gradient of electric field density regardless of the electric field direction (Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary movie 4). Dead Na accumulation suppression

The continuous formation of dead Na during battery cycling would result in serious dead Na accumulation, the amount of which is a direct index of dead Na reactivation efficiency. Therefore, the amount and form of dead Na in actual AFSBs are investigated by in situ or ex situ characterizations. In situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) was first employed to investigate the evolution of Na deposit on the collector of the AFSBs under CCC and RPIC protocols, respectively. It is found that both CCC and RPIC facilitate Na plating along the (110) plane, yielding a diffraction peak at about 30° (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Fig. 18)5. More importantly, a residual Na peak was found on the post-stripping current collector under the CCC protocol, whereas the same peak is absent for the RPIC counterpart, illustrating suppressed dead Na formation under RPIC. To determine the amount of the accumulated dead Na during battery cycling under CCC and RPIC, respectively, titration-gas chromatography (TGC) was utilized to measure the H2 generated from the reaction between H2O and dead Na (Fig. 4c)43. It is found that compared to the CCC protocol, the RPIC obviously diminished dead Na in the Al| |NFPP full cells after various numbers of cycling (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Figs. 20–22), indicating the higher efficiency of the RPIC protocol for suppressing the dead Na accumulation. In addition, with less dead Na accumulation under RPIC protocol, the AFSBs showed significantly lowered impedance after cycling compared to that under CCC (Supplementary Figs. 23–25 and Supplementary Table 4), indicating improved electrode kinetics by RPIC.

Subsequently, the morphology and distribution of dead Na on the Al current collector of cycled Al| |NFPP coin cells were characterized by the ex situ SEM and EDS (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Figs. 26, 27). For Al | |NFPP coin cells cycling under CCC protocols, large amount of unevenly distributed dead Na was found on the current collectors after Na stripping, whereas its RPIC counterpart shows smooth and surface of current collector with much less Na signals (Fig. 4e), which further supported by the XRD analysis of the collectors (Supplementary Fig. 28) and is consistent with the conclusions from the above experiments for dead Na amount determination. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that RPIC effectively mitigates dead Na accumulation during cycling with promoted electrode kinetics, which is beneficial for the high-rate and long-term cycling of sodium metal batteries.

The effect of RPIC on electrode kinetics

Generally, dead Na is generated from the dendritic Na deposits, which tend to lose electrical connection with the current collector during Na stripping44,45. The formation of dendritic Na on the electrode would be exacerbated under high concentration polarization in the electrolyte, which is the common situation for high-rate charging of Na metal. In this view, mitigation of concentration polarization in the electrolyte would contribute to less dead Na generation and stable high-rate operation of the Na metal. Therefore, the effects of RPIC on concentration polarization were further investigated. First, phase-field modeling was established to analyze the Na+ concentration distribution and the morphology evolution of Na dendrites through different plating protocols. During the charging process, Na+ migrates towards the negative electrode under the combined effects of concentration gradients and electric field-driven forces, and Na deposition occurs upon Na+ reaching the negative electrode surface. Under CCC protocol, where the rate of Na deposition on the electrode surpasses that of Na+ diffusion in the electrolyte, a localized Na+ depletion zone at the interface is formed (Fig. 5a), which would lead to significant electrode polarization as well as Na dendrite formation46,47. In contrast, the reverse current in RPIC protocol effectively homogenizes the Na+ concentration field and mitigates electrode polarization (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 29), thus generating a more uniform Na deposit morphology for prolonged charging process (Fig. 5b). This simulation result is further supported by in situ optical microscopic characterization of the Na deposit under CCC and RPIC, respectively (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, Na| |Al half cells were assembled to measure concentration polarization (ηconc) under different charging protocols, which makes up electrode polarization with another two kinds of polarizations, i.e., electrochemical polarization (ηreac) and Ohmic polarization (ηohm)48. After plating 0.25 mAh Na by different protocols on Al current collectors of the Na| |Al half cells (the current is similar to that of the Al| |NFPP coin cells), a 5-minute rest was applied, and the polarization magnitude was analyzed (Fig. 5d). ηreac and ηohm can recover in a short time, and the voltage recovery during the subsequent rest corresponds to ηconc (Fig. 5e)49. The average ηconc under the two different protocols shows that RPIC effectively reduces concentration polarization, which is more prominent under high current (Fig. 5f). It is noteworthy that the concentration polarization achieved a minimal value when the rest period is set right after the reverse current under RPIC protocol (Supplementary Figs. 30, 31), indicating the important effect of the reverse current pulse on concentration polarization mitigation. Besides, the inhibitory effect of RPIC on concentration polarization is also verified in Al| |NFPP full cells (Supplementary Fig. 32)32,48. Furthermore, the Sand’s times of the Na| |Al half cells were examined, which show 40.13 and 336.05 min for the cells under CCC and RPIC, respectively (Fig. 5g). The extended Sand’s time suggests that RPIC is effective in mitigating concentration polarization and inhibiting dendrite formation50,51.

Moreover, the kinetics of the Na| |Al half cells under RPIC were proved by their smaller direct current inner resistance (DC-IR), compared to their counterparts under CCC protocol (Fig. 5h)52. Besides the electrode polarization during Na plating, the Na stripping kinetics under CCC and RPIC protocols, respectively, are also determined. By analyzing the differential voltage-capacity curve (dV/dQ) of the Na| |Al half cells, the deceleration points during Na stripping were determined. It is found that the decelerated stripping points for the Na electrode under RPIC appear later than those of the CCC-formed Na electrode (Supplementary Fig. 33), indicating more facile Na stripping kinetics of the RPIC-formed Na electrode. In addition, as the state of charge (SOC) increases gradually (a plating capacity of 1 mAh corresponds to 100% SOC), it showed an enlarged difference in Na stripping capacity before the stripping deceleration between the cells under RPIC and CCC protocols, respectively (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Figs. 34, 35), suggesting the advantage of RPIC in high-capacity batteries. The above results verified the effectiveness of the RPIC protocol in improving electrode kinetics, especially in mitigating concentration polarization, thereby effectively inhibiting the formation of dead Na under high-rate charging.

Wide applicability of the dead metal reactivation strategy

Finally, the applicability of dead metal reactivation by charging protocol modulation is explored in diverse battery systems. Firstly, the adaptability of this strategy to diverse positive electrodes for sodium ion batteries is demonstrated by selecting Na3V2(PO4)3 (NVP) and Na(Ni1/3Fe1/3Mn1/3)O2 (NFM) positive electrodes to fabricate anode-free full cells. It was found that after 400 cycles, the capacity retention of the Al| |NVP full cell under RPIC is 19.01% higher than the CCC counterpart (Fig. 6a); For Al| |NFM full cells, RPIC protocol supported high-rate and stable cycling of the cells, whereas CCC protocol results in rapid capacity decay during the initial cycling (Fig. 6b). On the other hand, as the representative of sodium ion batteries, hard carbon (HC)| |NFPP full cells were also fabricated, which showed a capacity retention of 72.79% under RPIC to that of 64.47% under CCC protocol after 1000 cycling. Meanwhile, the RPIC strategy effectively prolongs the cycle life of Na| |NFPP coin cell (Supplementary Fig. 36). These results indicate the wide applicability of this dead Na reactivation strategy to sodium batteries based on diverse positive electrodes and negative electrodes. The applicability of this protocol to other anode-free batteries is also verified, including anode-free lithium batteries and anode-free potassium batteries. Moreover, we conducted a comparative analysis of the advantages and disadvantages of dead alkali metal reactivation strategies based on regulatory protocols (Supplementary Fig. 40). The RPIC strategy not only demonstrates strong performance in practicability and applicability, but also specifically addresses the contradiction between the reactivation efficiency and side effect prevention of the previous strategy. It is noted that the significantly extended cycle life by RPIC is accompanied by a decreased energy efficiency by 16% compared to that of CCC (95%), owing to the incorporation of reverse current. Nevertheless, for practical application, the extra energy consumption by RPIC may well be economically offset by the prolonged device lifetime, and also, further promotion in energy efficiency is viable by protocol optimization. Overall, the RPIC strategy achieved a balance between cycle life and energy efficiency of the batteries, which supports the feasibility of RPIC for practical application.

Furthermore, the application of RPIC in Ah-level pouch batteries has greatly enhanced their cycling and high-rate performance. Specifically, the pouch batteries exhibit high capacity retentions of 80% over 830 cycles and 74.6% capacity retention over 1000 cycles at 2 C under RPIC protocol (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 41). Subsequently, the accumulated dead Na in Al| |NFPP pouch battery after cycling at 2 C was investigated by ultrasonic imaging technology. For the pouch battery cycling under CCC protocols, a large amount of unevenly distributed dead Na was found on the current collectors after Na stripping (higher reflection intensity region), whereas its RPIC counterpart shows much less Na signals (lower reflection intensity region) (Fig. 6e, f). Particularly, a 180 Wh/kg Al| |NFPP pouch battery (loading: 25 mg/cm2) (Supplementary Table 5) demonstrates a long lifespan of over 380 cycles with 86.2% capacity retention (Fig. 6g). Overall, state-of-the-art cycling and rate performance of the AFSBs are realized by dead Na reactivation, and this facile RPIC protocol can be readily applied to practical pouch AFSBs and a wide category of battery systems, showing the practical application promise of this dead metal reactivation strategy (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Table 6)5,6,9,11,13,38,53,54.

In summary, dead Na reactivation in sodium metal batteries is realized through battery charging protocol modulation, as demonstrated by the improved performance of durable and high-rate AFSBs. A reverse-pulse-interspersed charging (RPIC) protocol is imposed on AFSBs, under which the dead Na moves back onto the Na electrode based on the dielectrophoresis effect and is finally reactivated. The successful reactivation of dead Na is verified by in/ex situ morphology and structural characterizations, electrochemical tests, and theoretical simulations. Consequently, the dead Na reactivation, along with diminished dead Na accumulation, improved the morphology of Na deposit and mitigated electrode polarization, which conjointly contributes to high cyclability and rate performances of the AFSBs. Specifically, the cycle life (80% capacity retention) of Al| |Na4Fe3(PO4)2P2O7 (NFPP) anode-free full cell using RPIC was doubled at 1 C, with cyclability even at a high rate up to 5 C. Moreover, the RPIC endows an Ah-level Al| |NFPP pouch battery with a high capacity retention of 80.0% after 830 cycles and 74.6% after 1000 cycles at 2 C. Moreover, a 3.5 Ah Al| |NFPP pouch battery delivers a high specific energy of 180 Wh/kg with a long lifespan at 1 C, demonstrating the promise for practical application of this dead Na reactivation strategy. Inspiringly, the RPIC protocol exhibits universal applicability to a wide variety of sodium batteries and other alkali metal batteries with greatly promoted cyclability. Besides, this strategy holds the practicability to the reported protocols that function in lithium-ion batteries during battery rest or discharging. Overall, the dead Na reactivation strategy developed in this work provides an avenue toward high-performance practical alkali metal batteries.

Methods

Materials

Sodium hexafluorophosphate (NaPF6, battery grade), Lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI, battery grade), Potassium hexafluorophosphate (KPF6, battery grade), diethylene glycol dimethyl ether (G2, 99.8%), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, battery grade), 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE, 99.8%) were purchased from Duoduo Chemical Co., Ltd. Sodium tetrafluoroborate (NaBF4, 98%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Technology Ltd. Diethylene glycol dibutyl ether (D2, 99%) and the metallic sodium blocks (≥99.7%) were purchased from Aladdin. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) (trimethylsiloxy terminated, M.W. 770) and perylene-3,4,9,10-tetracarboxylic dianhydride (PTCDA, 98%) were purchased from Alfa. The electrolyte of NP-002 (1 M NaPF6 in DEC:EC = 1:1 vol% with 5% FEC) was purchased from Duoduo Chemical Co., Ltd. All the above solvents were dehydrated by molecular sieves (4 Å, DoDoChem). A commercial carbon-coated Al foil (The purity of Al is 99.99%, the thickness is 15 μm, and the carbon loading is 0.05 mg/cm2) was purchased from Saibo Electrochemistry.

Preparation of electrolytes, electrodes and cells

The solution of 1 M NaPF6 in G2 was used as the electrolyte for Al| |NFPP, Na| |Al and Al| |NVP cells. The solution of 1.2 M NaFP6 and 0.6 mM NaBF4 in D2 was used as the electrolyte for Al| |NFM cells. The NP-002 was used as the electrolyte for HC| | NFPP cells. The solution of 1 M LiFSI in 1:3 (vol%) DME and TTE was used as the electrolyte for Cu| |LFP and Cu| |NCM cells. The solution of 1.4 M KPF6 in DME with 3% PDMS was used as the electrolyte for Al| |KPTCDA cells. All electrolytes were prepared in an argon (Ar)-filled glovebox at 25 °C (O2 and H2O content <0.01 ppm). Positive electrode Na4Fe3(PO4)2P2O7 (NFPP, ~11.5 mg/cm2), Na3V2(PO4)3 (NVP, ~7.7 mg/cm2), Na(Ni1/3Fe1/3Mn1/3)O2 (NFM, ~7.8 mg/cm2) were procured from Beijing Xibeidongli Technology Co., Ltd. The active materials loading is 94%, 93.8% and 95.5%, respectively. The conductive additive (super P) loading is 2%, 2% and 2.25%, respectively. The binder (PVDF, molecular weight: 1.1 million) loading is 4%, 4.2% and 2.25%, respectively. The prepared positive electrode slurry was uniformly dispersed in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP, Aladdin) and then coated onto the Al foil using an automatic coating machine. It was subsequently dried in a drying chamber at 120 °C. Positive electrode electrodes LiFePO4 (LFP, ~11.7 mg/cm2) and LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM, ~4 mg/cm2) were procured from Xiamen TOB New Energy Co., Ltd. The preparation method is as follows: 95 wt% active material, 3 wt% carbon black and 2 wt% PVDF are mixed and stirred in NMP, then coated on the Al foil by an automatic coating machine, and subsequently dried under vacuum at 80 °C overnight. The PTCDA positive electrodes were fabricated by mixing PTCDA, Super-P and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, Acros) at a mass ratio of 8:1:1 in deionized water. The mixture was cast on Al and dried at 60 °C for 12 h in a vacuum oven. Without further specification, all the anode-free full coin cells were assembled in the argon (Ar)-filled glovebox at 25 °C with positive electrode (11 mm), two layers of Celgard 2500 separator (The lateral dimension is 19 mm, the thickness is 25 μm, the theporosity is 55% and average pore size is 0.064 μm), negative electrode (19 mm) and 40 μL electrolyte. The anode-free pouch batteries (Cathode mass loading: 10, 20 or 25 mg/cm2) were assembled in a dry room with the electrolyte of 1 M NaPF6 in G2. The parameters of the pouch battery are shown in the Supplementary Table 5. The pouch batteries were degassed after aging for 48 h at 25 °C and formation cycles. And the pouch batteries were tested under a pressure of 1 MPa pressed by a splint. The sodium foil was prepared through the rolling and pressing process in an Ar gas-filled glove box (O2 and H2O content <0.01 ppm). The metallic sodium blocks were pre-treated with a surgical blade before rolling to remove surface minerals and oxide layers. The thickness of the Na discs is 0.6 mm by adjusting the roller gap between the two rotating cylindrical rollers. Na| |NFPP coin cells consist of a positive electrode (11 mm), two layers of Celgard 2500 separator, Na discs (19 mm) and 40 μL electrolyte. Na| |Al half cells consist of Na discs (11 mm), two layers of Celgard 2500 separator, Al (19 mm) and 40 μL electrolyte.

Electrochemical tests

All the coin cells were cycled by the Neware battery test system at 25 °C (BTS-5 V, 50 mA). The N/P ratios of all AFSBs in this work were set to 0, and no pre-plating was conducted before assembly of the full cells. Specifically, Al| |NFPP (HC| | NFPP or Na| |NFPP), Al| |NVP, Al| |NFM coin cells were cycled with different rates after activation (Supplementary Fig. 42) under 0.1 C and 0.2 C at 2–3.6 V (1 C = 129 mA/g), 2–3.65 V (1 C = 117 mA/g) and 2–4 V (1 C = 150 mA/g), respectively. Cu| |LFP, Cu| |NCM cells were cycled after activation (Supplementary Fig. 42) under 0.1 C at 2.4–3.8 V (1 C = 170 mA/g) and 2.8–4.3 V (1 C = 200 mA/g), respectively. Prior to assembling Al| |KPTCDA cells, the PTCDA was pre-potassiated in K| | PTCDA. Then, pre-potassiated PTCDA (KPTCDA) with the average mass loading of ~3.5 mg/cm2 was used as the positive electrode to assemble Al| |KPTCDA full cells and these cells were cycled at 1.5–3.6 V (1 C = 130 mA/g). Furthermore, all pouch batteries were cycled by the Neware battery test system at 25 °C. In addition, the 1 C is defined as 1.4 A in the Al| |NFPP pouch batteries (10 mg/cm2 active material loading) and 3 A in the Al| |NFPP pouch batteries (25 mg/cm2 active material loading). In all Na| |Al half cells experiment (0–1 V), 5 pre-cycles at 0–1 V were conducted at 0.1 mA. During the dead Na formation cycle, 1 mAh of Na with a specific current of 10 mA was planted on Al and stripped away. During the next cycle, another 1 mAh of Na with 1 mA was planted on Al by CCC or RPIC and stripped away to 1 V. In addition, in cells without pre-formed dead Na, the dead Na formation cycle was not performed, while the other conditions remained unchanged. For the deceleration point at Na stripping test, each cell was planted with Na for every SOC 5 at 1 mA and completely stripped to 1 V at 0.1 mA. Repeat this process until the SOC reaches 100%. By analyzing the dV/dQ values during the stripping phase to find deceleration points, the plating morphology in different methods was evaluated. Sand’s time was conducted on the Na | |Al half cells by different plating methods at 10 mA. All the EIS curves were performed on Solartron Analytical 1400 CellTest System in the frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz (three points per decade of frequency) with an amplitude of 10 mV by potentionstatic signal after maintaining open circuit voltage for 600 s at quasi-stationary potential. Materials characterization

An in situ optical cell was assembled in the glove box, in which NFPP and Al were used as two electrodes. The pulse charge/discharge was performed with Solartron Analytical 1400 CellTest System and was imaged simultaneously using an optical microscope with a camera (OLYMPUS, BX53M) at 25 °C. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed using a Rigaku D/MAX 7000 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). In situ XRD tests used a specially designed Swagelok cell, and it was conducted by the LAND CT3002A battery test system for charging/discharging with CCC and RPIC between 2–3.6V at 25 °C. The patterns were collected with an interval of 10 min at a rate of 1° per minute, and each acquisition required 2 min. In the TGC test for determining dead Na content, the Al in cells after fully discharged reacts with 1 mL H2O to produce H2. Then, 1 mL of the mixed gas is taken with a gas-tight syringe to the Shimadzu GC-2014C system to measure the H₂ concentration. The dead Na content is calculated by H₂ concentration. The Al characterization was conducted using a JEOL JSM-7500F field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM), which operated under vacuum transfer. The distribution of elements on the electrode surface was analyzed using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. The ultrasonic images were acquired on the ultrasonic scanner (FELI-1200, Beijing Wanlong Essential Technology Co., Ltd), and the ultrasonic frequency is 2.5 MHz.

Computational details

In the COMSOL simulation, the Butler-Volmer equation is used to describe the electrode dynamics on the electrode surface embedded in the electrolyte. In order to show the rough effect of reverse current on the growth of Na metal dendrites, the constant and reverse specific current were changed, which qualitatively led to different Na deposition behavior. For constant current, the deposition current is set to 2 mA/cm2. For pulsed current stimulation, a cycle consists of a 5 s forward pulse followed by a 0.5 s reverse pulse, with this cycle repeating continuously. As for the motion of dead Na in the electrolyte, the particle tracing for the fluid flow interface was used. Particle motion is driven by the spatially nonuniform electric field in the electrolyte via the phenomenon of dielectrophoresis29. The simplified model by COMSOL Multiphysics can simulate the Na+ concentration evolution, Na metal deposition behavior, and the recovery of dead Na. The deposition behavior of Na metal was simulated through the interface of the cubic current distribution model, and the square area was 50.0 × 50.0 μm55,2,56.
