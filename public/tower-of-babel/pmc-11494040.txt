Grip strength, genetic predisposition, and Incident Parkinson’s disease: a prospective cohort study in the UK Biobank
Authors: Wei Hu, Chun-Hua Zhao, Yue-Qing Huang, Bao-Peng Liu, Cun-Xian Jia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo examine the association and modifiable risk factors between grip strength (GS) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) incidence considering genetic factors, a total of 411,648 individuals without PD at baseline from the UK Biobank were included. GS was measured by a hydraulic dynamometer. The polygenic risk score assessed the genetic predisposition. Multivariable Cox regression models were performed. During a median follow-up of 12.3 years, 2409 individuals developed PD. Compared with those with high GS, low-GS individuals had a 58.5% increased risk of PD (42.7%–76.1%), and 16.3% of this excess risk could be explained by adjusted risk factors. Low GS and high genetic predisposition contribute to the highest PD risk in an additive interaction. We observed that low GS was associated with higher PD incidence, particularly among individuals with high genetic predisposition. In addition to enhancing GS, interventions targeting risk factors (e.g., unhealthy lifestyles) might also reduce the excess risk.Subject terms: Parkinson's disease, Risk factors

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD), the second most prevalent and fastest-growing neurological disorder, is typified by symptoms such as bradykinesia, muscle rigidity, and tremor, significantly burdening both individuals and society1. In 2019, more than 10 million individuals globally were afflicted by PD2. Predominantly arising from the demise of dopaminergic neurons, PD follows an irreversible course with no available cure, underscoring the urgent need for primary prevention1. Yet, most of the recognized predictors for PD are either non-modifiable (e.g., age and gender) or challenging to intervene (e.g., air pollutants)3. Hence, identifying modifiable predictors is paramount.

The role of muscle fitness is increasingly acknowledged as a modifiable predictor in preventing neurological disorders. Grip strength (GS) serves as a non-invasive, affordable, and accessible measure of muscle fitness. Although studies have reported that GS is associated with neurological disorders4,5(e.g., stroke and dementia), extrapolation to PD remains sparse. Existing studies on this subject have primarily been undertaken in cross-sectional settings6–8. To our knowledge, only a few surveys have used a longitudinal design9–12. A UK investigation used a dichotomous GS thereby preventing the assessment of a dose-response relationship10. Another study’s findings implemented only in males may not be applicable to the general population9. However, studies exploring the prospective association between GS and PD incidence in a large sample of the general population with long-term follow-up remain limited. Notably, none of these studies explored the underlying this association9–12. Although the exact biological mechanism is still unknown, decreased secretion of myokine, grey matter volume, and dopamine might be potential biological mechanisms linking low GS and PD risk9,13,14. Moreover, numerous studies have established that low GS is associated with unfavorable socioeconomic status (SES)15, unhealthy lifestyles16, and poor physical17 and psychological health18. Also, these factors are acknowledged as predictors of PD19. Therefore, we speculated that these factors might explain the increased risk of PD resulting from low GS. However, data-driven evidence is still lacking. Quantifying the contribution of these risk factors in the association could facilitate the development of targeted interventions to lower the risk of PD in individuals with low GS.

Moreover, current longitudinal studies examining the association rarely considered the role of genetic predisposition to PD9–12. PD incidence is the consequence of a sophisticated interplay of genetic and environmental factors19. A large meta-analysis has revealed that around 16–36% of the risk of PD may be ascribed to genetic factors20. Previous studies have evidenced that genetic predisposition may modify the associations of GS with neurological outcomes4,21, but have not yet included PD. Therefore, we hypothesized that genetic predisposition might modify the association between GS and PD incidence. Exploring the joint and interactive effects of GS and genetic factors could inform risk stratification and precision prevention of PD.

Utilizing a large population-based cohort, we sought to longitudinally examine the association between GS and the incidence of PD and further explore the contribution of various modifiable risk factors to this association. We also investigate the joint and interactive associations of GS and genetic predisposition with the risk of PD.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study utilized data from the UK Biobank, an ongoing prospective cohort that recruited over 500,000 middle-aged and older participants aged 37–73 years who completed touchscreen questionnaires, underwent physical examinations, and provided biological information across England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010. A detailed description of the cohort is available elsewhere22. In brief, the cohort aims to investigate the sociodemographic, lifestyle, environmental, and genetic determinants of various intricate diseases. The UK Biobank project received ethical approval from the Northwest Multi-center Research Ethics Committee (MREC reference: 21/NW/0157). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before recruitment.

In the study, we first excluded participants with incomplete GS or genetic information (n = 19,454). Participants who were withdrawn or lost to follow-up and those with PD at baseline were further excluded (n = 2100). Participants with missing covariates were also excluded (n = 69,212). A summary of missing information was provided in Supplementary Table 1. Finally, a total of 411,618 eligible participants were incorporated into the analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Definition of incident PD

Incident PD cases were defined by International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes G20 (Data category: 2406) among the “First occurrence fields” of the UK Biobank, as identified by integrating data from self-reported medical conditions, primary care, hospital admission, and death registrations. The date of PD diagnosis was the earliest recorded date across diverse sources. The follow-up time was computed as the time interval from the recruitment date to the date of PD diagnosis, death, or the censoring date (31 October 2022 for England, 31 August 2022 for Scotland, and 31 May 2022 for Wales), whichever occurred first.

Measurement of GS

GS was measured by a Jamar J00105 Hand Hydraulic Dynamometer capable of assessing up to 90 kg of isometric GS. During the UK Biobank baseline survey, each participant was instructed to sit upright in a chair while maintaining a 90-degree angle with their elbows and to squeeze the hand dynamometer as forcefully as possible for around 3 seconds. The same test protocol was used to measure the GS of each hand and subsequently recorded in absolute units of kilograms. Consistent with previous studies23,24, the current analyses used the mean values for both hands and categorized them into low, intermediate, and high GS according to age- and sex-specific thresholds (Supplementary Table 2).

Assessment of genetic predisposition

The polygenic risk score (PRS) for PD, sourced from the PRS release in the UK Biobank Research Analytics Platform, was employed to assess the genetic predisposition of participants for PD. The study applied the standard PRS for PD (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/field.cgi?id=26260), which is calculated by merging multiple external GWAS sources. More detailed information on the PRS can be found in previous studies25–27. Briefly, participants were genotyped multiple times using either the UK BiLEVE Axiom Array or the UK Biobank Axiom Array, following quality control guidelines. The PRS set was constructed using a Bayesian approach by combining information from multiple ancestors and related traits while adjusting for age, sex, and ancestry principal components. In line with previous studies4,21,27, participants were categorized into three groups of low, intermediate and high genetic predisposition based on the PD-PRS tertiles.

Assessment of covariates

A range of risk factors that might confound or mediate the association between GS and PD were considered as covariates in this study4,5,9,18,21,24,28, including demographic, SES, lifestyle, psychological, and physiological factors. Demographic factors included age (continuous), sex, and ethnicity (white vs. others). SES comprised education (college or university vs. others), employment (employed vs. unemployed), and Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI, continuous). The TDI covered information on social class, employment and housing, and reflected area-level deprivation, with higher scores indicating higher deprivation29. Lifestyles consisted of smoking, alcohol consumption, waist circumference (WC), physical activity (PA), and sedentary and sleep duration. Building on previous studies29–31, each lifestyle was treated as a binary variable (healthy vs. unhealthy). The unhealthy categories encompassed current smokers, daily or almost daily alcohol consumption, <7 or >8 hours/day of sleep duration, WC ≥ 94 cm for men or ≥80 cm for women, < 150 min/week of moderate-intensity PA or <75 min/week of vigorous-intensity PA, and ≥4 hours/day of sedentary duration. Psychological factors covered loneliness, depression, and a history of psychiatric services. Loneliness was evaluated by two questions validated in previous UK Biobank studies28. The Two-item Patient Health Questionnaire was employed to assess depression32. Hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), stroke, body mass index (BMI, continuous), heart failure, and heart disease were regarded as indicators of physical health. More detailed information on these factors can be seen in Supplementary Table 3.

Statistical analyses

ANOVA for continuous variables (expressed as mean ± standard deviation, SD) and chi-square tests for categorical variables (reported as frequency and percentage) were conducted to describe baseline characteristics by GS or incident PD. After the proportional hazards assumption was not violated by the Schoenfeld residual test, the Cox proportional hazards models with follow-up time as a timescale were utilized to explore the association of GS, and genetic susceptibility, with risk of incident PD, with results presented as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

First, the GS was processed as continuous variables to calculate HR per 1 kg increment. To estimate the risk of incident PD ascribed to low GS, we then analyzed GS as categorical variables, considering the highest GS (tertile 1) as the reference group. The linear trends were tested by designating the tertiles of GS as continuous variables. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was fitted to examine the dose-response relationship between absolute GS and PD incidence. Also, the cumulative hazard of PD in the GS subgroup was plotted using the Kaplan-Meier (KM) method. Similarly, the association between genetic predisposition and incident PD was examined. To gauge the contribution of the included risk factors in explaining the GS-PD association, the percentage of excess risk mediated (PERM(%))28 was calculated by (HRModel 1 – HRModel >1) / (HRModel 1 − 1) × 100. Similar methods have been used in previous studies based on UK Biobank data28,33. All models included demographic covariates. Model 1 included only demographic covariates; Model 2 additionally included SES; Model 3 added lifestyle factors to Model 1; Model 4 tested the role of psychological risk factors; Model 5 examined the role of physiological factors; Model 6 mutually adjusted PRS and GS; and Model 7 included all risk factors.

To investigate the extent to which the heightened risk of PD due to genetic predisposition can be attenuated by increased GS, we modeled the joint association of nine mutually exclusive groups. Participants with high GS and low PRS were utilized as the reference group. Also, the GS-PD association was estimated at different PRS stratifications. Multiplicative interaction was tested by incorporating cross-product terms of GS and PRS in the model. The likelihood test was applied to test the significance of the interaction term. An additive interaction model was constructed to assess whether and to what extent genetic susceptibility and GS would affect PD incidence in a synergistic manner. The relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and the attribution proportion due to interaction (AP) were adopted to assess the additive interaction. If an additive interaction exists, the 95% CI for RERI and AP will not contain zero34.

To enhance the robustness of our findings, several additional analyses were conducted. First, studies indicated that factors such as caffeine and tea intake, family history of PD and dementia, and baseline mental disorders might be potential confounders in the association35,36. Consequently, we further controlled for these factors. Second, to mitigate misclassification and reverse causation bias, we eliminated participants diagnosed with dementia at baseline, as well as cases of dementia and PD occurring within the initial two years of follow-up9. Third, to obtain ample follow-up duration and account for the higher prevalence of PD in the aging population28, participants below 50 years old at baseline were excluded. Fourth, covariates exhibiting missing values were imputed utilizing multiple imputations technique31. Fifth, considering the potential impact related to death events, we re-evaluated the associations using the competing risk model proposed by Fine and Grey37. Sixth, as performed in previous research24,38, we also employed relative GS or Fried-defined muscle weakness as the primary exposure. Seventh, the maximum GS reflected in the highest value for both hands was used to reanalyze the association. Eighth, since participants with a history of stroke may influence GS, we excluded participants with a history of stroke at baseline in the sensitivity analyses. Ninth, the association was reanalyzed using the quartile categorization of GS as the primary exposure. Furthermore, we re-analyzed the GS-PD association using the algorithmically defined outcomes provided by the UK Biobank. Moreover, we examined the potential moderating role of sex, age, SES, smoking, alcohol consumption, WC, PA, sedentary duration, sleep, loneliness, and depression on this association. Furthermore, to minimize concerns about reverse causation and explore whether GS could predict short- or long-term risk of PD, we performed association analyses stratified by follow-up period (<10 years and ≥10 years).

All analyses were conducted by SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R Statistical Software (version 4.0.2). A two-tailed P value lower than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The analytical samples consisted of 411,648 participants [mean age (SD), 56.5 (8.1) years; 46.0% male], with 2409 cases of incident PD recorded over a median follow­up of 12.3 years (interquartile range, 11.4–13.1 years; 4,903,617 person-years). The number of incident PD cases per 10,000 person-years in our study population was approximately 4.9. Also, 29.7% (n = 122,398) of the population was categorized as having low GS, 38.5% (n = 158,432) as intermediate GS, and 31.8% (n = 130,818) as high GS. Additionally, the percentage of PD incidence was 0.8% (n = 940), 0.6% (n = 888), and 0.4% (n = 581) in the low, intermediate, and high GS groups, respectively. The baseline characteristics of participants across the GS groups are summarized in Table 1. Participants with higher GS tended to be younger, male, and white. They also had a higher SES, as reflected by a greater likelihood of being employed, higher education levels, and lower TDI. Additionally, these individuals exhibited healthier lifestyle habits, such as being non-smokers, maintaining healthy sleep duration, being physically active, having a healthier WC, and spending less time in sedentary behavior. In contrast, those with lower GS were more likely to have worse physical health, as evidenced by higher prevalence of hypertension, DM, heart failure, stroke, and heart diseases, as well as more frequent psychological issues, such as loneliness, depression, and a history of psychiatric care. We obtained similar findings when describing baseline characteristics according to incident PD (Supplementary Table 4).

Association of GS with incident PD and the contribution of risk factors

RCS did not present a non-linear association between GS and incident PD (P for nonlinear = 0.213) (Supplementary Fig. 2). The KM plot revealed significant differences in the cumulative incidence of PD across GS groups, with the highest cumulative hazard in the low GS group (Supplementary Fig. 3). The risk of incident PD was 2.4% lower per 1 kg increment in GS (HR, 0.976; 95% CI, 0.971–0.982) (Table 2). In the Model 1, the HR for the incidence of PD was 1.243 (95% CI, 1.119–1.380) and 1.699 (95% CI, 1.531–1.884) for individuals with intermediate and low GS compared with those with high GS. Further adjustment for covariates attenuated the association. Among all risk factor sets, physical factors explained the largest proportion of the association (7.7%), followed by lifestyle (6.2%) and psychological factors (4.7%). The association was reduced by 16.3% overall in the fully adjusted Model 7 (low vs. high GS: HR (95% CI) = 1.585(1.427–1.761), P for trend < 0.001; intermediate vs. high GS: HR (95% CI) = 1.220(1.099–1.355), P for trend < 0.001) (Table 2). Except for education, smoking, and WC, all individual risk factors might explain this association to varying degrees (PERM: 0.1%-3.6%) (Supplementary Table 5). Among the included four factor sets (including SES, lifestyle, psychological, and physiological factors), employment, PA, depression, and DM were the largest explainers of this association, respectively (Supplementary Table 5). We observed a similar association pattern between PRS and PD risk (Supplementary Table 6).

Interaction and joint analysis of GS and genetic predisposition

Compared with those with high GS and low genetic predisposition, participants with low GS and high genetic predisposition had the highest risk of PD (HR, 3.592; 95% CI, 2.936–4.396) (Fig. 1), although no significantly multiplicative interaction was found (P for interaction = 0.814). In each stratum of genetic predisposition, the associations between GS and PD incidence were similar (Fig. 2). Table 3 lists the additive interaction results, indicating the presence of a significant additive interaction (RERI = 0.611, 95% CI: 0.115–1.106; AP = 0.170, 95% CI: 0.035–0.305). The highest risk of PD in individuals with high genetic predisposition and low GS can be explained by 17% by the additive interaction.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

The associations of GS and PRS with incident PD were not substantially modified after further adjustment for covariates (Supplementary Table 7). Excluding individuals with dementia diagnosed at baseline (n = 375) or who developed PD or dementia in the two years prior to follow-up (n = 2465) or who were younger than 50 years (n = 97,142) did not affect the robustness of the associations (Supplementary Table 8). When re-analyzed using imputed covariates and competitive hazard models, the findings remained robust (Supplementary Tables 9 and 10). Associations obtained when re-analyzed using relative GS and muscle weakness as the primary exposure were consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Tables 11 and 12). The robustness of the association was not modified when using the maximum GS of both hands as the primary exposure (Supplementary Table 13). The findings remained robust after excluding participants with stroke at baseline (n = 8433) (Supplementary Table 14). The quartile-based sensitivity analysis yielded similar findings to the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 15). Using algorithmically defined PD incidence did not change the robustness of the association (Supplementary Table 16). The association was similar across subgroups, and no significant moderation role was detected (all P for interaction > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 17). The findings stratified by follow-up period demonstrated that the association between GS and incident PD remained stable during both short- and long-term follow-up (Supplementary Table 18).

Discussion

Building on a large-scale prospective cohort, we observed that low GS was associated with higher PD incidence. The joint test unveiled that the incidence of PD was highest in low GS individuals with high genetic predisposition. Of note, this study provided first-hand evidence that low GS and high genetic predisposition to PD interact to contribute a higher incidence of PD in an additive manner. Poor SES, unhealthy lifestyle, and adverse psychological and physiological performance collectively might explain 16.3% of the increased risk of PD associated with low GS.

Our findings indicated that GS was significantly and inversely associated with incident PD, which supported previous research employing cross-sectional designs6,7. A case-control study that included 273 participants (117 PD cases) reported significantly lower GS in PD patients than in healthy controls6. A cross-sectional study implemented in 79 patients with PD found that reduced GS was associated with increased PD severity7. To date, only one longitudinal study in Sweden had specifically reported that low GS was associated with an elevated risk of PD10. However, this Swedish study exclusively involved males and the relatively young age at which PD was diagnosed, thus limiting the applicability of the findings10. Furthermore, in exploring frailty and PD risk, Zheng et al. found that low GS (a component of frailty) was associated with PD risk9, which was consistent with our sensitivity analysis. Nevertheless, the study only considered the dichotomized GS9, which may hinder analyzing the dose-response relationship with outcome. Supporting the findings of our study, a recent prospective investigation also corroborated this association, but did not consider the role of genetic factors11.

Previous studies have examined the interaction of GS and genetic factors leading to neurological outcomes21,24, but rarely extrapolated to PD. Our findings extended the existing evidence by elucidating that the risk of PD was similar in low-GS individuals across all genetic susceptibility strata, which underlies the benefits of improving GS for the whole population. Although previous studies examined the interaction between GS and genetic factors on PD12, the study revealed for the first time that GS and genetic predisposition interact in an additive model leading to the incidence of PD. We observed that individuals with low GS and high genetic predisposition had the highest risk of PD, with 17% of the risk ascribable to the additive interaction, hinting that the interaction effect of simultaneous exposure to low GS and high genetic predisposition was stronger than the sum of their individual effects. It is therefore reasonable to postulate that high GS might somehow mitigate the detrimental impact of high genetic susceptibility to PD. Additive interactions are known to have more public health significance. Our findings emphasised the imperative to prioritize monitoring the GS of populations with high genetic predisposition. Implementing targeted preventive measures to enhance their GS becomes crucial for reducing the risk of PD.

Although the exact mechanism underlying the increased risk of developing PD associated with low GS remains unclear, several potential biological mechanisms might explain the association. First, low GS was associated with neurobiological markers such as reduced grey matter volume and dopamine deficiency13, which are involved in the incidence and development of PD9. Second, decreased muscle strength might result in decreased release of myokines associated with neuroprotective roles, thereby raising the risk of PD14. Additionally, low GS might be associated with elevated risk of PD through inflammatory disturbances (e.g., higher levels of C-reactive protein)39,40. In addition to these potential biological mechanisms, the study quantified for the first time the contribution of SES, lifestyle, physiological and psychological risk factors in the association between GS and PD incidence. In line with prior studies16,41, we found that unhealthy lifestyles could partially explain the association. Muscle weakness defined by low GS (dichotomized) has been established to increase the risk of mortality through unhealthy lifestyles16. Unhealthy lifestyles are known to be risk factors for PD41. Additionally, we speculated that low GS might be related to the higher incidence of PD by worsening psychological health as this association was attenuated after adjusting for psychological factors (e.g. depression). Several previous studies have reported GS as a predictor of depression incidence and the elevated risk of PD associated with depression18,24,35,42,43, which could justify our speculation. Notably, this association was most diminished after considering several common chronic diseases, implying that a decline in GS was more likely to contribute to PD incidence through pathways such as inflammation and metabolism. Of these, DM contributed most significantly. In favor of this finding, a review including 10 cohort studies revealed that low GS was an independent risk factor for DM17. A large meta-analysis reported a 27% increased relative risk of PD in diabetics compared to non-diabetic individuals44. It would be warranted to implement further studies to validate these potential mediators identified in the study and explore additional mediating mechanisms that could inform the development of tailored interventions.

The large sample size, extended follow-up, and sufficient adjustment for covariates, including genetic factors, are primary strengths of this study. Multiple sensitivity and subgroup analyses enhanced the reliability of our findings. The observed interaction facilitated the precision prevention targeted at-risk populations. The identification of explanatory factors might facilitate the formulation of targeted interventions. The study has certain limitations. First, while studies have indicated replicable risk factor associations in the UK Biobank45, the low response rate (5.5%) may still introduce selection bias, potentially impacting representativeness45. Second, most risk factors are self-reported making recall bias inevitable. Third, the impact of residual confounders is unavoidable in observational studies. Fourth, the nature of observational studies makes it impossible to infer causation or to rule out reverse causation. Since PD has a long prodromal period, usually lasting 15 to 20 years46, during which subtle motor deficits (e.g., mild loss of muscle strength) may occur and may affect GS before the diagnosis of PD47, reverse causality may still exist. Therefore, caution is warranted in interpreting the association between GS and PD observed in this study. However, by excluding individuals diagnosed with PD within the first two years of follow-up, our sensitivity analysis may partially mitigate this concern. Furthermore, the results of our stratified analyses by follow-up period revealed that GS was still significantly and negatively associated with the risk of incident PD in individuals with longer follow-up (≥ 10 years), suggesting that reverse causality could not fully interpret our findings. Future well-designed clinical trials or Mendelian randomization studies are required to investigate causal temporality of this association. Fifth, missing data on risk factors considered as covariates were excluded reducing the sample size, although when the associations were replicated using the imputed complete data, the findings were similar. Sixth, the included participants were predominantly white (95.9%), thereby warranting replication of the findings in other ethnic groups. Seventh, data availability limitations prevented us from exploring the long-term effects of changes in GS over time on PD incidence. Future studies are warranted to address this point using repeated measures data with long-term follow-up. Eighth, although a previous study has confirmed the effectiveness of the method of integrating health records from multiple sources for the diagnosis of PD48, the diagnosis of PD in the study was not confirmed by neurologists using standardized clinical criteria, which might result in imprecise effect estimates. Future replication of these findings in patients with PD diagnosed by neurologists using standardized clinical criteria is warranted. Finally, the risk factors considered in the study only explained less than one-fifth of this association. Therefore, further studies are warranted to explore more mediating factors linking the association.

In summary, the prospective cohort study revealed that low GS was significantly associated with a higher incidence of PD, especially among individuals with high genetic predisposition. The excess risk of PD in individuals with low GS could be partially attributed to adverse SES, unfavorable lifestyles, and lower levels of physiological and psychological health.
