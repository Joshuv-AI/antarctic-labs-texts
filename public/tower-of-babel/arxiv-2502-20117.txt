Eigenvector-Based Sensitivity Analysis of Contact Patterns in Epidemic Modeling.
Evans Kiptoo Korir, Zsolt Vizi

Abstract
Understanding how age-specific social contact patterns and susceptibility influence infectious disease transmission is crucial for accurate epidemic modeling. This study presents an eigenvector-based sensitivity analysis framework to quantify the impact of age-structured interactions on disease spread. By applying perturbation analysis to the Next Generation Matrix, we reformulate the basic reproduction number, ℛ 0 \mathcal{R}_{0} , as a generalized eigenproblem, enabling the identification of key age group interactions that drive transmission. Using real-world COVID-19 contact data from Hungary, we demonstrate the framework’s ability to highlight critical transmission pathways. We compare these findings with results obtained earlier using Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) and Partial Rank Correlation Coefficients (PRCC), validating the effectiveness of our approach. Additionally, we extend the analysis to contact structures in the UK and British Columbia, Canada, providing broader epidemiological insights. This work enhances our understanding of demographic interactions in epidemic propagation and offers a robust methodological foundation for improving infectious disease modeling and informing public health interventions.

Introduction

The transmission of infectious diseases, such as COVID-19, is mainly driven by patterns of human social interaction and contact
[
13
,
17
,
22
]
. Although close contact remains a critical factor for person-to-person transmission, other factors, such as local and global travel and daily commutes, contribute significantly to the spread of diseases across regions. Estimating these social interactions, often represented by contact matrices based on proximity data, poses challenges—mainly when data is limited to a subset of the population
[
10
,
17
,
20
]
. Furthermore, while contact tracing applications notify users about their exposure to infected individuals, they face significant limitations due to privacy concerns and data reliability issues
[

.

Contact matrices are vital for parameterizing epidemiological models, especially when age is crucial in transmission risk and disease severity, as demonstrated during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic
[
19
,
25
]
. Studies have shown that age-related differences in susceptibility, alongside patterns of homophily (age-similar contacts), significantly influence the spread of infections. The age distribution of infections also shapes the overall disease burden, as different age groups exhibit varying susceptibilities to severe illness and mortality
[
3
,
15
,
24
,
27
]
. However, contact matrices often have limited applicability over time and in different geographic regions
[
18
]
. Behavioral factors, such as changes in daily mobility, interactions, and adherence to protective measures, fluctuate throughout a pandemic. Non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), like lockdowns, school closures, and mask mandates, profoundly influence these patterns. Social factors such as fear of infection, distrust in governmental policies, and misinformation can further alter contact behaviors, sometimes resulting in counterproductive responses like protests against public health regulations
[
6
]
.

Several comprehensive studies have attempted to quantify social contact patterns in various settings and countries
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
. These studies emphasize assortative mixing (contact within the same demographic groups) and other factors, such as intergenerational interactions, which are vital for understanding epidemic spread. The variability in contact frequency across age groups can substantially affect key epidemiological parameters, such as the outbreak’s temporal dynamics, final epidemic size, and the basic reproduction number,
\mathcal{R}_{0}
. Sensitivity analysis is widely used to evaluate how changes in input parameters impact such models.

Sensitivity analysis is a critical tool in epidemic modeling, offering insight into how parameter variations affect a model’s outcomes. Techniques such as Monte Carlo simulations randomly sample parameter values to generate various possible outcomes, reflecting the inherent variability in model predictions. More advanced methods, such as Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) and Partial Rank Correlation Coefficients (PRCC), provide greater efficiency and accuracy by evenly sampling the parameter space and quantifying the influence of each parameter on model outputs, respectively. These methods help researchers identify the most influential factors driving disease spread, informing more targeted public health interventions
[
47
]
.

The basic reproduction number denoted as
\mathcal{R}_{0}
, is a critical quantity in epidemic modeling. This parameter represents the average number of secondary infections produced by a single infected individual in a fully susceptible population. Understanding
\mathcal{R}_{0}
is essential for determining the potential for disease spread and assessing the impact of control measures. Analytical sensitivity analysis methods, such as those based on the Next Generation Matrix, provide a mathematical framework to understand how model parameters contribute to
\mathcal{R}_{0}
and other key outputs. For example,
[
4
]
applied formal perturbation analysis to the Next Generation Matrix to analyze the early spread of SARS-CoV-2 in Belgium. Such approaches benefit simpler models, where parameter interactions are more linear and can be explored without extensive computational simulations. However, they may become less effective in complex models with nonlinear parameter interactions or stochastic elements.

In this study, we apply an eigenvector-based sensitivity analysis method following the approach of
[
11
]
that leverages the dominant eigenvalue of the Jacobian matrix to assess the sensitivity of the epidemic threshold. By integrating this approach with an age-structured compartmental model and the Next Generation Matrix, we compute the basic reproduction number
\mathcal{R}_{0}
and formulate a generalized eigenproblem to derive age-specific sensitivity values. This formulation not only quantifies the contributions of various age groups to viral transmission but also provides a clear framework for analyzing the transmission potential as expressed through the contact matrix. We then present the results of our sensitivity analysis and compare them with previous study
[
47
]
to validate and extend existing findings. Additionally, we demonstrate the flexibility of our framework by applying it to other epidemic models and contact data from the literature, showcasing its utility in various settings.

2.1
Sensitivity Measures

A precise characterization of disease transmission is crucial for developing reliable epidemic models. In age-structured models, transmission dynamics are governed by age-dependent parameters and a social contact matrix. We denote this contact matrix by
\bar{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{16\times 16}

[
8
]

\mathcal{R}_{0}

\mathbf{K}

\mathcal{R}_{0}

\mathbf{K}

\mathcal{R}_{0}

2.4
Framework for Sensitivity Analysis

The sensitivity analysis framework is designed to flexibly assess the impact of changes in contact patterns on disease transmission across various structured epidemic models. By updating key input files, users can adapt the framework to accommodate different social contact matrices, varying levels of model complexity, and customized model parameters.

The framework’s primary input is a set of scaled contact matrices representing age-specific social interactions across various environments (e.g., homes, schools, workplaces, and other places). However, users can readily replace these matrices to reflect new or alternative social patterns. In addition, the framework supports the integration of simpler and more complex age-structured models, making it suitable for a wide range of epidemiological studies.

To compute the basic reproduction number (
\mathcal{R}_{0}
), the framework employs the Next Generation Matrix method
[
8
]
where
\mathcal{R}_{0}
is determined as the dominant eigenvalue of the transmission matrix
\mathbf{K}
. In the
Eigenvector Calculator
, the framework calculates the eigenvector associated with
\mathcal{R}_{0}
according to Eq. (
A.1
).
The
Gradient Calculator
then uses Eq. (
2.1
) to generate 136 sensitivity values, each capturing the impact of variations in specific elements of the contact matrix on
\mathcal{R}_{0}
. The sensitivity values are then aggregated using Eq. (
2.2
) to capture the total influence of each age group on
\mathcal{R}_{0}
. The results are presented as heat maps and charts, highlighting the interactions in the contact matrix that most significantly affect disease dynamics. The entire analysis process is automated and implemented in Python, using libraries such as
pytorch
and
matplotlib
for efficient computations and visualizations.
In a GitHub repository, we share the eigenvector-based sensitivity analysis code incorporating the different epidemic models
[
41
]
.

4
Discussion

Understanding how key parameters influence disease transmission is essential for developing effective public health strategies. Sensitivity analysis is a critical tool for identifying the factors that most significantly impact disease spread, guiding the design of targeted interventions
[
42
,
44
,
45
,
46
]
. However, traditional sensitivity analysis methods often rely on extensive simulations and rigid assumptions, limiting their flexibility and accuracy. To address these challenges, our proposed sensitivity analysis framework integrates detailed contact data into epidemic models, enabling more precise and adaptable assessments of transmission dynamics.

This framework effectively identifies the age groups that contribute most to virus transmission. While infections among younger individuals may initially seem less concerning due to their lower infectivity and higher rates of asymptomatic cases, shifts in contact patterns, such as increased interactions with older adults following the relaxation of control measures, can significantly raise transmission risks. By combining sensitivity values with contact data, our method clarifies how different demographic groups drive disease spread.
To enhance sensitivity analysis, we calculated cumulative sensitivities, providing deeper insight into age-specific contributions to COVID-19 transmission. This metric accounts for both the expected incidence of infection within a group and the long-term impact of an infected individual on overall epidemic growth. By integrating behavioral, epidemiological, and environmental factors, cumulative sensitivities offer a proportional measure of how the basic reproduction number,
\mathcal{R}_{0}
, responds to changes in transmission dynamics. These indices are crucial for assessing targeted interventions, such as remote work policies for working-age populations. Prioritizing age groups with the highest cumulative values can lead to more effective reductions in
\mathcal{R}_{0}
and overall transmission.

A combined analysis of sensitivity, cumulative values, and contact patterns highlights the primary age groups driving COVID-19 transmission. Their significant role is mainly due to frequent social and occupational interactions and their more extensive representation in the population, which increases transmission opportunities. The contact matrix reflects these dense interactions within and across age groups, reinforcing their impact on disease spread. For instance, while individuals under 20 (Fig.
4
) contribute to transmission, their overall impact is limited due to more substantial within-group contacts and higher asymptomatic rates. In contrast, adults over 55 have lower sensitivity as they engage in fewer cross-age interactions and constitute a smaller population share despite being more vulnerable to severe outcomes. These findings align with evidence showing that school closures reduce transmission by only 5.6%
[
26
]
, making them less effective than workplace restrictions or interventions targeting high-contact environments
[
Lovell
]
. Furthermore, the low incidence of COVID-19 cases in educational settings
[
21
,
25
]
supports the limited impact of focusing interventions on younger populations. Targeting age groups with higher contact rates and greater sensitivity, particularly the working-age population, remains essential for effectively reducing virus transmission.

The granularity of age group categorization also significantly influences sensitivity outcomes. Detailed age group breakdowns reveal consistent sensitivity patterns, as shown in Figs.
4
and
5
. However, when age groups are aggregated, as seen in Fig.
6
, sensitivity values vary depending on the size and distribution of the grouped categories. Uneven aggregation, such as inconsistent age intervals, can distort sensitivity and cumulative sensitivity measurements, leading to potentially misleading conclusions. This highlights the importance of using well-balanced and appropriately segmented age groups to ensure accurate and comparable results in sensitivity analyses.

The proposed sensitivity analysis framework offers several advantages for modeling disease transmission. Its deterministic nature ensures consistent and reproducible results across simulations, enhancing the reliability of outcomes. Additionally, its close alignment with the underlying dynamical system provides a more accurate representation of how parameter changes affect disease spread over time. This flexibility allows for precise assessments across various epidemic scenarios and age-structured models, supporting localized predictions and targeted public health interventions.
However, the framework has limitations. It lacks a confidence measure for cumulative sensitivity estimates due to its reliance on point estimations, making it difficult to assess the reliability of results. Moreover, its focus on the Next Generation Matrix restricts analysis to transmission-related outcomes, preventing the evaluation of mortality or other health impacts beyond disease spread.

5
Conclusion

This study introduces an eigenvector-based sensitivity analysis framework to evaluate the influence of age-specific contact patterns on disease transmission dynamics. Utilizing the Next Generation Matrix, the framework effectively identifies critical age groups and age group interactions that significantly contribute to the spread of infectious diseases. These insights underscore the importance of precise parameter estimation for these groups to inform targeted public health interventions.
The findings highlight the framework’s versatility and applicability across different epidemiological models and demographic settings. Results emphasize the central role of middle-aged groups as primary transmission hubs. At the same time, the impact on younger populations varies based on their susceptibility, social behavior, and the granularity of age group categorization. Rather than prescribing specific interventions, the framework’s key contribution lies in enhancing parameter estimation accuracy for high-impact age groups, thereby improving the reliability of epidemiological models.
Despite certain limitations, the framework provides valuable insights into age-specific transmission dynamics. Future research should aim to extend this approach by incorporating temporal variations and integrating them into more comprehensive modeling systems, ultimately enhancing its utility in public health planning and epidemic response.

Appendix A
General Sensitivity Measures

For a given matrix
\mathbf{K}
, the eigenvalue
\lambda
is associated with the left and right eigenvectors, which provide crucial insight into the relationship between
\mathbf{K}
and
\lambda
. These eigenvectors satisfy the following equations:

\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w}=\lambda\mathbf{w}

(A.1)

\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{K}=\lambda\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}.

Since
\mathbf{K}
is strictly positive, its dominant eigenvalue, denoted as
\mathcal{R}_{0}
, is real, positive, and algebraically simple. Furthermore, it admits eigenvectors
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{v}
with strictly real and positive components. Consequently,
\lambda=\mathcal{R}_{0}
.

Following the methodology outlined in
[
4
,
37
]
, we introduce a perturbation to
\mathbf{K}
in Eq. (
A.1
):

(\mathbf{K}+\Delta\mathbf{K})(\mathbf{w}+\Delta\mathbf{w})=(\mathcal{R}_{0}+\Delta\mathcal{R}_{0})(\mathbf{w}+\Delta\mathbf{w}).

Expanding this expression and neglecting higher-order terms yields:

\mathbf{K}\Delta\mathbf{w}+\Delta\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w}=\mathcal{R}_{0}\Delta\mathbf{w}+\Delta\mathcal{R}_{0}\mathbf{w}.

Rearranging to isolate
\Delta\mathcal{R}_{0}
:

\Delta\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w}=\Delta\mathcal{R}_{0}\mathbf{w}.

Multiplying both sides by
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}
, the left eigenvector, projects the perturbation onto
\mathcal{R}_{0}
:

\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w}=\Delta\mathcal{R}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{w}.

Solving for
\Delta\mathcal{R}_{0}
gives:

\Delta\mathcal{R}_{0}=\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{w}}.

If the perturbation affects only a single element
k_{ij}
of
\mathbf{K}
, then:

\Delta\mathcal{R}_{0}=\frac{v_{i}w_{j}}{\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{w}}\Delta k_{ij}.

Taking the limit as
\Delta k_{ij}\to 0
, the sensitivity of
\mathcal{R}_{0}
with respect to
k_{ij}
is:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}_{0}}{\partial k_{ij}}=\frac{v_{i}w_{j}}{\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{w}}.

(A.2)

To describe the contact data used in this study, we utilize Hungarian contact data estimated in

]
for 16 distinct age groups.

The contact matrices for different settings—home (

), school (
\tilde{C}^{\text{S}}
), work (
\tilde{C}^{\text{W}}
), and other environments (
\tilde{C}^{\text{O}}
)—are combined to form the full contact matrix:

\tilde{C}=\tilde{C}^{\text{H}}+\tilde{C}^{\text{S}}+\tilde{C}^{\text{W}}+\tilde{C}^{\text{O}}.

This full contact matrix
\tilde{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{16\times 16}
consists of elements
\tilde{c}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j}
, representing the average number of daily contacts between individuals in age groups
i
and
j
. However, due to sampling variability, the matrix is often asymmetric. In theory, the contact matrix should satisfy the balance condition:

\tilde{c}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j}N_{i}=\tilde{c}_{j\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}i}N_{j}\mathchar 59\relax

where
N_{i}
and
N_{j}
represent the population sizes of age groups
i
and
j
, respectively.

To correct this asymmetry, we apply the symmetrization method proposed by Knipl et al.
[
Knipl
]
, resulting in the symmetrized contact matrix
C
with elements:

c_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j}=\frac{\tilde{c}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j}N_{i}+\tilde{c}_{j\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}i}N_{j}}{2}.

(B.1)

To prepare the contact matrix for our analysis, we apply population scaling to Eq. (
B.1
):

c^{\mathrm{pop}}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j}=\frac{c_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{16}N_{k}}.

(B.2)

Based on this scaling, we further rescaled Eq. (
B.2
) by dividing by the population size of age group
j
to derive the adjusted full contact matrix:

\overline{c}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j}=\frac{c_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j}}{N_{j}}\mathchar 59\relax

(B.3)

The final contact matrices are defined as:

C^{\mathrm{pop}}=[c^{\mathrm{pop}}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j}]_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j=1}^{16}\mathchar 59\relax\quad\overline{C}=[\overline{c}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j}]_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j=1}^{16}\in\mathbb{R}^{16\times 16}.

Each matrix contains 136 distinct values, capturing the contact interactions between different age groups.
