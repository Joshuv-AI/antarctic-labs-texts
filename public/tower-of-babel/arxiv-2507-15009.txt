Relatively hyperbolic groups, Grothendieck pairs, and uncountable profinite ambiguity among fibre products Thanks: The second author was supported by NSF grant DMS-1812397. For the purpose of open access, the authors have applied a CC BY public copyright licence to any author accepted manuscript.
M.R. Bridson, A.W. Reid

Abstract
. These notes expand upon our lectures on profinite rigidity at the international colloquium on randomness, geometry and dynamics, organised by TIFR Mumbai at IISER Pune in January 2024. We are interested in the extent to which groups that arise in hyperbolic geometry and 3-manifold topology are determined by their finite quotients. The main theme of these notes is the radical extent to which rigidity is lost when one passes from consideration of groups with hyperbolic features to consideration of their direct products. We describe a general method for producing infinite sequences of Grothendieck pairs, i.e. embeddings P i ↪ G × G P_{i}\hookrightarrow G\times G inducing isomorphisms of profinite completions, with G G fixed and P i P_{i} finitely generated. In order to apply this method, one needs G G to map onto a subgroup of finite index in the commutator subgroup of a group Γ \Gamma with H 2 ​ ( Γ , ℤ ) = 0 H_{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})=0 , and Γ \Gamma should be relatively hyperbolic. By exploiting the flexibility of the construction, we explain how, under the same hypotheses on G G , one can construct uncountable families of pairwise non-isomorphic subgroups P λ P_{\lambda} such that P λ ↪ G × G P_{\lambda}\hookrightarrow G\times G induces an isomorphism of profinite completions. Examples of groups G G satisfying these conditions include the fundamental group of the Weeks manifold and the fundamental group of the 4-fold branch cover of the figure-8 knot complement. Both of these examples are profinitely rigid in the absolute sense and in each case Grothendieck pairs account entirely for the loss of profinite rigidity for G × G G\times G : if H H is a finitely generated group whose profinite completion is isomorphic to that of G × G G\times G , then there is an embedding H ↪ G × G H\hookrightarrow G\times G that is a Grothendieck pair.

1.
Introduction

A finitely generated, residually finite group
G
is
profinitely rigid
if any finitely generated, residually finite group
H

with the same set of finite quotients as
G
must be isomorphic to
G
; in more technical language,

\widehat{H}\cong\widehat{G}\implies H\cong G
, where
\widehat{G}
is the profinite completion on
G
,
which is defined to be the inverse limit
\varprojlim G/N
, where
N
runs over the set of all finite-index normal
subgroups of
G
.
It is not difficult to show that
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is profinitely rigid, but it is much more difficult to construct examples that
are full-sized in the sense that they
contain a non-abelian free group: we constructed the first such examples in our work with McReynolds and Spitler
[
9
,
10
]
.
Deviation from profinite rigidity is measured by the
profinite genus
of
G
, which is the set of isomorphism
classes of finitely generated, residually finite groups
H
with
\widehat{H}\cong\widehat{G}
. The
strong profinite genus
of
G
consists of isomorphism classes of finitely generated
groups
H
for which there is an embedding
u:H\hookrightarrow G
that induces
an isomorphism
\widehat{u}:\widehat{H}\hookrightarrow\widehat{G}
; if
H\neq G

then
H\hookrightarrow G
is called
Grothendieck pair
.
In
[
12
]
we exhibited infinite families of Seifert fibered 3-manifolds
M

with geometry
{\widetilde{{\rm{SL}}}}_{2}
such that
\pi_{1}M
is profinitely rigid but the
strong profinite genus of
\pi_{1}M\times\pi_{1}M
is infinite.
In the course of these notes, we will
touch on most of the ideas behind the main construction in
[
12
]
and explain how they can be extended.

The first examples of full-sized groups that are profinitely rigid
[
9
,
10
]
were
the fundamental groups of certain 2-dimensional and 3-dimensional hyperbolic orbifolds,
i.e. lattices in
{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{C})
.
We expect all lattices in
{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{R})

to be profinitely rigid, but our focus here is not on the programme to prove that.
Rather, the main point that
we want to emphasize here is how emphatically profinite rigidity fails when one passes from
consideration of hyperbolic groups such as these to consideration of direct products of hyperbolic groups.
The following special case of what we prove illustrates this point nicely. We frame this theorem in terms of two of
the early examples of 3-manifold groups that were proved to be profinitely rigid:
from
[
9
]
, the fundamental group of the Weeks manifold, which is the unique closed hyperbolic 3-manifold of minimal volume; and from
[
11
]
, the fundamental group of the hyperbolic manifold obtained by
taking the
4
-fold cyclic
branched cover of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
over the figure-8 knot.

Theorem A
.

Let
\Gamma
be the fundamental group of either of the Weeks manifold or the 4-fold cyclic
branched cover of

\mathbb{S}^{3}
over the figure-8 knot. Then,

(1)

\Gamma
is profinitely rigid;

(2)

for every finitely generated, residually finite group
\Lambda
, if
\widehat{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}\times\widehat{\Gamma}
then
there exists an embedding
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Gamma\times\Gamma
inducing this isomorphism of profinite completions;

(3)

there exist uncountably many pairwise non-isomorphic subgroups
u_{i}:P_{i}\hookrightarrow\Gamma\times\Gamma

such that
\widehat{u}_{i}:\widehat{P}_{i}\to\widehat{\Gamma}\times\widehat{\Gamma}
is an isomorphism, and

(4)

infinitely many of these
P_{i}
are finitely generated.

The proof that the groups
\Gamma
in Theorem
A

are profinitely rigid is based on the method of
Galois rigidity
which we shall recall in
Section
7
. This property imposes control on the
{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{C})
representations of any
finitely generated group with the same profinite completion as
\Gamma
.
While our main theme is that profinite rigidity is
lost when one passes from
\Gamma
to
\Gamma\times\Gamma
, it is nevertheless true that Galois
rigidity is inherited by
\Gamma\times\Gamma
, and in favourable circumstances
this can be used to create an embedding into
\Gamma\times\Gamma

of any abstract, finitely generated, residually finite group with the same profinite completion as
\Gamma
;
that is the content of item (2) in Theorem
A
.
Items (3) and (4), in contrast, are not specific to delicate features of the 3-manifold groups under consideration:
they follow from a much more general result concerning Grothendieck
pairs, Theorem
C
,
which is the main original feature of these notes.

The technique that we shall describe for constructing the
abundance of Grothendieck pairs in Theorem
C
extends a well-established
train of ideas which we will now explain.

Grothendieck
[
21
]
asked if there exist Grothendieck pairs of finitely presented groups. This problem was eventually solved by
Bridson and Grunewald
[
7
]
. Their proof builds in an essential way on an earlier argument of Platonov and Tavgen
[
32
]

who constructed the first Grothendieck pair of finitely generated groups with an argument whose essence can be abstracted as follows.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
f:G\to Q
be an epimorphism of groups, with
G
finitely generated and
Q
finitely presented, and
consider the fibre product

P=\{(g,h)\mid f(g)=f(h)\}<G\times G
. Then,

(1)

P
is finitely generated;

(2)

if
\widehat{Q}=1
and
H_{2}(Q,\mathbb{Z})=0
, then
P\hookrightarrow G\times G
induces an isomorphism
\widehat{P}\overset{\cong}{\rightarrow}\widehat{G\times G}
.

Platonov and Tavgen applied this criterion with

G
a free group of rank
4

and
Q
Higman’s famous 4-generator, 4-relator group with
\widehat{Q}=1
and
H_{2}(Q,\mathbb{Z})=0
.
In the course of constructing counterexamples to the Platonov Conjecture, Bass and Lubotzky
[
3
]
described infinitely
many Grothendieck pairs of the form
(\Gamma\times\Gamma,P)
by applying the full force
of Proposition
1.1
(which they identified as the essence of
[
32
]
).
In order to achieve this, they needed a technique for mapping hyperbolic groups onto finitely presented groups
Q
with

\widehat{Q}=1
and
H_{2}(Q,\mathbb{Z})=0
. This is achieved in two stages. First, a bespoke
small-cancellation argument due to Olshanskii
[
29
]
(for which
Rips has an alternative, unpublished proof) shows that every non-elementary hyperbolic group
G
maps onto a finitely presented group
with
\widehat{Q}=1
, but one does not have control over
H_{2}(Q,\mathbb{Z})
. If
G
is free,
or more generally
H^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})=0
, this is not a serious problem – one can replace

Q
by its universal central extension
\widetilde{Q}
and lift
G\to Q
to a surjection
G\to\widetilde{Q}
– but for an arbitrary hyperbolic
group
G
one cannot do this. Indeed, it remains unclear whether an arbitrary
non-elementary hyperbolic group (virtually) maps onto any infinite, finitely presented
group
\widetilde{Q}
with no finite quotients and
H_{2}(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Z})=0
. (Bass and Lubotzky get around this by using specific homological
properties of the groups that were their concern.) Such struggles with
H_{2}(-,\mathbb{Z})
will recur as a theme throughout these
notes.

Advances in the understanding of hyperbolic groups and significant advances in the theory of relatively hyperbolic groups, mean that
today one can replace Olshanskii’s carefully crafted argument with a more flexible and conceptually-easier construction.
In
[
12
]
we used this
flexibiity to prove a version of
the following theorem, whose proof relies heavily on ideas from
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
. The new embellishment that we
want to exploit in these notes lies with the technical condition on the torsion in the quotients
Q_{i}
.

Theorem B
.

Let
\Gamma
be a non-elementary relatively hyperbolic group, let
\pi_{0}
be the set of primes
p

such that
\Gamma
has an element of order
p
, and let
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},\dots
be an arbitrary sequence of primes.
Then, there is an infinite sequence of groups

Q_{i}
and epimorphisms
\Gamma\to Q_{1}\to Q_{2}\to\dots
such that

(1)

\widehat{Q}_{i}=1
for
i=1,2,\dots
;

(2)

if
\Gamma
is finitely presented, then so is each
Q_{i}
;

(3)

each
Q_{i}
has trivial centre;

(4)

Q_{i}
has an element of prime order
p
only if
p\in\pi_{0}\cup\{p_{1},\dots,p_{i}\}
;

(5)

Q_{i}
is generated by elements of order
p
for each prime
p\in\{p_{1},\dots,p_{i}\}
;

(6)

Q_{i}\not\cong Q_{j}
if
i\neq j
.

The point of arranging the quotients
Q_{i}
into a directed system rather than just a set is that we
can form the direct limit
Q_{\infty}=\lim Q_{i}
and consider the fibre product associated to
\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow Q_{\infty}

as well as the fibre products associated to
\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow Q_{i}
. Our goal is to obtain Grothendieck pairs using these fibre products, as in Proposition
1.1
. The point of controlling the torsion with a sequence of
primes is that we need an invariant that ensures the groups
Q_{\infty}
associated to different sequences are not isomorphic,
giving us uncountably many examples – see the proof of Theorem
C
.

Before we can appeal to Proposition
1.1
, we have to force
H_{2}(Q_{i},\mathbb{Z})=0
.
It seems unlikely that one can do this in general, but using a homological argument
presented in Lemma
2.3
,
one can do this for subgroups of finite index in the commutator subgroup of a
relatively hyperbolic group with trivial second homology.

Theorem C
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely presented,
non-elementary, relatively hyperbolic group
and suppose that for infinitely many primes
p
there is not
an element of order
p
in
\Gamma
.
Let
G
be either a hyperbolic group in which centralizers of non-trivial elements
are virtually cyclic, or a central extension of such a group.

If
H_{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})=0
and
G
maps onto a subgroup of finite index in
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
, then

(1)

there exist uncountably many
non-isomorphic groups
P_{\lambda}
with embeddings
P_{\lambda}\hookrightarrow G\times G

that induce an isomorphism of profinite completions, and

(2)

infinitely many of these
P_{\lambda}
are finitely generated.

The case where
\Gamma=G
in Theorem
C
restricts us to perfect groups, but there are also
many other groups to which the main idea behind Theorem
C
applies: the key property
that we require of
G
is that after arranging for it to map onto a group
Q
with no finite quotients, we need to be able to
lift the map to get a surjection
G\twoheadrightarrow\widetilde{Q}
. Finitely generated free groups
F
have this property
and consequently we obtain uncountably many non-isomorphic subgroups
P_{\lambda}<F\times F

for which the inclusion induces an isomorphism of profinite completions. In this case, it was already
known that there are infinitely many Grothendieck pairs
P\hookrightarrow F\times F
with
P
finitely generated
[
7
]
.
In contrast, if
F
is a finitely generated free, surface of limit group, then there are no
Grothendieck pairs
P\hookrightarrow F\times\cdots\times F
with
P

finitely presented

[
13
]
.

We mention this
in the context of Theorem
C
, because we want to emphasize that the families of finitely generated
subgroups
P_{\lambda}\hookrightarrow G\times G
constructed will not be finitely presented in
general. There do exist hyperbolic groups
H
in which one can use similar ideas to construct infinite sequences of
Grothendieck pairs
P_{n}\hookrightarrow H\times H
with
P_{n}
finitely presented
[
6
]
, but
those
H
are specially crafted to this end, and we do not know how
to do this when
H
is a profinitely rigid group.

Whilst discussing the importance of finiteness properties, we should also direct the reader’s attention to the
fact that it has been known for some time that there exist uncountable families of finitely generated, residually finite groups
that have the same profinite completion
[
33
,
27
]
, although of course this phenomenon cannot occur
among the subgroups of a fixed finitely generated (or countable) group.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Profinite completions

The finite quotients of a group
\Gamma
form a directed system, since
\Gamma/M\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma/N
if
N>M
.
The
profinite completion
of
\Gamma
is the inverse limit of this system.
\Gamma
inherits
a topology from the discrete topology on the finite groups
\Gamma/N
and in this topology it is a
compact, totally-disconnected group. The natural map

\Gamma\to\widehat{\Gamma}
is injective if and only if
\Gamma
is residually finite. A morphism of discrete groups

u:\Gamma_{1}\to\Gamma_{2}
induces a continuous morphism
\widehat{u}:\widehat{\Gamma}_{1}\to\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}
. We are interested
in knowing when the inclusion of a subgroup
P\hookrightarrow\Gamma
induces an isomorphism
\widehat{P}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}
.
When
\Gamma
is finitely generated, this amounts to proving two things: (1) for surjectivity, that for every
finite group
G
and every epimorphism
\pi:\Gamma\to G
, the restriction of
\pi
to
P
is surjective; and (2)
for injectivity, every epimorphism
P\twoheadrightarrow G
extends to an epimorphism from
\Gamma
.

A basic theorem in the field that we shall use without further comment is the following: finitely generated
groups
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
have the same set of finite quotients if and only if
\widehat{\Gamma}_{1}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}
(where the isomorphism can be taken to be an abstract isomorphism due to work of Nikolov and Segal
[
28
]
).

2.2.
Fibre products

The fibre product
P<\Gamma\times\Gamma
associated to an epimorphism
\pi:\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow Q
is the subgroup

P=\{(g,h)\mid\pi(g)=\pi(h)\}.

P
is a normal subgroup if and only if
Q
is abelian. If
\Gamma
is finitely generated and
Q
is finitely presented,
then
P
will be finitely generated (the 0-1-2 Lemma). The question of when
P
is finitely presented
is more delicate: it suffices that
\Gamma
is finitely presented,
K=\ker\pi
is finitely generated and
Q

has a classifying space with finite 3-skeleton (the 1-2-3 Theorem
[
4
]
). The fibre products
that we will be considering do not satisfy the conditions of the 1-2-3 Theorem and there is no reason to expect them to be finitely presented.

Notable subgroups in
P
include the diagonal
\Delta_{\Gamma}=\{(g,g)\mid g\in\Gamma\}\cong\Gamma

and the intersections of
P
with
\Gamma\times 1
and
1\times\Gamma
, which are
K_{1}=K\times 1
and
K_{2}=1\times K
.
The semidirect product decomposition
P=K_{1}\rtimes\Delta_{\Gamma}
(symmetrically,
K_{2}\rtimes\Delta_{\Gamma}
) is useful,
as is the observation that the action of
(g,g)\in\Delta_{\Gamma}
by conjugation on
K\times 1
is the same as the
action of
(g,1)
, so the following groups of coinvariants (defined in the next subsection) coincide

(1)

H_{0}(\Delta_{\Gamma},\,H_{1}(K_{1},\mathbb{Z}))\cong H_{0}(\Gamma,\,H_{1}(K,\mathbb{Z})).

Another useful observation is that
P/K_{1}K_{2}\cong Q
,
because the image of
P
under
(\pi,\pi):\Gamma\times\Gamma\to Q\times Q
is the diagonal subgroup.

2.3.
On the homology of groups

We shall assume that the reader is familiar with basic facts concerning the homology of groups, for which we refer to
[
14
]
. In particular, the reader will need to recall that
H_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
is the abelianisation of
G
, and that
if
N
is a normal subgroup of a group
G
, then the group of co-invariants
H_{0}(G,\,H_{1}(N,\mathbb{Z}))
is the quotient
of
H_{1}(N,\mathbb{Z})
obtained by factoring out the conjugation action of
G
, i.e. killing the subgroup

\langle[n]-[g^{-1}ng]\mid n\in N,\,g\in G\rangle
. We shall also need the 5-term exact sequence associated to a
short exact sequence
1\to N\to G\to Q\to 1
, which is

H_{2}(Q,\mathbb{Z})\to H_{0}(G,\,H_{1}(N,\mathbb{Z}))\to H_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})\to H_{1}(Q,\mathbb{Z})\to 0.

The following lemma will also be useful.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group that has a finite presentation with equal numbers of generators and relations. If
H_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
is finite then
H_{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})=0
.

Proof.

The standard 2-complex
K
of a presentation has one vertex, one 1-cell for each generator
a
, oriented
and labelled
a
, and one
2-cell for each relator
r
of the presentation: the attaching map of the 2-cell traces out the loop in the 1-skeleton labelled
r
. A classifying space for the group presented
G
can be obtained by adding cells of dimension greater than
2
, and hence

H_{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})
is a quotient of
H_{2}(K,\mathbb{Z})
, so in our setting we will be done if we can argue that
H_{2}(K,\mathbb{Z})=0
. To see that

H_{2}(K,\mathbb{Z})=0
we consider the cellular chain complex of
K
,

C_{2}\to C_{1}\to C_{0}.

By definition,
C_{1}\to C_{0}
is the zero map, so
H_{1}(K,\mathbb{Z})=H_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
will only be finite if
the image of
C_{2}\to C_{1}
is of finite index. By hypothesis, the free abelian groups
C_{2}
and
C_{1}
have the same rank,
so if the image of
d_{2}:C_{2}\to C_{1}
has finite index, then
d_{2}
must be injective, and therefore

H_{2}(K,\mathbb{Z})=\ker d_{2}=0
.

2.4.
Universal central extensions

The standard reference for the following well known facts is
[
26
, pp. 43-47]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
Q
is a perfect group, then there is a central extension
1\to Z\to\widetilde{Q}\overset{p}{\to}Q\to 1

where

(1)

\widetilde{Q}\overset{p}{\to}Q
is universal: if the kernel of
E\overset{r}{\twoheadrightarrow}Q
is central in
E
, then there is a
unique homomorphism
f:\widetilde{Q}\to E
such that
p=r\circ f
;

(2)

H_{1}(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Z})=H_{2}(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Z})=0
;

(3)

if
Q
has no non-trivial finite quotients, then neither does
\widetilde{Q}
.

If
Q
is finitely presented, then
H_{2}(Q,\mathbb{Z})
is finitely generated and
\widetilde{Q}
is finitely presented.

The following lemma from
[
12
]
will provide a useful mechanism for forcing
quotients to have trivial second homology so that the Platonov-Tavgen criterion (Proposition
1.1
)
can be applied. This is an idea that originates in
[
3
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
Q
be a perfect group with universal central extension
p:\widetilde{Q}\to Q
, let
G
be a group with
H_{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})=0

and let
F:G\to Q
be an epimorphism that restricts to
f:[G,G]\to Q
. Then, there exists an epimorphism
\tilde{f}:[G,G]\to\widetilde{Q}

with
p\circ\tilde{f}=f
.

Proof.

The fibre product of the maps
F
and
p
is the subgroup
\breve{G}=\{(x,y)\mid F(x)=p(y)\}<G\times\tilde{Q}
.
By projecting to the first factor we see that
\breve{G}
is a central extension

0\to Z\to\breve{G}\to G\to 1,

where
Z=\ker p\cong H_{2}(Q,\mathbb{Z})
.

Consider the standard 5-term exact sequence associated to this extension:

H_{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})\to Z\to H_{1}(\breve{G},\mathbb{Z})\to H_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})\to 0.

The first term is zero by hypothesis, so
Z
injects into
H_{1}(\breve{G},\mathbb{Z})
– in other words

Z\cap[\breve{G},\breve{G}]
is trivial. Thus
\breve{G}\twoheadrightarrow G
restricts to an
isomorphism
[\breve{G},\breve{G}]\to[G,G]
. By composing the inverse of this isomorphism
with the coordinate projection
\breve{G}\to 1\times\widetilde{Q}
we obtain the desired
map
\tilde{f}:[G,G]\to\tilde{Q}
, which is onto because its image contains
[\widetilde{Q},\widetilde{Q}]
and

\widetilde{Q}
is perfect.

2.5.
Nested fibre products

The following lemma allows us to bypass a discussion of the finiteness conditions
on the groups in Proposition
1.1
.
We include the proof because it provides a clear illustration of how the vanishing of
H_{2}(-,\mathbb{Z})
gets used.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group, let
p_{1}:\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow Q_{1}
and
p_{2}:Q_{1}\twoheadrightarrow Q_{2}
be epimorphisms and
let
P_{1}<\Gamma\times\Gamma
and
P_{2}<\Gamma\times\Gamma
be the fibre products associated to
p_{1}
and
p_{2}\circ p_{1}

respectively. If
\widehat{Q}_{1}=1
and
H_{2}(Q_{2},\mathbb{Z})=0
, then the inclusion
u:P_{1}\hookrightarrow P_{2}
induces an epimorphism

\widehat{u}:\widehat{P}_{1}\to\widehat{P}_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
N_{1}=\ker p_{1}\times\ker p_{1}
, let
N_{2}=\ker p_{2}\circ p_{1}\times 1
, let

K=\ker p_{2}\times 1
and note that
K<Q_{1}\times Q_{1}
is the image of
N_{2}<\Gamma\times\Gamma
.

Given a finite
quotient
\pi:P_{2}\twoheadrightarrow G
, we must argue that
\pi|_{P_{1}}
is surjective, for which it suffices to show
that
\pi|_{N_{1}}
is surjective. If it were not, then by taking the quotient of
G
by the normal subgroup

\pi(N_{1})
we would obtain a non-trivial finite quotient of the image of
P_{2}
in
Q_{1}\times Q_{1}=(\Gamma\times\Gamma)/N_{1}
.
This image
\overline{P}_{2}
is the fibre product of
p_{2}:Q_{1}\twoheadrightarrow Q_{2}
, which we recall is a semidirect product

\overline{P}_{2}=K\rtimes\Delta_{Q_{1}}
,
where
\Delta_{Q_{1}}<Q_{1}\times Q_{1}
is the diagonal subgroup.

We claim that
\overline{P}_{2}
has no non-trivial
finite quotients. To see this, first note that since
\widehat{Q}_{1}=1
,
any quotient would factor through
H:=(K\rtimes\Delta_{Q_{1}})/\langle\!\langle\Delta_{Q_{1}}\rangle\!\rangle
, which is
isomorphic to the group of co-invariants
H_{0}(Q_{1},\,H_{1}(K,\mathbb{Z}))
, for the following reason:
for each
k\in\ker p_{2}
we have
(k,k)\in\Delta_{Q_{1}}
, so the images of
(k,1)
and
(1,k)

in
H
coincide, and since
(1,k)
commutes with
K=\ker p_{2}\times 1
, the image of

The following theorem includes the awkward-sounding hypothesis that centralisers in the hyperbolic group

defined by
\theta\mapsto\theta_{i}
. These in turn can be used to build
n_{K}
Zariski-dense non-conjugate representations
\rho_{\sigma_{i}}\colon\Lambda\to\PSL(2,\mathbb{C})
with the property that
\mbox{\rm{tr}}\,(\rho_{\sigma_{i}}(\gamma))=\sigma_{i}(\mbox{\rm{tr}}\,\rho(\gamma))
for all
\gamma\in\Lambda
.
One refers to these as
Galois conjugate representations
.
The existence of these Galois conjugates shows that
|\mathrm{X}_{\mathrm{zar}}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C})|\geq n_{K_{\rho(\Lambda)}}
, where
\mathrm{X}_{\mathrm{zar}}(\Lambda,\mathbb{C})
denotes the set of Zariski-dense representations
\Gamma\to\PSL(2,\mathbb{C})
up to conjugacy.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely generated group and
\rho\colon\Lambda\to\PSL(2,\mathbb{C})
a Zariski-dense representation whose trace field
K_{\rho(\Lambda)}
is a number field. If

|\mathrm{X}_{\mathrm{zar}}(\Lambda,\mathbb{C})|=n_{K_{\rho(\Gamma)}}
, we say that
\Gamma
is
Galois rigid
(with associated field
K_{\rho(\Lambda)}
).

7.2.
From Galois rigidity to profinite rigidity

In outline, the way in which
the groups
\Gamma<{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{C})
in
[
9
]
were proved to be profinitely rigid was as follows. To begin,

\Gamma
needs to have only fiinitely many Zariski-dense representations into
{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{C})
and one needs
to understand these well enough to see that
\Gamma
is Galois rigid. It is also important that
all traces in these representations are
integers in the number field
K_{\Gamma}
.
The integrality of traces is used to ensure the boundedness of the non-archimedean representations

\Gamma\to{\rm{SL}}(2,\overline{\mathbb{Q}_{p}})
obtained by transporting Zariski dense representations

\Gamma\to{\rm{SL}}(2,\mathbb{C})
via (non-continuous) field isomorphisms
\mathbb{C}\cong\overline{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}
, for all primes
p
. This boundedness enables one to extend the representations to
\widehat{\Gamma}
, then restrict to any finitely generated
\Lambda
with

\widehat{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}
to obtain bijections between the sets of bounded, Zariski-dense characters of
\Gamma

and
\Lambda
at every finite place (Lemma 4.6 of
[
9
]
).

With this control established, one can argue
that the abstract group
\Lambda
is Galois rigid with a Zariski-dense representation
\rho\colon\Lambda\to\PSL(2,\mathbb{C})

whose arithmetic data match those of
\Gamma
– see
[
9
, Theorem 4.8]
for a precise statement. In good situations
(which include the low-degree number fields of our examples), this matching of data forces the number fields

K_{\rho(\Lambda)}
and
K_{\Gamma}
to be equal, and likewise the quaternion algebras of
\Gamma
and
\rho(\Lambda)
.
This in turn allows one to
conclude that (up to Galois conjugation) the image of
\rho:\Lambda\to\PSL(2,\mathbb{C})
is contained in
\Gamma
or a small
extension of it (we’ll be a bit more specific about this finite extension in the next section).
In the examples where the argument concludes successfully,
one can force the image to lie in
\Gamma
using specific information about the lattices in

{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{C})
that lie between
\Gamma
and the finite extension in question.

One then has to argue that every non-elementary subgroup
S<\Gamma
has a finite quotient that
\Gamma
does not
have
[
11
]
, which means that
\widehat{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}
cannot map onto
\widehat{S}
,
from which we conclude that

\rho(\Lambda)=\Gamma
.
The Hopf property for finitely generated profinite groups then tells us that
\widehat{\rho}
is
injective, and hence
\rho:\Lambda\to\Gamma
is an isomorphism.

7.3.
Extension to direct products

Because centralisers of non-trivial elements in Zariski-dense subgroups of

{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{C})
are virtually abelian, if
\Gamma
is a lattice in
{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{C})
then the Zariski-dense representations
of
\Gamma\times\Gamma
break into two families: those that factor through the projection to the first factor, and those that
factor through the projection to the second factor. Correspondingly,
if
\Gamma
is Galois rigid with integral
traces, as in section (
7.2
), and if
\Lambda
is a finitely generated, residually finite group with

\widehat{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Gamma\times\Gamma}
, then one can prove that the Zariski-dense representations of
\Lambda

into
{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{C})
break into two families, each consisting to the representations that factor through one of
the coordinate projections of
\Lambda
in
\widehat{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Gamma\times\Gamma}
. This is explained in detail in
[
12
]
.

If
\Gamma
is one of the groups for which one can deduce profinite rigidity from Galois rigidity because

[
9
, Theorem 4.8]
applies, then by applying the same argument to the projection of
\Lambda

to each coordinate in
\widehat{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Gamma\times\Gamma}
(being careful with some matching of data)
one can deduce that the image
\Lambda_{1}

of
\Lambda
in
\widehat{\Gamma}\times 1
, and the image
\Lambda_{2}
in
1\times\widehat{\Gamma}
, are both isomorphic
to
\Gamma
(see
[
12
, Section 3]
).
By composing with an isomorphism of
\widehat{\Gamma\times\Gamma}
, we may assume that
\Lambda_{1}

and
\Lambda_{2}
are equal to
\Gamma\times 1
and
1\times\Gamma
.
It follows that the original isomorphism
\widehat{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Gamma\times\Gamma}
is induced
by an inclusion
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Gamma\times\Gamma
. We refer the reader to
[
12
]
for more details.

This concludes our explanation of the theory behind
Theorem
A
(2). In the following two sections we will say a little more about
why this outline applies to the Weeks group
\Gamma_{W}
and the fundamental group
\Gamma_{4}
of the 4-fold cyclic
branched cover of the figure eight knot.

7.4.
The Weeks manifold

Let
M_{W}={\mathbb{H}}^{3}/\Gamma_{W}
denote the Weeks manifold; this is the unique closed orientable hyperbolic 3–manifold of minimal volume. It is arithmetic and
can be obtained by performing
(-5,1)
surgery on one component of the Whitehead link and
(5,2)
surgery on the other. The arithmetic structure of the Weeks manifold
M_{W}
is described in
[
23
, Ch 4.8.3, Ch 9.8.2]
): the invariant trace-field of

\Gamma_{W}
is
{\mathbb{Q}}(\theta)
where
\theta^{3}-\theta^{2}+1=0
; this is a field of discriminant
-23
. Amongst other things, arithmeticity implies that
\Gamma_{W}
has integral traces.

Using SnapPy
[
16
]
, a presentation for
\Gamma_{W}
can be computed:

\langle a,b\mid ababa^{-1}b^{2}a^{-1}b,abab^{-1}a^{2}b^{-1}ab\rangle.

From this, we see that
H_{1}(M_{W},{\mathbb{Z}})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}/5{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/5{\mathbb{Z}}
.

It is shown in
[
35
, Cor 6.3]
that
\Gamma_{W}
has only three characters of irreducible representations,
into
{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{C})
, namely the characters of the faithful discrete representation, its complex conjugate, and a
\PSU(2)
–representation arising from the real ramified place of
B_{W}
.
It follows that
\Gamma_{W}
is Galois rigid, and from above, has integral traces.
It is explained in
[
9
, Corollary 4.11 and Example 4.13]

why the structure of this field and
the invariant quaternion algebra
B_{W}
allow one to follow the template for establishing profinite
rigidity that is sketched in Section
7.2
, and hence why Section
7.3
applies.

The “small finite extension” of
\Gamma_{W}
that was alluded to in (
7.2
) is the group of units
\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1}

for a choice of maximal order
\mathcal{O}
in the quaternion algebra
B_{W}
. The Weeks group
\Gamma_{W}
is a normal
subgroup of index
3
in
\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1}
.
According to
[
25
]
, the orbifold
\mathbb{H}^{3}/\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1}
can be described as
(3,0)
Dehn surgery on
5_{2}
(i.e.
M_{W}
is the
3
-fold cyclic branched cover of the knot
5_{2}
).

The following lemma tells us that Theorem
C
applies to
\Gamma_{W}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

\Gamma_{W}
is the commutator subgroup of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1}
, and
H_{2}(\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1},\mathbb{Z})=0
.

Proof.

As with any knot, the first homology of
\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus 5_{2}
is
generated by a meridian curve
\mu\in\pi_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus 5_{2})
.
As
5_{2}
is a 2-bridge knot, it has a presentation with 2 generators and 1 relation. The orbifold group

\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1}
is obtained by adding the relation
\mu^{3}=1
to

\pi_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus 5_{2})
, so
\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1}
has a presentation
with 2 generators and 2 relations and its abelianization is cyclic of order
3
.
It follows from Lemma
2.1

that
H_{2}(\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1},\mathbb{Z})=0
.

As
\Gamma_{W}<\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1}

is normal of index 3, it must coincide with the commutator subgroup.

In

]

\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1}

\PSL(2,\mathbb{C})

\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1}=[\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}},\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}]
,
and so in principle we can apply the same argument as we did above for

H_{2}(\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}},\mathbb{Z})=0
. In addition,
[

\Gamma_{W}

9_{49}(2)

In our previous work
[

6.1

7.5.
A branched cover of the figure-eight knot

Let
K\subset S^{3}
denote the figure-eight knot, let
Q_{n}
be the orbifold obtained by

(n,0)
-Dehn filling on
K
and let
M_{n}
be the
n
-fold cyclic branched cover of
K
.
Note that
M_{n}
can also be regarded as an
n
-fold cyclic (orbifold) cover of
Q_{n}
; this is
the maximal abelian cover.
When
n\geq 4
, the manifold
M_{n}
and the orbifold
Q_{n}
are hyperbolic
(see
[
15
]
for example). Let
\Gamma_{n}=\pi_{1}M_{n}
. An explicit presentation of
\Gamma_{4}
is

\Gamma_{4}=\langle a,b\mid ba^{-2}ba^{-1}b^{2}ab^{2}a^{-1},a^{2}bab^{2}aba^{2}b^{-1}\rangle.

It is proved in
[
34
, Section 4.1]
that
\Gamma_{4}
is an arithmetic Kleinian group with (invariant) trace-field
k=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})

and that its invariant quaternion algebra
B
over
k
is ramified at the places
\nu_{2}
and
\nu_{3}
associated to the prime ideals of norm
4
and
3
, respectively, in
k
. We explained in
[
11
]
why this arithmetic information,
together with detailed information about the small number of lattices in
{\rm{PSL}}(2,\mathbb{C})
that contain
\Gamma_{4}
,
is sufficient to implement the basic strategy from
[
9
]
for establishing profinite rigidity.
This allows us to follow the analysis of profinite equivalences
\widehat{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}_{4}\times\widehat{\Gamma}_{4}

outlined in section
7.2
and deduce, as we did for the Weeks manifold, that Theorem
A
(2)
holds for
\Gamma_{4}
. To complete the proof of Theorem
A

we need a substitute for Lemma
7.2
. The proof of the following lemma is entirely
similar to that of Lemma
7.2
: once again we have an orbifold group obtained by killing a
power of the generator of homology in a 2-bridge knot group. In this
case,
\Gamma_{4}=[\Delta_{4},\Delta_{4}]
has index
4
in
\Delta_{4}
.

Lemma 7.5
.

\Gamma_{4}
is the commutator subgroup of the fundamental group
\Delta_{4}
of the
orbifold
Q_{4}
described above, and
H_{2}(\Delta_{4},\mathbb{Z})=0
.

We also showed in
[
11
]
that the manifold known as
\rm{Vol}(3)
has profinitely rigid fundamental group, as well as the corresponding group
\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}^{1}
and maximal group
\Gamma_{\mathcal{O}}
(for a maximal order

\mathcal{O}
in the quaternion algebra
B
as above).
However, as with the supergroups of
\Gamma_{W}
described above, we are unable to arrange for the hypotheses of Theorem
C
to hold for these groups.

7.6.
Other examples

We close by pointing out some families of Kleinian groups, which, whilst not replicating all of Theorem
A
, do allow us to prove some additional partial results which we summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
K\subset S^{3}
be a hyperbolic knot, let
X_{n}
be the
n
-fold cyclic branched cover of
K
, and let
\Gamma_{n}=\pi_{1}(X_{n})
. If
n>3
, then,
X_{n}
is hyperbolic and
there exist uncountably many non-isomorphic groups
P_{\lambda}
with embeddings
P_{\lambda}\hookrightarrow\Gamma_{n}\times\Gamma_{n}

that induce an isomorphism of profinite completions.

Moreover, when
K
is the figure-eight knot and
n
is prime, for every finitely generated, residually finite group
\Lambda
, for with
\widehat{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}\times\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}

there exists an embedding
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Gamma_{n}\times\Gamma_{n}

inducing this isomorphism of profinite completions.

Proof.

That
X_{n}
is hyperbolic is a consequence of the Orbifold Theorem (see
[
15
]
). As in the discussion in section
7.5
,
\Gamma_{n}
is the commutator subgroup of the orbifold group
H_{n}
obtained by

(n,0)
Dehn surgery on
K
.
The group of any non-trivial knot in
S^{3}
has deficiency one (as one can see from a Wirtinger presentation), so adjoining the relation
\mu^{n}
where
\mu
is a meridian of
K
we get a balanced
presentation of the orbifold group
H_{n}
.
From Lemma
2.1
we deduce that
H_{2}(H_{n},\mathbb{Z})=0
. Thus we can apply Theorem
C

to obtain the statement in the first paragraph of the theorem.

For the assertion in the second paragraph,
we note that it was shown in
[
11
]
that when
K
is the figure-eight knot and
n>3
prime, the groups
\Gamma_{n}
are Galois rigid. Hence, the discussion in section
7.3
can be applied to the groups
\Gamma_{n}
to complete the proof.
