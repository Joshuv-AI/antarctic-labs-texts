Construction of a hepatocellular carcinoma prognostic model based on the long non-coding RNA RHPN1-AS1
Authors: Xiaoxuan Li, Yujing Li, Xinchi Luan, Chuanyu Leng, Xin Li, Xueying Sun, Jingwen Tian, Jialin Song, Jing Guo, Wensheng Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeveral long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have been identified as oncogenic factors in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). This study aims to investigate the biological function and prognostic value of lncRNA RHPN1-AS1 in HCC.MethodsHCC data were drawn from TCGA database. Coexpression, enrichment, and immune infiltration analyses were performed to identify the roles and mechanisms of RHPN1-AS1 in HCC. Kaplan‒Meier analysis was used to determine overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were employed to discover independent prognostic indicators. A prognostic nomogram was established and validated.ResultsThe expression of RHPN1-AS1 was significantly upregulated in HCC samples, and was associated with poor OS and PFS. Univariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that RHPN1-AS1, AJCC stage, T stage, and M stage were correlated with OS. In multivariate Cox regression analysis, RHPN1-AS was significantly linked to HCC prognosis. The predictive model based on RHPN1-AS1 and clinical factors reached good accuracy. In addition, RHPN1-AS1 was positively co-expressed with TONSL, CHRAC1, JRK, RECQL4, STK3, and CYHR1. Functional enrichment analysis of RHPN1-AS1 co-expressed genes were indicated significant involvement in the cell cycle, DNA replication, and double-strand break repair.ConclusionsLncRNA RHPN1-AS1 was identified as a potential independent prognostic indicator for HCC patients. A prognostic nomogram integrating RHPN1-AS1 expression and T and M stage was constructed to predict the survival of HCC patients. Furthermore, our findings suggested the involvement of the cell cycle in the action of RHPN1-AS1, providing new insights into the molecular mechanism underlying HCC progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04331-z.

Introduction

Primary liver cancer ranks sixth in incidence among common cancers, yet it is third leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally [1]. The global number of new cases and related deaths from liver cancer is predicted to rise by 55.0% and 56.4%, respectively, between 2020 and 2040 [2]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most prevalent pathological type of primary liver cancer. Although significant advances have been made in HCC treatment strategies in recent decades [3, 4], most HCC patients present with advanced disease at the initial diagnosis and typically have a high recurrence and metastasis rate and a low 5-year survival rate [5]. Therefore, identifying reliable prognostic biomarkers for HCC patients is crucial for personalized treatment and better clinical outcomes.

RHPN1 antisense RNA 1 (RHPN1-AS1) is affiliated with the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) class, a novel class of RNAs that are longer than 200 nucleotides but are unable to encode proteins [6]. Previous studies have implied that lncRNAs are related to many hallmarks of HCC, such as cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and immunosuppression [7, 8]. Recent investigations have further elucidated the complexity of these regulatory roles in HCC. For instance, non-coding RNAs promote tumor progression through signaling axes like the hsa_circ_0102231/miR-873/SOX4 pathway [9]. Furthermore, modulation of the immune microenvironment is critical for prognostic evaluation. This involves mechanisms such as the regulation of tumor-associated macrophage heterogeneity [10]. RHPN1-AS1, an antisense lncRNA with a length of 2030 bp, is located at human chromosome 8q24.3. Recent studies have reported upregulated expression of RHPN1-AS1 in many malignancies [11–25]. Knockdown or downregulation of RHPN1-AS1 could significantly inhibit the malignant phenotype of multiple cancer cell types [12, 13, 15, 21, 23–25]. Moreover, RHPN1-AS1 overexpression indicated a poor prognosis in cancer patients [13–15, 17, 18, 22]. However, the molecular function and prognostic value of RHPN1-AS1 in HCC remain largely unexplored.

Thus, this study aims to investigate the expression profile, molecular function, and potential biological mechanisms of RHPN1-AS1 in HCC. Notably, a prognostic prediction model incorporating RHPN1-AS1 and clinical features was constructed and validated, hoping to identify novel prognostic biomarkers as well as provide theoretical evidence for personalized treatment of HCC.

Materials and methods

Data sources and LncRNA screening

All liver cancer cases in this study were HCC. The lncRNA and corresponding clinical data of HCC patients were drawn from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). RNA sequencing was utilized to generate gene expression values standardized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). A total of 374 HCC tumor samples and 50 unpaired normal liver tissue samples were included in the TCGA-LIHC cohort, and the normal samples were unpaired with the tumor samples. GSE14520 (n = 445), GSE76427 (n = 115) and GSE10143 (n = 75) datasets were used to construct and validate the prognostic model. All data were processed according to related TCGA human subject protection and data access policies.

Differentially expressed long noncoding RNAs (DElncRNAs) between HCC and normal liver tissues were identified using the ‘edgeR’ package. Following the cutoff criteria of log fold change (logFC) > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, a total of 1332 DElncRNAs were screened.

Functional and pathway enrichment analyses

To explore the potential molecular functions and signaling pathways associated with RHPN1-AS1 in HCC, enrichment analyses were conducted on its co-expressed genes. The ‘enrichGO’ function from the R package ‘clusterProfiler’ was utilized to perform gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, which focused on molecular function (MF), biological process (BP), and cellular component (CC). In addition, two collections (c5.go.v7.4.symbols.gmt and c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) were used to conduct gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Gene sets with p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Co-expression analysis

Co-expression analysis was performed using Spearman’s correlation analysis and visualized using the ‘ggplot2’, ‘ggpubr’, and ‘ggExtra’ R packages. Genes with a Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rho) > 0.4 and p < 0.001 were considered to be co-expressed genes with RHPN1-AS1 and were used for subsequent enrichment analyses. We further selected 6 genes with rho > 0.5 as representative coexpressed genes among the above coexpressed genes. All heatmaps were generated using the ‘ComplexHeatmap’ R package.

Additionally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an alternative threshold (rho > 0.3, p < 0.001) to assess the robustness of our findings. The results demonstrated that the core biological pathways identified through GO and KEGG enrichment analyses—particularly those related to the cell cycle and DNA replication—remained highly consistent across different thresholds (Supplementary Figure S1).

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune cell data were drawn from the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER). The R packages ‘e1071’, ‘preprocessCore’, and ‘limma’ were used to assess the correlations between RHN1-AS1 expression and various immune cell infiltration types. To provide a comprehensive characterization of the immune landscape within TCGA cohort, we employed the CIBERSORT algorithm to quantify the relative proportions of 22 distinct immune cell phenotypes, alongside single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) to calculate enrichment scores for specific immune signatures.

Survival analysis

Based on the median expression of RHPN1-AS1, patients were divided into low- and high-level groups. The outcomes of overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were gathered from TCGA Pan-Cancer Clinical Data Resource (TCGA-CDR) [26]. Kaplan‒Meier curves and log-rank tests were performed using the ‘survival’ and ‘survminer’ R packages. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted using the ‘survival’ package to identify independent prognostic markers. Clinicopathological parameters, including age, sex, grade, AJCC stage, and TNM stage, along with RHPN1-AS1 expression data, were incorporated into the univariate and multivariable Cox regression models. Furthermore, subgroup survival analyses were performed. Patients were stratified by key clinicopathologic variables, including age, sex, AJCC stage, T stage, M stage, and histologic grade.

Construction and validation of a nomogram

To predict the 1- to 5-year survival rates of HCC patients, we integrated the TCGA-LIHC cohort with the GSE14520 dataset and constructed the prognostic model. Based on the results from the merged cohort, a prognostic nomogram was developed by integrating RHPN1-AS1 expression with clinical features (T, M stage) using the ‘rms’ and ‘survival’ packages. RHPN1-AS1 expression data were complete, while T stage and M stage had 3 and 60 missing cases, respectively. Given that the proportion of missing data was relatively low and appeared to be random, we excluded these cases from the analysis to avoid introducing bias. Consequently, the final nomogram was constructed based on 269 patients with complete clinical information. The predictive performance of the nomogram model was assessed with time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, quantified by the area under the curve (AUC) using the ‘timeROC’ package. Additionally, calibration curves for 1- to 5-year OS were plotted to assess the consistency of the model predictions with actual clinical observations. External validation of the prognostic nomogram was performed using GSE76427 and GSE10143 datasets. ROC curves were also assessed in the external validation cohorts to confirm the generalizability of the model.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To explore HCC patients’ response to chemotherapy and targeted therapy in low- and high-RHPN1-AS1 expression groups, commonly used clinical drugs were plotted from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) and drug sensitivity analyses were conducted using the ‘pRRophetic’ package. The ‘pRRophetic’ package is a robust computational tool for predicting sensitivity to chemotherapeutic and targeted agents [27]. A threshold of p < 0.01 was set as the criterion to screen out potential drugs.

Statistical analysis

Survival differences across groups were analyzed using Kaplan‒Meier and log-rank tests. The Cox proportional hazards model was employed for univariate and multivariate analyses. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for all variables in the model. Spearman rank correlation analyses were performed to assess associations between two variables. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient was utilized to measure the strength and direction of the correlations. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2). p < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Results

Acquisition of DElncRNAs

The research included 374 HCC samples and 50 adjacent normal samples from the TCGA database. A total of 1332 DElncRNAs between HCC and nontumor tissues were identified using TCGA-LIHC data (logFC > 1, FDR < 0.05). The heatmap of the top 50 significant DElncRNAs is shown in Fig. 1a. Subsequently, lncRNAs that were independently associated with OS (p < 0.05) were further screened as potential prognostic factors. Among these, RHPN1-AS1 demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy, with an AUC of 0.67. Accordingly, RHPN1-AS1 was selected for further investigation in subsequent bioinformatics analyses.

Expression and prognostic significance of RHPN1-AS1

The expression profile of RHPN1-AS1 was analyzed using data from TCGA. Both paired and unpaired pan-cancer analyses revealed that RHPN1-AS1 was significantly upregulated in multiple tumor types, including bladder carcinoma (BLCA), breast carcinoma (BRCA), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) (Fig. 1b). Further analysis of the TCGA-LIHC cohort demonstrated that RHPN1-AS1 expression was markedly elevated in HCC tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1c). To assess its prognostic relevance across cancers, univariable Cox regression analyses were performed in a pan-cancer setting. Elevated expression of RHPN1-AS1 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis in several cancer types. Specifically, in HCC, high expression of RHPN1-AS1 was an independent risk factor for OS (HR = 2.388, 95% CI 1.655–3.444, p < 0.001), disease-specific survival (DSS) (HR = 2.877, 95% CI 1.784–4.641, p < 0.001), and PFS (HR = 1.698, 95% CI 1.265–2.279, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1d and e).

Coexpression analysis of RHPN1-AS

Then, we analyzed the gene sets co-expressed with RHPN1-AS1. Co-expression analysis results showed that 114 genes had a positive co-expression relationship with RHPN1-AS1 (correlation coefficient > 0.4, p < 0.001), and the corresponding co-expression heatmap is shown in Fig. 2a. Among the 114 genes, six genes with correlation coefficient > 0.5 were selected as representative co-expressed genes, namely, TONSL, CHRAC1, JRK, RECQL4, STK3, and CYHR1. The expression correlations between RHPN1-AS1 and the above six genes are illustrated in Fig. 2b-e.

Enrichment analysis and immune features of RHPN1-AS1 in HCC

To further explore the possible biological functions and signaling pathways of RHPN1-AS1 in HCC, we conducted enrichment and immune cell infiltration analyses. The GO enrichment analysis revealed that genes co-expressed with RHPN1-AS1 were mainly involved in regulating DNA replication and double-strand break repair and were mainly localized in the chromosomal region (Fig. 3a). GSEA indicated that cell cycle-related gene sets were significantly enriched in co-expressed genes, suggesting that RHPN1-AS1 might promote the malignant behavior of HCC cells by regulating the cell cycle. Moreover, the expression of co-expressed genes was positively correlated with the immunoglobulin complex and neuroactive ligand‒receptor interaction and negatively correlated with the T-cell receptor complex and various metabolic processes (Fig. 3b, c). In addition, we applied both the CIBERSORT and ssGSEA algorithms to characterize the associations between RHPN1-AS1 expression and immune-cell infiltration. These analyses demonstrated that RHPN1-AS1 expression was significantly correlated with multiple immune-cell subsets, most notably M0 macrophages (Fig. 3d, e, and f). Taken together, these findings suggest that RHPN1-AS1 may contribute to the modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment, particularly through its influence on macrophage polarization.

Drug sensitivity analysis of RHPN1-AS1

We conducted drug sensitivity analyses to select promising clinical drugs for the high RHPN1-AS1 expression population of HCC. Patients with high RHPN1-AS1 expression exhibited greater sensitivity to axitinib, ribociclib, cisplatin, oxaliplatin, gemcitabine, irinotecan, cytarabine, and fludarabine (Fig. 3e). These results support the utility of RHPN1-AS1 in predicting drug response and assist in guiding personalized treatment strategies in HCC.

To provide validation for our computational drug predictions, we interrogated the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) database. We hypothesized that heightened expression of RHPN1-AS1 would correlate with a greater functional reliance on the molecular targets of ribociclib, specifically CDK4 and CDK6. Consistent with this premise, analysis across 1,112 cancer cell lines revealed a significant positive correlation between RHPN1-AS1 expression and genetic dependency on the CDK4/6 signaling pathway (Spearman’s rho = 0.132, p = 1.06 × 10− 5; Supplementary Fig. 2). This finding corroborates our computational sensitivity prediction, indicating that tumors with high RHPN1-AS1 expression may exhibit increased reliance on CDK4/6 signaling for survival.

Evaluation and verification of the prognostic value of RHPN1-AS1 in HCC

The prognostic characteristics of RHPN1-AS1 were explored using TCGA-LIHC and GSE14520 data. Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis suggested that the OS and PFS times were significantly shorter in the high RHPN1-AS1 expression group compared to the low expression group (both p < 0.001) (Fig. 4a, b). To determine whether RHPN1-AS1 could serve as an independent prognostic biomarker in HCC patients, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted. Univariate Cox regression analysis indicated that RHPN1-AS1 (HR = 2.239, 95% CI 1.550–3.234, p < 0.001), AJCC stage (HR = 1.879, 95% CI 1.466–2.408, p < 0.001), T stage (HR = 1.816, 95% CI 1.443–2.287, p < 0.001) and M stage (HR = 3.924, 95% CI 1.230–12.519, p = 0.021) were linked to OS (Fig. 4c). In multivariate Cox regression analysis, RHPN1-AS1 (HR = 2.139, 95% CI 1.454–3.147, p < 0.001) was significantly correlated with HCC prognosis (Fig. 4d). These results suggested a significant association between RHPN1-AS1 and poor prognosis in HCC patients and may serve as a potential independent prognostic factor.

Based on these results, we designed a nomogram based on the expression level of RHPN1-AS1 and T and M stages integrating the TCGA-LIHC cohort with the GSE14520 dataset from GEO (Fig. 4e). The resulting model demonstrated excellent predictive performance in the merged cohort, with AUC values for 1- to 5-year overall survival rates of 0.722, 0.726, 0.773, 0.809, and 0.844, respectively (Fig. 4f). To further assess the reproducibility of the model across independent cohorts, we performed external validation using GSE76427 and GSE10143. The model maintained stable performance in these cohorts, yielding 1- to 5-year AUCs of 0.717, 0.757, 0.757, 0.742, and 0.803, respectively (Fig. 4g), thereby confirming its robustness across different populations. Moreover, the prediction model showed a good calibration curve, which implied that the nomogram model had good performance in predicting the survival probability of HCC patients (Fig. 4h).

Subgroup survival analysis

To further assess the clinical relevance of RHPN1-AS1, we performed subgroup survival analyses within the TCGA-LIHC cohort based on key clinicopathologic characteristics, including age, sex, AJCC stage, T stage, N stage, M stage, and histologic grade. Cox analysis was not feasible for the M1 and N1 subgroups due to the limited number of available samples. A comprehensive summary of the hazard ratios across subgroups is presented in Fig. 5.

We found that high RHPN1-AS1 expression was significantly associated with poorer OS in multiple subgroups, including all age, male and female, stage I-II, T1-T2, T3-T4, N0, M0, and grade G3-G4. These findings indicate that RHPN1-AS1 retains prognostic value in most subgroups, particularly in patients with early-stage HCC, suggesting that it may complement existing staging systems by improving risk stratification and identifying patients with worse outcomes.

Discussion

HCC is a devastating malignant tumor with high incidence and mortality rates that seriously endangers human health. At present, the 5-year survival rate of HCC patients is still less satisfactory [5]. Since the currently available serum biomarkers for HCC show limited sensitivity and specificity, new prognostic markers are urgently needed to monitor cancer development and improve patient outcomes. LncRNAs have been regarded as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for HCC [28]. In our work, we screened and identified an HCC-related lncRNA, RHPN1-AS1, investigated its prognostic value in HCC, and developed a practical nomogram based on TCGA and GEO database.

RHPN1-AS1 has been identified as a critical modulator in various human cancers. According to existing studies, the expression of RHPN1-AS1 is significantly upregulated in ovarian cancer [17, 20, 25], prostate cancer [22], hepatocellular carcinoma [15, 24], gastric cancer [14], breast cancer [13], cervical cancer [23], glioma [18], colorectal cancer [19], retinoblastoma [16], uveal melanoma [11], head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [12], and nasopharyngeal carcinoma [21]. Mechanistically, RHPN1-AS1 could contributes to tumor progression through diverse molecular pathways. For instance, in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, silencing RHPN1-AS1 reduced cell viability, proliferation, migration, and invasion, which was mediated via mTORC1 signaling modulated by CELF2 [21]. In uveal melanoma, RHPN1-AS1 could influence epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and promote cell proliferation, migration, and invasion both in vitro and in vivo [11]. RHPN1-AS1 serves as an oncogene in gastric cancer through the RHPN1-AS1/miR-1299/ETS1 positive feedback loop [14]. Together, these findings suggest that RHPN1-AS1 functions as a context-dependent oncogene and may represent a common therapeutic target across different tumor types.

Our research demonstrated that RHPN1-AS1 was significantly upregulated in HCC tissues and could act as a potential independent predictor of poor OS in HCC patients. These findings were consistent with previous studies [15, 24]. More importantly, a prognostic nomogram was developed by integrating the expression of RHPN1-AS1 with clinical factors. The nomogram model showed good calibration and AUC values, which indicated that the model was effective at making accurate predictions. Mu et al. highlighted the prognostic significance of PRDX6, showing that its knockdown suppresses HCC cell phenotypes by downregulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR and Notch signaling pathways [29]. The inclusion of RHPN1-AS1 in our nomogram, similar to the prognostic evaluation of PRDX6, may offer a refined tool for improving outcome prediction in patients with HCC.

Furthermore, our results revealed that the RHPN1-AS1 gene was positively coexpressed with TONSL, CHRAC1, JRK, RECQL4, STK3, and CYHR1, which were previously found to be overexpressed in several tumors and associated with poor prognosis [30–35]. Accumulating evidence suggests that these co-expressed genes are involved in critical oncogenic processes, such as cell cycle regulation, cell proliferation, DNA amplification, or the DNA damage response [30–35]. Consistent with these findings, our further analyses demonstrated significant enrichment of RHPN1-AS1 co-expressed genes in DNA replication, cell cycle, and double-strand break repair, highlighting a potential mechanistic link between RHPN1-AS1 and cell cycle dysregulation in HCC.

Although the regulatory mechanism of RHPN1-AS1 in HCC has not yet been elucidated, it has several explanations. One proposed mechanism is that RHPN1-AS1 could promote the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cells by regulating downstream target genes and oncogenic signaling pathways. Among the molecular functions attributed to lncRNAs, one widely studied mechanism is their role as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs). In this context, certain lncRNAs can sequester microRNAs (miRNAs), non-coding short RNAs 19 to 25 nucleotides in length, thus regulating the effect of miRNAs on downstream target genes [36]. In recent studies, lncRNA RHPN1-AS1 has been reported to participate in the carcinogenesis of HCC by acting as a ceRNA [15, 24, 37]. Specifically, RHPN1-AS1 has been reported to regulate several oncogenic axes in HCC, including miR-596/IGF2BP2 [15], miR-485-5p/BSG [37], and miR-485/CDCA5 [24] axes, all of which are involved in cell proliferation and survival pathways. Moreover, the transcription factor STAT1 has been found as an upstream regulator responsible for the transcriptional activation of RHPN1-AS1 in HCC [24]. These findings collectively suggest that RHPN1-AS1 may exert its oncogenic functions in HCC through a ceRNA-based mechanism and transcriptional regulation, although further experimental validation is warranted.

The dysregulation of cell cycle plays a pivotal role in the malignant progression of HCC. For instance, Wang et al. demonstrated that FIGNL1 promotes HCC formation by remodeling the extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interaction pathway via HMMR. They observed that FIGNL1 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation by inducing cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 phase [38]. Of note, our results suggested that RHPN1-AS1 might exert a tumor-promoting effect in HCC through regulation of the cell cycle. Functional enrichment analyses of RHPN1-AS1 co-expressed genes revealed significant involvement in DNA replication, double-strand break repair, and cell cycle progression, indicating a potential mechanistic link between RHPN1-AS1 and cell cycle dysregulation. Furthermore, Ding et al. found that depletion of RHPN1-AS1 in gastric cancer cells suppressed cell proliferation and induced cell cycle arrest in G0/G1 phase [14]. Other studies have reported the involvement of the cell cycle regulatory proteins CDCA5 [24] and CDC20 [39] in the role of RHPN1-AS1 in HCC. Together, these findings support the hypothesis that cell cycle modulation may be a key oncogenic mechanism through which RHPN1-AS1 contributes to HCC progression. However, further experimental validation is required to elucidate the direct targets and pathways involved.

In addition to its potential mechanistic role, RHPN1-AS1 expression may also serve as a potential biomarker for predicting therapeutic response in HCC. Drug sensitivity analysis suggested that patients with high RHPN1-AS1 expression might be more responsive to several targeted and chemotherapeutic agents, including oxaliplatin, cisplatin, gemcitabine, irinotecan, axitinib, and ribociclib. It should be noted that these findings were derived from bioinformatic predictions rather than experimental validation, and thus should be interpreted cautiously. Among these agents, oxaliplatin-based regimen is the standard chemotherapy for advanced HCC [40], whereas axitinib and ribociclib remain under investigation in clinical settings. Interestingly, several of these predicted drugs, including oxaliplatin, gemcitabine, and ribociclib, exert their antitumor effects by interfering with cell cycle progression or DNA synthesis [40, 41], which is consistent with the proposed cell cycle-related regulatory role of RHPN1-AS1.

The limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, due to the absence of some clinical data in TCGA and GEO database, some important clinical variables, such as surgery and neoadjuvant chemotherapy and hepatitis B virus infection status, were not incorporated into the model. This may potentially affect the accuracy and robustness of the model. Second, the nomogram was constructed based on retrospective data from the TCGA and GEO database, and prospective clinical trials should be designed further to validate the predictive capacity of the nomogram model. Third, the proposed functional mechanism of RHPN1-AS1 was primarily inferred from bioinformatics analyses, and thus further in vitro and in vivo studies are required to further elucidate the role and mechanism of RHPN1-AS1 in HCC. In particular, cell cycle-related experiments (e.g., flow cytometry-based cell cycle assays) are needed to verify the regulatory role of RHPN1-AS1.

Conclusions

In conclusion, lncRNA RHPN1-AS1 was upregulated in HCC samples and identified as a potential independent predictor of poor prognosis. A nomogram combining RHPN1-AS1 expression and clinical factors was constructed to evaluate the 1- to 5-year survival of HCC patients. Furthermore, we hypothesize that RHPN1-AS1 may play a pivotal role in HCC progression through its involvement in cell cycle regulation.
