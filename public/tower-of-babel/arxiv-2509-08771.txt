NuLattice : Ab initio computations of atomic nuclei on lattices Journal: Eur. Phys. J. A
M. Rothman addr1,e1, B. Johnson-Toth addr1,e2, G. Hagen addr3,addr1,e5, M. Heinz addr3,addr4,e3, T. Papenbrock addr1,addr3,e4

Abstract
We introduce NuLattice , a Python software package for ab initio computations of atomic nuclei on lattices. The computational tools consist of Hartree Fock, the coupled cluster method, the in-medium similarity renormalization group, and full configuration interaction. At present, the employed interactions are from pion-less effective field theory at leading order and consist of two-body and three-body contacts. We present results for light nuclei 2 H, 3,4 He, 8 Be, 12 C, and 16 O. NuLattice algorithms exploit the sparsity and locality of lattice interactions, and as a result computations can be run on laptops.

Description of problem:

Ab initio computations of atomic nuclei require considerable computing resources and a significant effort to generate and process the nuclear interactions used as input.

Method of solution:

Using a lattice in position space significantly simplifies the generation of nuclear interactions and, due to the short range of the nuclear force, allows computing techniques to exploit sparse data structures for efficient implementations. This program package contains full configuration interaction for light nuclei, Hartree Fock, coupled-cluster method, and the in-medium similarity renormalization group to solve the nuclear many-body problem. It can be used in research and education.

2
Using lattices

2.1
Spherical harmonic oscillator basis
vs.
lattice

The shell model is a basic concept of nuclear structure
Mayer and Jensen 1955
, and it is crucial for the understanding of key nuclear features
Otsuka et al. 2020
. For nuclei that are not too large, it is also a most useful computational tool. The use of the shell model ranges from computations with one or two valence shells above an inert core
Brown and Wildenthal 1988
;
Caurier et al. 2005
;
Shimizu et al. 2012
;
Stroberg et al. 2019
to the no-core shell-model
Navrátil et al. 2009
;
Barrett et al. 2013
to
ab initio
methods that employ a harmonic-oscillator basis as a starting point.

When computing nuclei in large model spaces, however, a shell-model-based approach quickly becomes very expensive. The main reason is that everything that makes the shell model attractive at first glance – the preservation of symmetries, the well-known analytical properties of the harmonic oscillator basis – loses its appeal when dealing with several oscillator shells and/or three-nucleon forces.

The real disadvantage, however, is that the spherical harmonic oscillator basis scrambles the nuclear interactions. An interaction that is local and short ranged becomes non-local and also long ranged when expressed as matrix elements in this basis. In pion-less effective field theory, for instance, the three-nucleon force simply is a short-ranged contact interaction. Expressed in the harmonic oscillator basis of the laboratory system, the three-nucleon contact generates tens of terabytes of data in presently employed model spaces
Miyagi et al. 2022
. For
ab initio
methods that use this basis as a starting point the generation of the Hamiltonian matrix elements takes more time and storage than the ensuing solution of the quantum many-body problem. In spite of clever approaches
Hebeler et al. 2015
;
Hebeler 2021
;
Miyagi et al. 2022
;
Miyagi 2023
, generating the matrix elements for the three-body interactions is presently a limiting factor.

Using a lattice for the single-particle basis avoids these problems
Lee 2009
;
Lähde and Meißner 2019
. The perceived drawbacks, e.g., the loss of rotational symmetry, are controlled approximations. The lack of degeneracy of states in an angular-momentum multiplet decreases with decreasing lattice spacing. The short range of nuclear interactions then leads to very sparse data objects, further reducing the computational cost for processing and using these interactions in quantum many-body solvers.

2.2
Lattice Hamiltonians

Let
D
be the dimension of the single-particle basis. On a cubic lattice with
L^{3}
sites we have

D=4L^{3}

(1)

for nucleons (because of four spin/isospin states per lattice site). Let
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{q}
with

q=(\mathbf{l},\tau_{z},s_{z})

(2)

create a nucleon on the lattice site
\mathbf{l}=(l_{x},l_{y},l_{z})
, isospin projection
\tau_{z}
, and spin projection
s_{z}
. Nuclear Hamiltonians from effective field theories of quantum chromodynamics consist of one-, two-, and three-body operators and have the form

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{pq}\varepsilon_{q}^{p}\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}+{1\over 4}\sum_{pqrs}V^{pq}_{rs}\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{s}\hat{a}_{r}

\displaystyle+{1\over 36}\sum_{pqrsuv}W^{pqr}_{suv}\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{r}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{v}\hat{a}_{u}\hat{a}_{s}\ .

(3)

The one-body operator is the kinetic energy. In leading order of small lattice spacings, it couples only neighboring sites
p,q
and therefore consists of
{\cal O}(D)
terms. Likewise, short-range two- and three-body forces only contain
{\cal O}(D)
terms because the lattice sites of interacting nucleons must be close to each other. Admittedly, for finite-range interactions the number of nonzero two- and three-body matrix element might be
{\cal O}(D)
multiplied with a factor of order 30 or so, but that factor is much smaller than
D
and a constant, i.e., for
L\gg 1
it is independent of the the mass number
A
and of
L
.

Let us compare this with a harmonic oscillator basis. Equation (
2
) has to be replaced by
q=(n,l,j,j_{z},\tau_{z})
where
n
is the principle quantum number,
l
the orbital angular momentum,
j
the total angular momentum, and
j_{z}
its projection. Then the number of nonzero one, two-, and three-body terms in the Hamiltonian (
2.2
) scales approximately as
{\cal O}(D)
,
{\cal O}(D^{3})
, and
{\cal O}(D^{5})
, respectively. This is so, because the conservation of
j_{z}
introduces a Kronecker delta and essentially knocks out one index in each of the sums. For
D\approx 4000
(corresponding to a lattice with
L=10
or a harmonic oscillator basis with excitations up to single-particle energy
16\hbar\omega
) one would need to generate and process about
10^{18}
matrix elements for the three-body interaction. For this reason,
ab initio
computations with a harmonic oscillator basis further truncate the matrix elements of the three-nucleon force such that the sum of three excitation quanta stays below about
30\hbar\omega

Miyagi et al. 2022
. In contrast, the lattice computations
Elhatisari et al. 2024
face no such problems and work with three-nucleon forces on
L=10
lattices.

We note that four-body forces enter in pion-less and in chiral effective field theory at next-to-leading and next-to-next-to-next-to leading order, respectively. For the foreseeable future, it is not practical to work with such forces in the harmonic oscillator basis
Schulz 2018
. In contrast, the lattice computations
Epelbaum et al. 2010
employed a four-body contact already long ago.

Thus the do-it-yourself
ab initio
computations use a lattice as the single-particle basis. This exploits the short range of the nuclear force, avoids scrambling of matrix elements, and allows one to use sparse data techniques in the ensuing solutions of the quantum many-body problem.

We store the nonzero matrix elements of the Hamiltonian (
2.2
) as Python lists

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{q}^{p}

\displaystyle\to\left[\left[p,q,\varepsilon_{p}^{q}\right],\ldots\right]\ ,

\displaystyle V^{pq}_{rs}

\displaystyle\to\left[\left[p,q,r,s,V^{pq}_{rs}\right],\ldots\right]\ ,

(4)

\displaystyle W^{pqr}_{suv}

\displaystyle\to\left[\left[p,q,r,s,u,v,W^{pqr}_{suv}\right],\ldots\right]\ .

Note here that we only store two-body matrix elements
V^{pq}_{rs}
for
p<q
and
r<s
and three-body matrix elements
W^{pqr}_{suv}
for
p<q<r
and
s<u<v
. At present, the code only uses real-valued matrix elements. One could contemplate to further reduce storage needs by exploiting the symmetry of the matrix elements
\varepsilon_{q}^{p}=\varepsilon^{q}_{p}
and
V^{pq}_{rs}=V_{pq}^{rs}
and

W^{pqr}_{suv}=W_{pqr}^{suv}
. However, this would complicate tensor contractions and is therefore not used.

We work on a lattice with spacing
a
and extent
La
and use periodic boundary conditions. We denote the nucleon mass as
m
; lattice energies are then given in units of

e_{\rm lat}\equiv\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2ma^{2}}\ .

(5)

In this paper we consider a simple nuclear Hamiltonian and choose to work in the framework of pion-less effective field theory at leading order. The Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{l}\mathbf{l}^{\prime}}\sum_{\tau s}T_{\mathbf{l}^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{l}}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}\tau s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}^{\prime}\tau s}

\displaystyle+{V\over 2}\sum_{\mathbf{l}}\sum_{ss^{\prime}\tau\tau^{\prime}}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}\tau s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}\tau^{\prime}s^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}\tau^{\prime}s^{\prime}}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}\tau s}

\displaystyle+W\sum_{\mathbf{l}}\sum_{\tau s}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}\tau\uparrow}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}\tau\downarrow}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}-\tau s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}-\tau s}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}\tau\downarrow}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{l}\tau\uparrow}\ .

(6)

The matrix elements of the kinetic energy are the leading-order approximation of the Laplacian on the lattice

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{l}^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{l}}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2ma^{2}}\sum_{i=x,y,z}\left(\delta_{\mathbf{l}^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{l}-\mathbf{e}_{i}}-2\delta_{\mathbf{l}^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{l}}+\delta_{\mathbf{l}^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{l}+\mathbf{e}_{i}}\right).

(7)

Here

is a unit vector in the direction

V=\frac{\hbar^{2}v}{2ma^{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad W=\frac{\hbar^{2}w}{2ma^{2}}\ ,

where

We note that the potential of the Hamiltonian (
2.2
) is diagonal in the lattice basis. For the three-body interaction this is so because the interaction is on-site and preserves spin projection. Likewise, the two-body potential is diagonal because it also is limited to on-site interactions, preserves spin projection, and exhibits Wigner’s SU(4) symmetry.
As the potential is diagonal in the lattice basis, the Hamiltonian (
2.2
) exhibits neither two-particle–two-hole nor three-particle–three-hole excitations. This has the following consequences. The lack of two-particle–two-hole excitations implies that Hartree-Fock computations (which transform the Hamiltonian such that it exhibits no one-particle–one-hole excitations) will yield the bulk of the ground-state energies and that contributions from two-particle–two-hole excitations in coupled-cluster theory are expected to be small. The lack of three-particle–three-hole excitations implies that the normal-ordered two-body truncation is exact (i.e., it is not an approximation) in coupled-cluster with singles and doubles computations

2.3
Full configuration interaction on lattices

Full configuration interaction (FCI) is available for nuclei with mass numbers
A=2,3,4

A

2.2
) to construct the matrices for the kinetic energy, the two-body interaction, and the three-body interaction. These are stored as a

scipy.sparse

linalg.eigsh
from
scipy.sparse

1

L=4

25\times 10^{6}

(4^{3})^{4}\approx 17\times 10^{6}

2.5
Coupled-cluster computations on lattices

The nuclear coupled-cluster cluster method were reviewed in Refs.
Hagen et al. 2014
;
Hergert 2020
. Here we recall essential aspects and point out unique features when performing these calculations on the lattice.

In the harmonic-oscillator basis, coupled-cluster computations would start from a Hartree-Fock reference state. On a lattice, however, this is probably not advised, because the Hartree-Fock basis scrambles matrix elements and takes away the advantage of sparse interaction input brought on by using a lattice. This means that the the impact of singles amplitudes will be significant.

The reference state is

|\phi\rangle=\prod_{i=1}^{A}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}|0\rangle\ .

(11)

Here and in what follows we use the convention that indices
i,j,k,\ldots
refer to occupied (hole) states and
a,b,c,\ldots
to unoccupied (particle) states. If no distinction is made, we use indices
p,q,r,\ldots
. Coupled-cluster computations then start from the normal-ordered Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=E_{\rm ref}+\sum_{pq}F_{q}^{p}\left\{\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}\right\}+{1\over 4}\sum_{pqrs}\Gamma^{pq}_{rs}\left\{\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{s}\hat{a}_{r}\right\}

\displaystyle+{1\over 36}\sum_{pqrstu}W^{pqr}_{stu}\left\{\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{r}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{u}\hat{a}_{t}\hat{a}_{u}\right\}\ ,

(12)

where the brackets
\{\cdot\}
indicate normal ordering with respect to the reference state. In Eq. (
2.5
) we introduced the reference state energy and normal-ordered matrix elements

\displaystyle E_{\rm ref}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{i}+{1\over 2}\sum_{ij}V_{ij}^{ij}+{1\over 6}\sum_{ijk}W_{ijk}^{ijk}\ ,

\displaystyle F_{q}^{p}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{q}^{p}+\sum_{i}V_{iq}^{ip}+{1\over 2}\sum_{ij}W^{ijp}_{ijq}\ ,

(13)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{pq}_{rs}

\displaystyle=V^{pq}_{rs}+\sum_{i}W_{irs}^{ipq}\ .

We note that the normal ordering is performed with respect to reference states that break rotation and translation invariance. In the shell model, one can devise a normal ordering that respects rotational symmetry
Frosini et al. 2021
. An attempt to do this here would probably also lead to a loss of sparsity.

In coupled-cluster theory one computes the matrix elements of the similarity transformed Hamiltonian

\overline{H}\equiv e^{-\hat{T}}\hat{H}e^{\hat{T}}\ .

(14)

In the coupled-cluster with singles and doubles (CCSD) approximation, the cluster operator

\displaystyle\hat{T}=\sum_{ia}t_{i}^{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{a}\hat{a}_{i}+{1\over 4}\sum_{ijab}t_{ij}^{ab}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{b}\hat{a}_{j}\hat{a}_{i}+\ldots

(15)

consists of one-particle–one-hole and two-particle–two-hole excitations.
The amplitudes
t_{i}^{a}
and
t_{ij}^{ab}
are determined by solving the nonlinear set of equations

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{i}^{a}

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\phi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{a}_{a}\overline{H}|\phi\rangle=0\ ,

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{ij}^{ab}

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\phi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{a}_{b}\hat{a}_{a}\overline{H}|\phi\rangle=0\ .

(16)

We refer to the left-hand sides of these equations as the coupled-cluster residuals and denote them by
r
. In what follows we use an equally compact notation and refer to
t^{a}_{i}
and
t^{ab}_{ij}
simply as
t
.
The CCSD Eqs. (
2.5
) are usually solved using the inexact Newton’s method
Yang et al. 2020
. This is an iterative approach which updates the coupled cluster amplitudes at step
(k+1)
as

\displaystyle t^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle\to t^{(k)}-\left[J^{(k)}\right]^{-1}r^{(k)}\ .

(17)

Here,
J^{(k)}
is the coupled-cluster Jacobian, which, in its simplest approximation, consists of the the diagonal parts
F_{p}^{p}
of the Fock matrix, i.e.
F_{i}^{i}-F_{a}^{a}
for the singles amplitudes and
F_{i}^{i}+F_{j}^{j}-F_{a}^{a}-F_{b}^{b}
for the doubles amplitudes
Kjønstad et al. 2020
. For product states on the lattice, however, these energy differences may vanish. This is because of translational invariance; the reference state is not unique but rather highly degenerate. To avoid this problem, we add a positive constant to the difference in the first iteration. In subsequent iterations we replace the diagonal matrix elements of the Fock matrix by the diagonal elements
\overline{H}_{p}^{p}
of the similarity-transformed Hamiltonian (
14
). This is a more accurate approximation of the coupled-cluster Jacobian.

Our solver of the coupled-cluster equations also allows one to mix old and new solutions via

t^{(k+1)}\leftarrow at^{(k+1)}+(1-a)t^{(k)}\ ,

(18)

(where
0\leq a\leq 1
), and we use the direct inversion of the iterative subspace (DIIS) method
Pulay 1980
;
Scuseria et al. 1986
for an accelerated convergence.

Once the coupled-cluster amplitudes
t_{i}^{a}
and
t_{ij}^{ab}
are known, the ground state energy becomes

E_{\rm CCS}=E_{0}+\sum_{ia}F_{a}^{i}t_{i}^{a}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijab}\Gamma_{ab}^{ij}t_{i}^{a}t_{j}^{b}

(19)

in the CCS approximation, and

E_{\rm CCSD}=E_{\rm CCS}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{ijab}\Gamma_{ab}^{ij}t_{ij}^{ab}

(20)

for CCSD. Equations (
19
) and (
20
) are both valid in the normal-ordered two-body approximation. The energy correction from three-body forces was presented in Ref.
Hagen et al. 2007
.

In
NuLattice
the coupled cluster computations start by construction of the normal-ordered two-body Hamiltonian (
2.5
), truncated at two-body rank. Coupled-cluster theory distinguishes between particle and hole states, and the normal-ordered two-body Hamiltonian contains six types of such matrix elements, namely
\Gamma^{ab}_{cd}
,
\Gamma^{ab}_{ci}
,
\Gamma^{ab}_{ij}
,
\Gamma^{ai}_{bj}
,
\Gamma^{ai}_{kl}
, and
\Gamma^{ij}_{kl}
(here we exploited that the Hamiltonian is Hermitian). In general, the particle indices run over many more single-particle states than the hole indices. Thus we store
\Gamma^{ab}_{cd}
and
\Gamma^{ab}_{ci}
as sparse lists (
2.2
) but
\Gamma^{ab}_{ij}
,
\Gamma^{ai}_{bj}
,
\Gamma^{ai}_{kl}
, and
\Gamma^{ij}_{kl}
as dense four-dimensional
numpy
arrays. Likewise, we store the matrix elements
\overline{H}^{ab}_{ij}
and the coupled-cluster amplitudes
t^{ab}_{ij}
as dense four-dimensional
numpy
arrays. The storage demands of these
numpy
arrays sets the limits on what lattice sizes and nuclei can be held in fast memory and on what can thus be computed efficiently.
We note here that the lion’s share of the required memory comes from storing about ten amplitudes
t^{ab}_{ij}
when using the DIIS method.

Let us also discuss the runtime requirements. Using dense tensors, the most expensive CCSD diagram (in the-normal-ordered two-body approximation) has a cost of

2.6
In-medium similarity renormalization group on the lattice

The IMSRG
Tsukiyama et al. 2011
was reviewed in Refs.
Hergert et al. 2016
;
Stroberg et al. 2019
, so here we only recap the most essential details necessary to understand the IMSRG calculations in this work.

As with coupled-cluster theory, one starts from a Slater determinant reference state, Eq. (
11
), and the normal-ordered Hamiltonian, Eq. (
2.5
).
To solve the many-body Schrödinger equation, the IMSRG computes a continuous unitary similarity transformation of the Hamiltonian

\overline{H}(s)\equiv U(s)\hat{H}U^{\dagger}(s)

(23)

via the integration of the IMSRG flow equation

\frac{d\overline{H}(s)}{ds}=\left[\eta(s),\overline{H}(s)\right]

(24)

with the initial condition that
U(s=0)=1
.
The generator
\eta(s)
is chosen to produce the desired unitary transformation.

In general, even if one starts with a Hamiltonian with only up to normal-ordered two-body terms,

\displaystyle\overline{H}(s=0)

\displaystyle=E_{\rm ref}+\sum_{pq}F_{q}^{p}\left\{\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}\right\}+{1\over 4}\sum_{pqrs}\Gamma^{pq}_{rs}\left\{\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{s}\hat{a}_{r}\right\}\ ,

(25)

the right-hand side of Eq. (
24
) will contain three-body (and later on higher-body) terms.
This means that the IMSRG induces effective many-body interactions,

\displaystyle\overline{H}(s)

\displaystyle=E(s)+\sum_{pq}F_{q}^{p}(s)\left\{\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}\right\}+{1\over 4}\sum_{pqrs}\Gamma^{pq}_{rs}(s)\left\{\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{s}\hat{a}_{r}\right\}

\displaystyle+{1\over 36}\sum_{pqrstu}W^{pqr}_{stu}(s)\left\{\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{r}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{u}\hat{a}_{t}\hat{a}_{u}\right\}+\dots\ .

(26)

The IMSRG(2) approximation truncates the IMSRG equation at the normal-ordered two-body level for all
s
, discarding, e.g., the induced normal-ordered three-body interactions
W^{pqr}_{stu}(s)
.
Recent work has extended the IMSRG to the IMSRG(3) level, where this truncation is relaxed, and shown the IMSRG(2) to be accurate for ground-state properties of medium-mass nuclei
Heinz et al. 2021
;
Stroberg et al. 2024
;
Heinz et al. 2025
.
In this work, we exclusively study the IMSRG(2) approximation.

In the IMSRG(2), the generator
\eta(s)
is chosen such that

\displaystyle F^{i}_{a}(s\to\infty)

\displaystyle=0\ ,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{ij}_{ab}(s\to\infty)

\displaystyle=0\ ,

(27)

reaching the same decoupling condition as in CCSD:

\displaystyle\langle\phi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{a}_{a}\overline{H}(s\to\infty)|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=0\ ,

\displaystyle\langle\phi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{a}_{b}\hat{a}_{a}\overline{H}(s\to\infty)|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=0\ .

(28)

Various choices for generators exist (see Ref.
Hergert et al. 2016
for details), and all are equivalent up to errors induced by the IMSRG(2) approximation.
The final IMSRG energy is then simply

E_{\mathrm{IMSRG}}=E(s\to\infty)
.

The main computational task in the IMSRG is the evaluation of the right-hand side of Eq. (
24
).
The computational cost is dominated by the normal-ordered two-body matrix elements resulting from the commutator of the two-body part of the generator, with matrix elements
\eta^{pq}_{rs}
, and the two-body part of the Hamiltonian, with matrix elements
\Gamma^{pq}_{rs}
.
One such contribution is

X^{pq}_{rs}\leftarrow\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tu}(\bar{n}_{t}\bar{n}_{u}-n_{t}n_{u})\left(\eta^{pq}_{tu}\Gamma^{tu}_{rs}-\Gamma^{pq}_{tu}\eta^{tu}_{rs}\right)\ ,

(29)

with the occupation number
n_{p}=1
for hole states, 0 for particle states, and
\bar{n}_{p}=1-n_{p}
.
This can be easily evaluated in Python using
opt_einsum
.

As the Hamiltonian
\overline{H}(s)
is transformed and evolved, its structure changes.
As a result, it is not as straightforward to develop a simple sparse approach to solving the IMSRG equations, as the sparsity and the sparsity structure is not constant.
For this reason we do not explore sparse IMSRG(2) calculations in this work,
focusing on lattice sizes

At
L=3

However, past work has explored importance truncation in IMSRG calculations
Hoppe et al. 2022

Such developments can be leveraged to also allow for sparse IMSRG calculations on a lattice, which would enable calculations on larger lattices. Similar efforts in self-consistent Green’s function theory may also prove instrumental when adding those methods to

Porro et al. 2021

5
Outlook

This package evolved from a research project and has also been used in education. The recent paper
Gu et al.
on quantum computing of atomic nuclei used routines from
NuLattice
to generate lattice Hamiltonians from pion-less effective field theory and to benchmark with results from full configuration interaction. Recent coupled-cluster computations used routines from
NuLattice
and reported on the exactness of the normal-ordered two-body approximation for zero-range three nucleon forces
Rothman et al.
. In the 2025 summer school “Emergence of Collective Motion in Atomic Nuclei” of the FRIB Theory Alliance,
NuLattice
routines from full configuration interaction were used by students to learn about (i) the convergence patterns of light nuclei with respect to increasing lattice sizes, (ii) the renormalization and “running” of coupling constants in pion-less effective field theory through variation of the lattice spacing, and (iii) the need of a three-body contact
Bedaque et al. 1999
. While
NuLattice
now contains several person-months of work and about 4500 lines (including doc strings) of Python code, the development and implementation of its routines was much simpler and faster than for
ab initio
computations that use the harmonic oscillator basis. Most importantly, interesting and useful computations can be performed on laptops.

Much work remains. Finite-range interactions
Elhatisari et al. 2016
;
Lu et al. 2019
and interactions from chiral effective field theory
Elhatisari et al. 2024
are lacking; higher precision in coupled-cluster and IMSRG computations is desirable; a sparse solution of the IMSRG equations would enable calculations on large lattice sizes; observables other than the ground-state energy are of interest; excited states need to be computed. The list goes on and on.

We invite readers to join us in this effort!
NuLattice
is documented, and the authors of each module have been identified and credited. This makes it easy to contribute to
NuLattice
and to receive recognition for one’s work.

6
Summary

This paper introduced the software package
NuLattice
, a Python package of computational tools for
ab initio
computations of nuclei on lattices. Using
NuLattice
, we performed
ab initio
computations of nuclei on lattices, using Hartree Fock, coupled-cluster theory, the in-medium similarity renormalization group, and full configuration interaction. The employed Hamiltonians were from pion-less effective field theory at leading-order and consisted of two-body and three-body contact interactions. We found that Hartree Fock and coupled-cluster with singles yielded the bulk of the ground-state energy for compact nuclei. We confirmed that pion-less effective field theory fails to bind
\alpha
particles into nuclei. We hope that
NuLattice
might be useful to researchers and educators and invite readers to contribute.
