Laparoscopic versus open partial hepatectomy for hepatocellular carcinoma in F4 cirrhosis: A propensity score-matched study of perioperative safety
Authors: Hiroaki Sugita, Shinichi Nakanuma, Tomokazu Tokoro, Ryohei Takei, Mitsuyoshi Okazaki, Kaichiro Kato, Satoshi Takada, Isamu Makino, Shintaro Yagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgrounds/AimsPatients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis often have limited hepatic reserve, making major hepatectomy difficult. Although laparoscopic liver resection is increasingly performed, its perioperative safety in this setting remains unclear. This study compared laparoscopic and open partial hepatectomy in these patients using propensity score matching (PSM).MethodsAmong 298 patients who underwent hepatectomy for HCC between 2006 and 2023, 112 with histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis who underwent partial hepatectomy were included (laparoscopic, n = 60; open, n = 52). PSM was performed using previous liver resection, difficulty score, tumor size, tumor number, and platelet count, yielding 32 matched pairs. Outcomes included operative time, blood loss, transfusion requirements, complications, hospital stay, posthepatectomy liver failure (PHLF), R0 resection rate, and 30-day mortality.ResultsAfter matching, intraoperative blood loss was significantly lower in the laparoscopic group than in the open group (50 mL vs. 295 mL, p < 0.001). Operative time, transfusion rate, complication rate, R0 resection rate, and 30-day mortality were comparable between groups. Hospital stay was significantly shorter in the laparoscopic group (13 vs. 22 days, p < 0.001). Grade A PHLF occurred in one patient in the open group, with no significant between-group difference.ConclusionsIn patients with HCC and histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis, laparoscopic partial hepatectomy was associated with less blood loss and shorter hospital stay than open surgery, without increasing perioperative morbidity. It may be a safe option in carefully selected patients undergoing limited liver resection.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Liver cirrhosis, Laparoscopy, Hepatectomy, Propensity score

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) often develops in patients with chronic liver disease, with cirrhosis being the most significant prognostic factor for postoperative complications and long-term outcomes. Patients with F4 cirrhosis, which corresponds to stage 4 fibrosis in international histological classifications, may have a diminished hepatic functional reserve. This reduction can limit the feasibility of major hepatectomy due to the heightened risk of posthepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) [1,2]. As a result, partial hepatectomy is typically preferred for those with F4 cirrhosis, as it preserves more functional liver tissue and aligns better with the reduced regenerative capacity associated with advanced cirrhosis [3].

Laparoscopic hepatectomy (LH) has gained acceptance as a minimally invasive alternative to open hepatectomy (OH). It offers several advantages, including reduced blood loss, less postoperative pain, and shorter hospital stays while maintaining oncological adequacy for appropriately selected patients [4-6]. In cirrhotic patients, LH may further reduce complications by minimizing abdominal wall trauma and preserving venous–lymphatic collateral pathways, potentially lowering the incidence of postoperative ascites and wound-related issues [7,8]. However, patients with histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis present a unique clinical challenge, and the use of LH in this group is approached with caution.

Previous studies comparing LH and OH often involved a mix of surgical procedures and various stages of fibrosis, leading to significant selection bias, as laparoscopic techniques were more frequently applied to patients with more favorable tumor characteristics. To address this, propensity score matching (PSM) has been increasingly employed to account for baseline differences and enhance the validity of retrospective comparisons [9].

Despite the growing use of LH for HCC, its application in patients with strictly F4 cirrhosis remains one of the more difficult scenarios for liver resection. This is due to potential limitations in hepatic reserve, portal hypertension, and thrombocytopenia, which considerably heighten technical complexity and perioperative risks. While previous PSM-based studies have shown that LH is safe and feasible for cirrhotic patients, most have concentrated on major or mixed resections and included varying stages of fibrosis [10-12]. In contrast, there is limited evidence specifically addressing laparoscopic partial hepatectomy in patients with histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis, despite this procedure often being considered for carefully selected patients with advanced cirrhosis. Thus, this study aimed to evaluate the technical feasibility and perioperative outcomes of laparoscopic versus open partial hepatectomy in patients with F4 cirrhosis, utilizing PSM analysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and patients

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Kanazawa University Hospital. From January 2006 to December 2023, a total of 298 patients underwent hepatectomy for HCC and were pathologically diagnosed with F4 liver cirrhosis after surgery. Among these patients, 153 underwent partial hepatectomy. After excluding cases involving the resection of organs other than the gallbladder, 112 patients were included in the final analysis. The focus on partial hepatectomy in this study is due to the limited hepatic functional reserve in patients with histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis, which often leads to the avoidance of major anatomical hepatectomy due to increased operative risks.

Surgical indications

Preoperative hepatic functional reserve was assessed using the Child-Pugh classification and the indocyanine green retention rate at 15 minutes. Liver resection was indicated for patients classified as Child-Pugh class A or B. According to the Makuuchi criteria, partial hepatectomy was deemed appropriate for patients without ascites or with well-controlled ascites and a total bilirubin level ≤ 1.5 mg/dL [13]. In this study, F4 cirrhosis was defined pathologically after surgery; however, preoperative assessment of chronic liver disease relied on routine clinical evaluations, including imaging findings, platelet count, liver surface nodularity, splenomegaly, history of viral hepatitis, elastography, and liver biopsy when applicable.

During the study period from January 2006 to December 2023, LH was introduced at our institution in September 2009, with its indication expanded to include patients with liver cirrhosis starting in October 2010. For cirrhotic patients, both LH and OH were performed by board-certified surgeons.

Treatment strategies for HCC were determined at a multidisciplinary cancer board conference, which included surgeons, hepatologists, and radiologists, following the Japanese Clinical Practice Guidelines for HCC [14].

The difficulty of LH was evaluated using the difficulty score for laparoscopic liver resection proposed by Ban et al. [15]. Since 2015, this scoring system has been applied prospectively to assess the technical difficulty of LH. In the current study, surgical difficulty was retrospectively assessed using the same scoring system for all cases, including those treated prior to its routine clinical implementation, and was included in the PSM analysis. For cases treated before the prospective implementation of the scoring system in 2015, the difficulty score was retrospectively assigned based on operative records and preoperative imaging by a board-certified hepatobiliary surgeon. Tumors located in segment 1 were classified as posterosuperior lesions when calculating the difficulty score.

The surgical strategy was determined comprehensively, taking into account tumor characteristics, liver function, and technical feasibility, rather than relying solely on the pathological fibrosis stage, which was confirmed postoperatively.

Surgical techniques

During the study period, hepatectomies were performed in both the laparoscopic and open groups following a consistent surgical approach. In both groups, the round ligament of the liver was preserved whenever feasible to maintain collateral circulation. Intraoperative ultrasonography was routinely utilized to confirm tumor location and determine the transection line.

In the laparoscopic group, patient positioning was based on tumor location. For tumors in the right liver, patients were positioned in the left semi-lateral decubitus position with a reverse Trendelenburg tilt, while for tumors in the left liver, a reverse Trendelenburg position was employed. Pneumoperitoneum was maintained at 10 mmHg. The camera port was placed near the umbilicus with careful attention to avoid injury to collateral circulation, and additional ports were positioned under the right costal margin according to the tumor’s location.

In both groups, hepatic parenchymal transection was performed using the intermittent Pringle maneuver (15 minutes of inflow occlusion followed by 5 minutes of reperfusion) with an ultrasonic aspirator that has a soft coagulation function. Vessels measuring ≤ 3 mm in diameter were divided using ultrasonic coagulating shears or a vessel sealing system. Vessels larger than 3 mm were managed by ligation or clipping in the open group, while in the laparoscopic group, they were clipped before division. Hemostasis during parenchymal transection was achieved through compression, soft coagulation, or suturing as appropriate. In this study, partial hepatectomy was defined as a limited non-anatomical resection of the liver, including the tumor with an adequate surgical margin; anatomical resections such as subsegmentectomy and segmentectomy were not included.

Outcomes

Short-term operative outcomes were assessed based on several factors: operative time, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion requirements, and the occurrence of intraoperative complications. Intraoperative blood loss was calculated by subtracting the volume of irrigation fluid from the total volume in the suction canister, along with an estimation of blood absorbed by surgical gauze. Postoperative outcomes included minor complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≤ II), major complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III), length of postoperative hospital stay, PHLF diagnosed according to the International Study Group of Liver Surgery criteria using postoperative day 5 bilirubin and international normalized ratio (INR) values (serum total bilirubin > 1.2 mg/dL and INR > 1.2 on or after postoperative day 5 were considered abnormal), R0 resection rate, and 30-day mortality.

Data collection

Clinical variables were extracted from electronic medical records, encompassing demographic characteristics, tumor-related factors, operative variables, pathological resection margin status, postoperative complications, and length of postoperative hospital stay. Complications were graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification. Portal hypertension-related findings were defined as the presence of at least one preoperative condition: esophagogastric varices, collateral vessels, splenomegaly, or ascites.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians with ranges and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages, analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical analyses were conducted using EZR version 1.68 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University), a graphical user interface for R [16]. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Propensity score matching

To minimize selection bias between the LH and OH groups, PSM was implemented using EZR. A logistic regression model was developed to calculate propensity scores based on clinically relevant covariates: history of previous liver resection, difficulty score, maximum tumor diameter, number of tumors, and platelet count. These covariates were selected a priori due to their potential impact on surgical approach and perioperative outcomes. To address the limited sample size, a parsimonious model was utilized, excluding additional liver function variables to avoid over-specification since major liver function parameters showed no significant baseline imbalance between the groups. One-to-one nearest neighbor matching without replacement was conducted, using a caliper width of 0.2 times the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. The balance of covariates before and after matching was evaluated using standardized mean differences.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kanazawa University (approval number: 3223-4). Given the retrospective design, patient consent was obtained through an opt-out method, in accordance with institutional and national ethical guidelines. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

The rate of postoperative minor complications, defined as Clavien-Dindo grade ≤ II, did not differ significantly between the LH and OH groups. Similarly, the incidence of major complications, defined as Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III, showed no statistically significant differences between the two groups.

Postoperative hospital stay was significantly shorter in the LH group compared to the OH group (median 13 days vs. 22 days, p < 0.001).

Clinically significant PHLF (≥ grade B) was not observed in any patients in either group. Only one patient in the OH group met the criteria for grade A PHLF, with no significant difference between the two groups. The 30-day mortality rate was 0% in both cohorts.

The R0 resection rate was comparable between the LH and OH groups, with no significant difference detected.

DISCUSSION

In this propensity score–matched study, which exclusively examined patients with HCC and histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis, we found that laparoscopic partial hepatectomy resulted in significantly less intraoperative blood loss and shorter postoperative hospital stays compared to open surgery. Importantly, this approach did not increase postoperative morbidity, major complications, or mortality. These results underscore the perioperative safety of laparoscopic partial hepatectomy in this carefully selected group of patients with advanced cirrhosis.

A key strength of this study is its focus on patients with histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis undergoing partial hepatectomy alone. Previous comparative studies often included a mix of fibrosis stages and surgical procedures, complicating the assessment of laparoscopic techniques in patients with advanced cirrhosis [10-12]. By employing PSM with clinically relevant covariates such as tumor burden, platelet count, and difficulty score, we established a well-balanced comparison between laparoscopic and open surgery. The use of propensity score methodology has become an essential tool for minimizing confounding in retrospective surgical studies [9].

The significantly lower blood loss in the laparoscopic group is clinically important, especially for patients with advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis, who often have limited hematologic and hepatic reserves [1,2]. This benefit aligns with the established advantages of laparoscopic liver resection highlighted in systematic reviews and consensus recommendations [4-6]. Factors unique to laparoscopy, such as optimized patient positioning and the tamponade effect of controlled pneumoperitoneum, may contribute to this advantage by reducing low-pressure venous bleeding from the transection surface. Notably, these benefits were maintained even in patients with F4 cirrhosis in this selected study population, despite the challenges posed by portal hypertension and fragile collateral vessels.

While the transfusion rate in the laparoscopic group may appear relatively high given the low median blood loss, transfusion decisions at our institution were influenced not only by intraoperative blood loss but also by perioperative hematological status. Ensuring safety for cirrhotic patients with thrombocytopenia and/or coagulopathy was a priority. Thus, transfusions were primarily administered perioperatively to address preexisting hematological abnormalities rather than as a response to significant intraoperative hemorrhage. This approach underscores the clinical necessity of maintaining safety in patients with cirrhosis, who are particularly vulnerable to perioperative hematologic instability.

The shorter postoperative hospital stay observed after laparoscopic surgery suggests enhanced recovery, a factor that is becoming increasingly important in the current era of multimodal HCC treatment. Treatment strategies for HCC have rapidly evolved with the development of molecular-targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors [17]. Recent evidence indicates that combining liver resection with systemic therapy may improve survival in selected patients [18]. Additionally, minimally invasive approaches may further enhance the feasibility and continuity of postoperative systemic treatment by reducing surgical stress and facilitating faster recovery [19].

Although the median hospital stay in the LH group may seem longer than that reported in some contemporary series, our institution typically discharges cirrhotic patients cautiously. Discharge requires confirmation of stable oral intake, mobilization, adequate pain control, absence of uncontrolled ascites or infection, and preserved liver function. This careful approach may explain the relatively long hospital stay in the laparoscopic group. In contrast, the significantly longer hospital stay in the open group may be partly due to a tendency for more frequent minor complications—though not statistically significant—and slower recovery of oral intake and pain control.

Importantly, no clinically relevant grade B or C PHLF was observed in either group, and only one patient in the open group developed grade A PHLF, with no significant difference between the groups. These findings further support the perioperative safety of laparoscopic partial hepatectomy in carefully selected patients with histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis undergoing limited liver resection.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and non-randomized design carries an inherent risk of selection bias and unmeasured confounding, although PSM was applied to mitigate baseline differences.

Although additional liver function variables were not included in the PSM model, this approach was chosen to avoid overfitting in a relatively small cohort. Second, this was a single-center study conducted at a high-volume institution, which may limit generalizability. Third, the long study period may have introduced era-related bias; LH was introduced for cirrhotic patients at our institution in 2010, and patients in the LH group were still significantly more likely to belong to the later study period even after PSM. Therefore, while the overall surgical procedures and treatment strategies, including postoperative management, remained generally consistent throughout the study period, advances in surgical devices, accumulated experience, and improvements in perioperative care may have influenced the perioperative outcomes. Additionally, although all patients had histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis, many had relatively preserved liver function. Consequently, the findings should primarily apply to carefully selected patients with compensated or mildly impaired cirrhosis undergoing partial hepatectomy. Finally, long-term oncological outcomes were not evaluated; however, the primary objective of this study was to assess perioperative safety in patients with histologically confirmed advanced cirrhosis undergoing limited liver resection.

In conclusion, laparoscopic partial hepatectomy for HCC in patients with histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis is associated with reduced blood loss and shorter hospital stays compared to open surgery, without increasing perioperative morbidity. These findings suggest that laparoscopic partial hepatectomy can be considered a safe surgical option for carefully selected patients with histologically confirmed F4 cirrhosis undergoing partial hepatectomy.
