Lensing, not luck! Detection prospects of strongly lensed gravitational waves
Ankur Barsode a, Koustav N. Maity b, Parameswaran Ajith c

Abstract
A small fraction of gravitational-wave (GW) signals detected by ground-based observatories will be strongly lensed by intervening galaxies or clusters. This may produce multiple copies of the signals (i.e., lensed images) arriving at different times at the detector. These, if observed, could offer new probes of astrophysics and cosmology. However, identification of lensed image pairs among a large number of unrelated GW events is challenging. Though the number of lensed events increases with improved detector sensitivity, the false alarms increase quadratically faster. While this “lensing or luck” problem would appear to be insurmountable, we show that the expected increase in measurement precision of source parameters will efficiently weed out false alarms. Based on current astrophysical models and anticipated sensitivities, we predict that the first confident detection could occur in the fifth observing run of LIGO, Virgo, and KAGRA. We expect computational costs to be a major hurdle in achieving such a detection, and show that the Posterior Overlap 2.0 method may offer a near-optimal solution to this challenge.

Under the geometric optics approximation, the waveforms of strongly lensed GWs are similar to those of unlensed GWs except for an overall magnification, a Morse phase shift of integer multiples of
\pi/2
, and a time delay
(
Dai & Venumadhav 2017
;
Ezquiaga et al. 2021
)
. Therefore, most such strongly lensed signals can be
detected
using the standard GW searches using unlensed waveform templates.

Despite this simplicity, it is challenging to
identify
pairs of lensed GWs among the multitude of possible pairs in the detected signals, owing to the modest signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) of GW detections and the rarity of lensing events. Thus, lensing searches typically compute the Bayesian likelihood ratio between the “lensed vs. unlensed” hypotheses from every pair of detected signals (see
Barsode et al. 2025
for a review). However, noise fluctuations and accidental consistency between unlensed events can cause false alarms
(
Caliskan et al. 2023
)
. Thus, one puts a high enough threshold on the Bayesian likelihood ratio corresponding to an acceptably low false positive probability (FPP).

The issue of false alarms will worsen with the number of GW detections
N
: While the expected number of lensed image pairs (true positives) will grow
\propto N
, the number of unlensed pairs among all detections (false positives) will grow
\propto N^{2}
. This is the problem of “lensing or luck”
(
Caliskan et al. 2023
)
. If, in the hope of encountering more lensed signals, one observes for a longer duration, it becomes
harder
to distinguish between truly lensed events and (un)lucky unlensed pairs showing accidental consistency.
Caliskan et al. 2023
argued that, in the upcoming observing runs of present-generation detectors, current search methods may not be enough to identify a lensed event before false alarms begin to dominate.

Presence of additional information, in the form of type-II lensed images
(
Dai & Venumadhav 2017
;
Ezquiaga et al. 2021
;
Vijaykumar et al. 2023
;
Janquart et al. 2021b
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
or electromagnetic counterparts
(
Smith et al. 2023
;
Smith et al. 2025
;
Birrer et al. 2025
;
Ryczanowski et al. 2025
;
Pastor-Marazuela 2025
)
, could improve the situation
(
Keitel 2025
)
. However, these effects are expected to be rare. One could also restrict the lensing search to a subspace of the signals’ parameter space where lensed events are predominant but false alarms are less likely; e.g., signal pairs which are loud or have short time delays
(
Wierda et al. 2021
;
Caliskan et al. 2023
;
Chakraborty & Mukherjee 2025
)
.
Hannuksela et al. 2025
have shown that time delay priors are particularly helpful in turning the growth of false alarms to
\propto{N}
. However, their results hold only in the limit of large observing duration, or, one risks losing a considerable fraction of truly lensed signals.

Better detector sensitivity will increase not only the number of GW detections, but also the precision in the measurement of signal parameters. In this paper, we show that the improved precision will indeed compensate for the growth in false alarms in upcoming observing runs. We forecast that the first 3
\sigma
-confident detection of strongly lensed GWs is likely to happen in the fifth observing run (O5) of the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) detectors
(
Aasi et al. 2015
;
Acernese et al. 2014
;
Somiya et al. 2012
;
Aso et al. 2013
;
Akutsu et al. 2021
)
. With the anticipated upgrades of current detectors and with the advent of XG detectors, lensing detection shall become routine. Much of the exciting science cases proposed in the literature may kick-start in the coming decade.

Part of the reason why these forecasts are challenging is because lensing searches are computationally costly. Robust frequentist detection claims require simulating “deep” backgrounds (low FPP), which also becomes quadratically more expensive as we target larger
{N}
. The forecasts reported in this paper are made possible by a computationally efficient Bayesian search method that we presented in
Barsode et al. 2025
, combined with a correspondence between the expected distributions of the Bayesian likelihood ratio under the
lensed
and
unlensed
hypotheses.

III

IV
. We defer technical details to the Appendices
A
,
B
, and
C

II
Overview of GW strong lensing search

Given data
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
containing two GW signals, we can compute the Bayesian odds between the

hypothesis
{\mathcal{H}_{L}}
(that the two signals are lensed copies of the same) and the

hypothesis
{\mathcal{H}_{U}}
(that the two signals are unrelated) as:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}^{L}_{U}}=\dfrac{P({\mathcal{H}_{L}}\mid d_{1},d_{2})}{P({\mathcal{H}_{U}}\mid d_{1},d_{2})}=\Pi^{L}_{U}~{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}},

(II.1)

where
\Pi^{L}_{U}
denotes the prior odds

{N}>100

u<0.01

{N}({N}-1)/2={N}^{2}/2
,
u/(1+u)=u

(
Hannuksela et al. 2025
)

\Pi^{L}_{U}=\dfrac{P({\mathcal{H}_{L}})}{P({\mathcal{H}_{U}})}=\dfrac{2u}{{N}},

(II.2)

while
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
is the Bayes factor that we compute from data. This is the likelihood ratio of getting a pair of signals
d_{1},d_{2}
under lensed vs. unlensed hypotheses

{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}=\dfrac{P(d_{1},d_{2}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}})}{P(d_{1},d_{2}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}})}.

(II.3)

All of the Bayesian search methods (e.g.,
Haris et al. 2018
;
Janquart et al. 2021a
;
Janquart et al. 2023a
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Lo & Magaña Hernandez 2023
;
Goyal et al. 2024

Barsode et al. 2025
), compute
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
or its approximation from the data (see,
Barsode et al. 2025
for a derivation). A large value of
{\mathcal{O}^{L}_{U}}
would favor the lensing hypothesis.

In practice, computing
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
from a large number of event pairs is computationally expensive. However,
Barsode et al. 2025
have demonstrated an efficient method to compute
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
using a set of realistic approximations (called
Posterior Overlap 2.0
, or, PO2.0). This amounts to computing the inner product of the posterior distributions of the source parameters estimated from the two events (i.e.,
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
), appropriately weighted by their prior distributions informed by the population properties of the sources and lenses.

The likelihood ratio
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
is also the optimal lensing identification statistic in the frequentist sense — something that maximizes the identification efficiency (
\eta
) for a given value of FPP (
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}
) from a pair of GW signals
(
Neyman & Pearson 1933
)
. Using the distribution
P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}})
obtained empirically from a simulation of unlensed (background) pairs, we can compute the FPP of any unlensed pair having
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
higher than the threshold
{\mathcal{B}_{t}}
:

{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}({\mathcal{B}_{t}})=\int\limits_{{\mathcal{B}_{t}}}^{\infty}d{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}~P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}}).

(II.4)

Similarly, the efficiency of lensing identification (the fraction of lensed events present in the data that are correctly identified by the search pipeline at a given threshold) can be computed from the distribution
P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}})
obtained from a simulation of lensed event pairs

\eta({\mathcal{B}_{t}})=\int\limits_{{\mathcal{B}_{t}}}^{\infty}d{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}~P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}}).

(II.5)

Since we typically search in a catalog of
{N}
signals, a more relevant FPP is that of finding
at least one
pair with
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
higher than the threshold in a catalog composed entirely of unlensed signals. Hence
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}
has to be multiplied by the number of “trials”, i.e., the number of pairs (
{N}^{2}/2
), resulting in the “catalog” FPP
(
Hannuksela et al. 2019
;
Caliskan et al. 2023
)

{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}({\mathcal{B}_{t}})=\dfrac{{N}^{2}}{2}~{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}({\mathcal{B}_{t}}).

(II.6)

The interpretation of
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}
as a probability is valid only in the limit of
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}{N}^{2}\ll 1
. However, it can always be interpreted as the expected number of false positives. In the frequentist picture, one may claim a detection of strong lensing if
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}
is sufficiently small, which is often expressed in terms of Gaussian standard deviations, such as
3\sigma~({\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}\simeq 3\times 10^{-3})
or
5\sigma~({\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}\simeq 6\times 10^{-7}
).

The expected number of lensed events that are detectable at a given threshold
{\mathcal{B}_{t}}
is

N_{\ell}({\mathcal{B}_{t}})=u\,{N}\,\eta({\mathcal{B}_{t}}),

(II.7)

where
u
is the lensing fraction. At the same threshold, the expected number of false alarms is
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}({\mathcal{B}_{t}})
. Thus, we can define a
catalog purity
as the fraction of truly lensed events in a catalog of lensing candidates.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pty}({\mathcal{B}_{t}})=\dfrac{u\,{N}\,\eta({\mathcal{B}_{t}})}{u\,{N}\,\eta({\mathcal{B}_{t}})+{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}({\mathcal{B}_{t}})}=\left({1+\frac{N}{2u}\frac{{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}({\mathcal{B}_{t}})}{\eta({\mathcal{B}_{t}})}}\right)^{-1}.

(II.8)

Impure catalogs with known purity are generally more useful than pristinely pure but less efficient ones (see, e.g.,
Jana et al. 2024
). Thus, instead of the usual frequentist significance bounds, catalog purity itself may be used as a criteria for setting detection thresholds.

In Equation (
II.8
),
{N}/{2u}\equiv 1/\Pi^{L}_{U}
will continue to increase in future observing runs as the number of GW detections will grow much faster than the lensing fraction
u
. An optimal analysis pipeline tries to minimise
{{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}({\mathcal{B}_{t}})}/{\eta({\mathcal{B}_{t}})}
. Due to the computational cost, it is difficult to get realistic estimates of
{{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}({\mathcal{B}_{t}})}/{\eta({\mathcal{B}_{t}})}
for most of the Bayesian lensing identification pipelines. However, thanks to the computational efficiency of the PO2.0 pipeline,
Barsode et al. 2025
has demonstrated a 70% efficient identification at a
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}\equiv 2.3\sigma
at current GW detector sensitivities (i.e. the projected sensitivity of the fourth observing run (O4) of LVK
(
LIGO-Virgo Collaboration 2020
)
). However, since the expected rate of lensed events is low (
uN\sim 0.2
) at current sensitivities, we must patiently wait until upgraded detectors come online in order to detect lensed events.

As GW detector networks improve, their higher sensitivity implies a higher S/N for a given source, allowing far-away sources to become detectable. This has both favorable and unfavorable effects on lensing searches:

Favorable effects

\uparrow

We detect more signals, leading to an increase in the number of lensed signals in the data
(u{N})
. There is also a small increase in the lensing fraction
u
due to the deeper observing horizon.

\uparrow

Thanks to the higher S/N and longer in-band duration of the signals, posterior distributions of source parameters shrink in size, reducing the probability of accidental consistency between unlensed pairs, which reduces
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}
.

\uparrow

With additional detectors joining the network, the sky localization improves independently of S/N, further reducing
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}
.

Unfavorable effects

\downarrow

The prior odds of lensing
(2u/{N})
decrease because the increase in
u
is negligible compared to the increase in
{N}
.

\downarrow

Time delay and magnification priors of lensed events broaden and become more similar to those of unlensed pairs, increasing
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}

(
More & More 2022
)
.

The decrease in prior odds is the dominant effect that degrades the lensing identification efficiency, while the shrinkage of posteriors due to higher S/N is the dominant effect that counters it. If our network is so sensitive that we can observe all the GW sources in the universe,
{N}
will not increase further. In this limit, lensing search efficiency will only increase with further improvements in sensitivity.

However, for the intermediate sensitivities in the near future, it is not trivial to gauge whether these conflicting effects will lead to an overall improvement or degradation in efficiency. Even in the era of XG detectors, where the above limit may be realized, it is unclear whether they themselves will be sensitive enough to identify lensing against formidable prior odds of
\sim 10^{-8}
(see Appendix
C
), or whether even higher sensitivity is required.

We could answer these questions by simulating unlensed and lensed GW signals in various observing scenarios, computing their
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
’s, and empirically obtaining the distributions
P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}})
and
P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}})
. These can then be used to estimate the pairwise FPP and efficiency using Equations (
II.4
) and (
II.5
) respectively. The challenge here is that as
{N}
increases, the number of unlensed simulations and
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
calculations required to probe low values of
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}
grows quadratically, making estimation of
P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}})
computationally prohibitive (see Figure
3
). In addition, the cost of estimating
P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}})
from simulations of lensed events, though independent of
{N}
, is still quite high.

In this study, we overcome these challenges using two key facts. First, under controlled conditions, there exists a relationship between
P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}})
and
P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}})
, and we use it to bypass running expensive unlensed simulations (see Appendix
A
for details). Second, we obtain
P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}})
expected in future observing runs by modeling it making use of the expected scaling of
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
with S/N (see Appendix
B
and
C
for details, including a description of observing scenarios assumed). Combined with the time delay prior ratio, this allows us to forecast strong lensing search efficiency at a manageable computational cost.

III.2
Computational efficiency

Confident identification of lensed events also poses a significant computational challenge — both in the actual search for lensed pairs and in the background simulations used to establish the significance of the lensed candidates.

The current approach for lensing search adopted by the LVK collaboration is to use a multi-stage strategy
(
Wright et al. 2025
)
, in which most of the less-significant event pairs are discarded using computationally inexpensive lensing search methods
(
Haris et al. 2018
;
Ezquiaga et al. 2023
)
. A small fraction (optimistically
\sim 1\%
) of the event pairs that report the highest significance are then followed up by computationally expensive joint parameter estimation pipelines that calculate the Bayes factor given in Equation (
II.3
). We optimistically assume that, for one pair of events, this analysis can be completed using
\sim 10
CPU hrs of computing.

Below, we now discuss some strategies for mitigating these costs:

1) Efficient Bayesian lensing identification methods:

The PO2.0 pipeline can compute the same Bayes factor, albeit with some approximations, in
\sim 10
CPU seconds, saving over three orders of magnitude in computational cost. Its efficiency was demonstrated in
Barsode et al. 2025
. In Appendix
A
of this work, we show that it is also very close to the optimal Bayesian performance.

2) Frequentist significance estimates from lensed foregrounds:

Optimal Bayesian pipelines, using model assumptions that are close to Nature’s ground truth, can use relatively-inexpensive simulations of
lensed
events to estimate the frequentist significance of an event pair (see Appendices
A
and
D
). However, analysis pipelines must first verify the correctness of their implementations using the
Bayes factor – Bayes factor (B-B)
plots (see Appendix
A
), which in turn require background simulations. Moreover, real data may deviate from our model assumptions. We therefore do not recommend relying on this approach unless the uncertainties in astrophysical models are substantially reduced and the detector noise is well understood.

3) Catalog purity instead of frequentist significance:

Traditional frequentist criteria require that lensed catalogs be almost perfectly pure, with chances of contamination as low as
\sim 10^{-3}
for
3\sigma
. The first lensing identification will probably demand such a high degree of confidence. However, as we detect more and more lensed signals, and focus shifts towards doing further science using them, such stringent criteria may be wasteful. For example,
Jana et al. 2024
shows that even a 50% pure catalog, containing equal number of lensed and unlensed pairs (but not knowing which pair is which), will enable excellent lensing cosmography.

Thus we could adopt catalog purity itself as a detection criterion. This leads to a considerable computational advantage: for
\mathcal{O}(1)
purity and efficiency, Equation (
II.8
) implies that one only needs to probe
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}
accurately down to
\sim u/N
. The required number of background simulations then scales
\propto N
, reducing the computational cost by orders of magnitude in future observing runs.

Equation (
II.8
) tells that estimation of catalog purity also requires simulations of lensed events, which rely on prior assumptions on the lens properties and population. However, unlike unlensed simulations whose costs grow
\propto{N}^{2}
, the costs for lensed simulations are independent of
{N}
, and are generally much lower. Thus, one can simply run multiple lensed simulations with different priors to assess their effects.

IV
Conclusion

When searching for strongly lensed GWs, the number of false positives increases quadratically with the number of GW detections, as compared to the number of true lensed events which grow linearly. While this “lensing or luck” problem would appear to be insurmountable, we show that the expected increase in measurement precision of source parameters would allow us to outrun the false alarms. Based on current astrophysical models and anticipated sensitivities, we forecast that the first
3\sigma
detection of lensing is likely to happen in O5, and it will be followed by a steady growth of high-purity catalogs. At XG sensivities, we can expect several hundreds of confident lensing detections per year, few tens exceeding
5\sigma
.

These forecasts assume
Madau & Dickinson 2014
redshift distribution of binary black hole mergers fitted to
Abbott et al. 2023b
inferred population, using the population of galaxies as lenses. Lensing detection remains feasible under pessimistic assumptions and the rate may be
2-3
times higher under optimistic ones. Improvements in sky localization due to better GW detector networks and measurement of subdominant modes of GW radiation may lead to even higher detection rates.

These forecasts are for pairwise searches using GW data alone. These may be further complemented by detections of individual type-II images or multi-messenger counterparts. There is also a possibility that some of the strongly lensed images may exhibit additional wave optics distortions due to stars and star clusters present in the lens galaxy
(
Mishra et al. 2024
;
Chan et al. 2025
)
. If detected, these distorted signals may in turn improve prospects of detecting strong lensing.

Computational cost of background simulations (which will grow quadratically with the number of GW events) is a major hurdle to making confident detections. The same hurdle lies in the way of making forecasts, though we circumvent it in this work by: 1) using the computationally efficient and near-optimal Bayesian lensing identification pipeline PO2.O
(
Barsode et al. 2025
)
, 2) using the expected scaling of the Bayes factors with S/N to predict the Bayes factor distribution of lensed events expected in future observing runs, and 3) using the correspondence between Bayes factor distribution of unlensed events and lensed events.

Even with efficient Bayesian or machine-learning methods
(
Goyal et al. 2021b
;
Magare et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Campailla et al. 2025
;
Offermans & Li 2024
)
, finding thresholds corresponding to conventional
3\sigma
or
5\sigma
detections will be prohibitive in XG era due to the need of expensive background simulations. We propose to use the purity of the lensed catalog for determining identification thresholds. Though prior dependent, such thresholds are considerably less computationally expensive to estimate, and may prove valuable for downstream science using lensed GWs. A reanalysis of open data from LIGO-Virgo for lensing identification is currently underway.

The techniques presented here can also be applied to forecast the efficiency of identifying lensed binary neutron star mergers as well. These would likely be higher than for binary black holes due to the signals being longer and their parameters better constrained. Together with lensed binary black holes, these promise an exciting future for astrophysics and cosmology based on strongly lensed GWs.

Appendix A
Bypassing unlensed simulations

Here we describe how to bypass the computationally expensive evaluation of the distribution Bayes factors under the unlensed hypothesis,
P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}|{\mathcal{H}_{U}})
. This is achieved by establishing a correspondence between the conditional distribution of the Bayes factor under competing hypotheses.

Given the competing hypotheses
{\mathcal{H}_{L}}
and
{\mathcal{H}_{U}}
(which include the signal, noise, and population assumptions), the Bayes factor
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
defined in Equation (
II.3
) is a real valued function of the two data vectors
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
. Thus, the expected distribution of
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
is given by

P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}})=\int{d}d_{1}~{d}d_{2}~P(d_{1},d_{2})~\delta\left[{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}-{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}(d_{1},d_{2})\right],

(A1)

where
\delta
denotes the Dirac delta function and
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}(d_{1},d_{2})
is given by Equation (
II.3
). Then, the conditional distribution of the Bayes factor under
{\mathcal{H}_{L}}
is

P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}})=\int{d}d_{1}\,{d}d_{2}~P(d_{1},d_{2}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}})\,\delta\left[{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}-{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}(d_{1},d_{2})\right],

(A2)

whereas the same under
{\mathcal{H}_{U}}
is

P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}})=\int{d}d_{1}\,{d}d_{2}~P(d_{1},d_{2}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}})\,\delta\left[{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}-{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}(d_{1},d_{2})\right].

(A3)

Using Equation (
II.3
), this can be rewritten as

P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}})=\int{d}d_{1}\,{d}d_{2}~\frac{P(d_{1},d_{2}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}})}{{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}}\,\delta\left[{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}-{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}(d_{1},d_{2})\right].

(A4)

Comparing Equations (
A2
) and (
A4
), it is easy to see that

P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}})=\dfrac{P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}})}{{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}}.

(A5)

This result is significant: it allows us to obtain the distribution of
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
under the unlensed hypothesis without performing costly background simulations. We instead run the relatively inexpensive lensed simulations, and construct
P({\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}})
by reweighting those
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
samples by
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
itself. This may seem odd at first: what is connecting the results of two seemingly independent simulations? The answer lies in the fact that the
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
function necessarily incorporates the population priors, as well as the signal and noise models of
both the hypotheses
. Conversely, this also means that Equation (
A5
) holds only when the data distribution matches the assumptions made in the calculation of
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
.

In the analysis of real GW signals, waveform systematics, noise artifacts, and population model uncertainties may result in the data
d_{1},d_{2}
being distributed differently from the assumptions that enter
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
calculation. Simulating an unlensed background then becomes necessary for a robust detection of strong lensing. However, for forecasts using simulated data as done in this paper, we can ensure that the model assumptions match between the hypotheses and the
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
calculation.

Bypassing lensed simulations in future observing runs

To begin, we note that, because arrival times – and therefore time delays – are measured with negligible error, the strong lensing Bayes factor
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
can be written as
(
Haris et al. 2018
;
Barsode et al. 2025
)

{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}={\mathcal{B}}\times{\mathcal{R}^{L}_{U}}

(B1)

where
{\mathcal{B}}
is the Bayes factor computed over BBH and lensing parameters excluding the arrival time and time delay, but conditioned on the observed time delay of the pair, and
{\mathcal{R}^{L}_{U}}
is the ratio of time delay priors under the lensed and unlensed hypotheses evaluated at the pair’s time delay
(
Haris et al. 2018
)

{\mathcal{R}^{L}_{U}}=\dfrac{P({\Delta t}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{L}})}{P({\Delta t}\mid{\mathcal{H}_{U}})}.

(B2)

The
{\mathcal{R}^{L}_{U}}
’s can be computed with relatively inexpensive simulations, since there is no need to perform parameter estimation here. However, parameter estimation is needed for evaluating
{\mathcal{B}}
’s for a large number of lensed GW injections, which is a computationally expensive task. Instead of running simulations in each future observing scenario, we will use an expected scaling of the Bayes factor with the S/N of the lensed images to extrapolate results from a single simulation set at current sensitivity. Assuming that the posteriors can be approximated by multivariate Gaussians, the expected scaling is
(
Gao et al. 2023
)

{\mathcal{B}}=b\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho_{0}}\right)^{D},~~~\mathrm{with}~\rho=\left({\dfrac{2\rho_{1}^{2}\rho_{2}^{2}}{\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}}}\right)^{1/2},

(B3)

where
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
are the S/N of the two individual events and
\rho_{0}
is some characteristic S/N (say, 8). We empirically estimate the scaling exponent
D
(which is expected to be close to the dimensionality of the posterior distributions that are used to compute
{\mathcal{B}}
) and the scaling constant
b
from simulations assuming O4 sensitivity. Note that noise will cause some scatter on
b
(see Figure
6
). Thus, we model
\log_{10}b
as belonging to a Gaussian distribution of mean
\mu
and standard deviation
\sigma
. We then estimate the maximum likelihood values of
\mu,\sigma
and
D
assuming a Gaussian likelihood
2
2

2

We find the best fit value of
D
to be 8.5, which is quite close to the effective dimensionality of the parameter space that is used to compute
{\mathcal{B}}
, i.e., masses (
m_{1},m_{2}
) and spin magnitudes (
a_{1},a_{2}
) of the black holes, sky location (
\alpha,\delta

\psi-\phi_{0}
), luminosity distance and inclination angle (highly correlated, and hence the effective dimensionality is between 1 and 2).
. This allows us to model the distribution of
{\mathcal{B}}
as a function of the S/N. In Figure
6

{\mathcal{B}}
and the orange shaded regions show the contours of the modeled distribution. This should hold, to a good approximation, in future observing runs also. To predict the
{\mathcal{B}}

The search for strongly lensed GWs is a classification problem between lensed and unlensed pairs. In the Bayesian approach, this is based on the largeness of the posterior odds
{\mathcal{O}^{L}_{U}}
, given by the product of prior odds and the Bayes factor
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
. On the other hand, when the frequentist statistic is chosen to be
{\mathcal{O}^{L}_{U}}
or
{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
3
3

3

Note that the frequentist
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{pair}}}
is a cumulative distribution function that is unchanged if the statistic is scaled by a constant, such as
{\mathcal{O}^{L}_{U}}\propto{\mathcal{B}^{L}_{U}}
, so long that the thresholds are also scaled appropriately.
, the classification criteria technically remain the same. Thus the lensed catalogs that would be generated by the two approaches would be identical for the same thresholds
{\mathcal{O}_{t}}=\Pi^{L}_{U}~{\mathcal{B}_{t}}
.

What may differ in the two approaches is the level of confidence assigned to each pair in the lensed catalog. This confidence is quantified by the probability that labelling a pair as “lensed” might have been a mistake. In Bayesian, it is the probability
1/(1+O)
that a pair with posterior odds
O
may be unlensed, while in frequentist, it is the probability
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}({\mathcal{O}_{t}}=O)
that an unlensed pair may have odds greater than
O
. Clearly, these two measures of confidence are not equal to each other. This is not surprising as such, while both of these provide a probability of making a mistake, the exact questions they are answering are completely different.

In Figure
8
, we show the mapping between catalog purity and frequentist
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}
(expressed in terms of Gaussian
\sigma
) against posterior odds
{\mathcal{O}^{L}_{U}}
for different observing scenarios. The threshold
{\mathcal{O}^{L}_{U}}
may vary by over 2 orders of magnitude at a given
\sigma
. Note that this is true even under controlled conditions where the correspondence from Equation (
A5
) holds. This is because
{\mathrm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cat}}}
and
\sigma
depend not only on the
{\mathcal{O}^{L}_{U}}
itself, but also on its probability density, which may differ between different scenarios. The variation between threshold
{\mathcal{O}^{L}_{U}}
is relatively lower at constant purity, and, if a background is unavailable, one may roughly take posterior odds of 1 as a threshold corresponding to a catalog purity of 90% with the caveat that
{\mathcal{O}^{L}_{U}}
may be biased due to various model uncertainties.
