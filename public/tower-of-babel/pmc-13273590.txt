Carbon balance zoning and carbon budget drivers for governance in the Chengdu Chongqing urban agglomeration
Authors: Tonghui Yu, Binqian Dai, Xuan Zhu, Yu Zhang, Ran Xu, Xi Chen, Jiqiang Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAgainst China’s “dual-carbon” backdrop, identifying carbon balance patterns and governance zones in rapidly urbanizing regions is central to territorial spatial optimization. Using the Chengdu-Chongqing Urban Agglomeration as a case, this study develops a three-dimensional framework based on net carbon emissions, economic contribution, and ecological support coefficients to link carbon accounting, driver diagnosis, and zoning governance. Results show that: (1) The region exhibits a “high-emission core-strong carbon-sink periphery” pattern, with emissions peaking at 1.42×108 t in 2010, rebounding to 1.28×108 t in 2020, and carbon sinks increasing by only 0.82%. (2) Low-carbon maintenance zones dominate, accounting for 45.15% of the area, whereas high-carbon optimization and carbon-intensity control zones cluster in the dual-core area and development corridor. (3) Population density, land-use intensity, and government intervention are key drivers, with interaction effects stronger than single-factor effects. These findings inform differentiated mitigation and territorial spatial planning in the region.Subject areas: Economic geology, Environmental science, Environmental policy

Introduction

Amid global warming and continuously rising anthropogenic carbon emissions (CEs), regional carbon imbalance has become increasingly prominent, especially in rapidly urbanizing regions characterized by intensive construction activities.1,2 Rapid urban expansion and land use transition weaken regional carbon sequestration capacity and ecosystem service functions by converting large areas of ecological land into construction land and cropland.3,4 At the same time, the expansion of transport infrastructure, the intensification of industrial activities, and rising energy consumption intensity continue to drive up CEs, thereby exacerbating the spatial mismatch and budget imbalance between regional CEs and sinks.5,6 Against this background, China has proposed the “dual-carbon” goals, while gradually embedding carbon-reduction requirements into high-quality development and territorial spatial planning practices.7 This indicates that achieving these goals depends not only on controlling aggregate CEs and advancing technological mitigation, but also on a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying regional carbon imbalance and the corresponding governance priorities across different regions, so as to promote a shift toward carbon reduction pathways that are more region-specific, differentiated, and operational.

In essence, regional carbon balance (CB) is by no means a simple quantitative comparison between CEs and carbon sinks (CSs). Rather, it constitutes a comprehensive governance issue that involves the coordinated optimization of economic development, ecological conservation, and spatial governance. Land serves not only as the spatial carrier of economic activities, population agglomeration, and infrastructure construction, but also as the material foundation for carbon sequestration, ecosystem service provision, and the maintenance of environmental carrying capacity.8 Therefore, land use patterns and their transitions directly shape regional CEs, CSs, and the spatial matching relationship between them.9 Existing studies further indicate that the core of land use optimization does not lie merely in increasing development intensity, but in coordinating the functional division among urbanized areas, agricultural production areas, and ecological function zones, thereby serving the goals of high-quality development.10 Similarly, studies on carbon-related ecosystem services show that mismatches between regional ecological supply and socioeconomic demand can significantly intensify environmental pressure. Accordingly, carbon governance must be incorporated into a broader framework of spatial governance and ecological protection.11,12 It follows that research on regional CB should move beyond the traditional carbon-accounting paradigm and instead turn to identifying the synergistic relationships among economic contribution, ecological support, and carbon budget, so as to better inform differentiated spatial governance.13

Focusing on the core issues outlined above, existing studies have made important progress in three main areas: measurement methods, driving mechanisms, and zoning-based governance. First, at the methodological level, a relatively integrated analytical framework has gradually taken shape, distinguishing the direct carbon budget associated with land use from the indirect CEs induced by construction land. By incorporating CE and CS coefficients for different land use types, together with energy-consumption-related emissions triggered by construction land, this framework provides a relatively systematic characterization of carbon processes within the coupled human-land system.14 With the development of remote sensing and big data technologies, the integration of nighttime light data, land use data, and statistical data has further improved the spatial precision of CEs and CSs estimation, thereby providing important support for identifying intra-regional heterogeneity.15,16,17 Second, with respect to driving mechanisms, the research focus has gradually shifted from “measurement accuracy” to “mechanism analysis,” with increasing attention being paid to the combined effects of multiple factors, including land use intensity, population density, industrial structure, investment scale, transport conditions, policy intervention, and technological progress, on CEs and CSs.18,19 Meanwhile, Geodetector and geographically weighted regression (GWR) have been widely used to examine the spatial heterogeneity and interaction effects of these driving factors, thereby enhancing the guiding role of driver identification for spatial governance.20,21,22 Third, at the level of zoning-based governance, a growing number of studies have combined CB analysis with spatial governance tools. Research on land use CB zoning shows that, by using indicators such as ecological support coefficients and spatial association structures, regions can be classified into categories such as CS conservation zones, low-carbon optimization zones, high-carbon regulation zones, and carbon intensity control zones, thereby providing effective support for differentiated low-carbon governance.23 Furthermore, studies on low-carbon governance zoning, major functional zone planning, the regulation of production-living-ecological space (PLEs), ecological conservation redlines, and territorial ecological restoration consistently emphasize that low-carbon transition cannot be separated from spatial functional zoning and ecological security patterns.24,25 Rather, it must rely on differentiated spatial governance to achieve coordinated development and conservation.26,27

Although existing studies have laid an important foundation, three major shortcomings remain in their capacity to support spatial governance practice under the “dual-carbon” goals. First, although the literature has separately examined CEs, carbon sequestration capacity, ecological carrying capacity, and economic benefits, most studies remain confined to single-dimensional analysis and have yet to establish an integrated evaluative framework capable of simultaneously identifying the synergistic relationships among net carbon budget, ecological support, and economic contribution. As a result, they have not fully addressed the core proposition that regional CB is not merely a matter of emission reduction, but also a question of coordinating economic development, ecological conservation, and carbon dynamics.28,29 Second, although research has extended to the county scale and increasingly incorporated multi-scalar effects, refined spatial identification remains insufficient in areas characterized by complex terrain, mixed land use, and grid-level units within urban agglomerations, making it difficult to accurately reveal the internal mismatch between CEs and CSs.30,31 Third, existing studies show insufficient integration across the “measurement-driver-governance” chain and tend to focus on only one segment at a time. Consequently, measurement methods, driving mechanisms, and spatial governance remain inadequately connected, and the logical alignment between research findings and governance objectives is still weak.32,33

The Chengdu-Chongqing urban agglomeration (CCUA) provides an ideal research setting for addressing the shortcomings outlined above. As one of the most densely populated and economically active regions in western China, the CCUA not only serves as a key strategic pivot in China’s regional development agenda and a major growth pole in the western region, but also faces pressing challenges arising from land expansion, the compression of ecological space, and the complex topography of hilly and mountainous areas.34 Accordingly, this study takes the CCUA as the study area and constructs an integrated analytical framework that combines CB identification, driving mechanism diagnosis, and differentiated spatial governance, while also taking into account the coordination among economic, ecological, and carbon dimensions (Figure 1). The marginal contributions of this study are 3-fold: First, from the perspective of coordinated governance, it develops a three-dimensional NetCE-ESC-ECC framework, thereby advancing CB research from single-dimensional carbon accounting toward an integrated assessment of economy-ecology-carbon interactions; Second, by focusing on grid-level units within an urban agglomeration characterized by complex terrain, it improves the spatial granularity of CB measurement; Third, it integrates measurement, driver analysis, and zoning-based governance into a unified analytical chain, thereby providing more targeted and operational decision support for territorial spatial planning under the “dual-carbon” goals.

Analytical framework for carbon balance zoning and driver analysis in the Chengdu-Chongqing urban agglomeration

The framework includes study area and classification criteria, carbon accounting and spatiotemporal analysis, carbon balance zoning, zoning evolution, and driving factor analysis with policy implications.

Study area

The study area of this paper is the CCUA, whose boundary is delineated according to the Development Plan for the CCUA (Figure 2). Therefore, the spatial scope adopted in this study corresponds to the official boundary of the CCUA rather than that of the Chengdu-Chongqing Economic Zone. Located in Southwest China, the CCUA spans the Sichuan Basin and its surrounding hilly and mountainous areas, with complex topography, high ecological sensitivity, and pronounced resource-environment constraints. Combined with its strong industrial base and rapid urbanization, these characteristics make it a representative case for examining carbon imbalance and differentiated low-carbon governance.35 The discontinuous areas shown in Figure 2 directly reflect the official boundary specified in the planning document.

Results

Carbon accounting results

Figure 3 illustrates the dynamic evolution of CEs, CSs, and NetCEs in the CCUA from 2005 to 2020. Overall, total CEs and NetCEs show clear stage-wise fluctuations, whereas total CSs increase only slightly, indicating a marked divergence between rapidly changing carbon sources and relatively stable CSs. Specifically, total CEs increase from 9.41×107 t in 2005 to 1.42×108 t in 2010, likely reflecting intensified urbanization, industrial expansion, and energy-intensive activities in the early development stage. Between 2010 and 2015, CEs declined to 1.23 × 108 t, suggesting the effects of industrial restructuring and strengthened energy-saving and emission-reduction policies. From 2015 to 2020, CEs rebounded slightly to 1.28 × 108 t, indicating renewed upward pressure from continued urban-economic growth, although at a slower pace than before 2010.

By contrast, total CSs remain comparatively stable, increasing only from 2.82 × 106 t in 2005 to 2.85×106 t in 2020, with a cumulative increase of merely 0.82%. As a result, the temporal variation of NetCEs closely follows that of CEs, exhibiting a clear “increase-decline-increase” pattern. Overall, Figure 3 suggests that short-to medium-term CB changes in the CCUA are driven mainly by emission-side dynamics, while sink growth contributes only marginally.

Spatiotemporal evolution characteristics of CEs and CSs at the grid scale

Building on a systematic analysis of temporal trends in CEs, CSs, and NetCEs in the CCUA, this study integrates standard deviational ellipse analysis with KDE to characterize differences in the spatial evolution of CEs and CSs (Figure 4). The major axis of the CEs ellipse consistently aligns along a northwest-southeast direction, whereas the major axis of the CSs ellipse runs southwest-northeast. This difference indicates that CEs are concentrated along the main urban-industrial corridor, while CSs are more closely associated with the surrounding ecological mountain-hill system. In addition, the centroids of both CEs and CSs are concentrated around Anyue County, but their migration trajectories differ. The CE centroid shifts from Lezhi County toward the Anyue area, whereas the CS centroid remains relatively stable, suggesting that the emission pattern is more dynamic than the sink pattern.

The kernel density results further reveal a clear core-periphery differentiation of carbon sources and sinks in the CCUA (Figure 5). For CEs, high-density areas are persistently concentrated in the central urban districts of Chengdu and Chongqing, forming a pronounced dual-core structure. CE density then decreases from the two urban cores toward the surrounding and peripheral areas, indicating a clear concentric attenuation pattern. In contrast, the spatial distribution of CSs is much more stable and mainly follows the ecological mountain-hill system around the urban agglomeration. High-density CS areas are consistently located in the southwestern margin of the Sichuan Basin, the southwestern hilly region, and the Daba and Wuling mountainous areas. Together, these patterns indicate that the CCUA exhibits a distinct “high-emission core—strong-sink periphery” structure, with CEs and CSs showing clear spatial separation.

Spatiotemporal dynamics of net carbon emissions at the grid scale

Building on a systematic analysis of the spatial evolution of kernel densities of CEs and CS CSs in the CCUA, this study further maps NetCEs (Figure 6). This mapping clarifies the spatiotemporal patterns and dynamic evolution of regional NetCEs, and makes the overall trends and stage-specific features of spatial differentiation more apparent. Overall, the NetCEs pattern in the CCUA exhibits pronounced spatial heterogeneity, forming a dual-core structure centered on Chengdu and Chongqing, with NetCEs gradually diffusing toward peripheral areas and generating marked inter-regional disparities. High-NetCE zones show a typical point-clustered clustering structure, mainly concentrated along the core economic corridor linking the two cities and their adjoining belts, reflecting the emission pressure associated with highly concentrated industries and intensive economic activity.

Spatial distribution of net carbon emissions from 2005 to 2020

(A–D) Spatial distribution of net carbon emissions (NetCE) in 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020. Warmer colors indicate higher NetCE values.

From a stage-specific perspective, total NetCEs in the CCUA increase rapidly from 9.12 × 107 t in 2005 to 1.39 × 108 t in 2010. High-value areas are primarily concentrated in the core urban districts and in peri-urban zones experiencing rapid urban expansion; the NetCEs footprint expanded continuously, and high-value patches gradually coalesce into contiguous belts, indicating a clear outward expansion trend. During 2010–2015, regional NetCEs declined slightly, and the outward spread of high-emission zones weakened, yet the overall spatial configuration remains largely unchanged, with the core areas remaining the main concentration belt of NetCEs. In 2015–2020, the NetCEs increased modestly relative to the previous stage, rising by 5.01×106 t. Although this growth rate was much lower than during 2005–2010, the spatial distribution continued to follow the “dual-core diffusion from Chengdu and Chongqing” pattern, high-value zones remain generally stable, and the anticipated significant reduction in NetCEs is not achieved.

Local spatial clustering patterns

Building on a spatiotemporal analysis of NetCEs in the CCUA, this study applies Local Moran’s I with visualization techniques to classify NetCEs across 7086 grid cells into four local spatial association types. This approach identifies the clustering patterns and spatial correlation characteristics of different spatial units.

Visualization of the results based on local Moran’s I (Figure 7) shows that “high-high” and “low-low” associations dominate, forming cohesive agglomeration belts in the dual-core metropolitan areas of Chengdu and Chongqing and their adjoining zones. Typical high-NetCE cores include the junction area of Jinjiang, Qingyang, and Wuhou districts in Chengdu, as well as sections of the riverfront economic corridor spanning Yuzhong, Jiangbei, and Nan’an districts in Chongqing. In addition, scattered “high-high” clusters appear along the Chengdu-Chongqing Economic Corridor (particularly in Ziyang and Neijiang), reflecting the gradual spillover of economic and industrial activities toward peripheral areas under the “dual-city economic circle” strategy and the associated diffusion of CEs. However, large-scale, contiguous high-NetCE belts have yet to form. In contrast, “low-low” clusters are concentrated in the western ecological barrier, the southern agricultural zone, and the northeastern fringe regions.

Spatiotemporal dynamics of ESC and ECC

Building upon a systematic analysis of CEs, CSs, and NetCEs, this study introduces three metrics—ECC, ESC, and NetCEs—to further characterize the dynamic state of regional CB and support spatial zoning and governance design. ECC measures the economic output generated per unit of CEs; values greater than 1 indicate relatively high carbon-use efficiency, whereas values below 1 reflect insufficient economic returns relative to emissions. ESC reflects the ecological carrying capacity for regional carbon sequestration; values greater than 1 imply strong CSs capacity and robust ecosystem resilience, while values below 1 indicate pronounced ecological stress. NetCEs represent the difference between CEs and CSs, with positive values indicating net carbon sources and negative values denoting sequestration. After defining these three indicators and establishing their threshold criteria, the analysis examines their spatiotemporal evolution (Figure 8).

(I–L) Spatial distribution of NetCE in 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020.

During the study period, regions with ECC <1 were mainly located within the core zone of the Chengdu-Chongqing area, with scattered occurrences in the central zones of some counties and districts. This pattern indicates that, although these areas make substantial contributions to economic output, they also exhibit high CE intensity. In quantitative terms, grid cells with ECC >1 account for 80.96% in 2005 and 81.06% in 2010, implying that the overall structure remains broadly stable during this phase. After 2010, the number of ECC >1 cells declines markedly, falling by roughly 200 cells, which corresponds to about 2.82% of all grids. Although a slight rebound occurs by 2020, the recovery remains limited. This indicates that, as economic expansion and urbanization accelerate, the growth rate of CEs outpaces improvements in economic efficiency. Consequently, overall regional CE economic efficiency displays a downward trend and does not achieve significant improvement.

Secondly, in terms of ecological carrying capacity, grid cells with ESC values greater than 1 are largely located in peripheral zones of the CCUA. These regions are characterized by high vegetation cover and complex topography, resulting in relatively strong ecological carrying capacity for carbon sequestration. By contrast, grid cells with ESC <1 are mainly located in the urban cores, particularly within central districts of the two cities and along the corridor connecting them. These areas are densely populated and characterized by intensive industrial agglomeration; ecological space has been extensively encroached upon, and carbon sequestration functions are severely constrained. Quantitatively, ESC >1 cells increased from 61.11% in 2005 to 63.10% in 2010, indicating an improvement in ecological carrying capacity. However, from 2010 to 2020, this indicator remained largely stable, without marked increases or declines, suggesting that improvements in ecological carrying capacity are limited.

Finally, the spatial analysis of NetCEs indicates that 999 grid cells have NetCE <0, accounting for about 15% of all cells. These cells are mainly located in the southwestern, northwestern, northeastern, and eastern peripheral zones of the CCUA, where ecological conditions are favorable, forest and grassland resources are abundant, mountain ecosystems remain relatively intact, and human disturbance is limited—factors indicating substantial carbon absorption potential. In contrast, grid cells with NetCE >0 are widely distributed across the core and inner-ring areas of the urban agglomeration, where population density and industrial concentration are high. The number of NetCE >0 cells increases from 85.19% to 85.90%. Although this increase is modest, it still indicates a sustained spatial expansion of high-emission areas. These findings indicate that, as the CCUA’s modernization process accelerates, the spatial extent and dominance of high-NetCE zones continue to grow, thereby intensifying pressure on the regional ecosystem carrying capacity.

Carbon balance zoning

Spatiotemporal dynamics of carbon balance zones

Building upon the systematic characterization of the spatiotemporal evolution of three indicators—NetCEs, ESC, and ECC—this study integrates these metrics into a three-dimensional discriminant system to support a classification system for regional CB zoning. Based on this framework, the CCUA is divided into six CB functional zones. Detailed criteria for the delineation of these zones are listed in Table 1. After functional zoning, KED is used to examine the spatial distribution and clustering patterns of each functional zone, thereby identifying their evolutionary trajectories and aggregation dynamics (Figure 9). The results indicate marked spatial heterogeneity and stage-specific variation among the identified zone types. Notably, no grid cells in the CCUA met the criteria for type II (ECC <1, ESC <1, and NetCE <0). The absence of this type is not accidental, but rather reflects the region’s core-periphery structure and structural carbon imbalance. Grid cells with NetCE <0 are mainly located in peripheral ecological areas, where ecological support capacity is relatively strong. In contrast, areas with ECC <1 and ESC <1 are mainly concentrated in the core cities and development corridors, where intensive development and ecological pressure usually lead to positive NetCE. This indicates that the CCUA has not formed a transitional low-carbon economic space characterized by weak ecological support but maintained net carbon sequestration capacity; instead, it exhibits a clear spatial differentiation of peripheral CSs and core CEs.

Specifically, the CS functional zones (type I) are primarily located in ecological areas on the periphery of the CCUA. Its spatial density decreases gradually, and its extent consistently contracts, indicating that these areas are under sustained pressure from urban expansion. The low-carbon maintenance zones (type III) exhibit a dispersed pattern, primarily located around Chengdu and Chongqing. Its spatial coverage expands marginally between 2005 and 2010 and then stabilizes, without forming significant clusters. The economic development zones (type IV) show a significant increase in both distribution density and spatial extent during the 2010–2015 period. Some areas previously classified as the low-carbon maintenance zones (type III) are reclassified into this category during this period, indicating a reduction in economic output efficiency per unit of CEs at these locations. The carbon intensity control zones (type V) exhibit a relatively stable spatial distribution along the core development axis of the CCUA. Although localized contractions occur in certain areas, the overall spatial pattern remains stable. By contrast, the high-carbon optimization zones (type VI) continuously expand throughout the study period, and by 2020, their spatial extent is significantly greater than in 2005. Newly added areas primarily extend into suburban fringes rather than remaining confined to central urban districts, indicating an outward spillover and intensification of CE pressure from core urban areas to peripheral regions.

Specifically, the CS functional zones (type I) account for approximately 14.10% of the total area and are mainly distributed in peripheral ecological barrier regions, such as the mountainous areas of northwestern Sichuan and the Daba Mountains in northeastern Chongqing. These areas feature high forest and grassland coverage and relatively intact ecosystems, indicating that they should be designated as priority ecological protection redline zones in future governance. The low-carbon maintenance zones (type III) constitute the largest share, at 45.15%, forming a roughly ring-shaped pattern in the transition belt of the urban agglomeration. Located adjacent to core urban centers but experiencing relatively low intensity of human activity, these zones are dominated by cropland and grassland, serving as critical buffers for regional ecological security and sustainable urban development. The economic development zones (type IV) account for only 3.92% of the CCUA and are scattered across its western, southwestern, and northeastern fringes. Positioned at the interface between ecological zones and development zones, these areas generally exhibit low industrialization levels and insufficient economic output per unit of CEs. The carbon intensity control zones (type V) cover about 19.63% of the CCUA and cluster along the development axis linking Chengdu and Chongqing. Characterized by intensive industrial activity and a fossil fuel-dominated energy structure, these zones form typical high-carbon-intensity belts and serve as key nodes for regional emissions management. The high-carbon optimization zones (type VI) occupy roughly 17.20% of the CCUA and form a ring-shaped pattern around the major urban centers. These zones feature high land-development intensity, dense populations, and significantly insufficient ecological carrying capacity, representing typical “high-emission—low-efficiency” areas that urgently require interventions to address their severe carbon imbalances.

Analysis of driving factors

Selection of driving factors

In the context of China’s “dual-carbon” goals and regional sustainable development, the selection of driving factors in this study is informed by the literature, the formation mechanisms of regional CEs and CSs, and the developmental context of the CCUA. Regional carbon-source and carbon-sink dynamics are jointly shaped by economic growth, population concentration, urban expansion, industrial transformation, government regulation, capital investment, and land development. Accordingly, eight socioeconomic indicators are selected: per capita GDP (X1), population density (X2), urbanization rate (X3), per capita science and education level (X4), industrial structure (X5), government intervention (X6), per capita financial development level (X7), and land use intensity (X8). These variables are well-suited to the CCUA, which is characterized by rapid urbanization, factor concentration, marked internal disparities, and the coexistence of development pressure and ecological conservation responsibilities.35 Among them, economic growth, population concentration, and urban expansion directly affect energy consumption and construction activities, whereas industrial structure, government intervention, science and education level, and financial development shape the low-carbon transition process. Land use intensity further reflects the effects of human activities on spatial patterns and CS carriers. Therefore, these variables capture the main socioeconomic channels underlying regional CEs and CS changes. On this basis, Geodetector and GWR are used to examine their explanatory power, interaction effects, and spatial heterogeneity.

Geodetector analysis

Geodetector results indicate pronounced differences in the explanatory power of individual drivers of CEs in the CCUA (Figure 11). Specifically, land use intensity (X8, q = 0.983) and population density (X2, q = 0.888) emerge as the dominant drivers, followed by science and education level (X4, q = 0.735) and financial development level (X7, q = 0.549). This pattern indicates that intensive land use, population concentration, advanced educational and research capacities, and financial development are pivotal in determining the spatial distribution of CEs. Figure 11 further shows that the pairwise interaction q-values are generally higher than those of the corresponding single factors. In particular, interactions involving land use intensity and population density exhibit especially strong explanatory power, such as X8∩X6 (q = 0.988), X8∩X5 (q = 0.985), X8∩X1 (q = 0.985), and X2∩X3 (q = 0.920), indicating a pronounced enhancement effect of multi-factor synergies on the spatial distribution of CEs.

In contrast, the driving mechanisms of CS capacity are weaker overall, but population density (X2, q = 0.570) remains the most influential factor, followed by land use intensity (X8, q = 0.358), government intervention (X6, q = 0.224), and financial development level (X7, q = 0.119). These results suggest that the spatial differentiation of CSs is more strongly constrained by demographic pressure, land use configuration, and policy-related interventions than by any single ecological or economic variable alone. Notably, when population density and land use intensity interact with other factors, their explanatory power increases significantly. For example, X2∩X1 (q = 0.699), X2∩X4 (q = 0.643), X2∩X3 (q = 0.638), and X2∩X6 (q = 0.613) all exceed the explanatory power of the corresponding single factors. These findings reinforce the notion that optimizing population distribution and strengthening land use planning are key pathways for enhancing regional CS capacity, while the spatial differentiation of CSs is shaped more by combined factor effects than by isolated drivers.

Geographically weighted regression analysis

While the Geodetector method effectively identifies and ranks the individual contributions of different drivers to CEs and CSs, as well as their interaction effects, it cannot adequately capture the spatially varying influences (promotive or suppressive) of these drivers, thereby overlooking their spatial heterogeneity within the region. To address this limitation, this study incorporates the key drivers that Geodetector ranks in the top 50% in terms of their contributions to CEs and CSs into the GWR model. Comparisons among OLS, GWR, and MGWR further confirm the necessity of accounting for spatial heterogeneity, and GWR is retained as the main analytical model due to its more direct interpretability at the county level. This approach shifts the analytical focus from measuring overall contributions to characterizing local spatial variations,36 thereby providing more spatially targeted decision support for differentiated carbon governance and policy formulation (Figure 12).

According to the 2020 GWR estimation results (Figure 12), the effects of population density (X2), science and education level (X4), financial development level (X7), and land use intensity (X8) on CEs in the CCUA exhibit pronounced regional variation. Specifically, the positive impact of population density on CEs gradually strengthens from west to east, whereas that of land use intensity increases from east to west. This pattern reflects clear spatial differences and structural characteristics in how regional population agglomeration and land development modes shape CEs. Overall, financial development level exhibits a mitigating effect on CEs, and this effect weakens progressively from the southwest toward the northeast, revealing spatial disparities in the efficiency of financial resource allocation for carbon reduction. Moreover, the impact of science and education level exhibits significant spatial heterogeneity. In western and northwestern areas, it demonstrates a strong inhibiting effect, potentially associated with relatively abundant science and education resources and higher public awareness of low-carbon practices. Conversely, in central and northeastern areas, this factor tends to promote CEs, a pattern likely linked to the spatial overlap between science and education resource allocation and high-carbon industrial clusters.

For CSs, the GWR results likewise reveal marked spatial differentiation. In the western, northwestern, and northeastern peripheral zones, population density shows a modest positive impact on CSs, whereas in the core area of the CCUA, it exerts a clear inhibiting effect, indicating the dual role of population agglomeration in regional carbon sequestration. Government intervention generally enhances CSs, but in some northern areas it exhibits a negative effect, potentially due to local policy orientations and industrial layout preferences. Financial development level and land use intensity both display varying inhibitory effects on CS capacity. The inhibiting effect of financial development intensifies from the southwest toward peripheral areas, while that of land use intensity strengthens from the southern part of the agglomeration toward the central area and then spreads outward. These findings indicate substantial spatial heterogeneity in how financial resource allocation and land development intensity influence regional CS capacity.

Discussion

Comparison with existing studies

Fine-grained accounting of carbon sources and sinks provides an essential basis for accurately identifying regional CB conditions and supporting the effective spatial implementation of the “dual-carbon” goals. The evolution of PLES is closely related to carbon mitigation potential and the directions of spatial optimization. However, the existing literature has focused primarily on spatial evolution, pattern optimization, or the assessment of emission-reduction potential, and has not directly incorporated PLES classification into the carbon accounting process.27,37 To address this gap, this study embeds the PLES perspective into the carbon accounting process. By distinguishing production, living, and ecological functions, it identifies the structural features of carbon budgets across different spatial units, thereby enhancing the spatial-functional interpretability and governance relevance of the accounting results. Meanwhile, this study introduces nighttime light and construction land data at the grid level to spatially calibrate the indirect CEs associated with construction land, thereby strengthening the analysis’s responsiveness to spatial differences in population activity density, development intensity, and energy consumption,38 while also enabling a clearer delineation of the internal boundaries between CE hotspots and CS-dominant areas. For the CCUA, which are characterized by multiple centers and complex terrain, conventional carbon accounting at the municipal or county level often fails to reveal internal mismatches between CEs and CSs. By contrast, the analytical framework developed in this study is better able to identify the risks of carbon imbalance across different functional spaces and their spatial differentiation, and, to some extent, extends the grid-based research perspective on carbon budget identification in urban agglomerations.39,40,41 Accordingly, the contribution of this study lies not only in refining the analytical scale to the grid level, but also in integrating the PLES functional framework, high-resolution spatial calibration, and CB accounting into a unified analytical pathway, thereby providing a more spatially adaptive quantitative basis for CB zoning and differentiated governance in complex hilly and mountainous urban agglomerations.

This study further finds that CEs and CSs in the CCUA exhibit a pronounced “high emission core-strong sink periphery” spatial pattern. Specifically, high-emission areas are concentrated in the dual-core region and the corridor connecting the two cities, whereas CS-dominant areas are mainly distributed across the mountainous ecological spaces along the western, northern, and eastern periphery. This finding is broadly consistent with the “high emissions in the center and high CSs in the periphery” pattern reported by Zhou et al. and Ye et al.42,43 and it also accords with Qian et al.,39 who document strengthening spatial associations and increasing polarization in CEs across urban agglomerations. This suggests that, under continued urbanization and the ongoing concentration of factors of production in core areas, a core-periphery structure,44 in which CEs concentrate in central areas while CSs cluster in peripheral ecological spaces, appears to have a certain degree of generality. Meanwhile, after a phase of temporary decline, the CCUA still exhibits some rebound in CEs and persistent carbon-imbalance pressures, and these pressures are more pronounced than those observed in downstream urban agglomerations of the Yangtze Economic Belt, such as the Yangtze River Delta.41

Further analysis indicates that this pattern is mainly driven by three factors. First, the inland relocation of energy-intensive industries and manufacturing continues to reinforce emissions lock-in in the core and corridor areas, thereby sharpening the tension between industrial transfer and emissions-reduction constraints. This is broadly consistent with the basic logic of the pollution haven hypothesis.44 Second, although the peripheral mountainous areas perform the principal carbon-sink function, the expansion of construction in the core areas and the extension of transport corridors continue to compress ecological buffer space, thereby weakening the capacity of CSs to offset expanding emissions. Third, compared with the relatively mature intercity collaborative governance networks in downstream regions, the CCUA still shows clear shortcomings in coordinated regional emissions reduction, in harnessing spillover effects from central cities, and in the coordination of governance rules.45,46 Therefore, the spatiotemporal pattern of CEs and CSs in the CCUA is not shaped by natural geographic conditions alone, but instead represents a composite spatial outcome jointly shaped by its polycentric spatial structure, mountainous terrain constraints, industrial relocation, insufficient ecological buffering, and the capacity for regional collaborative governance.

Recommendations for different CB zones based on driving factor analysis

Under the guidance of the “dual-carbon” goals, policy design for the CCUA should move beyond uniform and homogeneous governance approaches. Instead, it should fully take into account the constraints of cross-regional collaborative governance, mountainous topographic conditions, the core-periphery carbon pattern, and the spatial heterogeneity in the drivers of CEs and CSs. This issue is particularly critical for the CCUA, where industrial agglomeration along the dual-core corridor, the continued compression of ecological buffer space in peripheral areas, and the relative weakness of intercity collaborative governance jointly intensify regional carbon imbalance pressures. Accordingly, policy recommendations for different CB functional zones should not remain at the level of broad normative statements, but should instead be translated into more targeted governance priorities and operational policy instruments. Based on the results of the Geodetector and GWR analyses, this study proposes the following zonal policy recommendations.

First, the CS functional zones (type I) are located mainly in the peripheral ecological areas in the western and northwestern parts of the CCUA. The GWR results show that a moderate degree of population agglomeration helps enhance the CS capacity of these areas, whereas rising land-use intensity exerts a clear inhibitory effect on their carbon-sink function. Therefore, governance in these zones should prioritize the protection of ecological red lines, implement a “zero net loss” policy for forestland and grassland, strictly constrain the expansion of construction land, and focus on the development of forestry-based carbon sequestration projects and low-disturbance ecological industries.

Second, the low-carbon maintenance zones (type III) account for the largest share of the study area and are distributed mainly in scattered patches along the outer margins of the core cities and near economic corridors. As transitional belts between urban expansion and ecological space, these areas are particularly susceptible to the spillover effects of population agglomeration and land development, although government intervention still plays a positive role in maintaining their ecological stability. Accordingly, policy in these zones should focus on delineating urban growth boundaries, strictly controlling the conversion of ecological land and cultivated land, and supporting the development of low-carbon agriculture and ecological service industries.

Third, the economic development zones (type IV) account for a relatively small share of the study area and are scattered mainly across the southwestern part of the CCUA and other peripheral areas. The GWR results indicate that financial expansion and land development may, to some extent, weaken the carbon-sink capacity of these zones, whereas stronger government regulation helps preserve the stability of their ecological functions. Accordingly, governance in these zones should tighten entry screening for green projects, impose stricter thresholds for carbon intensity, energy consumption, and land-use efficiency, and channel a larger share of fiscal and financial resources toward green manufacturing, environmentally sound processing industries, and low-carbon agriculture.

Fourth, the carbon intensity control zones (type V) are concentrated mainly in the industrially dense core cities and along the Chengdu-Chongqing development corridor. In these areas, population density and land-use intensity are the principal drivers of rising CEs, whereas the emission-mitigating effect of financial development remains relatively limited and exhibits clear spatial variation. Therefore, governance in these zones should adopt more binding carbon-intensity reduction constraints, strictly limit the expansion of industrial land, accelerate the adoption of cleaner production and energy substitution, and strengthen coordinated oversight across adjacent areas over industrial siting and logistics nodes.47

Finally, the high-carbon optimization zones (type VI) are distributed mainly around the core cities, adjacent to the carbon-intensity control zones, with some scattered patches along the dual-core economic corridor. These zones are where the combined effects of high-density population agglomeration, intensive land development, and weakened carbon-sink capacity are most pronounced. Accordingly, policy in these zones should prioritize urban renewal over outward expansion, promote the orderly phase-out of inefficient industrial land uses, strictly restrict new energy-intensive projects, and rebuild the ecological buffering capacity around the core areas through the restoration of ecological corridors and green wedges.48

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the integrated framework developed in this study, which combines multi-source spatial data, refined grid-scale carbon accounting, the NetCE-ECC-ESC discriminant system, carbon-balance functional zoning, and Geodetector-GWR driver diagnosis, can effectively capture the spatial heterogeneity and complex interactions underlying regional carbon dynamics in the CCUA. Based on the above analysis, the main findings can be summarized as follows.

(1)CEs exhibit a pronounced core-periphery contrast, with elevated values concentrated in the Chengdu-Chongqing dual-core area and much lower levels across the outer regions, whereas CSs are mainly concentrated in peripheral areas with relatively favorable ecological conditions. Temporally, NetCEs follow a phased “increase–decline–rebound” trajectory; spatially, they cluster around the Chengdu-Chongqing dual-core area, indicating a significant regional CB imbalance.

Limitations of the study

This study systematically develops a framework integrating carbon accounting, CB spatial zoning, and driver analysis for the CCUA; however, several limitations remain. First, regarding the accuracy of CE and CS accounting, although this study adopts a multi-source approach using multiple datasets, including land classification data, nighttime light data, and regional energy consumption data, the available datasets remain insufficient to fully refine the depiction of the carbon accounting process. For example, due to the difficulty of obtaining certain critical climatic parameters, these variables are not fully incorporated into the CE accounting system, which to some extent compromises the comprehensiveness and accuracy of the estimates. Second, the analysis in this paper focuses primarily on the current assessment and spatial zoning of CB, without incorporating scenario-based projections or dynamic simulations of future CB evolution, limiting the ability to fully capture long-term trends and uncertainties critical to policy design. To address these limitations, future research could proceed in two directions: (1) To enhance the precision of carbon accounting, future work could further integrate remote-sensing inversion techniques with big-data monitoring approaches, appropriately introduce regional climate models, and systematically incorporate key meteorological factors. This integration would improve the accuracy and responsiveness of carbon accounting and enable a more precise characterization of the spatiotemporal evolution of CEs and their spatially heterogeneous distribution. (2) For scenario-based projections of CB spatial zoning, models such as PLUS and FLUS could be employed to simulate the dynamic evolution of CB under various policy orientations, socioeconomic development pathways, and climate change scenarios, thereby providing comprehensive guidance for regional carbon reduction pathways and the spatial planning of the national territory.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Carbon accounting and spatial pattern analyses were conducted within a spatially explicit, grid-based framework. The primary unit of analysis was the grid cell (n = 7086), and each cell covered 2.50 × 107 m2. Carbon emissions, carbon sinks, and net carbon emissions were quantified for 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020 using coefficient-based methods integrating land-use, nighttime light, energy consumption, population, and socioeconomic data. Spatial analyses included standard deviational ellipse analysis, kernel density estimation, and Local Moran’s I. Carbon balance zoning was based on the combined thresholds of net carbon emissions, the economic contribution coefficient, and the ecological support coefficient. After standardization of the explanatory variables, driving factors were analyzed using Geodetector and geographically weighted regression. Grid-based spatial analyses and geographically weighted regression were conducted in ArcGIS 10.8, whereas Geodetector analysis was performed in R 4.5.2 using RStudio.
