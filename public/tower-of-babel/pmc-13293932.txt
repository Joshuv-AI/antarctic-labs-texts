Fungal metabolite-based immunotherapy overcomes tumor-associated macrophage immunosuppression
Authors: Jing Hu, Xiaoman Ju, Biping Deng, Kaicheng Tang, Dongchen Yuan, Xiaofan Sun, Lingmei Kong, Yixin Xu, Zhuo Fu, Stephane Koda, Simin Shao, Yan Chen, Guowei Sun, Simin Zheng, Hongyu Zhao, Yaqi Liu, Hao Chen, Zilong Zong, Youzhen Ma, Huiping Yu, Yao Qin, Xiaoqing Li, Hui Hua, Yang Zhao, Renxian Tang, Kuiyang Zheng, Chunrong Tong, Michele Woei Ling Teng, Xinrui Dong, Xiaoran Deng, Yan Li, Yan-long Yang, Yongqiang Chen, Juming Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are key drivers of immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment, yet effective strategies to reprogram them remain limited. Here, we develop an exogenous precursor-assisted fungal metabolite (ePAFM) strategy, which generates bioactive fungal metabolites, and identify IM502, a meroterpenoid-like compound, as a potent TAM modulator. Mechanistically, IM502 primarily inhibits PI3Kγ and redirects STAT signaling from STAT3/6 to STAT1/2 dominance, thereby reversing TAM-mediated immunosuppression, substantially enhancing the abundance and functional quality of natural killer (NK) and T cells. Notably, IM502 reprograms human colorectal cancer TAMs, restoring CD8+ T cell proliferation. It suppresses established tumors and metastases across multiple cancer types, demonstrating superior efficacy to other macrophage-targeted agents, effects comparable to clinical drugs, and an ability to overcome PD-1 blockade resistance. This work establishes the ePAFM platform for metabolite generation and highlights the promise of IM502 for cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a complex ecosystem that dictates tumor progression and therapeutic response.1,2,3,4 Within the TME, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) emerge as the most abundant immunosuppressive myeloid population, and their high infiltration is strongly correlated with poor prognosis in most solid cancers.5,6 In tumors, TAMs are educated to become key architects of an immunosuppressive niche.7,8,9,10 They deploy multiple mechanisms to suppress anti-tumor immunity, including the secretion of inhibitory cytokines (e.g., IL-10 and TGF-β), expression of immune checkpoint ligands like PD-L1, and depletion of essential amino acids such as arginine via arginase 1 (Arg1), effectively paralyzing cytotoxic CD8+ T cells.11

Given their pro-tumorigenic role, TAMs have become a compelling therapeutic target.6,7 However, clinical translation of TAM-centric strategies has faced significant hurdles.7 Approaches aimed at depleting TAMs or blocking their recruitment (e.g., CSF-1R or CCR2 inhibitors) have shown limited efficacy as monotherapies.7 These stumbling blocks include the remarkable plasticity and diversity of mononuclear phagocytes in tumors, coupled with compensatory mechanisms such as the accumulation of other immunosuppressive myeloid cells like neutrophils, highlighting the redundancy within the chemokine system.

These limitations have spurred interest in alternative strategies that seek to reprogram, rather than eliminate, TAMs.2,7 A promising approach is to target intracellular signaling pathways that control TAM polarization and function. Central to this reprogramming are key signaling nodes like the PI3Kγ/Akt axis and the STAT family of transcription factors, which collectively orchestrate TAM phenotype and, critically, their metabolic state.12,13,14,15 Macrophages polarized toward an immunosuppressive (M2-like) state rely heavily on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), while immunostimulatory (M1-like) macrophages engage in glycolysis.16 This metabolic reprogramming is not merely a consequence but a fundamental driver of their function; OXPHOS supports an anti-inflammatory, reparative phenotype, whereas glycolysis provides the rapid bioenergetic and biosynthetic capacity needed for pro-inflammatory activation.11,17 The activation of STAT1/2 and STAT3/6 signaling pathways orchestrates the phenotype, metabolism, and function of M1-like and M2-like macrophages, which, respectively, promote and suppress anti-tumor immune responses.12 PI3Kγ, a class I phosphoinositide 3-kinase predominantly expressed in macrophages and other myeloid cells, drives tumor immune evasion.18,19 Its role, however, is not merely facilitatory but rather central to the immunosuppressive identity of TAMs.19 PI3Kγ acts as a key signaling hub, integrating inputs from various tumor-derived factors (such as CSF1, CCL2, and extracellular matrix components) to uphold a transcriptional and metabolic program that suppresses antitumor immunity.14,20 Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of PI3Kγ in myeloid cells is sufficient to disrupt this program, reprogramming TAMs to a pro-inflammatory state that, in turn, restores the cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells and inhibits tumor growth.13,14,15,21 Therefore, targeting key intracellular regulators like PI3Kγ represents a compelling approach to therapeutically reshape the TME.5,7,22,23,24

Fungal secondary metabolism is a renowned source of clinical agents, such as rapamycin, and a testament to the drug-like properties of natural product scaffolds.25,26,27,28 This is exemplified by groundbreaking immunosuppressants and anticancer drugs directly derived from fungi. The rapamycin analogs (rapalogs), such as temsirolimus and everolimus, are mTOR pathway inhibitors used in the treatment of renal cell carcinoma and other malignancies.29 Similarly, the echinocandin class of antifungals, including caspofungin, originated from fungal pneumocandins.30 Beyond these success stories, fungal metabolites continue to reveal complex immunomodulatory activities, as seen with cyclosporin A, a metabolite from Trichoderma polysporum, which is used after organ transplantation and autoimmune diseases.28 These examples underscore the immense capacity of fungal metabolism to produce sophisticated molecules with potent, therapeutically relevant biological activities. These complex metabolites are assembled by dedicated gene clusters. A significant bottleneck, however, is metabolic dormancy, frequently caused by the evolutionary loss of genes required for producing essential endogenous precursors.31,32 This limitation prevents the discovery of bioactive molecules from such silent pathways. To overcome this, we developed an exogenous precursor-assisted fungal metabolite (ePAFM) strategy. This approach bypasses the common problem of metabolic dormancy in fungi by providing an exogenous precursor to strains that are deficient in precursor synthesis but retain the sophisticated enzymatic machinery to transform them, thereby unlocking the production of bioactive metabolites.

Here, we applied the ePAFM platform to discover TAM-reprogramming agents. Applying the ePAFM strategy to the edible fungus Agrocybe pediades with geranylhydroquinone (GHQ), a known precursor in medicinal species,32,33 led to the generation of a set of meroterpenoid-like compounds. IM502 was identified as the top candidate for TAM reprogramming. We then investigated how IM502 works, showed it is effective in preclinical cancer models, and highlighted its potential for immunotherapy. This work represents an effort leveraging natural microbial metabolism principles to generate fungal metabolites to reprogram TAM immunosuppressive functions, addressing a significant unmet need in cancer therapy.

Results

IM502 diminishes the immunosuppressive phenotype and function of macrophages in vitro

To discover TAM-reprogramming agents, we employed the ePAFM workflow, encompassing compound generation, functional screening in macrophage-T cell co-cultures, and in vivo validation of lead compounds (Figure 1A). We cultured the edible fungus Agrocybe pediades—which is deficient in precursor synthesis but proficient in their metabolism—with different precursor compounds (Figure S1A) to generate NP (natural product)-like compounds (Figures S1B and S1C). An in vitro TAM mimic model was established by polarizing bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) with tumor-cell-conditioned medium (TCM), which conferred a suppressive phenotype that inhibited OT-I CD8+ T cell proliferation (Figures S2A and S2B).34 Initial screening of unfractionated fermentation solutions (FSs) demonstrated that GHQ-FS alleviated the immunosuppressive function of TCM-polarized macrophages, markedly improving CD8+ T cell proliferation (Figures S2C–S2E) and IFNγ production (Figure S2F). Bioactivity-guided fractionation of GHQ∗FS (Figure S2G) pinpointed IM502 as the responsible compound. Based on its potency and abundance in the GHQ∗FS, IM502 was prioritized for further characterization. We first determined the optimal biological activity dose, identifying 10 μM as the concentration that maximally rescued T cell function (Figures 1F–1H). The immunostimulatory effect of IM502 was not antigen restricted, as it also reversed macrophage suppression in anti-CD3-stimulated cultures (Figures S2H–S2J) and enhanced macrophage phagocytosis of tumor cells (Figures S2K–S2M). To understand the mechanism underlying this functional reprogramming, we analyzed macrophage phenotype. IM502 treatment significantly upregulated the co-stimulatory molecules CD86 and CD80, with expression levels (geometric mean fluorescence intensity) increasing 2-fold (Figures 1I–1K). This shift toward an immunostimulatory phenotype was corroborated at the transcriptional level, where IM502 downregulated key immunosuppressive genes (Arg1, Tgfb, and Il10) and upregulated pro-inflammatory genes (Nos2, Il6, and Cxcl9) (Figure S2N).

To determine the translational potential of IM502, we investigated its effects in a human primary cell model. A co-culture assay was established with human monocyte-derived macrophages and CD8+ T cells (Figure 1L). Using HepG2 human hepatocellular carcinoma-conditioned medium to polarize macrophages, we found that IM502 consistently alleviated human macrophage-mediated suppression, as evidenced by restored T cell proliferation (Figures 1M and 1N) and cytokine production (Figure 1O). Furthermore, IM502 induced a parallel shift in the transcriptional profile of human macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory state (Figure 1P), indicating a conserved mechanism of action across species.

IM502 demonstrates potent and well-tolerated anti-tumor activity in vivo

We next determined the therapeutic potential of IM502 in a murine orthotopic liver tumor model.34 A dose-ranging study (Figure 2A) revealed that intravenous IM502 (1.5–6 mg/kg) significantly inhibited primary tumor burden, as quantified by tumor nodule number and size (Figures 2B–2D). Concordant reductions in serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) indicated improved liver function (Figure 2E). IM502 treatment also substantially limited metastatic dissemination to the lungs (Figures 2F and 2G). Critically, this efficacy was achieved without overt toxicity, as body weight, liver histology, and spleen weight remained unaffected (Figures S3A–S3C). To establish the broad-spectrum efficacy of IM502, we tested it in four immunocompetent, syngeneic tumor models (Figures 2J–2N). IM502 significantly curtailed tumor growth not only in the Hepa1-6BL liver tumor model (Figure 2K) but also in the B16F10 melanoma and CT26 and MC38 colorectal tumor models (Figures 2L–2N). Importantly, to determine if efficacy extended beyond T cell-dependent subcutaneous growth, we tested IM502 in an in vivo B16F10 melanoma metastatic colonization model.34 IM502 treatment reduced B16F10 lung metastasis by >50% (Figure 2O), underscoring its multifaceted anti-tumor activity.

To elucidate the mechanism of action, we interrogated the contribution of host immunity, as IM502 lacked direct tumoricidal activity (Figures S3D–S3G). The anti-tumor efficacy of IM502 was critically dependent on an intact immune system, as it was abolished in Rag2-deficient mice devoid of adaptive immunity (Figure 2P). The cytokine IFNγ and natural killer (NK) cells were identified as essential mediators, with efficacy lost upon IFNγ neutralization (Figures 2Q and 2R) or NK cell depletion (Figures 2S and 2T). To characterize the tissue distribution of IM502, we performed a pharmacokinetic study following intravenous administration. IM502 exhibited favorable pharmacokinetic properties with significant tumor accumulation (AUCtumor/AUCplasma = 4.2) and prolonged tumor retention (t1/2 = 12.79 h) (Figure S4). These data support that IM502 effectively reaches and persists within the TME, aligning with its sustained immunomodulatory and anti-tumor effects.

IM502 enhances the effector profile of tumor-infiltrating T and NK cells

We first assessed changes in immune cell infiltration by flow cytometry. IM502 treatment led to a significant increase in the abundance and frequency of total CD45+ leukocytes, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells in Hepa1-6BL tumors (Figures 3A–3D; gating strategy in Figure S5). To delineate the underlying changes at higher resolution, we conducted single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on intratumoral CD45+ cells (Figure S6). Unsupervised clustering identified 13 transcriptomically different subpopulations (Figure 3E; Figures S6 and S7), annotated as seven major immune cell types (Figure 3F). Transcriptomic analysis revealed that IM502 orchestrates a profound shift toward immune activation. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) indicated upregulation of cytotoxic and proliferative pathways, and downregulation of OXPHOS, across T and NK cells (Figure 3H; Figure S8A), with lineage-specific enhancements in migration (CD8+ T cells) and cytotoxicity (NK cells; Figures 3I and 3J). T cell receptor repertoire was analyzed as T cell clonality is an indicator of functional state and predicts immunotherapy efficacy.35,36 IM502 treatment was associated with a broadening of the T cell receptor repertoire, characterized by an increase in unique clonotypes and a higher proportion of mid- and low-frequency clones, as shown by the diversity indices and clonal distribution (Figures S8B–S8F). These molecular changes translated to function: IM502-treated tumors exhibited a surge in cytokine-producing cytotoxic lymphocytes, particularly IFNγ cells (Figure 3K). The enhanced infiltration and activation of effector lymphocytes is underpinned by a requisite cellular synergy. Depletion of CD8+ T cells abolished IM502’s efficacy, confirming their essential role (Figure S9A). Crucially, the IM502-driven increase in tumor infiltration and IFNγ production by either NK or CD8+ T cells was strictly dependent on the presence of the other subset (Figures S9B–S9G). This reciprocal necessity defines a synergistic loop. In vitro, IM502-reprogrammed macrophages maximally enhanced CD8+ T cell proliferation and IFNγ production only when NK cells were present (Figures S10A–S10D). Thus, IM502 fosters an interdependent NK-CD8+ T cell axis for optimal anti-tumor immunity.

Similar gains in infiltration and effector output were observed in subcutaneous MC38 tumors (Figures S11A–S11I). The critical finding that local (intratumoral) and systemic (intravenous) administration yielded comparable efficacy (Figures S11A–S11I) firmly localized IM502’s site of action to the TME. In the spleens of tumor-bearing mice, immune cell composition was unchanged, but IFNγ-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells increased (Figures S12A–S12E). The absence of any direct effect on proliferation, cytotoxicity, cytokine secretion, or exhaustion marker expression of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells in vitro (Figures S13A–S13G) contrasted with the elevated IFNγ+ T cells in the spleen and tumors, underscoring that IM502 indirectly empowers T cell anti-tumor immunity.

IM502 suppresses tumor growth by reprogramming TAMs

To define the mechanism by which IM502 modulates the TME, we analyzed cellular crosstalk. scRNA-seq data revealed that IM502 enhanced immune-stimulatory interactions and weakened immunosuppressive TGFβ signaling within the macrophage-T cell-NK cell network (Figures S14A and S14B). Focusing on the myeloid compartment, they were clustered as macrophages (Fcgr2b), dendritic cells ([DCs], Itgax, and Cd86), neutrophils (Cd63 and S100a6), and monocytes expressing marker genes of macrophages at low levels. Macrophages comprised three distinct populations: Ly6CLow (cluster 0, Apoe), Ly6CHigh (cluster 1, Plac8), and tissue-resident macrophages (cluster 6, Cd81 and C1qb) (Figures 3E and 3F). IM502 reduced the proportion of the intratumoral macrophages, Ly6CLow macrophage subset. However, despite this reduction in proportion, the number of macrophages increased, concomitant with an overall rise in total immune cell infiltration (Figures 4A and 4B; Figure S15A). Most importantly, IM502 instigated a functional reprogramming of TAMs, redirecting their transcriptome toward immunostimulatory functions (antigen presentation, phagocytosis) and away from oxidative metabolism and immunosuppression (Figures 4C–4E). This molecular shift was confirmed at the protein level and the metabolic level. Arginase 1 level decreased in IM502-treated tumor tissues and macrophages (Figures 4F and 4G; Figures S15B–S15D), with a reduction in arginine metabolites (ornithine, proline, and creatine levels; Figures 4H–4J). VEGFA downregulation corresponded to fewer tumor vessels (Figures S15E–S15H). Flow cytometry data showed elevated CD80 and CD86 on TAMs in IM502-treated tumors (Figures S16A and S16B). The functional consequence was a loss of TAM-mediated suppression of CD8+ T cells (Figures S16C–S16G). The infiltration and activation of DCs in vivo (Figures S17A and S17B) likely represent a secondary consequence of TAM reprogramming, as IM502 had no direct effect on CD80 and CD86 expression and antigen presentation ability of DCs in vitro (Figures S17C–S17E). To directly test the human relevance of our mechanism, we isolated TAMs from clinical colorectal cancer samples (Figure 4K). IM502 consistently reversed the immunosuppressive function of these patient-derived TAMs, potently enhancing the proliferation of co-cultured autologous CD8+ T cells (Figure 4L). The absence of a direct effect on T cells (Figure 4M) confirmed that the compound acts through myeloid cells. We further characterized the systemic immunomodulatory effects of IM502, finding distinct patterns of immune cell expansion and activation in draining versus non-draining lymph nodes (Figures S18A–S18J). In IM502-treated mice, the draining lymph nodes exhibited enlargement and immune cell expansion, with no changes in the number of macrophages and DCs (Figures S18A–S18D). The expression of CD86 and MHC II slightly decreased in macrophages but increased in DCs (Figure S18C–S18E). In contrast, non-draining lymph nodes showed increased numbers of both macrophages and DCs following IM502 treatment, alongside elevated CD86 expression in macrophages (Figures S18F–S18J). The pivotal evidence came from a macrophage depletion study, which demonstrated that macrophages are required for the anti-tumor activity of IM502, as macrophage depletion completely abolished its therapeutic effect (Figures 4N–4Q). Therefore, the reprogramming of TAMs from immunosuppressive to immunostimulatory cells is not only a conserved mechanism across species but also essential for the anti-tumor efficacy of IM502.

IM502 targets PI3Kγ and redirects STAT signaling

We sought to define the mechanism of IM502 by identifying its direct molecular target. An inverse virtual screening against 2,765 proteins, prioritized by docking score and macrophage-specific expression, nominated the p110γ catalytic subunit of PI3Kγ (PIK3CG) as the leading candidate—a finding corroborated by pathway enrichment analysis (Figures S19A and S19B). Structural modeling localized IM502 to the ATP-binding site of PI3Kγ (Figure 5A). We then validated this prediction biochemically: IM502 directly inhibited PI3Kγ kinase activity with high potency (IC50 61.7 nM), while its activity against PI3Kα (252.3 nM), PI3Kβ (532.5 nM), and PI3Kδ (407.9 nM) was substantially lower (Figure 5B). It concentration dependently blocked Akt/S6 phosphorylation in macrophages without altering PTEN or MAPK signaling (Figures 5C–5E; Figure S19C). The crucial functional link was established through loss-of-function experiments. Knockdown of PI3Kγ completely prevented IM502 from inhibiting Akt phosphorylation (Figure 5F), upregulating CD80, and alleviating macrophage-mediated suppression of T cells (Figures 5G–5K). To understand the structural basis of IM502 activity, we compared its binding pose with that of IPI549. This analysis revealed a fundamental difference. As previously described,24 IPI549 adopts a planar propeller shape, anchoring its aminopyrazolopyrimidine to Val882 via bidentate hydrogen bonds and stacking its quinazolinone against Trp812 (Figure S19D). IM502, however, assumes a deeper, boat-shaped conformation, forming three hydrogen bonds with Lys833, Glu880, and Asp964 (Figures 5A; Figure S19E). This distinct interaction suggests a specific mechanism for altering kinase conformation and downstream signaling.

We next investigated the transcriptional circuitry governed by IM502. Factor activity analysis in TAMs highlighted the pronounced activation of the interferon-responsive ISGF3 complex (STAT1/STAT2/IRF9)37 alongside Spi1 and Junb (Figure 5L). Mechanistically, IM502 reciprocally modulated STAT phosphorylation. The activation of STAT1 (Ser727) was driven through a defined AKT-p38 MAPK-STAT1 axis initiated by PI3Kγ inhibition, as evidenced by concurrent AKT suppression and p38/STAT1 activation, and the abolition of STAT1 phosphorylation by p38 inhibition (Figure 5M; Figures S20A–S20C). This pathway also underpinned IM502-induced phenotypic changes in macrophages (Figures S20D and S20E) and was similarly engaged by the PI3Kγ inhibitor IPI549. In parallel, IM502 inhibited the immunosuppressive pathways mediated by STAT3 (Ser727) and STAT6 (Tyr641) (Figure 5M). This selective STAT redirection was reflected in the upregulated expression of ISGF3-regulated genes involved in immune activation and metabolic reprogramming (Figure 5N). Collectively, these data establish that IM502, through its engagement of PI3Kγ, orchestrates a transcriptional switch in macrophages by rebalancing STAT activity to favor an anti-tumor phenotype.

IM502 reprograms macrophage metabolism and alleviates oxidative stress

STAT3 controls the function of mitochondria and OXPHOS in macrophages.38 We hypothesized that IM502’s inhibition of PI3Kγ/STAT signaling would directly impact core metabolism. scRNA-seq profiling revealed a fundamental shift from OXPHOS to glycolysis in IM502-treated TAMs (Figures 4C–4E). Functional Seahorse analysis confirmed that IM502 reversed the TCM-induced hyper-OXPHOS state, normalizing oxygen consumption rates and ATP production (Figures S21A and S21B). This metabolic shift was functionally linked to STAT signaling, as the STAT1 and STAT3 inhibitors attenuated IM502-induced increases in lactate production, glucose consumption, expression of glycolysis-associated genes, and function changes (Figures S21C–S21H). Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of glycolysis with 2-deoxy-D-glucose abrogated the immunostimulatory reprogramming of macrophages by IM502, demonstrating that this metabolic shift is required for functional immune activation (Figure S22).

We next focused on the associated redox imbalance. Tumor-educated macrophages are known to sustain high levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS).39 IM502 treatment effectively reversed this phenotype, reducing mitochondrial ROS (Figures S23A and S23B) and downregulating transcriptional programs linked to ROS production (Figure S23C). Superoxide originates chiefly from mitochondria, peroxisomes, and NADPH oxidases (NOX).40 Mechanistically, IM502 suppressed expression of superoxide-generating systems, including NADPH oxidases (Nox1 and Nox2) and mitochondrial electron transport chain components (Figure S23D). This was coupled with a restoration of the antioxidant glutathione (GSH), which is depleted in TAMs (Figure S23E). Excess ROS drives lipid peroxidation and membrane damage.40,41 The net effect of this metabolic rewiring was a significant reduction in oxidative damage, evidenced by decreased lipid peroxidation (Figure S23F) and a recovery of mitochondrial membrane potential in both murine and human macrophages (Figures S23G and S23H). Thus, IM502 induces a coherent metabolic reprogramming that counteracts the pro-tumorigenic oxidative stress signature of TAMs.

IM502 matches the clinical drugs and overcomes resistance to PD-1 blockade

Further dose titration established 1.5 mg/kg as an effective dose of IM502, significantly reducing orthotopic liver tumor burden, whereas 0.375 mg/kg showed only a non-significant trend (Figures 6A and 6B). Direct comparisons with relevant clinical benchmarks revealed its competitive profile. IM502 at 1.5 mg/kg achieved a comparable antitumor effect to a much higher dose of IPI549 (12 mg/kg), a clinical-stage PI3Kγ inhibitor, and 1.5 mg/kg IPI549 itself was ineffective (Figures 6C–6E). Clinical drug PLX3397 (pexidartinib), a CSF1R (mainly expressed in macrophages) inhibitor, showed no activity at the standard dose of 10 mg/kg, while IM502 (1.5 mg/kg) again reduced tumor burden (Figures 6F–6I). We then evaluated IM502 in the context of immunotherapy resistance. Strikingly, IM502 monotherapy was effective in both orthotopic liver tumor and syngeneic melanoma models that were entirely refractory to anti-PD-1 checkpoint blockade (Figures 6N–6S). Consequently, we tested a rational combination strategy. The combination of IM502 with anti-PD-1 antibody acted synergistically, suppressing orthotopic liver tumor growth and reducing the number of large nodules (>2 mm) and spontaneous lung metastases by a decrease of up to 60% and 80%, respectively (Figures 7A–7G). Sorafenib, a standard of care for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma, is known to paradoxically promote intratumoral hypoxia and M2-like TAM polarization, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that limits its own efficacy. Given that IM502 reprograms TAMs toward an immunostimulatory M1-like state, we hypothesized that combining IM502 with sorafenib could counteract sorafenib-induced immunosuppression and achieve synergistic anti-tumor activity. As shown in Figures 7H–7M, the combination indeed produced superior efficacy compared to monotherapy. Discussion

TAMs constitute a dominant immunosuppressive population in most solid tumors. They restrain cytotoxic T and NK cells at both primary and metastatic sites, thereby impeding spontaneous and therapy-induced anti-tumor immunity.7,10,42 Despite intense effort, the clinical translation of TAM-targeted therapies has been hampered by the limitations of existing agents.7,43 Here, we introduce a solution derived from fungal metabolism: IM502, a meroterpenoid that effectively reprograms, rather than eliminates, TAMs. By concurrently inhibiting PI3Kγ and modulating STAT signaling, IM502 reverses TAM-mediated immunosuppression, leading to robust anti-tumor immunity, inhibition of metastasis, and the ability to overcome resistance to PD-1 blockade in multiple preclinical models—all with a favorable dosing schedule and toxicity profile.

The discovery of IM502 was enabled by our ePAFM strategy. It leverages the innate biosynthetic capacity of fungi, distinguishing it from synthetic biology approaches that often rely on extensive genetic redesign.44,45 By simply providing an exogenous precursor to a fungus that retains the necessary downstream enzymatic machinery, we generated IM502, a compound that achieves nanomolar inhibition of human PI3Kγ and potent anti-tumor activity. A key advantage of the ePAFM approach is its scalability; precursor feeding integrates seamlessly into standard fermentation processes, bypassing the need for complex genetic engineering and facilitating industrial-scale production.

The anti-tumor effect of IM502 is unequivocally mediated by the host immune system, with macrophages identified as the primary cellular target. The complete abolition of IM502’s efficacy upon clodronate-mediated macrophage depletion confirms this mechanism. Furthermore, the absence of any direct cytotoxic effect on tumor cells at pharmacologically relevant concentrations underscores that the anti-tumor activity of IM502 is immune mediated. These underscore its significant translational potential as a tumor immunotherapy.

IM502 remodels the TAM phenotype through a coordinated impact on key signaling nodes and cellular metabolism. First, its primary action is the inhibition of PI3Kγ, leading to reduced Akt activity. This directly dampens the phosphorylation of STAT3 at Ser727, a key driver of the immunosuppressive M2-like program.12,46 Concurrently, the inhibition of the PI3Kγ-Akt axis activates a p38 MAPK-STAT1 signaling cascade, providing a direct route to STAT1/2 activation independent of STAT3 modulation. Notably, this Akt-p38-STAT1 pathway was similarly induced by the PI3Kγ inhibitor IPI549, underscoring its core role in PI3Kγ-targeted reprogramming. Second, the diminution of STAT3 activity alleviates its well-characterized reciprocal inhibition of STAT1, thereby facilitating a robust switch toward a STAT1/STAT2/IRF9-dominated signaling program that promotes M1-like polarization, antigen presentation, and pro-inflammatory cytokine production.10,12,37,38 Furthermore, this signaling shift triggers an essential metabolic reprogramming from the OXPHOS axis to glycolysis, which is critical for maintaining the TAM identity.38,47 This metabolic shift is functionally linked to the concomitant STAT reprogramming, as inhibition of STAT1 attenuated the IM502-induced enhancement of glycolytic flux. This metabolic reprogramming is not an isolated event; it is intrinsically linked to the signaling changes. On one hand, it is driven by signaling network changes (e.g., via STATs). On the other hand, it provides the necessary energy and biosynthetic intermediates (e.g., for cytokine production) to support the immunostimulatory gene expression program and functional phenotype, a well-established paradigm in macrophage biology. IM502 curbs mitochondrial ROS generation—a known contributor to T cell dysfunction in the TME.40 Our scRNA-seq data corroborate this shift, showing a transcriptional move away from M2-associated pathways toward those involved in immune activation. The net result of this molecular and metabolic rewiring is a fundamental change in TAM function, culminating in their enhanced capacity to stimulate CD8+ T cell proliferation and potentiate NK cell cytotoxicity. We acknowledge that IM502 was identified through phenotypic screening, and its primary target was determined mainly by inverse virtual docking followed by biochemical validation. While this approach successfully identified PI3Kγ, a comprehensive selectivity screen against a broad panel of kinases will be performed in future studies to systematically rule out potential off-target effects.

A key translational advantage of IM502 is its potent in vivo efficacy, which is achieved with an infrequent dosing schedule (four intravenous doses) across a wide therapeutic window (0.75–6 mg/kg). This profile is particularly notable given its structural distinction from clinical-stage PI3Kγ inhibitors like IPI549.15,18,21,48 The concentration discrepancy between the biochemical IC50 (61.7 nM) and the cellular functional potency (1–10 μM) is well within the range commonly observed for small-molecule kinase inhibitors, reflecting inherent cellular barriers—including membrane permeability, intracellular protein binding, and metabolic stability—that are absent in purified enzyme systems. Importantly, the robust anti-tumor activity achieved with low-dose intravenous IM502 (0.75 mg/kg) confirms that sufficient target engagement is achieved in the TME, demonstrating that this concentration gap does not compromise its therapeutic efficacy. Supporting this, pharmacokinetic analysis revealed favorable tumor distribution, with a tumor-to-plasma area under the curve (AUC) ratio of 4.2 and an extended tumor half-life. This significant accumulation and prolonged retention likely underpin its sustained target engagement and durable anti-tumor activity despite infrequent dosing. The potency of IM502 is significantly greater, as a direct comparison showed that 1.5 mg/kg of IM502 was required to achieve the anti-tumor effect of 12 mg/kg IPI549, a dose at which IPI549 itself was ineffective. This order-of-magnitude difference in potency further supports a qualitatively superior mechanism of action, potentially involving more sustained target engagement. The durable efficacy observed with a limited dosing regimen is consistent with a pharmacokinetic profile conducive to prolonged target coverage and a sustained pharmacodynamic effect, enhancing its therapeutic utility.49

Our findings demonstrate that IM502 induces a comprehensive reshaping of the TME, catalyzing a coordinated anti-tumor immune response. This is characterized by the enhanced infiltration and functional activation of key effector populations—CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, and NK cells—culminating in a state of potent cytotoxicity.50 CD4+ T cells coordinate innate and adaptive immune responses, favoring anti-tumor CD8+ T cell responses.50,51 Recent evidence suggests that CD4+ T cells may also possess direct cytotoxic capabilities,52 raising the possibility that these cells contribute independently to tumor control in IM502-treated models. Elevated NK cell infiltration correlates with improved survival in several solid malignancies.53

A significant outcome of IM502 treatment is the reversal of NK cell dysfunction in the TME. In solid tumors, NK cells have historically been difficult to exploit therapeutically owing to their sparse baseline infiltration54,55 and rapid functional exhaustion upon entry into the TME.56 By reprogramming TAMs, IM502 achieves what many immunotherapies cannot: it enhances NK cell infiltration, prevents exhaustion, and restores cytotoxic function, thereby engaging a critical arm of innate immunity often neglected in solid tumor treatment. This dual effect is significant: beyond their direct tumor-killing capacity, activated NK cells orchestrate a broader anti-tumor response by licensing CD8+ T cell cross-priming and driving the recruitment of additional immune effectors into the TME.57,58

We identify IFNγ as the non-redundant cytokine underpinning this response. The simultaneous requirement for NK cells, CD8+ T cells, and IFNγ reveals that the efficacy of IM502 hinges on fostering innate-adaptive synergy, likely through NK-derived IFNγ licensing an effective T cell response. This aligns with a previous study, showing NK-derived IFNγ primes tumor-specific CD8+ T cells; depleting NK cells or IFNγ cripples CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor rejection, and IFNγ-deficient NK cells fail to activate CD8+ T cells.59 Beyond bridging innate and adaptive immunity, IFNγ upregulates PD-L1 on tumor cells and TAMs, potentially restricting CD8+ T cells via PD-1 engagement.60 This provides a mechanistic rationale for the observed synergy with anti-PD-1 therapy and positioning IM502 as a logical partner to overcome the resistance to immune checkpoint blockade.61

As metastatic disease remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality, therapies capable of engaging both innate and adaptive immunity may address an unmet clinical need.55 By potentiating NK cell function while concurrently activating T cell responses, IM502 could complement existing immunotherapies that focus predominantly on adaptive immunity. Future studies should delineate the precise molecular pathways by which IM502 promotes NK cell recruitment and activation within the TME. Such insights may inform rational combination strategies designed to further enhance NK cell functionality and surmount the long-standing challenges of NK cell-based therapy in solid tumors.

Pexidartinib (PLX3397), the sole FDA-approved CSF-1R inhibitor for tenosynovial giant cell tumor, depletes or reprograms TAMs via inhibiting the CSF-1/CSF-1R axis62 and is under clinical trial for liver cancer. Anti-PD-1 antibody and sorafenib remain standard first-line therapies for diverse tumors, respectively. In the Hepa1-6BL liver tumor metastasis model, IM502 monotherapy surpassed pexidartinib, PD-1 blockade, and sorafenib, reducing tumor burden and metastasis while overcoming ICB resistance and potentiating sorafenib. Furthermore, IM502 equaled FOLFOX activity in a syngeneic colorectal model, highlighting broad translational promise. Ex vivo data revealed that IM502 reprograms human TAMs isolated from colorectal cancer tissue and shifts macrophage phenotype, mirroring murine observations. These results support the clinical translation of IM502 for liver and colorectal malignancies. Emerging evidence that certain leukemias depend on PI3Kγ for survival63 warrants evaluation of IM502 in hematologic malignancies.

While PI3Kγ is critically involved in the migratory capacity of various myeloid cells, including DCs, its role in DC functions appears distinct from its role in other lineages like macrophages.64,65,66,67,68 Our data, consistent with prior literature, indicate that PI3Kγ deficiency or its inhibition with IM502 does not intrinsically impair core DC functions such as maturation, antigen uptake, or direct peptide presentation to T cells in vitro.66,69 However, a more complex role is revealed in specific contexts; for instance, PI3Kγ can influence T cell activation when DCs present antigen via immune complexes, and recent work highlights that PI3Kγ inhibition enhances macrophage—but not DC—efferocytosis within the TME.69,48 This dichotomy underscores that the immunomodulatory effects of IM502 observed in vivo are likely not a direct result of impairing DC antigen presentation, but rather stem from altering the complex cellular crosstalk within the TME. Therefore, conditional knockout models will be essential to definitively delineate the cell-type-specific contributions of PI3Kγ to the overall anti-tumor activity of IM502.

In summary, IM502 emerges as a potent therapeutic candidate originating from a fungal metabolite platform. It acts through a coherent mechanism—TAM reprogramming via PI3Kγ/STAT inhibition—to resolve immunosuppression and initiate a potent, coordinated immune attack. Its compelling preclinical profile nominates it for development as a next-generation immunotherapy, either alone or in combination, for challenging cancers.

Our data also inform the clinical translation of IM502. Intravenous and intratumoral administration showed comparable efficacy in controlling tumor growth and reprogramming the immune microenvironment, indicating that systemic delivery is effective. Pharmacokinetic studies confirm that intravenously administered IM502 accumulates and is retained in tumors. Therefore, an intravenous formulation represents a practical and broadly applicable development path, particularly for combination regimens with immune checkpoint or targeted therapies. The present findings directly support the clinical translation of an intravenous formulation of IM502, with advanced delivery strategies reserved for future study.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations merit attention. First, while murine models provide crucial insights, they cannot fully replicate human tumor complexity and heterogeneity. Future studies employing immunocompetent patient-derived organoids or humanized mouse models will be critical to better model the human TME. Second, the number of patient-derived TAM samples tested was limited. To strengthen the clinical relevance of our findings, future work will require validation in a larger, more diverse cohort of human samples. Third, although our data strongly support TAMs as the primary target of IM502, we cannot fully exclude potential secondary effects on other cell types within the complex TME. Future investigations using cell-type-specific knockout models will be essential to precisely delineate cellular targets of IM502. Fourth, the potential development of drug resistance, a common challenge in cancer therapy, was not addressed in the current study. In addition, a comprehensive pharmacokinetic profile of IM502 is necessary to optimize dosing regimens. Furthermore, exploring alternative formulations, such as oral delivery, represents an important goal for improving clinical practicality and patient compliance. Finally, the long-term safety profile of IM502 and potential mechanisms of resistance remain to be systematically investigated in future chronic dosing studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice and human samples

Adult C57BL/6J wild-type (WT), Rag2−/−, OT-1 (on a C57BL/6 background) mice and Balb/C WT mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences, Inc. (Guangzhou, China). All mice were housed in the Animal Center of Xuzhou Medical University (Xuzhou, Jiangsu, China) at an ambient temperature of 24°C with a 12-h on/12-h off light cycle and free access to food and water. Age- and gender-matched mice between 8 and 12 weeks of age were used in all experiments. Throughout the study, mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups. Both sexes were included with no significant sex-related differences observed in preliminary analyses. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. All animal experimental procedures were strictly performed according to the Guidelines for Animal Experiments of Xuzhou Medical University and the National Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All experiments were approved by the Xuzhou Medical University Animal Ethics Committee (202209S041).

Human tumor tissue samples and blood samples were taken from Xuzhou Central Hospital under the rules of the Xuzhou Central Hospital Ethics Committee (XZXY-LK-20240108-0006). Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research. Patient consent for publication is not applicable.

Cell lines

The colorectal cancer cell lines MC38, and CT26, and the melanoma cell line B16F10; and human hepatocellular cell line HepG2 and colorectal carcinoma cell HCT116 cell line cultured in DMEM and the mouse hepatocellular cell line Hepa1-6BL (derived from Hepa1-6 cells) in RPMI 1640 media, respectively, containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Umedium He Fei China), 100 U penicillin/ml and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma, and cell line authentication was routinely performed. Hepa1-6BL cell line was derived from Hepa1-6 cells as previously described.34 All cell lines were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The reagents and kits for biological assays are listed in the key resources table.

A total of 3 × 106 Hepa1-6BL cells, HepG2, and HCT116 were cultivated in a 10 cm dish for 36 h, respectively. Thereafter, the cell culture supernatant was collected and designated as tumor-conditioned medium (TCM).

Tumor models

For Subcutaneous tumor models, C57BL/6J WT or Rag2−/− mice were injected subcutaneously (s.c.) with Hepa1-6BL (7 × 105), MC38 (5 × 105), or B16F10 (2 × 105) tumor cells, and Balb/C WT mice with CT26 (2 × 105), to establish subcutaneous tumors, respectively. Cells with more than 95% viability were used for experiments. When the tumor size reached ∼17 mm2, which was around day 6 or day 7, mice were treated with the indicated dose of IM502 in the figures, 4 times every two days. B16F10 tumor-bearing mice were treated with cIg or anti-PD-1 antibody (100 μg/mouse, i.p.) on days 7, 10, and 13. Subcutaneous tumor sizes were determined by caliper square measurements and calculated as the product of two perpendicular diameters, with data presented as mean ± SEM (mm2) for each group or individual tumor size.

For the experimental lung metastases model, C57BL/6J WT mice were injected intravenously (i.v.) with B16F10 (2 × 105) tumor cells, through tumor cell lung colonization to assess lung metastases. Mice were randomly grouped and treated with control diluent or IM502 (6 mg/kg, i.v.) on days 0 and 3 relative to tumor inoculation. On day 14, the lungs were perfused, and the metastatic burden was quantified by counting colonies on the lung surface as previously described.72,73

The liver orthotopic model was established by intravenously injecting Hepa1-6BL (5 × 105) cells into the tail vein by hydrodynamic tail vein injection as described in our previous study.34,74 Mice were randomly grouped and treated with control diluent, IM502, PLX3397, sorafenib, or anti-PD-1 as indicated in the figure legends; or orally gavaged with control fermentation solution (CFS) or GHQ precursor∗ fermentation solution (PFS) (200 μL/mouse) on days 6, 8, 10, 12 and 14; or anti-IFNγ antibody or control antibody cIg (i.p. 250 μg/mouse, i.p.) on days 4, 8, 12, or anti-NK1.1 or cIg antibody (i.p. 100 μg/mouse) on days 2, 6, 10 and 14, or liposome or clodronate liposome (i.p., 1 mg/mouse) on days 3, 9 and 14, relative to tumor inoculation. Tumor-bearing mice were treated with cIg or anti-PD-1 antibody (100 μg/mouse, i.p.) on days 9, 12, and 15, relative to tumor inoculation. Mice were sacrificed on days 18–21, and livers, lungs, and blood were collected. The whole liver tissue was weighed, and the orthotopic liver tumor burden and spontaneous lung metastatic burden were quantified by counting colonies on the surface of the livers and lungs, respectively.

All in vivo experiments were blindly randomized to evenly distribute tumor burden among the different experimental groups at the time of treatment initiation. Mixed gender mice were used in different experiments. Following the 3R principles, 5–7 mice were used in each group to achieve statistical significance. All the mice in the study were maintained in the same location in the animal center.

Method details

Preparation of compounds and fermentation solutions

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich unless noted otherwise. NMR spectroscopic data were recorded on a Bruker Avance III-400 and Bruker Avance NEO 600, at 298K with TMS as an internal standard. Silica gel (200–300 mesh) for column chromatography (CC) and GF 254 (10–20 mm) for thin-layer chromatography (TLC) were produced by Qingdao Marine Chemical Company, China. The ODS silica gel (50 μm) was purchased from YMC Co., Ltd, Japan, and Sephadex LH-20 from Pharmacia Biotech, Sweden. Purification of compounds was performed on semipreparative HPLC (SHIMADZU LC-20A) with a C18 column (10 μm, 250 × 10 mm).

Chemical synthesis of geranylhydroquinone

Phenol (15.0 g, 159.4 mmol) and Al(OPh)3 aluminum chip (1.5 g, 55.6 mmol) in a two-necked flask equipped with a thermometer and a condenser were heated to 170°C. After the aluminum chip was completely dissolved in the phenol, the solution was cooled to 40°C and treated with hydroquinone (70.0 g, 635.7 mmol) and geraniol (44 g, 285.0 mmol). The reaction was performed at 180°C for 24 h. The course of the reaction was monitored by TLC. Next, the reaction mixture was extracted with EtOAc. The combined organic layer was dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated under a vacuum. The residue was purified with silica gel chromatography (PE/EtOAc = 10/1) to generate geranylhydroquinone (25.2 g, 36% yield).

Geranylhydroquinone, a pale yellow oil, was analyzed using 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) as previously described.32

Feeding the culture with geranylhydroquinone and HPLC analyses of extracts

The strain Agrocybe pediades was isolated from the tissue culture of its fruiting bodies collected in the Xing-Long Mountains, Gansu Province, People’s Republic of China, and identified by ITS sequence analysis as described.32 Fresh mycelia of the strain Agrocybe pediades were inoculated into a 1 L flask containing 500 mL of yeast malt medium (glucose 4.0 g/L, malt extract 10.0 g/L, and yeast extract 4.0 g/L). After a 7-day incubation at 28°C with an agitation of 140 rpm, precursor compound geranylhydroquinone dissolved in ethanol was fed to the cultures to a final concentration of 2 mM. After growing for the next 2 days in a 1 L Erlenmeyer flask, the culture (fermentation solution) was filtered, and the broth filtrate was extracted with an equal volume of EtOAc two times. The dried extract was dissolved in 0.5 mL of methanol and analyzed by an SHIMADZU LC-20A HPLC system using a C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, Shim-pack GIST, 5 μm; detector: UV) with a flow rate of 1 mL min−1. The mobile phase consisted of H2O (A) and MeCN (B) using a gradient elution from 5% to 100% B at 0–30 min and 100% B at 30–35 min.

One liter of filtered fermentation solution was concentrated by a rotary evaporator at around 45°C into a 200 mL stock fermentation solution for use in biological assays. The culture of A. pediades, without adding the precursor compound geranylhydroquinone, was used as a control fermentation solution.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from mouse tumors, lymph nodes, or spleens, and stained and analyzed by flow cytometry as described in our previous study.34,74 The cell numbers were counted by adding cell counting beads to the stained samples. Cell numbers in 200 mg of tumor tissue were calculated and presented. The flow cytometry antibodies utilized, including their source and dilution details, are listed in the Key Resource Table.

BD cytometric bead array (CBA)

The cytokines IFNγ and IL-2 were measured using the Easy MagPlex Human Cytokine Kit. To perform the test, 50 μL of cytokine capture antibody-coated fluorescent microspheres and 50 μL of cell supernatant were added to the test tubes. The mixture was then incubated for 1 h while being shaken and protected from light. After washing, 100 μL of cytokine detection antibodies labeled with phycoerythrin (PE) was added and incubated for 1 h, shaking at room temperature. 500 μL of Wash Buffer was added to each tube, vortexed briefly, and centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min. Finally, 200 μL of washing buffer was added to each tube, and flow cytometry analysis was performed on a DxFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed according to the standard curve.

ELISA

The level of mouse IFNγ in the culture supernatant of our co-culture assays was determined using an ELISA Kit (Elabscience) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Tumor cell viability and apoptosis assay

MC38 and Hepa1-6BL cells were seeded in 96-well plates (2 × 103 cells/well). The cells were treated with different concentrations of IM502 as indicated in the figure. Next, the treated cells were incubated with CCK8 reagent for 1 h, and the viability of the cells was analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. To measure apoptosis, MC38 and Hepa1-6BL cells were seeded in 24-well plates and treated with IM502 (10 μM) for 48 h. Cells were then harvested and stained for apoptosis analysis as described in our previous study.34

Generation of BM-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and dendritic cells (BMDCs)

Bone marrow cells were obtained as described in our previous study.34 The bone marrow cells were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 5% L929 cell supernatant for 5 days to generate BMDMs or supplemented with 10 ng/mL mouse GM-CSF for 6 days to generate BMDCs.

Generation of human macrophages and dendritic cells

Monocytes (CD45+CD14+7AAD−) cells were sorted from human PBMC from the blood of healthy donors. Monocytes were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 media containing M-CSF (25 ng/mL) for 5 days or GM-CSF (25 ng/mL) for 6 days to generate macrophages and dendritic cells, respectively. Human THP-1 cell-derived macrophages were generated through stimulation with PMA (100 ng/mL) for 12 h.

Sorting of immune cell populations

Spleens from OT-1 mice were harvested and mashed through a 70 μm cell strainer. Red blood cells were lysed with ACK buffer. Splenocytes were blocked with Fc-blocking antibody before staining with anti-CD8α, anti-CD4, and anti-TCRβ antibodies, and Zombie aqua or 7AAD. Live CD8+ T cells (TCRβ+ CD8+) and CD4+ T cells (TCRβ+ CD4+) were sorted on an Aria III.

To obtain TAMs, Hepa1-6BL tumors were harvested from C57BL/6 WT mice from control and IM502-treated groups at the end of the experiment, as described in the tumor model and treatment section of the methods. Tumor single-cell suspensions were stained with antibodies following Fc blockade and sorted by flow cytometry to obtain TAMs (CD45.2+CD11b+F4/80+7AAD−) according to the strategy in Figure S5. Sorted cells with a purity of over 95% were used in subsequent experiments.

Human PBMCs were prepared from whole blood from healthy donors using Ficoll treatment. PBMC were blocked with Fc-blocking antibody before staining with anti-CD8α, anti-CD45, and 7AAD. Live CD8+ T cells (CD45+ CD8+) were sorted on an Aria III flow cytometer.

To obtain human TAMs from CRC tissues (Table S1), human CRC tissues were immediately collected after surgery. Tumor tissue was finely minced and digested in 3 mL of digestion solution (1 mg/mL of Collagenase IV, 20 μg/mL of DNase I, and 1% FBS in RPMI 1640 medium) on a shaker at 37°C and 150 rpm for 35 min. After digestion, the tumor tissue was filtered through a 70 μm strainer and suspended in RPMI 1640 medium. Cells were pre-incubated with Fc-blocking antibody before staining with anti-CD45, anti-CD14, anti-CD68, and 7AAD. Live macrophages (CD45+CD14+CD68+ 7AAD−) were sorted on an Aria III flow cytometer.

Macrophage and dendritic cell with CD8+ T cell coculture assay

The co-culture assay was performed as previously described.34 Mouse T cells were labeled with CellTrace Violet (CTV) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. BMDMs or BMDCs were treated with fresh media (FM) or Hepa1-6BL tumor cell conditioned media (TCM) for 12 h, followed by treatment with the indicated compounds (10 μM) or the indicated concentrations of IM502 for 24 h. Next, cells were pulsed with 100 ng/mL ovalbumin (OVA) peptide (SIINFEKL) for 2 h. BMDMs and CTV-labeled CD8+ T cells were co-cultured at a ratio of 1:10. Likewise, an anti-CD3 antibody (2 μg/mL) was added to co-cultured T cells and BMDMs for assays without OVA peptide pulsing. T cell proliferation was measured 2 days later by flow cytometry. In some experiments, supernatants were collected for IFNγ detection by ELISA.

Human T cells from the blood of healthy donors were labeled with CellTrace Violet (CTV). Human PBMC monocyte-derived macrophages and dendritic cells were treated with fresh media or human HepG2 tumor cell TCM for 12 h, followed by treatment with 10 μM IM502 for 24 h. Next, human macrophages or dendritic cells were co-cultured with CTV-labeled human CD8+ T cells at a ratio of 1:10, in the presence of an anti-CD3 antibody (2 μg/mL). T cell proliferation was measured 3 days later by flow cytometry. In some experiments, supernatants were collected for IFNγ and IL-2 measurement by CBA.

Immunosuppressive function of TAMs

The immunosuppressive function of mouse TAMs was determined as described in our previous study.34 In brief, sorted TAMs were pulsed with 100 ng/mL OVA peptide for 2 h, then co-cultured with CTV-labeled OT-1 CD8+ T cells at a ratio of 1:10 for 2 days. CD8+ T cell proliferation was directly determined by flow cytometry. Granzyme B expression was determined after PMA (0.8 μM) and ionomycin (1 ng/mL) stimulation in the presence of protein transport inhibitor Brefeldin A (10 μM) for 6 h. After co-culture with TAMs, CD8+ T cells were added to 5(6)-carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE)-labeled Hepa1-6BL tumor cells at a ratio of 5:1 for 24 h, and the apoptosis of Hepa1-6BL was detected with Annexin V using flow cytometry, to measure the cytolytic ability of CD8+ T cells.

Human TAMs from colorectal cancer (CRC) tissues were treated with IM502 (10 μM, 24 h), supplemented with human M-CSF (25 ng/mL) and HCT116 TCM (50%) to maintain the survival and suppressive phenotype. Then TAMs were co-cultured with CellTrace Violet (CTV)-labeled human CD8+ T cells isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in the presence of an anti-CD3 antibody (2 μg/mL) at a ratio of 1:10 for 3 days. The T cells were analyzed with flow cytometry, and the total number was calculated based on counting beads.

T cell proliferation

Mouse CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells were sorted from the spleens of naive mice and then labeled with CTV. T cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and treated with IM502 (10 μM) in the presence of anti-CD3/CD28 (2 μg/mL) and IL-2 (100 U/ml), cultured in fresh media (FM) or Hepa1-6BL tumor cell conditioned media (TCM) for 2 days. T cell proliferation was determined by flow cytometry.

Human CD8+ T cells were sorted from PBMC after Ficoll treatment of whole blood from healthy donors. CD8+ T cells were stained with CTV and then plated in a 96-well plate pre-coated with anti-CD3 antibody in the presence of anti-CD28 antibody (2 μg/mL) and human IL-2 (100 U/ml) for 3 days. T cell proliferation was determined by flow cytometry, and the number was calculated based on the counting beads.

Phagocytosis assay

The phagocytosis ability of murine BMDM cells was determined through co-culturing with CFSE-labeled Hepa1-6BL cells at a ratio of 10:1 in the presence or absence of 10 μM IM502 for 12 h, as previously described.34

Tumor tissue histology staining and analysis

As previously described,34 paraffin-embedded tissue sections (4 μm) were prepared from specimens fixed in 4% neutral-buffered formaldehyde (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed by heating the slides in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95°C for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2 for 10 min, followed by three PBS washes. Non-specific binding was blocked with 10% normal goat serum for 30 min at room temperature. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody.

For immunofluorescence staining, following three 10 min PBS washes, fluorescein-conjugated secondary antibody (species and fluorophore specified in Key Resources Table) was applied for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL) (VICMED, VS1008-5) for 5 min, and slides were mounted with fluorescence-compatible medium. At least three non-overlapping fields per section were randomly selected and imaged under identical exposure settings (Olympus BX53×microscope, 20× objective, Japan). Quantification was performed with ImageJ v1.53t. The DAPI channel was used to delineate total tissue area. A single intensity threshold, kept constant for every image within the experiment, was applied to the target-protein channel to define positive signals. Fluorescence-positive cells (fluorescence- and DAPI-positive) were counted and expressed as positive cells per mm2. The percentage of fluorescence-positive area was calculated as (positive signal area/total DAPI area) × 100%.”

As for immunohistology staining, following three 10-min PBS washes, the sample was incubated with 1–3 drops of biotinylated secondary antibodies for 30–60 min. After washing with PBS, DAB (1:200, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) as an enzyme-substrate was added. The tissue was stained with the nuclear counterstain hematoxylin for better visualization of the tissue morphology and covered with a coverslip of an appropriate size, and placed on filter paper or a towel to drain excess mounting medium and allow it to dry. At least three non-overlapping fields per section were randomly captured under identical illumination and exposure settings (Olympus BX53×, 20× objective, Japan). Color deconvolution (H-DAB vector) was applied to isolate the DAB signal. After applying a constant threshold to the DAB channel, the integrated optical density (IOD = sum of pixel intensities within the positive area) was measured with ImageJ v1.53t. Target-protein positivity is reported as (DAB-positive area/total tissue area) × 100%.

OXPHOS assay

As previously described in our study,74 the oxygen consumption rate (OCR, pmol O2/min) was measured using a Seahorse XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer to assess OXPHOS. BMDMs (1.5 × 104) were plated into 96-well Seahorse microplates in 100 μL of culture media and treated with fresh media or Hepa1-6BL tumor cell conditioned media (TCM) for 12 h before the addition of IM502 (10 μM) for another 6 h. Cells were washed 1 h before the assay and cultured for 30 min in Seahorse assay media containing 2 mM glutamine, 1 mM pyruvate, and 10 mM glucose at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere without CO2. OCR was measured at the time points indicated in the figure and after the injection of oligomycin (2 μM), FCCP (1 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM). Measures of maximal respiration, spare respiration capacity, ATP production, and basal respiration were calculated.

ROS and glutathione (GSH) detection

BMDMs were seeded in a 24-well plate at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well. BMDMs were treated with Hepa1-6BL TCM for 12 h and incubated for another 6 h in the presence or absence of IM502 (10 μM). Cells were stained with CM-H2DCFDA (10 μM), Thiolite Green (10 μM) for 30 min, or MitoSOX Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (1 μM) to detect the levels of ROS, GSH, and mitochondrial ROS, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, as previously described.34

Mitochondrial membrane potential

Human THP-1-derived macrophages or murine BMDMs were seeded in a 48-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well). Cells were incubated for 12 h in the presence or absence of HepG2 or Hepa1-6BL TCM and then treated with IM502 (10 μM) for 6 h. Cells were washed and incubated with JC-1 dye (500-fold dilution) for 20 min in the dark. The shift of fluorescence from red (JC-1 aggregates) to green (JC-1 monomers) was determined using flow cytometry. The gMFI ratio of JC-1 monomer to JC-1 aggregate was calculated to assess the change in mitochondrial membrane potential.

Lipid peroxidation

Briefly, BMDMs were cultured in Hepa1-6BL TCM for 12 h, and then treated with IM502 (10 μM) for another 6 h. BMDM cells were incubated with the Lipid Peroxidation Sensor in the cell culture media in a humidified chamber at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed and analyzed by flow cytometry. The fluorescence of the sensor changes from red to green upon peroxidation in cells. Detection of peroxidation-dependent shift enables the ratiometric measurement of lipid peroxidation.

Western blotting

In the experiment measuring protein ARG1, murine BMDMs were exposed to Hepa1-6BL TCM for 12 h, followed by IM502 (10 μM) for 24 h. In other experiments, murine BMDMs or THP-1 monocyte-derived human macrophages were exposed to Hepa1-6BL or HepG2 TCM for 30 min, followed by treatment with the indicated concentrations of IM502 for 30 min or 8 h. The samples were prepared, and protein levels were detected as previously described.34,74 The source and dilution information for the primary antibodies used in western blot analysis are provided in the key resources table.

qPCR

BMDMs and BMDCs were harvested following a 6 h treatment with IM502 (10 μM) in the presence of Hepa1-6BL tumor cell conditional medium (TCM). Human THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages were treated with IM502 (10 μM, 6 h) in the presence of human HepG2 tumor cell conditional medium (TCM). Total RNA was extracted, and mRNA levels of genes were determined using SYBR Green I Master Mix on a Light Cycler 480 system (Roche Group, BSL, CH) as previously described.34 qPCR primer sequences were listed in the key resources table.

ALT and AST detection

Serum samples from mice bearing orthotopic liver tumors were collected on day 21 relative to tumor cell inoculation as described in the previous section of the method. The levels of Alanine Transaminase (ALT) and Aspartate Transaminase (AST) were assessed by determining the activity of ALT and AST using an activity assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Pharmacokinetic studies

Pharmacokinetic studies were performed in C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) bearing subcutaneous MC38 tumors. After a 12-h fast, mice received a single intravenous dose of IM502 (1.5 mg/kg). Blood (∼0.2 mL) and tumor tissue were collected at specified time points (5 min, 15 min, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 24, and 48 h post-dose; n = 3 mice per time point). Plasma was separated by centrifugation (4,000 rpm, 10 min). Tumor samples were weighed, homogenized in methanol, and centrifuged under the same conditions. Supernatants were stored at −80°C until analysis. IM502 concentrations in plasma and tumor homogenates were quantified by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Analyses were performed on an Agilent 1290 UPLC system coupled to an Agilent 6470B triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation used an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 30°C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min, with the following gradient: 0–1 min, 5% B; 1–6 min, 5% → 70% B; 6–6.1 min, 70% → 90% B; 6.1–7 min, 90% B; 7–7.1 min, 90% → 5% B; 7.1–11 min, 5% B. The injection volume was 5 μL. Detection was performed in negative electrospray ionization (ESI-) mode using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated with WinNonlin software (Certara).

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Tumor-bearing mice (6 mice per group) were treated with IM502 as described in the tumor model and treatment section of the methods. Tumor tissue single-cell suspensions were generated and stained as described above, and CD45.2+ cells were sorted on a FACS Aria III. Cellular suspensions containing around ten thousand cells for each sample were loaded on a 10x Genomics GemCode Single-cell instrument that generates single-cell Gel Bead-In-EMulsion (GEMs). Libraries were generated and sequenced from the cDNAs with Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Reagent Kits v3.1. As described in our previous study.34,74 Libraries were subsequently prepared and sequenced using a NovaSeq sequencer (Illumina). 10x Genomics Cell Ranger software (version 3.1.0) was used to convert raw BCL files to FASTQ files, alignment, and count quantification. Briefly, reads with low-quality barcodes and UMIs were filtered out and then mapped to the mouse reference genome (Ensembl release 106). The gene expression data for each sample were individually imported into Seurat version 2.2.1 for downstream analysis. Cells with less than 200 detected genes or >5% mitochondrial transcripts were excluded. Expression data were normalized with the NormalizeData function, using the “LogNormalize” approach, and further scaled by sequencing depth. Control and IM502-treated samples were corrected with batch effects before combined analysis using the “harmony” method in the Seurat package in R v3.6.0. Clustering of cells based on gene expression was performed on all cells from both conditions with default parameters (function FindClusters), as well as a cluster-specific marker analysis (FindAllMarkers function), using only genes that were expressed in at least 25% of cells for each cluster. Different cell types were assigned to clusters based on known gene markers highly expressed in the particular cell cluster and found as significant markers (FDR <0.05). For each defined cluster/cell type, individual differential gene expression was analyzed between control and IM502-treated cells using the FindMarkers function for genes expressed in at least 25% of cells for each cluster. For visualization, violin plots of gene expression were generated based on normalized gene expression data.

The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for each cell type was performed to identify significantly enriched functional pathways based on the differential gene list ranked by the log 2 FC between the IM502 treatment and the control using the clusterProfiler package with R software.

Activated regulons in each cell cluster were analyzed using pySCENIC version 3.7 (Single Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering) with a raw count matrix as input. The regulon activity for each cell was scored by AUCell. The differentially activated regulons in each subset were identified by the Wilcoxon test based on the DEGs between groups. The regulons with high AUCell score and their target genes were plotted using the R software.

The number and expression intensity of ligand-receptor pairs between cell types from scRNA-seq data were computed using the CellPhoneDB function to infer cell-cell interactions among the cell types.

TCR repertoire analysis

Along with the single-cell RNA sequencing, the same samples were submitted to TCR repertoire analysis, as previously described in our study.74 The Single Cell V(D)J Reagent Kit protocol produces V(D)J-enriched and 5′ gene expression Illumina-ready sequencing libraries. A library comprises standard Illumina paired-end constructs, which begin and end with P5 and P7. For V(D)J enriched libraries, Read 1 encodes the 16 bp 10x Barcode, 10 bp UMI, and 13 bp Switch Oligo, as well as the 5′ end of an enriched transcript. For 5′ gene expression libraries, Read 1 encodes the 16 bp 10x Barcode and 10 bp UMI. Due to Enzymatic Fragmentation, for both libraries, Read 2 encodes a random internal fragment of the corresponding insert. Sample index sequences are incorporated as the i7 index read. Chromium single-cell 5′ RNA-seq output was processed by Cell Ranger for V(D)J. Repertoire comparison analyses were visualized by the R package scRepertoire for TCR diversity analysis.75 Unique clonotypes indicate the total number of distinct TCR sequences detected. An increase suggests more T cell clones have been recruited or activated. Clonotype abundance distribution shows how many clones fall into different size categories (small, medium, large). A shift (clonotype number vs. abundance) indicates a change in the T cell population structure. Diversity indices (Shannon, Inverse Simpson) measure the richness and evenness of the repertoire, with very high diversity indicating a broad, polyclonal response; while lower diversity indicates a focused, oligoclonal expansion. V/J gene usage: Identifies if specific TCR gene segments are overrepresented, which can hint at a shared antigen target.

Metabolomics analysis

The metabonomic analysis was performed by AigenX Biosciences Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Six tumor samples from either the control or IM502-treated mouse groups were subjected to LC-MS analysis. The obtained LC-MS raw data were processed by the Compound Discoverer 3.1(CD) software (Nonlinear, Dynamics, Newcastle, UK) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, peak alignment, and normalization. Variable Importance of Projection (VIP) values obtained from the OPLS-DA model were used to rank the overall contribution of each variable to group discrimination. Unpaired t test was further used to verify whether the difference in metabolites between groups was significant. Differential metabolites were selected with VIP values greater than 1.0 and p-values less than 0.05 or |log2Foldchange| ≥1 and p ≤ 0.05.

Inverse virtual docking

Inverse virtual docking was employed to identify the potential targets of IM502 using AutoDock Vina software.76,77 Polar hydrogens were added to the IM502 structure with AutoDock Tools 1.4.5. The panel of protein targets was established by a search of crystallized structures in the Protein DataBank database (www.rcsb.org). The protein target database comprises 2765 proteins that have crystals with small molecule ligands. The protein structures were prepared with AutoDock Tools 1.4.5. In brief, water molecules were removed, and polar hydrogens were added.

Before the virtual screen, the ligands in the crystal proteins were removed. Then IM502 was redocked into 9279 binding pockets of 2756 proteins to reproduce its binding poses from the crystal structure. Molecular docking calculations were performed using AutoDock Vina software. The grids focused on target proteins were built by referring to the binding mode of crystallized ligands in the PDB database. The target proteins in the library were ranked based on the score functions best able to predict IM502 targets, including docking score, bump score, polar score, surface similarity, and shape Tanimoto. The top-scoring 20 protein targets were selected as potential targets of IM502 and subjected to further analysis to assess their interaction. The potential targets were enriched for Gene Ontology enrichment analysis.

Molecular docking studies were performed to predict the binding modes of IM502 and the reference PI3Kγ inhibitor IPI549 within the ATP-binding pocket of human PI3Kγ. The crystal structure of PI3Kγ in complex with IPI-549 (PDB ID: 6XRL) was obtained from the Protein DataBank according to the study by Drew et al.24 The protein structure was prepared as above. The original co-crystallized IPI549 ligand was extracted to serve as a positive control for docking validation. The three-dimensional structures of IM502 and IPI549 were sketched and energy-minimized. Docking simulations were carried out using AutoDock Vina. The grid box was centered on the centroid of the original IPI-549 ligand in the ATP-binding site. The dimensions of the grid box were set to 20 × 20 × 20 Å to encompass the entire binding pocket. The Lamarkian genetic algorithm was used to search for conformation. During the docking phase, ligands were thought to be flexible, and the protein was considered to be rigid. Docking scores were calculated by the default scoring function. The conformations of the highest binding energy (most negative) were selected for the binding model analysis of all ligands. To ensure a fair and technically consistent comparison between the two compounds, an identical docking protocol was applied to both IM502 and IPI549. The docking procedure for IPI-549 was first validated by comparing its top-scoring docking pose with its native conformation from the 6XRL crystal structure.24 The resulting docking poses were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL to identify specific protein-ligand interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts.

Silencing of PI3Kγ

The sequences of siRNA targeting murine PI3Kγ (gene Pik3cg) (sense, 5′- AUA UCA AUG CCU CUG AUC UUGTT-3′; antisense, 5′- CAA GAU CAG AGG CAU UGA UAUTT-3′), negative control-siRNA (sense, 5′- GCU AAU CAU UCG UAC UAU GCUTT-3′; antisense, 5′- AGC AUA GUA CGA AUG AUU AGCTT-3′) were purchased from Jima (Shanghai, China). RAW264.7 macrophages were transfected with the siRNA utilizing Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Invitrogen, US) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

In vitro PI3K kinase assay

As previously described,78 purified PI3K class I lipid kinases, including p110α/p85α, p110β/p85α, p110γ, and p110δ/p85α (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), were incubated with IM502 at the indicated concentrations in the figure for 30 min at room temperature. The activity of PI3K was assessed using a PI3-Kinase Activity ELISA kit (K-1000s, Echelon, Salt Lake City, UT, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After the PI3-Kinase reactions were completed, the reaction products were incubated with a PI(3,4,5)P3 detector protein and added to the PI (3,4,5)P3-coated microplate for competitive binding. A peroxidase-linked secondary detector was used to detect the binding of the PI(3,4,5)P3 primary detector to the plate. The colorimetric signal is inversely correlated to the amount of PI(3,4,5)P3 produced by PI3-Kinase. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a Multilabel Reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The activity of compounds was assessed by normalizing the absorbance value of compounds to that of the control. The IC502 was calculated using non-linear regression with an equation of dose-response inhibition in GraphPad software.

Quantification and statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software was used for statistical analysis. The unpaired t test was used for two-group comparisons, and the One-Way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. In other experiments, statistical differences were calculated by Unpaired t test or by permutation test, and the P-values were corrected with Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment. Differences between groups were statistically significant where the P-value was less than 0.05 (p < 0.05).
