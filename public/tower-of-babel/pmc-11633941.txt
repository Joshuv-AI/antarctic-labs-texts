Integrated electronic health record tools to access real-world data in oncology research
Authors: Michelle Casagni, Nicole Llewellyn, Maeve Kokolus, Miranda Chan, Robert Dingwell, Selina Chow, Nancy Campbell, Cassandra Elrahi, Steven Piantadosi, Andre Quina
Published: 2024

Introduction

Despite the widespread adoption of electronic health records (EHRs), the full potential of real-world data (RWD)—data captured from routine clinical practice—remains unrealized.1 RWD holds significant promise for enhancing clinical decision-making, accelerating the development of new therapies, and personalizing patient care.2,3 However, significant barriers such as unstructured data, isolated data silos, and incompatible systems limit the use of RWD.2 Additionally, electronic data capture (EDC) systems, used to systematically collect data on clinical trial participants, remain disconnected from EHRs, leading to costly and time-consuming manual data entry.4 These challenges highlight the need for solutions that bridge the gap between EHRs and EDCs, facilitating the integration of RWD into clinical research.

The Integrating Clinical Trials and Real-World Endpoints (ICAREdata) initiative sought to address these challenges by capturing standardized point-of-care data and comparing it with gold-standard EDC data to determine if the quality is high enough to use in research.5 By structuring key research data elements in EHRs using the minimal Common Oncology Data Elements (mCODE) data model,6 the ICAREdata method enables the extraction and transmission of health data in a standardized format, aiming to facilitate RWD for research. mCODE, an open-source set of structured oncology data elements, is part of the Health Level Seven International (HL7) Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) standard, which supports the electronic exchange of health data between systems.7 A related pilot study demonstrated the feasibility of integrating data capture tools for mCODE-structured data into EHRs for use in clinical care.8 The ICAREdata initiative sought to demonstrate the feasibility of using similar tools for research.

In this project, we captured essential clinical trial data elements, cancer disease status (CDS) and treatment plan change (TPC), from EHR systems across 10 clinical sites, including both large academic institutions and community cancer centers, spanning 15 trials. We compared this EHR data with corresponding data from the trials’ EDC system. An analysis comparing EHR and EDC data showed an overall concordance rate of 79% for TPC and 34% for CDS, increasing to 87% for CDS when disease evaluation was captured in both systems.4 This EHR-EDC agreement underscores the potential of using structured EHR data in clinical research. George et al. describe the clinical feasibility of the ICAREdata method as applied to multicenter cancer clinical trials in more detail.4

Beyond demonstrating clinical feasibility, the ICAREdata project provided valuable insights into the complexities of implementing a health information technology (IT) system across multiple institutions. Taking into account the complex relationships between people, processes, and technology, as described in a Socio-technical Model for Health IT,9 this report details and assesses the ICAREdata technical implementation and provides recommendations to benefit future projects with similar goals.

Materials and Methods

ICAREdata was launched in 2018 through a partnership between The Alliance Data Innovation Lab (Alliance), part of the National Cancer Institute’s National Clinical Trials Network (NCTN), and The MITRE Corporation (MITRE), a not-for-profit with expertise in engineering and data standards. The initiative sought to validate a method of low-burden, standardized collection of requisite oncology outcome data directly within the EHR and compare the data to that captured in the EDC to determine if it produced research quality data. We partnered with a subset of NCTN sites to activate 1 or more of the 15 institutional review board-approved ICAREdata-associated Alliance trials. Patients could consent to participate in ICAREdata when they consented to these Alliance trials.

Clinical sites were selected based on their existing involvement in Alliance trials and their capability to integrate the ICAREdata tools. This selection included large academic centers and smaller community clinics, ensuring a wide range of data capture environments. Staff at the clinical sites collected data for the consented patients at the point of care, extracted it from the EHR, and transmitted it to a secure cloud storage platform using the ICAREdata technical implementation. The ICAREdata team assessed data for completeness and conducted a concordance analysis, comparing EHR and EDC data. The ICAREdata component of these trials was for data validation purposes and did not affect the trial protocols’ activities.

In addition to the concordance analysis reported on by George et al., we assessed the ICAREdata technical implementation utilizing Sittig and Singh’s Socio-technical Model.4,9 This model addresses the complex nature of health IT systems by evaluating their effectiveness across 8 inter-related social and technical dimensions. By applying this model, we systematically identified challenges within this multi-institutional project and developed recommendations to assist future implementers.

ICAREdata technical approach

We began by identifying the data elements clinicians would collect. We collaborated with a team of oncologists and researchers to identify requisite outcome data not previously available in an EHR as discrete standardized data elements. Leveraging the expertise of clinical trial investigators, we developed the ICAREdata questions: CDS and TPC shown in Figure 1. The specific data elements and response options for CDS were then mapped to mCODE. TPC was represented with an extension on the FHIR Resource CarePlan10 as it was a research question specific to ICAREdata and not part of mCODE. The questions were designed to apply to solid tumor cancers, so the project included trials in breast, gastrointestinal/colorectal, genitourinary, and prostate cancers.

ICAREdata question summary.

We designed an automated, open-source interface for data capture, extraction, and transmission, ensuring it was standards-based and vendor-agnostic for broad compatibility. The technical implementation involved capturing structured data using the ICAREdata questions in a patient’s EHR, extracting the data, formatting the data into mCODE and FHIR standards, and transmitting the data to the ICAREdata Infrastructure—a secure cloud storage platform—via an automated FHIR interface to support analysis.

Although the ICAREdata technical implementation was EHR agnostic, site implementers needed to consider vendor- and site-specific requirements for how to integrate data capture into their clinical practice workflows. For this project, we partnered with Epic, an EHR software vendor serving over 305 million patients, including large coverage at the participating Alliance member sites.11Figure 2 shows a high-level overview of the ICAREdata technical implementation; each component of the implementation is described in subsequent sections.

ICAREdata questions and data capture

The ICAREdata CDS question was implemented using Epic SmartForms attached to the problem list, while the TPC question was implemented with Epic SmartPhrases used in the clinical note. The CDS SmartForm allowed clinicians or research staff to enter the date of assessment, disease status, and reason for disease status directly in the problem list. The TPC SmartPhrase inserted the TPC question into the clinical note where clinicians or research staff could select the appropriate treatment change. The data for these questions were stored as Smart Data Elements, allowing for extraction using queries or application programming interfaces (APIs).

Epic analysts at participating clinical sites configured their Epic instance with the ICAREdata CDS SmartForm and TPC SmartPhrase, allowing clinicians to answer these questions within the Epic EHR’s user interface. We trained participating clinicians with a live demonstration on how to use these tools to complete the ICAREdata questions in the 2 areas of the EHR encounter. Clinicians were asked to answer the questions during each patient visit following informed consent.

Data extraction and data transmission

Because FHIR APIs for mCODE were unavailable when the ICAREdata project began, we developed the mCODE Extraction Framework,12 an open-source tool to handle data formatting and transmission. Leveraging the mCODE Extraction Framework, we created the ICAREdata Extraction Client13 to support data extraction and transmission of mCODE-formatted data for participating clinical sites. The extraction client parses data source files that store the relevant data elements, formats the data into mCODE-based FHIR profiles, and transmits the formatted data to the ICAREdata Infrastructure using FHIR Messaging.14 The extraction client was an interim capability developed to establish mCODE- and FHIR-based transmission of data ahead of natively exposed interfaces from EHR vendors.

IT staff at participating clinical sites downloaded and installed the open-source ICAREdata Extraction Client on a host machine within their network. Site Epic analysts generated queries to extract the required mCODE data elements from their Epic instance and store them as comma-separated text files (CSV files). In turn, these CSV files were used as data sources for the extraction client. IT staff scheduled automated jobs to run the queries and the extraction client, ensuring monthly data transmission to the ICAREdata Infrastructure.

Secure data storage and analysis

The ICAREdata Infrastructure utilized the security features of Amazon Web Services (AWS) to create a secure cloud storage platform that could protect identifiable health information. The AWS GovCloud-based environment provided industry-standard, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant protections for data from clinical sites.

Using the extraction client, clinical sites transmitted data to the ICAREdata Infrastructure. The infrastructure had services to handle the data upon receipt, including one that generated a subset of data that was used to perform a concordance analysis comparing EHR data submitted by sites to that collected in the EDC.

Results

To assess the challenges encountered in the ICAREdata technical implementations across clinical sites, we conducted a systematic review using the 8 dimensions in Sittig and Singh’s socio-technical model: (1) hardware and software; (2) clinical content; (3) human computer interface; (4) people; (5) workflow and communication; (6) organizational policies and procedures; (7) external rules, regulations, and pressures; (8) system measurement and monitoring.9 This model demonstrates that the safety and efficacy of health IT systems depend on the complex interactions between each of these 8 concepts. Implementers of health IT systems must have a comprehensive understanding of all the factors in a healthcare setting that could impact how the system is used. Hardware and software

Health systems sharing IT resources and infrastructures, such as EHR instances, faced unique challenges. For example, these health systems needed to run separate queries for each site’s data, adding to the complexity of the ICAREdata installation. In some cases, clinical staff at one site were ready to begin data collection, while the technical implementation was still in process at the partner facility. This discrepancy in readiness led to delays and highlighted the complexities of coordinating technical implementations across multiple sites with shared resources. When possible, future initiatives should explore the nuances of an organization’s IT infrastructure to effectively navigate and adapt to any complexities during integration.

Clinical content

Prior to moving the ICAREdata implementation to production, clinical sites conducted test submissions of synthetic data to an ICAREdata test environment. This testing phase allowed us to identify and fix CSV file and data formatting errors. However, some data quality control issues were only detected with real patient data. For example, production data sometimes lacked a required effective date or had multiple codes in a field.

Additionally, we observed discrepancies when the numbers of TPC data encounters exceeded those with CDS data. This was due to sites’ queries pulling the CDS data from the table storing only the most recent entry and not including the historical data table. Therefore, if multiple CDS entries occurred prior to the initial data submission or between data submissions, only the most recent CDS value was transmitted, leading to loss of historical CDS values. When feasible, implementers should test with large quantities of synthetic data to capture as many real-world scenarios as possible.

Human computer interface

Users found accessing the ICAREdata tools within the EHR, specifically CDS from the problem list, burdensome, leading to incomplete data. Some clinical sites attempted to streamline this by copying CDS data into the clinical note. However, this led to updates being made within the clinical note instead of the intended form during subsequent visits, causing data discrepancies as the data were not properly stored and updated. Involving users in the interface design could have mitigated these challenges by improving data entry efficiency and minimizing the need to switch between different EHR sections.

People

Fostering collaboration across diverse teams within clinical organizations was essential for successful implementation. The ICAREdata team established a structured framework for interactions which helped to facilitate this coordination among legal, clinical research, IT, and analytic teams. We initiated each partnership with an introductory meeting to provide a comprehensive project overview and outline clear expectations for participation. This was followed by detailed sessions for the IT staff and training for clinicians to ensure successful adoption of new tools. As sites progressed with the technical implementation, the ICAREdata team maintained regular communication to monitor progress and provide support which was crucial for sustaining project momentum. Additionally, having a dedicated program manager or project champion at a site improved coordination and motivation, leading to faster implementation times.

Workflow and communication

Integrating ICAREdata questions into existing EHR workflows posed a significant challenge. Clinicians needed to incorporate answering new questions during each ICAREdata patient visit. In some cases, challenges in adopting a new workflow for a subset of patients resulted in missed data collection at the time of visit necessitating research nurses to review and update notes post-visit. This increased the workload for both research staff and clinicians. Clinical sites that utilized reminders or flags for ICAREdata patients’ data collection achieved more consistent data capture.

Organizational policies and procedures

Organizational policies and IT requirements impacted the timeline for technical implementation at clinical sites. For example, clinical sites needed to prioritize the ICAREdata implementation within their queue of IT projects, often ranking it lower than projects with more urgent needs, before the initiative could be resourced and scheduled. Additionally, many clinical sites required a risk assessment on the technical implementation. When possible, making progress on implementation steps in parallel, such as starting the IT risk assessment while waiting for full resourcing, can prevent project delays.

To accommodate differences in organizational policies across the multi-institutional project, we developed a flexible process that could be tailored according to site requirements. Each site was given a standard checklist of tasks with suggested staff roles, dependencies, and level-of-effort estimates that could be customized to meet the needs of an organization. This structured but tailored approach provided sites with the necessary project details while remaining flexible to their unique needs.

External rules, regulations, and pressures

Information security protocols at participating clinical sites required a risk assessment on the ICAREdata Extraction Client and Infrastructure. To streamline this process, the ICAREdata team provided sites with answers to frequently asked questions related to the privacy and security of ICAREdata.

The ICAREdata Infrastructure was obligated to meet privacy and security regulations, ensuring compliance with HIPAA and any additional site-specific requirements (eg, Type 2 Service Organization Control certification, API penetration testing). Site-specific requirements sometimes caused delays in a site moving tools to production. In these cases, advising sites to proceed with testing while waiting for completion of security prerequisites, maintained project momentum.

System measurement and monitoring

ICAREdata represents a prototype implementation for capturing and transmitting standardized point of care data for clinical research. As it is not a permanent IT system nor essential to patient care, the sites did not consistently monitor the status of the query and data transmission jobs. We requested sites transmit data monthly to allow us to track submission progress, assess data completeness, and begin testing of the concordance algorithms. This allowed us to identify and work with sites to fix issues along the way, such as missing patient encounters, ensuring the accuracy of data for the concordance analysis.

Discussion

The ICAREdata technical implementation, leveraging mCODE, allowed participating clinical sites to use uniform queries and send data to a single storage location, creating a consistent data set aligned with clinical trial endpoint requirements for analysis. The project demonstrated that EHR data can be structured and transmitted, making progress towards the overall goal of leveraging RWD in the EHR to contribute to oncology clinical research. While innovative and feasible, implementations of multi-institution initiatives such as the ICAREdata project face challenges across the 8 socio-technical dimensions. The resulting lessons learned from the ICAREdata project may accelerate the success of similar efforts to leverage standards-based RWD capture and transmission for clinical research. Though the project generated promising results, there were some limitations. First, the reliance on a single EHR vendor may limit the generalizability of these findings to health systems using other platforms. Second, variations in data quality across clinical sites presents challenges for multi-institutional scalability which future implementations will need to address.

Maximizing the potential of RWD in oncology research will require large-scale adoption of standardized oncology data elements like mCODE and data collection tools, like those used in the ICAREdata project. Compared to other data extraction techniques, such as manual data abstraction or proprietary EHR tools, employing the mCODE standard enhances interoperability and data consistency across multiple healthcare settings, facilitating research. Adoption at this scale requires buy-in from health systems and EHR vendors. By integrating standardized data elements and data collection and extraction tools directly into the EHR, many challenges faced during this early proof of concept would be overcome. For instance, as EHR vendors implement standards-based interfaces and mCODE-compliant data collection, the need for site-specific configuration of the ICAREdata question collection and extraction framework would be eliminated, significantly streamlining the process.

While the ICAREdata project focused on oncology research, the method has the potential to be used in other applications. By streamlining data collection and reducing redundant entries, it may improve the quality and consistency of patient information. Through promoting interoperability, it may enable data sharing across multiple institutions. On a large scale and beyond oncology, the ICAREdata method has broad applications in chronic disease research, rare disease studies, and population health monitoring. By bringing the ICAREdata method to other areas of healthcare, we can drive improvements in clinical trials and patient care outcomes in various medical disciplines. In addition, it holds the potential to leverage EHR data in real time enabling medical providers to make timely informed decisions improving patient care and outcomes.

Conclusion

The ICAREdata project developed a method to capture oncology-related outcomes in EHRs using discrete data elements of mCODE and FHIR, an essential capability for oncology not previously supported by EHRs.15 This project demonstrates the value of standards-based EHR data, aligned across clinical and research data requirements, integrated within routine care clinical workflows, and accessible via standard APIs to inform oncology research. The ICAREdata implementation utilizes the HL7 FHIR standard which provides a unified set of resource definitions and a common API to promote system interoperability. The ICAREdata Extraction Client uses the FHIR Resource definitions and mCODE to represent data extracted from EHR systems and sends data to the infrastructure using FHIR Messaging. We confirmed the value and technical feasibility of data standards for structured data capture, extraction, and transmission of point-of-care data, demonstrating how data standards can advance interoperability. We identified the socio-technical challenges, primarily from adapting existing technical infrastructure to the ICAREdata project. As standard workflows and data standards, such as FHIR and mCODE, are adopted more broadly, many of these challenges will be mitigated.

The ICAREdata project envisions a future where patients have expanded access to clinical trials and researchers have access to high-quality, standardized RWD driving advances in oncology treatment and improving patient care. This project represents a step toward modernizing data infrastructures to enable a better framework for the future.
