Tarikul Islam Tamiti, Anomadarshi Barua

Abstract
. Hearables are wearable computers that are worn on the ear. Bone conduction microphones (BCMs) are used with air conduction microphones (ACMs) in hearables as a supporting modality for multimodal speech enhancement (SE) in noisy conditions. However, existing works don’t consider the following practical aspects for low-power implementations on hearables: (i) They do not explore how lowering the sampling frequencies and bit resolutions in analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) of hearables jointly impact low-power processing and multimodal SE in terms of speech quality and intelligibility. (ii) They don’t discuss how GAN-like audio quality can be achieved without using actual GAN discriminators. And (iii) They don’t process signals from ACMs/BCMs at sub-Nyquist sampling rate because, in their frameworks, they lack a wideband reconstruction methodology from their narrowband parts. We propose SUBARU ( Sub -Nyquist A udio R esolution U psampling), which achieves the following: SUBARU (i) intentionally uses sub-Nyquist sampling and low bit resolution in ADCs, achieving a 3.31x reduction in power consumption; (ii) introduces novel multi-scale and multi-period virtual discriminators, which achieve GAN-like audio quality without using GANs’ adversarial training; and (iii) achieves streaming operations on mobile platforms and SE in in-the-wild noisy conditions with an inference time of 1.74ms and a memory footprint of less than 13.77MB.



1.
Introduction

A hearable device is a wearable computer that is worn on the ear. In addition to offering functions for listening to speech and music, a hearable device can be utilized as a voice assistant by connecting it to a smartphone. The continuous advancement of wearable technologies and hearable devices has significantly improved human-machine interfaces, enabling seamless audio capture and communication. Initially popularized through earbuds and headphones, the hearables market has expanded into diverse applications, including health monitoring, augmented reality (AR), and voice assistance. According to recent analyses, the hearables sector is projected to surpass USD 90 billion by 2030
(
Research 2024a
)
, with a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) exceeding 15%
(
Research 2024b
)
.

Traditionally, air conduction microphones (ACMs) are commonly found on hearables that convert air pressure into an electrical signal during music and speech activities. However, ACMs can easily pick up background noise, which can degrade speech quality. To solve this problem, speech enhancement (SE) has been applied significantly over the years
(
Berouti et al. 1979
;
Chen and Zhang 2016
;
Kamath et al. 2002
;
Reddy and Raj 2007
)
in terms of source separation and denoising. Moreover, multimodal methods, such as lip movement captured
by cameras
(
Ephrat et al. 2018
;
Afouras et al. 2018
)
or throat vibrations captured by millimeter-wave radar
(
Ozturk et al. 2023
)
and other research
(
He et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Tagliasacchi et al. 2020
)
have been proposed. Recently, bone conduction microphones (BCMs) and accelerometers are proposed with ACMs for multimodal SE in noisy conditions
(
Sui et al. 2024
;
Tagliasacchi et al. 2020
;
He et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
. BCMs are commonly used as an accessorial enhancer for ACMs. BCMs can typically be made of vibration sensors and accelerometers. BCMs are mounted on hearables and operate synchronously with the ACMs but at a lower sampling frequency. The BCMs capture the local bone vibrations induced by the voice of the speaker, while being relatively insensitive to external sources. Therefore, it can be used as a conditioning signal to enhance ACM’s signals and suppress noise.

There are a few works
(

Tagliasacchi et al. 2020
;
He et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
that use BCMs and accelerometers as a supporting modality to enhance audio in hearables in noisy conditions. They are typically multimodal SE algorithms and do not consider the following practical aspects of low-power, low-memory, and real-time applications on hearables:

•

Lowering sampling frequency and bit resolution:
Analog audio or vibration signals from ACMs and BCMs are first sampled and digitized at Nyquist rates (greater than 16 kHz) and over 12-bit resolutions by the analog-to-digital converter (ADC). After sampling, an audio codec inside the hearables compresses the data to reduce the bitrate, saving transmission energy and bandwidth. Later, multimodal SE algorithms are applied on the decompressed data on mobile platforms (cell phones) connected to hearables (see Fig.
1
).
However, they do not explore how lowering the sampling frequency and bit resolutions in ADCs of hearables jointly impact low-power processing and multimodal SE in terms of speech quality and intelligibility.

•

Model complexity:
State-of-the-art (SOTA) multimodal SE algorithms use generative adversarial networks (GANs) over U-Nets because of GANs’ promising performance over U-Nets.
However, GANs often exhibit high complexity with numerous associated parameters (on the order of hundreds of millions), thereby imposing significant constraints on efficiency in resource-constrained mobile platforms connected to hearables.

•

Lacking in multimodal SE methods:
SOTA multimodal SE methods
(
Ma et al. 2023
;
Han et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
He et al. 2023
)

use narrowband signals from BCMs (i.e., sampling frequency 4 kHz or lower) and full wideband (i.e., sampling frequency 16 kHz or greater) audio signals from ACMs.
Therefore, they do not process audio at sub-Nyquist sampling rate and they lack methods to generate wideband from the narrowband wave in their frameworks.
The method to generate wideband signals from the narrowband wave is known as bandwidth extension (BWE)
(
Birnbaum et al. 2019
)
.

In this paper, we aim to enable multimodal SE that adapts effectively to the unique low-power features by reducing the sampling frequency to the sub-Nyquist range with lower bit resolutions in hearables. We name our methodology SUBARU (

SUBARU also leverages bandwidth extension with multimodal SE jointly to recover missing high-frequency parts from the sub-Nyquist sampled and lower bit-resolution audio in noisy conditions, restoring audio quality and intelligibility (see Fig.
1
). By integrating advanced signal processing algorithms and neural network designs, SUBARU provides users with reliable communication while ensuring real-time performance by extending the battery life of hearables. SUBARU is designed to meet the high demands of smart hearables in low-power and real-world usage through the following four key design elements:

First
, SUBARU is designed to be deployed in a split architecture, where hearables run sub-Nyquist sampling in low bit resolution on signals from both ACMs and BCMs, and mobile platforms connected to hearables run the joint BWE and multimodal SE algorithms to reconstruct the high-resolution noise-free audio in noisy conditions. The low latency of SUBARU while doing joint BWE and multimodal SE will enable streaming enhancement that will make SUBARU deployable on mobile platforms.

Second
, GANs can reconstruct better perceptual audio through adversarial loss functions, and U-Nets generally have a lower memory footprint and faster convergence while training. To get the best out of these two domains, SUBARU adopts multi-scale and multi-period loss functions in its U-Net architecture to simulate the behavior of multi-scale discriminators
(
Kumar et al. 2019
)
and multi-period discriminators
(
Kong et al. 2020
)
of GANs. The multi-scale and multi-period loss functions behave like virtual discriminators in SUBARU without having actual GAN-like discriminator networks. Therefore, SUBARU achieves similar perceptual quality to GANs with smaller parameters (saving tens of millions) and stable training, enabling efficient generation in mobile platforms with higher perceptual audio.

Third
, Waveform-based SE methods process directly on raw waveforms, and spectrum-based SE methods process directly on spectrograms. Waveform-based methods are performed at the sample-point level, leading to a simpler architecture but relatively lower generation efficiency when compared to spectrum-based methods with frame-level operations. To get the benefits from both methods, SUBARU adopts a hybrid architecture by merging both waveform-based and spectrum-based methods, enabling joint training in both spectrum and waveform domains. This joint training also improves both time and spectral domain features during audio reconstruction.

Fourth
, As recent research
(
Yin et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2025
)
highlights the importance of phase reconstruction for improved perceptual quality, generating a clean phase is critical for robust audio reconstruction from sub-Nyquist sampled signals in noisy conditions for hearables. The joint training feature of SUBARU in both spectrum and waveform domains enables the use of instantaneous phase and group delay anti-wrapping losses to reconstruct clean phases in noisy conditions. This also helps to provide GAN-like perceptual quality in U-Net-based SUBARU, keeping the model size small and efficient for mobile platforms.

This paper implements SUBARU focusing on the above four design elements, and extensive experiments are conducted to evaluate its performance in real-time settings. The summary of the evaluations is written below:

•

SUBARU has been extensively evaluated against ten baselines. Five of them are GAN-based (i.e., SEANet, AERO, HiFi++, EBEN, and NVSR), one is diffusion-based (i.e., NU-Wave), and four are U-Net-based models (i.e., TFiLM, AFiLM, ATS-UNet, and VibVoice). SUBARU is evaluated using six evaluation metrics: LSD, VISQOL, NISQA-MOS, SI-SDR, PESQ, and STOI. The full form of the abbreviations is given in Section

.

•

By reducing the sampling frequency and bit resolution from {24 kHz, 12-bit} to {4 kHz, 8-bit}, SUBARU achieves a 3.31x reduction in power consumption, ideally increasing the battery life by

•

SUBARU is evaluated on both speech and music datasets and is superior to the SOTA U-Net models. Moreover, SUBARU achieves better performance than SOTA GANs under noisy conditions with the lowest inference time (i.e., 1.74 ms on a desktop with GPUs). Specifically, SUBARU needs 3.61x less inference time compared to HiFi++ and AERO (the two best-performing models). Thanks to the multi-scale and multi-period loss functions.

2.
RELATED WORK

SUBARU involves sub-Nyquist sampling of audio signals on edge or hearable platforms, along with joint BWE and SE of the sub-sampled audio in noisy conditions on smartphones or mobile platforms. As SUBARU can run both single-modal (i.e., microphone only with variable noise) and multimodal (i.e., microphone with accelerometer or vibration sensor with variable noise) SE,
we divide our discussion on related work into the following three sections: (i) Sub-Nyquist sampling of audio signals, (ii) Bandwidth extension, and (iii) Multimodal SE.

2.1.
Sub-Nyquist sampling and challenges with compressed sensing

Sub-Nyquist sampling is the process of sampling a signal below its Nyquist rate—that is, at a rate lower than twice the highest frequency component in the signal. A significant amount of work
(
Donoho 2006
;
Figueiredo et al. 2010
)
shows that compressed sensing (CS) can possibly reconstruct intelligible audio even when the sampling frequency is much less than the Nyquist-Shannon theorem. To ease the understanding, we calculate the lowest practical sampling frequency for CS of an 8 kHz-bandwidth audio signal for different signal sparsity, using Eqn.
1
.

(1)

\displaystyle f_{s}\geq C\cdot\kappa\cdot log_{e}\dfrac{N}{\kappa}\cdot\dfrac{1}{T}

Where N is the length of the original samples in the Nyquist rate,
\kappa
is the level of sparsity, C is a constant typically between 2 – 5 depending on algorithms and noise, and
f_{s}
is the sampling frequency required by CS. Therefore, for N = 16000 (original Nyquist sampling rate of 16 kHz), T = 1s, and C = 4, the minimum required sampling frequencies for CS for different sparsity audio signals are shown below in Table
1
.

Therefore, it will not help to reduce power consumption in hearables.

2.2.
Low-power and low-memory bandwidth extension on hearables

Bandwidth extension (BWE)
(
Su et al. 2021
)
, also known as audio super resolution, aims to increase the sampling rate and employs the low-frequency components of the low-resolution signal to predict and generate the high-frequency components of the audio signal. Therefore, BWE is an ideal fit for audio reconstruction from sub-Nyquist sampling. Recently, neural networks become the SOTA solutions for BWE, ranging from pure feedforward networks
(
Li and Lee 2015
;
Kuleshov et al. 2017
;
Gupta et al. 2019
;
Kuleshov et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Su et al. 2021
;
Han and Lee 2022
;
Lagrange and Gontier 2020
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Kumar et al. 2020
;
Eskimez and Koishida 2019
;
Li and Lee 2015
)
to generative solutions
(
Moliner and Välimäki 2022
;
Moliner et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Moliner et al. 2024a
;
Moliner et al. 2024b
)
. However, due to the large memory and power requirements, most of them are not directly suitable for hearables. To solve this problem, Li et al. proposed ATS-UNet
(
Li et al. 2022
)
, which is built upon the TFiLM
(
Birnbaum et al. 2019
)
backbone but replaces the TFiLM block with the audio temporal shift module (ATSM), resulting in a smaller footprint by compromising the audio quality. To improve audio quality, the model size needs to be increased. Therefore, recent work
(
Sui et al. 2024
)
proposes deploying BWE algorithms not on hearables but on mobile platforms, as mobile platforms have higher hardware capabilities than hearables. The model proposed in
(
Sui et al. 2024
)
is named TRAMBA (accepted in IMWUT, 2025).

TRAMBA is based on U-Net and uses scale-only attention-based feature-wise linear modulation (SAFiLM) block, which is a modified version of the attention-based FiLM
(
Rakotonirina 2021
)
. TRAMBA achieves a smaller model size without sacrificing performance. However, TRAMBA has the following limitations:

•

It operates on raw waveform and performs at the sample-point level, leading to relatively lower generation efficiency when compared to spectrum-based methods with frame-level operations
(
Mandel et al. 2023
)
.

•

It does not consider clean phase reconstruction in noisy conditions while reconstructing high-resolution audio from a low-resolution signal, sampled at a sub-Nyquist sampling frequency. As recent research highlights the importance of phase reconstruction for improved perceptual quality
(
Yin et al. 2020
)
, generating a clean phase is critical for robust BWE and SE in noisy conditions for hearables.

•

Last and most importantly, TRAMBA and no other SOTA work consider the effect of different bit resolutions and quantization noise during sub-Nyquist sampling in their BWE algorithms (see Section
3.2
for details).

GANs have been used for directly (i.e., SEANet
(
Tagliasacchi et al. 2020
)
, AERO
(
Mandel et al. 2023
)
, EBEN
(
Hauret et al. 2023
)
) reconstructing high-resolution signals and also as vocoders for indirectly (i.e., NVSR
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
) enhancing the reconstructed audio in BWE tasks. Moreover, diffusion probabilistic models
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
have been effectively utilized in speech BWE (i.e., NU-Wave
(
Lee and Han 2021
)
, NU-Wave 2
(
Han and Lee 2022
)
) by conditioning the noise predictor with narrowband waveforms with remarkably high perceptual quality. While these GANs and diffusion-based BWE methods have demonstrated promising performance, they still require numerous time steps, a.k.a. latency, in the reverse process for waveform reconstruction (on the order of hundreds of milliseconds) and often exhibit high complexity with numerous associated parameters (on the order of hundreds of millions), thereby imposing significant constraints on generation efficiency in low-power and low-memory resource-constrained systems like hearables. Moreover, they have not been properly explored for joint BWE and multimodal SE in hearables for noisy conditions. The comparison between our proposed SUBARU and these GANs and diffusion models is presented in Section

2.3.
Joint BWE and multimodal SE on hearables

BCMs are commonly used as an accessorial enhancer for ACMs. BCMs can typically consist of two sensors: vibration sensors and accelerometers. SEANet
(
Tagliasacchi et al. 2020
)
, a GAN model, uses accelerometers only for multimodal SE, without resorting to explicit joint BWE with SE. Therefore, SEANet does not work at the sub-Nyquist sampling rate. HiFi++
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
, a GAN framework, is proposed for joint BWE and SE of ACMs, but has not been adapted to the multimodal domain (i.e., works with audio signal only). ClearSpeech
(
Ma et al. 2023
)
leverages the different acoustic properties captured by in-ear and out-ear microphones to enhance ACM’s signals, but does not consider BWE, hence, it does not work at the sub-Nyquist sampling rate. EarSpeech
(
Han et al. 2024
)
enhances airborne speech by analyzing the cross-channel correlation between in-ear and airborne signals. VibVoice
(
He et al. 2023
)
employs bone-conducted vibrations captured through BCMs in headphones, showing potential for environments with heavy background noise. ClearBuds
(
Gandhi et al. 2022
)
uses a binaural microphone array formed by left and right earbuds for real-time SE.

All the above models have either one or multiple of the following limitations for which they are not suitable for our low-power hearables:
(i)
The joint BWE and SE algorithms are typically evaluated for single modality (i.e., audio only) and have not extended to multimodality.
(ii)
The multimodal SE algorithms use narrowband signals from BCMs (i.e., sampling frequency 4 kHz or greater) and full wideband (i.e., sampling frequency 16 kHz or greater) audio signals from ACMs. Therefore, they cannot handle audio at a sub-Nyquist sampling rate because of the absence of the BWE algorithm in their frameworks. And,
(iii
) These methods are not designed to be deployed on split architecture, where hearables run only sub-Nyquist sampling in both ACMs and BCMs, and mobile platforms run the BWE and multimodal SE algorithms. Their inference time/latency is not evaluated in real-world noisy conditions with a joint BWE and multimodal SE setup, which limits their generalizability to adapt to a split architecture. PRELIMINARY

3.1.
Power at different sub-Nyquist sampling frequencies and bit resolutions

The power consumption of ADCs in hearables increases with higher sampling rates and higher resolutions, following a simplified ideal equation:
P=k\cdot f_{s}\cdot 2^{N}
, where
P
is the consumed power,
k
is a proportionality constant,
N
is the bit resolution, and
f_{s}
is the sampling frequency of ADCs. Traditional Nyquist sampling requires ADCs to operate at high sampling frequencies (i.e.,
f_{s}
= 16 kHz) to accurately capture wideband (i.e., 8 kHz bandwidth) audio signals in hearables. However, sub-Nyquist sampling offers a powerful approach to reducing energy consumption in low-power applications like hearables.

To support this claim, we conduct experiments with an ACM (i.e., part
\#
B&K Type 4192
(
Brüel & Kjær Sound & Vibration Measurement A/S 2002
)
) and an in-built ADC of NRF52840
(
Semiconductor 2018
)
. We get a relative power savings of 2.45x between {16 kHz, 12-bits} vs {4 kHz, 8 bits} computations. The power savings will be even greater for high-fidelity hearables, which use 22 kHz sampling and 24-bit resolutions for music.

Please note that sub-Nyquist sampling and low-resolution bits in ADCs will reduce the audio quality in hearables. Therefore, our proposed SUBARU implements joint BWE and multimodal SE in mobile platforms (i.e., cell phones) to recover audio at high resolution. The increase in power consumption on mobile platforms to run additional BWE and SE algorithms is negligible, as SOTA mobile platforms typically have 100x or more battery capacity compared to hearables (i.e., Samsung Galaxy Buds2 Pro has a 50 mAh battery compared to a 5000 mAh battery in Samsung Galaxy S24 Ultra). A detailed explanation of power-related performance on both hearables and mobile platforms is discussed in Section
6.3
.

3.2.
Audio quality at different sub-Nyquist sampling frequencies and bit resolutions

To gain an understanding of how the bit resolutions and sub-Nyquist sampling frequencies impact the audio quality in hearables, we conduct several pilot studies. Fig.
2
presents time-frequency (T-F) spectrograms of 1s speech signals while a volunteer speaks wearing hearables at
N
= {12-bit, 8-bit} resolutions for different sampling frequencies,
f_{s}
= {24 kHz, 12 kHz, 10 kHz, 8 kHz, 6 kHz, 4 kHz}. The reference signal is collected at a 48 kHz sampling frequency for high-fidelity audio. Before every sampling, a decimation filter with an appropriate cut-off frequency is used to prevent aliasing. Fig.
2
indicates that with the decrease of the sampling frequency, the higher frequency components (a.k.a. formants) of audio are deprecated. The lower the sampling frequency, the higher the deprecation, resulting in lower sound quality (i.e., lower Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ) and higher Log Spectral Distance (LSD)). Moreover, we measure the audio quality for each sub-Nyquist sampling frequency at two different bit resolutions. The lower the bit resolution, the higher the quantization noise, resulting in lower audio quality. Therefore, the lower bit resolution of
N
= 8 bits provides lower quality audio compared to the 12-bit resolution. As bit resolution in sampling impacts audio quality, this factor needs to be considered while designing the BWE algorithms for hearables. Our proposed SUBARU considers the impact of the low bit resolution sampling in BWE algorithms that is absent in SOTA research.

(g) The LSD increases and (h) PESQ decreases from higher to lower sampling frequencies. (g and h) The 8-bit audio has less quality than 12-bit audio in terms of PESQ and LSD. 3.3.
Audio quality under noisy conditions in hearables

Fig.
3
presents the same T-F spectrograms of speech signals at the 24 kHz sampling frequency for ACM and at 4 kHz sampling frequency for BCM for {quite, 20 dB, and 60 dB} pink noise conditions. Typically, BCMs are sampled with a lower frequency compared to ACMs. It is clear from Fig.
3
that higher energy noise signals severely corrupt airborne signals of an ACM (i.e., reduce PESQ and signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR)) that may not be simply filtered out using SE algorithms. As BCMs are less sensitive to airborne noise signals, a multimodal SE algorithm with joint BWE is required to recover high-frequency formants as well as to remove high-energy noises in hearables. Moreover, Fig.
3
indicates that at the high-energy noise level, BCM can still pick noise energy, which can reduce PESQ and SNR. Therefore, if we assume that the BCM’s signal is always immune to airborne noise, this will not be correct. SEANet
(
Tagliasacchi et al. 2020
)
and TRAMBA
(
Sui et al. 2024
)
both are built based on this assumption, which is not correct under high-noisy conditions. At high noisy conditions, both the amplitude and phase get corrupted, and simply using time-domain reconstruction loss may not result in improved reconstruction quality. To close this research gap, our proposed SUBARU considers both amplitude and clean phase reconstruction for improved perceptual quality under noisy conditions.

4.
SUBARU ARCHITECTURE DESIGN

SUBARU is engineered to achieve the following three primary objectives:

•

SUBARU will handle dynamic interferences from extremely noisy conditions in mobile scenarios using joint BWE and multimodal SE algorithms.

•

SUBARU will process input audio signals in both the spectrum and waveform domains and enhance both the amplitude and phase of the time-frequency (T-F) spectrum to output high-quality speech signals from sub-Nyquist sampled data (i.e., low-resolution audio).

•

SUBARU will enable streaming enhancement and low-power and low-memory solutions that will make SUBARU deployable on mobile platforms.

SUBARU adopts a U-Net-based model and processes the incoming low-resolution audio from hearables in both spectrum and waveform domains (i.e., contrary to TRAMBA as TRAMBA processes only on the waveform). Before going further on explaining the SUBARU architecture, we will explain the reasons behind adopting a U-Net model with both spectrum- and waveform-based processing.

GAN vs U-Net models:
For BWE tasks, both U-Net and GAN-based models offer distinct advantages. U-Net models, built on an encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections, are well-suited for reconstructing high-frequency components through supervised learning, have a smaller size, are stable to train, and require less power and memory. In contrast, GAN-based models leverage adversarial training to generate more perceptually realistic and sharper audio compared to U-Net models, but they are harder to train and typically larger in size, requiring larger memory and higher power compared to U-Net. As we consider low-power and low-memory mobile platforms for running SUBARU, we adopt a U-Net-based model for SUBARU. However, to get the advantages of GANs for better perceptual audio, SUBARU adopts multi-scale and multi-period loss functions to simulate the behavior of multi-scale discriminators
(
Kumar et al. 2019
)
and multi-period discriminators
(
Kong et al. 2020
)
of GANs. The multi-scale and multi-period loss functions in SUBARU behave like virtual discriminators in SUBARU without having actual GAN-like discriminator networks. Therefore, SUBARU achieves similar perceptual quality to GANs with smaller parameters (saving tens of millions) and stable training, enabling efficient generation in mobile platforms.

Waveform-based vs spectrum-based method:
Waveform-based BWE methods process directly on raw waveform and aim to directly predict high-resolution waveforms from narrowband ones without any frequency domain transformation. Spectrum-based BWE methods process directly on spectrograms and aim to restore high-frequency spectral components for reconstructing wideband waveforms. For example, TRAMBA
(
Sui et al. 2024
)
is waveform-based, and AERO
(
Mandel et al. 2023
)
is a spectrum-based method. Both methods have their advantages and disadvantages. Waveform-based methods are performed at the sample-point level, leading to a simpler architecture but relatively lower generation efficiency compared to spectrum-based methods with frame-level operations. Moreover, spectrum-based methods enable learning auditory patterns at different frequency levels, resulting in better reconstruction for BWE and SE. However, spectrum-based methods are mostly amplitude-related, and recovering high-frequency phase information remains the primary challenge. Therefore, to get the benefits from both methods, SUBARU adopts both the waveform-based and spectrum-based processing that enables the inclusion of phase-spectrum loss function in the spectrum domain with the multi-scale and multi-period loss functions in the waveform domain. Joint training in both the spectrum and waveform domains provides GAN-like perceptual quality for SUBARU, keeping the model size small and efficient for mobile platforms.

General architecture of SUBARU:
Figure
4
illustrates the general framework of SUBARU. SUBARU first employs a spectrum enhancement network that extracts features from T-F spectrograms and learns low-resolution to high-resolution frequency mapping from the sub-Nyquist-sampled ACM’s audio signal. This network effectively retains informative low-frequency contents while eliminating noisy interference, ensuring that the next stage of SUBARU gets a cleaner and wideband spectrum to process. Then, the enhanced audio spectra of ACM from the spectrum enhancement network go through an upsampler, which converts the spectrum to its equivalent waveform-based signals. Next, it is combined with noisy BCM’s waveform in the time domain and is fed into a time enhancement network. The time enhancement network corrects the combined signal in the time domain. The corrected signal is then fed into an amplitude-phase enhancement network, which cleans up the signal in both the spectrum and phase domains. In this way, SUBARU provides a unified framework combining BWE, SE, and neural vocoding, which are crucial for reconstructing high-fidelity audio for low-power and low-memory mobile platforms. To ensure real-time performance, 1D convolutional networks within SUBARU are designed to be causal. This design significantly reduces computational load, making the system suitable for real-time applications on mobile platforms.

4.2.
Upsampling network

The upsampling network is inspired by version 2 of HiFi-GAN
(
Kong et al. 2020
)
, which takes the enriched spectrogram representation from the spectrum enhancement network as input and gives a raw waveform at its output. The input spectrogram for the upsampling network is a
\sim
1 second audio clip. Let’s say, if the target sampling frequency is 16 kHz, the 1s clip will have 16000 samples. The hop size is 256 for the spectrogram. This means that there are 16000/256
\approx
62 frames in the spectrograms. Therefore, the upsampling network needs to generate 16000 samples from 62 frames, which means the upsampling network needs
\sim
256x upsampling.

Figure 6.
The upsampling network has a 256 upsampling ratio, which is done in 4 stages: 8x, 8x, 2x, and 2x upsampling.

Fig.
6
shows the upsampling network in detail. Each upsampling layer is a transposed convolution, where the kernel size is twice the stride. A stack of transposed convolutional layers is used to upsample the input sequence to 256x. The 256x upsampling is done in 4 stages: 8x, 8x, 2x, and 2x upsampling. Each transposed convolutional layer is followed by a residual layer. Each of the residual layers has three dilated convolutions with dilation 1, 3, and 9, with kernel size 3, having a total receptive field of 27 timesteps. We use dilated convolution to provide large receptive fields so that it can capture the long-range correlation among the audio timesteps. The receptive field of a stack of dilated convolution layers increases exponentially with the number of layers. This effectively implies a larger overlap in the induced receptive field of far-apart time-steps, leading to better long-range correlation. Leaky ReLU activation is used in each residual layer after each dilated convolution for stable training.

4.3.
Time enhancement network

The time enhancement network is placed after the upsampling network and works as a multimodal SE network that operates in the full time domain. The network architecture is shown in Fig.
7
. The network is designed to fuse features from both the acoustic (i.e., ACM) and vibration (i.e., BCM) modalities. As signals from BCMs are less noisy compared to the signals from ACMs, the enhanced signals of ACMs from the spectrum enhancement network are further improved using the less noisy signal of BCMs by the time enhancement network. Specifically, the 1D sequences from the upsampling network are concatenated with the noisy 1D waveform from BCMs. The concatenated 1D sequences are given as input to the time enhancement network. This module operates directly in the time domain and can be considered a time domain postprocessing mechanism that improves the output of the upsampling network with the multimodal information from the BCMs.

Figure 7.
The time enhancement network is designed to fuse features from both the acoustic (i.e., ACM) and vibration (i.e., BCM) modalities. This network enhances the signals of ACMs using the less noisy signal of BCMs.

The time enhancement network is inspired by the well-known Wave-U-Net architecture
(
Stoller et al. 2018

, which is a fully convolutional 1D-UNet-like neural network. This network consists of a 1D convolution framework in U-Net, featuring 4 full 1D convolution layers as encoders, 4 full 1D convolution layers as decoders, and a Mamba
(
Gu and Dao 2023
)
block as a bottleneck layer. The details of each layer are shown in Table
4
. Each 1D convolution layer incorporates batch normalization followed by leaky ReLU activations. Similarly to the spectrum enhancement network, dilations are used to increase the receptive fields that help to capture long-range correlation among the audio timesteps over a dilated window. The skip connections between the encoders and decoders are implemented using reshaping and are followed by a 1D convolution for better gradient flow, rich feature fusion, and preserving temporal resolution. Mamba in the bottleneck layer captures the global correlation among consecutive timesteps in the waveform. Since Mamba is a specific sequence modeling architecture originally for 1D sequences and the encoders/decoders process 1D sequences, we don’t need to reshape Mamba. We compare Mamba with Transformers for the same embedding dimension = 80 and sequence length = 16 x 16 in our time enhancement network, which is shown in Table

. It shows that a roughly one-third reduction in parameter count happens for Mamba over Transformers (i.e., 25864 vs 76895). As Mamba is lightweight and faster than Transformers, we opt for a more lightweight version due to our focus on real-time processing and local deployment on mobile devices. This design choice ensures that SUBARU is optimized for performance on resource-constrained devices, balancing computational efficiency and enhancement quality.

4.4.
Amplitude-phase enhancement network

The 1D waveform sequences generated by the time enhancement network are given as input to the amplitude-phase enhancement network. The purpose of this module is to perform amplitude-domain and phase-domain postprocessing of the waveform signal generated by the time enhancement network (that is, generated with multimodal fusion of both ACM and BCM signals). We hypothesize that it is an efficient mechanism to remove artifacts and noise in both the amplitude and phase domains from the output waveform in a learnable way. This module helps to generate a clean phase from the noisy phase for successful reconstruction of audio signals in extremely noisy conditions (i.e., when both the ACMs and BCMs are noisy).

The network is shown in Fig.
8
. The waveform from the time enhancement network is first subjected to a short-time Fourier transformation (STFT) that gives an amplitude spectrum
X_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times F}
and a wrapped phase spectrum
X_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times F}
, where T = 125 and F = 513 denote the number of temporal frames and frequency bins, respectively. The STFT is obtained with a Hann window of 1024 samples, a hop length of 256 samples, and an FFT size of 1024. The amplitude
X_{a}
and phase
X_{p}
streams utilize an identical network of a series of 1D convolution operations with mutual coupling between the two streams. The 1D convolution operations contain a cascade of a large-kernel-sized depth-wise convolutional layer and a pair of point-wise convolutional layers that respectively expand and restore feature dimensions. The depth-wise convolution is implemented using the Conv1D operation, and the point-wise convolution is implemented using linear layers. The details of each layer are shown in Table
5
. Layer normalization
(
Ba et al. 2016
)
and Gaussian error linear unit (GELU) activation
(
Hendrycks and Gimpel 2016
)
are interleaved between the layers. Finally, the residual connection is added before the output to
prevent the gradient from vanishing.

Figure 8.
The amplitude-phase enhancement network helps to generate a clean phase from the noisy phase for successful reconstruction of audio signals in noisy conditions (i.e., when both the ACMs and BCMs are noisy).

The phase stream
X_{p}
does not have distinct structural features, and the 2
\pi
ambiguity associated with phase makes it more challenging to recover phase information. Therefore, mutual connections are established between two streams for information exchange, which is crucial for phase prediction
(
Yin et al. 2020
)
. Through the mutual coupling of the amplitude stream and phase stream, the amplitude-phase enhancement network predicts the enhanced amplitude spectrum and wrapped phase spectrum separately. Eventually, the enhanced amplitude spectrum
Y^{*}_{a}
and wrapped phase spectrum
Y^{*}_{p}
are used together at the end of the amplitude-phase enhancement network to generate the enhanced waveform
Y_{E}
using inverse STFT (iSTFT) as follows,
Y^{*}_{E}=iSTFT(Y^{*}_{a}\cdot{\rm e}^{jY^{*}_{p}})
.

4.5.
Multi-scale and multi-period losses to mimic GAN’s discriminators

The objective of designing SUBARU using U-net architecture rather than using a GAN-based model, is to provide a low-power and low-memory solution for mobile platforms as GAN-based models are harder to train and typically larger in size, requiring larger memory and higher power compared to U-Net-based models. However, recent work
(
Kumar et al. 2019
;
Kong et al. 2020
;
Mandel et al. 2023
)
shows that GAN-based models can generate more perceptually realistic and sharper audio compared to the U-Net models due to their multi-scale discriminators
(
Kumar et al. 2019
)
and multi-period discriminators
(
Kong et al. 2020
)
. Inspired by the superior performance of multi-scale and multi-period discriminators in GAN-based models, SUBARU proposes multi-scale and multi-period loss functions to simulate the behavior of multi-scale and multi-period discriminators of GANs. The multi-scale and multi-period loss functions in SUBARU behave like virtual discriminators without having actual GAN-like discriminator networks. Therefore, our proposed SUBARU can achieve comparable perceptual quality similar to GANs with smaller parameters (saving tens of millions) and smaller training epochs, enabling efficient generation on mobile platforms.

Figure 9.
The multi-scale and multi-period loss functions in SUBARU behave like virtual discriminators without having actual GAN-like discriminator networks.

Multi-scale loss function:
Multi-scale discriminators introduced in MelGAN
(
Kumar et al. 2019
)
use three discriminators (
D_{1},D_{2},D_{3}
) on the raw waveform at different scales to capture the audio structure at different levels. Inspired by this idea, SUBARU proposes a multi-scale loss function shown in Fig.
9
. For that, SUBARU generates three down-sampled audio
D^{E}_{1},D^{E}_{2},
and
D^{E}_{3}
using three max-pooling layers from the enhanced waveform
Y^{*}_{E}
. For example,
D^{E}_{1}
is generated directly from the enhanced waveform
Y^{*}_{E}
, whereas
D^{E}_{2}
and
D^{E}_{3}
are generated from the 2x and 4x down-sampled versions of the enhanced waveform
Y^{*}_{E}
. Parallelly, SUBARU generates three down-sampled audio
D^{C}_{1},D^{C}_{2},
and
D^{C}_{3}
using three similar max-pooling layers from the clean/ground-truth waveform
Y^{*}_{C}
. Next, SUBARU calculates the mean absolute error (MAE) loss between clean and enhanced audio at each down-sampled scale. The multi-scale loss is the summation of these three MAE losses at three scales, expressed by Eqn.
2
.

(2)

\displaystyle\text{Multi-scale loss}=\frac{1}{3\times samples}\sum^{Samples}|D^{C}_{1}-D^{E}_{1}|+|D^{C}_{2}-D^{E}_{2}|+|D^{C}_{3}-D^{E}_{3}|

Similarly to
(
Sui et al. 2024
)
, we find that the mean square error (MSE) loss generates lower audio quality compared to the MAE loss in Eqn.
2
. Multi-scale loss function introduced in Eqn.
2
with the max-pooling layers mimics the behavior of the GAN discriminators, resulting in better audio quality in terms of PESQ and LSD. The multi-scale loss provides better performance compared to the single-scale MAE loss. A comparative study is shown in Section
6.9
as the ablation study. Moreover, as the max-pooling layer does not increase the number of parameters, adding multi-scale loss using max-pooling layers does not increase the model size.

Multi-period loss function:
Multi-period discriminators
(
Kong et al. 2020
)
in GAN capture fine-grained periodic details, especially at high frequencies or in long-range dependencies. It solves this by specializing each discriminator to look at a different periodic view of the waveform. It is a mixture of sub-discriminators, each of which only accepts equally spaced samples of an input audio, where the space is given as period p. Inspired by this idea, SUBARU proposes a multi-period loss function shown in Fig.
9
.

Initially, SUBARU reshapes the enhanced waveform
Y^{*}_{E}
(the output of the amplitude-phase enhancement network) from a 1D waveform into a 2D representation by segmenting it based on a specific period (p) of 5 and 7 samples. After reshaping, the dimension of the 2D tensor is (1, T / p, p), where T = number of audio samples and p = periods. For example, SUBARU processes
\sim
1 second of an audio clip for which T = 16000 at 16 kHz target upsampling. For p = 5 samples, the dimension of the 2D tensor after reshaping is (1, 3200, 5), and for p = 7, the dimension is (1, 2285, 7). Let us denote these two 2D tensors generated for p = 5 and 7 samples from the enhanced waveform
Y^{*}_{E}
by
P^{E}_{5}
and
P^{E}_{7}
, respectively. Similarly, SUBARU generates two 2D tensors for p = 5 and 7 samples from the clean/ground-truth waveform
Y^{*}_{C}
and let us denote them by
P^{C}_{5}
and
P^{C}_{7}
, respectively. Next, SUBARU calculates the MAE energy loss between clean and enhanced audio at each period for 2D tensors. The multi-period loss is the summation of these two MAE losses at two different periods, expressed by Eqn.
3
.

(3)

\displaystyle\text{Multi-period loss}=|\sum_{x,y}P^{C}_{5}(x,y)^{2}-\sum_{x,y}P^{E}_{5}(x,y)^{2}|+|\sum_{x,y}P^{C}_{7}(x,y)^{2}-\sum_{x,y}P^{E}_{7}(x,y)^{2}|

Where,
x,y\in\text{2D tensor}
. SUBARU uses only 2 periods (i.e., p = 5 and 7) compared to 5 periods and 6 convolution layers in
(
Kong et al. 2020
)
to keep the network size small without sacrificing audio quality. The multi-period loss provides better performance when fused with the multi-scale loss. A comparative ablation study is shown in Section
6.9
.

Phase-spectrum loss function:
To reconstruct the clean phase when the BCMs are noisy, SUBARU includes a phase-spectrum loss function to enhance audio quality. Inspired by
(
Ai and Ling 2023
)
and considering the phase wrapping issue, SUBARU proposes to use two anti-wrapping losses: one for the instantaneous phase and one for group delay. These two anti-wrapping losses use MAE loss as shown in Eq.
4
.

(4)

\displaystyle\text{Instantaneous phase anti-wrapping loss}=\frac{1}{TF}\sum^{TF}|f_{AW}(Y^{C}_{P}-Y^{E}_{P})|

\displaystyle\text{Group-delay anti-wrapping loss}=\frac{1}{TF}\sum^{TF}|f_{AW}(Y^{C}_{GD}-Y^{E}_{GD})|

Where
Y^{C}_{P}
and
Y^{C}_{GD}
are the instantaneous phase and group delay for the clean/ground-truth wave
Y^{*}_{C}
, respectively. The
Y^{E}_{P}
and
Y^{E}_{GD}
are the instantaneous phase and group delay for enhanced wave
Y^{*}_{E}
, respectively. The group delay
Y^{C}_{GD}
and
Y^{E}_{GD}
are calculated by taking differentiation along the frequency axis of the T-F spectrogram. The
f_{AW}(x)
denotes the anti-wrapping function, which is defined as:
f_{AW}(x)=|x-2\pi\cdot\text{round}(\frac{x}{2\pi})|,x\in\mathbb{R}
. The final phase spectrum loss is the summation of the instantaneous phase and group delay anti-wrapping loss. The phase spectrum loss does not increase the model size as it does not require any learnable parameters. This ensures that SUBARU does not sacrifice efficiency to improve audio quality.

Multi-resolution STFT loss:
To improve the frequency content of the enhanced wave
Y^{*}_{E}
, SUBARU calculates the multi-resolution STFT loss
(
Tian et al. 2020
)
at S = 3 different resolutions, such as {frequency bins, hop sizes, window lengths} = {(256, 128, 256), (512, 256, 512), (1024, 512, 1024)}, following Eq.
5
.

(5)

\displaystyle\vskip-5.0pt\text{Multi-resolution STFT loss}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Big(L_{\mathrm{SC}}+L_{\mathrm{mag}}\Big)

Where, spectral convergence loss
L_{SC}

(
Tian et al. 2020
)
and log STFT magnitude loss
L_{mag}

(
Tian et al. 2020
)
are calculated for S= 3 different resolutions mentioned above.

Total loss:
The total loss of SUBARU is the summation of multi-scale loss, multi-period loss, phase-spectrum loss, and multi-resolution STFT loss. The multi-scale and multi-period losses are time-domain loss functions. Therefore, SUBARU employs training objectives in all three domains: time, phase, and frequency.

4.7.
Streaming operation of SUBARU

Inference time and frame size are two critical factors for the streaming operation of SUBARU. The low-resolution audio sampled at the sub-Nyquist frequency in hearables is transmitted over Bluetooth to the mobile platform for BWE and multimodal SE. The low-resolution audio transmitted from the hearables are received by the mobile platforms frame by frame. For real-time streaming applications, the inference time of each frame must be less than the frame duration. For example, we consider a 1s frame transmitted to the mobile platform from hearables, and SUBARU must reconstruct it in under 1s to prevent the queuing of the next incoming frame in the mobile platforms. From Table
6
, it is clear that SUBARU’s inference time for both GPUs, CPUs, and Pixel7 is much shorter (i.e., 1.74 ms/24.54 ms/70.41 ms) than the frame duration of 1s. Therefore, SUBARU is suitable for streaming audio from hearables to mobile platforms. The 1s frame size is chosen to provide sufficient contextual information for BWE and SE. Short frames lack sufficient contextual information, while long audio frames require more inference time, compromising real-time performance. To balance real-time output with adequate context, we employ a 1s frame in SUBARU. Fig.
10
shows the frame-by-frame operation in real-time.

Figure 10.
The frame-by-frame streaming operation of SUBARU for real-time performance.

In Fig.
10
, at t = 0, the clock starts and the low-resolution audio from hearables starts to be transmitted to the mobile platform (i.e., Google Pixel7). We use the NRF52840 chip with the Bluetooth sniffer to emulate the streaming operation of SUBARU. At t = 12 ms, low-resolution audio packets are received and unpacked on the mobile platform. Next, SUBARU starts audio reconstruction and takes around 71 ms to finish the reconstruction. Therefore, there is a 12 + 71 = 83 ms latency for each 1s audio frame for the end-to-end BWE + SE operation from hearables to mobile platforms. Please note that the 71 ms delay is for the actual model inference, and the 12 ms delay within the 83 ms total latency is not related to SUBARU, but rather related to the time required for audio frame packing, transmission, reception, and unpacking. This 12-ms latency can be reduced if the frame length is reduced from 1s to a shorter duration. However, shorter frames cannot ensure rich contextual information for BWE. Moreover, similar latency exists in all the commercial hearables, such as Apple AirPods Pro (1st & 2nd Gen) has a latency of
\sim
120 ms
(
Coyle 2022
)
, and Samsung Galaxy Buds Pro has a latency of
\sim
60 ms
(
RTINGS.com 2021
)
. For real-world audio communication, one-way delays up to 150 ms are considered acceptable for most user applications, including voice calls, according to the recommendation of the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) G.114
(
International Telecommunication Union 2003
)
. Therefore, we keep the 1s frame length for our operation. Please also note that, as the inference time is much shorter than the 1s frame duration, the frames are always streaming in real-time. If we consider a mobile platform with CPU only (see Table
6
), there is a 12 + 24 = 36 ms latency for each 1s audio frame for the end-to-end BWE + SE operation from hearables to mobile platforms. Moreover, SUBARU uses audio frames from the past and present data, not from future inputs. Therefore, convolution layers in SUBARU are causal, ensuring that real-time processing requirements are met without sacrificing audio quality.

5.2.
Dataset collection

As by definition, hearables are mounted on the ear, we collect an in-house dataset with our ACMs and BCMs mounted near the ear bone. The position of the BCM can be varied within a certain range near the earbone to determine the best location to capture vibration. Although there are a few datasets
(

Wang et al. 2022

Therefore, they don’t have simultaneous data from both vibration and accelerometer sensors with microphones at different ADC bit resolutions and at different noisy conditions. We need both BCM sensors to prove and verify the efficiency of SUBARU in low-power design. We ask 20 persons (11 males and 9 females, age: 18-36) to speak, and data are collected from the ACM and the two BCMs simultaneously at a 22 kHz sampling frequency. We select text from the VCTK dataset
(
Yamagishi et al. 2019
)
, which contains a variety of dialogues. For this purpose, we convert the VCTK audio to text using Whisper
(
Radford et al. 2022b
)
. We apply a high-pass filter with a cut-off frequency of 15 Hz to remove any body movement from the collected data. The data is then normalized and clipped within -1 to +1. We collect data from each person for 10 minutes in a quiet room (no noise) for 2 different bit resolutions - 12 bits and 8 bits - for the two different BCMs.

As the collected data amount for {ACM, BCM} pair is not sufficient, we create a synthetic dataset for better model training. To do this, we first need to model the two BCMs (vibration sensor and accelerometers) for two different bit resolutions of 12 and 8 bits. We choose SEANet
(
Tagliasacchi et al. 2020
)
for this purpose. We train four different SEANet models for four different input-target pairs using our in-house data, shown in Table

. Then, we use the four trained SEANets to generate synthetic data for four {ACM, BCM} pairs by feeding samples from the VCTK dataset.

5.3.
Noise addition

To test the joint BWE and multimodal SE, we need to add noise to the clean data collected from the ACM and BCMs for the two bit resolutions. Two noise sources are used: non-speech noise and speech noise. For non-speech noises, we choose from a diverse set of noise types from
(
Font et al. 2013
)
and randomly mix with the clean audio from the ACM. For speech noises, we employ speech samples from Librispeech from different speakers. For each clean speech sample, we generate five noisy variants by combining the two noise types: two from non-speech categories, two from speech, and one that blends speech with non-speech sounds. As BCM is less sensitive to noise, we use a 10x reduction of noise while adding noise to BCMs. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for these mixtures randomly varies within a range of -7 to 40 dB.

5.4.
Model training

We implement the entire deep learning framework of SUBARU using PyTorch. The training is carried out in an end-to-end fashion. The noisy audio from the ACM for 12-bit and 8-bit resolutions is given together as input to the model in the time domain. The signals from either of the BCMs are also given as an input in the time domain. For training our proposed SUBARU, all the audio clips underwent silence trimming and were sliced into
\sim 1s
clips. To generate the T-F spectrogram from the time-domain signals, we use STFT with a Hann window of 1024 samples, a hop length of 256 samples, and an FFT size of 1024. Therefore, for the training set, the number of frequency bins F is 513, and the number
of temporal frames T is 125. Since the synthetic data inevitably slightly differs from the real data, incorporating all the synthetic data together into a batch may degrade the model performance. To prevent the model from degrading during the training phase, we use an equal proportion of real and synthetic data in the initial training epochs. As training advances, the ratio of synthetic data is progressively reduced, eventually transitioning to batches comprised solely of real data.

The key training parameters include a batch size of 8 with
\sim
50 epochs, and the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
, weight decay of
1\times 10^{-5}
, and momentum parameters
\beta_{1}=0.5
and
\beta_{2}=0.999
. The learning rate is scheduled using cosine annealing warm restarts (with
T_{0}=10
and
T_{\text{mult}}=1
), gradient clipping (max norm of 10), and gradient accumulation (over 2 batches) to ensure stability. Training is first executed on a computer with a 4090 GPU with Intel(R) Xeon(R) Silver 4310 CPU. The trained model is then customized to run on a Google Pixel7 smartphone for inference (see Section
6
for details).

5.5.
Evaluation metrics

We evaluate SUBARU using the following widely adopted metrics.

•

Log-Spectral Distance (LSD):
LSD measures the difference between the log-magnitude spectra of two audio signals — typically a reference (ground truth) signal and a generated or reconstructed one.

•

Virtual Speech Quality Objective Listener (VISQOL):
VISQOL is a full-reference objective speech quality metric developed by Google. It predicts human-perceived audio quality by comparing a degraded signal against a clean reference signal. It has values in between 1 to 5.

•

Non-Intrusive Speech Quality Assessment - Mean Opinion Score (NISQA-MOS):
NISQA-MOS is a deep learning-based model used to estimate the perceived quality of speech audio without needing a reference signal. It predicts a MOS similar to human ratings with values between 0 to 5.

•

Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SI-SDR):
SI-SDR measures how close an estimated signal is to a clean target signal, ignoring any difference in gain.

•

Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ):
PESQ is widely used to evaluate how a degraded or processed speech signal (like after noise reduction, compression, or transmission) compares to a reference (clean) signal, closely modeling human perception. PESQ has values between -0.5 to 4.5

•

Short-Time Objective Intelligibility (STOI):
STOI is an objective speech quality/intelligibility metric specifically designed to predict how understandable speech is to human listeners, especially in the presence of noise, reverberation, or enhancement processing. STOI has values between 0 to 1.

6.3.

This work aims to reduce the sampling frequency of hearables so that hearables can save power, which will eventually extend the battery life. However, reducing the sampling frequency reduces the audio quality. Therefore, the mobile platform, such as a smartphone, will use SUBARU to recover high-fidelity audio from the low-resolution signal received from hearables, restoring audio quality and intelligibility. Ablation study on the loss function

In this section, we conduct an ablation study on different loss functions in SUBARU to understand the contribution of each loss function to overall performance. We evaluate the impact of the loss functions using the VCTK dataset in noisy conditions, with the results summarized in Table
17
. If the multi-resolution STFT loss function is removed, the model performance degrades drastically. The reason behind this is that the multi-resolution STFT loss function is the only loss function in the frequency domain in SUBARU, and without it, the relationship among the high- and low-frequency components cannot be learned effectively in noisy conditions between the low-resolution and high-resolution spectral frames. If multi-scale and multi-period loss functions are added one at a time with the multi-resolution STFT loss, the performance increases gradually, similar to multi-scale and multi-point discriminators in GANs. Multi-scale and multi-period loss functions work in the time domain and facilitate learning the time-domain low-resolution to high-resolution mapping effectively. Without these two loss functions, SUBARU would not be able to perform better than the best-performing GAN models such as AERO and HiFi++ (see Table
8
). The multi-scale and multi-period loss functions are designed in such a way that they combinedly simulate the behavior of multi-scale and multi-period discriminators of GANs. Table
17
also indicates that the phase spectrum loss function further improves the model performance by constructing a clean phase from the noisy phases.

6.11.
Evaluation on live noise data

In earlier sections, we simulated noisy speech by adding noise directly to clean recordings. In contrast, this section focuses on evaluating SUBARU in real-world conditions, where background noise occurs naturally and continuously. Since clean reference signals are unavailable in such live settings, we calculate the Character Error Rate (CER) using Whisper
(
Radford et al. 2022a
)
to assess performance. We conduct tests using five volunteers per scenario, each reading 20 consistent sentences. This consistency across tests allows us to analyze the effect of various environmental conditions on SUBARU’s accuracy. We test SUBARU inside and outside the lab in different scenarios.

6.11.1.
Inside the lab:

We design the following four scenarios to evaluate SUBARU inside the lab:

•

Quiet Room:
To provide a baseline for comparison, volunteers speak in a quiet environment.

•

Live speech:
While one volunteer speaks, another volunteer stands one meter away and talks simultaneously, simulating a live speech interference. The sound pressure level (SPL) for the target and interfering speakers are approximately 77 dB and 74 dB, respectively.

•

Music:
During the speech, two speakers on either side of the participant play music with SPLs of 65 dB and 64 dB, respectively. This setup introduces multimedia noise to test the system’s robustness.

•

Mobile scenarios:
Volunteers speak while engaging in physical activity, such as running on a treadmill, cycling on a stationary bike, or walking.

6.11.2.
Outside the lab:

We design the following three scenarios to evaluate SUBARU outside the lab:

•

Bus ride:
The target volunteer reads while sitting on a bus with typical background road-noise from the surrounding environment. The average SPL is 74 dB.

•

Classroom:
The target volunteer reads while surrounded by people talking in a crowded classroom. The average SPL is 73 dB.

•

Car ride:
The target volunteer reads in a car with an open window. The average SPL is 81 dB.

Table
20
summarizes the results. In all noisy scenarios, the unprocessed speech signal yields a high CER, particularly in the presence of competing speech and a car ride with an open window. After introducing SUBARU, the enhanced signals show a marked improvement in recognition accuracy, indicating effective suppression of live acoustic interference. Additionally, in mobile conditions, user movement has some impacts on the performance because of the presence of vibrations related to movements. To prevent this, we use a high-pass filter (HPF) with a cut-off frequency of 15 Hz to remove the movement-induced noises from the BCM. The results demonstrate that SUBARU can be used in voice communication in noisy public settings.

7.
Discussion

7.1.
Encryption and Codec

This work does not consider any encryption of the sampled audio before transmission to the mobile platform from the hearables. Moreover, we do not include any codec while evaluating its performance, power, and efficiency. Our future work will include codec and encryption while designing a joint BWE and multimodal SE algorithm. However, as a codec is a compression and decompression algorithm, codec can be used safely after sub-Nyquist sampling on hearables with our platform.

7.2.
New user

SUBARU still needs to fine-tune the model for a new user for a few minutes for performance improvement. This issue is because of the lack of sufficient data and will be handled using a large language model (LLM)-based audio signal processing in the future. The audio phoneme can be converted to tokens and will be processed by LLMs.

7.3.
Fine tuning on mobile platform

We fine-tune SUBARU on a desktop with GPU support due to the limitation of available open-source tools and computational resources for fine-tuning models on a mobile platform. Our future work will try to solve this problem by contributing to open-source tool development for on-device training.

8.
Conclusion

SUBARU is designed to be deployed in a split architecture, where hearables run sub-Nyquist sampling in low bit resolution on signals from both ACMs and BCMs, and mobile platforms connected to hearables run the joint BWE and multimodal SE algorithms to reconstruct high-resolution, noise-free audio in noisy conditions. The low latency of SUBARU while performing joint BWE and multimodal SE enables streaming enhancement, low power, and low memory solutions, making SUBARU deployable on mobile platforms. To get the best out of GANs and U-Nets, SUBARU adopts multi-scale and multi-period loss functions in its U-Net architecture to simulate the behavior of GAN discriminators. The multi-scale and multi-period loss functions behave like virtual discriminators in SUBARU without having actual GAN-like discriminator networks. Moreover, SUBARU adopts a hybrid architecture by merging both waveform-based and spectrum-based methods, enabling joint training in both spectrum and waveform domains. This joint training also improves both time and spectral domain features during audio reconstruction. This joint training also enables the use of instantaneous phase and group delay anti-wrapping losses to reconstruct clean phases in noisy conditions. SUBARU provides users with reliable communication while ensuring real-time performance by extending the battery life of hearables. Therefore, SUBARU is designed to meet the high demands of smart hearables in low-power and real-world usage by bridging the gap between power and the performance of current commercial technologies.
