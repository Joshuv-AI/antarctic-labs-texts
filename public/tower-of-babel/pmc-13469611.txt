Ziziphus leaves, probiotic, and fermented red mold rice reduce egg yolk cholesterol and sustain laying performance in hens
Authors: H Al-Khalaifah, T Surrayai, M Al-Musalam, H Sultan, G Ragheb, A Ahmad, A Al-Holi, M Khoshk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe production of low-cholesterol table eggs is an effective strategy to meet consumer demand for healthier foods and to support local food security. This study evaluated the effects of Ziziphus leaves, a locally formulated probiotic, and fermented red mold rice on egg yolk cholesterol, laying performance, egg quality, immune response, antioxidant status, sensory acceptance, and economic feasibility in laying hens. A total of 500 Lohmann Selected Leghorn (LSL) hens were used in two feeding trials. In Trial 1 (34–69 weeks of age), hens were allocated to ten dietary treatments to assess individual inclusion levels of each additive. In Trial 2 (75 weeks of age), hens were fed a combined dietary mix consisting of probiotic (150 mL in drinking water), fermented red mold rice (8 g/kg diet), and Ziziphus leaves (20 g/kg diet). In Trial 1, fermented red mold rice at 8 g/kg produced the greatest reduction in egg yolk cholesterol (35–45%, p < 0.05), while Ziziphus leaves and probiotic supplementation reduced yolk cholesterol by 20–40% (p < 0.05) compared with the control. These reductions occurred without adverse effects on egg production, feed efficiency, or egg quality. Immune response and antioxidant enzyme activity were significantly enhanced (p < 0.05). In Trial 2, the combined dietary mix reduced egg yolk cholesterol by approximately 25% (p < 0.05) with no negative impact on performance or sensory quality. Economic analysis confirmed the practical feasibility of using these additives in commercial production. Overall, locally available natural feed additives effectively produced low-cholesterol eggs without compromising productivity, supporting sustainable poultry production and food security.Keywords: Cholesterol, Table eggs, Fermented red mold rice, Probiotic, Ziziphus leavesSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Health care, Microbiology, Zoology

Introduction

Laying hens are a cornerstone of the poultry industry, providing a significant source of protein through eggs, which are one of the most widely consumed animal products globally. Potential consumers of chicken meat and eggs constitute approximately 45% of the world’s population1,2. Eggs contain a variety of important nutrients such as proteins, fats, vitamins, and minerals, which contribute to their value as a nutritious food for people3–5. The production of high-quality eggs is not only critical for the poultry industry but also for food security, contributing to human nutrition, especially in developing countries where eggs are a staple protein source6. However, egg quality and nutritional profile are influenced by hen health, immune function, oxidative stress, and gut integrity, necessitating optimized dietary management7–9.

Standard eggs contain approximately 74% water, 13% protein, 11% fat, and small proportions of carbohydrates, minerals, vitamins, and carotenoids. Structurally, eggs consist of 9–12% shell, 60% albumen, and 30–33% yolk. The yolk is lipid-rich, comprising triacylglycerols (63.3%), phospholipids (29.7%), and cholesterol (5.2%). Despite their high biological value, palatability, and low cost, eggs have been widely criticized for their cholesterol content, which is concentrated in the yolk. This concern has contributed to a “cholesterol phobia” among health-conscious consumers and has negatively influenced egg consumption trends, particularly in Western countries10. Although cholesterol itself is not inherently harmful, oxidative modification and excessive intake have raised public health concerns, necessitating strategies to improve the lipid profile of eggs.

These concerns have driven the development of “designer eggs,” which are produced by modifying laying hen diets to reduce health-negative components such as cholesterol and triglycerides while enriching eggs with health-positive nutrients, including omega-3 fatty acids, antioxidants, vitamins, and minerals10. Designer eggs are typically achieved through dietary supplementation with functional ingredients such as kelp, flaxseed, canola oil, and marigold extract, allowing targeted manipulation of egg composition without compromising productivity11.

The production of value-added poultry meat and eggs has garnered significant interest in recent years to diversify poultry offerings and meet the demands of individuals pursuing healthier lifestyles. This can be achieved by incorporating effective and functional components in poultry feed like medicinal herbs, probiotics, prebiotics, natural growth promoters, and n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA)12. Compared to other animal proteins, eggs contain relatively less saturated fat, which is widely recognized as a key dietary factor contributing to elevated low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels and a higher risk of coronary heart disease. Cholesterol itself is not inherently harmful, but it can become detrimental when oxidation occurs. In poultry production, numerous studies have demonstrated that dietary and pharmacological interventions can effectively reduce cholesterol levels in eggs and meat. Supplementation of poultry diets with probiotics, chromium, n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), copper, nicotinic acid, statins, garlic, basil, peanuts, and plant sterols has been shown to significantly decrease cholesterol concentrations in yolk and carcass tissues13. These strategies address the growing consumer demand for healthier poultry products, particularly those with reduced cholesterol and enhanced nutritional value14.

Egg cholesterol levels are very difficult to influence by dietary manipulation, but some improvement has been reported from supplementing with Co and Cr. It has been reported that supplementation with dietary micro minerals (Co, Cr, Zn and I), vitamins (Vit. A, Vit. E and niacin), various oils and herbal plants may change the yolk cholesterol level15. Influence of designer diets enriched with omega-3 fatty acids and antioxidants from natural sources on egg yolk composition of ‘white leghorn’ hens reduced the yolk cholesterol levels and increasing omega-3 fatty acid levels in the egg16. Another way of reducing the cholesterol concentration in yolk by supplementation of Lactobacillus acidophilus. Addition of different amount of Lactobacillus acidophilus to ration of laying hens resulted in decrease yolk cholesterol17. Supplementing the flaxseed meal or mixture with rapeseed meal or rice bran up to 10% of feed in the diet of poultry will help in reduction of yolk cholesterol18. Pumpkin seed meal (PSM) supplementation successfully reduced cholesterol concentration in both egg yolk and whole egg, making eggs healthier for human consumption19.

A significant body of research has explored nutritional strategies that improve laying hens’ productivity, health, and egg quality. Natural or synthetic functional feed additives are included in poultry diets to improve productivity, support immune health, and enhance egg nutritional quality. The studies found that functional feed additives significantly improved metabolism, gut function, and oxidative status9.

Feed additives are widely used in the poultry industry to support animal production traits and maintain their good health20. Feed additives such as probiotics, prebiotics, and medicinal plants are gaining attention for their beneficial roles in enhancing gut microbial balance, supporting immune responses, and promoting overall health21. Probiotics and prebiotics can be categorized as functional foods; they positively influence bodily functions to enhance health or may impact physiological or psychological aspects, surpassing conventional nutritional effects22. These additives not only improve the productivity of hens but also contribute to the production of healthier eggs, an important response to the growing consumer demand for lower-cholesterol and omega-3-enriched products23,24.

Against this background, there remains a need to evaluate sustainable, locally available, and biologically effective alternatives for producing designer eggs. In the present study, crude leaves derived from Ziziphus species and a locally produced probiotic were selected as potential natural feed additives to assess their effects on laying hen performance, egg lipid profile, yolk cholesterol content, immune response, and oxidative status. Ziziphus is recognized as one of the most nutritious plant genera, being rich in vitamins, minerals, amino acids, and bioactive compounds that contribute to its wide range of nutritional and medicinal applications.

Probiotics were recently established as a safe and effective dietary approach for enhancing growth and reducing gastrointestinal disorders25. Probiotics (particularly Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains) modulate gut microbiota, enhance nutrient absorption, reduce pathogenic bacteria, increase volatile fatty acid production, and improve immune function and oxidative status—collectively supporting hen health and egg quality26,27. Modulating the immune system and reducing oxidative stress through probiotics can enhance egg production and quality, which is essential for maintaining healthy eggs28,29. For comparison, fermented red mold rice (Monascus purpureus) was included as a third treatment due to its recognized cholesterol-lowering properties, attributed primarily to monacolin K, along with its content of bioavailable B-vitamins, amino acids, and antioxidants30. In poultry, adding fermented red mold rice as a feed additive can reduce the cholesterol content in eggs, offering potential health benefits for consumers31,32. The fermentation process enriches the rice with bioactive compounds such as monacolin K, which has been associated with reduced blood cholesterol levels33,34.

The present study aimed to evaluate the effects of Ziziphus leaves, a locally produced probiotic, and fermented red mold rice on laying hen performance, egg yolk cholesterol, immune response, and antioxidant status. The study was conducted in two sequential trials: Trial 1 utilized 500 hens allocated to ten dietary treatments to identify optimal individual inclusion levels of each additive, while Trial 2 assessed a combined dietary mix in 75-week-old hens to test for synergistic effects during the late-laying phase. For the combined treatment, doses were selected based on maximal biological efficacy observed in Trial 1—specifically fermented red mold rice at 8 g/kg (35–45% cholesterol reduction), probiotic at 150 mL, and Ziziphus leaves at 20 g/kg (20–40% cholesterol reduction with highest antioxidant capacity)—prioritizing these cholesterol-lowering and oxidative status benefits over statistical significance in production performance parameters.

Economic cost study

The cost evaluation of dietary treatments was calculated based on the feed consumption and supplementation used during the experimental period. All treatments were provided with a basal diet at a rate of 110 g feed/hen/day, corresponding to a total feed intake of 6545 kg for 250 hens throughout the 238 days of the experiment. Accordingly, each treatment consumed 654.5 kg of the basal diet, which served as the common feed base.

The control group (TRT1) received only the basal diet without any additional supplementation; therefore, no extra cost was associated with this treatment. In TRT2, TRT3, and TRT4, probiotics were added through the drinking water. A total of 10 g of probiotic powder was diluted in 2.5 L distilled water to prepare the probiotic solution, which was used weekly across these treatments. Over the 34-week experimental period, the total probiotic used amounted to 340 g (0.34 kg), with a unit price of KD 0.750/kg, resulting in a total expenditure of KD 0.255. The distribution of costs among the probiotic treatments was minimal, with KD 0.0425, KD 0.085, and KD 0.127 for TRT2, TRT3, and TRT4, respectively, indicating that the probiotic supplementation was nearly cost-free.

Fermented red mold rice was incorporated in TRT5, TRT6, and TRT7 at inclusion rates of 2, 4, and 8 g/kg of diet, respectively. Based on the total feed consumed, TRT5 required 1.31 kg of fermented red mold rice, with a cost of KD 58.9, while TRT6 required 2.62 kg with a cost of KD 117.9. The highest inclusion rate in TRT7 required 5.24 kg of fermented red mold rice, resulting in a cost of KD 235.8. Given its high market price (KD 45.0/kg), this supplement considerably increased dietary costs in proportion to the inclusion level.

Similarly, Ziziphus leaves were supplemented in TRT8, TRT9, and TRT10 at inclusion levels of 5, 10, and 20 g/kg of diet, respectively. TRT8 required 3.27 kg of Ziziphus leaves, costing KD 179.8, while TRT9 required 6.54 kg with a cost of KD 359.7. The highest inclusion in TRT10 required 13.1 kg, amounting to KD 720. Due to its relatively higher price (KD 55.0/kg) and higher inclusion rates, Ziziphus supplementation represented the most expensive dietary treatment among the tested alternatives (Table 15).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that dietary supplementation with probiotics, fermented red mold rice, and Ziziphus leaf extract did not adversely affect productive performance, organ integrity, or hematological indices in laying hens, while improving antioxidant status and selected egg quality attributes. Overall, these outcomes are largely consistent with previous reports evaluating natural feed additives in laying hens, where health-related benefits were observed in the absence of significant production responses.

Although numerical increases in egg number, egg mass, egg production rate, and feed efficiency were observed—particularly in hens receiving fermented red mold rice at 4 g/kg diet—these improvements were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). This finding is comparable to that reported by Perumal et al.35, who similarly observed minimal or non-significant effects on productive performance when using plant-based feed additives. In contrast to studies reporting performance enhancement with synthetic growth promoters, the present results align with the growing body of evidence suggesting that natural additives exert more subtle, supportive effects rather than direct productivity stimulation.

The absence of gross pathological changes in the heart, liver, kidneys, and intestines is in agreement with earlier studies demonstrating the safety and non-toxicity of Ziziphus leaf extract and other phytogenic feed additives36. Similar post-mortem outcomes have been reported in studies using herbal extracts and probiotics, where organ morphology remained unaffected even at higher inclusion levels. These consistent findings across studies support the conclusion that the tested additives are safe for dietary inclusion.

Hematological parameters were not significantly altered by dietary treatment (p > 0.05), which corresponds with previous investigations reporting stable blood profiles following probiotic or phytogenic supplementation. While slight numerical increases in red and white blood cell counts were observed in Ziziphus-treated groups, these responses were variable and non-significant, resembling patterns reported in other studies where immune cell counts fluctuated without indicating pathological stress. Thus, the present hematological findings are comparable to those documented in similar nutritional interventions.

One of the most pronounced effects of dietary supplementation in this study was the enhancement of antioxidant defense systems. Significant increases in TAC, TSOD, GPx, and hepatic antioxidant activity, along with reduced MDA concentrations, are in close agreement with previous reports demonstrating improved antioxidant capacity following probiotic and phytogenic supplementation37. Although catalase activity remained unaffected (p = 0.942), the general antioxidant profile suggests a protective role of the additives against oxidative damage, which may contribute to improved product quality (e.g., fresher eggs with lower lipid oxidation).

Fermented red mold rice has been extensively studied for its effects on lipid metabolism, primarily due to its monacolin K content, which inhibits HMG-CoA reductase activity38. The cholesterol-lowering effects of fermented red mold rice are dose-dependent but not linear. In the referenced study, the most pronounced reduction in egg cholesterol (13.89%) was achieved with 2.0% supplementation, while higher inclusion rates (5.0% and 8.0%) yielded comparatively lower reductions (12.98% and 5.73%, respectively). The non-linear dose–response pattern described in earlier studies—where moderate inclusion levels produced greater reductions in egg yolk cholesterol than higher doses—is consistent with previously reported findings39,40. As a result, hens fed diets supplemented with Fermented red mold rice produce eggs with lower cholesterol content, offering a potential strategy for producing “healthier” eggs tailored to consumer demand for functional foods.

The immunomodulatory role of probiotics observed indirectly in this study is comparable to findings reported by Xu et al.28, who documented reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines following probiotic supplementation. Several studies corroborate these effects. Chen et al.41 demonstrated that probiotics and their metabolites enhance immune responses across animal models, while Zhao et al.42 reported that dietary peptides reduced inflammatory markers. Similarly, Wang et al.43 showed that Clostridium butyricum supplementation reduced intestinal IL-6 expression, and sodium butyrate was found to suppress both pro-inflammatory cytokines and immunoglobulin levels. Compared with studies linking chronic inflammation to reduced egg production and compromised egg quality44,45, the absence of negative health or performance indicators in the present study suggests a favorable immune environment.

A deeper yolk color, especially with richer yellow and red tones, is often preferred by consumers, as it reflects higher levels of carotenoids in the hen’s diet. These pigments such as xanthophyll and carotene not only enhance yolk coloration but also serve as powerful antioxidants. According to Gul et al.46, the intensity of yolk color is directly linked to the concentration of these compounds, with greater amounts producing more vibrant hues. In addition to visual appeal, increased carotenoid levels help minimize lipid peroxidation, which can cause rancidity and reduce both the nutritional value and sensory quality of eggs. These findings are consistent with those of Dong et al.47, who observed more in-tense yolk coloration in hens fed Bacillus subtilis. Furthermore, the rise in yolk lightness (L* value) seen in probiotic-supplemented hens may be due to a decrease in solute con-centration in the yolk, prompting water to move from the intracellular to the extracellular space.

Sensory evaluation results showed no significant differences among treatments, which is consistent with previous studies reporting that dietary inclusion of probiotics and phytogenic additives does not compromise egg organoleptic quality. Likewise, the economic analysis aligns with earlier findings showing that probiotics are cost-effective, whereas phytogenic additives and fermented products increase feed costs in a dose-dependent manner.

While the present findings are largely consistent with previous studies, the experimental duration and absence of gut microbiota composition, and immune gene expression limit direct mechanistic comparisons. Future research should focus on longer-term feeding trials, direct lipid and immune assessments, and refined dose optimization to better align physiological outcomes with economic feasibility.

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the department committee of the Environment and Life Sciences Research Center in Kuwait Institute for Scientific Research under Project No. FA184K (2020). The procedures and protocols followed the official animal welfare guidelines and regulations (Reference No. PMO/PV/RP/115/2020). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study was carried out in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines to ensure transparent and complete reporting of all animal studies. No anesthesia or euthanasia procedures other than routine handling were performed in this study.

Experimental design

A total of 500 Lohmann LSL white one-day-old pullets were distributed randomly in one room of the growing pullet house until they were 19 weeks old. There was minimal vaccination of the pullets and laying hens as per the guidelines of the chicks supply company and Public Authority of Agricultural Affairs and Fish Resources (PAAFR) requirements for layers raised under Kuwait’s environment.

The study consisted of two sequential feeding trials. Trial 1 involved 500 Lohmann LSL white pullets randomly allocated into 10 dietary treatment groups, each containing 50 birds subdivided into 10 replicates of 5 hens. This trial lasted from 34 to 69 weeks of age, covering the initial laying period. Treatments included a control basal diet, three different doses of a local probiotic administered in drinking water (50, 100, and 150 mL per 1.5 L), three levels of fermented red mold rice supplementation (2, 4, and 8 g/kg feed), and three levels of Ziziphus leaf powder addition (5, 10, and 20 g/kg feed). The objective was to assess the individual effects of each additive on production performance, immune response, and antioxidant status. Based on the outcomes of Trial 1, Trial 2 was conducted using the most effective treatment levels combined into a formulated feed additive mix. The bird numbers for Trial 2 were similar to Trial 1, and the trial duration aligned with a full laying cycle to comprehensively evaluate the combined effects. The doses in Trial 2 were selected based on their demonstrated cholesterol-lowering efficacy, immune enhancement, and antioxidant benefits from Trial 1, focusing on optimization of positive effects without adverse impacts.

Housing of pullets and laying hens

The experiment was conducted in two closed system poultry houses, both fully insulated and electrically heated. The first house, referred to as the pullet house, was used to raise birds from day 1 to 19 weeks of age. The second house, designated as the layer house, housed birds from 20 weeks of age until the end of the experiment. Each house was equipped with cooling pads on one side and fans on the opposite side to provide proper cooling and ventilation. In the pullet house, Lohmann LSL light pullets were housed at a density providing 333 to 364 cm2/ pullet.

At 19 weeks of age, the pullets were transferred to the layer house. Two three-deck-high laying cage batteries were used. The cage, measuring 60 cm in length × 50 cm in width × 44 cm in height, housed five hens per cage, providing 1000 cm2/hen. Artificial bulbs were used as the source of light. The average initial body weight across the birds was approximately 40.0 ± 2.5 g. Birds were randomly allocated to treatment groups ensuring no significant differences in initial body weight between groups (ANOVA, p > 0.05). Feed additive details

Ziziphus Leaves

Ziziphus leaves used in this research were procured as crude leaves collected from improved cultivars of Z. mauritiana and Z. xylopyrus that had been previously evaluated and are maintained by the Environmental and Life Science Research Center at Kuwait Institute of Scientific Research, (KISR), Kuwait. The leaves were prepared as a crude dried powder; fresh leaves were dried and ground prior to incorporation into the basal diet. No chemical extraction was performed for the primary trial. Dried leaf powder contained bioactive compounds such as flavonoids like quercetin and kaempferol, along with saponins, alkaloids, and tannins, as well as vitamins C, A, B-complex, and minerals including calcium, magnesium, and potassium. The inclusion levels tested were 5, 10, and 20 g per kg of feed (g/kg), corresponding to treatments TRT 8, TRT 9, and TRT 10, respectively.

Probiotic mix

The locally produced probiotic mixture, developed by the Kuwait Institute for Scientific Research Biotechnology Department contained eleven lactic acid bacterial strains isolated from native Kuwaiti chickens, including Lactobacillus plantarum, L. acidophilus, and L. salivarius variants, and was administered as a liquid suspension. The mixture was supplied as a liquid suspension with a viable cell concentration of approximately 1 × 10^9 colony-forming units (CFU) per milliliter, suspended in a protective aqueous carrier containing glycerol and skim milk powder to preserve viability. The suspension was administered freshly mixed in drinking water at daily doses of 50, 100, and 150 mL per 1.5 L water, ensuring consistent probiotic delivery. The probiotic was expected to enhance gut health by creating an acidic intestinal environment that promoted beneficial bacteria proliferation and suppressed pathogens like E. coli and Salmonella through competitive exclusion and increased short-chain fatty acid production, all without adversely affecting feed conversion ratios or egg production rates.

Fermented red mold rice

The fermented red mold rice was commercially purchased from Kuwait. Fermented red mold rice derived from Monascus purpureus fermentation and containing the natural statin-like compound monacolin K, served as the commercial reference standard and demonstrated the most potent cholesterol-lowering effects among individual treatments when supplemented at 2, 4, or 8 g per kilogram of feed. This additive improved antioxidant capacity by increasing liver glutathione peroxidase activity, enhanced gut microbial diversity, reduced cecal pH to optimal levels and intensified yolk coloration, though it contributed significantly to feed costs compared to the probiotic alternative.

Diets and feeding program

The pullets were fed, ad libitum, corn- or soy-based diet, a diet that meets the National Research Council (NRC) and Kuwait’s environment requirements. The pullets were fed grower ration from day one until 8 weeks of age (18.5% protein, 2750 kcal kg), developer ration from 8 to 16 weeks of age (14.5% protein, 2750 kcal kg), and pre-lay from 16 to 24 weeks of age (17.5% protein, 2750 kcal kg), and laying ration from 24 weeks of age to the end of the laying period (18.0% protein, 2900 kcal kg). A basal diet was fed until the hens reached 33 weeks of age. Subsequently, the experimental diets were started when the chickens reached 34 weeks of age.

Data recording and sample collection

Mortality, temperature, and humidity were recorded daily. Information was gathered periodically on body weight, weekly feed consumption, and mortality until chicks were 5 months to 19 weeks old; thereafter, these pullets were moved to the egg laying house. For laying hens, which represent an extension of the previous pullet production period, information was gathered on the age at the start of the first egg-laying period, age at sexual maturity, age at peak production, number of eggs produced daily, percentage of egg production per week, weekly feed consumption, and percentage of mortality.

Production parameters for pullets

Feed efficiency (FE) was the most important information needed since it provides information on the efficiency of feed utilization for growth during the growing period and for egg production during the laying period. To calculate FE, data on body weight and feed consumption were required during the pullets growing period from day 1 until 19–20 weeks of age. Therefore, the pullet production data that were collected included body weight and feed consumption. Temperature and relative humidity were recorded daily and adjusted accordingly to avoid stressful conditions in the poultry house, especially during the summer time. The standard temperature range for newborn chicks is between 32 and 35 °C, with a gradual reduction of 3 °C per week until the normal temperature of approximately 23–26 °C is reached. Relative humidity was maintained between 50 and 65% to optimize bird welfare and performance. Mortality was recorded daily. However, these data were not considered, as comparison could not be done because all the pullets were fed the same basal diet. The experimental feeding trial started during the laying period at 33–43 weeks of age.

Production parameters for laying hens

During the laying period, hen-housed percent egg production, egg weight, egg mass, and feed consumption were measured weekly, and feed efficiency was calculated. Egg quality was measured starting at 34 weeks of age. Daily temperature and relative humidity were recorded and were adjusted accordingly. Mortality was monitored and recorded daily.

Hen-Housed Egg Production was determined by recording all eggs collected daily and was expressed on a weekly basis. Hen-housed production was used mainly to summarize the flock’s performance over an entire laying cycle. Since it measured the yield based on the original flock size and not on the current count, it had an economic value33.

Feed Consumption was measured weekly by recording the amount of feed offered to each cage and subtracting the residual feed remaining at the end of the week. Egg weight was measured by weighing all eggs produced within a 24-h period and calculating average egg weight.

Egg quality evaluation

Egg quality was assessed at 4-week intervals throughout the experimental period. Eggs produced within a 24-h period were collected, and a random sample of 10 eggs per treatment was used for egg quality analysis. The evaluated egg quality parameters included egg weight, Haugh unit, shell weight, shell percentage, yolk weight, yolk percentage, albumen weight, and albumen percentage. A multisystem egg analyzer was used to determine egg weight and Haugh unit. Shell thickness, including shell membranes, was measured using a micrometer.

Chicken immune and oxidative status

Sample Collection & Storage: Blood samples were collected from the branchial vein using sterile vacutainer tubes containing K2EDTA anticoagulant. Approximately 8–10 mL of blood was obtained from each bird per sampling occasion. Samples were immediately placed on ice and transported to the KISR physiology laboratory within one hour. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and aliquoted into cryotubes. To preserve enzyme activity and antibody integrity, samples for oxidative and immune assays were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Liver tissue for antioxidant enzyme assays was excised promptly after slaughter, rinsed in ice-cold saline, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until use. Fresh egg samples for lipid and antioxidant analyses were collected and stored at 4 °C, being processed within 24 h; alternatively, samples were frozen at − 20 °C for longer-term storage.

Haemocytometric Analyses: This involved the evaluation and numeration of immune and red blood cells using a computerized haemocytometer. Blood samples were collected from five chickens from each treatment, 8–10 ml of blood were collected in each tube. The samples were instantly analyzed by the Cell-Dyn 3500 Hematology system (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA) to measure the total and differential white blood cells (WBC) and blood quality parameters, including red blood cells (RBC), haemoglobin (HGB), haematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC), red cell distribution width (RDW), and platelet count (PLT).

The hemocytometer was previously maintained and calibrated for the use of poultry blood samples, which needed a special setting because the chicken RBC were nucleated and needed to be dealt with, in a special way, during the blood analysis. A demo of the blood samples was collected from chickens and analyzed by the hemocytometer. The results showed a normal and expected range of the count of the different types of immune cells. The machine was then ready to receive real blood samples for the current project. Figure 2 shows the standardization of the hemocytometer machine. Antioxidant status of sera, liver, and table eggs

In addition to the above mentioned procedures, the antioxidant status was determined by measuring the antioxidant indices in sera, livers, and table eggs of the laying hens supplemented with different dietary treatments. Total superoxide dismutase (TSOD) activity in liver was measured using Ransod kit from Randox Laboratories, UK, as described by Habibi et al.48. Liver glutathione peroxidase (GPx) was measured based on the protocol of Paglia et al.49. The activity of catalase (CAT) enzyme in liver was determined using the method of Aebi et al.50. Malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) were measured based on the protocols of Habibi et al.48 and El-Bahr et al.51.

Dietary modulation of egg lipids and cholesterol with ziziphus, probiotics, and red mold rice

The lipid composition of egg samples was investigated using Gas Chromatography (GC) technique. A Hewlett Packard (HP) 6890 series gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Basingstoke, UK) equipped with an FID detector and a capillary column was used. Calibration was performed using a certified reference standard mixture of fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) (Supelco 37 Component FAME Mix, Sigma-Aldrich, Catalog No. CRM47885). Internal standards were added to each sample to ensure quantification accuracy. Replicates were run to confirm reproducibility, and retention times were matched with standards. For lipid extraction, 5-ml chloroform: methanol (2:1 v/v, 50 mg/l butylatedhydroxytoluene (BHT), and 1 ml 1 M NaCl were added to the sample in a 10-ml glass tube. Tubes were vortexed and centrifuged at 930 × g for 10 min. The bottom layer was transferred to a glass tube and evaporated to dryness under nitrogen. For transmethylation, 400-μl toluene (+ BHT 50 mg/l) and 800 μl of 1.5% sulphuric acid in methanol were added to the dried samples. The tubes were vortexed and heated in a water bath at 70 °C for 1 h. After cooling, 2-ml neutralizing agent (0.1 M K2CO3, 0.1 M KHCO3) and 2-ml hexane were added, and the suspension was mixed and centrifuged at 1162 × g for 10 min. The upper layer was transferred to a glass tube and evaporated to dryness under nitrogen. The extract was suspended in 100-μl hexane, vortexed and transferred to a GC vial. Samples were run against a known fatty acid methyl esters standard solution52. Samples were analyzed in duplicates. Samples were collected on a monthly basis for three occasions.

Organoleptic evaluation of the produced table egg

Sensory evaluation was conducted by 20 untrained panelists to assess the impact of dietary treatments on egg acceptability and palatability. Hard-boiled eggs were prepared by boiling for 15 min and maintained in water at approximately 35 °C until serving53. Each panelist received peeled egg samples individually placed on plastic plates corresponding to all experimental treatments. Samples were randomly arranged and labeled using one-digit randomized codes representing the 10 treatments, and presented in randomized order to minimize bias. Apple juice was provided as a palate cleanser between samples. Panelists evaluated warm, halved eggs for color, smell, texture, taste, and overall acceptability using a hedonic scale. Testing was carried out in a room maintained at 24 °C, separate from the cooking area and under standardized lighting to avoid visual bias in yolk and albumen color. Sessions were scheduled from 11:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m. The organoleptic assessment was conducted for both fresh eggs and eggs stored for 28 days at 4 °C.

Second feeding trial using a mix of ziziphus, probiotic, and the fermented red rice

The best levels of the aforementioned treatments were used to formulate a mix of Ziziphus, probiotic, and the fermented red rice in the second feeding trial. This mix included probiotic at 150 mL in drinking water, fermented red mold rice at 8 g/kg of feed, and ziziphus leaves at 20 g/kg of feed.

Economic cost study

An Economic analysis in terms of profitability and feasibility of incorporating experimental treatments into commercial layer diets was assessed. The proposed dietary treatments were expected to cause changes in the costs of production. However, they were also expected to enhance productivity in terms of yields per bird, and hence, additional revenues to the poultry business. The decision to use the proposed dietary treatments was governed by the cost of this product and the ease of its applications against its potential benefits to improve production performance and to increase overall profits. The economic impact assessment linked the project outcome with broader food security concerns in Kuwait. From this point of view, the project had a strategic sense by studying the possibility of using different feed alternatives in different types of treatments for the laying hens. The economic impact assessment was started by calculating the cost of the dietary treatments. Second, the effect of the biological indicators was presented, and the impacts of the intervention, through the introduction of new feed alternatives on different performance parameters were measured. Finally, the best alternative was selected from the different dietary treatments used in the study by considering the previous measures.

Statistical analysis

Interpretation and statistical analysis of data were performed using the multivariate repeated measures analysis of variance (MANOVA), including linear and nonlinear correlation analysis to test any relationship among possible dependent variables. Means of different treatments were separated using Duncan’s multiple range test with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Conclusion

Supplementation of laying hen diets with Ziziphus leaves, locally produced probiotics, and fermented red mold rice effectively reduced egg yolk cholesterol without compromising laying performance, egg quality, or sensory attributes. These dietary strategies enhanced antioxidant status and supported immune function, demonstrating their potential as sustainable and locally sourced alternatives for the production of low-cholesterol table eggs. The combined dietary application showed practical feasibility and economic relevance, supporting its potential use in commercial poultry production systems.
