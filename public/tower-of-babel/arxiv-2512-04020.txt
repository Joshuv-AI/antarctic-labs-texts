On topological and algebraic structures of categorical random variables Thanks: Submitted to the editors September 2025.
Inocencio Ortiz, Santiago Gómez-Guerrero, Christian E. Schaerer

Abstract
Based on entropy and symmetrical uncertainty (SU), we define a metric for categorical random variables and show that this metric can be promoted into an appropriate quotient space of categorical random variables. Moreover, we also show that there is a natural commutative monoid structure in the same quotient space, which is compatible with the topology induced by the metric, in the sense that the monoid operation is continuous.



Correlation, entropy, similarity metric, distance metric, monoid

Introduction

Symmetric Uncertainty has been introduced in
[
6
]
, and it is defined as

SU(X,Y):=2\left[1-\frac{H(X,Y)}{H(X)+H(Y)}\right],

(1)

where
X,Y
are categorical random variables,
H(\cdot)
is the entropy, and
H(\cdot,\cdot)
is the joint entropy.

Generalization of SU for the multivariate case, as well as its properties, and, in particular, its potential to define a distance metric among categorical random variables have been explored in
[
4
,
5
,
12
,
11
]
. Indeed,
[
5
]
bring us to realize that entropic correlation is an example of similarity.

In this work, we further explore some properties of
SU
in two directions. On one hand we explore some properties of the topology introduced by the metric defined in terms of
SU
on a given space of categorical random variables. On the other hand, we explore some algebraic properties of a
joint
operation defined on the same space where the metric is defined. We conclude that this operation endows the metric space with a commutative monoid structure, and that it is compatible with the metric topology, in the sense that it is a continuous map.

Background: Entropic Correlation and Symmetric Uncertainty

In this section we recall the fundamental concepts involved in the definition of
SU
, as well as its main properties which we are going to need.

Let us consider a fixed probability space
(D,\Omega_{D},P)
, and let
X:D\to\Sigma_{X}
be a categorical (non-numeric, nominal or cualitative) random variable with possible values
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
and probability mass function
P_{X}
. The entropy
H
of the variable
X
is a measure of the uncertainty in predicting the value of
X
, or
size of the uncertainty
on
X
, and is defined as
[
9
]
:

H(X):=-\sum_{i}P_{X}(x_{i})\log_{2}(P_{X}(x_{i})),

(2)

where
P_{X}(x_{i})
is the prior probability of the value
x_{i}
of
X
. Any base could be used for the log, but base 2 is used for historical reasons connected to information theory
[
10
]
.
H(X)
can also be interpreted as a measure of the amount of information a discrete random variable
X
requires for describing its behavior; or more compactly, as the variety inherent to
X
.

Given another categorical random variable
Y
, the conditional entropy
H(X|Y)
quantif
ies the amount of information needed to describe the outcome of
X
given that the value of
Y
is known. It is def
ined as follows:

H(X|Y):=-\sum_{j}\left[P_{Y}(y_{j})\sum_{i}P(x_{i}|y_{j})\log_{2}(P(x_{i}|y_{j}))\right],

(3)

where
P(x_{i}|y_{j})
is the posterior probability of a value
x_{i}
for the variable
X
given that the value of variable
Y
is
y_{j}
.

Using entropy and conditional entropy, the concept of
joint entropy
is introduced, which for two categorical random variables
X,Y
is defined by

H(X,Y)=H(Y)+H(X|Y).

(4)

For more details, we refer the reader to
[
1
]
, page 15.

Mutual Information
. Using entropy and conditional entropy introduced above, the mutual information

I(X;Y)

MI(X|Y)
is introduced
[

]
. Mutual Information measures the reduction in uncertainty about the value of
X
when the value of
Y
is known, and is defined as:

MI(X|Y):=H(X)-H(X|Y).

(5)

Since
MI
measures how much the information provided by
Y
makes it easier to predict the value of
X
, it can be used as a
measure of correlation
. It should be noted that:

•

if
X
and
Y
are independent then in equation (
5
)
H(X|Y)=H(X)
, and hence
MI(X|Y)
= 0. That is, under independence, knowing
Y
gives no information about the value of
X
; and

•

if
X
and
Y
are fully correlated then
H(X|Y)=0
and hence
MI(X|Y)=H(X)
applying equation (
5
).

Mutual information taken as a measure of correlation, makes it possible to compare correlations with one another. For example, for any random variables
X
,
Z
and
Y
,
MI(X|Y)>MI(Z|Y)
means that when knowing the value of
Y
the reduction in uncertainty about
X
is greater than the reduction in uncertainty about
Z
, which is conducive to stating that
X
is more correlated to
Y
than it is
Z
.

By combining equations (
4
) and (
5
), one readily obtains

MI(X|Y)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)

(6)

and since
H(X,Y)
is symmetric by direct substitution of
X,Y
into equation (1), it results that
MI(X|Y)
is symmetric as well, a quite convenient property for a paired measure. A formal proof can be found, for example, as Theorem 1 in
[
3
]
.

Symmetric Uncertainty
. On the other hand, like with other informational association measures, MI tends to increase its value when the number of categories in
X
and/or
Y
increases, that is, it is biased towards high cardinality features
[
8
]
. Therefore, seeking to compensate for such bias, MI has to be normalized by the sum of entropies of the features. This measure, called
Symmetric Uncertainty

[
6
]
is expressed as:

SU(X,Y):=2\left[\frac{MI(X|Y)}{H(X)+H(Y)}\right]

(7)

or, combining with equation (
6
),

SU(X,Y):=2\left[1-\frac{H(X,Y)}{H(X)+H(Y)}\right].

(8)

Revisiting the two bullet points in the previous subsection, note now that:

•

if
X
and
Y
are independent then, from
MI(X|Y)
= 0, it follows that
SU(X,Y)=0
; and

•

if
X
and
Y
are completely correlated then from equation (
5
), it is obtained
MI(X|Y)=H(X)
. By symmetry of MI,
MI(Y|X)=H(Y)
and so
SU(X,Y)=1
.

As a final observation regarding
SU
, we recall from
[
12
]
the following results.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any two categorical random variables, we have:

1)

0\leq SU(X,Y)\leq 1

2)

The Entropic Ratio, defined as

R(X,Y):=\frac{H(X,Y)}{H(X)+H(Y)},

(9)

where
H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y|X)
,
satisfies the inequality

\frac{1}{2}\leq R(X,Y)\leq 1.

It will be convenient to relate the concept of entropy of a categorical random variable to that of the entropy of a partition, as follows: let us consider again our fixed probability space
(D,\Omega_{D},P)
. A partition of
D
is a countable collection of disjoint measurable subsets of
D
, whose union has measure
1
. We will denote by
{\cal D}
the trivial partition, namely
{\cal D}=\{D\}
. Observe that we will only need finite partitions.

Given a partition
{\cal Q}
of
D
,
the entropy of
{\cal Q}
is defined by

H({\cal Q}):=-\sum_{Q\in{\cal Q}}P(Q)\log(P(Q)).

Given two partitions
{\cal Q}
and
{\cal R}
of
D
, we say that
\cal{Q}
is coarser than
{\cal R}
, (or equivalently that
{\cal R}
is finer than
{\cal Q}
), denoted
{\cal Q}\leq{\cal R}
if every element of
{\cal R}
is contained in some element of
{\cal Q}
. The entropy of
{\cal Q}
relative to
{\cal R}
is defined by

H({\cal Q}|{\cal R}):=-\sum_{Q\in{\cal Q}}\sum_{R\in\cal R}P(Q\cap R)\log\left(\frac{P(Q\cap R)}{P(R)}\right).

Notice that, for any partition
{\cal X}
of
D
we have
H({\cal X}|{\cal D})=H({\cal X})
.

Finally, given two partitions
{\cal Q}
and
{\cal R}
, the partition
{\cal Q}\lor{\cal R}
is defined as the intersection of the elements of
{\cal Q}
with the elements of
{\cal R}
. Notice that we have
{\cal Q}\leq{\cal Q}\lor{\cal R}
as well as
{\cal R}\leq{\cal Q}\lor{\cal R}
.

Regarding these concepts, we have the following technical results.

Lemma 2.2

(Lemma 9.1.5 in
[
14
]
)
.

Given partitions
{\cal X},\cal{Y},{\cal Z}
, with finite entropy, the following hold:

a)

H({\cal X}\lor{\cal Y}|{\cal Z})=H({\cal X}|{\cal Z})+H({\cal Y}|{\cal X}\lor{\cal Z})
.

b)

If
{\cal X}\leq{\cal Y}
, then
H({\cal X}|{\cal Z})\leq H({\cal Y}|{\cal Z})
and
H(\cal{Z}|{\cal X})\geq H(\cal{Z}|{\cal Y})
.

c)

{\cal X}\leq{\cal Y}
if and only if
H({\cal X}|{\cal Y})=0
.

Notice that, taking
{\cal Z}={\cal D}
in item
a)
we get, for any two partition
{\cal X}
and
{\cal Y}
:

H({\cal X\lor{\cal Y}})=H({\cal X})+H({\cal Y|X)}\implies H({\cal X})\leq H({\cal X}\lor{\cal Y})

(10)

Also, from
{\cal Y}\leq{\cal Y}\lor{\cal Z}
and
{\cal Z}\leq{\cal Y}\lor{\cal Z}
we get (applying item
b)
)

H({\cal X}|{\cal Y}\lor{\cal Z})\leq H({\cal X}|{\cal Y}),\quad H({\cal X}|{\cal Y}\lor{\cal Z})\leq H({\cal X}|{\cal Z})

(11)

Now, if

X:D\to\Sigma_{X}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}\}

is a categorical random variable defined on
D
, then it defines a partition
{\cal X}
of
D
given by the inverse images of
x_{i}
, i.e.:

{\cal X}:=\{X^{-1}(x_{i}),i=1,2,\ldots,m\}.

The basic observation here is that the concept of entropy of a partition, and the entropy of a partition relative to another partition, recover the corresponding concepts regarding categorical random variables. In particular, given two categorical random variables
X
and
Y
defined on
D
, we have

H(X,Y)=H({\cal X}\lor{\cal Y}).

Topological structure of Categorical Variables

In this section we introduce a topology into a set of categorical variables. We will do so by defining a distance metric based on the SU measure. To motivate the definition, we will first explore some examples in order to get an idea of how the SU measure can give us a way to compare categorical variables.

Looking row-wise at the dataset, any two observations (rows) can be compared to determine their similarity. If two rows are equal throughout all their values, we say that they are in full similarity or equivalently, we say that the distance between these observations is zero. If not all the values on the two rows are equal, several techniques are available to compute distances in this

We can also look at the same dataset in a column-wise manner

Is it possible to find similarities between columns? Since each column measures a specific characteristic, and perhaps has been recorded in its own unit of measurement, we can rarely expect to find column similarity in the same way that we find similar rows. However, here one can think of similarity in a slightly different way. It is known that columns may relate to each other via correlations. Correlation does not necessarily imply causality, but it is reasonable to view high correlation between two variables
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
as an indicator of being linked to the same phenomenon. Because of this, we may think that if the correlation between
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
is high, then the two variables are “close of each other”; and conversely, low correlation between
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
sets these variables apart.

We address the problem of finding similarities (or from a complementary viewpoint, finding distances) between columns, variables or features of a database. That is, given any two variables of the dataset, what is the similarity between them and how can we calculate it?

For categorical variables, the Symmetrical Uncertainty measure of correlation computes this kind of similarity by comparing the joint entropy of the pair of variables with their individual entropies. So the answer to the above questions is: Yes, similarity expressed as correlation between two columns is computable as the SU. This similarity is based on variables belonging to a group of intertwined characteristics. The next example illustrates how two columns may be viewed as similar.

3.2
Entropic equivalence and the metric structure

In many situations, given a set
\Omega
, it is useful to have a function
f\colon\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
which tell us how similar or dissimilar are any given pair of elements of
\Omega
. One such a function is the well known
distance metric
, which is any function
d\colon\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following conditions:

1.

d(x,y)\geq 0
(non-negativity),

2.

d(x,y)=d(y,x)
(symmetry),

3.

d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)
(triangle inequality),

4.

d(x,y)=0
if and only if
x=y
. (identity of indiscernibles)

Less standardized is the concept of a
similarity metric
. Here we recall the definition given by Chen et al in
[
2
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a set
\Omega
, a real-valued function
s(x,y)
defined on the Cartesian product
\Omega\times\Omega
is a Similarity Metric if, for any
x,y,z\in\Omega
, it satisfies the following conditions:

1.

s(x,y)=s(y,x)
(symmetry)

2.

s(x,x)\geq 0
(non-negativity)

3.

s(x,x)\geq s(x,y)
(self-identity)

4.

s(x,y)+s(y,z)\leq s(x,z)+s(y,y)
(triangle inequality)

5.

s(x,x)=s(y,y)=s(x,y)
if and only if
x=y
. (identity of indiscernibles)

In
[
2
]
, the authors also gave conditions for a similarity metric to yield a distance metric. More concretely, they established a precise relationship between normalized distances and similarity metrics. Here,
normalized
means that both the distance and the similarity map into
[0,1]
. For the reader convenience, let us recall their result here.

Lemma 3.2

(
Corollary 1 in
[
2
]
).

If
s(x,y)
is a normalized similarity metric and for any
x
,
s(x,x)=1
, then
\frac{1}{2}(1-s(x,y))
is a normalized distance
metric. If, in addition,
s(x,y)\geq 0
, then
1-s(x,y)
is a normalized distance metric. If
d(x,y)
is a normalized distance metric, then

1-d(x,y)
is a normalized similarity metric.

Looking at pairs of categorical variables, we know that the more correlated two variables are, the greater their SU value, hence SU appears to be a similarity measure. Showing that SU is a similarity metric requires the concept of
indiscernibility
defined for categorical random variables.
Let us make this observation precise.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\cal C
be a set of categorical random variables defined on a sample space
D
. If
X\colon D\to\Sigma_{X}
and
Y\colon D\to\Sigma_{Y}
are two elements in
{\cal C}
, we say that they are
indiscernible
if they have the same histogram, modulo a permutation of the categories.

Let us recall that, given
X\colon D\to\Sigma_{X}
, the histogram is just the graph of the probability mass function
\mu_{X}\colon\Sigma_{X}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

P_{X}(S)=P(X^{-1}(S))=\sum_{x\in S}P(X=x).

Thus, in the previous definition, we are asking for the graphs of
P_{X}
and
P_{Y}
to coincide after, if necessary, relabeling or reordering the categories in one of the sets
\Sigma_{X}
or
\Sigma_{Y}
. In particular, for
X
and
Y
to be indiscernible, their codomains
\Sigma_{X}
and
\Sigma_{Y}
must have the same cardinality. In the following, we will refer to the indiscernibility condition as the existence of a bijection
\Sigma_{X}\to\Sigma_{Y}
and that
P_{X}=P_{Y}
.

The indiscernibility just defined introduces an equivalence relation in
\cal C
. Let us denote by
\overline{{\cal C}}
the space of equivalence classes. Notice that any two equivalence classes may have the same number of categories or a different number of categories.

Now we can state the following result, where, for simplicity, we will omit the bracket notation for equivalence classes.

Theorem 3.4
.

Given a space
\mathcal{C}
of categorical random variables, the SU as defined in equation (
7
), induces via (

) a positive normalized similarity metric in the space
\overline{\cal{C}}
. That is, for any
X
,
Y
and
Z
in
\overline{\cal{C}}
, the following conditions hold:

1.

Symmetry:
SU(X,Y)=SU(Y,X),

2.

Reflexivity:
SU(X,X)\geq 0,

3.

Self-similarity:
SU(X,X)\geq SU(X,Y),

4.

Triangle inequality:
SU(X,Y)+SU(Y,Z)\leq SU(X,Z)+SU(Y,Y),

5.

Identity of indiscernibles:
SU(X,X)=SU(Y,Y)=SU(X,Y)
if and only if
X=Y
.

6.

Positivity and normality:
SU(X,Y)\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

First, note that if
X
,
Y
and
Z
are any categorical random variables, SU as defined in equation (
7
) is a composite random variable, and at the same time it is a real-valued function on the Cartesian product
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
.

Condition 1
is straightforward by substitution into equation (
7
). Likewise, for the reflexivity
condition 2
, using equation (
7
) we get the self-similarity
SU(X,X)=1\geq 0
.

The self-similarity
condition 3
establishes that
SU(X,X)
is greater than or equal to any other value of SU(X, Y). This is indeed true as we have just seen that
SU(X,X)=1
which is the maximum value achievable by the measure.

Equivalent to the triangle inequality in a distance metric, the Triangle Inequality (
Condition 4
) states that the similarity between
X
and
Z
through
Y
is no greater than the direct similarity between
X
and
Z
plus the self similarity of
Y
. Let us start by rewriting inequalities for the two-variable cases, using entropic ratio notation (see Definition in Lemma
2.1
). For the variables
X
,
Y
, and
Z
, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\leq R(X,Y)\leq 1,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\leq R(Y,Z)\leq 1,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\leq R(X,Z)\leq 1.

Also note that
SU(X,Y)=2(1-R(X,Y))
. We now show Condition 4 in four steps.

a. Working with corresponding sides, add the first two inequalities above and then subtract the third,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq R(X,Y)+R(Y,Z)\leq 2

(13)

\displaystyle 1/2

\displaystyle\leq R(X,Y)+R(Y,Z)-R(X,Z)\leq 1

(14)

b. From here on use only the left and the middle members. Transpose two terms to the left, getting

\displaystyle 1/2-R(X,Y)-R(Y,Z)

\displaystyle\leq-R(X,Z)

(15)

c. Add (1 + ½) to each side, then multiply each side by 2.

\displaystyle 1-R(X,Y)+1-R(Y,Z)

\displaystyle\leq 1-R(X,Z)+(1/2)

(16)

\displaystyle 2(1-R(X,Y))+2(1-R(Y,Z))

\displaystyle\leq 2(1-R(X,Z))+2(1/2)

(17)

d. Remembering that
SU(Y,Y)
= 1,

\displaystyle SU(X,Y)+SU(Y,Z)

\displaystyle\leq SU(X,Z)+1

(18)

\displaystyle SU(X,Y)+SU(Y,Z)

\displaystyle\leq SU(X,Z)+SU(Y,Y)

(19)

Condition 5
results from applying
SU(X,X)
= 1 once again, and noting that
X=Y
is understood as equality in entropy.

Finally, notice that
Condition 6
was already established in Lemma
2.1
. This finalizes the proof.
∎

As a direct consequence of this Theorem, together with Lemma
3.2
, we have the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.5
.

Given a space
\cal C
of categorical random variables, let
\overline{\cal C}
be its coset defined by indiscernibility. The quantity
1-SU(X,Y)
is a normalized distance metric in
\overline{\cal C}
.

Let us notice that, since we are working with categorical variables, it may seem that the metric topology just introduced is discrete. However, this is not the case, as we show in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.6
.

The metric topology introduced in
\overline{\cal C}
is not discrete.

Proof.

Indeed, to see this, just consider two categorical random variables,
X
and
Y
, with categories
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
and
\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
, and probabilities
\{p_{1},1-p_{1}\}
and
\{q_{1}=p_{1}+\epsilon,q_{2}=1-q_{1}\}
. Then, we see that
H(X|Y)=0
, and thus

SU(X,Y)=2\frac{H(X)}{H(X)+H(Y)}.

Since
H(X)
and
H(Y)
are continuous functions of
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
, we can make
H(X)+H(Y)\approx 2H(X)
, resulting in
1-SU(X,Y)
as small as we want by letting
\epsilon\to 0
.
∎

4
Algebraic Structure of Categorical Variables

Let
A
and
B
, be two categorical random variables defined over a common sample space
D
. To fix the idea, let us assume that
A
stands for income, which we consider to have three possible values, namely
\{l,m,h\}
for “low”, “middle” and “high”, and that
B
stands for owning or not some house, which we consider to have two possible values, namely
\{y,n\}
, for “yes” and “no”. It is quite common to produce, out of
A
and
B
, another random variable
C
, which we will denote as
C=A*B
, by the following rule: Given any
p\in D
, then
C(p):=(A(p),B(p))
.

Thus, if
p\in D
is an individual with middle income, and owns some house, then
C(p)=(m,y)
. Let us observe that the possible values for
C
is the set

\{(l,y),(l,n),(m,y),(m,n),(h,y),(h,n)\}.

If we denote by
\Sigma_{A}
the codomain of the random variable
A
and by
\Sigma_{B}
the codomain of the random variable
B
, then we get that the codomain of
C
is
\Sigma_{C}=\Sigma_{A}\times\Sigma_{B}
. Let us also recall that, if
P
is the probability measure in the sample space
D
, and
P_{A}
,
P_{B}
are the probability mass function of
A
and
B
, respectively, then, in the measurable product space
\Sigma_{A}\times\Sigma_{B}
we have the joint probability mass function
P_{A}\times P_{B}\colon\Sigma_{A}\times\Sigma_{B}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

P_{A}\times P_{B}(x,y)=P(A=x\wedge B=y)=P(A=x|B=y)\cdot P(B=y).

Thus, if we denote by
{\cal C}
the set of all categorical random variables defined over
D
, what the previous procedure yields is an internal law of composition in
{\cal C}
.

In this section we establish that this operation introduces in
{\cal C}
an algebraic structure (more specifically, in the quotient space
\overline{\cal C}
given by the indiscernibility equivalence relation), and explore some of its basic properties.

4.1
Formal definitions

Let us fix again the space
{\cal C}
, and recall that it consists of all categorical random variables defined on the sample space
D
. Let us also denote again by
\overline{\cal C}
the quotient space given by the indiscernibility equivalence relation in
{\cal C}
.

Definition 4.1
.

Given
A\colon D\to\Sigma_{A}
and
B\colon D\to\Sigma_{B}
, two categorical variables in
{\cal C}
. Then we define a new categorical random variable

C\colon D\to\Sigma_{C}=\Sigma_{A}\times\Sigma_{B};C(p):=(A(p),B(p)).

Let us denote this by
C=A*B
, and call it the
joint
of
A
and
B
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Given
A,B\in{\cal C}
, the operation

[A]*[B]:=[A*B]

is well defined.

Proof.

Indeed, let
A^{\prime}\colon D\to\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}
and
B^{\prime}\colon D\to\Sigma_{B^{\prime}}
be two categorical random variables equivalent to
A
and
B
, respectively. Then, the bijections
\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\to\Sigma_{A}
and
\Sigma_{B^{\prime}}\to\Sigma_{B}
yield a bijection
\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\times\Sigma_{B^{\prime}}\to\Sigma_{A}\times\Sigma_{B}
. Also, since
A\sim A^{\prime}
, we have
P_{A}=P_{A^{\prime}}
and similarly,
P_{B}=P_{B^{\prime}}
. It follows that the joint probability measure
P_{A^{\prime}*B^{\prime}}
and
P_{A*B}
are also equal. Thus, we get that
A^{\prime}*B^{\prime}
and
A*B
are equivalent.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

The joint operation
*\colon\overline{\cal C}\times\overline{\cal C}\to\overline{\cal C}
is associative and commutative.

Proof.

Consider
[A],[B],[C]\in\overline{\cal C}
, and let us pick the representatives

A\colon D\to\Sigma_{A};\quad B\colon D\to\Sigma_{B};\quad C\colon D\to\Sigma_{C}.

Then, we have
B*C:D\to\Sigma_{B}\times\Sigma_{C}
, as well as
A*B\colon D\to\Sigma_{A}\times\Sigma_{B}
. Then, we get

A*(B*C)\colon D\to\Sigma_{A}\times(\Sigma_{B}\times\Sigma_{C})=\Sigma_{A}\times\Sigma_{B}\times\Sigma_{C}

and also

(A*B)*C\colon D\to(\Sigma_{A}\times\Sigma_{B})\times\Sigma_{C}=\Sigma_{A}\times\Sigma_{B}\times\Sigma_{C}.

Finally, we also have

\begin{split}[A*(B*C)](p)&=(A(p),(B*C)(p))=(A(p),(B(p),C(p)))\\
&=((A(p),B(p)),C(p))=[(A*B)*C](p).\end{split}

(20)

Hence, we get
A*(B*C)=(A*B)*C
. Passing to equivalence classes, we get the associativity

[A]*([B*C])=([A]*[B])*[C].

For the commutativity, let us observe that
B*A\colon D\to\Sigma_{B}\times\Sigma_{A}
. Hence, we have a bijection between
\Sigma_{A*B}
and
\Sigma_{B*A}
. Also, for the probability mass function we have

P_{A*B}(x,y)=P(A=x\wedge B=y)=P(B=y\wedge A=x)=P_{B*A}(y,x).

Thus, we get that
A*B
and
B*A
are equivalent. Hence

[A]*[B]=[A*B]=[B*A]=[B]*[A],

as claimed.
∎

So far, we have an internal operation in the space
\overline{\cal C}
, which is associative and commutative. Next we identify a categorical variable (or better, an equivalence class of categorical variables) that plays the role of neutral element.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\Phi\colon D\to\{\phi\}
be a categorical random variable whose only possible outcome is the singleton
\{\phi\}
. Then, for any other random variable
A\colon D\to\Sigma_{A}
, we have

[A]*[\Phi]=[A].

Proof.

Let us first observe that any random variable on
D
having a singleton as the only possible outcome is equivalent to
\Phi
.

Now we observe that
\Sigma_{A*\Phi}=\Sigma_{A}\times\{\phi\}
, so we have a bijection
\Sigma_{A}\to\Sigma_{A*\Phi}
given by
x\mapsto(x,\phi)
. Also, the probability mass function of
\Phi
is
P_{\Phi}(\phi)=1
, thus we get

P_{A*\Phi}(x,\phi)=P(A=x|\Phi=\phi)\cdot P(\Phi=\phi)=P(A=x)=P_{A}(x).

Hence,
A*\Phi
and
A
have the same histogram.

Thus, we conclude that
A*\Phi
is equivalent to
A
. It follows that

[A]*[\Phi]=[A*\Phi]=[A],

as claimed.
∎

Let us summarize the previous discussion in the following theorem

Theorem 4.5
.

The joint operation on
\overline{{\cal C}}
yields a commutative monoid structure.

4.2
Compatibility of the algebraic and topological structures

Let us recall that, in the previous section, we found a metric topology structure in the set
\overline{{\cal C}}
, given by the metric
d=1-SU
. Now we will show that the algebraic structure given on the same set by the joint operation and the topological structure in
{\overline{\cal C}}
are compatible.

To do so, let us first rewrite the distance function on a more symmetric form. For that, we recall the symmetry of mutual information, namely:

M(X|Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)=H(Y)-H(Y|X)=M(Y|X).

Hence, for the distance
d=1-SU
we get

\begin{split}d(X,Y)=1-SU(X,Y)&=1-\frac{2MI(X|I)}{H(X)+X(Y)}\\
&=\frac{H(X)+H(Y)-H(X)+H(X|Y)-H(Y)+H(Y|X)}{H(X)+H(Y)}\\
&=\frac{H(X|Y)+H(Y|X)}{H(X)+H(Y).}\end{split}

(21)

Also, let us observe that, given categorical random variables
X\colon D\to\Sigma_{X}
and
Y\colon D\to\Sigma_{Y}
, we have

H(X,Y)=H(X*Y)=H({\cal X}\lor{\cal Y}),

where
{\cal X}
and
{\cal Y}
are the partitions of
D
associated to the variables
A
and
Y
, respectively.

Now we have all the tools in place to prove the aforementioned compatibility.

Theorem 4.6
.

The joint operation

*\colon\overline{{\cal C}}\times\overline{{\cal C}}\to\overline{{\cal C}}

is continuous with respect to the topology on
{\overline{{\cal C}}}
given by
d=1-SU
and the natural product topology induced in
\overline{{\cal C}}\times\overline{{\cal C}}
.

Proof.

Let
X,Y,Z,W
be categorical random variables on
D
, for simplicity, we will avoid the equivalence class notation, and we will also use the same symbols for their associated partitions. Then we have

d(X*Y,Z*W)=\frac{H(X*Y|Z*W)+H(Z*W|X*Y)}{H(X*Y)+H(Z*W)}

(22)

Applying item
a)
from Lemma
2.2
, and the inequality given in equation (
11
) to the terms in the numerator, we get:

\begin{split}H(X*Y|Z*W)&=H(X|Z*W)+H(Y|X*Z*W)\leq H(X|Z)+H(Y|W)\\
H(Z*W|X*Y)&=H(Z|X*Y)+H(W|Z*X*Y)\leq H(Z|X)+H(W|Y).\end{split}

(23)

Thus, we get

d(X*Y,Z*W)\leq\frac{H(X|Z)+H(Z|X)}{H(X*Y)+H(Z*W)}+\frac{H(Y|W)+H(W|Y)}{H(X*Y)+H(Z*W)}

(24)

Now we apply inequality (
10
) to each summand in the denominators, to get

\begin{split}d(X*Y,Z*W)&\leq\frac{H(X|Z)+H(Z|X)}{H(X)+H(Z)}+\frac{H(Y|W)+H(W|Y)}{H(Y)+H(W)}\\
&=d(X,Z)+d(Y,W)\end{split}

(25)

Thus, the mapping is contractive, hence, continuous.
∎

5
Concluding Remarks

In this paper we have demonstrated that
1-SU
, where
SU
is the entropic correlation between categorical random variables, is a distance metric, thus endowing the space of categorical random variables with a topological structure. We have also showed that a natural joint operation (denoted
*
) endows the same space with an algebraic structure, concretely, a commutative monoid structure. Finally, we have proven that the topological and algebraic structures are compatible, given that the joint operation is continuous with respect to the topological structure.

The compatibility provides a smooth and straightforward way for the user to interpret and understand the value output from SU. This way, SU has gained intuitivity in the view of the statistics practitioner, giving everyone the ability to operate with and employ this entropic (non-parametric) correlation in much the same way as the Pearson (parametric) correlation has been employed throughout the years.

Equipped with compatible topology and algebraic structure,
SU
further formalizes CRVs as repositories and proper representations of the concepts conveyed by the variable’s categories. Thus, qualitative variables not only can be counted in their categories, but their mutual distances now tell us about similarities or likely associations. These new possibilities and measures are offered in an environment of mathematical rigor.

The authors are now working to extend the findings to the MSU measure on
n
categorical variables. Note that the MSU measure could as well be called Multivariable Entropic Correlation, as uncertainties get mutually cancelled when computing correlation.
