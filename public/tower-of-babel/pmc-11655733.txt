Current status of treatment for malignant ovarian sex cord-stromal tumors
Authors: Fang Chunli, Chen Hailin, Zhou Yingying, Xiao Huajing, Xu Boqun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Malignant ovarian sex cord-stromal tumors belong to a specific type of ovarian epithelial carcinoma, and their prognosis is related to the kind of pathology. Unlike epithelial ovarian cancer, most malignant ovarian sex cord-stromal tumors are low-grade malignant and have a good prognosis. Still, they have a tendency to recur in the long term, and the mortality rate of patients after recurrence is high. Surgery is the preferred treatment for malignant ovarian sex cord-stromal tumors with chemotherapy, and other comprehensive treatments. Recurrent and metastatic malignant ovarian sex cord-stromal tumors usually require systemic adjuvant chemotherapy, and hormonal or targeted therapy may be attempted on an individualized basis in cases where chemotherapy fails.

Ovarian cancer is one of the malignant tumors of the female reproductive system, with the eighth highest incidence and seventh highest mortality rate of female malignant tumors worldwide [1]. Ovarian sex cord-stromal tumors originate from sex cord cells. According to their sources, they can be classified into pure sex cord stromal tumors, pure stromal tumors, and mixed sex cord stromal tumors (Table 1). Malignant ovarian sex cord-stromal tumors (MOSCSTs) account for about 5–8% of all malignant ovarian tumors, with an annual incidence rate of about 2.1/100,000, including adult ovarian granulosa cell tumors (AGCTs), ovarian fibrosarcoma, and poorly differentiated stromal cell tumors (Sertoli-Leydig cell tumors, SLCTs). In contrast, other scarce pathological type, such as malignant steroid cell tumors, have very little information reported on them [2]. Most cases are asymptomatic and slow-growing, but some are aggressive and clinically fatal [3]. We searched terms including the following: “malignant ovarian sex cord-stromal tumors”, “adult granulosa cell tumor of the ovary”, “poorly differentiated stromal cell tumors”, “ovarian fibrosarcoma” and “malignant steroid cell tumors”, through an extensive, electronic search involving the PubMed database, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and the official website of the NCCN. Article types included clinical trials, research articles, reviews, systematic reviews, case reports and official guides. Surgery

Surgery remains the predominant treatment for MOSCSTs [4], including complete staging surgeries (CSS) and debulking surgeries. Complete staging procedures are often performed in early-stage patients, and the scope of surgery varies according to whether or not fertility is required. In addition to differences in uterine and bilateral ovarian-fallopian management, a fully staged procedure requires peritoneal cytologic examinations, inspections of peritoneal surfaces, random peritoneal biopsies, and omentectomy. For the fertility-spring surgeries (FSS), cystectomy (CYS), unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (USO) or bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (BSO) with uterine preservation can be performed according to the patient's condition. And, generally, CSS without fertility requirements means total hysterectomy with unilateral or bilateral salpingooophorectomy (TAH-BSO) with complete staging procedure [5].

Since 60 to 95% of MOSCSTs are diagnosed at early-stage and mostly in premenopausal women, more and more patients want to preserve their reproductive function. FSS is an appropriate option, although the prognosis is still debatable. The option of surgical procedures, histological subtypes, and different stages could be the reasons [5].

For AGCTs, primary surgeries are usually performed with standard staged procedures including TAH-BSO. FSS for patients with early-stage confined to one ovary is feasible according to the current guidelines [6]. Retrospective analytical studies concluded that there was no significant difference between the FSS-groups and TAH-BSO-groups in DFS (disease free survival) [7, 8, 11], while cystectomy-group showed a significantly worse DFS compared to USO [9]. It is therefore considered that FIGO stage IC and cystectomy retained significant predictive value for worse survival. USO should be the preferred procedure instead of cystectomy, and secondary surgical staging is crucial for the unstaged AGCTs [7, 9]. Tumor size and adjuvant chemotherapy were not significantly associated with DFS, yet FIGO stages and staging surgeries were independent risk factors for recurrence [7]. Surgical route, transabdominal or translaparoscopic, does not affect the oncological safety of patients with stage I AGCTs [10]. It's worth mentioning that in case of conservative treatment, an endometrial curettage (or hysterectomy in case of radical management) must be carried out to rule out concurrent endometrial carcinoma in patients with granulosa cell tumor [11].

For the SLCTs, accounting for less than 0.2% of all ovarian cancer, are typically unilateral tumors, and 97.5% are diagnosed at stage I [12]. Retrospective studies reported that the prognosis of SLCTs are closely related to the degree of differentiation and stage of disease. For SLCTs stage IC and tumor differentiation grade 3, the security of FSS requires more data to support and patients undergoing FSS should be extensively counseled and closely monitored [13, 14].

FSS was safe for patients with stage IA MOSCSTs [13], but maybe associated with a worse long-term cancer-specific survival (CSS) compared with TAH-BSO surgery for the larger tumor size [15].

Lymphadenectomy remains an essential part of staging surgery for ovarian malignancies and completely staging surgeries are still recommended in the treatments of malignant sex cord-stromal tumors [16]. Lymph node metastasis not only assists in determining a patient's prognosis but also plays a vital role in assessing the need for adjuvant therapy. However, peritoneally disseminated metastases have been reported to be the main metastatic route for AGCT compared to lymphatic and hematogenous metastases, and many patients with advanced MOSCSTs who undergo lymph node dissection do not have lymph node involvement [17, 18]. Lymphadenectomy, as an important procedure for accurate staging, is also associated with some surgical complications, prolonged operative time, increased blood loss, lymphedema et al. so further validation is needed to determine the survival benefit. A study of high-quality data from multiple national tumor registries spanned almost 25 years showed that patients with apparent stage I–II MOSCSTs were accompanied with a low risk of lymph node metastasis [19]. Recent studies have found no significant difference in tumor recurrence rates between the lymphadenectomy and non-lymphadenectomy groups and no significant relationship between lymphadenectomy and DFS [20]. Independent prognostic factors affecting the survival of MOSCST patients after lymph node dissection include younger age, smaller tumor size, earlier staging, and histology type [21]. Thus in the initial staging surgery of MOSCSTs lymphadenectomy is not recommended due to the extremely low lymph node metastasis rate [6, 17, 19]. The potential contribution of routine lymphadenectomy could be regarded as a predictor for assessing 20-Year Survival in MOSCSTs [21].

Chemotherapy

For MOSCST, platinum-based chemotherapy is recommended for patients at high risk for stage I (ruptured or poorly differentiated ICs or with xenogeneic components) and for patients at stage II and above after initial surgeries [16]. Among all the chemotherapy options including BEP (bleomycin/etoposide/cisplatin), paclitaxel/carboplatin, EP (etoposide/cisplatin), CAP (cyclophosphamide/doxorubicin/cisplatin) et al. BEP is the most preferred regimens but bleomycin should not be given to patients over 40 years old or with pre-existing pulmonary disease [22]. There is also evidence that paclitaxel/carboplatin combination have comparable therapeutic effects and are less toxic [23].

The course of chemotherapy is controversial, and it is recommended that high-risk stage I patients receive three courses of chemotherapy and that patients in stage II and above receive at least three courses of chemotherapy after surgery. The course of treatment for highly staged granular cell tumors was studied, and it was found that six or more courses of treatment were more effective than less than six courses of treatment in terms of tumor recurrence and 5-year survival [24]. Some scholars recommend two courses of consolidation therapy after tumor markers estrogen, inhibin, follicle regulatory protein, luteinizing hormone, and follicle stimulating hormone return to normal [25].

Radiotherapy (RT)

The role of radiotherapy in the treatment of MOSCSTs is unclear, and it is only recommended for patients who are inoperable, have residual lesions after surgery, or are resistant to chemotherapy. It is worth noting that RT techniques have changed completely over the years, so it is difficult to compare results in terms of survival and especially toxicity. Recent data in the literature support the use of stereotactic RT for the treatment of rare gynecologic diseases, both to achieve eradication and to delay systemic therapy in cases of chemotherapy-refractory disease or intolerance to systemic medications [26]. As for the most common malignant ovarian sex cord-stromal tumors, adult ovarian granulosa cell tumors (AGCTs), RT was conducted in unresectable, refractory, recurrent cases, with a CT good partial response (all lesions including index lesion more than 50% decrease) [27], 92–96% GTV (gross tumor volume) reduction and 3-months DFS [28].

Hormone therapy

Effective treatments for progressive and recurrent MOSCSTs are very limited. Currently, post-surgical chemotherapy and radiotherapy are still controversial despite their efficacy. Advantages of hormone therapy over chemotherapy and radiation include that it is well tolerated, applied over a long period of time, has no serious side effects, and may be effective for hormone-sensitive tumors, such as AGCTs. A systematic review retrospectively analyzed 31 patients from 19 studies with an overall response rate of 71%, a complete response rate of 25.8% and a partial response rate of 45.2%. The effects of different hormone treatments varied, with a 100% response rate for aromatase inhibitors versus 0% for tamoxifen, and a mean progression-free survival of 18 months after hormone therapy [29]. Aromatase inhibitors are more effective due to the expression of estrogen receptors in AGCTs, which may be related to their stronger anti-estrogenic effects, and thus may be ideal targets for aromatase inhibitor therapy. However, in another study of 22 patients with imaging suggestive of recurrence or residual lesions greater than 1 cm who were treated with hormone therapy (aromatase inhibitors, gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists, steroidal progestins, and selective estrogen receptor modulators), the objective response rate was only 18% (4/22) [30]. Recently, the first phase 2 prospective clinical trial of hormone therapy for AGCT was conducted in which 41 patients with recurrent ER/PR + positivity were treated with anastrozole (an aromatase inhibitor), resulting in a partial response in 2.6% of patients and stabilization in 76.3% of patients [31]. Hormone therapy may be a better option for the treatment of progressive and recurrent AGCT, and large prospective studies are needed to confirm this.

Targeted therapy

Due to the rarity of MOSCSTs, there are limited data on the role of targeted therapies in the treatment of MOSCSTs and their evaluation in reliable clinical trials.

A prospective, multi-institutional cooperative group trial was performed in women with recurrent, measurable SCSTs using bevacizumab 15 mg/kg intravenously on day 1 of every 21-day cycle. A total of 36 patients were enrolled, with a median PFS of 9.3 months, while median OS was not reached at 32.5 months of follow-up [32]. Another open-label, international, randomized phase 2 trial, also for the treatment of recurrent SCST, primarily granular cell tumors, showed that the addition of bevacizumab to weekly paclitaxel therapy did not improve clinical outcomes, with a 6-month progression-free rate of 71% (paclitaxel alone) vs. 72% (paclitaxel-bevacizumab) [33].

Tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) imatinib were reported to treat the recurrent ovarian granulosa cell tumors as a personalized prescription choice. Imatinib (400 mg/day for the first 2 months and 800 mg/day thereafter) showed significant remission after 6 months of treatment with no significant side effects [34]; another case (imatinib 400 mg/day) showed a partial response followed by long-term stabilization [35].

Conclusion

MOSCSTs are relatively rare, with the majority being low-grade malignant. The initial treatment for MOSCSTs is mainly surgical. For patients with malignant or potentially malignant pathological types, is recommended to undergo comprehensive staged surgery. For young and early-stage patients, fertility can be preserved and lymph nodes can be left unresected. This type of tumor is more sensitive to chemotherapy, but attention should be paid to chemotherapy indications. For patients with high-risk factors and stage II or above, postoperative chemotherapy should be given. Currently, the first-line chemotherapy regimens are BEP and TP, but the chemotherapy duration is still controversial. MOSCSTs have a good prognosis, with late recurrence. Surgery is still the main treatment for recurrence, supplemented by BEP and TP chemotherapy regimens. For chemotherapy-resistant patients, hormonal therapies are available, including progesterone and aromatase inhibitors. At present, bevacizumab and TKI drugs also have specific therapeutic effects on recurrent granulosa cell tumors, but large sample and prospective studies are still needed. Radiotherapy can be used for patients who cannot undergo surgery, have residual lesions after surgery, and are resistant to chemotherapy.
