Darboux first integrals of Kolmogorov systems with invariant n n -sphere
Supriyo Jana, Soumen Sarkar

Abstract
. In this paper, we characterize all polynomial Kolmogorov vector fields for which the standard n n -sphere is invariant. We exhibit completely integrable Kolmogorov vector fields of degree m m on 𝕊 n \mathbb{S}^{n} for any m > 2 m>2 . Then, we show that there is no cubic Hamiltonian Kolmogorov vector field that makes an odd-dimensional sphere invariant. We examine the conditions under which a cubic Kolmogorov vector field has a Darboux first integral. In many cases, we determine whether they constitute necessary and sufficient conditions. Moreover, we study the complete integrability of cubic Kolmogorov vector fields having an invariant n n -sphere.

1.
Introduction

Let
R_{1},\ldots,R_{d}
be polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
. Then, the following system of differential equations

(1.1)

\frac{{\rm d}x_{i}}{{\rm dt}}=R_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}),~i=1,\ldots,d,

is called a polynomial differential system in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The differential operator

(1.2)

\chi=\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}

is called the vector field associated with the system (
1.1
). The number
\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq d}\{\deg R_{i}\}
is called the degree of the polynomial vector field (
1.2
).

If
R_{1},\ldots,R_{d}
are homogeneous polynomials of the same degree, then
\chi
is called a homogeneous vector field.

The system (
1.1
) is called a polynomial Kolmogorov system in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if
R_{i}=x_{i}Q_{i}
for some
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{d}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
. The associated vector field is called a polynomial Kolmogorov vector field. Some classes of Kolmogorov systems in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{4}
were studied in
[
28
]
and
[
22
]
, respectively. The integrability of a class of Kolmogorov systems in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has been explored in
[
20
]
. The authors and Benny
[
3
]
discussed the phase portraits of cubic Kolmogorov vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. We note that Kolmogorov systems
[
15
]
have been found as models of various phenomena in plasma physics
[
17
]
, economics
[
1
]
, and other areas, including ecology. We note that a quadratic Kolmogorov system in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is known as a Lotka–Volterra system
[
20
,
24
]
. The latter system has been used to understand the dynamics of predator–prey interactions in ecological systems
[
1
,
26
]
and in disease spread
[
10
]
.

A subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called invariant for the vector field (
1.2
) if the set is a union of some trajectories of the vector field. The zero locus of an irreducible polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
is called an irreducible algebraic set of
f
. An irreducible algebraic set of
f
is invariant if there exists
K\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
such that
\chi f=Kf
. In this setting,
\chi
is called a polynomial vector field on the hypersurface
\{f=0\}
and
K
is referred to as the cofactor.

We note that the study of polynomial vector fields started blooming in the 1970s; see, for instance,
[
7
,
11
,
12
,
14
]
. One can ask for the number of possible invariant algebraic sets for a given vector field, see
[
4
,
5
,
27
]
.

Darboux
[
8
]
showed the existence of a (rational) first integral (see Definition
2.1
and the paragraph after that) when the polynomial vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
possesses a sufficient number of invariant algebraic curves. Jouanolou
[
14
]
extended similar results in higher dimensions. There are
(d-1)
functionally independent local first integrals of any vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
around non-singular points, see
[
2
, Chapter 10]
. Thus, one can ask whether a polynomial system in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has a polynomial or a rational first integral. Some more developments on this topic can be found in
[
6
,
19
,
23
]
. However, several interesting dynamical properties of polynomial vector fields, such as the Kolmogorov vector fields in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, remain to be explored. The existence of enough functionally independent first integrals means that the system is completely integrable, see Definition
2.3
. Llibre and Bolaños
[
18
]
showed that polynomial vector fields on a regular algebraic hypersurface having a sufficient number of invariant algebraic hypersurfaces possess a rational first integral. The Darboux theory of integrability for polynomial vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
has been studied in
[
19
]
. Therefore, it is essential to study various invariant algebraic hypersurfaces for a Kolmogorov vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and subsequently study their Darboux first integrals.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall the definitions of first integral, complete integrability, Hamiltonian vector field, syzygies of some polynomials, and some basic notions required in this paper.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
U
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A non-constant differentiable map
H\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be a first integral
of the vector field (
1.2
) on
U
if each solution curve of the system (
1.1
) contained in
U
lies on one of the level surface of
H
; i.e.,
H(x_{1}(t),\ldots,x_{d}(t))=
const for all values of
t
for which the solution
(x_{1}(t),\ldots,x_{d}(t))
is defined and contained in
U
.

Note that
H
is a first integral of the vector field (
1.2
) on
U
if and only if
\chi H=0
on
U
. When
H
is a rational function, it is referred to as a rational first integral. The existence of a first integral reduces the analysis of the system by one dimension. Moreover, if the vector field (
1.2
) admits a first integral, then it possesses infinitely many invariant algebraic sets. A proof of this result can be found on page 102 of
[
14
]
.

A restricted version of the Darboux theory of integrability is the following.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that a polynomial vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has
p
invariant algebraic hypersurfaces
f_{i}=0
with cofactor
K_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,p
. If there exist
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{p}\in\mathbb{R}
, not all zero, such that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}K_{i}=0
, then
f_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}\ldots f_{p}^{\lambda_{p}}
is a first integral of the vector field.

This type of first integral is known as a Darboux first integral. More general results using independent singular points and exponential factors can be seen in
[
14
,
21
,
29
]
. A set of
m
functions is called functionally independent in an open set
U
if the rank of the derivative of the mapping
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by these functions is
m
at some point of
U
.

Definition 2.3
.

The vector field (
1.2
) is said to be completely integrable in an open set
U
if it admits
(d-1)
functionally independent first integrals defined on
U
.

If the vector field (
1.2
) is completely integrable with
(d-1)
functionally independent first integrals
H_{1},\ldots,H_{d-1}
then the trajectories of the system are contained in
\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}\{H_{i}=c_{i}\}
for some
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. A discussion of the complete integrability of vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be found in
[
20
]
.
Considering a permutation of coordinates, Definition 1 in
[
25
, Section 2.14]
can be rewritten as follows.

Definition 2.4
.

A vector field
\chi=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{2n})
is called a Hamiltonian vector field if there exists a differentiable function
H\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
P_{2i-1}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{2i}}
and
P_{2i}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{2i-1}}
for
1\leq i\leq n
.

The function
H
is called a Hamiltonian for the vector field. It follows that
H
is a global first integral. So, the function
H
is conserved along any trajectories of the vector field. We note that
[
22
]
considers this definition to study Hamiltonian vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
R
be a ring and
M
be an
R
-module. Suppose that
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\in M^{m}
. A syzygy of
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
is a tuple
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})\in R^{m}
such that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}g_{i}=0
.

The set of all syzygies of
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
forms an
R
-module which is called the
(first) syzygy module of
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
and is denoted by
{\rm Syz}_{R}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
. For more information on syzygies, one can see
[
16
]
. In this paper, we consider
R=M=\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
, the polynomial ring in
d
variables over real.

The following result is algebraic. However, it is crucial to classify polynomial Kolmogorov vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{d})
be a syzygy of
(x_{1}^{k},\ldots,x_{d}^{k})
in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
for some
k\geq 1
. Then
Q_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}\tilde{A}_{ij}x_{j}^{k}
for some
\tilde{A}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]
such that
A=(\tilde{A}_{ij})_{d\times d}
is a skew-symmetric matrix.

The intersection of a hyperplane
L:=\Big\{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}+b=0\Big\}
with
\mathbb{S}^{n}
is said to be an
(n-1)
-sphere in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
where
a_{i},b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{n+1}^{2}=1
and
|b|<1
. If the hyperplane
L
passes through the origin (i.e.,
b=0
in
L
), the intersection is called a great
(n-1)
-sphere in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

3.
Polynomial Kolmogorov vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{n}

In this section, we characterize any polynomial Kolmogorov vector fields on the standard
n
-sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Then, we discuss their first integrals and complete integrability. We show that there is no cubic Hamiltonian Kolmogorov vector field on odd-dimensional spheres. We study invariant spheres of cubic and quartic homogeneous Kolmogorov vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. We note that the paper
[
30
]
gave a necessary and sufficient condition when a hypersurface is invariant of a polynomial vector field. However, the paper
[
13
]
presented a more explicit form of a polynomial vector field with an invariant sphere. We use the latter result for our convenience.

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

Suppose that
\chi=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n+1})
is a degree
m
Kolmogorov vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. By
[
13
, Remark 3.2]
, we have

(3.1)

P_{i}=\Big(1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}^{2}\Big)f_{i}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}A_{ij}x_{j},

for some polynomial
f_{i},A_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
with
\deg f_{i}\leq m-2
and
\deg A_{ij}\leq m-1
such that
(A_{ij})_{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
is a skew-symmetric matrix. Moreover,
x_{i}
divides
P_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
. Therefore,
P_{i}=\tilde{P}_{i}x_{i}
for some
\tilde{P}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
, for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
. Observe that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}\tilde{P}_{i}x_{i}^{2}=\Big(1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}^{2}\Big)\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}f_{i}x_{i}\Big).

This simplifies to
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}\tilde{P}_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}f_{i}x_{i}\Big)\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}f_{i}x_{i}.

Hence,
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}\tilde{f}_{i}x_{i}^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}f_{i}x_{i}
with
\deg\tilde{f}_{i}\leq m-3
, leading to
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}(f_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i}x_{i})x_{i}=0
. By
Lemma

2.6
, we have
f_{i}=\tilde{f}_{i}x_{i}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}B_{ij}x_{j}
for some
B_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
with
\deg B_{ij}\leq m-3
such that
(B_{ij})_{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
is a skew-symmetric matrix. Substituting
f_{i}
in the original expression (
3.1
) of
P_{i}
, we obtain

P_{i}=\Big(1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}^{2}\Big)\tilde{f}_{i}x_{i}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}C_{ij}x_{j},

where
C_{ij}:=\Big(1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}^{2}\Big)B_{ij}+A_{ij}
. Note that
\deg C_{ij}\leq m-1
. Since
P_{i}
is divisible by
x_{i}
, it follows that
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}C_{ij}x_{j}=L_{i}x_{i}
for some
L_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
. Thus,

0=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}C_{ij}x_{j}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}L_{i}x_{i}^{2}.

Hence, by
Lemma

2.6
,
L_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}\tilde{A}_{ij}x_{j}^{2}
where
\tilde{A}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
with
\deg\tilde{A}_{ij}\leq m-3
such that
\tilde{A}:=(\tilde{A}_{ij})_{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
is a skew-symmetric matrix. Therefore,

P_{i}=x_{i}\Big(\Big(1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}^{2}\Big)\tilde{f}_{i}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}\tilde{A}_{ij}x_{j}^{2}\Big).

One can verify that the converse part is also true with the cofactor
-2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}\tilde{f}_{i}x_{i}^{2}
.

∎

Remark 3.1
.

A polynomial Kolmogorov vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
has degree at least three.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
\chi=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n+1})
is a cubic Kolmogorov vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
where
P_{i}
are given by (
1.3
). If
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is a non-zero syzygy of
(\tilde{f}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{f}_{n+1})
and
\tilde{A}y=0
then
H=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{y_{i}}
is a first integral of
\chi
.

Proof.

Since
\chi
is a cubic vector field,
\tilde{f}_{i}
can be chosen as a constant for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
, and
\tilde{A}
can be chosen as a constant skew-symmetric matrix. Observe that
x_{i}=0
is invariant with cofactor
K_{i}=\Big(1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}^{2}\Big)\tilde{f}_{i}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}\tilde{A}_{ij}x_{j}^{2}
. We compute the following.

\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}y_{i}K_{i}=\Big(1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}^{2}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}y_{i}\tilde{f}_{i}\Big)+\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}y_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\tilde{A}_{ij}x_{j}^{2}=y^{t}\tilde{A}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{2}&\cdots&x_{n+1}^{2}\end{pmatrix}^{t}=0

since
\tilde{A}
is skew-symmetric. Hence, by Darboux Integrability Theory
[
8
,
19
]
,
H=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{y_{i}}
is a first integral of
\chi
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
be a linear polynomial. Then,
f
is a first integral of some Kolmogorov vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

Proof.

Assume that
f:=a_{0}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}
where
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
0\leq j\leq n+1
. We construct an
(n+1)\times(n+1)
non-zero skew-symmetric matrix
\tilde{A}=(\tilde{A}_{ij})
such that
a(x)^{t}\tilde{A}=0
where
\tilde{A}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
and
a(x)^{t}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}x_{1}&\cdots&a_{n+1}x_{n+1}\end{pmatrix}
. Note that, by our construction,
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n+1})
with
P_{i}=x_{i}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}\tilde{A}_{ij}x_{j}^{2}
is a non-zero vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
such that
f
is a first integral of it.

Suppose that
a_{k}\neq 0
for some
k\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
. We take an arbitrary
n\times n
non-zero skew-symmetric matrix
B:=(B_{ij})
where
B_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
. Now, we define a new skew-symmetric matrix
(A_{ij})=A:=x_{k}B
. We construct the matrix
\tilde{A}=(\tilde{A}_{ij})
as follows.

\tilde{A}_{ij}=\begin{cases}&A_{ij}~\mbox{if }i,j<k,\\
&A_{i(j-1)}~\mbox{if }i<k,j>k,\\
&A_{(i-1)j}~\mbox{if }i>k,j<k,\\
&A_{(i-1)(j-1)}~\mbox{if }i,j>k,\\
\end{cases}

\tilde{A}_{kj}=-\frac{1}{a_{k}x_{k}}\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1,i\neq k}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}\tilde{A}_{ij}\Big)
, and
\tilde{A}_{jk}=-\tilde{A}_{kj}
for
j\neq k
. Moreover,
\tilde{A}_{kk}=0
. Observe that
\tilde{A}
is a non-zero skew-symmetric matrix. For
j\neq k
, we have the following.

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}\tilde{A}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1,i\neq k}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}\tilde{A}_{ij}+a_{k}x_{k}\tilde{A}_{kj}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1,i\neq k}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}\tilde{A}_{ij}-\sum\limits_{i=1,i\neq k}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}\tilde{A}_{ij}

\displaystyle=0.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}\tilde{A}_{ik}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1,i\neq k}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}\tilde{A}_{ik}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a_{k}x_{k}}\sum\limits_{i=1,i\neq k}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}\Big(\sum\limits_{j=1,j\neq k}^{n+1}a_{j}x_{j}\tilde{A}_{ji}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a_{k}x_{k}}\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}\Big(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}a_{j}x_{j}\tilde{A}_{ji}\Big)-a_{k}x_{k}\sum\limits_{j=1,j\neq k}^{n+1}a_{j}x_{j}\tilde{A}_{jk}-a_{k}x_{k}\sum\limits_{i=1,i\neq k}^{n+1}a_{i}x_{i}\tilde{A}_{ki}\Big)

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore,

is orthogonal to the columns of

. Thus, the proof is complete.

4.

In this section, we examine when a cubic Kolmogorov vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{n}

.

\chi

K_{i}=\alpha_{i}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}(\tilde{A}_{ij}-\alpha_{i})x_{j}^{2}
where
\tilde{A}_{ii}=0
. By Darboux Integrability Theory
[
8
,
9
]
,
H=g_{n+2}^{\beta_{n+2}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{\beta_{i}}
is a first integral of
\chi
if and only if

(4.9)

\beta_{n+2}K_{n+2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\beta_{i}K_{i}=0,

where not all
\beta_{j}
is zero for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n+2\}
. Next, we compare the coefficients of each monomial in (
4.9
).

\displaystyle\mbox{Constant-term}:

\displaystyle~\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}+\beta_{2}\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{n+1}\alpha_{n+1}+\beta_{n+2}k_{0}=0

\displaystyle x_{1}^{2}:

\displaystyle~\beta_{1}(-\alpha_{1})+\beta_{2}(\tilde{A}_{21}-\alpha_{2})+\cdots+\beta_{n+1}(\tilde{A}_{(n+1)1}-\alpha_{n+1})+\beta_{n+2}k_{1}=0

\displaystyle x_{2}^{2}:

\displaystyle~\beta_{1}(\tilde{A}_{12}-\alpha_{1})+\beta_{2}(-\alpha_{2})+\cdots+\beta_{n+1}(\tilde{A}_{(n+1)2}-\alpha_{n+1})+\beta_{n+2}k_{2}=0

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\vdots

\displaystyle x_{n+1}^{2}:

\displaystyle~\beta_{1}(\tilde{A}_{1(n+1)}-\alpha_{1})+\beta_{2}(\tilde{A}_{2(n+1)}-\alpha_{2})+\cdots+\beta_{n+1}(-\alpha_{n+1})+\beta_{n+2}k_{n+1}=0.

Hence, (
4.9
) holds if and only if
\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}&\beta_{2}&\cdots&\beta_{n+2}\end{pmatrix}
is a non-trivial solution of the matrix equation
yB=0
. So,
H
is a first integral if and only if
{\rm rank}(B)<(n+2)
.
∎

We remark that if
\chi
is a homogeneous polynomial vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, then any sphere
g_{r}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{2}-r^{2}=0
is a first integral of
\chi
, see
[
13
, Theorem 5.4]
. The next result proves the converse part when
\chi
is a cubic Kolmogorov vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\chi
be a cubic Kolmogorov vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. If
g_{r}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{2}-r^{2}=0
with
r\neq 0,\pm 1
is an invariant sphere for
\chi
, then the vector field is homogeneous and
g_{r}
is a first integral.

Proof.

If
\chi=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n+1})
is a cubic Kolmogorov vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
then

P_{i}=x_{i}\Big(\Big(1-\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}x^{2}_{k}\Big)\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\tilde{A}_{ij}x^{2}_{j}\Big),

where
(\tilde{A}_{ij})
is a constant skew-symmetric matrix of order
(n+1)
and
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
. Suppose that
g_{r}=0
is invariant for
\chi
. Then,

(4.10)

\Big(1-\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}x^{2}_{k}\Big)\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\alpha_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n+1}\tilde{A}_{ij}x_{i}^{2}x_{j}^{2}=K\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{2}-r^{2}\Big)

for some
K\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
with
\deg K\leq 2
. Each monomial in the left-hand side of (
4.10
) has degree at least two. Hence, either
K
is zero or a quadratic homogeneous polynomial. Observe that
\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n+1}\tilde{A}_{ij}x_{i}^{2}x_{j}^{2}=0
since
(\tilde{A}_{ij})
is a skew-symmetric matrix. Hence,
\Big(1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x^{2}_{k}\Big)
divides
\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{2}-r^{2}\Big)
if
K
is quadratic homogeneous. This contradicts the assumption that
r\neq 1
. So,
K
must be zero. Consequently,
\alpha_{i}=0
for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
. Therefore,
\chi
is homogeneous and
g_{r}
is a first integral.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Suppose that
{\rm rank}(B)\leq 2
. Then, the matrix equation
yB=0
has at least
n
independent solutions, say
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
where
y_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}y_{i1}&y_{i2}&\cdots&y_{i(n+2)}\end{pmatrix}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. From the proof of
Theorem

4.2
, we recall that if
y
is solution of
yB=0
then
\langle y,(K_{1},K_{2},\ldots,K_{n+2})\rangle=0
where
K_{i}
is the cofactor of
x_{i}=0
for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
and
K_{n+2}
is the cofactor of
g_{n+2}=0
. Hence,

\sum_{j=1}^{n+2}y_{ij}K_{j}=0~\mbox{for}~1\leq i\leq n.

It follows that
H_{i}=g_{n+2}^{y_{i(n+2)}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}x_{j}^{y_{ij}}
is a first integral of
\chi
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. We claim that
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}
are functionally independent.

We compute
\frac{\partial H_{i}}{\partial x_{k}}=(\frac{g_{n+2}}{x_{k}}y_{ik}+y_{i(n+2)}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{k}})g_{n+2}^{y_{i(n+2)}-1}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}x_{j}^{y_{ij}}
. We define
Y:=(y_{ij})_{n\times(n+2)}
such that
\bf y_{1},\ldots,\bf y_{n+2}
are the columns of
Y
. Suppose that
J:=\frac{\partial(H_{1},\ldots,H_{n})}{\partial(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})}
having columns
J_{1},\ldots,J_{n+1}
. Then

J_{p}={\rm diag}(g_{n+2}^{y_{1(n+2)}-1}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}x_{j}^{y_{1j}},\ldots,g_{n+2}^{y_{n(n+2)}-1}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}x_{j}^{y_{nj}})(\frac{g_{n+2}}{x_{p}}{\bf y_{p}}+\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{p}}\bf y_{n+2}),

for
1\leq p\leq n+1
. We have to prove that
{\rm rank}(J)
is
n
at some point. Consider the matrix
\tilde{J}
having columns
\tilde{J}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{J}_{n}
where
\tilde{J}_{p}:=\frac{g_{n+2}}{x_{p}}{\bf y_{p}}+\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{p}}\bf y_{n+2}
where
1\leq p\leq n
. Note that
{\rm diag}(g_{n+2}^{y_{1(n+2)}-1}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}x_{j}^{y_{1j}},\ldots,g_{n+2}^{y_{n(n+2)}-1}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n+1}x_{j}^{y_{nj}})
is an invertible matrix at each
z_{ij}
for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
. Therefore,
{\rm rank}(J)={\rm rank}(\tilde{J})
at each
z_{ij}
. We prove that
{\rm rank}(\tilde{J})=n
at some
z_{ij}
.

If
{\bf y_{n+2}}=0
then
{\rm rank}(\tilde{J})=n
at each
z_{ij}
since
{\rm rank}(Y)=n
. If
{\bf y_{n+2}}\neq 0
then without loss of generality, we assume that
{\bf y_{3},\ldots,y_{n+2}}
are independent column vectors. Then,
\tilde{J}_{3},\ldots,\tilde{J}_{n+1}
are independent columns vectors at each
z_{ij}
. We claim that
\tilde{J}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{J}_{n+1}
are independent at
z_{1j}
, for
1\leq j\leq n+1
. Similarly, we can also prove
\tilde{J}_{1},\tilde{J}_{3},\ldots,\tilde{J}_{n+1}
are independent at
z_{2j}
, for
1\leq j\leq n+1
. Let
z_{1j}:=(s^{j}_{11},\ldots,s^{j}_{1(n+1)})
.

If
{\bf y_{2}}=0
then
\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n+1}c_{i}\tilde{J}_{i}(z_{1j})=0
gives
g_{n+2}(z_{1j})\sum\limits_{k=3}^{n+1}\frac{c_{k}}{s^{j}_{1k}}{\bf y_{k}}+(\sum\limits_{k=2}^{n+1}c_{k}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{k}}(z_{1j})){\bf y_{n+2}}=0
. Hence,
c_{k}=0
for
k=2,\ldots,n+1
. Therefore,
\tilde{J}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{J}_{n+1}
are independent at
z_{1j}
.

Now, consider
{\bf y_{2}}\neq 0
. Suppose that there exist
d_{2},\ldots,d_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}
, not all zero, such that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=2}^{n+1}d_{k}\tilde{J}_{k}(z_{1j})=0

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle g_{n+2}(z_{1j})\sum\limits_{k=2}^{n+1}\frac{d_{k}}{s^{j}_{1k}}{\bf y_{k}}+(\sum\limits_{k=2}^{n+1}d_{k}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{k}}(z_{1j})){\bf y_{n+2}}=0

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{\bf y_{2}}&\ldots&{\bf y_{n+2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{d_{2}}{s^{j}_{12}}g_{n+2}(z_{1j})&\ldots&\frac{d_{n+1}}{s^{j}_{1(n+1)}}g_{n+2}(z_{1j})&\sum\limits_{k=2}^{n+1}d_{k}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{k}}(z_{1j})\end{pmatrix}^{t}=0.

Rank of the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}{\bf y_{2}}&\ldots&{\bf y_{n+2}}\end{pmatrix}
is
n
. So, the nullity of it is 1, say
\begin{pmatrix}p_{2}&\ldots&p_{n+2}\end{pmatrix}^{t}
is a basis vector of the null space. Therefore,

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{d_{2}}{s^{j}_{12}}g_{n+2}(z_{1j})&\ldots&\frac{d_{n+1}}{s^{j}_{1(n+1)}}g_{n+2}(z_{1j})&\sum\limits_{k=2}^{n+1}d_{k}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{k}}(z_{1j})\end{pmatrix}^{t}=\alpha\begin{pmatrix}p_{2}&\ldots&p_{n+2}\end{pmatrix}^{t},

for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Hence, we obtain
p_{n+2}=\frac{1}{g_{n+2}(z_{1j})}(\sum\limits_{k=2}^{n+1}p_{k}s^{j}_{1k}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{k}}(z_{1j}))
. Thus,

\begin{pmatrix}s^{j}_{12}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{1}}(z_{1j})&\ldots&s^{j}_{1(n+1)}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{n+1}}(z_{1j})&-g_{n+2}(z_{1j})\end{pmatrix}^{t}

is a null space solution of
\begin{pmatrix}p_{2}&\ldots&p_{n+2}\end{pmatrix}
, for
1\leq j\leq n+1
. By the hypothesis,
\begin{pmatrix}p_{2}&\ldots&p_{n+2}\end{pmatrix}
has
n+1
independent null space solutions, which is impossible. So, our assumption is wrong. Therefore,
\tilde{J}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{J}_{n+1}
are independent at
z_{1j}
, for
1\leq j\leq n+1
. Hence,
{\rm rank}(\tilde{J})=n
at
z_{1j}
.

Now, we assume that
H_{i}
are functionally independent first integrals of
\chi
. By Darboux Integrability Theory
[
8
,
9
]
,
y_{i}
is a non-trivial solution of
yB=0
where
y_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}y_{i1}&y_{i2}&\cdots&y_{i(n+2)}\end{pmatrix}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. We claim that
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
are linearly independent. If not, assume that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}y_{i}=0
where not all
c_{i}
are zero. Hence,
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}H_{i}^{c_{i}}-1=0
. This implies
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}
are functionally dependent, which is a contradiction. Hence,
{\rm rank}(B)\leq 2
. Thus, the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

By the hypothesis of
Theorem

1.4
,
z_{ij}
must be distinct for any fixed
i
when
j
varies. However,
z_{i_{1}j_{1}}
can be equal to
z_{i_{2}j_{2}}
for some
i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
with
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Suppose that
g_{n+2}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{2}-1
in
Theorem

4.2
, and the rank of the matrix
B
in (
4.8
) is less than or equal to 2 for the cubic vector field (
1.3
). Then the vector field is completely integrable in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{x_{1}\cdots x_{n+1}=0\}
.

Proof.

The
n
-sphere given by
g_{n+2}=0
is invariant under the cubic vector field (
1.3
) with cofactor
-2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}\alpha_{i}x_{i}^{2}
. So,
k_{0}=0
and
k_{i}=-2\alpha_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
in matrix
B
. We choose
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\mathbb{S}^{n}
, where the
j
-th coordinate of
z_{j}
is 2 and all other coordinates are 1, for each
{j\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}}
. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
, evaluating the vector

\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{1}}&\ldots&x_{i-1}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{i-1}}&x_{i+1}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{i+1}}&\ldots&x_{n+1}\frac{\partial g_{n+2}}{\partial x_{n+1}}&-g_{n+2}\end{pmatrix}

at the points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n+1}
, we obtain the row vectors of the matrix

M=\begin{pmatrix}8&2&2&\cdots&2&-n-3\\
2&8&2&\cdots&2&-n-3\\
2&2&8&\cdots&2&-n-3\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
2&2&2&\cdots&8&-n-3\\
2&2&2&\cdots&2&-n-3\end{pmatrix}_{(n+1)\times(n+1)}.

We claim that
\det(M)\neq 0
. After subtracting the
(n+1)
-th row from the first row, we have

\det(M)=\begin{vmatrix}6&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
2&8&2&\cdots&2&-n-3\\
2&2&8&\cdots&2&-n-3\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
2&2&2&\cdots&8&-n-3\\
2&2&2&\cdots&2&-n-3\end{vmatrix}_{(n+1)\times(n+1)}.

Therefore,
\det(M)=6\begin{vmatrix}8&2&2&\cdots&2&-n-3\\
2&8&2&\cdots&2&-n-3\\
2&2&8&\cdots&2&-n-3\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
2&2&2&\cdots&8&-n-3\\
2&2&2&\cdots&2&-n-3\end{vmatrix}_{n\times n}
, and inductively,

\det(M)=6^{n-1}\begin{vmatrix}8&-n-3\\
2&-n-3\end{vmatrix}=-6^{n}(n+3).

Hence, by
Theorem

1.4
, the vector field is completely integrable in the open set
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{x_{1}\cdots x_{n+1}=0\}
.
∎

Example 4.6
.

Consider the vector field
\chi=(P_{1},P_{2},P_{3})
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
defined by

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{1}\Big(\beta\Big(1-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}^{2}\Big)+\alpha x_{2}^{2}\Big),

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle=-\alpha x_{2}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}),

\displaystyle P_{3}

\displaystyle=x_{3}\Big(\beta\Big(1-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}^{2}\Big)+\alpha x_{2}^{2}\Big),

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Assume that
g_{4}:=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}-1
. We compute the associated matrix

B=\begin{bmatrix}\beta&-\beta&\alpha-\beta&-\beta\\
0&-\alpha&0&-\alpha\\
\beta&-\beta&\alpha-\beta&-\beta\\
0&-2\beta&0&-2\beta\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Observe that
{\rm rank}(B)=2
. Moreover,
\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1&0\end{pmatrix}
and
\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\beta&0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
are two linearly independent solutions of
yB=0
. Therefore, the functions
x_{1}x_{3}^{-1}
and
g_{4}^{\alpha}x_{2}^{-2\beta}
are two functionally independent first integrals of
\chi
.

On the other hand, consider the points
z_{1}=(2,1,1,)
,
z_{2}=(1,2,1)
, and
z_{3}=(1,1,2)
. Then, for each
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, the vectors

\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\frac{\partial g_{4}}{\partial x_{1}}&\ldots&x_{i-1}\frac{\partial g_{4}}{\partial x_{i-1}}&x_{i+1}\frac{\partial g_{4}}{\partial x_{i+1}}&\ldots&x_{4}\frac{\partial g_{4}}{\partial x_{4}}&-g_{4}\end{pmatrix}

evaluated at
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
are linearly independent (See the proof of
Corollary

4.5
). Hence, the hypothesis of
Theorem

1.4
is satisfied.
