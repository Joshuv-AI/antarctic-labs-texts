Modelling factors associated with infant mortality in Northern Region of Ghana: survival analysis approach
Authors: Zafodor Fuseini Abubakari, Alhassan Faisal, Mahama Jato Asuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infant mortality remains a significant public health concern in Ghana, particularly in the Northern Region. This study investigated factors associated with infant survival using facility-based data from Kpandai Polyclinic and Tamale West Hospital. The Kaplan–Meier estimator showed no statistically significant survival differences across most categorical covariates, including child sex, maternal education, place of residence, marital status, and occupation; however, a significant difference was observed for mode of delivery. The mean gestational age at delivery was 35.6 weeks, which is below the standard full-term threshold, suggesting a relatively high prevalence of preterm births within the cohort. The standard Cox proportional hazards model identified gestational age and mode of delivery as significantly associated with infant mortality. However, the directions of these associations were counterintuitive and should be interpreted with caution. In particular, the association with mode of delivery is likely influenced by confounding by indication, as caesarean sections are typically performed in response to complications or high-risk pregnancies. Similarly, the effect of gestational age may be influenced by measurement limitations and the absence of detailed clinical variables, such as neonatal complications and birth conditions, which may obscure the true relationship between gestational age and survival. The piecewise time-dependent Cox model revealed that only a few variables particularly mode of delivery, gestational age, and place of residence exhibited notable time-varying associations, whereas the majority of demographic and socioeconomic factors remained largely non-predictive. The extended model provided a superior fit to the data compared to the standard Cox model.

Introduction

Infant mortality refers to the death of a child before their first birthday. It remains a critical public health concern in Ghana, particularly in the Northern Region, where disparities in access to healthcare persist. The death of children under one year of age has long been recognized as a key indicator of a nation’s socio-economic development and the effectiveness of child survival interventions [1]. It reflects the quality of healthcare systems, maternal health, and broader social and environmental conditions.

Globally, infectious diseases such as malaria, diarrhea diseases, and pneumonia alongside complications related to preterm birth, birth asphyxia, trauma, and congenital disorders remain the leading causes of infant mortality [2]. In 2013, the World Health Organization reported an infant mortality rate of approximately 6 deaths per 1,000 live births in high-income countries, compared to 53 per 1,000 in low- and middle-income countries [3]. These disparities underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions in resource-constrained settings, where the burden of infant mortality is highest.

In Ghana, despite notable progress in reducing overall child mortality, infant mortality remains a significant public health issue. Recent estimates suggest a national infant mortality rate of approximately 28 deaths per 1,000 live births [4]. However, substantial regional disparities persist, with the Northern Region recording some of the highest rates in the country. According to the Demographic and Health Surveys [5], the Northern Region has an infant mortality rate of approximately 53 deaths per 1,000 live births and an under-five mortality rate of about 111 per 1,000, figures considerably higher than those observed in more urbanized regions such as Greater Accra Region. Furthermore, studies indicate that children in the Northern Region have more than twice the risk of dying before their fifth birthday compared to those in Greater Accra (HR ≈ 2.08) [6, 7].

These patterns highlight the urgent need to better understand the factors associated with infant mortality in Northern Ghana, where socioeconomic challenges, limited health infrastructure, and cultural practices may interact to increase vulnerability. Although numerous factors contribute to infant mortality, the extent to which these factors influence survival over time in this region remains inadequately explored. It is therefore essential to develop a comprehensive model that examines the factors associated with infant mortality in Northern Ghana. This study seeks to address this gap by identifying key factors associated with infant mortality and providing evidence to inform strategies aimed at reducing preventable deaths. Developing a context-specific analytical framework is crucial for evidence-informed policy formulation and timely decision-making. The findings are expected to contribute to the broader literature on infant mortality modelling and offer practical insights for health planners and policymakers working in resource-constrained settings.

Several studies [8–27] have employed various analytical approaches to examine factors associated with infant and child mortality. However, most of these studies rely on cross-sectional methods, which limit insights into time-to-death dynamics during infancy, or they assume proportional hazards, potentially obscuring time-varying risk patterns. This study addresses these limitations by applying survival analysis techniques, including Cox regression models with time-dependent effects, to better capture early-life mortality dynamics.

In addition, this study offers several methodological strengths. The application of survival analysis provides a robust framework for analyzing time-to-event outcomes and enables a detailed characterization of mortality patterns over time. The combined use of Kaplan–Meier estimation and Cox regression modelling allows for complementary insights into survival probabilities and associated factors. Furthermore, the inclusion of sensitivity analyses and extended modelling approaches enhances the robustness of the findings. By employing a piecewise Cox model, this study contributes to the Ghanaian literature by explicitly modelling how the associations between risk factors and mortality evolve across neonatal and post-neonatal periods, thereby providing more nuanced and policy-relevant insights.

Importantly, this study contributes context-specific evidence from Ghana, where facility-based data remain a key source of information for understanding health outcomes. While acknowledging the inherent limitations of such data, the findings provide valuable insights into maternal and child health in similar settings. Understanding both the timing and associated factors of infant mortality is essential for designing targeted interventions. This study therefore aims to examine the survival experience of infants within the first 12 months of life using hospital-based data, while explicitly recognizing the constraints associated with the data source.

Materials and methods

Data source, survival time representation and study variables

The study was conducted using records of newborns collected over a four-year period (2018–2021) from Kpandai Polyclinic and Tamale West Hospital, both located in the Northern Region. The dataset includes all recorded births within the study period, comprising both infants who died within 12 months (events) and those who survived beyond 12 months (censored observations).

Specifically, the sample includes 176 newborns, of whom 82 experienced the event (death within 12 months) and 92 were right-censored (i.e., survived beyond 12 months or were not observed to die within the follow-up period). Right-censoring was applied at 12 months to align with the definition of infant mortality (death before one year of age). All infants were therefore included in the risk set, ensuring the validity of the survival analysis framework, whereby individuals who do not experience the event within the defined follow-up period are treated as censored at the end of that period.

The data were cleaned and coded to ensure consistency, completeness, and reliability prior to statistical analysis. As the dataset is restricted to infants recorded within hospital-based systems, it is subject to potential selection bias, particularly due to the underrepresentation of infants who survived without requiring facility-based care. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as associations within a selected clinical cohort rather than as population-representative estimates of survival.

Kaplan Meier (KM) estimator

The Kaplan–Meier (KM) estimator, proposed by [28], is a nonparametric method used to estimate survival probabilities directly from observed data. The survival function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S(t)$$\end{document}, represents the probability that an individual survives beyond a specified time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document}, that is, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S(t)=P(T>t)$$\end{document}. After estimating the survival function, Kaplan–Meier survival curves are plotted to visualise survival patterns across levels of categorical covariates. These plots provide a graphical representation of survival outcomes and allow for comparison between groups. In addition, the KM estimator provides estimates of median survival time for specific groups. Differences in survival between groups are formally assessed using the log-rank test for categorical predictors.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the fitted Cox model. These analyses included restricting the sample to early infant mortality (0–6 months) to evaluate whether the results were driven by late deaths, and excluding extreme values of gestational age to examine the influence of outliers. In addition, gestational age was assessed for potential nonlinear relationships with survival using restricted cubic spline functions within the Cox proportional hazards framework. Model comparisons between linear and nonlinear specifications were performed using likelihood ratio tests.

Furthermore, models were re-estimated after excluding the mode of delivery variable to assess whether it distorted the effects of other covariates. Robust standard errors were also employed to account for potential model misspecification and to evaluate the stability of the estimates. The results from these alternative specifications were compared with those from the primary model to assess the consistency of hazard ratio estimates and overall survival patterns.

These sensitivity analyses were designed to evaluate the robustness of the findings to alternative model specifications and potential sources of bias.

Extended Cox proportional hazards model with time partitioning

The extended Cox model generalizes the standard Cox proportional hazards model by incorporating both time-independent and time-dependent covariates through time partitioning. Time dependence was modelled using a piecewise (interval-based) approach, in which covariate effects were allowed to vary across predefined time intervals. This was implemented by introducing interaction terms between selected covariates and a categorical time-group variable within the extended Cox framework.

This approach was chosen for a number of reasons. First, it provides a flexible yet interpretable way to capture non-proportional hazards. Second, it avoids strong parametric assumptions about the functional form of time-varying effects and third, it is well-suited for moderate sample sizes, where fully continuous time-varying coefficient models may be unstable. Model comparison (AIC and BIC)

The standard Cox model and the piecewise time-dependent Cox model were compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The model with the lower AIC and BIC values was considered the best model.

Discussions

This study examined the survival experience of infants within the first year of life using facility-based data. The Kaplan–Meier estimates indicate a progressive decline in survival probability over the first 11 months of life. At 1 month, the survival probability was 0.9716, suggesting that the majority of infants survived the neonatal period. However, a notable concentration of deaths occurred around 11–12 months, which is inconsistent with established epidemiological patterns, where mortality is typically highest during the neonatal period. The infants included in the dataset were those captured within hospital records, which may over-represent complicated or high-risk cases, and age at death may also be affected by rounding or misclassification. Consequently, the estimated hazard ratios should be interpreted as associations within a selected clinical population rather than as population-level causal effects.

With respect to the sex of the child, the Kaplan–Meier test confirmed that there was no statistically significant difference in survival between males and females. Regarding maternal education, the Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed a clear separation, with infants of educated mothers exhibiting better survival probabilities than those of uneducated mothers. For marital status, the Kaplan–Meier curves suggested that infants born to unmarried mothers may have slightly higher survival probabilities, although this difference was not statistically significant. In terms of place of residence, the log-rank test showed no statistically significant difference in survival between urban and rural infants.

With regard to maternal occupation, the Kaplan–Meier estimates indicated no statistically significant difference in survival between infants born to mothers working in the informal sector and those in the formal sector. Finally, for mode of delivery, the Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a statistically significant difference in survival between infants delivered via caesarean section and those delivered vaginally, with the former exhibiting higher survival probabilities. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect underlying clinical conditions rather than a direct causal effect of delivery mode.

The average number of deliveries per mother was 2.24 (SD = 1.22), indicating a moderately multiparous population with substantial variability in reproductive history. The mean gestational age at delivery was 35.6 weeks (SD = 5.88), which is below the standard full-term threshold of 37 weeks, suggesting a relatively high prevalence of preterm births within the cohort. Maternal age averaged 28.8 years (SD = 6.14), with a wide range (16–48 years), reflecting a diverse reproductive age distribution. The mean birth weight was 2.44 kg (SD = 0.64), indicating that, on average, infants were below the normal birth weight threshold of 2.5 kg. This suggests a notable burden of low birth weight in the study population. Overall, the descriptive statistics indicate that the cohort is characterized by moderate variability in maternal characteristics and a high prevalence of clinically relevant risk factors, including preterm birth and low birth weight, which may influence infant survival outcomes.

Despite the presence of clinically relevant risk factors identified in Table 1, including preterm birth and low birth weight, the Cox proportional hazards model did not demonstrate statistically significant associations for most variables, with the exception of gestational age (wpreg) and mode of delivery (mdelivery). Specifically, each additional week of gestation was associated with an estimated 7.0% increase in the hazard of death. Likewise, infants delivered through normal vaginal delivery exhibited a 1.87-fold higher hazard of death before 12 months compared to those delivered via caesarean section. These findings should be interpreted strictly as statistical associations rather than causal effects, and may reflect confounding by indication, measurement limitations, or selection bias inherent in facility-based data.

The relatively low average gestational age (35.6 weeks) and birth weight (2.44 kg) suggest a high-risk cohort; however, the lack of statistical significance in the Cox model for most variables may reflect limited variability within the sample or the influence of unmeasured clinical factors such as neonatal complications and access to care. Maternal age and number of deliveries exhibited moderate variability, yet their effects were not statistically significant, indicating that these socio-demographic factors may not independently explain differences in survival within this cohort.

Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the main findings were generally robust across alternative model specifications. Restricting the analysis to the first six months of life did not substantially alter the direction of the associations, although some effects were attenuated. Exclusion of extreme gestational age values produced similar estimates, suggesting that the results were not driven by outliers. Modeling gestational age using spline functions did not provide strong evidence of nonlinearity, supporting the use of a linear specification. Additionally, exclusion of mode of delivery from the model did not materially change the estimates of other covariates, indicating limited confounding. The use of robust standard errors did not materially alter the conclusions, indicating stability of the estimated effects. Overall, these findings suggest that the observed associations are reasonably robust, although they should still be interpreted with caution given the limitations of the dataset.

Furthermore, all variables in the model recorded DFBETA values below the threshold, suggesting that the model is stable and that the parameter estimates are reliable, with no evidence of influential outliers distorting the overall results. Assessment of the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residual tests and plots revealed that the effects of some covariates varied over time, justifying the use of a time-dependent Cox model. The plots further indicated that the effects of several covariates changed primarily within the first six months of follow-up, with relatively stable effects thereafter. Based on these observed patterns, the follow-up time was partitioned into three intervals: early infancy (0–6.2 months), mid-infancy (6.2–9.9 months), and late infancy (9.9–12 months). These cut-points were selected to reflect distinct phases in infant mortality risk and to align with the temporal patterns observed in the residual plots.

The piecewise time-dependent Cox model revealed that, while some factors showed variation in risk across time intervals, most maternal and infant characteristics did not have a consistently significant impact on infant survival. Overall, the model highlights that only a few variables particularly mode of delivery, gestational age, and place of residence exhibited notable time-dependent associations, whereas the majority of demographic and socio-economic factors remained largely non-predictive. Specifically, place of residence demonstrated strong time-varying effects. In the early period, residence was associated with an elevated hazard, while in the late period it became significantly protective, suggesting a shift in the influence of environmental and socio-economic factors over time. These findings are consistent with previous studies [14, 22, 24, 27]. Gestational age showed a significant positive association with mortality in the early and mid-periods, but not in the late period, indicating that its impact is concentrated in early infancy. Mode of delivery was significantly associated with increased hazard in the early and mid-periods, with no significant effect observed later, highlighting the short-term influence of delivery-related complications. These findings suggest that mode of delivery exerts a time-varying influence on infant mortality, with caesarean delivery associated with lower early-life risk, consistent with previous studies [31–34]. Other covariates, including sex, maternal age, marital status, maternal education, birth weight, parity, and occupation, were not significantly associated with infant mortality.

The piecewise Cox model demonstrated a substantially better fit to the data compared to the standard Cox model, as evidenced by lower Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values, suggesting that allowing for time-varying effects improves model performance.

Despite these strengths, the study has limitations. The use of hospital-based data may limit generalizability, and residual confounding cannot be ruled out.

Strengths of the study

This study contributes to the existing literature in two important ways. First, by integrating Kaplan–Meier estimation, Cox proportional hazards modeling, and extended analytical approaches including sensitivity analysis and time-dependent modeling the study offers a more comprehensive assessment of survival dynamics than is typically reported in facility-based research. Second, the study explicitly addresses key methodological concerns such as selection bias and confounding by indication, thereby improving the transparency and interpretability of the findings. These combined contributions enhance both the methodological rigor and policy relevance of the study, distinguishing it from prior work in this area.

Furthermore, developing a comprehensive, context-specific model for the Northern Region is essential for evidence-informed policy formulation and timely decision-making in resource-constrained settings. The findings also contribute new knowledge to the broader field of infant mortality research and provide actionable guidance for health planners and policymakers seeking to improve infant survival outcomes, particularly in the Northern Region of Ghana.

In addition, the rigorous survival modeling approach including tests of the proportional hazards assumption and sensitivity analyses for influential observations strengthens the reliability of the findings and supports evidence-based recommendations for maternal and child health interventions in the region. These methodological approaches provide deeper insight into early mortality dynamics compared to studies that rely on cross-sectional regression models.

From a policy perspective, the findings highlight the importance of strengthening early-life interventions within the first year of life in Ghana. The absence of strong associations between most socio-demographic factors and infant mortality within this facility-based cohort suggests that structural and health system factors such as access to timely neonatal care, quality of delivery services, and early postnatal monitoring may play a more critical role. These results support ongoing national efforts to improve maternal and child health services, particularly through investments in skilled birth attendance, neonatal intensive care capacity, and referral systems. Targeted improvements in these areas may yield greater reductions in infant mortality than interventions focused solely on individual-level characteristics.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings and in guiding future research. First, the analysis relied on facility-based observational data from a relatively small sample, which may limit generalizability and introduce selection bias, as infants not captured in hospital records were excluded. In addition, the dataset may be subject to recording and reporting biases, and age at death may be affected by rounding or misclassification. While these limitations may influence the precise timing of events, they are less likely to fully explain the broader patterns observed.

Second, some relevant confounding variables including detailed socioeconomic and clinical factors were unavailable and may have influenced the estimated associations, particularly for mode of delivery and gestational age. Similarly, several important covariates (e.g., maternal comorbidities and birth complications) were not captured, which may have resulted in residual confounding.

Third, infants who survived beyond the observation period were treated as censored at 12 months under the assumption of non-informative censoring, which may not always hold.

Fourth, nonlinear relationships particularly for gestational age may not have been fully captured, as the primary analysis focused on linear specifications within the survival modeling framework. In addition, mortality was not disaggregated into neonatal and post-neonatal periods, limiting insight into potential nonlinear patterns and possibly contributing to some counterintuitive findings. Future analyses should consider (i) categorical gestational age groups (preterm < 37 weeks, term 37–40 weeks, post-term ≥ 41 weeks), and (ii) flexible modeling approaches such as restricted cubic splines.

Finally, the findings may have limited generalizability beyond the study setting, particularly to rural populations or other regions of Ghana.

Conclusion

This study examined the survival experience of infants within the first year of life using facility-based data. The Kaplan–Meier estimator revealed no statistically significant survival differences across most variables, including sex of the child, maternal education, place of residence, marital status, and occupation. However, mode of delivery showed a significant difference in survival, with infants delivered via caesarean section exhibiting better survival outcomes than those born vaginally. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect underlying clinical conditions rather than a direct causal effect of delivery mode.

Descriptive statistics indicate that the cohort is characterized by moderate variability in maternal characteristics and a high prevalence of clinically relevant risk factors, including preterm birth and low birth weight, both of which are known to influence infant survival. The standard Cox proportional hazards model did not identify statistically significant associations for most covariates, with the exception of gestational age and mode of delivery. Specifically, each additional week of gestation was associated with an increased hazard of death, and infants delivered vaginally had a higher hazard of death compared to those delivered via caesarean section. These counterintuitive findings should be interpreted cautiously in light of potential data limitations, residual confounding, and measurement constraints.

The consistency of results across multiple sensitivity analyses, together with the absence of influential observations, supports the robustness of the findings within the study context. The piecewise time-dependent Cox model further revealed that place of residence exhibited strong time-varying effects, suggesting a shift in the influence of environmental and socioeconomic factors over time. Gestational age showed a significant positive time-varying association with mortality during the early and mid-infancy periods, while mode of delivery also demonstrated a time-varying influence, with caesarean delivery associated with lower risk in early life. Other covariates including sex of the child, maternal age, marital status, maternal education, birth weight, parity, and occupation were not significantly associated with infant mortality.

Overall, the study identifies gestational age and mode of delivery as factors associated with infant survival in the Northern Region of Ghana, particularly in Tamale West Hospital and Kpandai Polyclinic. However, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects, given the observational and facility-based nature of the data. The results should therefore be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, underscoring the need for further research using population-based datasets and more comprehensive clinical information.

Finally, the extended Cox model with time partitioning provided a better fit than the standard Cox model, as evidenced by lower Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values. While the analysis highlights the presence of time-varying associations, the study design limits causal inference. Consequently, the findings should not be used to infer direct effects or to guide clinical decision-making. Rather, they highlight important areas for further investigation and reinforce the need to strengthen neonatal care and maternal health services within the broader public health framework.
