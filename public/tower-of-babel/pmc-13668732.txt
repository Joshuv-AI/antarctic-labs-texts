Schizophrenia-associated glycoprotein Adamtsl3 regulates perineuronal net formation, maintenance, and adult cortical plasticity
Authors: Teresa M L Cramer, Saad B Hannan, Lucie Petre, Mark F Bear, Takao K Hensch, Shiva K Tyagarajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerineuronal nets (PNNs) regulate the maturation of parvalbumin-positive (PV+) interneurons during postnatal critical periods and continue to support their function thereafter. Their disruption is implicated in neuropsychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia, yet key endogenous PNN regulators remain unknown. Here, we identify the schizophrenia-associated glycoprotein Adamtsl3 as a PV+ cell-autonomous regulator of PNN integrity. Using mouse genetics, morphological and biochemical analyses, we demonstrate that Adamtsl3 deletion, both in early postnatal and adult stages, results in PNN deficits. Mechanistically, Adamtsl3 modulates matrix metalloprotease-9 (MMP9) activity, and Adamtsl3 deletion results in elevated MMP9 levels, PNN reduction, decreased Otx2 uptake, and heightened oxidative stress in PV+ cells. MMP9 hyperactivity and PNN reduction can be rescued by pharmacological inhibition of MMP9. Deletion of Adamtsl3 in PV+ interneurons revealed its cell-autonomous function in modulating PNN integrity. Notably, conditional Adamtsl3 deletion in adult PV+ cells reactivated juvenile-like ocular dominance plasticity. These results position Adamtsl3 as a persistent, PV+ specific PNN regulator with direct implications for the pathophysiology of schizophrenia.Subject terms: Schizophrenia, Neuroscience

Introduction

Perineuronal nets (PNNs) are specialized extracellular matrix (ECM) structures that primarily enwrap parvalbumin-expressing (PV+) interneurons, a major subtype of GABAergic cells in the neocortex [1]. Composed of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs) such as aggrecan, versican, brevican, and neurocan [2], PNNs form dense, lattice-like sheaths around the somato-dendritic compartments of particular PV+ cells. PNN formation coincides with the maturation of PV+ cells during windows of heightened plasticity in early postnatal development called critical periods (CPs) [1, 3]. Once established, PNNs restrict experience-dependent plasticity, stabilize inhibitory networks, and maintain excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance [3, 4]. Growing evidence points to PNNs as key regulators of circuit architecture, and their disruption has been linked to several neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders, including schizophrenia, Alzheimer’s disease, and Fragile X Syndrome [5–10]. Defining the molecular pathways that govern PNN assembly and stability is therefore crucial for understanding both normal brain function and disease mechanisms.

Matrix metalloproteases (MMPs) and disintegrin and metalloproteinases with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTSs) are endogenous regulators of the ECM / PNN. The ADAMTS superfamily comprises seven ADAMTS-like proteins (ADAMTSL1–6) and papilin [11, 12]. All ADAMTSL proteins are secreted glycoproteins that localize to the ECM, but their physiological functions remain poorly defined [13]. Notably, a putative functional variant in the Adamtsl3 gene has been implicated in schizophrenia by genome-wide association studies [14, 15], yet its role in the ECM/ PNN remains unclear.

MMP9 is one of the most studied regulators of PNNs. In the primary visual cortex (V1), MMP9 expression and activity are developmentally regulated, peaking before PNN formation (prior to postnatal day 14, P14) and declining during PNN maturation (P21–P42) [16]. Juvenile MMP9 knockout mice display precocious PNN formation [7], highlighting MMP9’s critical role in modulating PNN assembly and maintenance. MMP9 activity is tightly controlled by tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1 (TIMP-1), which binds both its pro- and active forms to modulate enzymatic function in response to neuronal activity [17, 18]. Other inhibitors, including additional TIMPs and thrombospondins, further contribute to the localized regulation of MMP9, forming an intricate network that shapes PNN structure [19].

We previously identified Adamtsl3 as a critical regulator of GABAergic synapse organization in the hippocampus [20]. In the present study, we extend this work to examine its role at the PNN in V1, where PNN development and function are well characterized [1, 21]. Our findings reveal that Adamtsl3 is localized to PNNs and plays a critical role in PNN formation and adult maintenance. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that Adamtsl3 inhibits MMP9 activity. Adamtsl3 deletion increases MMP9 activity and reduces PNNs, leading to reduced Otx2 uptake and oxidative stress in PV+ cells, which are restored upon pharmacological MMP9 inhibition. Constitutive and conditional deletion of Adamtsl3 in PV+ cells demonstrates a cell-autonomous role, with removal in adulthood reinstating juvenile-like ocular dominance plasticity. Together, these results establish Adamtsl3 as a key PV+ cell–autonomous regulator of MMP9 activity, PNN integrity, and cortical plasticity.

Results

Adamtsl3 localization to PNNs is crucial for their maturation and maintenance in V1

To examine Adamtsl3 expression in V1, we used a previously validated home-made antibody [20] in wild-type (C57BL/6) mice. For all in vivo experiments, cohorts included both male and female mice, with sex balanced across experimental groups. At postnatal day 90 (P90), we observed Adamtsl3 expression in PV+ cells across all cortical layers of V1. In principal neurons, Adamtsl3 expression was lower and layer-specific, with relatively stronger labeling observed in layer 5 (L5) than in layer 2/3 (L2/3) neurons (Fig. 1A). To assess whether Adamtsl3 expression was associated with glial populations, we co-stained sections with the microglial marker Iba-1 and the astrocyte marker ALDH1L1 and observed no overt co-localization of Adamtsl3 immunoreactivity with either marker (Fig. S1). These findings are consistent with glial transcriptomic datasets, which report negligible Adamtsl3 expression in microglia, and astrocytic Adamtsl3 mRNA only emerging under reactive inflammatory conditions [22].

To assess Adamtsl3 localization to PNNs, we co-stained with Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA), which binds N-acetylgalactosamine β1 residues in PNN glycoproteins, and aggrecan, a major structural component of PNNs [23, 24]. Near super-resolution imaging revealed Adamtsl3 puncta that co-localized with both PNN markers across cortical layers (Fig. 1B, two representative cells shown per layer). Quantitative analysis showed that 37 ± 3.9% of Adamtsl3 puncta co-localized with WFA in L2/3 and 28 ± 1.1% in L5 (Fig. 1B). These findings indicate that Adamtsl3 is associated with PNNs in adult V1.

PNN formation coincides with CPs during early postnatal development, with mature PNNs emerging around P30 as the CP begins to close by ~P60-90 in V1 [1, 21]. To investigate a role for Adamtsl3 in PNN maturation, we deleted Adamtsl3 by injecting adeno-associated viruses (AAV) AAV8-hSyn1-RFP-Cre (Cre) or AAV8-hSyn1-RFP (control) into the lateral ventricles of Adamtsl3flox/flox pups at P0 (Fig. 2A). This approach enabled broad transduction across cortical layers and robust Cre expression by P17 (Fig. S2A), consistent with previous reports [25, 26]. We focused on L2/3, where PV+ cells form local microcircuits with principal cells [27]. Immunostaining confirmed a marked reduction in Adamtsl3 expression in Cre-injected animals at P17 (Fig. 2B), validating the efficiency of our gene-ablation strategy. To assess the impact on PNN maturation, we stained for WFA (Fig. 2C) and aggrecan (Fig. S2B) at key developmental stages of CP onset in V1 (P17, P21, P24, and P30). Signal intensities of both markers were significantly reduced surrounding L2/3 PV+ cells in Cre-injected mice compared to controls (Figs. 2C & S1B), indicating that Adamtsl3 contributes to PNN maturation in V1.

To assess the role of Adamtsl3 in maintaining PNN integrity in the adult brain, we utilized a tamoxifen-inducible Cre system (AAV8-hSyn1-ERT2-Cre) injected into the lateral ventricles of Adamtsl3flox/flox pups at P0. Cre recombinase expression was induced after CP closure (P60) via intraperitoneal administration of tamoxifen (TAM+) for five consecutive days, as previously described [28] (Fig. S2C). Analysis at P90 revealed reduced signal intensities for WFA (Fig. 2D) and aggrecan (Fig. S2D) in TAM+ animals compared to vehicle-treated (TAM-) controls. These findings indicate that Adamtsl3 supports not only PNN maturation but also the maintenance of PNN integrity in adult V1.

To investigate the neuron subtype-specific roles of Adamtsl3 in V1, we selectively deleted Adamtsl3 in either principal cells or GABAergic interneurons. P0 Adamtsl3flox/flox pups were injected with AAV expressing tamoxifen-inducible Cre recombinase driven by a principal cell-specific promoter [29] (AAV2/6-CaMKIIαERT2-Cre) or an interneuron-specific promoter [30] (AAV8-hDlxERT2-Cre) [20]. At P60, tamoxifen was administered intraperitoneally for five consecutive days to induce cell-type-specific recombination. WFA staining revealed intact PNNs after Adamtsl3 deletion in principal neurons (Fig. 2E). In contrast, reduced PNN intensity was observed following interneuron-specific deletion (Fig. 2E). These results demonstrate an interneuron-specific role for Adamtsl3 in the formation and maintenance of PNNs in V1.

Early loss of Adamtsl3 disrupts PNN composition, Otx2 uptake, and redox balance

The molecular composition of PNNs is heterogeneous [31]. While WFA and aggrecan are widely used, to further assess the impact of Adamtsl3 deletion on PNN structure, we surveyed several core PNN components in Adamtsl3flox/flox mice injected at P0 with either AAV8-hSyn1-RFP-Cre (Cre) or control AAV8-hSyn1-RFP. Consistent with our prior observations in V1, Cre-injected mice continued to show significantly reduced WFA and aggrecan signals at P90 (Fig. 3A). Tenascin-R, another major PNN constituent, was also reduced at P90, whereas brevican levels were unaffected (Fig. 3A). These findings indicate that early postnatal loss of Adamtsl3 alters PNN composition by affecting specific molecular components.

Next, we examined the expression of the transcription factor Otx2, whose PNN-mediated uptake promotes PV+ cell maturation and PNN maintenance through the CP [32, 33]. Immunolabelling revealed reduced Otx2 levels within L2/3 PV+ cells across CP timepoints and in P90 Cre-injected mice compared to controls (Fig. S3A). As postnatal Otx2 is synthesized in distant sources [32–34], we examined whether reduced Otx2 in cortical PV+ cells resulted from impaired PNN-mediated uptake or decreased retinal production. At P0, we injected Adamtsl3flox/flox pups with either AAV8-hSyn1-RFP-Cre (Cre) or control AAV8-hSyn1-RFP in the lateral ventricles and collected cortical and retinal tissues at P30 for Otx2 quantification by Western blot. Cortical Otx2 levels remained unchanged, whereas retinal Otx2 levels were elevated in Cre-injected mice (Fig. S3B), perhaps reflecting a compensatory response to impaired PNN-mediated uptake. Together, these findings suggest that PNN disruption following early postnatal Adamtsl3 deletion impairs Otx2 uptake into PV+ cells.

Enzymatic removal of PNNs has been shown to increase the vulnerability of adult PV+ cells to oxidative stress [35]. To determine whether Adamtsl3 deletion affects redox balance, we labeled 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-oxo-dG), a DNA adduct formed by guanine oxidation via hydroxyl radicals [36, 37]. While 8-oxo-dG levels in L2/3 PV+ cells were unchanged at P30, a significant increase was observed by P90 in Cre-injected mice compared to controls (Fig. S3C), pointing to a progressive accumulation of oxidative stress in adult PV+ cells. Together, these findings demonstrate that early postnatal deletion of Adamtsl3 disrupts PNN composition and integrity, resulting in impaired Otx2 uptake and increased oxidative stress in adult PV+ cells.

Adamtsl3 regulates MMP9 activity through two distinct functional domains

To investigate the mechanism underlying PNN reduction following Adamtsl3 deletion, we examined MMP9, a known regulator of PNN integrity [7, 11, 19]. In rat primary neurons, endogenous Adamtsl3 and recombinant MMP9 (pCMV-myc-MMP9) at 7 days in vitro (DIV7) revealed protein co-localization at DIV14 (Fig. 3B). To confirm biochemical interaction, we performed co-immunoprecipitation in HEK-293T cells co-transfected with plasmids encoding GFP-Adamtsl3 and myc-tagged MMP9. MMP9 is synthesized intracellularly as an inactive proenzyme (~95 kDa) that is secreted and activated by cleavage of its pro-domain to generate the mature form (~85 kDa) [38]. While no interaction was detected in whole-cell lysates, we observed an interaction between Adamtsl3 and the active form of MMP9 in serum-free media (Fig. 3C), indicating that Adamtsl3 preferentially binds to the secreted, enzymatically active form of MMP9.

Next, we tested whether Adamtsl3 modulates MMP9 enzymatic activity using gelatin zymography. Serum-free media were collected from HEK-293T cells transfected with plasmids encoding pCMV-myc-MMP9, pBP84-eGFP-Adamtsl3, or pCMV-FLAG-TIMP1 (tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 1), a known endogenous inhibitor of MMP9. Cells transfected with an auto-activating MMP9 mutant MMP9G100L (pCMV-myc-MMP9G100L) served as a positive control [39]. Compared to MMP9 alone, activity was significantly reduced in the presence of either TIMP-1 or Adamtsl3 (Fig. 3D, D′), indicating that Adamtsl3 reduces MMP9 activity.

To identify domains within Adamtsl3 required for inhibiting MMP9, we generated five Adamtsl3 deletion constructs (M#1–5) (Fig. S4A). In gelatin zymography assays, constructs M#2 (lacking the cysteine-rich spacer region) and M#5 (lacking thrombospondin repeat (TSR) domains 10–12) failed to reduce MMP9 activity, implicating these regions in Adamtsl3-mediated inhibition of MMP9 (Fig. S4B). Together, these results demonstrate that Adamtsl3 interacts with the active form of MMP9 in the extracellular space and modulates its enzymatic activity in vitro via distinct domains.

Adamtsl3 maintains PNN integrity via MMP9 activity

To assess the role of Adamtsl3 in regulating MMP9 activity in vivo, we used immunostaining to detect the active (processed) form of MMP9 in Adamtsl3flox/flox mice injected at P0 with AAV8-hSyn1-RFP-Cre (Cre) or AAV8-hSyn1-RFP (control). In Cre-injected mice, active MMP9 levels were significantly elevated around L2/3 PV+ cells at P17 compared to controls (Fig. 4A), and this increase persisted at P24, P30, and P90 (Fig. S4C). Because MMP9 activity is typically regulated by its endogenous inhibitor TIMP-1 [40], we examined whether changes in TIMP-1 expression could account for this increase. Western blot analysis of cortical tissue at P30 showed no difference in TIMP-1 levels between Cre-injected and control animals (Fig. 4B). These results indicate that Adamtsl3 deletion increases MMP9 activity in vivo through a mechanism independent of TIMP-1 levels.

To test whether elevated MMP9 activity drives PNN disruption in Adamtsl3-deficient mice, we treated animals with the synthetic gelatinase inhibitor SB-3CT [41, 42]. Adamtsl3flox/flox mice injected with AAV8-hSyn1-RFP-Cre at P0 were treated with SB-3CT (i.p.) for four consecutive days before the CP (P17–P20), and PNN integrity and Otx2 uptake were assessed at P21 (Fig. 4C). Active MMP9 levels were significantly reduced in Cre-injected mice treated with SB-3CT compared with vehicle-treated controls, while WFA, aggrecan, and Otx2 markers were elevated (Fig. 4D). These results demonstrate that Adamtsl3 functions as an endogenous brake on MMP9 in vivo, and that PNN deficits caused by Adamtsl3 deletion can be rescued by pharmacological blockade of MMP9.

Adamtsl3 functions autonomously within PV+ cells

To confirm whether Adamtsl3 functions autonomously within PV+ cells to regulate PNNs, we crossed Adamtsl3flox/flox mice with a PV-Cre driver line to generate PV-specific Adamtsl3 knockout (PV-KO) mice (Fig. 5A). At P120, we validated PV+ cell-specific deletion of Adamtsl3 using in situ hybridization (Fig. S5A) and by immunolabelling (Fig. S5B), which showed a near-complete loss of Adamtsl3 signal in PV+ cells. To confirm PV-specific Adamtsl3 deletion, we stained V1 for Adamtsl3 in L5 pyramidal neurons, confirming preserved expression in non-PV+ neurons (Fig. S5B′). PV-Cre control (Ctrl) and PV-KO mice were viable, fertile, and produced litters in Mendelian ratios with no abnormalities. Quantification of PV+ cell density in V1 revealed no differences between genotypes, indicating that Adamtsl3 deletion does not affect interneuron migration or survival to P120 (Fig. S5C). Analysis of PNN-associated markers showed reduced WFA staining in L2/3 PV+ cells (Fig. 5B) and significantly elevated levels of the oxidative stress marker 8-oxo-dG (Fig. 5C) and active MMP9 (Fig. 5D) in PV-KO mice compared to controls. These findings establish that Adamtsl3 functions autonomously within PV+ cells to regulate PNN integrity and MMP9 activity.

PV cell-specific deletion of Adamtsl3 disrupts GABAergic neurotransmission

PNN disruption has been linked to alterations in the electrophysiological properties of PV+ cells and nearby pyramidal neurons [43–45]. To assess the functional consequences of PV+ cell-specific Adamtsl3 loss in PV-KO mice, we first examined PV+ cell synapse density by labeling for Synaptotagmin 2 (Syt2), a marker of PV+ presynaptic terminals [46]. In naive mice, PNN maturation correlates with an increase in PV+ cell synapses, both onto other PV+ cells and onto pyramidal neurons [47]. In PV-KO mice, Syt2+ terminals were reduced on PV+ cell somata (Fig. S5D) and more broadly in L2/3 (Fig. S5D′). These findings suggest that PNN deficits caused by Adamtsl3 deletion reduce both PV+ to PV+ cell connectivity and inhibitory input from PV+ cells onto L2/3 pyramidal neurons.

To corroborate these findings, we performed whole-cell recordings of spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSC) from L2/3 pyramidal neurons in adult mice (P ~ 120–210). In PV-KO mice, the frequency and amplitude of sIPSC were significantly reduced compared to controls (Fig. 5E-E″), indicating reduced inhibition. Although PNN disruption may variably impact PV-circuit output across contexts, reduced inhibitory drive has been observed in other paradigms [48, 49], consistent with our findings in V1.

Adult PV cell-specific deletion of Adamtsl3 reinstates juvenile-like plasticity in V1

Given the well-established role of PNNs in restricting experience-dependent plasticity [1, 10], we investigated whether Adamtsl3 deletion alone could reinstate juvenile-like plasticity in adult V1. To selectively affect PV+ cells, we stereotactically injected AAV9/2-S5E3-RFP-Cre (S5E3-KO) or AAV9/2-S5E3-RFP (Ctrl) into V1 of P60-65 Adamtsl3flox/flox mice (Fig. 6A) and allowed 20–21 days for robust viral expression (Fig. S6A). Consistent with previous reports [50], the S5E3 promoter effectively targeted PV+ cells, with over 80% of RFP⁺ cells co-expressing PV (Fig. S6B). Immunostaining confirmed Adamtsl3 reduction (Fig. 6B) and a significant loss of WFA labeling around L2/3 PV⁺/Cre⁺ cells (Fig. 6B′) spanning V1 (Fig. S6C), validating the PV-target specificity and effective dismantling of PNNs by our gene ablation strategy.

We next investigated whether Adamtsl3 deletion altered baseline visual responses in adult V1. We injected V1 of P60–65 Adamtsl3flox/flox mice with either AAV9/2-S5E3-RFP-Cre (S5E3-KO) or AAV9/2-S5E3-RFP (Ctrl) virus. Visual evoked potentials (VEPs) recorded from layer 4 of binocular V1 at 20–21 days post-injection revealed significantly elevated absolute VEP amplitudes in S5E3-KO mice (Figs. S6D, 3E, E′). This finding is consistent with reduced GABAergic activity (Fig. 5E), as evidenced by cortical disinhibition and elevated baseline VEP responses [43, 51].

To directly assess whether PV-specific Adamtsl3 deletion reinstates juvenile-like plasticity, we performed a standard 4-day monocular deprivation (MD) protocol to measure ocular dominance (OD) in adult mice [52–54]. In S5E3-KO or Ctrl mice prepared as above, VEPs were recorded 20-21 days post-injection both before and 4 days after MD (Fig. 6C). In normally reared adults [52], responses driven by the contralateral eye are typically 2–3 times larger than those from the ipsilateral eye, resulting in a contralateral-to-ipsilateral (C/I) VEP ratio between 2 and 3 and an ocular dominance Index (ODI) between 0.3 and 0.5.

In Ctrl mice, there was no effect of 4-day MD on either the C/I ratio (Fig. 6D) and ODI (Fig. 6E) or the absolute amplitude of VEPs (Fig. 6F, H) evoked by the two eyes, consistent with a lack of OD plasticity in adult V1. In contrast, S5E3-KO mice exhibited a significant OD shift after 4 days of MD, with the C/I ratio shifting towards the non-deprived (ipsilateral) eye (Fig. 6D, E). This shift was entirely driven by a reduction in the deprived contralateral eye response (Fig. 6G, H), closely resembling the plasticity observed in juvenile mice around P30 [52]. These findings demonstrate that selective deletion of Adamtsl3 from adult PV+ cells is sufficient to restore juvenile-like plasticity in adult mouse V1, as expected when PNNs are dismantled [1, 10, 51].

Discussion

The formation of PNNs during CPs is crucial for limiting experience-dependent plasticity, stabilizing inhibitory circuits, and maintaining the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission. In this study, we identify Adamtsl3 as a glycoprotein associated with PNNs that is essential for both their developmental maturation and adult maintenance in V1. Notably, large-scale genome-wide association studies have robustly implicated ADAMTSL3 as a schizophrenia risk gene [55, 56], providing a strong rationale to investigate how ADAMTSL3 regulates extracellular matrix organization and inhibitory circuit stability in the cortex. By using conditional deletion of Adamtsl3 at specific developmental stages and in specific neuronal subtypes, we demonstrate that Adamtsl3 plays a vital role in both PNN maturation and maintenance. Furthermore, Adamtsl3 functions as an extracellular inhibitor of MMP9, which helps to constrain experience-dependent plasticity in the adult V1.

Adamtsl3 and MMP9 Regulation

MMPs, particularly MMP9, play a crucial role in the remodelling of the ECM and PNNs in the postnatal brain. While MMP9 activity is typically regulated by TIMPs, such as TIMP-1, which binds both pro- and active MMP9 [17, 18], additional modulators, including thrombospondins, have been implicated in fine-tuning MMP9 activity [19]. In this study, we have identified Adamtsl3 as an extracellular inhibitor of MMP9. Unlike TIMP-1, Adamtsl3 selectively interacts with the active, secreted form of MMP9 in the extracellular space, thereby reducing its enzymatic activity independently of TIMP-1. This inhibitory function is mediated by two critical Adamtsl3 domains: the cysteine-rich spacer region and C-terminal TSRs 10–12. Both regions are highly conserved within the ADAMTS superfamily and have been implicated in MMP regulation by related family members, such as ADAMTS-1 [57], ADAMTS-4 [58], and ADAMTS-13 [59]. Notably, TSR domains are known to inhibit MMPs [60].

These findings provide a mechanistic framework for interpreting human genetic associations at ADAMTSL3, linking schizophrenia-associated variation to a defined molecular mechanism that impacts PNN integrity. Notably, the schizophrenia-associated variant rs950169 (T1663I) is predicted to generate a truncated Adamtsl3 protein lacking C-terminal thrombospondin type 1 repeats (TSR10–12) [15]. Within the broad context of altered gene expression observed in cortical and hippocampal tissue from individuals with schizophrenia [61], and GWAS evidence implicating ADAMTSL3 among ECM‑related risk genes [62], this variant is likely to have functional consequences in brain regions critical for cognition and sensory processing. Loss of the C-terminal TSR domains would be expected to impair Adamtsl3-mediated inhibition of MMP9, thereby promoting excessive extracellular matrix proteolysis. Such dysregulation provides a plausible mechanistic link between ADAMTSL3 genetic risk, PNN destabilization, and PV+ interneuron dysfunction in schizophrenia [35]. Consistent with this model, overactivation of the MMP9–RAGE signaling pathway has been shown to drive oxidative stress and inhibitory–excitatory imbalance in both animal models and schizophrenia patients, and pharmacological inhibition of MMP9 has been reported to restore PNN integrity and promote PV+ interneuron maturation [63].

Although our data suggest a specific role for Adamtsl3 in regulating MMP9, its inhibitory profile may extend to other MMPs, such as the closely related MMP2. Nevertheless, MMP9 was the only metalloproteinase found to show mRNA changes in response to monocular deprivation [64], highlighting its role as the key ECM-remodelling enzyme linked to experience-dependent plasticity in V1. While our study focuses on PNN-associated MMP9, both Adamtsl3 and MMP9 are expressed in other neural compartments [20, 65]. Therefore, Adamtsl3 may also regulate MMP activity beyond PNNs, potentially influencing extracellular remodelling in the diffuse ECM as well as synaptic remodelling and dendritic structure.

Cell-Autonomous Roles of Adamtsl3 in PV+ Cell PNN Maintenance

While PV+ cells play a crucial role in maintaining their own PNN integrity through cell-autonomous mechanisms [51], external factors also contribute to PNN stability [32, 34, 66]. Given that Adamtsl3 is expressed in both principal cells and PV+ interneurons in V1, we employed a PV-specific Adamtsl3 knockout (PV-KO) strategy to investigate its cell-autonomous function. Our findings revealed that Adamtsl3 within PV+ cells is essential for preserving PNN integrity and GABAergic neurotransmission onto L2/3 pyramidal neurons. We focused on layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons because somatic GABAergic responses in these cells provide a sensitive readout of the plastic state of primary visual cortex [67], a process strongly shaped by PV+ interneuron function [4]. Consistent with this, prior work has indicated that changes in somatic GABAA receptor–mediated synaptic efficacy at PV-pyramidal synapses are critical for regulating experience-dependent plasticity [68]. Thus, considering our PV- specific knockout approach and the established PV contribution to L2/3 somatic inhibition [67, 68], the observed sIPSC changes most likely reflect altered PV+ interneuron function.

In a complementary approach, conditional deletion of Adamtsl3 in adult PV+ cells (S5E3-KO) also led to a significant reduction in PNN integrity and a substantial increase in baseline visual evoked potential (VEP) amplitudes, consistent with reduced inhibitory control. Prior work indicates that the functional consequences of PNN disruption for PV-circuit output are heterogeneous and context dependent. Prolonged PNN degradation has been associated with reduced PV excitability and weakened inhibitory drive, as well as a shift of V1 networks toward a more disinhibited, juvenile‑like state [43, 48, 69–71]. In contrast, others observed no changes in PV-pyramidal cell transmission but instead identified reductions in inhibitory input onto PV cells, FosB expression in PV interneurons, and gamma oscillatory activity [72, 73]. In our study, PV-specific Adamtsl3 deletion was accompanied by reduced inhibitory drive onto L2/3 pyramidal neurons and increased VEP amplitudes, supporting the idea that Adamtsl3 functions within PV+ cells to regulate MMP9 activity and PNN integrity, with downstream consequences for inhibitory circuit function. We further identified reduced Otx2 uptake in PV+ cells during development and elevated levels of oxidative stress markers in adulthood, which could be downstream consequences of reduced PNN integrity. These findings align with prior evidence implicating PNNs in supporting PV+ cell function by facilitating Otx2 uptake and reducing oxidative stress [32, 34, 35]. Pharmacological inhibition of MMP9 using SB-3CT partially restored PNN integrity and increased internalized Otx2 levels, emphasizing the therapeutic potential of targeting MMP9 in conditions where PNN disruption is implicated [9, 63].

We have previously shown that Adamtsl3 localizes to inhibitory presynaptic terminals in the hippocampus, where it regulates GABAergic neurotransmission via deleted in colorectal cancer (DCC) signalling [20]. Thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that the reduced inhibition observed here partly reflects a loss of presynaptic Adamtsl3 function, independent of PNN integrity. However, several aspects of the current data identifies a role for Adamtsl3 at PNNs: (1) PNN deficits caused by Adamtsl3 deletion can be rescued by pharmacological inhibition of MMP9, indicating that Adamtsl3 acts upstream of MMP9 in the PNN maintenance pathway; (2) the electrophysiological phenotype of both PV-KO and S5E3-KO mice mirrors that produced by enzymatic PNN removal [43–45, 51]; and (3) the PV+-specific deletion spares other inhibitory populations in V1, suggesting a localized, PNN-dependent mechanism.

We further propose that Adamtsl3 isoforms differentially contribute to the results presented here and in Cramer et al. [20]. In mice, three splice variants have been described, two of which contain a truncated C-terminal PLAC domain [15], a structural motif implicated in extracellular matrix interactions [74, 75]. Therefore, distinct isoforms may account for Adamtsl3’s compartment-specific functions at PNNs versus presynaptic sites. However, a key limitation of the present study is that our current antibody is directed against an N-terminal epitope and therefore does not distinguish between isoforms. Consequently, all immunolabeling and biochemical signals reported here likely reflect a composite of multiple Adamtsl3 isoforms. As a result, the relative contributions of individual isoforms to the PNN phenotype versus synaptic effects remain unresolved in the current study. Understanding how alternative splicing influences Adamtsl3 localization, its interaction with DCC, and its regulation of MMP9 will be crucial for disentangling its distinct roles at synapses and at the PNN. Notably, the schizophrenia-associated Adamtsl3 variant is predicted to generate a truncated C-terminal [15], likely compromising its localization and function at PNNs. Such disruptions could contribute to PNN deficits and the associated PV+ interneuron dysfunction observed in schizophrenia.

Adamtsl3 and experience-dependent plasticity

Given the well-established role of PNNs in restricting experience-dependent plasticity [10, 51], we investigated whether Adamtsl3 deletion alone could reinstate juvenile-like plasticity in adult V1. We found that PV-specific deletion of Adamtsl3 reduced PNNs and restored juvenile-like OD plasticity in adulthood. This supports the critical role of PNNs as gatekeepers of cortical plasticity. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that enzymatic degradation of PNNs using chondroitinase ABC shifts OD in adult V1 [43, 76]. MMP9 has also been directly implicated in OD plasticity: MMP9 knockout mice exhibit impaired plastic responses to MD during the CP [77], and MMP9 activity is required for reactivation of plasticity following antidepressant treatment in adults [78]. Together with our observation that Adamtsl3 inhibits MMP9 activity, these findings strongly suggest that elevated MMP9 activity following Adamtsl3 deletion provides the mechanistic basis for the reinstatement of OD plasticity in adulthood. While we cannot exclude the possibility that reduced inhibition due to loss of presynaptic Adamtsl3 function in PV+ cells contributes to the observed OD plasticity, the close phenotypic overlap between Adamtsl3 deletion and PNN-disrupting manipulations supports a PNN-mediated mechanism. The induction of robust OD plasticity after Adamtsl3 deletion, driven by reduced responses to the deprived eye, as observed in juveniles [52], underscores the essential role of the Adamtsl3–MMP9 brake in maintaining the PNN barrier to experience-dependent circuit remodeling after the CP. Dysregulation of this axis may therefore contribute to pathological plasticity or impaired critical-period closure in neurodevelopmental disorders such as schizophrenia [35, 62].

Animals and ethics statement

All procedures fulfilled the ARRIVE guidelines on experimental design, animal allocation to different experimental groups, blinding of samples to data analysis and reporting animal experiments. All procedures on animals were conducted in accordance with the European Directive 86/609 (EEC Council for Animal Protection in Experimental Research and Other Scientific Utilization), approved by the cantonal veterinary office of Zurich, the institutional animal care and use committees of Harvard University, Boston Children’s Hospital and MIT and followed the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health and the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International.

Mouse lines

Adamtsl3flox/flox mice with conditional alleles carrying loxP sites in exons 2 and 10 were generated using CRISPR/cas9 (Cyagen, USA) in BL6 background. Adamtsl3 knock-out mice were generated through lateral ventricle P0 injection of AAV8-hSyn1-RFP-Cre (or AAV8-hSyn1-RFP control), AAV8-hSyn1-ERT2-Cre, AAV2/6-CaMKIIα-ERT2-Cre, and AAV8-hDlx-ERT2-Cre as previously described [25, 26]. AAV injections were assigned at random to littermates from homozygous breeding. Both male and female mice were used for all in vivo experiments, and sex was balanced across experimental groups. Cre recombinase activity from ERT2-Cre was induced at 8 weeks by five consecutive days of tamoxifen injection (i.p) (dissolved in corn oil, Sigma T5648; 1 mg/ day). Control animals were injected with vehicle alone. Animals receiving tamoxifen (TAM+) were assigned at random. Adult PV-specific knock-out mice were generated through stereotactic injection of AAV9/2-S5E3-RFP-Cre (or AAV9/2-S5E3-RFP control) in V1 and animals receiving Cre or control vectors were assigned at random. All recombinant AAVs were produced by the Viral Vector Core at the University of Zürich, except for AAV2/6-CaMKIIα-ERT2-Cre, which was obtained from Vector Biolabs (#2014-1208). PV-Cre mice (B6;129P2-Pvalbtm1(cre)Arbr/J, Jackson ID 008069) were generously provided by Takao Hensch, Harvard University. Adamtsl3-PV-Cre mouse line was generated by crossing female, heterozygous PV-Cre mice with Adamtsl3flox/flox mice. All mouse lines were fed with water and food under ad libitum conditions and were housed in a 12 h light/dark cycle. None of the mouse strains used in this study showed any signs of gross abnormalities. The sample size was chosen to ensure adequate power to detect the pre-specified effect size based on the power calculation for a simple two-group comparison.

Animals were allocated to experimental groups using a randomized assignment procedure to minimize selection bias. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random sequence, ensuring that each animal had an equal probability of being assigned to any group. Where applicable, allocation was stratified to balance key variables (e.g., sex, or age) across groups. Samples were processed in a randomized order, and investigators were blinded to group allocation during data acquisition and analysis whenever possible.

Data variability within each group was assessed and is reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) (or standard deviation, as appropriate). Estimates of variation were derived from independent biological replicates (n = number of animals), and are presented in the figures and Results sections. The assumption of homogeneity of variance was evaluated prior to statistical testing (e.g., using Levene’s test or an equivalent test). Variances were comparable between groups, justifying the use of parametric statistical tests. Where unequal variances were detected, appropriate corrections (e.g., Welch’s correction) were applied.

Immunohistochemistry

All mice were anesthetized (Nembutal; 50 mg/kg i.p.) and perfused with ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF; 125 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 26 mM NaHCO3, 25 mM D-glucose, 2.5 mM CaCl2, and 2 mM MgCl2), which was oxygenated (95% O2, 5% CO2) for 15 min. Brains were immediately fixed in 4% PFA for 90 min at 4 °C. After rinsing in PBS, brains were incubated in 30% sucrose (in PBS) at 4 °C overnight for cryo-protection. The frozen brains were cut into 40 µm thick coronal sections using a sliding microtome for immunohistochemistry and stored in the anti-freeze buffer. Relevant sections were selected and after 3 times 10 min washes in Tris-Triton Solution (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Triton X-100, pH 7.4), sections were incubated in primary antibody solution (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.4% Triton X-100, 2% normal goat serum, pH 7.4) at 4 °C overnight. Primary antibodies are listed in Key Resources Table. Sections were washed 3 x 10 min in Tris-Triton Solution and incubated in secondary antibody solution (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Triton X-100, 2% normal goat serum, pH 7.4) for 30 min at room temperature with secondary antibodies raised in goat. All secondary antibodies used were diluted 1:500. Antibodies conjugated to AlexaFluor-488, AlexaFluor-594 and AlexaFluor-647 were purchased from Invitrogen. Sections were washed 3 times for 10 min in Tris-Triton Solution and mounted on gelatin-coated slides using Fluorescence Mounting Medium (Dako).

Image acquisition and fluorescence intensity quantification

Analyses were performed on coronal sections spanning AP + 0.64 to −4.55 mm relative to bregma, encompassing the full V1 extent. Layer 2/3 V1 fields of view (FOVs) were identified in both hemispheres using the Allen Brain Atlas. Within each FOV, PV+ interneurons were randomly selected based solely on PV immunoreactivity; the remaining channels (WFA/PGC/Tenascin/aMMP9/8-oxo-dG/OTX2) were not viewed during selection to blind the analyst to marker intensity. For AAV-based experiments, AAV8 and AAV2/6 spread was evaluated using a 10× objective, and mice exhibiting inadequate viral transduction/coverage were excluded from subsequent morphological analyses. Z-stack images were acquired for all sections using near–super-resolution Airyscan confocal microscopy (LSM 800 with Airyscan, Carl Zeiss). Images were collected with a 40× oil-immersion objective (NA 1.4) at a pixel size of 112 nm², with 6 optical sections acquired at 0.5 µm intervals. Laser power and detector gain/offset were set using the range indicator to ensure that no channel contained saturated pixels. All imaging parameters were kept constant between groups. Fluorescence intensity and puncta density was quantified from maximum-intensity projections in Fiji/ImageJ using a custom Python-based script (https://github.com/dcolam/Cluster-Analysis-Plugin) implemented as an openly available Fiji/ImageJ plugin [79, 80]. In brief, for puncta quantification, images were processed by generating an automated ROI/mask for PV+ interneuron somata based on PV immunolabeling. Puncta were then detected and counted within the PV mask to quantify somatic Syt2 puncta on PV+ neurons, and puncta in the surrounding neuropil were quantified using the inverse mask (excluding PV+ somata) with the same detection parameters. To reduce variability, images were captured from 3 sections per mouse, and a minimum of 3 mice were analyzed per genotype. For each mouse, 15 PV+ interneurons per section were quantified across 3 sections. Representative images were processed in Fiji/ImageJ. Statistical analyses were performed in Prism (GraphPad), with n ≥ 3 mice per group, and are provided in the figure legends.

Plasmids and Adamtsl3 deletion mutants

pBP84-eGFP-Adamtsl3 plasmid was generated and generously provided by Berangere Pinan Lucarre, University of Lyon. pCMV6-myc-MMP9 and pCMV6-FLAG-TIMP-1 plasmid were purchased from Origene. MMP9 point mutant G100L was generated in-house using site-directed mutagenesis, and Adamtsl3 deletion mutants were generated by VectorBuilder Inc using site-directed mutagenesis. All expression constructs used were confirmed and checked for protein expression before HEK293T cell/primary neuron transfection.

Immunoprecipitation and western blot

Immunoprecipitation and Western blot protocols were performed using whole cell lysate or serum-free media from HEK293T cells (ATCC, female donor origin) or mouse brain tissue in EBC lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100 containing CompleteMini Protease Inhibitor (Roche), and phosphatase inhibitor mixture 1 and 3 (Sigma Aldrich). For immunoprecipitation, 1 µg of anti-myc antibody (Key Resources Table) was used to precipitate myc-tagged MMP9 from transfected HEK293T cell lysate and serum-free, conditioned media for 90 min at 4 °C on a rotor. Complexes were collected by incubating lysates with 25 µl of Pierce Protein A/G Ultralink Resin (Thermo, Cat no. 53132) for 45 min at 4 °C on a rotor. Complexes were centrifuged and washed three times in ice cold EBC buffer to remove nonspecific interactions, followed by denaturation in 2xSDS loading buffer at 90 °C for 5 min. The immunoprecipitated complexes were analyzed using Western blot.

Retinas were isolated through a slit in the cornea and western blot analysis of mouse retina was performed following sonication in Tris-HCl (100 mM, pH 7.5). Western blot analysis of mouse brain was performed following homogenization and 1 h incubation on ice in EBC lysis buffer. Lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 RPM for 30 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were stored at −80 °C. Samples were run on Tris-glycine polyacrylamide gels and proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes. Primary antibodies (see Key Resources Table) were incubated in Tris-buffered saline with 0.05% Tween 20 (TBST), including 5% Western Blocking solution (Roche) overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were washed 5 × 5 min in TBST. HRP-coupled donkey secondary antibodies (1:30,000) and fluorescent-coupled donkey secondary antibodies (1:20,000) were incubated for 30 min at room temperature, and membranes were washed again 5 times 5 min in TBST. Fluorescent signals were captured using the Odyssey® CLx Imager. Super Signal West PICO Chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was applied to visualize HRP labelled antibodies and developed using the FUJIFILM Luminescent Image Analyzer LAS-1000 plus & Intelligent Dark Box II (Fujifilm). Images were analyzed using ImageJ and statistical tests were performed using Prism software (GraphPad) using a minimum of 3 mice.

Primary neuron culture transfection and immunofluorescence

Primary rat embryonic hippocampal neuron culture preparation and transfection were done as described previously [81]. Rat cultures were used because lipofection is more efficient and consistent than in mouse primary neurons. Cells were transfected after 7 DIV with 1 μg of myc-MMP9 using lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and magnetofection beads (1:10 diluted, CombiMag, OZ Bioscience). After 15 min of incubation at room temperature, the plasmid-lipofectamine-magnetofection mix was added to cover slips / culture well. Culture plates were placed on top of magnetic plates during the 45 min transfection in the 37 °C incubator. The coverslips were transferred to conditioned media and processed for immunofluorescence at 14 DIV. Coverslips were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS containing 10% normal goat serum, and stained using anti-myc and anti-Adamtsl3 antibody for 90 min at room temperature. Goat anti-Alexa 488 (Molecular Probes) and goat anti-Cy3 (Jackson Immunoresearch) were used to label the proteins for immunofluorescence. All secondary antibodies used were diluted at 1:500. Coverslips were mounted on glass slides using a fluorescence mounting medium (DAKO).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (ISH)

Brain tissue (fresh, not fixed) was embedded in OCT compound (Tissue-Tek, Torrance, CA, USA) and immediately flash frozen on dry ice. The embedded tissue was then sectioned at 20 μm on a cryostat, mounted directly onto Superfrost Plus slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Triple fluorescence ISH for Adamtsl3, Parvalbumin and CamKIIα was conducted on sections obtained from PV-Cre (control) and PV-Adamtsl3-KO mice using the probes Adamtsl3 (ACDbio, 474891) and Parvalbumin (ACDbio, 421931). All incubations were performed at 40 °C in a hybridization oven (HybEZ, ACD, Hayward, CA, USA) using RNAscope Fluorescent Multiplex Detection Kit (ACDbio, Cat no. 320851) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for fresh-frozen brain tissue. Images were taken using a 40x objective with a numerical aperture of 1.4, and pixel size of 112 nm2. All imaging parameters were kept constant between groups and representative example images were processed using ImageJ.

Slice Preparation and Electrophysiological Recordings

Acute brain slices were prepared from adult (P ~ 120–210) mice with 2–7 neurons recorded per animal. The brain was extracted after terminal anaesthesia with isoflurane and immersed in ice-cold slicing solution composed of (mM): 130 K-gluconate,15 KCl, 0.05 EGTA, 20 HEPES,4 Na pyruvate, 25 glucose and 2 kynurenic acid, pH 7.4, bubbled with 5% CO2 and 95% O2. 350 μm coronal sections containing the visual cortex were cut using a Leica VT1200S vibroslicer. The slicing solution was exchanged at 37 °C for 60 min with a recording solution containing (mM): 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 2 kynurenic acid, and 25 glucose, pH 7.4. Slices were equilibrated at room temperature for at least 1 hr before recording. Whole-cell recordings of inhibitory postsynaptic currents were carried out using patch electrodes of resistances 3 – 5 MΩ filled with an internal solution containing (mM): 120 CsCl, 1 MgCl2, 11 EGTA, 30 KOH, 10 HEPES, 1 CaCl2, and 2 K2ATP; pH 7.3. Slices were superfused with the recording solution, and cells were at a holding potential of −60 mV with optimized series resistance (Rs <10 MΩ) and whole-cell membrane capacitance compensation. Changes of Rs greater than 10% during the experiment resulted in the recording being excluded from analysis. IPSCs were analyzed using WinEDR and WinWCP (John Dempster, University of Strathclyde, UK), and the frequency was calculated over 60-second epochs of recording. IPSC amplitude distributions were constructed by using the first hundred IPSC amplitudes per cell. IPSC kinetics were measured from uncontaminated events.

Adult ocular dominance plasticity

P60-P65 Adamtsl3flox/flox mice of both sexes received stereotaxic injections of either AAV9/2-S5E3-RFP-Cre or control AAV9/2-S5E3-RFP, combined with implantation of tapered tungsten microelectrodes (300–500 MΩ) into layer 4 of the binocular zone, as previously described [53, 82]. Following surgery, a 21-day period was allowed for viral expression and Adamtsl3 deletion before experimental manipulations. Habituation began two days prior to the initial visual evoked potential (VEP) measurements. On the first day, mice were acclimated to restraint while viewing a gray screen binocularly for 30 min. On the following day, animals experienced 15 min of monocular viewing per eye, achieved by covering the non-stimulated eye with an opaque paddle. For VEP acquisition, animals were presented with sinusoidal gratings (0.2 cycles/degree, 100% contrast) that reversed phase at 2 Hz, following standard procedures [53, 82]. A novel stimulus orientation differing by at least 30° from prior sessions was used each day. Signals were processed using custom C++ and MATLAB scripts. Monocular deprivation (MD) was induced at P79-82, 1–3 h after recording baseline PreMD VEPs. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane (1–3% in oxygen) and kept on a heating pad throughout the procedure. One eye was sutured closed with 1–2 mattress stitches (Prolene 6-0, Ethicon). After four days, sutures were removed under anaesthesia, eyes were rinsed with sterile drops, and Post MD VEPs were collected ≥2 h later to ensure recovery. Eyes were examined pre- and post-MD to confirm ocular health before data collection, and only VEP recordings with stable baselines and clear signal-to-noise ratios were included in the analysis.

Gelatinase zymography assay

MMP9 activity was assessed using the gelatin zymography protocol by Abcam. HEK293T cells (ATCC, female donor origin) were grown to 50% confluency before transfection of eGFP-Adamtsl3, eGFP-Adamtsl3 deletion mutants, FLAG-TIMP-1, myc-MMP9 expression plasmids using standard PEI-based transfection at a ratio of 1 µg plasmid to 4 µg PEI. At 70–80% confluency, cells were washed and continued to grow cells in FBS-free media. 24–48 h later, conditioned media was collected and concentrated 10x. Relevant samples were prepared and combined in 5x non-reducing sample buffer at 10 µg/ml per protein sample and run on a 7.5% acrylamide gel containing gelatin (4 mg/ml). Gels were washed to remove SDS (2.5% Triton X-100, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 5 mM CaCl2, 1 µM ZnCl2) and incubated with incubation buffer (1% Triton x 100, 50 mM Tris HCl pH 7.5, 50 mM CaCl2) for 24 h at 37 °C. Gels were stained with Coomassie Blue for 1 h at RT and destained until clear bands were detected. For loading control, samples were run on Tris-glycine polyacrylamide gels and analyzed by Western blot as described above. Images were captured using FUJIFILM Luminescent Image Analyzer LAS-1000 plus & Intelligent Dark Box II (Fujifilm) analyzed using ImageJ using a minimum of 3 samples/group. Statistical tests were performed using Prism software (GraphPad).

SB-3CT administration

SB-3CT (APExBIO, Cat no. B4792) was dissolved in 25% DMSO, 65% PEG 400 and 10% water. Vehicle control injections received the same solvent mixture without SB-3CT. After filtration, animals were injected i.p. at 25 mg/kg for four consecutive days. At day 5, mice were anesthetized and perfused with ACSF as described above.
