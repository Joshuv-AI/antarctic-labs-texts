A novel thermal image based cold object detection and classification using machine learning algorithms
Authors: Siva Rajesh Chiluveru, Mogali Tarun, Potla Sai Lakshmi Chandana, Anjani Devi Tadiparthi, Emani Venkata Sai Rama Yeswanth, Shuvendu Rana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cold object detection and classification allow reliable identification and categorization of low-temperature objects using non-contact thermal analysis, thereby complementing standard heat-focused techniques. It supports accurate monitoring of cooling behaviour, improves fault detection, quality control and remains powerful under poor lighting or visually challenging situations. However, cold object classification remains challenging when using conventional visible-light images due to the absence of discriminative temperature information. As of now, dedicated and systematic research on cold object detection and classification using thermal images are still very limited, highlighting a clear research gap. To address this limitation, this work proposes a thermal image–based cold object detection and classification framework using machine learning algorithms, designed to achieve effectiveness, reduced cost, and lower processing time. In addition, a dedicated dataset is developed by capturing time-dependent temperature variations of multiple cold object categories, enabling well ordered analysis and classification based on their thermal behaviour. Several machine learning models, including Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, and other classification algorithms, were developed and assessed using the proposed dataset. Among these models, the Random Forest classifier gives the highest classification accuracy of 99.35%, demonstrating its effectiveness in capturing temporal thermal variations for accurate cold object detection and identification. This work establishes a new research direction in thermal image analysis by shifting the focus from heat anomaly classification toward reliable cold object classification.

Introduction

Thermal Infrared (TIR) imaging is a quite successful technology applied variably from the factory to the surveillance camera. This imaging is applied by calculating the amount of heat emitted by objects to form a heat map. There was a period when research was almost solely concentrated on spotting heat anomalies such as a dysfunctional semiconductor component1, a heated machine2, or a fire3. This was the easiest approach since heated objects always stood out from their surroundings, which were relatively cooler. Hence, it was quite simple to locate them using simple technology due to their larger wavelength.

However, a serious issue arises when considering the opposite end of this spectrum: cold objects. Cryogenic storage tanks4, freezer parts5, or cold chain products6 are commonly designed to be substantially colder than their environment. The characterization of these cold objects is a lot more difficult due to low thermal contrast. The warmer environment causes rapid degradation of object boundaries due to natural diffusion phenomena, where cold regions appear fuzzy and cannot easily be distinguished from background noise. This is a serious development concern in important fields like predictive maintenance in freezers or quality inspection of frozen products.Fig. 1Visible and thermal images of different cold objects used in the study.

Having a proper classification system to identify all the cold objects will be a game-changer. The distinct thermal patterns for different cold objects can be used for dataset generation and evaluation of the proposed cold object detection and classification framework.

In this work, a new specialized framework for cold object detection and classification using machine learning is proposed to overcome the limitations A highly effective system is proposed to process the raw thermal data for the extraction of unique features that accurately define a cold object. In this approach, the major objective is to make the system less costly as well as faster. To achieve accurate classification, several leading Machine learning models are developed and evaluated, i.e. Decision Tree, Random Forest, and, critically, the XGBoost algorithm, using the extracted thermal features.

By enabling reliable cold object detection and classification, the system moves beyond simple heat detection to offer major benefits like early failure prediction for cold equipment and improved energy optimization. This pioneering work sets a new and valuable direction for future research in thermal image analysis.

Related work

Thermal imaging, or infrared thermography, involves converting the radiated long-wavelengths into temperature-dependent pixels, such that objects can be sensed and analyzed depending on their thermal properties rather than visual appearance. There has been a considerable amount of study conducted towards detecting hot objects, where such objects usually form temperature spikes and hence are amenable to conventional thermal sensing techniques. On the other hand, cold object detection based on thermal imaging technology has not seen much progress because of the fact that low temperature spots are relatively more difficult to discern, especially against the noisy background, and has not been extensively studied previously. This work represents one of the first attempts in this direction. Hence, the survey first focuses on reviewing existing techniques for hot object detection and then moves on to describe novel work for cold object detection in thermal imaging.

Xiaojin Gong et al7. introduced a deep learning framework for the real-time, automatic detection and diagnosis of equipment parts within electric power systems using infrared thermography. Their method introduced a deep convolutional neural network (CNN) designed not only to predict the coordinates and class type of equipment but also to estimate the orientation angle of each part, incorporating a prior concerning orientation consistency to enhance accuracy.

Abeer D. Algarni et al8. proposed that research in Thermal Infrared (TIR) image analysis is primarily dominated by the detection of hot anomalies and positive thermal contrast. They noted that while early computer vision methods, such as the Histogram of Gradients (HoG) technique, and initial applications of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have proven highly effective for these high-contrast tasks, these established methodologies are inherently optimized for heat detection. Consequently, they fail when confronted with the negative thermal contrast and blurring typical of objects colder than the ambient environment.

Amith Khandakar et al9. validated the use of machine learning for the early diagnosis of diabetic foot ulceration risk by classifying plantar foot thermograms based on the Thermal Change Index (TCI). Their comparative study found that the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) classifier, using engineered features, achieved the best result with 90.1% accuracy in multi-class classification. This established the high potential of ML for automated thermal health diagnostics.

Bo Wang et al10. proposed the PVF-10 dataset, a high-resolution TIR image dataset of 10 photovoltaic(PV) fault classes (5 repairable, 5 irreparable). It validates Deep Learning algorithms, achieving up to 93.32% accuracy, and includes analysis of the faults’ circuit mechanisms. The use of resampling and padding improved classification results.

Publio Elon Correa da Silv and Jurandy Almeida11 proposed using Deep Learning (DL) with Edge Computing (EC) for real-time plant leaf disease classification via a new thermal image dataset. They evaluated models like MobileNet on resource-constrained devices, achieving speeds up to 2.13x faster than high-end GPUs using pruning and quantization. This optimization demonstrated that EC can efficiently handle agricultural monitoring tasks while maintaining high accuracy.

Fatih Mehmet Senalp and Murat Ceylan12 addresses the need for better thermal image quality by proposing Thermal Super Resolution Generative Adversarial Network (TSRGAN+), a super-resolution (SR) deep network, and creating three neonatal thermal image datasets. TSRGAN+ showed superior performance, increasing PSNR by 1–1.5 dB over state-of-the-art models. Ahmet Haydar Ornek and Murat Ceylan13 proposed a thermal-imaging–based method for assessing neonatal health, using a CNN model trained on real NICU thermograms to distinguish healthy from unhealthy cases. Their system achieved more than 99% accuracy across all datasets, and to address the black-box nature of CNNs, they applied several explainable-AI techniques—including GradCAM, GradCAM++, LayerCAM, EigenCAM, and an ensemble method called CodCAM—to visualize the image regions driving the model’s predictions.Fig. 2Temperature variation (in °C) over time for different cold objects under controlled conditions.

Similarly, most of the existing14–23 studies mainly focus on heat object detection and classification using thermal images. These methods rely on high-temperature regions as key discriminative features.

Recently, S R Chiluveru and S Rana24 proposed a machine-learning framework for classifying different metals using thermal imagery, showing that even subtle temperature variations can provide strong discriminatory power when modeled appropriately. In their work, decision tree and random forest classifiers were applied, and high classification accuracy was observed for datasets collected during both heating and cooling stages. The results showed that there was useful and learnable information in thermal response patterns, which could form a basis for more advanced tasks in the analysis of thermal images. Even though the work was focused on classification, the key concepts are relevant to the objectives of this project, specifically in the use of temperature gradients for the identification and analysis of hot and cold objects in a thermal image.

On the basis of an in-depth analysis of the existing literature, it has been found that the thermal image-based research interests have mainly concentrated on the identification and classification of hot objects or hotspots. There have been scant studies on cold objects in the past, primarily due to the effects of surrounding factors and the lack of distinct approaches or strategies to identify or classify cold objects from thermal images. The existing research gap has been identified as the primary motivation in conducting the proposed research work to analyze the identification and classification of cold objects from thermal images.

Thermal image acquisition

Thermal images were taken using the FLIR C5 thermal camera in controlled indoor setups. All the items were first stored in the refrigerator for a period of 12 hours to cool them uniformly. Upon taking the items out of the refrigerator, they were left in the environment; hence the images from the thermal camera were taken for a set time of 10 seconds. The images continued being taken until the items reached the environmental temperature. Items from different classes such as ice creams, drinks, medicines, and metals had to be considered to get a variety of behavior from the items in the experiment.

The influence of surrounding temperature was reduced by conducting experiments using two volumes (50 mL and 100 mL) for cold drinks, ice cream, water, and fruit juice. Smaller volumes undergo faster heat exchange with the environment due to lower thermal mass. The use of multiple volume levels helps minimize ambient thermal effects and improves the reliability of thermal measurements. Temperature data extraction

A data extraction system from thermal images is proposed to extract time-dependent temperature information of cold objects from thermal images acquired at fixed time intervals. Initially, an empty data structure is created to store time–temperature pairs, and the image acquisition interval is defined as 10 seconds. For every thermal image, its corresponding time of acquisition will be calculated based on a predefined interval.

Next, each thermographic image is further processed to obtain the temperature information at the pixel-level directly from the embedded information, leading to a temperature map which signifies the spatial distribution of temperatures in the scene25. Using this temperature map, the cold object region is localized by identifying pixels exhibiting lower temperature values relative to the surrounding background. Connected component analysis is subsequently applied to isolate the region corresponding to the cold object.

Once the cold object region is identified by using adaptive thresholding, morphological opening, and connected component analysis using 8-connectivity, followed by selection of the largest valid cold region for temperature estimation. After the cold object region is detected, a refined binary mask is obtained through morphological erosion, and a minimum enclosing circle is computed using OpenCV’s cv2.minEnclosingCircle() function. The circle is then overlaid on the thermal image to provide a compact visual representation of the detected object region and to indicate the area used for temperature estimation. The mean temperature of the region is computed to obtain a representative temperature value for that object at the given time instant. The computed temperature is paired with its corresponding acquisition time and stored in the data structure. This process is repeated for all images of different cold objects in the sequence, and the complete set of time–temperature pairs is returned as the final output for further analysis. Dataset preparation

A structured dataset26 was formed using the extracted temperature data. Each data entry in the dataset corresponds to a small temperature sequence which contains a randomly chosen initial temperature, along with three randomly generated time points to measure the temperature. The three subsequent time points were created by generating random intervals between 5 and 20 seconds and adding them successively to the starting time. For each generated time point, the nearest available temperature measurement was selected from the original thermal trajectory, ensuring that all generated points remained within the duration of the recorded experiment. This is similar to how samples can be taken in irregular conditions in a realistic scenario, thereby adding to the variability in the dataset. Initially, larger datasets of approximately 60,000 and 30,000 records were generated. These datasets contained numerous duplicate or highly similar samples, which caused overfitting during model training. After removing duplicate entries and reducing redundancy, a final dataset of 7,000 records was selected. The final dataset of 7,000 samples was synthetically augmented from experimentally measured thermal data by generating short time–temperature sequences with randomly selected observation intervals. This approach was used to simulate irregular sampling conditions and increase data variability. Since the generated samples were derived from the same underlying thermal observations, their statistical independence is limited, which may affect generalization to entirely new datasets. However, 10-fold stratified cross-validation demonstrated stable and reproducible model performance. The description of the dataset26 is explained in Section “Dataset generation”.

Classification and evaluation

The above-mentioned prepared dataset is then utilized for training and testing a variety of machine learning classifiers. These include Random Forest27, XGBoost28, Decision Tree29, SVM30, KNN31, Ridge Classifier32, K-Means33, and Naive Bayes34. All these classifiers were tested using the prepared dataset, in order to train and test them. All the classifiers were tested independently, for example, the accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and the confusion matrix. These steps are used to compare the classifiers to find out the one that performs the best in the classification of the cold object using the thermal dataset.

Equipment setup

Thermal images have been captured by using a FLIR C5 thermal camera, as shown in the Fig. 4. It works on a long-wave infrared LWIR spectrum. The experiments have been conducted for all objects by the camera with its default factory setting for consistency and to be able to reflect the realistic operating condition that can be encountered practically. Further, the camera was put at a fixed distance from the objects to maintain consistent image resolution and viewing geometry for all the measurements.

To minimize external thermal perturbations, the objects under study were put in front of a thermally neutral background in a controlled indoor environment. No extra calibration and/or tuning of parameters were carried out to allow the captured thermal images to represent the genuine thermal characteristics of the cold objects under test. This setup standardizes experiments and ensures repeatability; besides, it supports reliable cold object detection based exclusively on thermal image information.

The thermal images for each object were captured by a FLIR C5 thermal camera, with its default settings, every 10 seconds for a fixed time. The distance between the camera and the objects was kept constant, on top of a thermally neutral background, to reduce the interference from the environment. Images were captured until objects reached near ambient temperatures. Afterwards, the temperature data were extracted from the recorded thermal images and used for dataset preparation and cold object detection analysis.

Each data sample represents a short thermal trajectory. For each sample, an initial time–temperature pair was randomly selected from the source thermal measurements. Three random time intervals were independently generated and successively added to the initial time to determine the subsequent observation points. For each generated time point, the corresponding temperature values were recorded. The resulting time variables represent the elapsed durations relative to the initial measurement.

Dew point temperature was considered to account for ambient moisture conditions during thermal data acquisition, which may affect the observed thermal response of cold objects. The dew point temperature was computed using the ambient air temperature and relative humidity recorded during the experiment. A standard empirical formulation was employed to estimate the dew point, which relates temperature and humidity as given in Eq. 11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} T_{\text {dew}} = T - \frac{100 - RH}{5} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where, the dew point temperature(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\text {dew}}$$\end{document}) is computed using ambient temperature(T) and relative humidity(RH) (as record in the time of the experiment). The 100 represents 100% relative humidity, which corresponds to saturated air. The term (100 - RH) therefore indicates how far the current air humidity is from saturation. 5 is an empirical scaling constant that approximates how much the dew point temperature decreases for each 5% reduction in relative humidity.

In this case, high dew point means moist air. If a cold object is below the dew point, water vapour condenses on it and releases latent heat, helping the object warm faster and vice versa. Hence the temperature gap between the cold object and due point plays an important role. The dew point temperature difference is calculated using the Eq. 22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta T_{\text {dew}} = T_{\text {dew}} - T_{\text {start}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where, the dew point temperature gap(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta T_{\text {dew}}$$\end{document}), defined as the difference between the dew point temperature(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\text {dew}}$$\end{document}) and the starting object temperature(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\text {start}}$$\end{document}) of the sample, which is used as an additional thermal feature.

Discussion

The experiment results show that temporal thermal behaviors contain discriminative information for detecting and classifying cold objects. Through the combination of temporal thermal behaviors and the difference between the dew-point temperature and air temperature, the proposed approach can efficiently extract the warming features of various cold objects.

Among all the classifiers assessed, the tree based classifiers proved to be the most efficient. Random Forest had the best accuracy at 99.35%, followed by XGBoost at 99.28% and Decision Tree at 97.14%. The confusion matrix displayed dominance along the diagonal which suggested that the samples had been accurately classified.

The dew point temperature gap significantly improved performance by incorporating the effect of ambient moisture conditions. In addition, 10-fold cross-validation confirmed the robustness of the proposed framework, with Random Forest achieving a mean accuracy of 99.33% and a 95% confidence interval of 99.07%–99.58%, which is intuitively reflected in the MCC35 of each model in Table 7.

Moreover, the use of dew point breaks the boundaries of the controlled environment and makes the model applicable to all natural environments.

Overall, the results confirm that the proposed thermal image-based methodology provides a reliable and statistically robust approach for accurate cold object detection and classification even in new test samples as reflected in Table 8.

Conclusion

This research presents a novel thermal image–based framework for cold object detection and classification, addressing a largely underexplored problem in thermal image analysis. By leveraging thermal data and time-dependent temperature variations, the proposed approach enables reliable and non-contact classification of low-temperature objects under challenging visual conditions. The development of a dedicated dataset and the evaluation of multiple machine learning models demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed methodology, with the Random Forest classifier achieving a superior performance of 99.35% accuracy. The experimental results confirm that temporal thermal behaviour provides strong discriminatory information for cold object detection and classification. Overall, this work establishes a new direction beyond conventional heat-focused thermal analysis and lays the foundation for future research on cold object detection and classification and related temperature-based intelligent systems.

Future Work

Overall, the results validate the feasibility of cold object detection and classification using thermal images and temporal temperature information. The proposed methodology establishes a reliable baseline for cold object recognition and can be extended in future work by incorporating larger datasets by incorporating, additional object categories, and advanced deep learning techniques to further enhance classification robustness and generalization.
