Exploring the impact of 2D/3D urban morphology on land surface temperature within the diurnal cycle in Tianjin
Authors: Yujie Bai, Meng Wang, Yang Yan, Haitao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe urban heat island (UHI) effect not only impacts urban climates and residents’ quality of life but also poses challenges to energy consumption and sustainable development in cities. While many studies have explored the relative importance and marginal effects of two-dimensional (2D)/ three-dimensional (3D) urban morphology on land surface temperature (LST) to mitigate UHI, the interactive effects of these 2D/3D morphology metrics on daytime and nighttime LST at different grid scales have been largely overlooked. This study focuses on the area within the outer ring of Tianjin, and analyzes the relative importance, marginal effects, and particularly the interaction effects of 2D/3D urban morphology on LST. Our findings reveal the following: (1) The normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) has the most significant cooling effect across all variables and at all three grid scales. (2) NDVI and bare land coverage have the greatest impact on daytime LST, while building height and tree height (TH) predominantly influence nighttime LST. (3) The relationships between key 2D/3D metrics and LST are nonlinear. Overall, NDVI is negatively correlated with LST across all three grid scales. (4) Interactions between 2D/3D metrics affect LST; LST decreases when TH exceeds 1.8 m and building density is below 62%, or when TH is below 1.8 m and building density exceeds 62%. These findings provide valuable insights and recommendations for sustainable urban development and effective heat adaptation strategies in specific locations.Keywords: Urban morphology, Land surface temperature, Diurnal cycle, Interaction effectsSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

As urban areas continue to expand, natural vegetation and farmland are increasingly replaced by artificial impermeable surfaces. This irreversible land transformation not only alters the surface energy balance but also raises temperatures in urban areas compared to surrounding rural regions, a phenomenon known as the urban heat island (UHI) effect1. Under the backdrop of global climate change, the UHI effect has significant adverse impacts on human health and well-being2–4. It exacerbates issues like increased energy consumption5,6environmental pollution7and higher rates of heat-related mortality8–10. Additionally, UHI effects have been linked to psychological health issues and the potential rise in violent crimes11.

To address the challenges posed by the UHI effect, understanding the correlation between urban morphology and land surface temperature (LST) is crucial. Most studies on this topic have focused on the relationship between urban morphology and LST, since both air temperature (AT) and LST are commonly used indicators to evaluate the UHI effect12,13. Although air temperature data collected from meteorological stations better reflects human thermal perception14these stations are often spaced far apart and mostly located in suburban areas, making it difficult to achieve high spatial resolution. In contrast, satellite remote sensing provides an efficient way to obtain high-resolution LST data15.

Recent studies have extensively explored the relationship between urban morphology and LST, revealing that urban morphology is a key factor influencing LST16–20. Early research primarily analyzed this relationship from a two-dimensional (2D) perspective, examining how factors such as the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), building density (BD), and road density influence LST. For example, NDVI has been identified as a dominant factor affecting the spatial heterogeneity of LST during summer21. Additionally, studies have consistently shown that green space and impervious surface density are negatively and positively correlated with LST, respectively22,23. Landscape metrics, such as the largest patch index (LPI) and aggregation index (AI), have also been widely recognized for their role in characterizing LST9,24,25.

While much attention has been given to the horizontal expansion of cities, the vertical dimension—three-dimensional (3D) urban morphology—has emerged as another crucial factor in urban expansion. Recent studies have begun to investigate the impact of 3D urban morphology on LST, with various metrics used to quantify this form, including building height (BH)9sky view factor (SVF) and tree height (TH)26. These studies indicate that BD, rather than height, has a stronger influence on LST27. Moreover, combinations of buildings of different heights and forms can affect LST by creating shading, facilitating ventilation corridors, and altering solar radiation28,29. Additionally, trees are more effective at cooling than low-lying vegetation30.

Despite significant research on the relationship between urban morphology and LST across various regions, times, and scales, most studies have focused on the impact of single variables on LST. The interaction effects between 2D and 3D urban morphology on LST remain underexplored. Interaction effects refer to the combined influence of two or more variables on an outcome or response. These effects, which occur when the impact of one variable change depending on the level of another, are more complex than the main effects of individual variables and can provide more precise insights.

Previous studies have primarily used multivariate regression models or spatial regression techniques to explore the relationship between 2D/3D urban morphology and LST9,20,31. Scholars have employed these models to analyze how built environment factors affect LST31,32. Some studies have also applied local models, such as geographically weighted regression (GWR), to investigate the spatial heterogeneity of these factors. In addition to traditional statistical models, recent research has adopted nonlinear approaches, such as random forest (RF) and boosted regression trees (BRT), to assess the relative importance and marginal effects of urban morphology on LST. However, these nonlinear models are often considered “black boxes” due to the difficulty of interpreting the complex relationships within them.

To address this challenge, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) can enhance the interpretability of nonlinear models. SHAP provides a way to directly quantify the interactive effects of 2D and 3D urban morphology on LST, offering deeper insights. While previous studies have advanced our understanding of the nonlinear relationships between 2D/3D urban morphology and LST, the use of SHAP to reveal these interactions has been limited. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the interactive effects of 2D/3D urban morphology on LST.

In this study, we employed the RF algorithm to estimate our model and applied SHAP to interpret the contribution of each input variable. SHAP, grounded in game theory’s Shapley values, supports both global and local analysis, making it a powerful tool for model interpretation. The advantages of SHAP provide a solid foundation for our investigation. Specifically, this research aims to comprehensively assess the role of 2D/3D urban morphology in influencing LST across different grid scales, focusing on the following objectives: (1) compare the LST differences between key 2D and 3D urban morphology indicators; (2) explore the relative importance of 2D and 3D urban morphology on day and night LST across different grids; (3) uncover the marginal effects of key 2D and 3D urban morphology indicators; and (4) reveal the interactive effects of these key indicators on LST. These findings will offer strategies for optimizing urban landscapes to improve the thermal environment.

Data and methods

Study area

Tianjin is one of the four directly governed municipalities in China, located between 116.43°E and 118.04°E longitude and 38.30°N and 40.15°N latitude. As of the end of 2023, the permanent population of Tianjin had exceeded 13 million (Tianjin Statistics Bureau, 2024). The city has a warm temperate, semi-humid continental monsoon climate, with distinct seasons. Typically, the highest monthly average temperature, approximately 26 °C, occurs in the hot month of July, while the lowest monthly average temperature, around − 3 °C, occurs in January. The annual average temperature ranges between 12 °C and 15 °C, with an annual precipitation of 500–700 mm. Tianjin generally experiences low cloud cover, which facilitates the collection of ECOSTRESS temperature data.

Our study focuses on the central area of Tianjin, covering approximately 500 square kilometers within the outer ring road, as shown in Fig. 1. Since the beginning of the 21st century, the central urban area has undergone significant expansion: the period from 2001 to 2010 was characterized by rapid expansion at a rate of 29.9 km²/a; between 2010 and 2015, the expansion rate slowed to 12.51 km²/a, during which the structure of the central city was preliminarily established33.Over the past three decades, the central urban area of Tianjin has undergone a substantial increase in size. Driven by an influx of migrant workers and abundant employment opportunities, both land surface roughness within construction areas and population density have risen rapidly. Concurrently, persistent increases in solar radiation coupled with decreasing humidity have contributed to a declining trend in overall urban thermal comfort34,35. As one of the cities with higher residential density within the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei urban agglomeration36Tianjin experiences a more pronounced UHI effect, with its average land surface temperature (LST) rising from 25.32 °C in 2005 to 27.93 °C in 2020 and extreme heat areas (> 35 °C) expanding by 233%. The city’s urban core exhibits intensified heat island effects, where temperatures can be 3.87–7.26 °C higher than surrounding rural zones, exacerbating thermal discomfort37. These trends are further amplified by rapid urbanization, including a 618 km² increase in impervious surfaces and significant loss of vegetation and water bodies35,38.

Data source

LST

The LST data used in this study were obtained from NASA’s ECOSTRESS Level-2 products. The ECOSTRESS project has been operational since June 29, 2018, with a revisit cycle of approximately 3 to 5 days and a spatial resolution of 70 m, allowing for data collection at various times of the day39. Simulation and validation results have shown that these ECOSTRESS LST images are accurate to within 1 K, regardless of surface type40,41. This provides excellent spatial and temporal resolution for studying diurnal temperature variations in urban areas.

To explore effective ways to mitigate LST, this study focuses on analyzing the hottest months of the year in Tianjin. During humid and stifling periods, such as July and August, obtaining cloud-free ECOSTRESS data is challenging. The data incompleteness, along with the need for evenly distributed data collection times, further limits the available data for analysis. Therefore, the data were collected from 2019 to 2023, with six temporally similar time points selected: three during the daytime and three at night. Methods

Study framework

As shown in Fig. 3, the study began by obtaining day and night LST data from ECOSTRESS products, which were then gridded for analysis. A land cover map was created using canopy height and building datasets43,Subsequently, from the perspectives of landscape patterns, building morphology, and land use, 24 indicators were selected, and the variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to filter these indicators across different grids. Third, the RF was employed to explore the nonlinear between LST and the selected variables. Finally, the marginal and interactive effects of urban morphology on grid-based diurnal LST were examined.

Grid analysis

In previous studies, researchers often relied on Landsat 8 remote sensing images to retrieve LST. However, due to the 100 m resolution of Landsat 8’s LST data44using a grid size equivalent to the pixel size may introduce significant errors9,39as it does not capture finer spatial variations. Approaches like Wang et al.’s adoption of coarser grids (300–900 m) mitigate intra-grid heterogeneity but sacrifice spatial detail45.

In contrast, ECOSTRESS provides higher native resolution (70 m). This finer scale inherently reduces intra-pixel LST variability, enabling precise urban thermal characterization41. Consequently, we employ grids matching ECOSTRESS pixel dimensions (70 m) to preserve maximum spatial fidelity for morphology-LST quantification. For multi-scale analysis, we implement hierarchical grids (70 m → 140 m → 210 m) to systematically evaluate scale-dependent 2D/3D morphology impacts on LST.

Influencing factors selection

Urban morphology exerts a significant influence on LST, which is captured through various 2D and 3D indicators in this study. For 2D morphology, given that vegetation and impervious surfaces dominate the study area, we applied five landscape metrics to assess vegetation features, aiming to reduce multicollinearity. The selected metrics include: (1) the percentage of landscape (PLAND), which quantifies the proportion of a specific land cover type46; (2) patch density (PD), representing the number of patches per unit area; (3) LPI, which reflects the size of the largest patch relative to the landscape; (4) AI, measuring the spatial clustering of land cover types22,25; (5) landscape shape index (LSI), indicating the complexity of patch shapes47. Furthermore, NDVI provides a comprehensive measure of vegetation coverage derived from remote sensing spectral data48. While not based on direct spatial geometric calculations, its spatial distribution patterns (e.g., grid-mean NDVI) effectively quantify vegetation abundance and density—core characteristics of 2D land cover morphology49. Extensive validation in urban thermal research confirms NDVI as a key proxy for characterizing vegetation coverage patterns and their cooling effects on LST50. PLAND measures the proportion of vegetation coverage, LSI captures the complexity of patch shapes, and AI indicates the degree of aggregation. For instance, PLAND_T quantifies tree coverage, LSI_W measures the complexity of water bodies, and AI_I used to assess the aggregation of impervious surfaces.

For 3D urban morphology, we selected building density (BD), building height (BH), sky view factor (SVF), and tree height (TH) as core metrics based on their pivotal roles in regulating urban surface energy balance and extensive research validation. Among these factors, Building Density (BD) directly governs surface heat dissipation capacity, airflow circulation, and shadow coverage51In Tianjin, areas with high building density (BD > 52%) exhibit a significant positive contribution to LST, making BD one of the most critical drivers of urban warming52. while BH significantly modifies local wind environments, thermal radiative exchange efficiency, and mutual shading effects between structures53. SVF quantifies the geometric sky exposure of surface points, serving as the key determinant of longwave radiative cooling potential with lower values indicating stronger thermal retention26In Tianjin, SVF demonstrates a clear gradient increase from the city center to suburban areas52. TH dictates canopy shading effectiveness and transportive cooling capacity, directly controlling thermal conditions beneath and surrounding vegetation54In Tianjin, tall trees (TH > 12 m) are predominantly concentrated in urban parks (e.g., Tianjin Water Park, Nancuiping Park) and historical districts (e.g., Wudadao), while low shrubs dominate along roadsides, resulting in limited tree canopy coverage52. Collectively, BD, BH, SVF, and TH constitute fundamental 3D morphological elements that critically govern urban thermal radiation balance and ventilation efficiency, exerting dominant influence on spatial patterns of LST through their integrated effects on heat accumulation and dissipation processes.

Each grid served as a basic analysis unit, with the aforementioned indicators measured within each grid. These metrics form a comprehensive framework for evaluating the impact of urban morphology on LST. Table 2 provides detailed information on the selected indicators.

Multicollinearity

In this study, we used multiple linear regression to assess the impact of urban morphology on LST. With 24 independent variables included, multicollinearity could destabilize regression estimates, potentially compromising model accuracy and leading to misinterpretation55,56. To ensure stable and reliable model parameters, we performed a multicollinearity diagnostic using the VIF, which measures the strength of linear relationships between independent variables57. where Ri2 represents the coefficient of determination for the i explanatory variable in relation to all other independent variables.

VIF values allow us to assess correlations: a VIF of 1 indicates no correlation, 1 to 5 suggests mild correlation, and values above 5 indicate high correlation, necessitating the removal of highly correlated variables58.

We performed VIF diagnostics on data collected at six different time points across three grid scales: 70 m, 140 m, and 210 m. The analysis revealed varying degrees of multicollinearity. After filtering, 14 indicators were retained for the 70 m grid model, 11 for the 140 m model, and 16 for the 210 m model. This selection process minimized multicollinearity, ensuring that each model retained an optimal set of independent variables to enhance explanatory power.

Model selection

To further explore the relationship between LST and various influencing factors, we systematically evaluated the performance of multiple linear regression (MLR), random forest (RF), and XGBoost models (Table 3). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that compared to MLR and XGBoost, RF consistently exhibited significant advantages in capturing complex nonlinear relationships within urban thermal environment data. Across different grid scales (70 m, 140 m, 210 m), RF consistently achieved higher R2 values and lower RMSE values (e.g., at 210 m scale for the 06:12 time point: RF R2 =0.71/RMSE = 0.81 °C vs. XGBoost R2 =0.69/RMSE = 0.82 °C vs. MLR R2 =0.68/RMSE = 0.83 °C). Based on these validation results, this study ultimately selected RF as the core modeling approach. RF is a nonlinear machine learning method based on decision trees, which improves prediction accuracy and robustness by constructing multiple decision trees and aggregating their results. Among various machine learning algorithms, RF has demonstrated the highest accuracy and has been widely applied across different fields in recent years59. The “randomness” in RF refers to the selection of random subsets of features and samples for each decision tree, which reduces the risk of overfitting. The aggregated output of these trees produces the final prediction.

To maximize the accuracy of the model, we evaluated the performance of the RF model using repeated 10-fold cross-validation (CV) and compared the predicted values with observed values using Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). In each iteration of the repeated CV, the dataset was divided into 10 subsets, with 9 subsets used for training and 1 for testing. This process was repeated 10 times, and we repeated the data-splitting procedure three times to ensure result stability. RMSE measures the average difference between observed and predicted values, while R² reflects the goodness of fit, with lower RMSE and higher R² indicating better model accuracy.

Model interpretability methods

Partial Dependence Plots (PDP) demonstrate the marginal effects of features on the predictive outcomes of machine learning models, especially suitable for understanding the relationships between features. In regression models, PDP reveal the linear or nonlinear connections between the target variable and the features. Additionally, SHAP is a method for interpreting machine learning model predictions, making the results of complex models more transparent and understandable60,61. Results

Relative importance

As shown in Table 3, our model performed well across all grid scales. The average RMSEs for the three grid scales were 0.721 °C, 0.8701 °C, and 1.0924 °C, while the average R² values were 0.7021, 0.6513, and 0.5534, respectively. This indicates that the average error across all grid scales was below 1.10 °C, and more than 55% of the spatial variation in LST could be explained by the selected urban morphological features.

As shown in Fig. 4, our analysis across three grid scales (70 m, 140 m, and 210 m) identified six key factors influencing LST: NDVI, TH, BH, bare land coverage (PL_B), BD and the percentage of the largest impervious patch (LPI_I). NDVI consistently emerged as the most influential factor at all scales, underscoring its role in mitigating LST, particularly during summer. However, the importance of these factors varied at different times of the day. During transition periods (06:12 and 20:40), NDVI, BH, and TH had the most significant impact on LST. In contrast, during daytime (11:09 and 18:14), PL_B and TH gained prominence, especially in smaller areas where the shading effect of trees became more critical. At night (23:48), BH emerged as the dominant factor.

The relative importance of urban landscape indicators on diurnal and nocturnal LST across different grid scales.

At the 70 m grid scale, NDVI was most influential during transition periods, with BH and TH following closely. By midday, PL_B’s importance increased, while in the evening, TH became more crucial, and at night, BH dominated. At the 140 m grid scale, NDVI’s influence during transition periods was slightly lower than at the 70 m scale but remained dominant. During midday, NDVI’s influence decreased, while percentage of LPI_I and BD gained prominence. In the evening, NDVI’s importance lessened but still remained a key factor. Meanwhile, the influence of TH diminished, and BH became more impactful, suggesting a more dispersed role of urban morphology in regulating LST during this time.

At the 210 m grid scale, NDVI’s importance during transition periods showed a general downward trend. By midday, the significance of the impervious surface patch density (PD_I) and PL_B increased. In the evening, NDVI, BH, and TH showed a decline in importance but remained key factors.

The variation in factor importance across grid scales is due to differences in urban morphology. At smaller scales, NDVI, BH, and TH exert a stronger influence on LST, while at larger scales, LPI_I and BD are more significant. At night, the thermal capacity of buildings becomes especially critical, particularly during late-night hours.

In finer scales (e.g., 70 m), Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Building Height (BH), and Tree Height (TH) exert stronger influences on Land Surface Temperature (LST). This occurs primarily because smaller scales enable more direct characterization of, Evaporative cooling effects from vegetation coverage (NDVI), Canopy shading effects by trees (TH), and Direct radiative and aerodynamic impacts of buildings (BH) on land-atmosphere exchange processes (e.g., longwave radiation trapping and wind field alterations)64,65. The spatial configurations of these factors within micro-scale units dominate the distribution patterns of LST at this resolution.

Marginal effects

Based on the above analysis of relative importance, we identified the factors with the greatest impact on LST: NDVI, TH, BH, PL_B, PD_I, BD and the LPI_I.

Figure 5 shows that NDVI consistently exhibited a negative correlation with LST across all grid scales, indicating that increased vegetation coverage led to lower surface temperatures. The majority of the changes occurred within the NDVI range of 0.08 to 0.25. A particularly notable time point is 11:09 at the 70 m grid scale, where LST sharply rises to a peak of 36.69 °C as the NDVI value increases, then drops abruptly to a low of 35.12 °C before stabilizing. This indicates that, at midday, vegetation can lower LST by about 1.57 °C through evapotranspiration. NDVI had its strongest cooling effect at 06:12, reducing LST by 2.53 °C at the 70 m grid scale.

Partial dependence plots of LST on the top three important variables at different grid scales.

The relationship between BH and LST was nonlinear across all grid scales. For most time points, LST increased as BH ranged between 0 and 25 m, indicating a positive correlation—greater BH are associated with higher surface temperatures. Beyond approximately 25 m, LST began to stabilize. A notable exception was at 18:14 on the 140 m grid scale, where LST peaked at 36.39 °C at a BH of around 10 m, then decreased by about 0.61 °C before leveling off.

The influence of TH was observed within a range of 0 to 3 m, where LST showed a gradual upward trend. At the 70 m grid scale, NDVI exhibited its greatest effect at 06:12, reducing LST by about 2.53 °C, while TH had its minimal effect at 02:54, lowering LST by only 0.11 °C. At the 140 m grid scale, NDVI showed its maximum effect at 23:48, reducing LST by about 1.91 °C, while BH had its minimal effect at the same time, reducing LST by about 0.15 °C. On the 210 m grid scale, NDVI had the largest impact on LST at 23:48, with a maximum effect of 1.75 °C.

Interactive effects

We used SHAP value analysis to quantify the impact of urban morphology on diurnal and nocturnal LST, providing insights into the relative importance and variations of each feature across different grid scales. Moreover, this method enabled us to analyze the interactive relationships between urban morphology and LST, specifically the interaction effects of feature pairs (combinations of two variables) on the outcome. For instance, it quantified how the interaction between NDVI and BH influenced LST for each sample.

As shown in Figs. 6 and 7, the SHAP interaction values for the 2D/3D variables impacting LST were depicted, with the effects of individual features displayed along the diagonal and interaction effects between feature pairs represented off the diagonal. The results revealed strong interactions between feature pairs at the 70 m grid scale. At 11:09 and 18:14, strong interaction effects were observed between PD_I-BH and BH-NDVI, while BD-BH and LPI_C-NDVI interacted strongly at night. During transition periods, TH-BH and NDVI-BH were prominent.

At the 140 m grid scale, strong interaction effects were observed between feature pairs during the day (11:09 and 18:14), including BD-BH, LPI_I-BD, and NDVI-LPI_I. At night (02:54 and 23:48), BD-TH and TH-LPI_I exhibited strong interactions, while during the transition times (06:12 and 20:40), BD-TH and TH-NDVI were the most significant interacting pairs.

At the 210 m grid scale, daytime interactions (11:09 and 18:14) were notable for feature pairs such as PD_I-BD and NDVI-BH. At night (02:54 and 23:48), BH-NDVI showed strong interaction effects, and during transition times (06:12 and 20:40), BH-TH and TH-NDVI were the most prominent interacting pairs.

Through bee swarm plot analyses of SHAP values across different grid scales, we concluded that during daytime hours, the interaction between BD and BH was particularly significant. At night, however, BD and NDVI had the greatest influence on LST due to their effects on heat retention and dissipation.

Discussion

Impact of urban morphology on LST in Tianjin

We employed a comprehensive approach using model training, variable importance analysis, PDP and SHAP value analysis to investigate the relationships between 2D/3D urban morphological metrics and diurnal/nocturnal LST across three grid scales. The results indicate that larger grid scales generally yield higher prediction accuracy, whereas smaller grids are more susceptible to stochastic variations within local microenvironments. This increased sensitivity elevates the complexity of model fitting and may compromise its stability, aligning with findings on scale effects in other urban thermal environment studies66,67.

Our analysis confirmed that NDVI, BH and TH were the top three factors affecting LST. NDVI played a crucial role in reducing LST, particularly during midday when solar radiation is strongest, and in the early morning and late evening as heat radiation decreases1. This observation aligns with the established consensus that vegetation significantly mitigates daytime LST through enhanced heat dissipation capacity, particularly via transportational cooling mechanisms68,69. Additionally, the strong daytime transpiration of vegetation68,70,can effectively reduce LST.

However, divergent perspectives persist regarding vegetation’s influence on nocturnal LST. While some studies contend that attenuated nocturnal transpiration limits its cooling efficacy69. our results reveal a persistently significant negative correlation between NDVI and LST across both diurnal and nocturnal periods (i.e., increased vegetation coverage corresponds to LST reduction). This association peaked during transitional hours (06:12 local time) and exhibited scale-dependent attenuation with increasing grid sizes. These findings substantiate the paradigm that vegetation sustains nocturnal temperature modulation through lagged effects of evapotranspiration and soil thermal buffering – as evidenced by projects like Amsterdam’s urban canal vegetation system achieving 2–3 °C nighttime cooling71. Nevertheless, our data suggest that nocturnal cooling mechanisms may involve greater complexity than daytime processes and demonstrate pronounced scale sensitivity. as larger grids dilute localized cooling intensities of vegetation patches through spatial averaging67.

The relationship between buildings and LST is complex, with studies showing both positive and negative correlations between BH and LST17,72. Additionally, BD affects daytime LST by altering ventilation conditions and the surface energy balance. Buildings reduce LST by providing shade during the day but increase it at night by releasing stored heat17,73,74. Our study found that the impact of buildings on LST was relatively low during the day, but at night, BH showed a positive correlation with LST, this result strongly supports that with the disappearance of daytime shading, the massive sensible heat stored in high-rise building volumes is released at night, significantly raising local temperatures75,76.

Trees are universally regarded as effective nature-based cooling solutions68. Their cooling mechanisms involve transpiration releasing moisture to absorb latent heat77,78as well as canopy shade intercepting and scattering solar radiation79. Studies typically indicate trees’ superior cooling effectiveness compared to grassland vegetation77. However, this study observed a notable anomaly: trees at 0–3 m height (TH) exhibited a positive correlation with LST, most pronounced during sunset transition periods and consistently observed across scales. This contrasts with most studies emphasizing trees’ cooling effects. The key difference lies in vegetation height structure: taller community structures (e.g., mature trees) significantly reduce near-surface temperatures by enhancing canopy turbulent exchange, efficiently converting surface heat into latent rather than sensible heat80. Conversely, the short vegetation in this study (e.g., shrubs, young trees) experienced rapidly diminishing shading effects during sunset transitions, resulting in accelerated heat release from underlying and surrounding impervious surfaces; simultaneously, their dense canopies impeded near-ground airflow, suppressing heat dissipation and creating localized thermal retention zones.

The interactive effects of 2D/3D urban morphology on LST

After examining the interaction effects of 2D/3D urban morphology on LST using SHAP analysis (Sect. 3.3), we selected 18 feature pairs across three different grid scales based on the relative importance of single variables, the strength and trends of their interactions, and the feasibility of altering these urban morphological features through planning (as shown in Fig. 7). In the plots, the SHAP interaction values between two variables are shown on the left Y-axis, with the X-axis representing the value of the first variable and the right Y-axis representing the value of the second variable. Red indicates high values, and blue indicates low values. The discussion focuses on the interaction effects of three key feature pairs selected for each of the three grid scales.

At the 70 m grid scale, the interaction effect of 2D-3D urban morphology on LST is shown in Fig. 7(a3). At 18:14, the interaction between NDVI and TH has a cooling effect on LST when NDVI is greater than 1.2 and TH is less than 1.6. This phenomenon occurs because vegetation absorbs heat reflected from surrounding buildings through transpiration, and provides shade that covers impervious surfaces like roads and building facades, thus reducing temperatures81,82. Urban green spaces can also neutralize solar radiation83increasing the shading ratio to protect impervious surfaces from direct sunlight. This is consistent with findings from other studies84,85.

At the 140 m grid scale, the interaction effects of 3D-3D urban morphology on LST are shown in Fig. 7(b6). At 02:54, the interaction between TH and BH has a cooling effect on LST when TH is greater than 0.5 and BH is less than 7. In other words, shorter buildings combined with taller trees can lower nighttime LST. Contrary to our results, Han reported a positive correlation between taller buildings, trees, and nighttime LST30. Chang concluded that BH had no impact on nighttime LST, likely due to varying urban canyon characteristics (e.g., ventilation rates) or differences in research methodologies86.

At the 210 m grid scale, the interaction effects of 2D-3D urban morphology on LST are shown in Fig. 7(c1). At 06:12, the interaction between TH and BD has a cooling effect on LST when TH is between 1 and 4 and BD is between 35 and 45. This may be because taller trees inhibit the upward diffusion of heat from the ground, causing LST to rise. However, BD within this range can help reduce surface temperature by creating wind corridors through dense road networks84,87consistent with existing research.

In summary, the interaction between 2D/3D urban morphology and LST is significant, whether involving 2D-3D or 3D-3D combinations. This study offers new insights for developing heat adaptation strategies, emphasizing the importance of combining 2D and 3D data to achieve more effective cooling. This multidimensional approach not only enriches the theoretical foundation for heat adaptation strategies but also offers practical guidance for achieving harmony between the environment and society.

Limitations

This study conducted an in-depth analysis of the complex relationship between LST and urban 2D and 3D urban morphology, and evaluated the importance of these variables’ effects on LST. We focused particularly on the marginal effects of key urban morphology and their interactions.

Despite the achievements of this research, there are several limitations. First, data collection was restricted to the ECOSTRESS product, which makes it difficult to obtain LST data for different times of the same day. Consequently, we could only gather daytime and nighttime LST data from different dates between June and September. Future studies that collect data at multiple times on the same day will offer a more comprehensive understanding of diurnal LST variations.

Second, this study did not include data from other seasons, limiting our ability to assess the year-round impact of urban morphology. Additionally, we only selected a subset of 2D and 3D urban morphology, such as the absence of nighttime lighting data. Although we standardized the data through reclassification, higher-resolution multi-source data will be necessary to capture urban morphology more accurately.

Third, while we analyzed 18 urban morphologies, they do not fully represent all potential urban morphology that could influence LST. The influence of urban morphology varies across different geographic environments, and further experiments in more representative urban planning contexts are needed to enhance our understanding of the relationship between urban morphology and LST. With these improvements, our study will better inform the development of heat adaptation plans and promote regional sustainable development.

Conclusion

Using ECOSTRESS LST data, this study examined the impact of 2D/3D urban morphology on daytime and nighttime LST at grid scales. A RF regression model was used to explore the relative importance, marginal effects, and interaction effects of 2D/3D indicators. NDVI exhibited the most significant cooling effect across all grid scales. Additionally, this study recommends the 210 m grid as the optimal scale for urban thermal environment research in Tianjin. This resolution not only demonstrates the highest model accuracy (R² improvement of 3–5%) but also effectively integrates macro-scale urban morphological patterns, avoiding random interference from local thermal anomalies inherent in finer-scale grids (70 m).

During the day, NDVI and PL_B had the greatest impact on LST, whereas at night, BH and TH were the most influential factors. The relationship between key 2D/3D urban morphology and LST was nonlinear, with NDVI showing a consistent negative correlation, particularly within the range of 0.08 to 0.25. Regardless of whether the combinations involved 2D-2D, 2D-3D, or 3D-3D urban morphology, significant interactions with LST were observed. For example, the interaction between NDVI and TH had a cooling effect when NDVI was greater than 1.2 and TH was less than 1.6.

These findings reveal the critical role of 2D/3D urban morphology in regulating diurnal and nocturnal LST at the block scale and provide important insights for optimizing urban landscapes to improve thermal comfort at the neighborhood level.

This study, based on empirical analysis of a single city (Tianjin), revealed nonlinear regulatory mechanisms of 2D/3D urban morphology on diurnal and nocturnal land surface temperature (LST). However, potential influences from climatic backgrounds were not systematically investigated. As a coastal city characterized by a temperate semi-humid monsoon climate, Tianjin’s summer conditions of high temperature and humidity may amplify vegetation transpiration cooling effects, potentially resulting in distinct LST patterns compared to non-coastal cities36. Future research should therefore conduct comparable studies across diverse climate zones to better understand how climatic conditions modulate UHI effects. Methodologically, while VIF tests-controlled multicollinearity among factors, minor residual correlations may persist, potentially affecting result accuracy55,56. Future studies may require advanced analytical models and interpretation tools to further mitigate these limitations.
