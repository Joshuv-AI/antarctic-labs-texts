Genomic and genetic dissection underlying seedling drought resilience in oats
Authors: Shuhui Wang, Dongqing Liu, Yingying Li, Qinglin Sun, Xingyu Liu, Minghao Li, Wei Li, Ziyue Wang, Ju Zhang, Qiang He, Yu Wang, Qingbin Sun, Xiaoying Hu, Jia Liu, Yuanyuan Peng, Zhizhong Gong, Huilong Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drought threatens global crop yields, and common oat, a vital nutritional source for food and feed, is particularly constrained in the semi‑arid regions where it is widely cultivated. Here, we report two high-quality genome assemblies for drought-resilient (Borris37) and drought-sensitive (XymC06) oat accessions with distinct seedling survival rates and genome sizes of 10.92 Gb and 10.96 Gb, and construct comprehensive landscapes of insertion‑deletions (InDels) and structural variants (SVs). Integrating population-level genomic, transcriptomic and phenotypic (seedling survival rate), we demonstrate that InDels and SVs underpin divergent drought resilience and identify 52 candidate genes associated with drought resistance whose expression is significantly modulated by these variants. Borris37 accumulates 36 favorable alleles of these genes. An InDel in the AsNF-YB3 promoter enhances binding to AsARF1, upregulating AsNF‑YB3 under drought, and overexpression of AsNF‑YB3 reduces ROS accumulation. Our findings provide resources and targets for drought‑resistance breeding in oat, thereby supporting global food security.

Introduction

Drought is a major disaster for agriculture, accounting for over 65% of its losses, greater than floods, storms or volcanic activities1. Drought impairs plant physiology, biochemistry, and molecular regulation, which triggers the cessation of photosynthesis and metabolic dysfunction, ultimately reducing grain yield1–3. Common oat (Avena sativa L., 2n = 6x = 42, AACCDD) is among the world’s major cereals, ranking seventh in production (FAOSTAT, accessed May 2021) and serving as an essential source of energy and nutrition for both human and animal diets4,5. Oats, a grass species characterized by high water requirement and low water use efficiency6, are frequently cultivated in barren, arid, and cold regions7,8 and exhibit sensitivity to drought conditions. Therefore, the development of drought-resilient oat varieties and the genetic dissection of drought resistance are urgent priorities for safeguarding global food and forage security under climate change.

Genomic variations include single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), small insertions and deletions (InDels, 1–50 bp) and structural variations (SVs, ≥ 50 bp). SVs typically affect larger genomic regions than single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP)9–13 and are contributors to genetic diversity with effects on gene function and regulation14. Given that both InDels and SVs play a pivotal role in trait performance, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) relying solely on SNPs are inadequate to fully account for the genetic basis of drought-related traits15. Although previous studies have provided preliminary evidence of structural rearrangements underlying phenotypic divergence in cultivated oats16–21, the overall role of InDels and SVs in population divergence and phenotypic differences between drought-resistant and drought-sensitive oats remains largely uncharacterized. Besides, the substantial genome size (~11 Gb), highly repetitive sequences (~85%), and complex polyploid nature of oats22,23, pose significant challenges for InDels and SVs detection, genetic diversity assessment, evolutionary history reconstruction, and the identification of genomic regions and genes associated with agronomically important traits.

Recently, the high-quality genome assemblies of the oat hulled cultivar ‘Sang’22 and the oat hulless cultivars ‘Sanfensan’23, ‘Pinyan6’24 have been published, which greatly promote the genomic study of oats. The super-pangenome analysis revealed abundant specific genetic resources in oat varieties and characterized the landscape of SVs associated with abiotic stress20,21. Although recent pangenome publications include genome assemblies from arid regions such as Australia and Spain20, and landraces such as Sanfensan23 are known for their adaptation to dry environments, the genetic basis underlying differential drought resistance in oat remains largely unexplored. Obtaining precise genomic information from accessions with contrasting drought responses is therefore particularly urgent.

Here, we report two genome assemblies for drought‑resilient (Borris37) and drought‑sensitive (XymC06) oat accessions. Our analysis uncovers a large number of InDels and SVs with significant impacts on gene expression and drought resistance in oats. Integration of multi-omics data, including comparative genomics, transcriptome profiling, variations dataset and phenotype analysis, reveals that the elite drought-resistant accession Borris37 harbors favorable InDel or SV alleles at multiple loci, such as AsNF-YB3. Our findings advance the genomic understanding of oat drought resistance and provide valuable genomic resources for oat genetic research.

Results

Genome assemblies of two drought-resistant and drought-sensitive oats

Through screening for seedling survival rates among 211 oat accessions and for fresh weight reduction under 20% PEG6000‑simulated drought stress among 394 oat accessions, we identified Borris37, a hulled oat variety derived from a cross between Lyngby Heath Oats and Golden Rain and a sister variety to Borris Stand25, as a drought‑resistant variety, and XymC06, a naked oat landrace originating from Shanxi, China, as a drought‑sensitive landrace. Specifically, among the accessions obtained from the National Germplasm Bank, Borris37 exhibited stronger drought resistance than XymC06, as evidenced by higher seedling survival rates and a smaller reduction in fresh weight under 20% PEG6000‑simulated drought (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Data 1, 2), and a higher seedling survival rate compared to the dominant cultivar Baiyan No. 2, which is widely cultivated in China (Fig. 1a, b). In addition, under 20% PEG6000‑simulated drought stress, Borris37 exhibited increased root number, fresh weight of root, and primary root length, as well as a lower reduction in root length, compared to XymC06 (Supplementary Fig. 2a–f). Besides, significantly higher activities of peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT), as well as higher proline content, were detected in Borris37 under dehydration treatment compared with XymC06 and Baiyan No. 2 (Supplementary Fig. 2g–i). Besides, we compared the growth and development of Borris37 and XymC06 in fields within a rain-out shelter. Under normal conditions, Borris37 plants grew and developed normally in a manner similar to XymC06 (Fig. 1c). After drought stress, Borris37 exhibited higher relative plant height (drought/optimum %) and higher relative yield per plant (drought/optimum %) compared to XymC06. Additionally, under drought stress, Borris37 showed higher relative yield per plant than Baiyan No. 2 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3a–d). Although the above‑ground fresh weight and dry weight of Baiyan No. 2 were higher than those of Borris37 and XymC06 under normal conditions, no significant differences were observed among the three accessions under drought conditions, nor in their relative above‑ground fresh and dry weight (Supplementary Fig. 3e–h). Regarding below‑ground fresh weight and dry weight, Baiyan No. 2 was significantly higher than Borris37 and XymC06 under drought conditions. However, for relative below‑ground fresh weight and dry weight, there was no significant difference between Baiyan No. 2 and Borris37, and both were significantly higher than those of XymC06 (Supplementary Fig. 3i–l). Collectively, Borris37 showed advantages at the seedling stage and in yield stability following drought stress.

To investigate the genetic basis of drought resistance in oat, we generated high-quality genome assemblies for the drought-resistant accession Borris37 and the drought-sensitive accession XymC06. The assembled genomes are characterized by high continuity and completeness. BUSCO analysis indicated 99.6% recovery of core conserved genes in both assemblies (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). The long terminal repeat assembly index (LAI) was estimated at 16.66 for Borris37 and 17.22 for XymC06, respectively (Supplementary Table 1), further supporting the high quality of both genome assemblies. For Borris37 and XymC06, 99.80% and 99.67% of the sequences were anchored to chromosomes, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, the quality value (QV) scores for Borris37 and XymC06 were 63.45 and 59.80, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). Accordingly, two high-quality and complete genomes were obtained.

To further validate the accuracy of our genome assemblies, we aligned the Borris37 and XymC06 genomes to the OT3098 genome (https://wheat.pw.usda.gov/jb?data=/ggds/oat-ot3098v2-pepsico) (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). In the Borris37 genome, we identified two large inversion regions on chromosomes 7D (28,484,624 bp) and 7A (60,352,541 bp) (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). Similarly, in the XymC06 genome, two large inversion regions were observed on chromosomes 1 A (26,588,449 bp) and 7D (28,484,624 bp) (Supplementary Fig. 4g, h). Based on genome collinearity and Hi-C heatmaps, we further validated these large inversions (Supplemental Fig. 4i–l). These results confirm the integrity and high-quality of our assemblies, providing strong evidence for their reliability.

A total 128,299 and 129,010 protein-coding genes are identified in Borris37 and XymC06, respectively. The average gene length, coding sequence length, and number of exons per gene in Borris37 were 3794 bp, 1045 bp and 3.7, closely resembling those of XymC06 (Supplementary Table 2). The BUSCO evaluation of the annotated gene set showed that at least 99.3% of core eukaryotic genes were captured, confirming the completeness of gene annotation (Supplementary Table 1).

The landscape of variations between two oats (Borris37 vs. XymC06)

To identify the genetic elements contributing to the prominent drought resistance of Borris37, we conducted comprehensive comparative genomic analyses between Borris37 and the drought-sensitive accession XymC06. A total of 41.83 million single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), 3,908,885 Insertions/deletions (InDel, <50 bp) and 57,223 SVs (≥50 bp) were identified using Borris37 as the reference. The SVs comprised 51,180 PAVs, 412 inversions, 1749 translocations and 3882 duplications (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Tables 3, 4). These variations provide a potential genetic basis for the observed phenotypic divergence in drought resistance and reflect the extensive genetic diversity among oat varieties. Further analysis of the distribution characteristics of InDels and SVs, in the genome revealed that the number of InDels and SVs overlapping genic regions was similar between the A and D subgenomes, and significantly higher than that in the C subgenome (Fig. 2c). A proportion of 13.56% of these InDels and SVs were in the gene body and gene-regulatory regions (including the coding regions and flanking regions within ±3 kb, InDel- or SV-containing genes were referred to as InDel/SV-Genes) (Fig. 2c). We found 85.82% of InDels and SVs overlapped with transposable elements (TEs) (Fig. 2d). Gene functional analysis of InDel/SV-Genes revealed the significant enrichment in the pathways related to “signaling and cellular processes”, “cellular response to stress” and “cellular response to stimulus” terms (Fig. 2e), which are critically implicated for plant development and adaptation. Overall, our study provides the landscape of InDels and SVs between drought-resistant and drought-sensitive oat accessions, which has important implications for elucidating the regulatory roles of InDels and SVs on the variations of gene expression as well as revealing the genomic basis of phenotypic differences.

The impact of InDels and SVs on the regulation of gene expression under drought stress

To systematically evaluate the regulatory role of InDels and SVs between drought-resistant and drought-sensitive oat accessions in drought response, we generated transcriptome data from 118 RNA-seq samples (59 oat accessions) under normal and drought conditions (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Fig. 6a, Supplementary Data 4). The number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) varied significantly among accessions, ranging from 15,790 to 31,865 (Supplementary Fig. 6b, Supplementary Data 5), reflecting substantial transcriptomic diversity. To enhance the reliability of DEGs identification, DEGs consistently detected in at least 10 accessions were defined as “Reliable DEGs”, considering the stability and reproducibility of their expression differences between normal and drought conditions (Supplementary Fig. 6c). As the number of included accessions increased, the intersection of common DEGs gradually decreased. To investigate the impact of InDels and SVs on gene expression under drought stress, we obtained whole-genome resequencing data for 211 accessions26, including above 59 oat accessions with transcriptome data. InDels and SVs calling were conducted based on the genome, which integrated the landscape of InDels and SVs between drought-resistant (Borris37) and drought-sensitive oat (XymC06) accessions. We obtained 4.34 million InDels and SVs for these oat accessions, and a total of 83,066 genes harboring InDels or SVs were determined (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 6d, Supplementary Table 5). We found InDel/SV-Genes exhibited significantly higher expression levels compared to No-InDel/SV Genes under drought stress, suggesting a potential regulatory influence of InDels and SVs (Supplementary Fig. 6e). Our results are inconsistent with those previously reported pangenomes, such as by Zhang et al.21, which may be attributed to differences in the screening criteria for variations (≥1 bp in the present study vs. >50 bp in Zhang et al.’s research) and the experimental conditions for detecting gene expression levels (under drought stress in the present study compared with under normal growth conditions in Zhang et al.21). In addition, InDels and SVs located in coding regions exerted a stronger impact on gene expression than those located in introns or upstream/downstream regulatory regions (Supplementary Fig. 6f). Under drought stress, a high proportion of InDel/SV-Genes exhibited 2- to 4- fold expression changes compared to No-InDel/SV-Genes (Supplementary Fig. 6g). To elucidate whether InDels and SVs promote or suppress gene expression, we compared expression levels of each InDel/SV-Gene between the InDel/SV-presence and InDel/SV-absent groups in the 59 oat accessions. This result showed that a significantly greater number of InDel/SV-Genes (11,591 genes) were suppressed by InDel/SVs compared to those that were upregulated (7227 genes) (Fig. 3e), indicating a prevalent repressive influence of InDels and SVs on gene activity under drought stress.

To further identify drought resistance-related genes with expression affected by InDels and SVs, we combined three analytical strategies: (i) Genes harboring InDels or SVs within their upstream 2 kb region, (ii) Reliable DEGs, and (iii) statistical significance of InDels/SVs’ impact on gene expression levels identified via Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (P < 0.05) (InDels/SV-eGenes). A total of 7727 potential candidate genes were obtained (defined as “Gene Set1”) (Fig. 3f). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the genes in Gene Set1 were significantly enriched in pathways related to “response water stress”, “glutathione metabolism”, “protein kinase activity”, and “autophagy regulation” (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Data 6). Within this Gene Set1, we also identified many homologs of several known drought-related genes. AVESA.00030x.r1.5Dg005456 (designated AsNHX2) is a homolog of Arabidopsis NHX2 (AT3G05030), encoding the vacuole-localized NHX2 protein, which is highly expressed in guard cells, plays a critical role in vacuolar K+ accumulation and thereby participates in the regulation of stomatal function and transpiration27. RNA-seq analysis showed that AsNHX2 expression is induced under drought stress treatment, and a 12-bp InDel located 1122 bp upstream of its translation start site significantly influences its expression levels (Fig. 3g). Similarly, the gene AVESA.00030x.r1.7Ag001370 (AsDREB1C) is a homolog of Arabidopsis DREB1C (AT4G25470), encoding a dehydration-responsive element-binding protein 1C, which has been reported to function as a negative regulator in drought stress28. Transcriptome data showed that AsDREB1C is upregulated under drought stress. Furthermore, sequence analysis identified a 302-bp SV at 422 bp upstream of its translation start site, and this variation was also found to significantly affect gene expression (Fig. 3h). Collectively, these results highlight the crucial role of InDels and SVs in regulating gene expression under drought stress and propose a large number of candidate genes related to the drought stress response of oats.

The contribution of genetic variants to oat drought resistance

To elucidate the genetic basis of drought resistance in oat, we performed the SNP、InDel/SV-based genome-wide association study (GWAS) to pinpoint genetic loci responsible for variations in drought resistance, using SNPs, InDels and SVs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of at least 0.05. Significant association peaks were detected on four chromosomes using a mixed linear model (MLM) with kinship correction (InDel/SV-GWAS, P < 2.60 × 10−⁶) (Fig. 4a, b). To elucidate the genetic basis of the superior drought resistance in accession Borris37, we evaluated the survival rates associated with different genotypes of the identified candidate gene loci. We selected several genes previously reported to be associated with resistance. Accessions with Borris37 genotypes (SNPs and InDels/SVs) exhibited significantly higher drought survival rates than those with XymC06 alleles (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Data 8), indicating that Borris37 harbors more superior alleles and explaining its prominent drought performance. InDel/SV- and SNP-based GWAS consistently identified a significant peak on chromosome 1 A associated with drought survival rate. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis of the 1 Mb region flanking the lead SNP revealed a strong LD block, which contained 14 genes and also encompassed the top InDel (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Furthermore, significant genetic differentiation was observed for SNPs and InDels/SVs (P < 2.60 × 10−6) in this peak between accessions with divergent drought resilience. The HapB (Borris37) genotype was strongly associated with drought resistance (>50% survival rate) (Supplementary Fig. 7b-g), suggesting this may represent a major locus controlling drought resistance.

An InDel in the promoter of AsNF-YB3 enhances oat drought resistance by affecting the binding of AsARF1

To further investigate to the drought-responsive candidate genes influenced by InDels and SVs, we integrated the previously defined Gene Set1 and GWAS candidate genes. Finally, we obtained 52 candidate genes with InDel or SV significantly associated with drought resistance and gene expression under drought treatment (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Data 9). The superior alleles of 36 genes were contained in Borris37, which were defined as if the accession carrying the allele averagely had a significantly higher survival rate after drought than those harboring the counterpart allele (Supplementary Data 10). AVESA.00030x.r1.1 Ag005961 (AsNF-YB3) is homologous to NF-YB3 in Arabidopsis (AT4G14540), which encodes a putative nuclear factor-Y (NF-Y) transcription factor, and was identified in the significant peak on chromosome 1 A in GWAS. This region is located within the previously characterized 1A/1 C translocation interval, a structural variant that likely contributes to population structure, ecotypic differentiation, and local adaptation in oat20,32. The leading InDel (4-bp), located at position -657 bp upstream of the AsNF-YB3 promoter, encompasses an auxin response factor (ARF) binding site, and demonstrates a strong association with drought resistance (Fig. 5a). Based on the InDel, we categorized the oat accessions into two haplotype groups: Hap1 (without 4-bp) and Hap2 (with 4-bp). RT-qPCR assays indicated that the AsNF-YB3 exhibited a strong response to drought stress (Supplementary Fig. 8a). To further explore the role of AsNF-YB3 in drought resistance, we generated AsNF-YB3-overexpressing (OE) lines under the control of maize ubiquitin (Ubi) promoter (Fig. 5d). After drought treatment for 14 days, we observed that the AsNF-YB3-OE lines showed dramatically increased drought survival rate compared with the wild-type (WT) plants at seedling stage (Fig. 5e, f). Additionally, we employed the tobacco rattle virus-mediated virus-induced gene silencing (TRV-VIGS) system to generate plants with significantly reduced AsNF-YB3 expression (Supplementary Fig. 8b-d). Following drought stress, these knockdown plants exhibited substantially impaired drought survival rate compared to the wild-type (Supplementary Fig. 8e). The leaf-water-loss rate was consistently substantially slower in AsNF-YB3-OE plants relative to WT plants (Supplementary Fig. 9). In conclusion, our results demonstrate that AsNF-YB3 acts as a positive regulator of drought resistance in seedlings.

Phenotypic observation further confirmed that oat accessions lacking the 4-bp exhibited significantly lower drought resistance compared to those carrying the 4-bp, which contains an auxin response element (TGA motif, AACGAC), (Supplementary Fig. 10a). It has been reported that SlARF4 can bind to the promoter of SlMYB52 through a TGA motif33. To assess whether AsARF binds differentially to the two haplotype variants of the AsNF-YB3 promoter, we identified 67 putative AsARFs in oat genome (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Among them, drought-induced AsARFs were selected for yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assays (Supplementary Fig. 10c). Results showed that only one protein, AsARF1 (AVESA.00030x.r1.6Dg002537) specifically bound to the AsNF-YB3Hap2 (with 4-bp), but not to the AsNF-YB3Hap1 (without 4-bp) promoter (Fig. 5g). To further evaluate whether the 4-bp in the TGA motif influence its binding to AsARF1, we performed an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA). The recombinant glutathione S-transferase (GST)-AsARF1 protein generated an intensely shifted band when interacting with the AsNF-YB3Hap2 probe containing the TGA sequence, while its binding to the AsNF-YB3Hap1 probe was reduced (Supplementary Fig. 11a). In addition, to further investigate whether AsARF1 transcriptionally regulates AsNF-YB3, we performed a dual-luciferase reporter assay in tobacco epidermal cells. In this assay, AsARF1 was co-expressed with a LUC reporter driven by the AsNF-YB3Hap1 and AsNF-YB3Hap2 promoters, respectively. Under drought stress, the presence of AsARF1 resulted in a markedly higher LUC activity driven by the AsNF-YB3Hap2 promoter compared to the AsNF-YB3Hap1 promoter (Fig. 5h). We further investigated the regulation of AsNF-YB3 by AsARF1. As expected, AsNF-YB3 expression was greatly suppressed in plants with VIGS-mediated low expression of AsARF1 under dehydration but not under normal treatment (Supplementary Fig. 11b). In summary, these results indicate that AsARF1 directly and positively regulates the expression of AsNF‑YB3 through a 4‑bp motif in its promoter.

To further utilize the superior allele of AsNF-YB3, we analyzed the geographical distribution of its two haplotypes. The results showed that the AsNF-YB3Hap2 haplotype with high drought resistance is primarily distributed in Denmark, the United States, and Canada, while the AsNF-YB3Hap1 haplotype with low drought resistance is mainly found in major oat-producing regions of China, such as Inner Mongolia, Shaanxi, Shanxi, and Gansu, indicating that the AsNF-YB3Hap2 allele still holds significant potential for utilization in Chinese oat breeding programs (Fig. 5i, Supplementary Table 6). This distribution pattern likely reflects breeding history and ecological adaptation. Hulless oats, primarily grown in China34,35, are predominantly associated with AsNF-YB3Hap1, whereas hulled oats, widely used as high-quality forage globally34,35, mainly carry AsNF-YB3Hap2. The preferential cultivation of hulless oats in China probably explains the lower frequency of AsNF-YB3Hap2 in Chinese accessions, highlighting the role of artificial selection and ecological adaptation in shaping haplotype distribution. Additionally, we observed that AsARF1 is localized to the nucleus and its upregulation under drought stress was determined by RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c). Furthermore, we employed the TRV-VIGS system to generate plants with significantly reduced AsARF1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 11d, e). Under drought stress, these gene knockdown plants exhibited increased drought sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. 11f). These results indicate that the 4‑bp variation in the AsNF‑YB3Hap2 promoter enhances AsARF1 binding, thereby increasing AsNF‑YB3 expression and ultimately improving drought resistance in oat.

AsNF-YB3 regulates drought resistance by modulating ROS accumulation

NF-Y is a heterotrimeric transcription factor (TF) complex that is evolutionarily conserved in eukaryotes36,37. The NF-Y transcription factor is a complex composed of NF-YA, NF-YB, and NF-YC subunits, participating in the regulation of plant growth, development, and abiotic stress responses, including drought stress38–41. Extensive studies in mammals have established that a core heterodimer of NF-YB and NF-YC, formed via their histone fold domains, mediates non-specific DNA interactions, whereas NF-YA confers sequence-specific recognition by binding the CCAAT box42–44. We conducted RT-qPCR with collections of different tissues grown under normal condition and found that AsNF-YB3 preferentially expressed in the root, leaf, flag leaf, flower and seed tissues (Supplementary Fig. 12a). To understand the biological function of AsNF-YB3, subcellular localization analysis of the AsNF-YB3-GFP protein revealed that it colocalized with nuclear (Supplementary Fig. 12b). To further explore the biological functions of AsNF-YB3, we performed a yeast two-hybrid screening and identified AsNF-YC5 as an interacting protein. This interaction was further confirmed by Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays (Supplementary Fig. 13a, d). Additionally, we identified and cloned oat NF-YA members that are induced under drought stress according to transcriptome data. Among them, AsNF-YA1 was found to interact with AsNF-YB3 (Supplementary Fig. 13b, d). Furthermore, we also observed an interaction between AsNF-YC5 and AsNF-YA1 (Supplementary Fig. 13c, d). The yeast three-hybrid (Y3H) assay demonstrated that AsNF-YA1, AsNF-YB3 and AsNF-YC5 can assemble into a hetertrimeric complex (Supplementary Fig. 13e). The expression of AsNF-YC5 and AsNF-YA1 were also increased under drought (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b).

The function of AsNF-YA1 in drought response was confirmed by TRV-VIGS silencing of AsNF-YA1 in oat accession C999 (Supplementary Fig. 14c). Under normal irrigation, AsNF-YA1 mRNA levels were significantly reduced in V-AsNF-YA1 plants (Supplementary Fig. 14d), compared with the TRV1:TRV2. We observed that the V-AsNF-YA1 gene-down plants showed dramatically decreased drought resistance compared with the TRV1:TRV2 plants (Supplementary Fig. 14e), indicating AsNF-YA1 positively regulates the drought resistance of oats. These results demonstrate that AsNF-YB3 forms a heterotrimeric complex with AsNF-YC5 and AsNF-YA1. To explore the mechanism of AsNF-YB3-mediated drought resistance, we conducted RNA-seq analysis using WT and AsNF-YB3-OE plants under normal and dehydration treatment. A total of 2035 and 1701 AsNF-YB3-regulated genes were identified as DEGs (P < 0.05, |fold change| > 2) under normal and dehydration treatment, respectively (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 15a). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed their enrichment in “oxidoreductase activity” at both conditions (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 15b), suggesting a potential role for AsNF‑YB3 in maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis. To verify this notion, we measured the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) in leaves of 14‑day‑old WT and AsNF‑YB3‑OE seedlings under normal treatment and dehydration treatment. After dehydration treatment, both SOD and CAT activities were significantly higher in OE lines than in WT (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6c, d). Furthermore, we measured hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) accumulation in the third leaves of 14-day-old WT and AsNF‑YB3-OE seedlings after 4 h dehydration treatment. DAB (3,3’-diaminobenzidine) staining revealed lower H2O2 accumulation in AsNF‑YB3-OE plants compared to WT plants after dehydration treatment, indicating that AsNF‑YB3-OE plants were less injured by the overproduction of ROS (Fig. 6e). As AsNF‑YB3-OE plants exhibited enhanced drought resistance at the seedling stage, we further analysed the yield performance of these lines in the field (non-open-field conditions) in Baoding, China. When the plants reached the jointing stage, drought treatment was initiated. Under normal conditions, there were no significant differences in plant growth and development between WT and AsNF-YB3-OE plants (Fig. 6f, g, Supplementary Fig. 16). However, under drought conditions, AsNF-YB3-OE exhibited higher plant height, more effective tillers, and more grain yield per plant compared with the WT (Fig. 6f-h, Supplementary Fig. 16). Discussion

Drought stress is a primary constraint on global oat production, threatening both food and feed security. Unraveling the genetic architecture of drought resistance in this complex hexaploid crop requires high-quality genomic resources of germplasms and comprehensive approaches to decipher the functional role of various genetic variations. In this study, we generated two oat accessions with great divergence in drought resistance for de novo genome assemblies and annotations, providing foundational resources for oat genomics research. Borris37 is ecologically adapted to dry and infertile sandy soils and was traditionally planted in dry environments, which lays a solid foundation for its strong drought resistance. In contrast, XymC06 is a local naked oat landrace originating from Shanxi Province, which was mainly selected for superior grain quality under favorable growth conditions. We found that Borris37 maintains a significantly higher yield stability under drought than XymC06. Collectively, long-term divergent selection has formed completely different drought adaptation strategies with Borris37 adopts a stable yield-maintaining strategy under water scarcity, while XymC06 is more vulnerable to severe yield reduction. Moreover, Borris37 shows strong drought resistance at both seedling and reproductive stages, indicating an intrinsic, developmentally stable mechanism. Borris37 exhibits a significantly higher survival rate and yield stability under drought stress compared to the major cultivar Baiyan NO.2 (Fig. 1), highlighting its potential as a valuable parent for developing drought-resistant varieties through hybridization breeding.

Genomic approaches have been widely applied to dissect the molecular basis of abiotic stress responses in plants45,46. The genomes of a salt-tolerant variety and a salt-sensitive variety of Sesbania were assembled. Using comparative genomics approaches, the impact of genomic variations on sequence divergence and population differentiation in Sesbania was investigated. Furthermore, natural variation in the anthocyanin synthase gene SbANS was identified as a key factor contributing to differences in salt tolerance among these varieties45. A high-quality genome assembly of the elite drought-resistant genotype CIMBL55 has also helped reveal key genetic determinants of drought tolerance, including favorable alleles in this genotype and structural variations in the ZmRtn16 gene that underlie differences in drought resistance among maize varieties46. Here, we integrated a comprehensive landscape of InDels and SVs with transcriptomic profiling and InDels/SV-GWAS; we demonstrated that InDels and SVs played the central role in divergent drought resistance in oat by affecting gene expression. Population-level genomic, transcriptomic, and phenotypic analyses reveal the central role of InDels and SVs in divergent drought resilience in oat and identify 52 drought resistance-associated candidate genes (Figs. 3, 4). Among these, we functionally validated the pivotal role of AsNF-YB3 (Fig. 5). Our work provides genomic insights into drought resistance and characterizes a feasible path for genetic improvement. The 36 candidate genes and their elite alleles represent a valuable resource for molecular breeding, and the strategic combination of these favorable alleles through marker-assisted selection holds great promise for developing oat varieties with superior and durable drought resistance.

This study identifies a drought resistance QTL within the well-characterized chromosome 1A translocation segment originating from 1C. While this genomic region has been previously associated with winter types19. Our finding suggests a broader adaptive role, revealing either pleiotropic effects or close genetic linkage with drought stress. This expands the functional significance of this conserved structural variant, suggesting it may act as a multi-stress adaptive hub. Our result aligns with and extends broader genomic studies which indicate that major chromosomal rearrangements in oat, such as this translocation, are key drivers of local adaptation19,20. Our haplotype-based mapping now defines specific targets within it for drought resilience. By utilizing these markers, breeders can more precisely select for this adaptive haplotype block, potentially improving multiple stress tolerances. An InDel in the AsNF-YB3 promoter affect its binding affinity for the transcription factor AsARF1. Specifically, the AsNF-YB3Hap2 variant displays a higher binding affinity for AsARF1, which leads to enhanced expression level of AsNF-YB3 and, consequently, increased drought resistance, suggesting the AsNF-YB3Hap2 allele can be a valuable target for increasing drought resistance in oat germplasm improvement (Fig. 5). For example, replacing the drought-sensitive TaNAC071Del-693 allele with the TaNAC071In-693 allele in sensitive varieties significantly enhances TaNAC071-A expression and confers drought tolerance demonstrating that the introgression of elite alleles like TaNAC071In-693 represents a promising breeding strategy to develop stress-tolerant wheat varieties47–49. In addition, the AsNF-YB3Hap2 is predominantly distributed in Denmark, the United States, and Canada. Conversely, the AsNF-YB3Hap1 dominates major oat-producing regions of China, suggesting that its potential for improving drought resistance has not been fully exploited in current breeding programs. This highlights the untapped potential of this haplotype for enhancing drought resilience in Chinese oat germplasm, as targeted deployment through molecular marker-assisted breeding could significantly improve the adaptation of local cultivars to water-limited environments in major production areas.

Numerous studies have reported the involvement of the NF-Y family in the drought stress response. In soybean, GmNF-YC14 interacts with GmNF-YB2 and GmNF-YA16 to form a complete and active NF-Y transcription complex, which enhances stress resistance by regulating the ABA signaling pathway mediated by PYR/PYL receptors under drought stress50. Under water deficit conditions, the transcription factor PdFUS3 interacts with PdNF-YB21. This complex directly activates the promoter of PdNCED3, a key gene in abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis, leading to a significant accumulation of ABA in the roots51. In Arabidopsis, the homologous gene AsNF-YB3 enhances heat stress tolerance32, suggesting that their biological functions and target genes are not fully conserved between the two species. Although the functions of NF-Y transcription factors in drought stress responses have been reported extensively in plants, our work functionally validates the positive regulatory role of AsNF-YB3 in drought response in oats, identifies key variation in its promoter region and demonstrates that the 4-bp variation is significantly associated with differences in gene expression and drought-resistant phenotypes. Furthermore, we reveal significant differences in the frequency distribution of the AsNF-YB3Hap2 allele between global and Chinese oat accessions. This finding links molecular variation with breeding geographic patterns. Therefore, this study identifies a candidate gene for drought resistance in oats and provides specific allelic variations that can be utilized for marker-assisted selection, laying a theoretical and material foundation for the targeted breeding of drought-resistant oat varieties.

In summary, we generated high-quality genome assemblies for two oat accessions with divergent drought resistance and utilized them to decipher the molecular mechanisms underlying this key agronomic trait. These assemblies could be applied for comprehensive genomic analysis and gene functional studies, enhancing our understanding of drought resistance mechanisms and providing valuable genomic information for dissecting other pivotal traits in oat.

Methods

Plant materials and sequencing

The cultivated oat (Avena sativa L.) population used in this study was obtained from the National Crop Germplasm Bank of China. XymC06 denotes the accession “Xiaoyoumai” with our laboratory code DHLC000006.

The seedlings were grown in a growth chamber at 18 °C under a 14/10 h light to dark cycle. For PacBio HiFi sequencing, 4 (15–20 kb) SMRT Bell libraries were constructed and sequenced on a PacBio Sequel II platform. Effective circular consensus sequencing reads with high accuracy were obtained for subsequent analysis.

Hi-C library construction was performed as follows: (1) Crosslinking: Samples were fixed with 1% formaldehyde in MS buffer (10 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.0; 50 mM NaCl; 0.1 M sucrose) under vacuum for 30 min at room temperature, then quenched with 0.15 M glycine in MC buffer for 5 min. (2) Nuclei isolation: Fixed tissue was frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground, resuspended in nucleus isolation buffer, and filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer. (3) Digestion: Crosslinked DNA was digested with HindIII restriction enzyme. (4) End repair and biotin labeling: Sticky ends were repaired and biotin‑labeled. (5) Ligation: T4 DNA ligase (NEB) was used to ligate interacting fragments at 16 °C for 4–6 h. (6) Reverse crosslinking and DNA purification: Proteinase K was added and incubated overnight at 65 °C to reverse crosslinks, followed by DNA purification. Unligated ends were removed. (7) Fragmentation and biotin pull‑down: Purified DNA was fragmented to 300‑500 bp, end‑repaired, and biotin‑labeled fragments were captured using Dynabeads® M‑280 Streptavidin (Life Technologies). (8) Sequencing: The final library was sequenced on a DNBSEQ‑T7 platform in PE150 mode. Hi-C libraries and sequencing were conducted by a commercial company (Jizhi Gene Technology).

Drought survival tests for oat seedlings in the lab

As for the drought survival rate tests under lab conditions, different genotype plants were planted side by side in a cultivation box (25 × 17 × 10 cm, length × width × depth) that was filled with 0.5 kg of enriched soil (vermiculite and matrix soil with 1:1). A drought treatment was applied to plants at the two-leaf seedling stage by withholding water. Approximately 15 days of water withholding was applied to the plants for the comparison between the Baiyan NO.2 vs. Borris37 (Baiyan NO.2 vs. XymC06) plants and 12 days for comparison of the XymC06 vs. Borris37 plants. Water was resumed to allow plants to recover when the difference in leaf wilt became most obvious. The number of surviving plants was recorded around 3-5 days later. Plants that exhibited tender green and fully expanded leaves were classified as survivors.

Evaluation of drought resistance under non-open-field conditions

As we did not apply for a permit for transgenic field planting, reproductive stage phenotypic evaluations were performed in rain-out shelters, which are fully enclosed and isolated from the outside environment. The AsNF-YB3-OE lines and their wild-type were planted in a rain-out shelter (non-open-field conditions) in Baoding, China. Irrigation was withheld for 25 days during the jointing stage. In this period of time, the temperature was kept at around 15 °C under a 14 h light / 10 h dark cycle and the soil water potential gradually dropped to about 20–25%. The phenotypes of plant height, effective tillering number, and grain yield per plant were collected.

The same procedures were applied to accessions XymC06 and Borris37, with the temperature maintained at around 20 °C under a 14 h light/10 h dark cycle, while the soil water potential gradually dropped to approximately 10–15%. Given the limitations of yield per plant measurements, it should be noted that these data are restricted exclusively to the specific drought stress treatments and growth stages examined in this study.

Statistical analysis of fresh weight reduction in oat seedlings

Seeds were surface-sterilized in 75% ethanol for 5 min, 5% sodium hypochlorite for 15 min, and rinsed five times with sterile distilled water. The sterilized seeds were germinated under an 14 h/10 h light/dark cycle for 3 days, after which the seedlings were transplanted into 1/2 Hoagland nutrient solution and grown for 10 days. Subsequently, the seedlings were subjected to 20% PEG6000 treatment for 12 days. Fresh weight was measured before and after the 20% PEG6000 treatment. The reduction in fresh weight was calculated as the ratio of the fresh weight loss to the fresh weight of untreated plants. A higher ratio indicates a greater reduction in fresh weight under 20% PEG6000 treatment. A total of 394 oat accessions were evaluated, with three biological replicates, each comprising three seedlings.

For Borris37, XymC06, and Baiyan No. 2, above-ground and below-ground fresh and dry biomass were measured at the early heading stage (sown in the soil for about 72 days). Fresh weights of shoots and roots (soil‑washed) were recorded. Samples were then oven‑dried at 80 °C for three days to obtain dry matter weights. The relative values for yield per plant and fresh/dry weight were calculated as the percentage of the drought measurement relative to the normal measurement.

Genome assembly and quality assessment

Fresh leaves were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and high-quality DNA was isolated using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. The PacBio HiFi was de novo assembled using Hifiasm (v0.19.9)52. The Hi-C data were then processed and aligned to the raw contigs using Juicer (v1.5)53. These contigs were subsequently clustered, reordered, and oriented with 3D-DNA (V180114)54. Manual inspection and adjustment were performed in Juicebox (v1.11.08), focusing primarily on refining chromosome segment boundaries and correcting assembly errors. Following manual curation, the final genome assembly consisted of 21 chromosomes and unanchored sequences. The completeness of the genome assembly was assessed using BUSCO (v5.3.2)55. LTR_retriever (v2.6)56 software was used to assess the assembly completeness of intergenic regions. Meanwhile, the accuracy of the assembly was evaluated via Illumina and HiFi reads with the merqury (v1.3)57 software.

Genome annotation

Protein-coding genes were predicted through an integrated approach that combined ab initio, homology-based, and RNA-seq-guided methods. RNA-seq reads were aligned and assembled using HISAT2 (v2.2.1)58 and StringTie (v2.2.1)59. De novo transcriptome assembly was performed with Trinity (v2.13)60 under default parameters. The resulting transcripts were further refined iteratively using PASA (v2.1.0)61. Putative protein-coding regions within transcripts were identified using TransDecoder (v3.0.0) (http://transdecoder.github.io). For ab initio prediction, Augustus (v3.4.0) and GeneMark-ES (v1.0)62 were employed. Protein homology evidence was incorporated through Exonerate (v2.2.0)63. Finally, a consensus gene set was integrated using EvidenceModeler (v1.1.1)64.

Variants identification

We aligned the XymC06 genome to the Borris37 reference genome using MUMmer (v4.0)65 for whole-genome comparison. We detected InDels, PAVs, translocations, inversions and duplications between the Borris37 and XymC06 genomes with the SYRI (v1.6.3)66 software. To validate the quality of the detected SVs, a total of 4 randomly selected SVs were assessed for accuracy using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (v2.14.1) and Hi-C heatmaps, and an additional 21 SVs were verified by PCR amplification.

The SNP genotype data were obtained from Li et al.26. To genotype the InDels and SVs across 211 accessions, the cleaned reads were aligned separately to the Borris37 and XymC06 reference genomes using BWA-MEM67, and only the best alignments were retained. The SAMtools (v.1.7)68 was used to convert SAM files to BAM files, and the BAM files were further sorted using Sambamba (v0.8.2)69. For each accession and each candidate InDel and SV, reads spanning the breakpoint region of the variant in both the Borris37 and XymC06 genomes were examined. At each breakpoint, a minimum of three split reads were required to support the presence of the InDels or SVs, and only InDels or SVs located within genes and up/downstream 3 kb regions (length ≥ 1 bp) were retained. In total, 529,545 InDels and SVs were used as targets for genotyping, of which 393,199 (386,395 InDels and 6804 SVs) were successfully genotyped with high confidence.

TEs identification

First, RepeatModeler (v1.0.11)70 was used for the initial identification of repetitive sequence families. Subsequently, the predictions from LTR_FINDER (v1.05)71 LTRharvest (v1.5.11)72 and LTR_retriever (v2.6.0)56 were integrated to construct a comprehensive de novo repeat library. Following this, RepeatMasker (v4.1.1)73 was employed to systematically annotate and mask repetitive elements across the entire genome. Finally, TEsorter (v1.4.6)74 was utilized to classify all the annotated transposable elements.

GO and KEGG analysis

In Borris37, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) terms for genes were directly retrieved from their corresponding InterPro entries75. Enrichment analyses for GO and KEGG were performed using TBtools76, and terms with a corrected P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

InDel/SV-eGenes identification

“InDel/SV-Genes” refers to genes harboring either an InDel or a structural variant (SV) within the gene body or its flanking 3 kb upstream/downstream regions77. “InDel/SV-Genes” was specifically defined as a gene whose expression level under drought stress was significantly influenced by the presence of the InDel or SV (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.05). The influence of InDel and SV on gene expression was divided into two types: promoting and suppressing. The genotype with the InDels/SVs were designated as “present”, while the genotype identical to the reference genome was designated as “absent”. We selected InDel/SV-Genes that had different InDel/SV genotypes in at least three accessions for gene expression analysis. A InDel/SV was defined as “promotion” if the average FPKM of genotypes where the InDel/SV was present was higher than that of genotypes where it was absent. If the average FPKM was lower, the InDel/SV was defined as “suppression”.

A. sativa transformation

Oat transformation was performed with modifications to the published protocol78. In brief, seeds were surface sterilized with 75% ethanol for five minutes, followed by a 15-minute treatment in a 5% sodium hypochlorite solution, and then rinsed five times with distilled water. Mature embryos from healthy oat cultivar Bayou No.18 were excised and cultured on L3 medium (4.6 g/L L3 base salts with vitamins, 30 g/L maltose, 2 mg/L 2,4-D, 1 mg/L Dicamba, 4 g/L phytagel). After one month, the callus was used for Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 harboring the recombinant plasmid (pUBI: AsNF-YB3-GFP) was collected, resuspended in WLS solution (4.6 g/L L3 base salts with vitamins, glucose 10 g/L, 0.5 g/L 2-Morpholinoethanesulphonic acid, 150 μM acetosyringone), and adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0. Selected calli were soaked in Agrobacterium suspension for 30 min. Following 5 days co-cultivation (4.6 g/L L3 base salts with vitamins, 10 g/L glucose, 0.5 g/L 2-Morpholinoethanesulphonic acid, 1.25 mg/L CuSO4.5H2O, 150 uM acetosyringone, 0.85 mg/L AgNO3, 4 g/L phytagel), infected callus were transferred to selection medium plates (4.6 g/L L3 base salts with vitamins, 30 g/L maltose, 2 mg/L 2,4-D, 1 m/L Dicamba, 4 g/L phytagel, 0.1 g/L casein hydrolysate, 0.5 g/L glutamine, 0.75 g/L MgCl2ˑ6H2O, 1.95 g/L Mes, 100 mg/L ascorbic acid, 5 mg/L basta, 0.85 mg/L AgNO3, 200 mg/L timentin) for screening. After 3–4 weeks, surviving calli were transferred to regeneration medium (4.6 g/L L3 base salts with vitamins, 15 g/L sucrose, 0.5 g/L 2-Morpholinoethanesulphonic acid, 200 mg/L IBA, 200 mg/L timentin, 1 mg/L Dicamba) until root development was confirmed. Regenerated plants were then acclimatized in soil, followed by genotyping and transgene expression analysis. All the primers used in this research and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 11. All newly generated materials are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Genome-wide association analysis

For GWAS, the seedling SR tests were conducted on an association-mapping panel consisting of 211 Avena sativa L. accessions26 in Baoding, China. All accessions were randomly planted in a cultivation pool (9 × 1.5 m) filled with a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of vermiculite and matrix, with an initial water content of approximately 80%. The cultivation pool is placed under a rain shelter to avoid any impact from natural rainfall. Drought treatment was initiated at the two-leaf stage (approximately 14 days after planting). The soil was maintained under severe drought stress, with water content kept at 20–25%. When this condition corresponded to severe wilting in all seedlings (approximately 12 days after treatment initiation), watering of the entire pool was resumed simultaneously to allow the surviving plants to recover. Three days after rehydration, the survival rate of each genotype was recorded. For each accession, a total of 45 plants were cultivated. GWAS was performed using the average survival rate at the seedling stage. Given the limitations of controlled‑environment survival assays, it should be noted that these data are restricted exclusively to the specific drought stress treatments and growth stages examined in this study.

The 46,211,070 SNPs, 386,395 InDels and 6804 SVs (missing rate <0.1 and minor allele frequency [MAF] > 0.05) of 211 oat accessions were used in the study. Based on these SNPs, InDels, and SVs, we performed principal component analysis using PLINK79 and kinship analysis using GEMMA80. The GWAS was conducted using the mixed linear model (MLM-GEMMA), and a threshold cutoff was determined by the average value of 1/effective number of InDels and SVs for each of the A, C, and D subgenomes.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

EMSA was performed following a published protocol81. Specifically, the recombinant GST-AsARF1 was incubated with biotin-labeled probes, competition probes, or mutant probes, using a LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Thermo Fisher, Cat. #89880), at 25 °C for 20 min. The binding reactions were terminated by adding 5x native sample loading buffer, and the samples were separated on a native-PAGE gel. The binding signal was detected using a Nucleic Acid Detection Module kit (Thermo Fisher). The DNA sequences of the probes are listed in Supplementary materials. All the primers used in this research and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Phylogenetic tree construction

The full-length protein sequences of ARFs in oats and their homologs in Arabidopsis thaliana were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm in MEGA7 software. A neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was then constructed based on the alignment, employing the Poisson correction method and pairwise deletion of gaps. The reliability of the tree was evaluated through bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates.

Subcellular localization

To investigate the subcellular localization of AsNF-YB3 and AsARF1, the coding sequence of AsNF-YB3 and AsARF1 were fused with GFP to generate pUBI: AsNF-YB3 and pUBI: AsARF1. The vector was transformed into Agrobacterium strain GV3101 carrying P19. After infection, the plants were grown at 25 °C under 16 h light/8 h dark cycle in a greenhouse. Tobacco leaf epidermal cells were used to observe GFP fluorescence with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm (Carl Zeiss, LSM900) after Agrobacterium transfection 48 h. All the primers used in this research and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Transcriptome sequence and quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

For RNA-seq assay, plants were grown in a growth chamber maintained at 18 °C, 50% relative humidity, under a 14/10 h light/dark cycle. At the two-leaf stage (14-day-old seedlings), the second leaf was excised and subjected to bench dehydration. Samples were collected for RNA-seq when the leaves had lost approximately 30% of their fresh weight, with each sample comprising at least three individual plants.

Total RNA was extracted from the tissue samples using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA library was constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep kit for Illumina (Vazyme, NR604-01). RNA sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq sequencing platform (Illumina) (Annoroad Gene Technology, Beijing, China). Raw RNA-seq reads were quality-trimmed using Fastp (v0.23.4)82, and the trimmed paired-end reads were aligned to the Borris37 reference genome with HISAT2 (v2.2.1)59. Further, the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) of genes using StringTie (v2.2.1)59 to represent gene expression levels.

Differential expression gene (DEGs) analysis was performed independently within each accession to identify gene expression changes induced by drought stress. To reduce background noise and enhance the statistical power of subsequent analyses, gene expression was filtered. Genes with FPKM values below 0.5 under both normal and drought conditions were classified as “low-expression” and were excluded from the DEGs analysis for the accession. Genes with |log₂ (FPKM_drought / FPKM_normal)| ≥ 1 were considered DEGs.

For RT-qPCR analysis, total RNAs were extracted from 14-day-old seedlings grown in soil with TRIzol (Invitrogen), and cDNA was synthesized from the total RNA using a Prime ScriptTM RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Japan) as described in the supplier’s instructions. RT-qPCR analyses were performed with qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme) (Takara) on a Lc96 Real-Time PCR Detection (Roche) System. AsACTIN1 was used as the internal control. All the primers used in this research and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Yeast-one-hybrid assay

For the Y1H assay, the 100-bp sequence containing a 4-bp InDel on the AsNF-YB3Hap2 promoter was cloned into the pAbAi vector, then transformed into the yeast strain Y1H Gold. Yeast libraries were screened according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The empty AD plasmid was used as a negative control, and the yeast solution was serially diluted and spotted on SD-Ura-Leu plates with or without 200 ng/mL AbA. All the primers used in this research and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Yeast-three-hybrid assay

The encoding sequences of AsNF-YB3 and AsNF-YC5 were cloned into the pBridge vector, and AsNF-YA1 was cloned into the pGADT7 vector, then transformed into the yeast strain Y2H Gold (WEIDI). The transfected yeast cells were then plated on culture plates with the SD-Leu-Trp and SD-Leu-Trp-His-Ade-Met medium at 30 °C for 3 d. All the primers used in this research and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Leaf water loss measurement

For the measurement of leaf water loss, for each sample, the second leaves were collected separately from wild-type plants and AsNF-YB3-OE at the three-leaf stage grown under normal conditions. All the samples were placed on a clean bench at room temperature. The fresh weights of the samples were measured at the indicated time points. The statistical data were obtained from three biological replicates.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay (BiFC assay)

For BiFC assay, the constructs AsNF-YA1-nYFP & AsNF-YC5-cYFP, AsYB3-nYFP & AsYA1-cYFP and AsNF-YB3-nYFP & AsNF-YC5-cYFP were co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves for 48 h. GFP fluorescence was visualized by confocal microscopy (Carl Zeiss, LSM900) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm. All the primers used in this research and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay (Co-IP)

To verify the interaction between AsNF-YC5-GFP & AsNF-YA1-Myc, AsNF-YB3-GFP & AsNF-YA1-Myc and AsNF-YB3-GFP & AsNF-YC5-Myc were co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. After 48 h transformation, total proteins were extracted using lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-Hcl (pH 7.5), 150 mM Nacl, 1% NP-40, 1x protease inhibitor cocktail [Roche, cat. 4693116001], 10 mM dithiothreitol). Then centrifuge the tube at 12,000 g for 10 min, the supernatant was total protein. Next, 1 mL of total protein extract was incubated with anti-GFP-Magnetic beads (MBL, cat. #D153-11) at 4 °C for 2 h. The beads were washed 3-5 times with GFP lysis buffer. Proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE and analyzed via immunoblotting with anti-GFP (Abclone, cat. #AE012; dilution 1:5000), anti-Myc (Sigma, cat. #M4439; dilution 1:5000). All the primers used in this research and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 10.

Transcriptional activity assay

The ORF of AsARF1 was cloned into 35S-GFP to generate the effector plasmid. A 100-bp promoter fragment of AsNF-YB3 was inserted into pNC-Green-LUC to construct the reporter plasmid. Tobacco leaves were transformed using the Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. After 48 h of incubation at 25 °C, leaf tissues were collected for analysis with firefly luciferase (LUC) and Renilla luciferase (REN) using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) with an Luminometer System (Promega). All the primers used in this research and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Virus-induced gene silencing mediated by pTRV2

For VIGS (virus-induced gene silencing), a unique 200-bp fragment from the cDNA of AsNF-YB3 was amplified and cloned into the pTRV2 vector by NC clone. pTRV1 and pTRV2 were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens (strain GV3101) and subsequently used for the infection of Avena sativa L. seedlings83. All the primers used in this research and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Endogenous substance measurement

The second leaves were collected from oat plants at the three-leaf stage grown under normal conditions, as well as from leaves subjected to bench-drying until 30% loss of fresh weight (approximately 0.1 g per sample). All collected leaf samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into a fine powder. Each treatment group included at least four biological replicates. Subsequently, the proline (Pro) content and the activities of reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging enzymes, including catalase (CAT)、peroxidase (POD) and Superoxide dismutase (SOD) were measured. These endogenous compounds were quantified by spectrophotometry using corresponding assay kits (Cat# BC0290, BC0200, BC0090 and BC5165; Solarbio Technology, Beijing, China), respectively.

Detection of ROS levels in leaves

The third leaves of 14-day-old seedlings grown under WW conditions, or detached and dehydrated to 30% water loss on a clean bench (WS), were collected for ROS detection. H₂O₂ was visualized by DAB staining. For DAB staining, the leaves were incubated in a 1 mg/mL DAB solution for 14 h, then immersed in a decolorization solution (ethanol to acetic acid to glycerol = 1:1:1) and boiled for 15 min, followed by photographing.
