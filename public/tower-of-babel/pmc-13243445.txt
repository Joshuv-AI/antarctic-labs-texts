Sympatho-vagal balance and intrinsic sinoatrial node activity in larval zebrafish quantified by heart rate variability
Authors: Layan Hamed, Nir Almog, Inbar Brosh, Yael Yaniv, Limor Freifeld
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe larval zebrafish is a powerful in vivo model for studying cardiac disease. Alterations in sympatho-vagal balance and intrinsic sinoatrial node activity, key contributors to cardiac dysfunction, can be assessed using heart rate variability (HRV) indices. However, a validated method for HRV quantification in larval zebrafish has not been established. Here, we address this unmet need by (a) validating that beat intervals (BI) can be accurately extracted from fluorescence-based heart imaging, (b) adapting HRV indices from humans to larval zebrafish at 6 days post-fertilization (dpf), and (c) quantifying HRV indices in response to altered or absent sympatho-vagal balance. We show that: (a) BI and HRV extracted from fluorescence imaging and electrophysiological measurements are comparable; (b) The majority of the power spectral density (PSD) of BI sequences in larval zebrafish, which quantify the power of a signal that is distributed across different frequencies, resides within the high-frequency range (0.458–1.564 Hz); (c) Sympathetic activity is higher than vagal activity and predominantly modulates the PSD of BI sequences in high and very low frequency bands (0.0033–0.079 Hz), whereas vagal activity mainly affects the low frequency range (0.079–0.458 Hz); and (d) Intrinsic pacemaker mechanisms contribute to the low- and high-frequency regimes of the PSD of BI sequences. Thus, even at the larval stage a synergy between sympatho-vagal and intrinsic sinoatrial node activity exists, with dominant sympathetic activity. Moreover, our methodology for BI measurement and drug treatment in this model could serve as a basis for high-throughput drug screening for cardiac arrhythmias.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47536-0.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Development model, Frequency, Heart rhythmSubject terms: Cardiology, Physiology

Introduction

The heart pumps blood to the body at a rate ranging from 60 to 180 beats per minute, depending on the body’s needs. Changes in average heart rate are closely associated with changes in rhythm; a decrease in average heart rate is typically accompanied by an increase in heart rhythm variability indices, and vice versa1. Interestingly, the same primary mechanisms that regulate the average heart rate, the autonomic nervous system and intrinsic sinoatrial node mechanisms, also govern heart rhythm2. Because specific heart rate variability (HRV) indices reflect both autonomic activity and intrinsic sinoatrial node function, they offer a promising approach for assessing the dynamic interaction between sympathetic and vagal activity and intrinsic pacemaker mechanisms3,4.

The larval zebrafish model has proven to be a valuable tool for studying cardiac development and disease, offering unique advantages for in vivo heart research5–7. One significant advantage of the zebrafish model is its optical transparency during the embryonic and larval stages, which allows for non-invasive, in vivo monitoring of cardiac function. The similarities between the zebrafish and the human cardiac conduction systems support using zebrafish as a model for studying cardiac electrophysiology. In addition, zebrafish are amenable to gene-editing, enabling the generation of several orthologous mutant models for studying genetic variations associated with cardiac conditions. Further advantages of this model include spawning hundreds of eggs weekly, low breeding costs, and relatively fast drug absorption from the surrounding medium, such that drugs can be applied at high-throughput and non-invasively. Thus, larval zebrafish constitute an efficient model for high-throughput drug-screening. Six days post-fertilization (6 dpf) represents the earliest developmental stage at which zebrafish larvae exhibit a structurally mature and fully functional two-chambered heart8 and an established sinoatrial-to-ventricular conduction pathway with a defined atrioventricular delay9. At 6 dfp, the preferred time point for our experiments, the sympathetic10 and vagal11 systems are already functional. Using HRV, their roles in regulating cardiac dynamics can be explored. While HRV indices in time, frequency and in nonlinear domains in humans12 and their adaptation to mammals13 are well established, the correct method to calculate HRV indices in time and frequency regimes in larval zebrafish still needs to be defined.

Here, we report on the relative contributions of sympatho-vagal balance and intrinsic sinoatrial node activity in 6 dpf larval zebrafish by analyzing cardiac dynamics. Specifically, we (a) Established a method for measuring beat intervals (BI) with accuracy comparable to electrophysiological recordings; (b) Formulated HRV indices tailored to the physiology of larval zebrafish (c) Provided evidence for the relative role in the spectrum of sympatho-vagal balance in regulating heart dynamics whilst exploring the contribution of intrinsic sinoatrial node activity to cardiac dynamics.

Adaptation of HRV indices for larval zebrafish

Our goal was to characterize HRV in larval zebrafish and compare these indices with those previously measured in humans. However, HRV indices often require adaptation when applied to different species, as heart rate dynamics can vary significantly between humans and animal models13. Table S1 outlines which HRV indices required adaptation to heart dynamics of 6 dpf zebrafish larvae rather than humans. Table S3 describes the updated HRV calculations. To define the frequency bands, we employed the method described in14, using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) trained on data from 35 6 dpf zebrafish larvae. Based on the GMM fitting, the very-low frequency (VLF) band was defined as 0.0033–0.079 Hz, the low frequency (LF) band as 0.079–0.458 Hz, and the high frequency (HF) band as 0.458–1.564 Hz. Figure 3 shows the fitted GMM components overlaid on a histogram of the prominent peak locations in the power spectral density (PSD) of the NN sequence. In addition, the pNNxx index was updated (see Methods section). In human, pNNxx is calculated for the percentage of NN intervals that differed by more than 50 ms. On average, in larval zebrafish the average beat interval was 300 ms (N = 35; compared to 800 ms in humans). Scaling accordingly, pNNxx is calculated for the percent of NN intervals that differ by more than 18 ms.

The effect of sympathetic activity on cardiac rate and HRV

It was shown that sympathetic and parasympathetic activities affect HRV. We wanted to test the individual effect of each system on HRV indices and specifically on the PSD in each frequency regime. To test the effect of sympathetic activity on cardiac rate and rhythm, we used drugs with opposite effects on β-adrenergic receptor (β-AR) activity. In particular, isoproterenol (ISO, a non-specific β-agonist) and propranolol (PRO, a β-blocker). Table S5 shows that 10 µM ISO or PRO resulted in significant changes in heart rate and rhythm. Figure 5 shows that increased β-AR activity elevates heart rate and reduces variability, as quantified by the standard error of the mean NN interval (SEM), percentage of short segments (PSS) and the standard deviation perpendicular to the line of identity in the Poincaré plot (SD1). Additionally, the normalized PSD in the LF band decreases. Only at 100 µM, ISO reduced the PSD in the normalized VLF band. For additional HRV indices, see Table S5. Fig. S3 shows similar trends with changes in heart rate and variability in response to 100 µM ISO and Fig. S4 shows a bland response to 1 µM ISO. In contrast, Fig. 5 shows that decreased β-AR activity has a higher impact than increased β-AR activity, reduces heart rate and increases variability, as quantified by SEM, PSS, SD1 and NN interval standard deviation along the line-of-identity in the Poincare plot (SD2). Additionally, the normalized PSD in the VLF and LF bands increases, while the PSD in the HF band decreases. In parallel, the entropy at high time scales from 1 to 5 and from 9 to 15 also increases (Table S5), consistent with the relative increase in VLF and LF PSD. For additional HRV indices, see Table S5. Fig. S4 shows similar trends with changes in heart rate and variability in response to 100 µM PRO and Fig. S7 shows a bland response to 1 µM PRO.

HRV indices under the control of intrinsic mechanisms

To isolate the intrinsic mechanisms controlling the heart rate from autonomic influences, we pharmacologically blocked sympathetic activity using 100 µM PRO and parasympathetic activity using 100 µM ATR, as previously reported in other species15. Table S9 shows that 100 µM PRO and ATR together resulted in significant changes in heart rate and rhythm. Figure 7 shows that dual blockade of both pathways reduces heart rate and increases the β-slope. Discussion

Larval zebrafish have the potential to serve as a model for understanding the mechanisms that control heart rate and rhythm, as well as for drug screening. However, several essential steps must be ensured before this model can be reliably used: (1) BI must be shown to precisely match electrophysiological measurements of BIs; (2) HRV indices must be adapted from human physiology to larval zebrafish; and (3) the relative contribution of each regulatory system to specific HRV indices must be defined. Here, we demonstrate how each of these gaps has been addressed.

Validating that BI can be accurately extracted from fluorescence-based heart imaging

Schwerte et al.11 also used a non-invasive imaging method to assess HRV indices in zebrafish embryos; however, we were the first to quantify them against a ground truth. We demonstrated that there were no significant differences in cardiac interval distributions, differences between consecutive cardiac intervals, or HRV indices when derived from either extracellular recordings or optical imaging. Two out of twelve larvae showed differences in their cardiac interval distributions according to the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, but not when comparing the beat-to-beat differences in cardiac intervals obtained from the two modalities. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test is highly sensitive and can be prone to false positives in the presence of noise, which may explain the observed discrepancies. While BI extraction from optical imaging has been shown previously11,16–19, our method enables BI detection across multiple larvae simultaneously. Moreover, the similarity in HRV indices obtained from both measurement methods we applied, optical and electrophysiological, supports the validity of beat-to-beat BI extraction using optical imaging. The ability to non-invasively extract BI and HRV from larval zebrafish, capturing multiple larvae simultaneously to increase throughput, further strengthens the utility of this model.

Adaptation of HRV indices for larval zebrafish

A large group of HRV indices, particularly those in the frequency domain, must be adjusted to account for differences in average heart rate in different animal models13. Schwerte et al.11 estimated only the overall spectral bandwidth without defining specific HRV sub-bands or frequency cut-offs. In this study, we established the frequency cutoffs in larval zebrafish. We found that the frequency cutoffs in larval zebrafish differ from those in humans: the VLF and LF bands are approximately twice as wide, while the relative width of the HF band remains unchanged. This disproportionate increase in frequency band widths may reflect distinct underlying mechanisms influencing each frequency band. Most of the PSD in larval zebrafish is concentrated in the HF band (Fig. 4), which is consistent with the higher sample entropy values observed in the short range compared to the long range. This pattern resembles that seen in both anesthetized adult humans and awake dogs13. Other studies have reported HRV in larval fish. Schwerte et al. demonstrated a broader frequency range with increasing dpf after day 511. The peak of the PSD was in the HF range, consistent with our findings. Additional studies confirmed the presence of HRV in larval fish, but did not quantify specific HRV indices20,21.

The effect of the autonomic nervous system on heart rate and rhythm

Changes in sympathetic activity affect both heart rate and rhythm. The observation that a reduction in sympathetic activity has a greater impact than an equivalent increase suggests that, under basal conditions, sympathetic activity dominates over parasympathetic activity. This conclusion is further supported by the prolongation of the BI observed following dual autonomic nervous system blockade. Consistent with findings at the single-cell level22, in isolated tissue23, and in the whole heart24, a decrease in sympathetic activity has a greater effect on increasing variability than an increase in sympathetic activity has on reducing it. Our results are consistent with other results documented in zebrafish at 5 and 7 dpf25.

PSD analysis shows that increases and decreases in sympathetic activity result in relatively elevated power within the VLF band. In the case of increased sympathetic activity, this is driven by a relative reduction in PSD within the LF band. Thus, sympathetic tone influences both VLF and HF bands. Conversely, in response to decreased sympathetic activity, the relative reduction in HF power leads to the observed increase in relative PSD in the VLF band. This suggests that as sympathetic influence wanes, sinoatrial mechanisms, which primarily contribute to VLF components, become more dominant in regulating HRV. In contrast, changes in parasympathetic activity have a smaller effect than changes in sympathetic activity. As shown by Schwerte et al.11, both ISO and atropine increased the average heart rate at 6 dpf. In our study, atropine also led to an increase in the relative PSD in the HF range, likely due to a shift in sympatho-vagal balance. Thus, these findings further support the conclusion that sympathetic activity contributes to the HF band.

The effect of the intrinsic mechanisms on heart rate and rhythm

Prolongation of the BI in response to double blockade has been previously demonstrated in mice, in rabbits15 and even in snakes26,27. Similar to other species, double blockade reduces the relative PSD in the VLF band and increases it in the HF band. These findings suggest a dominant role for intrinsic mechanisms over sympatho-vagal modulation. Although in adult humans, double blockade leads to a shortening of the BI and abolishes most of the relative PSD in the HF range28, this was not the case here. In contrast to adult humans, our results indicate not only a dominant role of intrinsic mechanisms in heart rhythm regulation but also a clear contribution of these mechanisms to the PSD in the HF range.

Limitation

We measured the BI and calculated HRV. However, HRV is known to vary as a function of circadian rhythms29. Longer BI recordings will be necessary to determine whether such circadian patterns exist in larval zebrafish. Moreover, HRV is known to be sex-dependent30. However, the sex of larval zebrafish cannot be determined at this developmental stage.

Autonomic balance in zebrafish may continue to mature fish beyond 6 dpf. The sympathetic contribution to HF power observed here could reflect developmental state rather than a fixed species distinction. Future studies examining juvenile and adult stages will be necessary to resolve this question.

Solution temperature may affect HRV indices (Fig. S9). To minimize this effect, control and post-drug treated larval zebrafish were measured in the same dish.

Note that 6 dpf larvae represent an early developmental stage prior to sexual maturation. Therefore, sex-specific differences in autonomic regulation and cardiac physiology cannot be assessed at this stage. Similarly, modeling age-related cardiac dysfunction is not feasible in larval animals and would require adult zebrafish models.

Although propranolol is commonly used to assess sympathetic blockade, it is important to note that it can also directly inhibit cardiac ion channels, including Na⁺ and K⁺ currents31–33. These off-target effects may influence cardiac excitability and pacemaker activity independently of β-adrenergic modulation. Therefore, some of the observed HRV alterations may reflect intrinsic electrophysiological effects rather than solely autonomic suppression. Future studies employing selective modulation of sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways could complement pharmacological approaches via other means, such as optogenetic manipulations of the autonomic nervous system34, and thus further refine the validation of the HRV framework.

Methods

Zebrafish husbandry and larval development

All experiments here described were conducted on Tg(actb2:EMTB-3xEGFP) (EMTB-3GFP) larval zebrafish35, where microtubules throughout the body and thus also the heart are labeled by the green fluorescent protein (GFP), obtained from the Elkouby lab (Hebrew University of Medicine, Israel). Experiment protocols and procedures were approved by the Technion- Israel Institute of Technology animal care and use committee (IACUC) (protocol number IL0300224). Moreover, all methods are reported and described in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Adult fish were maintained under standard husbandry conditions at 28 °C, with a pH of 7, and with a 14-hour light/10-hour dark cycle (14 L:10D), in reverse osmosis water, supplemented with 60 µg/mL Instant Ocean Sea salts.

Fertilized embryos were transferred to Petri dishes containing E3 medium (5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl 2, 0.33 mM MgSO4, 0.00001% Methylene Blue) and screened for GFP expression in the myocardium (see Fig. 1A)36. The embryos were then incubated at a temperature of 28.5 °C and a 14 L:10 D cycle, until they reached 6 dpf, the developmental stage targeted for this study.

Experimental set-up

We immobilized awake larval zebrafish during experiments by embedding them in 1.5% ultra-low melting point agarose gel (gelling temperature 32 °C; A2576-5G. Sigma-Aldrich) to achieve a gentle and controlled restraint. Larvae were carefully immersed in the liquid agarose and then pipetted into a 1.6 mm-wide capillary tube, ensuring their heads entered last for proper orientation. Once in place, the capillary tube was rapidly cooled with an ice pack to solidify the agarose. Finally, the agarose-embedded larvae were partially extruded from the glass tube into a temperature-controlled E3 bath chamber maintained at 27 °C ± 0.5 °C. This custom-designed, 3D-printed chamber was specifically engineered to facilitate imaging of multiple larvae in parallel (see Fig. S2).

The bath chamber was positioned on the stage of an Olympus MVX10 wide-field macro-zoom microscope, equipped with a high-speed, high-sensitivity Prime BSI Express sCMOS camera (Photometrics) for image acquisition. Recordings were captured at 40 Hz over a 5-minute period with a 2.5x magnification air objective. To analyze these recordings, we employed our custom software ((Fig. 1B), see details in the Supplement), which extracted the time-varying average fluorescence intensity from a defined region of interest (ROI) within the beating heart. As the cardiac muscle contracted and relaxed, the distribution of fluorescently labeled tissue within the ROI shifted, resulting in measurable fluctuations in fluorescence intensity. These fluctuations directly reflected cardiac contractions, producing an intensity waveform over time. By identifying the peaks in this waveform, we calculated the beat intervals, enabling precise quantification of cardiac rhythm dynamics from the recorded fluorescent signals (Fig. 1C). When drugs were used, an incubation period of at least 1 h was applied prior to imaging heart rate.

The experiments were performed in a static aqueous bath containing 50 mL of medium. The recording duration for each set of larvae was limited to 5 min, and the medium was replaced between recordings. Therefore, the setup was not actively aerated during the experiment. Oxygen was supplied through passive diffusion from the dissolved oxygen present in the fresh medium.

Establishing the correlation of optical imaging and electrical activity

To validate the accuracy of our imaging method for assessing heartbeat intervals, we performed simultaneous optical and electrophysiological recordings in zebrafish larvae (6–8 dpf). This dual-recording approach was designed to directly correlate fluorescence-based cardiac motion with the underlying electrophysiological activity. Cardiac contractions were visualized in real time using an Axio Observer.Z1 microscope equipped with an Axiocam 506 camera (Zeiss). Concurrently, extracellular electrical activity was recorded using a two-electrode setup connected to a data acquisition system (Axon Digidata 1550 A digitizer and MultiClamp 700B amplifier, Molecular Devices). Glass micropipette electrodes were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries (outer diameter: 1.5 mm; inner diameter: 0.86 mm) with a Flaming/Brown P-97 micropipette puller5,6. The electrodes, which had a resistance of 3–8 MΩ, were filled with E3 electrolyte solution using MicroFil (MF28G, World Precision Instruments). Electrical contact was established via a chloride-coated silver wire inserted into the pipette and connected to the acquisition system through a gold rod mounted on a micromanipulator (Scientifica Microstar).

For stabilization, larvae were immobilized in ultra-low melting point agarose and placed in 3-cm-diameter Petri dishes containing 2 mL of E3 medium. The recording electrode was positioned near the ventral side of the heart using a micromanipulator (see Fig. S1A), while the reference electrode was placed approximately 1 cm away in the surrounding solution. Electrical signals were recorded for 5 min per larva at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. Simultaneously, video footage was captured at 33.3–60 Hz (depending on the region of interest) with a 12.25-25 ms exposure time and subsequently resampled to 1000 Hz. The raw ECG data were digitized and processed in MATLAB, including post-acquisition signal conditioning with a 20 Hz low-pass filter and a 50 Hz notch filter to reduce electrical noise and interference.

Drugs

Isoproterenol (202188, Santa Cruz-Biotechnology), Propranolol (3580, Santa-Cruz Biotechnology), and Carbachol (PHR1511, Sigma-Aldrich) were each prepared as 1 mM stock solutions in E3 medium. Atropine (252392, Santa-Cruz Biotechnology) was prepared as a 200 µM stock solution in E3. All stock solutions were diluted in E3 to final concentrations of 100, 10 and 1 µM prior to administration to the larvae.

Adaptation of HRV indices for zebrafish larvae

HRV indices often require adaptation when applied to different species, as heart rate dynamics can vary significantly between humans and animal models13. With such adaptation, the information captured by the indices is preserved across species. Currently, there is no standardized HRV analysis protocol for zebrafish larvae, particularly at 6 dpf, the developmental stage examined in this study. This section outlines the modifications made to standard HRV indices based on methodologies previously developed in our lab14. Table S1 summarizes the HRV indices analyzed, provides their definitions, and highlights the default parameters that were adjusted to account for the specific heart rate characteristics of zebrafish larvae (Table S3).

BI intervals are defined as the time intervals between consecutive peaks (i.e., all detected heartbeats). Outliers exceeding three standard deviations from the mean were removed, yielding a refined dataset referred to as NN intervals, which was then used for HRV indices calculation. HRV analysis was performed on NN intervals collected from 35 larvae across at least two experimental days, each with a 5-minute recording duration.

Time domain HRV indices

Most time-domain HRV indices are independent of average HR, making them broadly applicable across species. However, an exception is pNNxx, which represents the percentage of NN intervals that differ by more than xx milliseconds (commonly xx = 50 ms in humans). To adapt this metric for non-human species, two main scaling approaches have been proposed: The first is respiratory rate–based scaling, which assumes that vagal modulation of HRV is linked to the respiration-to-heart-rate ratio. In this method, the xx threshold is adjusted by scaling the average respiratory cycle length of the species relative to that of humans. The second approach, which we adopted in this study, is AVNN-based scaling. Here, the xx threshold is determined by the ratio of the average NN interval (AVNN) in zebrafish larvae to the human AVNN. This method ensures that pNNxx reflects species-specific cardiac dynamics more accurately.

Frequency domain HRV indices

The power spectral density (PSD) of the HRV is usually divided into three frequency bands: VLF, LF, and HF. The cutoffs for these bands have been standardized in humans based on empirical research. Because the HR varies across species, the frequency cutoffs must be adjusted accordingly. We used a similar approach as Behar et al.14, who adapted frequency band definitions for various mammals and isolated mice sinoatrial tissue using a data-driven approach (see Fig. 3). Specifically, we tailored the frequency bands for larval zebrafish using a GMM with three Gaussian components fitted to the histogram of all detected peaks of the normalised PSD. The 10 most prominent peaks (with a minimum threshold of 0.05) in each of the bands were identified. The lower boundary of the VLF band was determined as one over the window length of the NN interval (300 s) because in a T-second window, the smallest resolvable non-zero frequency is 1/T. The VLF-LF and LF-HF boundaries were set at the intersection points of consecutive Gaussian distributions. Lastly, the upper boundary of the HF band was defined as two standard deviations above the mean of the Gaussian representing the HF region.

Non-linear domain HRV indices

Unlike other HRV indexes, non-linear HRV indices remain consistent across species and do not require adaptation13. However, in this domain, the β coefficient, which quantifies system complexity, was calculated as the slope of the linear interpolation of the power spectral density on a log–log scale, limited to frequencies below the upper bound of the adjusted VLF band.

Software development for automated cardiac motion analysis in zebrafish larvae

We developed an open-source program for simultaneously detecting multiple heart signals in vivo and in real-time from zebrafish larvae. See supplement for further details.

Statistics

Experimental results are presented as median and interquartile range: MED (Q1 – Q3). Each pairwise comparison of cardiac interval distributions obtained from optical imaging and electrical activity was performed using the two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which evaluates the null hypothesis that the two heart rate vectors are drawn from the same continuous distribution. In addition, a paired t-test was performed to assess the difference between the two cardiac interval vectors and HRV indices derived from both vectors. Significant differences of the drug compared to the corresponding control condition were determined using Welch’s t-test (unequal variance). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.001, ***p < 0.0001.
