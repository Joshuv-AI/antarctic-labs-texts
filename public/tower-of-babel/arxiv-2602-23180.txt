Hierarchy of bounds in free orthotropic material optimization: From convex relaxations to Hashin–Shtrikman via sequential global programming
Marek Tyburec, Michael Stingl, Shenyuan Ma

Abstract
We study free orthotropic material optimization for two-dimensional plane-stress compliance minimization with two well-ordered isotropic phases, motivated by the gap between tensors admissible in classical free-material optimization and tensors realizable by composites. To reduce this gap, we construct a hierarchy of realizability-aware admissible sets induced by zeroth-order, Voigt, and Hashin–Shtrikman (HS) energy bounds, moving from convex relaxations to a tighter nonconvex model. In the convex zeroth-order and Voigt settings, the Voigt set is strictly tighter for intermediate volume fractions and coincides with the zeroth-order set at pure-phase endpoints, and the Voigt model reduces to an isotropic variable-thickness-sheet formulation. In the single-loadcase continuum zeroth-order problem, at least one optimal solution can be chosen orthotropic. For HS constraints, we rewrite the bound as a Voigt term minus a nonnegative correction, clarifying strict tightening for interior volume fractions and local nonconvexity. We further prove that the convex hull of the HS feasible set equals the Voigt set and derive reduced formulations via active-constraint analysis and explicit elementwise volume characterization, including reductions specialized to orthotropic effective tensors. In the single-loadcase continuum setting, the HS relaxation is tight with the Allaire–Kohn relaxed problem, attained in the relaxation sense by sequential laminates, whereas in generic multi-loadcase settings it provides a lower bound on optimal compliance over general microstructures. The resulting nonconvex orthotropic HS problem is solved by sequential global programming, and numerical results confirm the predicted compliance hierarchy and show close agreement with finite-rank laminate references.



1
Introduction

Free material optimization (FMO) is a class of topology-optimization methods in which the elasticity tensor field is directly optimized. By treating the local constitutive response as the design variable, FMO offers significantly greater design freedom than density-based topology optimization with prescribed material interpolation laws
[
43
,
11
,
13
,
54
]
.

Typically, FMO formulations impose positive semidefiniteness of elasticity tensors and invariant-based material-budget constraints. These constraints control stiffness magnitude, but they do not directly characterize phase-wise realizability as effective properties of composites with prescribed constituent phases
[
52
,
50
]
. A complementary asymptotic result is due to
37
: in the extreme high-contrast limit, arbitrary positive-definite effective tensors can be realized by hierarchical constructions. However, this asymptotic realizability result does not by itself provide computable finite-contrast admissible sets for fixed constituent phases.

This paper addresses the fixed-phase inverse question relevant for material-constrained design: for given well-ordered isotropic phases, which effective tensors are admissible in the model? We pursue this question through computable admissible sets induced by energy bounds and organize them into a hierarchy: zeroth-order, Voigt, and Hashin–Shtrikman bounds. This hierarchy provides progressively tighter, realizability-informed relaxations, but it does not provide a complete characterization of realizability in general. We focus on orthotropy because, in the two-dimensional anisotropic setting, bound-attaining laminate constructions are orthotropic
[
6
,
5
]
, making orthotropy both physically motivated and analytically tractable.

1.1
Free material optimization

Early FMO formulations combined compliance minimization with invariant-based resource constraints on positive-semidefinite constitutive tensors, typically using trace- or Frobenius-type measures. This includes the constitutive-matrix model of
43
. Building on that setting,
11
derived analytical local optimal-material laws and a reduced single-loadcase formulation, while
13
extended the framework to multiple independent loadcases via weighted mean compliance. These formulations were later cast in mathematical-programming form for multiple loadcases and contact settings by
54
. This line culminated in semidefinite-programming formulations of worst-case compliance minimization under multiple loadcases and contact constraints, together with accompanying existence results
[
10
]
. Overview articles
[
29
,
30
]
document this shift from early constitutive formulations to linear and nonlinear semidefinite programming approaches.

The framework was subsequently extended in several practically relevant directions. Formulations with local stress constraints, together with tailored scalable numerical solution methods, were developed in
[
31
,
32
]
. Vibration requirements were addressed by imposing constraints on the fundamental eigenfrequency
[
48
]
. On the theoretical side,
26
established existence and convergence results for such multidisciplinary extensions using
H
-convergence. In parallel, stress-based trace-constrained free material formulations were developed for single and multiple loadcases
[
22
,
23
]
. These developments were further unified across anisotropic, isotropic, and fixed-Poisson-ratio settings
[
34
]
and generalized within admissible-cone frameworks with existence analysis
[
17
]
. A recent isotropic specialization reformulates the trace-constrained problem as second-order cone programming
[
53
]
.

Despite this breadth, most FMO formulations share a common structural feature: admissibility is primarily encoded through positive-semidefiniteness and trace- or Frobenius-norm-based material-resource constraints. These conditions control stiffness levels but do not directly characterize phase-wise realizability for fixed constituent phases.

1.2
Energy-based bounds

To address the realizability gap left by FMO formulations that control material usage through invariant-based measures, one can use admissible sets induced by energy bounds for two-phase composites. Classical interval estimates of Voigt and Reuss type were discussed for reinforced solids by
27
, and substantially tightened by the Hashin–Shtrikman variational framework
[
25
]
. For two isotropic phases with prescribed volume fractions,
9
established optimal bounds for imposed uniform strain or stress ensembles and proved attainability by finite-rank laminates, making the link between optimal bounds and realizable microgeometries explicit.

This framework was then sharpened in directions directly relevant here.
6
treated mixtures of two possibly anisotropic well-ordered phases and derived optimal upper and lower bounds for elastic and complementary energies (and related sums of energies), with parallel Hashin–Shtrikman and translation-method interpretations. In the two-dimensional isotropic two-phase case,
5
gave explicit optimal formulas and attainability results that also cover non-well-ordered regimes. In the orthotropic setting,
35
derived optimal correlated bounds for the six independent orthotropic effective moduli of two well-ordered isotropic phases and identified finite-rank orthotropic laminates that attain these bounds.

Later work focused on turning bound theory into optimization-ready ingredients.
12
analyzed interpolation schemes through the lens of energy bounds and realizability constraints, clarifying the modeling role of Voigt-type and Reuss-Voigt-type descriptions versus tighter Hashin–Shtrikman-consistent descriptions.
19
derived explicit lower Hashin–Shtrikman complementary-energy formulas in 2D well-ordered settings, and later provided explicit 3D Hashin–Shtrikman upper primal and lower complementary bounds together with saturating laminate constructions in
[
20
]
. At the opposite end,
36
revisited constituent-only zeroth-order bounds, provided lower-bound constructions, and derived explicit bounds for individual tensor components. A broad synthesis of variational bounds, translation methods, attainability, and
G
-closure viewpoints is provided by
38
.

1.3
Homogenization-based optimization

Building on these energy bounds and microstructures attaining them, the homogenization literature developed topology optimization formulations in which microstructured composites appear as relaxed design variables. The seminal contribution of
14
introduced a material-distribution approach on a fixed reference domain, where design variables parameterized effective anisotropic properties of periodic rectangular-hole cells, thereby avoiding repeated remeshing while permitting topology changes. In a related relaxed setting,
7
considered a plane-stress formulation with a weighted objective combining compliance and mass through extremal microstructures and used an alternating-minimization stress-based framework. In a complementary setting,
28
restricted the admissible composites to rank-2 laminates, analytically eliminated microstructural variables through a sequence of reduced optimization problems, and solved the resulting mixed finite-element formulation with coupled macroscopic-microscopic updates.

The same period also established inverse and multi-loadcase perspectives.
46
formulated inverse homogenization, namely the optimization of a periodic base cell to match prescribed effective constitutive properties.
2
extended homogenization-based optimization to multiple loadcases, emphasizing that explicit optimal composite parameters available in single-loadcase settings are not available in the multi-loadcase regime. This direction was strengthened in
[
3
]
, which proved a general relaxation result in two and three dimensions and introduced a penalized topology-optimization algorithm to recover classical phase-separated designs.
8
consolidated these developments into a unified framework linking nonexistence of classical minimizers, relaxation by homogenization, and practical numerical algorithms. In the same framework of alternating minimization and sequential laminates,
19
derived a 2D optimality-criteria method for compliance minimization using explicit lower Hashin–Shtrikman complementary-energy bounds for laminates made of two well-ordered isotropic phases, while
21
extended this strategy to 3D settings and combined it with penalization to recover classical designs.

Finally, dehomogenization and projection methods have addressed the reconstruction of manufacturable designs from relaxed optima.
41
proposed a post-treatment that projects not only density but also lamination information, producing single-scale geometries with controlled detail.
24
simplified and scaled this idea to high-resolution manufacturable lattices with performance close to homogenization-based predictions.
4
improved optimization-projection consistency for modulated periodic microstructures, including conformal orientation treatment through a harmonic angle field.

1.4
Aims and contributions

The aim of this paper is to develop a realizability-aware free material optimization framework for two well-ordered isotropic phases in plane stress, and to clarify what is gained when moving from zeroth-order constraints to Voigt and then to Hashin–Shtrikman (HS) admissible sets. We make this progression explicit through a pointwise inclusion hierarchy and use it as the organizing principle of both the analysis and the algorithms.

To achieve this aim, the main contributions of this work are:

1.

We formulate a realizability-aware free material optimization framework for two-dimensional plane stress, organized as a hierarchy of admissible sets induced by zeroth-order, Voigt, and HS energy bounds. We establish well-posedness by proving existence of minimizers for the zeroth-order and Voigt models and existence in the
H
-relaxed sense for the HS model.

2.

For the convex zeroth-order and Voigt models, we prove strict inclusion of admissible sets for intermediate volume fractions and coincidence at pure-phase endpoints. We also prove that the Voigt model reduces to an isotropic variable-thickness-sheet formulation, and that in the single-loadcase continuum zeroth-order setting an optimal solution can be chosen orthotropic.

3.

For HS constraints, we derive an explicit Voigt-minus-correction representation of the energy bound and prove that the convex hull of the HS feasible set equals the Voigt admissible set. We further derive reduced HS formulations via active-constraint analysis and explicit elementwise volume characterization, with dedicated reductions for orthotropic effective tensors.

4.

We prove that in the two-dimensional single-loadcase continuum setting with well-ordered isotropic phases, the HS relaxation is tight with respect to the Allaire–Kohn relaxed problem. For generic multi-loadcase configurations, we clarify that the model provides a lower bound on compliance minimization over general microstructures.

5.

We develop a sequential global programming method for the resulting nonconvex orthotropic HS problem and validate the hierarchy numerically across phase contrasts and mesh resolutions, confirming the predicted compliance ordering and close agreement with finite-rank laminate references.

Notation

We focus on the planar setting,
d=2
, and adopt the following conventions for the notation used throughout the paper. Vectors and matrices are written in boldface sans-serif font. Vectors are written in lowercase, e.g.,
\mathsf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and matrices in uppercase, e.g.,
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Tensors are written in boldface serif font. First-order tensors are written in lowercase italics, e.g.,
\bm{\mathit{x}}
. For second-order tensors we use lowercase upright, e.g.,
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, while fourth-order tensors are written in uppercase upright, e.g.,
\bm{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d\times d}
.

For vectors and tensors of compatible sizes,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the Frobenius inner product. We write
\lvert\cdot\rvert
for the associated norm and use
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{F}
when emphasizing the Frobenius norm. For
\bm{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
we write
\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{a}})
for its trace and
\operatorname{Det}(\bm{\mathbf{a}})
for its determinant. Moreover, for
\bm{\mathbf{a}},\bm{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
we denote by
\bm{\mathbf{a}}\otimes\bm{\mathbf{b}}
the tensor product defined by
(\bm{\mathbf{a}}\otimes\bm{\mathbf{b}})_{ijkl}:=a_{ij}b_{kl}
. For a fourth-order tensor
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
acting on a second-order tensor
\bm{\mathbf{a}}
, we write
\bm{\mathbf{E}}:\bm{\mathbf{a}}
, and also use the shorthand
\bm{\mathbf{E}}\bm{\mathbf{a}}
. In particular,
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle:=\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}:\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
.

By
\mathbb{S}^{n\times n}
we denote the space of real symmetric
n\times n
matrices and by
\mathbb{S}^{n\times n}_{\succeq 0}
the cone of symmetric positive semidefinite matrices. More generally, let
V
be a finite-dimensional real inner product space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. For self-adjoint linear maps
A,B:V\to V
we write
A\succeq 0
if
\langle Au,u\rangle\geq 0
for all
u\in V
and define the (Loewner) order
A\preceq B
by
B-A\succeq 0
(strict inequalities
\prec,\succ
analogously). This convention applies, in particular, to symmetric matrices (
V=\mathbb{R}^{n}
) and to fourth-order elasticity tensors acting as self-adjoint maps on
V=\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
. In this sense,
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d}_{\succ 0}
denotes the subset of
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d}
that is positive definite as a self-adjoint linear map on
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
.

Let
\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d}
denote the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Moreover, let
\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d\times d\times d}
denote the fourth-order identity on
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
, i.e.,
(\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d\times d\times d}:\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})=\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
for all
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
. For a fourth-order tensor
\bm{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d}
acting as a self-adjoint linear map on
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
, we define its trace by
\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}):=\bm{\mathbf{E}}:\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d\times d\times d}
. Equivalently,
\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}})
coincides with the trace of the Kelvin–Mandel matrix representation of
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
on
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
.

For an isotropic fourth-order tensor
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
with shear modulus
\mu
and bulk modulus
\kappa
, the constitutive law
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}=\bm{\mathbf{E}}:\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
takes the volumetric–deviatoric form

\forall\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d},\qquad\bm{\mathbf{E}}:\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}=\kappa\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d}+2\mu\left(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d}\right).

(1)

The inverse tensor is then

\forall\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d},\qquad{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}^{-1}:\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}=\frac{1}{\kappa d}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}})\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d}+\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}})\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d}\right).

(2)

Given two isotropic tensors
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-},\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
, we say they are well ordered if
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\prec\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
, equivalently
\mu^{-}<\mu^{+}
and
\kappa^{-}<\kappa^{+}
.

3
Methods

This section formulates the free material optimization problem and the corresponding energy bounds. We work in plane stress (
d=2
) with two isotropic constituent tensors
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-},\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d}_{\succ 0}
satisfying
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\prec\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
. For a local stiff-phase volume fraction
v^{+}\in[0,1]
, mixtures of these phases generate effective tensors, and our goal is to characterize as tightly as possible the admissible set of orthotropic effective tensors through energy bounds. The design variables are the effective tensor field
\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})
and the local volume fraction
v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}})
, coupled by pointwise bound constraints.

We start from a continuum multi-loadcase compliance-minimization problem and a unified strain-energy representation of admissibility of the energy bounds (Section
3.1
). We then derive discrete models with increasing tightness: zeroth-order bounds (Section
3.2
), Voigt upper bounds (Section
3.3
), and Hashin–Shtrikman-type upper bounds (Section
3.4
). Next, we specialize to orthotropic effective tensors (Section
3.5
) and solve the resulting nonconvex problems by sequential global programming (Section
3.6
). Section
3.7
recalls the alternating-minimization laminate method of
19
, used as a comparison reference.

3.1
Continuum formulation and solution existence

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Its boundary is decomposed as
\partial\Omega=\overline{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\cup\overline{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}
with
\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\cap\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}=\emptyset
and
\lvert\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\rvert>0
. We prescribe homogeneous Dirichlet conditions on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}
and set

\mathcal{U}_{0}:=\left\{\bm{\mathit{v}}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\;:\;\bm{\mathit{v}}=\bm{\mathit{0}}\ \text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\right\}.

(3)

For
\bm{\mathit{u}}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}
we set the linearized strain

\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}(\bm{\mathit{u}}):=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\bm{\mathit{u}}+\nabla\bm{\mathit{u}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}.

(4)

Let
n_{\mathrm{lc}}\in\mathbb{N}
denote the number of load cases.
For each
j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{lc}}
, we prescribe a traction
\bm{\mathit{f}}_{j}\in L^{2}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and define

\ell_{j}(\bm{\mathit{v}}):=\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}\langle\bm{\mathit{f}}_{j},\bm{\mathit{v}}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma,\qquad\forall\bm{\mathit{v}}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}.

(5)

Given a design
\bm{\mathbf{E}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d}_{\succ 0})
, we introduce the bilinear form

a_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}(\bm{\mathit{u}},\bm{\mathit{v}}):=\int_{\Omega}\left\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}(\bm{\mathit{u}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})),\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}(\bm{\mathit{v}}(\bm{\mathit{x}}))\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}},

(6)

where
\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})
has the usual minor and major symmetries and acts as a self-adjoint linear map on

\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
. For each loadcase
j
, we define
\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}=\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{E}})\in\mathcal{U}_{0}
as the solution of the weak equilibrium problem

a_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}(\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j},\bm{\mathit{v}})=\ell_{j}(\bm{\mathit{v}}),\qquad\forall\bm{\mathit{v}}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}.

(7)

The compliance associated with the
j

c_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}):=\ell_{j}(\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}))=a_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}(\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}),\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{E}})),

(8)

and the objective functional is the sum of the individual compliance terms

J(\bm{\mathbf{E}}):=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}c_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}).

(9)

To encode energy bounds on the effective tensors, we introduce a local volume fraction
v^{+}:\Omega\to[0,1]
of the stiffer isotropic phase
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
and restrict the effective elasticity tensors through pointwise strain-energy inequalities. Specifically, for a.e.
\bm{\mathit{x}}\in\Omega
, we require

\Phi_{\mathrm{lb}}\big(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\big)\leq\left\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle\leq\Phi_{\mathrm{ub}}\big(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\big),\qquad\forall\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}.

(10)

We assume the chosen energy bounds satisfy the sandwich condition

\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\;\leq\;\Phi_{\mathrm{lb}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v)\;\leq\;\Phi_{\mathrm{ub}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v)\;\leq\;\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle,\qquad\forall\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d},\ \forall\,v\in[0,1],

(11)

with fixed
0\prec\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\prec\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}

0<\alpha\leq\beta<\infty
such that every admissible design satisfies

\alpha\,\lvert\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rvert^{2}\leq\left\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle\leq\beta\,\lvert\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rvert^{2},\qquad\forall\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d},\ \text{for a.e. }\bm{\mathit{x}}\in\Omega.

(12)

For instance, one may take
\alpha:=\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})
and
\beta:=\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})

with respect to the Frobenius inner product on
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}

Different choices of

\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(v^{+})\supseteq\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(v^{+})\supseteq\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(v^{+}),\qquad\text{for all }v^{+}\in[0,1].

(13)

After imposing the global volume constraint

\int_{\Omega}v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}\leq\overline{V}\,\lvert\Omega\rvert,\qquad\overline{V}\in[0,1],

, Theorem 3.16]
, any minimizing sequence admits a subsequence

(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{k},v_{k}^{+})\ \overset{\star}{\rightharpoonup}\ (\bm{\mathbf{E}},v^{+})\qquad\text{in }L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d})\times L^{\infty}(\Omega).

(20)

The simple constraints
0\leq v^{+}\leq 1
and the volume constraint (
14
) are weak-
\star
closed. It remains to discuss the weak-
\ast
lower semicontinuity of the objective and the stability of the pointwise realizability constraint

\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\in\mathcal{A}(v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}}))
.

3.1.1
Zeroth-order and Voigt bounds

For the zeroth-order and Voigt admissible sets defined later in (
28
) and (
43
), respectively, the graph

\mathcal{G}:=\{(v,\bm{\mathbf{E}})\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d}:\ \bm{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathcal{A}(v)\}

(21)

is a closed convex subset of a finite-dimensional space. Consider the induced set of admissible fields

\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}:=\big\{(\bm{\mathbf{E}},v^{+})\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d})\times L^{\infty}(\Omega):\ (v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}}),\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}}))\in\mathcal{G}\ \text{for a.e. }\bm{\mathit{x}}\in\Omega\big\}.

(22)

Let
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{G}}
be the Euclidean distance to
\mathcal{G}
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is closed and convex,
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{G}}
is convex and continuous, and, therefore, the functional

(\bm{\mathbf{E}},v^{+})\ \mapsto\ \int_{\Omega}\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{G}}\big(v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}}),\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}

(23)

is weak-
\star
lower semicontinuous on
L^{\infty}\times L^{\infty}
. Hence, if
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{k},v_{k}^{+})\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{k},v_{k}^{+})\rightharpoonup^{\star}(\bm{\mathbf{E}},v^{+})
, then the above integral is
0
along the sequence and thus also at the limit, which implies
(\bm{\mathbf{E}},v^{+})\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}
. Consequently, for these convex bound choices, the full admissible set
\mathcal{D}
in (
18
) is weak-
\star
closed, and since it is bounded it is weak-
\star
compact by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem.

It remains to verify lower semicontinuity of the objective. For each loadcase
j
, the compliance admits the dual representation

c_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{E}})=\sup_{\bm{\mathit{u}}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}}\Big(2\,\ell_{j}(\bm{\mathit{u}})-a_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}(\bm{\mathit{u}},\bm{\mathit{u}})\Big),

(24)

obtained from the minimum principle for the potential energy. For fixed
\bm{\mathit{u}}
, the map
\bm{\mathbf{E}}\mapsto a_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}(\bm{\mathit{u}},\bm{\mathit{u}})
is weak-
\star
continuous (it is linear in
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
), hence (
24
) is the supremum of weak-
\star
continuous affine functionals and, therefore, weak-
\star
lower semicontinuous. Thus,
J=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}c_{j}
is weak-
\star
lower semicontinuous as well, and the direct method yields existence of a minimizer
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star},v^{+\star})\in\mathcal{D}
. The associated states
\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star})
exist uniquely by the Lax–Milgram theorem.

3.1.2
Hashin–Shtrikman-type bounds

For Hashin–Shtrikman-type constraints (with the pointwise admissible set
\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(v)
defined in (
56
)), the feasible set is convex in
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
for fixed
v^{+}
but in general not jointly convex in
(v^{+},\bm{\mathbf{E}})
(cf. Lemma
3.1
). In particular, the pointwise constraint set (
22
) need not be weak-
\star
closed. Moreover, even under the uniform ellipticity bounds (
12
), weak-
\star
convergence of coefficients is in general not compatible with passing to the limit in the state equation; see, e.g., the discussion in
[
26
, Sections 2.2–2.3]
.

Following
[
26
]
, we, therefore, formulate an existence statement in the homogenization topology of
H
-convergence for the stiffness field. Let

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\beta}:=\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d}):\alpha\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d\times d\times d}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\preceq\beta\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d\times d\times d}\ \text{a.e. in }\Omega\},

(25)

where
\alpha,\beta
are as in (
12
). Then any admissible stiffness field belongs to
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\beta}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\beta}
is sequentially compact with respect to
H
-convergence (cf.
[
26
, Theorem 2.2]
).

Define the
H
-relaxed admissible set as the closure of
\mathcal{D}
in the product topology
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{k}\to_{H}\bm{\mathbf{E}})\times(v_{k}^{+}\rightharpoonup^{\star}v^{+})
, i.e.,

\mathcal{D}^{H}:=\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\,(\to_{H})\times(\rightharpoonup^{\star})}.

(26)

Let
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{k},v_{k}^{+})\subset\mathcal{D}
be a minimizing sequence. By the Banach–Alaoglu theorem, we may assume (up to a subsequence)
v_{k}^{+}\rightharpoonup^{\star}v^{+\star}
in
L^{\infty}
. By
H
-compactness of
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\beta}
we may further assume
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{k}\to_{H}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star}
. Hence
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star},v^{+\star})\in\mathcal{D}^{H}
by definition.

Finally, along any
H
-convergent sequence
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{k}\xrightarrow{H}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star}
, the corresponding state solutions satisfy

\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{k})\rightharpoonup\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star})\quad\text{in }\mathcal{U}_{0},\qquad j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{lc}},

and the compliance is lower semicontinuous with respect to this convergence; see the
H
-lower semicontinuity condition
[
26
, (2.14)]
and the fact that the compliance is a typical example satisfying it. Therefore,

J(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star})\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}J(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{k}),

(27)

and the direct-method existence argument (cf.
[
26
, Theorem 2.8]
) yields existence of a minimizer of the relaxed problem (
15
) posed over
\mathcal{D}^{H}
. We emphasize that
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star},v^{+\star})
is an
H
-relaxed limit of admissible designs; in general, it need not satisfy the original Hashin–Shtrikman pointwise constraints in a strongly closed sense under weak-
\star
convergence.

Remark 3.1

(Orthotropic restriction)
.

If we further restrict admissible tensors to the orthotropic symmetry class with free rotations, we mean fields
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
such that, for a.e.
\bm{\mathit{x}}\in\Omega
, there exist an angle
\varphi(\bm{\mathit{x}})
and an orthotropic base tensor
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{b}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})
with
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{b}})_{1112}=(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{b}})_{2212}=0
, and
\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})
is obtained from
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{b}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})
by planar rotation; see Section
3.5
. This pointwise admissible set is, in general, not weak-
\star
closed: weak-
\star
limits may average rapidly oscillating orientation fields, while convex combinations of differently rotated orthotropic tensors need not remain orthotropic. Existence is, therefore, understood in the relaxed sense, i.e., by minimizing over the closure of orthotropic-admissible designs in
(\to_{H})\times(\rightharpoonup^{\star})
.

For the model classes considered in this paper:

1.

Zeroth-order bounds: in the single-loadcase continuum setting, at least one minimizer of (
34
) can be chosen orthotropic (Proposition
3.1
). This is a property of the original model without imposing orthotropy a priori; consequently, adding an orthotropic restriction does not increase the optimal value in that setting.

2.

Voigt bounds: isotropic optimal minimizers exist both in the discrete model (
47
) and in the continuum Voigt setting (Proposition
3.3
). Thus, without imposing symmetry a priori, one can choose an isotropic (hence orthotropic) minimizer, and adding an orthotropic restriction does not change the optimal value.

3.

Hashin–Shtrikman bounds: here the orthotropic class is not weak-
\star
closed, so existence is interpreted via the relaxed closure in
(\to_{H})\times(\rightharpoonup^{\star})
. Theorem
3.1
shows that, in the two-dimensional single-loadcase continuum setting with well-ordered isotropic phases, the relaxed value is attained in the relaxation sense by rank-one and rank-two sequential laminates, which are orthotropic. Therefore, imposing orthotropy does not change the continuum relaxed optimal value.

For numerical solution, we discretize the state space by
\mathcal{U}_{h}\subset\mathcal{U}_{0}
and parametrize
(\bm{\mathbf{E}},v^{+})
by elementwise constants. Then (
10
) becomes a collection of local constraints on the element variables. For the zeroth-order and Voigt bounds, these constraints are jointly convex and can be written as linear matrix inequalities, while Hashin–Shtrikman-type bounds are convex in
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
for fixed
v^{+}
but not jointly convex in
(\bm{\mathbf{E}},v^{+})
. The resulting discrete formulations are given in Sections
3.2
–
3.4
.

3.2
FMO problem under zeroth-order bounds

We start with the weakest pointwise admissible set
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(v)
, obtained by imposing only uniform bounds on the effective elasticity tensor
[
36
,
43
]
,

\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(v):=\left\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d}_{\succ 0}\;:\;\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\right\},\qquad v\in[0,1],

(28)

where
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-},\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
are the (isotropic) tensors of the soft and stiff constituents such that
0\prec\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\prec\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
. In contrast to the Voigt and Hashin–Shtrikman bounds considered later, (
28
) does not couple
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
to a local volume-fraction variable
v^{+}
. We therefore first derive the discrete FMO formulation under zeroth-order bounds for computation and then return to the single-loadcase continuum setting to show that an orthotropic minimizer exists.

For numerical treatment, we now pass to a finite-element discretization of
\Omega
into
n_{\mathrm{e}}
elements and approximate the effective tensor field by elementwise constants
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
. Following the standard trace-constrained resource modeling in free material design
[
43
,
13
,
54
,
10
,
22
,
23
]
, and since (
28
) does not include an explicit local volume fraction variable, we introduce auxiliary elementwise volume fractions
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
and enforce a local trace-level mixture inequality

\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})\leq(1-v_{i}^{+})\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})+v_{i}^{+}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}),\qquad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}}.

(29)

Assuming equisized elements and imposing the average volume-fraction bound
\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}v_{i}^{+}\leq\overline{V}
, summing (
29
) over all elements gives

\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})\leq\overline{V}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})+(1-\overline{V})\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}).

(30)

Conversely, if
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
and the global trace bound above holds, then defining

v_{i}^{+}:=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})-\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})}{\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})-\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})}\in[0,1]

(31)

yields (
29
) with equality, and
\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i}v_{i}^{+}\leq\overline{V}
. Hence, after eliminating
\{v_{i}^{+}\}
, this auxiliary formulation is equivalent to the global trace constraint (
32d
).

For each loadcase
j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{lc}}
, let
\mathsf{f}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof},j}}
be the discrete load vector and
\mathsf{K}_{j}(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\})\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{\mathrm{dof},j}\times n_{\mathrm{dof},j}}_{\succ 0}
the assembled stiffness matrix. The multi-loadcase compliance admits the standard Schur-complement epigraph reformulation as a semidefinite program
[
16
, Appendix A]
, as commonly used in free material and compliance optimization
[
10
,
33
]
. The resulting minimum-compliance free material optimization problem reads

\displaystyle\min_{\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}c_{j}

(32a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}&-\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{j}&\mathsf{K}_{j}\!\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}\right)\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0,\qquad j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{lc}},

(32b)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},\qquad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}},

(32c)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})\leq\overline{V}\,\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})+(1-\overline{V})\,\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}).

(32d)

The linear matrix inequality (
32b
) enforces
c_{j}\geq\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{K}_{j}(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\})^{-1}\mathsf{f}_{j}
by the Schur complement, so that at optimality
c_{j}
equals the compliance of loadcase
j
. The tensor bound (
32c
) is equivalent to the strain-energy inequalities

\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\leq\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\leq\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle,\qquad\forall\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d},

(33)

i.e., no element can be stiffer than
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
or softer than
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}
in any direction.

Problem (
32
) is a convex finite-dimensional semidefinite program and can, therefore, be solved to global optimality. We refer to (
32
) as the zeroth-order free material optimization model (ZO-FMO). Its main drawback is that the admissible set (
32c
) is typically very large; in particular, it does not encode any microstructural information beyond the uniform ellipticity range
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
.

Before introducing tighter bounds, we record a structural property that connects the zeroth-order (ZO) model to our orthotropic focus at the continuum level. In the single-loadcase setting, the continuum ZO problem

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d}_{\succ 0})}\quad

\displaystyle c_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{E}})

(34a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\ \text{for a.e. }\bm{\mathit{x}}\in\Omega,

(34b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}\leq\overline{V}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})+(1-\overline{V})\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})

(34c)

is the reference model below; we will show that it admits an orthotropic optimal solution.

For later use, recall the complementary-energy representation (minimum complementary energy principle): for
n_{\mathrm{lc}}=1
and any admissible
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
,

c_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{E}})=\min_{\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{ad},1}}\int_{\Omega}\left\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}(\bm{\mathit{x}}),\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}},

(35)

where

\Sigma_{\mathrm{ad},1}:=\left\{\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{d\times d})\;:\;\int_{\Omega}\langle\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}(\bm{\mathit{v}})\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}=\ell_{1}(\bm{\mathit{v}})\quad\forall\bm{\mathit{v}}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}\right\}.

(36)

The minimum in (
35
) is attained:
\Sigma_{\mathrm{ad},1}
is a closed affine subspace of

L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{d\times d})
, and the functional

\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}

is coercive and strictly convex for admissible
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
.

The structural role of principal directions in compliance-driven design is classical in orientation optimization of orthotropic materials
[
42
]
and in homogenization-based laminate constructions
[
5
,
8
]
. The next proposition gives a corresponding representative-minimizer statement directly for the single-loadcase continuum zeroth-order FMO model (
34
).

Proposition 3.1

(Orthotropic minimizer for the single-loadcase continuum ZO problem)
.

Assume
n_{\mathrm{lc}}=1
and
d=2
. Then (
34
) admits a minimizer, and at least one minimizer
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{ortho}}
can be chosen such that, for a.e.
\bm{\mathit{x}}\in\Omega
,
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{ortho}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})
is orthotropic in a principal basis of an optimal stress field for (
35
).

Proof.

Existence of a minimizer for (
34
) follows from Section
3.1
. Let
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star}
be such a minimizer.

Pick
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{ad},1}
minimizing (
35
) for
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star}
. Using the spectral decomposition in 2D, choose a measurable orthogonal field
\mathsf{U}(\bm{\mathit{x}})
that diagonalizes
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}(\bm{\mathit{x}})
for a.e.
\bm{\mathit{x}}
(and set
\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{I}
on repeated-eigenvalue points). Set
\mathsf{M}:=\mathrm{Diag}(1,-1)
and
\mathsf{Q}:=\mathsf{U}\mathsf{M}\mathsf{U}^{\mathsf{T}}
. For any fourth-order tensor
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
, denote by
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})
its orthogonal transform:

\big(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})\big)_{ijkl}:=\sum_{p,q,r,s=1}^{d}(\mathsf{Q})_{ip}(\mathsf{Q})_{jq}(\mathsf{Q})_{kr}(\mathsf{Q})_{ls}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})_{pqrs}
.
Define

\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{ortho}}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star}+\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star})\big)\quad\text{pointwise in }\Omega.

(37)

Because
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-},\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
are isotropic,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{Q}}
preserves the bounds
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\cdot\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
; by convexity,
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{ortho}}
is feasible for (
34
). Also
\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}))=\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})
, so the trace constraint is unchanged.

For fixed
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}(\bm{\mathit{x}})
, the map
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\mapsto\langle\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\rangle
is convex on positive-definite tensors. Moreover, by orthogonality and
\mathsf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\mathsf{Q}=\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}
, so that
\big(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})\big)^{-1}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{Q}}\!\left(\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\right)
and

\left\langle\big(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star})\big)^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\right\rangle=\left\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\right\rangle.

(38)

Hence, pointwise in
\Omega
,

\left\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{ortho}})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\right\rangle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\right\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle\big(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star})\big)^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\right\rangle=\left\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\right\rangle.

(39)

Integrating gives

\int_{\Omega}\left\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{ortho}})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}\leq\int_{\Omega}\left\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}=c_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star}).

(40)

By (
35
),

c_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{ortho}})\leq\int_{\Omega}\left\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{ortho}})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}\leq c_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star}).

(41)

Since
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star}
is optimal for (
34
), we conclude
c_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{ortho}})=c_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\star})
, so
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{ortho}}
is also optimal. In the principal basis of
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\star}
, averaging with
\mathsf{M}
cancels the shear-coupling terms, hence
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{ortho}}
is orthotropic.
∎

Proposition
3.1
is a global continuum statement for single-loadcase ZO-FMO: at least one minimizer can be chosen orthotropic. This argument is continuum-level and does not transfer directly to fixed finite-element discretizations. For low-order elements, stresses can be elementwise constant, but such discretizations are prone to checkerboarding unless regularized; this regularization averages neighboring tensors and generally destroys local orthotropic structure. Using higher-order elements mitigates these artifacts, but stresses are then no longer elementwise constant, so local tensors must be optimal with respect to multiple stress states, which again typically breaks orthotropy.

We now tighten ZO-FMO by adding Voigt upper bounds; in that case, we obtain an even stronger structural result (existence of isotropic optimal minimizers, Proposition
3.3
).

3.3
FMO problem under Voigt upper bounds

To strengthen the connection between the FMO problem and the realizable elasticity tensors without sacrificing convexity, we proceed by exploiting the Voigt upper bound
[
27
]
for effective elasticity tensors of composites

\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{V}}(v_{i}^{+}):=(1-v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},

(42)

where
v_{i}^{+}
is the volume fraction of the stiffer material
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
in the
i
-th element. The inequality (
42
) is equivalent to the strain-energy bound
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\leq\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{V}}(v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
for all
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
. Moreover, since
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
and
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
, we also have
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{V}}(v_{i}^{+})\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
. The Voigt constraint (
42
) thus induces the first-order admissible set

\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(v):=\Big\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d}:\ \bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}\preceq(1-v)\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\Big\},\qquad v\in[0,1].

(43)

In what follows, we exploit the Voigt upper bound (
42
) to provide a better estimate of the microstructural relative volume. In particular, due to the Loewner ordering, we have

\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})\leq\operatorname{Tr}((1-v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})\leq\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}),

(44)

and, thus, also for the average of the elasticity tensors
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
over all elements, assuming equisized elements for simplicity,

\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})\leq\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})+\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}[\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})-\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})]\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}v_{i}^{+}\leq\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}).

(45)

Now, allowing the average trace to be at most
\overline{V}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})+(1-\overline{V})\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})
as in (
32d
), the tighter volume estimator reads as

\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})+\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}[\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})-\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})]\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}v_{i}^{+}\leq\overline{V}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})+(1-\overline{V})\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}),

(46)

which, after simplification, provides the Voigt free material optimization problem (V-FMO)

\displaystyle\min_{\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\},\{v_{i}^{+}\}}\;

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}c_{j}

(47a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}&-\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{j}&\mathsf{K}_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}\right)\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0,\quad j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{lc}},

(47b)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq(1-v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},\quad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}},

(47c)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}v_{i}^{+}\leq\overline{V},

(47d)

\displaystyle 0\leq v_{i}^{+}\leq 1,\forall i\in 1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}}.

(47e)

Notice that the constraint (
47d
) now directly limits the average volume fraction of the stiffer material
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
.

The V-FMO problem (

) is indeed tighter than the FMO problem under zeroth-order bounds (

). In particular, for
\overline{V}\in(0,1)
and after projecting out the auxiliary variables
\{v_{i}^{+}\}
, the feasible set in

.

Proposition 3.2

(Strict feasible-set inclusion of V-FMO in ZO-FMO)
.

Assume
0<\overline{V}<1
and
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\prec\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
, i.e., the phase bulk and shear moduli satisfying
0<\kappa^{-}<\kappa^{+}
and
0<\mu^{-}<\mu^{+}
. With

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ZO}}:=\{(\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}):\ (\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\})\ \text{feasible for \eqref{eq:fmo}}\},
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{FO}}:=\{(\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}):\ \exists\{v_{i}^{+}\}\ \text{s.t.}\ (\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\},\{v_{i}^{+}\})\ \text{feasible for \eqref{eq:fmo_voigt}}\}
, it holds that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{FO}}\subsetneq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ZO}}
.

Proof.

Inclusion
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{FO}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ZO}}
is immediate: (
47b
) is the same as (
32b
), (
47c
) with
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
implies (
32c
), and

\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})\leq\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})+\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})-\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}v_{i}^{+}\leq\overline{V}\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})+(1-\overline{V})\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}),

(48)

so (
32d
) holds.

For strictness, write the bulk and shear moduli differences as
\Delta\kappa:=\kappa^{+}-\kappa^{-}>0
,
\Delta\mu:=\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}>0
, and

\alpha:=\frac{\Delta\kappa}{\Delta\kappa+2\Delta\mu}\in(0,1),\qquad\theta:=\min\{1,\overline{V}/\alpha\},

(49)

so
\theta>\overline{V}
and
\alpha\theta\leq\overline{V}
.
Define an isotropic tensor
\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}
by
\widehat{\kappa}:=\kappa^{-}+\theta\Delta\kappa
and
\widehat{\mu}:=\mu^{-}
, and set
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}=\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}
for all
i
. Then
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
. Moreover, for
d=2
,

\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})-\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})}{\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})-\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})}=\frac{2\theta\Delta\kappa}{2\Delta\kappa+4\Delta\mu}=\alpha\theta\leq\overline{V},

(50)

hence (
32d
) holds. Choosing
c_{j}:=\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{K}_{j}(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\})^{-1}\mathsf{f}_{j}
gives (
32b
), so
(\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\})\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ZO}}
.

If
(\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\})\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{FO}}
, then for some
\{v_{i}^{+}\}
, (
47c
) implies
\kappa^{-}+\theta\Delta\kappa=\widehat{\kappa}\leq\kappa^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\Delta\kappa
, hence
v_{i}^{+}\geq\theta
for all
i
. Therefore
\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i}v_{i}^{+}\geq\theta>\overline{V}
, contradicting (
47d
). Thus,
(\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\})\notin\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{FO}}
, and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{FO}}\subsetneq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ZO}}
.
∎

The endpoint cases satisfy
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{FO}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ZO}}
for
\overline{V}\in\{0,1\}
. Indeed, for
\overline{V}=0
, the Voigt volume constraint (
47d
) forces
v_{i}^{+}=0
for all
i
, hence (
47c
) reduces to
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}
, i.e.,
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}=\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}
, which is exactly what (
32d
) enforces in the zeroth-order formulation. For
\overline{V}=1
, the volume constraints are inactive in both models, and any zeroth-order-feasible tensor field also satisfies the Voigt bounds by taking
v_{i}^{+}=1
for all
i
.

For the Voigt-constrained problem (
47
), we now show that isotropic optimal minimizers exist in both the discrete and continuum settings.

Proposition 3.3

(Existence of isotropic minimizers for V-FMO (discrete and continuum))
.

Let
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}
and
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
be well-ordered isotropic elasticity tensors with
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
. Then:

1.

The discrete V-FMO problem (
47
) admits an optimal solution in which each elasticity tensor saturates the upper bound in (
47c
), i.e.,

\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}=\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{V}}(v_{i}^{+\star}):=(1-v_{i}^{+\star})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+\star}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},\quad\text{for all }i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}}.

In particular, there always exists an optimal discrete solution consisting entirely of isotropic tensors.

2.

In the continuum setting of Section
3.1
, if
(\bm{\mathbf{E}},v^{+})
satisfies the Voigt pointwise admissibility
\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\in\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}}))
for a.e.
\bm{\mathit{x}}\in\Omega
, with
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
from (
43
), and

\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}(\bm{\mathit{x}}):=(1-v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}}))\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},

then
(\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}},v^{+})
is Voigt-admissible and
J(\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}})\leq J(\bm{\mathbf{E}})
. In particular, one may always choose an isotropic minimizer in the continuum Voigt-constrained model.

Proof.

For part 1, existence of a discrete minimizer for (
47
) follows from finite-dimensional compactness: by (
47c
) and
0\leq v_{i}^{+}\leq 1
, all
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
are uniformly bounded between
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}
and
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
, and for fixed
(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\},\{v_{i}^{+}\})
the minimal admissible
c_{j}
is given by the Schur-complement value
\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{K}_{j}(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\})^{-1}\mathsf{f}_{j}
. Let
\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}\},\,\{c_{j}^{\star}\},\,\{v_{i}^{+\star}\}\right)
be such an optimal solution, and suppose that for some index
i
we have
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}\neq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{V}}(v_{i}^{+\star})
. Since
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{V}}(v_{i}^{+\star})
, there exists a symmetric positive semidefinite tensor
\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{i}\succeq 0
,
\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{i}\neq\bm{\mathbf{0}}
such that
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{V}}(v_{i}^{+\star})=\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}+\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{i}
.

Define now a modified design by replacing
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}
with
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}_{i}:=\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{V}}(v_{i}^{+\star})
while keeping all other variables unchanged. The constraints (
47c
) and (
47d
) remain satisfied by construction. Since the element stiffness contributions are linear in
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
, the assembled matrix
\mathsf{K}_{j}
is linear in
\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}
. Therefore,
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}_{i}\succeq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}
implies
\mathsf{K}_{j}(\{\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}_{i}\})\succeq\mathsf{K}_{j}(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}\})
and, thus,

\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}^{\star}&-\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{j}&\mathsf{K}_{j}(\{\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}_{i}\})\end{pmatrix}\succeq\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}^{\star}&-\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{j}&\mathsf{K}_{j}(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}\})\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

(51)

By the Schur complement lemma, this implies
c_{j}^{\star}\geq\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{K}_{j}(\{\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}_{i}\})^{-1}\mathsf{f}_{j}
, so the compliance remains at most
c_{j}^{\star}
, and the updated design is feasible. Repeating this replacement for all
i
such that
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}\neq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{V}}(v_{i}^{+\star})
yields a fully isotropic design, in which all elasticity tensors lie on the Voigt upper bound.

For part 2, from
\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\in\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}}))
and (
43
), we have
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\preceq\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})
for a.e.
\bm{\mathit{x}}
, so
(\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}},v^{+})
is feasible. Moreover, for every loadcase
j
and every
\bm{\mathit{u}}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}
,
a_{\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}}(\bm{\mathit{u}},\bm{\mathit{u}})\geq a_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}(\bm{\mathit{u}},\bm{\mathit{u}})
. Using the dual representation (
24
),

c_{j}(\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}})=\sup_{\bm{\mathit{u}}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}}\big(2\ell_{j}(\bm{\mathit{u}})-a_{\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}}(\bm{\mathit{u}},\bm{\mathit{u}})\big)\leq\sup_{\bm{\mathit{u}}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}}\big(2\ell_{j}(\bm{\mathit{u}})-a_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}(\bm{\mathit{u}},\bm{\mathit{u}})\big)=c_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}).

(52)

Summing over
j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{lc}}
yields
J(\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{E}}})\leq J(\bm{\mathbf{E}})
. Since existence of a continuum minimizer for the Voigt model is established in Section
3.1
, applying this isotropic replacement to any minimizer yields an isotropic minimizer.
∎

Using the discrete part of Proposition
3.3
, optimal elasticities can be parameterized directly by the Voigt mixture. Hence, we reparametrize the optimized elasticities as

\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}(v_{i}^{+}):=\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}-\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})v_{i}^{+}

(53)

so that the constraint (
47c
) is always satisfied. This allows us to eliminate the tensor variables and parametrize the tensors only using the scalars
\{v_{i}^{+}\}
. Then, the reduced version of the free material optimization problem under Voigt upper bounds reads as

\displaystyle\min_{\{c_{j}\},\{v_{i}^{+}\}}\;

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}c_{j}

(54a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}&-\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{j}&\mathsf{K}_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}(v_{i}^{+})\}\right)\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0,\quad j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{lc}},

(54b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}v_{i}^{+}\leq\overline{V},

(54c)

\displaystyle 0\leq v_{i}^{+}\leq 1,\forall i\in 1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}}.

(54d)

This corresponds to the standard variable-thickness-sheet problem
[
44
]
as also observed by
12
. We refer to (
54
) as the reduced (equivalent) V-FMO formulation.

3.4
FMO problem under Hashin–Shtrikman upper bounds

Although the Voigt upper bound presented in the previous section tightened the space of elasticity tensors
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
, it can be further sharpened by exploiting the second-order microstructure characteristics, provided by the Hashin–Shtrikman (upper) bounds. Nevertheless, there is an associated cost: loss of convexity of the optimization problem (Lemma
3.1
).

3.4.1
Finite-dimensional semi-infinite formulation

The Hashin–Shtrikman upper bounds on elastic energy take the form

\forall\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}:\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\leq f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+}),\qquad f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+}):=\langle[(1-v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}]\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+}),

(55)

where
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
is the strain tensor and
q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
is a nonnegative scalar (which we show in Proposition
3.4
) that serves as a correction from the Voigt upper bound (
42
): by setting
q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})=0
, the inequality
\forall\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}:\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\leq\langle[(1-v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}]\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
is equivalent to
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq(1-v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
. In the notation of (
13
), the Hashin–Shtrikman-type admissible set reads

\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(v):=\Big\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2\times 2\times 2}:\ \bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}},\ \langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\leq f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v),\ \ \forall\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}\Big\}.

(56)

Thus, (
57d
) is the elementwise constraint
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(v_{i}^{+})
.

In contrast to the Voigt upper bound, Hashin–Shtrikman upper bounds are tight in the sense that, for each strain
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
, there exists a sequentially laminated composite whose effective tensor attains equality in (
55
)
[
6
,
5
]
. The corresponding optimization FMO problem then writes as

\displaystyle\min_{\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\},\{v_{i}^{+}\}}\;

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}c_{j}

(57a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}&-\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{j}&\mathsf{K}_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}\right)\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0,\quad j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{lc}},

(57b)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}},

(57c)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\leq\big\langle\big[(1-v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\,\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\big]\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\big\rangle-q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})\quad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}},\forall\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2},

(57d)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}v_{i}^{+}\leq\overline{V},

(57e)

\displaystyle 0\leq v_{i}^{+}\leq 1,\forall i\in 1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}}.

(57f)

Define now
t=\lvert\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rvert
and
s=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})^{2}-4\operatorname{Det}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})}=\sqrt{(\varepsilon_{11}-\varepsilon_{22})^{2}+4\varepsilon_{12}^{2}}
. In addition, let
\Delta\kappa=\kappa^{+}-\kappa^{-}>0
and
\Delta\mu=\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}>0
be the contrast of the bulk and shear moduli of the isotropic constituents. Then, based on the result of
5
, for anisotropic composites made of well-ordered isotropic constituents,
q
has the explicit form:

q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &q_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+}):=\frac{v_{i}^{+}(1-v_{i}^{+})\left(\Delta\kappa t-\Delta\mu s\right)^{2}}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-(\Delta\kappa+\Delta\mu)v_{i}^{+}}\\
&\qquad\text{if }\;v_{i}^{+}t\Delta\kappa\leq(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\kappa v_{i}^{+})s\;\text{ and }\;v_{i}^{+}s\Delta\mu\leq(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu v_{i}^{+})t,\end{aligned}\\
\begin{aligned} q_{2}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+}):=(1-v_{i}^{+})\left[\frac{v_{i}^{+}t^{2}\Delta\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\kappa v_{i}^{+}}-s^{2}\Delta\mu\right]\;\text{ if }\;v_{i}^{+}t\Delta\kappa>(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\kappa v_{i}^{+})s,\end{aligned}\\
\begin{aligned} q_{3}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+}):=\left(1-{v_{i}^{+}}\right)\left[\frac{{v_{i}^{+}}s^{2}{\Delta\mu}^{2}}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu v_{i}^{+}}-t^{2}\Delta\kappa\right]\;\text{ if }\;v_{i}^{+}s\Delta\mu>(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu v_{i}^{+})t.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(58)

The correction term
q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
in (
58
) is given by three explicit formulas chosen via two inequalities. These cases form a consistent partition (there is no overlap where both strict inequalities hold), and the expressions coincide on the branch boundaries (in particular
q_{1}=q_{2}
on
\Gamma_{12}
and
q_{1}=q_{3}
on
\Gamma_{13}
), so that
q
(and hence
f^{\mathrm{HS}}
) is continuous across branch transitions; see Proposition
A.1
in the Appendix.

With
q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
defined in (
58
), (
55
) is invariant under scalar scaling of the strain tensor. Indeed, for any
\alpha\neq 0
we have
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}(\alpha\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}),\alpha\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=\alpha^{2}\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
,
\lvert\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rvert=\lvert\alpha\rvert\,\lvert\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rvert
, and
\operatorname{Det}(\alpha\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})=\alpha^{2}\operatorname{Det}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})
. Since the Hashin–Shtrikman envelope
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v)
is positively
2
-homogeneous in
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
, both sides of (
55
) scale quadratically with
\alpha
and the inequality is equivalent for all
\alpha\neq 0
. Hence, it suffices to enforce (
55
) on any fixed sphere in
\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}
. We choose the normalization
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{2}/2
, and introduce the invariants

t:=\lvert\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rvert,\qquad s:=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})^{2}-4\operatorname{Det}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})}=\sqrt{(\varepsilon_{11}-\varepsilon_{22})^{2}+4\varepsilon_{12}^{2}}.

(59)

For
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{2}/2
these satisfy
t\in[0,1]
,
s\in[0,1]
, and the identity
t^{2}+s^{2}=2\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rVert_{F}^{2}=1
. Consequently, we may eliminate
s=\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
and parameterize the constraint solely by
t\in[0,1]

q(t;v_{i}^{+})=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &q_{1}(t;v_{i}^{+}):=\frac{v_{i}^{+}(1-v_{i}^{+})\left(\Delta\kappa t-\Delta\mu\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\right)^{2}}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-(\Delta\kappa+\Delta\mu)v_{i}^{+}}\\
&\qquad\text{if }\;v_{i}^{+}t\Delta\kappa\leq(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\kappa v_{i}^{+})\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\;\text{ and }\;v_{i}^{+}\Delta\mu\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\leq(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu v_{i}^{+})t,\end{aligned}\\
\begin{aligned} &q_{2}(t;v_{i}^{+}):=\left(1-{v_{i}^{+}}\right)\left[\frac{v_{i}^{+}{t}^{2}\Delta\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\kappa v_{i}^{+}}-(1-t^{2})\Delta\mu\right]\\
&\qquad\text{if }\;v_{i}^{+}t\Delta\kappa>(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\kappa v_{i}^{+})\sqrt{1-t^{2}},\end{aligned}\\
\begin{aligned} &q_{3}(t;v_{i}^{+}):=\left(1-{v_{i}^{+}}\right)\left[\frac{{v_{i}^{+}}{\Delta\mu}^{2}(1-t^{2})}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu v_{i}^{+}}-t^{2}\Delta\kappa\right]\\
&\qquad\text{ if }\;v_{i}^{+}\Delta\mu\sqrt{1-t^{2}}>(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu v_{i}^{+})t,\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(60)

which shows that the correction term
q
depends only on
t=\lvert\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rvert
and the volume fraction
v_{i}^{+}
. In contrast, the full Hashin–Shtrikman upper bound (
55
) still depends on the whole strain tensor
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
through the Voigt part
\langle[(1-v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}]\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
. At this stage, the
t
-parameterization is used only for the correction term; the corresponding one-parameter reduction of the activating-volume map is obtained later for orthotropic tensors in Section
3.5
(Proposition
3.6
).

Using this form, we now prove that
q(t;v_{i}^{+})\geq 0
for all
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
and
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
, which shows that the Hashin–Shtrikman upper bound (
55
) is tighter than the Voigt upper bound (
42
) (see also Figure
2
for a graphical illustration).

Proposition 3.4

(Nonnegativity and equality cases of the HS correction term)
.

For the Hashin–Shtrikman upper bounds (
55
) with
q(t;v_{i}^{+})
defined in (
60
), it holds for all
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
and
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}
with the Frobenius norm
\sqrt{2}/2
and
t=\lvert\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rvert
that
q(t;v_{i}^{+})\geq 0
. Moreover,
q(t;v_{i}^{+})=0
if and only if
v_{i}^{+}\in\{0,1\}
or
\Delta\kappa t=\Delta\mu\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by first analyzing the boundary values
v_{i}^{+}\in\{0,1\}
and then showing positivity or nonnegativity of each branch in (
60
) for
v_{i}^{+}\in(0,1)
.

Consider now
v_{i}^{+}\in\{0,1\}
. For
v_{i}^{+}=0
, the conditions of the first branch are satisfied for all
t\in[0,1]
and hence for all strains
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
, because the left-hand side of the inequalities are zero and the right-hand sides nonnegative. Using arbitrary
t
,
q(t;0)=q_{1}(t;0)=0
and, therefore, the Voigt upper bound is tight for
v_{i}^{+}=0
. For
v_{i}^{+}=1
, all three branches in (
60
) evaluate as zero due to the
(1-v_{i}^{+})
prefactor. Hence,
q(t;1)=0
, so the Voigt upper bound is also tight in this case.

Let us further proceed with the branches of
q(t;v_{i}^{+})
for
v_{i}^{+}\in(0,1)
. The first branch in (
60
) is nonnegative due to
v_{i}^{+}\in(0,1)
,
0\leq\mu^{-}<\mu^{+}
,
0\leq\kappa^{-}<\kappa^{+}
, hence
v_{i}^{+}\Delta\kappa<\kappa^{+}
and
v_{i}^{+}\Delta\mu<\mu^{+}
. Moreover,
q_{1}(t;v_{i}^{+})=0
if and only if
\Delta\kappa t=\Delta\mu\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
, i.e.,
t=\Delta\mu/\sqrt{\Delta\mu^{2}+\Delta\kappa^{2}}
. Under this equality, the two branch conditions reduce to
\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-v_{i}^{+}(\Delta\kappa+\Delta\mu)\geq 0
, which holds for all
v_{i}^{+}\in(0,1)
, so the related strains are indeed in this branch. For any other strains
\mathcal{E}:=\{\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}:\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\neq\Delta\mu/\sqrt{\Delta\mu^{2}+\Delta\kappa^{2}}\}
satisfying the branch conditions (for example, set
t=1/\sqrt{1+(v_{i}^{+}\Delta\kappa/d_{2})^{2}}
),
q_{1}(t;v_{i}^{+})>0
for all
v_{i}^{+}\in(0,1)
.

For the second branch in (
60
), the denominator
d_{2}:=\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\kappa v_{i}^{+}
is positive due to ordered materials, so we only need to show positivity of

(1-v_{i}^{+})\left[v_{i}^{+}t^{2}\Delta\kappa^{2}-\Delta\mu d_{2}(1-t^{2})\right]\quad\text{for}\quad v_{i}^{+}t\Delta\kappa\geq d_{2}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}.

(61)

Since both sides are nonnegative, squaring preserves inequality and yields
(v_{i}^{+})^{2}t^{2}\Delta\kappa^{2}>d_{2}^{2}(1-t^{2})
, hence
d_{2}(1-t^{2})<(v_{i}^{+})^{2}t^{2}\Delta\kappa^{2}/d_{2}
. Substituting this upper bound into the negative term in (
61
) gives the lower bound

(1-v_{i}^{+})v_{i}^{+}{t}^{2}\Delta\kappa^{2}\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-(\Delta\kappa+\Delta\mu)v_{i}^{+}}{d_{2}}>0,

(62)

which is positive due to
v_{i}^{+}\in(0,1)
and because
t^{2}=0
violates the branch condition.

For the third case in (
60
), the denominator
d_{3}=\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu v_{i}^{+}
is again positive, so we only need to show positivity of

(1-v_{i}^{+})\left[v_{i}^{+}\Delta\mu^{2}(1-t^{2})-\Delta\kappa d_{3}t^{2}\right]\quad\text{for}\quad v_{i}^{+}\Delta\mu\sqrt{1-t^{2}}>d_{3}t.

(63)

Both sides of the right inequality are nonnegative, so it is after squaring equivalent to
(v_{i}^{+})^{2}(1-t^{2})\Delta\mu^{2}/d_{3}>d_{3}t^{2}
. Inserting this upper bound into the negative term in (
63
), we obtain

(1-v_{i}^{+}){v_{i}^{+}}(1-t^{2})\Delta\mu^{2}\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-(\Delta\kappa+\Delta\mu)v_{i}^{+}}{d_{3}}>0,

(64)

which is also positive for
v_{i}^{+}\in(0,1)
as
t^{2}=1
violates the branch condition.
∎

3.4.2
Geometric properties of the Hashin–Shtrikman feasible set

For any fixed
v_{i}^{+}
and strain sample
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
, the Hashin–Shtrikman constraint (
55
) is affine in the entries of
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
, hence the feasible set in
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
is convex (see Figure
2
); with an outer approximation by a finite set of sampled strains it becomes a polyhedron. However, (
55
) is nonconvex in
(v_{i}^{+},\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
, which we show in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1

(Nonconvexity of the HS-feasible set)
.

Let
0<\kappa^{-}<\kappa^{+}
and
0<\mu^{-}<\mu^{+}
. Then, the Hashin–Shtrikman upper bound (
55
) with
q(t;v_{i}^{+})
defined in (
60
) and
t=\lvert\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rvert
is nonconvex in
(v_{i}^{+},\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
.

Proof.

Consider the feasible pairs
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-},0)
and
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},1)
. By Proposition
3.4
, for any strain
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
(and thus
t
) we have
q(t;0)=q(t;1)=0
, so (
55
) holds with equality at both endpoints. Fix any
\theta\in(0,1)
and set
\widehat{v}:=\theta
and
\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}:=(1-\theta)\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+\theta\,\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
. Evaluating (
55
) at
(\widehat{v},\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{E}}})
gives, for any strain
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
,

\big\langle\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\big\rangle\;\leq\;\big\langle\big[(1-\widehat{v})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+\widehat{v}\,\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\big]\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\big\rangle-q(\lvert\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rvert;\widehat{v}).

(65)

Since
\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{E}}}=(1-\widehat{v})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+\widehat{v}\,\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
, the two quadratic terms are identical and the inequality reduces to
0\;\leq\;-\,q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\widehat{v})
. However, for any interior
\widehat{v}\in(0,1)
the strains
\hat{\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}}\in\{\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}:\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\neq\Delta\mu/\sqrt{\Delta\mu^{2}+\Delta\kappa^{2}}\}
result in
q(\lvert\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}})\rvert;\widehat{v})>0
. Hence, the inequality fails for such a strain, and
(\widehat{v},\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{E}}})
is infeasible. Therefore, although
(0,\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})
and
(1,\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})
are feasible, every interior convex combination
\big((1-\theta)\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+\theta\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},\theta\big)
with
\theta\in(0,1)
is infeasible. The feasible set in
(v_{i}^{+},\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
is thus nonconvex.
∎

The nonconvexity of the Hashin–Shtrikman bounds in
(v_{i}^{+},\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
is illustrated in Figure
3
, which shows the feasible sets for varying
v_{i}^{+}
. The figure also suggests that, when combined with the zeroth-order lower bound
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
, the Voigt relation
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{V}}(v_{i}^{+})
describes the closed convex hull of the Hashin–Shtrikman feasible set in
(v_{i}^{+},\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
, which we prove next.

Lemma 3.2

(Convex hull of the HS-feasible set)
.

Define the feasible sets

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}}:=\{(v,\bm{\mathbf{E}})\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2\times 2\times 2}:\ \bm{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(v)\},

and

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{V}}:=\{(v,\bm{\mathbf{E}})\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2\times 2\times 2}:\ \bm{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(v)\},

Then, taking the convex hull in the product space
[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2\times 2\times 2}
(with coordinates
(v,\bm{\mathbf{E}})
), we have

\operatorname{Conv}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}})=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{V}}.

Proof.

By definition of
\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(v)
we have
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}
for all
(v,\bm{\mathbf{E}})\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}}
. Moreover, since
q\geq 0
(cf. Proposition
3.4
), we obtain

f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v)=\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{V}}(v)\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v)\leq\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{V}}(v)\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\qquad\forall\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\ \forall v\in[0,1].

(66)

Hence, every
(v,\bm{\mathbf{E}})\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}}
satisfies
\bm{\mathbf{E}}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{V}}(v)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{V}}
. Since
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{V}}
is convex, this implies that
\operatorname{Conv}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}})\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{V}}
.

For the reverse inclusion, let
(v,\bm{\mathbf{E}})\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{V}}
be arbitrary. If
v=0
, then
\bm{\mathbf{E}}=\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}
and
(0,\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}}
(cf. the endpoint identity
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\cdot;0)=\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, e.g. Proposition
3.4
). Assume
v\in(0,1]
and define

\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{1}:=\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+\frac{1}{v}\bigl(\bm{\mathbf{E}}-\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\bigr).

(67)

From
\bm{\mathbf{E}}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{V}}(v)=\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}-\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})
we infer
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{1}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
and clearly
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{1}
. Using the endpoint identity
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\cdot;1)=\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\cdot,\cdot\rangle
(Proposition
3.4
), it follows that
(1,\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{1})\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}}
. Consequently,

(v,\bm{\mathbf{E}})=(1-v)\,(0,\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})+v\,(1,\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{1})\in\operatorname{Conv}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}}),

(68)

which shows
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{V}}\subseteq\operatorname{Conv}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}})
. Together with
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{V}}\supseteq\operatorname{Conv}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}})
this yields
\operatorname{Conv}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{HS}})=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{V}}
.
∎

3.4.3
Eliminating the volume fractions

As in the Voigt case, we show that the Hashin–Shtrikman constraint is active at optimality, which allows us to reformulate (
57
) by eliminating
v_{i}^{+}
. By Propositions
A.1
and
A.2
in the Appendix, for every fixed
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
the map
v\mapsto f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v)
is continuous and nondecreasing. Hence, for fixed
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})
the feasibility set in
v_{i}^{+}
is an interval,

\{v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]:\ \langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\leq f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})\}=[\hat{v}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}),1],

(69)

where
\hat{v}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})\in[0,1]
denotes the minimal admissible volume fraction (equivalently, the smallest
v
such that the inequality holds; when
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
lies on the graph this means
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\hat{v}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}))
). Imposing the Hashin–Shtrikman inequality for all normalized strains is, therefore, equivalent to

v_{i}^{+}\geq\overline{v}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}):=\sup_{\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}:\ \lVert\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{2}/2}\hat{v}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}).

(70)

Lemma 3.3

(Elementwise activity of the HS constraint at optimality)
.

There exists an optimal solution of (
57
) such that, for every element
i
,
v_{i}^{+}=\overline{v}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})=\inf\{v\in[0,1]:\ \bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(v)\}
.

Proof.

Let
(c_{j}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star},v_{i}^{+\star})
be any optimal solution. By monotonicity (Proposition
A.2
) and continuity (Proposition
A.1
), for each
i
we can decrease
v_{i}^{+\star}
down to

\widetilde{v}_{i}^{+}:=\inf\{v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]:\ \langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\leq f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+}),\forall\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\}=\overline{v}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}),

(71)

without violating (
57d
). This operation leaves
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}
unchanged, so all matrices
\mathsf{K}_{j}(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\})
and the objective
\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}c_{j}
remain the same. It only reduces the left-hand side of the volume constraint (
57e
), so feasibility is preserved. Hence,
\left(c_{j}^{\star},\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star},\widetilde{v}_{i}^{+}\right)
is also optimal, with each
\widetilde{v}_{i}^{+}
equal to the minimal admissible value for fixed
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\star}
.
∎

In particular, activity here is with respect to the design variable
v_{i}^{+}
for fixed
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
:
v_{i}^{+}
is reduced to its minimal admissible value, not interpreted as equality in (
55
) for every strain.
It remains to compute
\hat{v}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
explicitly. The following result provides closed-form expressions by solving the equality
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
for each branch of (
60
).

Proposition 3.5

(Closed-form minimal admissible volume for HS activity)
.

For any fixed
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\in\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}:\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\}
and strain
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}
with the Frobenius norm
\sqrt{2}/2
, the minimal
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
such that the Hashin–Shtrikman upper bound (
55
) with
q(t;v_{i}^{+})
defined in (
60
) is active, i.e.,
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
, is given by

\hat{v}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\hat{v}_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}):=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle(\Delta\mu+\Delta\kappa)+(2t^{2}-1)(\Delta\kappa\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu\kappa^{+})-\sqrt{D}}{2\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa(t+\sqrt{1-t^{2}})^{2}}\\
&\qquad\text{if }\hat{v}_{1}\leq\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{t+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\min\left\{\frac{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}{\Delta\kappa},\frac{t}{\Delta\mu}\right\}\end{aligned}\\
\begin{aligned} \hat{v}_{2}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}):=\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{\Delta\kappa}\left[1-\frac{t^{2}(\kappa^{-}+\mu^{+})}{\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-\mu^{+}(1-2t^{2})}\right]\;\text{ if }\;\hat{v}_{2}>\frac{(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+})\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}{\Delta\kappa(t+\sqrt{1-t^{2}})}\end{aligned}\\
\begin{aligned} \hat{v}_{3}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}):=\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{\Delta\mu}\left[1-\frac{(1-t^{2})(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{-})}{\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle+\kappa^{+}(1-2t^{2})}\right]\;\text{ if }\;\hat{v}_{3}>\frac{(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+})t}{\Delta\mu(t+\sqrt{1-t^{2}})}\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(72)

with

\begin{multlined}D=\left[\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle(\Delta\mu+\Delta\kappa)+(1-2t^{2})(\Delta\mu\kappa^{+}-\Delta\kappa\mu^{+})+\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}+t)^{2}\right]^{2}\\
-4\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+})(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}+t)^{2}(\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle+(t^{2}-1)\mu^{-}-t^{2}\kappa^{-}).\end{multlined}

(73)

Proof.

Define, for
t\in[0,1]
,

v_{12}(t)=\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{\Delta\kappa}\frac{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}{t+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}},\quad v_{13}(t)=\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{\Delta\mu}\frac{t}{t+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}.

(74)

Then the branch conditions in (
60
) are equivalently
v_{i}^{+}\leq\min\{v_{12}(t),v_{13}(t)\}
in the first branch,
v_{i}^{+}>v_{12}(t)
in the second branch, and
v_{i}^{+}>v_{13}(t)
in the third branch.

For the first branch in (
60
), after unification to a common denominator and grouping powers of
v_{i}^{+}
, the Hashin–Shtrikman equality reads as

0=\frac{(v_{i}^{+})^{2}(-\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}+t)^{2})-[(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+})(\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle+\Delta\mu(1-t^{2})+\Delta\kappa t^{2}-\kappa^{+}t^{2}-\mu^{+}(1-t^{2}))]}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-(\Delta\kappa+\Delta\mu)v_{i}^{+}}\\
+\frac{v_{i}^{+}\left[\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle(\Delta\mu+\Delta\kappa)+(1-2t^{2})(\Delta\mu\kappa^{+}-\Delta\kappa\mu^{+})+\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}+t)^{2}\right]}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-(\Delta\kappa+\Delta\mu)v_{i}^{+}}.

(75)

Thus, we search for the roots of the quadratic polynomial in the numerator, which can be expressed as

\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle(\Delta\mu+\Delta\kappa)+(2t^{2}-1)(\Delta\kappa\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu\kappa^{+})\pm\sqrt{D}}{2\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa(t+\sqrt{1-t^{2}})^{2}}

(76)

with
D
in (
73
). Due to uniqueness of the activating volume fraction (Corollary
A.1
),
D\geq 0
but only one root can be admissible in
[0,1]
, so it remains to show which one. To this end, set
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}=\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}
. Then, (
76
) simplifies to

\frac{1}{2}+\frac{(t^{2}\Delta\kappa+(1-t^{2})\Delta\mu)(\kappa^{-}+\mu^{-})\pm[(t^{2}\Delta\kappa+(1-t^{2})\Delta\mu)(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+})-(\Delta\kappa t-\Delta\mu\sqrt{1-t^{2}})^{2}]}{2\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa(t+\sqrt{1-t^{2}})^{2}}=\\
=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} 0\end{aligned}\\
\begin{aligned} 1+\frac{(\Delta\mu(1-t^{2})+\Delta\kappa t^{2})(\kappa^{-}+\mu^{-})}{\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa(t+\sqrt{1-t^{2}})^{2}}.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(77)

Clearly, the “negative” root gives admissible
v_{i}^{+}=0
, whereas the “positive” root gives
v_{i}^{+}>1
. By strict monotonicity in
v_{i}^{+}
(Proposition
A.2
), the admissible root for any
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\in[\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-},\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}]
is, therefore, the “negative” root, i.e.,

\hat{v}_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle(\Delta\mu+\Delta\kappa)+(2t^{2}-1)(\Delta\kappa\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu\kappa^{+})-\sqrt{D}}{2\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa(t+\sqrt{1-t^{2}})^{2}}.

(78)

Consider now the second branch in (
60
). Rearranging the equality
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
gives

0=\frac{\Delta\kappa(\mu^{+}(2t^{2}-1)+\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle)v_{i}^{+}+(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+})(\kappa^{-}t^{2}+\mu^{+}(1-t^{2})-\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle)}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\kappa v_{i}^{+}}.

(79)

Equating the numerator gives the solution

\hat{v}_{2}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})=\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{\Delta\kappa}\left[1-\frac{t^{2}(\kappa^{-}+\mu^{+})}{\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-\mu^{+}(1-2t^{2})}\right].

(80)

For the third branch in (
60
), rearranging the equality
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
gives

0=\frac{\Delta\mu(\kappa^{+}(1-2t^{2})+\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle)v_{i}^{+}+(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+})(\kappa^{+}t^{2}+\mu^{-}(1-t^{2})-\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle)}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu v_{i}^{+}},

(81)

with the numerator providing

\hat{v}_{3}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})=\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{\Delta\mu}\left[1-\frac{(1-t^{2})(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{-})}{\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle+\kappa^{+}(1-2t^{2})}\right].

(82)

∎

By Lemma
3.3
, at optimality one may always take
v_{i}^{+}=\overline{v}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
, with
\overline{v}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
defined in (
70
) and
\hat{v}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
given in Proposition
3.5
. The free material optimization problem under Hashin–Shtrikman upper bounds (
57
) can, therefore, be written as

\displaystyle\min_{\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}}\;

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}c_{j}

(83a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}&-\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{j}&\mathsf{K}_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}\right)\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0,\quad j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{lc}},

(83b)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},\quad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}},

(83c)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sup_{\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}:\ \lVert\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{2}/2}\hat{v}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})\leq\overline{V}

3.4.4
Single-loadcase equivalence in the continuum setting

The nonconvexity of the Hashin–Shtrikman upper bounds in
(v_{i}^{+},\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
(Lemma
3.1
) carries over to the nonconvexity of (
57
) and (
83
). Moreover, due to the nature of the volume function in Proposition
3.5
, both these problems are nonsmooth (this can be seen by evaluating the derivatives at the branch boundaries, we refer to Proposition

in the appendix for explicit formulas). Nevertheless, for the special case of a single-loadcase (

Theorem 3.1

(Tightness of the HS/AK relaxation in the single-loadcase continuum setting)
.

Assume the standing continuum hypotheses of Section
3.1
(in particular, the regularity of
\Omega
, boundary conditions ensuring coercivity on
\mathcal{U}_{0}
, and a bounded linear load functional
\ell:=\ell_{1}
from (

\bm{\mathbf{0}}\prec\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\prec\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
, and a single-loadcase.
Define

\mathcal{V}_{\overline{V}}:=\left\{v^{+}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;[0,1])\;:\;\int_{\Omega}v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}\leq\overline{V}\,\lvert\Omega\rvert\right\}.

(84)

Let
c_{\mathrm{HS\text{-}FMO}}^{\mathrm{cont}}
be the infimum value of the continuum FMO problem (
15
) from Section
3.1
(with
n_{\mathrm{lc}}=1
), taken over all pairs
(v^{+},\bm{\mathbf{E}})\in\mathcal{V}_{\overline{V}}\times L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2\times 2\times 2})

\bm{\mathbf{E}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})\in\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(v^{+}(\bm{\mathit{x}}))
for a.e.
\bm{\mathit{x}}\in\Omega

\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
is given by (
56

c_{\mathrm{AK,pr}}^{\mathrm{cont}}:=\inf_{v^{+}\in\mathcal{V}_{\overline{V}}}\sup_{\bm{\mathit{u}}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}}\left\{2\,\ell(\bm{\mathit{u}})-\int_{\Omega}f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}(\bm{\mathit{u}});v^{+})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}\right\}.

(85)

(88)

c_{\mathrm{HS\text{-}FMO}}^{\mathrm{cont}}=c_{\mathrm{AK,pr}}^{\mathrm{cont}}=c_{\mathrm{AK,du}}^{\mathrm{cont}}=:c_{\mathrm{AK}}^{\mathrm{cont}}.

(89)

Moreover, the common value is attained in the relaxation sense by a sequence of orthotropic sequential laminates (of rank at most two) with vanishing microstructural length scale.

Proof.

,

\bm{\mathbf{E}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2\times 2\times 2})

Because

(93)

\sup_{\bm{\mathit{u}}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}}\left\{2\,\ell(\bm{\mathit{u}})-\int_{\Omega}f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}(\bm{\mathit{u}});v^{+})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}\right\}=\inf_{\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{ad}}}\int_{\Omega}f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathit{x}}.

(96)

Taking the infimum over

on both sides yields
c_{\mathrm{AK,pr}}^{\mathrm{cont}}=c_{\mathrm{AK,du}}^{\mathrm{cont}}
. Hence,
c_{\mathrm{HS\text{-}FMO}}^{\mathrm{cont}}=c_{\mathrm{AK,pr}}^{\mathrm{cont}}=c_{\mathrm{AK,du}}^{\mathrm{cont}}=c_{\mathrm{AK}}^{\mathrm{cont}}

∎

Theorem
3.1
is a continuum relaxation statement based on scale separation: the relaxed optimum is attained in the relaxation sense by sequences of sequential laminates whose characteristic length tends to zero. One should therefore not interpret Theorem
3.1
as an exact equivalence for a fixed finite element discretization of (
57
) or (
83
). Three issues are relevant.

First, while numerical stabilization (filters, perimeter or gradient penalties on design variables) is often necessary in practice
[
28
]
, it is not innocuous from the viewpoint of laminate realizability: the set of orthotropic sequential laminate tensors (at fixed
v^{+}
) is generally not convex in
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
(see Fig.
4
). Consequently, regularization mechanisms that enforce local averaging or smoothness of
\bm{\mathbf{E}}
may produce tensors that are no longer representable in this class, even if each unregularized pointwise optimum is.

Second, the Hashin–Shtrikman envelope
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v)
is tight in the sense that, for each fixed strain
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
, there exist sequential laminates attaining equality in (
55
). In a discretization with nonconstant strains inside an element (e.g., higher-order elements or multi-point quadrature), the elemental contribution involves multiple strain modes. Optimality then drives
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
against a superposition of strain directions. There is, in general, no guarantee that a single orthotropic sequential laminate simultaneously saturates all active bounds; thus laminate constructions designed for a single strain direction need not be exact discrete optimizers on a fixed mesh.

Last, Allaire’s continuum duality framework constructs laminates pointwise from the local macroscopic fields. In discrete implementations this is often approximated by designing laminates for an elementwise representative strain/stress (e.g., midpoint or average
[
8
]
). Such a replacement is theoretically justified precisely in the scale-separation regime where the macroscopic strain is (approximately) constant at the sampling scale; otherwise it should be viewed as an approximation rather than an exact characterization of the discrete HS-FMO optimum.

In summary, Theorem
3.1
identifies the correct continuum limit of the HS-FMO formulation. In the single-load, well-ordered isotropic case, the HS-relaxed problem is tight: it attains the same optimal value as the relaxed compliance minimization problem of
7
, and both optima are attained by sequential laminates. Nevertheless, in our formulation we do not restrict the feasible set of the elasticity tensors to sequential laminates or any other specific microstructure class
[
1
]
. Moreover, for a fixed finite element discretization, discrete equivalence with laminate-based constructions is expected only asymptotically, and it may be altered by numerical regularization. For multiple loadcases, the optimal solution of (
57
) or (
83
) is generally not tight and therefore provides only a lower bound on the optimal value of compliance minimization over all microstructures; tightness in the multi-loadcase setting would additionally require enforcing Hashin–Shtrikman upper bounds on the sum of elastic energies
[
6
,
2
,
9
]
.

3.5
Free orthotropic material optimization under Hashin–Shtrikman upper bound

In what follows, we consider the FMO problem under the Hashin–Shtrikman upper bounds (
83
), with the additional restriction that
\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}
are orthotropic. We refer to this setting as free-orthotropic material optimization (FOMO), and to its Hashin–Shtrikman-constrained version as HS-FOMO. Orthotropic materials possess three mutually orthogonal planes of symmetry, which in two dimensions implies that the stiffness tensor has four independent components. In a suitable coordinate system, the off-diagonal shear couplings vanish, i.e.,
E_{1112}=E_{2212}=0
.

To make the dependence on the material orientation explicit, for each orthotropic tensor
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
we denote by
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}}
its representation in a material (base) coordinate system in which
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})_{1112}=(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})_{2212}=0
. We parametrize its global orientation by an angle
\varphi_{i}\in[0,\pi)
and the corresponding planar rotation

\mathsf{Q}(\varphi):=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi&-\sin\varphi\\
\sin\varphi&\cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}.

(97)

The stiffness tensor
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
in global coordinates is then obtained by the standard tensor rotation rule

(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})_{ijkl}=\sum_{p,q,r,s=1}^{2}(\mathsf{Q}(\varphi_{i}))_{ip}(\mathsf{Q}(\varphi_{i}))_{jq}(\mathsf{Q}(\varphi_{i}))_{kr}(\mathsf{Q}(\varphi_{i}))_{ls}\,(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})_{pqrs}.

(98)

Since the Hashin–Shtrikman envelope
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v)
for isotropic phases is isotropic in
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
, and both
\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})
and
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rVert_{F}
are rotation-invariant, the minimal activating volume is invariant under simultaneous rotations of the microstructure and the macroscopic strain. In particular, with
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{b}}:=\mathsf{Q}(\varphi_{i})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\,\mathsf{Q}(\varphi_{i})
we have

\hat{v}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})=\hat{v}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{b}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}}).

(99)

Consequently, the worst-case volume fraction
\overline{v}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})=\sup_{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{2}/2}\hat{v}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
is independent of the orientation angle
\varphi_{i}
and depends on
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
only through its base coefficients.

As described in the previous section, for two-dimensional composites with well-ordered isotropic constituents, the Hashin–Shtrikman upper energy bound (
55
), with
q(t;v_{i}^{+})
defined in (
60
), is tight and admits an explicit form that splits into three regimes
[
5
]
: The first regime is attained by rank-one sequential laminates (which are orthotropic), while the other two are realized by rank-two sequential laminates with orthogonal layering directions, so the resulting effective tensors are again orthotropic. Hence, the bound (
55
) remains tight even when the admissible set of tensors
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
is restricted to the orthotropic symmetry class with free rotations of the material coordinate system.

Under this restriction, and using (
99
) to work in the base coordinate system of
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
, the minimal activating volume
\hat{v}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
in Proposition
3.5
can be simplified and depends on
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
only through
t
, as stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.6

(Trace-only reduction of the HS activating-volume map for orthotropy)

∎

By Propositions

and

\hat{v}(t;\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})
is univariate on

, obtained by composing

with the branchwise formulas in Proposition

, yielding the orthotropic FMO formulation

\displaystyle\min_{\{c_{j}\},\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}}\;

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}c_{j}

(106a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}&-\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{j}&\mathsf{K}_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}\right)\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0,\quad j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{lc}},

(106b)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},\quad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}},

(106c)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\hat{v}(t;\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})\leq\overline{V},

(106d)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\text{ orthotropic},\quad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}}.

(106e)

in which—for each

—the univariate supremum in (

Problem (

3.6
Sequential global programming for free-orthotropic material optimization

To solve problem (
106
), we apply the sequential global programming (SGP) approach, which was first suggested in
[
45
]
and later applied in different contexts, see, e.g.
[
40
,
51
]
. The SGP algorithm is an iterative method in which, in each iteration, a first-order model is established that is separable with respect to the anisotropic material tensors
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i},i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{e}}
. To explain this in more detail, we first rewrite problem (
106
) in the following form:

\displaystyle\min_{\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}}\;

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}c_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}\right)

(107a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},\quad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}},

(107b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\hat{v}(t;\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})=\overline{V},

(107c)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\text{ orthotropic},\quad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}},

(107d)

where
c_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}\right)=\mathsf{f}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{K}_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}\right)^{-1}\mathsf{f}_{j}
. We note that we have expressed the volume constraint as an equality constraint, as the volume is fully utilized at an optimal solution (recall Lemma
3.3
). Since constraints (
107b
) and (
107d
) are formulated element-wise and the volume constraint (
107c
) is separable with respect to
\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}
, it remains to approximate the cost function (
107a
) by a separable first-order model. Following
[
51
]
, we use a variant of the separable model introduced in
[
47
]
for FMO problems. To do so, we denote by
\{\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}}\}
the corresponding Kelvin–Mandel matrices and, at the expansion point
\{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}}\}
, we define

\mathsf{G}_{i}^{j}:=\frac{\partial c_{j}\left(\{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}}\}\right)}{\partial\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}}}\in\mathbb{S}^{3\times 3}.

(108)

We then approximate the compliance for the loadcase
j
as

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\};\{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}}\}\right)

\displaystyle=C+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\left\langle(\overline{\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}}}-\mathsf{L}_{i}^{j})\mathsf{G}_{i}^{j}(\overline{\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}}}-\mathsf{L}_{i}^{j}),(\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}}-\mathsf{L}_{i}^{j})^{-1}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{S}^{3\times 3}},

(109)

where the constant
C
is chosen such that the function value of
\hat{c}_{j}
coincides with that of
c_{j}
at the expansion point
\{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}}\}
and
\{\mathsf{L}_{i}^{j}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{e}}}^{j=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{lc}}}
are generalized asymptotes, which are chosen to be zero matrices in the sequel. Using a Lagrangian approach, we obtain the model problem

\min_{\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\text{ orth.}\}}\max_{\lambda}\;\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}\hat{c}_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\};\{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}}\}\right)+\lambda\left(\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\hat{v}(t;\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})-\overline{V}\right).

(110)

We note that due to the orthotropy and volume constraints, (
110
) is a nonconvex optimization problem. Nevertheless, after switching the order of
\min
and
\max
, we obtain the Lagrangian dual problem:

\max_{\lambda}\;\psi(\lambda),

(111)

where the function value of the dual function
\psi
for a given
\lambda
is computed by solving

\displaystyle\min_{\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}}\;

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}\hat{c}_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\};\{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}}\}\right)+\lambda\left(\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\hat{v}(t;\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})-\overline{V}\right)

(112a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},\quad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}},

(112b)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\text{ orthotropic},\quad i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}},

(112c)

to global optimality. From the saddle point theory we see that, if we find a pair
(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{*}\},\lambda^{*})
, which is a saddle point for the Lagrangian

L(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\},\lambda)=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}\hat{c}_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\};\{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}}\}\right)+\lambda\left(\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\hat{v}(t;\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})-\overline{V}\right),

then this pair is also optimal for (
112
). Obviously this is the case, if for a given
\lambda^{*}
, the corresponding (globally optimal) solution
(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}(\lambda^{*})\})
is feasible with respect to the volume constraint (stated as equality constraint), which can be easily checked in practice. Thus, we use the following strategy: as in the classical optimality criteria method, we solve the dual problem (
111
) using a bisection strategy, where we aim to solve (
112
) to global minimality in each iteration. Rather than solving (
112
) at once, thanks to separability, we can solve for each
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
individually, i. e., we solve
n_{\mathrm{e}}
problems of the form:

\min_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+},\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\text{ orth.}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}\left\langle\overline{\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}}}\mathsf{G}_{i}^{j}\overline{\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}}},(\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}})^{-1}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{S}^{3\times 3}}+\frac{\lambda}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\hat{v}(t;\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}}).

(113)

We note that we skipped the constant
C-\lambda V
in the objective here and set all asymptotes to zero.

In order to treat the orthotropy constraint, we parametrize each element tensor
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}
by its base coefficients and a rotation angle. In Kelvin–Mandel form, the base orientation reads

\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M,b}}:=\begin{pmatrix}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})_{1111}&(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})_{1122}&0\\
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})_{1122}&(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})_{2222}&0\\
0&0&2(\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})_{1212}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}}=\mathsf{R}(\varphi_{i})\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M,b}}\mathsf{R}(\varphi_{i})^{\mathsf{T}},

(114)

with
\varphi_{i}\in[0,\pi)
. The matrix

is the Kelvin–Mandel rotation induced by

in (

\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}

.

Since
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}
and
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
are isotropic, the Loewner bounds are rotation invariant and can be imposed directly on the base matrix:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})_{1111}&(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})_{1122}\\
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})_{1122}&(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})_{2222}\end{pmatrix}\preceq\begin{pmatrix}(\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M,b}})_{11}&(\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M,b}})_{12}\\

\displaystyle\preceq\begin{pmatrix}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})_{1111}&(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})_{1122}\\
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})_{1122}&(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})_{2222}\end{pmatrix},

(115a)

\displaystyle 2(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})_{1212}\leq(\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M,b}})_{33}

\displaystyle\leq 2(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})_{1212}.

(115b)

With this parametrization, (

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M,b}},\,\varphi_{i}\\
\text{s.t.\ }\eqref{eq:ortho_param_voigt}\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}\left\langle\overline{\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}}}\mathsf{G}_{i}^{j}\overline{\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}}},(\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}})^{-1}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{S}^{3\times 3}}+\frac{\lambda}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\hat{v}(t;\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}}),

(116)

where
\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M}}
depends on
(\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{M,b}},\varphi_{i})
through (

Problem (

As no global optimization solvers are available, which could treat problems with this structure directly, we have implemented a sampling based solution approach. To do so, we discretized the interval
[0,\pi/2]
equidistantly by
9001
angles. The resulting discrete set of angles is denoted by
\Phi^{\mathrm{G}}
. Similarly, the cuboid

\Theta^{\mathrm{G}}
. Finally, for each grid point
(E^{\mathrm{G}}_{11},E^{\mathrm{G}}_{22},E^{\mathrm{G}}_{33})_{i}\in\Theta^{\mathrm{G}}
we compute the interval
I_{i}
, which contains all choices
E_{12}^{\mathrm{G}}
such that

\begin{pmatrix}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})_{1111}&(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})_{1122}^{-}\\
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})_{1122}^{-}&(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})_{2222}^{-}\end{pmatrix}\preceq\begin{pmatrix}E^{\mathrm{G}}_{11}&E_{12}^{\mathrm{G}}\\
E_{12}^{\mathrm{G}}&E^{\mathrm{G}}_{22}\end{pmatrix}\preceq\begin{pmatrix}(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})_{1111}&(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})_{1122}^{+}\\
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})_{1122}^{+}&(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})_{2222}^{+}\end{pmatrix}.

Then, if

101

\mathcal{X}_{i}

However, taking into account that the 5D-grid has about 100 billion points, this strategy would not be tractable. Instead, we use a hierarchical grid approach, which only needs about 10 million function evaluations. Moreover, we would like to emphasize that we pre-compute the inverse tensors as well as estimated volume in formula (

) offline for each grid point.

Remark 3.2
.

1.

We have tested the resolution of our sampling set
by projecting a solution we obtained from the algorithm described in Section
3.7
onto our sampling grid. The loss in the objective function value we observed was typically very small.

2.

We cannot guarantee that we find the best point in the grid using our hierarchical grid approach in general. However, in our experiments we observed that the function value for the points we find were either optimal (on the full grid) or at least correct up to at least 5 digits.

3.

Without globalization, the iterative solution strategy we described above for the solution of problem (
107
) is not guaranteed to converge. A remedy is to add a line-search step after each solution of a sub problem of type (
110
). However our experiments revealed that for the compliance minimization problems we solve in this article no globalization is needed. Thus, we omit the description here.

3.7
Single-loadcase via alternating minimization and sequential laminates

After introducing the bound-based HS-FOMO models and the SGP solver, we now present the single-loadcase laminate-based alternating minimization baseline used in Section
4
. Unlike the primal strain-energy formulation used above, this method updates material through the dual Hashin–Shtrikman complementary-energy envelope using explicit continuum minimizers of the local material subproblem; in 2D and single-loadcase, these minimizers are orthotropic sequential laminates of rank one or two. The complementary-energy envelope is dual to the primal HS envelope, so this viewpoint is consistent with the continuum tightness statement in Theorem
3.1
. For completeness, we recall only the formulas needed for implementation; supporting derivations are deferred to Appendix
B
.

Definition 3.1

(Optimal lower bound on complementary energy
[
8
, Definition 2.3.15]
)
.

Let
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\prec\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
be two well-ordered isotropic elasticity tensors and let
v^{+}\in[0,1]
be the volume fraction of
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
. Denote by
G_{v^{+}}
the set of all effective elasticity tensors
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{*}
obtainable by homogenization of the two phases
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-},\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})
with volume fraction
v^{+}
.
For all
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}
, the HS lower bound of complementary energy holds:

\langle{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{*}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\rangle\geq f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})
, where

f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+}):=\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\rangle+(1-v^{+})\max_{\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}}\left\{2\langle\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-\left\langle\left((\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})^{-1}-(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{-1}\right)^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle-v^{+}\,g^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\right\}

(117)

Here,
g^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})
is the nonlocal term

g^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}):=\max_{\lVert\bm{\mathit{e}}\rVert=1}\langle\bm{\mathbf{F}}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}}(\bm{\mathit{e}})\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
. For each lamination direction
\lVert\bm{\mathit{e}}\rVert=1
, the tensor
\bm{\mathbf{F}}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}}(\bm{\mathit{e}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d\times d}
satisfies

\langle\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathit{e}})\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-\frac{1}{\mu^{+}}\lVert(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\bm{\mathit{e}}\rVert^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\mu^{+}}-\frac{1}{\kappa^{+}+2\mu^{+}(d-1)/d}\right)\left\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\bm{\mathit{e}},\,\bm{\mathit{e}}\right\rangle^{2}.

(118)

The quantity
f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})
is the local envelope minimized in the material step of alternating minimization. For two-dimensional mixtures of well-ordered isotropic phases, this lower bound is tight in the relaxation sense, and equality is attained by finite-rank laminates (cf.
[
8
, Proposition 2.3.25]
and
[
19
, Theorem 2]
). We now state the explicit branch-wise formula used in the algorithm.

D_{v}f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v)
follows from basic perturbation theory for optimization problems.
∎

Accordingly, each local update is computed by bisection on the one-dimensional optimality condition

D_{v}f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v)=-\lambda
.
Algorithm
1
is a descent method: compliance decreases along iterations, so the objective values converge. There is no guarantee of convergence to a global minimizer, but
3
reported low sensitivity to initialization and similar final compliance values.

4
Results

Building on the theoretical developments of the previous sections, we present numerical examples that illustrate the hierarchy of bounds and evaluate the performance of the sequential global programming algorithm for free-orthotropic material optimization. We compare these results for ZO-FMO (Section
3.2
), V-FMO (Section
3.3
), and finite-rank laminates computed by alternating minimization (Section
3.7
).

In all examples, we use dimensionless material parameters with strong phase Young’s modulus
E=1
and Poisson’s ratio
\nu=0.3
. The weak phase has the same Poisson ratio and a smaller Young modulus (
10^{-2}
,
10^{-3}
or
10^{-6}
). The volume constraint is fixed at
\overline{V}=0.2
. The finite
-
element discretization is based on Q
8
serendipity elements. Since the elasticity tensors are piecewise constant at the element level and the element kinematics are sufficiently rich, no additional regularization is applied, in line with the observation of
28
.

For ZO-FMO and V-FMO, the resulting linear semidefinite programs (
32
) and (
54
) are solved using the Mosek optimizer with default settings, and we implement elementwise arrow decomposition of the compliance constraints for improved scalability
[
33
]
.

For the sequential global programming algorithm we stop if the relative change in the merit function

\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{lc}}}c_{j}\left(\{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\}\right)+\lambda\left(\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\hat{v}(t;\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathrm{b}})-\overline{V}\right)

(126)

is below
10^{-7}
or there is no progress after five consecutive iterations. We note that the second term is not automatically zero after every iteration. Due to the finite-resolution approximation of the feasible set used in the SGP approach, the precision of the volume constraint retrieved from the bisection strategy is typically on the order of
10^{-6}
. This is the reason why we monitor the merit function (
126
) rather than pure compliance. Moreover, the same reason may lead to minimal under- and over-shooting close to the solution. To avoid cycling, we thus include the additional improvement test.

In the alternating

minimization scheme of
3

-

[
28
]

10^{-5}
and (ii) the relative compliance change is below
10^{-8}

\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}

5
Summary

This paper considers the two-dimensional plane-stress setting with well-ordered isotropic weak and strong phases. In this setting, we establish a clear hierarchy of free material optimization models based on increasingly tight energy bounds, from zeroth-order to Voigt and then to Hashin–Shtrikman. The continuum problem (
15
) is used as a theoretical reference that organizes this hierarchy through (
13
) and supports existence statements. Within the convex formulations considered here, the Voigt model is the strongest relaxation. It is strictly tighter than the zeroth-order model for
0<\overline{V}<1
(Proposition
3.2
) and coincides with it at
\overline{V}\in\{0,1\}
. It also admits an optimal isotropic representation, which reduces the problem to variable-thickness-sheet optimization with one scalar variable per element (Proposition
3.3
and (
54
)). In addition, for the single-loadcase continuum zeroth-order model, at least one optimal solution can be chosen orthotropic (Proposition
3.1
). For continuum well-posedness, minimizers exist directly for the zeroth-order and Voigt models, while for Hashin–Shtrikman bounds we obtain existence in the
H
-relaxed sense (Section
3.1
).

For the Hashin–Shtrikman model, the results clarify both its strength and its algorithmic implications. The HS correction is nonnegative and vanishes at endpoints (Proposition
3.4
). For every interior volume fraction
v_{i}^{+}\in(0,1)
, there exist strains for which this correction is strictly positive (while equality can still occur for a specific invariant relation), giving strict interior improvement over Voigt and leading to nonconvexity of the feasible set in
(v_{i}^{+},\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i})
(Lemma
3.1
). At the same time, its convex hull is exactly the Voigt feasible set (Lemma
3.2
), which explains why no tighter convex alternative is available in the same setting. Geometric visualizations of the admissible sets (Figs.
1
,
2
, and
3
) make these relations directly visible. Separately, we show that the local HS volume variables can be eliminated through active-constraint characterization and explicit activating-volume formulas (Lemma
3.3
, Proposition
3.5
), giving the reduced model (
83
). A key by-product is an explicit volume estimator that, for a given effective elasticity tensor, quantifies the minimum stiff-phase fraction required for realizability through a worst-case strain evaluation. For orthotropic tensors this further reduces to a one-parameter search in
t=\lvert\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rvert
(Proposition
3.6
and (
106
)).

To connect the discrete HS-FMO model with classical homogenization theory, we additionally analyze the single-loadcase continuum limit: in that setting, the HS-FMO relaxation is tight and has the same optimal value as the Allaire–Kohn relaxed formulation (Theorem
3.1
). The corresponding laminate-construction viewpoint is illustrated in Fig.
4
. The tightness statement should be interpreted as a continuum relaxation result, not as exact equivalence on a fixed finite-element mesh. For multiple loadcases, HS-FOMO is generally not tight and should be interpreted as a lower bound unless additional coupled energy bounds are enforced.

Computationally, the nonconvex orthotropic HS problem is handled with sequential global programming (Section
3.6
). Numerically, both benchmarks confirm the predicted ordering
c^{\mathrm{ZO\text{-}FMO}}<c^{\mathrm{V\text{-}FMO}}<c^{\mathrm{HS\text{-}FOMO}}
(Tables
1
and
2
). In the cantilever test, HS-FOMO remains close to finite-rank laminate solutions (compliance gap about
0.78
–
1.27\%
), while in the multi-loadcase test the ordering
c^{\mathrm{ZO\text{-}FMO}}<c^{\mathrm{V\text{-}FMO}}<c^{\mathrm{HS\text{-}FOMO}}
is preserved across phase contrasts and mesh refinements, with stable SGP empirical convergence behavior (Figs.
9
and
17
).

Natural extensions of this work include several directions. A first step is to build inner approximations of the realizability set by designing connectable microstructures via inverse homogenization
[
46
]
and by sampling the energy-bound feasible set. A second direction is to move beyond compliance minimization and consider other objectives and constraints within the same free material framework
[
49
]
. A third direction is to further analyze the sequential global programming approach from both computational and theoretical viewpoints, in particular with respect to numerical efficiency, scalability, and convergence behavior. Finally, on the modeling side, it is important to investigate tighter energy bounds, broader material symmetry classes, and fully three-dimensional formulations
[
21
]
.

Appendix A
Properties of the Hashin–Shtrikman upper bound function

This section collects regularity properties of the one-dimensional map

v_{i}^{+}\mapsto f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
(for fixed
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
) that are used in the main text when eliminating local volume fractions and characterizing activating volumes. We first prove continuity, then monotonicity, and finally derive existence and uniqueness of the activating volume fraction solving

\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
.

Proposition A.1

(Continuity of the HS upper-bound function in
v_{i}^{+}
)
.

For any fixed strain
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}
with the Frobenius norm
\sqrt{2}/2
and
t=\lvert\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rvert
, the Hashin–Shtrikman upper bound function
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(v_{i}^{+})=\langle[(1-v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}]\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-q(t;v_{i}^{+})
with
q(t;v_{i}^{+})
defined in (
60
) is continuous in the volume fraction
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

The Voigt part is affine in
v_{i}^{+}
, hence continuous. Thus, it suffices to show that
q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
is continuous.

For the denominators in (
60
), set

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-(\Delta\kappa+\Delta\mu)v_{i}^{+},

(128a)

\displaystyle d_{2}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\kappa v_{i}^{+},

(128b)

\displaystyle d_{3}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}-\Delta\mu v_{i}^{+}.

(128c)

Because
\Delta\kappa=\kappa^{+}-\kappa^{-}>0
and
\Delta\mu=\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}>0
with
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
, we have
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}>0
. Hence, the branch formulas are rational with strictly positive denominators, so
q(t;v_{i}^{+})
is continuous in
v_{i}^{+}
on each branch interior. It remains to match the boundary values.

Define the branch-separation sets

\displaystyle\Gamma_{12}:\;

\displaystyle v_{i}^{+}\Delta\kappa t=d_{2}\sqrt{1-t^{2}},

(129a)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{13}:\;

\displaystyle v_{i}^{+}\Delta\mu\sqrt{1-t^{2}}=d_{3}t,

(129b)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{23}:\;

\displaystyle\frac{d_{3}t}{v_{i}^{+}\Delta\mu}<\sqrt{1-t^{2}}<\frac{v_{i}^{+}\Delta\kappa t}{d_{2}}.

(129c)

On
\Gamma_{12}
, substituting
v_{i}^{+}\Delta\kappa\,t=d_{2}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
into the formulas for
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
from (
60
) gives

q_{1}(t;v_{i}^{+})=q_{2}(t;v_{i}^{+})=\frac{\Delta\kappa^{2}v_{i}^{+}(1-v_{i}^{+})t^{2}d_{1}}{d_{2}^{2}}\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{12}.

(130)

On
\Gamma_{13}
, substituting
v_{i}^{+}\Delta\mu\sqrt{1-t^{2}}=d_{3}\,t
into
q_{1}
and
q_{3}
yields

q_{1}(t;v_{i}^{+})=q_{3}(t;v_{i}^{+})=\frac{\Delta\mu^{2}v_{i}^{+}(1-v_{i}^{+})(1-t^{2})d_{1}}{d_{3}^{2}}\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{13}.

(131)

Finally, let (
129c
) hold. Multiplying the strict inequalities gives
td_{2}d_{3}<(v_{i}^{+})^{2}\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa t
, which is equivalent to

t(d_{1}+v_{i}^{+}\Delta\kappa)(d_{1}+v_{i}^{+}\Delta\mu)<(v_{i}^{+})^{2}t\Delta\kappa\Delta\mu.

(132)

However, this cannot hold for any
v_{i}^{+}\in(0,1)
since
d_{1}>0
. Thus,
\Gamma_{23}=\emptyset
.

At
v_{i}^{+}=0
we have
q(t;0)=0
(the first branch applies), and at
v_{i}^{+}=1
each branch carries a factor
(1-v_{i}^{+})
, so
q(t;1)=0
. Moreover, using the explicit boundary expressions above and
v_{i}^{+}(1-v_{i}^{+})\to 0
as
v_{i}^{+}\to 0^{+}
or
1^{-}
,we see
\lim_{v_{i}^{+}\to 0^{+}}q(t;v_{i}^{+})=q(t;0)
and
\lim_{v_{i}^{+}\to 1^{-}}q(t;v_{i}^{+})=q(t;1)
. Therefore,
q
is continuous on
[0,1]
, and so is
f^{\mathrm{HS}}
.
∎

With continuity established, we next prove monotonicity with respect to
v_{i}^{+}
.

Proposition A.2

(Monotonicity of the HS upper-bound function in
v_{i}^{+}
)
.

For any fixed strain
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}
with the Frobenius norm
\sqrt{2}/2
, the Hashin–Shtrikman upper bound function
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(v_{i}^{+})=\langle[(1-v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}]\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-q(t;v_{i}^{+})
with
q(t;v_{i}^{+})
defined in (
60
) is nondecreasing in the volume fraction
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

By Proposition
A.1
,
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(v_{i}^{+})
is continuous in
v_{i}^{+}
. It, therefore, suffices to show
\partial f^{\mathrm{HS}}(v_{i}^{+})/\partial v_{i}^{+}\geq 0
on the interior of each branch and then pass to the boundary by continuity.

Write the Voigt term as

\langle[(1-v_{i}^{+})\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}]\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=(\kappa^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\Delta\kappa)t^{2}+(\mu^{-}+v_{i}^{+}\Delta\mu)(1-t^{2}),

(133)

whose derivative in
v_{i}^{+}
is
\Delta\kappa t^{2}+\Delta\mu(1-t^{2})\geq 0
.

For the first branch in (
60
), let
\eta_{1}=d_{2}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}-v_{i}^{+}t\Delta\kappa
,
\eta_{2}=d_{3}t-v_{i}^{+}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\Delta\mu
. A direct computation yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f^{\mathrm{HS}}_{1}(v_{i}^{+})}{\partial v_{i}^{+}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta\mu\eta_{1}^{2}+\Delta\kappa\eta_{2}^{2}-\frac{(\Delta\kappa\eta_{2}-\Delta\mu\eta_{1})^{2}}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}}{d_{1}^{2}}.

(134)

Using the elementary identity

(\Delta\kappa+\Delta\mu)(\Delta\kappa\eta_{2}^{2}+\Delta\mu\eta_{1}^{2})-(\Delta\kappa\eta_{2}-\Delta\mu\eta_{1})^{2}=\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2})^{2}\geq 0.

(135)

and since
\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}>\kappa^{-}+\mu^{-}>0
, we may write

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f^{\mathrm{HS}}_{1}(v_{i}^{+})}{\partial v_{i}^{+}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\Delta\kappa\eta_{2}^{2}+\Delta\mu\eta_{1}^{2})(\kappa^{-}+\mu^{-})+\Delta\mu\Delta\kappa(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2})^{2}}{d_{1}^{2}(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+})}\geq 0.

(136)

For the second and third branch in (
60
), differentiation gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f^{\mathrm{HS}}_{2}(v_{i}^{+})}{\partial v_{i}^{+}}

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{2}(\kappa^{-}+\mu^{+})(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+})\Delta\kappa}{d_{2}^{2}}>0,

(137a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f^{\mathrm{HS}}_{3}(v_{i}^{+})}{\partial v_{i}^{+}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-t^{2})(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{-})(\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+})\Delta\mu}{d_{3}^{2}}>0,

(137b)

with the strict inequality due to the branch conditions. Since the three expressions are nonnegative on the interiors and the branch values of
q
agree on
\Gamma_{12}\cup\Gamma_{13}
(Proposition
A.1
), continuity implies that
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(v_{i}^{+})
is globally nondecreasing in
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
.
∎

Combining endpoint values with continuity and monotonicity gives the following existence and uniqueness consequence.

Corollary A.1

(Existence and uniqueness of the activating HS volume fraction)
.

For any fixed strain
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}
with the Frobenius norm
\sqrt{2}/2
and elasticity tensor
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2\times 2\times 2}
with
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
, there exists a unique volume fraction
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
such that
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
.

Proof.

Since
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
, we have
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\leq\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle\leq\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
. Moreover, by the endpoint evaluation in Proposition
3.4
,
q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};0)=q(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};1)=0
. Hence,
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};0)=\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
,
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};1)=\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
, and
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\cdot)
is continuous on
[0,1]
by Proposition
A.1
. Therefore, the intermediate value theorem gives a (possibly nonunique)
v_{i}^{+}\in[0,1]
with
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
.

By Proposition
A.2
,
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\cdot)
is nondecreasing on
[0,1]
. We now show it is in fact strictly increasing, which implies uniqueness. On each branch interior, the derivative
\partial f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})/\partial v_{i}^{+}
is nonnegative, and in the second and third branches the derivatives are strictly positive (cf. Eq. (
137
)). In the first branch, the derivative is nonnegative and vanishes only at points where
\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}=0
, which occurs exactly on the branch boundaries
\Gamma_{12}\cup\Gamma_{13}
(see the proof of Proposition
A.2
). Thus,
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\cdot)
cannot be flat on any nontrivial interval contained in a branch interior. At the boundaries, adjacent-branch formulas coincide, and
\Gamma_{23}=\emptyset
. Hence, any interval
[v_{1},v_{2}]\subset[0,1]
decomposes into finitely many subintervals lying in branch interiors, separated by isolated boundary points. Integrating the (strictly) positive derivatives over each interior subinterval shows
v_{2}>v_{1}\Rightarrow f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{2})>f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{1})
. Therefore,
f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};\cdot)
is strictly increasing on
[0,1]
, and the solution to
\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=f^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}};v_{i}^{+})
is unique.
∎

Appendix B
Regularity of the Hashin–Shtrikman lower complementary-energy bound

Following
19
, the cell-wise material update contains the one-dimensional volume-fraction subproblem

\text{find }v^{+}\in\argmin_{v\in[0,1]}f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v)+\lambda v.

(138)

In this section, for fixed
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}
, we establish regularity properties of the map
v\mapsto f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v)
. These properties justify an efficient and globally optimal solution strategy for this one-dimensional minimization problem.

Definition B.1

(Optimal lower bound on complementary energy
[
8
, Definition 2.3.15]
)
.

Let
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
be two well-ordered isotropic elasticity tensors and let
v^{+}\in[0,1]
be the volume fraction of
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
. Denote by
G_{v^{+}}
the set of all effective elasticity tensors
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{*}
obtainable by homogenization of the two phases
(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-},\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})
with volume fraction
v^{+}
.

A function
f(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})
is called a (complementary-energy) lower bound if, for every
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
and every
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{*}\in G_{v^{+}}
,

\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{*})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\rangle\;\geq\;f(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+}).

The lower bound is optimal if, for every
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
, there exists
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}})\in G_{v^{+}}
such that equality holds, i.e.,

\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}))^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\rangle\;=\;f(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+}).

The Hashin–Shtrikman lower complementary-energy bound is optimal. Let
\kappa^{+}
and
\mu^{+}
denote the bulk and shear moduli of
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
, and let
\bm{\mathit{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy
\lVert\bm{\mathit{e}}\rVert=1
. Define
\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathit{e}})\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d\times d\times d}
by, for all
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
,

\langle\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathit{e}})\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=\langle\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-\frac{1}{\mu^{+}}\lVert(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\bm{\mathit{e}}\rVert^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\mu^{+}}-\frac{1}{\kappa^{+}+2\mu^{+}(d-1)/d}\right)\left\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\bm{\mathit{e}},\,\bm{\mathit{e}}\right\rangle^{2}.

(139)

The tensor
\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathit{e}})
admits a geometric interpretation. For a fixed unit direction
\bm{\mathit{e}}
, define the subspaces of
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
:

\displaystyle V(\bm{\mathit{e}})

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{span}\{\bm{\mathit{e}}\hat{\bm{\mathit{e}}}^{\mathsf{T}}+\hat{\bm{\mathit{e}}}{\bm{\mathit{e}}}^{\mathsf{T}};\hat{\bm{\mathit{e}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\},

(140)

\displaystyle W(\bm{\mathit{e}})

\displaystyle:=\{\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}:\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\bm{\mathit{e}}=\bm{\mathit{0}}\}.

(141)

Since
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
is positive definite, it admits a unique square root
\left({\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}=\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
, and
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
has the orthogonal decomposition

\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}=(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}V(\bm{\mathit{e}})\oplus(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{-\frac{1}{2}}W(\bm{\mathit{e}}).

Let
\pi_{\mathcal{W}}
be the projection onto a subspace
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
. Then,

\langle\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathit{e}})\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle=\left\lVert\pi_{(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{-\frac{1}{2}}W(\bm{\mathit{e}})}\left((\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\right)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\left\lVert(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-\left\lVert\pi_{(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}V(\bm{\mathit{e}})}\left((\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\right)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

Theorem B.1

(HS lower bound on complementary energy; optimality

[
8
, Proposition 2.3.25]
)
.

Assume two well-ordered isotropic phases
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-}\preceq\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
and let
v^{+}\in[0,1]
. Let
G_{v^{+}}
be the corresponding
G
-closure. Define, for
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
,

f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+}):=\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\rangle+(1-v^{+})\max_{\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}}\Big\{2\langle\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-\big\langle\big((\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})^{-1}-(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{-1}\big)^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\big\rangle-v^{+}\,g^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\Big\},

(142)

where
g^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}):=\max_{\lVert\bm{\mathit{e}}\rVert=1}\langle\bm{\mathbf{F}}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}}(\bm{\mathit{e}})\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
. Then,
f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}
is a (complementary-energy) lower bound in the sense of Definition
B.1
: for all
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
and all
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{*}\in G_{v^{+}}
,
\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{*})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\rangle\geq f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})
. Moreover, the bound is optimal: for every
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}
there exists
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}})\in G_{v^{+}}
generated by a finite-rank sequential laminate such that equality holds. In
d=2
, orthotropic rank-two laminates aligned with the principal stress directions are sufficient.

The nonlocal term
g^{\mathrm{c}}
admits a closed-form expression.

Lemma B.1

(
[
7
, Eq. (4.15)]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
be the two eigenvalues of
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}
, and let
\mu^{+}
and
\kappa^{+}
be the shear and bulk moduli of
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
. Then,

g^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})=\frac{4\kappa^{+}\mu^{+}}{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}\max\{\varepsilon_{1}^{2},\varepsilon_{2}^{2}\}.

(143)

Moreover, if
\bm{\mathit{e}}_{1},\bm{\mathit{e}}_{2}
are eigenvectors of
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
associated with the eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
, then
\max_{\lVert\bm{\mathit{e}}\rVert=1}\langle\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathit{e}})\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle
is attained by choosing
\bm{\mathit{e}}=\bm{\mathit{e}}_{i}
, where
i\in\{1,2\}
satisfies
\varepsilon_{i}=\max\{\varepsilon_{1}^{2},\varepsilon_{2}^{2}\}
.

The Fenchel conjugate of
g^{\mathrm{c}}
is explicitly known.

Lemma B.2

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
be the two eigenvalues of
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}
, let
v^{+}>0
, and let
\mu^{+}
and
\kappa^{+}
be the shear and bulk moduli of
\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
. Then,

\max_{\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}}\left\{2\langle\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-v^{+}g^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\right\}=\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{4\kappa^{+}\mu^{+}v^{+}}(\lvert\sigma_{1}\rvert+\lvert\sigma_{2}\rvert)^{2}.

(144)

Identity (
144
) can be interpreted as the convex (Fenchel) conjugate of the function
\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\mapsto v^{+}g^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})
, evaluated at
2\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}
. More precisely,

[v^{+}g^{\mathrm{c}}]^{*}(2\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}})=\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{4\kappa^{+}\mu^{+}v^{+}}(\lvert\sigma_{1}\rvert+\lvert\sigma_{2}\rvert)^{2}.

(145)

Moreover, for any convex function
\phi
and
v^{+}>0
, one has the scaling identity
(v^{+}\phi)^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})=v^{+}\phi^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}/v^{+})
. Applying this with
\phi=g^{\mathrm{c}}
gives

[v^{+}g^{\mathrm{c}}]^{*}(2\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})=v^{+}[g^{\mathrm{c}}]^{*}\!\left(\frac{2\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}}{v^{+}}\right).

(146)

Hence,
(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},v^{+})\mapsto[v^{+}g^{\mathrm{c}}]^{*}(2\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})
is the perspective of
[g^{\mathrm{c}}]^{*}
and is jointly convex in
(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},v^{+})
.

Define
\bm{\mathbf{R}}=\left((\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{-})^{-1}-(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{-1}\right)^{-1}
. By standard convex duality, we obtain:

\max_{\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}}\left\{2\langle\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rangle-\left\langle\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle-v^{+}\,g^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\right\}=\inf_{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}}\langle\bm{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}),(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})\rangle+[v^{+}g^{\mathrm{c}}]^{*}(2\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})

(147)

We now analyze the regularity of
v^{+}\mapsto f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})
for fixed
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\neq\bm{\mathbf{0}}
. Define

\displaystyle\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})

\displaystyle:=\langle\bm{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}),(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})\rangle+[v^{+}g^{\mathrm{c}}]^{*}(2\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})

(148)

\displaystyle\Psi(v^{+})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}}\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})

(149)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(v^{+})

\displaystyle:=\argmin_{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}}\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})

(150)

so that

f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})=\langle(\bm{\mathbf{E}}^{+})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}},\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}\rangle+(1-v^{+})\Psi(v^{+}).

(151)

By the explicit expression of
[v^{+}g^{\mathrm{c}}]^{*}(2\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})
(cf. (
144
)), for each fixed
\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}
the map
v^{+}\mapsto\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})
is differentiable on
(0,1]
, with

D_{v^{+}}\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})=-\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{4\kappa^{+}\mu^{+}(v^{+})^{2}}(\lvert\tau_{1}\rvert+\lvert\tau_{2}\rvert)^{2}.

(152)

In particular,
(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},v^{+})\mapsto D_{v^{+}}\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})
is continuous on
\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}\times(0,1]
. Hence, regularity of
f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})
reduces to regularity of the value function
\Psi
.

To apply the Danskin-type results of
15
to

\Psi(v^{+})=\inf_{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}}\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+}),

we need two ingredients: (i) inf-compactness, ensuring bounded minimizing sequences, and (ii) uniqueness of the minimizer for each fixed
v^{+}>0
. We prove these next.

Proposition B.1
.

For all
0<v^{+}_{0}\leq 1
,
\psi
has the inf-compactness property at
v^{+}_{0}
.

Proof.

It is enough to construct a compact set
C\subset\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}
, a constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and a neighborhood
V
of
v^{+}_{0}
such that, for every
v^{+}\in V
, the sublevel set
\{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}:\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})\leq\alpha\}
is contained in
C
.

Set
V=(\frac{v^{+}_{0}}{2},v^{+}_{0}+1)
. By the explicit expression of
[v^{+}g^{\mathrm{c}}]^{*}(2\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})
(cf. (
144
)), the map
v^{+}\mapsto[v^{+}g^{\mathrm{c}}]^{*}(2\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})
is decreasing on
(0,1]
for each fixed
\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}
, hence

\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})\leq\psi\left(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};\tfrac{v^{+}_{0}}{2}\right)

for all
v^{+}\in V\text{ and }\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}.

Now fix
\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}
and set
\alpha=\psi\left(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}_{0};\frac{v^{+}_{0}}{2}\right)
, so that the above sublevel set is nonempty. Let
r>0
be the smallest eigenvalue of
\bm{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}
and define

C:=\left\{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}:\ r\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{4\kappa^{+}\mu^{+}(v^{+}_{0}+1)}\left(\lvert\tau_{1}\rvert+\lvert\tau_{2}\rvert\right)^{2}\leq\alpha\right\}.

The set
C
is closed and bounded in finite dimension, hence compact. Finally, if
v^{+}\in V
and
\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})\leq\alpha
, then using
r\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq\langle\bm{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}),(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})\rangle
and
v^{+}\leq v^{+}_{0}+1
we obtain

r\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{4\kappa^{+}\mu^{+}(v^{+}_{0}+1)}\left(\lvert\tau_{1}\rvert+\lvert\tau_{2}\rvert\right)^{2}\\
\leq\langle\bm{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}),(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})\rangle+\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{4\kappa^{+}\mu^{+}v^{+}}\left(\lvert\tau_{1}\rvert+\lvert\tau_{2}\rvert\right)^{2}=\ \psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})\leq\alpha,

(153)

which implies
\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\in C
.
∎

The strong convexity of the quadratic term then gives uniqueness of the minimizer.

Proposition B.2
.

For all
0<v^{+}_{0}\leq 1
,
\mathcal{S}(v^{+}_{0})
is single valued.

Proof.

This follows from the strong convexity of
\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\mapsto\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+}_{0})
.
∎

With inf-compactness and uniqueness established, we can invoke Danskin’s theorem to differentiate
\Psi
with respect to
v^{+}
.

Theorem B.2
.

\Psi
is Fréchet differentiable in
(0,1]
.

Proof.

Fix
v^{+}_{0}>0
. We verify the assumptions of
15
:

1.

\forall\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2\times 2}
,
\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};\cdot)
is Gâteaux differentiable near
v^{+}_{0}
,

2.

(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},v^{+})\mapsto\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})
and
(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},v^{+})\mapsto D_{v^{+}}\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})
are continuous near
v^{+}_{0}
,

3.

\psi
has the inf-compactness property at
v^{+}_{0}

Since
\mathcal{S}(v^{+}_{0})
is single valued, Fréchet differentiability and the gradient formula follow (cf.
[
15
, Remark 4.14]
):

D_{v^{+}}\Psi(v^{+}_{0})=-\frac{\kappa^{+}+\mu^{+}}{4\kappa^{+}\mu^{+}(v^{+}_{0})^{2}}(\lvert\tau_{1}(v^{+}_{0})\rvert+\lvert\tau_{2}(v^{+}_{0})\rvert)^{2}.

(154)

∎

Consequently, for fixed
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}
, the map
v^{+}\mapsto f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})
is differentiable on
(0,1]
. Using (
151
),

D_{v^{+}}f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})=-\Psi(v^{+})+(1-v^{+})D_{v^{+}}\Psi(v^{+}).

(155)

We now prove convexity of
f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}
with respect to
v^{+}
.

Theorem B.3
.

The lower bound
f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}
is convex in
v^{+}
on
(0,1]
for fixed
\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}
.

Proof.

By (
151
), it suffices to prove that
v^{+}\mapsto(1-v^{+})\Psi(v^{+})
is convex on
(0,1]
. We first note that
\Psi
is nonincreasing on
(0,1]
: for each fixed
\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}
, the map
v^{+}\mapsto\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})
is nonincreasing (cf. the explicit expression (
144
)), and taking the infimum over
\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}
preserves monotonicity. Next,
\Psi
is convex as a partial minimization of a jointly convex function: indeed,

[v^{+}g^{\mathrm{c}}]^{*}(2\bm{\mathbf{\tau}})=v^{+}(g^{\mathrm{c}})^{*}\!\left(\frac{2\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}}{v^{+}}\right)
for
v^{+}>0
, which is the perspective of
(g^{\mathrm{c}})^{*}
and therefore jointly convex in
(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},v^{+})
; hence
\Psi(v^{+})=\inf_{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}}\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}};v^{+})
is convex.

Finally, let
v_{\theta}=\theta v_{1}+(1-\theta)v_{2}
with
v_{1},v_{2}\in(0,1]
and
\theta\in[0,1]
. By convexity of
\Psi
,
(1-v_{\theta})\Psi(v_{\theta})\leq(1-v_{\theta})\bigl(\theta\Psi(v_{1})+(1-\theta)\Psi(v_{2})\bigr)
. Since
\Psi
is nonincreasing, the latter is bounded above by
\theta(1-v_{1})\Psi(v_{1})+(1-\theta)(1-v_{2})\Psi(v_{2})
, because the difference equals
\theta(1-\theta)(v_{2}-v_{1})\bigl(\Psi(v_{1})-\Psi(v_{2})\bigr)\geq 0
.
∎

Since
v^{+}\mapsto f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})
is convex and differentiable on
(0,1]
, the elementwise update

v^{+}\in\argmin_{v\in[0,1]}\bigl(f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v)+\lambda v\bigr)

(156)

can be performed efficiently. Since
v^{+}\mapsto D_{v^{+}}f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})+\lambda
is nondecreasing, we use bisection: if
D_{v^{+}}f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};1)<-\lambda
, set
v^{+}=1
; otherwise, bracket a root of
D_{v^{+}}f_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HS}}(\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}};v^{+})=-\lambda
by dyadic steps (or set
v^{+}=0
at tolerance) and bisect on
[v^{+,1},v^{+,0}]
.
