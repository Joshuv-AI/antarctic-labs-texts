Use of cells and/or biologics combined with scaffolds for soft tissue regeneration around dental implants
Authors: Eduardo Montero, Zhaozhao Chen, Nerea Sánchez, Javier Sanz Esporrin, Hom‐Lay Wang, George A. Mandelaris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this systematic review is to evaluate the efficacy of cell‐based therapies and biologically enhanced scaffolds for peri‐implant soft tissue regeneration, including the increase in soft tissue thickness (STT) and/or keratinized mucosa (KM) augmentation.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in Ovid MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Dentistry and Oral Sciences Source until August 2025. A focused question was developed: “In patients with soft tissue defects requiring soft tissue augmentation procedures prior to, at the time of implant placement or after it, what is the efficacy of combining cell therapy and/or biological treatments with a soft tissue scaffold compared to the use a soft tissue scaffold without cells and/or biologics, or no scaffold, on the increase of STT and/or KM in clinical trials or prospective case series?”. Post‐operative morbidity, interproximal bone level changes, clinical peri‐implant outcomes or patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) were collected. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2.0, ROBINS‐I and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Study Quality Assessment Tool.ResultsTen publications were included (six randomized clinical trials [RCTs] and four prospective case series). The use of free gingival grafts (FGGs) lead to significantly greater KM augmentation when compared with platelet‐rich fibrin (PRF) membranes (weighted mean difference [WMD] = −2.0; 95% confidence interval [CI]: −3.3, −0.8; p < 0.001) although PRF membranes significantly increased the amount of KM when compared to no scaffold/graft (n = 2; WMD = 1.1 mm; 95% CI: 0.2, 2.1; p = 0.017) and seem to increase also STT. Available data on other biologics (e.g., recombinant human platelet‐derived growth factor BB [rhPDGF‐BB]) are scarce. No studies evaluating cell therapies were identified.ConclusionsPRF membranes are effective in increasing the KM and STT around dental implants, even if KM augmentation by means of autogenous grafts lead to superior outcomes. There is a lack of evidence on cell‐based therapies or other biologics for their use in soft tissue regeneration around dental implants.

INTRODUCTION

Soft tissue regeneration is a fundamental concern in dental implantology and periodontology, where peri‐implant tissues are crucial for long‐term success. Soft tissue deficiencies, can compromise peri‐implant tissue stability and aesthetics, leading to unfavorable clinical outcomes. 1 Improving soft tissue volume, thickness and/or quality through augmentation procedures has thus become essential in dental practice. Typical approaches to soft tissue augmentation have relied on autogenous grafts, such as connective tissue grafts and soft tissue substitutes like xenogeneic collagen matrices or acellular dermal matrices. 2 , 3 Although effective in increasing soft tissue volume and keratinized mucosa (KM), these methods have limitations. Autogenous grafts can lead to donor site morbidity, while the clinical outcomes of soft tissue substitutes can vary significantly. 4 Novel strategies to improve the outcomes of soft tissue regeneration and to reduce adverse events have been introduced recently, particularly by combining soft tissue scaffolds with biologics and cell‐based therapies, which may offer better healing and regenerative potential compared to the use of scaffolds alone.

Among the most studied approaches are tissue engineering strategies using cell therapy, where scaffolds are seeded with different cells to enhance their performance for soft tissue phenotype modification. Several split‐mouth randomized clinical trials (RCTs) observed keratinized tissue gain close to 2 mm when using a living construct containing either gingival fibroblasts and/or keratinocytes seeded into a collagen scaffold. 5 , 6 , 7 Also, promising results in terms of keratinized tissue gain have been observed when bioprinted acellular dermal matrix substitutes were seeded with gingival fibroblasts. 8 However, not all scaffolds show enhanced results, as other studies showed no significant increase in gingival keratinized tissue between cell‐containing and non‐cell containing scaffolds. 9

Studies assessing the combination of biologic agents such as autologous blood‐derived products (ABPs), enamel matrix derivatives (EMD), and recombinant human platelet‐derived growth factor BB (rhPDGF‐BB) seeded into scaffolds are lacking in the literature when soft tissue regeneration is the goal, as these biologics have commonly been studied together with connective tissue grafts, and mainly for root coverage purposes. 10

Despite these promising findings, the clinical efficacy of combining cells and/or biologics with scaffolds remains debated. Some studies suggest that the addition of these agents leads to significant improvements in soft tissue thickness (STT) and KM, 11 while others have reported limited or inconsistent results. 9 Furthermore, heterogeneity in study designs, follow‐up periods, and treatment protocols presents challenges in drawing definitive conclusions.

This systematic review aims to provide a clear understanding of the efficacy of cell‐based therapies and biologically enhanced scaffolds for peri‐implant soft tissue regeneration, including the increase in STT and/or KM augmentation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Protocol development and objectives

This systematic review follows the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) 12 and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The review protocol is registered in the International Prospective Register of Ongoing Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database under registration number CRD42024628175.

Focused question

“In patients with soft tissue defects requiring soft tissue augmentation procedures prior to, at the time of implant placement or after it (population), what is the efficacy of combining cell therapy and/or biological treatments with a soft tissue scaffold (either autologous grafts or soft tissue substitutes; intervention) as compared to the use a soft tissue scaffold without cells and/or biologics or no scaffold (comparison) on the increase of STT and/or KM (outcome) in RCTs, controlled clinical trials (CCTs) and prospective case series (study design)?”

Eligibility criteria

Population (P): Patients presenting soft tissue defects requiring soft augmentation procedures prior to, at the time of implant placement, or after it.

Intervention (I): Use of any type of cell therapy and/or biological treatment (e.g., ABPs, EMD, hyaluronic acid, etc.) combined with a soft tissue graft (either autologous grafts or soft tissue substitutes).

Comparison (C): Use of a soft tissue scaffold without cell therapy and/or biological agents, or no scaffold.

Outcomes (O): Primary Outcomes: Increase in STT (measured digitally or clinically) or KM.Secondary Outcomes: Aesthetic outcomes, volumetric gains, keratinized tissue width, marginal tissue recession, shrinkage of the autogenous graft/soft tissue substitute, patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs), and adverse effects.

Information sources and search

Electronic search and screening methods

Two independent examiners (Z.C. and G.M.) conducted the literature search using three databases (Ovid MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Dentistry and Oral Sciences Source) to identify publications up to August 2025. The search was limited to human subjects and to studies reported in English. Search strategies were developed for PubMed but revised appropriately for each database to account for differences in controlled vocabulary and syntax. The same reviewers selected full manuscripts of studies meeting the inclusion criteria, or those with insufficient data in the title and abstract to make a clear decision. Any disagreement was solved by discussion with a third reviewer (H.L.W.). Reasons for exclusion during full‐text screening were reported. The inter‐reviewer reliability (percentage of agreement and kappa correlation coefficient) of the full‐text analysis was calculated.

Manual search

All reference lists of the selected studies and previously published systematic reviews were checked for cross‐references.

The search was combined as population AND intervention and comparison AND outcomes.

In addition, a search for references in the included papers was performed. Finally, a hand search was carried out in implant related peer‐reviewed journals.

Study selection

The study selection process was conducted in two stages. In the first stage, two independent reviewers (Z.C. and G.M.) screened all titles and abstracts based on the eligibility criteria. If an abstract was selected, the full‐text article was obtained. When an abstract failed to provide sufficient information or was unavailable, the full text was also retrieved. Any disagreements between the reviewers were solved through discussion, and if necessary, a third reviewer (H.L.W.) was consulted.

In the second stage, the full texts of the selected papers were comprehensively evaluated using the same eligibility criteria. Again, this assessment was carried out independently by two reviewers (Z.C. and G.M.). If any disagreements arose, discussions with a third reviewer (H.L.W.) took place until a consensus was achieved.

Data extraction

The pre‐established outcomes of interest were extracted from the included studies by two independent reviewers (N.S. and E.M.) for subsequent qualitative and quantitative analyses. The data collected from each study included authors' names, year of publication, study design, sample size, age and sex of the participants, type of cells/biologics, and type of scaffold. The analyses covered both primary and secondary outcomes. If additional clarification regarding study methods or more detailed data was needed, the corresponding authors of the reviewed citations were contacted for further information. When the results of a study were published more than once, the data were used upon necessity. If a study is comparing more than two arms, the data from the groups of interest were extracted.

Quality assessment (risk of bias of individual studies)

The risk of bias assessment for the included studies was performed independently by two reviewers (Z.C. and H.L.W.). Appropriate tools were used based on the type of studies included: the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool 13 for randomized controlled trials, the ROBINS‐I tool 14 for non‐randomized clinical studies and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Study Quality Assessment Tool for case series (https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health‐topics/study‐quality‐assessment‐tools).

Data analyses

The statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Q test based on chi‐square statistics as well as the I2 index 15 in order to know the percentage of variation in the global estimate that was attributable to heterogeneity. To summarize and compare studies, mean values of primary and secondary outcomes were directly pooled and analyzed with weighted mean differences (WMDs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Study specific estimates were pooled with both the fixed and random‐effect models. 16 If a significant and high heterogeneity is found, then the random‐effect model results will be presented. Forest plots were created to illustrate the effects of the different studies and the global estimation. STATA 18.0 (StataCorp LP, Lakeway Drive, College Station, Texas, USA), or OpenMeta [Analyst] intercooled software will be used to perform all analyses. Statistical significance will be set as a p‐value < 0.05.

Interventions and relevant aspects of study design

Of the 10 studies, six were RCTs, and four were prospective case series. Of the RCTs, three presented a split‐mouth design 17 , 19 , 20 while three studies employed a parallel design, 18 , 21 , 22 with all of them comparing two treatment groups. Four trials evaluated different forms of platelet‐concentrates (platelet‐rich fibrin [PRF], leukocyte–platelet‐rich fibrin [L‐PRF], and titanium‐prepared platelet‐rich fibrin [T‐PRF]) placed at the time of implant placement (either around healing abutments or covering the implant following submerged healing) 17 , 18 , 21 or at the time of second‐stage surgery, 19 comparing it with a connective tissue graft, 18 an acellular dermal matrix 17 or with a no attempt to graft the area; 19 , 20 while two trials evaluated the combination of an apically‐positioned flap (APF) with PRF or L‐PRF membranes at the time of second‐stage surgery to augment the KM and compare it with an FGG. 17 , 20

Regarding the case series, one of them evaluated the combination of an APF with a collagen matrix embedded in what they called “micronized‐gingival connective tissue” obtained after harvesting a small piece (approximately 3 mm3) of maxillary tuberosity and disrupting it using a specific machine, theoretically providing gingival mucosa‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (GMMSCs). 25 Another case series evaluated a rhPDGF‐BB infused collagen matrix (Mucograft) placed over the bone crest at the time of removing an expanded polytetrafluoroethylene membrane (PTFE‐e) to increase peri‐implant STT. 24 Another case series included, aimed to increase the peri‐implant KM using an amnion/chorion allograft membranes (obtained from placenta) placed around healing abutments at first or second stage of implant surgery. 23 Finally, the last case series aimed to augment KM at the second‐stage surgery by combining an APF with a third‐generation platelet concentrate (i.e., concentrated growth factor [CGF]). 17

The systematic review included a total of 171 patients and 203 surgical sites. Follow‐up ranged substantially from 6 weeks 19 , 20 to 42 months. 24 All the included studies were related to procedures aiming to increase KM, peri‐implant STT, or both.

Effects of interventions: Primary outcomes

KM augmentation (Table 4)

The change in the width of KM was assessed on five RCTs. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 and three case series. 23 , 25 , 26 In most of the investigations, KM was measured with a periodontal probe, with two studies staining the KM before the measurements were performed. 19 , 20 Just one investigation performed the measurements using STL files. 17

Two comparative studies particularly aimed to increase the KM at the time of second‐stage surgery combining an APF with either a PRF/L‐PRF membrane or an FGG. 17 , 20 The results of the meta‐analysis showed significantly greater KM augmentation with FGG than with PRF membranes (n = 2; WMD = −2.0; 95% CI: −3.3, −0.8; p < 0.001. Figure 2). The remaining included RCTs evaluated PRF membranes placed around the healing abutment either at the time of implant placement or at the time of second‐stage surgery and compare it with no further attempt to increase the soft tissue 18 , 19 or the placement of a CTG. 21 The meta‐analysis showed that the addition of a PRF/L‐PRF membrane around the healing abutment significantly increased the amount of KM when compared to no scaffold/graft (n = 2; WMD = 1.1 mm; 95% CI: 0.2, 2.1; p = 0.017. Figure 3). However, the only study comparing T‐PRF membranes versus a CTG reported a significantly higher increase in KM for the autogenous graft. 21

One prospective case series evaluated pre–post changes in KM after aiming to augment it with a amnion/chorion allograft membrane around healing abutments (leaving it exposed) at first or second stage of implant surgery showed a significant augmentation at 60 days post‐surgery. 23 The other study evaluated the combination of an ARP with a CGF at the time of second‐stage surgery, showing an increase of ≈1 mm in KM at the 12‐month examination. 17

Soft tissue thickness (STT; Table 5)

STT was evaluated on four RCTs and one case series inserting endodontic spreaders or files through the soft tissue and digital calipers or periodontal probes for the measurements. 18 , 19 , 21 , 24 Measurements were most commonly performed at the occlusal aspect of the alveolar crest, 18 , 19 , 21 , 24 slightly apical to the midfacial mucosal level, 18 , 19 , 21 , 24 and/or slightly apical to the mucogingival line. 21 , 24

For comparative studies, no meta‐analysis could be carried out for the increase in STT, as significant heterogeneity existed among the locations in which these measurements were carried out, the control interventions (no attempt to increase STT, CTGs, or ADMs) and/or follow‐ups. When comparing PRF versus no attempt to increase the STT, two studies reported significant benefits of the addition of a PRF membrane surrounding the healing abutment either at the time of implant placement 18 or at the time of the second‐stage surgery, 19 reporting increases in STT at the midfacial mucosal level ranging between 0.9 and 2.8 mm. The only RCT comparing a T‐PRF membrane versus a CTG placed around the healing abutment at the time of implant placement reported a significant increase in STT both at the occlusal aspect, at the midfacial mucosal level and 1 mm beyond the mucogingival line for both groups without significant inter‐group differences. 21

Soft tissue healing indexes

One study 19 used the Wachtel´s Early Healing Index (EHI) 27 at 1, 2, 4, and 6 weeks. During the first 2 weeks, there were significant higher chances to receive a score of 1 or 2 (i.e., complete flap closure) in the control group (no attempt to increase STT during second‐stage surgery) versus those sites receiving an L‐PRF membrane around the healing abutment. No differences were observed between groups at 4 and 6 weeks.

Interproximal marginal bone level changes

Two studies reported marginal bone levels combined (pooled measurements for mesial and distal sites), 18 , 19 while another study reported the data separately for mesial and distal sites. 17 All studies considered as baseline the implant loading appointment 17 , 18 or measured the distance between the level of the implant platform and the most coronal aspect of the bone crest at the second‐stage surgery. 19 Marginal bone loss was lower than 0.5 mm in all studies at the end of the follow‐up (3 to 12 months). No relevant differences between study groups in comparative studies were reported.

Plaque indexes

Four studies evaluated and reported implant biofilm accumulation, using either the modified plaque index, 28 that was employed in three studies, 17 , 18 , 26 or the full mouth plaque score, 29 that was used in one study. 23 Overall, the studies demonstrated that plaque levels remained relatively low throughout the observation period.

Peri‐implant tissue health status, including probing depth (PD), bleeding on probing (BoP) and incidence of biological complications (peri‐implant mucositis or peri‐implantitis, based on specified diagnostic criteria)

PD was evaluated only in two studies, 17 , 26 demonstrating consistent values over time with minimal fluctuation and values ranging from 2 to 3 mm. Similarly, just four studies 17 , 18 , 23 , 26 evaluated soft tissue inflammation using BoP or different gingival/mucosal indexes. 30 , 31 Overall, the levels of inflammation were observed to be relatively low, with mean values falling below 0.5 for gingival indices and below 20% for bleeding scores. The occurrence of biological complications, as defined by a specific peri‐implant mucositis or peri‐implantitis criteria, was not documented in most of the included studies, except for Zhan et al. (2025), which specifically reported the absence of biological complications. 26

PROMs and surgical time

One study 20 evaluating the combination of APF plus L‐PRF membranes or FGGs to augment KM on the posterior mandible used the Mc Gill Pain questionnaire (Visual Analogue Scale [VAS] from 0 to 10) filled the day of the surgery every 4 h and afterwards daily until Day 7. 32 Pain values from Day 1 until Day 6 were significantly higher in the sites receiving an FGG. The same study also evaluated the mean surgical time for test and control sites, being significantly lower in the sites receiving an L‐PRF membrane (29.1 ± 4.8 min vs. 48.1 ± 7.7; p < 0.01).

DISCUSSION

The present systematic review aimed to evaluate the efficacy of cell‐based therapies and biologically enhanced scaffolds in KM augmentation and/or increasing the STT. Six RCTs and four prospective case series were included in the analyses.

Despite numerous reviews have discussed soft tissue augmentation around dental implants, 4 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 this is, to the best of the author's knowledge, the first systematic review to assess the effectiveness of cell‐based therapies and biologically enhanced scaffolds in increasing KM or STT around dental implants. However, around natural teeth, studies evaluating the use of biologics in root coverage procedures have shown that the adjunctive application of ABPs or EMD does not provide significant clinical or patient‐reported outcome measures (CROM/PROM) benefits beyond those achieved with coronally advanced flaps (CAF) alone. Furthermore, their results were inferior in mean root coverage and complete root coverage rates, recession depth reduction and keratinized tissue width gain compared to the combination of subepithelial CTGs with CAFs. Although existing studies have reported comparable outcomes between rhPDGF‐BB combined with a CAF and the SCTG+CAF, the available evidence remains limited. 10 What is more, a systematic review and meta‐analysis on non‐root‐coverage procedures around natural teeth reported that FGGs achieved approximately 1.5 mm greater KT gain compared to living cellular constructs. 38

Regarding PRF, the evidence derived from this systematic review suggests that its application‐across various forms (at the time of implant placement or at the second‐stage surgery) yield a greater KM gain (≈1 mm) compared to no grafting, but remains inferior to autogenous grafts such as FGG (with a difference of ≈2 mm). It is important to note that in the current literature, PRF has either been placed around healing abutments or used in combination with an APF. Compared to no scaffold or graft, the addition of a PRF or L‐PRF membrane around healing abutments significantly increased KM and was associated with notable improvements in STT, as demonstrated by three studies, 18 , 19 , 26 showing midfacial thickness gains ranging from 0.9 to 2.8 mm. When PRF was used in combination with an APF, a RCT with a split‐mouth design (Temmerman et al., 2018) 20 found that the FGG group achieved a significantly greater mean bucco‐lingual KM gain than the PRF group, with a difference of 1.30 ± 0.90 mm, even if both approaches met the clinical objective of attaining at least 2 mm of KM width on the vestibular aspect of the implant (the mean vestibular KM width was 3.3 ± 0.9 mm at sites treated with PRF and 3.8 ± 1.0 mm at those treated with FGG, without statistically significant differences). In terms of shrinkage, it was slightly higher for PRF (32.1%) than at FGG sites (23.6%) after 6 weeks, similarly to what Al‐Diasty et al. (2022) reported. 17 For soft tissue thickening, when PRF membranes where compared to no attempt to increase STT, the augmentation at the midfacial mucosal level ranged between 0.9 and 2.8 mm 18 , 19 and was ≈1.5 mm at the occlusal level when used simultaneous to implant placement.

In terms of surgical time and postoperative pain sensation, compared to FGG, Temmerman et al. 20 found that the use of PRF significantly reduced operative time (≈30 vs. ≈50 min), as well as postoperative pain and discomfort during the first 6 days. This improvement is largely attributed to the absence of a second surgical site and PRF's role as a local immune modulator, which helps control inflammation and lower the incidence of postoperative infections, thereby reducing the need for analgesics. Additionally, PRF‐treated sites exhibit more natural tissue color and consistency, avoiding the “patch effect” commonly associated with autogenous grafts. In addition to the studies included in the present analysis, the use of PRF/L‐PRF to accelerate the healing of FGG around implants has been documented by one case report. 39 Around natural teeth, in one animal study (Mogharehabed et al. 2022) 40 , FGGs were performed at two separate sites, with and without L‐PRF application. A statistically significant difference in the degree of inflammation was observed between groups both at 7 and 28 days.

Regarding other biologically enhanced scaffolds, currently, there is a lack of RCTs combining substitutes with biologics in soft tissue augmentation around dental implants. In terms of STT, only one prospective case series 24 involving six patients demonstrated that the application of a xenogeneic collagen matrix (Mucograft) infused with rhPDGF‐BB during secondstage surgery in the anterior maxilla led to a moderate increase in peri‐implant soft tissue volume at the apical, central, and occlusal aspects (0.87 ± 2.13, 2.14 ± 3.27, and 0.35 ± 3.20 mm, respectively). A preclinical study using a canine immediate implant placement model found that subepithelial CTGs and volume‐stable collagen matrices (VCMX) provided comparable STT below the implant platform, while the addition of polydeoxyribonucleotides to VCMX did not improve the outcomes. Anyway, all groups experienced some degree of volume reduction. 41

In terms of KT augmentation around dental implants, no clinical studies to date have evaluated the use of soft tissue substitutes in combination with biologics. One recent systematic review 4 compared soft tissue substitutes (allografts and xenografts), alone with autogenous grafts (FGGs and CTGs) in the augmentation of KM mucosa around dental implants, reporting that FGGs were more effective (WMD ≈ 1 mm) than soft tissue substitutes. One preclinical study in dogs 42 evaluated the effect of epithelial growth factor combined with a collagen matrix (CM) on KT gain around buccally positioned implants and found that while all groups (including FGG, CM alone, CM with 1 µg/g epithelial growth factor, and CM with 10 µg/g epithelial growth factor) showed an increase in KT, the FGG achieved the greatest gain (4.66 vs. 3.14 mm, 3.09 mm, and 3.11 mm, respectively), with no added benefit observed from the use of epithelial growth factor. In another canine preclinical model conducted by the same group, 43 a collagen matrix loaded with 2 mg/mL polydeoxyribonucleotide demonstrated KT formation – both clinically and histologically – comparable to FGG (4.85 ± 1.43/3.95 ± 0.87 vs. 4.70 ± 1.00/3.94 ± 0.93 mm, respectively) around buccally positioned implants. Additionally, the biologically enhanced scaffold group exhibited a marginal mucosal level approximately 0.5 mm higher than the FGG group.

Evidence on cell‐based therapies around dental implants remains scarce. However, a pilot case series using micronized gingival connective tissues (MGCTs) harvested from a small maxillary tuberosity graft demonstrated that transplantation of an atelocollagen matrix combined with MGCTs, processed via a tissue disruptor technique, was safe and showed preliminary efficacy in peri‐implant mucosa regeneration, suggesting a promising, less invasive alternative to traditional FGG or CTG approaches. 25

Limitations of the current review and recommendations for future research

Several limitations of this systematic review should be acknowledged, including (i) most of the included studies presented small sample sizes and short follow‐up periods; (ii) there were inconsistencies in methodologies (e.g., measurements), various treatment modalities (e.g., grafting prior, simultaneous or posterior to implant placement) and follow up periods; (iii) most of the current evidence on biomaterials focuses on different forms of platelet‐rich fibrin (i.e., PRF, L‐PRF, and T‐PRF), whereas research on cell‐based therapies or biologically enhanced soft tissue substitutes is still limited and primarily confined to the preclinical stage. Therefore, there is a clear need for well‐designed RCTs with longer follow‐up periods and adequately powered sample sizes to assess the efficacy of cell‐based therapies and biologically enhanced scaffolds for soft tissue augmentation around dental implants. Future research should also investigate the potential of soft tissue substitutes as delivery platforms for molecular biologics in this context. Additionally, comparative studies evaluating biologics in combination with autogenous grafts versus autogenous grafts alone are essential to fully elucidate the therapeutic value of these adjunctive strategies, particularly in challenging clinical scenarios. Incorporating patient‐reported outcomes and cost‐effectiveness analyses will further strengthen the evidence base and inform clinical decision‐making.

CONCLUSIONS

Within the limitations of this systematic review, highlighting the inclusion of just a few underpowered RCTs, it can be concluded that PRF membranes are effective in increasing the KM and STT around dental implants, even if KM augmentation by means of autogenous grafts lead to superior outcomes. Currently, there is a lack of evidence on cell‐based therapies or other biologics for their use in soft tissue regeneration around dental implants.
