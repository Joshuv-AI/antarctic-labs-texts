Frailty among adult people living with HIV in Tanzania: a single-centred hospital-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Regina Protas Mmanda, Mary-Winnie Asifa Nanyaro, Joan Rugemalila, Tumaini Nagu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFrailty is recognised as a significant health challenge among adults living with HIV. The increasing ageing population in more than 21.1 million people living with HIV (PLHIV) in Eastern and Southern Africa is of public health concern; however, data on the prevalence and associated factors of frailty in this population are limited. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of pre-frailty, frailty, identify associated factors and assess its impact on quality of life (QoL) among adult PLHIV attending Muhimbili National Hospital in Tanzania.MethodsA hospital-based, cross-sectional study was done in 2023 at the Care and Treatment Clinic (CTC). Adult PLHIV aged 35 years and above were enrolled using a linear systematic sampling. Frailty was assessed using the FRAIL scale which measured fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illnesses and loss of weight, where scores of 3–5, 1–2 and 0 indicated frailty, pre-frailty and healthy, respectively. WHOQOL-HIV BREF instrument measured QoL scores (4–20) using physical, psychological, level of independence, social relationships, environmental health and belief domains. Factors associated with frailty were determined using ordinal logistic regression. Comparison of QoL scores between frailty groups was determined using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.ResultsA total of 268 PLHIV were included with a mean age of 51.8±10.0 years. Prevalence of pre-frailty and frailty was 23.5% and 27.6%, respectively. Factors associated with frailty included older age (≥50 years), being female and ever being diagnosed with a mental disorder. The QoL score was 16.2±1.6. Low scores in physical health and level of independence domains were significantly associated with increasing frailty severity status.ConclusionMore than 50% of adult PLHIV had pre-frailty and frailty status. This is a multifactorial condition not solely a consequence of HIV infection itself. Its association with reduced QoL highlights the importance of integrating routine frailty screening into CTC care.

Introduction

Frailty is described as a clinical state of physical weakness and susceptibility brought on by reductions in physiological reserves, impairing the body’s capacity to deal with a variety of acute or long-term stressors. It is characterised by a lowered functional capacity of an individual, thus reduced functional capacity to perform basic daily activities. Frailty is assessed through physical performance tests, self-reported questionnaires and medical history.12 Moreover, it is assessed using various frailty screening tools; however, a few can be used in people living with HIV (PLHIV) including the FRAIL scale, Fried Frail Phenotype, Frailty Index, Short Performance Physical Battery, Edmonton Frail Scale and Veterans Aging Cohort Study Index.3–5

With a global prevalence of frailty in PLHIV of approximately 5%–28% and that of sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) to be 3.2%–19.4%, the condition has proven to be of public health importance.56 HIV affects about 40.8 million people worldwide, with 39.4 million people being adults. Of those, 31.6 million infected people are on anti-retroviral therapy (ART). Eastern and Southern Africa account for 21.1 million cases of HIV, with 83% being an adult population accessing ART.6 Tanzania has a population of 61 741 120 million (Census 2022). While the prevalence of HIV was 4.4% among people aged 15 years and above, the HIV burden rose with age, peaking in 45–49 years to 13% especially among women.7

As Tanzania envisions to achieve the 95–95–95 target by 2030 set by UNAIDS, currently the country has 82.7% of adults living with HIV aware of their HIV status; among those who are aware of their status, 97.9% are on ART; and among those on ART, 94.3% had viral load suppression.7 Effective and accessible use of ART has led to increased life expectancy and decreased mortality among PLHIV. This has resulted in an increased population of aged PLHIV who are prone to various comorbidities including geriatric conditions such as frailty as compared with the age-matched seronegative group.89

In SSA, the burden of frailty among PLHIV is likely to be substantial, although comprehensive data remain limited. As a standalone predictor of frailty, it is believed that HIV contributes to frailty by promoting immunological senescence, lowering adaptive immunity and inducing systemic inflammation through immuno-inflammatory phenomena that ultimately augment the biological ageing mechanisms.10 Duration of HIV positivity which determines the effect of HIV inflammation on an individual, delayed initiation of ART, low CD4+ count and high HIV viral load are also associated with an increased risk of frailty.11 These have been linked to frailty and eventually morbidity and mortality in HIV individuals.12

Sociodemographic factors including being female, having low education level, poverty, social isolation and increased age1113; lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol use, being obese or underweight (malnutrition), presence of mental disorders,14 malignancies (both AIDS and non-AIDS defined) and physical inactivity are also implicated with frailty in PLHIV.15 In addition to that, frailty is associated with decreased quality of life (QoL), since it affects various aspects of physical, psychological and social factors in an individual.1617

Nevertheless, key gaps persist, including limited use of context-specific adapted frailty assessment tools, and insufficient integration of frailty screening and management into routine HIV care.18 Like in the rest of SSA, the success of national HIV programmes in Tanzania has extended survival for PLHIV19; however, there remains a paucity of data on the burden and clinical implications of frailty in this population. Frailty can substantially compromise QoL, limit functional independence and increase healthcare needs.20 Yet, its prevalence, associated factors and impact on health outcomes among Tanzanian adults living with HIV remain limited. This study seeks to address this critical gap by examining the prevalence and associated factors of pre-frailty and frailty among adults living with HIV at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) and highlighting its clinical impact on the QoL.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a hospital-based, analytical cross-sectional study conducted over 4 months, from 1 August to 30 November 2023, at the Care and Treatment Centre of MNH in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. MNH is a tertiary hospital operating two campuses: Upanga, serving approximately 3500 PLHIV, and Mloganzila, serving around 500 PLHIV.21 Both clinics function 5 days a week, with an average of 60 adult patients attending daily for routine medication refills and clinical follow-ups.

Participants and sampling

The study population comprised adults living with HIV attending either campus at MNH. Eligibility criteria included confirmed HIV-positive status, age ≥35 years and provision of informed consent as seen in online supplemental material 1. The sample size required for this study was calculated by using the single proportion formula.22 Using a standard normal deviation set at 1.96 (corresponding to a confidence level of 95%), the prevalence of frailty among patients with HIV of 19.4%,23 margin of error at 5% and adjusting for non-response rate of 10%, the sample size was approximated to 270 participants.

A linear systematic sampling technique was employed to recruit participants. Adjusting for the 4-month data collection period, the estimated sampling frame was approximately 1366 patients. Based on the required sample size of 270, the sampling interval (k) was initially calculated as approximately 5. A random starting point was selected from the first five patients on the clinic attendance list using a simple random method (lottery method), after which every fifth patient was approached for participation until the desired sample size was achieved.

Data collection

The participants who were eligible to take part in the study were recruited on the same day they attended the Care and Treatment Clinic (CTC). The study used a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire and the review of medical records to collect the data. The research assistants used paper-based forms to collect the data. The interview was conducted in a private room to ensure privacy. The names of the patients were not noted. Double entry was done for the collected information from the forms into an excel in the computer, with identification numbers to protect the identity of the participants. Therefore, the information entered into the computer for analysis was anonymous, password-protected and accessible to the principal investigator and designated personnel only. The forms used to collect the data were secured and safely stored to be retrieved when needed.

Study outcomes

The outcomes included frailty and QoL. Frailty was assessed using the FRAIL scale as seen in online supplemental material II). It is a 5-item survey approved for use with patients with HIV denoting fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illness and loss of weight domains. A score of 1 point for every ‘yes’ response was given. Scores of 3–5 were interpreted as frail, scores of 1–2 were interpreted as prefrail and a score of 0 was interpreted as healthy. This tool has been validated for use in screening for frailty in patients with HIV, African ancestry and in low-income community-dwelling individuals.2425

It was selected because it is simple, quick and feasible to use in routine clinics. It is culturally adaptable, captures multiple relevant health domains and is resource-efficient, making it suitable for high-volume clinics such as those in this study. The scale relies on self-reported questions, avoiding the need for physical performance tests required by other measures like the Fried criteria. Cronbach’s alpha was used to assess the reliability of the FRAIL scale tool, a value of 0.6–0.7 indicated acceptable internal consistency and values of ≥0.8 indicated good internal consistency.26

The QoL scores were evaluated using the WHOQOL-HIV BREF instrument which is a short version of the WHOQOL-HIV (120 items) developed by UNAIDS and WHO aiming at assessing QoL in PLHIV. It covers six domains which are physical, psychological, level of independence, social relationships, environment and spirituality/personal beliefs.27 WHOQOL-HIV BREF Likert responses are on a 5-point scale total. Negative experience items (Q3, Q4, Q5, Q8, Q9, Q10 and Q31) were coded in a reverse manner for scoring. Higher scores for all items indicated better QoL. The average score for each domain was multiplied by 4 to yield domain scores ranging from 4 to 20, scores of 4 being the lowest and 20 being the highest. This tool has been validated for use in the HIV population in Tanzania.28

Covariates

The exposures, including information on sociodemographic characteristics, clinical details and HIV-related variables, were obtained through four sections as seen in online supplemental material II) questionnaire. The sociodemographic characteristics like age, sex, marital status, living conditions, level of education, physical address and employment status were assessed. The questionnaire also accounted risk factors for frailty including smoking history in pack years and alcohol use in units per week.

Clinical parameters including current weight in kg, height in m, body mass index (BMI) in kg/m2, presence of multiple comorbidities such as hypertension, chronic kidney diseases, diabetes, obesity, malignancy and history of mental disorder diagnosis were collected. Likewise HIV related factors such as HIV viral load in the previous 6 months, current CD4 count, ART adherence (ie, good adherence being defined as correct dosing, timing and consuming of more than 95% of the prescribed ART medication29), duration of ART use, time from diagnosis to initiation of ART, duration of HIV positivity and WHO HIV clinical staging. Stable patients with HIV have six monthly scheduled CTC visits for medication refills and follow-up investigations.30

Data analysis

Data were analysed using STATA V.16 after being cleaned and organised. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means with SD or medians with IQRs, depending on data distribution. Frailty status was categorised into healthy, pre-frail and frail groups, and prevalence was reported as percentages. Associations between independent variables (sociodemographic and clinical factors) and frailty status (frailty, pre-frailty and healthy) were assessed using ordinal logistic regression.

Variables with a p value <0.20 in univariate analysis, summarised using unadjusted OR (uOR), along with clinically relevant variables, were included in the multivariable model to get the adjusted OR (aOR) after controlling for confounders. The final model selected was based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The AIC was used to evaluate model fit while penalising for the number of parameters to avoid overfitting. For each model, the AIC value was calculated, and the model with the lowest AIC was chosen as it represents the best balance between goodness-of-fit and model simplicity. This approach ensured that the final model is both parsimonious and adequately explains the variability in frailty status. The WHOQOL-HIV BREF QoL domain scores (range 4–20) were compared across frailty categories using one-way analysis of variance. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant at 95% CIs.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not directly involved in the design or development of the study. However, the hospital administration and CTC clinic personnel were engaged during the study, and clinicians involved in the care of the patients provided input on the development of the study questionnaires. The study was conducted among adult PLHIV attending the CTC clinics at MNH. Dissemination of the findings has been carried out to the relevant hospital departments involved in CTC services, and there are plans to share the results with patients attending the CTC clinics at the MNH Upanga and Mloganzila campuses.

Prevalence of prefrailty and frailty among adults living with HIV

The prevalence of pre-frailty and frailty among adult PLHIV attending MNH in Tanzania was found to be 23.5% and 27.6%, respectively. Moreover, internal validity, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha for the FRAIL scale, was 0.64, indicating moderate internal consistency.

Factors associated with frailty among adults living with HIV at MNH in Tanzania

Prior to adjusting for other factors, in social demographic factors, participants aged 50 years and above had two times higher odds of increasing frailty status compared with their younger counterparts (p=0.001). Female participants had 2.5 times higher odds of developing healthy to prefrail to frail status compared with male participants (p=0.001). People who took alcohol had 1.6 times significantly higher odds of increasing frailty severity compared with those who did not take alcohol (p=0.039) (table 4).

Discussion

The prevalence of frailty observed in this study (27.6%) is within the global range reported in the literature (5–28%), but remains higher than that reported in most studies conducted in SSA. A study done in Northern Tanzania reported a prevalence as low as 2.7% using the Fried frailty phenotype.31 This may be explained by population differences as the majority of the current study population had a longer duration of HIV infection, more advanced disease (WHO clinical stages III and IV) and a higher burden of comorbidities, all of which are known contributors to frailty.

Variations in frailty prevalence across settings may also reflect differences in frailty measuring tools used. Comparable prevalence estimates have been reported in Kenya (19.4% overall and 23.9% among older adults using the Edmonton Frail Scale)32 and in Uganda (19% using Fried criteria), although the Ugandan population had a younger mean age of 44 years.33 In contrast, a study from the USA reported a lower prevalence of 9% using the Fried criteria in a predominantly male population.34

The combined prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty in this study (51.1%) indicates a substantial burden of vulnerability among PLHIV, especially in lower- and middle-income countries. Similar findings have been reported in Indonesia and Uganda, where more than half of participants were classified as pre-frail or frail using the Fried phenotype.3335 This pattern suggests that PLHIV are at a high risk of frailty, emphasising the need for early detection and intervention.

Older age (≥50 years) was independently associated with increased odds of frailty, consistent with findings from other settings, including South Africa.23 Ageing is associated with the accumulation of comorbidities, functional decline and reduced physiological reserve, all of which contribute to frailty.36 This reinforces the need for integrating geriatric assessment into HIV care to mitigate adverse outcomes of frailty.

Female sex was also strongly associated with frailty, a finding consistent with other studies.2331 This may reflect a combination of biological, social and economic factors, including differences in body composition, longevity and increased access to healthcare resources by women.37 Interestingly, divorced participants had significantly lower odds of frailty compared with single individuals, a finding that is not widely reported and may reflect unmeasured social or behavioural factors. This warrants further investigation.

Advanced HIV and frailty are interconnected, sharing immune suppression, chronic inflammation, functional decline and muscle wasting, and both are linked to higher morbidity and mortality.363839 Frailty can be viewed as both a consequence and a marker of advanced HIV disease; therefore, the observed trend toward increased odds of frailty with advanced WHO stage is clinically plausible and consistent with current understanding of HIV pathophysiology although the associated higher odds in this study were borderline statistically significant.

Certain antiretroviral therapy regimens promote frailty by causing metabolic complications like dyslipidaemia, insulin resistance, mitochondrial toxicity and fat redistribution which lead to muscle loss and reduced physical function.40 In addition, patients on alternative or second-line regimens often have a history of treatment failure, drug resistance, or long-standing HIV infection, which may further increase vulnerability to frailty.4142 These findings show, while no significant association was found, the observed trend indicates a potential link that merits investigation in larger, longitudinal studies.

Mental health disorders showed a borderline association with frailty, an important but often neglected dimension of HIV care. Depression and anxiety promote frailty both by influencing behaviours, such as lower physical activity, poor nutrition and medication non-adherence, and through biological effects like chronic inflammation and neuroendocrine disruption.43 There is also a reciprocal relationship between frailty and depression, whereby each condition elevates the likelihood of developing the other;14 however, results emphasise that statistical non-significance does not equate to clinical irrelevance hence the need for integrated physical and mental healthcare in PLHIV.

Frailty has a profound impact on QoL among PLHIV, affecting physical, psychological, social and environmental domains. In this study, participants with frailty reported consistently lower QoL scores across multiple domains, highlighting the tangible burden of frailty on daily functioning and well-being. Although the overall QoL in this cohort was relatively high compared with other African studies, such as those from Tunisia and Nigeria,4445 the decline observed in frail individuals underscores a clinically significant vulnerability that can have long-term implications for health outcomes.

The majority of PLHIV reside in the Eastern and Southern African region where healthcare systems often face high patient loads, limited resources and gaps in geriatric expertise. In these settings, routine assessment of frailty is uncommon, and older PLHIV may not be systematically screened for functional decline or multi-morbidity. This contrasts with European countries where frailty assessment is increasingly integrated into routine clinical visits as per European AIDS Clinical Society, particularly for older adults and is used to guide tailored interventions.46 Evidence from these settings shows that early identification and management of frailty can improve physical function, reduce complications and maintain QoL, preventing the progression from pre-frailty to frailty.47

In Tanzania, where geriatrics is not yet fully developed as a clinical specialty,48 the lack of routine frailty screening represents a missed opportunity to identify at-risk individuals and implement early interventions for frailty. Integrating frailty assessment into routine HIV care could serve as a practical strategy to identify individuals at risk of declining QoL and adverse outcomes. Clinicians could also prioritise targeted interventions for frail patients, including approaches that address comorbidities and social support.49–52 Moreover, the differences on frailty estimates highlight the influence of both assessment tools and demographic characteristics and underscore the need for context-appropriate and standardised frailty measures, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Limitations of the study

This was a hospital-based study, and the results cannot be generalised to the community. Moreover, the causal effect relationship could not be captured due to the cross-sectional design of the study. The self-reported nature of questions in the FRAILTY scale and WHOQOL-HIV BREF could have introduced recall bias; however, review of the medical records would have reduced the social desirability bias in the clinical and laboratory parameters obtained. Internal validity was moderately consistent, indicating prevalence (frail vs pre-frail vs healthy) might be slightly overestimated or underestimated because the tool is not perfectly reliable in these adult PLHIV population.

Conclusion

In summary, despite 70.5% of adults living with HIV in this study achieving viral suppression, more than half of them demonstrated a pre-frailty or frailty status. Frailty was associated with older age, female sex, marital status and ever being diagnosed with a mental health disorder. There were significantly reduced QoL scores across multiple domains in those with frailty. These findings highlight the need for routine frailty screening and integration of geriatric principles including comprehensive management of comorbidities into HIV care to improve long-term outcomes in this population.
