Early versus delayed laparoscopic appendectomy for acute uncomplicated appendicitis in adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Iftikhar Khan, Soban Ali Qasim, Haris Mumtaz Malik, Muhammad Hamza, Umna Bhatti, FNU Sawaira, Syeda Wania Haider, Saad Khan, Ehsanullah Alokozay, Muhammad Abdullah Ali, Fariha Hasan, Muhammad Riyyan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal timing of laparoscopic appendectomy for acute uncomplicated appendicitis remains uncertain. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare operative and safety outcomes following early versus delayed laparoscopic appendectomy in adults.MethodsPubMed, Cochrane Central, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched through October 2024 for randomized and cohort studies. Six studies (one RCT, five cohorts) including 2,429 patients were analyzed. Risk of bias was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale and Cochrane tools. Meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan and R (meta/metafor).ResultsThere were no significant differences in operative time (mean difference − 2.3 min) or postoperative length of stay (− 0.7 days). Rates of perforation (RR 0.87), mortality (RR 0.87), surgical-site infection (RR 0.98), overall complications (RR 0.94), and 30-day readmission (RR 1.16) were comparable between groups. Cohort studies were of high quality, while the single RCT showed minor bias concerns.ConclusionsOur Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis produced comparable operative and safety outcomes between early and delayed appendectomy, indicating that a delay in operation of 8 h, due to logistics issues, can be acceptable in terms of clinical outcomes. This recommendation can be solidified via further randomized controlled trials and larger umbrella reviews.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03428-0.

Introduction

Laparoscopic appendectomy is a minimally invasive procedure that removes the appendix through small incisions using a camera and specialized instruments. It offers faster recovery, less pain, better cosmetic results, and a high success rate, making it the preferred treatment for acute appendicitis (https://www.facs.org/for-patients/the-day-of-your-surgery/appendectomy/, https://www.verywellhealth.com/laparoscopic-appendectomy-7099629, https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/procedures/21922-appendectomy). Laparoscopic appendectomy has demonstrated high success rates, making it the procedure of choice for most patients with acute appendicitis. The timing of laparoscopic appendectomy in appendicitis, whether early (within 12 h of diagnosis) or delayed (after 12 h), remains debated and is influenced by patient condition, operating room availability, and hospital protocols [1, 2].

Traditionally, early appendectomy has been the preferred approach for treating acute appendicitis, with the rationale that a prompt operation reduces the risk of complications such as appendiceal perforation, peritonitis, and sepsis [3]. The concern is that delaying surgery increases the likelihood of these complications, potentially leading to worse outcomes. Early intervention is thought to limit the progression of the disease and mitigate the risks associated with advanced appendicitis [4–6].

However, as surgical practices have evolved, there has been growing interest in delayed appendectomy, particularly for patients who present at night or during off-hours. The idea behind delayed surgery is that the risk of complications may not be as high as previously thought, and it may provide advantages such as improved resource management and a better working environment for surgical teams [7]. Some studies suggest that delayed appendectomy may be as safe as early surgery, with no significant increase in complications or hospital stay, particularly for uncomplicated cases [8–11].

The existing literature on this topic has yielded mixed results. While some studies support early appendectomy as the safer option, with lower rates of complications and shorter hospital stays, others indicate that delayed appendectomy does not significantly affect patient outcomes. A few studies have even shown that delayed surgery might lead to fewer complications in certain cases, such as when performed during daytime hours when surgical teams are better rested. Nonetheless, many studies have been retrospective, with variations in patient selection, timing of surgery, and definitions of early and delayed surgery, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions [1, 8, 9].

Despite multiple studies, it remains unclear whether delayed appendectomy should be standard for acute appendicitis. The varying findings and the need to balance patient outcomes with healthcare resources make a comprehensive review necessary. We believe that a meta-analysis will help clarify the effectiveness and safety of early versus delayed laparoscopic appendectomy, providing clearer guidelines for clinical practice.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [5] and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The study’s protocol has been registered and is accessible on PROSPERO under the ID (CRD420251230623 ).

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed, Cochrane Central, and ClinicalTrials.gov from inception through October 2024. The search utilized Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, including “Delayed,” “Early,” “Appendectomy,” and “Appendicitis.” Additionally, the reference lists of all included articles were manually reviewed to identify any further relevant studies. The complete search strategy is detailed in Supplementary File. All retrieved records were imported into Rayyan, where duplicate entries were removed.

Eligibility criteria

We included full-text, peer-reviewed randomized controlled trials and observational studies in English that directly compared delayed (intervention) versus early (control) appendectomy in adults (≥ 18 years) with acute uncomplicated appendicitis, defining early appendectomy as < 8 h and delayed as ≥ 8 h, and reporting at least one prespecified outcome (operation time, length of stay, perforation rate, mortality, surgical-site infection, postoperative complications, or 30-day readmission). We excluded non-comparative reports (e.g., case series without both early and delayed arms), pediatric cohorts, publications lacking sufficient data to derive effect estimates, duplicate analyses of the same patient population (retaining only the most comprehensive report), and non-English articles.

Study selection

Two independent reviewers (M.H and S.W.H) screened titles and abstracts to exclude studies that did not meet the predefined inclusion criteria. The full texts of potentially eligible studies were then assessed in detail for final inclusion. Discrepancies during the selection process were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (S.A.Q.).

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two researchers (U.B and S.A.Q) using a standardized data collection form in Microsoft Excel. Extracted data included study title, first author, year of publication, study location, total number of participants, and details of each treatment group. Baseline characteristics, such as patient age and sex, were recorded, along with outcome measures including operation time, length of hospital stay, perforation rates, mortality, surgical site infections, postoperative complications, and 30-day readmission rates. Safety data and other relevant clinical endpoints were also documented.

Quality assessment

The methodological rigor of randomized controlled trials was appraised by Hamza. Using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) [12]. Each study was judged as having a low, unclear, or high risk of bias across five key domains: the randomization process; deviations from intended interventions; completeness of outcome data; accuracy of outcome measurement; and selective reporting. Any discordant judgments were reconciled through discussion with a third investigator (S.A.Q). Cohort studies underwent quality evaluation via the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [13], which awards up to four points for cohort selection, two points for group comparability, and three points for outcome assessment (maximum score = 9). Studies achieving a score of ≥ 7 were classified as high quality, whereas those with scores ≤ 6 were considered to possess moderate or low methodological quality.

Statistical analysis

Dichotomous outcomes were analyzed using pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The Mantel-Haenszel method with a random-effects model was used to consolidate the RRs. Continuous outcomes were analyzed using pooled weighted mean differences (WMDs) with corresponding 95% CIs. Heterogeneity among studies and subgroups was assessed using the Cochran Q test and I² statistic. I² values were interpreted as low (< 25%), moderate (25%–50%), substantial (50%–75%), or high (> 75%) heterogeneity, in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Sect. 10.10). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Where substantial heterogeneity was identified (I² >50%), sensitivity analyses were conducted by systematically omitting one study at a time to assess its influence on the overall heterogeneity and effect size. Due to the limited number of included studies, publication bias was assessed using DOI plots rather than traditional funnel plots. The LFK index from the DOI plot was interpreted as follows: values between 1 and + 1 indicated symmetry, values between ± 1 and ± 2 suggested minor asymmetry, and values beyond ± 2 indicated major asymmetry [14].

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio version 4.4.1. Forest plots were used to visually represent the effect estimates.

Results

Study selection

A total of 2831 records were retrieved from different databases. After removing 262 duplicates, 2569 records were screened based on their abstracts and titles. Following this, 13 records were assessed in full texts, and after excluding those that did not meet the inclusion criteria, 6 studies were included in the final review. The selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics

A total of 2,429 adult patients with acute appendicitis were pooled from six studies, of whom 1,278 (52.6%) were male and 1,141 (47.4%) were female. These participants were enrolled in five observational cohort studies and one randomized controlled trial, all published between 2014 and 2024. Detailed baseline characteristics — including age distribution, study design, setting, and exact sample sizes are provided in Supplementary File S1.

Study findings

This meta-analysis assessed the impact of delayed versus early appendectomy on key clinical endpoints such as Operation time, Postoperative length of hospital stay, and a spectrum of Unfavorable clinical outcomes.

Primary outcomes

Operation time

A total of six studies (n = 2,429 patients) compared operation time between delayed and early appendectomy groups. The pooled mean difference, calculated using a random effects model, was − 2.26 min (95% CI: -4.90 to 0.39), favoring delayed appendectomy, though this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.0946). Moderate heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I² = 61.3%) (Fig. 2). A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis revealed that excluding the study by Arriaga et al. (Fig S1). reduced heterogeneity the most (I² = 51.5%). Additionally, a DOI plot with an LFK index of 0.12 indicated no significant asymmetry among the included studies (Fig S2).

Postoperative length of hospital stay

The pooled analysis of postoperative length of hospital stay, based on four studies (n = 2,111), demonstrated a pooled mean difference of -0.73 days (95% CI: -2.05 to 0.58), suggesting a slightly shorter duration in the delayed group. However, the results were not statistically significant (P = 0.2752) and high heterogeneity was observed (I² = 94.7%) (Fig. 3). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the study by Arriaga et al. [9]. contributed most to the heterogeneity, and its exclusion reduced the heterogeneity value to I² = 66.8% (Fig S3). Additionally, a DOI plot with an LFK index of -4.96 revealed major asymmetry among the included studies (Fig S4).

Unfavorable clinical outcomes

Under the umbrella of Unfavorable Clinical Outcomes, we evaluated key adverse events to compare the safety profiles of delayed versus early appendectomy. Specifically, we assessed rates of appendicular perforation, all-cause mortality, surgical-site infection, general postoperative complications, and 30-day readmission.

Perforation of appendicitis

The pooled analysis, based on four studies (n = 1,905), demonstrated no significant difference in the risk of appendicitis perforation between the two groups. The overall risk ratio (RR) was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.66–1.13), with low heterogeneity observed (I² = 14.1%), and a p value of 0.2953 (Fig. 4).

Mortality

The pooled analysis of two studies (n = 406) demonstrated no significant difference in the risk of Mortality between the two groups. The overall risk ratio (RR) was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.66–1.13), with no heterogeneity observed (I² = 0%) and a p value of 0.74 (Fig. 5).

Surgery site infections

The pooled analysis, based on four studies (n = 2,012), demonstrated no significant difference in the risk of surgical site infection between the two groups. The overall risk ratio (RR) was 0.98 (95% CI: 0.49–1.96), with no heterogeneity observed (I² = 0%) and a p value of 0.9614 (Fig. 6).

Postoperative complications

In the pooled analysis of postoperative complication outcomes across six studies (n = 2,428), patients who underwent early appendectomy demonstrated a higher risk of complications compared to the delayed group. The overall risk ratio (RR) was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.71–1.25), indicating no statistically significant difference (p = 0.6790), with low heterogeneity (I² = 25.9%) (Fig. 7).

Readmission within 30 days

In the pooled analysis of Readmission within 30 days across 5 studies (n = 2138), patients who underwent delayed appendectomy demonstrated a higher risk of readmission compared to the early group. The overall risk ratio (RR) was 1.16 (95% CI: 0.63–2.11), indicating no statistically significant difference (p = 0.6353), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. 8).

Quality assessment

A randomized control trial conducted by Patel et al. [11] demonstrated some concerns of bias due to deviation from intended deviation in the D2 domain, but overall, a low risk of bias across all domains was observed using the RoB 2 tool (Fig S5, S6). Regarding observational studies methodological quality of the five included cohort studies was uniformly high, as assessed by the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, Overall, study scores ranged from 8 to 9, reflecting a low risk of bias specifically, Terrón-Arriaga et al. shin [9] and Aiken et al. [8] each achieved a perfect score of 9/9 by fully addressing selection, comparability (awarded both points for controlling key confounders), and outcome domains. Shin et al. [2], Seudeal et al. [15], and Motomura et al. [10] each scored 8/9; all three studies were strong in cohort representativeness, control selection, exposure ascertainment, absence of baseline outcome, outcome assessment, follow-up duration, and follow-up completeness, but each lost one comparability point for not adjusting for a second key confounder (Table S2).

Discussion

This study is a systematic review and meta-analysis investigating the effectiveness and safety of delayed appendectomy as compared to early appendectomy. Currently, the Traditional concept stands that appendectomy should be performed as early as possible once the diagnosis is made [3]. However, our studies showed comparative outcomes in both approaches. Our study showed that no significant differences were observed in operative time, postoperative length of stay, and adverse outcomes such as perforation, mortality, and surgical site infections. Additionally, readmission rates were also reported to be similar overall.

Literature shows that operative time in open appendectomy is expected to be 57.6 min (40.2–70.2) (median, IQR) [16]. The mean operative times in our included studies varied around this, with the lowest being 53.50 min in the delayed appendectomy group in the study by Shin et al. [2] and 53.50 min reported by Patel et al. [11] in the early appendectomy group. Our meta-analysis showed that the pooled mean difference was 2.26 min, which was statistically insignificant. Postoperative length of stay is also reported to be similar in our studies, with a mean difference of 0.73, also reported to be statistically insignificant. This is in accordance with the literature, as previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses report statistically insignificant mean differences [17]. A meta-analysis by Amstel et al. performed in 2020 [18] showed greater length of stay and readmission rates in children having interval appendectomy. Our meta-analysis showed no such significant difference as we analyzed the difference in re-admission rates within 30 days of the procedure. This shows that literature is quite uncertain in terms of operative time, length of stay, and readmission rates. The logical conclusion, as backed by our analysis, is to consider both the early and delayed appendectomy to be equally effective in terms of these variables. Thus, we believe that the decision of whether to perform early or delayed appendectomy should be left to the surgeon.

A Systematic review and Meta-Analysis of 17 studies reported more complications in emergency appendectomy as compared to conservative management [19]. Our meta-analysis showed no such significant differences, and our findings are in accordance with meta-analyses performed in 2018 and 2021 [17, 20]. However, a meta-analysis in 2017 also mentioned 30% higher morbidity in early appendectomy [21], but this meta-analysis only included 2 studies, which are, in our humble opinion, too few to be considered a significant pool of studies. Individual adverse outcomes, such as appendix perforation and surgical site infection, also showed no significant difference in both groups when pooled. However, a nationwide quality improvement project performed by the American College of Surgeons reported significantly higher superficial surgical site infections in patients who had appendectomy on the 3rd day of hospital stay as compared to those who had appendectomy on the 1st or 2nd day. Other outcomes such as deep surgical site infections, sepsis, readmissions, etc., were reported to be similar in these groups [22]. Aside from this study, other meta-analyses showed similar adverse outcomes [17]. Most of the studies in our systematic review and meta-analysis added a maximum limit of 8 h to differentiate between early and delayed appendectomy, with 1 study by Aiken et al. [8] reporting the limit of 12 h to differentiate between the groups. We also studied the pooled difference in mortality among both the groups and the result showed statistically insignificant difference. This, however, has a limitation of only two studies and therefore, cannot be used as definitive evidence. Thus, we conclude that once the diagnosis is made, appendectomy can be safely delayed overnight. Delaying further may slightly increase the risk of superficial surgical site infections, but more studies are still needed to back this claim.

There are some limitations to our study. Firstly, we had a small number of studies, i.e., one randomized controlled trial and 5 cohorts. This results in lower quality of evidence synthesis. Additionally, one of the outcomes, the post-operative stay, showed high heterogeneity, and a study by Arriaga [9]. After carefully reading the article, we found that the cause of this increased heterogeneity was possibly the higher age group (Mean = 69.2 ± 33 years) in the early appendectomy group of this particular study resulting in a post-operative stay of 7.4 ± 4.4 days in this group. However, all the other outcomes did not show significant heterogeneity. This means that most of the outcomes can be considered for generating guidelines determining the surgical delay for appendectomy. Another limitation in our study is that the mortality was reported in only 2 studies, limiting the strength of evidence. However, even in these 2 studies, the total population of 406 participants. This may not be a very large population but it provides a preliminary indication that mortality rates may be comparable.

We believe that the decision to delay an appendectomy lies with the surgeon, availability of resources and patient load at the hospitals. As our review article demonstrates, differences in outcomes as statistically non-significant.Therefore, we recommend that a surgeon should perform appendectomy early to relieve the patient of the symptoms, a short delay caused by logistical factors, however, should not adversely affect the patient outcomes. We recommend that an umbrella review be performed for all the systematic reviews on the topic. Such a review may provide more comprehensive data for the surgeons.
