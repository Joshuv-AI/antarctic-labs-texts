Sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid profiling in post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection
Authors: Shinya Yamamoto, Makoto Kurano, Koh Okamoto, Masayuki Kubota, Takeya Tsutsumi, Junken Aoki, Kyoji Moriya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Post-acute sequelae of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infection (PASC) is a heterogeneous multisystemic condition with unclear pathogenesis. Emerging evidence suggests that the disruption in metabolism, including lipid metabolism, can be involved in the pathogenesis of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). We previously measured the bioactive lipids in acute COVID-19, and we hypothesized that bioactive lipid dysregulation play a role in PASC pathogenesis. We conducted targeted LC–MS/MS analysis of sphingolipids and glycerophospholipids in serum samples from individuals with acute-phase COVID-19, including those who developed PASC, alongside healthy controls. We systematically examined lipidomic changes in serum samples, including analyses using linear mixed model, and found that no sphingolipid or glycerophospholipid species differed between the COVID-19 and PASC groups. Although these results are exploratory, the group x time interaction in the linear mixed model suggested that phosphatidylglycerol (PG) may represent a bioactive lipid distinguishing the COVID-19 and PASC groups. Given the small PASC sample size and that the signal was detected only at specific time points, these findings are hypothesis generating and should be confirmed in larger, adequately powered, and independently validated cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50505-2.

Introduction

Post-acute sequelae of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection (PASC) or long coronavirus disease (COVID) is a multifaceted condition with multiorgan symptoms affecting millions of individuals worldwide1. Manifestations of PASC, including fatigue, exertional dyspnea, chest pain, and neurocognitive dysfunction, heterogeneous and multisystemic and can evolve over time. Over the past 5 years, several academic disciplines have aimed to elucidate the underlying pathogenesis of PASC. However, no significant breakthroughs have been achieved. Considering its high public health significance, clarifying the molecular mechanism of PASC and identifying reliable biomarkers and therapeutic targets valuable for the prediction diagnosis and treatment are urgently needed.

Lipid metabolism was associated with the severity of acute coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), and the severity is an established risk factor for the development of PASC2,3. Therefore, it is plausible that metabolic disturbances may contribute to PASC development. Emerging evidence suggests that persistent lipid metabolism abnormalities can contribute to PASC development4. Disease development stems from genetic and environmental factors, with disruption in lipid metabolism reflecting the environmental factors, including diet and physical activity. An association exists between PASC and lifestyle-related diseases, indicating that individual lifestyle differences influence its risk5,6. As lipidomic profiles capture these variations, lipidomics provides a promising approach to understanding PASC and facilitating tailored risk assessment and intervention.

Among lipids, specific types have bioactive capabilities, which exert potential biological properties, including playing crucial roles in maintaining homeostasis, and some bioactive lipids have attracted attention as drug targets. For example, fingolimod, a functional antagonist against sphongosin-1-phosphate (S1P) receptor 1, is clinically used as a treatment option for multiple sclerosis7. Regarding the relationship between viruses and sphingolipids, intracellular sphingolipids are key regulators of human viral replication and spread, supporting viral cell entry, membrane fusion, genome replication, assembly, budding, and proliferation8. Their biosynthesis induces structural changes in viral receptors and facilitates escape from endosomes. In glycerophospholipids, phosphoinositides (PI) are used during viral infection to support viral entry, formation of replication organelles, assembly, and release9. Previously, we measured bioactive lipids, including sphingolipids and glycerophospholipids, in samples from patients with acute COVID-193,10 and several were modulated.

Limited data exist on PASC and bioactive lipid metabolism. A study analyzing plasma samples from 147 patients with PASC reported increased lysophosphatidylglycerols (LPG) and phosphatidylethanolamines (PE) levels, along with reduced lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC) levels, suggesting that these lipids are potential PASC biomarkers4. Another study, examining lipidomic profiles and clinical parameters 2 years post-COVID-19, found higher levels of several phosphatidylcholine (PC) and sphingomyelin (SM) species in post-COVID-19 patients than in controls11. However, only a limited number of studies have investigated the relationship between bioactive lipids and PASC, and no consensus exists, highlighting gaps in the literature. Therefore, this study aimed to elucidate the role of bioactive lipid mechanisms in disease pathogenesis.

Results

Difference in serum sphingolipid levels between participants with COVID-19 with PASC and those without PASC

Initially, we assessed the serum concentrations of all quantified sphingolipids across the following three groups: 808 samples from participants with COVID-19 (n = 199), 115 samples from healthy controls (n = 109), and 51 samples from those with COVID-19 who subsequently developed PASC (n = 13) (Supplemental Table 1, 2). Compared with the healthy control group, the serum levels of dihydrosphingosine 1-phosphate (dhS1P), sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P), ceramide (Cer), hexosylceramide (Hex Cer), SM, lactosylceramide (Lac Cer), Deoxy dihydroceramide (Deoxy dhCer), Deoxy ceramide (Deoxy Cer), were significantly decreased in the COVID-19 group (all p < 0.01, Fig. 1, Supplemental Table 3). Moreover, Cer, Hex Cer, SM, Lac Cer and Deoxy dh Cer (all p < 0.01, Supplemental Table 3) levels were also significantly decreased in the PASC group compared to healthy controls. Cer (p = 0.0961) levels were also observed in the PASC subgroup compared with the group without PASC, but these differences did not reach statistical significance. Other lipids showed no significant differences between COVID-19 and PASC in this analysis. In contrast, compared with the healthy control group, Ceramide 1-phosphate (Cer1P) levels (p < 0.001), were elevated in both the COVID-19 groups. Difference in serum glycerophospholipid levels between participants with COVID-19 with PASC and those without PASC

Subsequently, we evaluated the serum glycerophospholipid levels to compare the profiles between healthy controls and individuals with COVID-19, both with and without PASC. An overview of the alterations in all measured serum glycerophospholipids is presented in Fig. 2. The COVID-19 groups exhibited significantly reduced LPC, lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI), phosphatidylserine (PS), and phosphatidylglycerol (PG) levels compared with the healthy control group (all p < 0.001, Supplemental Table 4). LPI have also significant differences between healthy control and PASC groups (p = 0.0235). In contrast, the COVID-19 group demonstrated significantly elevated lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE), PC, and PE levels compared with the healthy control group (each p < 0.01). Moreover, PC (p = 0.041) and PE (p = < 0.01) levels differed significantly in the PASC group compared with healthy controls. However, no glycerophospholipid shows a statistically significant difference between PASC and non-PASC participants. These findings showed that differences in sphingolipids and glycerophospholipid were primarily observed between COVID-19 participants and healthy controls. The group × time interaction was not significant for most lipids; however, PG showed a significant group × time interaction (group × time: F(6, 669.779) = 5.373, p < 0.001) (Table 1). Using the phase beyond Day 25 as the reference, fixed-effect estimates indicated that overall PG levels were higher at Day 10–12 (β = 49,699.773, SE = 9,092.193, t = 5.466, p < 0.001), whereas other time windows did not differ significantly. Discussion

In this study, we mainly focused on characterizing the modulations of serum sphingolipids and glycerophospholipids in response to COVID-19 and PASC. Utilizing a rigorously designed study framework, we systematically analyzed the modulations of the lipidome using serum samples including LMM analysis to identify that no sphingolipids and glycerophospholipids exhibited differences across the COVID-19 and PASC groups. While the results are exploratory in nature, group x time interaction in LMM analysis suggest that PG appeared to be a bioactive lipid between the COVID-19 and PASC groups. Given the small PASC sample size and the signal was observed only in a specific time points, these findings are hypothesis-generating, and require confirmation in larger, well-powered, and independently validated cohorts.

A previous study investigating PASC and lipidomics analyzed plasma samples from 147 patients with PASC and evaluated approximately 400 polar lipids4. The study noted increased LPG and PE levels, along with decreased LPC levels, suggesting that these lipids can serve as potential biomarkers for PASC. In contrast, our study did not identify any PASC-specific lipids, leading to a different conclusion. In an exploratory data of time x group LMM analysis of this study, PG levels in particular Day10-12, and PG (32:0) and PG (34:4) suggested differences that may indicate between patients with COVID-19 with or without PASC (Table 1, Supplemental Table 5). The association between PG and COVID-19 has been previously reported. PG levels decrease in response to COVID-1910,12,13. PG is a second-order glycerophospholipid in pulmonary surfactant14, an essential material that covers the inner surface of the alveoli to maintain a low variable surface tension. The pulmonary surfactant is affected in alveolar type 2 cells (ATII cells), which are the main targets of SARS-CoV-2 infection. More than 90% of the surfactant composition comprises glycerophosoholipids15,16. The reduced PG levels observed in COVID-19 may be attributed to various factors, including direct viral damage to ATII cells, inflammation-induced surfactant dysfunction, enzymatic degradation, and potential alterations in lipid metabolism10,12,13.

This study had several limitations. First, although PASC presents with a diverse spectrum of clinical phenotypes, our study analyzed PASC as a single unified group. Although this approach may overlook subtype-specific variations, it facilitates the identification of common molecular signatures across the broader PASC population, which may serve as more universally applicable biomarkers. Second, this study was based on biospecimens collected during the acute phase of COVID-19. Given the uncertainty in case numbers during the early COVID-19 pandemic, the sample size could not be prospectively determined. All eligible cases within the prespecified period were included. And the control samples were obtained from an existing cohort used in another COVID-19 study3,10, matching between cases and controls was not feasible. This may have introduced unmeasured or residual confounding. To evaluate the persistence and progression of the observed lipidomic profiles, longitudinal follow-up and sampling are needed. Conversely, the early timing of sample collection may provide a valuable opportunity to identify prognostic biomarkers that can predict subsequent PASC development during the acute phase.

In conclusion, individuals who progressed to PASC following acute COVID-19 infection exhibited distinct alterations in no specific sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid species. Exploratory analyses suggested that PG may showed signals of potential group x time related differences, but further validation is needed.

Methods

Samples

In this study, we utilized the data on the serum levels of bioactive lipids in the residual serum samples as outlined in our previous studies3,10. This study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for the sample analysis was obtained from some participants. For those unable to provide written consent owing to hospital discharge or transfer, an opt-out mechanism on a website was used to obtain informed consent. The University of Tokyo Medical Research Center Ethics Committee approved the study design (2020206NI).

Serum samples

We collected residual serum samples from 212 participants diagnosed with COVID-19 who had not received prior SARS-CoV-2 vaccination between April 2020 and June 2021. The diagnosis was based on reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assay results. We aimed to collect one sample from each participant at as many of the following eight timepoints as possible: pre-onset, Days 1–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12, 13–15, 16–18, 19–24, and beyond Day 25. Considering the diverse timing of RT-PCR testing among patients, we used the day following symptom onset as the initial measurement in our lipid modulation investigation. PASC was defined based on the World Health Organization criteria17 : persistence or onset of new symptoms occurring 3 months after the initial SARS-CoV-2 infection, with symptoms lasting for at least 2 months and not attributable to alternative diagnoses. Participants in this study were identified from among patients who sought care at the post-COVID-19 sequelae outpatient clinic of our institution. The control groups comprised 115 samples collected from 109 healthy volunteers.

LC–MS/MS methods for measurement of sphingolipids and glycerophospholipids

For sphingolipids, 10 μL of each serum sample was combined with 10 μL of internal standards (SPLASH LipidoMix™, Cer1P-d7, HexCer, C17S1P, C17dhS1P, C17:1 sphingosine [Sph], C17:1 dihydrosphingosine [dhSph], and d18:1/17:0 ceramide; Avanti Polar Lipids) to achieve a final concentration of 1 ng/mL. Sphingolipids were extracted by adding 100 μL of methanol containing 0.1% formic acid (Wako Pure Chemical Industries) and 80 μL of acetonitrile (Wako Pure Chemical Industries). After 5 min of sonication, the mixtures were centrifuged at 16,400 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis as previously described and validated18.

For LC–MS/MS, 5.0 μL of each extract was injected and separated on a normal-phase column (U-Inert Sustain Swift Amide, 3 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm; UP). Gradient elution was performed at 0.6 mL/min using solvent A (10 mM ammonium acetate in 95% acetonitrile/5% water) and solvent B (10 mM ammonium acetate in 50% acetonitrile). The gradient program was as follows: 99% A/1% B for 1 min, 90% A/10% B for 1 min, 60% A/40% B for 3 min, and 20% A/80% B for 2 min, for a total run time of 11 min. Column and sample temperatures were maintained at 50 °C and 10 °C, respectively. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in positive electrospray ionization mode with cone gas at 50 L/h, desolvation gas at 1000 L/h, a source temperature of 150 °C, and a desolvation temperature of 600 °C. Sphingolipids were quantified using multiple reaction monitoring in positive-ion mode, and data were processed with MassLynx and TargetLynx XS (Waters Corporation).

This approach enabled monitoring of multiple sphingolipid species, including Cer1P species, Lac Cer species, dhCer species, Deoxy dhCer species, DeocyCer species, S1P species, dhS1P species, Sph species, dhSph species, Cer species, Hex Cer species, and SM species. Assay performance was reliable, with coefficients of variation below 20%.

For glycerophospholipids, two independent LC–MS/MS methods were used using an LC8060 system comprising a quantum ultra-triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), as previously described and validated19,20. Multiple glycerophospholipids were monitored, including LPC, LPS, LPE, LPG, LPI, PC, PE, PI, PG, and PS.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and analysis were conducted using JMP Pro (version 17, SAS Institute, Tokyo, Japan), Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (Chicago, IL, USA), Metaboanalysis 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/), and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). We used a Kruskal–Wallis test and/or multivariable comparison with false discovery rate correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) to evaluate statistically significant differences in sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid levels among the groups, based on median lipid levels. The results regarding lipid levels were presented as means and standard deviations. Each bioactive lipid, we fitted a linear mixed model with fixed effects for group (COVID-19 with or without PASC), time since symptom onset (categorized into eight intervals: days 1–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12, 13–15, 16–18, 19–24, and ≥ 25), and their interaction (group x time). The reference level for time was the ≥ 25 days category. Repeated measurements within individuals (case ID) were modeled using a compound symmetry (CS) covariance structure. Given short follow-up period was short and the limited number of time points, we selected the CS structure to facilitate stable estimation. As a sensitivity analysis, we also evaluated an autoregressive covariance structure (AR (1)). No random effects were included within-subject correlation was modeled through the residual covariance. Models were estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) using SPSS MIXED and using Type III tests with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Estimated marginal means (EMMs) were used to compare groups, and pairwise contrasts were Bonferroni-adjusted. Missingness was handled naturally by REML estimation under a missing-at-random assumption. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No observations were excluded as outliers, and all models were fitted to the raw data. Outlier handling was not applied for statistical inference. Total lipid levels were calculated by summing the values of each lipid species and were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test and/or a multivariable, linear mixed-effects model.
