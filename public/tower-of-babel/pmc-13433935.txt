Multicentric data challenge for artificial intelligence-based classification of leukocytes: results from the CytologIA consortium
Authors: Elise Sourdeau, Franck Geneviève, Lucile Baseggio, Bouchra Badaoui, Charles Chevalier, Mélanie Pannetier, Alexandre Janel, Ahmadreza Arbab, Laurent Weinmann, Camille Debord, Patrick Cohen, Agathe Maillon, Sandrine Girard, Frédérique Dubois, Véronique Baccini, Pierre Lemaire, Yaël Berda-Haddad, Thomas Tassin, Anne-Camille Faure, Soufiane Azdad, Samy Dahmani, Lauriane Armand, Agathe Delaune, Naama Bak, Paul Steffen, Eric Ben Hamou, Valérie Bardet, Thomas Boyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate morphological classification of blood leukocytes remains a cornerstone of hematological diagnostics but still relies on manual expertise, leading to variability across laboratories. The CytologIA data challenge was organized to benchmark artificial intelligence (AI) models for automated classification of normal and pathological leukocytes from peripheral blood smears. Twenty hematology laboratories from France, Belgium, and Switzerland contributed to a multicentric, expert-annotated database of 69,168 images encompassing 23 leukocyte classes. A total of 245 teams from academia, hospitals, and industry participated in the challenge. The top-performing model—combining a YOLOX-based detection module with an ensemble of transformer and convolutional classifiers—achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.94 on the hidden test set, markedly outperforming the baseline CNN (0.82). While abundant cell types such as neutrophils were identified with near-perfect accuracy (>0.97), rarer and morphologically overlapping categories remained challenging. All data and models were released openly on data.gouv.fr and GitHub to ensure full reproducibility. CytologIA represents the first large-scale, open, and collaborative AI benchmark in hematological morphology, establishing a new reference for the development of robust, transferable diagnostic algorithms across institutionsSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Microscopic analysis of blood smears prepared with the May-Grünwald Giemsa stain is an essential part of the diagnosis and monitoring of haemopathies1. Qualitative and quantitative analysis of leukocytes, as well as red blood cells and platelets, is essential and provides an important guide to the suspected presence of a blood disorder. Traditionally, this morphological analysis has been carried out using an optical microscope. This method remains the gold standard, but it is time-consuming and has a number of other drawbacks, in particular, high inter-operator variability, lack of traceability of the cells observed and high observer fatigue2. In recent years, there has been a growing demand for the development of automated microscopy systems, mainly to save technical time. Most of them use a digital camera coupled to a computer. Digitized images of individual cells are then classified by an algorithm according to parameters such as geometry, color or texture3; an analysis of erythrocyte and platelet morphology can also be carried out4. Even if the recognition of physiological cells is correct, these algorithms are no longer effective when it comes to recognizing pathological cells, which creates a disparity in correct diagnosis between laboratories, since the morphologist’s experience is fundamental to the diagnosis of blood diseases.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has taken an interest in the field of cellular hematology in blood or bone marrow, particularly through the development of convolutional neural networks (CNNs). In general, the image recognition process to assist the reading of pathological or physiological blood smears can be divided into 5 stages: sample processing and collection, preprocessing, detection and segmentation, feature extraction and selection, assessment and classification. These steps apply equally to cell recognition on blood and bone marrow slides5–7. Neural networks, combined with classifiers, can also carry out classification tasks. However, the vast majority of these studies are single-center studies, carried out on a local database and therefore generating recognition algorithms that cannot be generalized.

The CytologIA data challenge is a healthcare data challenge organized jointly by the French-Speaking Cellular Hematology Group (GFHC), the Health Data Hub (Paris, France), Algoscope (Compiègne, France) and Trustii.io (Nanterre, France) and was held from 29 November 2024 to 06 January 2025. The aim of this data challenge is to develop an AI system for classifying normal and pathological leukocytes, based on a multicentre database of images from blood smears, acquired with an automated microscope, to develop reliable and accessible solutions for automating the analysis. Then, it would allow patients to be diagnosed quickly and accurately, regardless of their geographical location. This project was supported as part of the France 2030 plan. Here we present the results of this data challenge, which brought together 245 participants from around the world.

Results

A total of 245 teams registered for the CytologIA data challenge and submitted at least one valid model for evaluation. Participants represented a broad spectrum of expertise, including academic research groups, hospital laboratories, start-ups, and independent data scientists. This diversity highlights the strong interest in the application of artificial intelligence to hematological diagnostics.

Overall performance

The official evaluation metric selected by the organizers was the balanced accuracy, in order to account for the multi-class setting (23 leukocyte categories) and the variable frequency of each cell type. This metric is well-suited for imbalanced datasets, as it accounts for the sensitivity of each class independently of its frequency. The baseline model provided by the challenge organizers—based on a conventional convolutional neural network trained without sophisticated optimization—achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.82 on the hidden test set.

In contrast, the top three teams reached 0.94, 0.935, and 0.93, respectively, representing a clear improvement over the baseline and establishing a new benchmark for automated leukocyte classification. These results are extremely close and showed no difference in diagnostic performance. Across all valid submissions, the mean balanced accuracy was 0.87 (SD = 0.05), demonstrating that a majority of participants were able to substantially surpass the reference model. The top nine on the private leaderboard all scored 0.93, with very close margins in the three decimal places.

Per-class performance

Although overall accuracy was high, the classification performance varied across the 23 leukocyte categories. Common cell types such as neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils were predicted with near-perfect accuracy (>0.97), reflecting their abundance in the training set and distinctive morphology.

In contrast, rare or morphologically ambiguous categories, such as immature granulocytes and undifferentiated blasts, proved significantly more challenging, with balanced accuracies frequently dropping between 0.80 and 0.90. Misclassifications occurred predominantly between morphologically similar immature cell populations, suggesting that the intrinsic overlap in cytological appearance remains difficult to resolve even for state-of-the-art algorithms. Nevertheless, certain pathological classes were recognized with excellent accuracy, such as APL blasts with a score of 0.98. Conversely, lymphocytes were not recognized with excellent performance (score of 0.80).

Characteristics of the top-performing model

These strategies collectively contributed to the robust performance and highlight the methodological convergence towards modern best practices in medical image classification.

A two-stage approach was applied to accurately detect and classify white blood cells (WBCs) from microscopic images using a robust and interpretable machine learning pipeline (Fig. 2). While end-to-end object detection architectures such as the YOLO family are fast and efficient for general detection tasks, they often struggle with fine-grained visual distinctions between closely related WBC subtypes. To address this limitation, the problem was divided into two specialized stages: a first stage dedicated to cell localization, and a second focusing on subtype classification.

Pipeline overview of the top-performing model.

A two-step approach was used, combining a YOLOX-type algorithm with several convolutional neural network classifiers.

In the first stage, the goal was to localize all white blood cells within each image using a single-class object detector. A modified version of YOLOX was employed, trained with the Generalized Intersection-over-Union (GIoU) loss to improve bounding-box precision. Some light data preparation was performed, including the removal of duplicate or slightly misaligned annotations and the generation of additional background images to reduce false positives. Training relied on strong image augmentations such as MixUp, Mosaic, and horizontal or vertical flips, over 150 and 110 epochs for the two YOLOX variants (512 and 640). The resulting models achieved a mean average precision (mAP@0.95) of 0.943, providing accurate and reliable detections for the subsequent classification stage.

The second stage focused on classifying the cropped WBC regions, each extended by a 16-pixel margin to preserve contextual cues. A diverse set of model families was explored, including EfficientNetV2 convolutional networks, transformer architectures such as ViT, NextViT, and TinyViT, and a self-supervised foundation based on DINOv2. Both multi-class and multi-label formulations were considered, with some models incorporating an additional background class. Data augmentation again played a crucial role, combining geometric transformations, brightness and contrast shifts, noise injection, CutOut, CutMix, and MixUp to enhance generalization. Models were trained using the AdamW optimizer with cosine-annealing learning rate schedules and a mix of cross-entropy and focal losses, leveraging mixed-precision training for computational efficiency. Exact augmentation probabilities were tuned empirically during model development.

To ensure robust validation, a four-fold cross-validation scheme with multi-label stratification was used, balancing complex label combinations and maintaining representative distributions across folds. This strategy, combined with careful control of data leakage, was key to achieving stable and trustworthy performance estimates.

During inference, the best-performing models from both multi-class and multi-label families were combined into an ensemble. Weight optimization between the two groups (approximately 45% and 55%) maximized the cross-validation F1 score while respecting the competition’s strict 500-millisecond runtime constraint per image. The final ensemble achieved an internal cross-validation F1 score of 0.9313, a public leaderboard score of 0.9378, and a private leaderboard score of 0.9371, demonstrating strong generalization and robustness.

Finally, we externally validated this algorithm on a public database containing 12 classes of normal and pathological leukocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, promyelocytes, myelocytes, metamyelocytes, monoblasts, myeloblasts, erythroblasts, smudge cells)8. The average F1 score obtained was 0.632 but revised to 0.96 taking into account the imbalance.

Discussion

AI models are highly dependent on the quantity and quality of the database they use. This is particularly true in hematology, where limited databases can lead to difficulties in identifying rare entities. Furthermore, data labeling requires human experts, which increases the subjectivity of classification, particularly when the number of experts is very limited. Among the publicly available databases, the database compiled by Acevedo’s team, containing approximately 17,000 images and 8 classes of leukocytes, and the Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) database, with more than 189,000 images and 16 classes, are among the most diverse9,10. In another study, using the same database, Acevedo et al. used CNNs to correctly classify these images with an excellent F1 score of 96%, confirming that this model is one of the most effective for leukocyte recognition, even though this technique is not methodologically innovative and reflects the state of the art9. Another team proposed a very interesting workflow to enable an EfficientNet network to efficiently label cells with fine-tuning and color transformation11. Finally, in a similar vein to the data challenge, a dataset—reviewed by two experts—comprises 18 classes of normal and pathological leukocytes, with nearly 26,000 images of a size comparable to those in CytologIA, sourced from 72 patients12. Nevertheless, the databases used in these studies may lack classes and, above all, variability, as they are single-center datasets. Thus, the vast majority of AI models cannot be transferred from one center to another, as they are trained only on their local database13. This observation explains the great value of the CytologIA database, as it was compiled by 20 different centers using different spreading and staining methods, thus introducing greater variability, which makes it possible to generate more powerful and robust models that can be transposed from one center to another. In addition, the images were labeled several times, not only by the morphologists at each center that submitted the images, but also by four additional experts, thereby ensuring the robustness of the database and limiting the inter-observer variability greatly.

One of the disappointing findings of the data challenge is the low rate of lymphocyte recognition by the best algorithms, whereas models with superior performance have been published in the literature, whether for reactive lymphocytes or lymphoma cells14,15. One explanation is that the database includes physiological lymphocytes as well as CLL lymphocytes, which are morphologically close. This may explain the poor performance of the algorithm. The same applies to granulocyte precursors, where performance is not as good as for other classes, as a significant number of cells were present between two successive stages of differentiation (promyelocytes and myelocytes, for example). We have not found any explanation in flow cytometry or genetic data to account for these discrepancies. This confusion between immature granular cells should, however, be viewed with caution, given its limited clinical significance, unlike the confusion between a reactive lymphocyte and a lymphoma cell.

The average F1 score obtained on the external validation cohort is also disappointing, well below the algorithm’s performance on the CytologIA database. Several hypotheses can be put forward to explain this score: the magnification of the cells to x1000, compared to x500 for the data challenge, the poor quality of a large proportion of the images (blurred) and the significant imbalance in the number of images per class (for example, there were 16 images in the monoblast category and 8433 in the neutrophil category). Perhaps some pre-processing normalization (smear preparation…) or domain adaptation would have helped to mitigate these confounders before validation. This raises the question of whether standardized preprocessing is necessary and, if so, which factors actually affect the model’s generalization ability, given that different staining methods introduce variability and thus allow for the generation of more robust models. For the data challenge, all leukocyte classes contained at least 1000 images, which was a prerequisite for them to be offered to competitors.

One of CytologIA’s first objectives is to integrate the winning algorithm into automated microscope software and work on a solution capable of performing a formula on at least 100 leukocytes, as CytologIA operates on a single-cell level. The algorithms integrated into these systems perform very well with normal cells but fail as soon as pathological cells are present on the slide. This is one of the major limitations of the CytologIA data challenge, as the models were trained on isolated images containing a single cell rather than on entire slides. This critical issue will need to be addressed in the future to enable the algorithm to generate a formula and thus enable its use in clinical practice.

The CytologIA data challenge was carried out on blood smear images. One of the challenges ahead is to carry out a challenge of this scale on images from bone marrow smears. Here again, the number of public datasets remains very limited and monocentric. One solution could come from the synthetic generation of bone marrow cell images, enabling AI models to be trained on databases designed according to the center’s needs16. Developing a model to classify images obtained from bone marrow smears would be a significant step toward standardizing the reporting of results in clinical laboratories.

Methods

Creation of the database

The constitution of the database is summarized in Fig. 3. Briefly, the images are retrieved from the participating centers retrospectively and anonymized, all acquired with an automated microscope. The images to be uploaded to the platform are selected by the morphologist at each center. Importantly, these images are labeled by the morphologist and reviewed and corrected if necessary by four other expert morphologists to ensure the database is highly reliable. Cohen’s kappa was 0.96, using a cytology atlas as a reference. Finally, after a final verification step, the database is divided into two parts: one for training the algorithms (46,479 images, 67% of the database) and the other for the testing phase (22,689 images, 33% of the database). A total of 20 French-speaking centers (France, Belgium, Switzerland) participated in the development of the database.

Process for creating the CytologIA database used for the data challenge.

The training and test databases represented 67% and 33%, respectively.

Database content

23 different classes of normal and pathological leukocytes were recovered in satisfactory proportions, namely more than 1000 images per class, to ensure good image variability and avoid overfitting of the algorithms. The images were captured by automated microscopes, generally with a resolution of 370 × 368 pixels, corresponding to an area of 13 × 13 centimetres.

To build this database, we were able to collect enough images for 11 classes of physiological leukocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, metamyelocytes, myelocytes, promyelocytes, erythroblasts, reactive lymphocytes, large granular lymphocytes (LGL)), 10 classes of pathological leukocytes (faggot cells from acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), myeloblasts from acute myeloid leukemia (AML), lymphoblasts, monoblasts, undifferentiated blast cells, lymphoma cells from various lymphomas (chronic lymphocytic leukemia, follicular lymphoma, mantle cell lymphoma, Sézary syndrome and marginal zone lymphoma)) and two other elements that can be found on blood slides (smudge cells, giant platelets).
