A lightweight deep learning method to identify different types of cervical cancer
Authors: Md Humaion Kabir Mehedi, Moumita Khandaker, Shaneen Ara, Md Ashraful Alam, M F Mridha, Zeyar Aung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer is the second most common cancer in women’s bodies after breast cancer. Cervical cancer develops from dysplasia or cervical intraepithelial neoplasm (CIN), the early stage of the disease, and is characterized by the aberrant growth of cells in the cervix lining. It is primarily caused by Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection, which spreads through sexual activity. This study focuses on detecting cervical cancer types efficiently using a novel lightweight deep learning model named CCanNet, which combines squeeze block, residual blocks, and skip layer connections. SipakMed, which is not only popular but also publicly available dataset, was used in this study. We conducted a comparative analysis between several transfer learning and transformer models such as VGG19, VGG16, MobileNetV2, AlexNet, ConvNeXT, DeiT_tiny, MobileViT, and Swin Transformer with the proposed CCanNet. Our proposed model outperformed other state-of-the-art models, with 98.53% accuracy and the lowest number of parameters, which is 1,274,663. In addition, accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1 score were used to evaluate the performance of the models. Finally, explainable AI (XAI) was applied to analyze the performance of CCanNet and ensure the results were trustworthy.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Cancer type identification, Deep learning, Lightweight algorithmSubject terms: Cancer imaging, Mathematics and computing, Computational science

Introduction

The term “Cervical cancer” is characterized by an abrupt and unusual growth of cells in the cervical lining. The cause of this disease is the human papillomavirus (HPV) attack on the body and the medium of transmission is sexual intercourse. This abnormal cell growth is called dysplasia or Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN) which is the first stage of cervical cancer1. Cervical cancer is primarily attributable to chronic infection with specific types of HPV, with HPV 16 and 18 accounting for about 70% of cervical cancer cases. Despite the availability of HPV vaccines, the virus still infects many people worldwide, particularly in low-income areas. Some more facts are causing cervical cancer, for instance, smoking increases the risk by two times because chemicals from cigarettes mutate cervical cells’ DNA and also compromise the immune system to eliminate the HPV virus. Immunosuppressed women, for example, those with HIV or undergoing treatment for AIDS or with other immunosuppressive disorders or taking immunosuppressive therapies are also at a higher risk because their immune systems are not strong enough to clear persistent HPV infections. Taking oral contraceptives has adverse health effects among women especially if they use it for the long term, it increases the chances of cervical cancer mainly because of hormonal effects on cervical cells. Additionally, women who have successfully carried multiple pregnancies to term face an increased risk, as the cervix becomes particularly vulnerable during pregnancy due to hormonal changes and the physical stress of childbirth. Other factors associated with increased risks include engaging in sexual activities at early ages with multiple sexual partners and economic factors including low access to adequate healthcare. Traditional treatments include surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, according to the patient’s case study2.

Cervical cancer alone affects the lives of 57,000 women every year with 30,000 deaths recorded annually. Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women all over the world. In 2020, 604,000 new cases were recorded, and the death toll was 342,0003. Most of them are detected in developing and developed nations, and preventive methods including HPV vaccination and regular examination, are still hard to come by in these parts. Surveys indicate that low and middle-income countries, including Malawi in Sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and some parts of Asia, have experienced the highest increase in these incidences. In South Asian countries like India and Bangladesh cervical cancer still poses a great threat to women and this is attributed to poor awareness, lack of immunization, and poor cervical cancer screening services. As per the data of GLOBOCAN 2020, on the one hand, in Bangladesh, 8267 women are newly diagnosed every year with cervical cancer and 5214 women die from cervical cancer annually. On the other hand, in India 123,907 new cervical cancer cases are estimated in women annually and 77,348 women die of cervical cancer annually. This is equivalent to a mortality-to-incidence ratio of 62.4%4.

On a larger scale, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that cervical cancer results in over $3 billion in lost productivity globally each year. Globally in 2020, there were an estimated 604,127 cervical cancer cases and 341,831 deaths, with a corresponding age-standardized incidence of 13.3 cases per 100,000 women-years (95% CI 13.3–13.3) and mortality rate of 7.2 deaths per 100,000 women-years (95% CI 7.2–7.3). Cervical cancer rate per 100,000 women ranged from 2.2 (2.9–2.4) in Iraq to 84.6 (74.8–94.3) in Eswatini. Mortality varied between 1.0 (95% CI 0.8–1.2) in Switzerland to 55.7 (95% CI 47.7–63.7) in Eswatini. Age-standardized incidence ranged from 67.9 (95% CI 65.7–70.1) per 100,000 person-years in Malawi and 65.5 (95% CI 63.0–67.9) in Zambia in Africa, 36.6 (95% CI 35.0–38.2) in Bolivia and 34.1 (95% CI 32.1–36). Such expenses in addition to stripping away female employees have a definite economic and social cost5.

In the fight against cancer, early diagnostic techniques will aid in addressing the disease in its early stages and implementing preventive measures. Gardasil and Cervarix vaccines can prevent cervical cancer and HPV and it is most effective when given to girls and boys of 9 to 14 years before infection. Studies, such as “A Hybrid Deep Learning-Based Automated System for HPV Vaccination Impact Prediction”6 as noted by others Aylin et al., where robust vaccination programs are in places such as Australia and the UK, cervical cancer incidences are considerably low. Preliminary data sustained that vaccination in the ‘adjuvant’ setting following conization reduces the risk of recurrent Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN) by about 50–80%7–9. However, as with many other prices, the issue at hand proves extremely costly in low-income countries such as Bangladesh, where awareness is low, and the essentials of implementation; vaccination infrastructure remains severely underdeveloped. A Cost-Effective Analysis of HPV Vaccination in Low-Income Countries10 calls for a support system to overcome these barriers and enhance vaccination coverage in developing nations. As the majority of HPV-related lesions occur in the uterine cervix, hysterectomy protects from the risk of developing most HPV-related lesions11.

One of the studies on cervical cancer found that while CNN models performed similarly to ViT models, CNNs were less effective at handling image complexity, particularly with diverse datasets6. Another study, which applied the Swin Transformer model for brain tumor classification, was limited by its evaluation on a single MRI dataset, affecting its generalizability10. One more study where a modified Swin Transformer for skin cancer diagnosis, despite improvements, had an accuracy of 89.36%, struggling with the diverse characteristics of skin cancer images12. A further study that utilized an enhanced EfficientNetv2 model for brain tumor classification achieved high accuracy but faced limitations due to its reliance on a single dataset, affecting its real-world applicability13.

Related works

In recent years, researchers have explored and exploited several models namely, machine learning (ML), and deep learning (DL). In addition, there adaptive learning-based methods are available for elucidating crack image datasets. In the upcoming years, this has not only dramatically escalated the development but also the enactment of ML models. Multiple investigations have been performed on the cataloging of cervical cancers. The alarming statistics of cervical cancer rates globally have pushed researchers to perform more work in the identification, treatment, and diagnosis of this disease. As a result, much research has been conducted in the recent past on image data sets using deep learning methodologies. By analyzing medical images and databases, it has been understood that technologies have greatly impacted the efficiency and accuracy of identifying several types of disorders. For instance, machine learning and deep learning techniques have had a positive impact on Pap smear analysis for cervical cancer screening to overcome frequent challenges such as unauthorized data and image quality problems.

Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models are essential for advancing medical diagnostics. Pap smear analysis (cervical cancer detection) was performed using ML and DL methodologies while labeling common snags such as data insufficiency and image quality issues14.

Cervical cancer, for the most part, is caused by human papillomavirus (HPV), and is a major health concern for women. Successful treatment depends on early detection. Traditional methods such as the Pap test, colonoscopy, and HPV have been tested using advanced ML and DL models to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Kalbhor et al.15 and Devi and Thirumurugan proposed the successful classification of cervical cancer using multiple ML techniques. However, these techniques achieve high accuracy rates. Recent studies have employed septet mechanisms either trait engineering to improve classification performance or achieve notable accuracy improvements.

Deep learning models have some rare abilities in medical image analysis. First, the multi-axis vision transformer framework uses ConvNeXtv2. Second, GRN-based MLP blocks attained 99.02% accuracy on the SIPaKMeD dataset16. Other noteworthy models included ResNet50V2, InceptionV3, and Xception, with ResNet50V2 displaying the highest accuracy for cervical smear images. progress urges that deep learning models to be replaced more incisively. Moreover, diagnostic procedures, enabling earlier and more accurate disease detection are easily improved through DL.

Furthermore, cervical cancer, ML, and DL models are widely used to diagnose other categories of cancers and medical conditions, such as breast cancer, lung cancer, and colorectal cancer. In addition, it is instrumental in Alzheimer’s diagnosis, medical imaging, and pathology image segmentation. Advanced diagnostic tools, for instance, EEG encoding, CT imaging, and surgical navigation, are also being developed using these technologies.

There are several feature reduction and classification techniques. Efficient feature depletion and classification are crucial for handling medical datasets; however, they often face problems owing to issues such as class imbalance and missing data. Several studies have attempted in different manners to overcome these challenges. In addition, Tanimu et al.17 not only used the decision tree (DT) classification algorithm but also LASSO for reducing features, addressing dataset challenges with SMOTETomek to attain exorbitant accuracy. An excellent classification performance was achieved by Abdoh et al.18 through the use of random forest (RF) with SMOTE along with additional feature reduction approaches.

Adaptive learning-based methods are based on improving the model performance by addressing particular provocations such as class imbalance and incomplete datasets. For example, to generate synthetic data points for underrepresented classes, the ADASYN algorithm was used to stabilize the dataset and increase the detection accuracy of cervical cancer (Pap smear images). In this paper19, the commonly occurring obstacles in PAP smear such as insufficient data sufficiency not being enough, images containing varieties, and lack of clarity in quality have been minimized by providing an advanced framework multi-axis vision transformer. By integrating ADASYN with support vector machine (SVM) and convolutional neural network (CNN), researchers achieved an accuracy of 99.99%. Combined with ensemble learning techniques namely extreme gradient boosting, random forest, and extra tree classifier, this method acquires not only a high accuracy of 99.41% in guessing cervical cancer, but also shows the value of adaptive learning in handling incomplete and imbalanced datasets.

Transfer learning-based techniques support the knowledge obtained from initially learned models to improve the performance of related tasks when data is shortened. Moreover, pre-trained deep convolutional neural network (CNN) models were used to classify Pap smear images into seven groups in the setting of cervical cancer detection, and DenseNet-201 (a pre-trained CNN model) was employed for this multi-class classification task, displaying higher-ranking accuracy and performance.

In this paper20, by introducing a multi-axis vision transformer, the authors address common barriers in PAP smear analysis such as scant data and poor image quality. This framework featured ConvNeXtv2 and GRN-based MLP blocks and achieved 99.02% accuracy using the SIPaKMeD dataset. Another study21 elucidates that an ensemble of machine learning algorithms such as random forest and decision tree classifiers, achieves a 98.01% accuracy in trial detection. For fragmented datasets, a model using not only ADASYN with SVM, but also CNN features ensured 99.99% accuracy22. Moreover, using SVM and ResNet101, a multi-class procedure achieved 100% accuracy28.

Methodology

In this section, we discuss the working procedures of our project as shown in Fig. 1, including the dataset, preprocessing, models, and evaluation metrics.

Workflow of our research from dataset to cervical cancer prediction.

Dataset description

We used a popular, publicly available dataset named SipakMed (cipakmed)29 that was taken from Kaggle. In the dataset, there were 3 stages of cells: normal, benign, and abnormal, in a total of 966 images. As in a single image with multiple types of cells, the author cropped the images to create 5 types of cells, which fell under the 3 stages of cells. Here superficial-intermediate and parabasal fell under normal, metaplastic fell in benign, and lastly, dyskeratotic (diskeratotic) and koilocytotic fell under abnormal cells. There was a total of 4049 cells. In Fig. 2, each image represented their class.

Sample images from the dataset 1 for each class.

Dataset preprocessing

In the dataset, all the images were not the same in size, so we resized them to 128 × 128. We normalized the image by dividing 1.0/255 and rescaling the image range from 0 to 1. Different data augmentation techniques have also been applied, such as width shift, height shift, and horizontal flips.

Deep learning models

We applied four transfer learning models to the proposed dataset. To train the models we used pretrained weights of the models and then added one global average pooling, two dense with 512 and 256 neuron. Subsequently, a dropout layer with a value of 0.3. Finally, an output layer with number of classes was predicted.

The input passes a global average pooling (GAP) operation, which calculates the average value of each feature in the single feature map. This average value is then returned as the output node. GAP divides input feature map in non-overlapping regions in matrix form39. In our applied model global average p they put this pulling operation when there was pooling Layer reduces the dimensions of input layer in huge amount also lessening the number of parameters which lead to deduction in overfitting. Data is passed from Max Pool layer after filtrating the maximum valued feature to the GAP layer taking the average value.

Why do we use global average pooling layer, not flatten layer?

There is a layer named flatten layer used in the fully connected neural network where every node is densely connected to each other, pooled feature maps are passed on input nodes. These pooled feature maps are 2 dimensional, which is transformed into 1 dimensional through flattening. We have not used flatten layer in our model, instead global average pooling Layer has been implemented. GAP and flattening both are used to deduct the dimensions of feature maps in CNN before putting as input in a fully connected network40. But GAP is considered as a better technique for various reasons. In GAP the number of parameters after taking the average out of convolutional layer decreases. As it generates a vector of only considering parameters of D. Whereas flattening generates a vector of ( which feeds on a large number of parameters depending on Hand Wvalues in the fully connected network. Flattening may lead to overfitting by passing on many parameters and not having a large enough dataset. In GAP the risk is reduced of overfitting due to smaller number of parameters. There is a specific input size necessary for the fully connected layers in terms of flattening. But in case of GAP the input size can vary since each feature map is transformed into a single value, which gives more diversity in input sizes. GAP attains a low dimensional structure that makes the architecture simpler and easily manageable whereas flattening makes the interpretation more difficult41.

Conclusion and future works

Cervical cancer is a major concern for women in underdeveloped nations, as it is one of the leading causes of death for them. overall health. The key to reducing its catastrophic effects is prompt, automated diagnosis and treatment in the early stages. Cervical cancer detection at an early stage is more efficiently treatable. Not only does it ensure better recovery chances, but also the treatment choices are less rigorous. PAP smears and HPV testing intervene in the progression toward the advanced stages of cervical cancer, preventing further invasion. Timely treatment can reduce the common aftereffects of cancer treatment, such as future morbidity, complications, and side effects. A patient is less burdened with the expenses of treatment with a higher life expectancy if proper detection is accomplished initially. To smoothen the automatic detection process with better efficiency and cost-effectiveness, we proposed CCanNet, a deep learning model. This model analyzes medical images with high accuracy, enabling it to detect subtle abnormalities. This customized network undergoes training on a wide range of datasets, achieving a high level of specificity in distinguishing between benign and malignant lesions. An automated screening reduces the risk of false positives and false negatives. The model surpassed other state-of-the-art models such as VGG16, VGG19, MobileNetV2, AlexNet, ConvNeXT, DeiT_tiny, MobileViT, and Swin Transformer with a healthy accuracy value. This model not only achieved the highest accuracy but also had a smaller number of parameters than the others. In the future, we will apply our custom CCanNet model to other medical fields, such as breast and prostate cancer detection. It will provide an enhanced mammogram analysis platform in radiology for breast cancer detection. Also, in high resolution MRI and ultrasound image analysis for prostate tumor identification, this model can leave an impact significance. For faster performance on portable devices, we plan to simplify and optimize the model.
