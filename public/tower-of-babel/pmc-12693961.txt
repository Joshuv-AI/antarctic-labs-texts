Unlocking megawatt-peak-power laser emission with colloidal quantum dots
Authors: Yuting Wu, Hancheng Zhu, Zhigao Huang, Hao Zhang, Yinjuan Ren, Hua Shen, Yiming Wu, Yue Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Output power of a laser device critically defines the application prospects. Colloidal quantum dots (QDs) have emerged as auspicious laser media; however, the exclusively low-power emission obscures the practical deployment. Here, we resolve this challenge by designing an architecture of QD laser with unprecedented peak powers in megawatt regime. The achievement is enabled by integrating the QDs with exceptional gain metrics with a seed-amplifier laser structure. Transient spectroscopy and theoretical simulation reveal that the narrow emission linewidth and large biexciton binding energy of the QDs act synergistically to enable an enhanced gain performance with submonoexcitonic threshold and record high–saturated gain cross section. A Littman-Metcalf cavity is designed to deliver the continuously tunable laser emission, and the synchronous amplification reaches the megawatt output. The high-power QD laser exhibits high spatial coherence, linear polarization, and operational endurance, which has proved effective as the pump source for spectroscopic research.

INTRODUCTION

Colloidal quantum dots (QDs), which have been commercialized in color-conversion displays, also exhibit transformative potential as cost-effective, solution-processable laser gain media, owing to their exceptional optical properties including large absorption cross sections, near-unity photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs), and versatile cavity integration capabilities (1–4). Notably, the optical gain in QDs features broadband tunability across the entire visible spectrum, particularly in covering spectral regions where conventional laser technologies remain scanty (5–7). The yellow spectral range (576 to 597 nm), for instance, presents notable opportunities for applications in medical diagnostics, sodium guide star astronomy, and next-generation display technologies (8). Nevertheless, the “yellow gap” challenge remains unresolved, as the conventional schemes relying on nonlinear frequency doubling or organic dye laser systems are fundamentally limited by their inherent complexity, thermodynamic instability, and unsatisfactory efficacy (8, 9). As a result, QD lasers are emerging as an enabling platform for coherent light sources, offering promising solutions to overcome these longstanding technological limitations (10, 11).

The output power of a laser device critically defines the application prospects. A high peak power is imperative for diverse fields, including laser surgery, materials processing, and precision manufacturing (12–15). Since the inaugural demonstration of optical gain in colloidal QDs, substantial progress has been achieved in the development of both electrically and optically pumped QD lasers (3, 16) The key milestones encompass the realization of electrically driven amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) (17), the stable device operation from liquid QD lasers (18, 19), and the continuous-wave (cw) QD lasing (20). However, until now, the output powers of QD lasers are typically low in the milliwatt regime, with only a few demonstrations achieving watt-level peak power, which constitutes the primary barrier to practical implementations (2, 19, 21). This fundamental limitation stems from the relatively high pump thresholds, modest gain coefficients, and suboptimal pumping configurations inherent to QD gain media, leading to the poor photon-conversion efficiency and device degradation under intense photoexcitation (2, 3).

Previous investigations have established the nonradiative Auger recombination as the dominant pathway dissipating population inversion, which is primarily responsible for the high pump thresholds (22, 23). Through precise control of core-shell architectures and interfacial properties (e.g., via graded alloy shells or thick inorganic shells), researchers have developed QDs exhibiting substantially suppressed Auger recombination rates (24, 25). Paradoxically, the gain thresholds of these Auger-suppressed QDs remain prohibitively high for practical deployment, underscoring the necessity for additional threshold reduction strategies. In this context, Wu et al. (26) proposed using the charged CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnS core-gradient-shell QDs as gain media to achieve near-zero–threshold lasing. However, this approach relies on photochemical or electrochemical processes, exacerbating photodegradation under high pumping intensities and consequently constraining the attainable output power. Leveraging the efficient carrier transfer from shell to core, the low-threshold optical gain has been demonstrated in the “giant” CdSe/CdS QDs; nevertheless, their small active core volume limits the gain coefficient and the power scaling potential (27). Regarding the device architecture, while being compatible with various cavity configurations, the miniaturized microresonators (e.g., whispering gallery mode cavities and distributed feedback cavities) are predominantly used for QD lasers by virtue of their high-quality (Q) factors, which facilitates lasing initiation (28). However, the restricted footprint of these microresonator geometries restrains their power scaling capabilities. Consequently, the majority of literature on QD lasers emphasizes the spectral characteristics, while omitting the detailed reporting on output power performance.

In this work, we report the QD laser with megawatt peak powers. We use a gain medium of tailored ternary QDs featuring a narrow emission linewidth (16.5 nm) and large biexciton binding energy (50.5 meV). Corroborated by the transient spectroscopy and theoretical simulation, the QDs exhibit the submonoexcitonic threshold and record high–saturated gain cross section, which allows for the sustained increase in output emission under high pump intensities. A Littman-Metcalf cavity is designed to deliver the continuously tunable laser emission with narrow linewidth (0.17 nm), while the ensuing synchronous amplification achieves the megawatt-level output with an extraction efficiency approaching 40%. The high-power laser emission readily covers the yellow spectral range, holding the potential to solve the yellow gap challenge. Last, the application of the QD laser for spectroscopic research is demonstrated. This work opens an avenue toward the practical laser device based on colloidal QDs and may offer possibilities for applications, such as medical diagnostics, laser display, and laser manufacturing.

RESULTS

Model analysis and design of QDs with enhanced optical gain

The classic three-level model involving the equal transition energies of single-exciton and biexciton states could well describe the biexciton gain threshold in QDs (Fig. 1A) (24). However, this model neglects both the exciton-exciton interactions and the spectral broadening effects, which are prominent in QD ensembles. In real situation, the biexciton transition is shifted relative to the single-exciton analog EX by the exciton-exciton interaction ∆XX, and the transition has finite linewidth, leading to an incomplete spectral overlap in their absorption/emission spectra (Fig. 1, B and C) (26). This implies that a large biexciton shift ∆XX or a narrow linewidth Γ could suppress the ground-state absorption at the shifted energy (EX−∆XX) and allow optical gain to occur below the biexciton threshold. According to this formula, the gain threshold can reach the submonoexcitonic regime when r exceeds the critical value of ~0.4. A minimum threshold of <N0> = 0.4 (the average number of excitons per QDs <N0>) is achievable, as r surpasses ~1.5 (inset in Fig. 1D). This critical r cannot be attained in the traditional QDs due to their small ∆XX (<30 meV) and/or large Γ (>80 meV), such that the three-level model in Fig. 1A is established with the typical biexciton threshold for optical gain (2, 29). Further, the net optical absorption spectra are simulated for QDs with different r values (see details in note S1). The results demonstrate that optical gain at the submonoexcitonic level can be achieved when r is sufficiently large, which is enabled by either increasing ∆XX or reducing Γ (Fig. 1D).

Motivated by the theoretical analysis, we developed a synthetic strategy to achieve the narrow emission linewidth from CdZnSe-based core-shell QDs that have proved superior environmental stability and facile composition tunability (2, 19) (see Materials and Methods for synthesis details). Specifically, we used a kinetically controlled nucleation approach with dilute Se precursors to prolong the nucleation duration of CdSe particles, followed by the continuous supplementation of Se precursors to promote the secondary growth. These two stages yielded the large CdSe nuclei (mean size of 7.9 nm) with a uniform size distribution (fig. S1) and a moderate PL width of ~25 nm (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, the Se and Zn precursors were slowly injected at elevated temperatures, ensuring the efficient cation diffusion between the added Zn atoms and Cd atoms in nuclei to form the spherical CdZnSe cores. A ZnSe shell was then epitaxially deposited on the alloyed core, which would passivate the surface and confine the exciton inside the core. To mitigate the lattice mismatch, we introduced an alloyed ZnSeS intermediate layer and thin ZnS shell (fig. S2). Compared with the synthesis of traditional CdZnSe QDs (2, 19), the secondary growth of uniform nuclei under low nucleation rate and the concentration-controlled cation exchange is beneficial for obtaining the optically uniform QDs with narrow emission linewidth. To improve the photostability and dispersibility in solution, we posttreated the QDs with the deprotonated thiol ligands (see Materials and Methods for synthesis details). Unlike traditional thiol ligands coordinated via the weakly binding R-SH groups (30), the deprotonated RS− structure can tightly bind to the cations on the QD surface, which notably enhances the dispersion of QDs in solution and endurance under high pump intensity (fig. S3).

The final product of CdZnSe/ZnSe/ZnSeS/ZnS QDs shows a near-unity PLQY of 96% and a single-exponential PL lifetime of ~20 ns (fig. S4). The high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy image reveals the well-defined and regular faceted morphology with a diameter of ~15 nm, indicating the ordered epitaxy growth and high crystallinity (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, the energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy element mapping exhibits broad spatial distributions of Cd and Zn elements throughout the QDs, and the line scanning shows a slow decrease in the Cd signal with the increased Zn signal from the core to shell periphery, which confirm the compact compositional gradient for the QDs (Fig. 2, B and C). Figure 2D depicts the steady-state absorption and PL spectra of the QD solution. The PL spectrum exhibits a Lorentzian shape with a narrow linewidth of 16.5 nm (Γ ~ 52.3 meV), and the absorption spectrum displays the multiple exciton features, indicating the size and composition uniformity. The lowest four peaks at band edge can be assigned to the transitions of 1Se-1Shh, 1Se-1Slh, 1Se-2Shh and 1Pe-1Phh, respectively (31). These peaks are more distinct in the transient absorption (TA) spectra, and they show a sequential occupation with the increase in carrier density <N0> (see the average number of excitons per QDs <N0> in fig. S5), demonstrating the well-ordered energy level quantization of the QDs (Fig. 2E). In contrast, the conventional spherical CdZnSe-based QDs exhibit only two resolvable lowest transitions (i.e., 1Se-1Shh and 1Pe-1Phh) and a broad PL profile of ~25 nm with a Gaussian shape (fig. S6). The high spectral purity of the QDs is also associated with the reduced electron-phonon interaction in the QDs and the suppressed carrier coupling to the environment via the exciton confinement in the core (fig. S7) (32). To derive the r value, we measure the biexciton emission spectra by cw pumping. As shown in Fig. 2F, a new emission peak appears on the low-energy side of PL spectra with increasing excitation intensity and exhibits a quadratic dependence on the pump intensity, indicative of the biexciton emission (24, 25). Because of the enhanced spatial confinement of charge carriers in the gradient structure, the shift energy or ∆XX is determined to be ~50.5 meV, which, together with the narrow emission linewidth, results in a large r of 0.93. This large r renders the notable spectral separation between the biexciton emission and single-exciton transition, which is beneficial for the submonoexcitonic gain in the QDs.

Transient and steady-state characterizations of optical gain

To explore the light amplification from the QDs, we loaded the QD solution (300 mg/ml) into the quartz cuvette and excited by 400-nm femtosecond pulses in a stripe-pump configuration. With the increase in pump fluences, the emission from the cuvette edge evolves from the broad spontaneous emission to the ASE at low-energy position, accompanied by a highly directional ASE beam (Fig. 3A). The onset fluence of the ASE is determined to be ~1.27 ± 0.09 μJ/cm2, which corresponds to a threshold exciton number of <Nth> ~ 0.64 (see the determination of exciton number in fig. S5). To gain more insights into the submonoexcitonic gain properties, we performed the white-light TA spectroscopy. Figure 3B plots the gain spectra g=−(Δα+α0) under different carrier densities by summing the TA spectra Δα and the linear absorption α0 (2, 33). The optical gain is found to occur at <Nth> ~ 0.62 and at the similar spectral position to that of ASE, which agrees with the theoretical threshold of <Nth> ~ 0.60 with r of 0.93 (inset in Fig. 1D and fig. S8). Figure 3C shows that the carrier density dependence of the normalized absorption bleaching (−Δα/α0) for the QDs can be well reproduced by the modified gain model. In addition to lowering the gain threshold, the model indicates that the large r value could also increase the saturated optical gain at high carrier densities. In the traditional CdZnSe-based QDs with typical biexciton threshold of <Nth> ~ 1.20, the gain undergoes saturation at a value of |Δα| ~ 2.2α0 (Fig. 3C); in contrast, the saturated gain in the tailored QDs is enhanced to |−Δα|/α0 ~ 10.5 by virtue of the reduced competition from ground-state absorption, resulting in a record large gain cross section of 2.5 × 10−15 cm2 (fig. S9), which is almost one order of magnitude higher than those of state-of-the-art CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnS QDs at similar wavelengths (29). Furthermore, a long gain duration of >3 ns and a stimulated emission rate (τSE−1) of 2 ns−1 are obtained at high carrier densities (Fig. 3D).

The low gain threshold and long gain lifetime allow the ASE accessible by the nanosecond laser pumping (Nd:YAG laser with pulse width of 10 ns) as shown in fig. S10. For commercial dye laser systems, the nanosecond lasers (e.g., N2 laser and Nd:YAG laser) are generally adopted as the pump source owing to the compactness and cost-effectiveness (2, 19), indicating that the nanosecond pulse–pumped QD laser can be highly relevant for practical implementations. We further examine the effect of QD concentration on ASE performance under nanosecond pumping. The onset of ASE is found to occur at a low critical concentration of ~60 mg/ml (Fig. 3F), and the threshold decreases initially with the increase in concentration and then keeps nearly constant at the high concentrations of >200 mg/ml. The corresponding theoretical simulation reveals that the large r could reduce the threshold upon nanosecond pumping and the threshold value increases with the rise of τpulse (Fig. 3E) (see more detail in note S2). The dependence of pump threshold on QD concentration is consistent with the theoretical modeling (Fig. 3F and see details in note S3).

Design and construction of QD seed-amplifier laser architecture

A dual-gain series system (DSS) consisting of a low-power laser seed and an amplifier was designed on the basis of the QD gain medium. The schematic diagram of the DSS design is presented in Fig. 4A, and the photograph of the setup is shown in fig. S11. Briefly, the pump laser is divided by a beam splitter into two beams, where one part is directed to the laser seed and the other part to the amplifier. Both of the pump beams are focused transversely onto the QD cuvettes (10 mm by 10 mm). To facilitate the amplification process, we use the Littman-Metcalf cavity incorporating a near-grazing incidence diffraction grating and a rotatable reflection mirror to build the laser seed (see details in Materials and Methods) (34). The optical coupling between the laser seed and the amplifier is accomplished through a lens set. The optical path lengths of the two pump beams are regulated to be identical to ensure the synchronization (i.e., the temporal overlap in the amplifier between the pump beam and the input laser seed). Figure 4 (B and C) shows the PL spectra from the laser seed and amplifier under varied seed pump intensities. A narrow emission peak centered at 643 nm with a linewidth of 0.17 nm (fig. S12A) emerges when the pump intensity is above 23.0 mJ/cm2, indicating the development of the laser action. Furthermore, this narrow-linewidth emission is maintained without observable broadening as the increase in pump fluence. The plot of the emission intensity as a function of pump fluence reveals the lasing threshold to be as low as 22.1 mJ/cm2 (fig. S12B). By projecting the emission of laser amplifier onto a white screen, a distinct and bright output beam can be observed (insets of Fig. 4C). The beam directionality of the laser amplifier is examined by the spatial profiling method at various distances (see details in Materials and Methods). The beam profiles in both the x and y directions reveal the narrow divergence angles. Figure 4D displays that the beam radius of x direction expands from 0.8 mm at 8 cm to 1.25 mm at 360 cm. The distance-dependent beam radius aligns with a hyperbolic profile, which reveals a low-divergence half angle of 0.26 mrad. The beam profile along the y direction also conforms to a hyperbolic fit, where the beam radius expanded from 1.48 mm at 8 cm to 1.56 mm at 360 cm, exhibiting a low-divergence half angle of 0.14 mrad (fig. S14). The emission polarization measurements unravel that the degree of linear polarization (DOP) increases from 0.78 for the laser seed to 0.91 for the amplifier (fig. S15). This DOP difference arises from the polarization-selective nature of stimulated emission in the amplifier, which preferentially amplifies the dominant polarization component of the seed while suppressing the depolarized spontaneous emission (35).

To probe the transient behavior of the laser emission, we recorded the temporal response by a 25-GHz oscilloscope equipped with a high-speed photodetector (see details in Materials and Methods). Both of the seed and amplifier exhibit the typical nanosecond pulse traces, where the seed yields a pulse width of 7.1 ns (Fig. 4E), slightly shorter than the pump pulse (10 ns), and the amplifier yields a pulse width of 9.1 ns (Fig. 4F). The pulse-width broadening after the laser amplification is attributed to the gain saturation effect that differentially amplify the leading and trailing edges of the pulse (36).

To achieve the maximized output power, the allocation of the pump energy on the seed and amplifier has to be optimized (37). The theoretical analysis indicates that the pump energy allocated to the seed should slightly exceed the lasing threshold and the remaining pump energy should be directed to the amplifier to maximize the output power (see details in note S4). Therefore, we first fix the seed pump energy at 1.0 mJ and fine-tune the amplifier pump energy to optimize the output power. Figure 5A displays the evolution of the amplifier output power versus pump energy. Three distinct operational regions are observed: (i) a linear growth region when the pump energy is below 21.4 mJ. In this case, the population inversion density increases proportionally with the pump energy; (ii) a transition region between 21.4 and 28.1 mJ, where the output power rises by only 12.6%, indicating the onset of gain saturation; and (iii) a postsaturation plateau above 28.1 mJ, where further increase in the pump energy yields negligible increase in the output power, signifying that the population inversion density has reached the maximum. As a result, the optimal amplifier pump energy lies within 21.4 to 28.1 mJ, where the gain medium operates in the saturation regime.

Subsequently, we fix the amplifier pump energy at 28.1 mJ and monitor the output power by fine-tuning the seed pump energy. The output power initially increases with the seed pump energy and gradually plateaus at higher pump energies (>3.0 mJ) (Fig. 5B). On this basis, the output peak power reaches an unprecedented 1.25 MW, corresponding to an energy per pulse of 11.4 mJ. The QD laser achieving the output with megawatt peak power or millijoule-level energy per pulse represents impressive progress (table S1) (2, 33) and is competent for a variety of applications, including medical diagnostics, precision manufacturing, and spectroscopic research. Besides, the threshold and emission linewidth of the laser device are comparable to the best reported values (table S1). To quantify the amplification effect on seed by the amplifier, we introduce the concept of extraction efficiency. The extraction efficiency is defined as η=(Eoutput−Einput)/Epump, where Eoutput is the output energy of the amplifier, Epump is the pump energy of the amplifier, and Einput is the seed energy. When the seed pump energy increased to 3 mJ, the seed energy reached 0.11 mJ, yielding a high η of 40% (Fig. 5C).

To evaluate the operational endurance, we first monitor the output intensity for over 400 min at an initial peak power of 0.41 MW (3.7 mJ per pulse). The QD laser maintains stable output without emission degradation throughout the entire period (fig. S16). In stark contrast, a reference red organic dye laser subjected to similar conditions exhibits rapid power decay and complete signal loss within 200 min, indicating the feasibility to replace the commercial dye lasers by the QD lasers. Next, we proceed to monitor the pulse shape and peak power performance under continuous excitation. As shown in fig. S17, the pulse width fluctuated slightly from an initial value of 9.1 ns, reaching 9.5 ns at 210 min and then reverting to 9.3 ns at 420 min, with fluctuations remaining within a range of 4.4%. To characterize the long-term stability of peak power, we tested the pulse energy while monitoring the pulse shape. We tuned the initial pulse energy to 3.72 mJ and recorded pulse energies at 210 min (3.73 mJ) and 420 min (3.71 mJ). This allowed us to calculate that the peak power changed from an initial 0.41 to 0.39 MW at 210 min and 0.40 MW at 420 min, with fluctuations remaining within a range of 4.9%. This demonstrates that the system did not suffer from degradation during prolonged operation but experienced fluctuations within reasonable limits.

Notably, by rotating the reflection mirror in the Littman-Metcalf cavity, the lasing wavelength can be tuned from 589 to 640 nm (Fig. 5D) by virtue of the broadband optical gain intrinsic to the QDs (Fig. 3B). Moreover, by varying the Cd-to-Zn ratios in the ternary alloy QDs, the laser output spectra can be widely tuned in a continuous way (Fig. 5D), where the technologically important yellow (576 to 597 nm) range can be covered. To evaluate the emission characteristics of QD lasers across a broad spectral range, we evaluated the key metrics of peak power, linewidth, and DOP for the green, yellow, and red emission wavelengths. As illustrated in table S3, while red QDs exhibit the highest peak power due to their superior gain characteristics, the peak power of green and yellow QD lasers remain at the megawatt level.

Application of high-power QD laser in spectroscopic research

As a proof of concept, the application of the QD laser for spectroscopic research is demonstrated. In doing so, the output beam from the QD laser is directed by a mirror set and focused onto the solid film composed of weakly confined CdSe/CdS nanocrystals (38, 39). The spontaneous emission of the nanocrystals peaks at 710 nm (fig. S17A). Under low excitation intensities, the broadband spontaneous emission is detected from the nanocrystal film, while, with the increase in the excitation intensity, a new narrow peak appears (fig. S17B), indicating the achievement of ASE pumped by the QD laser. The observation demonstrates that the QD laser can be readily serve as the coherent pump source requiring high powers.

DISCUSSION

The QD lasers with unprecedented peak powers in megawatt level are reported. The success is made possible by incorporating the QDs into a seed-amplifier design. The comprehensive transient spectroscopy and theoretical simulation confirm the exceptional gain metrics in the QDs. The laser device exhibits the megawatt output with high coherent beam quality, polarization, and operational endurance. The high-power laser emission readily covers the yellow spectral range, holding the potential to solve the yellow gap challenge. Moreover, it is known that the dye lasers, prized for their broad spectral tunability, are being outdated because of the poor dye stability. In this regard, the high-power QD lasers, which offer similar broadband tunability while exhibiting notably enhanced stability, are positioned to address this deficiency and become a key enabling laser technology. With the aim of developing solid-state devices, it is anticipated that the QD thin films with proper designs could be adopted as the gain media in the seed-amplifier laser structure in the future. Moreover, the realization of cw pumping in our laser configuration is attractive, which represents an important direction for the future work.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Zinc acetate [Zn(Ac)2; 99.99%], cadmium oxide (CdO; 99.9%), oleic acid (OA; 99%), decanoic acid (98%), 1-octadecene (ODE; 90%), selenium powder (Se; 99.9%), sulfur powder (S; 99.9%), tri-n-octylphosphine (TOP; 97%), 1-octanethiol (OT; 98%), octadecanethiol (ODT; 97%), diisopropylamine (DIPA; 99%), ethanol (99.8%), and hexane (98%) were purchased from Aladdin’s Reagent and not further purified.

Fabrication of CdZnSe/ZnSe/ZnSeS/ZnS QDs

First, 0.3 mmol of CdO, 3.5 mmol of Zn(Ac)2, 4 ml of OA, and 8 ml of ODE were placed in a 50-ml flask and heated to 130°C under flowing high-purity argon for 60 min. Then, the flask was heated to 310°C. At this temperature, 0.1 mmol of Se powder (dissolved in 1 ml of TOP) was injected into the reaction flask. The reaction temperature was kept at 300°C for 12 min for the core growth. After that, 1 mmol of Se (dissolved in TOP and ODE) was injected into the flask (0.05 ml/min) and reacted for 60 min. Then, 0.25 mmol of Se and 0.25 mmol of S (dissolved in TOP and ODE) were injected into the flask (0.05 ml/min) and reacted for 20 min. Afterward, 0.25 mmol of Se (dissolved in TOP and ODE) was injected into the flask (0.05 ml/min) and reacted for 10 min. Last, the reaction was cooled to room temperature. The QDs were purified two times by the centrifugation precipitation and redispersion method using a mixed solvent of hexane (6 ml)/ethanol (36 ml). The centrifuged QDs were stored in 5 ml of hexane. In the next step, to achieve deprotonated thiol ligands, 10 mmol of OT, 5 mmol of ODT, and 15 mmol DIPA were placed in a 50-ml flask and heated to 60°C under flowing high-purity argon for 12 hours. Then, the deprotonated thiol ligands were mixed and stirred with the QD solution for ligand exchange reaction. After purification, QDs with deprotonated thiol ligands on the surface were obtained.

Optical characterization

The PL spectra were measured from the QD solution with a low concentration (~0.8 mg/ml) using a Varian Cary Eclipse instrument with the excitation wavelength set to 365 nm, while the absorption spectra were measured with a Shimadzu UV-3600. PLQY measurements were conducted using a Quantaurus-QY Absolute yield spectrometer (C11347-11, Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) with an excitation wavelength of 365 nm.

TA measurements

TA spectroscopy was performed using a femtosecond amplified Ti:sapphire laser source (Solstice Ace), whose pulse duration is ~100 fs and the central wavelength is 800 nm. The laser output was divided into two parts, and the major part was used to generate pump beam through the double-frequency method. The white light was focused onto sample by an off-axis parabolic mirror. The time delay between pump and probe beam was controlled by a motorized stage.

ASE measurements

For the cw excitation experiments, the commercial solid-state laser module with the type of LASER440nm-3W (hv = 2.81 eV, Pmax = 3 W) was used as the excitation source. The spot size on the sample was ~0.5 mm. For ASE measurement, the pump beam was focused onto the cuvette using a cylindrical lens, creating a narrow stripe. The emission signal was collected from the edge of the stripe and detected using a spectrometer.

Lasing measurements

For lasing measurement, the nanosecond Nd:YAG laser (355 nm, 10 ns, 10 Hz) was used as the pump source. Regarding the configuration of the seed cavity, the Littman-Metcalf configuration incorporating a near-grazing incidence diffraction grating and an additional tunable reflection mirror was used. The first-order diffracted beam is redirected onto this mirror, which reflects it back to the grating, forming a folded cavity. The wavelength tuning is achieved by rotating the reflection mirror rather than the grating, decoupling the grating orientation from the output beam direction. The cavity length is ~9 cm, and the cavity photon lifetime is ~0.8 ns, which is shorter the gain lifetime (3 ns) and the pump pulse width (10 ns), enabling the lasing to occur.

In the Littman-Metcalf cavity, the sealed QD-filled quartz cuvette was positioned between a dichroic mirror and a combination of a diffraction grating (1200 lines/mm) and a tunable mirror. The dichroic mirror is highly transmissive (90%) for pump laser and highly reflective for light from 400 to 700 nm, establishing a resonant feedback loop in conjunction with the tunable mirror. The grating ensured the narrow-linewidth operation.

To amplify the seed photons, the seed pulse was directed to the amplifier composed of a quartz cuvette containing the QD solution. The seed pulse was adjusted through a sequential optical chain: A reflective mirror directs the seed laser toward an adjustable iris diaphragm that spatially filters undesired stray light and controls the beam diameter for optimal mode matching. The refined beam is collimated by a lens to suppress divergence, homogenize the intensity profile, and preserve phase-front integrity. This collimated beam is then focused by a lens into the amplifier, creating a focused beam with a radius of ~0.15 mm. At the same time, the second pump beam from the splitter passes through two right-angle prisms to introduce a controlled temporal delay and undergoes identical collimation and focusing optics to synchronize its spatiotemporal profile with the seed light in the amplifier. The pump beam is focused onto the cuvette using a cylindrical lens, creating a narrow stripe with a dimension of 10.0 mm by 0.32 mm.

The DOP was measured by the spectrometer in combination with a rotatable half-wave plate and a linear polarizer. The DOP was calculated as DOP = (Imax − Imin)/(Imax + Imin), where Imax and Imin are the maximum and minimum values measured, respectively. The pulse width of the seed laser is measured via a free space–coupled photodetection system. The output beam was collimated and directed onto a high-speed photodetector. The electrical signal was acquired by a 25-GHz oscilloscope with 50-ohm termination to minimize reflections. System temporal response was precalibrated using a reference laser.
