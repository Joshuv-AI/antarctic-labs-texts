An hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm for logistic regression
Bernard Bercu, Luis Fredes, Eméric Gbaguidi

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate a second-order stochastic algorithm for solving large-scale binary classification problems. We propose to make use of a new hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm that includes two weighted components in the Hessian matrix estimation: the first one coming from the natural Hessian estimate and the second associated with the stochastic gradient information. Our motivation comes from the fact that both parts evaluated at the true parameter of logistic regression, are equal to the Hessian matrix. This new formulation has several advantages and it enables us to prove the almost sure convergence of our stochastic algorithm to the true parameter. Moreover, we significantly improve the almost sure rate of convergence to the Hessian matrix. Furthermore, we establish the central limit theorem for our hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm. Finally, we show a surprising result on the almost sure convergence of the cumulative excess risk.

Introduction

Our goal is to investigate the standard stochastic optimization problem in
\mathds{R}^{d}

\min_{h\in\mathds{R}^{d}}G(h),

(
\mathcal{P}
)

where

G(h)=\mathds{E}[g(\Phi,Y,h)].

(1)

In what follows, we focus our attention on the well-known logistic regression
[
1
,
3
,
6
]
. For that purpose, we introduce a sequence
(\Phi_{n},Y_{n})
of random vectors taking values in
\mathds{R}^{d}\times\{0,1\}
. Furthermore, we assume that
(\Phi_{n})
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed random vectors such that for all
n\geqslant 1
, the conditional distribution of
Y_{n}
is a Bernoulli distribution given by

\mathcal{L}(Y_{n}\lvert\Phi_{n})=\mathcal{B}(\pi(\theta^{T}\Phi_{n})),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\pi(x)=\dfrac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}},

and
\theta
is the unknown parameter belonging to
\mathds{R}^{d}
of the logistic regression. Denote by
G
the convex function given, for all
h\in\mathds{R}^{d}
, by

G(h)=\mathds{E}\left[\log\Big(1+\exp(h^{T}\Phi)\Big)-h^{T}\Phi Y\right],

(2)

where
\mathcal{L}(Y\lvert\Phi)=\mathcal{B}(\pi(\theta^{T}\Phi))
and
\Phi
shares the same distribution as the sequence
(\Phi_{n})
.
The gradient and Hessian matrix of
G
are respectively given by

\nabla G(h)=\mathds{E}\left[(\pi(h^{T}\Phi)-Y)\Phi\right],

(3)

and

\nabla^{2}G(h)=\mathds{E}\Big[\pi(h^{T}\Phi)\big(1-\pi(h^{T}\Phi)\big)\Phi\Phi^{T}\Big].

(4)

Hence, our goal is to find the unique solution of (

P

) which satisfies
\nabla G(\theta)=0
.
One can observe that
\mathds{E}[Y\lvert\Phi]=\pi(\theta^{T}\Phi)
and

\displaystyle\Sigma(\theta)=\mathds{E}\left[\Big(\pi(\theta^{T}\Phi)-Y\Big)^{2}\Phi\Phi^{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left[\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(\pi(\theta^{T}\Phi)-Y\Big)^{2}\Big\lvert\Phi\Big]\Phi\Phi^{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\Big[\pi(\theta^{T}\Phi)\big(1-\pi(\theta^{T}\Phi)\big)\Phi\Phi^{T}\Big]

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}G(\theta).

(5)

This simple calculation highlights our motivation to propose our hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm defined in Section
2
.

Several methods have been investigated in order to find
\theta

These methods are more able to deal with the problem where the Hessian matrix of the objective function has eigenvalues with significantly different values. A truncated version of the standard stochastic Newton algorithm has been proposed in
[
3
]
. This truncation was necessary to ensure the convergence of the algorithm but it significantly reduces the rate of convergence to the Hessian matrix. We also refer the reader to
[

[
3
]

[
14

. This tool avoids the inversion of large matrix at each iteration.

More recently, a weighted averaged version of the stochastic Newton algorithm was proposed in
[
6
]
for more general problems including the case of linear, logistic, and softmax regressions. The almost sure convergence of the iterates to the optimum and their rates of convergence as well as a central limit theorem have been established in
[

. As mentioned before, the same truncated version of stochastic Newton algorithm as
[

]
has been maintained in
[

]
for the specific case of logistic regression. Godichon-Baggioni et al.
[
11
]

focused on the recursive estimation technique for the inverse of the Hessian matrix using a Robbins-Monro procedure and considered a weighted averaged version to further enhance the rates of convergence. However, we will significantly improve their rates with our hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm.

Bach
[
1
]
borrowed tools from self-concordance to analyze the statistical properties of logistic regression estimate and established some oracle inequalities on the excess risk based on concentration inequalities for U-statistics. For their part, De Vilmarest and Wintenberger
[
9
]
provided explicit bounds with high probability on this convergence for the same truncated stochastic Newton algorithm named there the static extended Kalman filter in the logistic regression case. They derived local bounds on the cumulative excess risk with high probability via a matrix concentration inequality. However, the result are proved under a strong convergence assumption which is that with high probability, there exists a convergence time after which the algorithm stays trapped in a local region around the optimum.

Another variants of the stochastic Newton algorithm have been investigated to address large-scale applications
[
7
,
17
,
23
,
25
]
. For example, Kovalev et al.
[
16
]
proposed the stochastic cubic Newton algorithm which combines cubic regularization with the stochastic Newton technique for minimizing the average of a very large number of sufficiently smooth and strongly convex functions. Furthermore, in the deterministic case, Hazan
[
13
]
introduced the online Newton step which is also known as a quasi-Newton method or Gauss-Newton algorithm
[
2
,
8
,
22
]
. However, strictly speaking, this algorithm is first order, in the sense that it only uses gradient information. Next, Hazan
[
13
]
provided the logarithmic regret guarantee of the online Newton step algorithm for exp-concave functions. Recently, Wintenberger
[
24
]
showed that the stochastic online Newton step algorithm achieves fast-rate stochastic regrets under the stochastically exp-concave assumption.
Moreover, Yousefian et al.
[
26
]
studied an iterative regularized stochastic quasi-Newton method for the regularized problems with nonstrongly convex objective functions and Lipschitz continuous gradient. They showed the almost sure convergence of this scheme towards the optimal value of the problem under the bounded variance hypothesis.

In what follows, we highlight our main contributions. On the one hand, we introduce our new hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm motivated by (
5
). Our parametric approach incorporates two components in the Hessian matrix estimation: one from the natural Hessian estimate and the second from the stochastic gradient information. This helps to establish many convergence guarantees while avoiding the useless introduction of truncation as it was done in the previous literature. On the other hand, beyond the results of almost sure convergence, we have significantly improved the rate of convergence to the Hessian matrix compared to the current state of art. Lastly, we establish the central limit theorem for our hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section

2
Our hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm

In this section, we introduce our new hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm. Next, we will discuss the assumptions required for analysis. Our parametric approach provides a unified framework for the stochastic Newton algorithm and the online Newton step. Our hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm is given, for all
n\geqslant 0
, by

\displaystyle a_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\pi(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{T}\Phi_{n+1})(1-\pi(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{T}\Phi_{n+1})),

\displaystyle b_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\pi(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{T}\Phi_{n+1})-Y_{n+1}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle c_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\alpha a_{n+1}+\beta b_{n+1},

(6)

\displaystyle S_{n+1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=S_{n}^{-1}-c_{n+1}(1+c_{n+1}\Phi^{T}_{n+1}S_{n}^{-1}\Phi_{n+1})^{-1}S_{n}^{-1}\Phi_{n+1}\Phi_{n+1}^{T}S_{n}^{-1},

(7)

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\theta}_{n}-S_{n+1}^{-1}\Phi_{n+1}\left(\pi(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{T}\Phi_{n+1})-Y_{n+1}\right),

(8)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are two positive constants such that
\beta>0
and the initial value
\widehat{\theta}_{0}
is a bounded vector of
\mathds{R}^{d}
which can be arbitrarily chosen.
We take
S_{0}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
and observe that for all
n\geqslant 0
,

S_{n+1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}c_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}+\mathbf{I}_{d}=S_{n}+c_{n+1}\Phi_{n+1}\Phi_{n+1}^{T}.

(9)

The first key idea underlying the hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm is that we incorporate two weighted components in the Hessian matrix estimation: one from the natural Hessian estimate and the second from the stochastic gradient information. The motivation comes from (
5
) which shows that both parts evaluated in
\theta
are equal to the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}G(\theta)
. This interesting relation is specific to the logistic regression problem. The remarkable advantage of this new formulation is also that we do not need anymore truncation to obtain the convergence guarantees.

Moreover, the another cornerstone in our new algorithm is that we update
S_{n+1}
and
S_{n+1}^{-1}
before updating
\widehat{\theta}_{n+1}
. Our motivation is to make use of the maximum information available for the estimation of the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}G(\theta)
at the current time
n+1
. We wish to point out that in all the previous literature
[
3
,
6
,
11
]
,
\widehat{\theta}_{n+1}
is updated before
S_{n+1}
and
S_{n+1}^{-1}
. Hence, this is the second key point in our analysis and we will see below the advantages of that choice. In fact, our approach makes a significant difference in the convergence analysis, but raises other problems of measurability. However, we find how to properly overcome these limitations.

In the same vein as previous literature on the Newton algorithm
[
3
,
9
,
13
]
, the inverse of the Hessian estimate is updated via the useful Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury inversion formula (see e.g.
[
14
, p. 19]
) applied here in (
7
). We recall that the classical analysis of the stochastic Newton methods is based on the study of the eigenvalues convergence of the Hessian matrix estimation. This will not be the case here at all and we adopt another strategy. Therefore, we make extensive use of this recursive form (
7
) from which we establish new interesting equations for the convergence of estimates. Finally, notice that our hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm with
\alpha=0
and
\beta=1
, leads to the online Newton step.
Furthermore, we consider the positive definite matrices defined, for all
n\geqslant 1
, by

H_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Sigma_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T},

as well as their averaged versions given by,

\overline{H}_{n}=\dfrac{1}{n}H_{n},\qquad\overline{\Sigma}_{n}=\dfrac{1}{n}\Sigma_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{S}_{n}=\dfrac{1}{n}S_{n}.

We will prove that these three matrices
\overline{H}_{n}
,
\overline{\Sigma}_{n}
and
\overline{S}_{n}
converge almost surely to the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}G(\theta)
. Throughout the paper, we will use the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})
given, for all
n\geqslant 1
, by
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma\left((\Phi_{1},Y_{1}),(\Phi_{2},Y_{2}),\dots,(\Phi_{n},Y_{n})\right)
.
That choice is a quite of importance and we will see in our proofs how to overcome the measurability problems due to
S_{n+1}
in the algorithm update (
8
). Hereafter, our main assumptions are as follows.

Assumption 2.1
.

The Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}G(\theta)
and the matrix
\mathds{E}[\Phi\Phi^{T}]
are positive definite.

Assumption 2.2
.

It exists a positive constant
d_{\Phi}
such that
\lVert\Phi\rVert\leqslant d_{\Phi}
almost surely.

Assumption
2.1
is a classical regularity hypothesis in the literature
[
3
,
6
]
. Assumption
2.2
requires that the vector
\Phi
is bounded. This condition seems rather restrictive, but it is made in most of all papers dealing with stochastic Newton algorithm for logistic regression
[
1
,
9
,
24
]
and is satisfied in statistical applications.

Theorem

states that the asymptotic covariance matrix is the inverse of the Hessian of the function

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have investigated the theoretical and numerical properties of a new hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm for solving the logistic regression problem which is widely used and has many applications, including in the fields of machine learning, public health, social sciences, ecology, and econometrics. Furthermore, we have proposed a new approach on the analysis of second-order stochastic algorithms, which consists to update the Hessian matrix estimate before updating the algorithm. This approach combined with the modification made in the Hessian matrix estimation, allows us to establish several convergence guarantees while avoiding the useless introduction of truncation as it was done in the previous literature.
Therefore, we provide the almost sure convergence of our new algorithm and his asymptotic efficiency via a central limit theorem. We highlight that our hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm also leads to improvement of the almost sure rate of convergence to the Hessian matrix. Moreover, the numerical experiments performed on simulated and real-world datasets, provide additional empirical evidence of the relevance of our proposed method and validate its applicability. In particular, we find that our hybrid stochastic Newton algorithm achieves performance at least equivalent to that of the best method among the ONS and the standard stochastic Newton algorithm. Furthermore, our method remains adapted when the dimension of the feature vector increases. It would therefore be worthwhile to extend our approach to a broader class of optimization problems for machine learning.

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

Let us define, for all
n\geqslant 1
, the positive random variable

T_{n}=G(\widehat{\theta}_{n})-G(\theta).

(20)

By a Taylor expansion of the twice continuously differentiable functional

, there exists
\xi_{n+1}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
such that

T_{n+1}=T_{n}+\nabla G(\widehat{\theta}_{n})^{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{n+1}-\widehat{\theta}_{n})+\dfrac{1}{2}(\widehat{\theta}_{n+1}-\widehat{\theta}_{n})^{T}\nabla^{2}G(\xi_{n+1})(\widehat{\theta}_{n+1}-\widehat{\theta}_{n}).

However, we clearly have from (

)

\text{Tr}\,[\nabla^{2}G(\xi_{n+1})]\leqslant\dfrac{1}{4}\mathds{E}[\lVert\Phi\rVert^{2}].

Hence, we obtain that

T_{n+1}\leqslant T_{n}+\nabla G(\widehat{\theta}_{n})^{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{n+1}-\widehat{\theta}_{n})+\dfrac{1}{8}\mathds{E}[\lVert\Phi\rVert^{2}]\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{n+1}-\widehat{\theta}_{n}\rVert^{2}.

(22)

The recursive definition (
8
), combined with the previous inequality, leads to

T_{n+1}\leqslant T_{n}-\nabla G(\widehat{\theta}_{n})^{T}S_{n+1}^{-1}Z_{n+1}+\dfrac{1}{8}\mathds{E}[\lVert\Phi\rVert^{2}](Y_{n+1}-\pi(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{T}\Phi_{n+1}))^{2}\Phi_{n+1}^{T}S_{n+1}^{-2}\Phi_{n+1},

(23)

where
Z_{n+1}=(\pi(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{T}\Phi_{n+1})-Y_{n+1})\Phi_{n+1}
. By taking the conditional expectation on both sides of (

), we obtain that almost surely

\mathds{E}\Big[T_{n+1}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{n}\Big]\leqslant T_{n}-\mathds{E}\Big[\nabla G(\widehat{\theta}_{n})^{T}S_{n+1}^{-1}Z_{n+1}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{n}\Big]+\dfrac{1}{8}\mathds{E}[\lVert\Phi\rVert^{2}]\mathds{E}\Big[b_{n+1}\Phi_{n+1}^{T}S_{n+1}^{-2}\Phi_{n+1}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{n}\Big],

(24)

where
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma\left((\Phi_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(\Phi_{n},Y_{n})\right)
.
The last two terms in this inequality require a particular attention because
S_{n+1}^{-1}
is not
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable. However, we obtain from (
7
) the very useful relation

S_{n+1}^{-1}\Phi_{n+1}=\dfrac{1}{1+g_{n+1}}S_{n}^{-1}\Phi_{n+1},

(25)

with
g_{n+1}=c_{n+1}\Phi_{n+1}^{T}S_{n}^{-1}\Phi_{n+1}
. Moreover, remark also that Assumption

has been chosen such that
c<1/2
which means that
0<a<1
. Then, we obtain for all
n\geqslant 2
that

L_{n}\leqslant\left(\dfrac{2}{n}\right)^{e}L_{1}+\dfrac{A}{(1-a)}\sqrt{\dfrac{\log(n)}{n}}\qquad\text{a.s.}

Since
e>1/2
, we deduce that

L_{n}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{\log(n)}{n}\right)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(85)

Hence, we have from (
81
) and (
85
) that

\lVert\Delta_{n}\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{\log(n)}{n}\right)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(86)

Finally, the combination of (
60
), (
71
) and (
86
) ensures that

\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{\log(n)}{n}\right)\qquad\text{a.s.},

(87)

which is exactly the almost sure rate of convergence (
13
).
Hereafter, we can rewrite the matrix
\overline{S}_{n}
for all
n\geqslant 1
as

\displaystyle\overline{S}_{n}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}+\dfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{I}_{d},

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big\{c_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}-\mathds{E}\Big[c_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\Big\}+\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\Big[c_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]+\dfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{I}_{d},

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}T_{n}+\dfrac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\Big[a_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]+\dfrac{\beta}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\Big[b_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]+\dfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{I}_{d},

(88)

where

T_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(c_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}-\mathds{E}\Big[c_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\Big).

By using the same technique as in equation (
64
), we have that

\mathds{E}\Big[b_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]=S+\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(\pi(\widehat{\theta}_{k-1}^{T}\Phi_{k})-\pi(\theta^{T}\Phi_{k})\Big)^{2}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\qquad\text{a.s.}

(89)

Moreover, we immediately obtain that

\mathds{E}\Big[a_{k}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]=S+\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(a_{k}-a_{k}(\theta)\Big)\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\qquad\text{a.s.}

(90)

Hence, it follows from (
89
) and (
90
) that

\displaystyle\overline{S}_{n}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}T_{n}+(\alpha+\beta)S+\dfrac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\Big[(a_{k}-a_{k}(\theta))\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]

\displaystyle\;+\dfrac{\beta}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\Big[(\pi(\widehat{\theta}_{k-1}^{T}\Phi_{k})-\pi(\theta^{T}\Phi_{k}))^{2}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]+\dfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{I}_{d},

(91)

which leads to

\displaystyle\lVert\overline{S}_{n}-(\alpha+\beta)S\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\dfrac{1}{n}\lVert T_{n}\rVert+\dfrac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\lVert\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(a_{k}-a_{k}(\theta)\Big)\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\right\rVert

\displaystyle\;+\dfrac{\beta}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\lVert\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(\pi(\widehat{\theta}_{k}^{T}\Phi_{k})-\pi(\theta^{T}\Phi_{k})\Big)^{2}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\right\rVert+\dfrac{d}{n}.

(92)

However, we have as in the inequalities (
48
) and (
66
) that almost surely,

\left\lVert\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(\pi(\widehat{\theta}_{k-1}^{T}\Phi_{k})-\pi(\theta^{T}\Phi_{k})\Big)^{2}\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\right\rVert\leqslant\dfrac{d_{\Phi}^{4}}{16}\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{k-1}-\theta\rVert^{2},

and

\left\lVert\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(a_{k}-a_{k}(\theta)\Big)\Phi_{k}\Phi_{k}^{T}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\right\rVert\leqslant\dfrac{d_{\Phi}^{3}}{12\sqrt{3}}\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{k-1}-\theta\rVert.

Consequently, it follows from (
92
) that

\lVert\overline{S}_{n}-(\alpha+\beta)S\rVert\leqslant\dfrac{1}{n}\lVert T_{n}\rVert+\dfrac{\alpha d_{\Phi}^{3}}{12\sqrt{3}}\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{k-1}-\theta\rVert+\dfrac{\beta d_{\Phi}^{4}}{16}\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{k-1}-\theta\rVert^{2}+\dfrac{d}{n}.

(93)

Finally, we deduce from (
13
) and (
93
) that there exist finite positive random variables
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\lVert\overline{S}_{n}-(\alpha+\beta)S\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\dfrac{1}{n}\lVert T_{n}\rVert+\dfrac{\alpha d_{\Phi}^{3}E_{1}}{12\sqrt{3}}\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sqrt{\dfrac{\log(k)}{k}}+\dfrac{\beta d_{\Phi}^{4}E_{2}}{16}\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{\log(k)}{k}+\dfrac{d}{n},

\displaystyle\leqslant\dfrac{1}{n}\lVert T_{n}\rVert+\dfrac{\alpha d_{\Phi}^{3}E_{1}}{6\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{\dfrac{\log(n)}{n}}+\dfrac{\beta d_{\Phi}^{4}E_{2}}{16}\dfrac{\log^{2}(n)}{n}+\dfrac{d}{n}\qquad\text{a.s.}

(94)

Moreover, by construction, the sequence
(T_{n})
is a locally square-integrable multidimensional martingale. Since the random vector
\Phi
is bounded, we have from the strong law of large numbers for multidimensional martingales given, e.g., by Theorem 4.3.16 in
[
10
]
that

\lVert T_{n}\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n\log(n))\qquad\text{a.s.}

which ensures with (
94
) that

\lVert\overline{S}_{n}-(\alpha+\beta)S\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{\log(n)}{n}\right)\qquad\text{a.s.},

The last result (
15
) is obtained by using (
14
) and the following equation

\overline{S}_{n}^{-1}-(\alpha+\beta)^{-1}S^{-1}=\overline{S}_{n}^{-1}\Big((\alpha+\beta)S-\overline{S}_{n}\Big)(\alpha+\beta)^{-1}S^{-1},

(95)

which completes the proof of Theorem
3.2
.
∎

C
Proof of Theorem
3.3

Proof.

We recall from (
60
) that for all
n\geqslant 0
,

\widehat{\theta}_{n+1}-\theta=-\dfrac{1}{n+1}M_{n+1}-\Delta_{n}

(96)

which leads to

\sqrt{n+1}(\widehat{\theta}_{n+1}-\theta)=-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}M_{n+1}-\sqrt{n+1}\Delta_{n}.

(97)

where
\sqrt{n+1}\Delta_{n}=O_{n}+P_{n}+Q_{n}
and

\displaystyle O_{n}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Big(\overline{S}_{k+1}^{-1}-\overline{S}_{k}^{-1}\Big)Z_{k+1},

\displaystyle P_{n}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Big(\overline{S}_{k}^{-1}-S^{-1}\Big)S(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta),

\displaystyle Q_{n}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\overline{S}_{k}^{-1}\delta_{k}.

It immediately follows from (
80
) that

\lVert O_{n}\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\qquad\text{a.s.},

which ensures that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\lVert O_{n}\rVert=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(98)

Moreover, we deduce from (
13
) and (
14
) that

\lVert P_{n}\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{\log(k)}{k}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{\log^{2}(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\qquad\text{a.s.},

which implies that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\lVert P_{n}\rVert=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(99)

Furthermore, we have from (
48
) and (
56
) that

\displaystyle\lVert\delta_{n}\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}G(\theta+t(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta))(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)\text{dt}-\nabla^{2}G(\theta)(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}G(\theta+t(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta))-\nabla^{2}G(\theta)\right\rVert\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta\rVert\text{dt}

\displaystyle\leqslant\dfrac{d_{\Phi}^{3}}{24\sqrt{3}}\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta\rVert^{2}.

(100)

Consequently, we obtain from (
13
) that

\lVert Q_{n}\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta\rVert^{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{\log(k)}{k}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{\log^{2}(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(101)

which leads to

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\lVert Q_{n}\rVert=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(102)

By combining equations (
98
), (
99
) and (
102
), we deduce that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\sqrt{n+1}\lVert\Delta_{n}\rVert=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(103)

Hence, it only remains to study the asymptotic behavior of the martingale
(M_{n})
. We already proved in (
70
) that its predictable quadratic variation satisfies

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\langle M\rangle_{n}=\left(\nabla^{2}G(\theta)\right)^{-1}\qquad\text{a.s.}

(104)

Once again, since the random vector
\Phi
is bounded, we have

\lVert\varepsilon_{n+1}\rVert=\lVert Z_{n+1}-\nabla G(\widehat{\theta}_{n})\rVert\leqslant\lVert\Phi_{n+1}\rVert+\mathds{E}[\lVert\Phi\rVert]\leqslant 2d_{\Phi}\qquad\text{a.s.}

which leads to

\underset{n\geqslant 1}{\sup}\,\mathds{E}\big[\lVert\varepsilon_{n+1}\rVert^{4}\big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{n}\big]\leq 16d_{\Phi}^{4}\qquad\text{a.s.}

(105)

Consequently, the sequence
(M_{n})
satisfies Lindeberg’s condition. Therefore, we deduce from the central limit theorem for martingales given by Corollary 2.1.10 in
[
10
]
that

\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}M_{n}\quad\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\underset{n\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\quad\mathcal{N}_{d}\left(0,\left(\nabla^{2}G(\theta)\right)^{-1}\right),

which achieves the proof of (
16
) thanks to (
97
) and (
103
).
∎

D
Proof of Theorem
3.4

Proof.

We recall from (
60
) that for all
n\geqslant 1
,

\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta=-\dfrac{1}{n}M_{n}-\Delta_{n-1}

which implies that

(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)^{T}=\dfrac{1}{n^{2}}M_{n}M_{n}^{T}+\Delta_{n-1}\Delta_{n-1}^{T}+\dfrac{1}{n}\Big(M_{n}\Delta_{n-1}^{T}+\Delta_{n-1}M_{n}^{T}\Big).

(106)

By summing on both sides of (
106
), we obtain that

\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)^{T}=\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{k^{2}}M_{k}M_{k}^{T}+\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta_{k-1}\Delta_{k-1}^{T}+N_{n}

(107)

where

N_{n}=\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{k}\Big(M_{k}\Delta_{k-1}^{T}+\Delta_{k-1}M_{k}^{T}\Big).

We already saw from (
70
) that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\langle M\rangle_{n}=\left(\nabla^{2}G(\theta)\right)^{-1}\qquad\text{a.s.}

(108)

Moreover, we have already checked that the martingale
(M_{n})
satisfied Lindeberg’s condition. Furthermore, it follows from (
105
) that

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n\log(n))^{2}}\mathds{E}\big[\lVert\overline{S}_{n}^{-1}\varepsilon_{n+1}\rVert^{4}\big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{n}\big]<\infty\qquad\text{a.s.}

Consequently, we deduce from the quadratic strong law for multi-dimensional martingale given e.g. by Theorem A.2 in
[
4
]
that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{k^{2}}M_{k}M_{k}^{T}=\left(\nabla^{2}G(\theta)\right)^{-1}\qquad\text{a.s.}

(109)

Hereafter, the almost sure rate of convergence of
(\Delta_{n})
established in (
86
) is not enough to properly handle the second term in the right-hand side of (
107
). We need to be more precise on that rate by exploiting the upper-bound in (
100
). For that end, we have from (
62
) and (
80
) combined with the almost sure rates of convergence (
13
) and (
15
) that

\lVert\Delta_{n}\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{\log^{2}(n)}{n}\right)\qquad\text{a.s.},

(110)

which leads to

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert\Delta_{k}\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{\log^{4}(k)}{k^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}(1)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(111)

Hence, we immediately deduce that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert\Delta_{k}\rVert^{2}=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(112)

By using twice the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we also obtain from
(
109
) and (
112
) that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}N_{n}=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(113)

Therefore, it follows from (
107
), (
109
), (
112
) and (
113
) that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)^{T}=\left(\nabla^{2}G(\theta)\right)^{-1}\qquad\text{a.s.}

(114)

Furthermore, we have from the Taylor expansion of
G
with integral remainder that

G(\widehat{\theta}_{n})-G(\theta)=\dfrac{1}{2}(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)^{T}\nabla^{2}G(\theta)(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)+I_{n},

where

I_{n}=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)^{T}\Big(\nabla^{2}G(\theta+t(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta))-\nabla^{2}G(\theta)\Big)(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)\text{dt}.

(115)

Then, we obtain that

\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(G(\widehat{\theta}_{k})-G(\theta)\Big)=\dfrac{1}{2\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)^{T}\nabla^{2}G(\theta)(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)+\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}I_{k}.

(116)

However, one can easily see that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)^{T}\nabla^{2}G(\theta)(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\text{Tr}\,\Big((\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)^{T}\nabla^{2}G(\theta)(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)\Big)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\text{Tr}\,\Big(\left(\nabla^{2}G(\theta)\right)^{1/2}(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)^{T}\left(\nabla^{2}G(\theta)\right)^{1/2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\,\left(\left(\nabla^{2}G(\theta)\right)^{1/2}\left(\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)^{T}\right)\left(\nabla^{2}G(\theta)\right)^{1/2}\right).

(117)

Consequently, we deduce from (
114
) that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)^{T}\nabla^{2}G(\theta)(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta)=d\qquad\text{a.s.}

(118)

Moreover, as in equation (
100
), we obtain from (
115
) that

\lvert I_{n}\rvert\leqslant\dfrac{d_{\Phi}^{3}}{48\sqrt{3}}\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta\rVert^{3},

(119)

which implies that

\left\lvert\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}I_{k}\right\rvert\leqslant\dfrac{d_{\Phi}^{3}}{48\sqrt{3}}\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta\rVert^{3}.

(120)

Nevertheless, the almost sure rate of convergence
(\widehat{\theta}_{n})
given by (
13
) implies that

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta\rVert^{3}=\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{\log^{3/2}(k)}{k^{3/2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}(1)\qquad\text{a.s.}

which immediately leads to

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta\rVert^{3}=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(121)

Therefore, we deduce from the combination of (
120
) and (
121
)
that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}I_{k}=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(122)

Finally, we conclude from (
116
), (
118
) and (
122
) that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{1}{\log(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(G(\widehat{\theta}_{k})-G(\theta)\Big)=\dfrac{d}{2}\qquad\text{a.s.}

which completes the proof of Theorem
3.4
.
∎
