Immunogenicity of PCV13 during Hospitalization soon after infection resolution: Vaccis study
Authors: Julie Mérindol, Irit Touitou, Coralie Cruzel, Barbara Seitz-Polski, Sophie Breaud, Johan Courjon, Fredéric Batteux, Marion Cremoni-Gauci, Vesna Brglez, Didier Payen, Michel Carles, Elisa Demonchy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vaccination against Streptococcus pneumoniae in high-risk patients is crucial and hospitalization is a valuable opportunity. Adult patients hospitalized (>48 hours) for various infectious conditions and eligible for 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccination from April 2021 to 2023 were included. The primary endpoint was the vaccine response rate (VRR) at least one month after vaccination. Of the 725 potentially eligible patients, 143 were included in the study. The most common comorbidities were diabetes mellitus (47%) and chronic cardiovascular disease (42%). VRR was acceptable at 46%, with a lower geometric mean concentration (GMC) of IgG in non-responders than in responders (1.27 µg/mL vs. 9.49 µg/mL, p < 0.001). In the multivariate analysis, the strongest negative predictor of VRR was a Charlson score of >3 (OR: 0.23 [0.06–0.81], p = 0.022). Vaccination within 48 hours after apyrexia (OR: 6.25 [1.85–20], p = 0.004) and the presence of diabetes (OR: 2.70 [1.05–7.14], p = 0.039) were independently associated with a better VRR. Hospitalization for acute infection should not be viewed as a barrier to vaccination but as an opportunity for optimized pneumococcal prevention after a short delay of apyrexia and should not be routinely postponed.

Introduction

Vaccination is a cornerstone of preventive healthcare, especially in vulnerable populations such as individuals with weakened immune systems or chronic comorbidities1–3. These groups face a dual challenge: they are at a higher risk of severe infection and often exhibit a suboptimal immune response to vaccines because of underlying conditions, such as immune dysregulation or immunosuppression4. Despite the clear benefits of vaccination, coverage in these high-risk groups remains low worldwide5,6.

Among the infections affecting vulnerable populations, Streptococcus pneumoniae infection is a major public health concern7. Vaccination against S. pneumoniae remains a crucial preventive strategy8 recommended by most national health authorities9–11. To mitigate the impact of this infection, broad vaccine coverage targeting high-risk groups is essential8.

Hospitalization provides a great opportunity to improve vaccine coverage among at-risk groups, especially infectious disease departments (IDD), where healthcare providers are well-versed in vaccination strategies12–15. Administering vaccines during hospitalization ensures that these individuals do not miss the opportunity to be vaccinated. However, this approach raises questions regarding the immune response following acute infection. Patients admitted for infection may experience altered immune states, such as immune dysregulation or immunoparalysis, which can affect the effectiveness of vaccination16–18. Vaccination is traditionally delayed during febrile episodes; a recent review of the literature, including prospective studies, showed that vaccination during febrile illnesses is an opportunity that should not be missed19.

However, no data are currently available regarding the vaccine response to the 13-valent pneumococcal vaccine (PCV13) in patients hospitalized for a recent infection. Vaccine response is highly dependent on the serotype and population studied20–24. In immunocompetent adults, the immunogenicity of PCV13 ranges from 30% to 70%20. Likewise, vaccine responses in at-risk patients are often lower23–26.

Our study aimed to evaluate the immunogenicity of pneumococcal vaccination one month after vaccination in patients hospitalized with an ongoing infection in the Infectious Disease Department.

Results

Patient characteristics

During the 2-year study period, 725 patients admitted to the Infectious Diseases Department (IDD) were eligible. Of these, 143 (20%) were enroled in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The median hospitalization duration was 10 days (IQR: 7–19). The cohort had a predominantly male population (66%) with a median age of 66 years (IQR: 57–73). The most common diagnosis during hospitalization was bronchopulmonary infection related to severe COVID-19 pneumonia (32%, n = 46), with more than 2/3 requiring high-flow oxygen therapy or non-invasive ventilatory support. Table 1 and Table S1)

Microbiological documentation of infection was obtained in 94% (n = 135) of the cases. The primary bacterial pathogens were Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus species, and Enterobacteriaceae species. Blood cultures were positive in 36% of patients, and 18% had septic shock treated in the intensive care unit before admission to the IDD. The median duration of fever was 8 days (IQR: 3–18).

At inclusion, hypogammaglobulinemia (<7 g/L) was observed in 10% of the patients, and lymphopenia (<500 G/L) was observed in 9 (6%) patients. Before vaccination, 27 patients (18%) had pre-existing serotype-specific immunity, likely due to prior vaccination that was unknown to them. Additionally, 95 patients (66%) had protective IgG levels (≥1 µg/mL) against at least one serotype at baseline (T0), suggesting previous natural pneumococcal infection.

According to CTCAE version 5.0, 12 adverse events were reported (Table S5). Of these, 6 were classified as serious adverse events, including 4 that resulted in death (2 cases of skull base osteomyelitis, 1 case of endocarditis, and 1 case of metastatic lung cancer). No adverse events were associated with vaccination. Additionally, 25 patients (17%) received co-administration of other vaccines such as influenza or diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DTP) vaccines (Table S6). Vaccination was performed after a median interval of 5.5 days (interquartile range [IQR], 3–10 days) after fever resolution.

Vaccine response and serotype-specific outcomes

T1 averaged 42 days (+/−16), with 69% of the patients being evaluated between days 30 and 60. Using the French evaluation method, the vaccine response rate (VRR) was 46% (95% CI: [38–54%]). A similar proportion was observed when American guidelines were applied (VRR, 48%; 95% CI [40–56%]). Notably, the VRR per serotype did not differ between the French and American methods. Non-responders differed significantly from responders (9-13 serotypes) in terms of age, Charlson score and immunosuppression status (Table S4). Patients hospitalized initially in intensive care (n = 51) had significantly lower T0 GMC (Geometric Mean Concentration) than other patients (n = 92) (0.9 vs. 0.5, p < 0.001), but at M1, GMC reached a value similar to that of the other patients.(Fig. 2, Table S2)Fig. 2Distribution of the geometric mean concentration (GMC).A Distribution of the geometric mean concentration (GMC) of IgG measured by ELISA at Time 0 (T0) vs Time (T1). GMC at T1 was significantly higher in responders than in non-responders (9.49 [7.67–11.74] vs. 1.27 [0.99–1.63], p < 0.001) across all serotypes (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S2). Similarly, GMC values increased significantly between T0 and T1 in responders and non-responders, except for serotype 3 in non-responders, who had the lowest global response rate (14%, n = 20/143). In contrast, serotype 7 showed the highest response rate (68%; n = 97/143). Among the non-responders, seven serotypes (6A, 6B, 7F, 14, 18C, 19A, and 19F) demonstrated protective IgG levels (>1 µg/mL), whereas seven serotypes (1, 4, 5, 7F, 9V, 14, and 18C) showed good seroconversion rates (T1/T0 ratio >2).

Factors associated with vaccine response

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified six factors significantly associated with vaccine response (VR) at T1 (Table 2). Among the negative predictors, immunodepression, whether due to medication or an underlying disease, was significantly associated with a lower vaccine response (OR: 0.30 [0.13–0.69], p = 0.003). Similarly, a higher Charlson comorbidity score (>3) (OR: 0.35 [0.18–0.71], p = 0.003) and age >65 years (OR: 0.50 [0.25–0.97], p = 0.040) were associated with a reduced response to vaccination. In contrast, neither the pathogen type nor the disease severity influenced the VRR. Patients with severe SARS-CoV-2 who received tocilizumab and steroid therapy were not identified as poor responders. Co-vaccination also had no significant impact on the vaccine response (OR: 0.49 [0.20–1.22], p = 0.123). Clinical, infection severity, and immune factors influencing vaccine response at t1 (univariate analysis)

The study of the risk factors for response to vaccination was conducted using logistic regression models. No patients had neutropenia (<1.8 G/L), so this parameter was not included in the univariate analysis.

Among the immune parameters, a low baseline IgG level (<6.10 g/L) was identified as a significant risk factor for a weaker humoral response (OR: 0.17 [0.04–0.80], p = 0.025). However, the counts of CD19 B, CD4 T, and NK T lymphocytes were not significantly associated with vaccine response (OR: 1.66 [0.79–3.49], p = 0.181; OR: 1.15 [0.59–2.27], p = 0.680; OR: 1.61 [0.73–3.57], p = 0.241, respectively).

In contrast, the following two factors were associated with better vaccination responses. Surprisingly, diabetes, irrespective of the treatment type (insulin or oral medication) or glycemic control (HbA1c level), was significantly associated with improved vaccine immunogenicity (OR: 3.22 [1.62–6.39], p = 0.001). Additionally, vaccination within 48 h after apyrexia, was associated with a higher response rate, correlating with better immunogenicity (OR: 2.55 [1.18–5.50], p = 0.017).

The vaccine response for each serotype was also examined to determine whether the associated factors differed across serotypes (Fig. S2, Table S4).

Discussion

This study is the first to evaluate vaccine-induced immunogenicity, as measured by IgG concentrations, in patients vaccinated shortly after hospitalization for infectious diseases. Overall, the vaccine response rate (VRR) was 46%, closely matching the 48% calculated using thee the American definition27. highlighting the consistency of our results. Multivariate analysis identified a Charlson score of >3 and IgG < 6.10 g/l as significant factors associated with poor response, consistent with prior studies20,28,29. Interestingly, vaccination within 48 hours after apyrexia, as well as the presence of diabetes, were associated with better vaccine responses. Although immunocompromised status was not a significant factor in the multivariate model, the proportion of responders was significantly lower in immunocompromised patients than in immunocompetent patients.

Importantly, 18% of patients were pre-immunized, highlighting the challenge of obtaining an accurate self-reported vaccination history for this vaccine. However, revaccination in these patients boosted antibody levels without adverse effects, supporting a favourable risk-benefit balance, regardless of prior immunization status.

A VRR of 46% can be considered modest. Published studies in ambulatory immunocompetent adults of similar age report variable response rates to PCV13, generally ranging from 30% to 70%, depending on the serological definitions applied (20,35). In this context, the 46% VRR observed in our hospitalized cohort does not appear to be substantially lower than that reported in non-hospitalized populations. Even if immunogenicity is moderate, hospitalization represents a key opportunity to prevent missed vaccination in high-risk individuals, many of whom may not receive vaccination after being discharged.

However, owing to the limited available evidence and absence of a strict consensus regarding the serotype-specific IgG thresholds required for protection against pneumococcal infections4,27, a cautious interpretation of our results is warranted. Therefore, we performed a detailed serotype-specific analysis of IgG responses. Notably, serotypes 3 and 4 are known to be poor responders, as confirmed in our study, which likely contributes to lowering overall VRR25,30–32. This low immunogenicity was observed in both responders and non-responders to the vaccine. This phenomenon is likely due to the intrinsic properties of these serotypes, as their polysaccharide capsule structure facilitates immune evasion, rather than being linked to individual immune deficiencies. Furthermore, 12% of patients met the criteria for response to seven or eight serotypes, which would increase the VRR to 58% if a less stringent definition were applied. In the defined “non-responders” group, vaccination still induced IgG protection above the defined threshold for seven of the 13 serotypes, suggesting that while these individuals did not meet the strict response criteria, they still exhibited a partial immune response.

In our study, at T1, the GMC values in non-immunocompromised patients (Fig. S3A) were comparable to those observed in healthy adults aged 60–65 years30 and in the CAPITA study20 but were lower than those reported in healthy adults aged 18–49 years30. These findings suggest that both age and clinical context may significantly influence the serotype-specific immune responses. In immunocompromised patients, the GMC values at T1 were consistent with those previously reported in specific populations such as individuals with haematological malignancies33, HIV infection32, kidney transplantation34, solid tumours35, and autoimmune diseases treated with either csDMARDs or bDMARDs22 (Fig. S3B). Interestingly, patients from the ICU had lower T0 GMC, reflecting a transient immunodepression status. Immune stimulation by vaccination reached a normal response at T1, indicating that post-ICU immunosuppression did not affect the vaccination response. This positive observation reinforces the validity of vaccination during hospital stay.

Interestingly, older age and immunodepression were not significantly associated with poor vaccine response in multivariate analysis. This contrasts with previous reports and may be explained by two key factors22–24: the limited statistical power of the study, which increases the risk of type 1 errors, and the recent infectious disease context, which may have altered the baseline immune status or microbiome reactivity before vaccination36.

Unexpectedly, diabetic patients exhibited a better vaccine response than non-diabetic individuals in the multivariate analysis. This phenomenon, which may depend on the vaccine studied, has been poorly explored in the literature, although it has been mentioned previously37. Notably, this effect appeared to be independent of the type of diabetes treatment (insulin, oral medication, or both) and glycemic control (HbA1c < or > 7.5). Similar findings were reported in a post-hoc analysis of the CAPiTA trial, where improved vaccine response in patients with diabetes was independent of age, sex, smoking status, and other comorbidities38. Although hyperglycaemia has been associated with impaired innate immune responses, this relationship could not be assessed in our cohort because HbA1c levels were comparable between groups39,40. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than a causal relationship. The underlying mechanisms driving this enhanced immunogenicity in diabetes remain unclear and warrant further molecular investigations to explore the potential metabolic, inflammatory, and immune-related factors37,38.

The second factor that improved the vaccine response (multivariate analysis) was vaccination within 48 hours after apyrexia. In the absence of previous reports, the reasons for this effect can only be speculated. The benefit of a short delay after apyrexia may correspond to a different post-infection inflammatory status with limited immune cell exhaustion (18), and may serve as an adjuvant-like effect. Supporting this hypothesis, Fourati et al. (2022) demonstrated in a pan-vaccine system immunology analysis that distinct pre-vaccination immune endotypes can predict antibody responses across 13 different vaccines41. In particular, individuals with a pro-inflammatory transcriptional profile developed significantly higher antibody responses one month after vaccination. These findings suggest that the immune state at the time of vaccination may influence subsequent responsiveness.

Although safety was not the primary focus of this study, adverse events were recorded. No events were attributable to vaccination. Injection-site reactions and other mild adverse events were likely underestimated, given the patients’ infectious profile and the timing of AE recording (T1).

This study had several limitations related to its design and methodology that should be considered. First, the study did not include a control group. However, the observed results are consistent with real-world observational data, in which vaccine response rates in immunocompromised individuals were comparable to those in healthy populations42. Second, selection bias cannot be excluded, as a substantial proportion of eligible patients were not approached due to operational constraints, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic when clinical and research resources were redirected toward pandemic-related care; however, baseline characteristics were similar between completers and non-completers (Table S1). Another important limitation of this study is that it assessed immunogenicity but not clinical efficacy. Therefore, the observed vaccine response rate should not be interpreted as a measure of clinical protection against pneumococcal disease. The post-vaccination follow-up time (T1) was chosen because it corresponds to the peak humoral response4. However, extending the follow-up to one year with repeated measurements could provide valuable insights into long-term immunogenicity trends24,42–45. In addition, immunogenicity assessment at T1 consisted of a minimum delay but not a strict timing. This pragmatic approach is applicable in the real world. The potential impact on antibody levels cannot be ruled out. Given that peak antibody responses to conjugate vaccines generally occur between 4 and 8 weeks after vaccination, variability in the timing of T1 sampling (mean 42 ± 16 days) could theoretically influence the measured IgG levels. However, in exploratory analyses, the time from vaccination to T1 sampling was not significantly associated with vaccine response, suggesting that this variability did not materially affect the overall findings. Third, antibody levels alone do not fully reflect functional immunity, as they do not assess opsonophagocytic activity (OPA), a key determinant of protective immunity. OPA assays were not performed in this study because they require the absence of recent antimicrobial therapy. An alternative approach to evaluate immune function could involve measuring the cellular response using an enzyme-linked immunospot assay or cytokine release, which was not assessed in this study3,46.

Despite these limitations, our study provides real-world data from hospitalized patients with infections, directly addressing an important public health question. The overall vaccine response rate of 46% remained acceptable given the high comorbidity burden and the infectious context. Notably, when less stringent criteria were applied, a larger proportion of patients achieved adequate seroprotection, suggesting that immune benefits extend beyond conventional response definitions. Patients with a high Charlson score appeared to be at increased risk of non-response and may benefit from tailored vaccination strategies. Although routine post-vaccination serological monitoring is unlikely to be feasible in routine practice, this subgroup may represent a population in whom optimized vaccination strategies, including potential booster approaches, should be evaluated in prospective studies.

In conclusion, our findings support the implementation of a systematic vaccination strategy during hospitalization, including in patients admitted for an infectious episode once apyrexia has been achieved for 48 hours. They also highlight the importance of focused follow-up in patients with a high comorbidity burden and the need to further investigate vaccine responses in patients with diabetes.

Methods

Study design

The study protocol was approved by an Ethics Committee for Research Involving Human Subjects (Comité de Protection des Personnes, CPP) under the reference ID RCB 2020-A02946-33. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study. Figure 1 shows the design of a single-center prospective observational study that evaluated the immunogenicity of PCV13 in adult patients treated for infection. The study was conducted at the IDD of Nice University Hospital (France), with an enrolment period spanning April 19, 2021, to April 19, 2023.

Study population

The study (NCT 04823039) included adult patients aged < 80 years who were hospitalized in the IDD department for more than 48 h due to an infection and met the criteria for PCV13 vaccination as recommended by the French National Authority for Health (HAS). Infection was defined as a body temperature greater than 38 °C associated with blood markers of inflammation, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and/or leucocyte count, and either confirmed by microbiological data or strongly suspected with no alternative diagnosis. The non-inclusion criteria were as follows: a declared previous pneumococcal vaccination, either with a prime–boost strategy administered within the past five years or with a single dose of PPSV23 administered within the past year; documented hypersensitivity to vaccine components or diphtheria toxoid; pregnancy or breastfeeding; and individuals under legal protection.

To maintain a balanced study cohort, after the 50th COVID-19 enrolment, the recruitment of these patients was terminated, minimizing the risk of over-representation of SARS-CoV-2 cases.

Vaccination

After informed consent was obtained, the patients received PCV13 (Prevenar 13, Pfizer), which included 13 pneumococcal capsular polysaccharides conjugated to the CRPT197 protein: 1, 3, 4, 5, 6A, 6B, 7F, 9V, 14, 18C, 19A, 19F, and 23F43. The protocol imposed a minimum requirement of one day of apyrexia combined with clinical improvement before proceeding with the vaccination. The actual delay in vaccination was carefully monitored.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the vaccine response rate (VRR), assessed at T1 (at least one month after vaccination). According to the Vaccinal ImmunoMonitoring Platform of the Paris Immunology Laboratory at Cochin Hospital23,24,27, the vaccine response criteria for each serotype were defined as follows: a positive response based on a two-fold increase in [IgG] concentration compared to the baseline, indicating favourable seroconversion23,24,27, and an [IgG] concentration ≥1 µg/mL, indicating favourable protection47.

Patients were classified as responders if they met these French criteria for at least 70% of the serotypes assessed (i.e., 9 out of 13)23,24. Patients were classified as pre-immunized at baseline when ≥9 of 13 serotype-specific antibody levels were ≥1 µg/mL. Pre-immunized patients were retained in the primary analysis to reflect real-life hospital practice where baseline serostatus is unknown. This definition was consistently used for all analyses, including those examining factors associated with vaccine response.

In the absence of consensus, we also assessed the following American guidelines27: IgG concentration ≥ 1.3 µg/mL AND at least twice as high as the baseline level. Moreover, if an IgG concentration of <1.3 µg/mL was observed, a strong level of seroconversion was required (four times higher IgG levels than the baseline).

Safety assessment

Adverse events (AEs) were collected at the T1 visit. At each assessment, the clinical team evaluated the severity of each AE (mild, moderate, or severe) and determined its potential relationship with vaccination. As the safety profile of PCV13 has already been reviewed, no particular focus has been placed on vaccination-related adverse events48. Adverse events were classified according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0.

Laboratory techniques

Just before vaccination, innate and adaptive immunity were evaluated by neutrophil and monocyte counts for innate immunity and gammaglobulins, B, or T lymphocyte counts for adaptive immunity. IgG antibodies for the 13 serotypes were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), following the WHO guidelines, at the Paris Immunology Laboratory (NF EN ISO 15189 accredited). The results were expressed as geometric mean concentrations (GMC).

Statistical analysis

Study data were collected and managed using the REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the CHU NICE49,50. The sample size was determined a priori based on estimation precision, as specified in the study protocol (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04823039). Given the absence of robust data on pneumococcal vaccine response rates in patients recovering from sepsis, a vaccine response rate of approximately 50% was considered clinically meaningful in this high-risk hospitalized population (19). Assuming this response rate, a sample size of 100 evaluable patients would yield a 95% confidence interval with a precision of approximately ±10% (39.8%–60.2%). Anticipating approximately 10% of non-evaluable patients, the planned sample size was set at 110 participants. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to analyze the associations between vaccine response, clinical factors, infection severity, and immune and inflammatory parameters. The multivariate model included all factors with a p-value ≤ 0.20 in univariate analyses. The results were presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All analyses were performed using the SAS Enterprise Guide software (version 7.1; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism v9.1.2 (GraphPad Software Inc.).
