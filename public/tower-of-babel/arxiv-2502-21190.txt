Geodesic Slice Sampler for Multimodal Distributions with Strong Curvature
Bernardo Williams, Hanlin Yu, Hoang Phuc Hau Luu, Georgios Arvanitidis, Arto Klami

Abstract
Traditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling methods often struggle with sharp curvatures, intricate geometries, and multimodal distributions. Slice sampling can resolve local exploration inefficiency issues and Riemannian geometries help with sharp curvatures. Recent extensions enable slice sampling on Riemannian manifolds, but they are restricted to cases where geodesics are available in closed form. We propose a method that generalizes Hit-and-Run slice sampling to more general geometries tailored to the target distribution, by approximating geodesics as solutions to differential equations. Our approach enables exploration of regions with strong curvature and rapid transitions between modes in multimodal distributions. We demonstrate the advantages of the approach over challenging sampling problems.



1
Introduction

Sampling from a differentiable unnormalized log-density defined on a Euclidean space is a core problem in machine learning and statistics. While gradient-based Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods have proven effective in many scenarios, they often face significant challenges when the target distribution exhibits complex geometry (sharp curvature) or multimodal behavior. The two core challenges are largely addressed with complementary techniques, with little work in algorithms that excel for targets that are
both
multimodal and complex in shape.

Complex shapes and sharp curvatures are often addressed by using a suitably chosen Riemannian geometry within the sampling algorithms
[
Girolami and Calderhead 2011
]
. Instead of operating in a Euclidean space and metric, the samplers carry out the necessary operations using a metric that adapts to the curvature of the parameter space. In practice, the methods follow flows induced by the metric, in most cases by numerical integration, and consequently the methods are sometimes called
geodesic
methods as in our title. Various practical metrics and sampling algorithms have been shown to improve sampling of targets with strong curvature
[
Girolami and Calderhead 2011
,
Byrne and Girolami 2013
,
Lan et al. 2015
,
Hartmann et al. 2022
,
Yu et al. 2024
,
Williams et al. 2024
]
, albeit always with increased computational cost.

Multimodality, in turn, is most commonly addressed by tempering or diffusion techniques
[
Earl and Deem 2005
,
Chen et al. 2024
]
. These methods use tempered – smoothed – version of the target to improve exploration over multiple modes, intuitively changing the problem itself so that the modes are connected with areas of sufficient probability. At high degree of tempering these methods can efficiently explore the different modes, but low tempering is needed for accurate sampling within the modes, necessitating adaptive or parallel sampling with different degrees of tempering.
The efficiency of parallel tempering depends on the swap acceptance rate between adjacent temperatures, which can decrease in high dimensions if the temperature schedule is not well-tuned
[
Woodard et al. 2009
]
.
Diffusion-based approaches, in turn, require careful choice of the noise schedule to balance exploration and accuracy
[
Song and Ermon 2019
,
Chen et al. 2024
]
. Unlike tempering, diffusion methods can achieve smooth transitions between modes without explicitly maintaining a set of parallel chains, but the acceptance rate of noisy samples can be low
[
Chen et al. 2024
]
.

Even though the two approaches are efficient in addressing the two challenges separately, there is very little work on samplers designed for the general setup where both difficulties may arise simultaneously. One could consider e.g. parallel tempering in a Riemannian metric – see
Byrne and Girolami 2013
for a rare example in this intersection – but ideally we would like to address both aspects using a common mechanism. This work explores one such approach, developing a Riemannian sampler capable of efficiently exploring multiple modalities, without any tempering for the target distribution. Instead, we seek to improve mode exploration by change of the metric, in the spirit of the early work by
Lan et al. 2014
that developed a specific metric solely for this purpose. Their metric, however, requires explicit identification and tracking of the modes and is more like a conceptual demonstration, and we are not aware of any other works aiming for efficient multi-modal samplers solely by the change of the metric.

Our starting point is the idealized slice sampler, with computable level sets.
The performance of such an idealized sampler is theoretically independent of the sampling problem, as pointed out by
Durmus et al. 2023
:

“This means that the performance of the idealized slice sampler is ignorant of the introduction of, e.g., multimodality, local modes, or anisotropy as long as the volume of the level sets is not modified.”

From a practical perspective, we build on the (Euclidean) Hit-and-Run slice sampler by
Bélisle et al. 1993
, which at each iteration selects a random direction and then samples from the resulting one-dimensional distribution formed by the intersection of the line and the slice.
In effect, it transforms multi-dimensional sampling into sequential one-dimensional sampling tasks, but the overall sampler may be inefficient. Especially in higher dimensions the intersection with the slice can be small for almost all directions
Murray et al. 2010
.

Both
Habeck et al. 2023
and
Durmus et al. 2023
recently considered generalizations of the Hit-and-Run sampler for Riemannian manifolds, replacing the lines with geodesics. We build on the general algorithmic framework introduced by
Durmus et al. 2023
and adapt it to the task of sampling from a distribution with a complex geometry. Specifically, we begin by embedding the (Euclidean) sampling space into a higher-dimensional space that incorporates the target distribution’s geometric information, such as Fisher information or Monge embedding
[
Hartmann et al. 2022
]
. This transforms the problem into sampling from a particular Riemannian manifold where the target distribution corresponds to the Hausdorff density (see Section
3
).
Note that even though we leverage components proposed by
Durmus et al. 2023
, our task is fundamentally more difficult. Their starting point was sampling of a density on a known manifold (e.g. a sphere) where the geodesics are known exactly, whereas
the complexity of our embedding manifold requires us to approximate the geodesics using numerical integrators.

In particular, we introduce two new computationally efficient metrics that both improve sampling over multimodal targets by, in a sense, pulling the modes closer to each other; see Figure

\lambda
controlling how much the metric warps the space. In addition,

that combines the proposed method with separate sampler for improved exploration of local modes.

We empirically demonstrate improved sampling over Euclidean methods for complex targets, and highlight improved mixing over multiple modes in high dimensional-cases when compared against parallel tempering

Background: Slice sampling

The classic work of
Neal 2003
introduces Slice Sampling as a method for generating samples by uniformly sampling from the
\R^{D+1}
manifold defined by the graph of the probability density.
Let
p(\bx)
be an unnormalized continuous target density that satisfies
\int p(\bx)\dbx<\infty
. Suppose that direct sampling from
p(\bx)
is not feasible.
We consider densities where
\bx\in\R^{D}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

Idealized slice sampling defines a uniform distribution over the volume under the graph of
p(\bx)
and generates samples through the following two steps:

1.

Sample
s\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,p(\bx))
.

2.

Sample
\bx\sim\mathrm{Unif}(L(s))
.

where the slice is given by
L(s):=\{\bx\mid p(\bx)>s\}
. For special cases, such as log-concave or rotationally invariant densities, the slice sampler has theoretical performance guarantees
[
Natarovskii et al. 2021
]
. However, for more complex distributions, drawing uniform samples from
L(s)
is often impractical
[
Rudolf and Ullrich 2018
]
.

To address this, the step-out and shrinkage procedures are used. Below, we provide an informal explanation of these procedures. The full algorithm is detailed in the appendix (Algorithms
3
and
4
).
Both procedures were first introduced by
Neal 2003
, but we adopt a modified version of the shrinkage step as proposed by
Durmus et al. 2023
. Both versions are equally valid. For a moment, assume a univariate density
p(x)
and a current position
x\in\R
. The procedures are as follows:

The Step-Out Procedure

The step-out procedure, illustrated in Figure
1
, takes two parameters: the width
w\in\R
and maximum steps
m\in\N
. Given the slice
L(s)
, the goal is to expand an interval around the current point
x
. Consider the auxiliary function
\gamma_{x}(t)=x+t
.

The initial left
\ell
and right
r
points are set at a random distance
w
apart. This is done by sampling
u\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,w)
and setting
\ell=-u
and
r=\ell+w
. To ensure that at most
m+1
expansion steps are performed (combined for both directions), a random integer
\iota\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{1,\dots,m\})
is sampled. The right limit is expanded up to
\iota
times, and the left limit up to
m+1-\iota
times.

The expansion proceeds as follows:
The right limit
r
is expanded by adding
w
until
p(\gamma_{x}(r+w))<s
, meaning
\gamma_{x}(r+w)\notin L(s)
. The left limit
\ell
is expanded by subtracting
w
until
p(\gamma_{x}(\ell-w))<s
, meaning
\gamma_{x}(\ell-w)\notin L(s)
.
The procedure returns the updated interval
(\ell,r)
. We denote it by
\text{Step-out}_{w,m}(s,\gamma_{x})
.

The Shrinkage Procedure

The shrinkage procedure selects a sample from the interval
(\ell,r)
by gradually reducing its size until a point is found within
L(s)\cap(\ell,r)
.

The interval
J=(\ell,r)
is treated as a circular domain, meaning that if we move past
r
, we continue from
\ell
. The procedure starts by sampling two points
y
and
z
uniformly within
(\ell,r)
. If neither
\gamma_{x}(y)
or
\gamma_{x}(z)
fall inside
L(s)
, the interval is shrunk as follows:

•

Form the interval
(y\wedge z,y\vee z)
. Update the circular region by

J=\begin{cases}J\cap(y\wedge z,y\vee z),&\text{if }0\in J,\\
J\setminus(y\wedge z,y\vee z),&\text{if }0\notin J.\end{cases}

•

Set
y=z
and update
z\sim\mathrm{Unif}(J)
.

•

This process repeats, each time reducing the size of the interval, until
\gamma_{x}(z)\in L(s)
.

We denote the procedure
\text{Shrink}_{l,r}(s,\gamma_{x})
.
One complete step of the slice sampler is:

1.

Sample
s\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,p(x))

2.

Obtain
\ell,r=\text{Step-out}_{w,m}(s,\gamma_{x})

3.

Sample
t^{*}=\text{Shrink}_{\ell,r}(s,\gamma_{x})
.

4.

Set
x=\gamma_{x}(t^{*})
.

Hit-and-Run

One way to extend slice sampling to multivariate distributions is to combine it with Hit-and-Run sampling,
here presented following
Bélisle et al. 1993
. Let
\dS^{D-1}(\bx)=\{\bbv\in\R^{D}:\norm{\bbv}^{2}=1\}
, and
\bbv\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\dS^{D-1}(\bx))
. An iteration of the whole sampler is:

•

Obtain
\bbv\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\dS^{D-1}(\bx))
.

•

Obtain a sample from the density evaluated along the geodesic
t\mapsto p(\bx+t\bbv)/\int p(\bx+t\bbv)\differential t
.

Figure
3
illustrates a step of the Hit-and-Run
When directly sampling a value
t
from the density along geodesics
t\mapsto p(\bx+t\bbv)/\int p(\bx+t\bbv)\differential t
is not feasible we can use slice sampling on
t\mapsto p(\bx+t\bbv)
, since it is an unnormalized univariate distribution. Define
\gamma_{(x,v)}(t)=\bx+t\bbv
.
The step-out procedure outputs
\ell,r=\text{Step-out}_{w,m}(s,\gamma_{(x,v)})
and
the shrinkage procedure will return
t^{*}=\text{Shrink}_{\ell,r}(s,\gamma_{(x,v)})
. The new sample is
\bx=\gamma_{(x,v)}(t^{*})
.
This is called Hit-and-Run slice sampling or hybrid Slice sampling
[
Łatuszyński and Rudolf 2014
]
. This method extends slice sampling to probability distributions defined over
\R^{D}
.

3
Method

Our main contribution is a geodesic slice sampler that can accommodate arbitrary metrics. It extends the Hit-and-Run slice sampler described above for non-Euclidean geometries, similar to the recent works of
Durmus et al. 2023
and
Habeck et al. 2023
, but instead of leveraging closed-form analytic geodesics of predefined manifolds we induce metrics using characteristics of the target density itself to guide the sampling. Now the geodesics need to be approximated by numerical integrators.
This section explains the sampler and a meta-sampler that combines the core method with separate local sampler for improved efficiency for general metrics, always using
\bG(\bx)
to denote the metric tensor. We will discuss specific metrics in Section
4
.

Straight-line Hit-and-Run sampling can be inefficient because proposals often move away from high-probability regions
[
Murray et al. 2010
]
. To resolve this, we perform slice sampling along geodesic curves that can accommodate the geometry of the target distribution. This improves efficiency when the target distribution is highly curved, or multimodal distributions; see Section
4.2
.
We are interested in sampling problems defined in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
but allow using different plug-in metrics (preferably using the target density information) to enhance exploration. This can be cast as sampling from a distribution defined on a Riemannian manifold where the algorithm in
Durmus et al. 2023
can be employed. Alternatively, it can be seen as Hit-and-Run where straight lines are replaced by curves that better wrap around the level sets of the target density (given the metrics are good enough). However, since the metric is quite general, geodesics can not be obtained in closed-forms and we need to use numerical integrators.
To correctly sample along geodesics, we need three key components: Adjusting for the correct density on the manifold, properly sampling directions using the Riemannian metric, and solving the geodesic equations.

Hausdorff Density:

To ensure we sample from the correct distribution on the manifold with metric
\bG(\bx)
, we must account for the change in measure from the Euclidean space to the manifold. The correct density is the Hausdorff density

p_{\cH}(\bx)=\frac{p(\bx)}{\sqrt{\det\bG(\bx)}}.

(1)

The denominator adjusts for local volume distortion introduced by the metric
\bG(\bx)
, ensuring that the volume over the manifold is preserved and hence maintaining proper sampling behavior. See Appendix
A.5
for further details.

Sampling from the Riemannian Unit Ball:

Instead of sampling a random direction in Euclidean space, we must now sample from the unit geodesic ball under the Riemannian metric, where we can directly use the method proposed by
Durmus et al. 2023
. Given a position
\bx
, a velocity
\bbv
is sampled as follows: First draw
\bbv\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bG^{-1}(\bx))
, and then normalize it to obtain a unit-length vector in the Riemannian metric with:

\bbv\leftarrow\frac{\bbv}{\|\bbv\|_{g}},\quad\text{where}\quad\|\bbv\|_{g}=\sqrt{\bbv^{\top}\bG(\bx)\bbv}.

This ensures that the direction is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere under the metric
\bG(\bx)
. See Appendix
A
for additional implementation details.

Approximating Geodesic Curves

Given a sampled velocity
\bbv
, we need to follow the geodesic curve starting at
\bx
in direction
\bbv
. In general, the geodesic equation

\displaystyle\dot{\bx}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bbv_{k},

\displaystyle\dot{\bbv}_{k}

\displaystyle=-\norm{\bbv}^{2}_{\Gamma^{k}},\quad\mathrm{for}\ k=1,\ldots,D.

(2)

where
\Gamma^{k}_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{2}g^{km}(\partial_{i}g_{mj}+\partial_{j}g_{im}-\partial_{m}g_{ij})
,
does not have a closed-form solution for arbitrary
\bG(\bx)
. See more detail in appendix
A.3
.
Instead, we numerically approximate the exponential map
\gamma_{(\bx,\bbv)}(t)
by solving these differential equations with an ordinary differential equation (ODE) solver, denoted as
\hat{\gamma}_{(\bx,\bbv)}(t)
.
The choice of the metric determines the shape of geodesic trajectories, allowing the sampler to adapt to different target distributions; see Section
4
.

3.1
Meta Sampler and Multimodality

The sampler as described above is valid as such, but we also introduce a simple extension that can further improve sampling for multi-modal targets with complex local structure.

Following
Tjelmeland and Hegstad 2001
,
Łatuszyński et al. 2025
, we create a
meta-sampler
that alternates between using MAGSS for global moves and an arbitrary local MCMC for sampling within each mode.
To generate one sample, we first run
K
-steps of MAGSS followed by
L
-steps of any local MCMC sampler.
We refer to this combined strategy as Meta-MAGSS, detailed in Algorithm
2
(in Appendix).
The main motivation for this hybrid strategy is to leverage gradient-based algorithms for fast exploration of the mode, to utilize their efficient mixing and fast per-iteration computation when they are sufficiently good for the local target. We could in principle use any sampler for the local part, including Riemannian samplers, but we in practice use standard Euclidean Metropolis-adjusted Langevin Algorithms (MALA)
[
Roberts and Tweedie 1996
]
in our experiments.

4
Metrics

The sampler is general, applicable for arbitrary metric and only requiring
\bG(\bx)
to be positive definite and vary continuously. By selecting an appropriate metric we can influence how the geodesics explore the space, controlling the overall sampling behavior. There is no single metric that is optimal for all targets, and the metrics proposed in the literature are motivated by complementary argumentation, with notable emphasis in computational efficiency.

Next we discuss the metric choice.
The literature has exclusively focused on metrics that improve local exploration for complex target distributions, with several practical solutions that we re-cap in Section
4.1
. We then turn our attention on how to improve exploration of multiple modes, presenting novel metrics specifically designed for this in Section
4.2
.

4.1
For adapting to local curvature

The Fisher metric

The Fisher Information Metric (FIM) is defined as the covariance of the score function, and was predominantly used in the early Riemannian methods
[
Girolami and Calderhead 2011
]
due to its close connection to estimation theory. A general form of the metric is

\bG_{F}(\bx)=\Ex_{\by|\bx}\left[\nabla_{\bx}\log p(\by|\bx)\nabla_{\bx}\log p(\by|\bx)^{\top}\right],

but the specific form depends on the underlying problem, due to integration over the conditional density. Furthermore, it requires direct matrix inversion for computing
\bG_{F}^{-1}(\bx)
that is required during geodesic computations (Eq.
2
), with complexity of
\mathcal{O}(D^{3})
. This makes the metric impractical and inefficient for high-dimensional problems.

The Monge Metric

The computational cost of solving the geodesic equations (Eq.
2
) is primarily determined by the inversion of the metric tensor, and consequently metrics with closed-form inverse offer significant savings. The Monge metric by
Hartmann et al. 2022
naturally arises from the geometry of the graph of log-density function when viewed as a submanifold embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
. Let
\alpha^{2}\geq 0
and
\lambda\geq 0
. The Monge metric and its inverse are given by

\displaystyle\bG_{M}(\bx)

\displaystyle=\bI_{D}+\alpha^{2}\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top},

\displaystyle\bG_{M}^{-1}(\bx)

\displaystyle=\bI_{D}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\|\nabla\ell\|^{2}}\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top}.

(3)

As
\alpha^{2}\to 0
, the metric reduces to the Euclidean metric
\bI_{D}
. The determinant required for computing the Hausdorff density (Equation (
1
)) is
\det\bG_{M}(\bx)=1+\alpha^{2}\|\nabla\ell\|^{2}
.
Figure
3
illustrates the exponential map of geodesic balls with increasing radius under the Monge metric. This metric adapts to the geometry of the target distribution, expanding regions based on the local structure of the density.

The Generative Metric

Another efficient metric, the Generative metric that is proportional to the target density function, was recently proposed by
[
Kim et al. 2024
]
. One of its advantages is that computing the Christoffel symbols
\Gamma^{k}_{ij}
only requires first-order derivatives of the density, whereas the Monge metric (Equation
4.1
) introduces second-order terms. For
p_{0}>0
and
\lambda\geq 0
, the Generative metric and its inverse are:

\displaystyle\bG_{g}(\bx)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p(\bx)+\lambda}{p_{0}+\lambda}\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{D},

(4)

\displaystyle\bG_{g}^{-1}(\bx)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p_{0}+\lambda}{p(\bx)+\lambda}\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{D}.

(5)

As
\lambda\to\infty
, the metric reduces to the Euclidean metric. The determinant is

\det\bG_{g}(\bx)=\left(\tfrac{p(\bx)+\lambda}{p_{0}+\lambda}\right)^{2D}
.
Figure
1
illustrates the effect of
\lambda
on the Hausdorff density along geodesics
t\mapsto p_{\mathcal{H}}\left(\hat{\gamma}_{(\bx,\bbv)}(t)\right)
, and Figure
3
again shows how the metric transforms the space.

Figure 3:

. Each color represents a bigger radius from the base point (

4.2
For Bridging the Modes

The above metrics adapt for the local curvature and have been designed to improve sampling of, for instance, narrow funnels by re-defining the proximity (see Figure
3
). For assisting exploration of multimodal targets we need different kinds of metrics: Now we would want a metric that makes modes that are far away in the original Euclidean sense appear closer (see Figure
1
).
With the exception of the construction of
Lan et al. 2014
, which we will discuss more closely in Section
6
, we are not aware of any previous metrics designed for this. Next, we introduce two computationally efficient metrics, with fast inverses and determinants, for assisting multi-modal sampling.

We start by reminding that any matrix
\bG(\bx)
defines a valid Riemannian metric as long as it is positive definite for every
\bx\in\man
and varies continuously on
\man
. Since the inverse of a positive definite matrix is also positive definite, we observe that it is possible to use any of the previously formulated
\bG^{-1}(\bx)
for defining a metric. Concretely, we propose:

The Inverse Monge Metric

We use

\displaystyle\bG_{IM}(\bx)

\displaystyle=\bI_{D}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\|\nabla\ell\|^{2}}\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top}.

as the metric tensor, with the inverse
\bG_{IM}^{-1}(\bx)=\bG_{M}(\bx)
given by the previously introduced metric tensor of the standard Monge metric
(Eq. (
4.1
)). The determinant of this metric is
1/\det(\bG_M)_{M}
, and hence it retains the computational efficiency of the original Monge metric.

The Inverse Generative Metric

Similarly, we define

\displaystyle\bG_{Ig}(\bx)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p_{0}+\lambda}{p(\bx)+\lambda}\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{D},

and obtain the inverse
\bG_{Ig}^{-1}(\bx)=\bG_{g}(\bx)
as the metric tensor of the standard Generative metric (Eq. (
4
)) and the determinant as
1/\det(\bG_g)_{g}
. Again, the computational efficiency of the original Generative metric is retained.

Next, we explain how the inverse Monge and inverse Generative achieve the desired mode-bridging ability. Geodesic curves maintain a constant velocity norm in the Riemannian sense by construction. Let
\gamma_{(\bx_{0},\bbv_{0})}(t)=\bx_{t}
be a geodesic curve with velocity
\dot{\gamma}_{(\bx_{0},\bbv_{0})}(t)=\bbv_{t}
, starting from
\bx_{0}
with initial velocity
\bbv_{0}
. If the geodesic moves toward a low-probability region where
p(\bx_{t})\to 0
, then we the “mode bridging” behavior occurs if the Euclidean velocity norms satisfy
\norm{\bbv_0}_{0}_{2}\gg\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{2}
.
This means that as
t
increases in low-density regions, geodesics traverse these areas quickly,
bringing distant modes closer. Figure
1
illustrates this effect for the inverse Generative metric. And the following observation collects this idea which we discuss further in Appendix
A.6
:

Observation 1
.

Let
p(\bx)
be a smooth density function. Let
(\bx_{t},\bbv_{t})
be the geodesic flow with initial conditions
(\bx_{0},\bbv_{0})
with respect to the inverse Generative/Monge metric. Then, for
t
such that
p(\bx_{t})\to 0
, the Euclidean velocity norm
\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{2}
gets small, implying the
low value density regions are covered faster as function of time
t
.

Comparison methods

To showcase the effect of the metric we will be running MAGSS also with in Euclidean metric, with
\bG(\bx)=\boldsymbol{I}_{D}
, and we additionally compared against parallel tempering and diffusive Gibbs sampling.

Parallel Tempering (PT) runs many Markov Chains in parallel, each of which has
p(\bx)^{1/\tau_{i}}
as targets for different temperatures
\tau_{i}\geq 1
, where for
\tau_{1}=1
the original target is recovered. As
\tau\to\infty
, the density flattens, facilitating transitions between regions of higher densities that are far apart from each other. The parallel chains jumps randomly between each other, thus visiting the modes more often according to a Metropolis-Hastings ratio
[
Swendsen and Wang 1986
,
Geyer 1991
]
.
Our implementation of PT follows
Łatuszyński et al. 2025
.

Diffusive Gibbs sampling (DiGS) by
Chen et al. 2024
is a sampler designed for addressing multi-modality.
It approaches the sampling task by using an auxiliary variable with a Gibbs scheme. It uses the variance preserving (VP)
[
Song et al. 2021
]
noise scaling:
p(\tilde{\bx}|\bx)=\cN(\tilde{\bx}|\alpha_{t}\bx,\sigma^{2}_{t})
, where
\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}^{2}}
, sampled directly and
p(\bx|\tilde{\bx})\propto p(\tilde{\bx}|\bx)p(\bx)
sampled through a local MCMC sampler. It has an additional Metropolis within Gibbs proposal scheme
q(\bx|\tilde{\bx})=\cN(\bx|\tilde{\bx}/\alpha_{t},(\alpha_{t}/\sigma_{t})^{2})
. VP has the property that at when
\alpha_{t}\to 0
then
p(\tilde{\bx}|\bx)=\cN(\tilde{\bx}|0,\bI_{D})
and when
\alpha_{t}\to 1
then
p(\tilde{\bx}|\bx)=\delta_{\bx}
.

6
Related work

Lan et al. 2014
constructed the Wormhole Hamiltonian Monte Carlo where a specific geometry is constructed to (only) connect the modes of multi-modal distributions. The modes are first identified along the Markov Chain evolution. After a new mode identification, it "stores" the mode’s location for later use. A jump using the updated mode candidates guarantees correct detailed balance equations. While this work served as an inspirational motivation for us, it requires notable additional components. MAGSS does not require separate identification or storage of the modes, but instead shrinks the distances by naturally warping the space.

7
Conclusions

Our aim was to show that multimodality and local curvature can be address by the same set of tools, namely Riemannian geometry. We provided a concrete Riemannian slice sampler, introduced two new metrics for improving mixing between modes, and showed that we can achieve accuracy and mixing comparable to recent samplers designed specifically for multi-modal targets, by only using a metric for this.

The obvious limitation is the computational cost. We form geodesics by numeric integration and even when using metrics with closed-form inverses the per-iteration cost of MAGSS is larger than of competing methods. However, we note that we used maximally exact solvers rather than seeking for the highest computational efficiency. Now that the principle has been demonstrated, use of more approximative numerical integrators for speeding up the overall computation could be studied in future work.

A.3
The Geodesic Equations

We provide only the necessary background on Riemannian geometry.

A Riemannian metric tensor is the map
g(\cdot,\cdot):T_{\bx}\man\crossproduct T_{\bx}\man\to\R
where
T_{\bx}\man
is the tangent space. The map must satisfy positive definiteness for every
\bx\in\man
. Namely, the components
\bG(\bx)
are such that for all
\bbv,\boldsymbol{u}\in T_{\bx}\man
, then
g(\bbv,\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\bG(\bx)\boldsymbol{u}>0
(this is equivalent to saying that
\bG(\bx)
is positive definite).

Geodesic equations generalize straight lines to curved manifolds. Let
\man
be a Riemannian manifold with metric tensor
\bG
. The tangent space at a point
\bx
is denoted by
T_{\bx}\man
. A geodesic curve is determined by solving a second-order differential equation with initial conditions
\bx_{0}\in\man
and
\bbv_{0}\in T_{\bx}\man
. The metric components are given by
g_{ij}=\bG(\bx)_{ij}
, and their inverse by
g^{km}=\bG^{-1}(\bx)_{km}
. The geodesic equations are:

\displaystyle\dot{\bx}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bbv_{k},

\displaystyle\dot{\bbv}_{k}

\displaystyle=-\norm{\bbv}^{2}_{\Gamma^{k}},\quad\mathrm{for}\ k=1,\ldots,D.

The Christoffel symbols, using Einstein summation convention, are:
\Gamma^{k}_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{2}g^{km}(\partial_{i}g_{mj}+\partial_{j}g_{im}-\partial_{m}g_{ij})
.

A.4
Sample Uniformly from the Unit Tangent Sphere

Recall the unit tangent sphere is defined as:

\dS^{D-1}_{g}(\bx):=\{\bbv\in\R^{D}:\ \norm{\bbv}_{g}^{2}=1\}.

Durmus et al. 2023
justify the existence of the uniform distribution distribution over
\dS^{D-1}_{g}(\bx)
. One method for producing samples from the uniform distribution on the unit tangent sphere is:

1.

Sample
\boldsymbol{z}\sim N(0,\bI)
.

2.

Transform
\boldsymbol{v}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{G}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{z}
, then
\bbv
is a sample from
\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{G}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}))
.

3.

Compute the Riemannian norm
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{g}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\bG(\bx)\boldsymbol{v}}
.

4.

Project to the boundary
\boldsymbol{v}\leftarrow\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{g}}
.

A.5
Hausdorff measure

The volume form of a Riemannian Manifold with metric
\bG(\bx)
is defined as
V(\dbx):=\sqrt{\det\bG(\bx)}\dbx
.
For the technical details about the volume form interested readers can consult Proposition 2.41 in
Lee 2018
.
The volume element gives the natural measure on the manifold, analogous to the Lebesgue measure in the Euclidean space
[
Durmus et al. 2023
]
. The Hausdorff density is defined as the density which
integrates to one with respect to the volume element:

p_{\cH}(\bx)=\frac{p(\bx)}{\sqrt{\det\bG(\bx)}}.

A.6
Observation and Proposition

Observation 1
.

Let
p(\bx)
be a smooth density function. Let
(\bx_{t},\bbv_{t})
be the geodesic flow with initial conditions
(\bx_{0},\bbv_{0})
with respect to the inverse Generative/Monge metric. Then, for
t
such that
p(\bx_{t})\to 0
, the Euclidean velocity norm
\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{2}
gets small, implying the
low value density regions are covered faster as function of time
t
.

Denote by
\bG_{IM}(\bx)
the inverse Monge metric and by
\bG_{Ig}(\bx)
the inverse generative metric.
The metrics are defined as:

\bG_{Ig}(\bx)=\left(\frac{p_{0}+\lambda}{p(\bx)+\lambda}\right)^{2}\bI,\quad\bG_{IM}(\bx)=\bI-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\nabla\ell(\norm{\nabla\ell(\bx)})^{2}}\nabla\ell(\bx)\nabla\ell(\bx)^{\top}.

Analysis for the Generative Metric

Assume a geodesic curve starting at
(\bx_{0},\bbv_{0})
satisfies
p(\bx_{0})\geq p(\bx_{t})
for all
t\geq 0
and
p(\bx_{t})\to 0
. Recall that along a geodesic curve, the magnitude of the velocity with respect to the metric remains constant:

\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}^{2}_{\bG_{Ig}}=\norm{\bbv_0}_{0}^{2}_{\bG_{g}}\quad\forall t.

Thus, the equality holds:

\displaystyle\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}^{2}_{\bG_{Ig}}

\displaystyle=\norm{\bbv_0}_{0}^{2}_{\bG_{Ig}}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{p_{0}+\lambda}{p(\bx_{0})+\lambda}\right)^{2}\norm{\bbv_0}_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p_{0}+\lambda}{p(\bx_{t})+\lambda}\right)^{2}\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{p(\bx_{t})+\lambda}{p(\bx_{0})+\lambda}\right)^{2}\norm{\bbv_0}_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{2}^{2}.

Since
p(\bx_{0})\geq p(\bx_{t})
, it follows that
\norm{\bbv_0}_{0}_{2}^{2}\geq\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{2}^{2}
.

Analysis for the Inverse Monge Metric

Assume a geodesic curve starting at
(\bx_{0},\bbv_{0})
satisfies
p(\bx_{0})\geq p(\bx_{t})
for all
t\geq 0
and
p(\bx_{t})\to 0
. Assume
\bx_{0}
is not a critical point, i.e.,
\nabla\log p(\bx_{0})\neq 0
. Since
p(\bx)\geq 0
is smooth with finite integral, we have
\nabla p(\bx_{t})\to 0
. Then:

\displaystyle\norm{\bbv_0}_{0}_{\bG_{IM}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}^{2}_{\bG_{IM}}

\displaystyle\norm{\bbv_0}_{0}_{2}^{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\nabla\ell(\norm{\nabla\ell(\bx_0)}_{0})^{2}}\left\langle\nabla\ell(\bx_{0}),\bbv_{0}\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{2}^{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\nabla\ell(\norm{\nabla\ell(\bx_t)}_{t})^{2}}_{2}\left\langle\nabla\ell(\bx_{t}),\bbv_{t}\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\norm{\bbv_0}_{0}_{2}^{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\nabla\ell(\norm{\nabla\ell(\bx_0)}_{0})^{2}}\left\langle\nabla\ell(\bx_{0}),\bbv_{0}\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{2}^{2}.

Since
\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\nabla\ell(\norm{\nabla\ell(\bx_0)}_{0})^{2}}\left\langle\nabla\ell(\bx_{0}),\bbv_{0}\right\rangle^{2}>0
, it follows that
\norm{\bbv_0}_{0}_{2}^{2}>\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{2}^{2}
.

Joint conclusion

Intuitively, this means that if we are on a position
\bx_{t}
along the trajectories we described before where the velocity is of small Euclidean norm
\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{2}
then the Riemannian norm of that vector is much higher
\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{G}
. Then the Exponential map evaluated at that point and velocity is:

\displaystyle\gamma_{(\bx,\bbv)}(1)=\gamma_{(\bx,\bbv/\norm{\bbv}_{G})(\norm{\bbv}_{G})},

(1)

this means that even a velocity vector with small euclidean norm
\norm{\bbv_t}_{t}_{2}
, corresponds to a larger a unit
\norm{\bbv}_{G}
of time along the geodesic equation.

Note that for a multimodal distribution in dim
\geq 2
Observation
1
guarantees that low density regions will be transversed quickly, but we do not have guarantees any that the geodesics given by the inverse Generative and inverse Monge metrics will connect modes.

Note that this analysis also holds for the inverse Monge metric when the geodesic curve approaches a local maximum of the distribution, which explains the behavior observed in Figure
1
around the local maximum values when
\alpha=0.1
.

Proposition 1
.

An MCMC sampler targeting the Hausdorff density on a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
with metric tensor
\bG(\bx)
also targets the correct distribution on the Euclidean space.

For a proof of the Proposition in a more general setting, consult 1.5 Proposition of section XII.1 in
Amann et al. 2005
.

The volume element on the manifold is defined as
V(\dbx)=\sqrt{\det\bG(\bx)}\dbx
, where
\bG(\bx)
is the Riemannian metric tensor. Let
\bX
be a random variable on
\mathcal{M}
whose law is
p_{\mathcal{H}}
where
p_{\mathcal{H}}(\bx)
is the Hausdorff density. Let
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{M})
be a Borel set on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. The probability of
\bX
being in
B
, under the Hausdorff target density, is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bX\in B)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}p_{\mathcal{H}}(\bx)V(\dbx).

(2)

Substituting
p_{\mathcal{H}}(\bx)=\frac{p(\bx)}{\sqrt{\det\bG(\bx)}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bX\in B)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\frac{p(\bx)}{\sqrt{\det G(\bx)}}\sqrt{\det\bG(\bx)}\dbx

\displaystyle=\int_{B}p(\bx)\dbx.

(3)

Thus, the integral of the Hausdorff density with respect to the volume element on the manifold coincides with the integral of the Euclidean density
p(\bx)
over the same set
B
.

Since the probabilities computed for any
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{M})
are identical whether using (
2
) or (
A.6
), the corresponding estimators for the probabilities also coincide. Consequently, an MCMC sampler on the manifold targeting the Hausdorff density
p_{\mathcal{H}}(\bx)
correctly targets the Euclidean density
p(\bx)
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

Appendix C
Mathematical Derivations

C.1
The Generative Metric

The Generative metric components are:

\bG(x)=f(x)\bI=\exp(\log f(x) )\bI,

where the scalar factor is
f(x)=\left(\frac{p(x)+\lambda}{p_{0}+\lambda}\right)^{2}
.

Square root and inverse square root

\displaystyle G^{\tfrac{1}{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=\exp{\tfrac{1}{2} \log f(x)}\bI,

\displaystyle G^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=\exp{-\tfrac{1}{2} \log f(x)}\bI,

\displaystyle\log|\det\bG(\bx)|

\displaystyle=D\log f(x),

where
f(x)
is the scalar factor.

Christoffel symbols derivation

Given the Riemannian metric

G(x)=\left(\frac{p(x)+\lambda}{p_{0}+\lambda}\right)^{2}I,

The metric tensor entries are

G_{ij}(x)=f(x)\delta_{ij}.

The partial derivative of
f(x)
with respect to
x_{i}
is

\partial_{i}f(x)=\frac{d}{dx_{i}}\left(\frac{p(x)+\lambda}{p_{0}+\lambda}\right)^{2}=2\left(\frac{p(x)+\lambda}{p_{0}+\lambda}\right)\frac{1}{p_{0}+\lambda}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{i}}.

The Christoffel symbols for this metric are given by

\displaystyle\Gamma^{k}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2f(x)}\left(\delta_{jk}\partial_{i}f(x)+\delta_{ik}\partial_{j}f(x)-\delta_{ij}\partial_{k}f(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta_{jk}\partial_{i}\log f(x)+\delta_{ik}\partial_{j}\log f(x)-\delta_{ij}\partial_{k}\log f(x)\right).

In matrix notation, let
e_{k}
be the standard basis vectors,

\Gamma^{k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\log f(x)\,e_{k}^{\top}+e_{k}\,\nabla\log f(x)^{\top}-\partial_{k}\log f(x)\bI\right).

The geodesic equations require computing

\norm{\bbv}^{2}_{\Gamma^{k}}=\left\langle\bbv,\nabla\log f\right\rangle\bbv_{k}-\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\bbv}^{2}\partial_{k}\log f.

The geodesic equations read

\displaystyle\dot{\bx}

\displaystyle=\bbv,

\displaystyle\dot{\bbv}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\bbv}^{2}\nabla\log f-\left\langle\bbv,\nabla\log f\right\rangle\bbv.

C.2
The Monge metric

The Monge metric and its inverse,

\bG(x)=\bI+\alpha^{2}\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top},\quad\bG^{-1}(x)=\bI-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}}\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top}.

Square root and inverse square root

Define the quantity
L_{\alpha}:=1+\alpha^{2}\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}
, then the values are:

\displaystyle G^{1/2}(x)

\displaystyle=\bI+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\sqrt{L_{\alpha}}}\nabla\ell(\bx)\nabla\ell(\bx)^{\top},

\displaystyle G^{-1/2}(x)

\displaystyle=\bI-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{L_{\alpha}+\sqrt{L_{\alpha}}}\nabla\ell(x)\nabla\ell(x)^{\top},

\displaystyle\log|\det\bG(\bx)|

\displaystyle=\log(1+\alpha^2 \norm{\nabla\ell}^2).

We now derive the quantities.

Hartmann et al. 2022
gave:

\displaystyle\bG^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}(\bx)=\bI+\frac{1}{\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{\alpha}}}-1\right)\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top}

Let us find a numerical sable form for
\bG^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}(\bx)
. Multiplying by a convenient quantity, gives:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{\alpha}}-1}\right)=\frac{1}{\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}}\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{L_{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{L_{\alpha}}}\right)\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{L_{\alpha}}}{1+\sqrt{L_{\alpha}}}\right)=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{L_{\alpha}+\sqrt{L_{\alpha}}}.

The computation of
\bG^{\tfrac{1}{2}}(\bx)
.
For convenience take
\by=\nabla\ell(x)
. The the metric is

\bG(y)=I+yy^{\top}
. Let us assume the square root is of the form

\bG^{\tfrac{1}{2}}(\by)=\bI+\lambda\by\by^{\top}
. Let us formulate a quadratic equation for
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\bG^{\tfrac{1}{2}}(\by)\bG^{\tfrac{1}{2}}(\by)

\displaystyle=\bI+\by\by^{\top}

\displaystyle I+2\lambda\by\by^{\top}+\lambda^{2}\norm{\by}^{2}\by\by^{\top}

\displaystyle=\bI+\by\by^{\top}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(1-2\lambda-\lambda^{2}\norm{\by}^{2}\right)\by\by^{\top}.

The solutions of the quadratic equation are:

\lambda=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1+\|\by\|^{2}}}{\|\by\|^{2}}.

Let us simplify
\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+\|\by\|^{2}}}{\|\by\|^{2}}
, multiply by conjugate

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{1+\|\by\|^{2}}-1}{\|\by\|^{2}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{1+\|\by\|^{2}}+1}{\sqrt{1+\|\by\|^{2}}+1}\right)=\frac{\norm{\by}^{2}}{\norm{\by}^{2}\sqrt{1+\|\by\|^{2}}+1}.

Substitute
\by=\alpha\nabla\ell(\bx)
and we obtain the result:

G^{1/2}(\bx)=\bI+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}\|\nabla\ell(\bx)\|^{2}}+1}\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top}.

Christoffel symbols

For the Monge metric the Christoffel symbols taken from
Hartmann et al. 2022
are:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{k}(\bx)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}}\nabla^{2}\ell\partial_{k}\ell.

The geodesic equations read

\displaystyle\dot{\bx}

\displaystyle=\bbv,

\displaystyle\dot{\bbv}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{L_{\alpha}}\norm{\bbv}^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\ell}\nabla\ell.

The Christoffel symbols associated to the inverse Monge metric are, the derivation in Section
C.4
:

\Gamma^{k}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big[L_{\alpha}\left(\nabla f\nabla\ell^{\top}+\nabla\ell\nabla f^{\top}+2f\nabla^{2}\ell\right)\partial_{k}\ell+\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top}\partial_{k}f+\alpha^{2}\left\langle\nabla\ell,\nabla f\right\rangle\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top}\partial_{k}\ell\Big].

The geodesic equations read:

\displaystyle\dot{\bx}

\displaystyle=\bbv,

\displaystyle\dot{\bbv}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big[2L_{\alpha}\left(\left\langle v,\nabla f\right\rangle\left\langle\nabla\ell,v\right\rangle+f\norm{v}^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\ell}\right)\nabla\ell+\left\langle\nabla\ell,v\right\rangle^{2}\nabla f+\alpha^{2}\left\langle\nabla\ell,\nabla f\right\rangle\left\langle\nabla\ell,v\right\rangle^{2}\nabla\ell\Big].

C.3
Monge metric: Christoffel symbols derivation

For completeness let us do a different derivation from
Hartmann et al. 2022
of the Christoffel symbols.
Take the auxiliary function
f(x)=-\frac{1}{L_{\alpha}}
, where
L_{\alpha}=1+\alpha^{2}\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}
. The metric and inverse components are:

\displaystyle g_{ij}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}\ell,

\displaystyle g^{km}

\displaystyle=\delta_{km}+\alpha^{2}f(x)\partial_{k}\ell\partial_{m}\ell.

The derivatives of the metric are

\displaystyle\partial_{i}g_{mj}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\left(\partial_{im}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+\partial_{m}\ell\partial_{ij}\ell\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{j}g_{im}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\left(\partial_{ij}\ell\partial_{m}\ell+\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{jm}\ell\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{m}g_{ij}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\left(\partial_{im}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{jm}\ell\right).

The Christoffel symbols read,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{k}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g^{km}\left(\partial_{i}g_{mj}+\partial_{j}g_{im}-\partial_{m}g_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}g^{km}\left(2\partial_{m}\ell\partial_{ij}\ell\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\sum_{m}\left(\delta_{km}+\alpha^{2}f(x)\partial_{k}\ell\partial_{m}\ell\right)\partial_{m}\ell\partial_{ij}\ell

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\left(\partial_{k}\ell\partial_{ij}\ell+\alpha^{2}f(x)\sum_{m}(\partial_{m}\ell)^{2}\partial_{k}\partial_{ij}\ell\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}\ell\partial_{ij}\left(1-\tfrac{\alpha^{2}\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}}\partial_{k}\ell\partial_{ij}.

Thus, the Christoffel symbols are
\Gamma^{k}_{ij}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{L_{\alpha}}\partial_{k}\ell\partial_{ij}
.
Writing in matrix form
\Gamma^{k}
of size
D\times D
with components
[\Gamma^{k}]_{ij}=\Gamma^{k}_{ij}
,

\Gamma^{k}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}}\nabla^{2}\ell\partial_{k}\ell.

Let us compute
\norm{\bbv}^{2}_{\Gamma^{k}}
, which appears in the geodesic equations:

\displaystyle v^{\top}\Gamma^{k}v

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}}\norm{v}^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\ell}\partial_{k}\ell.

The geodesic equations read:

\displaystyle\dot{\bx}

\displaystyle=\bbv,

\displaystyle\dot{\bbv}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{L_{\alpha}}\norm{\bbv}^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\ell}\nabla\ell.

C.4
Inverse Monge metric: Christoffel symbols derivation

Again the auxilary function is
f(x)=-\frac{1}{L_{\alpha}}
, where
L_{\alpha}=1+\alpha^{2}\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}
, the metric and inverse components are:

\displaystyle g_{ij}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}+f(x)\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}\ell

\displaystyle g^{km}

\displaystyle=\delta_{km}+\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}\ell\partial_{m}\ell.

The derivatives of the metric are (we mark with the same color repeating terms):

\displaystyle\partial_{i}g_{mj}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\left(\partial_{i}f\partial_{m}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}f\partial_{im}\ell\partial_{j}\ell}+\color[rgb]{1,0,1}{f\partial_{m}\ell\partial_{ij}\ell}\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{j}g_{im}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\left(\partial_{j}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{m}\ell+\color[rgb]{1,0,1}{f\partial_{ij}\ell\partial_{m}\ell}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{jm}\ell}\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{m}g_{ij}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\left(\partial_{m}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}f\partial_{mi}\ell\partial_{j}\ell}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{mj}\ell}\right).

Let us compute the Christoffel symbols of the first kind (blue and red terms will cancel out, pink terms add to each other):

\displaystyle\Gamma_{kij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{i}g_{mj}+\partial_{j}g_{im}-\partial_{m}g_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left(\partial_{i}f\partial_{m}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+\partial_{j}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{m}\ell-\partial_{m}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}2f\partial_{ij}\ell\partial_{m}\ell}\right).

The Christoffel symbols of the second kind read:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{k}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g^{km}\left(\partial_{i}g_{mj}+\partial_{j}g_{im}-\partial_{m}g_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\sum_{m}\left(\delta_{km}+\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}\ell\partial_{m}\ell\right)\left(\partial_{i}f\partial_{m}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+\partial_{j}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{m}\ell-\partial_{m}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+2f\partial_{ij}\ell\partial_{m}\ell\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\bigg(\partial_{i}f\partial_{k}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+\partial_{j}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{k}\ell-\partial_{k}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+2f\partial_{ij}\ell\partial_{k}\ell

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}\ell\left(\partial_{i}f\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}\partial_{j}\ell+\partial_{j}f\partial_{i}\ell\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}-\left\langle\nabla f,\nabla\ell\right\rangle\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+2f\partial_{ij}\ell\norm{\nabla\ell}^{2}\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\bigg(\partial_{k}\ell\left(L_{\alpha}\partial_{i}f\partial_{j}\ell+L_{\alpha}\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}f-\alpha^{2}\left\langle\nabla f,\nabla\ell\right\rangle\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}\ell+2L_{\alpha}f(x)\partial_{ij}\ell\right)-\partial_{k}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}\ell\bigg).

Thus, the Christoffel symbols are:

\Gamma^{k}_{ij}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big[\partial_{k}\ell\left(L_{\alpha}\left(\partial_{i}f\partial_{j}\ell+\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}f+2f(x)\partial_{ij}\ell\right)-\alpha^{2}\left\langle\nabla f,\nabla\ell\right\rangle\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}\ell\right)-\partial_{k}f\partial_{i}\ell\partial_{j}\ell\Big].

Written in matrix form:

\Gamma^{k}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big[\partial_{k}\ell\left(L_{\alpha}\left(\nabla f\nabla\ell^{\top}+\nabla\ell\nabla f^{\top}+2f(x)\nabla^{2}\ell\right)-\alpha^{2}\left\langle\nabla f,\nabla\ell\right\rangle\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top}\right)-\partial_{k}f\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top}\Big].

Let us compute
\norm{v}^{2}_{\Gamma^{k}}
which appears in the geodesic equations:

\displaystyle v^{\top}\Gamma^{k}v

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big[\partial_{k}\ell\left(2L_{\alpha}\left(\left\langle v,\nabla f\right\rangle\left\langle\nabla\ell,v\right\rangle+f\norm{v}^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\ell}\right)-\alpha^{2}\left\langle\nabla f,\nabla\ell\right\rangle\left\langle\nabla\ell,v\right\rangle^{2}\right)-\partial_{k}f\left\langle\nabla\ell,v\right\rangle^{2}\Big].

Then the geodesic equations read:

\displaystyle\dot{\bx}

\displaystyle=\bbv,

\displaystyle\dot{\bbv}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big[\left(2L_{\alpha}\left(\left\langle v,\nabla f\right\rangle\left\langle\nabla\ell,v\right\rangle+f\norm{v}^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\ell}\right)-\alpha^{2}\left\langle\nabla f,\nabla\ell\right\rangle\left\langle\nabla\ell,v\right\rangle^{2}\right)\nabla\ell-\left\langle\nabla\ell,v\right\rangle^{2}\nabla f\Big].

Where the gradient of
f
is:

\nabla f=\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{L^{2}_{\alpha}}\nabla^{2}\ell\nabla\ell.

Add Location and Scale parameters for Complex Distributions

In Experiment
5.3
we consider the mixture of two complex distributions. We introduce a location and scale parameter for the components of the mixture.
The Funnel, Squiggle and Rosenbrock distributions are smooth bijective transformations from a
Z\sim\cN(\mu,\Sigma)
to
Y=f(X)
.
Let us add a location and scale parameters by an additional transformation
g(y)=x
, where
g(y)=\Sigma_{y}\bI+\mu_{y}

Z\overset{f}{\xmapsto{\hskip 17.07182pt}}Y\overset{g}{\xmapsto{\hskip 17.07182pt}}X

Change of variables for
g\circ f
gives

\displaystyle p_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=p_{Z}\left((g\circ f)^{-1}(x)\right)\left|\det\partialderivative{x}{z}\right|

\displaystyle=p_{Z}\left((f^{-1}\circ g^{-1})(x)\right)\left|\det\partialderivative{x}{y}\right|\left|\det\partialderivative{y}{z}\right|

Plug in
g^{-1}(x)=\Sigma_{y}^{-1/2}(x-\mu)
,
\det\partialderivative{y}{z}=\det\Sigma^{-1/2}
, and
p_{Z}(z)=\cN(z|\mu,\Sigma)
to obtain the expression for the density of the Funnel and Squiggle distributions with a location and scale parameters
(\mu_{y},\Sigma_{y})
:

p_{X}(x)=\cN\left(f^{-1}(\Sigma^{1/2}(x-\mu))\bigg|\mu,\Sigma\right)\left|\det\partialderivative{x}{y}\right|\left|\det\Sigma_{y}^{-1/2}\right|.
