Can Language Models Represent the Past without Anachronism?
Ted Underwood, Laura K. Nelson, Matthew Wilkens

Abstract
Before researchers can use language models to simulate the past, they need to understand the risk of anachronism. We find that prompting a contemporary model with examples of period prose does not produce output consistent with period style. Fine-tuning produces results that are stylistically convincing enough to fool an automated judge, but human evaluators can still distinguish fine-tuned model outputs from authentic historical text. We tentatively conclude that pretraining on period prose may be required in order to reliably simulate historical perspectives for social research.



1
Introduction

Since human history cannot be repeated in a lab, historical research generally works through observation rather than experiment. And observation is enough to illuminate many aspects of the past. To understand why a bill failed, we read documents and count votes. Questions about cultural change, however, may hinge on forms of influence that are hard to observe directly. It is plausible that the New Woman novel changed early twentieth-century attitudes about marriage, but the extent of that influence remains a largely speculative question.

Artificial intelligence could, in principle, cast new light on this kind of question by enabling researchers to simulate the written culture of past eras. If we want to ask how a particular group of writers affected opinions about marriage, we could fine-tune models with and without their works, and measure changes in model behavior. In this essay we will consider another potential source of distortion, specific to research on the past: the risk that models trained in the present—however carefully prompted or tuned—will import contemporary knowledge, assumptions, styles, or norms into historical contexts where they ought to be absent.

One can, in principle, avoid this risk of anachronism by pretraining models only on material from the past. But pretraining is an expensive, brute-force solution. If historical researchers have to pretrain a cutting-edge model, from scratch, every time they investigate a new social context, LLM-assisted research could demand an impractically large investment of computation and human labor. “Machine forgetting” is not likely to be easier to implement—especially not in a context like this where the content being erased is not a single fact but a whole social context

If it were possible to get performant contemporary models to simulate the past just through appropriate prompting or tuning, that would be a vastly simpler solution. Our goal in this paper is to provide an initial assessment of the difficulty of that task. Can prompting a model with examples from an older period get it to “shift temporal gears”? If not, is fine-tuning sufficient?

In describing this as an “initial assessment of difficulty” we mean to set a fairly low bar. The paper that follows will not exhaustively test all possible solutions or determine best practices. Rather, it tests a few obvious, simple strategies to see if they work better or worse than expected.

Our conclusions are mixed.

•

While in-context learning is generally ineffective,

•

fine-tuning a model on a few hundred examples can produce a fairly good simulation of period style.

•

In head-to-head comparisons, however, human readers still discern the difference between a fine-tuned model and ground truth.

In short, fine-tuning alone is unlikely to erase the risk of anachronism. But it is effective enough to suggest that tuning strategies might eventually be one element of a recipe for producing models that can represent different historical and social vantage points.

2
Prompting with examples from the period

Our first experiment tested the limits of prompting, also known as in-context learning. At first glance, language models seem reasonably good at shifting between voices: certainly they can write a Shakespearean sonnet or “talk like a pirate” well enough to be amusing. Can they, given examples of prose from an earlier period, continue in the same style?

Human beings are not especially good at precisely dating short passages of text, so for this experiment we relied on a RoBERTa model as a judge. We fine-tuned it to predict publication date using a subset of the Corpus of Historical American English (COHA), 1810–2009.

Then we prompted two models to generate continuations of passages from books that were first published between 1905 and 1914 (inclusive). To test a prompting strategy we used GPT-4o, with a system prompt (illustrated in figure

3
Fine-tuning on examples from the period

We might expect fine-tuning to be more effective, since it actually changes the model. And if fine-tuning turned out to be an adequate solution, it would certainly be easier than pretraining models like GPT-1914 from scratch. To assess how well this works, we conducted two different experiments. First, we tested fine-tuning using the same judge model employed above to assess the apparent stylistic “date” of continuations. Then we ran an experiment with human evaluation.

However, this is not a very sensitive test. The RoBERTa model used as a judge here is only trained to predict a date, not to discriminate authentic passages from anachronistic ones. It probably uses coarse stylistic evidence to make that prediction. Human readers, or larger models, might still be able to detect anachronistic content in passages that superficially sound “in-period.”

3.2
Human evaluation

To frame a sufficiently challenging test, we manually selected 250 passages from early twentieth-century books (1905–1914) addressing topics that are understood quite differently a century later.

Our list included, for instance, an encyclopedia entry on Alsace (which was then part of Germany) and one on beri-beri (which was then often explained as a fungal disease rather than a nutritional deficiency). While those are differences of fact, we also selected passages that would display subtler differences of attitude, rhetoric, or imagination. For instance, descriptions of non-European places in the early twentieth century tend to slide into racial generalization. A description of sunrise on the moon written in 1913 imagines rich chromatic phenomena, because no one had yet seen photographs of a world without an atmosphere

Then we generated three sets of completions, using the default versions of 4o and 4o-mini, as well as the five fine-tuned models (using each on the set held out from it).

3.2.1
First evaluation strategy: direct judgment

Our next task was to define a protocol for comparing these completions to ground truth. Our first attempt at human evaluation asked three expert human coders to examine 120 completions each, and decide in each case whether the completion was plausibly appropriate—or perceptibly anachronistic—for a writer in 1914. This intuitive approach worked poorly, but we feel the failure is illuminating and worth discussing.

After a preliminary round of calibration and discussion, raters agreed about the plausibility of a model continuation 79.4% of the time. But since verdicts were imbalanced (“plausible” being twice as common as “not plausible”), this produced a Cohen’s kappa score of only 0.554, which might be described as a “moderate” level of agreement. Moreover, all the annotators characterized this task as quite difficult, often requiring them to do supplementary research in order to decide whether an answer was consistent with the factual state of the world in 1914. Many questions also required delicate judgment calls about attitude (“How ethnocentric is ethnocentric enough to be plausible for 1914?”) One might sum up by saying that readers in 2025 are not especially good at assessing a passage’s congruity with the state of the world a hundred-odd years ago. Even for readers with a doctorate, who have studied the period, this is not a sharply marked boundary about which it is easy to reach consensus.

Second evaluation strategy: intruder test

To frame a more interpretable experiment, we constructed an “intruder test.” For each question, a new set of expert annotators were given four unlabeled responses (including three model answers along with the ground truth passage), and asked to select the “intruder”—the passage actually drawn from a period source. This experiment doesn’t require annotators to directly judge the plausibility of a model’s response. 4
Conclusions

We regard the results of our preliminary experiments as both promising and sobering. Disappointingly, we do not find evidence that simply prompting state-of-the-art language models to generate text “as if” they were historically situated actors produces convincing results. Prompted generations of this type were judged to be plausible in fewer than two thirds of instances. And that number is likely an overestimate of the models’ performance, since it does not penalize cases in which the outputs included telltale disclaimers such as “in 1914, it is not yet known that
x
” or “I am not familiar with
x
as of 1914.” Such disclaimers occurred in as many as 20% of generations, depending on the model used.

The poor performance of in-context learning is unfortunate, because these methods are the easiest and cheapest ones for AI-based historical research. We emphasize that we have not explored these approaches exhaustively. It may turn out that in-context learning is adequate—now or in the future—for a subset of research areas. But our initial evidence is not encouraging.

More promisingly, a commercial LLM fine-tuned on roughly 200 in-period passages at a total cost of $29.50 using API access (that is, without local GPU use) produced outputs that were stylistically similar to our original sources. While fine-tuning requires slightly more work than in-context learning, its demands were far lower than those required to pretrain from scratch even a small and low-performing model like GPT-1914. However, we found that human readers can still distinguish the output of a fine-tuned model from authentic ground truth. Recent work by Fittschen et al. tends to confirm this conclusion, showing that “leakage” from pretraining poses real problems for fine-tuned models in historical linguistics
[
27
]
. While fine-tuned models are an inadequate solution at present for historical research, they may suffice for some research purposes—for instance, where the goal is simply to avoid stylistic dissonance.

Finally, we observe that GPT-1914, a custom GPT-2–class model pretrained on books published between 1880 and 1914, represents a third path for historical language modeling. Its technical and computational requirements are significantly greater than those of the other two methods employed in our study. (If we had used commercial cloud compute rather than NSF resources, training might have cost $1,440.) However, this is the only method guaranteed to be free of anachronism. Nevertheless, GPT-1914 in its current form has serious limitations. It is a small fraction of the parameter size of the other models we used, and so falls short of authentic sources in fluency and cogency, even if it remains uncontaminated by later ideas. To improve fluency, we could of course train a larger model at higher cost.

The extent to which verifiably anachronism-free historical models can match the fluency and reasoning ability of models trained on contemporary web-scale data is an open question for future work. In exploring this question, we should also consider the possibility that anachronism may be in some sense unavoidable. Whether we represent the past by instruction-tuning historical models so they can hold conversations, or by teaching contemporary models to ventriloquize an older period, some compromise may be necessary between the goals of authenticity and conversational fluency. There are, after all, no “authentic” examples of a conversation between a twenty-first-century questioner and a respondent from 1914. Researchers attempting to create such a conversation will need to reflect on the hermeneutic premise that interpretation always involves a negotiation between present and past, or as Hans-Georg Gadamer puts it, a fusion of two cultural “horizons”
[
28
]
.

For the time being, we regard our results as consistent with the hypothesis that simple, low-cost approaches to implementing humanistically credible AI for applied research are not yet sufficient. Further research will be needed before best practices become clear. Part of the challenge may be to construct automated benchmarks, because twenty-first-century human readers (even experts) find it relatively difficult to evaluate the appropriateness of a passage for a given historical period.
