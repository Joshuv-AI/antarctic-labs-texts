Fundamental Scaling Limit in Critical Quantum Metrology
Ju-Yeon Gyhm, Hyukjoon Kwon, Myung-Joong Hwang

Abstract
Critical quantum metrology aims to harness critical properties near quantum phase transitions to enhance parameter estimation precision. However, critical slowing down inherently limits the achievable precision within a finite evolution time. To address this challenge, we establish a fundamental scaling limit of critical quantum metrology with respect to the total evolution time. We find that the winding number of the system’s phase space trajectory determines the scaling bound of quantum Fisher information. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the exponential scaling of the quantum Fisher information can be obtained, and for this, it is necessary to increase the winding number by the total evolution time. We explicitly construct a time-dependent control to achieve optimal scaling from a simple on-off scheme depending on the system’s phase and discuss its topological nature. We highlight that such an exponential scaling of quantum Fisher information remains valid even without reaching the critical point and in the presence of thermal dissipation, albeit with a decreased exponent.

In fully connected models, the Heisenberg scaling of the QFI can be achieved by adiabatically driving the system’s ground state toward the critical point
Garbe et al. 2019
, where the susceptibility diverges. However, this approach suffers from a critical slowing down, as the time required to reach the ground state also diverges, limiting the QFI scaling to
T^{4}

T

;
Gietka et al. 2022

;
Garbe et al. 2022b
, revealing that
T^{6}
scaling and even exponential scaling can be achieved, respectively. Nevertheless, the ultimate scaling limit of critical quantum metrology with respect to the total evolution time
T
, a key performance metric in the presence of critical slowing down, remains unclear.

In this Letter, we establish a fundamental scaling limit in critical quantum metrology and construct an explicit protocol that achieves this scaling. To this end, we introduce the winding number
n
determined by the system’s phase space trajectory, which plays a central role in both bounding the QFI scaling and establishing optimal control. Our QFI scaling bound applies to both adiabatic
Garbe et al. 2019
and non-adiabatic protocols
Chu et al. 2021
;
Garbe et al. 2022a
, as well as non-monotonic protocols
Abah et al. 2022
;
Garbe et al. 2022b
, providing a unified perspective on various scaling behaviors of QFI from different approaches in critical quantum metrology. More surprisingly, we demonstrate that the exponential scaling of the QFI is achievable by determining the optimal winding number for a given time
T
.

We also provide an explicit on-off protocol for achieving optimal scaling, in which the winding number determines how many times to turn the control on and off, depending on the system’s phase. The optimal control undergoes a radical transition at the point where the winding number changes, implying its topological nature.

Finally, we highlight that the QFI scaling with total evolution time is determined solely by the winding number rather than the ability to drive the system close to the critical point. As a result, the exponential scaling of the QFI is achievable even without reaching the critical point and remains robust under thermal dissipation.

Dynamical framework of quantum metrology.—
Suppose a time-dependent Hamiltonian of a quantum system,

\hat{H}(t)=\omega\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{c}(t),

(1)

with
\omega=\omega_{0}+\delta\omega
. Here, an unknown parameter
\delta\omega
is encoded in
\hat{H}_{0}
with a reference parameter
\omega_{0}
, and
\hat{H}_{c}(t)
can be controlled by some tunable parameters. When an initially prepared state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
evolves under
\hat{H}(t)
for total evolution time
T
, the unknown parameter
\delta\omega
can be estimated from the measurement on the final state
|\psi_{T}\rangle
. In this case, the ultimate precision bound, known as the quantum Cramér-Rao bound
Braunstein and Caves 1994
, of estimating
\delta\omega
is given in terms of the QFI,

{\cal F}_{\omega}=\left.4\left[\langle\partial_{\omega}\psi_{T}|\partial_{\omega}\psi_{T}\rangle-|\langle\partial_{\omega}\psi_{T}|\psi_{T}\rangle|^{2}\right]\right|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}.

(2)

To optimize the performance of quantum metrology, the QFI should be maximized for a given total evolution time
T
, subject to constraints in
\hat{H}_{c}(t)

Yuan and Fung 2015
;
Pang and Jordan 2017
;
Yang et al. 2022
. This framework incorporates an arbitrary time-dependent protocol
\hat{H}_{c}(t)
for critical quantum metrology, incorporating conventional quantum metrology as a special case with
\hat{H}_{c}(t)=0
.

Critical quantum metrology with a fully connected system.—
We focus on a fully connected system, described by the following effective Hamiltonian
Hwang et al. 2015
;
Bakemeier et al. 2012
(
\hbar=1
),

\hat{H}(t)=\omega\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-\frac{\epsilon(t)}{4}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a})^{2},

(3)

where
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
are the bosonic creation and annihilation operators, respectively, and a single control parameter
\epsilon(t)
induces squeezing. The critical point lies at
\epsilon=\omega
, where the energy spectrum becomes continuous with the closing energy gap. We limit our analysis to the symmetric phase,
0\leq\epsilon(t)\leq\omega
, where the order parameter remains zero prior to the onset of spontaneous symmetry breaking
Hwang et al. 2015
.

We assume that the system is initially prepared in the vacuum state
|\psi_{0}\rangle=|0\rangle
with
\epsilon=0
. As the Hamiltonian is quadratic in
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, the final state becomes a Gaussian state
Weedbrook et al. 2012
, represented as

|\psi_{t}\rangle=\hat{S}(\xi(t))|0\rangle=e^{\frac{1}{2}(\xi^{*}(t)\hat{a}^{2}-\xi(t)\hat{a}^{\dagger 2})}|0\rangle,

(4)

where
\hat{S}(\xi(t))
is the squeeze operator with
\xi(t)=r(t)e^{-i\varphi(t)}
. From the Schrödinger equation,
\frac{d|\psi_{t}\rangle}{dt}=-i\hat{H}(t)|\psi_{t}\rangle
, the equation of motion can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{r}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon(t)}{2}\sin\varphi(t),

(5)

\displaystyle\dot{\varphi}(t)

\displaystyle=2\omega-\epsilon(t)(1-\coth 2r(t)\cos\varphi(t)).

We then obtain the explicit form of QFI from Eq. (
2
),

{\cal F}_{\omega}=2\left|\int_{0}^{T}dt\sinh 2r(t)e^{i\theta(t)}\right|^{2},

(6)

where
\theta(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\epsilon(t^{\prime})\cos\varphi(t^{\prime})}{\sinh 2r(t^{\prime})}dt^{\prime}
(see Appendix
A
for detailed derivation).

\Phi

\Phi_{\rm opt}^{T}

T_{c}

T=T_{c}

QFI scaling in terms of the winding number.—
We show that the total phase accumulated during the dynamics,
\Phi=\int_{0}^{T}\dot{\varphi}(t)dt
, determines the scaling limit of the QFI. We introduce the winding number

n\equiv\left\lfloor\frac{\Phi}{2\pi}\right\rfloor,

(7)

a topological quantity that counts the number of cycles completed throughout the entire phase-space trajectory, which is independent of the detailed shape of the trajectory (see Fig.
1
).

We present a theorem that establishes the polynomial scaling bound of QFI when the winding number is fixed.

Theorem 1
.

The scaling of QFI with a fixed winding number
n
is bounded by

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}(T)\leq c_{n}T^{4n+6}+o(T^{4n+6}),

(8)

where
c_{n}
is independent of
T
and
o(T^{4n+6})
grows much slower than
T^{4n+6}
.

An explicit form of
c_{n}
can be found in the Appendix
B
. This implies that the winding number
n
determines the order of the polynomial scaling of the QFI with respect to
T
. Based on this, we demonstrate the following no-go theorem on critical quantum metrology with any monotonically increasing
\epsilon(t)
:

Theorem 2
.

For any control with monotonically increasing
\epsilon(t)
, the QFI scaling cannot exceed
T^{6}
as the winding number remains zero.

We note that the monotonically increasing
\epsilon(t)
with a trivial winding number
n=0
, encompasses both adiabatic
Garbe et al. 2019
and quenching processes
Chu et al. 2021
;
Garbe et al. 2022a
widely studied in critical quantum metrology. Thus, Theorem
2
demonstrates that the QFI in such monotonic protocols is fundamentally limited to
T^{6}
scaling, irrespective of the rate at which
\epsilon(t)
changes, while the sudden quench protocol
Chu et al. 2021
saturates the optimal scaling.

Theorems
1
and
2
establish a fundamental limitation on the QFI scaling when the winding number
n
is fixed, regardless of the total evolution time
T
. Surprisingly, however, when the winding number
n
increases with
T
, this limitation can be overcome. In this case, we demonstrate that the exponential scaling of the QFI is achievable with the following bound of the exponent:

Theorem 3
.

The scaling bound of the QFI for a long-time limit (
T\gg 1
) is given by

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}(T)\propto e^{\Gamma\omega T}

(9)

with
\Gamma\approx 0.9745
. This bound can be saturated by taking the winding number
n\approx 0.169\omega T
.

Theorem
3
identifies that the exponential scaling of QFI stems from increasing the winding number with total evolution time. Compared to previous results utilizing periodic driving with a fixed time period
Garbe et al. 2022b
, Theorem
3
improves the exponent by approximately a factor of
7
. We also note that this represents the ultimate bound on the exponent, achievable by optimizing the winding number for a given total evolution time.

On-off control for the optimal QFI scaling.—
Having established the fundamental scaling limit of the QFI, it is important to identify a time-dependent control,
\epsilon(t)
, that realizes the optimal scaling. Remarkably, this can be achieved by a simple on-off scheme based on the phase of the quantum state.

As we are interested in the scaling behavior in the long-time limit
T\gg 1
, we consider a system with sufficiently large squeezing,
|r|\gg 1
after a finite time. In this limit, Eq. (
5
) becomes

\displaystyle\dot{r}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\epsilon(t)\sin(\varphi/2)\cos(\varphi/2),

(10)

\displaystyle\dot{\varphi}(t)

\displaystyle\approx 2\omega-2\epsilon(t)\sin^{2}(\varphi/2),

and
\theta(t)
becomes constant over time. The QFI in Eq. (
6
) is then simplified as
{\cal F}_{\omega}\approx 2\left(\int_{0}^{T}dt\sinh(2r(t))\right)^{2}
, solely governed by squeezing.

We note that
\varphi(t)
is independent of
r
and monotonically increasing by
t
as
\dot{\varphi}(t)\geq 0
. This allows us to express
\epsilon(t)=\frac{2\omega-\dot{\varphi}}{2\sin^{2}(\varphi/2)}
, which leads to an integral expression of the squeezing as

r(T,\Phi)=\int_{0}^{\Phi}d\varphi\left(\frac{2\omega}{\dot{\varphi}}-1\right)\frac{\cot(\varphi/2)}{2},

(11)

with two constraints of the total evolution time
T
and the total phase
\Phi=\int_{0}^{T}\dot{\varphi}(t)dt
. The control parameter’s range
0\leq\epsilon(t)\leq\omega
yields the boundary condition
\frac{1}{2}\leq\tfrac{\omega}{\dot{\varphi}}\leq\frac{\sec^{2}(\varphi/2)}{2}
.

Let us first consider the case with a fixed winding number
n
. We show that there is a unique local maximum of
r(T,\Phi)
within the region
n\pi\leq\Phi<(n+1)\pi
. The corresponding optimal protocol is given by the following on-off scheduling (see Appendix
B
for more details),

\frac{\epsilon}{\omega}=\begin{cases}1&(\cot(\varphi/2)\geq\cot(\phi_{n}^{T}/2))\\
0&(\cot(\varphi/2)<\cot(\phi_{n}^{T}/2)),\end{cases}

(12)

where
\phi_{n}^{T}
is determined by
T
and the winding number
n
. The optimal squeezing behaves as

r(T,\Phi_{n}^{T})\equiv\max_{n\pi\leq\Phi\leq(n+1)\pi}r(T,\Phi)\approx(n+1)\ln(\omega T)+c_{n},

(13)

with some constant
c_{n}
. Here,
\Phi_{n}^{T}
is the total phase within
n\leq\frac{\Phi}{2\pi}<n+1
that gives the optimal squeezing. This achieves to the optimal scaling in Theorem
1
as
\mathcal{F}_{\omega}\approx 4\left|\int_{0}^{T}dt\sinh^{2}(2r)\right|\propto T^{4n+6}
(see Appendix
B
for more details).

Next, we find the global optimum of squeezing
r(T,\Phi)
for a given total evolution time
T
, by maximizing over all possible winding numbers,

r_{\mathrm{max}}(T)=\max_{n}r(T,\Phi_{n}^{T})=r(T,\Phi^{T}_{\rm opt})\approx\frac{\Gamma}{4}\omega T,

(14)

where
\Phi_{\rm opt}^{T}
is the optimal phase among
\{\Phi_{n}^{T}\}
that gives the optimal squeezing. As
r_{\rm max}
linearly scales with
T
, the QFI
\mathcal{F}_{\omega}\approx 2\left|\int_{0}^{T}dt\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\Gamma}{2}\omega T\right)\right|\propto e^{\Gamma\omega T}
scales exponentially on
T
, achieving the optimal scaling in Theorem
3
. We also note that the optimal winding number linearly increases as
n^{T}_{\mathrm{opt}}\approx 0.169\omega T
for a sufficiently large
T
. The numerical simulation in Fig.
2
(a) verifies that the on-off controls yield the optimal scaling of QFI for both cases with a fixed winding number (Theorem
1
) and with an increasing winding number by total evolution time (Theorem
3
).

Interestingly, upon increasing
T
, we observe a series of abrupt jumps in the optimal phase
\Phi_{\rm opt}^{T}
, accompanied with a unit increase in the optimal winding number. At each transition point, the maximum squeezing
r_{\rm max}(T)
changes its functional form, from
\propto(n+1)\ln(\omega T)
to
\propto(n+2)\ln(\omega T)
. This discontinuity associated with the optimal winding number is a consequence of a first-order phase transition in the squeezing parameter, as illustrated in Fig.
2
(b) and (c). This reflects the underlying topological nature in the optimal phase-space trajectory, marked by a change in the topological winding number.

Scaling of the QFI away from the critical point.—
We further explore the role of criticality in QFI scaling by considering a scenario where the system cannot reach the critical point. For a fixed winding number, the squeezing parameter
r
saturates at a finite maximum value, which is in stark contrast to the critical case (Theorem
1
), where
r
continues to increase with the evolution time:

Theorem 4
.

For
0\leq\epsilon(t)\leq\epsilon_{\rm max}<\omega
and a fixed winding number
n
, the squeezing
r
is upper bounded as

\sinh 2r(T)\leq\frac{1}{(1-(\epsilon_{\rm max}/\omega))^{n+1}},

(15)

regardless of the total evolution time
T
. This implies that the QFI eventually saturates to
T^{2}
.

Nevertheless, the exponential scaling of QFI can still be achieved by allowing the winding number to increase with the total evolution time, which leads to the following generalization of Theorem
3
:

Theorem 5
.

With the control parameter in the range
0\leq\epsilon(t)\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}
, the fundamental scaling limit is given as

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}(T)\propto e^{\Gamma(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\omega T}.

(16)

We highlight that
\Gamma(\omega)=\Gamma
described in Theorem
3
, and
\Gamma(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})

smoothly
decreases with decreasing
\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}
and does not vanish even when
\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}
is far from the critical point
\omega
(see Fig.
3
(a)). This observation has interesting implications for designing the optimal protocol to enhance QFI, suggesting that implementing a non-monotonic control to increase the winding number is a more crucial factor than driving the system near the critical point. Since the energy gap opening due to the finite-size effect plays a role analogous to moving away from the critical point in critical dynamics
Hwang et al. 2015
, our analysis is expected to remain valid for capturing the scaling behavior of the QFI beyond the thermodynamic limit.

Robustness under thermal dissipation.—

We analyze the scaling limit of QFI under thermal dissipation by considering the following Lindblad equation,

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}{\hat{\rho}}=&-i[\hat{H}(t),{\hat{\rho}}]+\gamma(\bar{n}+1)(\hat{a}{\hat{\rho}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},{\hat{\rho}}\})\\
&+\gamma\bar{n}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}{\hat{\rho}}\hat{a}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger},{\hat{\rho}}\}),\end{split}

(17)

where
\gamma
is the thermalization rate,
\bar{n}
is the mean occupation number of the bath, and
\{A,B\}=AB+BA
.

Remarkably, the exponential scaling persists under thermal dissipation but with a decreasing exponent (see Appendix
D
for more details),

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}(T)\propto e^{\left(\frac{\Gamma(\epsilon_{\rm max})\omega}{2}-\gamma\right)T}.

(18)

The exponent decreases with increasing thermalization rate
\gamma
but is not affected by the mean occupation number
\bar{n}
. The factor
1/2
in the exponent arises from the long-time behavior under thermal dissipation
T\gg\ln(1/\gamma)
. For effectively closed dynamics within the time window
T\lesssim\ln(1/\gamma)
, the exponent is given by
\Gamma(\epsilon_{\rm max})
. These two different behaviors can be confirmed through numerical simulation in Fig.
3
(b).

Remarks.—

We have established the fundamental scaling limit of critical quantum metrology for a given total evolution time
T
. We have demonstrated that when the winding number
n
in phase space trajectory is fixed, the QFI scaling is upper bounded by
{\cal F}_{\omega}(T)\propto T^{4n+6}
. Our work reveals the role of the topological properties of phase space trajectories as a unifying principle for maximizing accuracy, applicable across various critical metrology scenarios explored in previous studies. Furthermore, we have shown that an exponential scaling of the QFI,
{\cal F}_{\omega}(T)\propto e^{\Gamma(\omega T)}
, can be achieved by increasing the winding number with total evolution time and have derived the fundamental bound of the exponent
\Gamma
.

We have also developed an explicit protocol to achieve optimal scaling of QFI. This protocol can be implemented by simple on-off scheduling of
\epsilon(t)
, thereby providing an experimentally feasible approach to high-precision metrology through a simple dynamic control of the quantum system. We emphasize that such dynamic control becomes even more crucial in realistic scenarios where reaching the critical point is not feasible. In such cases, as we have shown, a monotonically increasing
\epsilon(t)
is limited to achieving only quadratic scaling of the QFI; hence, non-monotonic control, such as on-off scheduling, is essential to attain exponential scaling. We have also shown the robustness of the exponential scaling of QFI under thermal dissipation.

The theoretical framework in our work offers a potential avenue for exploring optimal control beyond critical quantum metrology, for example, enhancing squeezing
Abah et al. 2022
or charging quantum batteries
Abah et al. 2022
;
Downing and Ukhtary 2024
. An intriguing future research direction would be extending our formalism beyond the Gaussian limit. Another interesting question is whether the periodic control studied in this work could provide a promising route to circumvent critical slowing down in quantum many-body dynamics.

Appendix A
Dynamics of squeezed states and the quantum Fisher information

A.1
Dynamics of fully connected system

In this section, we derive the equations of motion under the following effective time-dependent Hamiltonian of the fully connected system:

\hat{H}(t)=\omega\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-\frac{\epsilon(t)}{4}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a})^{2}.

(19)

Let us express the quantum state at time
t
as
|\psi_{t}\rangle=\hat{U}_{t}|\psi_{0}\rangle
, where the dynamics of
U_{t}
is given by the Schrödinger equation:

\frac{d\hat{U}_{t}}{dt}=-i\hat{H}(t)\hat{U}_{t}.

(20)

By noting that Eq. (
19
) only contains quadratic terms in
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, the evolution unitary
U_{t}
can be fully characterized by the following Gaussian unitary operations without displacement:

\hat{U}_{t}=e^{i\chi}\hat{R}\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right)\hat{S}\left(r\right)\hat{R}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right),

(21)

where
\hat{R}(\phi)=e^{-i\phi\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}
is the rotation operator and
\hat{S}(r)=e^{\frac{r}{2}(\hat{a}^{2}-\hat{a}^{\dagger 2})}
is the squeezing operator with a real-valued squeezing
r
. We note that when
\hat{U}_{t}
is acting on the vacuum state
|\psi_{0}\rangle=|0\rangle
, the resulting state becomes a squeezed vacuum state,

|\psi_{t}\rangle=\hat{U}_{t}|0\rangle=\hat{S}(\xi)|0\rangle,

(22)

where
\hat{S}(\xi)=e^{\frac{1}{2}(\xi^{*}\hat{a}^{2}-\xi\hat{a}^{\dagger 2})}
with
\xi=re^{-i\varphi}
. We note that the state is independent of
\theta
and the overall phase
\chi
does not have any physical significance.

From Eq. (
20
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{U}_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{de^{i\chi}}{dt}\hat{R}\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right)\hat{S}\left(r\right)\hat{R}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)+e^{i\chi}\left[\left(\frac{d\hat{R}\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right)}{dt}\right)\hat{S}\left(r\right)\hat{R}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)+\hat{R}\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right)\left(\frac{d\hat{S}\left(r\right)}{dt}\right)\hat{R}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)+\hat{R}\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right)\hat{S}\left(r\right)\left(\frac{d\hat{R}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}{dt}\right)\right]

(23)

A.2
Quantum Fisher information for dynamical encoding

We provide the closed form of the quantum Fisher information (QFI) when estimating the system parameter
\omega=\omega_{0}+\delta\omega
encoded in the evolved state after time
T
,
|\psi_{T}\rangle=\hat{U}_{T}|\psi_{T}\rangle
. The explicit form of the QFI is given as

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}=4(\langle\partial_{\omega}\psi_{T}|\partial_{\omega}\psi_{T}\rangle-|\langle\psi_{T}|\partial_{\omega}\psi_{T}\rangle|^{2})|_{\omega=\omega_{0}},

(34)

where
|\partial_{\omega}\psi_{T}\rangle=\frac{\partial|\psi_{T}\rangle}{\partial\omega}=\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}}{\partial\omega}|\psi_{0}\rangle
.
We note that by introducing the following hermitian operator,

\hat{G}_{T}=i\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}}{\partial\omega}\right)|_{\omega=\omega_{0}},

(35)

the QFI can be expressed as the expectation value over the initial state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\omega}

\displaystyle=4\left(\langle\psi_{0}|\left(\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}}{\partial\omega}\right)\left(\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}}{\partial\omega}\right)|\psi_{0}\rangle-\left|\langle\psi_{0}|i\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}}{\partial\omega}\right)|\psi_{0}\rangle\right|^{2}\right)|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}

(36)

\displaystyle=4\left(\langle\psi_{0}|\left[(-i)\left(\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}}{\partial\omega}\right)\hat{U}_{T}\right]\left[i\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}}{\partial\omega}\right)\right]|\psi_{0}\rangle-\left|\langle\psi_{0}|i\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}}{\partial\omega}\right)|\psi_{0}\rangle\right|^{2}\right)|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}

\displaystyle=4(\langle\psi_{0}|\hat{G}_{T}^{2}|\psi_{0}\rangle-\langle\psi_{0}|\hat{G}_{T}|\psi_{0}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=4{\rm Var}_{|\psi_{0}\rangle}(\hat{G}_{T}),

where
\hat{G}_{T}=\hat{G}_{T}^{\dagger}=(-i)\left(\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}}{\partial\omega}\right)\hat{U}_{T}|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}
is a hermitian operator from the fact that
\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{T}=\mathbb{I}\Leftrightarrow\left(\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}}{\partial\omega}\right)\hat{U}_{T}+\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}}{\partial\omega}\right)=0
. This is consistent with the conventional form of the QFI, given by the variance of the generator.

Now, we evaluate the explicit form of
\hat{G}_{T}
for general time-dependent dynamics with

\hat{U}_{t\rightarrow t^{\prime}}=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}dt\hat{H}(t)},

(37)

where
\hat{U}_{T}=\hat{U}_{0\rightarrow T}
is the special case with
t=0
and
t^{\prime}=T
. We note that

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{T}

\displaystyle=i\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{U}_{T}}{\partial\omega}|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle=i\hat{U}_{T}^{\dagger}\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{\tau}^{T}\hat{H}(t)dt}(-i)\left(\frac{\partial\hat{H}(\tau)}{\partial\omega}\right)|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{0}^{\tau}\hat{H}(t)dt}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\hat{U}_{0\rightarrow T}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{\tau\rightarrow T}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{H}(\tau)}{\partial\omega}\right)|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}\hat{U}_{0\rightarrow\tau}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\hat{U}_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{H}(\tau)}{\partial\omega}\right)|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}\hat{U}_{0\rightarrow\tau}.

For the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
19
), we have

\frac{\partial\hat{H}(t)}{\partial\omega}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},

(39)

which is time-independent. We also note that the time-evolution operator given by Eq. (
21
) reads

\hat{U}_{0\rightarrow\tau}=e^{i\chi(\tau)}\hat{R}\left(\frac{\varphi(\tau)}{2}\right)\hat{S}\left(r(\tau)\right)\hat{R}\left(\frac{\theta(\tau)}{2}\right),

(40)

at time
\tau
. From this, we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\hat{U}_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{\dagger}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a})\hat{U}_{0\rightarrow\tau}

(41)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\hat{R}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\theta(\tau)}{2}\right)\hat{S}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{r(\tau)}{2}\right)\hat{R}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\varphi(\tau)}{2}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{R}\left(\frac{\varphi(\tau)}{2}\right)\hat{S}\left(\frac{r(\tau)}{2}\right)\hat{R}\left(\frac{\theta(\tau)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\left(\cosh 2r(\tau)\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-\frac{e^{-i\theta(\tau)}\hat{a}^{2}+e^{i\theta(\tau)}\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}}{2}\sinh 2r(\tau)\right).

By taking the initial state
|\psi_{0}\rangle=|0\rangle
, we note that

\hat{G}_{T}|0\rangle=-\int_{0}^{T}d\tau e^{i\theta(\tau)}\sinh(2r(\tau))\left(\frac{\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}}{2}\right)|0\rangle,

(42)

which leads to

\displaystyle\langle 0|\hat{G}_{T}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

(43)

\displaystyle\langle 0|\hat{G}_{T}^{2}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\sinh(2r(\tau))e^{i\theta(\tau)}\int_{0}^{T}d\tau^{\prime}\sinh(2r(\tau^{\prime}))e^{-i\theta(\tau^{\prime})}\langle 0|\left(\frac{\hat{a}^{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}}{4}\right)|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\sinh(2r(\tau))e^{i\theta(\tau)}\right|^{2}.

Finally, we obtain the closed form of the QFI,

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}=4(\langle\psi_{0}|\hat{G}_{T}^{2}|\psi_{0}\rangle-\langle\psi_{0}|\hat{G}_{T}|\psi_{0}\rangle^{2})=2\left|\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\sinh(2r(\tau))e^{i\theta(\tau)}\right|^{2},

(44)

which corresponds to Eq. (6) in the main text.

Proof of Theorems with finite winding number

B.1
Proof of Theorem 1

In this section, we provide the detailed proof of Theorem 1:

Theorem 1
.

The scaling of QFI with a fixed winding number
n
is bounded by

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}\leq\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}(\omega T)^{4n+6}+o(T^{4n+6})

(45)

where
o(T^{4n+6})
grows much slower than
T^{4n+6}
.

Proof.

By using Eq. (
44
), the upper bound of QFI is given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{\omega}&=2\left|\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\sinh 2r(\tau)e^{i\theta(\tau)}\right|^{2}\leq 2\left(\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\sinh 2r(\tau)\right)^{2},\end{split}

(46)

where the right side only depends on the squeezing parameter. Hence, the maximum scaling of QFI is bounded by the maximum scaling of
r
.

In order to show the bound for the fixed winding number, we first show the upper bound of squeezing without increasing the winding number, summarized as the following Lemma:

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
\varphi(t_{k})=2k\pi
for any non-negative integer
k
. Then, the squeezing after time
\Delta_{k}
without increasing the winding number (i.e.,
\varphi(t_{k}+\Delta_{k})<2(k+1)\pi
) is upper bounded as

\cosh(2r(t_{k}+\Delta_{k}))\leq(\omega^{2}\Delta_{k}^{2}+1)\cosh(2r(t_{k})).

(47)

The proof of Lemma
1
is provided at the end of the section.

We provide a proof of Theorem 1 by using Lemma
1
. For a fixed winding number
n
, let us define
t_{k}
as the time points satisfying
\varphi(t_{k})=2k\pi
for each
k=0,1,\cdots,n-1,n
. As there is a region with non-decreasing
\varphi
, i.e.,
\dot{\varphi}(t)>0
when
|\cos\varphi|>\tanh(2r)
, the winding number cannot decrease. This implies that there is a single
t_{k}
that satisfies
\varphi(t_{k})=2k\pi
for each
k
. Now let us set the time duration
\Delta_{k}=t_{k+1}-t_{k}
, during which the winding number remains at
k
. We note that
t_{0}=0
so that
\cosh(r(t_{0}))=1
and
\tau=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Delta_{k}
. Then, from the bound from Lemma
1
, for each time interval
\Delta_{k}
, the squeezing is bounded as
\cosh 2r(t_{k}+\Delta_{k})\leq(\omega^{2}\Delta_{k}^{2}+1)\cosh 2r(t_{k})
. By repeating this step for each
k=0,1,\cdots,n
, we obtain

\displaystyle\cosh 2r(\tau)\leq\prod_{k=0}^{n}(\omega^{2}\Delta_{k}^{2}+1)\leq\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\omega^{2}\Delta_{k}^{2}+1}{n+1}\right]^{n+1}=\left[\frac{\omega^{2}}{n+1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Delta_{k}^{2}\right)+1\right]^{n+1}\leq\left[\frac{\omega^{2}}{n+1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Delta_{k}\right)^{2}+1\right]^{n+1}=\left(\frac{\omega^{2}\tau^{2}}{n+1}+1\right)^{n+1},

(48)

where the second inequality comes from the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means and the third inequality comes from
\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\leq(\sum_{i}x_{i})^{2}
for
x_{i}\geq 0
.

By noting that
\sinh x\leq\cosh x
, we have the following bound of the squeezing parameter for a fixed winding number
n
and the total evolution time
\tau
,

\sinh 2r(\tau)\leq\left(\frac{\omega^{2}\tau^{2}}{n+1}+1\right)^{n+1}.

(49)

Consequently, the QFI can be bounded as

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}\leq 2\left(\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\sinh 2r(\tau)\right)^{2}\leq 2\left(\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\left(\frac{\omega^{2}\tau^{2}}{n+1}+1\right)^{n+1}\right)^{2}=2T^{2}\left[{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2},-(n+1),\frac{3}{2},-\frac{\omega^{2}T^{2}}{n+1}\right)\right]^{2},

(50)

where
{}_{2}F_{1}
is the hypergeometric function. When we focus only on the leading order term in
T
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}\leq\frac{2T^{4}}{(2n+3)^{2}}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}T^{2}}{n+1}\right)^{2n+1}+o(T^{4n+6})=c_{n}T^{4n+6}+o(T^{4n+6}),

(51)

with
c_{n}=\frac{2\omega^{4n+2}}{(2n+3)^{2}(n+1)^{2n+1}}
, which completes the proof.
∎

B.3
Proof of Theorem 4

In this section, we prove Theorem 4 in the main text. Let us state Theorem 4 again here.

Theorem 4
.

For
0\leq\epsilon(t)\leq\epsilon_{\rm max}<\omega
and a fixed winding number
n
, the squeezing
r
is upper bounded as

\sinh 2r(T)\leq\frac{1}{(1-(\epsilon_{\rm max}/\omega))^{n+1}},

(68)

regardless of the total evolution time
T
. This implies that the QFI eventually saturates to
T^{2}
.

Proof.

We prove the statement, similarly to Theorem
1
, by starting with the following lemma:

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
\varphi(t_{k})=2k\pi
for any non-negative integer
k
. Then, for any protocol with
\epsilon(t)\leq\epsilon_{\rm max}<\omega
, the squeezing after time
\Delta_{k}
without increasing the winding number (i.e.,
\varphi(t_{k}+\Delta_{k})<2(k+1)\pi
) is upper bounded as

\cosh(2r(t_{k}+\Delta_{k}))\leq\left(\frac{1}{1-(\epsilon_{\rm max}/\omega)}\right)\cosh(2r(t_{k})).

(69)

The proof of Lemma
2
is provided at the end of the section. The main difference from Lemma
1
is that the bound does not depend on
\Delta_{k}
. This directly leads to the proof of Theorem
4
, by taking the total evolution time

and following the same logic as the proof of Theorem
1
as,

\sinh(2r(T))\leq\cosh(2r(T))\leq\left[\prod_{k=0}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{1-(\epsilon_{\rm max}/\omega)}\right)\right]\cosh(2r(t_{0}))=\frac{1}{\left(1-(\epsilon_{\rm max}/\omega)\right)^{n+1}},

(70)

where
\cosh(2r(t_{0}))=1
as
r(t_{0})=0
at the initial point
t_{0}=0
.

Schematic figure for the proof of Lemma
2
.

Now let us prove Lemma
2
. Similarly to the proof of Lemma
1
, we only need to focus on the regime

, as the squeezing is non-increasing when

, or equivalently
\sin\varphi\leq 0
(see Eq. (
33
)). We then define the quantity,

D(t)=(2\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\sqrt{x^{2}(t)+y^{2}(t)+1}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}x(t).

(71)

We note that the time derivative of
D(t)
for

is non-positive, i.e.,

\begin{split}\frac{dD}{dt}&=(2\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+1}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{dx}{dt}\\
&=(2\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\epsilon(t)y-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}(2\omega-\epsilon(t))y\\
&=2\omega(\epsilon(t)-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})y\\
&\leq 0,\end{split}

(72)

as
\epsilon(t)\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}
. This implies that
D
is monotonically decreasing for
y\geq 0
, which leads to

D(t^{\prime})\leq D(t),

(73)

for any time
t^{\prime}\geq t
as long as
y
remains non-negative over the time interval
[t,t^{\prime}]
, i.e., the trajectory remains in the upper half plane (see Fig.
6
).

Now, similarly to the proof of Lemma
1
, we first consider the case where the trajectory starts with
x(t_{k})=\sinh(2r(t_{k}))
and

(see Fig.
6
(a)). By direct calculation, we obtain

\displaystyle D(t_{k})

\displaystyle=(2\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\sqrt{x^{2}(t_{k})+y^{2}(t_{k})+1}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}x(t_{k})

(74)

\displaystyle=(2\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\cosh(2r(t_{k}))+\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}\sinh(2r(t_{k}))

\displaystyle=2\omega\cosh(2r(t_{k}))-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}e^{-2r(t_{k})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\omega\cosh(2r(t_{k})),

where
x(t_{k})=\sinh(2r(t_{k}))
and
y(t_{k})=0
.

Meanwhile, for
t_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle D(t_{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(2\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\sqrt{x^{2}(t_{k}^{\prime})+y^{2}(t_{k}^{\prime})+1}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}x(t_{k}^{\prime})

(75)

\displaystyle=2(\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\sqrt{x^{2}(t_{k}^{\prime})+y^{2}(t_{k}^{\prime})+1}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\sqrt{x^{2}(t_{k}^{\prime})+y^{2}(t_{k}^{\prime})+1}+x(t_{k}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2(\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\cosh(2r(t_{k}^{\prime})),

by noting that
\sqrt{x^{2}(t_{k}^{\prime})+y^{2}(t_{k}^{\prime})+1}=\cosh(2r(t_{k}^{\prime}))
and
\sqrt{x^{2}(t_{k}^{\prime})+y^{2}(t_{k}^{\prime})+1}+x(t_{k}^{\prime})\geq 0

Then, from Eq. (

\displaystyle 2(\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\cosh(2r(t_{k}^{\prime}))\leq D(t_{k}^{\prime})\leq D(t_{k})\leq 2\omega\cosh(2r(t_{k}))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\cosh(2r(t_{k}^{\prime}))\leq\left(\frac{1}{1-(\epsilon_{\rm max}/\omega)}\right)\cosh(2r(t_{k})).

For the other case, where the trajectory starts with

\displaystyle\dot{y}(t_{k}^{*})=(2\omega-\epsilon(t_{k}^{*}))x(t_{k}^{*})+\epsilon(t)\sqrt{1+x^{2}(t_{k}^{*})+y^{2}(t_{k}^{*})}=-(\omega-\epsilon(t_{k}^{*}))e^{2r(t_{k}^{*})}+\omega e^{-2r(t_{k}^{*})}\geq 0.

(77)

This leads to the bound of

\displaystyle D(t_{k}^{*})

\displaystyle=(2\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\sqrt{x^{2}(t_{k}^{*})+y^{2}(t_{k}^{*})+1}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}x(t_{k}^{*})

\displaystyle=(2\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\cosh(2r(t_{k}^{*}))-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}\sinh(2r(t_{k}^{*}))

\displaystyle=(\omega-\epsilon_{\rm max})e^{2r(t_{k}^{*})}+\omega e^{-2r(t_{k}^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\omega e^{-2r(t_{k}^{*})}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\cosh(2r(t_{k}^{\prime}))\leq\left(\frac{1}{1-(\epsilon_{\rm max}/\omega)}\right)\cosh(2r(t_{k})),

Appendix C
Optimal control for the large squeezing limit

C.1
Equations of motion for
r\gg 1

For large
r
limit,
r\gg 1
, we can rewrite Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\epsilon(t)\sin(\varphi/2)\cos(\varphi/2),

(81)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=2\big(\omega-\epsilon(t)\sin^{2}(\varphi/2)\big),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=0,

since
\coth(2r)\approx 1
and
\frac{1}{\sinh(2r)}\approx 0
. In particular, we note that the control parameter
\epsilon(t)
can be explicitly written

\epsilon(t)=\frac{2\omega-\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}}{2\sin^{2}(\varphi/2)},

(82)

as a function only depending on
\varphi
and
\dot{\varphi}
. We also note that
\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}=2\big(\omega-\epsilon(t)\sin^{2}(\varphi/2)\big)\geq 0
,
\varphi(t)
is a monotonically increasing function of
t
. From this, we can express
t
as an inverse function of
\varphi
. Then, the squeezing parameter after time
T
with the total phase
\int_{0}^{T}\dot{\varphi}(t)dt=\Phi=\varphi(T)
can be rewritten as

\begin{split}r(T,\Phi)&=\int_{0}^{\Phi}d\varphi\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}\frac{\partial r}{\partial t}\\
&=\int_{0}^{\Phi}d\varphi\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}\epsilon(t)\sin(\varphi/2)\cos(\varphi/2)\\
&=\int_{0}^{\Phi}d\varphi\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}\left(\frac{2\omega-\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}}{2\sin^{2}(\varphi/2)}\right)\sin(\varphi/2)\cos(\varphi/2)\\
&=\int_{0}^{\Phi}d\varphi\left(2\omega\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}-1\right)\frac{\cot(\varphi/2)}{2},\end{split}

(83)

where the condition for the total evolution time
\int_{0}^{T}dt=T
is given by

T=\int_{0}^{T}dt=\int_{0}^{\Phi}d\varphi\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}.

(84)

We also note that the boundary condition
0\leq\epsilon(t)\leq 1
becomes

\frac{1}{2\omega}\leq\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}\leq\frac{\sec^{2}(\varphi/2)}{2\omega}.

(85)

C.2
Optimal control protocol for given
T
and
\Phi

We show that the optimal control protocol of
\epsilon
is given by the on/off control given as the following Lemma:

Lemma 3
.

For a given total evolution time
T
and total phase
\Phi
, a maximum value of
r(T,\Phi)
is achievable only by the following on/off process:

\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}=f(\varphi)=\begin{cases}{\frac{\sec^{2}(\varphi/2)}{2\omega}},&\mathrm{for}\;\,\cot(\varphi/2)>\cot(\phi^{T}/2)\\
\frac{1}{2\omega},&\mathrm{for}\;\,\cot(\varphi/2)\leq\cot(\phi^{T}/2),\end{cases}

(86)

which is equivalent to

\epsilon(t)=\begin{cases}\omega,&\mathrm{for}\;\,\cot(\varphi(t)/2)>\cot(\phi^{T}/2)\\
0,&\mathrm{for}\;\,\cot(\varphi(t)/2)\leq\cot(\phi^{T}/2),\end{cases}

(87)

where
\phi^{T}
is defined such that
\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}
satisfies the normalization condition,
T=\int_{0}^{\Phi}d\varphi\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by contradiction. Let us assume that the function,
f(\varphi)
from Eq. (
86
) does not provide the maximum value. This indicates that there is another function,
g(\varphi)\neq f(\varphi)
, such that

r(T,\Phi)|_{\frac{dt}{d\varphi}=g(\varphi)}>r(T,\Phi)|_{\frac{dt}{d\varphi}=f(\varphi)}.

(88)

As any function
\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}=g(\varphi)
should obey the boundary condition in Eq. (
85
), and by noting that
f(\varphi)
is either the maximum or the minimum of the bound, we obtain

\Delta(\varphi)=g(\varphi)-f(\varphi)\begin{cases}\leq 0,&\mathrm{for}\;\,\cot(\varphi/2)>\cot(\phi^{T}/2)\\
\geq 0,&\mathrm{for}\;\,\cot(\varphi/2)\leq\cot(\phi^{T}/2).\end{cases}

(89)

With the normalization condition in Eq. (
84
), i.e,
\int_{0}^{\Phi}d\varphi f(\varphi)=T=\int_{0}^{\Phi}d\varphi g(\varphi)
, Eq. (
89
) yields

\int_{0}^{\Phi}\Delta(\varphi)d\varphi=\int_{\cot(\varphi)>\cot(\phi^{T}/2)}\Delta(\varphi)d\varphi+\int_{\cot(\varphi)\leq\cot(\phi^{T}/2)}\Delta(\varphi)d\varphi=0.

(90)

We now rewrite Eq. (
88
) as

\displaystyle\left[r(T,\Phi)|_{\frac{dt}{d\varphi}-g(\varphi)}\right]-\left[r(T,\Phi)|_{\frac{dt}{d\varphi}=f(\varphi)}\right]

(91)

\displaystyle=\left[\int_{0}^{\Phi}d\varphi\left(2\omega\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}|_{\frac{dt}{d\varphi}-g(\varphi)}-1\right)\frac{\cot(\varphi/2)}{2}\right]-\left[\int_{0}^{\Phi}d\varphi\left(2\omega\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}|_{\frac{dt}{d\varphi}-f(\varphi)}-1\right)\frac{\cot(\varphi/2)}{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\omega\int_{0}^{\Phi}\cot(\varphi/2)\Delta(\varphi)d\varphi

\displaystyle=\omega\left[\int_{\cot(\varphi/2)>\cot(\phi^{T}/2)}\cot(\varphi/2)\Delta(\varphi)d\varphi+\int_{\cot(\varphi/2)\leq\cot(\phi^{T}/2)}\cot(\varphi/2)\Delta(\varphi)d\varphi\right]

\displaystyle=\omega\left[\int_{\cot(\varphi/2)>\cot(\phi^{T}/2)}\cot(\varphi/2)\Delta(\varphi)d\varphi+\int_{\cot(\varphi/2)\leq\cot(\phi^{T}/2)}\cot(\varphi/2)\Delta(\varphi)d\varphi-\cot(\phi^{T}/2)\int_{0}^{\Phi}\Delta(\varphi)d\varphi\right]

\displaystyle=\omega\left[\int_{\cot(\varphi/2)>\cot(\phi^{T}/2)}(\underbrace{\cot(\varphi/2)-\cot(\phi^{T}/2)}_{\geq 0})\underbrace{\Delta(\varphi)}_{\leq 0}d\varphi+\int_{\cot(\varphi/2)\leq\cot(\phi^{T}/2)}(\underbrace{\cot(\varphi/2)-\cot(\phi^{T}/2)}_{\leq 0})\underbrace{\Delta(\varphi)}_{\geq 0}d\varphi\right]

\displaystyle\leq 0,

which is a contradiction with Eq. (
88
).
∎

C.3
Optimal squeezing for a given winding number
n

Now let us turn our focus to evaluating the optimal value of
\Phi
that yields the maximum value of
r(T,\Phi)
for the given total time
T
. Let us first find the optimal value for a fixed winding number
n
, i.e., for the cases where
\Phi_{n}=2n\pi+\tilde{\phi}_{n}
with
\tilde{\phi}_{n}<2\pi
. For this case, the optimal control protocol with the fixed winding number
n
,
\frac{dt}{d\varphi}=f_{n}(\varphi)
, given by Lemma
3
leads to

\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}=f_{n}(\varphi)=\begin{cases}\frac{\sec^{2}(\varphi/2)}{2\omega}&\mathrm{for}\;\,2m\pi\leq\varphi<2m\pi+\phi_{n}\\
\frac{1}{2\omega},&\mathrm{for}\;\,2m\pi+\phi_{n}\leq\varphi<2(m+1)\pi\\
\frac{\sec^{2}(\varphi/2)}{2\omega},&\mathrm{for}\;\,2n\pi\leq\varphi<2n\pi+\tilde{\phi}_{n},\end{cases}

(92)

where
0\leq m<n
is an integer. The maximum squeezing
r(T,\Phi_{n})
obtained from the protocol can be directly evaluated as

\begin{split}r(T,\Phi_{n})&=\int_{0}^{\Phi_{n}}\left(2\omega f(\varphi)-1\right)\frac{\cot(\varphi/2)}{2}d\varphi\\
&=n\int_{0}^{\phi_{n}}(\sec^{2}(\varphi/2)-1)\frac{\cot(\varphi/2)}{2}d\varphi+\int_{0}^{\tilde{\phi}_{n}}(\sec^{2}(\varphi/2)-1)\frac{\cot(\varphi/2)}{2}d\varphi\\
&=n\int_{0}^{\phi_{n}}\frac{\tan(\varphi/2)}{2}d\varphi+\int_{0}^{\tilde{\phi}_{n}}\frac{\tan(\varphi/2)}{2}d\varphi\\
&=-n\ln(\cos(\phi_{n}/2))-\ln(\cos(\tilde{\phi}_{n}/2)).\end{split}

(93)

We also note that the normalization condition becomes

\begin{split}T&=\int_{0}^{\Phi_{n}}f_{n}(\varphi)d\varphi\\
&=n\Big(\int_{0}^{\phi_{n}}\frac{\sec^{2}(\varphi/2)}{2\omega}d\varphi+\int_{\phi_{n}}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2\omega}d\varphi\Big)+\int_{0}^{\tilde{\phi}_{n}}\frac{\sec^{2}(\varphi/2)}{2\omega}d\varphi\\
&=\frac{n}{\omega}\tan(\phi_{n}/2)+\frac{n}{\omega}(\pi-\phi_{n}/2)+\frac{1}{\omega}\tan(\tilde{\phi}_{n}/2).\end{split}

(94)

Hence, to find the optimal
\Phi_{n}^{T}=\arg\max_{\Phi_{n}}r(T,\Phi_{n})
that maximizes Eq. (
93
) for given
T
, we take the derivatives with respect to
\phi_{n}
to meet the following condition:

\frac{\partial r(T,\Phi_{n})}{\partial\phi_{n}}=\frac{n}{2}\tan(\phi_{n}/2)+\frac{1}{2}\tan(\tilde{\phi}_{n}/2)\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}_{n}}{\partial\phi_{n}}=0.

(95)

Meanwhile, by taking the derivatives of the normalization condition in Eq. (
94
), we obtain

0=\frac{\partial T}{\partial\phi_{n}}=\frac{n}{2\omega}(\sec^{2}(\phi_{n}/2)-1)+\frac{1}{2\omega}\sec^{2}(\tilde{\phi}_{n}/2)\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}_{n}}{\partial\phi_{n}},

(96)

which leads to

\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}_{n}}{\partial\phi_{n}}=-\frac{n(\sec^{2}(\phi_{n}/2)-1)}{\sec^{2}(\tilde{\phi}_{n}/2)}=-n\cos^{2}(\tilde{\phi}_{n}/2)\tan^{2}(\phi_{n}/2).

(97)

By substituting this expression to Eq. (
95
), we obtain

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[n\tan(\phi_{n}/2)+\tan(\tilde{\phi}_{n}/2)\left(-n\cos^{2}(\tilde{\phi}_{n}/2)\tan^{2}(\phi_{n}/2)\right)\right]=0

(98)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\tan(\phi_{n}/2)\left(1-\sin(\tilde{\phi}_{n}/2)\cos(\tilde{\phi}_{n}/2)\tan(\phi_{n}/2)\right)=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sin\tilde{\phi}_{n}=\frac{2}{\tan(\phi_{n}/2)}.

Within the range
0\leq\tilde{\phi}_{n}\leq 2\pi
, we obtain the solution satisfying Eq. (
98
) as

\tilde{\phi}_{n}^{T}=\pi-\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2}{\tan(\phi_{n}^{T}/2)}\right).

(99)

This leads to the optimal phase

\Phi_{n}^{T}=2n\pi+\tilde{\phi}_{n}^{T}=(2n+1)\pi-\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2}{\tan(\phi_{n}^{T}/2)}\right),

(100)

where the angle
\phi_{n}^{T}
corresponding to this optimal control protocol is given by solving the normalization condition in Eq. (
94
) as

T=\frac{n}{\omega}\tan(\phi_{n}^{T}/2)+\frac{n}{\omega}(\pi-\phi_{n}^{T}/2)+\frac{1}{\omega}\tan\left[\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2}{\tan(\phi_{n}^{T}/2)}\right)\right].

(101)

The explicit control protocol is composed of two periods. Eq. (
92
) indicates that
\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}
has to be
\frac{\sec^{2}(\varphi/2)}{2\omega}
or
\frac{1}{2\omega}
, which correspond to
\epsilon(t)=\omega
or
\epsilon(t)=0
. By integrating
\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}
for each period, we obtain the explicit time interval for turning on the squeezing (i.e.,
\epsilon(t)=\omega
) as follows:

\Delta T_{\mathrm{ON}}=\int_{2m\pi}^{2m\pi+\phi_{n}^{T}}d\varphi\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}=\int_{2m\pi}^{2m\pi+\phi_{n}^{T}}d\varphi\frac{\sec^{2}(\varphi/2)}{2\omega}=\frac{1}{\omega}\tan(\phi^{T}_{n}/2),

(102)

for each
m
. Similarly, the time interval for turning off
\Delta T_{\mathrm{OFF}}
(i.e.,
\epsilon(t)=0

\Delta T_{\mathrm{OFF}}=\int_{2m\pi+\phi_{n}^{T}}^{2(m+1)\pi}d\varphi\frac{\partial t}{\partial\varphi}=\int_{2m\pi+\phi_{n}^{T}}^{2(m+1)\pi}d\varphi\frac{1}{2\omega}=\frac{1}{\omega}(\pi-\phi^{T}_{n}/2).

(103)

Appendix D
Proof of Theorems with exponential scaling

D.1
Proof of Theorem 3

In this section, we prove Theorem 3 in the main text. Let us state Theorem 3 again here.

Theorem 3

Proof of Theorem 5

r(T,\Phi_{n}^{T})
,

\begin{split}r(T,\Phi_{n}^{T})&=n\int_{0}^{\phi_{n}^{T}}\Big(\frac{1}{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)\sin^{2}(\varphi/2)}-1\Big)\frac{\cot(\varphi/2)}{2}d\varphi+\int_{0}^{\tilde{\phi}_{n}^{T}}\Big(\frac{1}{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)\sin^{2}(\varphi/2)}-1\Big)\frac{\cot(\varphi/2)}{2}d\varphi\\
&=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)\sin^{2}(\phi^{T}_{n}/2))-\frac{1}{2}\ln(1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)\sin^{2}(\tilde{\phi}^{T}_{n}/2)),\end{split}

(119)

and the normalization condition

\begin{split}T&=\int_{0}^{\Phi}f_{n}(\varphi)d\varphi=n\Big(\int_{0}^{\phi_{n}^{T}}\frac{1}{2(\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}\sin^{2}(\varphi/2))}d\varphi+\int_{\phi_{n}^{T}}^{\pi}\frac{1}{2\omega}d\varphi\Big)+\int_{0}^{\tilde{\phi}_{n}^{T}}\frac{1}{2(\omega-\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}\sin^{2}(\varphi/2))}d\varphi\\
&=\frac{n}{\omega}\frac{\tan^{-1}(\sqrt{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)}\tan(\phi_{n}^{T}/2))}{\sqrt{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)}}+\frac{n}{\omega}(\pi-\phi_{n}^{T}/2)+\frac{1}{\omega}\frac{\tan^{-1}(\sqrt{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)}\tan(\tilde{\phi}_{n}^{T}/2))}{\sqrt{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)}}.\end{split}

(120)

Similarly to the case with
\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}=\omega
, we can calculate the optimal scale of
r(T)
. In the limit of
T\gg 1
we obtain

\displaystyle r(T,\Phi_{n}^{T})

\displaystyle\overset{T\gg 1}{\approx}-\frac{n}{2}\ln(1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)\sin^{2}(\phi^{T}_{n}/2))

(121)

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\overset{T\gg 1}{\approx}\frac{n}{\omega}\left[\frac{\tan^{-1}(\sqrt{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)}\tan(\phi_{n}^{T}/2))}{\sqrt{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)}}+(\pi-\phi_{n}^{T}/2)\right],

which leads to the following expression

r(T,\Phi_{n}^{T})=-\frac{\omega T}{2}\ln(1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)\sin^{2}(\phi_{n}^{T}/2))\left[\frac{\tan^{-1}(\sqrt{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)}\tan(\phi_{n}^{T}/2))}{\sqrt{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)}}+(\pi-\phi_{n}^{T}/2)\right]^{-1}.

(122)

The maximum value of
r
for a given total evolution time
T
is then obtained by

r_{\rm max}(T,\epsilon_{\rm max})=(\omega T)\max_{0\leq\phi_{n}^{T}\leq\pi}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\ln(1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)\sin^{2}(\phi_{n}^{T}/2))\left[\frac{\tan^{-1}(\sqrt{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)}\tan(\phi_{n}^{T}/2))}{\sqrt{1-(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}/\omega)}}+(\pi-\phi_{n}^{T}/2)\right]^{-1}\right].

(123)

The result of the optimization can be obtained numerically, which is given in the main manuscript Fig. 3(a) (inset figure). From this, we obtain the bound of QFI with
\epsilon(t)\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}<\omega
,

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}\propto e^{4r_{\rm max}(T,\epsilon_{\rm max})}=e^{\Gamma(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\omega T},

(124)

where
\Gamma(\epsilon_{\rm max})=\frac{4r_{\rm max}(T,\epsilon_{\rm max})}{\omega T}
.
∎

D.3
QFI scaling under thermal dissipation

We analyze the scaling behavior of the QFI under thermal dissipation described by the following Lindblad equation,

\frac{d}{dt}{\hat{\rho}}=\mathcal{L}(\rho)=i[{\hat{\rho}},\hat{H}(t)]+\gamma(N_{0}+1)(\hat{a}{\hat{\rho}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},{\hat{\rho}}\})+\gamma N_{0}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}{\hat{\rho}}\hat{a}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger},{\hat{\rho}}\}).

(125)

We provide the bound on QFI as follows:

Theorem 6
.

For
0\leq\epsilon(t)\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}
, the fundamental scaling limit of QFI is given by

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}(T)\leq ATe^{\left(\frac{\Gamma(\epsilon_{\rm max})\omega}{2}-\gamma\right)T},

(126)

with some coefficient
A
.

Proof.

Since both the Hamiltonian evolution and thermal dissipation are Gaussian processes without displacement, the quantum state at each time
t
is a Gaussian state, completely described by the covariance matrix,

\begin{split}V&=\begin{pmatrix}\langle\hat{x}^{2}\rangle&\langle\hat{x}\hat{p}+\hat{p}\hat{x}\rangle/2\\
\langle\hat{x}\hat{p}+\hat{p}\hat{x}\rangle/2&\langle\hat{p}^{2}\rangle\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\frac{2\bar{N}+1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\cosh{2r}-\sinh{2r}\cos{\varphi}&\sinh{2r}\sin{\varphi}\\
\sinh{2r}\sin{\varphi}&\cosh{2r}+\sinh{2r}\cos{\varphi}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{2\bar{N}+1}{2}R(\phi/2)S(2r)R^{T}(\phi/2),\end{split}

(127)

where

S(2r)=\left(\begin{matrix}e^{-2r}&0\\
0&e^{2r}\end{matrix}\right)
with the squeezing
r
, and

R(\varphi/2)=\left(\begin{matrix}\cos\varphi/2&\sin\varphi/2\\
-\sin\varphi/2&\cos\varphi/2\end{matrix}\right)
with the rotation angle
\varphi
, and mean photon number
\bar{N}
.
For convenience, we will define
\mu
, the mean photon number, as

\mu=\frac{2\bar{N}+1}{2}.

(128)

The equation of motion of elements in
V
is given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}\langle\hat{x}^{2}\rangle\\
\langle\hat{p}^{2}\rangle\\
\langle\hat{x}\hat{p}+\hat{p}\hat{x}\rangle/2\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\gamma&0&2\omega&\gamma\frac{2N_{0}+1}{2}\\
0&-\gamma&2\epsilon(t)-2\omega&\gamma\frac{2N_{0}+1}{2}\\
\epsilon(t)-\omega&\omega&-\gamma&0\\
0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\langle\hat{x}^{2}\rangle\\
\langle\hat{p}^{2}\rangle\\
\langle\hat{x}\hat{p}+\hat{p}\hat{x}\rangle/2\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(129)

If we rewrite the time evolution of the covariance in terms of
r
,
\varphi
, and
\mu
, we obatin,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}\mu\cosh 2r\\
\mu\sinh 2r\cos\varphi\\
\mu\sinh 2r\sin\varphi\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\gamma&0&\epsilon(t)&\gamma\frac{2N_{0}+1}{2}\\
0&-\gamma&\epsilon(t)-2\omega&0\\
\epsilon(t)&2\omega-2\epsilon(t)&-\gamma&0\\
0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mu\cosh 2r\\
\mu\sinh 2r\cos\varphi\\
\mu\sinh 2r\sin\varphi\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(130)

which provides the equation of motion,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma\mu+\gamma\cosh 2r\frac{2N_{0}+1}{2},

(131)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon(t)\sin\varphi}{2}-\gamma\sinh 2r\frac{2N_{0}+1}{4\mu},

(132)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\omega-\epsilon(t)(1-\coth 2r\cos\varphi).

(133)

We also note that the QFI is determined by the final state
\hat{\rho}(T)
and its derivative
\partial_{\omega}\hat{\rho}(T)
as
Braunstein and Caves 1994

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}(T))=2\sum_{k,l}\frac{|\langle k|\partial_{\omega}{\hat{\rho}}(T)|l\rangle|^{2}}{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{l}},

(134)

where
\lambda_{k}
and
|k\rangle
are the eigenvalues and eigenstates of
\hat{\rho}
, respectively. We then show the following bound of the QFI:

Lemma 4
.

The QFI for a given total evolution time
T
is bounded by the time integral of the squeezing at each time, given as,

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}(T))\leq 4T\int_{0}^{T}dt\sinh^{2}2r(t).

(135)

We will provide the proof of the Lemma at the end of this section.

Let us now derive an upper bound for the squeezing parameter
r(t)
. We note that Eq. (
133
) is the same as the dynamics of the closed system. Moreover, we obtain the following equation from Eq. (
131
) and Eq. (
132
),

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mu\sinh 2r=-\gamma\mu\sinh 2r+\epsilon(t)\mu\sin\varphi\cosh 2r.

(136)

For the large
r\gg 1
limit, we have asymptotic equations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon(t)\sin(\varphi/2)\cos(\varphi/2)

(137)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(\omega-\epsilon(t)\sin^{2}(\varphi/2)),

(138)

by reparameterizing
s=(\gamma t+\ln(\mu\sinh 2r)/2
, which is exactly the same form as the asymptotic equation of a closed system described by Eq. (
81
). Hence, the maximum scaling of
s
becomes

s_{\rm max}=\frac{\Gamma(\epsilon_{\rm max})}{4}(\omega T).

In the section about the closed system, we illustrate that the best scaling is given by

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{s}{t}\leq\frac{\Gamma(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\omega}{4},

(139)

where
\Gamma(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\omega/4
is the function of maximum value of
\epsilon(t)
, which is equal to
0.2436
typically when
\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}=\omega
.
By definition, we have the maximal scaling,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln(\mu\sinh 2r)}{t}\leq\frac{\Gamma(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\omega}{2}-\gamma.

(140)

Now we derive the maximum scaling of
r
from Eq. (
140
). Let us assume that there is some process
\epsilon(t)
which gives

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln(\mu\sinh 2r)}{t}=\Gamma^{\prime}\leq\frac{\Gamma(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\omega}{2}-\gamma.

(141)

Meanwhile, we note that Eq. (
131
) can be rewritten as

\frac{d}{dt}\mu^{2}=-2\gamma\mu^{2}+\mu\gamma\cosh 2r(1+2N_{0}).

(142)

In the limit of
r\gg 1
, this equation can be rewritten as

\frac{d}{dt}\mu^{2}=-2\gamma\mu^{2}+\mu\gamma\sinh 2r(1+2N_{0}).

(143)

By solving the first-order differential equation, we obtain that

\mu(t)^{2}=e^{-2\gamma t}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau e^{2\gamma\tau}\mu(\tau)\gamma\sinh 2r(\tau)(1+2N_{0}).

(144)

The scaling of
\mu(t)
is given by

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln(\mu)}{t}=-\gamma+\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\ln\left(\int_{0}^{t}d\tau e^{2\gamma\tau}\gamma\mu(\tau)\sinh 2r(\tau)(1+2N_{0})\right).

(145)

By using the fact that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\ln(e^{2\gamma t}\gamma\mu(t)\sinh 2r(t)(1+2N_{0}){2})=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{2}+\gamma,

(146)

the scaling of
\mu
is

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln(\mu)}{t}=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{2}.

(147)

It is obvious that the scaling of the squeeze parameter is equal to

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{r}{t}=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{4}.

(148)

Since
\Gamma^{\prime}
is upper bounded by
\Gamma(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})-\gamma
, the maximum scaling of
r
is

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{r}{t}\leq\frac{\Gamma(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\omega}{8}-\frac{\gamma}{4}.

(149)

Hence, from Lemma
4
, we obtain the bound,

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}\leq T\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{F}(\rho(t),i[\rho(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}])\leq 4T\int_{0}^{T}dt\sinh^{2}2r(t)\approx ATe^{\frac{\Gamma(\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}})\omega T}{2}-\gamma},

(150)

which completes the main proof.

Finally, we prove the bound provided in Lemma
4
:

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}(T))\leq 4T\int_{0}^{T}dt\sinh^{2}2r(t).

(151)

We express the QFI in terms of
\hat{\rho}(T)
and
\partial_{\omega}\hat{\rho}(T)
as

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}(T))=2\sum_{k,l}\frac{|\langle k|\partial_{\omega}{\hat{\rho}}(T)|l\rangle|^{2}}{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{l}}={\cal B}(\hat{\rho},\partial_{\omega}\hat{\rho}),

(152)

where we define
{\cal B}(\hat{\rho},\hat{A})=2\sum_{k,l}\frac{|\langle k|\hat{A}|l\rangle|^{2}}{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{l}}
with the eigendecomposition
\hat{\rho}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}|k\rangle\langle k|
.
Both
\hat{\rho}(T)
and
\partial_{\omega}\hat{\rho}(T)
can be directly obtained by the following time-ordered integral,

\displaystyle{\hat{\rho}}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{L}(t)}|0\rangle\langle 0|,

(153)

\displaystyle\partial_{\omega}{\hat{\rho}}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}\left(\partial_{\omega}{\cal L}(t)\right)=\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}].

(154)

However, the presence of the time-ordered integral poses limitations when deriving general properties and upper bounds of the QFI. To overcome these limitations, we introduce a method to derive an upper bound by eliminating the time-ordered integral.

Let us first consider two hypothetical Lindbladian operators,
\mathcal{L}_{1}(t;\omega)
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}(t;\omega)
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}(t;\omega=\omega_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}(t;\omega=\omega_{0})=\mathcal{L},

(155)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{1}(t;\omega)}{\partial\omega}|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}(\hat{\rho})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}2\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\omega}(\hat{\rho})|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}=2i[\hat{\rho},\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]&\quad\text{for }0\leq t<\frac{T}{2}\\
0&\quad\text{for }\frac{T}{2}\leq t<T\end{cases}

(156)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{2}(t;\omega)}{\partial\omega}|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}(\hat{\rho})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{for }0\leq t<\frac{T}{2}\\
2\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\omega}(\hat{\rho})|_{\omega=\omega_{0}}=2i[\hat{\rho},\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]&\quad\text{for }\frac{T}{2}\leq t<T\end{cases}.

(157)

The two hypothetical Lindbladian operators generate the dynamics and the state at
T
,

\displaystyle{\hat{\rho}}_{1}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{L}_{1}(t)}|0\rangle\langle 0|=\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{L}(t)}|0\rangle\langle 0|={\hat{\rho}}(T),

(158)

\displaystyle{\hat{\rho}}_{2}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{L}_{2}(t)}|0\rangle\langle 0|=\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{L}(t)}|0\rangle\langle 0|={\hat{\rho}}(T),

(159)

\displaystyle\partial_{\omega}{\hat{\rho}}_{1}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{T/2}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}_{1}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]=2\int_{0}^{T/2}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}],

(160)

\displaystyle\partial_{\omega}{\hat{\rho}}_{2}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{T/2}^{T}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}_{2}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]=2\int_{T/2}^{T}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}],

(161)

where we use the identity from Eq. (
155
).
The original
{\hat{\rho}}(T)
can be recovered as the average of
{\hat{\rho}}_{1}(T)
and
{\hat{\rho}}_{2}(T)
,
{\hat{\rho}}(T)=\tfrac{1}{2}({\hat{\rho}}_{1}(T)+{\hat{\rho}}_{2}(T))
. From the convexity property of the QFI, we obtain the inequality,

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}(T))=\mathcal{F}_{\omega}(\tfrac{1}{2}({\hat{\rho}}_{1}(T)+{\hat{\rho}}_{2}(T)))\leq\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}_{1}(T))+\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}_{2}(T))).

(162)

Moreover, we can obtain QFI corresponding to each of
{\hat{\rho}}_{1}(T)
and
{\hat{\rho}}_{2}(T)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}_{1}(T))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}_{1}(T),\frac{\partial{\hat{\rho}}_{1}(T)}{\partial\omega}\right)

(163)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),2\int_{0}^{T/2}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\int_{0}^{T/2}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right),

(164)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}_{2}(T))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}_{2}(T),\frac{\partial{\hat{\rho}}_{2}(T)}{\partial\omega}\right)

(165)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),2\int_{T/2}^{T}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\int_{T/2}^{T}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right).

(166)

Combining Eq. (
162
), Eq. (
164
), and Eq. (
166
), the inequality holds:

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}(T))\leq 2\left(\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\int_{0}^{T/2}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)+\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\int_{T/2}^{T}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)\right).

(167)

By generalizing this inequality to an arbitrary integer
M
, we obtain the following:

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}(T))&\leq M\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\int_{\tfrac{j-1}{M}T}^{\tfrac{j}{M}T}dt\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)\\
&=M\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\frac{T}{M}\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(\tfrac{jT}{M}),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)\\
&=T\frac{T}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(\tfrac{jT}{M}),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right),\end{split}

(168)

whose summation becomes integration at the limit
M\to\infty
, such that,

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}(T))\leq T\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right).

(169)

Finally, from the monotonicity property of the QFI under a quantum channel,

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)=\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}\rho(t),\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)\leq\mathcal{B}\left(\hat{\rho}(t),i[\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right).\end{split}

(170)

In order to show this, we consider the following identity between
\mathcal{B}
and QFI with respect to
\theta
:

\mathcal{B}\left(\hat{\rho}(t),i[\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\theta}({\hat{\rho}}_{\theta}(t)),

(171)

where
{\hat{\rho}}_{\theta}(t)=\exp(-i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}){\hat{\rho}}(t)\exp(i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a})
. Now, let us make use of the relationship between the QFI and the quantum fidelity,

\mathcal{F}_{\theta}({\hat{\rho}}_{\theta}(t))=4\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}(1-F({\hat{\rho}}_{-d\theta/2}(t),{\hat{\rho}}_{d\theta/2}(t))),

(172)

where the fidelity between two quantum states is defined as
F({\hat{\rho}},{\hat{\rho}}^{\prime})=\mathrm{tr}(\sqrt{\sqrt{{\hat{\rho}}}{\hat{\rho}}^{\prime}\sqrt{{\hat{\rho}}}})^{2}
.
By using the fact that the fidelity is monotonic increasing under a quantum channel, which includes the time evolution under Lindblad dynamics, we obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{\theta}\left(\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}{\hat{\rho}}_{\theta}(t)\right)&=4\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}\left(1-F\left(\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}{\hat{\rho}}_{-d\theta/2}(t),\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}{\hat{\rho}}_{d\theta/2}(t)\right)\right)\\
&\leq 4\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}(1-F({\hat{\rho}}_{-d\theta/2}(t),{\hat{\rho}}_{d\theta/2}(t))\\
&=\mathcal{F}_{\theta}({\hat{\rho}}_{\theta}(t)).\end{split}

(173)

Moreover, we can find that

\mathcal{F}_{\theta}\left(\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}{\hat{\rho}}_{\theta}(t)\right)=\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}\hat{\rho}(t),\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)=\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right).

(174)

Combining Eq. (
171
), Eq. (
173
), and Eq. (
174
), we have the inequality,

\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)\leq\mathcal{B}\left(\hat{\rho}(t),i[\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right).

(175)

By combining, Eqs. (
169
) and (
175
), we obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{\omega}({\hat{\rho}}(T))&\leq T\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{B}\left({\hat{\rho}}(T),\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[{\hat{\rho}}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)\\
&=T\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}\hat{\rho}(t),\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t}^{T}d\tau\mathcal{L}(\tau)}i[\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]\right)\\
&\leq T\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{B}(\hat{\rho}(t),i[\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}])\\
&=T\int_{0}^{T}dt\mathcal{F}_{\theta}({\hat{\rho}}_{\theta}(t)).\end{split}

(176)

As a result, the time-ordered integral is no longer required, and the QFI is upper bounded by the integral of the instantaneous QFI over time,
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}({\hat{\rho}}_{\theta}(t))
for
{\hat{\rho}}_{\theta}(t)=\exp(-i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}){\hat{\rho}}(t)\exp(i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a})
. As a quantum state
{\hat{\rho}}(t)
at every time
t
is described by a squeezed thermal state, its QFI can be directly evaluated as a function of mean photon number
\mu
and squeezing
r

Pinel et al. 2013
,

\mathcal{F}_{\theta}({\hat{\rho}}_{\theta}(t))=\frac{8\mu(t)}{2\mu(t)+1}\sinh^{2}2r(t)\leq 4\sinh^{2}2r(t).

(177)

By combining this with Eq. (
