Female sexual dysfunction is independently associated with depressive symptom burden in premenopausal pelvic floor clinic patients: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Mengxiong Li, Yun Liu, Dan Mo, Juanhua Li, Juan He, Tian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFemale sexual dysfunction (FSD) is common among women attending pelvic floor clinics, yet its independent association with depressive symptom burden has not been systematically examined within a multidomain pelvic floor cohort.AimTo determine whether FSD is independently associated with Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) scores in premenopausal women undergoing structured pelvic floor evaluation, and whether cumulative pelvic floor phenotype load (urinary, pain, and FSD domains) relates to SDS in an overall positive ordinal pattern.MethodsRetrospective cross-sectional study of 876 premenopausal women at one pelvic floor center (2020); multivariable regression used a complete-case sample of 764 women with all covariates. FSD was defined by FSFI total ≤ 26.55; domains included POP-Q stage ≥ I prolapse, urinary SUI/OAB, pelvic pain (chronic pain or VAS ≥ 1), and FSD. Primary phenotype burden counted three non-prolapse domains (urinary, pelvic pain, FSD; range 0–3); sensitivity analyses used four-domain scores including prolapse. HC3-robust linear regression evaluated FSD in clinical and extended (PFDI-20 and I-QOL) models.OutcomesSDS (continuous); phenotype burden gradient; adjusted regression coefficients.ResultsFSD was present in 530 women (60.5%). Mean SDS was higher with than without FSD (33.04 ± 8.36 vs. 30.15 ± 7.96; P < 0.001). Mean SDS increased across higher three-domain burden strata (0 domains: 29.0, n = 164; 1: 32.4, n = 353; 2: 32.1, n = 267; 3: 34.5, n = 92; Spearman r = 0.158, P < 0.001), with similar means in the one- and two-domain groups. In the clinical model (n = 764; R² = 0.115), FSD had the largest coefficient (β = 3.14; 95% CI 1.97–4.31), with smaller independent effects for urinary phenotype and defecation dysfunction; lactation was inversely associated (β = −4.02; 95% CI − 5.40 to − 2.64). In the extended model (R² = 0.164), FSD remained significant (β = 2.52; 95% CI 1.36–3.67) while urinary effects attenuated; PFDI-20 and I-QOL were independently associated with SDS.Clinical implicationsFSD showed the largest adjusted association with depressive symptoms among evaluated phenotypes in this cross-sectional cohort. These findings support considering sexual function assessment alongside brief mood screening in pelvic floor clinics, particularly among women with higher multidomain burden, while recognizing that only associations—not causality—can be inferred.Strengths & limitationsStrengths include a large premenopausal cohort, standardized multidomain phenotyping, self-administered FSFI with physician-recorded total scores, a continuous SDS endpoint, and HC3 regression. Limitations include cross-sectional single-center data, near-universal POP stage I+ (limiting POP subgroup contrasts), and systematic differences between regression-eligible and excluded participants.ConclusionFSD was the strongest independent correlate of depressive symptom burden and remained significant after adjustment for global symptom distress and incontinence-specific quality of life. An overall positive ordinal association between three-domain phenotype burden and SDS supports phenotype-aware screening considerations and integrated psychosocial care pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04890-5.Keywords: Female sexual dysfunction, Depression, Self-rating depression scale, Pelvic floor dysfunction, Urinary incontinence, Premenopausal women

Introduction

Sexual dysfunction is common in women attending pelvic floor clinics, where urinary, bowel, pain, and prolapse-related complaints frequently coexist. Reported rates of FSD in urogynecological populations range from roughly 40% to over 60%, depending on the instrument and clinical setting used [1, 2]. Pelvic floor symptoms compromise sexual function through multiple channels: pain during intercourse, fear of leakage, anatomical changes after delivery or surgery, and the broader erosion of body confidence that accompanies chronic pelvic conditions [3]. Beyond classical bidirectional associations between depression and sexual dysfunction [4], recent gynecologic studies highlight psychosexual distress and impaired well-being across diverse clinical settings. Karadağ et al. reported that psychosexual stress frequently accompanies sexual dysfunction in women undergoing HPV evaluation, emphasizing the psychological dimension of female sexual health beyond purely biological disease factors [5]. Similarly, Erkok et al. demonstrated significant impairment in both sexual function and psychosocial well-being among Somali women exposed to female genital mutilation [6]. Güner et al. showed that sexual dysfunction remains highly prevalent among endometrial cancer survivors and may negatively affect both patients and their partners, underscoring broader psychosocial consequences even after successful cancer treatment [7]. At the same time, the relationship between sexual dysfunction and depression appears bidirectional. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 26 studies found that each condition roughly doubled the risk of the other, with plausible pathways running through reduced reward processing, relationship distress, avoidance behavior, and altered hypothalamic-pituitary signaling [4]. Despite this, sexual dysfunction is seldom placed at the center of depression research in pelvic floor populations; it is more often treated as one secondary outcome among many, or omitted entirely.

The literature on pelvic floor dysfunction and mood has concentrated on urinary symptoms. Cross-sectional and cohort studies in several countries have linked SUI and OAB with elevated rates of depression and anxiety, and population-level analyses document a consistent association between incontinence and lower health-related quality of life [8–12]. These findings support routine mental health inquiry in continence clinics. They do not resolve, however, a practical question that multidomain pelvic floor units face daily: when a patient presents with concurrent sexual dysfunction, urinary symptoms, pelvic pain, and prolapse, which of these findings is the most reliable indicator of elevated depressive burden?

Answering that question requires simultaneously modeling all relevant phenotypes in the same cohort so that each domain’s contribution can be estimated independently of the others. Few studies have done this with sexual dysfunction as the primary exposure. The Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), a 19-item multidimensional self-report instrument validated by Rosen et al. [13] and shown to discriminate dysfunction reliably at a total-score cutoff of 26.55 [14], provides the operational foundation for such an analysis. Deploying the FSFI alongside urinary, pain, and prolapse assessments in one cohort allows a specific question to be posed: does the FSD-depression association persist after adjustment for global pelvic floor symptom distress and incontinence-specific quality of life, or is it fully explained by overall pelvic burden?

We examined this in 876 premenopausal women attending a single pelvic floor center, using the Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) as a continuous primary outcome. The sample was restricted to premenopausal women to limit confounding from menopause-related endocrine changes and genitourinary syndrome of menopause, both of which independently affect sexual function and mood [15, 16]. We pre-specified two hypotheses: that FSD would show the largest adjusted association with SDS among all pelvic floor phenotype domains; and that the number of concurrent non-prolapse phenotype domains would show an overall positive ordinal association with SDS consistent with cumulative burden, without requiring strict stepwise monotonicity.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional analysis of a single pelvic floor specialist clinic dataset from 2020. Pelvic floor phenotyping, instrument administration, and pain measurement were carried out by a dedicated team that included two physical therapists who remained consistent across all patient assessments throughout the study period. This internal consistency limits measurement heterogeneity, which is especially important when sexual function, pain, and mood instruments are used in combination.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Seventh Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University (approval number: KY-2026-002-01). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because this study used only anonymized medical records without any intervention. All data were de-identified and analyzed confidentially.

Participants

We included premenopausal women with complete pelvic floor evaluation records and non-missing SDS scores. Postmenopausal women were excluded before analysis to reduce confounding from menopause-related endocrine shifts, genitourinary syndrome of menopause, and changing sexual function trajectories that otherwise blur interpretation when urinary, sexual, and mood outcomes are studied simultaneously. Of 877 premenopausal women, one had a missing SDS score; the descriptive sample comprised 876 women. Complete-case regression used 764 participants with all required covariates.

Outcomes and phenotypes

Primary outcome

The Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) was the primary endpoint, analyzed as a continuous variable. The SDS is a 20-item self-report instrument validated for use in outpatient and gynecological populations; higher scores indicate greater depressive symptom burden [15, 16].

Phenotype domains

We defined four pelvic floor phenotype domains per the clinic’s standardized assessment protocol.

Female Sexual Dysfunction (FSD)

Assessed using the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), a validated 19-item questionnaire covering six domains: desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain [13]. The FSFI was self-administered by patients during clinic assessment. Attending physicians recorded the diagnosis from the total FSFI score; a total score of 26.55 or below was classified as FSD, consistent with the cross-validated cutoff established by Wiegel et al. [14]. All women in the analytic cohort reported sexual activity in the prior 4 weeks at the time of assessment. Exported clinical data contained FSFI total scores only; domain-level scores were not available for this retrospective analysis.

Urinary phenotype

Positive if the patient had stress urinary incontinence (SUI), overactive bladder (OAB), or both. SUI and OAB were ascertained through structured clinical evaluation.

Pelvic pain

Positive if chronic pelvic pain was clinically documented or if pain severity on the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS; 0–10) was 1 or above. VAS pain assessment was performed by the two consistent physical therapists at the time of examination.

Prolapse

Defined as Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP-Q) stage I or above in any vaginal compartment (anterior wall, uterus/vault, or posterior wall).

Phenotype burden

The primary burden score summed three non-prolapse domains—urinary phenotype, pelvic pain, and FSD—(range 0–3). Because 871 of 876 women (99.4%) had POP-Q stage I or above, prolapse contributed near-constant variance and was excluded from the primary burden count. Sensitivity analyses repeating the burden score with prolapse included (POP-Q stage I+, range 1–4) and with POP-Q stage II + are reported in the Results and Supplementary Material. Prolapse was not included as an independent regression predictor because of lack of variation.

Additional variables

Defecation dysfunction (self-reported constipation) was treated as an additional symptom covariate in regression models. Pelvic floor muscle function was assessed by electromyography but could not be operationalized with a clinically grounded binary cutoff within the available data structure and was therefore not included as a phenotype domain. The Pelvic Floor Distress Inventory-20 (PFDI-20) quantifies symptom-related distress across urinary, prolapse, and colorectal domains [17, 18]. The Incontinence Quality of Life questionnaire (I-QOL) measures the impact of urinary incontinence on daily activities and emotional wellbeing [19].

Covariate selection

Covariates were selected a priori based on prior clinical and epidemiological evidence for their role as shared determinants of pelvic floor phenotypes and mood outcomes. Age and BMI are associated with both pelvic floor symptom severity and depressive risk. Delivery route was represented by two binary variables (ever vaginal delivery; ever cesarean delivery) to avoid the arithmetic collinearity that arises when parity counts and delivery-mode counts enter the same model simultaneously. Macrosomia, assisted vaginal delivery, and defecation dysfunction were included as additional obstetric and symptom covariates. Lactation was included because breastfeeding relates to postpartum hormonal milieu and mood, though its causal role is uncertain in a cross-sectional design. Intrapartum epidural analgesia was not modeled as a covariate, although prospective cohort studies have compared postpartum pelvic floor and sexual outcomes by labor epidural analgesia exposure (e.g., higher postpartum dyspareunia without consistent differences across other measured pelvic floor symptoms in the same cohort) [20].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean with standard deviation (SD). Group differences in SDS by phenotype domain used Welch t-tests, which are robust to unequal variances. The burden–SDS association was quantified with Spearman rank correlation. SDS was analyzed as raw continuous total scores (not standardized). Missing-data patterns for regression covariates are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Multiple imputation (m = 20 imputations) for missing macrosomia and assisted vaginal delivery was performed as a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Tables S2a–S2b). Pooled MI estimates were similar to complete-case results for key exposures, including FSD (clinical model pooled β ≈ 3.03 vs. complete-case β = 3.14).

We fit two pre-specified multivariable linear regression models with heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC3) standard errors, since residual variance in psychological scale outcomes is often non-constant. Residual-versus-fitted patterns were reviewed informally; formal residual normality testing was not emphasized because HC3 standard errors were used to address heteroskedasticity in psychological scale outcomes. The clinical model regressed SDS on FSD (primary exposure), urinary phenotype, pelvic pain, defecation dysfunction, lactation, and clinical-obstetric covariates (age, BMI, ever vaginal delivery, ever cesarean delivery, macrosomia, assisted vaginal delivery). The extended model additionally included PFDI-20 and I-QOL total scores to evaluate whether the FSD-SDS association was explained by global pelvic floor symptom distress and incontinence-specific quality of life. Attenuation of the FSD coefficient in the extended model would suggest the association operates through symptom burden appraisal; persistence would indicate a more direct or distinct pathway. All tests were two-sided with alpha = 0.05.

Software

Statistical analyses were performed in Python (statsmodels library). Figures were generated in Python (matplotlib) at 350 DPI.

Results

Cohort profile

Of 939 women in the dataset, 877 were premenopausal; one had a missing SDS score, leaving 876 for descriptive analyses. Regression models used 764 women with complete covariate data. The 112 excluded women were older (38.1 ± 7.3 vs. 32.5 ± 6.1 years; P < 0.001), had lower lactation rates (20.5% vs. 62.0%; P < 0.001), and had higher mean SDS (34.3 ± 8.1 vs. 31.5 ± 8.3; P = 0.001) than those included in regression, driven primarily by missing data on macrosomia and assisted delivery (Supplementary Table S1). Except for near-universal prolapse (POP-Q ≥ stage I: 763/764 vs. 108/112; P = 0.001), phenotype proportions did not differ systematically between regression-included and excluded groups (FSD, urinary phenotype, pelvic pain, and defecation: all P > 0.4). SDS differences by phenotype domain

FSD was present in 530 women (60.5%). Women with FSD had meaningfully higher mean SDS than those without (33.04 ± 8.36 vs. 30.15 ± 7.96; P < 0.001), the largest difference observed across all domain comparisons. Urinary phenotype showed a consistent pattern: SUI and/or OAB positivity was associated with higher SDS (32.87 ± 8.54 vs. 31.00 ± 8.01; P < 0.001), with both SUI (33.00 ± 8.75 vs. 31.28 ± 8.01; P = 0.004) and OAB (33.70 ± 8.47 vs. 31.27 ± 8.18; P < 0.001) retaining associations when examined individually. Defecation dysfunction was also linked to higher SDS (33.27 ± 8.42 vs. 31.47 ± 8.25; P = 0.007). The pelvic pain contrast was essentially null (31.94 ± 8.28 vs. 31.89 ± 8.34; P = 0.94). Prolapse comparisons were not interpretable because only 5 of 876 women were POP-Q negative; complete domain comparisons are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. SDS distributions for the three domains with statistically significant univariate associations (FSD, urinary phenotype, and defecation dysfunction) are shown in Fig. 2.

Burden-response relationship

The primary three-domain burden score (urinary, pain, FSD) showed an overall positive ordinal association with SDS (Fig. 3; Spearman r = 0.158, P < 0.001, Table 2). Mean SDS was 29.0 ± 7.2 (n = 164) with zero positive domains, 32.4 ± 8.6 (n = 353) with one, 32.1 ± 8.0 (n = 267) with two, and 34.5 ± 8.8 (n = 92) with three domains. Means were similar in the one- and two-domain strata relative to the three-domain stratum, so the pattern should not be interpreted as a strictly monotonic stepwise gradient. The 5.5-point difference between zero- and three-domain strata indicates higher average depressive symptom burden with greater multidomain load.

Multivariable regression

Clinical model (n = 764, R² = 0.115)

After adjustment for all covariates, FSD showed the largest coefficient among phenotype domains (β = 3.14; 95% CI 1.97–4.31; P < 0.001). Urinary phenotype (β = 1.61; 95% CI 0.44–2.78; P = 0.007) and defecation dysfunction (β = 1.67; 95% CI 0.27–3.07; P = 0.020) were also independently associated with higher SDS. Pelvic pain was not significant (β = −0.37; 95% CI − 1.68 to 0.94; P = 0.58). Lactation was the largest negative coefficient in the model (β = −4.02; 95% CI − 5.40 to − 2.64; P < 0.001, Table 3). Delivery mode, age, and BMI were not independently associated with SDS. Full coefficient estimates are in Table 3; a forest plot of the clinical model is shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

Extended model (n = 764, R² = 0.164)

Adding PFDI-20 and I-QOL increased explained variance by 4.9% points. FSD remained independently significant with modest attenuation (β = 2.52; 95% CI 1.36–3.67; P < 0.001, Table 4), indicating that the sexual dysfunction signal is not fully carried by global symptom distress or incontinence-related quality of life. By contrast, the urinary coefficient attenuated substantially and was no longer significant (β = 0.22; 95% CI − 0.97 to 1.41; P = 0.72), suggesting that the urinary-depression association is largely mediated through incontinence burden appraisal and quality-of-life pathways. PFDI-20 was positively associated with SDS (β = 0.040 per point; 95% CI 0.01–0.06; P = 0.002), and I-QOL was inversely associated (β = −0.11 per point; 95% CI − 0.17 to − 0.06; P < 0.001). Lactation remained protective (β = −3.64; 95% CI − 4.98 to − 2.31; P < 0.001). Defecation dysfunction attenuated to borderline significance (β = 1.19; 95% CI − 0.23 to 2.60; P = 0.101, Table 4). Figure 4 displays adjusted coefficients for the extended model. Variance inflation factors for extended-model covariates were below conventional thresholds (Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

This study asked whether FSD carries an independent association with depressive symptom burden when other pelvic floor domains are simultaneously accounted for. It does. The FSD coefficient was the largest among all phenotype domains in the clinical model and retained significance in the extended model after PFDI-20 and I-QOL were added (β = 2.52; 95% CI 1.36–3.67). The 20% attenuation from the clinical to the extended model contrasts sharply with the near-complete attenuation of the urinary coefficient (88%), which was no longer significant once symptom distress and incontinence-specific quality of life were controlled. In this cross-sectional analysis, the FSD coefficient showed greater attenuation resistance than the urinary coefficient after adding PFDI-20 and I-QOL, suggesting that the FSD–SDS association persists after adjustment for measured global pelvic floor distress and incontinence-specific quality of life. This should not be interpreted as proof of a distinct causal pathway, as unmeasured confounding (relationship quality, psychiatric history, medications, sleep, and socioeconomic factors) remains possible.

Patient-reported outcomes and clinical assessment

Pelvic floor care requires integration of validated patient-reported outcomes (PFDI-20, I-QOL, FSFI) with mood assessment rather than anatomy alone [17–19]. Contemporary gynecologic studies further highlight psychosexual distress accompanying sexual dysfunction across HPV evaluation, FGM-affected, and oncology survivorship settings [5–7]. Several mechanisms are plausible. FSD directly affects intimate relationships, and relationship strain is among the most consistently identified psychosocial drivers of depression [4]. Sexual dysfunction can also generate shame and self-concealment behaviors that are distinct from the practical impairments captured by I-QOL—clothing adjustments, travel avoidance, and activity limitations. Sexual concerns are more likely to remain undisclosed unless clinicians actively invite discussion, which means the psychological cost of FSD may accumulate without the help-seeking that often accompanies urinary or bowel complaints. Prior work has documented the FSD-depression bidirectional association and established FSD prevalence in urogynecological settings [1, 4]. The contribution here is to isolate FSD’s independent coefficient within a multidomain regression that simultaneously adjusts for urinary symptoms, pain, prolapse, and obstetric history—a structure closer to the actual complexity of pelvic floor clinic patients than most prior analyses. Recent pelvic reconstructive series emphasize combining objective anatomical outcomes with validated patient-reported pelvic floor symptom measures rather than anatomy alone [21]. In robotic sacrocolpopexy, d’Altilia et al. further applied trifecta endpoints that integrate anatomical correction, functional recovery, and patient-reported symptom improvement as a more comprehensive standard of treatment success [22].

The attenuation of the urinary coefficient in the extended model should not be read as evidence that urinary symptoms are unimportant for mental health. The opposite conclusion is supported by the substantial univariate association (32.87 vs. 31.00; P < 0.001) and by a large literature linking SUI and OAB with depression and anxiety [8–10]. The mechanistic interpretation is more specific: the urinary-depression coupling appears to operate through how women experience and appraise incontinence burden and through the quality-of-life restrictions that I-QOL quantifies. When those constructs are held constant, the incremental effect of the urinary label itself shrinks. This finding has practical implications for intervention design: treatments that reduce incontinence burden appraisal and restore participation may reduce depressive risk even before the underlying physiology is fully corrected [23]. Defecation dysfunction showed a parallel attenuation pattern—significant in the clinical model (β = 1.67; P = 0.020), borderline in the extended model (β = 1.19; P = 0.10)—likely reflecting overlap with PFDI-20’s colorectal distress subscale [24].

The overall positive ordinal association between three-domain burden and SDS (29.0 to 34.5 from zero to three domains; Spearman r = 0.158, P < 0.001) supports a cumulative burden interpretation: pelvic floor problems compound psychological distress rather than simply adding in parallel. A patient with three positive non-prolapse domains carries a mean SDS more than 5 points above a patient with none, a difference that may be clinically relevant for screening prioritization though it should not be equated with a validated minimal clinically important difference for the SDS [25]. Pelvic floor clinics already collect multidomain data as a matter of routine; this gradient gives those data a mood-risk interpretation that can guide screening effort without requiring additional instruments. Universal brief mood screening for all patients, with prioritized follow-up for women with multiple positive domains and FSD in particular, translates directly into a clinical workflow. Related cohort data from a specialty postpartum perineal referral clinic indicate that pelvic floor symptoms (including urinary incontinence across pregnancy and the postpartum period and pain-related referral indications) can be independently associated with a positive postpartum depression screen in that setting [26].

Lactation was consistently associated with lower SDS in both models (clinical: β = −4.02; extended: β = −3.64). Interpretation should remain cautious in this cross-sectional sample: lactation may reflect postpartum timing, healthier baseline status, or reverse causation (earlier breastfeeding cessation among more depressed women) rather than a direct biological effect. Three non-mutually-exclusive explanations deserve consideration. Oxytocin release during breastfeeding has anxiolytic and antidepressant properties in animal models and may exert protective mood effects in the postpartum period. The relationship may also be partly confounded by postpartum timing: lactating women are more often in an earlier postpartum window, when some mood trajectories are naturally improving. A third possibility involves reverse causation: women with more severe depression may discontinue breastfeeding earlier, making lactation status a marker of lower baseline distress rather than a cause of it. The cross-sectional design cannot separate these explanations; prospective cohort studies with repeated mood and lactation assessments are needed [27].

Postpartum pelvic floor care increasingly recognizes that local tissue healing and sexual well-being may be influenced by adjunctive strategies. Longitudinal data indicate that postpartum sexual dysfunction follows distinct psychosocial trajectories from early pregnancy through 12 months postpartum, supporting integrated assessment of sexual function and mood in postpartum pelvic floor follow-up [28]. Brezeanu et al. reported that intravaginal lactic acid gel improved post-episiotomy healing and patient-reported outcomes in a randomized trial [29], supporting attention to vulvovaginal comfort in postpartum pelvic floor follow-up. Although our cohort was premenopausal, pelvic floor muscle training remains relevant to sexual function across the life course; Șerbănescu et al. showed that Kegel exercises augmented local estriol therapy for menopausal atrophic vulvovaginitis [30], illustrating how pelvic floor interventions may complement symptom-based care in other populations. The null association for pelvic pain warrants careful interpretation. A VAS score of 1 or above on a single assessment captures an extremely wide range of experiences, from brief post-examination discomfort to moderate chronic pain, and the available pain variable did not include neuropathic features, catastrophizing, or temporal pain trajectories—constructs that are among the strongest predictors of pain-related depression [31]. A composite pain phenotype built from these dimensions would likely show a different result. The null finding reflects measurement constraints, not the clinical irrelevance of pelvic pain for mood. Our pelvic pain phenotype (chronic pain documentation or VAS ≥ 1) likely captured heterogeneous pain severity; non-pharmacological pelvic pain interventions such as transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation have been reviewed for genito-pelvic pain conditions [32], highlighting the need for more specific pain phenotyping in future pelvic floor–mood research. Using chronic pelvic pain documentation only and a VAS ≥ 3 threshold among women with recorded VAS scores yielded similar FSD coefficients (see Supplementary Table S5).

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference in any direction; the observed associations could reflect bidirectional pathways or shared upstream determinants [33]. Women excluded from regression were systematically older, less likely to be lactating, and had higher mean SDS than those included, suggesting complete-case estimates may modestly underestimate depression associations in the full population (Supplementary Table S1). Residual confounders not captured in the dataset include socioeconomic status, relationship quality, prior psychiatric diagnosis, sleep disorders, thyroid function, and medications known to affect both sexual function and mood. The single-center design limits external generalizability, though it ensures consistent instrument administration and phenotype ascertainment across all participants. The FSFI cutoff of 26.55 has reasonable sensitivity and specificity in research samples but may misclassify some women; domain-level scoring distributions were not available. Near-universal prolapse positivity (99.4%) precluded meaningful prolapse subgroup analyses. Against these limitations, the study’s strengths include a large premenopausal cohort with standardized multidomain phenotyping, FSFI-based FSD classification, a continuous depression endpoint sensitive to subthreshold symptoms, two dedicated physical therapists performing all pain assessments, and pre-specified hypotheses tested with a two-model regression strategy designed to allow mechanistic interpretation of attenuation patterns.

Conclusion

In premenopausal women attending a pelvic floor specialty clinic, female sexual dysfunction—assessed by FSFI—carries the strongest and most adjustment-resistant independent association with depressive symptom burden among all evaluated pelvic floor phenotypes. The association persists after accounting for global pelvic floor distress and incontinence-specific quality of life, while acknowledging unmeasured confounding. Three-domain phenotype load showed an overall positive ordinal association with SDS, identifying women with greater concurrent burden as a group that may warrant enhanced mood screening in pelvic floor clinics, alongside a stepped-care response that scales with phenotype burden. Prospective studies are needed to clarify directionality, residual confounding, and whether integrated sexual-function and mood screening improves outcomes beyond standard pelvic floor care.
