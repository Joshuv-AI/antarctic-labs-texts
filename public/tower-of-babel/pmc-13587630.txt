TRIM47, stabilized by YTHDF3-mediated m6A modification, promotes cardiac lipid accumulation and aggravates diabetic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Weiyan Wang, Lingli Xie, Guilan Chen, Xin Zuo, Meiling Zhong, Tong Yang, Weihua Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a prevalent and severe cardiovascular complication of diabetes mellitus, characterized by progressive cardiac dysfunction and pathological remodeling driven by myocardial metabolic disturbances. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated.MethodsA type 2 DCM mouse model was established by feeding C57BL/6 mice a high-fat diet combined with streptozotocin injections. Cardiomyocyte-specific TRIM47 knockdown was achieved via AAV9-mediated delivery of a cardiomyocyte-specific cTnT promoter-driven shRNA. In vitro, primary neonatal mouse ventricular cardiomyocytes (NMCMs) were treated with palmitic acid (PA) to model cardiomyocyte injury.ResultsTRIM47 was significantly upregulated in diabetic hearts. Cardiomyocyte-specific TRIM47 knockdown improved left ventricular ejection fraction and fractional shortening, attenuated chamber dilation, and reduced heart weight-to-tibia length ratio. TRIM47 silencing attenuated cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and cardiomyocyte apoptosis, alongside reduced myocardial inflammatory cytokine levels (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β). Furthermore, TRIM47 knockdown alleviated myocardial lipid accumulation and reduced triglyceride levels, accompanied by downregulation of the fatty acid translocase CD36. In vitro, PA-treated NMCMs exhibited elevated TRIM47 expression, and its knockdown mitigated PA-induced cardiomyocyte injury, inflammation, apoptosis, and lipid deposition. Mechanistically, the m6A reader YTHDF3 was upregulated under PA conditions and directly bound to m6A-modified TRIM47 mRNA, enhancing its stability. Rescue experiments demonstrated that YTHDF3 overexpression exacerbated PA-induced cardiomyocyte injury, whereas concurrent TRIM47 knockdown largely reversed these deleterious effects.ConclusionsOur results revealed that TRIM47 may contribute to the progression of DCM by promoting abnormal cardiac lipid metabolism, exacerbating inflammation and apoptosis, and these effects are partially mediated through the upstream m6A reader YTHDF3.

Introduction

Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a major cardiovascular complication of diabetes mellitus, characterized by structural and functional alterations of the myocardium that develop independently of coronary artery disease, hypertension, and valvular heart disease [1,2]. In its early stages, DCM typically presents with diastolic dysfunction, which may progressively deteriorate and ultimately lead to systolic dysfunction and overt clinical heart failure [3]. Clinically, this condition initially impairs diastolic relaxation and subsequently compromises cardiac pumping efficiency, and in severe cases results in heart failure and death [4]. The pathogenic mechanisms of DCM remain incompletely understood to date. Thus, elucidating the molecular underpinnings of DCM and identifying precise therapeutic targets are critically needed.

Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is recognized as a cardiac metabolic disorder characterized by aberrant cardiac lipid accumulation [[5], [6], [7]]. This phenomenon may arise from enhanced fatty acid influx into cardiomyocytes, or from impaired mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation [8,9]. Such lipid overload can induce cardiomyocyte death [10]. Moreover, disordered myocardial lipid metabolism triggers pro-inflammatory signaling and activates fibrogenic pathways, thereby promoting chronic inflammation and myocardial fibrosis [11,12]. However, the upstream regulators of cardiac lipid homeostasis disruption and the ensuing inflammatory response remain poorly understood, highlighting the urgent need to identify key molecular drivers.

Tripartite motif 47 (TRIM47) is a highly conserved E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase, characterized by a canonical N-terminal TRIM domain and a C-terminal PRY-SPRY domain [13,14]. TRIM47 belongs to the TRIM protein family [15], which comprises over 80 members in humans, most of which possess E3 ubiquitin ligase activity and play critical roles in diverse cellular processes, including innate immunity, inflammation, cell proliferation, apoptosis, and metabolic regulation [15,16]. Current research on TRIM47 has primarily focused on cancer; however, accumulating evidence suggests that TRIM47 is also associated with inflammatory responses and tissue damage. Studies have reported that TRIM47 activates inflammatory responses in endothelial cells and exacerbates lipopolysaccharide-induced acute lung injury [17]. TRIM47 also promotes inflammatory responses and pyroptosis in bronchial epithelial cells [18]. These findings collectively imply that TRIM47 is a key regulator of inflammation-related signaling pathways. Beyond its roles in inflammation, emerging evidence suggests a link between TRIM47 and lipid metabolism. Ji and colleagues found that TRIM47 expression is upregulated in palmitic acid-treated hepatocytes and that TRIM47 promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of CYLD, thereby participating in the regulation of CYLD-mediated protective effects against non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [19]. Furthermore, evidence indicates that inhibition of TRIM47 alleviates LMO7 knockdown-induced lipid metabolism disorders and inflammatory responses in hepatocytes [20]. However, whether TRIM47 is involved in DCM remains unknown.

In this study, we investigated the expression and functional role of TRIM47 in DCM. Specifically, we examined whether TRIM47 contributes to cardiac dysfunction and lipid metabolism, and explored the potential upstream mechanism, focusing on the m6A reader YTH domain-containing family protein 3 (YTHDF3).

Material and methods

Online data analysis

The mouse DCM dataset GSE123975 was obtained from the GEO database. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened using the criteria of |Log2FC|> 1 and adjusted p-value <0.01. The volcano plot was generated using the R package ggplot2, the heatmap was created with the R package ComplexHeatmap, the raincloud plot was produced using the R package enrichplot, and the bubble plot was generated using ggplot2.

Animal information

Five- and seven-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to different groups. They were housed under a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 22 ± 1 °C and 45–55% relative humidity, with free access to food and water. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Third People's Hospital (approval No. 2026-29-01).

HFD/STZ-induced type 2 DCM mouse model

In the first section, seven-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to two groups: normal diet (ND) and STZ + HFD. Mice in the STZ + HFD group were fed an HFD for 24 consecutive weeks. After 8 weeks of HFD feeding, mice received intraperitoneal injections of 35 mg/kg streptozotocin (STZ, S6089, Macklin, China) once daily for three consecutive days. Mice in the ND group were injected with an equal volume of citrate buffer at the same time points. Following the start of HFD feeding, body weight and fasting blood glucose levels were measured every four weeks.

In the second section, five-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to four groups: ND, STZ + HFD, STZ + HFD + AAV9sh-NC, and STZ + HFD + AAV9sh-TRIM47. For TRIM47 knockdown, each mouse in the corresponding groups received a single tail vein injection of 200 μL of AAV9sh-NC or AAV9sh-TRIM47. Two weeks after injection, the DCM model was induced using the STZ + HFD method.

At 24 weeks of HFD feeding, echocardiography was performed to assess cardiac function, including quantitative analysis of left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), fractional shortening (LVFS), and left ventricular (LV) mass. Subsequently, serum and cardiac tissue samples were collected for further analyses. Cardiac photographs were taken, and heart weight (HW) and tibia length (TL) were measured to calculate the HW/TL ratio. For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), fixed tissue samples were sectioned, stained, and observed under a transmission electron microscope.

Triglyceride (TG) detection

TG level was measured by a detection kit (BL1200B, Biosharp, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For tissue samples, tissue was mixed with extraction buffer and homogenized on ice, followed by centrifugation. For cell samples, cells were collected, mixed with extraction buffer, and disrupted by ultrasonic sonication on ice, followed by centrifugation. Serum samples were directly used for measurement. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit (BL521S, Biosharp, China). The reaction mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, and the absorbance was read at 510 nm by a multimode microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, USA).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from heart tissues or cultured cells using the AG RNAex Pro RNA reagent (AG21101, Accurate Biology, China). The RNA quality and concentration were determined spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop One, Thermo Scientific, USA). Reverse transcription for cDNA synthesis was performed using HP All-in-one qRT Master MixⅡ (RT203, YoungGen, China). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction was performed with SYBR GREEN (SR4110, Solarbio, China) and 2 × Fast Taq plus PCR Master Mix (BL1014, Biosharp, China). The reaction conditions were as follows: initial incubation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 15 s, with a melting curve step from 60 °C to 94 °C. Real-time fluorescence quantification was performed using the Pangaea 3 system (Aperbio, USA) and the Exicycler 96 real-time PCR system (Bioneer, South Korea). The following primer sequences were used for qPCR: TRIM47 (forward, 5′-CAGAGTTTCCAGAACGAGGTGAT-3'; reverse, 5′-GTTGTGCCGTGCCTTGCTT-3′), IL-1β (forward, 5′-TTCCCATTAGACAACTGC-3'; reverse, 5′-GATTCTTTCCTTTGAGGC-3′), TNF-α (forward, 5′-TCTCATTCCTGCTTGTGG-3'; reverse, 5′-ACTTGGTGGTTTGCTACG-3′), IL-6 (forward, 5′-TAACAGATAAGCTGGAGTC-3'; reverse, 5′-TAGGTTTGCCGAGTAGA-3′), ANP (forward, 5′-GGCTTCTTCCTCGTCTT-3'; reverse, 5′-TCTCCTCCAGGTGGTCTA-3′), BNP (forward, 5′-GAAGGTGCTGTCCCAGATGA-3'; reverse, 5′-TCCAGCAGCTTCTGCATCTT-3′), β-MHC (forward, 5′-GCCTCCATTGATGACTCTG-3'; reverse, 5′-TCGTTGCCCACTTTGACT-3′), and YTHDF3 (forward, 5′-ATGCTTATGCTGGTGTC-3'; reverse, 5′-GCTTGAAAGTAGCGATTA-3′). β-actin served as the internal control, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot

Homogenate of heart tissues or the collected cultured cells was lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (R0010, Solarbio, China) with PMSF (P0100, Solarbio, China) and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and quantified by BCA protein assay (PC0020, Solarbio, China). Protein extractions were separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with membrane sealing solution (SW3010, Solarbio, China), the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the membranes were washed and incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h, and bands were detected with the ECL reagent (PE0010, Solarbio, China). β-actin was used as a control. The target band was analyzed using the Tanon Image (Shanghai Tianneng Company) image analysis software. Antibodies: TRIM47 (1:2000, 26885-1-AP, Proteintech, China), YTHDF3 (1:300, sc-377,119, Santa Cruz, USA), CD36 (1:2000, 32731-1-AP, Proteintech, China), ANP (1:2000, A22075, ABclonal, China), BNP (1:2000, A23996, ABclonal, China), β-MHC (1:5000, A22140, ABclonal, China), and HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:3000, SE134, Solarbio, China).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

For cells cultured on glass coverslips, fixation was performed with 4% paraformaldehyde (80,096,618, Sinopharm, China) for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (ST795, Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 30 min. After blocking with 1% BSA (A602440-0050, Sangon, China) for 15 min, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody against cTnT (1:200, 68300-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), then with Alexa Fluor® 555-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:200, #4409, CST, USA) at room temperature for 60 min. For tissue samples, 5-μm sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by boiling in retrieval solution followed by low-heat incubation for 10 min. Sections were blocked with 1% BSA for 30 min, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TRIM47 (1:200, 26885-1-AP, Proteintech, China) and cTnT (1:200, 68300-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, #4412, CST, USA) and Alexa Fluor® 555-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:200, #4409, CST, USA) secondary antibodies at room temperature for 90 min. For both cells and tissue sections, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (D106471-5 mg, Aladdin, China), and after mounting with an anti-fade reagent, fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Histological analysis

After fixation, mouse heart tissues were embedded in paraffin and cut into 5-μm-thick sections. Deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; H8070, Solarbio, China; and A600190, Sangon, China), Masson's trichrome (DC0032, Leagene Biotech, China), and Alexa Fluor 488-labeled wheat germ agglutinin (WGA; L4895, Sigma, USA) according to the manufacturers' instructions to assess the pathological changes of myocardial tissue, collagen content and cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (BX53, Olympus, Japan).

Tunel staining

An In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (12,156,792,910, Roche, Switzerland) was used to assess apoptosis levels in cardiomyocytes. Cells cultured on glass coverslips were permeabilized with 200 μL of 0.1% Triton X-100 (ST795, Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, 200 μL of TUNEL reaction mixture was added to completely cover the coverslips, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 60 min in a humidified, light-protected environment. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with DAPI (D106471-5 mg, Aladdin, China) counterstaining solution for 5 min. The coverslips were then mounted onto glass slides with a drop of anti-fade mounting medium (S2100, Solarbio, China), and staining was observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope.

Oil Red O staining

For Oil Red O staining, fixed tissues were sequentially dehydrated in 20% and 30% sucrose solutions, embedded in OCT compound, and cut into 10-μm-thick sections. The sections were stained with Oil Red O working solution in a staining jar for 5 min, followed by differentiation in 60% isopropanol. After washing with distilled water, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min and then rinsed in tap water for blue development. The sections were mounted with glycerol gelatin, and staining outcomes were observed and photographed under a light microscope.

For Oil Red O staining of cultured cells, an Oil Red O staining kit (G1262, Solarbio, China) was used. Cells were washed with PBS, followed by fixation with the provided fixative. After that, the cells were rinsed with 60% isopropanol for 20–30 s and then incubated with the freshly prepared staining solution, followed by a second rinse with 60% isopropanol for 10–20 s to clear the background. Nuclei were counterstained with Mayer's hematoxylin from the kit. After washing, the cells were incubated with the buffer for 1 min for blue development. The buffer was then discarded, and the cells were observed and photographed under a light microscope.

Cell isolation

Primary neonatal mouse ventricular cardiomyocytes (NMCMs) were isolated from 1- to 2-day-old neonatal mice. After removal of the atria, the ventricular tissues were placed in 0.1% trypsin (T8150, Solarbio, China) and digested overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the tissues were subjected to three successive digestions with 0.05% collagenase II (BS164, Biosharp, China), each lasting 10 min at 37 °C. The dissociated cells were collected and purified. The isolated cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (BL305A, Biosharp, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 11,011–8611, Tianhang, China) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cytosine arabinoside was added to the culture medium to inhibit the proliferation of non-cardiomyocytes.

Cell treatment

To establish an in vitro model, NMCMs were exposed to 100 μmol/L palmitic acid (PA, P815432, Macklin, China) for 72 h before detection. For TRIM47 knockdown, adenovirus stored at −80 °C was thawed, gently mixed, and added to the culture medium of cells, which were then incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Following gentle mixing, the cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. In some experiments, NMCMs were infected with adenovirus for 24 h, then treated with 100 μmol/L PA for 72 h before detection. In other experiments, following the same adenovirus infection and PA treatment, the cells were subsequently exposed to 5 μg/mL actinomycin D (A432787, Aladdin, China) for varying lengths of time before qPCR.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)-PCR

RIP was performed using the RNA Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (JKR23003, Genecreate, China). Cells were lysed with the provided Complete RIP Lysis Buffer. For IP, anti-YTHDF3 antibodies or control IgG were first coupled to protein A/G agarose beads for 2 h at room temperature. The antibody–bead complexes were then incubated with RIP Buffer and cell lysate overnight at 4 °C. After five washes, the beads were divided into two aliquots: one for Western blotting and the other for RNA purification. For RNA extraction, samples were treated with TRIzol and chloroform, precipitated with isopropanol at −80 °C for 2–4 h, washed with 75% ethanol, and dissolved in DEPC-treated water. The RNA isolated from the RNA-protein complexes was then analyzed by qPCR using specific primers. The primer sequences were as follows: TRIM47 forward, 5′-TCTTCAACCACAGGCT-3'; reverse, 5′-GGAGTTGTCCCAGCTAA-3'.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD. For animal experiments, n = 6 per group; for cell experiments, n = 4. Unpaired Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

TRIM47 is elevated in the cardiac tissues of DCM mice

To elucidate the transcriptomic landscape of DCM, we analyzed the GSE123975 dataset derived from cardiac tissues of control and STZ-induced diabetic mice. The volcano plot (Figure 1A) revealed discrete populations of significantly upregulated (blue) and downregulated (gray) DEGs in DCM hearts compared with controls. Consistently, hierarchical clustering in the heatmap (Figure 1B) displayed distinct expression profiles between the two groups, confirming robust transcriptomic reprogramming in the diabetic myocardium. To explore the functional implications of genes in this dataset, we conducted GSEA analyses for GO and KEGG pathway enrichment. The top 10 positively enriched terms and the top 10 negatively enriched terms (i.e., the 10 highest and 10 lowest NES) were displayed, encompassing diverse biological processes and signaling pathways. As shown in Figure 1C,D, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed consistent functional enrichment in DCM. Activated pathways were predominantly related to lipid metabolism (PPAR signaling, fatty acid biosynthesis/elongation, glycosphingolipid metabolism) and inflammatory/remodeling processes (myeloid leukocyte migration, complement cascades, peptidase inhibitor activity, collagen-containing extracellular matrix). Conversely, suppressed pathways were uniformly associated with mitochondrial dysfunction (TCA cycle, 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism, mitochondrial respiratory chain assembly, mitochondrial gene expression/translation, and mitochondrial ribosomal subunits) and ribosomal protein synthesis (structural constituent of the ribosome). Cardiomyocyte-specific knockdown of TRIM47 ameliorates cardiac dysfunction and myocardial injury in DCM mice

To determine whether TRIM47 contributes to DCM pathogenesis, we employed a loss-of-function approach using AAV9-shTRIM47 driven by the cTnT promoter. Mice received tail-vein injections of AAV9-shTRIM47 or a control vector, followed by DCM induction via STZ/HFD two weeks later (Figure 3A). After 24 weeks of HFD feeding, serum and cardiac tissues were harvested. Echocardiography showed that TRIM47 knockdown preserved cardiac function, as evidenced by reduced LVEDD and LVESD, improved LVEF and LVFS, and decreased LV mass (Figure 3B–C). The inhibited TRIM47 expression in TRIM47-suppressed STZ/HFD mice was confirmed by qPCR and Western blotting (Figure 3D–E). Representative images of whole hearts (Figure 3F) and quantification of the heart weight-to-tibia length (HW/TL) ratio (Figure 3G) indicated that DCM-associated cardiac enlargement was significantly attenuated by TRIM47 silencing. H&E staining (Figure 3H) revealed ameliorated myocardial architecture in TRIM47 knockdown mice. WGA staining and subsequent quantification of cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area demonstrated that TRIM47 deficiency markedly attenuated cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (Figure 3I). Furthermore, Masson trichrome staining and collagen deposition quantification showed that myocardial fibrosis was significantly reduced following TRIM47 knockdown (Figure 3J). At the molecular level, the expression of β-MHC, ANP, and BNP was markedly downregulated in left ventricular tissues of TRIM47 knockdown mice compared with control DCM mice (Figure 3K-L). Inflammation is a key contributor to the multifactorial pathogenesis of DCM, as supported by both animal and human evidence [2]; accordingly, we observed that TRIM47 silencing significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, in the myocardium (Figure 3M). Finally, TUNEL staining revealed apoptotic cells in left ventricular tissues were significantly reduced following TRIM47 knockdown (Figure 3N). Cardiomyocyte-specific knockdown of TRIM47 attenuates myocardial lipid accumulation in DCM mice

DCM is characterized by myocardial lipid accumulation [5] and contributes substantially to cardiac lipotoxicity; we next examined whether the salutary effects of TRIM47 silencing were associated with amelioration of myocardial lipid accumulation. Oil Red O staining of left ventricular tissue revealed extensive lipid deposition and infiltration throughout the myocardium of DCM mice receiving a control vector, whereas cardiomyocyte-specific TRIM47 knockdown markedly attenuated this pathological lipid accumulation (Figure 4A). To further characterize these changes at the ultrastructural level, TEM was performed. As shown in Figure 4B, cardiomyocytes from control DCM mice exhibited numerous large electron-dense lipid droplets dispersed within the cytoplasm, indicative of severe intracellular lipid overload. In contrast, TRIM47 knockdown substantially reduced lipid accumulation. TG levels were significantly elevated in both cardiac tissue and serum of control DCM mice compared with non-diabetic controls. Notably, TRIM47 silencing markedly decreased TG concentrations in both compartments (Figure 4C). Moreover, we assessed the expression of CD36, a key fatty acid translocase responsible for long-chain fatty acid uptake in cardiomyocytes [21]. Western blotting analysis demonstrated that CD36 protein levels were significantly upregulated in the left ventricular tissues of control DCM mice. Strikingly, cardiomyocyte-specific TRIM47 knockdown substantially suppressed CD36 expression (Figure 4D). TRIM47 knockdown reduces PA-induced lipid deposition in cardiomyocytes

To corroborate the in vivo findings and elucidate the direct role of TRIM47 in cardiomyocyte injury, we isolated primary NMCMs from 1- to 2-day-old mice. The purity of the isolated cardiomyocytes was confirmed by IF staining for cTnT (Figure 5A). After that, NMCMs were exposed to 100 μmol/L PA for 72 h to mimic the environment characteristic of DCM (Figure 5B). As shown in Figure 5C and5D, PA treatment significantly upregulated TRIM47 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels compared with untreated controls, recapitulating the in vivo observations in DCM hearts. To investigate the functional role of TRIM47 in PA-induced cardiomyocyte injury, we suppressed TRIM47 in NMCMs using adenoviral delivery of shRNA. qPCR and Western blotting confirmed that sh-TRIM47Ad efficiently suppressed TRIM47 expression under basal conditions (Figure 5E–F). Subsequent analyses revealed that TRIM47 knockdown significantly ameliorated PA-induced cardiomyocyte injury. PA exposure markedly elevated the mRNA levels of β-MHC, ANP, and BNP in sh-NCAd-infected NMCMs, whereas TRIM47 silencing substantially attenuated their induction (Figure 5J). Similarly, PA treatment increased the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) in control cells, and TRIM47 knockdown significantly suppressed this upregulation (Figure 5K). Furthermore, TUNEL staining demonstrated that PA exposure induced cell apoptosis, whereas TRIM47 deficiency reversed this trend in NMCMs (Figure 5L), underscoring the cytoprotective effect of TRIM47 knockdown.

Then, we evaluated whether TRIM47 knockdown influenced lipid accumulation in NMCMs. Oil Red O staining revealed extensive lipid formation in PA-treated sh-NCAd NMCMs, whereas TRIM47 silencing significantly reduced lipid accumulation (Figure 5M). Consistently, intracellular TG levels were markedly elevated in PA-treated sh-NCAd NMCMs but significantly diminished following TRIM47 knockdown (Figure 5N). Finally, Western blotting analysis showed that PA treatment upregulated CD36 protein levels in sh-NCAd-infected NMCMs; this upregulation was substantially attenuated by TRIM47 knockdown (Figure 5O). Collectively, these in vitro findings implied that TRIM47 knockdown protects against PA-induced cardiomyocyte injury, inhibits inflammation and apoptosis, and reduces lipid accumulation.

YTHDF3 stabilizes TRIM47 mRNA via m6A modification

Having established the critical role of TRIM47 in DCM pathogenesis, we next sought to investigate its potential upstream regulators. Bioinformatics analysis predicted the presence of m6A modification sites within the TRIM47 transcript (Figure 6A). YTHDF3 (YTH domain family protein 3) is an m6A reader that specifically binds to m6A-modified mRNA and promotes its stability. It was previously reported to be upregulated in the cardiac tissues of diabetic mice [22]. Additionally, knockdown of YTHDF3 reduces the expression of the key regulators of fatty acid synthesis and β-oxidation in cancer cells, indicating its role in lipid metabolism [23]. Therefore, we suspected that YTHDF3 might be a potential upstream regulator of TRIM47. Western blotting revealed that YTHDF3 protein levels were significantly upregulated in NMCMs following PA treatment (Figure 6B). We next investigated the regulatory relationship between YTHDF3 and TRIM47. NMCMs were infected with Ad-YTHDF3 or Ad-null for 24 h qPCR and Western blotting confirmed that YTHDF3 overexpression substantially increased both YTHDF3 and TRIM47 expression (Figure 6C–D). To determine whether YTHDF3 enhanced TRIM47 mRNA stability, NMCMs were treated with actinomycin D following Ad-YTHDF3 infection and PA exposure. qPCR analysis showed that YTHDF3 overexpression markedly prolonged the half-life of TRIM47 mRNA compared with control cells (Figure 6E). To further investigate whether YTHDF3 functions through TRIM47, rescue experiments were performed. NMCMs were co-infected with adenoviruses for TRIM47 knockdown and YTHDF3 overexpression, followed by PA treatment (Figure 6G). As displayed, YTHDF3 overexpression enhanced PA-induced TNF-α mRNA expression, apoptosis, and lipid accumulation. Notably, these pro-injurious effects were largely reversed upon TRIM47 knockdown (Figure 6H–J). Taken together, these results indicated that YTHDF3 acts upstream of TRIM47 to promote cardiomyocyte injury, at least in part by stabilizing TRIM47 mRNA.

Discussion

In this study, we identified TRIM47 as a novel mediator of DCM. We demonstrated that TRIM47 expression is significantly upregulated in diabetic hearts and that cardiomyocyte-specific knockdown of TRIM47 alleviates cardiac dysfunction, inflammation, apoptosis, and abnormal lipid metabolism in a mouse model of DCM. In vitro, TRIM47 knockdown similarly ameliorated PA-induced cardiomyocyte injury and lipid accumulation. Mechanistically, we uncovered that the m6A reader YTHDF3 directly binds to m6A-modified TRIM47 mRNA, enhancing its stability and expression. Rescue experiments further confirmed that YTHDF3 promotes PA-induced cardiomyocyte injury through TRIM47. Collectively, our findings implied a YTHDF3-TRIM47 axis that drives DCM progression.

The upregulation of TRIM47 in DCM hearts aligns with the emerging recognition of TRIM family proteins as critical regulators in cardiovascular diseases [24,25]. While TRIM72 (MG53) has been extensively studied in DCM [26,27], where it promotes lipotoxicity and insulin resistance through PPARα activation and IRS-1 degradation [27], the role of TRIM47 in cardiac pathophysiology has remained largely unexplored. In this study, we found that TRIM47 was highly expressed in the heart tissues of DCM mice and was co-localized with cardiomyocytes. To investigate the function of the abnormally upregulated TRIM47 in DCM, we suppressed TRIM47 using a cardiomyocyte-specific promoter. The results showed that inhibition of TRIM47 could alleviate cardiac function and myocardial damage in DCM mice. Studies have reported that TRIM47 is closely associated with inflammatory responses [17,18]. Apart from these, TRIM47 is associated with hepatic lipid metabolism [19,20]. Emerging evidence has implicated TRIM47 in lipid metabolism disorders in the cardiovascular system. Qin and colleagues recently reported that TRIM47 expression is upregulated in endothelial cells upon oxidized low-density lipoprotein stimulation, and that TRIM47 promotes atherosclerotic plaque formation by facilitating IκBα ubiquitination and subsequently activating the NF-κB signaling pathway [28]. Studies also suggested that excessive accumulation of lipids within myocardial cells can lead to lipotoxicity, and abnormal lipid metabolism can cause myocardial cells to release pro-fibrotic factors and trigger chronic inflammatory responses [11,12]. These findings further prompted us to investigate whether TRIM47 affects lipid metabolism in DCM.

In DCM mice, we observed that TRIM47 knockdown significantly reduced intramyocardial lipid accumulation, lowered tissue and serum triglyceride levels, and suppressed CD36 expression. CD36, also known as fatty acid translocase, is a transmembrane glycoprotein widely expressed in various tissues. It participates in multiple physiological processes, including long-chain fatty acid uptake, lipid metabolism, and regulation of chronic metabolic inflammation [29]. In the heart, CD36 plays a critical role in maintaining lipid homeostasis by facilitating long-chain fatty acid uptake [29,30]. In DCM, deficiency of CD36 alleviated disease progression [11,31]. Our in vitro experiments in PA-treated NMCMs further corroborated these findings, showing that TRIM47 deficiency attenuated lipid formation and intracellular triglyceride accumulation concurrent with CD36 downregulation. Besides that, TRIM47 knockdown also attenuated inflammation, apoptosis, and fibrosis in DCM hearts. Excessive lipid accumulation triggers oxidative stress and endoplasmic reticulum stress, which in turn activate inflammatory cascades and promote cell death [32]. Our in vitro experiments recapitulated these observations, confirming that TRIM47 knockdown protects against PA-induced lipid accumulation in cardiomyocytes. Overall, the above results indicated that inhibiting TRIM47 may alleviate the progression of DCM by improving abnormal lipid metabolism, as well as by suppressing inflammation and cell death.

Beyond the functional characterization of TRIM47, we elucidated a critical post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism governing its expression. m6A modification is the most prevalent internal modification in eukaryotic mRNA [33] and profoundly influences mRNA stability, translation, and degradation [34]. Our study identified YTHDF3, an m6A reader protein, as a key upstream regulator of TRIM47. YTHDF3 regulates the stability and translation of mRNAs by recognizing m6A-modified transcripts [35]. Several lines of evidence support the relevance of YTHDF3 in both cardiac pathology and lipid metabolism. First, YTHDF3 is upregulated in the cardiac tissues of diabetic mice [22]. Second, its overexpression induces cardiomyocyte apoptosis under ischemic conditions [36]. Third, YTHDF3 knockdown downregulates key enzymes involved in fatty acid synthesis (FASN, ACC1) and β-oxidation (CPT1A) in cancer cells, implicating it in lipid metabolism [23]. Additionally, YTHDF3 has been reported to reduce ROS production in Leydig cells and suppress M1 macrophage polarization, as evidenced by decreased TNF-α and iNOS expression [37,38]. Collectively, these findings suggested that YTHDF3 may mediate m6A modification of TRIM47 and promote its expression, thereby influencing the progression of DCM. Therefore, after discovering that TRIM47 could be m6A-modified, we prioritized studying YTHDF3 for further investigation. We demonstrated that YTHDF3 expression was elevated under PA conditions and that YTHDF3 overexpression increased TRIM47 mRNA stability and protein expression. RIP-qPCR confirmed a direct physical interaction between YTHDF3 and TRIM47 mRNA. Importantly, rescue experiments revealed that YTHDF3 overexpression aggravated PA-induced cardiomyocyte inflammation, apoptosis, and lipid accumulation, whereas simultaneous TRIM47 knockdown largely abrogated these effects. This placed TRIM47 downstream of YTHDF3 in a coherent pathogenic axis and suggested that YTHDF3 may exert its deleterious effects in DCM partly through stabilization of TRIM47 mRNA.

Also, some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, we recognize that YTHDF3, as an m6A reader, may recognize multiple m6A-modified transcripts simultaneously. Previous studies have identified diverse YTHDF3 targets in different pathological contexts, including FOXO3 [39], NKD1 [40], and LRP6 [23]. While our rescue experiments demonstrate that TRIM47 is a functionally critical downstream mediator of YTHDF3 in DCM, we do not exclude the possibility that additional YTHDF3 targets may also contribute to the disease process. Second, in the present study, we demonstrate that YTHDF3 promotes DCM progression primarily through stabilizing TRIM47 mRNA, thereby activating lipid metabolism, inflammatory, and apoptotic pathways. However, YTHDF3 has been reported to regulate diverse signaling pathways in other disease contexts, including WNT/β-catenin [40], JAK/STAT [41], and autophagy-related signaling [39]. Whether these pathways are also involved in YTHDF3-mediated cardiac pathology warrants further investigation, which may represent a promising avenue for our future research on YTHDF3 in DCM.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings establish the YTHDF3-TRIM47 axis as a previously unrecognized pathogenic cascade in DCM. Mechanistically, YTHDF3-mediated m6A modification enhances TRIM47 mRNA stability, leading to TRIM47 upregulation, which in turn promotes cardiac lipid accumulation, inflammation, and cardiomyocyte injury. Collectively, this study uncovers a novel mechanism driving DCM progression and may provide new insight into TRIM47 in DCM pathogenesis.
