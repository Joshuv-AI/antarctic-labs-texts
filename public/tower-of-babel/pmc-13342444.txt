Integrated geophysical, geological, and machine learning approach for structural characterization of the Balakot-Bagh fault zone associated with the 2005 Mw 7.6 Kashmir earthquake
Authors: Shahab Noor, Muhammad Younis Khan, Syed Ali Turab, Yonatan Garkebo Doyoro, Chih-Ping Lin, Muhammad Farooq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Active fault reactivation poses significant hazards, and understanding their near-surface structure is crucial for mitigating seismic risk. Along the Balakot-Bagh fault (BBF), the source of the 2005 Mw 7.6 Kashmir earthquake, geomorphic evidence is gradually eroded and sedimented. Traditional Electrical Resistivity Tomography (ERT) often produces smoothly varying tomograms that obscure sharp structural boundaries. This study introduces an integrated interpretation framework that combines geological mapping, high-resolution ERT imaging, and machine learning (ML) k-means clustering to improve characterization of the BBF’s shallow deformation zone at Sar Pain (S1) and Naushahra (S2), Pakistan. Geological surveys at S1 document numerous NW–SE-trending coseismic rupture strands with vertical displacements of 0.1–3 m, defining an actively deforming damage zone, while at S2, no surface rupture is preserved due to thick alluvial cover. Inverted ERT models at both sites reveal low-resistivity anomalies associated with fractured, water-saturated materials and fault gouge; however, conventional inversions smooth sharp resistivity gradients, limiting structural interpretation. Applying k-means clustering as a post-inversion segmentation tool transforms continuous resistivity fields into discrete lithological and structural domains. The Elbow method is used to determine the optimal number of clusters to improve interpretability. The clustered models sharpen structural discontinuities, delineate fault cores and subsidiary strands at S1, and reveal concealed deformation at S2 where surface evidence is absent. This integrated interpretation framework significantly enhances the resolution and interpretability of near-surface fault architecture within a crustal-scale thrust system. The approach is particularly effective for imaging buried fault segments and has important implications for seismic hazard assessment and land-use planning.

Introduction

Characterizing active faults is essential for assessing seismic hazards and mitigating earthquake risks in tectonically vulnerable regions. The Himalayan orogenic belt is a significant zone of active continental collision on Earth, accommodating substantial convergent strain through a complex network of thrust and strike-slip faults. This intense tectonic activity often leads to high-magnitude earthquakes, posing considerable hazards to the region1. A notable example is the Mw 7.6 Kashmir earthquake that severely impacted northern Pakistan on October 8, 2005. The rupture associated with this event propagated along the Balakot-Bagh fault (BBF), a northeast-dipping thrust fault that extends from Balakot in the northwest to Bagh in the southeast. Geologically, the BBF marks a significant lithological boundary, juxtaposing Precambrian limestone and shale to the northeast with the Miocene Murree Formation to the southwest, particularly near Muzaffarabad. Further southeast, the fault either lies entirely within the Murree Formation or defines the geological contact between the Murree and Kamlial Formations. The 2005 earthquake caused widespread devastation across Pakistan-administered Jammu and Kashmir and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, resulting in a tragic loss of over 80,000 lives and extensive infrastructural damage2–4. Similar blind-thrust earthquakes in other segments of the Alpine–Himalayan orogenic belt, such as the 2011 Van earthquake, share characteristics like limited surface rupture, notable hanging-wall deformation, and complex subsurface fault architecture5–7. These similarities underscore the need to integrate near-surface geophysical imaging with geological and seismological observations to better understand fault-zone processes, particularly in areas where surface expressions are incomplete or absent49. Other significant earthquakes, such as the 2008 Sichuan earthquake (Mw 7.9) and the 2023 Turkey–Syria earthquakes (Mw 7.8 and 7.5), further emphasize the global seismic hazard posed by active fault systems8,9. The proximity of major population centers to these fault zones worldwide emphasizes the importance of detailed fault studies for effective risk mitigation and informed urban planning 3,10,11,55.

Traditional fault-investigation approaches have relied heavily on surface-based structural, geomorphic, and stratigraphic analyses to infer fault geometry and kinematics. However, surface expressions of faults are often subtle, discontinuous, or degraded over geological timescales due to erosion, deposition, and human modification. These factors reduce the reliability of interpretations based solely on surface observations12,13. Subsurface structures associated with regional faults, such as the BBF, often preserve information that is unclear or missing at the surface, making geophysical methods crucial for accurate fault characterization. Electrical Resistivity Tomography (ERT), Seismic Refraction Tomography (SRT), and Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR) effectively delineate subsurface fault geometries and near-surface structural features14,15,58. These methods offer complementary insights into near-surface structures. In this study, the ERT method is used to image fault structures. ERT is particularly advantageous due to its sensitivity to resistivity variations associated with lithological contrasts, fluid saturation, clay content, and fracturing12,16–19,50,53,54. Machine learning serves as a post-inversion analysis tool to improve the interpretation of ERT model results. When combined with detailed geological mapping, ERT provides a robust framework for investigating shallow fault architecture52-54.

Recent developments in data analytics have introduced machine learning (ML) as a powerful tool for enhancing geophysical data interpretation. ML algorithms identify hidden patterns in resistivity distributions in ERT data, thereby increasing interpretative consistency and reproducibility20,21. Among unsupervised ML methods, k-means clustering is effective for classifying geophysical datasets based on similarities in physical properties. This method partitions resistivity data into distinct groups, minimizing intra-cluster variance while maximizing inter-cluster contrast. Unlike fuzzy clustering, which may assign a data point a degree of membership across multiple clusters, k-means assigns each data point to a single cluster, producing clear, non-overlapping classes that are easier to interpret geologically22,23. Compared to alternative techniques, such as hierarchical, Gaussian mixture, or fuzzy clustering22–25, k-means offers an optimal balance of computational efficiency, reproducibility, and interpretive simplicity for geophysical datasets26,27. In this study, k-means clustering is employed as a post-inversion classification tool to segment continuous resistivity fields into geologically meaningful domains, enhancing the visualization of structural discontinuities such as faults and lithological contacts. To determine the optimal number of clusters, this study uses the Elbow method28,29, which evaluates the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) as a function of the number of clusters and identifies the optimal value at the point where additional clusters yield only marginal improvements in data representation.

Despite extensive geological and geospatial studies along the BBF trace30–33, high-resolution geophysical investigations integrated with machine learning techniques have not been explored. This study addresses this gap by combining geological field mapping, high-resolution ERT data acquisition, and a clustering algorithm to improve the interpretation of subsurface structures associated with the BBF. This study aims to fill this gap by combining geological field observations with high-resolution ERT surveys to improve the delineation of shallow fault-zone architecture at selected sites in the Azad Jammu and Kashmir region. An unsupervised clustering approach is used as a post-processing tool to enhance the interpretative consistency of the resistivity models. The primary objectives are: (1) to characterize surface and shallow subsurface geological features associated with the BBF, (2) to estimate the depth, geometry, and structural continuity of fault-related anomalies using high-resolution ERT data; and (3) to evaluate the effectiveness of k-means clustering-based segmentation in supporting structural interpretation of ERT tomograms.

The proposed framework provides a structured methodology for characterizing shallow fault-zone architecture in seismically active regions. It is important to note that the results are limited to the near-surface domain but do not resolve the deeper crustal-scale structure of the BBF. By combining ERT imaging with systematic resistivity-domain segmentation, this study enhances the clarity and consistency of structural interpretation, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of near-surface fault behavior relevant to seismic hazard assessment in tectonically active areas.

Methodology

This study employs an integrated interpretation framework that combines geological field surveys, Electrical Resistivity Tomography (ERT), and machine learning techniques to characterize the shallow structural architecture of the Balakot-Bagh fault (BBF). The following subsections outline the data acquisition, processing, and analysis methods used in this work.

ERT field procedure and data inversion

Electrical Resistivity Tomography (ERT) was used to image shallow subsurface structures related to the BBF and to complement geological observations at Sites S1 and S2. Three profiles were acquired: two 150 m-long transects at Site S1 (T1 and T2), oriented to intersect mapped surface ruptures, and one 300 m-long transect at Site S2 (T3), positioned across the inferred continuation of the fault (Fig. 3). All surveys employed a uniform inter-electrode spacing of a = 5 m, ensuring consistent lateral resolution across profiles. In the Wenner–Schlumberger quadrupole configuration, the separation factor n was progressively increased to enhance depth penetration. For T1 and T2 (31 electrodes), n ranged from 1 to 6, while for T3 (61 electrodes), n ranged from 1 to 12, allowing for greater depth of investigation along the longer profile. Data were collected using a Geomative GD-10 multi-electrode imaging system configured in the Wenner–Schlumberger array. This array was chosen for its balanced sensitivity to both vertical and horizontal resistivity variations and its effectiveness in resolving structurally complex fault-zone anomalies17,35,36. The acquisition parameters ensured adequate lateral coverage and penetration depth to characterize shallow deformation associated with the BBF.

Quality control measures were implemented during both data acquisition and processing. Multiple stacks were recorded to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio, and data points with relative errors exceeding 5% were removed before inversion. All inversions were performed using AGI EarthImager™ 2D v2.4.2. The inversion mesh used rectangular cells measuring half the electrode spacing (2.5 m × 2.5 m), resulting in a vertical-to-horizontal cell size ratio of 1:1. This configuration was chosen to balance model smoothness and spatial resolution while ensuring numerical stability. A smoothness-constrained least-squares inversion scheme based on the L2 norm was applied37,56. The objective function iteratively minimizes the difference between measured and modeled apparent resistivities subject to smoothness constraints. Damping and smoothing parameters were initially set to 50 and progressively reduced in subsequent iterations to enhance structural resolution while maintaining inversion stability. The final root-mean-square (RMS) errors were 6.0%, 4.8%, and 4.3% for profiles T1, T2, and T3, respectively. The higher RMS error in T1 is likely due to strong near-surface resistivity contrasts and localized surface topography. Significant elevation changes can distort current flow paths and introduce uncertainties in apparent resistivity measurements. Additionally, sharp lateral resistivity variations are not fully captured by the inversion algorithm, leading to localized data misfit38,39.

To evaluate model reliability and penetration depth, the Depth of Investigation (DOI) index is computed to distinguish regions influenced mainly by the reference model rather than by the measured data (Binley and Kemna 2005;40). Machine learning

To improve the interpretability of inverted ERT models and enhance the delineation of subsurface geological features, this study applies the k-means clustering algorithm, an unsupervised machine learning technique from the open-source Python library scikit-learn41. The inverted resistivity model is treated as a two-dimensional image, with pixel values representing resistivity variations. Clustering is performed as a pixel-based segmentation process42, grouping pixels into classes based on their intensity (i.e., resistivity) values. The algorithm partitions the inverted resistivity model into k clusters by grouping pixels with similar intensity values. Consistent with pixel-intensity-based segmentation approaches42, clustering is performed in the resistivity attribute space, without incorporating spatial coordinates as input variables.

The centroids are iteratively updated until convergence, yielding a partition that minimizes intra-cluster variance.

While k-means offers a computationally efficient and reproducible classification method, it has limitations, including sensitivity to initial centroid selection, dependence on the chosen number of clusters (k), and a lack of direct geological constraints. The algorithm is particularly sensitive to centroid initialization, as widely discussed in the clustering literature26,27,44. To evaluate stability and convergence, the algorithm was executed with multiple random initializations. In this study, the resulting cluster geometries and boundaries are consistent across runs, indicating stable convergence and limited sensitivity to initialization. Therefore, the clustering results are interpreted alongside geological observations and regional stratigraphic information rather than as independent geological evidence.

The Elbow method is used to determine the optimal number of clusters (k) in k-means clustering. It relies on the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS), defined as the sum of squared distances between each data point and its corresponding cluster centroid. The method evaluates how WCSS decreases as the number of clusters increases28,29. WCSS is calculated for a range of k values and plotted against k. As k increases, WCSS decreases because the data are partitioned into more refined clusters. However, beyond a certain point, the rate of decrease slows significantly. This transition point, where the curve shifts from a steep decline to a more gradual slope, is referred to as the "elbow." The elbow indicates the optimal number of clusters, balancing intra-cluster variance reduction with model simplicity. Selecting a larger k beyond this point yields only marginal improvement while increasing the risk of overfitting. For all transects (T1, T2, and T3), the elbow occurs at k = 5 (Fig. 4). This approach minimizes interpreter bias, ensures objective clustering of the ERT model, and enhances the reliability of the structural interpretations presented in Section "Results".

Results

Geological investigation

Geological investigations at Sar Pain (S1) and Naushahra (S2) provide crucial insights into the shallow subsurface structure of the BBF and near-surface deformation linked to the 2005 Mw 7.6 Kashmir earthquake (Fig. 4). At S1, well-preserved coseismic rupture strands were identified, displaying a consistent northwest-southeast strike that aligns with the local trace of the BBF (Table 1, Fig. 5). The fault scarps range from several meters to over 150 m in length, with vertical displacements between approximately 0.1 m and 3.0 m; the northeastern block is uplifted relative to the southwestern block3,31,34. Openings measured perpendicular to the strike range from a few centimeters to about 2.0 m in width (Fig. 5). A comparative analysis of field photographs from 2008 and 2023 shows progressive smoothing and partial degradation of the scarps due to erosion, sediment infill, and human activities. Structural observations at Sites S1 and S2 reveal pronounced variations in the expression and preservation of near-surface deformation along the BBF. Detailed structural mapping included measurements of fault orientation, dip, rupture length, fracture width, and vertical displacement. Fourteen rupture strands (F1–F14) were identified (Table 1), cutting through poorly sorted reddish fluvial-terrace deposits that unconformably overlie the Miocene Murree Formation. At S1, the mapped rupture strands form a narrow but continuous deformation zone characterized by steep northeast dips (70–86°) and predominantly normal-sense displacement within the hanging wall (Fig. 7). Vertical offsets range from several centimeters to approximately 3.0 m, with the largest displacement occurring along rupture F8, indicating localized zones of intense strain. These structures traverse fluvial terrace deposits overlying the Murree Formation, and their geometric coherence suggests that S1 occupies an actively deforming segment of the fault’s shallow damaged zone. In contrast, S2 lacks preserved surface ruptures, likely because a thick alluvial cover obscures subtle deformation within the floodplain. This absence, despite the site’s location along the inferred BBF trace, illustrates the strong influence of local geological and geomorphic conditions on concealing the surface manifestations of the deformation and hence the preservation of structural features in the shallow subsurface. At Site S2 (Naushahra), transect T3 was acquired in an area without visible surface rupture from the 2005 Kashmir earthquake. The profile trends northeast–southwest, nearly perpendicular to the inferred MBF trace. The inverted resistivity model (Fig. 10A) reveals a broad, well-defined zone with a depth of investigation (DOI) of ≤ 0.1 and moderate lateral resistivity variation. A shallow conductive layer in the upper few meters corresponds to alluvial heterogeneity and moisture variations within the Jhelum River floodplain, representing a non-tectonic near-surface effect. Beneath this layer, three distinct low-resistivity discontinuities appear at 80 m, 170 m, and 270 m along the profile. They likely represent fault-related structures composed of clay-rich or water-saturated fractured materials. Using k-means clustering, the subsurface is divided into five resistivity classes (Fig. 10B): Cluster 1 (< 200 Ω m) corresponds to saturated alluvium and fault fill; Cluster 2 (200–350 Ω m) represents coarse alluvium or low-clay fault material; Cluster 3 (350–800 Ω m) reflects highly weathered and fractured bedrock; Cluster 4 (800–1300 Ω m) denotes moderately fractured bedrock; and Cluster 5 (> 1300 Ω m) indicates slightly fractured bedrock. Two major structural features at 300 Ω m identified by Cluster 2 are interpreted as concealed fault strands. Adjacent higher-resistivity zones (> 550 Ω m) delineate the Murree Formation units. The geometry, lateral continuity, and depth persistence of these low-resistivity anomalies are consistent with the inferred BBF trace, supporting a tectonic interpretation. However, the clustered model shows locally reduced resolution compared to the inverted resistivity model. In particular, inclined fault-related features are partially represented as more horizontally continuous clusters (e.g., Cluster 2), especially in the southwestern portion of the profile. This effect is more pronounced in areas with lower data sensitivity (DOI > 0.1), where clustering may lack adequate resolution of the structures. As a result, some fault geometries appear less distinct in the clustered model. Therefore, structural interpretation in these areas requires integration with the original ERT model and supporting geological information. Discussion

Machine learning and geophysical interpretation

Resolving the shallow fault architecture of the BBF requires imaging distinct lateral and vertical resistivity contrasts linked to lithological variability, fracture density, and fluid saturation. Although smoothness-constrained ERT inversion captures the primary resistivity patterns, its inherent regularization tends to smooth abrupt structural boundaries. In this context, k-means clustering is applied as a post-inversion segmentation tool to enhance the structural interpretability of the resistivity models. By partitioning continuous resistivity gradients into discrete, consistent domains, clustering enhances the visibility of fault-controlled lithological and structural features. Specifically, it transforms the continuous resistivity field into discrete classes, enhancing boundary definition, reducing the influence of noise and minor resistivity fluctuations, and improving interpretability within a reproducible and data-driven framework.

The clustered models more clearly delineate subsurface boundaries and facilitate the identification of distinct lithological units. Fault structures are also more consistently resolved, as indicated by the interpreted fault traces (Figs. 8, 9, 10). Which closely correspond to resistivity contrasts and mapped geological features. This improvement arises from clustering’s ability to suppress minor variability while emphasizing dominant resistivity contrasts, thereby producing sharper and more interpretable boundaries. Clusters with low-resistivity correspond to mechanically weak, clay-rich, or water-saturated zones, whereas higher-resistivity clusters represent more competent bedrock units, including the Miocene Murree Formation. These contrasts likely influence strain localization and rupture propagation by acting as mechanical boundaries within the fault zone.

Despite these advantages, clustering may locally reduce resolution in areas with gradual resistivity transitions or complex structural geometries. For example, the inclined fault feature observed in Fig. 10B is not fully resolved in the clustered model and is represented as a more laterally continuous domain (e.g., Cluster 2), particularly in the southwestern portion of the profile. This simplification reflects the inherent behavior of k-means clustering, which groups pixels based on dominant resistivity values and may fail to capture localized variations. As a result, small-scale structural features or inclined geometries may appear less distinct. Therefore, the clustered model should be interpreted alongside the continuous ERT model and supporting geological constraints, particularly in heterogeneous or low-contrast zones.

A key advantage of k-means clustering is its ability to improve interpretability by automatically grouping resistivity values into geologically meaningful classes. Conventional interpretation may lead to inconsistencies; clustering reduces this variability and minimizes the impact of noise and minor resistivity fluctuations. However, as an unsupervised method, k-means clustering does not incorporate geological constraints, which may result in minor inconsistencies in areas affected by inversion uncertainty or weak resistivity contrasts. These effects are limited and do not significantly impact the overall structural interpretation. Across both S1 and S2, the clustered models consistently outperform conventional inversion in delineating fault cores, associated fracture networks, and lithological boundaries. The consistency of clustered domains across profiles, along with their alignment with resistivity profile curve-change points and mapped structural features, indicates that the identified boundaries represent genuine geological contrasts rather than clustering artifacts. Clustering should be regarded as a complementary interpretative tool rather than a standalone method, especially in areas of weak resistivity contrast or complex structural geometry. Integration with ERT models and independent geological information is essential for robust interpretation.

Although the lack of borehole data limits direct lithological validation, applying uniform clustering parameters across all profiles enhances interpretation and reduces sensitivity to minor variations. Regions with DOI values greater than 0.1 may introduce artifacts; these areas should be down-weighted during interpretation. The achieved depth of investigation constrains only the shallow portion of the fault system and does not resolve deeper structural geometry. While ERT and clustering results provide valuable insights into shallow fault architecture, integrating borehole data and complementary deep-penetration geophysical methods (e.g., seismic reflection and magnetotellurics) would further validate and refine the structural interpretation. Overall, the ERT–clustering approach provides a systematic and robust interpretation framework for identifying fault cores, damage zones, and lithological interfaces along the BBF.

Regional tectonic implications

Despite extensive research since the 2005 Mw 7.6 Kashmir earthquake, the subsurface structure of the Balakot-Bagh fault (BBF) remains poorly understood. The BBF is a northeast-dipping thrust that accommodates significant crustal shortening in the northwestern Himalaya and is a key structural component of the Hazara–Kashmir Syntaxis (HKS) (see dashed rectangle in Fig. 2). It branches from the western limb of the HKS and extends obliquely through its interior with a NW–SE trend along the Upper Jhelum River (Fig. 2). The fault tracks the northern bank of the river from Muzaffarabad, crosses it between Garhi and Chikar Khas, and continues along a small tributary toward Chikar Khas (Fig. 2). The BBF, dipping at 25°–50° NE, separates the Muzaffarabad and Murree formations on the western limb of the Muzaffarabad anticline and is exposed east of Muzaffarabad city4. The BBF is folded along the Neelum River before extending northwest toward Balakot, where it merges with the MBT and Panjal Thrust at the western limb of the HKS (Figs. 1, 2). Rather than a single sharp contact, the BBF forms a broad deformation zone, likely due to its passage through lithologically similar units (Fig. 5A–B). This region represents a major deformation node within the HKS, where the MBT bends sharply, linking the Himalayan frontal thrusts with hinterland shear zones. However, surface expressions of faulting, especially dip-slip motion, degrade quickly due to erosion, sedimentation, and human activity, often obscuring geomorphic indicators of active deformation.

At Site S1 (Sar Pain), field investigations document prominent NW–SE-trending surface ruptures with vertical displacements ranging from 0.1 to 3 m (Fig. 5), confirming the site’s alignment with the BBF. Comparative photographs show that scarps measuring 3.3 m and 1.4 m immediately after the earthquake had decreased to 3.0 m and 1.0 m by 2023, indicating progressive sediment infill and surface smoothing. In contrast, Site S2 (Naushahra), located farther southeast along the inferred fault trace, shows no visible surface rupture. Continuous alluvial deposition within the Jhelum River floodplain likely conceals subtle deformation. These contrasting expressions demonstrate that surface rupture alone is an unreliable indicator of fault activity, as sedimentation, erosion, and human modification can obscure geomorphic evidence of active tectonics.

ERT data from transects T1 and T2 at S1 reveal broad low-resistivity anomalies interpreted as fractured, water-saturated zones and fault gouge within the Murree Formation (Figs. 8 and 9). The geological and tectonic implications of the clustered models are reflected in distinct conductive and resistive anomalies. The conductive zones are interpreted as fault gouge, whereas the resistive anomalies correspond to the Murree Formation. These features are consistent with the mapped NW–SE-trending fracture system. These contrasts influence fault propagation and act as mechanical boundaries that control rupture termination and strain localization.

Field observations, inverted resistivity model, and clustered models indicate the coexistence of reverse faulting and secondary normal faulting in the hanging wall, consistent with earlier studies31. The BBF generates coseismic normal faults and fractures within the hanging wall45,46. These features align with elastic stress relaxation47 and bending-moment faulting mechanisms48, which produce near-surface extensional structures in the direction of the main thrust’s motion. At Site S2, the longer ERT transect (T3) reaches an investigation depth of approximately 60 m and reveals significant lateral and vertical resistivity contrasts. A distinct low-resistivity anomaly between 80 and 270 m (Fig. 10) corresponds to the inferred trace of the BBF, interpreted as a buried or blind fault segment. This discontinuity likely represents fractured, fluid-saturated bedrock. The absence of a corresponding surface rupture, despite the clear subsurface anomaly, suggests the presence of concealed structural features in this area. Subsurface fractures inferred from resistivity contrasts indicate structural weaknesses that could be reactivated in the future. Fault dips interpreted from clustered models (56–86° NE) align with field measurements at S1 (Table 1). Lithological and structural contrasts at S1 and S2 indicate that BBF-related structures influence fault reactivation potential and control the localization of deformation within the Murree Formation. Geological cross-sections (Fig. 4B) indicate that the BBF dips 25–50° NE and merges with the Main Himalayan Detachment at depth, confirming mechanical coupling between shallow deformation and the deeper regional thrust system. This structural behavior aligns with other blind thrust systems in convergent tectonic settings, particularly the 2011 Van earthquake. In such systems, deformation is distributed within the hanging wall, and surface rupture may be discontinuous or absent5,6. Seismological studies indicate that these zones are characterized by reduced seismic velocities, high attenuation, elevated Poisson’s ratio, and post-seismic fluid migration5–7. These characteristics align with the low-resistivity anomalies identified in this study, supporting the interpretation that they are fractured, fluid-saturated zones that facilitate strain localization within the hanging wall.

The integrated interpretation indicates that the BBF is a composite deformation system comprising both surface-rupturing and blind-fault segments. The models reveal structural continuity across regions with contrasting surface expressions, providing new insights into fault segmentation and strain distribution within the HKS. These findings enhance understanding of the BBF’s structural architecture linking localized rupture patterns to the broader Himalayan thrust architecture and offer important implications for seismic hazard assessment, land-use planning, and regional tectonic modeling in the northwestern Himalaya.

Conclusion

This study introduces a novel integrated interpretation framework that combines geological mapping, high-resolution electrical resistivity tomography (ERT) imaging, and k-means clustering to effectively characterize the near-surface structure of the BBF. Geological observations at Sar Pain (S1) document multiple NW–SE-trending coseismic rupture strands, while no surface rupture is preserved at Naushahra (S2) due to thick alluvial cover. ERT imaging at both sites delineates low-resistivity zones associated with fractured, water-saturated fault gouge, and higher-resistivity domains correspond to the Murree Formation.

K-means clustering is a post-inversion segmentation tool that enhances subsurface interpretation by converting continuous resistivity changes into discrete geological domains. It sharpens structural boundaries, reduces noise and minor resistivity fluctuations, improves interpretability, and provides a data-driven, reproducible classification framework. This leads to more consistent interpretations and clearer delineation of structural features. At S1, clustered resistivity domains correlate well with mapped surface fractures and the regional geometry of the BBF, indicating active deformation within the shallow damage zone. At S2, clustering identifies a distinct low-resistivity anomaly at depth, interpreted as a buried continuation of the fault. Collectively, these results indicate that the BBF comprises both surface-rupturing and blind segments, with deformation strongly influenced by lithology and sedimentary cover. The findings suggest that sections of the hanging-wall block may remain susceptible to renewed ground deformation during future seismic events.

From a regional tectonic perspective, the results support the interpretation of the BBF as a segmented thrust system exhibiting depth-dependent structural behavior and localized strain partitioning within the Hazara–Kashmir Syntaxis. Lithological heterogeneity controls rupture propagation, strain localization, and potential rupture termination, which are critical factors in evaluating future earthquake scenarios along the northwestern Himalayan front. The identification of buried or weakly expressed fault segments has significant implications for seismic hazard mitigation. Areas lacking visible rupture may still host active deformation capable of generating substantial ground displacement. Incorporating subsurface structural characterization into hazard assessments can enhance land-use planning, infrastructure resilience, and risk reduction strategies in densely populated regions of northern Pakistan. Overall, the integrated interpretation framework presented in this study provides a systematic, reproducible methodology for refining fault-zone models and improving seismic hazard assessment in tectonically complex regions worldwide.
