Assessment of stability and yield performance of onion (Allium cepa L.) genotypes across diverse Indian environments
Authors: Amar Jeet Gupta, Anil Khar, Kavya V Aribenchi, Supriya Kaldate, Hem Raj Bhandari, Pranjali A Gedam, Yogesh P Khade, Rajiv B Kale, Vijay Mahajan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOnion productivity in India is strongly influenced by genotype × environment interaction (GEI), complicating the identification of stable and high-yielding cultivars. Nineteen onion genotypes were evaluated in multi-environment trials across six diverse locations during two consecutive kharif seasons (2023–24), with locations treated as six distinct environments (E1-E6). Combined ANOVA revealed significant effects of genotypes, environments, and their interactions, indicating substantial GEI. AMMI analysis identified RO-1771 (G15), RO-1768 (G13), and RO-1774 (G17) as the most stable and high-yielding genotypes, while Junagadh (E3) was the most representative and discriminating environment. The first two principal components of the GGE biplot explained 83.86% of total GEI variation, efficiently capturing interaction patterns. Among the genotypes, ‘Bhima Dark Red’ (G18) consistently showed superior yield and stability, followed by RO-1768 and RO-1774, which also demonstrated broad adaptability. Junagadh was identified as an ideal site for both cultivation and varietal testing. Collectively, the GGE and AMMI analyses revealed that Bhima Dark Red (G18), RO-1768 (G13), and RO-1774 (G17) combined high yield with stability across multiple environments. These findings provide valuable guidance for onion breeding programs and support the development of cultivars adapted to diverse Indian agro-climatic conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30152-9.Keywords: Onion, GGE biplot, AMMI, Stability, GEI, Bulb yieldSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Onion (Allium cepa L.) is one of the most extensively grown and economically important vegetable crops worldwide1. In India, it is an integral part of daily cuisine and is often referred to as the ‘Queen of the Kitchen’2. The bulb is rich in bioactive compounds and serves as a potent antioxidant, with extensive use in both fresh consumption and food processing3,4. According to FAOSTAT5, India has recently emerged as the world’s largest onion producer, surpassing China, with an estimated output of 30.21 million tons from 1.74 million hectares. The growing demand for onion is driven by its nutritional value and health-promoting constituents, including essential minerals (K, P, Ca, Mg, Na, Fe) and vitamins from the B, C, and E groups6. Historically, onions have also played an important role in traditional medicine, being recognized for their therapeutic properties in treating fever, bronchitis, and cardiovascular ailments, as well as for their diuretic, digestive, and lipid-lowering effects6,7.

Despite its economic and nutritional significance, onion productivity in India remains highly variable across regions and seasons, primarily due to strong genotype × environment interaction (GEI) effects. With increasing demand and the challenges posed by climate change, the development of high-yielding, stable, and climate-resilient cultivars has become a critical breeding objective8. The success of onion improvement programs depends on identifying genotypes that maintain consistent performance across diverse environments. However, many genotypes show unpredictable yield stability, reflecting the complex interplay between genetic potential and environmental variability9–11.

To address this, breeders routinely conduct multi-environment trials (METs) across sites and years to evaluate genotype adaptability prior to cultivar release12,13. Reliable interpretation of MET data requires robust statistical tools capable of partitioning GEI and identifying stable genotypes14. Several multivariate approaches such as principal component analysis (PCA), the additive main effects and multiplicative interaction (AMMI) model, and the genotype main effect plus genotype-by-environment interaction (GGE) biplot have been employed for this purpose15,16. While AMMI effectively quantifies GEI and genotype stability, the GGE biplot model offers superior visual interpretation by simultaneously capturing genotype performance (G) and GEI effects, making it more useful for identifying broadly and specifically adapted genotypes15,16. Together, these models assist in visually identifying genotypes suited for general or specific adaptations.13.

In onion, only limited studies have used GGE biplot and AMMI analyses to define mega-environments and identify stable genotypes across diverse Indian locations17–19. Most available reports focus on yield performance or stability within specific agro-climatic zones, leaving a research gap in comprehensive multi-location evaluation using both AMMI and GGE modeling. Therefore, a more integrative assessment across representative environments is essential to guide breeding decisions and enhance cultivar adaptability.

With this background, the present study evaluated nineteen onion genotypes across six distinct locations in India during two consecutive kharif seasons using both the GGE and AMMI biplot approach. The objectives were to (i) identify genotypes combining high yield with broad adaptability (ii) quantify GEI and visualize genotype performance across environments, and (iii) delineate potential mega-environments to aid future breeding and testing strategies. By addressing a key gap in multi-environment onion evaluation, this study contributes to developing stable, high-yielding cultivars suited to India’s variable agro-climatic conditions, thereby supporting long-term productivity and food security.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

The study was conducted at six locations across India during two consecutive kharif seasons (2023–24). The same set of locations was used in both years to ensure comparability across environments. For analysis, these six locations were treated as six distinct environments, while the two years of testing enhanced the reliability and consistency of results. The experimental sites included the Regional Research Station of the National Horticulture Research and Development Foundation (RRS, NHRDF), Karnal; Chandra Shekhar Azad University of Agriculture and Technology (CSAUAT), Kanpur; Junagadh Agricultural University (JAU), Junagadh; ICAR-Directorate of Onion and Garlic Research (ICAR-DOGR), Pune; ICAR-Indian Institute of Horticultural Research (ICAR-IIHR), Bengaluru; and Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU), Coimbatore (Table 1). These sites represent diverse agro-ecological zones differing in soil type, temperature, rainfall pattern, and humidity, providing a robust test of genotype adaptability. Supplementary file S1 summarizes the seasonal weather patterns across these experimental sites.

Field trials were conducted at six locations following a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications per environment. All recommended agronomic practices were followed as per the ICAR-DOGR guidelines. Forty-five-day-old seedlings were transplanted on 1 × 1 m raised beds (15 cm height) with a spacing of 15 × 10 cm. Each replication comprised 70 plants arranged in seven rows. From each replication, five plants were randomly selected for statistical analysis, resulting in a total of 15 plants per genotype (5 plants × 3 replications).

Harvesting was done when more than 50% of plants showed neck-fall, indicating physiological maturity. Bulbs were field-cured for seven days to improve storability, after which tops were trimmed. Marketable yield (q ha⁻1) was estimated from the total weight of harvestable bulbs from all plants within each plot and expressed on a per-hectare basis. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 2025.05.1). Combined ANOVA across environments and stability parameter estimation were conducted using the metan package, including AMMI analysis for genotype × environment interaction. GGE biplots were constructed using the GGE biplot package. Post-hoc mean separation of genotypes was performed using Fisher’s LSD at a significance level of P < 0.05 in the agricolae package to identify statistically significant differences across environments. These standardized procedures ensured accurate interpretation and comparability of all graphical and statistical outputs.

Effect of season on bulb yield

Weather conditions varied across locations and months (Supplementary file S1). Minimum and maximum temperatures ranged from 17.41 to 27.03 °C and 27.72 to 38.40 °C, respectively, while relative humidity and rainfall also showed considerable variation. Seasonal fluctuations influenced onion growth and yield, with higher yields generally recorded under moderate temperature and adequate rainfall conditions (e.g., JAU, Junagadh and RRS, Karnal). Genotype × season interactions were observed, indicating differential performance of genotypes across the two kharif seasons.

GGE biplot analysis of genotype performance across environments

The GGE biplot (Fig. 1) based on PC1 and PC2, effectively illustrates the interaction patterns between genotypes and environments. These two components together accounted for 83.86% of the total variation associated with genotype and genotype-by-environment interaction, thereby capturing the majority of the meaningful variation. Within the GGE biplot, the proximity of each genotype relative to the origin serves as an indicator of its yield performance across the evaluated environments. Genotypes situated to the left of the origin specifically G1 (POS22K), G2 (POS23K), G3 (POS27K), G9 (RO-1744), G10 (RO-1748), G12 (RO-1767), G13 (RO-1768), and G18 (Bhima Dark Red) showed higher-than-average yield. Conversely, genotypes like G15 (RO-1771), G17 (RO-1774), and G19 (Bhima Super) displayed average performance, while the remaining genotypes are classified as low-yielding under the tested conditions.

Relationships among environments

In the GGE biplot (Fig. 3), each line radiating from the origin to a test site reflects an environmental vector. The angle between two vectors, through its cosine, provides an estimate of the environmental correlation. An acute angle between environments E1 and E3, as well as between E1 and E2, indicates a positive correlation, suggesting similar genotypic responses across these sites. In contrast, an obtuse angle between E1 and E6 indicates a negative correlation. Whereas approximate right angles between E1-E4 and E1-E5 suggest negligible or no correlation. Broadly, large obtuse angles between vectors are indicative of strong crossover genotype-by-environment interactions. In this study, the angle between E1 and E6 slightly exceeded 90°, pointing to a moderate level of genotype × environment interaction.

GGE biplot illustrating relationships among the test environments.

Discriminating and representative ability of test environments

In the GGE biplot (Fig. 4), the length of an environmental vector indicates its discriminating ability, with longer vectors showing a stronger capacity to distinguish among genotypes. Accordingly, environments such as E1, followed by E3 and E4, demonstrated the highest discriminating power, while E2 and E6 were the least effective in discriminating genotypic performance. The average environment axis (AEA) arrowhead shows the average of all test environments from the centre point. An environment’s vector angle relative to the AEA indicates how representative it is: smaller angles suggest greater similarity to the overall testing environment. In this case, E3 and E1 emerged as the most representative environments, whereas E6 was the least representative. Environments like E1 and E3, which combine strong discriminating ability with high representativeness, are considered ideal for recognizing genotypes with wide-ranging adaptability and stable performance.

The discriminativeness versus representativeness view of the GGE biplot.

Assessment of mean yield and stability in genotypes

The GGE biplot’s average environment coordination (AEC) view (Fig. 5) facilitated evaluation of onion genotypes for their average yield and stability, across different test environments. The AEC abscissa, which runs through the origin of the biplot, is generated using the mean scores of the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) of all environments, and represents the axis of increasing mean performance. Genotypes positioned further along this axis in the direction of the arrow demonstrate superior average yield. Based on this context, G12 (RO-1767) recorded the highest mean yield, followed by G18 (Bhima Dark Red), G17 (RO-1774), G2 (POS23K), and G13 (RO-1768). Genotypes such as G10 (RO-1748), G11 (RO-1752), and G9 (RO-1744) had yields close to the average mean, whereas G6 (RO-1739) exhibited the lowest average yield.

The average-environment coordination (AEC) view: Mean versus stability of the genotypes.

The ordinate of the AEC, drawn perpendicular to the mean performance axis, indicates genotype performance stability across varying conditions. Genotypes located nearer to this axis are considered more stable, while those situated farther away show greater genotype-by-environment interaction and hence, indicate lower stability. In this context, genotypes such as G13 (RO-1768), G15 (RO-1771), G16 (RO-1773), and G19 (Bhima Super) demonstrated higher stability. In contrast, G2 (POS23K), G9 (RO-1744), and G11 (RO-1752) were identified as less stable, due to their greater deviation from the AEC ordinate. Considering both mean yield and stability, genotypes such as G18 (Bhima Dark Red), G17 (RO-1774), and G13 (RO-1768) were identified as the most promising performers, exhibiting a desirable combination of high productivity and consistent performance across environments.

Ranking genotypes based on nearness to the ideal genotype

A genotype is considered ideal, if it combines superior mean performance with stability across varied environments. This ideal genotype is visually represented at the centre of the innermost concentric circle of the biplot (Fig. 6), with genotypes positioned nearer to this point considered more favourable for selection and breeding purposes. In the present study, genotype such as G18 (Bhima Dark Red) emerged as the most desirable, followed closely by G13 (RO-1768) and G17 (RO-1774), due to their proximity to the ideal genotype and their balanced performance and stability. Conversely, G6 (RO-1739), G16 (RO-1773), G5 (RO-1630), and G8 (RO-1742) were located furthest from the ideal genotype, showing decreased yield potential and reduced stability, and are therefore considered less suitable for selection.

Which-won-where

The "which-won-where" pattern of the GGE biplot (Fig. 8) was used to identify the best-performing genotypes across different environments. Lines drawn perpendicular from the origin to the edges of the polygon, referred to as equality lines, divide the biplot into different sectors. The genotypes at the vertices of this polygon namely G2 (POS23K), G6 (RO-1739), G9 (RO-1744), G11 (RO-1752), G12 (RO-1767), G16 (RO-1773), and G18 (Bhima Dark Red) represent those with either outstanding or poor performance in one or more environments. Perpendicular lines drawn from the origin to the sides of the polygon, known as equality lines, divide the biplot into distinct sectors. These sectors, help to illustrate genotype performance within particular environment. In this study, the six test environments were divided into three sectors. Genotype such as G2 (POS23K) showed the highest performance in environment E1, while G11 (RO-1752) was superior in E6. The remaining environments (E2, E3, E4, and E5) were grouped within a sector where G18 (Bhima Dark Red) emerged as the best performer. These results indicate the possible existence of three mega-environments under the tested conditions. Conversely, G6 (RO-1739), G9 (RO-1744), G12 (RO-1767), and G16 (RO-1773) showed no association with any environment-specific sector, indicating persistently suboptimal performance throughout all locations and limited adaptation.

The which-won-where view of the GGE biplot.

Comparative analysis of two contrasting genotypes

In the GGE biplot, two genotypes can be evaluated by joining them with a straight line, while a perpendicular dropped from the origin to this line serves as the reference for evaluating their relative performance. Such a perpendicular is called the equality line and signifies that both genotypes are expected to show similar performance in all environments positioned along it. In Fig. 9, two genotypes are highlighted with points and connected by a straight line. The figure shows that all test environments are positioned on the side of genotype G18 (Bhima Dark Red), suggesting its overall superior performance across these locations. In contrast, no environment lies on the side of G16 (RO-1773), suggesting that this genotype did not perform well in any of the tested environments.

AMMI1 analysis

The AMMI1 biplot (Supplementary file S3), which presents PC1 on the vertical axis, illustrates the magnitude of G × E interaction. Genotypes or environments positioned close to the zero point on PC1 are considered less influenced by interaction effects. Among environments, E3 showed minimal interaction, while genotypes G15 (RO-1771), G13 (RO-1768) and G17 (RO-1774) exhibited stable performance with PC1 scores near zero, reflecting their low sensitivity to environmental changes.

AMMI2 analysis

The AMMI2 biplot (Supplementary file S3), which integrates both PC1 and PC2, provided a more detailed depiction of genotype stability and G × E interactions. Together, PC1 and PC2 accounted for 85.1% of the total variation. Genotypes and environments located near the origin where both PC1 and PC2 values are close to zero were identified as stable due to their limited interaction with environmental variability. Environments with shorter vector lengths, such as E3, showed weaker interaction effects and are therefore ideal for selecting stable genotypes. Similarly, genotypes G15 (RO-1771), G10 (RO-1748), and G13 (RO-1768) were positioned near the origin, indicating consistent performance across environments and their potential suitability for breeding programs focused on yield stability.

AMMI stability value (ASV)

A lower ASV value indicates greater stability of marketable yield across locations, while higher ASV values suggest reduced consistency in performance17. Based on AMMI Stability Value (ASV) analysis, genotypes G15 (RO-1771), G13 (RO-1768), G19 (Bhima Super), G10 (RO-1748), G7 (RO-1740), G4 (RO-1628), G17 (RO-1774), and G18 (Bhima Dark Red) exhibited the highest stability across the six environments, as reflected by their low ASV scores (Table 6). In contrast, G2 (POS23K), G9 (RO-1744), G6 (RO-1739), G3 (POSK27K), and G12 (RO-1767) were the least stable, having higher ASV values.

Discussion

The successful release of a cultivar for large-scale cultivation depends largely on its ability to perform consistently and adapt effectively across diverse environmental conditions. However, genotype × environment interaction (GEI) often complicates breeding goals by causing location-dependent variation in genotype performance. Managing GEI remains a critical challenge for plant breeders, geneticists, and agronomists involved in evaluating crop performance12,23. To address this, multi-location trials (METs) are indispensable in plant breeding programs, as they provide valuable insights into how genotypes respond to varying environmental conditions16. Among the available analytical tools, the GGE and AMMI biplot methodologies are particularly powerful for analyzing MET data and effectively interpreting complex GEI patterns14,24.

In the present study, 19 onion genotypes were evaluated through field trials conducted across six agro-ecological locations over two years to assess their performance and environmental adaptability. As GEI can significantly influence yield performance, understanding its magnitude and pattern is crucial for identifying consistent and high-yielding genotypes19,25. Combined ANOVA from both GGE and AMMI analyses revealed significant GEI effects (P < 0.05) for marketable yield among the tested genotypes (Tables 4 and 5). Given that no single genotype is likely to perform optimally across all environments, this pronounced GEI emphasizes the need for developing genotypes with stable and reliable performance under diverse conditions. Thus, the identification and recommendation of region-specific genotypes remain key to achieving maximum productivity. These observations are consistent with those of Hagos and Abay26. Similar findings have been reported in multi-environment trials involving winter lentils27, wheat28, and grain sorghum29, all of which demonstrated notable GEI effects.

Heatmap analysis effectively summarized yield performance of the onion genotypes (G1-G19) across environments during the kharif season (Fig. 2), highlighting distinct variations in yield response patterns. By integrating statistical insights with visual representation, the heatmap facilitated interpretation of complex GEI structures and enabled the identification of genotypes exhibiting broad adaptability and high productivity. Comparable applications of heatmap-based visualization have been reported in sweet corn30, wheat31, and cotton32. Consistent with these reports, the present findings reaffirm the importance of accounting for G × E interactions in onion breeding to develop resilient cultivars with stable yields under fluctuating environments. Seasonal variations in temperature, humidity, and rainfall significantly influenced onion growth and yield across test locations. Environments with moderate temperatures and adequate rainfall, such as Junagadh (E3), supported superior bulb development and higher productivity, whereas hotter or drier sites limited yield performance. Despite these environmental fluctuations, the genotypes Bhima Dark Red (G18), RO-1768 (G13), and RO-1774 (G17) exhibited consistent performance, indicating their wide adaptability across diverse conditions. Similar findings have been reported by Li et al.33 and Kunjaroenruk et al.34 where genotype performance was strongly influenced by environmental and seasonal factors. These observations emphasize the importance of evaluating genotypes across diverse environments to identify stable and high-yielding cultivars.

In the GGE biplot, obtuse angles between environmental vectors indicate crossover GEI, where genotype rankings differ across locations. In this study, moderate crossover interactions were observed, whereas closer vectors reflected consistent ranking patterns (Fig. 3). The coexistence of crossover and non-crossover GEI, commonly reported in MET studies, highlights differential genotypic responsiveness and stability24,25. The GGE biplot also provides valuable insights into the discriminating power and representativeness of environments. Concentric circles in the biplot serve as visual references, where the vector length represents the discriminatory ability of each environment. In this study, RRS, Karnal (E1) and JAU, Junagadh (E3) exhibited both strong discriminating power and high representativeness (Fig. 4), similar to the findings of Glaz & Kang35 and Oyekunle et al.36 who emphasized that environments combining these two attributes are most informative for MET analyses.

Stability in a cultivar refers to its ability to maintain consistent phenotypic performance across diverse conditions, reflecting uniform productivity under varying environments37. Graphical representation of GEI through biplots allows for straightforward identification of both ideal genotypes and representative environments. A truly stable genotype is characterized by high mean yield coupled with minimal GEI effects38. In this study (Fig. 5), Bhima Dark Red (G18) emerged as the most promising genotype, followed by RO-1768 (G13) and RO-1774 (G17), based on mean performance and stability. These genotypes demonstrated superior adaptability and are recommended for cultivation across the tested environments. Similar approaches have been applied in maize39, chickpea40, and sorghum41 for selecting top-performing and stable genotypes.

According to Yang et al.42, an ideal test environment should exhibit a high PC1 value (strong discrimination among genotypes) and a PC2 value close to zero (high representativeness). In the present study (Fig. 7), JAU, Junagadh was identified as the most desirable testing site, effectively distinguishing bulb yield differences among genotypes. Locations such as JAU, Junagadh, RRS, Karnal, and ICAR-DOGR, Pune were positioned near the ideal environment, indicating their suitability for onion production. Genotypes Bhima Dark Red (G18), RO-1768 (G13), and RO-1774 (G17) exhibited superior yields and stability across these regions. Similar observations were made by Kocaturk et al.43, who used the ideal environment concept to identify favorable sites for genotype evaluation.

A major strength of the GGE biplot approach lies in its ability to visually interpret the “which-won-where” pattern in GEI, allowing identification of superior genotypes within specific environments and delineation of mega-environments24,44. In the present study, three distinct mega-environments were identified across six test sites (Fig. 8). POS23K (G2) performed best at RRS, Karnal (E1), while RO-1752 (G11) excelled at TNAU, Coimbatore (E6). Bhima Dark Red (G18) consistently achieved high yields across the remaining locations, indicating broad adaptability. However, since this delineation was based on two years of data, extended multi-year evaluations are required to confirm the stability of these mega-environment groupings. Similar GGE-based mega-environment delineations have been reported for sorghum45, cowpea46, spring barley47, field pea48, and wheat49. The “which-won-where” analysis further identified seven vertex genotypes, representing the most responsive (either superior or inferior) performers across environments. Among these, POS23K (G2) and RO-1752 (G11) exhibited environment-specific superiority, while Bhima Dark Red (G18) remained the top performer across multiple locations, reaffirming its wide adaptability. RO-1767 (G12) recorded the highest average yield, whereas RO-1752 (G11) and RO-1744 (G9) produced yields close to the grand mean, reflecting moderate stability. In contrast, RO-1739 (G6) and RO-1773 (G16) yielded the lowest outputs, suggesting poor adaptability and limited cultivation suitability. Similar findings have been reported by Yihunie and Gesesse48 and Akter et al.50 in field pea and rice, respectively.

Complementing the GGE analysis, the AMMI model was employed to assess GEI and genotype stability (Supplementary file S3). While the GGE biplot effectively identifies “which-won-where” patterns and defines mega-environments, the AMMI model statistically partitions additive and multiplicative effects, enhancing precision in stability estimation51,52. Environments represented by shorter vectors and located near the origin are considered more dependable for identifying broadly adapted genotypes53. Based on AMMI analysis, RO-1771 (G15), RO-1768 (G13), and RO-1774 (G17), along with E3 (JAU, Junagadh), were identified as the most stable and productive combinations. Comparable AMMI-based findings have been documented in groundnut28 and wheat29.

Collectively, the GGE and AMMI analyses revealed that Bhima Dark Red (G18), RO-1768 (G13), and RO-1774 (G17) combined high yield with stability across multiple environments (Fig. 10). These genotypes are, therefore, suitable for kharif season cultivation. Beyond identifying high-yielding lines, their adaptability also makes them promising parental lines for hybridization programs aimed at enhancing productivity and resilience. Furthermore, delineation of mega-environments provides breeders with practical guidance for region-specific varietal selection and trial planning, optimizing resource allocation and accelerating cultivar release. These insights hold direct implications for breeding strategies, seed production, and regional policy, ensuring consistent onion supply and farmer profitability under variable agro-climatic conditions.

The study identified stable and high-yielding onion genotypes across six tested environments, suitable for kharif season cultivation.

Although this study effectively identified yield and stability variation among onion genotypes across diverse Indian environments, it primarily relied on agro-morphological evaluations. Consequently, mechanistic understanding of the observed variability remains limited. Future studies integrating molecular and genomic tools such as SNP- or SSR-based genotyping, QTL mapping, and transcriptomic profiling are essential to elucidate the genetic basis of yield stability and adaptability. Such integrative approaches would not only validate the phenotypic trends observed here but also accelerate marker-assisted selection and the development of climate-resilient onion cultivars.

Conclusion

The present study highlights the significant effects of genotype, environment, and genotype × environment interaction (GEI) on the marketable yield of onion, emphasizing the critical importance of multi-location evaluation for accurate varietal selection. Among the 19 genotypes tested across six environments, Bhima Dark Red (G18), RO-1768 (G13), and RO-1774 (G17) consistently demonstrated superior yield performance, stability, and broad adaptability, identifying them as promising candidates for cultivation and breeding programs. Among the test sites, JAU, Junagadh emerged as the most representative and discriminating environment, followed by RRS, Karnal, and ICAR-DOGR, Pune, confirming their suitability as key locations for onion evaluation and production. These findings underscore the value of incorporating these stable and high-yielding genotypes as parental lines in hybridization programs aimed at enhancing yield stability and environmental resilience. Furthermore, the results have broader relevance beyond the study regions, as the identified genotypes can serve as valuable resources in other onion-growing areas with similar agro-climatic conditions. The methodological framework and findings provide a practical model for assessing GEI effects and guiding onion breeding programs toward stable and high-performing cultivars. Future research integrating molecular markers, genomic selection, and transcriptomic profiling with multi-environment testing will further elucidate the genetic mechanisms underlying yield stability and adaptability, enhancing the precision of marker-assisted selection and facilitating the development of climate-resilient onion cultivars.
