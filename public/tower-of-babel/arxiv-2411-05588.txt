An Evidence-Based Curriculum Initiative for Hardware Reverse Engineering Education
René Walendy, Markus Weber, Steffen Becker, Christof Paar, Nikol Rummel

Abstract
. The increasing importance of supply chain security for digital devices—from consumer electronics to critical infrastructure—has created a high demand for skilled cybersecurity experts. These experts use HRE ( HRE ) as a crucial technique to ensure trust in digital semiconductors. Recently, the US and EU have provided substantial funding to educate this cybersecurity-ready semiconductor workforce, but success depends on the widespread availability of academic training programs. In this paper, we investigate the current state of education in hardware security and HRE to identify efficient approaches for establishing effective HRE training programs. Through a systematic literature review, we uncover 13 relevant courses, including eight with accompanying academic publications. We identify common topics, threat models, key pedagogical features, and course evaluation methods. We find that most hardware security courses do not prioritize HRE , making HRE training scarce. While the predominant course structure of lectures paired with hands-on projects appears to be largely effective, we observe a lack of standardized evaluation methods and limited reliability of student self-assessment surveys. Our results suggest several possible improvements to HRE education and offer recommendations for developing new training courses. We advocate for the integration of HRE education into curriculum guidelines to meet the growing societal and industry demand for HRE experts.

1.
Introduction

Digital microelectronics in the form of
IC
(
IC
) have become ubiquitous in today’s digital society.
To be able to trust the various systems we interact with every day, it is essential that not only their software, but even more so the underlying hardware, is trustworthy.
Assuring trust in hardware, however, is a major challenge:
Most
IC
are not manufactured domestically, making the global semiconductor supply chain a lucrative target for insider and outsider attackers seeking to compromise hardware reliability and integrity
(
Bhunia et al., 2014
)
, or gain unfair economic advantage
(
Guin et al., 2014
)
.
Such hardware vulnerabilities and manipulations pose a serious threat to the privacy and security of both individuals and entire industries.

The US and EU have recently begun to address this concern through massive regulatory efforts
(
Rep. Ryan, 2022
;
European Parliament and Council, 2023
)
, calling for urgent measures and providing substantial funding to ramp up educational programs for a cybersecurity-ready semiconductor workforce.
The EU and US semiconductor industries require highly skilled hardware security experts to achieve two major goals:

(1)

in the long term, to increase the domestic production of trustworthy semiconductors

(2)

in the medium term, to validate designs and parts procured through the global
IC
supply chain

HRE
(
HRE
) is an important technique particularly for the latter goal.
As it can be used to both protect and attack
IC

(
Quadir et al., 2017
;
Wallat et al., 2017
)
, making
HRE
knowledge more available to defenders is important in the arms race against resourceful and often state-sponsored attackers.
Despite strong industry demand, there exist few dedicated hardware security courses at academic institutions worldwide
(
Cabaj et al., 2018
)
.
Many of the major courses in the field focus primarily on side-channel protection measures.
Thus, we see a need for courses more comprehensively covering the
HRE
skills required for supply chain validation.

Survey on
HRE
Education

To empirically assess the current demand for educational programs in
HRE
, we conducted anonymous surveys at two leading events in the field: the academically focused HARRIS workshop and the industry-oriented Hardwear.io conference.
We collected 68 responses with participant experience ranging from none (3 participants) over up to three years (30), 4-6 years (10), 7-9 years (2), to at least ten years (18).
Five participants did not report years of experience.
Participants rated the importance of four learning environments for acquiring expertise on a Likert scale (1=not important, 5=very important).
Independent learning was rated as most important (
mean=4.07,SD=1.01
), followed by on-the-job learning (
mean=3.79,SD=1.45
), workshops (
mean=3.38,SD=1.12
), and academia (
mean=2.91,SD=1.28
).
Participants also rated four factors affecting the difficulty of entering the
HRE
field (1=not at all, 5=extremely).
The greatest hurdle was the unavailability of mentors (
mean=3.51,SD=1.2)
, followed by the lack of affordable tooling (
mean=3.19,SD=1.33
) and educational programs (
mean=3.17,SD=1.3
).

It is therefore worthwhile for academic institutions to broaden their cybersecurity curricula.
Specifically, incorporating dedicated
HRE
training into more programs will better prepare the next generation of hardware security professionals to meet industry demand.
To this end, instructors would benefit from more thorough research into the optimal structure for academic
HRE
courses, expanding on the presently few recommendations in prominent curriculum guidelines
(
Joint Task Force on Cybersecurity Education, 2018
)
.
Identifying limiting factors and ways to overcome them can further support the setup of new
HRE
courses.

Research Questions

With the overarching goal of identifying efficient approaches for establishing
HRE
training programs to meet the demand for a cybersecurity-ready domestic semiconductor workforce, we answer the following research questions:

RQ1a:

What are the main contents of existing hardware security courses? Do they comprise
HRE
?

RQ1b:

What are the typical pedagogical features of existing hardware security courses?

RQ2a:

What evidence is there on their effectiveness in teaching knowledge of and skills for
HRE
?

RQ2b:

Are the typical methods for evaluating course effectiveness adequate?

2.
Background

2.1.
The Need for
HRE
Education

Computer security research has long focused on
software
security, treating the underlying hardware that executes it as an inherently trustworthy environment.
With the discovery of hardware-level vulnerabilities such as Spectre
(
Kocher et al., 2020
)
in 2019, cybersecurity researchers more and more have come to realize: Any (software) system can only ever be as secure as the hardware running it
(
Bloom et al., 2012
)
.

2.1.1.
The US CHIPS and Science Act and the European Chips Act

As the problem of secure hardware at the
IC
level arises during production, it cannot be solved by widespread end user education.
Instead, highly skilled experts trained in hardware verification are urgently needed to thwart attacks on the complex
IC
supply chains and maintain the digital sovereignty of both the EU and the US.

Both governments have recently launched large-scale initiatives to this end, namely the European Chips Act of 2023
(
European Parliament and Council, 2023
)
and the US CHIPS Act of 2022
(
Rep. Ryan, 2022
)
.
Either aims to secure the semiconductor supply through massive investment in domestic—hence independent—manufacturing and testing capabilities, with a projected workforce growth of more than one million additional workers until 2030
(
Weisz et al., 2022
)
.

2.1.2.
Hardware Reverse Engineering for Security Assurance

A primary technique in hardware verification is
HRE
(
HRE
), the
“act of creating a set of specifications for a piece of hardware by someone other than the original designers, primarily based upon analyzing and dimensioning a specimen.”

(
Rekoff, 1985
)

First, it supports large-scale hardware verification
(
Tehranipoor and Koushanfar, 2010
)
, which often works by comparing the product to an exhaustively validated
golden model
.

HRE
can prove this model to be genuine and free of malicious manipulations
(
Bhunia et al., 2014
;
Puschner et al., 2023
)
, commonly called hardware Trojans
(
Becker and Paar, 2019
)
.
Second,
HRE
allows hardware security analysts to discover new vulnerabilities and previously unseen or particularly subtle types of tampering that may elude other verification techniques aimed at detecting signatures of well-known attacks
(
Ludwig et al., 2021
;
Lippmann et al., 2023
)
.
Thus,
HRE
helps to gain an advantage in the hardware security arms race.

2.2.
Curriculum Guidelines for
HRE
Education

While both the European Chips Act and US CHIPS Act provide extensive funding and frameworks for educational programs
(
Rep. Ryan, 2022
, Sec. 10318)
,
(
European Parliament and Council, 2023
, Operational Objective 4)
, various scholars have warned that the current state of security education is inadequate to meet the industry demand for a cybersecurity-ready workforce
(
Cabaj et al., 2018
;
Goupil et al., 2022
;
Blažič, 2021
)
.
This systematic shortage appears to be even more pronounced in the
HRE
subfield
(
Cohen et al., 2018
)
, which we argue is partly caused by a lack of actionable curriculum guidelines.
For instance, the just-released iteration of the joint ACM and IEEE curriculum guideline for computer science
(
Joint Task Force on Computing Curricula, 2024
)
makes no provision for
HRE
-specific content—despite mentioning the term
“security”
more than 400, and
“hardware”
more than 100 times.
The 2017 guideline for post-secondary cybersecurity education
(
Joint Task Force on Cybersecurity Education, 2018
)
does call for the inclusion of both
HRE
techniques and countermeasures as well as supply chain security in the
“Component Security”
knowledge area.
However, it does not provide any suggestions for course packaging.
Other guidelines still consider hardware assurance as an optional knowledge area, despite pressing concerns from the US Department of Defense
(
Cohen et al., 2018
)
.
In this paper we analyze to which extent
HRE
education has proliferated and how it could benefit from specific curriculum guidance.

3.
Methods

We conducted a systematic literature review

HRE

We chose this approach over a web search for course listings with the goal of replicability in mind, rather than out of any consideration of course quality.

3.1.
Sampling Procedure

We queried the electronic literature database Semantic Scholar using its Academic Graph bulk search interface
(
Kinney et al., 2023
)
on May 1, 2024.
We chose Semantic Scholar as the single search provider because it aggregates publications from numerous literature databases under a single, well-defined search interface.
It covers major publishers in computer science and computer science education, such as the ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, DBLP, and Wiley Online Library.
It also includes gray literature through the arXiv preprint library.

In our initial corpus, we included any result that matched the following search string in its title or abstract, with no limit on publication date:

⬇

("

reverse

engineering
"

|

(

|

)

We decided to include the broader term
“hardware security”
as an alternative to
“hardware reverse engineering”
in order to discover related courses that may still offer limited
HRE
content.

3.2.
Selection Process

We manually screened the 66 results in two sifts, each under the following inclusion criteria:

•

Full research papers written in English

•

Works presenting full-semester, for-credit academic courses

We further applied the following exclusion criteria:

•

Results not pertaining to an individual academic research publication, such as journal editorials

•

Courses not primarily about hardware security or
HRE

For the first sift, we considered title and abstract only, leaving 20 publications.
For the second sift, we read these papers in full and re-evaluated the above criteria, resulting in a collection of nine publications.
To ensure broad coverage, we repeated our search using a second well-known database, Web of Science, on November 4, 2024.
This second search yielded no additional results after the first sift.

3.3.
Collection of Course Materials

For all the courses described in the publications, we obtained publicly available materials through the teaching websites of the respective institutions and the personal websites of the instructors involved.
Where material was no longer publicly available, we used the Internet Archive
2
2

2

https://web.archive.org/
to obtain historical versions where possible.

We made the best effort to add related courses
(
Farahmandi, 2019
;
Forte, [n. d.]
;
Tehranipoor, 2012
;
Tehranipoor, 2019
)
taught at the same institution to our corpus, even when they were not covered by a scholarly publication.
This approach allows us to better gauge the breadth of
HRE
and hardware security education at a given institution.

During collection of courses, we made one notable exception to our strategy:
Through discussions with instructors in the field, we became aware of an
HRE
course developed at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute.
While there is no formal publication on this course, most of the course materials are freely available online.
Since data on
HRE
courses is scarce, we decided to still include this course, resulting in a corpus of 13 academic courses from nine institutions.

3.4.
Analysis

We identified the main topics and structure of each course, as well as the materials used in the respective lectures and assignments, from syllabi and lecture slides to the extent available, and from the corresponding publications otherwise.
Timelines were extracted either from the publications or from historical versions of the course websites.
From this data, we distilled a set of major threat models considered in the courses.
We also identified all means of didactic evaluation described in the corresponding publications, as well as the key findings of these evaluations.

We derive the distinction between these types of courses and analyze the resource requirements for establishing similar courses at other institutions.

4.3.
Common Threat Models: Novel threats and attribution are overlooked

The ten hardware security courses without a strong focus on
HRE
address the following prevalent threat models:

Cryptographic Secret Extraction.:

Using side channels, an attacker with physical access to a device wants to extract a secret value that is being processed or stored on the device. Students may be in the role of either the attacker or the hardware designer implementing side-channel countermeasures.

Hardware Trojans.:

A malicious modification changes a circuit’s functionality to the advantage of the attacker. Students put themselves in the shoes of the attacker or the
OEM
(
OEM
) at the end of the supply chain
who must detect tampered
IC
before they are deployed.

IP
Infringement.:

An unauthorized party discloses, i. e.,
“pirates”
, the design files or manufactures and sells counterfeits to a third party.
Students can play two different defensive roles: either role similar to that of the Trojan case,
detecting
counterfeit
IC
, or the role of a hardware designer aiming to prevent the
production
of counterfeits by incorporating protection mechanisms into the design.

We find that within these threat models, the courses focus on design-time countermeasures and non-destructive analysis looking for known signatures of attacks.
They can therefore be complemented well with
HRE
-specific courses, as
HRE
is a powerful tool for detecting new or particularly subtle manipulations that would otherwise evade detection.
In addition, when tampering is found,
HRE
allows for a deeper understanding of how and where the tamper occurred – e. g., at the design, manufacturing, or packaging stage – enabling the removal of bad actors from the supply chain.

4.4.
Educational Approaches

Having covered
which

how

4.4.1.
Course structure: Combination of lectures with applied projects

All courses in our corpus have a strong focus on hands-on elements, and all except
C8

The specifics of the hands-on part vary from course to course:
Many include multiple graded assignments (
C6
,
C7
,
C12
), a longer final project (
C1
,
C2
), or both.
Another common feature are regular in-person lab sessions (
C2
,
C5
,
C7
,
C8
,
C9
,
C10
).
In
C10
and
C5
, students have access to an extensive hardware inspection laboratory.
However, the two courses differ in their approach to interactivity: While
C10
allows students to use the equipment on their own, the lab sessions of
C5
are designated as
“demonstration”
.
This suggests that the instructor performed the experiment while the students observed.

4.4.2.
Materials: Wide variation in type and cost

While several courses use primarily digital environments for their labs and assignments (
C1
,
C4
,
C7
), others use commercial devices (
C11
,
C13
), and
C8
utilizes custom hardware developed for the course.

Two publications stood out in our analysis:
Karam et al.
(
Karam et al., 2022
)
make a strong case for
“accessible”
hardware security training and designed the labs for
C13
around low-cost hardware which students can take home, supported by open-source software.
True et al.
(
True and Asadizanjani, 2023
)
, in stark contrast, showcase the extensive selection of high-cost on-site laboratory equipment used in
C10
.
Replicating this type of course format at other institutions would require extensive lead time, skilled laboratory equipment operators, and may be prohibitively costly for many institutions.

Hardware simulations are commonly used, especially when physical experiments would be too costly.
For instance, many of the courses in our analysis cover the attacker’s perspective of hardware Trojans, i. e., Trojan insertion (
C3
,
C9
,
C13
).
Naturally, none of them go as far as physically manufacturing a trojanized
IC
.

Two courses (
C8
,
C7
) have found a middle ground that allows students to tangibly experience the impact of design manipulation:
By deploying a manipulated design on reconfigurable hardware, students can easily test and iterate their implementation.

5.
Discussion and Recommendations

Below, we discuss our findings on the content, pedagogical features, effectiveness, and assessment methods of existing hardware security and
HRE
courses based on the research questions outlined in
Section 1
.
From these findings, we derive initial recommendations for efficiently implementing
HRE
training programs.

5.1.
RQ1: Contents and Pedagogical Features

Courses focus on non- or semi-invasive attacks and defenses, less on
HRE

Few hardware security courses include (invasive)
HRE
as a core topic, despite its inclusion in the current cybersecurity curriculum guidelines
(
Joint Task Force on Cybersecurity Education, 2018
)
.
Most focus on non- or semi-invasive attacks and defenses, primarily
SCA
(
SCA
), hardware Trojans,
IP
(
IP
) protection and counterfeits, and fault attacks.
They further convey theoretical knowledge of both
IC
manufacturing and cybersecurity fundamentals.

The three courses primarily covering
HRE
do so with varying focus;
two emphasize physical aspects, such as
PCB
(
PCB
) reverse engineering, destructive microscope imaging of
IC
(
IC
), and subsequent image processing;
the third primarily covers the analysis of logic circuits, or netlists.

Courses combine lectures with hands-on projects using a variety of materials

All 13 courses place a strong emphasis on practical experience, whether through projects, in-person lab sessions, or technical demonstrations by an instructor.
Hands-on assignments are commonly used to evaluate student performance and account for a large portion of the final grade.
Almost all the courses further combine the practical projects with a lecture series.

The materials chosen to engage students vary widely, as does the cost of those materials.
Some courses primarily use digital environments including hardware simulations.
Others use off-the-shelf or custom hardware, and commercially available laboratory equipment; the latter two requiring the most financial and human resources.
Several instructors make a deliberate choice to use low-cost, portable hardware to increase student access to hardware security education by lowering the financial entry barrier.

Recommendations:

We call for action to develop
HRE
courses for inclusion in the curricula of existing cybersecurity programs.
This will require empirically assessing the demand of various stakeholders such as industry, politics, society, and academia.
Subsequently, curriculum guidelines should be enriched with concrete suggestions for effective course packaging for the various topics in
HRE
.

Based on the findings of this review, we offer the following initial suggestions:
Institutions should start by using digital environments and simulated hardware for cost-effective lab sessions.
As budgets allow, incorporating commercially available hardware can enhance hands-on learning, covering concepts such as
PCB
reverse engineering or circuit manipulation based on
FPGA
.
Project design should vary over the length of the course:
Beginner projects should be scaffolded
(
Collins et al., 1988
;
Reiser and Tabak, 2014
)
to guide students step-by-step through novel problems, while later projects should provide less guidance, simulating real-world
HRE
settings.
This approach allows students to develop the competencies needed to perform
HRE
independently, such as navigating obscure documentation and a high tolerance for frustration.

Instructors of
HRE
training programs that primarily use digital environments and simulations should consider making their course contents available online.
Given the limited number of existing
HRE
courses, this would be a valuable step in rapidly expanding access to a larger student body.

5.2.
RQ2: Evaluating Course Effectiveness and Assessment Methods

Limited available evaluation suggests effectiveness, but high workload may hamper learning

Evaluation results indicate that the courses are effective in imparting hardware security and
HRE
knowledge and skills.
The combination of theoretical lectures with practical exercises and projects receives positive feedback in formal
QA
(
QA
) surveys.
Students report substantial learning gains, suggesting the efficacy of this instructional approach.
Grading data show that students generally achieve good or excellent final grades, indicating successful project completion.
However, concerns arise regarding the perceived high workload.
This workload could impose excessive cognitive load and stress on students, potentially compromising overall learning outcomes.

Comprehensive evaluation requires methods beyond student self-assessment

Most hardware security courses are not thoroughly evaluated for learning outcomes beyond relying on student self-reporting.
Such student self-assessments appear to have limited reliability:
Olney et al.
(
Olney et al., 2023
)
found little statistical significance but an overall increase in self-rated knowledge levels across all items, despite some topics not being covered in the course.
This increase may have been influenced by concurrent courses covering related topics and the tendency of students to rate their knowledge higher post-course, reflecting expected learning outcomes and increased confidence after completing the semester.

Recommendations:

To maintain a manageable student workload, we recommend offering advanced classes on the
HRE
topics from
Table 1
.
Additionally, widely accessible introductory courses are essential to attract and prepare new students entering the field.

Given the inconsistencies in course evaluation, we propose that educational guidelines for
HRE
should include evaluation criteria to facilitate designing new courses based on concrete evidence of effectiveness.
Testing-based assessments should be used to measure student knowledge beyond self-assessment and participation in formal
QA
surveys should be prioritized.
We advocate for transparently reporting these evaluation results in course-related publications.

5.3.
Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations of this literature review.
While starting our search for courses from scientific publications ensures replicability and provides insight into their pedagogical considerations, future work should consider additional relevant courses which may be discoverable through university catalogs.
Additionally, we only analyzed publicly available course materials.
Requesting materials from individual instructors could enable more precise insight into course content and pedagogical features.
This analysis further focused on English-language publications only.
Considering that the three dedicated
HRE
courses identified are located in the United States and Germany, the inclusion of works in other languages may help to mitigate cultural bias.

6.
Conclusion and Outlook

HRE
(
HRE
) is crucial for protecting the global semiconductor supply chain, yet guidance for teaching
HRE
is limited.
Based on our systematic literature review of existing courses, we proposed several recommendations for designing
HRE
training programs that are viable under time and budget constraints, and for evaluating their effectiveness.
Further research is needed to formalize these recommendations for inclusion in cybersecurity curriculum guidelines.
To ensure the continuity of existing programs, we advocate for the integration of hardware security and
HRE
training into mandatory curricula.

Several related concerns warrant further exploration by practitioners and researchers:
Improving student access to
HRE
training requires better discoverability of existing courses and public availability of course materials.
We were able to obtain course materials for only four of the 13 courses, and discovering courses without accompanying publications was difficult.
Additionally, accessible materials often disappear without proper archival when courses are discontinued.
Closer collaboration between academic institutions and industry partners may allow
HRE
materials to be shared more freely, overcoming the
IP
concerns and resulting secrecy that are common in the semiconductor industry.
Establishing a more transparent and coordinated archival system can then make materials more discoverable and accessible, allowing instructors to reuse well-evaluated course materials.
We believe that these steps can make
HRE
education available to a larger student body and will help close the semiconductor workforce gap.
