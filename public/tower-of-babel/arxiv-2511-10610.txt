On the Rigidity of Projected Perturbed Lattices
Youssef Djellouli, Pierre Yves Gaudreau Lamarre

Abstract
We study the occurrence of number rigidity and deletion singularity in a class of point processes that we call projected perturbed lattices . These are generalizations of processes of the form Π = { ‖ z ‖ α + g z } z ∈ ℤ d \Pi=\{\|z\|^{\alpha}+g_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}} where ( g z ) z ∈ ℤ d (g_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}} are jointly Gaussian, α > 0 \alpha>0 , d ∈ ℕ d\in\mathbb{N} , and ∥ ⋅ ∥ \|\cdot\| is a norm. We develop a new technique to prove sufficient conditions for the deletion singularity of Π \Pi , which improves significantly on the conditions one can obtain using the standard rigidity toolkit (e.g., the variance of linear statistics). In particular, we obtain the first lower bounds on α \alpha for the deletion singularity of Π \Pi that are independent of the dimension d d and the correlation of the g z g_{z} ’s.

Introduction

1.1
General Set-Up

Let
G
be a countably infinite set (typically some graph or lattice). We are interested in point processes of the form
\Pi=\{V(z)+g_{z}\}_{z\in G}
, where
V:G\to\mathbb{R}
is a deterministic function and
\{g_{z}\}_{z\in G}
are random variables such that
\Pi
is locally finite almost surely. For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, a recurrent example will be the projected perturbed lattice model

\displaystyle\{\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{\alpha}+g_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}},

(1.1)

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
is some norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\alpha>0
is fixed, and
(g_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is a gaussian process. This is motivated by its use as a toy model of the eigenvalue process of the random Schrödinger operator
\hat{H}=-\Delta+\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{\alpha}+g_{z}
using the standard Laplacian on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(our hope is that the methods in this paper could eventually be applied to this model).

In this paper, we are interested in studying the occurrence of the following closely related notions of “rigidity” in these point processes:

Definition 1.1
.

For any
S\subset G
, denote
\Pi_{S}=\{V(z)+g_{z}\}_{z\in G\setminus S}
, i.e.,
\Pi
with the points coming from

•

k

[
18
]

. We say that

, the laws of
\Pi

are mutually absolutely continuous (resp. singular).
If
\Pi
is

We can think of deletion singularity as the ability to almost-surely detect if a set of points was deleted from the process, and deletion tolerance as the inability to do so.

•

[
15

there exists a measurable function

such that

See, for instance,
[
18
,
14
,
15
]
for additional notions of “rigidity” such as insertion tolerance/singularity, and rigidity for particular functions. The relationship between deletion singularity and number rigidity has been studied extensively. For instance, the following is a special case of

1.2
Previous Results and Motivation

Two techniques have been developed and are commonly used to prove or disprove rigidity for various point processes. The first of these relies on using a pushforward to view the point process as an ordered random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
in order to show deletion tolerance. The second technique studies the decay of the variance of certain linear test statistics to prove rigidity.
More specifically:

Proposition 1.3
.

[
15
,
14
]

Let us enumerate the elements of
G
as
G=\{z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},\ldots\}
.
Let
P=(V(z_{i})+g_{z_{i}})_{i\geq 1}
and
P_{S}=(V(z_{i})+g_{z_{i}})_{i\geq 1:z_{i}\not\in S}
be random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
whose components are given by the points in
\Pi
and
\Pi_{S}
, consistently with
the order of the enumeration of
G
’s elements. If
P
and
P_{S}
are mutually absolutely continuous, then
\Pi
and
\Pi_{S}
are mutually absolutely continuous.

Proposition 1.4
.

[
15
]
If there exists a sequence of Borel measurable functions
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that converge uniformly to
1
on every compact set and such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum\limits_{a\in\Pi}f_{n}(a)\right)=0,

then
\Pi
is number rigid
and deletion singular.

Proposition
1.3
is especially powerful when the noise is independent, because it can be used in concert with Kakutani’s dichotomy to give a characterization of absolute continuity that relies largely on computational methods.
Proposition
1.4
is often easy to apply because

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{a\in\Pi}f(a)\right)=\sum_{a,b\in\Pi}\mathrm{Cov}\big(f(a),f(b)\big),

(1.2)

which further simplifies to
\sum_{a\in\Pi}\mathrm{Var}\big(f(a)\big)
when the points in
\Pi
are independent. Like Proposition
1.3
, this often reduces the issue of rigidity to a largely computational question that relies on pairwise correlations within
\Pi
.

Remark 1.5
.

In actual applications of Proposition
1.4

(e.g.,
[
12
,
21
,
15
,
18
]
),
it is customary to define
f_{n}(x)=f(x/n)
for a smooth test function

satisfying
f(0)=1
.
In many cases (e.g., Gaussian noise), this specific choice makes the computation of
\mathrm{Var}\big(f(a)\big)
amenable to analysis by elementary methods, such as change of variables.

Remark 1.6
.

If
\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{a\in\Pi}f_{n}(a)\right)

is only bounded above as
n\to\infty
, then one can still prove deletion singularity/number rigidity if for every fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
, one has

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Cov}\left(\sum_{a\in\Pi}f_{n}(a),\sum_{a\in\Pi}f_{m}(a)\right)=0.

This more general sufficient condition was used in
[
18
, Lemma 7.2]
to prove the deletion singularity/number rigidity of two-dimensional perturbed lattices, and in
[
10
,
13
]
to prove the deletion singularity/number rigidity of stochastic Airy operators.

Examples of prior results that use Propositions
1.3
and
1.4
to investigate the rigidity of simple special cases of
\Pi
are as follows:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let

[

Problem 1.15
.

Which features of the point process
\Pi=\{V(z)+g_{z}\}_{z\in G}

Given the limitations of Propositions
1.3
and

Previous works

have found success using Proposition

Exponential test functions can be used to find tolerance thresholds for correlated eigenvalues in some cases, but these test function are not suitable for noise with heavy tailed distributions, and even for non-heavy tailed distributions the correlations must decay sufficiently quickly
[

2
Main Result and Applications

We now state our main result and some of its applications. We begin with some assumptions:

Assumption 2.1
.

Let
G
be any countably infinite set. Suppose that
V:G\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the following conditions:

a.

The image
\mathrm{Im}(V)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:V(z)=x\text{ for some }z\in G\}

has no accumulation point.

b.

For every
x\in\mathrm{Im}(V)
,
the set
\{z\in G:V(z)=x\}

is finite.

In particular,
V
is unbounded.
Suppose that we denote

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(V)=\{t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},\ldots\}\cup\{r_{0},r_{1},r_{2},\ldots\},

(2.1)

where

\cdots<t_{2}<t_{1}<t_{0}<0\leq r_{0}<r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots
.
(Since
V
is unbounded, at least one of the sets
\{t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},\ldots\}
or
\{r_{0},r_{1},r_{2},\ldots\}
must be infinite, but one of them will be finite if
V
is bounded below or bounded above). We assume that
(g_{z})_{z\in G}
are random variables that satisfy the following:

i.

If
V
is bounded below, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}\max_{z\in G:V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]}|g_{z}|=0\qquad\text{almost surely}.

(2.2)

ii.

If
V
is bounded above, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|t_{n}-t_{n-1}|}\max_{z\in G:V(z)\in[t_{n},0)}|g_{z}|=0\qquad\text{almost surely}.

(2.3)

iii.

If
V
is neither bounded below nor bounded above, then both (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) hold.

Our main result is as follows:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\Pi=\{V(z)+g_{z}\}_{z\in G}
.
If
G
,
V
, and
(g_{z})_{z\in G}

satisfy Assumption
2.1
,
then for every finite
S,T\subset G
such that
|S|\neq|T|
, the processes

\Pi_{S}
and
\Pi_{T}
are mutually singular.

Remark 2.3
.

When we say that two point processes are mutually singular, we mean that the
completion
of the probability distributions of the point processes on the set of counting measures (equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra generated by vague convergence) are mutually singular. See Section
4.1
for a detailed statement, and
Remark
3.2
and Lemma
4.4
for more details on the necessity of the probability measures we consider being complete.

Remark 2.4
.

If we take
T=\varnothing
(meaning that
\Pi_{T}=\Pi
), then Theorem
2.2
implies that
\Pi
is deletion singular under Assumption
2.1
.

We now discuss applications and the optimality of Theorem
2.2
.

2.1
Applications

Our main application consists of the following improvement of Theorem
1.10
:

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
d\geq 1
. Let
(g_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
be any Gaussian process such that
g_{z}
has mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}>0
for every
z
(i.e., we make no assumption on the covariance). Consider the point process
\Pi=\{\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{\alpha}+g_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
is either the
\ell^{1}
or the
\ell^{\infty}
norm. If
\alpha>1
, then
\Pi
is deletion singular.

Proof.

In this case, the function
V(z)=\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{\alpha}
satisfies Assumption
2.1
a. and b. Moreover, the image of
V
can be written as
\{r_{0},r_{1},r_{2},\ldots\}
with
r_{n}=n^{\alpha}
. By the intermediate value theorem, it is easy to see that
r_{n}-r_{n-1}\geq cn^{\alpha-1}
for some constant
c>0
. By a standard union bound/Borel-Cantelli argument, the fact that the
g_{z}
are Gaussian with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
implies that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log|\{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},~\left\lVert z\right\rVert\leq n\}|}}\max_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},~\left\lVert z\right\rVert\leq n}|g_{z}|<\infty\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Given that

\displaystyle\log|\{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},~\left\lVert z\right\rVert\leq n\}|=\log\big(O(n^{d})\big)=O(\log n),

(2.4)

we get that
(g_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
satisfies Assumption
2.1
i. whenever
\alpha>1
.
∎

There are two features of this result that are most significant:

1.

In Theorem
1.10
, the lower bound on
\alpha
for deletion singularity grows with
d
. In sharp contrast to this, our main result implies that we can replace this by the dimension-independent lower bound
\alpha>1
; this improves on
\alpha\geq d/2
as soon as
d\geq 3
. This works because the dimensional-dependence in our argument occurs as a multiplicative factor in a logarithmic term (i.e., (
2.4
)). Conversely, in the variances of linear statistics (i.e., (
1.3
)), the dimension occurs as a power law on
n
. This mechanism is crucial in the design of the proof of Theorem
2.2
; see Section
3
for more details.

2.

Suppose that we replace the i.i.d. Gaussians in Theorem
1.10
with a centered stationary Gaussian process with a covariance decay of the form
|\mathbb{E}[g_{z}g_{w}]|\lesssim\left\lVert z-w\right\rVert^{-\beta}

as
\left\lVert z-w\right\rVert\to\infty
for some
\beta>0
. Then, Proposition
1.4
implies deletion singularity whenever

\alpha>\begin{cases}d/2&\text{if }\beta>d,\\
d-\beta/2&\text{if }\beta<d.\end{cases}

(See, e.g.,
[
12
, Theorem 3.16]
for a similar result). Thus, the sufficient conditions for deletion tolerance that one can get through linear statistics conditions will typically depend significantly on the correlation within the noise. This is because nontrivial covariances can significantly increase the size of the variance of linear statistics, because of the non-diagonal terms in the sum (
1.2
). Thus,
in addition to dimension-independence, our result allows to provide correlation-independent sufficient conditions for deletion singularity. Again, our method of proof (outlined in Section
3
) was consciously designed to make this improvement: In the proof of Corollary
2.5
, we bound the maxima of
|g_{z}|
using a union bound, which is completely insensitive to correlations.

Remark 2.6
.

Despite these improvements, it is interesting to note that Corollary
2.5
does not improve on every case in Theorem
1.10
. For instance, when
d=1
, Theorem
1.10
implies that
\{|z|^{\alpha}+g_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}

in general (i.e., with no assumption on the correlation between the

Let

(

2.2
Optimality

In this section, we discuss the optimality of various conditions in Theorem
2.2
. We start by giving an example to show that it is in fact necessary to assume
|S|\neq|T|
.

Example 2.8
.

Consider the point process
\Pi=\{\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{\alpha}+g_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
is the
\ell^{1}
or
\ell^{\infty}
norm and
(g_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
are i.i.d gaussian with mean 0 and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Let
S,T\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be finite and such that
|S|=|T|
. Then for all
\alpha>0
,
\Pi_{S}
and
\Pi_{T}
are mutually absolutely continuous.

This implies that even in the best circumstances, we can only detect how many points were deleted from
\Pi
, not which specific points were deleted;
see Section
5.4
for a proof.

Recall Assumption
2.1
iii. We show by giving an example that the double sided condition in the case where
V
is unbounded in both directions is necessary. Intuitively we can think of a process failing to fulfill either side of the condition as being able to “hide” the deletion in the side of the process where the noise effects are too strong to be able to find deletions.

Example 2.9
.

Consider the point process on the real line

\Pi=\{-z^{2}+g_{z}:z\in\mathbb{Z}^{-}\}\bigcup\{z^{\alpha}+g_{z}:z\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\cup\{0\}\}

where
(g_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}
are i.i.d gaussian with mean
0
and variance
1
, and
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
. Then,
\Pi
and
\Pi_{\{0\}}
are mutually absolutely continuous.

See Section
5.5
for a proof.

Finally, following-up on Remark
2.6
, we show that Theorem
2.2
cannot be used to prove the deletion singularity of processes at a level of generality similar to Corollary
2.5
(i.e., with general covariances) below the threshold
\alpha=1
:

Example 2.10
.

Consider
\Pi=\{z^{\alpha}+g\}_{z\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
g
is any random variable such that
g
and
g+k
are mutually absolutely continuous for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

1.

If
\alpha=1
, then
\Pi_{\{1,\ldots,m\}}
and
\Pi_{\{1,\ldots,n\}}
are mutually absolutely continuous for every
m,n\geq 1
. In particular,
\Pi
is not deletion singular.

2.

If
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, then

\Pi
is deletion singular.

See Section
5.6
for proof.

3
Outline of Proof

We will only outline the proof of Theorem
2.2
in the case where
(g_{z})_{z\in G}
satisfies Assumption
2.1
i., as the other cases follow similarly.

Let
S,T\subset G
be finite and such that
|S|\neq|T|
; we can
assume without loss of generality that
|S|<|T|
.
We want to find some function
f
such that, almost surely,
f(\Pi_{S})=0

and
f(\Pi_{T})\neq 0
. To achieve this, consider the following class of functions: Given an integer
k\geq 0
and any countable set
A
(e.g.,
A=\Pi_{S}
or
A=\Pi_{T}
), let

\displaystyle f_{k}(A)=\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subset G\\
|B|=k\end{subarray}}~\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi:G\setminus{B}\to A\\
\text{bijection}\end{subarray}}~\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}~\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in G\setminus B\\
V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]\end{subarray}}|\psi(z)-V(z)|.

(3.1)

The rationale for
the design of the latter is as follows:

1.

We make an apriori guess,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, that represents how many points we believe are missing from either
\Pi_{S}
or
\Pi_{T}
; i.e., we are trying to guess the cardinalities of
S
and
T
.

2.

Once we have made this guess, we try to guess which
k
points were deleted from
\Pi
in
\Pi_{S}
or
\Pi_{T}
. In the definition of
f_{k}
, this corresponds to choosing a subset
B\subset G
with
|B|=k
, which matches the deleted points.

3.

On the one hand, if
k=|S|
, then the infimum over all
B
such that
|B|=k
in
f_{k}
will eventually find
B=S
. If
B=S
, then the infimum over
\psi:G\setminus S\to\Pi_{S}
will include the “identity” map

\psi(z)=V(z)+g_{z},\qquad z\in G\setminus S.

In that case, we have the almost-sure limit

\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}~\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in G\setminus B\\
V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]\end{subarray}}|\psi(z)-V(z)|=\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}~\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in G\setminus B\\
V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]\end{subarray}}|g_{z}|=0

by Assumption
2.1
i.
Thus, we conclude that
f_{k}(\Pi_{S})=0
almost surely.

4.

On the other hand, if
|T|>k
, we show that no matter how we choose
B
and
\psi
, we are forced to have an infinite chain of “mismatches” between the points missing from
\Pi_{T}
and our guess
B
, i.e., points
z
such that
\psi(z)\neq V(z)+g_{z}
. Using this infinite chain of mismatches, we will be able to show that
f_{k}(\Pi_{T})\geq 1
almost surely.

In summary, if
|S|\neq|T|
, then evaluating
f_{k}(\Pi_{S})
and
f_{k}(\Pi_{T})
for
k=0,1,2,\ldots
eventually yields some
f
such that
f(\Pi_{S})=0\neq f(\Pi_{T})
almost surely.

Remark 3.1
.

In the above outline, we do not describe what happens when we evaluate
f_{k}
in
\Pi_{S}
for
|S|<k
.
However, by starting at
k=0
and iterating upwards, we are able to avoid dealing with this situation altogether.

Remark 3.2
.

By virtue of including an infimum over an uncountable set, we are unable to show that the functionals
f_{k}
are measurable. Thus, the fact that
some of these functionals output different values for
\Pi_{S}
and
\Pi_{T}
does not necessarily imply
that the point processes are mutually singular. That being said, we are able
to get around this issue by completing
the distributions of the point processes;
see Lemma
4.4
for the details.

This approach is partially inspired by the one used to prove Theorem 1.3 in
[
23
]
(see Section 2.1 therein). In that paper, the authors characterize the tolerance and singularity of the perturbed lattice
\Pi=\{x+Y_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
where
Y_{x}\sim N_{d}(0,\sigma^{2}I)
are independent
d
-dimensional normal random vectors with independent components of variance
\sigma^{2}
. Both our approach and the one in that paper rely on the following two-step strategy:

1.

attempting to match points in a perturbed-lattice-type model to a set of “unperturbed” deterministic lattice points, and then

2.

examining the fluctuations between random points and their matched deterministic lattice points in order to determine if points were deleted.

That being said, the mechanism that we exploit in step 2 of this strategy is entirely different from the one used in
[
23
]
. On the one hand, our strategy relies on comparing the maximum of these mismatches with the growth rate of
V
’s possible outputs (as evidenced by the design of
f_{k}
in (
3.1
)).
[
23
]
on the other hand, uses an averaging technique to check for deletions. The authors show that if the variance of the perturbations is small enough they can distinguish between the average deviations of the points in the deleted lattice and those of the original lattice along a particular path. Despite similarity in philosophy, the differences in the approach combined with the different setting result in some additional flexibility. In particular, we do not require independence of the noise since we do not rely on greedy lattice animals (or similar mechanisms) and, crucially for our setting, we are also able to have multiple points in
\Pi
that originate from the same
z\in G
(i.e., the function
V(z)
need not be injective). Furthermore, our approach allows us to not only distinguish between the original process
\Pi
and some deleted process
\Pi_{S}
, but also between two different deleted processes
\Pi_{S}
and
\Pi_{T}
with
|S|\neq|T|
, resulting in a mechanism that can tell us precisely how many points were deleted.

4
Proof of Main Result

4.1
General Setup

Let
G=\{z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},\ldots\}
be an arbitrary enumeration of
G
.
Let
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}=\{\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}:\omega_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}
be the set of real-valued sequences, equipped with the product topology
generated by the Euclidean topology on
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})
be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra generated by this topology. By virtue of being a countable product of Polish spaces,
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a Polish space.
On this space, we define the set of random variables
(g_{z_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

as
g_{z_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\omega_{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
; we then equip
(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}))
with the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
that corresponds to the joint distribution of the
g_{z}
’s.
Let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
denote the set of sequences such that the set
|\{i\in\mathbb{N}:V(z_{i})+\omega_{i}\in K\}|
is finite for every compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}
. It is a simple exercise to show that
\mathcal{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})
(e.g.,
[
21
, Appendix B]
);
as mentioned in the introduction, we assume that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{L}]=1
.

Let
\mathbb{M}
be the space of locally finite Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}
, and let
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{M})
be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on that space generated by the vague topology (e.g.,
[
19
, Page 109]
). Under this topology,
\mathbb{M}
is also a Polish space (e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 4.2]
).
For every finite
S\subset G
, we define the map
\Pi_{S}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{M}
as

\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~z_{i}\not\in S}\delta_{V(z_{i})+\omega_{i}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{L}\}}=\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~z_{i}\not\in S}\delta_{V(z_{i})+g_{z_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{L}\}},

where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac mass at
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
(This includes the case where
S=\varnothing
, which we simply denote
\Pi=\Pi_{\varnothing}
).
It is a straightforward exercise to show that
\Pi_{S}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{M})
(e.g.,
[
21
, Appendix B]
).

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\Pi_{S}}=\mathbb{P}\circ\Pi_{S}^{-1}
denote the probability distribution of
\Pi_{S}
on
(\mathbb{M},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{M}))
, and let
\overline{\mathbb{P}}^{0}_{\Pi_{S}}
be its completion, denoting the corresponding complete
\sigma
-algebra as
\mathcal{F}_{\Pi_{S}}
. Finally, to ensure that our distributions are operating over the same
\sigma
-algebra, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\bigcap\limits_{S\subset G:|S|<\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\Pi_{S}},

(4.1)

and we let
\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{\Pi_{S}}
be the restriction of
\overline{\mathbb{P}}^{0}_{\Pi_{S}}
on
\mathcal{F}
.

Following-up on Remark
2.3
,
our aim is to show that
\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{\Pi_{S}}
and
\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{\Pi_{T}}
are mutually singular in the space
(\mathbb{M},\mathcal{F})
when
|S|\neq|T|
are both finite.
Our proof of this consists of constructing disjoint measurable sets
A_{S},A_{T}\in\mathcal{F}
such that

\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{S}[A_{S}]=1
and
\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{T}[A_{T}]=1
. We now go through a case-by-case proof of this.

4.2
Case 1.
V
is Bounded Below.

For the sake of clarity we break the proof up into several lemmas that when combined give the desired result. We prove the theorem under (
2.2
) and then describe in Section
4.3
how the other two cases follow similarly.
For the rest of this section we can assume without loss of generality that
|S|<|T|
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f_{k}
be defined as in (
3.1
).
If
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{L}
, then

f_{|S|}(\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\leq\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}\max_{z\in G:V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]}|g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|.

Proof.

If
k=|S|
, then the function
f_{k}
is able to make the correct guess for every point in
\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
through the subset
B=S
and the bijection
\psi^{\prime}:G\setminus S\to\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
such that
\psi^{\prime}(z)=V(z)+g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
. Thus by (
3.1
),

\displaystyle f_{k}(\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))

\displaystyle\leq\inf\limits_{\psi:G\setminus{S}\to\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega})}\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in G\setminus B\\
V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]\end{subarray}}|\psi(z)-V(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in G\setminus B\\
V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]\end{subarray}}|\psi^{\prime}(z)-V(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}\max_{z\in G:V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]}|g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|,

as desired.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
V
is bounded below, and that it satisfies Assumption
2.1
a. and b. If
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{L}
, then
for every
0\leq k<|T|
, the function
f_{k}
defined in (
3.1
) satisfies

f_{k}(\Pi_{T}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\geq 1-\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}\max_{z\in G:V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]}|g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|.

Proof.

Let
0\leq k<|T|
.
By definition of
f_{k}
in (
3.1
), it suffices to prove that for every choice of subset
B\subset G
such that
|B|=k
and bijection

\psi:G\setminus B\to\Pi_{T}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
, one has

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in G\setminus B\\
V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]\end{subarray}}|\psi(z)-V(z)|\geq 1-\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}\max_{z\in G:V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]}|g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|.

(4.2)

Thus, for the remainder of this proof,
we assume that
B
and
\psi
are a fixed choice of such a subset and bijection.
The first ingredient in our proof of (
4.2
) is to show that there exists an infinite sequence of distinct elements
u_{0},u_{1},u_{2},\ldots\in G\setminus B

that form a chain of mismatches, in the sense that
\psi(u_{n})=V(u_{n+1})+g_{u_{n+1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
for all
n\geq 0
.
One can generate this sequence using the following algorithm (see Figure
1
in the appendix for an illustration):

Since
|T|>k=|B|
, there exists
u_{0}\in T\setminus B
. Since
V(u_{0})+g_{u_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\not\in\Pi_{T}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
, this means that there exists some
u_{1}\in G\setminus T
such that
u_{1}\neq u_{0}
and
\psi(u_{0})=V(u_{1})+g_{u_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
.
If
u_{1}\in G\setminus B
, then the fact that
\psi
is a bijection means that there exists
u_{2}\in G\setminus T
such that
u_{2}\not\in\{u_{0},u_{1}\}
and
\psi(u_{1})=V(u_{2})+g_{u_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
.
With this in hand, we may now attempt to generate
u_{n}
for
n\geq 3
using the following recursive procedure: For every
n\geq 3
, if
u_{n-1}\in G\setminus B
, then use the fact that
\psi
is a bijection to conclude that there exists
u_{n}\in G\setminus T
such that
u_{n}\not\in\{u_{0},\ldots,u_{n-1}\}
and
\psi(u_{n-1})=V(u_{n})+g_{u_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
.

The only mechanism that could prevent this algorithm from generating an infinite sequence would be the existence of some
n_{\star}\geq 1
such that
u_{n_{\star}}\in B
. In such a case, the recursive step fails because
\psi(u_{n_{\star}})
is not defined.
However, if that happens, then we can restart the algorithm by choosing a new initial point
u_{0}^{\prime}\in T\setminus B
that is different from
\{u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n_{\star}}\}
.
It is always possible to choose such a
u_{0}^{\prime}
because the algorithm ensures that
u_{0}\in T\setminus B
,
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n_{\star}-1}\in G\setminus(T\cup B)
, and
u_{n_{\star}}\in B\setminus T
.
In particular, the sequence
u_{0},\ldots,u_{n_{\star}}
contains one element from
T
and one distinct element from
B
; hence there will be at least one element left in
T\setminus B
because
|T|>|B|
.
Since
\psi
is a bijection, the new sequence of mismatches
\psi(u_{n-1}^{\prime})=V(u_{n}^{\prime})+g_{{u_{n}^{\prime}}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
recursively generated from
u_{0}^{\prime}
will not contain any element in
\{u_{0},\ldots,u_{n_{\star}}\}
.
In conclusion, since
|T|>|B|
, we can repeatedly restart the algorithm with a new initial point in
T\setminus B
until we either generate an infinite sequence, or until we exhaust all the points in
B
(in which case the next starting point is guaranteed to generate an infinite sequence since there are no more points in
B
that can terminate it).

For the remainder of this proof, we thus assume that
u_{0},u_{1},u_{2},\ldots\in G\setminus B
are distinct and such that
\psi(u_{n})=V(u_{n+1})+g_{u_{n+1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
for
n\geq 0
. The second ingredient in our proof is to extract a subsequence
0\leq n_{0}<n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots
that satisfies the following properties:

\displaystyle 0\leq V(u_{n_{0}+1})<V(u_{n_{1}+1})<V(u_{n_{2}+1})<\cdots,

(4.3)

and

\displaystyle 0\leq V(u_{n_{k}})<V(u_{n_{k}+1})\qquad\text{for every }k\geq 0.

(4.4)

Before we show that such a subsequence exists, we explain how it can be used to prove (
4.2
), and thus conclude the proof of Lemma
4.2
:
Let us denote
s_{0},s_{1},\cdots
and
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots
such that

r_{s_{k}}=V(u_{n_{k}+1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad r_{h_{k}}=V(u_{n_{k}}),\qquad k\geq 0;

by (
4.3
) and (
4.4
),

s_{k}<s_{k+1}
and

h_{k}<s_{k}
for
k\geq 0
.
Given this, we note that

\text{left-hand side of }\eqref{Equation: fk lower bound}\geq\limsup\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{s_{k}}-r_{s_{k}-1}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in G\setminus B\\
V(z)\in[0,r_{s_{k}}]\end{subarray}}|\psi(z)-V(z)|,

where we get the lower bound by taking the
\limsup
along the subsequence
\{s_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
.
If we lower bound the maximum above by making the choice
z=u_{n_{k}}
(which we can make because
V(u_{n_{k}})=r_{h_{k}}<r_{s_{k}}
), then this yields

\text{left-hand side of }\eqref{Equation: fk lower bound}\geq\limsup\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{|\psi(u_{n_{k}})-V(u_{n_{k}})|}{r_{s_{k}}-r_{s_{k}-1}}.

By definition of
h_{k}
,
s_{k}
, and the sequence of mismatches
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, we note that

\psi(u_{n_{k}})-V(u_{n_{k}})=V(u_{n_{k}+1})+g_{u_{n_{k}+1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})-V(u_{n_{k}})=r_{s_{k}}+g_{u_{n_{k}+1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})-r_{h_{k}}.

Therefore, we get

\text{left-hand side of }\eqref{Equation: fk lower bound}\geq\limsup\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{|r_{s_{k}}+g_{u_{n_{k}+1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})-r_{h_{k}}|}{r_{s_{k}}-r_{s_{k}-1}}.

By the triangle inequality, this yields

\text{left-hand side of }\eqref{Equation: fk lower bound}\geq\limsup\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{|r_{s_{k}}-r_{h_{k}}|-|g_{u_{n_{k}+1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|}{r_{s_{k}}-r_{s_{k}-1}}.

Given that
h_{k}<s_{k}
, we have that

r_{h_{k}}\leq r_{s_{k}-1}
; hence
|r_{s_{k}}-r_{h_{k}}|\geq r_{s_{k}}-r_{s_{k}-1}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\text{left-hand side of }\eqref{Equation: fk lower bound}\geq 1-\liminf\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{|g_{u_{n_{k}+1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|}{r_{s_{k}}-r_{s_{k}-1}}.

(4.5)

Finally, if we use the fact that
z=u_{n_{k}+1}
is such that
V(z)=r_{s_{k}}
, then we get that

\liminf\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{|g_{u_{n_{k}+1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|}{r_{s_{k}}-r_{s_{k}-1}}\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{s_{k}}-r_{s_{k}-1}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in G\setminus B\\
V(z)\in[0,r_{s_{k}}]\end{subarray}}|g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|\\
\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in G\setminus B\\
V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]\end{subarray}}|g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|\leq\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}\max_{z\in G:V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]}|g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|.

If we combine this with (
4.5
), then we finally obtain (
4.2
).

We now wrap up the proof of Lemma
4.2
by constructing the subsequence that satisfies (
4.3
) and (
4.4
). We claim that this can be done explicitly as follows: First, define

\displaystyle n_{0}=\inf\{n\geq 0:V(u_{n+1})>V(u_{n})\geq 0\},

(4.6)

and then for
k\geq 1
, if we are given
n_{k-1}
, then we define

\displaystyle n_{k}=\inf\{n\geq n_{k-1}+1:V(u_{n+1})>V(u_{n_{k-1}+1})\}.

(4.7)

Indeed, if
n_{0}<n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots
are well-defined (in the sense that the corresponding infima are finite), then the only claim in (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) that is not immediate from these definitions
is that
V(u_{n_{k}})<V(u_{n_{k}+1})
for all
k\geq 1
. Toward this end, suppose by contradiction that

\displaystyle V(u_{n_{k}})\geq V(u_{n_{k}+1})\qquad\text{for some }k\geq 1.

(4.8)

By definition of
n_{k}
, we have that
V(u_{n_{k}+1})>V(u_{n_{k-1}+1})
; combining this
with (
4.8
) implies that
V(u_{n_{k}})>V(u_{n_{k-1}+1})
,
and thus
u_{n_{k}}\neq u_{n_{k-1}+1}
(by definition of
n_{k}
, this is equivalent to
n_{k}>n_{k-1}+1
). However, if this were true, then
n=n_{k}-1\in[n_{k-1}+1,n_{k})
would be such that
V(u_{n+1})=V(u_{n_{k}})>V(u_{n_{k-1}-1})
, which contradicts the definition of
n_{k}
as the smallest integer in
[n_{k-1}+1,\infty)
that satisfies this property.

It now only remains to show that the infima in (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) are finite.
Given that
V
is bounded below, that
\mathrm{Im}(V)
has no accumulation point (Assumption
2.1
a.), and that
V
’s level sets are finite (Assumption
2.1
b.), we conclude that for every
R\in\mathbb{R}
, the set
\{z\in G:V(z)<R\}
is finite. Therefore, since the points
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots\in G
are distinct,
V(u_{n})\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. This immediately implies that (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) are finite.
∎

Now that we have shown that there exists some
k
such that
f_{k}(\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))=0\neq f_{k}(\Pi_{T}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))
for all
\boldsymbol{\omega}
in a probability-one event (i.e.,
\mathcal{L}
intersected with the event where (
2.2
) holds), it remains only to address the measurability concern of Remark
3.2
. We begin with the following straightforward lemma about the completion of pushforward measures:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a probability space, let
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{B})
be a measurable space, and let
T:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
be measurable. Suppose that
A\in\mathcal{A}
is such that
\mu(A)=1
. If
T(A)
is in the completion of
\mathcal{B}
under
\mu\circ T^{-1}
, then
\overline{\mu\circ T^{-1}}(T(A))=1
,
where
\overline{\cdot}
denotes the completion of a measure.

Proof.

Recall that by definition of completion, if
C
is in the completion of
\mathcal{B}
, then

\overline{\mu\circ T^{-1}}(C)=\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}\\
C\subset B\end{subarray}}\{\mu\circ T^{-1}(B)\}.

Thus, it suffices to show that for every
B\in\mathcal{B}
such that
T(A)\subset B
we have
\mu\circ T^{-1}(B)=1
.

Toward this end, let
B\in\mathcal{B}
be such that
T(A)\subset B
.
For every
\omega\in A
, we have
T(\omega)\in T(A)
and so
T(\omega)\in B
. Then,
A\subset\{\omega\in\Omega:T(\omega)\in B\}
and since
\mu(A)=1
, we have
\mu\circ T^{-1}(B)=\mu(\{\omega\in\Omega:T(\omega)\in B\})\geq\mu(A)=1
.
∎

We now conclude the proof of Theorem
2.2
in the case where
V
is bounded below with the following lemma:

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that
V
is bounded below, and that it satisfies Assumption
2.1
a. and b. Suppose in addition that (
2.2
) holds.
The measures
\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{\Pi_{S}}
and
\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{\Pi_{T}}
are mutually singular.

Proof.

Define the random variables
X
on
(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}))
as

X(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}\max_{z\in G:V(z)\in[0,r_{n}]}|g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|

(
X
is clearly Borel-measurable by virtue of each
g_{z}
being Borel measurable).
Define the sets
A_{S},A_{T}\subset\mathbb{M}
as

A_{S}=\Pi_{S}(\{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:X(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0\}\cap\mathcal{L})\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{T}=\Pi_{T}(\{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:X(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0\}\cap\mathcal{L}).

By virtue of being the images of a Borel subset of the polish space
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}
through Borel maps,
A_{S}
and
A_{T}
are analytic sets, hence universally measurable (i.e., measurable with respect to every completion of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{M})
); see, e.g.,
[
20
, Theorem 21.10]
. In particular,

A_{S},A_{T}\in\mathcal{F}
. By (
2.2
) and the assumption that
\mathcal{L}
has probability one,
\mathbb{P}(\{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:X(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0\}\cap\mathcal{L})=1
. Therefore, it follows from Lemma
4.3
that

\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{\Pi_{S}}(A_{S})=\overline{\mathbb{P}\circ\Pi_{S}^{-1}}(\Pi_{S}(\{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:X(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0\}\cap\mathcal{L}))=1,

and similarly
\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{\Pi_{T}}(A_{T})=1
.

By Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
, for every
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{L}
,
the maps
f_{k}
satisfy the following:

if
k=|S|
, then

\displaystyle f_{k}(\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\leq X(\boldsymbol{\omega});

if
k<|T|
, then

\displaystyle f_{k}(\Pi_{T}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\geq 1-X(\boldsymbol{\omega}).

Given that
A_{S}
(resp.
A_{T}
) only includes images of
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{L}
through
\Pi_{S}
(resp.
\Pi_{T}
) for which
X(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0
, this means that

A_{S}\subset\{\mu\in\mathbb{M}:f_{|S|}(\mu)\leq 0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{T}\subset\{\mu\in\mathbb{M}:f_{|S|}(\mu)\geq 1\}.

In particular,
A_{S}\cap A_{T}=\varnothing
, which concludes the proof.
∎

4.3
Cases 2 and 3.
V
is Bounded Above or Unbounded

In the cases where
V
is bounded above (and Assumption
2.1
ii. holds) or where
V
is neither bounded below nor bounded above (and Assumption
2.1
iii. holds),
the proof of Theorem
2.2
is nearly identical with minor changes that we illustrate below.

When
V
is bounded above, we must first make a minor modification to the functional
f_{k}
in order to account for the fact that the image of
V
now has infinitely many negative outputs
\{t_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and only finitely many positive outputs. To do this, we define

\hat{f}_{k}(A)=\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subset G\\
|B|=k\end{subarray}}~\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi:G\setminus{B}\to A\\
\text{bijection}\end{subarray}}~\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|t_{n}-t_{n-1}|}~\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in G\setminus B\\
V(z)\in[t_{n},0]\end{subarray}}|\psi(z)-V(z)|.

Using the functional
\hat{f}_{k}
, the proof follows identically to the one under Assumption
2.1
i. In particular, we can repeat the proof of Lemma
4.1
using the exact same
\psi^{\prime}:G\setminus S\to\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
such that
\psi^{\prime}(z)=V(z)+g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
in order to get

\hat{f}_{|S|}(\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\leq\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|t_{n}-t_{n-1}|}\max_{z\in G:V(z)\in[t_{n},0]}|g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|,\qquad\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{L}.

Similarly, by direct analogy to the proof of Lemma
4.2
, given any set
B\subset G
with
B=k<|T|
and a bijection
\psi
, we construct an infinite chain of mismatches
u_{0},u_{1},u_{2},\ldots
such that
\psi(u_{n})=V(u_{n+1})+g_{u_{n+1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
. Then by a symmetric argument to the one in the first case we can extract a subsequence satisfying the same properties as the one in the first case but going in the opposite direction (i.e.
V(u_{n_{0}+1})>V(u_{n_{1}+1})>V(u_{n_{2}+1})>\cdots
and
0\geq V(u_{n_{k}})>V(u_{n_{k}+1})
). Using this monotone subsequence allows us to follow the same chain of inequalities as in Lemma
4.2
and get

\hat{f}_{k}(\Pi_{T}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\geq 1-\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|t_{n}-t_{n-1}|}\max_{z\in G:V(z)\in[t_{n},0]}|g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|,\qquad\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{L},

for
0\leq k<|T|
.

At this point, if we restrict to the event where (
2.3
) holds, then we can find some
k\geq 0
such that
\hat{f}_{k}(\Pi_{S})\leq 0<1\leq\hat{f}_{k}(\Pi_{T})
; the mutual singularity is then proved from this using Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
.

Under Assumption
2.1
iii., we once again modify the functional, and consider
h_{k}=\max\{f_{k},\hat{f}_{k}\}
. We immediately get by choosing
\psi^{\prime}:G\setminus S\to\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
such that
\psi^{\prime}(z)=V(z)+g_{z}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
, that
h_{|S|}(\Pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\leq 0
whenever
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{L}
satisfies both (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) (this is clear since that inequality holds for both
f_{|S|}
and
\hat{f}_{|S|}
by the previous two cases).

Once again using the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
4.2
, given a set
B\subset G
with
B=k<|T|
and a bijection
\psi
we can construct an infinite chain of mismatches,
u_{0},u_{1},u_{2},\ldots
such that
\psi(u_{n})=V(u_{n+1})+g_{u_{n+1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
. The key difference in this case comes when we construct the subsequence that we use in the chain of inequalities. Because
V
is unbounded in both directions we do not know a priori whether we can construct an infinite subsequence that is increasing or decreasing (i.e.
V(u_{n_{i+1}+1})>V(u_{n_{i}+1})
,
as in Case 1., or
V(u_{n_{i+1}+1})<V(u_{n_{i}+1})
, as in Case 2.). Nevertheless, Assumption
2.1
a. and b. still implies that at least one of the following claims must hold:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}V(u_{n})=\infty\qquad\text{or}\qquad\liminf_{n\to\infty}V(u_{n})=-\infty.

In particular, it is clear by direct analogy to the arguments in the proof of Lemma
4.2
that at least one of these subsequences (i.e., increasing or decreasing) must exist. This means that either
f_{k}(\Pi_{T}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\geq 1
or
\hat{f}_{k}(\Pi_{T}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\geq 1
whenever
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{L}
satisfies both (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), which immediately gives us the desired
h_{k}(\Pi_{T}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\geq 1
.
We then get the mutual singularity by
Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
.

5.1
Proof of Theorem
1.10

The deletion tolerance of
\Pi
for
\alpha<1/2
follows immediately as a corollary to the first part of Proposition
1.14
; we will prove this in the next section.

The deletion singularity of
\Pi
for
\alpha>d/2
is proved using the following application of Proposition
1.4
: Suppose that the
g_{z}
’s are i.i.d. copies of the random variable
g
.
Using the collection of test functions
f_{n}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-x/n}
we get:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[f_{n}(\Pi)]=\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{Var}[f_{n}(\left\lVert m\right\rVert^{\alpha}+g)]

\displaystyle\leq C\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}m^{d-1}\mathrm{Var}[f_{n}(m^{\alpha}+g)]

\displaystyle=C\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}-1)\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}m^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2m^{\alpha}/n},

where we used the variance of the lognormal distribution and
|\{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\left\lVert k\right\rVert=m\}|\leq Cm^{d-1}
for a positive constant
C
depending only on
d
.

We then have:

\displaystyle C\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}-1)\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}m^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2m^{\alpha}/n}

\displaystyle=C\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}-1)\sum\limits_{m\in\frac{\mathbb{N}}{n^{1/\alpha}}}(mn^{1/\alpha})^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2m^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=C\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}-1)n^{(d-1)/\alpha}n^{1/\alpha}\frac{1}{n^{1/\alpha}}\sum\limits_{m\in\frac{\mathbb{N}}{n^{1/\alpha}}}m^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2m^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=C\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}-1)n^{d/\alpha}\frac{1}{n^{1/\alpha}}\sum\limits_{m\in\frac{\mathbb{N}}{n^{1/\alpha}}}m^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2m^{\alpha}}

Then,
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}C\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}=C
and by l’Hôpital’s rule,

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}-1)n^{d/\alpha}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}-1)}{n^{-d/\alpha}}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{2\sigma^{2}\alpha\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}/n^{2}}}{dn^{2-d/\alpha}}

which we can see goes to
0
if
\alpha>d/2
.

Finally, using a Riemann sum,

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{1/\alpha}}\sum\limits_{m\in\frac{\mathbb{N}}{n^{1/\alpha}}}m^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2m^{\alpha}}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}x^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{\alpha}}dx

which is finite for all
\alpha>0
.

Thus,
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}[f_{n}(\Pi)]=0
for
\alpha>d/2
and we have deletion singularity for
\Pi
if
\alpha>d/2
by Proposition
1.4
. ∎

5.2
Proof of Proposition
1.14
(1)

Let
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
|S|=n
. We start by enumerating
\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\{z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},\ldots\}
in non-decreasing order of norm and take the random vector
P=(\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert^{\alpha}+g_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
with components given by
\Pi
ordered by our chosen enumeration. We would like to compare this with the random vector
P_{S}
that has components given by
\Pi_{S}
ordered by the same enumeration of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. However, if we simply remove the entries in
P
that came from
S\subset G
, we would be left with null coordinates in the vector
P_{S}
. In order to avoid null entries where the deletions would be, we must first shift the coordinates of
P_{S}
to align them with
P
.

To that end, we define the non-decreasing, surjective, deletion counting function
s:\mathbb{N}\to\{0,...,n\}

s(i)=|S\cap\{z_{0},...,z_{i}\}|.

Using this function, we define the vector
P_{S}=(\left\lVert z_{i+s(i)}\right\rVert^{\alpha}+g_{i+s(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
that has components given by
\Pi_{S}
ordered by our chosen enumeration of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

We make one final simplification, in order to center the coordinates of our random vectors, by applying the pushforward map

\Xi((a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}})=(a_{i}+\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert^{\alpha})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

to both vectors. Then,
\Xi(X)
gives the vector
P
and
\Xi(Y)
gives the vector
P_{S}
where

X=(g_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\quad Y=(g_{i+s(i)}+\left\lVert z_{i+s(i)}\right\rVert^{\alpha}-\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert^{\alpha})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

Since pushforwards preserve absolute continuity it suffices to show that the vectors
X
and
Y
are mutually absolutely continuous if and only if
\alpha<1/2
. Recall that
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
were taken to be i.i.d so we can rewrite
Y
equivalently as
(g_{i}+\left\lVert z_{i+s(i)}\right\rVert^{\alpha}-\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert^{\alpha})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in order to simplify notation.

Notice that
Y
is simply a shift of
X
since
Y_{i}=X_{i}+(\left\lVert z_{i+s(i)}\right\rVert^{\alpha}-\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert^{\alpha})
. Then it follows from
[
25
, Theorem 1]
that
X
and
Y
are mutually absolutely continuous if and only if the difference of their means converges in
\ell^{2}
.

Therefore, it suffices for mutual absolute continuity to show that the sum

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(\left\lVert z_{i+s(i)}\right\rVert^{\alpha}-\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert^{\alpha})^{2}

(5.1)

converges if and only if
\alpha<1/2
.

Now notice that when
s(i)=1
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and all
i
such that
\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert=k
, we have that
\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert^{\alpha}
and
\left\lVert z_{i+1}\right\rVert^{\alpha}
can differ for at most
1
value of
i
and that the difference will be
(k+1)^{\alpha}-k^{\alpha}
. Similarly, when
s(i)=2
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and all
i
such that
\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert=k
we have that
\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert^{\alpha}
and
\left\lVert z_{i+2}\right\rVert^{\alpha}
can differ for at most
2
values of
i
and that the difference will be at most
(k+2)^{\alpha}-k^{\alpha}
. Continuing up to
s(i)=n
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and all
i
such that
\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert=k
we have that
\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert^{\alpha}
and
\left\lVert z_{i+n}\right\rVert^{\alpha}
can differ for at most
n
values of
i
and that the difference will be at most
(k+n)^{\alpha}-k^{\alpha}
. Finally, by definition of
s(i)
, there exists
M
sufficiently large such that
s(i)=n
for all
i>M
. We can now bound the sum as follows:

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(\left\lVert z_{i+s(i)}\right\rVert^{\alpha}-\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert^{\alpha})^{2}\leq n^{2}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}((i+n)^{\alpha}-i^{\alpha})^{2}

which converges for
\alpha<1/2
by an immediate application of the mean value theorem.

We’ve now shown that for
\alpha<1/2
,
P
and
P_{S}
are mutually absolutely continuous for any
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfying
|S|<\infty
. Notice that for the specific choice,
S=\{0\}
, the sum (
5.1
) reduces to
\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}((i+1)^{\alpha}-i^{\alpha})^{2}
which converges only if
\alpha<1/2
. Thus we get that
P
and
P_{S}
are mutually absolutely continuous for every finite cardinality
S
if and only if
\alpha<1/2
. ∎

5.3
Proof of Proposition
1.14
(2)

Throughout this proof, we let
C
denote a positive constant independent of
n
whose exact value may change from line to line.
Let the
g_{z}
’s be i.i.d. copies of
g
.
By passing to the subsequence
r_{n}=n^{\alpha}
we can bound the variance of the linear statistic as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[f_{r_{n}}(\Pi)]=\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{Var}[f((\frac{\left\lVert m\right\rVert}{n})^{\alpha}+\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}})]

\displaystyle\geq C\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}m^{d-1}\mathrm{Var}[f((\frac{m}{n})^{\alpha}+\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}})]

\displaystyle\geq n^{-2\alpha}C\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}m^{d-1}\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}((\frac{m}{n})^{\alpha}+\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}})^{2}],

where the first inequality follows from the fact that there is on the order of
n^{d-1}
points
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\left\lVert z\right\rVert=n
as
n\to\infty
, and the final inequality follows from
[
6
, Proposition 3.2]
. We know that there exists an interval
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
and a real constant
\delta>0
such that
f^{\prime}(x)^{2}\geq\delta
for all
x\in[a,b]
. Therefore we get:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[f_{r_{n}}(\Pi)]\geq

\displaystyle Cn^{-2\alpha}\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}m^{d-1}\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}((\frac{m}{n})^{\alpha}+\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}})^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle Cn^{-2\alpha}\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}m^{d-1}\mathbb{P}[(\frac{m}{n})^{\alpha}+\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\in[a,b]]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle Cn^{-2\alpha}\sum_{m=\lceil a^{1/\alpha}n\rceil}^{\lfloor b^{1/\alpha}n\rfloor}n^{d-1}\mathbb{P}[g\in[n^{\alpha}a-m^{\alpha},n^{\alpha}b-m^{\alpha}]]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C\delta n^{-2\alpha}n^{d},

where the first inequality is the same as in the previous display; the second inequality follows from

\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(X)^{2}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\delta\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}(X)]=\delta\mathbb{P}[X\in[a,b]]
;
the third inequality follows from restricting which values of
m
we are summing over, then taking
m\geq\lceil a^{1/\alpha}n\rceil
, then noting that

(\frac{m}{n})^{\alpha}+\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\in[a,b]\iff g\in[n^{\alpha}a-m^{\alpha},n^{\alpha}b-m^{\alpha}];

and finally the last inequality follows from the fact that

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[an^{\alpha},bn^{\alpha}]\\
n\geq 1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}[g\in[n^{\alpha}a-y,n^{\alpha}b-y]]=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[an^{\alpha},bn^{\alpha}]\\
n\geq 1\end{subarray}}\int_{n^{\alpha}a-y}^{n^{\alpha}b-y}\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}dx=\int_{0}^{(b-a)}\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}dx>0

because the standard Gaussian density function is symmetric decreasing, and that the number of points
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\lceil a^{1/\alpha}n\rceil\leq m\leq\lfloor b^{1/\alpha}n\rfloor
is bounded below by a constant times
n
.
The proof is thus complete. ∎

0
, while leaving the negative elements in their place. As such, we let

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=(0+g_{0},1^{\alpha}+g_{1},-1^{2}+g_{-1},2^{\alpha}+g_{2},-2^{2}+g_{-2},\ldots),

\displaystyle P_{\{0\}}

\displaystyle=(1^{\alpha}+g_{1},2^{\alpha}+g_{2},-1^{2}+g_{-1},3^{\alpha}+g_{3},-2^{2}+g_{-2},\ldots).

Applying
[
25
, Theorem 1]
, we get that
P
and
P_{\{0\}}
are mutually absolutely continuous if the
\ell^{2}
difference in their means converges, i.e.,

\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}((n+1)^{\alpha}-n^{\alpha})^{2}<\infty.

This clearly holds for
\alpha<1/2
and thus
P
and
P_{\{0\}}
are mutually absolutely continuous for
\alpha<1/2
. Finally, the absolute continuity of
\Pi
and
\Pi_{\{0\}}
follows by Proposition
1.3
. ∎

5.6
Proof of Example
2.10

For
\alpha=1
we have the processes
\Pi_{\{1,...,m\}}=\sum\limits_{k>m}\delta_{k+g}
and
\Pi_{\{1,...,n\}}=\sum\limits_{k>n}\delta_{k+g}
. Note that, by a simple change of variables, we can rewrite these as

\Pi_{\{1,...,m\}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\delta_{k+(g+m)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Pi_{\{1,...,n\}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\delta_{k+(g+n)}.

Our assumption that
g
and
g+k
are mutually absolutely continuous for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
implies that
g+m
and
g+n
are mutually absolutely continuous for any fixed
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
; it immediately follows that the processes
\Pi_{\{1,...,m\}}
and
\Pi_{\{1,...,n\}}
are mutually absolutely continuous.

Now for
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, we will construct a measurable function
f
that distinguishes between the processes
\Pi
and
\Pi_{S}
for any
S\subset\mathbb{N}
. For a countable set
A=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
enumerated in non-decreasing order of
x_{i}
, define
f_{n}(A)=|x_{n+1}-x_{n}|
. Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
f_{n}(\Pi)=(n+1)^{\alpha}-n^{\alpha}
. When computing
f_{n}(\Pi_{S})
we consider two cases. When
S=\{1,\ldots,k\}
, we see that
f_{1}(\Pi_{S})=(k+2)^{\alpha}-(k+1)^{\alpha}>f_{1}(\Pi)=2^{\alpha}-1
. For any other
S
, there exists
n\in S
such that
n-1\notin S
. Then
f_{n-1}(\Pi_{S})>n^{\alpha}-(n-1)^{\alpha}=f_{n-1}(\Pi)
. It follows that
\Pi
and
\Pi_{S}
are singular for every choice of
S
and
\Pi
is deletion singular. ∎
