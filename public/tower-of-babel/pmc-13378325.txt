Nailfold capillaroscopy findings after anthracycline-based chemotherapy and their association with subclinical changes in left ventricular ejection fraction
Authors: Murat Kiracı, Gülşah Soytürk, Fahriye Tuğba Köş, Perihan Perkin, Şükran Erten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnthracyclines can induce endothelial and microvascular injury that precedes measurable myocardial dysfunction. However, reliable early markers of such injury are lacking. Nailfold capillaroscopy (NFC) is a noninvasive imaging technique that visualizes microcirculatory alterations. This pilot prospective study aimed to explore post-treatment NFC findings after anthracycline-based chemotherapy and their association with subclinical changes in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) in patients with breast cancer.MethodsTwenty-six patients with breast cancer scheduled for four cycles of neoadjuvant doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide (AC) were prospectively enrolled. All patients underwent baseline and post-chemotherapy echocardiography and post-treatment NFC assessment. Capillaroscopic abnormalities (tortuosity, crossing capillaries, extravasation, hemorrhage, dilated capillaries, giant capillaries, and neoangiogenesis) were recorded to generate a composite NFC score (0–7). Early cardiotoxicity was defined as ≥ 10% reduction in LVEF or LVEF < 53%. Correlations between change in ejection fraction (ΔEF) and NFC score were analyzed using Spearman’s test. Exploratory ROC analysis was performed using any EF decline as a sensitive pilot endpoint, and an additional sensitivity analysis was performed using a more conservative threshold of ΔEF ≥ 3%.ResultsThe mean age was 51.8 ± 11.2 years. Baseline and post-AC LVEF were 60.1 ± 2.2% and 58.4 ± 2.6%, respectively (ΔEF = -1.73 ± 2.15%). No clinical cardiotoxicity was observed. The mean composite NFC score was 3.15 ± 0.73. Higher NFC scores showed a negative correlation with ΔEF that did not reach statistical significance (Spearman ρ = -0.36, 95% CI: -0.67 to 0.00, p = 0.072). Exploratory ROC analysis using any EF decline as a sensitive pilot outcome yielded an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.57–0.95), with an optimal cutoff of NFC ≥ 4. In a sensitivity analysis using ΔEF ≥ 3%, the findings remained directionally consistent (AUC 0.79; optimal cutoff NFC ≥ 4).ConclusionsPost-treatment NFC abnormalities were frequently observed after anthracycline-based chemotherapy and showed an exploratory association with modest subclinical declines in left ventricular ejection fraction. As a prospective pilot study integrating NFC with paired echocardiographic assessment in a homogeneous anthracycline-treated breast cancer cohort, this work provides early hypothesis-generating evidence for a potential microvascular imaging approach in cardio-oncology. Given the absence of baseline NFC assessment and the non-significant primary correlation, these findings should be interpreted cautiously rather than as evidence of anthracycline-induced microvascular injury. Larger longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify the potential role of NFC in cardio-oncology.Keywords: Nailfold capillaroscopy, Anthracycline cardiotoxicity, Breast cancer, Microvascular injury, Cardio-oncology

Introduction

Anthracyclines such as doxorubicin and epirubicin remain a cornerstone of systemic therapy in breast cancer, yet their clinical success is constrained by dose-dependent cardiotoxicity that may progress to heart failure [1, 2]. Traditionally, surveillance strategies have focused on myocardial injury and overt ventricular dysfunction. However, recent evidence indicates that microvascular and endothelial injury may represent the earliest step in the anthracycline toxicity cascade [3]. Such endothelial perturbations impair nitric oxide bioavailability, alter capillary morphology, and reduce microcirculatory reserve long before detectable declines in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) [4].

The early detection of vascular injury remains a critical unmet need in cardio-oncology. Noninvasive tools capable of assessing the integrity of the microcirculation could transform early diagnosis and prevention. Nailfold capillaroscopy (NFC) is a well-established, noninvasive technique for direct in vivo assessment of peripheral microvascular architecture and is particularly well validated in rheumatologic disorders such as systemic sclerosis and Raynaud’s phenomenon [5]. Beyond simple visualization, NFC enables qualitative and semiquantitative assessment of morphologic abnormalities including tortuosity, dilated and giant capillaries, microhemorrhages, avascular areas, and neoangiogenesis, which may reflect different patterns and stages of microangiopathic injury [5–9]. In contemporary literature, these abnormalities are increasingly interpreted not merely as local nailfold findings, but as structural correlates of broader systemic microvascular dysfunction and endothelial injury. This conceptual framework is especially relevant in cardio-oncology, where endothelial perturbation and microcirculatory damage are increasingly recognized as early components of anthracycline-related cardiovascular toxicity. Despite this rationale, NFC has rarely been explored in oncology. Preliminary reports suggest that chemotherapy exposure may be associated with capillary morphological abnormalities detectable by capillaroscopy [10]. Yet, the temporal relationship between these microvascular alterations and early cardiac function has not been fully elucidated.

In this pilot prospective study, we sought to describe NFC findings after four cycles of neoadjuvant AC chemotherapy in breast cancer patients and to examine their association with early, subclinical changes in LVEF. Rather than establishing causality, this study was designed as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating assessment of whether post-treatment NFC abnormalities may parallel early cardiac functional changes in this setting.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-center, prospective, observational pilot study conducted at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital, Turkey, in collaboration between the Departments of Medical Oncology and Rheumatology. The study was performed between June 1, 2025, and November 1, 2025, and included consecutive women with histologically confirmed breast cancer scheduled to receive neoadjuvant AC chemotherapy. The study protocol was approved by the Ankara Bilkent City Hospital Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient selection

A total of 26 patients with histologically confirmed breast cancer were prospectively enrolled. Eligible participants were ≥ 18 years old, had accessible clinical, laboratory, imaging, and treatment data, and were scheduled to receive neoadjuvant anthracycline-based chemotherapy (doxorubicin + cyclophosphamide, AC regimen). Patients with previous exposure to cardiotoxic chemotherapy, documented ischemic heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or known connective tissue or vascular acrosyndrome disorders were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included documented exposure to toxic industrial agents (e.g., vinyl chloride), chronic microtrauma related to percussion or vibration instruments, and clinically evident upper-extremity lymphedema either before or after neoadjuvant therapy. No strict lower cutoff for baseline LVEF was applied; patients with baseline LVEF values < 55% were not excluded to better reflect real-world cardio-oncology practice. This approach aimed to capture a broader physiological spectrum of anthracycline exposure and potential microvascular response.

All patients were planned to undergo echocardiographic assessment both at baseline and after completion of four AC cycles, whereas NFC evaluation was scheduled only after treatment completion. As a pilot feasibility study, no formal sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was determined by consecutive enrollment during the study period.

Echocardiographic assessment

Echocardiographic evaluations were performed both at baseline and after completion of four cycles of AC chemotherapy using standard two-dimensional echocardiography. LVEF was determined in accordance with current echocardiographic guidelines, based on the biplane Simpson’s method. During the study period, serial GLS acquisition and archiving at both study time points were not systematically incorporated into the routine institutional echocardiography workflow; therefore, standardized paired GLS measurements were not consistently available for this pilot cohort. Accordingly, LVEF was used as the pragmatic echocardiographic parameter for within-cohort comparison, while recognizing that GLS would have been more sensitive for the detection of early subclinical systolic dysfunction. Echocardiograms were interpreted by cardiologists within the same institutional echocardiography unit, following routine clinical practice standards.

NFC protocol

Nailfold capillaroscopic examinations were performed only after completion of four cycles of AC chemotherapy, consistent with the original exploratory pilot design. Baseline NFC assessments were not included in the study workflow; therefore, temporal changes in capillaroscopic findings could not be assessed within individual patients. Evaluations were conducted by an experienced rheumatologist who was blinded to the patients’ echocardiographic findings. The second through fifth digits of both hands were examined under standard magnification using a digital capillaroscope. Capillary morphology was assessed for the presence of tortuosity, crossing capillaries, extravasation, hemorrhages, dilated capillaries, giant capillaries, and neoangiogenesis. These features were selected a priori because they are commonly described morphologic abnormalities in the capillaroscopy literature and were considered relevant to capturing a broad spectrum of potential post-treatment microvascular alterations in this exploratory pilot setting. Each feature was scored as absent (0) or present (1). A composite NFC score (ranging from 0 to 7) was then calculated to quantify the overall degree of microvascular abnormality. This composite score was developed de novo as a study-specific exploratory measure for the present pilot cohort and was not based on a previously validated scoring system. A binary scoring approach was chosen to provide a simple summary measure of abnormality burden in the absence of an oncology-specific validated NFC scoring system.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical data were presented as counts and percentages.

Changes in left ventricular ejection fraction (ΔEF) before and after chemotherapy were calculated by subtracting post-treatment values from baseline measurements. Differences between pre- and post-treatment EF values were analyzed using the paired samples t-test for normally distributed variables.

The relationships between the composite NFC score and ΔEF were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) due to the ordinal nature of the NFC data. Exploratory receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the ability of the NFC composite score to discriminate post-treatment LVEF decline. Because no patient met guideline-defined clinical cardiotoxicity criteria, “any EF decline” was analyzed as a sensitive exploratory pilot endpoint rather than a clinically established cardiotoxicity outcome. To address the clinical permissiveness of this endpoint, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed using a more conservative threshold of ΔEF ≥ 3%. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for both correlation coefficients and AUC were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 10,000 iterations. The Youden index was applied to identify the optimal cutoff point.

A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 26 patients receiving neoadjuvant AC chemotherapy were prospectively enrolled. The mean age was 51.8 ± 11.2 years, and the mean BMI was 27.3 ± 3.3 kg/m². Cardiovascular comorbidities included hypertension in 7 patients (26.9%), diabetes mellitus in 2 (7.7%), hypercholesterolemia in 2 (7.7%), and no patient had a documented history of ischemic heart disease, heart failure, arrhythmia, or prior cardiotoxic therapy. Six patients (23.1%) were active or former smokers. All patients had preserved systolic function at baseline, and none had prior exposure to cardiotoxic treatment. Exploratory ROC analyses for post-treatment LVEF decline

Exploratory ROC analysis using any post-treatment EF decline as a sensitive pilot endpoint yielded an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.57–0.95), with an optimal cutoff of NFC ≥ 4 (sensitivity 70.0%, specificity 87.5%) (Fig. 4). Because no patient met guideline-defined CTRCD criteria, this permissive endpoint was selected to explore early directional changes in LVEF rather than to model clinically established cardiotoxicity. In a sensitivity analysis using a more conservative endpoint of ΔEF ≥ 3%, the ROC findings remained directionally consistent, with an AUC of 0.79 and the same optimal cutoff of NFC ≥ 4. These analyses should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, rather than as evidence of a validated clinical prediction threshold.

Discussion

In this prospective pilot study of breast cancer patients receiving neoadjuvant AC chemotherapy, we observed a high prevalence of post-treatment NFC abnormalities in the absence of overt cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction. Although no patient developed guideline-defined cardiotoxicity, modest subclinical declines in LVEF were observed, and higher composite NFC scores were associated with greater EF reduction. These findings suggest that post-treatment capillaroscopic abnormalities may be associated with early cardiac functional changes; however, in the absence of baseline NFC assessment, they do not allow causal attribution to anthracycline exposure.

Anthracycline cardiotoxicity has traditionally been attributed to direct myocyte injury via topoisomerase IIβ-mediated DNA damage, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, calcium dysregulation, and apoptosis [11]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that the vascular endothelium and microcirculation are also primary targets of anthracycline toxicity. Endothelial cell damage can reduce nitric oxide bioavailability, promote inflammation and thrombosis, and impair vasodilatory reserve, thereby contributing to later myocardial dysfunction and heart failure [1]. Large animal models exposed to anthracyclines have demonstrated coronary microcirculatory rarefaction and perfusion defects that appear before overt systolic dysfunction, supporting the concept that microvascular injury is an early and potentially irreversible step in the cardiotoxic cascade [3].

Clinical data using flow-mediated dilation (FMD) and other endothelial metrics further support this paradigm. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of brachial FMD in anthracycline-treated patients showed consistent impairment in endothelial-dependent vasodilation, suggesting that vascular endothelial dysfunction is detectable early and may precede classic imaging evidence of cardiotoxicity [2]. Current position papers and state-of-the-art reviews increasingly frame anthracycline cardiotoxicity as a cardiovascular injury rather than a pure myocyte disease [12]. Our results add to this emerging view by showing that almost all patients exhibited capillaroscopic evidence of microvascular damage after only four cycles of AC, even though conventional LVEF remained above the threshold for CTRCD.

Contemporary cardio-oncology guidelines recommend a multimodal surveillance strategy for anthracycline-treated patients that integrates serial echocardiography (preferably with GLS), high-sensitivity cardiac troponins, natriuretic peptides, and clinical risk stratification [13]. Troponin elevation can identify patients at higher risk of subsequent LV dysfunction, although the data are heterogeneous and timing of sampling is critical [14]. Strain imaging improves sensitivity for subclinical dysfunction, but it remains operator-dependent, requires specific software and expertise, and may be limited by image quality in real-world oncology practice [15]. In our study, serial GLS measurements were not systematically available within the routine institutional echocardiography workflow during the study period; therefore, we relied on paired LVEF assessment as the pragmatic echocardiographic endpoint. This real-world constraint should be considered when interpreting the modest magnitude of functional change observed in the present cohort.

In contrast, NFC is widely available in rheumatology and microvascular research, where it is used to characterize microangiopathy in systemic sclerosis, mixed connective tissue disease, Behçet’s disease, and other vasculopathies [16]. Several recent studies have extended NFC to systemic inflammatory and microvascular conditions such as COVID-19 and Kawasaki disease, showing that capillaroscopic abnormalities can mirror endothelial dysfunction in other vascular beds [17]. Importantly, NFC is noninvasive, relatively inexpensive, and can be repeatedly performed at the bedside or in the outpatient setting.

Despite this, the use of NFC in oncology remains extremely limited. A small body of work has explored capillaroscopy to evaluate vascular side effects induced by chemotherapy, suggesting that NFC may detect treatment-related capillary changes, but systematic data in anthracycline-treated breast cancer cohorts are scarce [10]. Very few studies have specifically assessed NFC in relation to post-treatment microvascular abnormalities and cardiac functional change in anthracycline-treated patients. This positions the present study as an early contribution to defining NFC’s potential role in cardio-oncology.

In our cohort, tortuosity, crossing capillaries, and extravasation were the dominant NFC abnormalities, observed in 96.2%, 88.5%, and 76.9% of patients, respectively, whereas giant capillaries and neoangiogenesis were absent. This pattern differs from the “scleroderma spectrum” capillaroscopic profile, where giant capillaries, avascular areas, and neoangiogenesis are more characteristic [18]. Instead, the anthracycline-associated pattern in our patients appears more compatible with functional and structural changes related to endothelial injury, capillary wall permeability, and mild architectural remodeling, rather than advanced obliterative microangiopathy.

The moderate inverse relationship between the composite NFC score and ΔEF supports a dose-response-like association between the burden of nailfold microvascular abnormalities and subclinical LV impairment. Patients with higher NFC scores had more negative EF changes, even though absolute EF values remained above the conventional cardiotoxicity threshold. This is concordant with mechanistic models in which endothelial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, and microvascular rarefaction precede measurable LVEF decline and may eventually facilitate progressive cardiomyocyte loss [12].

Several important limitations of our correlation analysis warrant emphasis. First, the modest sample size (n = 26) limits statistical power, and the observed correlation, while moderate in magnitude (ρ=-0.36), approached but did not achieve statistical significance (p = 0.072). This borderline p-value suggests a potentially meaningful association that requires validation in larger cohorts. The 95% confidence interval for the correlation coefficient ranged from − 0.67 to 0.00, indicating substantial uncertainty in the true population parameter. Furthermore, NFC score explained only 13% of the variance in ΔEF (r²=0.129), suggesting that multiple factors beyond microvascular injury contribute to early LVEF changes. These findings underscore the exploratory nature of this pilot study and highlight the critical need for adequately powered prospective validation studies before NFC can be recommended for clinical risk stratification.

The ROC findings should be interpreted with caution. In the absence of overt CTRCD events, ROC analysis based on “any EF decline” was used only as a permissive exploratory endpoint to detect early directional changes in LVEF in this small pilot cohort. While this approach may be useful for signal detection, it is not equivalent to a clinically validated cardiotoxicity outcome. To address this limitation, we additionally examined a more conservative threshold of ΔEF ≥ 3% as a sensitivity analysis, and the ROC findings remained directionally consistent (AUC 0.79; optimal cutoff NFC ≥ 4; sensitivity 71.4%; specificity 78.9%). Overall, these results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as clinically actionable thresholds.

If confirmed in larger cohorts, our findings suggest that NFC may warrant future evaluation within multimodal risk stratification frameworks in anthracycline-treated patients. One possible application would be a stepwise approach, in which conventional risk factors (age, hypertension, cumulative dose) and guideline-based tools (baseline LVEF, strain, troponin) are used to define a baseline risk category, and NFC is then deployed in intermediate-risk patients to refine risk estimates based on microvascular injury [15].

Another potential implication is translational: NFC may be explored as a research biomarker in future mechanistic studies. Several emerging cardioprotective approaches such as dexrazoxane, statins, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, beta-blockers, or lifestyle interventions may exert part of their benefit via improved endothelial function and microvascular stability [15]. Incorporating NFC into interventional trials could provide mechanistic insight into whether such therapies truly preserve the microcirculation in parallel with myocardial function.

The strengths of this study include its prospective design, homogeneous population of breast cancer patients receiving a standardized AC regimen, and systematic assessment of both cardiac function and microvascular status within a real-world oncology setting. We used a composite NFC score that captures the cumulative burden of multiple abnormalities, which aligns with the notion that the overall injury load may be more informative than any single morphological feature.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Most critically, baseline capillaroscopy was not performed. Therefore, we cannot determine whether the observed post-treatment abnormalities were newly developed during chemotherapy or were already present before treatment. Pre-existing microvascular changes related to age, hypertension, diabetes, smoking exposure, or other unmeasured factors may have contributed to the high prevalence of findings such as tortuosity and crossing capillaries. Although we excluded patients with known connective tissue disease, vascular acrosyndrome, toxic industrial exposure, chronic vibration-related microtrauma, and clinically evident upper-extremity lymphedema, residual confounding from pre-existing microvascular vulnerability cannot be excluded. Accordingly, our data should be interpreted as showing an association between post-treatment NFC abnormalities and subclinical LVEF change, rather than demonstrating anthracycline-induced microvascular injury. Future studies incorporating both pre-treatment and serial longitudinal post-treatment NFC assessments will be essential to clarify temporal changes, determine whether microvascular abnormalities persist or progress over time, and better define any treatment-related component as well as their relationship to longer-term cardiac outcomes.

Second, the sample size is modest, and the study was not powered to detect hard clinical outcomes or rare cardiotoxic events. The absence of overt CTRCD may reflect both the relatively short follow-up limited to the neoadjuvant phase and the relatively preserved baseline risk profile of this cohort. Third, we relied on LVEF rather than GLS, which is more sensitive for detecting early subclinical systolic dysfunction and is preferred in contemporary cardio-oncology guidance. This reflected the fact that serial, standardized GLS acquisition was not systematically incorporated into the institutional echocardiography workflow during the study period, and paired GLS measurements were therefore not consistently available for all participants. As a consequence, our echocardiographic endpoint may have underestimated very early functional changes. Future confirmatory studies should incorporate both baseline and serial GLS assessments. In addition, the ROC cutoff and performance metrics stem from exploratory analysis and may be unstable; they should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive thresholds for clinical use. Finally, although NFC provides morphological assessment of peripheral microcirculation, its correlation with coronary microvascular injury remains inferential and warrants further validation. In addition, the composite NFC score used in this study was developed de novo for exploratory purposes and has not been externally validated; therefore, its performance and interpretability require confirmation in independent cohorts.

As this was a pilot feasibility study, no formal sample size calculation was performed a priori. However, to inform the design of a future confirmatory study, we performed a post hoc sample size estimation based on the observed correlation coefficient (ρ = -0.36). Using a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and 80% power, approximately 59 evaluable patients would be required to detect a similar association. Allowing for a modest proportion of non-evaluable cases or missing paired assessments, a target sample size of approximately 65 patients would be reasonable for prospective validation.

Conclusions

In this pilot prospective cohort, post-treatment NFC abnormalities were frequently observed after anthracycline-based chemotherapy and showed an exploratory association with modest subclinical declines in LVEF. Given the absence of baseline NFC assessment, these findings should not be interpreted as establishing anthracycline-induced microvascular injury. Rather, they support further longitudinal studies with pre- and serial post-treatment capillaroscopy to determine whether NFC may have future value as an adjunctive marker of early cardiovascular change in cardio-oncology, including whether capillaroscopic abnormalities persist or progress over time and whether they relate to longer-term cardiac outcomes.
