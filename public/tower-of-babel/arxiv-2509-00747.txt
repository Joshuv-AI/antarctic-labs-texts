Self-Organising Memristive Networks as Physical Learning Systems
Francesco Caravelli, Gianluca Milano, Adam Stieg, Carlo Ricciardi, Simon A. Brown, Zdenka Kuncic

Abstract
Learning with physical systems is an emerging paradigm that seeks to harness the intrinsic nonlinear dynamics of physical substrates for learning. The impetus for a paradigm shift in how hardware is used for computational intelligence stems largely from the unsustainability of artificial neural network software implemented on conventional transistor-based hardware. This Perspective highlights one promising approach using physical networks comprised of resistive memory nanoscale components with dynamically reconfigurable, self-organising electrical circuitry. Experimental advances have revealed the non-trivial interactions within these Self-Organising Memristive Networks (SOMNs), offering insights into their collective nonlinear and adaptive dynamics, and how these properties can be harnessed for learning using different hardware implementations. Theoretical approaches, including mean-field theory, graph theory, and concepts from disordered systems, reveal deeper insights into the dynamics of SOMNs, especially during transitions between different conductance states where criticality and other dynamical phase transitions emerge in both experiments and models. Furthermore, parallels between adaptive dynamics in SOMNs and plasticity in biological neuronal networks suggest the potential for realising energy-efficient, brain-like continual learning. SOMNs thus offer a promising route toward embedded edge intelligence, unlocking real-time decision-making for autonomous systems, dynamic sensing, and personalised healthcare, by enabling embedded learning in resource-constrained environments. The overarching aim of this Perspective is to show how the convergence of nanotechnology, statistical physics, complex systems, and self-organising principles offers a unique opportunity to advance a new generation of physical intelligence technologies.

2
Brain-like dynamics in SOMNs

To understand how SOMNs may be used as physical learning systems, it is first necessary to
examine the physical mechanisms behind their nonlinear dynamics.

Nanoscale transport dynamics and a heterogeneous network connectivity underpin the ability of SOMNs to produce emergent brain-like properties, including: plasticity – the ability to adapt to changing input signals; and avalanche criticality – signal propagation exhibiting long-range spatio-temporal correlations
[
24
,
28
]
.
This section describes how these dynamics arise in the context of the most well studied SOMNs, comprised of nanowire (NW) and nanoparticle (NP) networks.
Both NW and NP networks show similar brain-like dynamics related to collective memristive switching effects (see Fig.
2
). However, the underlying nanoscale transport physics in single NW-NW and NP-NP junctions is notably different.

2.1
Physics of ionic/electronic transport

The dynamics of NW networks are governed by ionic transport in the junctions between NWs. Here, NWs comprised of an electromigrating metal and a dielectric are assembled in a random arrangement where each contact region between two NWs forms a nanoscale electrical junction
[
41
,
42
]
. Most commonly, NW networks employ silver as the electromigrating metal with either a polymer coating (e.g., Polyvinylpyrrolidone, PVP)
[
43
,
24
,
44
]
and/or metal chalcogenide (e.g., Ag
2
S
[
12
,
45
]
), though other materials have also been explored (e.g. Ag-TiO
2

[
46
]
, AgI
[
47
]
, Ag
2
Se
[
48
,
49
]
). In the nanoscale junctions, the electric field is high enough to drive the dissolution of atomic Ag to Ag
+
that then electromigrates towards the opposite wire, where it nucleates back to metallic Ag, forming conductive nanofilaments in the junction
[
44
]
. Counter to this additive process, minimisation of the interfacial energy and local electromotive force effects (nanobattery effect) act to dissolve the nanofilaments, becoming the dominant process when the applied voltage is removed. Therefore, similarly to biological neurons where the synaptic strength is associated with changes in ionic flow and to membrane depolarisation and spontaneous repolarisation, NW junctions modulate their conductance depending on input history and are characterised by a short-term memory ranging from milliseconds to seconds. This mechanism is one of the most common memristive behaviours, at the basis of Electrochemical Metallization (ECM) memories and atomic switch devices
[
50
]
. In addition to the external electrical bias, the main intrinsic factors are ion mobility and redox rates
[
51
]
.

Zooming out at the macroscopic level, NW networks are characterised by metastable states induced by the collective interactions among many memristive junctions where nanofilaments are continuously reshaped due to the local redistributions of electrical potential and current. Under voltage sweep stimulation, NW networks exhibit a collective hysteresis response characteristic of memristive systems (cf. Fig.
2
(a)).

When large external biases are applied or local inhomogeneities are present, Joule heating can induce the local breakdown of single nanowires, creating gaps where metal ions electromigrate and form nanofilaments, similarly to NW junctions
[
52
]
. While the short-term dynamics of such new memristive elements is likely indistinguishable from those of a nanowire junction, the network topology, along with all the local redistributions of electrical potential and current, is irreversibly changed, thus giving to the network a long-term memory characteristic. With a terminology borrowed from the human connectome, the two mechanisms are soundly referred to as reweighting, when an existing synapse (junction) is modulated in conductance, and rewiring, when a new synapse (gap) is created
[
44
]
.

The conductivity mechanisms in NP networks differ from those in NW networks. There is currently no evidence for electrochemical ionic effects, at least in the most studied system comprised of tin NPs
[
53
,
16

that are typically studied in vacuum. Interestingly, Au discontinuous films
[

have similar mechanisms, in both air and in vacuum. Electrochemical effects might be expected in, for example, networks of Ag NPs (where electrochemical properties are expected to be similar to that in NW networks)
[
55
]
, but detailed studies of the mechanisms are still required.

The memristive mechanism in NP networks is the following: under an external electrical bias, the strong electric field in the gaps between NPs causes surface atoms to move

These properties result in self-adaptation to changes in the input signals (synapse-like plasticity) and as well as critical behaviour (neuron-like avalanches). These topics are explored in more depth in the next two subsections.

2.2
Plasticity

Plasticity refers to the capacity of a system to modulate its internal states or connectivity in response to external stimuli.
In biological neuronal networks, plasticity is fundamental to learning and memory, enabling organisms to retain, discard, and process information in response to new experiences.
Such adaptability spans multiple timescales and mechanisms, from transient modulation of synaptic strength (short-term plasticity) to longer-lasting changes that underlie memory formation. Physical systems such as SOMNs can similarly exhibit dynamic reconfiguration in response to stimulation,
reflecting not only direct, stimulus-specific conductance changes (analogous to homosynaptic plasticity), but also broader network-level adaptations and structural changes (analogous to heterosynaptic plasticity). These capabilities emerge naturally from the interplay of local transport dynamics and the global architecture of the network.

Following experimental demonstrations of memristive devices as synaptic elements in neuromorphic circuits
[
85
,
86
]
, a wide range of synaptic functionalities has been realised in SOMNs due to their capability to dynamically reconfigure in response to external electrical stimuli, particularly in multiterminal configurations.
Experimental results show that the emergent behaviour of NW networks can emulate input-specific changes in synaptic conductance pathways, resembling
homosynaptic plasticity
. Additionally, multiterminal memristive devices demonstrate that plasticity can also extend to synaptic pathways not directly stimulated, effectively mimicking
heterosynaptic plasticity
effects
[
44
,
87
]
.
These networks also exhibit
short-term plasticity
, where synaptic weights—represented by the conductance between neuron-like terminals—depend on recent stimulation history, as evidenced by potentiation–during stimulation followed by spontaneous relaxation after the end of stimulation (see Fig.
2
(b)). This effect exploits volatile memristive
mechanisms inherent in network elements
[
44
,
88
,
31
]
. Short-term memory effects, experimentally demonstrated to be controllable across multiple timescales
[
44
]
, enable the emulation of working memory processes observed in biological brains. Working memory includes not only the temporary retention of information but also its active manipulation
[
88
,
89
]
. Crucially, these short-term memory effects facilitate the physical implementation of reservoir computing paradigms, discussed in sec.
4.1
.

Beyond short-term effects, NW networks can also preserve previously established pathways while forming new connections. This capability mirrors
metaplasticity
in biological systems, where synaptic plasticity depends on the historical context of prior modifications
[
89
]
. Furthermore, changes in the NW network’s physical topology, known as
structural plasticity
, have been observed through electrically induced rupture and electrochemical rewiring of NWs
[
44
,
52
]
. These long-lasting changes in SOMNs can be exploited to emulate long-term plasticity effects, a crucial aspect for storing knowledge in the system during learning. In this context, using tomographical techniques, it has been experimentally demonstrated that spatially correlated short-term plasticity effects can evolve into long-lasting memory traces resembling biological
memory engrams
, which represent physicochemical changes underlying stored experiences in the brain
[
56
]
.
Similarly, short-term and long-term memory functionalities have been reported in NP networks
[
90
]
. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the potential of SOMNs as substrates for both processing information and storing knowledge at the material level.

In summary, brain–like plasticity effects exhibited by SOMNs reflects their adaptive dynamics and memory properties. While these properties originate from localised memristive junctions, their dynamic network circuitry results in global reconfigurations and associated memory changes. The following section highlights another example of dynamic reconfiguration exhibited by both biological neuronal networks and SOMNs.

2.3
Criticality

Critical phenomena play a fundamental role in various physical systems, characterised by divergent spatio-temporal correlations and the breakdown of mean-field approximations. Classic equilibrium examples include the paramagnetic–ferromagnetic phase transition described by the Ising model. Importantly, similar critical behaviours emerge in non-equilibrium biological neuronal networks
[
91
]
, where they are believed to underpin efficient information processing.
Here, we highlight that similar criticality has also been experimentally observed in SOMNs, specifically NW and NP networks
[
16
,
75
,
92
,
93
,
94
,
95
]
.

NW networks, which are generally fabricated above the percolation density threshold, exhibit criticality when networks are electrically stimulated just above a critical applied voltage
[
93
,
94
]
.
Avalanche criticality observed in Ag-based NW networks is due to electrical percolation when the network dynamics is dominated by electron tunnelling in the junctions
[
96
,
97
]
.
This occurs when junction tunnel gaps form as Ag filaments evolve. These gaps continuously close and re-open as filaments grow and stochastically decay, driving rapid memristive switching between metastable conductance states.
At voltages well above the critical voltage, some memristive junctions can form stable filaments that support ballistic transport. When this occurs, NW networks undergo a discontinuous dynamical phase transition resembling dielectric breakdown (also referred to as a “bolt phase”), characterised by the emergence of large amplitude avalanche events
[
98
,
99
,
93
,
97
]
.
Recent experiments
[
95
]
provide deeper insights into critical phenomena in NW networks. These studies demonstrate the programmable induction of critical states via targeted electrical stimulation. Importantly, spatially distinct regions within NW networks can exhibit and sustain local critical dynamics, facilitating spatially correlated interactions that directly influence computational functionality.

While both NW and NP networks show critical dynamics, their measured exponents deviate from known universality classes, hinting at novel behaviour.
This may arise due to higher–order interactions or specialised percolation dynamics not fully captured by theoretical percolation models
[
100
,
101
]
, as previously suggested
[
102
,
58
,
103
]
. In particular, the combination of tunneling transport and memristive switching dynamics may distinguish SOMNs from classical percolation universality classes.
Recent studies show that tuning network dynamics, via global feedback
[
104
]
or local control of junctions
[
95
]
, can steer systems toward different universality classes, offering pathways to control and apply criticality in SOMNs.

Avalanche criticality in SOMNs is an important concept, as it is a prominent feature that mirrors experiments performed in neuroscience
[
105
]
. It is in fact hypothesised that functioning near this point optimises information processing
[
77
,
78
,
91
]
. SOMNs are controllable and experimentally accessible physical systems, and offer valuable abiotic platforms for rigorously testing this hypothesis through direct implementation and analysis of computational tasks (discussed further in sec.
4
).

3
SOMNs as dynamical complex systems

As highlighted in the previous section, SOMNs are complex and dynamic electrical circuits that produce emergent nonlinear dynamics. Here, we present a theoretical framework that explicitly treats SOMNs as dynamical complex systems subject to the physical constraints of Kirchhoff’s electrical conservation laws. This theoretical framework enables models to be developed to gain deeper insights into how the experimentally observable collective dynamics arise from the memristive network interactions.

In particular, these methods are useful for identifying dynamical transitions and to characterise the dynamical regimes that may enhance learning
[
106
,
107
,
108
]
, as elaborated in sec.
4
.

3.1
Theoretical Methods

A practical theoretical framework for modelling SOMNs is the lumped circuit approximation.
By representing their conductance dynamics using memristive elements, with voltage drops across the junctions, SOMNs can be modelled effectively as assemblies of interacting subcircuits, each corresponding to an individual memristive junction (neglecting the resistance of individual NWs or NPs). Their interactions are governed by Kirchhoff’s laws, resulting in complex, high-dimensional nonlinear dynamical systems.

A convenient way to mathematically capture the diverse nonlinear dynamics observed experimentally is to model each junction’s conductance as a dynamical variable dependent on its voltage or current history. This approach provides a simplified, circuit-inspired model that captures memory and adaptation using only voltage, current, and time, e.g. quantities that are physical observables.
This method applies to both NW
[
109
]
and NP
[
110
]
networks and can be extended to other systems
[
111
]
.

The internal state of each memristive junction typically evolves according to a first-order differential equation of the general form (cf.
Box 1
):

\frac{dg}{dt}=F\bigl(g,\,\delta v(t),\,t\bigr),\quad G=G(g),

(3)

where
g\in[0,1]
represents a normalized internal memory parameter, and
v(t)
denotes the local instantaneous voltage across the junction. The function
F
encodes how the conductance changes in response not only to
v(t)
but also to make explicit time dependence, allowing modelling of more complex memory behaviours. Equation
G(g)
represents the electrical conductance of the junction in terms of
g
.
An equivalent representation can also be made in terms of electrical resistance.

While the lumped-element model introduced above is widely applicable (particularly for NW networks), alternative modeling approaches have been developed for NP networks, where different memristive mechanisms are at play. For instance, a potentiation–depression rate balance equation, originally derived from detailed studies of single memristive junctions
[
112
]
, has been used to describe dynamic conductance changes at both the junction level
[
27
]
and in spatially extended networks via a grid-graph modeling framework
[
87
]
. These rate-based models offer a complementary theoretical perspective by naturally capturing key plasticity phenomena, including short-term plasticity, metaplasticity, and structural plasticity, as emergent from cumulative electrical activity. As such, they are especially well-suited for describing the temporal evolution of NP networks, where conductance modulation often involves field-induced atomic rearrangement rather than ionic transport mechanisms typical of NW systems.

The lumped-element approximation simplifies the system by reducing each junction to a single memory variable, omitting other physical degrees of freedom that may affect network dynamics. Small but finite capacitive effects at the junctions, though often excluded from basic memristive models, have been experimentally observed and may become significant under certain conditions
[
113
,
114
]
. These effects introduce distinct memory-like dynamics and can be examined using techniques such as Nyquist impedance spectroscopy.

From a practical standpoint, Kirchhoff’s laws can be numerically solved for a given network and initial junction states to determine voltages and currents across each element. Integrating the dynamical equations (
3
) then reveals the time evolution of the conductances. This direct approach is widely used in the literature
[
75
,
110
,
27
,
93
,
74
]
to assess the effective conductance and functional behaviour of SOMNs.

To expose how topology and constraints shape dynamics and to gain analytical insight,
an analytical method using the projector operator formalism (see
Box 2
) has been developed
[
115
]
. This incorporates Kirchhoff’s constraints into the junction evolution equations via projection matrices:
\Omega_{B}
in the nodal formalism and
\Omega_{A}
in the mesh formalism.
Within this framework, the network dynamics reduce to a system of coupled nonlinear ordinary differential equations:

\frac{d\vec{g}}{dt}=\vec{\mathcal{F}}\bigl(\vec{g},\,\vec{\delta v(t)},\,\Omega_{B},\,t\bigr),

(4)

where
\Omega_{B}
ensures explicit enforcement of Kirchhoff’s laws.

In equations like (
4
), nonlinear memory effects emerge from the interplay between network topology and time-dependent junction conductances. Unlike resistive networks with fixed internal states, memristive networks adapt their conductance (or resistance) in response to external inputs. The topology is captured via projector matrices,
\Omega
, which typically appear in the equations when Kirchhoff’s laws are implemented analytically
also via matrix inverses of the form
(I\pm\chi\Omega\mathcal{G})^{-1}
, where
\mathcal{G}_{ii}=g_{i}
is a diagonal matrix
[
115
,
109
]
.

This formalism can be extended to more general network motifs, where edges represent subcircuits with complex behaviour, such as memristive, inductive, or capacitive coupling
[
116
]
. Though not explored in detail here, such extensions may be important for accurately modelling phenomena like the experimentally observed capacitive effects in SOMNs
[
113
,
114
]
.
Crucially, the matrix inverse structure in the projector formalism induces nontrivial feedback loops within the network. These loops underpin emergent behaviours such as
self-organisation and dynamical phase transitions
[
109
]
, providing a unified analytical framework for linking local memristive dynamics to rich global behaviour.

Incorporating stochasticity into SOMN dynamics offers a powerful extension of the theoretical framework.
Stochasticity is a fundamental characteristic of NP networks
[
75
]
, particularly in the high-voltage regime where the formation and rupture of conductive filaments leads to continuous redistributions of voltage and current, thereby triggering new switching events. The inherent complexity of these networks
[
58
]
further implies that the timing of such events is effectively random. Moreover, the filament formation process itself is intrinsically stochastic
[
76
]
.
Accordingly, SOMNs can be modelled as stochastic dynamical systems
[
30
]
, with network conductance described, for example, by an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process that blends stimulus-driven evolution with intrinsic fluctuations. This stochastic mean-field approach captures features such as conductance noise and jumps, while enabling analysis of the potential landscape and the interplay between noise, steady states, and computational capability. In this way, stochastic models provide deeper insight into collective behaviour that cannot be fully described by deterministic frameworks.

The “solid–liquid brain” metaphor
[
117
]
illustrates the adaptability of SOMNs: unlike static resistive networks (“solid brains”), SOMNs act as “liquid brains”, flexibly adapting to stimuli, like biological systems
[
118
,
119
]
.
Moreover, equations of the form (
4
) incorporate memory and topology while enabling Lyapunov function construction, allowing stability analysis akin to Hopfield networks. These functions reveal attractor dynamics in a potential landscape
[
120
]
, underpinning computational features such as nonlinear responses, metastability, and configuration exploration, which are key for optimisation tasks.

3.2
Mean field theory of conductance transitions

Critical phenomena in SOMNs are closely linked to self-organisation and feedback dynamics, which enable adaptive states under external driving. These behaviours often appear as scale–free event distributions—such as avalanches
[
16
]
—and are key to understanding emergent correlations. In SOMNs, global conductance can exhibit sharp transitions with voltage or component density, arising from the interplay between local dynamics and global connectivity. The mechanisms behind these critical states warrant further theoretical investigation.
Related studies in neuromorphic nanowire networks
[

,
94

95

, percolating NP networks
[

Theoretical insights into such transitions have also been drawn from analogies to random fuse models
[

, dielectric breakdown
[

, and tunneling-dominated percolation
[

, which are relevant to understanding the observed deviations from classical universality classes in SOMNs
[

Mean-field models have been developed for the percolation transition
[

]

[

While such mean-field approaches offer insight into general system behaviour, they fall short in accurately predicting critical exponents, which require more refined analysis
[

and consideration of effects like tunneling that are normally not consisdered in percolation models
[

Box 2: From Circuit Topology to Projector Operators

To build intuition, a memristive network can be viewed as a dynamic plumbing system: voltages act like pressures, currents like fluid flow. Projector operators then serve as mathematical tools that enforce physical constraints, ensuring flow conservation at junctions. They help capture how electrical signals propagate through the network following Kirchhoff’s laws, enabling analysis of dynamic information pathways.
The projector operator formalism described here for the study of memristive networks is derived from the lumped circuit topology approximation of a memristive network.
For a graph-theoretic and more formal introduction to these projectors, see Ref.
[

116

The projector operators are of the form

\Omega_{B}=B^{T}(BB^{T})^{-1}B,\hskip 28.45274pt\Omega_{A}=A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}

Projector operators satisfy

For a resistive circuit, each branch

B_{ji}=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if edge }j\text{ leaves vertex }i,\\
+1&\text{if edge }j\text{ enters vertex }i,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

On the other hand, given arbitrary branches (currents) and mesh orientations (defined as a closed sequence of branches), Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL) can be written in terms of

A_{il}=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if edge }i\text{ is oriented alike

In summary, theoretical models provide a foundation for understanding how SOMNs generate the collective nonlinear dynamics observed experimentally.

With an understanding of the mechanisms that underlie adaptive dynamics in SOMNs, we now show how these properties can be operationalised in physical learning systems.

Here, we highlight how physical learning can be achieved with SOMNs by exploiting their local and global brain-like nonlinear dynamics (including memory), without requiring direct addressability or control of their memristive circuit components.

4.2
Associative learning

Associative learning, i.e. the formation of associations between stimuli and responses, can be implemented in SOMNs by taking advantage of their adaptive conductance changes (i.e. plasticity, cf. sec.

).
External electrical stimuli applied to SOMNs drive local conductance changes that can lead to global reconfigurations in network states as unique conductance pathways are established for different patterns in the stimuli

In contrast to reservoir computing, learning is intrinsic to the network: repeated stimulation leads to material-level changes in conductance pathways, enabling SOMNs to self-organise and recall associations without external training mechanisms. This is more analogous to biological synaptic plasticity than statistical optimisation.
Teaching SOMNs input–output relationships via feedback, without requiring explicit programming also contrasts sharply with conventional implementations of physical neural networks, which typically rely on direct internal state addressability, for example, to physically implement Hopfield associative memory networks
[

Both positive and negative feedback enables SOMNs to adapt their electrical connectivity and conductance paths iteratively, to promote the emergence of robust functional mappings.

5
Outlook

The brain demonstrates that intelligence can emerge from the physical properties of matter. This realisation continues to motivate the search for materials and architectures with innate brain-like physical properties that may thus be considered promising substrates for emulating core cognitive functions such as memory and learning. Self-organising memristive networks are compelling candidates in this endeavour. As highlighted in this Perspective, the emergent nonlinear dynamics produced by these non-equilibrium physical systems can be exploited in adaptive learning paradigms, without the need for explicit programming.

This sets the stage for developing broader technological applications and exploring long-term implications.

Central to their role in physical learning systems is the emergence of global, collective dynamics from relatively simple, local interactions. Nanoscale transport processes (i.e. electron and ionic migration, and atomic rearrangement) continuously modulate junction-level conductance. These local dynamics propagate globally under Kirchhoff constraints and the complex network topology gives rise to dynamical phase transitions, a hallmark of emergent phenomena.
This interplay between local plasticity and global self-organisation underpins the functional properties that serve to encode information and enable learning.

Here, we have highlighted two learning paradigms, reservoir computing and associative learning, that leverage the internal physics of self–organising memristive networks. A range of other schemes are now under development, such as those based on spiking computation
[
82
]
, probabilistic computing
[
83
,
84
]
and energy–based algorithms (e.g. Hopfield networks, Boltzmann machines) that use binary units which may be physically instantiated with thresholded memristive switching. Recently it was shown that SOMNs that incorporate both spiking neural characteristics and memristive synapses allow new types of learning behaviour
[
167
]
.
Another research question that arises is: what determines the upper bound of the ability of these memristive networks to process information (i.e. compression, coding/decoding of data)? In some cases, it may be possible to minimise the amount of external computation required to obtain a desired output, effectively making the learning training–free and independent of learning algorithms
[
168
]
.

From a theoretical standpoint, progress in modeling memristive networks, using tools such as graph-theoretic projector operators or mean-field approximations, continues to enhance our understanding of their functional capacity. These models enable us to describe and even guide the evolution of useful dynamical states, allowing for a form of
guided self-organisation

[
169
]
. By tuning network density, topology, or stimulation protocols, one can steer the system toward regimes that balance flexibility, stability, and computational richness. On a pragmatic level, recent modelling work has demonstrated the way that different memristor types affect reservoir computing performance
[
170
]

Altogether, self-organising memristive networks represent a material route toward physically embodied intelligence. They shift the paradigm from computing architectures that are engineered and explicitly programmed, to systems that are grown and trained through physical interaction and feedback. By embedding function into structure, and adaptation into dynamics, these materials may offer a path toward scalable, autonomous learning platforms that mirror both the form and function of biological cognition. Taken together, this convergence of physical dynamics and computational function positions SOMNs not merely as neuromorphic analogs, but as a distinct, material foundation for intelligent physical systems.

Ultimately, such systems suggest a future where intelligence is not imposed onto matter, but emerges from it; where computation is not merely symbolic, but physical; not strictly deterministic, but adaptive; and not rigidly encoded, but continuously evolving. In this framework, self–organising memristive networks are a resource that can be harnessed for a new generation of brain–inspired physical learning architectures.
