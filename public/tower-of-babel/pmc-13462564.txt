Ca2+ regulation of PMCA2 calcium pump expression in hair cells of Tmc1 deafness mutants
Authors: Amanda Bryn Rolseth, Maryline Beurg, Dakota Elle Konrad, Benjamin K. August, Robert Fettiplace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCochlear hair cell transduction arises by activation of mechanotransducer (MET) channels containing transmembrane channel-like protein 1 (TMC1). Mutations in Tmc1 cause hair cell death and deafness in mice by postnatal day (P)21. However, at P6, MET channels are gated normally, though with diminished Ca2+ permeability. What therefore triggers apoptosis and deafness? In those Tmc1 mutants studied, the PMCA2 Ca2+ pump which extrudes Ca2+ ions from the stereocilia decreased in density. Furthermore, culturing isolated cochleae in reduced Ca2+ lowered PMCA2 density. We argue that the PMCA2 protein is stored in vesicles near the hair bundle, and Ca2+ regulates its insertion into stereocilia, which is reduced in Tmc1 mutants. Our results support a link between PMCA2 density, Ca2+ homeostasis, and deafness.

The sensation of hearing is realized in the cochlea, where sound-evoked mechanical vibrations are converted into electrical signals by the sensory hair cells via activation of their mechano-electrical transducer (MET) ion channels (1). The TMC1 protein is thought to be the pore-forming component of the hair cell MET channel (2–4), and there are multiple point mutations of the TMC1 gene yielding single amino acid substitutions that culminate in human deafness (5). Previously described semidominant mouse (and human) mutations, Tmc1 p.M412K, Tmc1 p.T416K, and Tmc1 p.D569N are all linked to early hearing loss and hair cell degeneration (6, 7). While the phenotypic changes in MET channel properties of these Tmc1 mutants have been well documented (6, 8–11), the pathways to hair cell degeneration and apoptosis are unknown, especially since large MET currents can still be observed in these mutants at P6. It is paramount to uncover the mechanisms leading to apoptosis in Tmc1 mutants, because hair cell death limits the ability of gene therapy to fully restore hearing, especially at high frequencies (12, 13), where some irreversible cell death may have already occurred at the time of gene correction. We previously found that Tmc1 mutations were associated with reduced expression of the plasma membrane PMCA2 calcium pump, and we hypothesize that any resulting elevation in hair cell [Ca2+] may contribute to hair cell death in Tmc1 mutants (14), though we have not directly measured increases in cytoplasmic [Ca2+].

There are two main sites of Ca2+ entry in cochlear hair cells: one via the MET channels in the hair bundle and the other through Cav1.3 voltage-dependent Ca2+ channels on the cell body adjacent to the auditory nerve fiber synaptic terminals. A likely minor and intermittent source of Ca2+ influx is the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor mediating efferent inhibition, but the medial olivocochlear synapse on outer hair cells (OHCs) does not mature until after the onset of hearing (15). Ca2+ influx could also occur via P2X receptors, which in mouse are localized to the apical surface of OHCs (16), though it is not known whether these receptors are active in neonatal mice. For OHCs, the MET channels are the major site of Ca2+ influx, and Ca2+ then exits through the PMCA2 CaATPase pump, which is largely confined to the stereocilia (17, 18) where it exists at a high density of 2,200/µm2 (19). The alternatively spliced isoform, PMCA2w/a, is specifically targeted to mammalian auditory hair bundles (20). For inner hair cells (IHCs), Ca2+ enters the cell via both MET channels and Cav1.3 channels, the latter mediating synaptic transmission onto the auditory afferents; the divalent cation is extruded through PMCA1 calcium pumps on the cell lateral wall, and to a lesser extent through PMCA2 in the IHC hair bundles (17, 19, 21).

The pathway linking Tmc1 mutation to reduced PMCA2 density has not been firmly established, and its further clarification is the goal of this work. Since the hair bundle PMCA2 is constantly replaced during the first postnatal week (22), we hypothesize that over this period, PMCA2 insertion into stereocilia is regulated by Ca2+ concentration, which will be largely dictated by Ca2+ influx through the MET channels. The reduced Ca2+ permeability of mutant Tmc1 channels may lead to a lower stereociliary [Ca2+], and consequently a diminished pump density. A comparison may be drawn between a lower pump density in Tmc1 mutations and a weaker activity in PMCA2 mutations. In the most extreme condition, in the deafwaddler2J mutation, PMCA2 is absent from the stereocilia, which causes OHC defects and deafness (23). In less extreme cases, such as Oblivion (24) and Tommy (25), a mutant PMCA2 is still present in the hair bundle, but it is less efficient in pumping, a behavior associated with age-dependent hearing loss (25). We argue by analogy that the smaller PMCA2 density in Tmc1 mutants will result in a loss of Ca2+ extrusion from OHCs and concomitant hearing impairment. The PMCA2 also has an accessory subunit, Neuroplastin (NPTN) (26), a single span membrane protein which is required for the stability of PMCA proteins and their delivery to the surface membrane (27). The Np55 isoform of NPTN is expressed in OHC stereocilia (28) and is needed for PMCA2 expression in OHCs (29) and for rapid Ca2+ extrusion (30). Moreover, NPTN knockout results in OHC degeneration and deafness (29). We have used immunolabeling of PMCA2 and Ca2+ imaging to delineate the factors influencing hair bundle [Ca2+]. We propose that during the early postnatal period, cytoplasmic Ca2+ regulates the insertion of PMCA2-containing membrane vesicles at the hair cell apex.

Materials and Methods

Mouse Mutants.

Tmc1 p.D569N mutant mice were made by Applied StemCell Inc (Milpitas, CA) (31) and Tmc1 p.T416K and Tmc1 p.D528N mice were made by Horizon Sage Labs Inc (St. Louis, MO) using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. For these mutations, genotypes were verified by 500 base-pair sequencing around the mutation site, and mice were subsequently bred for five generations, after which any off-target effects should have been eliminated. Tmc1 p.M412K (Beethoven) mice were a gift of Walter Marcotti (Sheffield University, UK) originating from Karen Steel (Kings College London, UK). For all experiments, measurements were performed on a Tmc2−/− background to avoid complications arising from different channel properties of TMC2, which is also expressed early in development (3, 32). Tmc2−/− mice (B6.129S5-Tmc2tm1Lex/Mmucd) were obtained from the Mutant Mouse Regional Resource Center (University of California, Davis, CA). Tmc1 p.D569N and Tmc1 p.T416K mice were created on C57BL/6 strains but Tmc1 p.D528N was made on an FVB strain to increase litter size. Neonatal mice were killed by decapitation according to the animal protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. For all strains, a mixture of male and female mice was used and no gender-specific effects were noted.

MET currents in response to hair bundle deflections were obtained by patch clamping OHCs in isolated organs of Corti as previously documented (7) with patch pipettes filled with an intracellular solution (in mM): 125 CsCl, 2.5 Mg2Cl, 2.5 Na2ATP, 10 Tris phosphocreatine, 1 EGTA [ethylene glycol-bis(βaminoethyl ether)-N,N,N’,N’-tetracetic acid], and 10 Cs-HEPES, pH 7.2. To measure the Ca2+ changes during stimulation, 0.25 mM of the calcium-indicator Fluo-4FF (Invitrogen, Carlsbad CA) was included in the pipette solution to which 10 mM ascorbic acid as an antioxidant was also added. During patch clamping, we waited 5 min after patch breakthrough to allow dye equilibration into the cell cytoplasm. For executing fluorescence measurements, the organ of Corti was illuminated with a 100 W Hg lamp through a Leica K3 filter cube containing an FITC excitation filter; the Ca2+ signal was recorded from the OHC apex and stereociliary bundle with a 63× 0.9 NA objective (axial resolution = 0.5 µm) onto an Evolv 128 CCD camera (Photometrics, Tucson AZ) (33). Images were captured with Metamorph and were analyzed in ImageJ over a region of interest of the hair bundle drawn from bright field measurements. Acoustic brainstem responses (ABRs) were measured on P13 to P21 wild-type and mutant mice that had been anesthetized with 10 mg/mL ketamine plus 1 mg/mL xylazine, and their auditory threshold was determined using the Tucker-Davis Technology (Alachua, FL) Auditory Workstation.

Immunolabeling.

Immunofluorescence labeling for PMCA2 was performed as described previously (19), using an affinity-purified rabbit polyclonal antibody (NR2, PA1-915, Thermo Fisher Scientific). P5 to P8 mice were decapitated, and isolated cochleas were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate buffer for 30 min at room temperature then treated with 0.5% TritonX-100 and blocked with 10 percent goat serum for 1 h. Isolated cochlear coils were incubated 2 h at room temperature with primary antibody (1:400 dilution) and subsequently labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti–rabbit IgG secondary antibody followed by Alexa Fluor 568 phalloidin (Invitrogen) to stain bundle actin. Preparations were mounted on coverslips with Fluoromount-G (Southern Biotech, Birmingham AL) and viewed with a 60× oil-immersion Planapochromat (NA = 1.4) or a 100× oil-immersion Planapochromat (NA = 1.45) on a Nikon A1 laser-scanning confocal microscope; 0.3 to 0.5 µm sections were acquired along the cell length, and fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ (Fiji). In some sections, images were acquired at 0.1 µm increments and deconvolved with the point spread function of the objective using Nikon NI Elements. Immunolabeling for NPTN was performed with sheep polyclonal anti-mouse antibody detecting NP65 and NP55 (pan-NP55/65) (R&D Systems, Minneapolis MN, AF7818) (28, 29, 34). The specificity of the antibody has been confirmed by the absence of label in the knockout Nptn−/− (29). After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, treatment with 0.5% TritonX-100 and blocking with 10 percent donkey serum for 1 h, cochlear coils were incubated 2 h at room temperature with NPTN antibody (1:300 dilution), then labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti–sheep IgG secondary antibody (Invitrogen) for 75 min. For all hair bundle imaging, sequential 0.4 µm confocal sections were acquired, at an estimated Z resolution of ~0.6 µm (35), which is less than the hair bundle height of 3 to 4 µm in apical OHCs of both wild type and mutants. Fluorescence intensities were measured using ImageJ software for 50 or more OHC bundles chosen at random in each preparation. For immunolabeling of mice older than P8 (19), animals were anesthetized with isoflurane, decapitated and the cochlea rapidly isolated. A small hole was made in the cochlear apex and the tissue fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate buffer for 1 h at room temperature, then washed and decalcified in 120 mM Na ethylene diamine tetra-acetic acid, pH 7.4, for up to 12 h. Cochlear coils were extracted, processed and immunolabeled as for young mice, then mounted and viewed in a Nikon A1 laser scanning confocal microscope.

Experimental Treatments.

PMCA2 block was achieved in two ways (33), either with the membrane-permeant inhibitor carboxyeosin diacetate succinimidyl ester (Invitrogen Life Sciences, Carlsbad, CA; 50 µM) or by raising the extracellular pH to 9.0 in saline buffered with 10 mM TABS (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) over a period of 30 min. The PMCA2 calcium pump extrudes Ca2+ in exchange for extracellular H+, so elevating extracellular pH minimizes that exchange. These agents were added to saline of normal (1.5 mM) [Ca2+], and cells were labeled with MitoTracker-Orange CM-H2 TMRos M7511 (ThermoFisher, 1.0 µM) to detect the presence of active mitochondria, the fluorescent level being taken to indicate mitochondrial density. To confirm the target of the Mitotracker, on occasions, the mitochondrial uncoupling agent carbonylcyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP; Sigma-Aldrich, 2 µM) was also added to the bathing solution, so greatly diminishing the fluorescent signal. Pitstop2, an endocytosis blocker (36) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Milwaukee WI), and cycloheximide, a protein synthesis inhibitor, was obtained from Avantor Sciences (Radnor, PA). Phenylarsine oxide (PAO; Thermo Fisher), an inhibitor of phosphatidylinositol-4-kinase (37–39), which prevents synthesis of phosphatidyl-4,5-bisphosphate [PtdIns(4,5)P2], was made up as a 100 mM stock in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and then diluted to 100 µM in DMEM incubation solution. A second inhibitor of PtdIns(4,5)P2 synthesis, 5-(2-Amino-1-(4-morpholinophenyl)-1H-benzimidazol-6-yl)-N-(2-fluorophenyl)-2-methoxypyridine-3-sulfonamide (GSK-A1, Sigma-Aldrich; ref. 40 and 41) was also dissolved in DMSO and diluted to a final concentration of 1 µM. For both PAO and GSK-1, stock solutions in DMSO were sonicated at 37 °C for 30 min prior to dilution in saline. Control solutions contained 0.1 mM DMSO. For most experimental manipulations (e.g., reduction in Ca2+ or pharmacological application), the preparation was exposed to the test treatment in GIBCO DMEM/F12 cell culture solution for 1 h prior to fixation (42), with the [Ca2+] adjusted appropriately. All measurements were made on both cochleas of at least two animals.

Electron Microscopy.

Organs of Corti from the apical-middle section of the cochlea were dissected from P6 mice and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde plus 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M Na cacodylate buffer, pH 7.3, supplemented with 2 mM CaCl2, for 1.5 h. Coils were washed and postfixed en bloc with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in a series of acetone concentrations and embedded in Embed 812 resin. Ultrathin 80 nm sections were cut transversely, stained with saturated uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined on a Philips CM 120 transmission electron microscope. Immunogold postembedding labeling for PMCA2 was performed as previously described (19). Cochlear segments were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde + 0.1% glutaraldehyde + 2.5 mM CaCl2 for 1 h, washed in phosphate buffer and dehydrated, then infiltrated and embedded with LR-white resin for 48 h at 50 °C. Ultrathin, 80 nm, sections were collected on glue-coated nickel grids and allowed to dry. In some instances, slot grids were used, covered in Formvar, carbon coated and plasma etched to render them more hydrophilic. After washing, grids were blocked in 10% goat serum + 0.2% Tween-20 in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) for 30 min and were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in the primary NR2 antibody (1:25) in 50 mM TBS + 1% BSA + 20 mM Tween-20. They were subsequently washed and incubated in goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to 15 nm gold particles for 1 h at room temperature, and then washed and stained in aqueous uranyl acetate and lead citrate for 20 min. For negative controls, grids were processed as above but with the primary antibody omitted, and no labeling was ever observed.

Statistical Test.

All results are quoted, unless indicated, as mean ± 1 SD and statistical tests used a two-tailed t test, significance tests indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001. ABRs and immunological labeling were performed on at least three mice for each mutation.

Results

Effects of Tmc Mutation on PMCA2 Expression.

In all four of the Tmc1 mutations studied, PMCA2 labeling of OHC hair bundles of P6 mice was significantly reduced relative to wild-type Tmc1+/+ (Fig. 1 A and B). Little or no label was evident on IHC stereocilia as these possess smaller PMCA2 densities (19). For each mutant, Ca2+ entry into the hair bundle, displayed as the red bars in Fig. 1D, was calculated from the product of the maximum MET current (Fig. 1C) and the Ca2+ permeability of the MET channel (Fig. 1D) at P6 in each mutant (7, 31). The mutants exhibit a range of reductions in Ca2+ entry, attaining 67 percent of the control in the least severe Tmc1p.T416K to 8 percent of the control in the most severe Tmc1p.D569N. The maximum Ca2+ entry calculated (Fig. 1D) is a proxy for the Ca2+ entry at rest under quiescent conditions, which will depend upon the resting open probability of the MET channel, POPEN. However, there was little variation in POPEN between P6 mutants: Tmc1+/+ 0.033 ± 0.010 (N = 10); Tmc1 p.T416K 0.040 ± 0.010 (N = 4); Tmc1 p.M412K 0.040 ± 0.010 (N = 9); Tmc1 p.D569N 0.046 ± 0.010 (N = 14); Tmc1 p.D528N 0.061 ± 0.020 (N = 4), which justifies use of the maximum Ca2+ influx value. The PMCA2 labeling was directly proportional to the relative Ca2+ entry (Fig. 1E), implying that the protein expression may be regulated by hair bundle [Ca2+]. However, the Y-axis intercept was still 0.28 in the Tmc1−/−; Tmc2−/− double knockout, suggesting that a fraction of the bundle PMCA2 is independent of Ca2+ entry. The diminished PMCA2 labeling in the P6 mutant mice (Fig. 1E) was preserved in animals at the onset of hearing, the fluorescence ratio of mutant/wild type being 0.42 (D569N, P12) and 0.34 (M412K, P14). Thus, the reduced PMCA2 expression persists in the Tmc1 mutant mice with hearing onset.

We examined the effects on Ca2+ homeostasis of inhibiting the PMCA2 calcium pump by monitoring Ca2+ with the low-affinity fluorescent indicator Fluo-4FF (KD = 10 µM). The hair bundle was deflected while the OHC was held at +76 mV so there was no Ca2+ entry, but repolarizing the cell to −84 mV (with the bundle held deflected) initiated a burst of Ca2+ because the MET channels were still open (Fig. 2 B and C). This technique circumvents contamination of the fluorescent signal caused by bundle motion (43). Upon repolarization, the [Ca2+] rose exponentially and then, at the end of the mechanical stimulus, it declined with a time constant of ~50 ms in the stereocilia and top of the hair cell. When the PMCA2 calcium pump was blocked by raising the pH to 9.0, there was an increase, ΔF, in resting [Ca2+] above the baseline level, FO, and ~10-fold slower decay (time constant 460 ms) back to baseline (Fig. 2D). Ca2+ measurements were made in four OHCs, which gave a mean increase in resting fluorescence ΔF/FO of 0.69 ± 0.24. The Ca2+ fluorescence transient evoked by hair bundle deflection increased in amplitude by 1.7 ± 0.5 times in high pH, presumably due to the lack of clearance of Ca2+ from the stereocilia. These observations confirm that a reduction in the PMCA2 pumping elicits an elevation in hair bundle [Ca2+]. Block of the PMCA2 calcium pump, either by raising the pH or treating with 50 µM carboxyeosin (33), also significantly reduced labeling with the mitochondrial marker Mitotracker (Fig. 2 E and F). These results suggest that PMCA2 inhibition, which is likely to elevate hair-cell cytoplasmic Ca2+, impacts mitochondrial function. Labeling with Mitotracker has also shown that Tmc1 mutations have a similar negative effect on mitochondrial function (14).

The Atp2b2 gene, which codes for the PMCA2 protein, has been found to interact with the Cdh23G753A gene modifier, which generates a defective version of the tip link component Cdh23ahl. The ahl genotype is present in C57BL/6 and DBA/2J mouse strains in which it causes early onset progressive hearing loss (44), and it also exacerbates the hearing loss in PMCA2 mutants (24, 45, 46). Although some of our Tmc1 mutations (Tmc1 p.D569N) were on C57BL/J background, others (Tmc1 p.D528N) were on FVB background which lacks the ahl defect. We had no evidence of a difference in hearing loss assessed by acoustic brainstem thresholds in these two mutants. Both Tmc1 p.D569N and Tmc1 p.D528N showed similar losses by P15 (Fig. 1S), suggesting the modifier made no contribution to early hearing loss in these Tmc1 mutants.

Development of PMCA2 Expression.

Expression of the PMCA2 calcium pump in the stereocilia developed over the first postnatal week in parallel with the MET current (Fig. 3 A–C). While there may be changes in the structure of the hair bundle over this period, it is unlikely that the measured PMCA2 density would be subject to the structural changes, as confocal measurements of immunolabeling had a resolution of ~0.6 µm (Materials and Methods), less that the stereociliary height of all rows (~1 to 4 µm). In the Tmc1+/+; Tmc2−/− mouse strain used for PMCA2 labeling, the MET current also appeared over the first postnatal week in the absence of TMC2 (47) (Fig. 3C). Both the MET current (reflecting TMC1) and PMCA2 were maximal after P8. However, there was finite expression of the PMCA2 at P0, about 37 percent of the total, when there was no MET current, which is comparable to the expression in the Tmc1−/−; Tmc2−/− double knockouts (Fig. 1E). The residual PMCA2 label in the absence of an MET current is unlikely to be due to nonspecific staining. One argument is that labeling of IHC hair bundles was lower, ~11 percent (Fig. 3B), even though they are known to contain some PMCA2 (17, 19). The codevelopment of MET current and PMCA2 suggests a causal relation and there was a good linear correlation (r = 0.98) between the two (Fig. 3D). Although Ca2+ will flow through MET channels that are present only in stereociliary rows 2 and 3 (43), PMCA2 adorns all three rows of stereocilia, as seen in the higher resolution after deconvolution of the fluorescent image. Quantification of the total label along the stereociliary rows demonstrated that there was PMCA2 and NPTN in OHC rows 1 and 2 (Fig. 2S). At P6, row 2 was labeled more than row 1, but by P14 row 1 and row 2 exhibited equal fluorescence intensities of both PMCA2 and NPTN. This observation indicates that the pump is not just present on stereocilia containing MET channels. Nevertheless, we hypothesize that PMCA2 expression is partly controlled by Ca2+ entering through MET channels. To test this hypothesis, the effects of reducing extracellular Ca2+ from 1.5 to 0.45 mM for an hour prior to fixation was determined.

Exposure to reduced extracellular [Ca2+] lowered PMCA2 labeling at P6. However, the extent of reduction became smaller with developmental age until by P11, there was no longer an effect (Fig. 4 A–D). Reducing extracellular [Ca2+] from 1.5 to 0.45 mM is estimated to approximately halve the Ca2+ influx (48). A comparable reduction in Ca2+ entry can be achieved by half block of the MET channels with tubocurarine, which has a KI of 4 µM (49, 50). This concentration of tubocurarine also lowered PMCA2 expression in the hair bundle (Fig. 4 A–D), bolstering the conclusion about the effect of reduced extracellular Ca2+. There are several notable features of these experiments. The pump turnover in the stereocilia at P6 was very fast, and the effects on PMCA2 expression of reducing extracellular [Ca2+] in the culture medium occurred in an hour. High-resolution images showed that row2 stereocilia had a higher labeling at P6 (Fig. 2S) and this distribution persisted in the presence of tubocurarine (Fig. 4B). The regulatory effects of Ca2+ disappeared at P11 (Fig. 4C), so that alterations in PMCA2 density that accrued prior to that time are predicted to become permanent. While we did not examine whether the MET channels were functional in animals of P11 or older, previous results have shown functional mechanotransduction using FM1-43 accumulation in P14 and older mice [(7), Fig. 3S], and patch recordings from OHCs in P11 mice (10). We would argue therefore, that changes in PMCA2 density during the first postnatal week that are attributable to Tmc1 mutations will persist in adulthood.

NPTN Labeling Echoes PMCA2.

Although there has been disagreement on the localization and exact role of the deafness protein NPTN in hair cells (28, 29, 51), current structural evidence indicates it is an accessory protein of the PMCA2 Ca2+ pump where it facilitates fast transport cycles (26, 27, 30). We therefore investigated whether NPTN exhibited the same developmental regime as PMCA2. Immunolabeling showed it was localized to the OHC hair bundles and was present in all three rows of stereocilia (Figs. 2S and 5A), but it was not prominent in the OHC soma or IHC soma or bundles. Furthermore, in the neonatal period prior to the onset of hearing, NPTN expression in OHC bundles developed along a time course like PMCA2. There was some amount of NPTN present at birth (P0), and this increased about threefold with time and saturated at P10 (Fig. 5B). Another similarity was that expression was reduced in the Tmc1 mutants. For example, the mean fluorescence counts (±SD) were 1,029 ± 250 (N = 37 bundles) in Tmc1 p.D569N, significantly smaller than 1,819 ± 195 (N = 195) in the P6 controls. One developmental difference between PMCA2 and NPTN (Fig. 5B) was that the latter increased from the baseline more than 2 d later (half-time PMCA2 = 4.2 d postnatal, NPTN = 6.7 d postnatal). Such a delay indicates that there is a period between P4 and P7 when more PMCA2 than NPTN is present in the stereocilia and therefore not complexed with it. It seems possible that the absence of NPTN is linked to PMCA2 turnover, because with NPTN knockout, PMCA2 is downregulated (29). Furthermore, when NPTN became maximal after P10, the PMCA2 was no longer susceptible to external Ca2+ changes. We suggest that the PMCA2 proteins in the stereocilia are replaced when they are not complexed with NPTN. NPTN was less sensitive to changes in the environmental Ca2+ concentration than PMCA2 (Fig. 5 C and D). The NPTN fluorescence counts in P7 Tmc1+/+; Tmc2−/− was 1,688 ± 58 (N = 151 hair cells) in 1.5 mM Ca2+, not significantly different from 1,663 ± 38 (N = 152 cells) in 0.45 mM Ca2+. However, when the Ca2+ concentration in the bathing solution was lowered to 0.1 mM for an hour, a significant reduction in NPTN fluorescence was observed (Fig. 6D) with control P7 of 2,392 ± 42 counts (N = 161) compared to 1,043 ± 21 counts (N = 169) in 0.1 mM Ca2+. This result suggests differential Ca2+ regulation of NPTN relative to PMCA2.

Using Ca2+ imaging with Fluo-4FF, we investigated the effects of reducing the extracellular Ca2+ concentration, a manipulation used to study PMCA2 regulation by Ca2+. Lowering [Ca2+] in the saline around the hair bundle from 1.5 mM to 0.15 mM caused a significant reduction in the bundle’s resting Ca2+ fluorescence (Fig. 6 A and B); the mean fluorescence change had ΔF/FO of −0.29 ± 0.13 in four OHCs. This finding confirms that the resting [Ca2+] in the bundle is altered by changing the extracellular concentration of the ion, and by implication its influx into the stereocilia. The response to bundle stimulation was also reduced in lowered extracellular [Ca2+]. The results are consistent with the proposal that reduced Ca2+ influx in Tmc1 mutants is likely to lower stereociliary [Ca2+].

Phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate.

Phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate [PtdIns(4,5)P2] is a lipid component of the plasma membrane involved in several signaling pathways. The lipid is present in the hair bundle and disappears after treatment with PAO, which blocks its synthesis (38). PtdIns(4,5)P2 is known to modulate PMCA2 (52) through a mechanism that involves binding to NPTN (26). We therefore tested whether inhibition of PtdIns(4,5)P2 synthesis by PAO affected expression of the PMCA2 pump and found a strong effect of 100 µM PAO on pump density (Fig. 5 E and F). P7 apical OHCs had a PMCA2 fluorescence of 3,171 ± 38 counts (N = 133 bundles) whereas after treatment with PAO, OHC fluorescence was significantly reduced to 1,186 ± 34 (N = 149) counts, the ratio of test/control being 0.37. The effect of inhibition of PtdIns(4,5)P2 synthesis was confirmed using a second inhibitor, GSK-A1 (41). PMCA2 fluorescence in OHCs was significantly reduced from 1,933 ± 129 (N = 129) counts in P7 controls to 554 ± 114 (N = 114) after 1 µM GSK-A1 treatment for 1 h, the ratio of test/control being 0.29. Results with both inhibitors argue that lack of PtdIns(4,5)P2 may impair the stabilization of PMCA2 by NPTN and therefore reduce its density. NPTN fluorescence in P7 OHCs was also reduced by PAO (Fig. 5F). PtdIns(4,5)P2 is present along the stereociliary length above the ankle taper region (38), a distribution that is consistent with that of PMCA2 (19).

PtdIns(4,5)P2 is necessary for fast effective Ca2+ transport by PMCA2 (26), and its inhibition is therefore likely to elevate stereociliary [Ca2+], similar to block of the pump (Fig. 2D). Consistent with this possibility, we found an increase in resting [Ca2+] assayed with Fluo-4FF before and after PAO treatment. Inhibiting PtdIns(4,5)P2 synthesis with PAO generated an increase in the background Ca2+ (Fig. 6 C and D), which developed 20 min after beginning to perfuse PAO into the bath. The mean increase in resting fluorescence, ΔF/FO, was 1.41 ± 0.44 in four OHCs, and occurred with no change in the leak conductance of the hair cell. There has been an earlier indication for an increase in resting [Ca2+] on PAO treatment (figures 7 B and C of ref. 39). Measurements of fluorescence changes were acquired when a decrease in MET current was observed, this being taken to indicate an effect of PAO. In all cells, there was a decline in MET current following PAO application, the ratio of the current in PAO compared to that in control being 0.66 ± 0.16, and this change was accompanied by a diminished Ca2+ increment during hair bundle deflection; the fluorescence ratio, ΔF, in PAO relative to control was 0.62 ± 0.14 (N = 4 OHCs). The similarity of the current and fluorescence ratios suggests that the latter is due to a reduced Ca2+ influx through the MET channels, which develops over the course of PAO perfusion. An increase in resting stereociliary [Ca2+] may partly account for the action of PAO on MET channel properties and transduction that have been reported (38, 39). It is envisaged that a higher stereociliary Ca2+ would partially block the MET channel (48), thereby reducing the apparent single-channel conductance, and would also alter MET channel adaptation. The latter effect manifests as a rightward shift and decreased slope (sensitivity) in the MET current-displacement curve (39). Similar changes in the current-displacement curve were observed after mutation or knockout of PMCA2 (53), or block with high pH (33). Compared to the PAO treatment, block of the PMCA2 with high pH causes no reduction in either the MET current or fluorescence change (Fig. 2D), suggesting the two maneuvers may work differently. However, the high pH block experiment occurred with a briefer perfusion of 5 min, which may partly account for the difference.

Vesicular Pool as Source of PMCA2.

To investigate the possible mechanism underlying PMCA2 turnover, we need to understand how cytoplasmic Ca2+ might control expression. We hypothesize that there are PMCA2 proteins present in the membranes of a pool of vesicles at the top of the cell that are ready for insertion into the apical plasma membrane. These 50 to 100 nm-diameter vesicles, the approximate size of synaptic vesicles, were originally reported on the abneural side of hair bundles of nonmammalian, turtle and frog, hair cells (54, 55). They have been proposed to be a source of membrane turnover, both exocytosis and endocytosis (56), which might be used for trafficking of stereociliary proteins. It is envisaged that proteins would be inserted into the apical plasma membrane by fusion of vesicles whose membranes contain PMCA2. PMCA2 molecules would diffuse along the stereocilia and then return to the apical membrane whence they would be reabsorbed by endocytosis to restock the vesicle pool (22). We searched for this vesicular compartment in transmission electron micrographs (TEMs) of OHCs and confirmed a collection of vesicles abneural to the tallest stereociliary row (Fig. 7 A–C). Clustered beneath the apical membrane were a set of vesicles of diameter 80 ± 17 nm (N = 38; mean ± SD) on six hair cells, with examples of membrane fusion in evidence. Other significant features of this cellular area were mitochondria and microtubules along which the vesicles might be ferried from the Golgi apparatus. The vesicle numbers were less evident in TEMs of OHCs from mutant Tmc1 p.D569N/D569N mice (Fig. 7 D and E) and vesicle counts in the region abneural to the hair bundle confirmed this observation (Fig. 7F). A distinctive feature of OHCs of Tmc1 D569N mutants was the presence in the subcuticular plate region of multivesicular bodies, about 0.5 µm in diameter (Fig. 7 D and E). Such bodies were observed in the cell apex of 12 out 22 OHCs of Tmc1 D569N mutant mice compared to 2 out of 18 Tmc1+/+ controls. Multivesicular bodies are part of the endosomal pathway associated with autophagy (57, 58) and indicate the apoptotic state of hair cells of mouse mutants (14).

If the replacement of PMCA2 in the stereocilia depends upon vesicle turnover, then it should be subject to certain experimental maneuvers. PMCA2 fluorescence was reduced after exposure to 30 µM Pitstop2, an endocytosis inhibitor (36) (Fig. 5 G and H). The fluorescence counts in controls were 2,692 ± 670 (N = 163 hair bundles) and after Pitstop2 (771 ± 309, N = 122 hair bundles), the ratio of test/control being 0.29. An endocytosis inhibitor might reduce PMCA2 replacement because it caused depletion of the PMCA2-containing vesicle pool. The large effect of Pitstop2 is consistent with the notion of a rapid membrane turnover (22). PMCA2 insertion was also reduced by lowering the temperature, the ratio of PMCA2 fluorescence for control (21 °C)/test (5 °C) being 3.45, corresponding to a Q10 of 2.2. For comparison, the temperature dependence of evoked transmitter release at the neuromuscular junction has a Q10 of 1.9 (59). PMCA2 insertion into the stereocilia was unaffected by 50 µM cycloheximide, a protein synthesis inhibitor, suggesting no new protein was required for the PMCA2 turnover. There was no significant difference between PMCA2 fluorescence in control preparations (1,511 ± 307, N = 87) and 1 h after treating with cycloheximide (1,457 ± 426, N = 151).

PMCA2 Immunogold Labeling.

The distribution of PMCA2 in the apical region of the OHC was examined by postembedding immunogold labeling with the NR2 antibody. Between 1 and 5 immunogold particles were visible in the hair bundle whereas up to 14 gold particles were present in the apical 1.5 µm of the cell (Fig. 7 G and H). As usual, there was sparse label with postembedding because only those antigenic sites projecting from the surface of the section would be labeled. Particle counts (Fig. 7J) indicated a concentration of particles in the cytoplasm beneath the apical membrane (cyto1) but not deeper into the cell (cyto2). For cyto1 and cyto2, the entire width of the cell was assayed so counts were spread over a broader cytoplasmic area than in the hair bundle. If these values were corrected for the area difference, the immunogold density in the hair bundle was 10 ± 3.6 particles/µm2, in the apical cytoplasm (cyto1) was 7.5 ± 2.6 particles/µm2, and in the deeper cytoplasm (cyto2) was 3.0 ± 1.5 particles/µm2. The conclusion is that PMCA2 is concentrated in the 1.5 µm beneath the apical membrane, in the cytoplasm surrounding the cuticular plate, abneural and lateral to the hair bundle, which corresponds to the location of vesicles (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

The mechanotransducer ion channel is essential for correct hair cell function and hearing, and in this role, Ca2+ influx through the ion channel pore is a critical factor (42). Mutations of Tmc1, the purported ion channel, and of other accessory subunits of the MET channel complex, such as Tmie (60) and Cib2 (61–63), result in hair cell death that culminates in permanent hearing loss, because cochlear hair cells once lost do not regenerate (64). One hypothesis linking MET channel defects to hair cell death is a change in Ca2+ entry. We have previously shown that one predictor of apoptosis in Tmc1 mutants was mitochondrial dysfunction and that the time of onset correlated with the degree of reduction in MET channel Ca2+ permeability, the greater the reduction the sooner the occurrence of apoptosis (14). An incidental observation was the concomitant reduction in density of the PMCA2 calcium pump that lines the stereocilia and is the major mechanism for Ca2+ extrusion from OHCs. Here we sought more definitive evidence for the involvement of the PMCA2 pump. We found that during the first 2 wk of postnatal cochlear development in the mouse, PMCA2 expression density increased with age to a final level that depended on Ca2+ entry into the stereocilia. When MET channels had a smaller Ca2+ permeability, the PMCA2 density was reduced, suggesting PMCA2 expression is regulated by intracellular Ca2+. Consistent with this observation, pump expression could be reduced by lowering extracellular [Ca2+] in early neonatal cultures. A striking feature of this metabolic effect was that pump replacement occurred within an hour, implying that during postnatal development, the PMCA2 protein turns over rapidly in OHCs. However, the pump turnover and its regulation by Ca2+ ended just prior to the onset of hearing at P12. Consequently, the PMCA2 will be under expressed in MET channel mutants after the onset of hearing, and this is likely to be a major factor in apoptosis, thus aligning with the apoptosis in PMCA2 mutants with diminished pumping rates, such as Oblivion (24) and Tommy (25) that exhibit progressive hearing loss.

It should be emphasized that with respect to Ca2+ regulation, OHCs exist over two separate regimes, early postnatal and later after the onset of hearing. We propose that during the early postnatal period, a decreased Ca2+ influx results in reduced PMCA2. The net consequence is that stereociliary [Ca2+] remains roughly balanced, which may be necessary to orchestrate proper hair bundle development (42, 65). However, when the reduction in PMCA2 density has become permanent, after the onset of hearing, a subsequent increase in stereociliary Ca2+ influx due to sound stimulation through a patent middle ear or augmented by the developing endolymphatic potential (EP)] will be incompletely extruded. Under these circumstances, hair cell [Ca2+] will increase and may become toxic.

Our findings raise several questions. How does stereociliary [Ca2+] control PMCA2 expression? We propose that PMCA2 is held in the membrane of a subset of vesicles at the top of the hair cell (Fig. 6) and that Ca2+ causes fusion of these vesicles with the apical plasma membrane, akin to synaptic exocytosis, delivering new PMCA2 to the bundle. We imagine that prior to the onset of hearing at P12, the stereociliary PMCA2 continually turns over and its insertion into the apical membrane and subsequent diffusion into the stereocilia is driven by cytoplasmic [Ca2+] at the top of the hair cell. The precise mechanism of calcium’s action is unknown, but it may engage a synaptotagmin-like protein as with the synaptic release of neurotransmitter. Further experiments are needed to investigate this problem.

PMCA2 density increases from a nonzero level at birth to a maximum around the onset of hearing. If all PMCA2 turnover is Ca2+ dependent, why is the level nonzero at birth when Ca2+ influx via TMC1 or TMC2 is zero? It is possible that there is another source of Ca2+ influx at birth, for example through Piezo2 channels that are present in the OHC apical membrane at birth. Alternatively, a fraction of PMCA2 is relatively immobile, about 30 to 40 percent as indicated by GFP tagging of PMCA2 (22), consistent with results in Figs. 1E and 3D. Finally, why does all the stereociliary PMCA2 become immobile around the onset of hearing? An explanation of both phenomena is the association with NPTN, which is likely to be required for stabilizing the pump complex (26, 29). About a third of the PMCA2 protein is present in the hair bundle at P1 (Fig. 3B), as is a similar fraction of NPTN (Fig. 5C). Moreover, over the first week of development, PMCA2 precedes NPTN by more than 2 d, and becomes immobile with an increase in expression of NPTN, both reaching a maximal density by P10. We propose that PMCA2 becomes immobile in the stereocilia when complexed with NPTN. Whether this complex facilitates connection to other stereociliary components such as actin is unknown. An implication of the time difference in expression of the two proteins is that the complex must be formed in the stereocilia not in the soma. Besides the different expression times of the two proteins, there was also a difference in Ca2+ sensitivity, with PMCA2 being regulated by a lower affinity Ca2+ binding receptor and NPTN by a higher affinity Ca2+ binding receptor (Figs. 4B and 5D). Pursuing the notion of these pump constituents being inserted into the hair cell membrane by vesicular fusion, PMCA2 and NPTN must occur in distinct subpopulations of vesicles that possess different Ca2+ binding receptors. For example, among the synaptotagmins, it is known that Syt1 is a low-affinity receptor (Kd ~ 60 µM) whereas Syt7 is a high affinity receptor (Kd < 1 µM) (66).

If the primary insult with Tmc1 mutants is loss of Ca2+ extrusion by PMCA2, what is the subsequent pathway for cell death? There are two potential mechanisms, an increase in intracellular [Ca2+] or a decrease in extracellular (endolymph) [Ca2+]. The intracellular pathway is probably implemented via secondary mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 2F) (14). An extracellular pathway arises because Ca2+ extrusion by the stereociliary PMCA2 contributes to the endolymphatic [Ca2+], and in the PMCA2 deafwaddler knockout, the [Ca2+] drops from around 20 µM to 6 µM (21), below the concentration required to maintain transduction (figure 7 of ref. 67]. The endolymph compartment is like a cell with the EP as its resting potential, and its Ca2+ concentration determined by the Nernst equilibrium plus any active pumping. Given an EP of 90 mV (68), the endolymph [Ca2+] is predicted to be about 2 µM applying the Nernst equation and taking a perilymph [Ca2+] of 1.3 mM. The predicted endolymph [Ca2+] is well below that needed for viability of the tip links, where the support of mechanotransduction has a half-concentration of 12 µM (67). However, under normal conditions, pumping by PMCA2 increases the endolymph [Ca2+] to about 20 µM (21, 69). Therefore, as the performance of the OHC PMCA2 declines, so does the endolymph [Ca2+]. This effect is further exacerbated by acquisition of the EP after hearing onset. Thus EP grows from 27 mV at P9 to 80 mV at P21 (68), the increase causing a drop in the endolymph [Ca2+]. Any impairment of the PMCA2 Ca2+ pump will have a major effect on mechanotransduction.
