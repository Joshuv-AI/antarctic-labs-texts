Probing the passion: evaluating Nigerian medical students’ interests in research
Authors: Tobi Olajide, Chukwuebuka Asogwa, Oluwatimilehin Oladapo, Emmanuel Ibitunde, Ayomide Fatola, Ridwanullah Abdullateef, Chioma Ezebialu, Elizabeth Ayegboyin, Chisom Obah, Kenneth Christian, Simon Aina, Mafusat Ojewumi, Oluwadabira Adewara, Angel Ogiemudia, Ihunanyachi Agha-Okoro, Christabel Uche-Orji, Nouh Sanusi, Isaac Adejumo, Oluwatobi Joshua, Chimdalu Ezeani, Esther Olaoye, Jolly Thomas, Chisom Okoye, Joshua Alabi, Godson Omumuawuike, Jessica Gambo, Joy Adeniyi, Precious Wagwula, Lawrence Rejoice, Olamide Odusola, Oghenemaro Jerry-Ogeme, Opeyemi Ilori, Atoyebi Olaadura, Promise Enyesiobi, Peace Alemede, David Uzoechi, Abdulaziz Ahmad, Olumide Bankole, Peace Gbenle, Oladotun Olalusi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionClinical research is key to advancing healthcare, driving innovation, characterising and addressing both local and global health challenges. Medical students’ participation in research during their education is essential for developing future clinician-scientists. However, in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) including Nigeria, several barriers restrict student involvement in research. In this study, we evaluated the interest, awareness, and research engagement of Nigerian medical students, identify extant barriers, while proposing strategies to enhance participation in clinical research.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among 691 Nigerian medical students across 42 accredited medical schools using a convenience sampling technique. Participants, aged 18 years and above, were recruited from all academic levels (100–600 levels). A self-administered online questionnaire assessed sociodemographics, research awareness, interest, participation, and barriers to research. Data were analyzed using Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) version 27.0 and descriptive statistics, and chi-square tests were employed to identify associations between demographic factors and research participation, awareness, and engagement.ResultsThe mean (SD) age (in years) of study participants was 21.88 (3.14), and 68.2% were aged 20–25 years with 50.5% females. Most participants (27.2%) were in their fourth year of medical training. Most students reported moderate (53.4%) or low (40.9%) research awareness, and 55.1% had participated in research activities, primarily data collection (31.1%) and literature review (21.8%). While 91.2% of the study participants showed interest in research activities, barriers included time constraints (66.6%), lack of awareness about research opportunities (60.6%), lack of mentorship (59.9%), and financial constraints (52.7%). Chi-square analysis revealed significant associations between academic level, research awareness, engagement and interest (p < 0.001) as well as gender and research interest (p = 0.04).ConclusionThis study highlights the strong interest in research among Nigerian medical students but also reveals notable barriers limiting their engagement. By addressing these challenges through structured mentorship programs, early exposure to research, curricular integration, equitable resource allocation, and regional interventions, institutions can bridge the gap between interest and active research participation. Establishing a supportive research culture is essential for empowering students to contribute meaningfully to scientific innovation and addressing local health challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08277-2.

Introduction

The 21st century has seen an unprecedented surge in medical knowledge and information. To accurately and effectively manage these seeming data deluge, research methodologies have also evolved. Research contributes heavily to the dynamic nature of medical education and practice. It helps healthcare professionals stay abreast of latest developments, improving medical knowledge and propelling evidence-based practice to improve patient outcomes. For medical students, research enhances analytical skills, helps develop communication skills and improves cooperation and problem-solving ability, which medical students find handy during medical practice [1, 2]. The scientific world has also witnessed important discoveries by medical students, including the sinoatrial node, heparin, Raynaud’s phenomenon, insulin, spermatozoa, the Fogarty catheter and ether anesthesia [3]. Engaging in research as an academic extracurricular activity enables students to deepen their understanding of coursework and develop valuable skills. It has been shown to influence career choices, as medical students involved in research tend to record higher scientific output post-graduation [4].

Research output by medical students is significantly higher in the Western world compared to developing countries. An analysis of PubMed-indexed papers written by medical students showed that the United States of America topped the list with 68%, followed by Europe with 19%, Asia with 13%, and Africa with 0.5% [5]. Unlike developed countries, where research training and funded summer research opportunities are provided for medical students [6], research participation is significantly lower in developing countries including Nigeria. Although the curriculum for training medical students in Nigeria, published by the Nigerian University Commission (NUC), includes research components [7], a small proportion of students actively participate in it. Despite the critical role of medical research in advancing healthcare, there is a notable gap in the level of interest and engagement of Nigerian medical students in research activities. A study at Ebonyi State University reported that 24.3% of students had previously participated in research, although 96.6% believed that research is a vital component of medical care [8]. Identified barriers to research among medical students include poor mentorship, lack of role models, the perceived lower salaries of academic physicians as key deterrents, lack of time due to the overwhelming workload of medical school, limited supervisor availability, the perception of inadequate research contribution recognition, inadequate research training and a perceived disconnect between research and clinical practice [9–11].

While medical research holds the potential to address local health challenges and contribute to the global fund of knowledge, there remains uncertainty about the interest and participation of Nigerian medical students in medical research. While Low and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) including Nigeria bear a higher disease burden, data from the Institute for Scientific Information show significant disparities in research output between developed and developing nations [12]. These challenges constitute a significant barrier to promoting a robust research culture within Nigerian medical education institutions and may hinder efforts to nurture future clinician-scientists. In this study, we evaluate the interest, awareness, and research engagement of Nigerian medical students, identify extant barriers, while proposing strategies to enhance participation in clinical research.

Methods

Study design

This was a descriptive cross-sectional study.

Study setting

The study was conducted in Nigeria, the most populous country in Africa with six geopolitical zones [13], among medical students enrolled in programs at accredited medical schools across the nation.

Study participants

Participants included all consenting Nigerian medical students, from all levels (100–600 levels, corresponding to the first through sixth years of medical training) in the 42 Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria (MDCN)-accredited medical schools. These schools include 18 federal government-owned, 17 state-owned, and 7 privately-owned institutions. Only participants who filled the questionnaires completely were included in the final analysis. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from each participant before completing the survey.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants included all Nigerian medical students currently enrolled in medicine and surgery programs who consented to the study, across all academic levels, provided they could understand and adequately complete the questionnaire. Students who declined to provide consent, were on academic leave, had temporarily withdrawn from school, or had participated in a previous iteration of the study were excluded. Additionally, foreign nationals were ineligible for inclusion.

Sample size and technique

The sample size was determined using the Qualtrics sample size calculator [14], applying a 95% confidence level and a 3.6% margin of error. Based on an estimated population size of 10,000 Nigerian medical students, the calculated sample size was 691 participants. Convenience sampling technique was utilized.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected using an online self-administered questionnaire created on Qualtrics. The questionnaire, developed for this study [15], was designed in English—the primary language of instruction in Nigerian universities—and was user-friendly and standardized. It was developed a new, following an extensive literature review, and was pretested among members of the research team prior to data collection to enhance clarity and internal validity. Selected students were contacted via WhatsApp, where a message explaining the study’s purpose, the importance of participation, and a link to the survey was shared. Participants were required to read and sign an electronic informed consent form detailing the voluntary nature of the study, the assurance of confidentiality, and the purpose of the research before accessing the questionnaire. The survey captured demographic information, awareness of, and engagement in research, interest in research, perceptions about research, factors influencing interest, and the availability of research opportunities and resources. Data analysis was performed using SPSS Version 27.0. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic variables, research interest levels, and factors influencing interest. Pearson’s Chi-square test was employed to find associations between categorical variables such as sociodemographics and levels of research interest, engagement, and awareness. Statistical significance, p-value, was set at 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the University of Ibadan/University College Hospital (UI/UCH) Ethics Review Committee with assigned number UI/EC/24/0683. Participants provided informed consent after being briefed about the study objectives and the voluntary nature of their involvement. They were informed that they could withdraw at any time without prejudice and that all information collected was anonymized and used only for collective analysis. The study posed no foreseeable risks to participants, as no personal data were collected. However, participants were informed that the only potential inconvenience was the time spent completing the survey, which was estimated to take approximately seven minutes. No compensation was provided for participation. There were no conflicts of interest reported by the researchers conducting this study.

The majority (68.2%) were aged between 20 and 25 years, followed by those under 20 years (21.6%), 26–30 years (9.4%), and above 30 years (0.6%). Among those with research experience, the most common activities were data collection (31.1%) and literature review (21.8%). Participation in data analysis (14.1%), manuscript writing (7.9%), and the presentation of research findings (15.8%) were less frequent. A minority (9.3%) reported involvement in other unspecified research processes. The most frequently reported types of research conducted included term papers (23.1%), case reports (12.3%), and cross-sectional studies (14.2%). Participation in systematic reviews (9.1%) and narrative review articles (9.9%) were less common. Only 4.2% had been involved in randomized controlled trials, while 27.3% of participants indicated no involvement in any research activity (Table 2).

Awareness of medical research was reported as moderate by 53.4% of participants, low by 40.9%, and high by only 5.7%. Regarding interest in pursuing research as part of their career, 56.4% were somewhat interested, and 34.8% were extremely interested, while 8.8% were not interested. Most respondents (59%) viewed the integration of research into the medical curriculum as very beneficial, with 38.3% considering it somewhat beneficial. Only 2.6% did not perceive any benefit. Additionally, 79.9% of respondents believed that engaging in research would enhance their clinical skills and knowledge, while 18.5% were uncertain.

Gender differences in research Awareness, engagement and interest

When considering interest in pursuing research careers, males exhibited a significantly (p = 0.003) higher level of interest (41.1% vs. 28.7%), while females had a higher proportion of students who were not interested (9.5% vs. 7.8% in males) (Supplementary material 2).

Academic level vs. Research Awareness, engagement and interest

Research awareness and engagement were found to significantly increase with academic progression (Table 3). Students in their final (600) level had the highest percentage of high awareness (12.7%), while those in their 100 level reported the lowest (1.8%). Engagement in research activities showed a similar progression, with 600-level students reporting the most frequent engagement (12.7%) and 100-level students the lowest (3.6%). Despite the upward trend in awareness and engagement, extreme interest in pursuing a research career was most pronounced among 100-level students, where 60.0% expressed extreme interest. However, this enthusiasm declined as students advanced, with only 30.4% of 600-level students maintaining extreme interest (Supplementary material 2).

Discussion

This study provides an in-depth examination of the interest, awareness, and engagement of Nigerian medical students in research, as well as the barriers and institutional challenges they face. While the findings reveal substantial interest in research among students, with 91.2% expressing moderate to high interest, there is a considerable gap between this enthusiasm and actual engagement. Only 55.1% of participants reported any form of research activity, highlighting the systemic and contextual limitations that restrict student participation. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence emphasizing the need for structural reforms in medical education to foster a robust research culture, particularly in low-resource settings such as Nigeria [4, 9, 16].

The findings of this study resonate with and extend insights from recent Nigerian research exploring medical students’ engagement with scholarly activities. [11], in a national cross-sectional survey of recent graduates, reported a considerably higher research participation rate (92%) than observed in the present study (55.1%). This disparity may reflect differences in sampling cohorts and institutional exposure, as many graduates would have completed mandatory research projects prior to graduation. Nevertheless, both studies converge on the identification of time constraints, inadequate mentorship, and limited funding as the predominant barriers to research participation. Importantly, regional disparities reported by Kingpriest et al.—with the South-South demonstrating the highest engagement (98%) and the South-West the lowest (85%)—parallel the geographic variations noted in our study, suggesting that structural inequities in institutional support and access to mentorship opportunities persist across Nigeria’s medical schools. Similarly, [17], in a Lagos-based study, documented even lower participation rates (52.9%) and found that only 21.3% of respondents demonstrated adequate research knowledge. Their findings, particularly regarding insufficient training in methodology, lack of a structured research curriculum, and poor access to research facilities, reinforce the present study’s observation that Nigerian students are often confined to rudimentary research roles such as data collection and literature review, with minimal exposure to data analysis or manuscript preparation. In contrast, Olajide et al. [9] proposed a transformative institutional framework through the College Research and Innovation Hub (CRIH), which integrates mentorship, peer collaboration, and capacity-building workshops to strengthen research culture among undergraduates. The enthusiasm for such interventions reflected in our participants’ recommendations emphasizes the growing consensus that targeted institutional mechanisms, mentorship networks, and early curricular integration are indispensable to translating students’ research interest into tangible scholarly productivity.

The high interest in research observed among Nigerian medical students aligns with global trends. Moraes et al. reported that 81.7% of Brazilian students were interested in research [18], while Klowak et al. documented an 89% participation rate in research among students at McMaster University [19]. However, engagement levels in Nigeria remain significantly lower than in North America and Europe, where research is more seamlessly integrated into the medical curriculum [20]. This disparity underscores the role of structural factors such as mentorship, availability of opportunities, and institutional support in shaping student research engagement [20].

In this study, the predominance of participation in preliminary activities such as data collection and literature review, with fewer students engaging in manuscript writing or data analysis, reflects a gap in advanced research training. This pattern mirrors findings from Al-Halabi et al., who noted that students in Kuwait were more likely to engage in basic rather than critical research tasks [10]. Such limitations may restrict Nigerian students from producing higher-level outputs, such as publications and conference presentations, which are increasingly important for academic and professional advancement. Addressing this imbalance requires embedding progressive research skill-building into the medical curriculum.

The barriers identified—time constraints (66.6%), lack of mentorship (59.9%), and limited awareness of opportunities (60.6%)—echo findings across low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Cuschieri highlighted similar deterrents in Malta, including inadequate institutional support and competing academic demands [6]. Financial constraints (52.7%) and insufficient research skills (57.3%) further compound these challenges. Time constraints, in particular, emerged as the most prevalent barrier, consistent with global literature emphasizing the conflict between heavy academic workloads and research participation [20, 21]. Öcek et al. similarly reported that students in Turkey considered overwhelming academic and clinical responsibilities a major obstacle [21]. Beyond this, lack of mentorship and limited access to research resources, such as libraries and databases, remain significant challenges in resource-limited contexts like Nigeria [15, 21].

The lack of awareness of research opportunities, reported by 60.6% of participants, is particularly striking. Unlike findings from Canada and Pakistan—where awareness increases with academic progression [16, 18]—this study revealed low awareness across all academic levels, suggesting systemic institutional gaps. Without deliberate efforts to promote research engagement from the earliest stages of training, Nigerian students risk losing enthusiasm as they advance, as reflected in the declining interest observed from 100 level to final year. Sustained mentorship and structured opportunities throughout training are therefore essential to prevent attrition of research interest.

Regional disparities further complicate the picture. Students from the South-West reported the highest levels of awareness and engagement, while the North-East lagged behind. These inequities likely reflect differential access to faculty expertise, funding, and institutional research infrastructure. Similar regional variations have been reported in Lagos-based studies and broader West African literature [17]. Tackling these disparities requires health policy interventions that ensure equitable distribution of research resources, capacity-building programs, and collaborative networks across medical schools nationwide.

Institutional support was perceived as moderate or low by 91.5% of respondents, with only 8.5% rating it as high, consistent with findings from Brazil [18]. Furthermore, more than half of participants (54.1%) reported limited availability of funding for student-led research. These findings highlight the urgent need for medical schools and policymakers to prioritize institutional reforms that provide dedicated funding, strengthen mentorship programs, and improve access to research infrastructure.

Beyond institutional disparities, gender and academic progression patterns observed in this study also reflect broader socio-cultural and educational contexts within Nigeria. The higher interest levels among male students and greater disinterest among females may mirror enduring gendered expectations within academia, where women often face implicit biases, reduced access to mentors, and competing social roles that limit research engagement. Additionally, the decline in research enthusiasm with advancing academic level could stem from escalating curricular demands and the transition to clinical postings, which leave little protected time for scholarly activity. These patterns emphasize the need for gender-responsive mentorship programs, protected time allocations, and institutional incentives that sustain research motivation across all training stages.

Overall, the findings have several implications. For medical education, there is a pressing need to integrate structured, progressive research training into the curriculum, ensuring early exposure and sustained engagement. For health policy, interventions should focus on equitable resource allocation, national student-research support programs, and capacity-building to reduce regional disparities. For research culture development, cultivating mentorship networks, expanding institutional funding mechanisms, and embedding research as a core component of medical professionalism are essential. Without such systemic reforms, the persistent interest–engagement gap risks perpetuating a cycle of underrepresentation of African medical students in global research output, undermining the potential of this generation to contribute meaningfully to locally relevant and globally impactful health research.

Conclusion

This study highlights the gap between Nigerian medical students’ strong research interest and their relatively low participation, with advanced tasks like manuscript writing and data analysis being least common. Barriers such as time constraints, lack of mentorship, financial limitations, and regional disparities hinder engagement, reflecting patterns in other resource-limited settings. Addressing these challenges through early research exposure, structured mentorship, equitable access to resources, and targeted skills training can bridge this gap. Strengthening undergraduate research capacity is essential for cultivating clinician-scientists who can advance healthcare and innovation in Nigeria.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design restricts causal inferences, and reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall and social desirability bias. The use of convenience sampling and the sample size distribution may also limit representativeness, potentially influenced by survey fatigue and internet connectivity challenges. As a result, findings may not be fully generalizable to all Nigerian medical students. Nonetheless, the study’s large, geographically diverse sample provides meaningful insights into the factors influencing research engagement in resource-limited settings.

Future directions

To bridge the identified gaps, future efforts must move beyond broad recommendations to the design of actionable interventions with clear roles, timelines, and measurable outcomes. One important step is the early integration of research into medical training. Structured research modules covering methods, ethics, and proposal writing should be introduced at the preclinical stage (100–300 level), with supervised mini-projects embedded into the clinical years. Responsibility for this lies with the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria (MDCN), the National Universities Commission (NUC), and medical school curriculum committees, with pilot programs achievable within two years and full integration possible within five. The success of such reforms can be tracked through the proportion of students completing supervised projects and the annual number of undergraduate publications and conference presentations.

Mentorship remains another cornerstone for strengthening student engagement. Establishing structured mentorship programs—linking students to faculty supervisors and peer mentors—would not only build technical skills but also sustain enthusiasm. Universities, teaching hospitals, and professional bodies such as the Nigerian Medical Association (NMA) should champion such schemes, incentivizing faculty involvement through recognition and workload credits. These could be initiated within a year and scaled nationally over three years, with progress evaluated by the number of active mentor–mentee pairs, frequency of meetings, and tangible student outputs such as abstracts and manuscripts.

Equitable allocation of research resources is also essential, particularly in addressing regional disparities. A national student research support fund, administered by the Federal Ministry of Health and the Tertiary Education Trust Fund (TETFund), could provide grants, access to laboratory facilities, and subscriptions to online databases. Ensuring nationwide access within three years would help level the playing field between well-resourced and under-resourced institutions. Success could be measured by monitoring the regional distribution of resources and the number of students benefitting from funded opportunities.

In addition, regular capacity-building workshops on methodology, proposal writing, data analysis, and academic publishing should be instituted as recurring events at least quarterly. These can be delivered in hybrid formats to broaden access. Universities and national research networks can lead these efforts, and their effectiveness can be evaluated through attendance records, pre- and post-training assessments, and participant feedback.

Looking ahead, innovative and technology-driven approaches offer unique opportunities. A centralized online research engagement portal could connect students with mentors, projects, and global collaborations. Such a platform, developed through partnerships between the MDCN, NUC, and global research networks such as the African Academy of Sciences, could be piloted within two years and expanded nationally within four. Evaluation should focus on platform usage statistics, number of active collaborations, and participation in international joint projects. Complementing this, regional research consortia across Nigerian medical schools could pool resources and expertise, while collaborations with international universities would expose students to global research standards. Joint publications and co-hosted conferences would serve as tangible indicators of success.

Finally, future research should adopt mixed-methods approaches, incorporating semi-structured interviews to capture nuanced student motivations and institutional barriers. Longitudinal studies are also needed to track the long-term effects of these interventions on research participation, productivity, and career trajectories. Such evidence would provide critical feedback for refining national strategies.

Through coordinated reforms in medical education, proactive health policy, and deliberate cultivation of a research culture, Nigerian institutions can transform student enthusiasm into sustained engagement. These efforts would not only empower medical students to contribute meaningfully to scientific knowledge but also strengthen Nigeria’s position in the global research landscape.
