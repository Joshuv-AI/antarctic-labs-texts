From operating room to leadership room: a systematic review of simulation-based interventions for leadership development in general surgery
Authors: Nadia Gulnaz, Faiza Hashim Soomro, Izza Nazir, Mehwish Ansar, Rabia Arshad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the effect of simulation-based leadership training on behavioral and safety-related outcomes in surgical and clinical environments.Summary background dataLeadership and non-technical skills (NTS) are critical for safe surgical practice. Simulation-based education can be helpful in the improvement of these skills. While simulation-based education is widely used to improve technical skills, its influence on leadership, teamwork, and patient safety outcomes is reported inconsistently.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA guidelines, studies evaluating leadership or NTS using simulation within surgical or perioperative contexts were included. Study characteristics, simulation format, assessment frameworks, leadership outcomes, and patient safety indicators were extracted. Risk of bias was evaluated using ROBINS-I and RoB-2, and visualised through the robvis platform.ResultsTwelve studies with 430 participants met inclusion criteria. Sample sizes ranged from 14–69; most involving multidisciplinary teams of 2–6 members. High-fidelity modalities were used in approximately 70% of studies, while others incorporated wet-lab, video-based, or in-situ simulations. Leadership performance improved, with reported gains of 2–4 points on behavioral scales. Communication and teamwork scores increased by approximately 2–3 points, and Non-Technical Skills for Surgeons (NOTSS) domain means typically ranged from 2.8–3.5 on 4-point scales. Technical performance differences between senior and junior trainees averaged 30–40%. Checklist-supported teams missed only 6% of critical actions.ConclusionsSimulation-based training was consistently associated with improvements in leadership, communication, teamwork, and safety-related performance across surgical and multidisciplinary settings. Although results were positive, heterogeneity in design and measurement shows the need for further high-quality, standardised research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09486-z.

Introduction

Non-technical skills (NTS), which are leadership, decision making, situational awareness, communication and teamwork, have become pillars for safe and effective surgical practice. Although technical proficiency has been the main focus of traditional surgical training, human factors research has shown the importance of cognitive and social competencies to be the main predeterminants of intraoperative performance and patient outcome [1]. These are not ‘soft extras’, but the key elements of high-reliability surgical teams; their lack has been attributed to medical errors, sentinel events, and near-misses [2]. Leadership, in particular, allows the operating surgeon or senior trainee to coordinate complex team dynamics, guide decision-making under pressure, and maintain situational awareness, which collectively contribute to resilience in crisis situations [3].

Surgical adverse events often reflect failures in NTS rather than only technical mistakes. Indeed, studies suggest that communication breakdowns, poor escalation of issues, and ineffective leadership contribute significantly to intraoperative errors [4]. The issue of leadership and teamwork is particularly critical in emergency general surgery that often deals with high-risk patients and unstable workforce structure, as higher rates of complication and mortality are linked to it [5]. Despite this, surgical leadership training is not commonly integrated in surgical training; most surgeons have ended up in leadership positions without training to be leaders [6]. Although leadership is acknowledged in standard surgical reference books, conventional surgical schools of thought concentrated more on the mastery of the procedure, rather than on the mastery of the behavior [7, 8].

Simulation-based education provides the potent approach to prepare and evaluate NTS under the controlled and risk-free conditions. By conducting the simulated crises in the operating room, teams are able to practice the coordinated response to the hemorrhage, cardiac arrest, or other emergency situations, allowing the participants to improve the leadership, communication, and decision making [9]. This method enables repetition, systematic debriefing and instantaneous feedback, which are key factors in competent learning of behavioral competencies. In fact, simulation interventions have been found to enhance the NTS of the surgical residents, such as leadership behaviors, situational awareness, and teamwork [10]. Simulation has also been shown to have better results in the wider scope of medical training, not only in terms of skills acquisition but also patient safety [11].

Despite that the potential of simulation in NTS training is well established, there are still major gaps in the literature. In the past, majority of studies have shown concerned regarding technical capabilities or the overall team training, but have not specifically investigated leadership development in the context of surgery [12]. Even though several assessment instruments are available (ex: Non-Technical Skills for Surgeons (NOTSS), Non-Technical Skills (NOTECHS)), there is inconsistency in the definition, measurement and reporting of leadership outcomes [13]. Moreover, the sustainability, obstacles to implementation and practical transfer of simulation-based leadership training is not well-researched. Although the results of these programs in the simulation lab are encouraging, it is less clear whether these leadership behaviors will continue to be the same in the real clinical environment and are associated with patient-level outcomes [14]. Also very little information has been given regarding the adaptation of a simulation-based leadership training in international surgical settings, including low resource settings, although its role is increasingly being recognized [15].

Considering these major gaps, this study aims to conduct a review of simulation-based interventions in leadership development in general surgery. This review can offer practical findings to surgical educators, training organizations, and patient safety stakeholders who are interested in incorporating structured leadership training into surgical curricula.

Materials and methods

This study is a systematic review conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of simulation-based interventions for leadership development among general surgery trainees and practicing general surgeons. The review was designed and executed in accordance with established methodological guidelines for systematic reviews of educational interventions. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided as Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251176965).

Eligibility criteria

Population

Studies were eligible if they involved general surgery trainees, including foundation doctors, core trainees, specialty trainees or residents, as well as practicing general surgeons. Only studies where participants took part in leadership or NTS training were included.

Intervention

Eligible interventions were simulation-based educational activities explicitly designed to develop leadership or crisis resource management (CRM) capabilities. These were high-fidelity simulation, in-situ simulation, virtual or manikin-based scenarios and other structured simulation modalities of leadership skills including team leadership, decision-making, communication, situational awareness, escalation behaviors, or multidisciplinary coordination.

Comparators

Included studies involved comparing simulation training to (1) traditional didactic leadership teaching, (2) other non-simulation leadership education, or (3) no structured leadership intervention. Studies without a comparator group were eligible if they used a pre-post or mixed-methods design assessing change over time.

Outcomes

Outcomes were analysed at the study-reported follow-up time points. Given heterogeneity in outcome definitions and measurement tools, effect measures and time points varied across studies and were synthesised descriptively rather than pooled quantitatively.

Study designs

The review included primary research studies only, encompassing: Randomised controlled trials (RCTs), Quasi-experimental studies, Pre-post intervention studies, Mixed-methods evaluations and Qualitative studies reporting outcomes of simulation-based leadership development with or without a comparator.

Reviews, commentaries, editorials, conference abstracts without full data, and studies not using simulation as a primary modality were excluded.

Only studies developing or assessing leadership or NTS within a surgical training or surgical practice context were included. Studies involving non-surgical clinicians or general medical trainees were excluded unless the data specific to general surgeons were independently extractable.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search has been made and implemented in the following databases: MEDLINE, Embase (via Ovid), Scopus, CENTRAL (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials), and The Cochrane Library (CLIB). A complete search strategy could be provided in Supplementary Table 3. Searches were restricted to English-language publications. In accordance with the protocol timeline, the databases were searched for articles published up to 26 November 2025. Only peer-reviewed published studies were considered; unpublished studies, theses, and grey literature were not sought.

Backward citation searching (reference list screening) was performed for all included papers to identify any additional eligible studies.

Study selection process

All retrieved citations were screened in two stages: title/abstract screening followed by full-text review. Screening was conducted independently by at least two reviewers, with any disagreements resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer. Eligibility assessment was guided strictly by the predefined inclusion criteria. After full-text screening, 12 primary studies met all eligibility requirements and were included in the final review. Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers using a standardised extraction template aligned with the PROSPERO protocol. No contact was made with study authors for missing or unclear information; only published data were used.

Quality assessment (risk of bias)

Quality appraisal of the included studies was conducted using standardized risk-of-bias frameworks, and all visualizations were generated using the robvis online tool [17]. The traffic-light plot of non-randomized studies (Supplementary Fig. 1) showed that the methodological rigor of many studies was diverse, with some of them having moderate to severe issues in different areas such as confounding, participant selection, and non-adherence to the planned interventions. To address confounding in future studies, the use of predefined baseline assessments and statistical adjustment for prior simulation exposure or leadership training would reduce bias. Such limitations are used as the complexity of the evaluation of the educational intervention based on simulation because the blinding, standardized exposure, and control of contextual variables are difficult to attain. Future non-randomized research would benefit from the implementation of structured intervention protocols and fidelity monitoring processes to ensure consistent exposure to the intervention. These issues were further brought into light by the summary plot of non-randomized studies (Supplementary Fig. 2) which revealed that risks were most eminent in outcome measurement significantly due to the use of subjective behavior-based measurement and inconsistent assessment training of the assessor and the apparent lack of methodological transparency in some studies.

Conversely, the randomized controlled trials had a relatively high methodological strength. The visualization of the RCTs traffic-light chart (Supplementary Fig. 3) revealed that the risks of generation and allocation of the random sequence were generally low, and the majority of the trials provided structured simulation protocols and standardized debriefing procedures. Future randomized studies further strengthen these domains by detailing allocation concealment procedures and ensuring reproducibility of the randomization process. Risks remained in areas to do with not adhering to intended interventions and outcomes evaluation assessments, which was similar to the problems present in the non-randomised literature specifically the inability to blind participants and raters in a simulated environment, and the subjectivity of leadership and NTS ratings. Use of blinded video-based assessments, standardized facilitation protocols, and independent fidelity checks would help reduce bias caused by deviations from intended interventions and subjective outcome scoring. The randomized studies (Supplementary Fig. 4), summary plot showed that the overall risk of bias was low to moderate across domains, which means that even though the RCTs were able to present more credible evidence, there is still space in which the methods of the studies could be improved, especially in the standardization of measurement instruments used, in the blinding of the studies, and in the unification of reporting practices between the studies. A formal assessment of reporting bias and certainty of evidence (such as GRADE) was not performed because of variability in study designs and outcome measures.

Results

A total of 12 studies were included [18–29], collectively representing a broad span of surgical training contexts and participant profiles. Across all investigations, sample sizes ranged from 14 to 69 participants, with most studies enrolling groups in the 20–60 participant range, and together they contributed over 430 trainees and clinicians when considering all reported samples. Participants included general surgery residents, thoracic and urology trainees, emergency medicine interns involved in surgical care, consultant surgeons, and multidisciplinary operating room teams; overall, the population covered training levels from PGY-1 to fully trained consultants, with experience ranging from < 1 year to more than 15 years in clinical practice. Studies that included demographic factors had majority of their participants being male with percentages often reaching 60–70% and those with age data were often between the late 20s to early 40s. Majority of the studies did not specify the exposure to leadership or simulation-based training before but there were isolated reports that specified that some of the participants had prior formal certifications like ATLS or ACLS or general experience of simulation. Study settings encompassed a wide variety of environments from simulation laboratories and high-fidelity training centers to real operating rooms and district or university hospitals. Across all included studies, simulation practices demonstrated substantial variability in format, intensity, fidelity, and instructional design. In total, the studies encompassed an extensive variety of modalities, such as high-fidelity manikin-based simulation, wet-lab surgical simulation, video-based simulation, actual observation of an operating room, and hybrid in-situ simulation, which indicates the scale of the manner of developing leadership and NTS in surgical training. The number of sessions varied considerably, from single-session simulations to multi-session programs involving up to 10 structured scenarios, while session durations ranged from brief 2–15-minute interactions to full-day simulation courses lasting 4–6 h. The multidisciplinary character of the surgical care was consistently observed in terms of the composition of the teams and the vast majority of the simulations involved the participation of 2–6 active participants, including surgeons, anesthesiologists, nurses, technologists, and trainees. The most common type of simulation was high-fidelity simulation, as over two-thirds of the studies used technologies such as SimMan 3G, video-based replications of the operating room, and complex procedural models, and only few studies used low-fidelity and video-based and non-simulation feedback interactions. Clinicians who were often trained and studied in senior surgeons, anesthesiologist, simulation faculty and human-factors faculty, and many studies have depended on formal training of instructors by training programs in NOTSS, or courses on trauma leadership or simulation-based pedagogy. Leadership training was consistently integrated into NTS instruction, most commonly through structured frameworks such as NOTSS, NOTECHS, Ottawa CRM, and other validated behavioral taxonomies. More than two-thirds of the studies directly relied on NOTSS or a system derived from it, with the rest relying on models of CRM-based leadership or procedure-specific checklists to inform behavioral evaluation. The main elements of leadership focused on generally included decision-making, communication, tasks management, situational awareness, teamwork and stress management, which are the domains of the core areas common to NTS models. Debriefing was a key instructional intervention in most interventions, with formats of only a few minutes of focused feedback to more in-depth formats of 30 min and more of structured format, e.g., the Diamond debrief and BID (Briefing–Intraoperative Teaching-Debriefing) model, also typically including video review. This was a heterogeneous set of assessment methods which were mainly based on validated behavioral rating instruments, such as NOTSS checklists, NOTECHS metrics, CRM global rating scales, leadership-specific behavioral checklists, procedural safety checklists, and psychometric instruments (Global Operative Assessment of Laparoscopic Skills (GOALS) or Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) derived scales). About one-third of the studies used a comparator, contrasting junior versus senior trainees, pre- versus post-training performance, or checklist-supported versus standard practice conditions, while the remainder were descriptive or intervention-only designs. Leadership and non-technical skill outcomes also showed a consistent improvement after the interventions through simulation, and most of the studies found significant improvement in leadership, communication, teamwork, and decision-making aspects. The performance scores of leadership based on numerical values were moderate to high with some of the studies demonstrating a change of 2–4 points on standardized scores or a clear difference between junior and senior trainees with the latter frequently scoring higher by the margin of 2–4 points on leadership and technical checklists. The area of communication and teamwork also rose within the evidence base, with pre- to post-training improvement of about 2 to 3 points in those studies that reported a numeric change, and observed results of increased coordination and situational awareness in crisis situations. Other subdomains of NOTSS like situation awareness, decision-making, communication, and teamwork typically rated with the 2.8–3.5 range on 4-point scales, which are statistically significant but generally positive. Measures of patient safety indicators increased, and the teams with checklists lacked as few as 6% of essential actions compared to 23% in unsupported conditions. Technical performance outcomes, where reported, favored more experienced trainees, with senior participants scoring approximately 30–40% higher than juniors on global rating scales or procedural checklists. Training satisfaction was generally high, often ranging above 90–97% and confidence levels were self-reportedly consistently improved across studies with simulation reportedly useful by the participants in terms of preparedness and real-world use. The perceived utility was equally high, and the majority of the studies characterized the high degree of acceptance and positive attitude towards the leadership development based on simulations. These were supported by the qualitative findings that identified themes of the improved teamwork culture, the increased level of reflection, the improved strategies of communication, the increased crisis management skills, and the increased acknowledgment of simulation as the effective way of practicing the leadership behaviors in the safe environment. Discussion

The findings of this review suggest that simulation-based training may support improvements in non-technical skills, including leadership, within surgical and multidisciplinary teams. The current review outcomes support its increased applicability in various clinical settings. The included studies generally reported improvements in leadership, communication, teamwork, and situational awareness; aligning with previous literature emphasizing the importance of structured behavioral training in high-risk healthcare settings [30, 31]. The ability to use leadership benefits across a variety of simulation modalities such as high-fidelity manikin simulations, video-based review, and in-situ operating room exercise attests to the versatility of simulation to a variety of training requirements and operations. These findings are consistent with larger observations that simulation can be useful to encourage safer team practice and minimize preventable mistakes in acute care disciplines [32].

One of the strengths of the incorporated literature was that all of the validated behavioral frameworks, including NOTSS, NOTECHS, and CRM-based models, played a role in standardized measurement and organized feedback. The predominance of NOTSS in particular underscores its acceptance as a reliable framework for evaluating leadership and decision-making in surgical teams. This finding is comparable to previous systematic reviews demonstrating the robustness of NOTSS-based training in improving surgeon leadership, cognitive skills, and team interaction quality [33, 34]. Such frameworks can be used to reinforce systematized behavioral indicators and in this way, they aid in ensuring uniform examination among learners, which leads to greater interrater reliability and increased reproducibility of the training of non-technical skills.

Debriefing was identified as a fundamental component that supported the concept of leadership development in all the studies, which underscores its use as the central driving force through which learners link simulated experience with real-life practice. Such structured formats as the Diamond debrief, BID model, and focused video review were prevalent to promote reflective learning. This is in line with the existing knowledge that proves guided debriefing as one of the most effective factors in consolidating behavioral changes following simulation-based encounters [35, 36]. The reliability of this observation in varied environments supports the importance of focusing on faculty training in debriefing to optimize educational impact.

The gains made in communication were evident in a number of studies, which showed that leadership and communication behavior were interdependent in high-acuity clinical activities. Simulation provided repetition of the critical situations when the effective information sharing, closed-loop communication, and coordinated task performance were needed. The previous literature also adds to the point that simulation improves both verbal and non-verbal communication skills, which result in more efficient work of the team and a decreased cognitive load when facing a crisis [37]. The pattern of demonstrating the gains of communication in the included literature can lead to the conclusion that communication-centered simulation must be introduced as a regular part of surgical education.

Team coordination and crisis management skill showed improvements, especially in high-fidelity and time-pressured situations in multidisciplinary operating room groups. These results can be attributed to the fact that simulation has been long known to improve team adaptability, shared mental models, and distributed leadership behaviors in the course of complex procedures [38]. The indicated lack of critical tasks that were missed when the use of checklists was combined with simulation also proves the effectiveness of combined interventions in strengthening safer team behaviors [39].

The technical performance outcomes, which were not the main focus of the literature, showed regular patterns where the senior trainees or more experienced clinicians scored much higher as compared to their juniors. This finding can be reconciled with the current literature according to which technical competence and leadership should support each other, and more experienced clinicians can tend to exhibit higher situational judgment and coordination during stress [40, 41]. The performance difference between the two groups though enhanced by simulation still indicates that leadership training might have to be adapted to various career stages.

The level of satisfaction and confidence among the participants was consistent across all the studies and it was in many cases more than 90% which could be regarded as a wide acceptance of simulation as a psychologically safe and valuable modality of practicing leadership behaviors. These favorable responses are similar to the results of other studies that have indicated that learners find simulation as direct, engaging and realistic in equipping them with clinical leadership issues [42]. Simulation supports the educational viability of the common introduction of leadership-based simulation into surgical education programs.

Although a measure of patient safety outcomes is not universal, they proved helpful in the research involving the evaluation of the real-time error reduction in simulated crises. Teams that were assisted with systematic cognitive aids and simulation training had vastly less critical steps compared to teams that were left to rely on merely memory. These findings support prior evidence demonstrating that non-technical skill performance is intimately linked with patient safety outcomes, and that simulation can help close performance gaps before they manifest in real clinical environments [43].

Qualitative findings across studies reinforced the quantitative outcomes, emphasizing enhanced teamwork culture, reflective practice, improved coping under stress, and appreciation for structured behavioral frameworks. These themes are the reflection of larger qualitative studies of simulation-based training that point to the importance of psychological security, practice of challenging conversations, and the emergence of shared team norms [44]. Together, the combination of qualitative and quantitative data supports the idea that simulation in the context of leadership development is multifaceted and can contribute to improving the safety culture among the members of the surgical and multidisciplinary teams.

Limitations

This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The included studies demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in design, simulation modalities, participant groups, and outcome measures, limiting direct comparability across datasets. A critical limitation across the included studies is the absence of longitudinal follow-up, with minimal evidence assessing retention of leadership behaviours or their transfer into real clinical practice, and a near-complete lack of objective patient-centered outcomes. Many studies relied on small sample sizes, lacked randomization, or used observational designs, reducing the strength of causal inference. Leadership and non-technical skill assessments were frequently subjective and dependent on trained raters, which introduces the potential for interrater variability despite the use of validated frameworks. Some studies did not provide sufficient detail about prior training exposure, session fidelity, or facilitator expertise, making it difficult to assess potential confounders. Publication bias might have resulted in favoring of studies which had shown positive effects of simulation-based leadership training. Limiting to English-language, published studies may have caused language or publication bias and restricted the inclusion of unpublished or non-English simulation research.

Future research and clinical implications

Further studies must be done to create standard leadership training, combining simulation and longitudinal evaluation to gain insight into the sustainability of skill mastery. Multi-center randomized studies involving a large base of subjects on the comparative effectiveness of various simulation models can potentially be used to identify the most effective simulation formats towards the acquisition of leadership skills in diverse surgical and interprofessional contexts. More effort is required to improve and authenticate objective leadership metrics that reduce the subjectivity and allow performance more objectively. The use of simulation-based leadership training for patient safety improvement and clinical outcomes, which are less evidence-based, should be studied. There are clinically relevant implications of these results, such as, increased integration of structured leadership simulation in surgical education programs, orientation, and multidisciplinary team building efforts.

Conclusions

This review suggests that simulation-based training may be associated with improvements in leadership, communication, teamwork, and other NTS across surgical and multidisciplinary settings.The most common modality was high-fidelity simulation, and organized systems like the NOTSS and CRM models, that facilitated the regular evaluation of behavior and provided feedback. It has been found that debriefing has become one of the key mechanisms of learning reinforcement and skill consolidation. There were also positive changes in patient safety behaviors, technical performance differences as per experience levels, confidence and satisfaction among the participants. While these findings are encouraging, they remain preliminary due to heterogeneity in study design, outcome measures, and limited longitudinal data. Further high-quality, standardized research is required to better define the impact of simulation-based leadership training on sustained clinical performance and patient outcomes.
