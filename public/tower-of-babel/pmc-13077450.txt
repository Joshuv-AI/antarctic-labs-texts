Blood eosinophil count as a biomarker for therapy guidance in COPD and asthma exacerbations
Authors: Anuradha Nalika Godallage, Dil Afrose, Melika Valizadeh, Augusta Beech, Jens-Ulrik S. Jensen, Pradeesh Sivapalan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This Journal club evaluates recent evidence on the role of blood eosinophil count (BEC) as a biomarker to guide therapy in patients with acute exacerbations of COPD and asthma. The review focuses on three studies with different methodological approaches: a biomarker-directed corticosteroid trial (Bafadhel et al., 2012), an acute biologic therapy trial using benralizumab (ABRA, 2025) and a long-term biologic therapy trial using mepolizumab (COPD-HELP, 2025). The biomarker-directed corticosteroid study demonstrated non-inferior symptom improvement while reducing steroid exposure, highlighting the potential to personalise therapy based on BEC. The ABRA trial showed that a single injection of benralizumab reduced treatment failure, prolonged time to exacerbation and improved symptom scores in patients with high eosinophils. By contrast, the COPD-HELP trial found no significant effect of mepolizumab on readmission or mortality, despite substantial eosinophil reduction. Limitations include small sample sizes, heterogeneous populations and reliance on surrogate end-points in some studies. These findings support the use of BEC-guided therapy in acute settings but suggest that long-term biologic treatment post-exacerbation may not improve hard outcomes. Future research should focus on precision medicine strategies targeting eosinophilic exacerbations and identifying patient subgroups who benefit most from biomarker-guided interventions.

Background

COPD is a heterogeneous, progressive disease characterised by chronic inflammation and respiratory symptoms, punctuated by periods of worsening throughout the course of the disease (exacerbations), leading to irreversible pathophysiology and often progressive airflow limitation [1]. Long-term exposure to irritants, such as cigarette smoke, household gases and occupational pollutants, drives chronic airway inflammation and structural lung damage [1, 2]. COPD contributes to significant morbidity and mortality worldwide, and there is a need to develop targeted therapeutic interventions to tackle the burden of disease.

Blood eosinophil counts as a biomarker

The peripheral blood eosinophil count (BEC) has recently been considered as a diagnostic tool for identifying eosinophilic COPD and assessing response to treatment [3, 4]. Patients with higher BEC have better responses to drugs that target type 2 inflammation, such as corticosteroids and monoclonal antibodies [5]. A meta-analysis has shown that inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) can reduce exacerbation rates by 20% in COPD patients with a BEC of 2% and above, 35% in patients with BEC ≥150 cells·µL−1 and 39% in patients with BEC ≥300 cells·µL−1 [6]. The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease statement suggests that in patients with a history of exacerbations, a benefit from ICS may be observed in patients with a BEC >100 cells·µL−1, with the greatest benefit observed in those with a BEC >300 cells·µL−1 [7]. Systemic corticosteroids as a treatment in the acute exacerbation phase also play a role in reducing the recurrence and length of hospitalisation due to COPD exacerbation in patients with elevated BEC; however, the side-effects, such as hyperglycaemia, hypertension and osteoporosis, and the risk of infections like pneumonia are increased [5, 8]. A moderate relationship has been observed between BEC and sputum eosinophil counts. Furthermore, elevated BEC is associated with a wider pattern of type 2 pulmonary inflammation as evidenced by gene- and protein-expression studies in COPD. Therefore, BEC may also be used as a biomarker of type 2 inflammation [3].

Targeting type 2 inflammation in stable state COPD

Recently, biological treatments targeting eosinophils and type 2 inflammation, such as antibodies against interleukin (IL-5) and the shared receptor for IL-4/IL-13 (IL-4Rα), have been investigated in COPD patients, and their benefits have been demonstrated in subgroups of patients with high BEC. For example, the BOREAS and NOTUS studies showed that adding dupilumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks IL4-Rα, to inhaled triple therapy reduced the annual rate of exacerbation and improved lung function [9–12]. In another study anti-IL-5 receptor antibody treatment numerically reduced the COPD exacerbation rate in patients with high eosinophil counts compared with the placebo group although the difference was not statistically significant.

The MATINEE clinical trial, a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled study of mepolizumab (an IL-5-targeting monoclonal antibody), also demonstrated that mepolizumab resulted in a lower annualised rate of moderate or severe exacerbations compared with placebo (0.80 versus 1.01 events per year; rate ratio 0.79, 95% CI 0.66–0.94; p=0.01) among patients with COPD and an eosinophilic phenotype [11].

Eosinophils are key players in understanding and managing asthma patients and a high-burden subgroup of COPD patients. Considering that elevated BEC is associated with higher exacerbation rate, addressing this specific inflammation is essential to reducing the burden of both diseases and the exacerbation risk. By identifying the molecular pathways involved in type 2 inflammation, these advances can inform targeted therapeutic approaches based on patients' individual characteristics, such as BEC and related inflammatory profiles, thereby improving outcomes and reducing the side-effects of drugs like corticosteroids in COPD and asthma patients.

This Journal club evaluates the role of BEC in guiding targeted therapy for eosinophilic exacerbations in COPD and asthma, focusing on treatments that target eosinophil numbers, including oral corticosteroids and biologics. We assess the impact of these treatments on treatment response, symptom control and hospitalisation risk.

Methods

Bafadhel et al. 2012

This single-centre, randomised, double-blind trial from Glenfield Hospital, Leicester (UK) was conducted between 2007 and 2010 [13]. The trial included 164 patients with acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD). The study investigated whether BEC could guide corticosteroid use.

Participants were randomised to biomarker-directed therapy or standard therapy. In the biomarker-directed arm, patients with BEC >2% received prednisolone 30 mg daily for 14 days, while those with BEC ≤2% received a placebo. In the standard arm, all patients received prednisolone regardless of BEC. All participants were also treated with antibiotics for 7 days (amoxicillin or doxycycline).

Primary end-points included health status as measured by the Chronic Respiratory Questionnaire (CRQ), equivalence in treatment failure and reduced steroid exposure in the biomarker-directed arm. Secondary analyses compared outcomes between biomarker-positive and biomarker-negative exacerbations.

ABRA trial

The acute exacerbations treated with benralizumab (ABRA) trial was a multicentre, phase 2, randomised, double-blind, double-dummy, placebo-controlled study conducted at two UK hospitals to evaluate benralizumab in acute exacerbations of COPD or asthma [14]. Adults (≥18 years) with physician-diagnosed COPD and/or asthma, BEC ≥300 cells·µL−1, and requiring systemic glucocorticoids were eligible. Between May 2021 and February 2024, 287 patients were enrolled.

Patients were randomised (1:1:1) to: 1) prednisolone 30 mg orally for 5 days plus placebo injection (PRED); 2) benralizumab 100 mg s.c. injection plus placebo tablets (BENRA); or 3) benralizumab 100 mg s.c. injection plus prednisolone 30 mg orally for 5 days (BENRA+PRED). Randomisation was stratified by diagnosis, baseline forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), smoking and exacerbation history. Antibiotics were not permitted. Follow-up occurred on days 7, 14, 28 and 90 with spirometry, biomarkers and patient-reported outcomes.

For the primary analysis, the benralizumab treatment arms were pooled based on the prolonged biological activity of benralizumab; however, treatment effects were numerically greater in the BENRA+PRED group, and outcomes for individual treatment arms are also reported. The co-primary outcomes were treatment failure within 90 days and total visual analogue scale (VAS) symptom score at day 28. Secondary outcomes were 30-day treatment failure, time to failure, lung function and quality-of-life.

COPD-HELP trial

The COPD-HELP trial was a phase 2b, single-centre, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled study conducted at Glenfield Hospital, UK, to evaluate mepolizumab in patients hospitalised with AECOPD [15].

Eligible patients were ≥40 years, had prior ICS use and had at least one BEC ≥300 cells·µL−1. Patients with asthma or alternative causes of eosinophilia were excluded. Between August 2020 and November 2023, a total of 238 patients were randomised 1:1 to receive s.c. mepolizumab 100 mg or placebo every 4 weeks for 24–48 weeks, with the first dose given before or within 7 days of discharge. Randomisation was stratified by the Medical Research Council (MRC) dyspnoea scale and prior hospitalisation history.

The primary end-point was time from randomisation to first hospital readmission or death within 48 weeks. Secondary end-points included exacerbations, readmissions, patient-reported outcomes, BEC and safety.

Results

Bafadhel et al. 2012

A total of 109 patients (166 exacerbations) were randomised: 55 to the biomarker-directed arm (86 exacerbations) and 54 to the standard arm (80 exacerbations). Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were comparable between the groups. The study achieved its predefined sample size (a minimum of 53 subjects in each arm) and was adequately powered to assess non-inferiority for the primary outcomes.

The primary outcome was achieved [13]. Health status improvement at 2 weeks and was non-inferior between groups (CRQ change: biomarker-directed 1.1 versus standard 0.8; mean difference 0.3; p=0.05). Treatment failure rates were equivalent (5% versus 13%; p=0.07), demonstrating equivalence with a trend favouring biomarker-directed therapy. Importantly, nearly half of biomarker-directed exacerbations were managed without steroids.

In secondary analyses, biomarker-positive patients benefited from prednisolone (mean improvement in CRQ 1.11 versus 0.56, p<0.01). By contrast, biomarker-negative patients had worse outcomes with steroids than placebo, including higher failure rates and slower recovery. Biomarker-negative exacerbations were neutrophil-driven with greater bacterial load and airway inflammation.

Eosinophil phenotype (BEC >300 cells·µL−1) showed moderate stability, although 22% switched between events.

ABRA trial

A total of 158 patients were randomised: 53 to PRED, 53 to BENRA, and 52 to BENRA+PRED. Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were well balanced across groups.

At 90 days, treatment failure occurred in 39 (74%) of the PRED group, 25 (47%) of the BENRA group, and 22 (42%) of the BENRA+PRED group. When the BENRA arms were pooled, there was a significant reduction in treatment failure risk compared with PRED (OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.12–0.57; p=0.0005) and prolonged time to failure (hazard ratio (HR) 0.39; 95% CI 0.23–0.65; p=0.0003). At 30 days, treatment failure rates were 45% (PRED), 25% (BENRA), and 19% (BENRA+PRED), again favouring the pooled BENRA group (OR 0.30, 95% CI 0.14–0.63; p=0.0015) [14].

Regarding symptoms, the pooled BENRA group showed significant improvements in total VAS symptom score (least-square mean difference –49 mm; p=0.0065) and MRC dyspnoea scale (difference 0.39; p=0.013) compared with PRED. Lung function improved across all groups, without significant between-group differences.

Adverse events were reported in 81% of patients overall; steroid-related effects occurred only in the prednisolone-treated groups.

COPD-HELP trial

A total of 238 patients were randomised: 119 to mepolizumab and 119 to placebo. Baseline characteristics were balanced across groups.

The primary end-point showed no difference between groups [15]. The median time to first readmission or death was 25.4 weeks (mepolizumab) versus 26.1 weeks (placebo) (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.72–1.28; p=0.811), with 63% versus 66% reaching the composite end-point at 48 weeks. Mortality rates were 7.6% and 9.2% in the mepolizumab group and in the placebo group, respectively.

Secondary outcomes showed modest reductions in readmissions (1.65 versus 1.85 per year) and exacerbations (2.80 versus 3.45 per year; rate ratio 0.81, 95% CI 0.66–0.99) in the mepolizumab group compared with placebo. No differences were observed in other time-to-event outcomes.

Patient-reported outcomes showed no consistent differences between groups, although mepolizumab improved the St George's Respiratory Questionnaire-C symptom domain (adjusted mean difference −3.91; 95% CI −7.10 to −0.72).

Adverse events occurred in ∼76% of patients in both groups, with no new safety concerns identified.

Discussion

The studies reviewed in this Journal club provide essential insights into precision medicine approaches for patients with eosinophilic phenotypes in COPD and asthma. Across these trials, it is clear that BEC is a useful biomarker for identifying those patients most likely to benefit from corticosteroids and for guiding therapy intensity during exacerbations.

It is important to recognise that not all eosinophilic exacerbations are the same. Differences in underlying inflammatory pathways, tissue versus blood eosinophil activity [16], and coexisting neutrophilic or infectious triggers probably explain why some patients respond robustly to corticosteroids or biologics, while others do not [17, 18]. Understanding these mechanistic differences may help refine biomarker-guided therapy and identify patients most likely to benefit from targeted interventions.

The COPD-HELP trial examining mepolizumab post-hospitalisation showed no significant reduction in time to readmission or death over 48 weeks, even in patients with persistent eosinophilic inflammation (figure 1) [15]. While modest improvements in exacerbation rates and symptom scores were observed, the results suggest that routine use of mepolizumab in this setting is unlikely to be justified. These findings underscore that eosinophilic inflammation alone may not fully capture the complexity of post-hospitalisation exacerbation risk, and that biologic therapies may need to be targeted more selectively, as not all biologic treatments demonstrate the expected therapeutic benefit.

Overview of the trial findings. BEC: blood eosinophil count.

The findings of Bafadhel et al. [13] have recently been reinforced by the STARR2 trial, a large multicentre, double-blind, non-inferiority study conducted in primary care [19]. STARR2 demonstrated that blood eosinophil-guided prednisolone therapy was non-inferior to standard care with respect to treatment failure, while substantially reducing systemic corticosteroid exposure. Importantly, these results extend the original proof-of-concept findings from a single-centre hospital-based study to a broader, real-world primary care population, supporting the generalisability and safety of eosinophil-guided corticosteroid strategies across different healthcare settings. Along with the CORTICO-COP trial in hospitalised patients [17], these studies collectively demonstrate that blood eosinophil-guided corticosteroid therapy can be safely applied across the full spectrum of AECOPD severity, from primary care to inpatient settings.

By contrast, the ABRA trial demonstrated that a single s.c. injection of benralizumab, alone or combined with a short course of prednisolone, reduced treatment failures, prolonged time to first exacerbation, and improved symptom scores and quality of life compared with prednisolone alone (figure 1) [14]. Importantly, steroid-related adverse events were confined to the prednisolone-treated group in the ABRA trial; 81% of patients overall and 76% in the COPD-HELP trial. However, there were no new safety concerns, which support strong safety conclusions. Although lung function gains were similar across treatment groups, these results suggest that benralizumab offers a promising, well-tolerated alternative or adjunct to corticosteroids for eosinophilic exacerbations, particularly in patients at high risk of steroid-related adverse effects. Limitations include its focus solely on benralizumab, leaving the efficacy of other biologics unaddressed. Another important limitation of ABRA is that it included patients with asthma, and it was not powered to do a subgroup analysis for the COPD patients only.

The GALATHEA and TERRANOVA studies evaluated benralizumab in patients with eosinophilic COPD and found no significant reduction in annual exacerbation rates compared with placebo [20]. However, post hoc analyses suggested that a subgroup of patients with high blood eosinophils, frequent prior exacerbations, and on triple therapy might benefit from benralizumab. These findings align with the ABRA trial and highlight that patient selection and underlying disease characteristics are critical determinants of response to biologic therapy [20].

Finally, the study by Bafadhel et al. [13] on biomarker-directed corticosteroid therapy further reinforced the utility of BEC as a practical, repeatable biomarker (figure 1). Biomarker-positive patients derived clear benefit from prednisolone, whereas biomarker-negative individuals not only experienced less benefit but also had more treatment failures than those receiving a placebo. This strategy offers a safe and effective way to reduce unnecessary steroid exposure, particularly in moderate, outpatient-managed exacerbations, but requires further validation in more severe populations. The STARR2 trial [19] is a larger multicentre version of the Bafadhel et al. [13] study, and it concluded that blood eosinophil-directed prednisolone therapy at the time of an AECOPD is non-inferior to standard care and can be used safely to reduce systemic glucocorticoid use in clinical practice.

Although patients in COPD-HELP represent a clinically high-risk population due to recent hospitalisation, the neutral primary outcome suggests that hospitalisation and blood eosinophilia alone are insufficient to identify patients who derive substantial benefit from mepolizumab. This highlights that “high-risk” COPD is a heterogeneous entity, and that biologic therapies may need to be targeted more selectively within high-risk populations based on additional biological and mechanistic characteristics, including persistence of type 2 inflammation, timing of intervention, and competing inflammatory or infectious drivers.

Similarly, the CORTICO-COP trial extended this concept to hospitalised patients with AECOPD, demonstrating that eosinophil-guided corticosteroid therapy safely reduced steroid duration without increasing treatment failures, mortality or length of stay [17]. These findings supported the role of BEC as a reliable guide for individualised steroid use even in more severe, inpatient settings [19] .

Taken together, these studies emphasise a precision medicine approach to eosinophilic COPD and asthma: tailoring corticosteroid therapy to BEC and considering biologic therapies such as benralizumab for selected patients. Despite promising results, several gaps remain, including the role of other biologics, long-term outcomes and applicability in severe, hospitalised populations. Future research should also clarify how eosinophil-directed strategies interact with other common contributors to exacerbations, including bacterial infections and comorbidities.

In clinical practice, these findings support careful patient stratification: reserving systemic corticosteroids for biomarker-positive patients, considering biologics in high-risk individuals, and avoiding overtreatment in biomarker-negative cases. Such an approach may improve outcomes, minimise adverse effects and optimise resource use, representing a step towards personalised care in respiratory medicine.

Future perspectives

Future research should focus on refining biomarker-guided strategies, integrating blood eosinophils and including exhaled nitric oxide fraction, with additional molecular or imaging markers to predict treatment response better [20]. The role of other biologics beyond benralizumab and mepolizumab, alone or in combination with corticosteroids, warrants exploration. Studies in severely ill hospitalised patients are needed to assess the real-world applicability. Understanding mechanistic heterogeneity among eosinophilic exacerbations could help identify responders versus non-responders. Ultimately, these approaches aim to optimise personalised therapy, improve outcomes and minimise unnecessary exposure to systemic corticosteroids.
