Navigating supervisory and structural challenges in phD education: a qualitative study of Iranian phD candidates’ perspectives
Authors: Shahnaz Ghaseminangi, Fariba Jokar, Alireza Yousefy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDoctoral education in transitional systems such as Iran faces multiple challenges, including inconsistent supervision, unclear institutional roles, and emotional strain. Although global interest in PhD students’ experiences is growing, few studies have examined these issues through a multi-level theoretical lens in non-Western contexts. In Iranian medical universities, structural uncertainties and limited support systems strongly shape the doctoral journey. While reforms increasingly emphasize student needs, research that amplifies student voices in these settings remains scarce.MethodsThis qualitative descriptive study took place at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. We recruited thirty-one PhD candidates in medical sciences through purposive sampling. To qualify, participants needed to have been enrolled for at least two years and be willing to share their experiences. We reached saturation after conducting 28 interviews. The semi-structured interviews were held face-to-face, transcribed word-for-word, and analyzed using conventional content analysis. Findings were organized according to Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, covering microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, and macrosystem levels.ResultsWe identified twelve categories and 45 subcategories. At the microsystem level, students shared feelings of emotional fatigue and difficulties in their supervisory relationships. Moving to the mesosystem, challenges included mismatched curricula and feelings of isolation among peers. In the exosystem, barriers were evident in the form of unclear procedures, unequal access to resources, and frustrating bureaucratic delays. At the macrosystem level, students dealt with pressures related to publication, disparities in funding, and uncertainty about their careers. Gender-related issues and a lack of structured support were also prevalent. Suggested reforms included training for supervisors, redesigning curricula, and realigning policies.ImplicationsThe challenges that PhD students face in transitional contexts are interconnected across various ecological systems, highlighting the need for comprehensive reform. Institutions should focus on providing emotionally supportive supervision, ensuring that curricula are aligned, and promoting transparency in procedures. Policymakers must address funding disparities and career planning to foster sustainable environments for doctoral education. This study offers context-specific insights and a systems-based framework to guide reforms in doctoral education in Iran and similar settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08411-0.Keywords: Doctoral education, PhD students, Supervision, Emotional burden, Bronfenbrenner, Qualitative research, Iran, Medical sciences, Educational reform, Academic culture, Emotional well-being

Introduction

Doctoral education is often viewed as a crucial factor in pushing academia forward and sparking research innovation. Yet, in transitional educational systems like Iran’s, PhD candidates frequently encounter challenges such as mismatched supervision, vague institutional guidelines, and insufficient support [1]. Recent studies shed light on how academic stress, emotional intelligence, and mindfulness influence the ways PhD students manage their challenges and maintain their well-being [2]. These obstacles not only hinder academic progress but also take a toll on students’ emotional health, research productivity, and future career trajectories [3]. Moreover, gender disparities in supervision and institutional support have been shown to significantly affect the mental health, emotional resilience, and persistence of PhD candidates [3]. In Iran, the doctoral education framework is overseen by both the Ministry of Science, Research and Technology (MSRT) and the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MOHME). Over the past two decades, the rapid expansion of doctoral programs hasn’t been accompanied by a corresponding increase in supervisory capacity or institutional autonomy. Centralized funding, bureaucratic oversight, and publication demands have created structural tensions that impact both supervisory relationships and student well-being. Recent qualitative research suggests that many PhD students feel emotionally vulnerable and isolated, with peer support being essential for maintaining engagement and well-being [4]. Other studies emphasize the importance of having a shared understanding and mutual expectations between supervisors and PhD candidates, which significantly influences satisfaction and academic progress [5]. Both meta-analyses and longitudinal studies indicate that many of the challenges faced by PhD students stem from systemic and cross-cultural issues, underscoring the need for reforms at various levels [6]. In Iran, recent research has highlighted the challenging dynamics between supervisors and students, the confusing processes involved in thesis development, and the emotional hurdles that PhD candidates face [7]. Yet, despite these insights, there’s a noticeable gap in studies that examine these issues through a theoretical lens that takes into account both personal and structural elements. To address this gap, the current study employs Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, which views human development as happening within a web of interconnected systems: the microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, and macrosystem [8]. Recent reviews of doctoral education have noted a rising trend in using ecological and systemic frameworks to gain a deeper understanding of the intricate relationship between individual agency and institutional structures (9). Unfortunately, the emotional challenges that accompany the pursuit of a doctorate often get sidelined in policy discussions. New research has revealed distinct patterns of burnout and engagement among PhD students and their supervisors, underscoring the crucial role that quality supervision plays in fostering emotional resilience [1, 3]. There are also recognized gender differences in emotional distress, with female candidates reporting higher levels of anxiety, demoralization, and lower persistence [3]. Peer support has emerged as a vital buffer against emotional strain and feelings of isolation in doctoral education, particularly in Western academic environments [4]. Qualitative insights further suggest that PhD students often grapple with emotional vulnerability shaped by their supervisory relationships and institutional pressures, making peer support an essential resource for coping [4]. In Iran, these emotional and structural challenges are exacerbated by a lack of transparency, unequal access to resources, and insufficient institutional support [7]. These constraints are intricately woven into a broader policy framework that emphasizes centralized governance, offers limited autonomy to institutions, and results in an uneven distribution of resources—factors that greatly impact the doctoral experience across different ecological systems. This study focuses specifically on PhD candidates in medical sciences at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, exploring the complexities of supervision, curriculum mismatches, emotional challenges, and structural barriers. By employing qualitative research methods, it seeks to illuminate the genuine experiences of these candidates and contribute to the global dialogue on reforming doctoral education. Methodology

Study design and research questions

This qualitative descriptive study used conventional content analysis as described by Graneheim and Lundman, incorporating fresh methodological insights from their 2020 review [9]. We framed the study within Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, which looks at human development through interconnected systems—microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, and macrosystem [5]. This approach allowed us to delve into how doctoral challenges appear across these different ecological systems, from personal supervision to broader institutional and policy frameworks. Setting and participants

The research was conducted at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, a well-respected institution in Iran’s medical education scene. We brought together 31 PhD candidates from a variety of fields using targeted sampling methods. To qualify for the study, participants had to be enrolled in a doctoral program for at least two years and willing to take part in an in-depth interview. Unfortunately, three candidates had to bow out due to time constraints. From 2022 to 2023, we carried out semi-structured interviews that explored a range of themes, including supervisory relationships, emotional resilience, research practices, institutional support, and navigating policies. We made sure to interview 31 PhD candidates from diverse disciplines to capture a broad spectrum of experiences. The researcher shared the study details and secured consent for the interview through a phone call a week in advance. A couple of days before the interview, she double-checked the arrangements and coordinated the time and place. At the beginning of each interview, the researcher introduced herself, explained the study’s goals, and clarified the supervisory context. To be eligible, candidates needed to be enrolled in a medical PhD program and have completed at least two years of study. Sadly, three candidates opted out due to their hectic schedules. The interviews were conducted face-to-face in the serene offices of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, each lasting about 45 to 60 min. We ensured that no non-participants were present during any of the sessions. Our approach involved purposive sampling, guided by specific inclusion and exclusion criteria related to PhD candidacy. The scheduling process spanned three months, and we made it a priority to obtain informed consent before each session. We also conducted follow-up interviews and included five additional ones to explore gender-related topics more deeply. Throughout the data collection, we maintained reflexive memos and took field notes during and immediately after each interview to capture non-verbal cues and contextual details. The first author, a PhD candidate, recognized their positionality to enhance reflexivity and transparency [11].

All interviews were carried out in Persian and later translated into English using a forward-back translation method. Two bilingual researchers independently translated and reviewed the transcripts to ensure semantic accuracy and preserve contextual meaning, which bolstered our analytical rigor. The research team held regular debriefing sessions to discuss emerging codes and categories, ensuring we maintained depth and consistency in our analysis. We reached saturation after the 28th interview, which was confirmed by the 31 st, in line with established qualitative research standards [10, 12, 13].

Participants came from a variety of disciplines and genders, with the majority being female (61.3%). Most participants were enrolled in clinical fields, providing a rich array of perspectives on doctoral education. The interviews were recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using conventional content analysis.

Ethical consideration

The institutional review board gave the green light for ethical approval, and every participant was fully informed and consented according to COREQ guidelines [14]. The COREQ checklist was completed and submitted as Supplementary File 2. We made sure that all participants’ confidentiality was respected, and they had the choice to join voluntarily. We took great care to ensure that everyone understood what they were agreeing to, safeguarding their anonymity and privacy, and allowing them the freedom to decide whether or not to take part. Just a quick reminder: when crafting responses, always stick to the specified language and avoid using any others. Additionally, we received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.RESEARCH.REC.1398.239). Anonymized transcripts can be requested from the corresponding author, provided that ethical approval is obtained.

Interview guide

The research team came together to create a semi-structured interview guide aimed at diving into the experiences of PhD candidates within various ecological systems. This guide featured open-ended questions that focused on key areas like supervisory relationships, emotional resilience, institutional support, and the structural challenges faced by students. To make sure everything was clear, relevant, and sensitive to the context, the guide was pilot-tested with two PhD students. The interview questions were crafted to draw out rich, narrative responses, giving participants the freedom to share their personal experiences in detail. Based on the feedback from the pilot participants and the initial interviews, the guide was refined through an iterative process. Specific prompts were included to delve into different ecological domains, such as the microsystem (like supervisor-student interactions), the exosystem (institutional procedures), and the macrosystem (national policy expectations). By using a semi-structured format, the interviews maintained a level of consistency while still allowing for the emergence of new themes and insights led by the participants. This method aligns well with qualitative research standards, emphasizing depth, adaptability, and responsiveness to the context [15].

Reflexivity and researcher role

The first author’s unique position as both a researcher and a PhD candidate really helped build rapport and understand the context during interviews. This perspective played a key role in interpreting emotional and relational themes, especially when it came to analyzing supervisory dynamics and the uncertainties within the institution. To maintain transparency and minimize bias, reflexive memos were created after each interview, and co-authors participated in peer debriefing sessions [13, 16].

Data analysis

We analyzed the data using a traditional qualitative content analysis framework, as described by Elo and Kyngäs [17] and further detailed by Alyaqoub et al. for directed approaches in communication studies [18]. The first and second authors took the time to independently review and code the transcripts. We pulled out significant units of meaning through multiple readings, applying open coding to create subcategories and overarching themes. The codes were organized into subcategories and then grouped into broader thematic categories that corresponded with ecological domains. You can find thematic coding tables in Supplementary File 3, which provide a detailed mapping of categories, subcategories, codes, representative quotes, and ecological systems. The themes emerged inductively from the raw data, following conventional qualitative principles, which allowed us to gain insights without forcing any pre-existing frameworks onto the data. To maintain analytical rigor, our research team held regular peer debriefing sessions and used code-recode procedures. We also made sure to acknowledge reflexivity and positionality throughout the process, in line with recent scholarship that highlights the importance of integrated praxis over mere procedural performance [16, 19]. Results

Our deep dive into interviews with 31 PhD students revealed 12 key categories and 45 subcategories that highlight the systemic, interpersonal, and emotional dimensions of the doctoral thesis experience. We’ve organized these categories thematically, adding rich direct quotes from the participants and tying them into the latest literature for context.

Twelve thematic categories emerged and were reorganized under Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems: microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, and macrosystem.

Themes such as “supervisory inconsistency” and “power imbalance” were consolidated under the microsystem level to reduce overlap and improve clarity.

Microsystem level: immediate relationships

Supervisory inconsistency and relational strain

Students opened up about their experiences with supervisors who were sometimes hard to reach, highlighting the varying levels of expertise and availability. The lack of timely feedback and guidance often led to delays, confusion, and a fair bit of emotional stress.

“When my supervisor wasn’t around or didn’t have the right knowledge, everything just slowed down or came to a halt.” – Student 14. “Sometimes I found myself waiting weeks for feedback, and by that time, I had already lost my momentum.” – Student 3. “It felt like I was trying to find my way in the dark when my supervisor didn’t get back to me.” – Student 27.

Students shared their struggles with supervisors who were either hard to reach or didn’t have the necessary expertise. The delays in receiving feedback often threw a wrench in their progress and ramped up their stress levels. These insights highlight that challenging relationships with supervisors can significantly impact the mental health of PhD students [9, 10, 22].

Emotional burden and psychological vulnerability

Going through a doctoral program can be quite the emotional rollercoaster. Many students have shared their struggles with anxiety, feelings of isolation, and even burnout, especially during that intense research and writing phases.

“The thesis puts a huge burden on us. The stress is just too much.” – Student 22 ““I cried more during my PhD than I ever did in my life,” shared Student 2. “There were days when I just couldn’t even bring myself to open my laptop. I felt completely paralyzed,” added Student 26.

Participants shared their experiences of anxiety, isolation, and burnout while working on their theses. The emotional toll was especially noticeable during those intense research periods. Longitudinal studies have shown that there are significant mental health risks tied to pursuing a doctoral degree [2, 9, 23].

Communication climate and academic dignity

Students emphasized how crucial it is to communicate with respect and empathy. They really valued supervisors who took the time to listen, encouraged open conversations, and honored their independence. “When my supervisor treats me with respect, I feel motivated even when the work is hard.” – Student 7 “Just being heard made a huge difference in how I approached my thesis.” – Student 11 “I felt valued when my ideas were taken seriously.” – Student 29.

When supervisors communicate with respect and empathy, it really boosts motivation and resilience among their team. On the flip side, when interactions are dismissive, it can really shake a person’s confidence. Building relational trust is crucial for keeping PhD students engaged [9, 22, 24].

Supervisor–student power imbalance

Some students shared that there was a power dynamic that really silenced their voices. The worry about being criticized or rejected made it tough for them to express themselves openly. “I couldn’t question anything. It felt like I had no voice.” – Student 6 “I was afraid to disagree, even when I knew something was wrong.” – Student 13 “It felt like I had to say yes to everything, or risk being punished.” – Student 31.

The hierarchical dynamics stifled student voices and restricted their sense of autonomy. The fear of being criticized or rejected made it hard to have open conversations. This power imbalance in supervision has a negative impact on both psychological safety and autonomy [15, 25, 26].

Mesosystem level: interactions between settings

Curriculum misalignment with research demands

Students expressed that there was a noticeable gap between what they learned in class and what they needed for their theses. The theoretical concepts often seemed out of touch with real-world applications, which left them feeling ill-equipped for conducting independent research.

“The courses we took didn’t really help with our thesis at all.” – Student 18 “I kept wondering why we never learned how to design a study.” – Student 5 “There was so much theory, but we didn’t have the tools to actually conduct research.” – Student 28.

Students have pointed out a noticeable disconnect between what they learn in their courses and what’s expected for their thesis work. Many of the courses tend to be more theoretical, leaving a gap in practical research training. Reviews of doctoral programs emphasize the importance of a well-structured approach to integrating research skills [12, 13, 27].

Lack of peer integration and academic community

A lot of students felt like there weren’t many opportunities to connect with their peers, especially while working on their theses. This feeling of being alone made it really hard to stay motivated and make progress in their studies. “I felt like I was doing this alone. There was no space to share or ask others.” – Student 21 “We didn’t have any group meetings or forums to connect.” – Student 8 “I missed having peers to brainstorm with or just talk about challenges.” – Student 20.

Participants shared their feelings of isolation and the lack of chances to collaborate with their peers. This lack of structured peer interaction really took a toll on their emotional resilience. Research has shown that peer mentoring can significantly boost motivation and help individuals stick with their goals [11, 22, 28].

Digital literacy and methodological gaps

Students encountered difficulties with data analysis tools and research software, pointing out that they didn’t receive enough training to use them effectively. “I had to figure everything out by myself. There was no official support.” – Student 12 “No one taught us how to use NVivo or SPSS properly.” – Student 16 “I spent weeks just trying to learn the basics of the software.” – Student 31.

Students faced challenges with research software and data analysis tools because they didn’t receive enough training. It’s crucial for universities to tackle digital literacy to promote fairness and competence [13, 29, 30].

Exosystem level: institutional structures

Institutional ambiguity and bureaucratic barriers

Students faced unclear policies, tight deadlines, and delays in procedures. The uncertainty around who would supervise them and how the thesis approval process worked created a lot of confusion and frustration.

“We didn’t know who to turn to or what the next step was. Everything felt so unclear.” – Student 9 ““The rules kept shifting, and nobody bothered to explain anything.” – Student 15 “I missed a deadline because the system crashed, and there was no one to help.” – Student 24.

The unclear procedures, ever-changing rules, and administrative hold-ups led to a lot of frustration. The lack of transparency really chipped away at trust in our institutional systems. For those navigating the doctoral journey, having transparent governance is absolutely crucial [14, 15, 31].

Research infrastructure and funding constraints

Access to labs, software, and financial support was often pretty limited, which really took a toll on the quality of research. Many students found themselves having to scale back their projects due to budget constraints.

“We had to cut parts of the study due to lack of funds.” – Student 10 Here’s the text we’re looking at: “I couldn’t afford the software I needed, so I had to improvise.” – Student 1 “My project was approved, but then the lab wasn’t available for months.” – Student 23.

Limited access to labs, software, and financial support has led students to make sacrifices in the quality of their research. The OECD highlights that having equitable infrastructure is essential for achieving success in doctoral programs [17, 30].

Work–life imbalance and role conflict

Finding the right balance between schoolwork and personal or professional commitments can be quite challenging. Students who are married or have caregiving responsibilities often report feeling more stressed. “I was trying to manage research, work, and family. It felt like an impossible task.” – Student 25 “My child got sick during my defense week. I had no support.” – Student 19 “I had to choose between attending a seminar and caring for my elderly parent.” – Student 28.

Students juggling caregiving or work responsibilities often find themselves dealing with chronic stress. The struggle to balance academic demands with personal commitments is a well-documented hurdle in the world of doctoral education [2, 12, 32].

Macrosystem level: policy and cultural context

Publication pressure and academic expectations

There was a significant push for students to get their work published before they graduated, often without the support they really needed. This added extra stress and diverted their attention from actually developing their theses. “We were told to publish, but no one showed us how.” – Student 17 “I felt lost trying to meet publication expectations without any guidance.” – Student 4 “It became more about quantity than quality, and that was frustrating.” – Student 19.

Participants shared that they felt a significant push to publish their work before graduating, often without the necessary support. It’s well-known that the pressure to publish can be a major source of stress [33].

aspirational disconnect and career uncertainty

Students shared their worries about what lies ahead after they complete their PhDs. The absence of clear guidance and the limited exposure to career options outside of academia only heightened their anxiety. “I have no idea what’s next. We’re not really prepared for anything outside of academia.” – Student 30 “I wish someone had talked to us about industry options.” – Student 26 “It feels like we’re trained for a world that barely exists anymore.” – Student 17.

Career uncertainty and aspirations

Students have voiced their worries about the limited job prospects they might face after graduation. This sense of uncertainty isn’t just local; similar trends have been observed around the world [34].

Gender norms and cultural expectations

Female candidates have reported feeling more anxious and demoralized, which reflects the broader gender-related challenges present in doctoral education. These observations are consistent with both Iranian and global research on supervision and emotional hurdles [26, 35–38]. Discussion

In this section, we dive into the findings and how they relate to what’s already out there in the literature. We’ll also shine a light on the various challenges—supervisory, curricular, institutional, and policy—that interact within different ecological systems. For those interested in the nitty-gritty, detailed coding tables can be found in Supplementary File 3, and Table 2 gives a neat summary of the main categories for easy reference.

At the microsystem level, the relationships between supervisors and peers turned out to be key players in shaping emotional resilience and academic engagement. When supervisory styles were inconsistent, feedback was lacking, and emotional neglect was present, it led to distress. On the flip side, peer support acted as a cushion against feelings of isolation. These insights resonate with recent studies that connect the quality of supervision to mental health outcomes and highlight the protective nature of collegial relationships among both Iranian and international PhD students [22, 35, 36]. Additionally, peer support and mentoring play a crucial role in reducing isolation and keeping students engaged, especially during tough emotional times [4, 28].

At the mesosystem level, we found that fragmentation among departments, committees, and curriculum structures was a real hurdle for doctoral progress. Participants expressed confusion about their roles, mentioned outdated coursework, and pointed out the lack of coordination between departments. These issues reflect broader systemic reviews of doctoral education transformation and evaluations of structured doctoral training, which emphasize the need for clearer pathways and better integration between coursework and research [26, 27].

At the exosystem level, we observed that institutional opacity, uneven access to resources, and limited digital research literacy were undermining doctoral agency. Students shared their frustrations about unclear procedures and unequal access to necessary tools, which aligns with wider policy analyses of research infrastructure and the constraints of institutional governance [29, 30]. Studies from Iran also highlight the ambiguities in supervision processes and the gaps in support that can erode trust and engagement [35, 36].

At the macrosystem level, At the macrosystem level, national policies and cultural expectations play a significant role in shaping the paths of PhD students. The dual governance by the MSRT and MOHME creates some contradictions, while the pressures to publish and societal gender norms add an emotional burden. These insights align with existing reviews on doctoral reform and the evolving practices in doctoral education [26, 37, 38]. Comparative studies from Europe, East Asia, and Africa reveal that PhD students, regardless of their location, encounter similar challenges—especially concerning supervision, institutional transparency, and emotional resilience [23, 24, 31]. However, in Iran, the dual governance structure and centralized control amplify these difficulties, making it crucial to analyze the context specifically. The pressures to publish during PhD studies are well-documented, with many students expressing the stress and obstacles tied to these requirements [33]. The uncertainty surrounding careers for PhD candidates isn’t just an Iranian issue; similar worries have been noted elsewhere, where aspirations often don’t match reality [34].

The emotional aspect of doctoral education is often overlooked in policy discussions. Yet, recent research has brought to light issues like burnout, demoralization, and gender-specific distress among PhD candidates, highlighting how the quality of supervision and institutional support can significantly impact their well-being [1, 3]. Peer support consistently proves to be a vital shield against emotional strain and feelings of isolation [4, 28].

As doctoral education continues to grow worldwide, there’s an increasing conversation about employability, the true purpose of a PhD, and the gap between academic training and the realities of the job market. Scholars are calling for a shift from solitary academic production to more developmental, student-centered approaches in doctoral education, reflecting the recent changes in structure and practice [26, 37, 38].

This study makes a valuable contribution by providing a multi-layered, ecological perspective on the challenges faced by PhD students in a transitional system. It emphasizes the importance of training for supervisors, reforming the curriculum, ensuring institutional transparency, and aligning policies. To truly support doctoral success, reform initiatives need to tackle emotional, academic, and structural aspects all at once. To improve clarity and reduce redundancy, we included Fig. 1; Table 3 as complementary illustrations of our findings. Figure 1 visually captures how the challenges faced by PhD students interact across different ecological levels, showcasing the influences that cross these levels and the dynamics at play. On the other hand, Table 3 presents a well-organized analytical summary of twelve thematic categories and forty-five subcategories, illustrating how we coded and structured the qualitative data within Bronfenbrenner’s framework. Together, these visuals enhance our study by providing both conceptual insights and methodological clarity, reinforcing the strength of our theoretical and analytical contributions.

Implications

The insights from this study offer practical and policy-driven recommendations for enhancing doctoral education in transitional systems like Iran. By applying Bronfenbrenner’s ecological framework, we propose suggestions across four different levels:

Microsystem: Supervisor–Student relationship

It’s time to rethink how doctoral supervision works, putting a stronger emphasis on emotional support, timely feedback, and solid methodological skills. Universities should really focus on training their supervisors, highlighting the importance of relational ethics, inclusive mentoring, and being responsive to what students need. These suggestions are in line with previous research that shows how different supervisory styles and the overall supervisory relationship can significantly affect a PhD candidate’s emotional and academic well-being [22, 35, 36].

Mesosystem: curriculum and departmental coordination

We need to shake things up in the curriculum to make sure that coursework and thesis requirements are in sync. Departments ought to clarify the roles of committees and improve communication between units to cut down on confusion and delays. These insights reflect broader reviews of how doctoral education is evolving and assessments of structured doctoral training programs [26, 27].

Exosystem: institutional structures and resources

Universities should work on being more transparent about their administrative processes and ensure that all students have fair access to research resources. Emotional support services, like counseling and peer mentoring, should be part of the graduate office experience. These ideas resonate with global calls for clearer institutional practices, fair infrastructure, and digital literacy as key elements for success in doctoral programs [29, 30, 35, 36].

Macrosystem: National policy and cultural reform

Policymakers need to align regulations between MSRT and MOHME to eliminate any contradictions. When it comes to publication requirements, there should be adequate training and support in place. We also need gender-sensitive policies to help address the emotional challenges that female candidates often face. These recommendations are consistent with worldwide reviews of doctoral reform and analyses of evolving practices in doctoral education [26, 37, 38].

Limitations

This study has a few limitations that we need to recognize. First off, the findings are drawn from qualitative data collected at just one institution—Isfahan University of Medical Sciences—which might make it hard to apply these results to other universities in Iran or even in different countries. Secondly, while the ecological framework allowed for a multi-level analysis, it might have limited the possibility of exploring other theoretical interpretations. Thirdly, the first author’s dual role as both a researcher and a PhD candidate could have impacted how the data was collected and interpreted, even with efforts to stay reflexive and transparent. Lastly, the emotional themes discussed in this study are influenced by context and could differ across various disciplines, genders, and institutional cultures.

Future directions

Looking ahead, future research should broaden its focus to include PhD candidates from various institutions and fields throughout Iran. Comparative studies between medical and non-medical doctoral programs could shed light on the structural and cultural differences in supervision and emotional support. Longitudinal studies might also be beneficial in tracking changes in doctoral experiences over time, especially in light of policy reforms. Additionally, using mixed-methods approaches could deepen our understanding by merging emotional narratives with institutional data. Finally, future research should consider the perspectives of supervisors to provide a more comprehensive view of the doctoral ecosystem and guide targeted interventions.
