Risk Factors and Liability Distribution in Medical Disputes Related to Spinal Diseases: A Six-Year Retrospective Study in Beijing
Authors: Hongzhen Li, Haoyuan Li, Jie Huang, Shuaiqi Zhu, Weiwei Xia, Zhenqi Zhu, Haiying Liu, Chenjun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo summarize the characteristics and liability distribution of medical disputes related to spinal diseases in Beijing and to identify factors associated with major harm and liability allocation.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 180 spine-related dispute cases obtained from the Beijing Medical Dispute Mediation Committee (January 2015-December 2020). We extracted information on outcomes, liability allocation, and potential risk factors, and performed descriptive and regression analyses.ResultsThe main adverse events leading to disputes were misdiagnosis/mistreatment (61 cases, 33.9%), surgical/operation errors (54 cases, 30.0%), infection (13 cases, 7.2%), new neurologic deficit (13 cases, 7.2%), and insufficient symptom relief (10 cases, 5.6%). Additionally, 29 cases (16.1%) were determined as disease/treatment itself. Physicians were primarily liable in 116 cases (64.4%), and major patient harm occurred in 33 cases (18.3%). Major harm was associated with misdiagnosis/mistreatment (univariate analysis, P=0.006; multivariate analysis, OR= 3.281, 95% CI 1.359–7.918, P=0.008), emergency (univariate analysis, P=0.020; multivariate analysis, OR=13.446, 95% CI 1.042–173.422, P=0.046), and treatment during holidays (univariate analysis, P=0.035; multivariate analysis, OR=4.125, 95% CI 1.182–14.399, P=0.026). Liability distribution for misdiagnosis/mistreatment was unfavorable to the doctors (univariate analysis, P=0.028; multivariate analysis, OR=2.144, 95% CI 1.071–4.291, P=0.031). Overall, 116 cases (64.4%) were resolved through mediation, with an actual average compensation amount of 220,272.72 RMB (range 5,000–2,381,523 RMB).ConclusionThis study characterizes the clinical features, liability distribution, and factors associated with major harm in spine-related medical disputes handled through formal mediation in Beijing. The findings provide region-specific evidence that may support targeted quality improvement, clinical risk management, and dispute-prevention strategies in spine care.Keywords: medical disputes, spinal diseases, risk factors, misdiagnosis/mistreatment, liability distribution

Introduction

In the current medicolegal environment, spinal surgery is among the medical subspecialties most frequently involved in litigation.1–3 Medical disputes typically arise when healthcare professionals’ practices deviate from standard protocols, leading to patient harm or dissatisfaction.4 Such disputes impose significant financial strain, time requirements, and emotional distress for the physicians involved. Identifying the patient and surgeon factors that are associated with a higher likelihood of formal complaints is essential.5 Recognizing these risk factors for complaints may enable surgeons and healthcare systems to define reasonable strategies and advocate for necessary reforms aimed at minimizing their risk of malpractice litigation.6

Despite the increasing prevalence of spine-related medical issues, limited data exist specifically addressing the characteristics and resolution of these disputes in Beijing. In China, medical disputes are commonly addressed through private settlement, mediation, administrative resolution, or judicial litigation.4 The Beijing Medical Dispute Mediation Committee handles formally mediated medical disputes, and its records include finalized information on liability, patient harm, and mediation outcomes. Compared with national-level databases or court-based litigation records, formal mediation files provide a distinct source for examining the characteristics and resolution of medical disputes.

This research provided a general description and risk factor analysis of spine-related dispute claims by reviewing the mediation files provided by the Beijing Medical Dispute Mediation Committee for medical disputes, to investigate the features and risk factors of spine-related medical disputes in Beijing, and minimize medical disputes as much as possible in the future, fostering a more effective and harmonious healthcare environment.

Methods

Study Design and Case Selection

A retrospective cross-sectional study was carried out using all available mediation files on spine-related medical disputes provided by the Beijing Medical Dispute Mediation Committee from January 2015 to December 2020. Cases were eligible if they (1) primarily concerned the diagnosis or treatment of spinal diseases or spine-related procedures, (2) had a completed mediation record with a finalized responsibility appraisal, and (3) contained sufficient information on key variables, including adverse event category, liability allocation, and patient harm appraisal (death or disability grade). We excluded cases that (1) did not primarily concern the diagnosis or treatment of spinal diseases or spine-related procedures, (2) were duplicates, (3) were ongoing or lacked a finalized appraisal, or (4) were missing core outcome information required for analysis.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee (Institutional Review Board) of Peking University People’s Hospital (approval No. 2015PHB186-01). Informed consent was waived by the committee due to the retrospective nature of the study. All patient information was de-identified before analysis, and patient confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study. The reporting of this study was reviewed with reference to the STROBE statement for cross-sectional studies.

Data Extraction and Quality Control

Two researchers independently extracted data on case characteristics, potential risk factors, outcomes, and liability distribution from the mediation records using a predefined data extraction form. Any discrepant entries were reviewed by two additional investigators before the final dataset was established.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were compared using the χ2-test or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with major harm and liability allocation. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariable analyses were considered for inclusion in the multivariable models based on their clinical relevance. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Based on the predefined criteria, 246 cases were identified. Among them, 34 cases lacked essential data, and 32 cases were excluded after full-record review because the spinal condition was not the main subject of the dispute, leaving 180 cases for the final analysis (Figure 1).

Flowchart describing cases that were included and those that were excluded.

Responsibility categories were extracted directly from the finalized appraisal records. We divided the liability distribution into patients’ favor (doctors bear no less than 50% of the responsibility) and doctors’ favor (doctors bear less than 50% of the responsibility). In this study, patients’ favor (doctors bore the main responsibility) included full responsibility, primary responsibility, and equal responsibility, while doctors’ favor included secondary responsibility, minor responsibility, and no responsibility. According to the Medical Accident Classification Standard (Trial) issued by the Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China, patient harm was classified as death or disability grades 1–107 In this study, major harm was defined as death or disability grades 1–6, which generally indicate markedly limited self-care ability. Minor harm was defined as disability grades 7–10 or no obvious harm.

We further evaluated factors associated with major harm and liability allocation. Univariate analysis (Table 1) indicated that the rate of major damage to patients was greater in relation to the following parameters: misdiagnosis/mistreatment [18 cases (54.5%) vs 43 cases (29.3%), P=0.006], emergency [3 cases (9.1%) vs 1 case (0.7%), P=0.020], spinal region [P=0.040], holidays [6 cases (18.2%) vs 8 cases (5.4%), P=0.035], night shift [8 cases (24.2%) vs 13 cases (8.8%), P=0.029]. At the same time, the distribution of liability for the following situations was unfavorable to doctors: type of intervention [P<0.001] and misdiagnosis/mistreatment [46 cases (39.7%) vs 15 cases (23.4%), P=0.028]. Hospital tier was not significantly associated with major harm or liability allocation (P=0.626 and P=0.180, respectively).

Discussion

In recent years, medical disputes have drawn increasing attention and widely affected healthcare professionals in numerous countries, including China. Despite this, there remains a relative shortage of analysis and research on litigation associated with medical disputes in China. Spinal surgery, in particular, involves greater technical challenges and higher neurological risks compared to other surgical specialties. In this study, an overall description and risk factor analysis were conducted on medical disputes of spinal surgery in Beijing. We systematically examined the common characteristics of these cases, aiming to utilize the findings to enhance the quality of medical services and lower the occurrence of such disputes. Meanwhile, we found that misdiagnosis/mistreatment, emergency presentation, and treatment occurring during holidays were associated with major harm. Misdiagnosis/mistreatment was also associated with physician-unfavorable liability allocation.

In our study, the most frequently reported adverse events (AEs) causing claims included misdiagnosis/mistreatment, surgical/operation errors, infections, new neurologic deficit (NND), and insufficient symptom relief. Misdiagnosis encompasses diagnostic error, missed diagnosis, delayed diagnosis, or a failure to perform proper investigations, while mistreatment refers to follow-up treatment based on an incorrect diagnosis.4 Previous studies have shown that the most frequent plaintiff allegations in spine litigation involve procedural complications, absence of informed consent, and failure to diagnose or treat promptly.8 Yue et al found that the predominant AEs resulting in claims were NND (47.6%, 137 cases), followed by infection (11.5%, 33 cases), and insufficient symptom relief (10.4%, 30 cases).2 Similarly, Debono et al identified 22 cases of malpractice and found that the most frequent fault was delayed diagnosis, followed by insufficient surgical management, improper antibiotic therapy, and insufficient follow-up.9 In response to delayed diagnosis, we should also allow patients to complete relevant examinations as quickly as possible, with attention to subtle clinical manifestations. Also, Zhang et al found that the most common injuries leading to litigation were refractory pain and suffering (37%, n=149), followed by permanent neurological deficits (26%, n=106).10 Although the most common adverse events may vary in different studies, the types of adverse events are similar. The majority of medical errors attributed to orthopedists in malpractice cases were associated with inadequate patient supervision or monitoring, improper procedural execution, and oversight in instructing or communicating with the patients.11,12

Given the fact that there are no spinal treatments that are completely free of nerve damage, our aim should be to avoid nerve damage as much as possible. To avoid the occurrence of adverse events, we need to clarify prevention and control strategies and make every effort to minimize them. Moreover, we should improve diagnostic and treatment standards, actively manage complications, and communicate promptly with patients both before and after surgery.13

In our cohort, major harm occurred in 33 cases (18.3%), including 18 deaths (10.0%) and 15 cases of disability grades 1–6 (8.3%), whereas physicians bore at least half of the responsibility in 116 cases (64.4%). This difference suggests that physician-unfavorable liability allocation was not limited to cases involving death or severe disability. From a clinical risk-management perspective, attention should therefore extend beyond catastrophic outcomes to the diagnostic and treatment processes underlying a broader range of disputes. This pattern differs from several reports from other jurisdictions, where defendant-favoring verdicts are frequently observed in spine-related malpractice litigation. Park et al found that in their research, 182 (71%) resulted in a defendant verdict, 44 (17%) plaintiff verdict, and 31 (12%) settlement; the data showed that the verdict was favorable to the defendant’s surgeon.14 Similarly, Agarwal et al analyzed 98 spine surgery-related lawsuits from 2010 to 2015 and found that 62 cases (63.3%) resulted in a verdict favorable to the defendant.15 Medical lawsuits can also have a significant impact on surgeons. Adelani et al noted that orthopedic surgeons who had faced lawsuits reported higher rates of medical errors.16 Among these surgeons, supportive interventions are necessary to protect professional well-being and mitigate the factors contributing to increased medical errors.17 Additionally, the proportion of physician-unfavorable outcomes in our cohort appears higher than that reported in some international litigation series. However, direct comparisons should be made cautiously, given differences in medicolegal frameworks and case inclusion mechanisms.18–20 This difference may be partly related to the evidentiary approaches used in medical-accident disputes. In many negligence-based systems, plaintiffs typically bear the burden of proving negligence, whereas in China, such disputes often rely heavily on medical record completeness and expert appraisal; the practical burden may differ by case type and jurisdiction.18,21 The current medical environment may encourage doctors to engage in “defensive medicine”, which may increase the use of low-yield screening and procedures.22,23 Moreover, Li et al found that patient safety and healthcare quality were better in more financially developed regions.24 Continued improvements in healthcare resources, quality assurance, and dispute-resolution mechanisms may help reduce the frequency and impact of medical disputes over time.

In the course of our research, we found that misdiagnosis/mistreatment was a risk factor for physician-unfavorable liability allocation. Yang et al reported that the mortality rate among misdiagnosed patients is higher than that of accurately diagnosed patients.25 Misdiagnosis/mistreatment is mainly related to the physician’s medical technology. Such errors can result in patients not receiving timely and accurate treatment, potentially leading to further disease progression. Clinical practitioners should be aware of the importance of early diagnosis and timely treatment and adopt various examinations to assist clinical diagnosis.

In our study, the most common adverse events causing claims were misdiagnosis or mistreatment, followed by surgical/operation errors. Misdiagnosis/mistreatment and surgical/operation errors are often related to negligence inconsistent with existing medical expertise or technical standards, indicating substandard medical care. Differences in clinical workflows, training exposure, and resource availability may influence dispute patterns. Meanwhile, compared to China, Western countries have a wider range of information sources and more comprehensive and specific data. The absence of publicly available data presents a significant barrier to evaluating patient safety in China, and it also prevents us from learning from past claims cases.26,27 We need to focus on better monitoring patient-centered care, rather than merely patient satisfaction. Adverse outcomes must be measured with the same precision we apply to patient safety events, utilizing root cause analysis to drive corrective action. Furthermore, interventions must be formally evaluated, and important findings should be shared, even if some results are inconclusive.28

Extensive research has also been conducted on preventive strategies for medical claims after spinal surgery. Spinal surgeons should attach great importance to relevant risk factors and some past medical accident claim cases. There is research demonstrating the potential need for more comprehensive informed consent discussions regarding elective spinal procedures and enhanced patient education before surgery. Effectively expressing the risks, benefits, and alternatives associated with procedures, involving patients in the decision-making process, and documenting goals and expectations can mitigate the likelihood of medical malpractice lawsuits.29 Additionally, the high number of lawsuits in the study citing inadequate postoperative management as grounds for litigation underscores the need for improved patient monitoring to timely diagnose potential postoperative complications.30 Harlianto et al found that comprehensive analyses of medical negligence are essential for identifying specific risk patterns and raising awareness of pitfalls in the care of orthopedic patients, which helps reduce the probability of future medical errors.31 At the same time, Bernardinangeli et al pointed out how necessary it is to fully understand litigation outcomes in formulating preventive strategies to reduce the number of claims.32 It is essential to strengthen communication between doctors and patients, further standardize the diagnosis and treatment process, and reflect on previous mistakes promptly. Doctors can only minimize the risk of litigation by fulfilling their responsibilities as much as possible.

In recent years, there has been an increasing demand for minimally invasive procedures and faster recovery. This has put forward an urgent need for the further development of minimally invasive spinal surgery with less intraoperative damage and faster recovery speed.33 However, the disputes arising from inappropriate minimally invasive spinal surgery should also draw our attention and reflection. We found that traditional open surgery accounted for 76.7% and minimally invasive surgery accounted for 22.2% of all medical dispute cases in this study. The proportion of patients’ favorable outcomes was higher for claims related to minimally invasive spine surgery. We have also conducted further analysis on medical claims after minimally invasive surgery. Jin-Tao et al pointed out that for minimally invasive spine surgery, surgeons need more training and experience; otherwise, minimally invasive surgery may lead to unsatisfactory results for patients.34 Sometimes people are too proactive in choosing minimally invasive surgical treatment, which can lead to an expansion of surgical indications, and the postoperative effect may not meet expectations. Meanwhile, to meet the expectations of patients for small incisions, many doctors often exaggerate the effectiveness of the surgery and fail to fully inform patients of the potential risks or complications. In research, many patients have shown unsatisfactory results after surgery, which can lead to patient dissatisfaction or litigation.35

Our findings also showed that emergency presentation and treatment during holidays were associated with major harm. Due to the urgent condition of emergency patients, doctors may have limited time to complete all diagnostic examinations before treatment.36,37 The resulting reduction in preparation time may partly contribute to the observed association. During holidays, reduced staffing and limited medical resources may also affect the timeliness and coordination of care.38 These factors may provide a possible explanation for the observed association between holiday care and major harm.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, key demographic variables were not consistently recorded across all mediation files and, therefore, could not be incorporated into the analysis, which limited subgroup exploration and sensitivity analyses. Second, we analyzed only disputes that entered the formal mediation process and had a finalized appraisal; cases resolved through other channels or without complete appraisal documentation were not captured. Accordingly, selection by dispute-resolution channel may have influenced the observed distributions of adverse events and liability allocation. Third, we only focused on a six-year sample (2015–2020) because documentation during this period was sufficiently complete and standardized to support reliable variable extraction under the current data-access framework. Future studies with broader coverage and more granular clinical and demographic information would help further validate and refine these findings.

Conclusion

In our study, we found that in most disputes, doctors bore the main responsibility (116 cases, 64.4%), while only a small proportion of cases involved major harm (33 cases, 18.3%). Misdiagnosis/mistreatment, emergency presentation, and treatment during holidays were associated with major harm, while misdiagnosis/mistreatment was also associated with physician-unfavorable liability allocation. This study provides a comprehensive retrospective analysis of medical disputes associated with spinal diseases in Beijing. These findings highlight diagnostic accuracy, emergency and holiday care organization, and clinical risk management as potential priorities for quality improvement and dispute prevention in spine care.
