Urbanization increases the complexity of soil microbial interaction networks
Authors: Xin Liu, Guixiang Li, Mingzhao Han, Peng Li, Wei Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrbanization reshapes soil ecosystems, yet its impacts on the composition and interactions of soil microbial communities remain insufficiently understood. Here, we use high-throughput sequencing to profile bacteria, fungi, and protists across four representative habitat types of different urbanization level—campuses, roadside greenbelts, suburban unmanaged lands, and concrete pavements in four Chinese cities spanning a broad latitudes (Changchun, Beijing, Hangzhou, Haikou). Vegetated habitats consistently structure microbial communities, whereas concrete pavements form a distinct disturbed habitat with the lowest microbial richness. Bacterial richness is highest in campuses and roadside greenbelts, while fungal and protistan richness peaks in suburban unmanaged lands. Despite regional variation, communities under similar vegetation types show convergent compositions, jointly shaped by regional climate and land-use intensity. Bacterial assemblages are more sensitive to environmental variation than fungi and protists. Proteobacteria dominate bacteria communities, whereas fungal and protistan communities display marked habitat specificity. Co-occurrence networks complexity peaks in roadside greenbelts, remains stable in suburban unmanaged lands and collapses significantly in concrete pavements. Microbial diversity patterns are strongly associated with temperature, soil pH, and latitude. Together, these results demonstrate that environmental conditions and urbanization intensity drive the biogeography and interaction patterns of soil microbiomes, providing insights for ecological restoration and urban green-space management.Subject terms: Biodiversity, Bacteria, Environmental microbiology, Microbial ecology

Introduction

Soil is a complex and diverse microbial habitat on earth and serves as a primary site of microbial activity in terrestrial ecosystems. Soil microorganisms play critical roles in connecting human, animal, plant, and environmental health, making them essential for the functioning and sustainability of the entire ecosystems1–3. As core components of ecosystems, soil microbes are pivotal to nutrient cycling, biogeochemical processes, environmental monitoring, and overall ecological health4. Microbial communities, including bacteria and fungi, drive material cycling within ecosystems by decomposing organic matter, recycling nutrients, and transforming essential elements5,6. Fungi contribute significantly to soil structure, nutrient mobilization for plants, and carbon storage through processes such as hyphae network formation and organic matter decomposition6,7. Protists, displaying immense diversity, play crucial roles as primary producers, consumers, decomposers, and pathogens, and certain groups (including parasitic protists like Myxozoa) are valuable as bioindicators of environmental changes8.

Urbanization is the concentration of population and non-agricultural activities in urban areas, driving deep modernization of societal structures and landscapes. Accelerated urbanization reshapes soil ecosystems by altering soil properties, restructuring microbial communities9, and disrupting biogeochemical cycles10,11. Consequences include soil acidification in urban forests12 and anthropogenic activities exceeding climatic controls on urban vegetation in regions like Nanjing and the Sichuan–Chongqing area of China13,14. Crucially, urbanization imposes distinct environmental pressures through profound land use conversion (e.g., green-space fragmentation, sealed surfaces, pollution deposition). These anthropogenic stressors fundamentally reshape the structure and function of soil microbial communities9. Land use changes specifically impact the structure of bacterial and fungal communities differently. Bacterial communities often exhibit increased alpha diversity15 and functional homogenization16 in response to altered land use, with greater diversity detected among reductive processes17. In contrast, fungi demonstrate enhanced sensitivity to land use alterations, agricultural activities significantly reducing fungal abundance18 and community homogenization occurs more slowly within fungal assemblages than in bacterial ones16.

Furthermore, urbanization alters key abiotic drivers of microbial diversity, including soil pH, microclimate via the urban heat island effect and modified hydrology, and local biogeochemistry. In forest soils, soil pH predominantly structured microbial bata diversity, with fungi exhibiting higher bata diversity in acidic soils and bacteria showing higher bata diversity in neutral to alkaline soils19. Temperature fluctuations can suppress microbial metabolic activity, with abrupt temperature shifts in subtropical regions found to reduce the activity of bacterial carbon-degrading enzymes20. Such modifications to the physicochemical environment interact with land use driven pressures to influence microbial metabolic activity, community composition, and functional potential19–21. Thus, a deeper understanding of these driving factors is essential for clarifying the functional roles of microbial communities in key ecosystem processes.

Fluxes of carbon and nitrogen cycling mediated by microbes have been shown to change under urbanization11, with their intensity displaying a nonlinear positive correlation with soil sealing rates9. However, current research remains limited regarding newly emerging high-intensity land use types. Compared to well-studied habitats such as agricultural fields and urban forests, typical urban environments resulting from intensified urbanization, such as densely populated urban campuses, managed roadside greenbelts, and concrete pavements, have received limited research attention. Mechanisms of microbial stress and ecological impacts in these environments of different urbanization are not well understood, performing cross-habitat research then to uncover microbial adaptation strategies under intense urban disturbance will highlight the understanding the ecological effects of urbanization.

Microbial co-occurrence networks provide a system-level lens to decode complex community assemblies beyond taxonomic inventories, transforming species abundance matrices into interaction graphs where nodes represent operational taxonomic units (OTUs) and edges reflect statistically robust correlations22. Meanwhile, the networks could also quantify critical topological properties23–25, including niche partitioning, community stability, and functional redundancy. In the context of urban ecosystems, the impact of human activities on the environment is evident through various forms of disruption, such as chemical pollution, fragmentation of habitats, and alteration to microclimates. The resulting simplification of network complexity is theorised to occur through discrete pathways. Consequently, these processes pose a threat to microbiome multifunctionality by impairing nutrient cycling and disrupting biotic interactions23,26,27.

In this study, soil samples from four representative cities across different latitudes and climate zones—Changchun, Beijing, Hangzhou, and Haikou, were selected to clarify the effects of urbanization and associated land use types on the composition, structure, and symbiotic networks of soil microbial communities, as well as to identify key environmental drivers shaping microbial diversity distribution. In order to characterise these dynamics, our analytical framework specifically addresses methodological limitations through compositionality-aware correlation inference via SparCC27 with centered log-ratio transformations28, non-random structure validation against Erdős-Rényi null models29, and keystone taxon identification using betweenness-centrality thresholds exceeding three standard deviations25. This study sought to provide an in-depth understanding of the dynamics of soil microbial communities under urbanization, providing scientific evidence to support informed urban planning, ecological restoration, and sustainable soil management, and ultimately contributing to the healthy development of urban ecosystems.

Results

Characterization of urban environmental gradients

Based on environmental monitoring and socioeconomic data, the distinct climatic and ecological gradients of these four representative cities can be observed (Data 1). Changchun, Beijing, Hangzhou and Haikou are characterized by temperate continental monsoon climate, temperate monsoon climate, subtropical monsoon climate, and tropical maritime monsoon climate, respectively. Across this latitudinal gradient, urban population density, gross domestic product (GDP), temperature, precipitation, PM2.5 concentration, and relative humidity showed clear spatial differentation. Within each city, soil samples were collected from four typical land-use types, including densely populated urban campuses, managed roadside greenbelts, and concrete pavements, representing increasing levels of urbanization intendity. Together, these environmental ans land use gradients established a structured urbanization framework for subsequent analyses of microbial diversity, community composition, and interactions patterns.

Overall characteristics of high-throughput sequencing data

A total of 16,317,302 high-quality bacterial sequences, 5,853,018 fungal sequences, and 6,321,057 protistan sequences were obtained across all soil samples using high-throughput sequencing. The Q30 values exceeded 95% for all sequencing runs, indicating consistently high sequencing quality. Rarefaction curves approached saturation with increasing sequencing depth (Figure S1), suggesting adequate coverage of microbial diversity. At a 97% sequence similarity threshold, clustering analysis identified 31,895 OTUs for bacteria (Data 2a), spanning 61 phyla, 151 classes, 331 orders, 407 families, 789 genera, and 726 species. Fungi communities comprised 11,117 OTUs (Data 2b), representing 16 phyla, 65 classes, 194 orders, 459 families, 1,038 genera, and 1092 species. Protistan communities included 6831 OTUs (Data 2c), encompassing 12 phyla, 23 classes, 22 orders, 147 families, 302 genera, and 306 species.

Taxonomic composition of soil microbial communities

The composition of microbial communities reflects the unique and complex characteristics of their surrounding environments. Based on species annotation results, bacterial communities in soils exhibited a shared core taxonomic framework across the cities (Beijing, Changchun, Hangzhou, Haikou) and habitat types (urban campuses, roadside greenbelts, concrete pavements, and suburban unmanaged lands), as determined by taxa with relative abundance >1% at the phylum, class, and order levels (Fig. 1a–c). Dominant bacterial phyla included Acidobacteriota, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidota (Fig. 1a); dominant classes included Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, and Vicinamibacteria (Fig. 1b); and dominant orders included Vicinamibacterales, Burkholderiales, and Rhizobiales (Fig. 1c). Although the core dominant taxa remained compositionally stable, their relative abundances showed significant divergence across the urban gradient and different land use types. Among the four cities, bacterial communities in Changchun, Beijing, and Hangzhou exhibited similar patterns of variation across taxonomic levels. Contrastingly, bacterial communities in Haikou (tropical climate) displayed distinct tropical-associated responses across multiple taxonomic levels. At the phylum level, Acidobacteriota and Bacteroidota were reduced, whereas Chloroflexi and Verrucomicrobiota showed higher relative abundances in Haikou. At the class level, Gammaproteobacteria decreased and Verrucomicrobiae increased, while at the order level, Burkholderiales were functionally replaced by Chthoniobacterales. Land use type was a key driver of bacterial community restructuring. Urban campuses, roadside greenbelts, and suburban unmanaged lands displayed consistent trends in abundance across the phylum, class, and order levels. Stronger community similarity was observed between urban campuses and roadside greenbelts than between either of the two communities and suburban unmanaged lands. In contrast to natural habitats, concrete pavements exhibited systematic functional succession. Taxonomic shifts across the phylum–class–order hierarchy in concrete pavements soils showed a decline in Acidobacteriota (phylum), Alphaproteobacteria (class), and Chthoniobacterales (order), along with enrichment of Chloroflexi (phylum), Gammaproteobacteria (class), and Burkholderiales (order). The relative abundance of Vicinamibacterales decreased progressively from urban campuses to roadside greenbelts, suburban unmanaged lands, and concrete pavements.

In fungal communities, taxa with relative abundance >1% at the phylum, class, and order levels exhibited a stable core composition across the four cities and four land use types (Fig. 1d-f). The dominant fungal phyla included Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Mortierellomycota (Fig. 1d); dominant classes included Dothideomycetes, Agaricomycetes, and Sordariomycetes (Fig. 1e); and dominant orders included Pleosporales, Agaricales, and Hypocreales (Fig. 1f). Despite this taxonomic stability, the relative abundance of core taxa showed notable spatial heterogeneity and clear responses to land use. At the class level, the relative abundance of Pezizomycetes in Changchun was significantly lower than that in the other cities. Dothideomycetes were most abundant in Beijing, Tremellomycetes were markedly reduced in Hangzhou, and Eurotiomycetes were highly enriched in Haikou. At the order level, Ascomycota and Basidiomycota communities in Beijing and Haikou were compositionally similar, while those in Changchun and Hangzhou showed convergence. Among the four land use types, urban campuses, roadside greenbelts, and suburban unmanaged lands demonstrated similar patterns in fungal composition at both the class and order levels. Contrastingly, concrete pavements emerged as anomalously disturbed environments. At the class level, Tremellomycetes and Mortierellomycetes were significantly reduced, while at the order level, Chaetothyriales showed a marked increase in relative abundance.

Protistan communities showed consistent dominance patterns across the four cities and four land use types at the phylum, class, and order levels for taxa with relative abundance >1% (Fig. 1g–i). Specifically, SAR (Stramenopiles, Alveolates, and Rhizaria), Nematozoa, and Rotifera were the dominant protistan phyla (Fig. 1g); Glissomonadida, Chrysophyceae, and Intramacronucleata were the dominant classes (Fig. 1h); and Spirotrichea, Conthreep, and Chromulinales were the dominant orders (Fig. 1i). Further analysis revealed that two heterotrophic groups (Intramacronucleata and Glissomonadida) were consistently present across all habitats in the four cities, suggesting strong ecological adaptability. Contrastingly, Cercomonadidae, another heterotrophic group, exhibited a significant decrease in abundance in concrete pavements environments, potentially due to the unique physical and chemical properties of these sites. Similarly, Thecofilosea showed reduced abundance in both Beijing and Haikou, with a particularly pronounced decline in concrete pavements habitats, indicating sensitivity to specific environmental changes. Among photoautotrophic taxa, Chrysophyceae exhibited decreased abundance in Beijing and Haikou, yet showed significant enrichment in concrete pavements. This pattern may be associated with altered light conditions or nutrient availability unique to the concrete pavements environments. Bacillariophyceae exhibited a significant increase in relative abundance in Hangzhou and showed elevated abundance in concrete pavements, suggesting a potential adaptability to the environmental conditions associated with concrete pavements habitats. Overall, the protistan community structure did not follow a consistent gradient across cities; however, the relative abundance of Bdelloidea (class within Rotifera) was significantly higher in Haikou compared to that in the other cities.

These findings indicate that while the core dominant taxa of soil bacteria, fungi, and protists remained stable across cities and land use types, their relative abundances were significantly influenced by specific urban environments, and by intensively disturbed habitats such as concrete pavements. These environmental pressures led to distinct structural reorganization within microbial communities.

Microbial diversity across different sites

Both alpha and beta diversity analyses were conducted to assess the microbial diversity across different cities and land-use types (Fig. 2). The results indicate that concrete pavements represent biodiversity decline hotspots for soil microorganisms. Richness indices for bacterial (Kruskal–Wallis test P < 0.01), fungal (P < 0.001), and protistan (P < 0.001) communities were significantly lower in concrete pavements compared with other land-use types. Bacterial communities showing the strongest response to land-use type, with consistent declines across all three alpha diversity indices (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2d–f). Urban campuses and roadside greenbelts exhibited higher levels of microbial diversity than those in suburban unmanaged lands (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2d). No significant differences were observed in bacterial richness and Simpson indices, fungal Shannon index, or protistan Simpson and Shannon indices among these three land use types (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2d–f). However, PERMANOVA based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity revealed significant differences in bacterial (P = 0.001, R2 = 0.134), fungal (P = 0.001, R2 = 0.068) and protistan (P = 0.001, R2 = 0.070) community composition among land-use types (Fig. 2a-c). The community composition of urban campuses and roadside greenbelts was highly similar, whereas bacterial community diversity was more complex than that of fungi and protists.

Microbial diversity analysis across cities (Figure S2) revealed significant differences in beta diversity for bacteria, fungi, and protists (P = 0.001), while alpha diversity showed group-specific responses. For bacteria, the richness index differed significantly between Changchun and Haikou (P < 0.05), Beijing and Hangzhou(P < 0.05), and Hangzhou and Haikou (P < 0.01) (Figure S2d). For fungi, significant differences in richness were observed between Beijing and Hangzhou (P < 0.05), and between Hangzhou and Haikou (P < 0.05) (Figure S2e). However, protists showed no significant differences in richness (P > 0.05) across the four cities. According to the shannon index, only bacterial communities exhibited significant differences between Beijing and Haikou (P < 0.01), and between Hangzhou and Haikou (P < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were found for fungi (P > 0.05) or protists (P > 0.05) across cities (Figure S2f). In addition, the shannon index showed no significant differences across the four cities (P > 0.05) for any of the three microbial groups.

Alpha and beta diversity analyses across cities with the same habitat type (Figure S3) further revealed significant beta diversity differences for bacteria, fungi, and protists across all four habitat types. In urban campus environments (Figure S3a-f), bacterial alpha diversity varied significantly among cities: Haikou exhibited significantly lower richness (P < 0.05) and simpson (P < 0.05) indices than the other cities, while its shannon index was significantly higher (P < 0.01). No significant differences in alpha diversity were observed for fungi (P > 0.05) or protists (P > 0.05) in the campus habitats across cities. In roadside greenbelts (Figure S3g–l), bacterial alpha diversity analysis showed that Haikou had a significantly lower shannon (P < 0.05) index than the other three cities, and Hangzhou had a significantly lower richness (P < 0.05) index than Changchun and Beijing. Alpha diversity for fungi (P > 0.05) and protists (P > 0.05) did not show significant differences among cities with this habitat type. In suburban unmanaged lands and concrete pavements (Figure S3m–x), no statistically significant differences were observed in richness, simpson, and shannon indices for bacteria (P > 0.05), fungi (P > 0.05), protists (P > 0.05) across the four cities.

To identify systematic differences in the community structures habitat types, LEfSe analysis was further emplyed to detect habitat-associated taxa preferences among core bacterial, fungal, and protistan taxa (Figure S4). For bacterial communities, Chloroflexi were enriched in urban campus and concrete pavements habitats, while Verrucomicrobiota dominated the suburban unmanaged lands. Bacteroidota were significantly enriched in urban roadside greenbelts. Proteobacteria, a ubiquitous and dominant phylum, were prevalent across all habitat types. Fungal communities exhibited habitat-specific enrichment as well: Mortierellomycota and Ascomycota were highly enriched in campus soils, whereas Basidiomycota showed significant enrichment in concrete pavements, urban roadside greenbelts, and suburban unmanaged lands. Among protists, Cercozoa were predominantly enriched in both concrete pavements habitats and suburban unmanaged lands.

The random forest model was used to identify habitat-specific indicator taxa, with variable importance assessed based on mean decrease in accuracy(Fig. 3b, d, f). Within urban campuses, roadside greenbelts, and concrete pavements habitats, bacterial communities were predominantly composed of the family Nitrospiraceae. Particularly, Sphingomonadaceae emerged as the dominant bacterial group in urban roadside environments, while Acidobacteriales were the primary bacterial taxa in suburban unmanaged lands habitats—a pattern consistent with the LEfSe (Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size) analysis findings, which supported that this group exhibited a high LDA (Linear Discriminant Analysis) score (Figure S4a). In fungal communities, the relative abundances of Penicillium and Serendipita were markedly reduced in urban campuses, roadside greenbelts, and concrete pavements environments (Fig. 3d). For protistan communities, Ischnamoeba dominated both urban campuses and roadside greenbelts, whereas JBNA46 was the dominant group in concrete pavements and campus habitats. In suburban unmanaged lands, Gymnophrys emerged as the primary taxon (Fig. 3f).

Habitat-specific responses at the OTU level revealed that, compared to the suburban unmanaged lands habitat (P < 0.01), a total of 4544 bacterial, 1740 fungal, and 1332 protistan OTUs exhibited significant differential abundance (Fig. 3a, c, e), highlighting taxon-specific disturbance response mechanisms. In bacterial communities, members of the phylum Chloroflexi dominated both the upregulated and downregulated top 10 OTU lists, while Acidobacteriota formed the core disturbance-responsive unit (Fig. 3a). Fungal communities exhibited a clear bidirectional imbalance, with both Ascomycota and Basidiomycota dominating the top 10 upregulated and downregulated OTUs, suggesting a synchronous decline in key fungal taxa (Fig. 3c). In protistan communities, the SAR supergroup displayed overwhelming regulatory dominance, leading the top upregulated OTUs. The SAR supergroup also accounted for the majority of downregulated Rotifera OTUs, indicating its central role in environmental filtering (Fig. 3e).

Structural differences in domain-specific microbial co-occurrence networks

Co-occurrence networks of key microbial taxa exhibited significant differences across the four land use habitats (Fig. 4 and Table 1), implying potential variation in community organization and functional dynamics. Bacterial networks showed clear structural variations across land use types. The concrete pavements habitat exhibited the most simplified structure with the lowest edge count (8), average degree (1.14), and clustering coefficient (0). In contrast, the most complex bacterial networks existed in the suburban unmanaged lands, evidenced by the highest edge count (71), average degree (5.46), average weighted degree (3.91), and clustering coefficient (0.67). Urban campuses had the highest modularity (0.52) and longest network diameter (6), while roadside greenbelts displayed intermediate topological metrics. Fungal networks demonstrated inverse complexity patterns. Roadside greenbelts developed exceptionally high-connectivity networks featuring the highest average degree (12.27) and average weighted degree (11.77). Despite having the highest edge count (97), urban campuses show lower centralization. The concrete pavements maintained high clustering (0.95) with substantially low modularity (0.04). The shortest path lengths (1.01) were observed in suburban unmanaged lands. Protistan networks were dominated by highly clustered structures. The roadside greenbelts achieved perfect clustering (1.0), while suburban unmanaged lands had the highest edge count (52) and modularity (0.25). The urban campuses showed unusually high average degree (7.23), and dense interactions (density = 0.60). Specifically, the concrete pavement exhibited high modularity (0.44) and the longest path length (1.39).

Network Characteristics of Networks

Furthermore, soil microbial communities of different groups exhibit multidimensional responses to urbanisation. In bacterial co-occurrence networks, complexity decreases as the order: suburban unmanaged lands > roadside greenbelts > urban campuses > concrete pavements. Unmanaged lands sustain functional potential through 71 high-density edges, whereas concrete pavements retain only eight edges, with the clustering coefficient collapsing to zero, indicating a highly simplified and weakly connected network structure. Within managed green spaces, roadside greenbelts maintain semi-natural connectivity (49 edges), whereas intense management in urban campuses reduces edge density by 14% and prolongs energy-transfer pathways (average path length = 2.61). Among the fungal networks, which undergo structural reorganization, their average clustering coeficient were stable among the four different land use samples. Roadside greenbelts form hyper-condensed networks (15 nodes, 92 edges and a density of 0.88), enabling efficient flux but albeit at the cost of modularity collapse (0.01). Concrete pavements establish dense connections (96 edges) but retain minimal modularity (0.04). Unmanaged lands preserve resilience through exceptional clustering (0.99) and preserved modularity (0.13), forming 84 connections. Despite with high connectivity (22 nodes and 97 edges), urban campuses suffer functional chaos with ineffectual modularity (0.05). Protistan networks demonstrate habitat-specific adaptations, ordered by suburban unmanaged lands > roadside greenbelts > urban campuses > concrete pavements. Roadside greenbelts exhibit complete interconnection (clustering coefficient = 1.0) and instantaneous node access (network diameter = 1). Conversely, concrete pavements display maximum fragmentation (modularity = 0.44) into isolated micro-communities (only 25 edges). Unmanaged lands maintain ecological resilience by balancing scale (18 nodes) with critical modularity (0.25) and clustering (0.90). Urban campuses conceal homogenisation risks under high-density connections (density = 0.60, with 47 edges among 13 nodes), despite having critically low modularity (0.03).

Characteristics of multi-domain microbial networks

A comprehensive analysis of microbial networks (bacteria, fungi and protists) reveals a unified integrity hierarchy, which are ordered as roadside greenbelts > suburban unmanaged lands > urban campuses > concrete pavements (Figure S6 and Table S1). Roadside greenbelts maintain optimal connectivity, they are sustained by the densest edge formation (edge = 41 for 16 nodes) and the highest clustering coefficient (0.81) (Figure S6b). These habitats ensure material flow efficiency through the shortest average path length (1.41 steps) and moderate-to-low modularity (0.35), which then establishes them as critical urban ecological hubs. Concrete pavements undergo ecological collapse (Figure S6d): with the lowest average degree (1.02) and minimal density (0.03), this kind of land use exhibits structural disintegration. Its high modularity (0.75) indicates fragmentation into isolated communities, as evidenced by the severely limited number of connections (only 21 edges among 16 nodes). Urban campuses exemplify a management paradox (Figure S6a), which present the maximum network diameter (9) and the longest path length (3.41 steps), indicating that microbial interactions rely on indirect transmission pathways spanning more than three intermediate steps. Despite comprising 24 nodes, the network maintains only 41 functional edges,resulting in a critically low connectivity density (0.05) and reflecting a highly constrained interaction structure.Suburban unmanaged lands exhibite buffering capacity (Figure S6c). Although they lack extreme parameter values, their balanced modularity (0.47) and clustering coefficient (0.43) effectively mitigate path length expansion (2.55 steps versus 3.41 in urban campuses), providing essential ecological resilience.

Relationships Between Soil Microbial Communities and Environmental Factors

Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) was employed to test hypothesised relationships among key variables. Here, the SEM (Fig. 5) and CCA (Canonical Correspondence Analysis) (Fig. 6) collectively revealed taxon-specific responses of microbial diversity to multi-scale environmental drivers. SEM results showed that bacterial diversity was directly influenced by pH (P < 0.05), latitude (P < 0.01), and city(P < 0.05), while land-use type indirectly regulated bacterial diversity through its effect on soil pH. Fungal diversity was driven solely by city, with no significant associations with pH or latitude. Protistan diversity was influenced by city and latitude, but it was not significantly affected by land use. CCA results indicated that temperature (P < 0.001) had a positive effect on microbial diversity in the warmer cities of Hangzhou and Haikou but negatively affected it in the colder cities of Changchun and Beijing. Precipitation exhibited an opposite spatial pattern: it was positively associated with microbial diversity in low-temperature regions (Changchun and Beijing), but significantly negatively associated with microbial diversity in high-temperature regions (Hangzhou and Haikou). Overall, bacterial diversity was shaped by complex interactions among multiple environmental factors. Contrastingly, fungal diversity showed a highly specific response to urban stressors, while protistan diversity was primarily constrained by geographic location. Microbial communities in warmer regions (Hangzhou and Haikou) exhibited thermophilic tendencies, whereas those in colder regions (Changchun and Beijing) demonstrated a preference for moisture.

Discussion

Soil microorganisms play an indispensable role in urban ecosystems. They not only function as producers, consumers, and decomposers, but also as key carriers of matter and energy, thereby supporting the stability of the entire ecosystem. Within soil microbial communities, bacteria, fungi, and protists are the dominant biological groups, each contributing actively to essential ecological processes such as nutrient cycling and plant nutrient uptake. This study analyzed the diversity of fungal, bacterial, and protistan communities in soil samples collected from urban campuses, roadside greenbelts, suburban unmanaged lands, and concrete pavement areas across four cities at different latitudes. The findings provide preliminary insights into the response mechanisms of microbial groups to varying degrees of urbanization and land-use types in urban soils.

Across cities and soil types, microbial communities exhibited a combination of convergent core taxonamic structure and stress-driven taxon-specific reassembly along climatic and urbanization gradients. Within bacterial communities, Acidobacteriota, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidota were consistently identified as dominant core phyla across all soil types (Fig. 1a). The functional complementarity among these groups forms the fundamental pillar supporting biogeochemical cycling in urban soils30. Among fungi, the phyla Ascomycota and Basidiomycota overwhelmingly dominated (Fig. 1d), further confirming the existence of a globally conserved framework for urban soil fungal communities31,32. Within protists, members of the SAR supergroup were ubiquitously present across all sampling sites, and their diverse trophic strategies may offer adaptive advantages in heterogeneous urban environments. Notably, no significant differences in alpha diversity of bacterial, fungal, and protistan communities were observed between suburban unmanaged lands and concrete pavements areas across cities (Figure S3).

Furthermore, this study revealed that environmental stressors drive taxon-specific reassembly. In the temperate region of Beijing, Acidobacteriota and Chloroflexi exhibited concurrent enrichment33. Acidobacteriota contribute to soil carbon storage through carbon fixation34, while Chloroflexi facilitate nutrient release by degrading recalcitrant organic compounds35, indicating a functional compensation mechanism to maintain carbon–nitrogen balance under cold and arid climatic conditions. Contrastingly, under the hot and humid stress typical of tropical Haikou, microbial communities shifted toward stress-tolerant functional groups. This was marked by a significant expansion of taxa such as Russulales, a key fungal group involved in lignin oxidation36,37, and Bdelloidea, rotifers adapted to periodic flooding38. These shifts reflect a fundamental restructuring of organic matter turnover rates and oxygen gradients under high-temperature and high-humidity conditions.

Notably, across all climatic regions, concrete pavements induced a convergence decline in microbial community structure. The physical impermeability of concrete39 and the associated microenvironmental degradation led to a reduction in heterotrophic taxa such as Cercomonadidae, while selectively enriching taxa with stress-resistant traits. These traits include hydrophobic extracellular structures in Chloroflexi, desiccation-resistant morphologies in Chaetothyriales, and even phototrophic and autotrophic lineages like Chrysophyceae, which reassemble into ‘mat-like,’ benthic algal analogues40. Microbial community similarity across the four cities’ concrete pavements sites exceeded the dissimilarity among their respective natural habitats, indicating that abiotic stress induced by anthropogenic surface hardening has surpassed climate as the primary driver of microbial community homogenization in urban environments31.

Thus, microbial groups such as bacteria, fungi, and protists could employ a dual adaptive strategy—conservation of core functional architecture and taxon-specific compositional responses to cope with cross-city environmental filtering pressures.

With ongoing urbanization, the increasing prevalence of concrete pavements41 represents an extreme, multifactorial stress environment for soil microbial communities. The physical sealing of soil with concrete reduces soil organic carbon content42, exacerbates carbon emissions43, and contributes to elevated surface temperatures44. Compared to suburban unmanaged lands, all microbial groups exhibited significantly reduced diversity in concrete pavements environments, with both richness and Shannon indices for bacteria, fungi, and protists showing marked declines (Fig. 2), consistent with findings by Hu et al45. Amid this overall decline in diversity, the abundance of Rotifera, a sensitive indicator group within protists, decreased sharply. Despite the cities being located across distinct climatic zones, microbial alpha diversity within concrete pavements areas exhibited no significant differences among cities (Figure S3). This pattern suggests that the environmental filtering pressure exerted by concrete pavements surpasses climatic influences, driving the emergence of homogenized microbial community structures across the regions.

Despite the decline in alpha diversity, environmental stressors maintain core ecological functions through the selective enrichment of multifunctional, stress-tolerant taxa. For instance, within bacterial communities, Chloroflexi contribute to ecosystem resilience through their capacity to degrade organic pollutants46, while Burkholderiales support interspecific interactions via secondary metabolite-mediated mechanisms47, forming a functionally complementary relationship. In the fungal communities, potential stress-resilient symbiotic taxa such as Glomeromycota, alongside lignin-depolymerizing Agaricomycetes, particularly Russulales36, jointly respond to environmental pressures. Among protists, mixotrophic taxa such as Chrysophyceae and Bacillariophyceae leverage phototrophic stress-resistance traits48,49 for energy supplementation. These multidimensional coordination strategies help sustain essential ecological functionality under stressful environmental conditions.

Significant domain-specific variations in the complexity of microbial co-occurrence networks exist in cement-covered habitats (Fig. 4). However, system-wide functional integration capacity is significantly reduced. While fungal taxa maintain relatively high connectivity rates, pivotal bacterial networks undergo complete structural disintegration and protistan communities exhibit critically low modularity. Further analysis of integrated meta-networks demonstrates that cement habitats drive microbial communities towards isolated micro-unit configurations (Figure S6). These configurations are characterised by the lowest average connectivity and interaction density across domains. This ‘low diversity–low functional integration’ collapse pattern contrasts starkly with that of natural/semi-natural habitats, such as urban green spaces. Notably, stress-selected functional core taxa such as carbon-degrading Chloroflexi, secondary metabolite-producing Burkholderiales, lignin-degrading Russulales, and phototrophic Chrysophyceae, persistently attempt to establish functional modules via positive microhabitat interactions50. Unlike controllable stressors such as heavy23 metal contamination51, cement-induced physical barriers catastrophically erode microbial habitat connectivity52.This fundamentally constrains cross-module compensatory cooperation, driving a critical downshift in the migration of ecosystem resilience thresholds.

Despite being subject to varying degrees of anthropogenic disturbance, urban campus lawns and roadside greenbelts maintained stable alpha diversity indices for bacteria, fungi, and protists, showing no statistically significant differences compared with suburban unmanaged lands (Fig. 2). More importantly, both environments demonstrated a notable buffering capacity against the diversity collapse observed in concrete pavements. Comparative analysis across climatic zones further revealed that microbial Shannon diversity indices in urban campuses, roadside greenbelts, and suburban unmanaged lands did not differ significantly between cities (Figure S3). This cross-climatic stability in microbial diversity highlights the universal ecological value of artificial green spaces as foundational components of urban ecosystems. Future studies should integrate vegetation physiological traits with soil biological processes to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of herbaceous–shrub systems in sustaining microbial ecosystem functions.

While overall microbial diversity was maintained, this study identified a significant enrichment of specific functional microbial taxa within roadside greenbelt habitats (Figure S5). Notably, Sphingomonas, a bacterial genus known for its capacity to mineralize organic toxicants53, and Penicillium, a fungal genus with strong antimicrobial activity capable of producing various antibiotics to inhibit soil-borne pathogens54, were prominently enriched. The selective accumulation of these functional groups suggests that microhabitat optimization within roadside greenbelts may selectively recruit microbial taxa that provide key ecosystem services, such as pollutant degradation and pathogen suppression.

Urban green infrastructure has been demonstrated to simultaneously maintain microbial alpha diversity and exhibit management-adaptive plasticity in interaction networks. Roadside greenbelts exemplify exceptional performance, wherein fungi establish ultra-high-connectivity networks and protists attain perfect mutualism (Fig. 4). It is of particular significance that cross-domain meta-networks exhibit the highest clustering coefficient and shortest material transfer pathways (Figure S6), thus establishing these habitats as functional cornerstones of urban ecosystems. Conversely, the campus green spaces exhibited a “dysfunctional complexity trap” under intensive management, characterised by an 85% loss of potential interactions and prolonged energy-transfer chains, despite sustaining higher node richness (Figure S6). This within-green-space differentiation underscores the imperative to prioritise microhabitat connectivity optimisation while conserving foundational diversity55.

This buffering effect and functional selection mechanism are likely closely associated with several factors provided by green-space vegetation, including the continuous input of plant root exudates56, improved microclimatic conditions57, and relatively healthy soil physical structure58. It is important to note that while specific plant species can influence the composition of rhizosphere microbial communities, the data in this study were derived from bulk (non-rhizosphere) soils beneath plant communities. These findings indicate that, at broader spatial scales, artificial green spaces are still capable of maintaining core microbial community characteristics similar to those found in suburban unmanaged lands.

The structural variation of urban soil microbial communities was primarily regulated by soil physicochemical properties and climatic factors (Figs. 5 and 6), with soil pH exerting the most prominent direct filtering effect59. In this study, pH was found to significantly influence bacterial diversity (Fig. 6). Specifically, in soils transitioning from alkaline to neutral conditions, an increase in pH was associated with a sharp decline in the relative abundance of Acidobacteriota60. Contrastingly, under extremely acidic conditions, Acidobacteriota may activate intracellular pH homoeostasis mechanisms to maintain functionality61. Land-use type also plays a critical role by altering the microbial habitat and shaping selection pressures through mechanisms such as physical disruption of soil structure62, nutrient loss63, and accumulation of pollutants64.

Seasonal and latitudinal temperature fluctuations primarily influence soil microbial diversity by modulating basal microbial metabolic rates. The temperature sensitivity of microbial respiration, such as ‘heat shock protein induction and enzyme activity acclimation,’ is highly dependent on community-level adaptive capacity65. In subtropical to tropical urban environments, microbial diversity exhibited a phase-dependent positive correlation with temperature (Fig. 6), consistent with the stimulatory effects of short-term warming on respiratory metabolism. However, long-term thermal adaptation, such as the succession of heat-tolerant microbial taxa, can mitigate excessive metabolic expenditure, hence preventing diversity collapse due to thermal overstress65. This adaptive mechanism was particularly evident in anthropogenically disturbed soils, such as concrete pavements areas. However, when temperature increase is compounded by additional stressors such as cadmium contamination, the beneficial effects of temperature on microbial diversity can be diminished or even reversed66, suggesting that urban heat island effects may be approaching the thermal tolerance thresholds of soil microbial communities.

Urbanization, through stressors such as habitat fragmentation caused by concrete expansion, toxin accumulation67, and heat island-induced drying, significantly reduces soil microbial alpha diversity while simultaneously driving community homogenization at a global scale67,68.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that urban soil microbial communities employ multi-level adaptive strategies to cope with the pressures resulting from urbanization gradients. Core functional taxa domonstrate convergent conservation across climatic zones and soil types, thereby providing functional support for fundamental material cycles in urban soils. Moderately disturbed artificial green spaces have been found to maintain microbial diversity characteristics effectively similar to those found in suburban unmanaged lands. The construction of interaction networks by these organisms is conducive to the establishment of a functional foundation for their roles as ecological hubs. Conversely, under conditions of extreme stress such as concrete habitats, a substantial decline in microbial diversity happened. The deterioration of community network structure and functional integration is marked by a significant decline in the scope of functional maintenance. These findings further indicate that in moderate disturbance conditons present in urban stressed environments, soil microbes demonstrate functional robustness through the maintenance of foundational diversity, the optimisation of network connectivity, and the selective enrichment of functional groups. However, under conditions of extreme stress, the microbial communities will disintegrate into a stage of microbial diversity and network structure collaspse. Our results underline the critical role of urban artificial green spaces in mitigating environmental disturbances, maintaining soil microecological stability, and enhancing ecosystem services, which provides a microbiome-level scientific foundation for urban soil health assessment, spatial optimization planning, and ecological restoration practices.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Soil samples were collected from four cities in China, spanning from north to south: Changchun (temperate), Beijing (temperate), Hangzhou (subtropical), and Haikou (tropical). Within each city, four representative land use types were selected: urban campuses, roadside greenbelts, suburban unmanaged lands, and concrete pavements. For concrete pavements, soil samples were obtained from the 0–20 cm soil layer beneath recently excavated impervious pavement during road maintenance. All soil samples were collected during a highly synchronized period: from August 5 to August 13, 2024. For urban campuses, roadside greenbelts, and suburban unmanaged lands, soil samples were collected from six distinct locations for each site. For concrete pavements, three sample replicates were collected in per site. At each location, soil samples were collected from six directions and thoroughly mixed to form one composite sample. In total, 84 soil samples were collected across the four cities and land use types (Fig. 7). All soil samples were taken from a depth of 5–10 cm below the surface. Following collection, samples were immediately stored in insulated containers with ice packs.

Study areas in China and locations of sampling sites in Changchun, Beijing, Hangzhou, and Haikou.

Collection and measurement of environmental factors

All data on non-biological environmental factors in this study were sourced from the China Statistical Yearbook 2024 (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024) and integrated into the sampling points via analytical calculations. Climate indicators include four types of data, average monthly temperature in August (Temp), relative humidity (Humid), average monthly precipitation in August (TMP) and hours of sunshine (Sun). These values are referenced directly from the average figures in the China Statistical Yearbook. Urbanization-related variables, including permanent resident population (POP), GDP, PM2.5 levels, and green-space coverage, are all sourced from the China Statistical Yearbook 2024 (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024).

The Urban Stress Index (USI) was calculated as the annual-mean PM2.5 concentration (µg m−3) divided by the fractional green cover (%) multiplied by 0.01 to yield a dimensionless ratio. Values greater than 1 indicate that particulate pollution pressure exceeds the mitigating capacity of urban vegetation, whereas values less than 1 denote a prevailing green-space buffer. This one-step metric, adapted from the ecological-pressure model of Fu et al.69, directly scales airborne pollutant load against the biophysical attenuation provided by existing greenery. This allows cross-city comparison of the balance between environmental stress and natural buffering. The climate index, which is calculated as the mean annual temperature multiplied by the annual precipitation, divided by 1000, has been shown to condense the joint warmth-moisture effect that governs plant metabolism and ecosystem productivity70.

The natural logarithm of city-level gross domestic product, or log_GDP, was utilised to compress the orders-of-magnitude span between megacity and small-city economies. This was achieved by mitigating leverage from extreme values, thereby yielding an approximately normal distribution for subsequent parametric tests.

The soil pH was determined potentiometrically at 25 °C using an LPH-A pH meter (Labgic Technology Co., China) fitted with a glass–calomel electrode. Ten grams of air-dried soil (particle size <2 mm) were suspended in 50 mL of CO2-free water (soil:water ratio of 1:5), stirred for one minute, left to stand for 30 min, and then measured after calibration against pH 4.01, 7.00 and 10.01 standard buffers. The stable reading was recorded in triplicate and averaged.

Soil DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted from soil samples using the DC306 Magnetic Bead Soil DNA Extraction Kit (Fangzhou Biosafety Technology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). The purity and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Genomic DNA served as the template for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification. Specific barcoded primers and Premix Taq were used according to the targeted sequencing regions. The 16S rRNA V4 region primers (515 F and 806 R) were used for bacterial diversity profiling: the 18S rRNA V4 region primers (528 F and 706 R) for eukaryotic microbial diversity and the ITS1 region primers (ITS5-1737F and ITS2-2043R) for fungal diversity. PCR products were verified with 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm fragment size and concentration, ensuring that the main bands fell within the expected size range. Only samples meeting the size criteria were retained for subsequent analysis. Following verification, the concentration of each PCR product was quantified using GeneTools Analysis Software (Version 4.03.05.0, SynGene). Based on equimolar pooling principles, appropriate volumes of PCR products were calculated and pooled accordingly. The mixed PCR products were then purified using the E.Z.N.A.® Gel Extraction Kit (Omega, USA), and target DNA fragments were eluted with TE buffer for downstream sequencing.

Library preparation, sequencing and quality control

DNA libraries were prepared following the standard protocol of the ALFA-SEQ DNA Library Prep Kit. Library fragment size was assessed using the Qsep400 High-Throughput Nucleic Acid-Protein Analysis System, and library concentration was quantified using the Qubit 4.0 fluorometer. Libraries that met the quality standards were sequenced using paired-end 250 bp reads (PE250) on the DNBSEQ-G99 platform.

Quality control of raw paired-end reads was performed using fastp (v 0.14.1) with sliding window parameters set to -W 4 -M 20. Primer sequences at both ends of the reads were removed using cutadapt, and paired-end reads were subsequently merged to obtain Clean Reads. USEARCH (-fastq_mergepairs) was used to merge paired-end reads and filter out unmatched tags, generating raw merged sequences (Raw Tags). These were further filtered with fastp for additional sliding window trimming to obtain high-quality merged sequences (Clean Tags).

Microbial diversity and differential abundance analyses

Rarefaction curves were generated from the operational taxonomic unit (OTU) abundance table using USEARCH and relevant R packages. Alpha diversity metrics (richness, Shannon, and Simpson) were calculated based on the OTU abundance table, using the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test implemented in R. Beta diversity analysis was performed using the vegan package in R, based on Bray–Curtis distance metrics.

Differential taxa were identified using Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe), with linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score thresholds set at >4.0 for bacteria and >3.5 for fungi and protists. Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to identify taxa with significant abundance differences, followed by Wilcoxon rank-sum test for pairwise comparisons. Differential abundance volcano plots were generated using DESeq2 with thresholds of P < 0.01 and |log2FoldChange | > 1.

Environmental association, network analysis and integrated modelling

Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) was conducted to evaluate relationships between microbial communities and environmental variables. Detrended correspondence analysis indicated unimodal responses, supporting the use of CCA. Random forest models were constructed using genus-level relative abundance data to identify key predictors and assess model performance.

The microbial interaction network was constructed using CLR-transformed abundance data following pseudocount addition (0.5). Pearson correlation were calculated, and significanct associations were identified using Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (FDR < 0.05). Bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) was utilised to assess edge stability. Network topology was visualized using igraph and Gephi,with topological metrics including node degree, clustering coefficient, average path length, and modularity index.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using a piecewise SEM framework in R (piecewiseSEM v2.3.0) to assess causal relationships between microbial diversity (richness index) and environmental factors. Model fit was evaluated using Fisher’s C test.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.3.0). For soil sampling, each composite sample represented one biological replicate, derived from multiple subsamples collected at a single location. For urban campuses, roadside greenbelts, and suburban unmanaged lands, six biologically independent samples (n = 6) were collected per city. For concrete pavements, three biologically independent samples (n = 3) were collected per city. Alpha diversity comparisons among land-use types and cities were conducted using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test. When significant effects were detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Differences in community composition were evaluated using PERMANOVA based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities with 999 permutations. All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Exact P values are reported in the Results.
