Learning Orthogonal Multi-Index Models: A Fine-Grained Information Exponent Analysis
Yunwei Ren, Jason D. Lee

Abstract
The information exponent ( [ 4 ] ) and its extensions — which are equivalent to the lowest degree in the Hermite expansion of the link function (after a potential label transform) for Gaussian single-index models — have played an important role in predicting the sample complexity of online stochastic gradient descent (SGD) in various learning tasks. In this work, we demonstrate that, for multi-index models, focusing solely on the lowest degree can miss key structural details of the model and result in suboptimal rates. Specifically, we consider the task of learning target functions of form f ∗ ​ ( 𝒙 ) = ∑ k = 1 P ϕ ⁡ ( 𝒗 k ∗ ⋅ 𝒙 ) f_{*}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\phi({\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\cdot{\bm{x}}) , where P ≪ d P\ll d , the ground-truth directions { 𝒗 k ∗ } k = 1 P \{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k=1}^{P} are orthonormal, and the information exponent of ϕ \phi is L L . Based on the theory of information exponent, when L = 2 L=2 , only the relevant subspace (not the exact directions) can be recovered due to the rotational invariance of the second-order terms, and when L > 2 L>2 , recovering the directions using online SGD require O ~ ​ ( P ​ d L − 1 ) \tilde{O}(Pd^{L-1}) samples. In this work, we show that by considering both second- and higher-order terms, we can first learn the relevant space using the second-order terms, and then the exact directions using the higher-order terms, and the overall sample and complexity of online SGD is O ~ ​ ( d ​ P L − 1 ) \tilde{O}(dP^{L-1}) .

Introduction

In many learning problems, the target function exhibits or is assumed to exhibit a low-dimensional structure.
A classical model of this type is the multi-index model, where the target function depends only on a

P
-dimensional subspace of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
P
typically much smaller than
d
.
When the relevant dimension
P=1
, the model is known as the single-index model, which dates back to at least

[
18
]
.
Both single- and multi-index models have been widely studied, especially in the context of neural network
and stochastic gradient descent (SGD) in recent years, sometimes under the name “feature learning”

[
4
,
7
,
12
,
1
,
2
,
11
,
14
,
25
,
15
]
.

In
[
4
]
, the authors show that for single-index models, the behavior of online SGD can be split
into two phases: an initial “searching” phase, where most of the samples are used to boost the correlation with the
relevant (one-dimensional) subspace to a constant, and a subsequent “descending” phase, where the correlation further
increases to
1
.
They introduce the concept of the information exponent (IE), defined as the index of the first nonzero coefficient
in the Taylor expansion of the population loss around
0
, which also corresponds to the lowest degree in the Hermite
expansion of the link function in Gaussian single-index models.
They prove that the sample complexity of online SGD is
\tilde{O}(d^{(\mathrm{IE}-1)\vee 1})
.
After that, various lower and upper bounds have been established for single-index models in
[
7
,
13
,
14
]
.
Similar results for certain multi-index models have also been derived in
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
25
]
. In all cases, the sample complexity of online SGD scales with
d^{\mathrm{IE}-1}

when
\mathrm{IE}\geq 3
.

Later, it was realized that the notion of information exponent is not stable under modifications of the algorithm.
In particular, the information exponent of a link function can be greatly reduced by reusing batches or applying a suitable
label transformation
[
3
,
15
,
21
,
14
]
.
For example, the IE of any fixed degree polynomial can be reduced to at most
2
via monomial transformations.
This observation leads to the notion of generative exponent (GE)
[
14
]
, which is
defined as the lowest information exponent among all
L^{2}
transform of the original link function. It yields
bounds that match the previous results for non-gradient-based methods
[
10
,
27
,
9
]
. Despite the improvement over the vanilla information exponent, in the framework of
generative exponents, still only the lowest order is considered.
As we will discuss later, this makes it suffer from the
same issue of information exponent in the context of multi-index models.

Consider multi-index models of form
f_{*}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\phi({\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\cdot{\bm{x}})
, where

\{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k}
are orthonormal vectors.
In this setting, there are two types of recovery: recovering each direction
{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}
and recovering
the subspace spanned by
\{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k}
. The former notion is stronger, and once the directions are learned, the
learning task essentially reduces to learning the one-dimensional
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
However, directional recovery is not always possible. To see this, consider the case

\phi(z)=h_{2}(z)
, where
h_{l}
is the
l
-th (normalized) Hermite polynomial. One can show that this corresponds
to decomposing the projection matrix (a second-order tensor) of the subspace
\mathrm{span}\{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k}
.
Hence, recovering the directions is impossible due to the rotational
invariance (see Section
3.1
for more discussion).
In other words, in any framework that considers only the lowest order (IE or GE), if the lowest order is
2
,
1
1

1

The
\mathrm{IE}=2
case is particularly important as the information exponent of many functions, including all fixed degree
polynomials, can be reduced to at most
2
by a suitable label transform
[
14
]
,
and the information exponent of any even functions is at least
2
.

we cannot get guarantees beyond subspace recovery due to the existence of the
\phi=h_{2}
example.

On the other hand, if
\phi
contains some higher-order terms, e.g.,
\phi=h_{2}+h_{4}
, then one should expect
that directional convergence is possible, even though
\mathrm{IE}(\phi)
is still
2
, because of identifiability property
of (higher-order) orthogonal tensor decomposition problem
[
16
,
20
,
17
]
.
In addition, we should be able to first recover the subspace using the second-order terms, which should require

\tilde{O}(d\poly P)
samples, and then recover the directions using the higher-order terms. Moreover, since
we have learned the relevant subspace, the number of samples needed in the second step should be much smaller than what
is needed if there were no second-order terms.

In this work, we formally prove the above conjecture and show that the overall sample complexity
is
\tilde{O}(dP^{L-1})
, where
L
is the (lowest) order of the higher-order terms.
Note that this bound scales linearly with the ambient dimension
d
, and it matches the sample complexity of
separately learning
P
independent single-index models with the relevant subspace known but the noise scales with

d
, up to potential logarithmic terms. More formally, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal version of Theorem
2.1
)
.

Suppose that the target function is given by
f_{*}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\phi({\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\cdot{\bm{x}})
where

\phi=\hat{\phi}_{2}h_{2}+\sum_{l=L}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{l}h_{l}
, with
L\geq 3
,
\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2},\hat{\phi}_{L}^{2}>0
,
and
\{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k=1}^{P}
are orthonormal, and the input
{\bm{x}}
follows the standard Gaussian distribution

\mathcal{N}\left(0,\bm{I}_{d}\right)
. Then, we can use online SGD (followed by a ridge regression step) to train a two-layer network
of width
\tilde{O}(P)
to learn (with high probability) this target function using
\tilde{O}(dP^{L-1})
samples and steps.

1.1
Related work

In this subsection, we discuss works that are directly related to ours or were not covered earlier in the introduction.

Along the line of information exponent, the paper most related to ours is
[
25
]
. They show that for
near orthogonal multi-index models, the sample complexity of recovering all ground-truth directions using online SGD is

\tilde{O}(Pd^{\mathrm{IE}-1})
when
\mathrm{IE}\geq 3
. Their results do not apply to the case
\mathrm{IE}=2
for the reason
we have discussed earlier. They propose first removing the second-order terms using the technique in
[
12
,
11
]
,
which requires
d^{2}
samples. Our result considers the situation where both the second and
L
-th order terms are present
and show that in this case, the sample complexity of online SGD (without any preprocessing) is
\tilde{O}(dP^{L-1})
.

Another recent related work is
[
5
]
.
Our main results are not directly comparable since the settings are different. They run SGD on the Stiefel manifold,
which automatically prevents the model from collapsing to a single direction, but allow the target model to have condition number larger than
1
.
In addition, only the lowest degree is considered in their work. However, they also show (in their setting) that
when the second order term is isotropic, the subspace and only the subspace can be recovered. A similar idea is also used
in our analysis of Stage 1.1 (cf. Section
3.1
).

Another related line of research is learning two-layer networks in the teacher-student setting
(
[
33
,
22
,
28
,
20
,
32
,
17
]
).
Among them, the ones most relevant to this work are
[
20
]
and the follow-up
[
17
]
,
both of which consider orthogonal models similar to ours and use similar ideas in the analysis of the population process.
However, they do not assume a low-dimensional structure and only provide very crude
\poly(d)
-style sample complexity
bounds.

1.2
Our contributions

We summarize our contributions as follows:

•

We demonstrate that information/generative exponent alone is insufficient to characterize certain structures in the learning
task and show that for a specific orthogonal multi-index model, if we consider both the lower- and higher-order
terms, the sample complexity of directional recovery using online SGD can be greatly improved over the vanilla
information exponent-based analysis.

•

As a by-product, we derive a collection of user-friendly technical lemmas to analyze the difference between noisy
one-dimensional processes and their deterministic counterparts, which may be of independent interests
(cf. Section
4.1
and Appendix
E
).

Setup and main result

In this section, we describe the setting of our learning task and the training algorithm, and then formally state our
main result. We will also convert the problem to an orthogonal tensor decomposition task using the standard Hermite
argument as in
[
16
]
.

Notations

We use
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{p}
to denote the
p
-norm of a vector. When
p=2
, we often drop the subscript and simply write

\left\|\cdot\right\|
. For
a,b,\delta\in\mathbb{R}
,
a=b\pm\delta
means
|a-b|\leq|\delta|
and

a\vee b=\max\{a,b\}
and
a\wedge b=\min\{a,b\}
. Beside the standard
asymptotic (big
O
) notations, we also use the notation
f_{d}=O_{\phi}(g_{d})
, which means there exists a constant

C_{\phi}>0
that can depend only on
\phi

f_{d}\leq C_{\phi}g_{d}

2.1
Input and target function

We assume the input
{\bm{x}}
follows the standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\bm{I}_{d}\right)
and the target function has
form
f_{*}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\phi({\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\cdot{\bm{x}}),

where
\log^{C}d\leq P\leq d
for a large universal constant
C>0
,
\{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k=1}^{P}
are orthonormal and

\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the link function. In addition, we assume
\phi
satisfies the following.

Assumption 1

(Assumptions on the link function)
.

Let
h_{k}
denote the degree-
k
normalized Hermite polynomial and
\phi=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{k}h_{k}

denote the Hermite expansion of
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{N}\left(0,\bm{I}_{d}\right))
.

(a)

(IE structure)
For some constant
L>2
,
\phi(z)=\hat{\phi}_{2}h_{2}(z)+\hat{\phi}_{L}h_{L}(z)+\sum_{l>L}\hat{\phi}_{l}h_{l}(z)
.

(b)

(IE regularity)

\hat{\phi}_{2},\hat{\phi}_{L}=\Omega(1)
and

\left\|\phi^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}l\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}\leq C_{\phi}^{2}
for some constant
C_{\phi}>0
.

(c)

(Polynomial growth)
There exists universal constants
C,q>0
such that
|\phi(x)|\vee|\phi^{\prime}(x)|\leq C(1+x^{2})^{q/2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Our target model and algorithm will all be invariant under rotation. Hence, we will assume w.l.o.g. that
{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}=\bm{e}_{k}
where
\{\bm{e}_{k}\}_{k}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.2
Learner model and the training algorithm

Our learner model is a width-
m
two-layer network

f({\bm{x}}):=f({\bm{x}};\bm{a},\bm{V}):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\phi({\bm{v}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{x}}),

where
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\bm{V}=({\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{m})\in(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})^{m}
are the trainable parameters.
We call
\{{\bm{v}}_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
the first-layer neurons. We measure the difference between the learner and
the target model using the correlation loss. Given a sample
({\bm{x}},f_{*}({\bm{x}}))
, we define the per-sample and population MSE
loss as

l_{\mathrm{MSE}}({\bm{x}}):=l({\bm{x}};\bm{a},\bm{V}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(f_{*}({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{x}};\bm{a},\bm{V})\right)^{2},\quad\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\bm{a},\bm{V}):=\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{{\bm{x}}}l_{\mathrm{MSE}}({\bm{x}};\bm{a},\bm{V}).

Now, we describe the training algorithm. First, we initialize each output weight
a_{i}
to be
1
. Then, we symmetrically
initialize the first layer neurons. That is, for
i\in[m/2]
, we initialize
{\bm{v}}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
independently
and set
{\bm{v}}_{m/2+i}=-{\bm{v}}_{i}
for the other half of the neurons.
After the initialization, we fix the output weights
\bm{a}
and train the first-layer weight
{\bm{v}}_{i}
using online
(spherical) SGD with the correlation loss
l_{\mathrm{corr}}({\bm{x}})=-f_{*}({\bm{x}})f({\bm{x}})
and step size
\eta>0
for
T
iterations.
Then, we fix the first-layer weights and use ridge regression to train the output weights
\bm{a}
.

Let
\{({\bm{x}}_{t},f_{*}({\bm{x}}_{t}))\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be our samples where
\{{\bm{x}}_{t}\}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian vectors,
and let
\tilde{\nabla}_{{\bm{v}}}=(\bm{I}-{\bm{v}}{\bm{v}}^{\top})\nabla_{{\bm{v}}}
denote the spherical gradient.
Then, we can formally describe the training procedure as follows:

\displaystyle\text{Initialization:}\quad

\displaystyle a_{0,i}=1,\quad{\bm{v}}_{0,i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),\quad{\bm{v}}_{0,m/2+i}=-{\bm{v}}_{0,i}

\displaystyle\forall i\in[m/2];

(1)

\displaystyle\text{Stage 1:}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\hat{{\bm{v}}}_{t+1,i}={\bm{v}}_{t,i}+\eta f_{*}({\bm{x}}_{t})\nabla_{{\bm{v}}_{i}}\phi({\bm{v}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{x}}),\\
&{\bm{v}}_{t+1,i}=\hat{{\bm{v}}}_{t+1,i}/\left\|\hat{{\bm{v}}}_{t+1,i}\right\|,\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle\forall i\in[m],t\in[T];

\displaystyle\text{Stage 2:}

\displaystyle\bm{a}=\argmin_{\bm{a}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}l({\bm{x}}_{T+n};\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{V}_{T})+\lambda\left\|\bm{a}^{\prime}\right\|^{2}.

Here, the hyperparameters are the network width
m>0
, step size
\eta>0
, time horizon
T>0
, the number of
samples
N
in Stage 2, and the regularization strength
\lambda>0
.

We will show that after the first stage, for each ground truth direction

{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}
, there will be some neurons
{\bm{v}}_{i}
that has converged to that direction.
As a result, in the second stage, we can use ridge regression to pick out those neurons and use them to fit the target
function. The analysis of this second stage is standard and has been done in
[
12
,
1
,
8
,
21
,
25
]
. Hence, we will not further discuss this stage in the
main text and defer the proofs of this stage to Appendix
C
.

For the gradient update in Stage 1, we have the following lemma on its expectation and tail.
The proof of this lemma is rather standard and can be found in, for example,
[

We also provide a proof in Appendix

Lemma 2.1

(First-layer gradients)
.

Consider the setting described above.
Suppose that
\phi
satisfies Assumption
1
and
a_{i}=1
for all
i\in[m]

and
\{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k}
are orthonormal. Then, for each

\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left[f_{*}({\bm{x}})\nabla_{{\bm{v}}_{i}}\phi({\bm{v}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{x}})\right]=2\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{P}\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*},{\bm{v}}_{i}\right\rangle{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}+\sum_{l\geq L}\sum_{k=1}^{P}l\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*},{\bm{v}}_{i}\right\rangle^{l-1}{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}.

(2)

Then, for each fixed neuron
(a,{\bm{v}})
and direction
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

{\bm{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\bm{I}_{d}\right)

\displaystyle|\left\langle f_{*}({\bm{x}})\nabla_{{\bm{v}}_{i}}\phi({\bm{v}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{x}}),\bm{u}\right\rangle|\lesssim_{\phi}P^{1/2}\log^{2(1+q)}\log(m/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})\quad\text{with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}$}.

2.3
Main result

The following is our main result. The proof of it can be found in Appendix
D
.

Theorem 2.1

(Main Theorem)
.

Consider the setting and algorithm described above. Let
C>0
be a large universal constant.
Suppose that
\log^{C}d\leq P\leq d
and
\{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k\in[P]}
are orthonormal. Let
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\in(\exp(-\log^{C}d),1)
and
\varepsilon_{*}>0
be given. Suppose that we choose
a_{0},\eta,T,N
satisfying

m=\tilde{\Theta}(P),\quad N=\tilde{\Theta}\left(\frac{P^{2}}{\varepsilon_{*}^{2}\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}^{2}}\right),\quad\eta=\tilde{\Theta}_{\phi}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{*}^{2}\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{PdP^{L/2-1}}\right),\quad T=\tilde{O}_{\phi}\left(\frac{P^{L/2-1}}{\eta\varepsilon_{*}^{4}\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}\right).

Then, there exists some
\lambda>0
such that at the end of training, we have
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\bm{a},\bm{V})\leq\varepsilon_{*}
with
probability at least
1-O(\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})
.

Remark
.

Note that
N\ll T
. Hence, the total number of samples needed is
T=\tilde{O}_{\phi}(dP^{L-1})
, which matches
the sample complexity of separately learning
P
single-index models with the relevant subspace known

a priori
and the noise scales with the ambient dimension
d
.
∎

3
The gradient flow analysis

In this section, we consider the situation where the training algorithm in Stage 1 is gradient flow over the population
correlation loss instead of online SGD. The discussion here is non-rigorous and our formal proof does not rely on anything in
this section. Nevertheless, this gradient flow analysis will provide valuable intuition on the behavior of online SGD.

For notational simplicity, we will assume w.l.o.g. that
{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}=\bm{e}_{k}
.
In addition, we will assume
\phi=h_{2}+h_{L}
with
L>2
for ease of presentation.
Let
{\bm{v}}
be an arbitrary first-layer neuron. By Lemma
2.1
,
the dynamics of
{\bm{v}}
are controlled by
2
2

2

In the main text, we use
\tau
to index the time in this continuous-time process
(as
t
has been used to index the steps in the discrete-time process) and will often omit it when it is clear from the context.

\textstyle\dot{{\bm{v}}}_{\tau}\approx 2\sum_{k=1}^{P}v_{k}(\bm{I}-{\bm{v}}{\bm{v}}^{\top})\bm{e}_{k}+L\sum_{k=1}^{P}v_{k}^{L-1}(\bm{I}-{\bm{v}}{\bm{v}}^{\top})\bm{e}_{k}.

The second term on the RHS comes from the normalized/projection. For each
k\in[d]
, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}v_{k}^{2}\approx 2\mathbbm{1}\{k\leq P\}\left(2+Lv_{k}^{L-2}\right)v_{k}^{2}-2\left(2\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}+L\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|_{L}^{L}\right)v_{k}^{2}.

(4)

We further split Stage 1 into two substages. In Stage 1.1, the second-order terms dominate and

\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}/\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}
grows from
\Theta(P/d)
to
\Theta(1)
. In Stage 1.2,

{\bm{v}}
converges to one ground-truth direction relying on the signal from the higher-order terms.

The direction to which
{\bm{v}}
will converge depends on the index of the largest
v_{k}^{2}
at the beginning of Stage 1.2.
With some standard concentration/anti-concentration argument, one can show that
\max_{k\in[P]}v_{k}^{2}
is at least
1+c
times larger
than the second-largest
v_{k}^{2}
for a small constant
c>0
with probability at least
1/\poly(P)
at the initialization
(of Stage 1.1). Hence, as long as this gap can be preserved throughout Stage 1, we can choose
m=\poly(P)

to ensure all ground-truth directions can be found after Stage 1.2.

3.1
Stage 1.1: learning the subspace and preservation of the gap

In this substage, we track
\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}/\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}
and
v_{p}^{2}/v_{q}^{2}
where
p,q\in[P]

are arbitrary.
The goal is to show that
\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}/\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}
will grow to a constant while

v_{p}^{2}/v_{q}^{2}
stay close to its initial value.

For the norm ratio, by (
4
), we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}=\frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}-\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}=\frac{4\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}+\frac{2L\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|_{L}^{L}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\\
-\cancel{\frac{2\left(2\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}+L\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|_{L}^{L}\right)\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}}+\cancel{\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\frac{2\left(2\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}+L\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|_{L}^{L}\right)\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}}.

In particular, note that the terms coming from normalization cancel with each other. Moreover,
this implies
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\geq 4\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}
,
and therefore, it takes only at most
\frac{1+o(1)}{4}\log(d/P)=\Theta(\log(d/P))
amount of time for the ratio to grow from
\Theta(P/d)
to
\Theta(1)
.
If we choose a small step size
\eta
so that online SGD closely tracks the gradient flow, then the number of
steps one should expect is
O(\log(d/P)/\eta)
.

Meanwhile, for any
p,q\in[P]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\frac{v_{p}^{2}}{v_{q}^{2}}

\displaystyle=2\left(2+Lv_{p}^{L-2}\right)\frac{v_{p}^{2}}{v_{q}^{2}}-2\left(2\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}+L\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|_{L}^{L}\right)\frac{v_{p}^{2}}{v_{q}^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{v_{p}^{2}}{v_{q}^{2}}\left(2\left(2+Lv_{q}^{L-2}\right)-2\left(2\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}+L\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|_{L}^{L}\right)\right)=2L\left(v_{p}^{L-2}-v_{q}^{L-2}\right)\frac{v_{p}^{2}}{v_{q}^{2}}.

Note that not only those terms coming from normalization cancel with each other, but also the second-order terms.
In particular, this also implies that we cannot learn the directions using only the second-order terms.
At initialization, with high probability
v_{k}^{2}=\tilde{O}(1/d)
for all
k\in[P]
.
Hence, if we assume the induction hypothesis
v_{p}^{2}=\tilde{O}(1/P)
, then above will become

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}v_{p}^{2}/v_{q}^{2}\lesssim P^{-1}v_{p}^{2}/v_{q}^{2}
. As a result,

v_{t,p}^{2}/v_{t,q}^{2}\leq(1+o(1))v_{0,p}^{2}/v_{0,q}^{2}
for any
t\leq\Theta(\log(d/P))
,
as long as
P\geq\poly\log d
.

3.2
Stage 1.2: learning the directions

Let
{\bm{v}}
be a first-layer neuron with
v_{1}^{2}\geq(1+c)\max_{2\leq k\leq P}v_{k}^{2}
for some small constant

c>0
at initialization. By our previous discussion, we know at the end of Stage 1.1, the above bound still holds
with a potentially smaller constant
c>0
. In addition, since
\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}=\Theta(1)
, we also have
v_{1}^{2}\geq\Omega(1/P)
at the end of Stage 1.1. We claim that
{\bm{v}}
will converge to
\bm{e}_{1}
. The argument here is similar to
the proofs in
[
20
]
and
[
17
]
.

Again, by (
4
), we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}v_{1}^{2}\approx 2\left(2-2\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}+Lv_{1}^{L-2}-L\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|_{L}^{L}\right)v_{1}^{2}\geq 2L\left(v_{1}^{L-2}-\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|_{L}^{L}\right)v_{1}^{2}

Assume the induction hypothesis
v_{1}^{2}\geq(1+c)\max_{2\leq k\leq P}v_{k}^{2}
and write

v_{1}^{L-2}-\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|_{L}^{L}=v_{1}^{L-2}\left(1-v_{1}^{2}\right)-\left(\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}-v_{1}^{2}\right)\sum_{k=2}^{P}\frac{v_{k}^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}-v_{1}^{2}}v_{k}^{L-2}

Note that the summation is a weighted average of
\{v_{k}^{L-2}\}_{k\geq 2}
and therefore can be upper bounded by

\left(v_{1}^{2}/(1+c)\right)^{L/2-1}\leq(1-c_{L})v_{1}^{L-2}
for some constant
c_{L}>0
that can only depend on
L
.
Thus, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}v_{1}^{2}\gtrsim 2L\left(v_{1}^{L-2}\left(1-v_{1}^{2}\right)-\left(\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}-v_{1}^{2}\right)(1-c_{L})v_{1}^{L-2}\right)v_{1}^{2}\geq 2c_{L}L\left(1-v_{1}^{2}\right)v_{1}^{L}.

When
v_{1}^{2}\leq 3/4
, this implies
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}v_{1}^{2}\gtrsim_{L}v_{1}^{L}
. As a result, it takes at most

O_{L}(P^{L/2-1})
amount of time for
v_{1}^{2}
to grow from
\Omega(1/P)
to
3/4
under gradient flow.
It is important that
v_{1}^{2}=\Omega(1/P)
instead of
\Omega(1/d)
at the start of Stage 1.2, since otherwise the time
needed will be
O_{L}(d^{L-1})
. After
v_{1}^{2}
reaches
3/4
, we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}(1-v_{1}^{2})\lesssim_{L}-\left(1-v_{1}^{2}\right)
. Thus,
v_{1}^{2}
will converge linearly to
1
afterwards.

4
From gradient flow to online SGD

In this section, we discuss how to convert the previous gradient flow analysis to an online SGD one. Our actual proof
will be based directly on the online SGD analysis, but the overall idea is still proving that the online SGD dynamics
of certain important quantities closely track their population gradient descent (GD) counterparts. Our choice of
learning rate
\eta
will be much smaller than what needed for GD to track GF, so the bottleneck comes from the
GD-to-SGD conversion, not the GF-to-GD one. Provided that SGD tracks GD well, the number of
steps/samples it needs to finish each substage is roughly the amount of time GF needs, divided by the step size
\eta
.

The rest of this section is organized as follows. In Section
4.1
, we collect a few useful lemmas for controlling the difference between noisy dynamics
and their deterministic counterparts. The idea behind them has appeared in
[
4
]
and is also
used in
[
1
]
. Here, we simplify and slightly generalize their argument and provide
a user-friendly interface. When used properly, it reduces the GD-to-SGD proof to routine calculus. Then, in
Section
4.2
, we discuss how to apply those general results to analyze the
dynamics of online SGD in our setting.

4.1
Technical lemmas for analyzing general noisy dynamics

We start with the lemma that will be used to analyze
\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}/\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}
. The formal proofs of it
and all other lemmas in this subsection can be found in Section
E
.

Lemma 4.1

(Stochastic Gronwall’s lemma)
.

Suppose that
(X_{t})_{t}
satisfies

X_{t+1}=(1+\alpha)X_{t}+\xi_{t+1}+Z_{t+1},\quad X_{0}=x_{0}>0,

(5)

where the signal growth rate
\alpha>0
and initialization
x_{0}>0
are given,
(\xi_{t})_{t}
is an
adapted process, and
(Z_{t})_{t}
is a martingale difference sequence. Define
x_{t}=(1+\alpha)^{t}x_{0}
.

Let
T>0
and
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\in(0,1)
be given.
Suppose that there exists some
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},\xi}\in(0,1)
and
\Xi,\sigma_{Z}>0
such that for every
t\geq 0
,
if
X_{t}=(1\pm 0.5)x_{t}
, then we have
|\xi_{t+1}|\leq(1+\alpha)^{t}\Xi
with probability at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},\xi}

and
Z_{t+1}
is conditionally
((1+\alpha)^{t}\sigma_{Z}^{2},\theta)
-subweibull.
Then, if

\Xi\lesssim\frac{x_{0}}{T}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{Z}^{2}\lesssim\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{T\log^{\theta+1}(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})},

(6)

we have
X_{t}=(1\pm 0.5)x_{t}
for all
t\in[T]
with probability at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}-T\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},\xi}
.

Condition (
6
)
.

One may interpret
Z_{t+1}
as those terms coming from the difference between the population and mini-batch gradients,
whose variance is typically quadratic in
\eta
, and
\xi_{t+1}
as the higher-order error terms.
\alpha
is usually small.
In our case, it is proportional to the step size
\eta
.
T
is usually the time needed for
X_{t}
to grow from a small

x_{0}>0
to
\Theta(1)
, which is roughly
\alpha^{-1}\log(1/x_{0})
. Since the LHS’ of (
6
)
are
O(\eta^{2})
while the RHS’ are
\Omega(\eta)
, (
6
) can be alternatively viewed
as a condition on
\eta
.
∎

Stochastic induction
.

One important feature of this lemma is that it only requires the bounds
|\xi_{t+1}|\leq(1+\alpha)^{t}\Xi
and

\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Z_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq(1+\alpha)^{t}\sigma_{Z}^{2}
to hold when
X_{t}=(1\pm 0.5)x_{t}
. This can be viewed
as a form of induction and is particularly useful when considering the dynamics of, say,
v_{k}^{2}
.
Similar to how the RHS of
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}v_{\tau,k}^{2}=2v_{\tau,k}\dot{v}_{\tau,k}
depends on
v_{\tau,k}
,
the size of
\xi_{t+1},Z_{t+1}
will usually depend on
X_{t}
. Hence, we will not be able to bound them without
suitable induction hypotheses.
∎

Remark on the subweibull condition
.

We assume the martingale difference terms
(Z_{t+1})_{t}
are conditionally subweibull. This allows us to get
poly-logarithmic dependence on
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
, which is important as we will eventually take union bound over

\poly P
events. One may replace this condition with the weaker condition

\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Z_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq(1+\alpha)^{t}\sigma_{Z}^{2}
. This will lead to a linear dependence on
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
.
∎

Proof sketch of Lemma
4.1
.

For the ease of presentation, we assume that
|\xi_{t+1}|\leq(1+\alpha)^{t}\Xi
with probability at least

1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},\xi}
and
\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Z_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq(1+\alpha)^{t}\sigma_{Z}^{2}
always hold. This step can be
made formal using a stopping time argument. See Section
E
for details. Then,
Unroll (
5
) to obtain

X_{t+1}=(1+\alpha)^{t+1}x_{0}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1+\alpha)^{t-s}\xi_{s+1}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1+\alpha)^{t-s}Z_{s+1}.

Divide both sides with
(1+\alpha)^{t+1}
and replace
t+1
with
t
. Then, the above becomes

\textstyle X_{t}(1+\alpha)^{-t}=x_{0}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1+\alpha)^{-s}\xi_{s}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1+\alpha)^{-s}Z_{s}.

The second term is bounded by
T\Xi
(uniformly over
t\leq T
) with probability at least
1-T\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},\xi}
.
Note that
(1+\alpha)^{-s}Z_{s}
is still a martingale difference sequence. Hence, by Doob’s
L^{2}
-submartingale
inequality, the third term is bounded by
x_{0}/4
with probability at least
16\sigma_{Z}^{2}/(\alpha x_{0}^{2})
.
Thus, when (
6
) holds, the RHS is
(1\pm 0.5)x_{0}
with probability at
least
1-T\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},\xi}-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
. Multiply both sides with
(1+\alpha)^{t}
, and we complete the proof.
To improve the dependence on
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
from linear to poly-logarithmic, it suffices to replace Doob’s

L^{2}
-submartingale inequality with a variant of Freedman’s inequality that works with subweibull variables
(cf. Appendix
E
).
∎

Using the same strategy, one can prove a similar lemma that
deals with the case
\alpha=0
, which will be used to show the preservation of the gap in Stage 1.1. Another
interesting case is where the growth is not linear but polynomial. This is the case of Stage 1.2 in our setting.
For this case, we have the following lemma.

3

conditions on the noises when
X_{t}
grows as in Lemma
4.1
. We thank Eshaan Nichani

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t}
be a non-negative stochastic process satisfying

X_{t+1}\geq X_{t}+\alpha X_{t}^{p}+Z_{t+1}+\xi_{t+1},\quad X_{0}=x_{0}>0,

(7)

where
\alpha>0
,
(Z_{t+1})_{t}
is a martingale difference sequence, and
(\xi_{t})_{t}
is an adapted process.
Let
\hat{x}_{t}
be the solution to the deterministic recurrence relationship

\hat{x}_{t+1}=\hat{x}_{t}+\alpha\hat{x}_{t}^{p},\hat{x}_{0}=x_{0}/2.

Let
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\in(0,1)
be given and

T:=\inf\left\{t\lesssim\left(p\alpha(x_{0}/2)^{p-1}\right)^{-1}\,:\,X_{t}\geq 1\right\}.

Suppose that there exists
\Xi,\sigma_{Z}>0
and
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},\xi}\in(0,1)
such that if

X_{t}\geq\hat{x}_{t}
and
t\leq T
, we have
|\xi_{t}|\leq\Xi X_{t}
with probability at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},\xi}
and

Z_{t+1}
is conditionally
(\sigma_{Z}^{2}X_{t},\theta)
-subweibull.
Then, if

\alpha\lesssim x_{0}^{p-1}/p,\quad\Xi\lesssim p\alpha x_{0}^{p-1},\quad\sigma_{Z}^{2}\lesssim p\alpha x_{0}^{p}\poly\log(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}),

(8)

we have
X_{t}\geq\hat{x}_{t}
for all
t\leq T
and
X_{t}\geq 1
with
probability at least
1-T\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},\xi}-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
.

The proof of this lemma can be found in Section
E
.
It is similar to the previous proof in spirit: we replace
(1+\alpha)^{t}
with

\prod_{s=0}^{t-1}(1+\alpha X_{s}^{p-1})
and unroll the recurrence. However, unlike the linear case, it is generally
difficult to upper bound the difference between
X_{t}
and
\hat{x}_{t}
, as this type of polynomial systems exhibit
sharp transitions and blow up in finite time. Consequently,
|\xi_{t}|\leq\Xi X_{t}
and
\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Z_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq\sigma_{Z}^{2}X_{t}

do not directly imply that
|\xi_{t}|\lesssim\Xi\hat{x}_{t}
and
\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Z_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\lesssim\sigma_{Z}^{2}\hat{x}_{t}
,
and this makes the analysis tricky as the RHS’ are not deterministic. To handle this issue, we use the following
recoupling argument: whenever
X_{t}\geq 4\hat{x}_{t}
, we replace the current
\hat{x}_{t}
with
X_{t}/2
. Clear that
this can only increase
\hat{x}_{t}
, and it ensures
X_{t}\leq 4\hat{x}_{t}
always holds. Moreover, after each
recoupling,
\hat{x}_{t}
will at least double. As a result, the conditions we need to absorb the noises will also
become weaker.

4.2
Sample complexity of online SGD

In this subsection, we demonstrate how to use the previous results to obtain results for online SGD and discuss why
the sample complexity is
\tilde{O}(dP^{L-1})
instead of
\tilde{O}(d^{L-1})
even though we are relying on the

L
-th order terms to learn the directions.

4.2.1
A simplified version of Stage 1.1

As an example, we consider the dynamics of
Pv_{p}^{2}/(dv_{q}^{2})
where
p\leq P
and
q>P
and assume both of
v_{p}
and
v_{q}
are
small and
Pv_{p}^{2}/(dv_{q}^{2})\leq 1
.
This can be viewed as a simplified version of the analysis of
\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}/\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}
in Stage 1.1.
The analysis of other quantities/stages is essentially the same — we rewrite the update rule to single out martingale
difference terms and the higher-order error terms, and apply a suitable lemma from the previous
subsection (or Section
E
) to complete the proof.

For the ease of presentation, in this subsection, we ignore the higher-order terms. In particular, we assume the approximation

\hat{v}_{t+1,k}\approx v_{t,k}+2\eta\left(\mathbbm{1}\{k\leq P\}-\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}\right)+\eta Z_{t+1,k},

for all
k\in[d]
,
where
Z_{t+1,k}
represents the difference between the population and mini-batch gradients. Then, we compute

\hat{v}_{t+1,k}^{2}\approx\left(1+4\eta\left(\mathbbm{1}\{k\leq P\}-\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}\right)\right)v_{k}^{2}+2\eta v_{k}Z_{k}\pm C_{L}\eta^{2}(1\vee Z_{k}^{2}).

Here, the last term is the higher-order term and will eventually be included in
\xi
. For simplicity,
we will also ignore them in the following discussion.
The second term is the martingale difference term. Its (conditional) variance depend on
v_{k}
, and this necessitates
the induction-style conditions in Lemma
4.1
. Note that

v_{t+1,p}^{2}/v_{t+1,q}^{2}=\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}/\hat{v}_{t+1,q}^{2}
. Hence, we have

\frac{v_{t+1,p}^{2}}{v_{t+1,q}^{2}}\approx\frac{\left(1+4\eta\left(1-\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}\right)\right)v_{p}^{2}+2\eta v_{p}Z_{p}}{\left(1-4\eta\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}\right)v_{q}^{2}+2\eta v_{q}Z_{q}}.

Repeatedly use the elementary identity

\frac{1}{a+\delta}=\frac{1}{a}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{a}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{a+\delta}\right)\right)\approx\frac{1}{a}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{a}\right)
for any
a>0
and small
\delta>0
, we can rewrite the above equation as

\frac{Pv_{t+1,p}^{2}}{dv_{t+1,q}^{2}}\approx\left(1+4\eta\right)\frac{Pv_{p}^{2}}{dv_{q}^{2}}-\frac{Pv_{p}^{2}}{dv_{q}^{2}}\frac{2\eta v_{q}Z_{q}}{v_{q}^{2}}+\frac{2P\eta v_{p}Z_{p}}{dv_{q}^{2}}.

Suppose that
v_{p}^{2}\approx v_{q}^{2}
at initialization and assume the induction hypothesis
Pv_{p}^{2}/(dv_{q}^{2})=(1\pm 0.5)(1+4\eta)^{t}Pv_{0,p}^{2}/(dv_{0,q}^{2})
. Then, by Lemma
2.1
, the conditional variance of the martingale
difference terms (the last two terms) is bounded by
O_{L}((1+4\eta)^{t}\eta^{2}P^{2}/d)
. Using the language of
Lemma
4.1
, this means
\sigma_{Z}^{2}\leq O_{L}(\eta^{2}P^{2}/d)
.
Meanwhile, by our gradient flow analysis, the number steps Stage 1.1 needs is roughly
\log d/\eta
.
Hence, in order for (the second condition of) (
6
) to hold, it suffices to choose

\eta=\tilde{O}(1/d)
. One can also show that for the higher-order terms to be small, it suffices to choose

\eta=\tilde{O}(1/(dP))
. As a result, for Stage 1.1, the sample complexity is
\tilde{O}(dP)
.

4.2.2
The improved sample complexity for Stage 1.2

To see why the existence of the second-order terms can reduce the sample complexity from
d^{\mathrm{IE}-1}
to
d\poly(P)
,
first note that after Stage 1.1,
\max_{p\in[P]}v_{p}^{2}
will be
\Omega(1/P)
. Also
note that the conditions in Lemma
4.2
depend on the initial value.
With the initial value being
\Omega(1/P)
instead of
\tilde{O}(1/d)
, the largest possible step size we can choose
will be
O(1)/(PdP^{L/2-1})
, which is much larger than the usual
O(1/(Pd^{L/2}))
requirement from the vanilla
information exponent argument. Meanwhile, by our gradient flow analysis, we know the number of iterations needed
is
O(P^{L/2-1}/\eta)
. Combine these and we obtain the
\tilde{O}(dP^{L-1})
sample complexity.

5
Conclusion and limitations

In this work, we study the task of learning multi-index models of form
f_{*}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\phi({\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\cdot{\bm{x}})

with
P\ll d
,
\{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k}
be orthogonal and
\phi=\hat{\phi}_{2}h_{2}+\sum_{l=L}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{l}h_{l}
. By considering both the lower- and
higher-order terms, we prove an
\tilde{O}(d\poly(P))
bound on the sample complex for strong recovery of
directions using online SGD, which improves the results one can obtain using vanilla information exponent-based analysis.

The main limitation of this work is the orthogonality condition. This can potentially be relaxed to near-orthogonality
as in
[
25
]
. Extending this result beyond near-orthogonal teacher neurons is an interesting but
challenging future direction, as when the teacher neurons are not near-orthogonal, this task is hard in general.
However, we conjecture that when the target model has a hierarchical structure across different orders, online SGD can
gradually learn the directions using those terms of different order sequentially.

Another limitation of this work is the assumption that the signal strengths are isotropic. When this is not true,
training with the second-order terms and correlation loss will make all neurons collapse to the largest direction
or require
d^{2}
samples if we perform only one gradient step
[
12
,
11
]
. That being
said, it is still reasonable to expect the overall sample complexity to be improved if
we can leverage the second-order terms properly. Formally establishing this is also a potential future direction.

Appendix A
Preliminaries

A.1
Population and per-sample gradients

In this subsection, we show that the task of learning the multi-index target function
f_{*}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\phi({\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\cdot{\bm{x}})

can be reduced to tensor decomposition and prove the tail bounds in Lemma
2.1
.

For the first goal, we will need the following classical result on Hermite polynomials
(cf. Chapter 11.2 of
[
24
]
) and correlated Gaussian variables.

Lemma A.1

(Proposition 11.31 of
[
24
]
)
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
denote the normalized Hermite polynomials.
Let
\rho\in[-1,1]
and
z,z^{\prime}
be
\rho
-correlated standard Gaussian variables. Then, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{z,z^{\prime}}\left[h_{k}(z)h_{j}(z^{\prime})\right]=\mathbbm{1}\{k=j\}\rho^{k}.

Lemma A.2
.

Under the setting described in Section
2
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{{\bm{x}}}\left[f_{*}({\bm{x}})\nabla_{{\bm{v}}}\phi({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}l\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*},{\bm{v}}\right\rangle^{l-1}{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}.

Proof.

Let
\phi=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{k}h_{k}
be the Hermite expansion of
\phi
where the convergence is in
L^{2}
sense.
For any
\rho\in[-1,1]
and
\rho
-correlated standard Gaussian variables
z,z^{\prime}
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{z,z^{\prime}}\left\{\phi(z)\phi(z^{\prime})\right\}=\sum_{k,l=0}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{k}\hat{\phi}_{l}\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{z,z^{\prime}}\left\{h_{k}(z)h_{l}(z^{\prime})\right\}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{k}^{2}\rho^{k},

where the first equality comes from the Dominated Convergence Theorem and the second from Lemma
A.1
. Therefore, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{{\bm{x}}}\left[f_{*}({\bm{x}})\phi({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{{\bm{x}}}\left[\phi({\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\cdot{\bm{x}})\phi({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*},{\bm{v}}\right\rangle^{l}.

Then, we compute

\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{{\bm{x}}}\left[f_{*}({\bm{x}})\nabla_{{\bm{v}}}\phi({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{v}}}\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*},{\bm{v}}\right\rangle^{l}=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}l\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*},{\bm{v}}\right\rangle^{l-1}{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}.

∎

Now, we consider the per-sample gradient. The goal here is to prove variance and tail bounds for

\left\langle f_{*}({\bm{x}})\nabla_{{\bm{v}}}\phi({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}}),\bm{u}\right\rangle
, where
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is an arbitrary direction that is
independent of
{\bm{x}}
.
First, we upper bound
f_{*}
. To this end, we will use need the following concentration inequality for
the sum of independent subweibull variables. It is a consequence of Theorem 3.2 of
[
19
]

and the discussion after Definition 2.3 of the same paper.

Lemma A.3

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\psi_{\alpha}(x)=\exp(x^{\alpha})-1
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}
denote the corresponding Orlicz norm.
Let
\alpha\leq 1
and
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. mean zero random variables with variance
\sigma^{2}
and

\left\|X_{1}\right\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}<\infty
.
Then, for any
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\in(0,1)
, we have

{\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right|}\lesssim_{\alpha}\sqrt{n}\sigma\log^{1/2}(1/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})+\sqrt{n}\left\|X_{1}\right\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\log^{1/\alpha}(n/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}),\quad\text{with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}$.}

Lemma A.4
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds and
\{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k}
are orthonormal.
Then, for any
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\in(0,1)
, we have

|f_{*}({\bm{x}})|\lesssim\sqrt{P}\log^{q}(P/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}),\quad\text{with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}$.}

Proof.

Write
Y_{k}:=\phi({\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\cdot{\bm{x}})
. By the orthonormality of
\{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k}
,
\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
are independent
variables. For any
p\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|Y_{1}\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)}|\phi|^{p}(z)\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)}\left[(1+z^{2})^{pq/2}\right]\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq Cp^{q}\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)}\left[(1+z^{2})^{q}\right]}\lesssim p^{q},

where the first inequality in the second line comes from the Gaussian hypercontractivity. This implies that

\left\|Y_{1}\right\|_{\psi_{1/q}}\lesssim 1
and
\mathop{\mathbf{Var\/}}Y_{1}\lesssim 1
. Thus, by Lemma
A.3
, we have with probability at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
that

|f_{*}({\bm{x}})|\lesssim\sqrt{P}\log^{1/2}(1/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})+\sqrt{P}\log^{q}(P/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})\lesssim\sqrt{P}\log^{q}(P/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}).

∎

Lemma A.5
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds and
\{{\bm{v}}_{k}^{*}\}_{k}
are orthonormal. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\langle f_{*}({\bm{x}})\nabla_{{\bm{v}}}\phi({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}}),\bm{u}\right\rangle^{2}\lesssim_{\phi}P,

\displaystyle|\left\langle f_{*}({\bm{x}})\nabla_{{\bm{v}}}\phi({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}}),\bm{u}\right\rangle|\lesssim_{\phi}P^{1/2}\log^{2(1+q)}\log(m/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})\quad\text{with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}$},

where
q
is the degree of
\phi
if it is a polynomial and
Q=0
if
\phi
is Lipschitz.

Proof.

Note that
\left\langle\nabla_{{\bm{v}}}\phi({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}}),\bm{u}\right\rangle=\phi^{\prime}({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\left\langle{\bm{x}},\bm{u}\right\rangle
.
First, for the variance, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}(f_{*}({\bm{x}})\phi^{\prime}({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\left\langle\bm{u},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle)^{2}\lesssim\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}f_{*}^{6}+\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}(\phi^{\prime}({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}}))^{6}+\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\langle\bm{u},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle^{6}\lesssim P,

where the second inequality comes from Assumption
1
and the hypercontractivity of Gaussian.
This implies
\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\langle\nabla f({\bm{x}}),\bm{u}\right\rangle^{2}\lesssim P
.
For the tail bound, first recall from the previous lemma that
|f_{*}({\bm{x}})|\lesssim\sqrt{P}\log^{q}(P/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})

with probability at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
. The proof of it also implies
|\phi^{\prime}({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}})|\lesssim\log^{q}(P/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})
with probability at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
. Finally, since
\left\langle{\bm{x}},\bm{u}\right\rangle

is
1
-subgaussian, we have
{\left|{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{u}\right|}\lesssim\log^{1/2}(1/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})

with probability at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
. Combine these bounds, take the union bound over
m
learner neurons,
and we complete the proof.
∎

A.2
Typical structure at initialization

In this subsection, we use the results in Section
A.3
to analyze
the structure of
{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{m}
at initialization. Recall that we initialize
{\bm{v}}_{i}
with
\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
independently. Meanwhile,
note that for
{\bm{v}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, we have
{\bm{v}}\overset{d}{=}\bm{Z}/\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|
where

\bm{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\bm{I}_{d}\right)
.

We start with a lemma on the largest coordinate.

Lemma A.6

(Largest coordinate)
.

Let
{\bm{v}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. For any
K\geq 1
, we have

\max_{i\in[d]}|v_{i}|\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2K\log d}}{\sqrt{d}}\quad\text{with probability at least }1-\frac{4}{d^{K}}.

As a corollary, for any
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\in(0,1)
, at initialization, we have

\max_{i\in[m]}\left\|{\bm{v}}_{i}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2\log(4m/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})}}{\sqrt{d}}\quad\text{with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}$}.

Proof.

Let
\bm{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\bm{I}_{d}\right)
. Recall that
\bm{Z}/\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|
follows the uniform distribution over the sphere.
By Lemma
A.11
, we have
\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|\geq\sqrt{d}/2
with
probability at least
1-2\exp(-d/18)
. Then, by Lemma
A.13
,
with probability at least
1-2e^{-d/18}-2e^{-s^{2}/2}
, we have

\frac{\max_{i\in[d]}|Z_{i}|}{\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\log d}+s}{\sqrt{d}/2}=\frac{2\sqrt{2\log d}}{\sqrt{d}}+\frac{2s}{\sqrt{d}}.

Let
K\geq 1
be arbitrary. Choose
s=\sqrt{2K\log d}
and the above becomes

\frac{\max_{i\in[d]}|Z_{i}|}{\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|}\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2K\log d}}{\sqrt{d}}\quad\text{with probability at least }1-\frac{4}{d^{K}}.

For the corollary, use union bound and choose
K=\log(4m/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})/\log d
, we have

\max_{i\in[m]}\left\|{\bm{v}}_{i}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2\log(4m/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})}}{\sqrt{d}}\quad\text{with probability at least }1-\frac{4m}{d^{K}}=1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}.

∎

Suppose that we only have higher-order terms. Then, for a neuron
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
to converge to a ground-truth
direction
\bm{e}_{k}
in a reasonable amount of time, we need
v_{k}^{2}
to be the largest among all
v_{i}^{2}
and there is gap between
it and the second largest
v_{i}^{2}
. The following lemma ensures that when
m
is large, for every ground-truth direction

\{\bm{e}_{k}\}_{k\in[P]}
, there will be at least one neuron satisfying the above property. Note that in our case, we
only need to ensure
v_{k}^{2}
is the largest among all
\{v_{i}^{2}\}_{i\in[P]}
instead of
\{v_{i}^{2}\}_{i\in[d]}
, as
the second-order term will help us identify the correct subspace.

Lemma A.7

(Existence of good neurons)
.

Let
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\in(e^{-\log^{C}d},1)
be given. Suppose that
m\geq 2P\log(P/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})=\tilde{\Theta}(P)
.
Then, at initialization, with probability at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
, we have

\forall p\in[P]\,\exists i\in[m]\quad\text{such that}\quad\frac{v_{i,p}^{2}}{\max_{q\in[P]\setminus\{p\}}v_{i,q}^{2}}\geq 1+\frac{1}{200\log(48P)}=1+\tilde{\Theta}(1).

Proof.

Let
\delta_{0}
be a parameter to chosen later and let
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},0}
be the probability that

\max_{q\in[P]}v_{q}^{2}
is smaller than
1+\delta_{0}
times the second largest
v_{q}^{2}
.
For each
p\in[P]
, let
B_{p}
be the event
\left\{\forall k\in[m],v_{k,p}^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{0})\max_{q\in[P]\setminus\{p\}}v_{k,q}^{2}\right\}
.
To bound
\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}[B_{p}]
, we write

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}[B_{p}]

\displaystyle=\left(\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}_{{\bm{v}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\left[v_{p}^{2}\leq(1+c)\max_{q\in[P]\setminus\{p\}}v_{q}^{2}\right]\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left[v_{p}^{2}\neq\max_{q\in[P]}v_{q}^{2}\right]+\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left[v_{p}^{2}\leq(1+c)\max_{q\in[P]\setminus\{p\}}v_{q}^{2}\;\bigg|\;v_{p}^{2}=\max_{q\in[P]}v_{q}^{2}\right]\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left[v_{p}^{2}=\max_{q\in[P]}v_{q}^{2}\right]\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{P}+\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},0}}{P}\right)^{m}.

By Corollary
A.12
, if we choose
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},0}=1/2
, then we
can choose

\delta_{0}=\frac{1}{200\log(48P)}.

With the above choices of parameters, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left[\bigcup_{p\in[P]}B_{p}\right]\leq P\left(1-\frac{1}{2P}\right)^{m}\leq P\exp\left(-\frac{m}{2P}\right).

For the last term to be bounded by
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
, it suffices to choose
m\geq 2P\log(P/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})
.
∎

Lemma A.8

(Typical structure at initialization)
.

Let
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\in(e^{-\log^{C}d},1)
be given and
c_{g}>0
be a small constant.
Suppose that
\{{\bm{v}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
independently with

m=\Theta\left(P\log(P/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})\right).

Then, with probability at least
1-3\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
, we have

\displaystyle\forall p\in[P]\,\exists i\in[m]\quad\text{such that}\quad\frac{v_{i,p}}{\max_{q\in[P]\setminus\{p\}}|v_{i,q}|}\geq 1+\frac{\Theta(1)}{\log P},

(9)

\displaystyle\forall i\in[m],\,\quad\left\|{\bm{v}}_{i}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{20\sqrt{\log(P/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})}}{\sqrt{d}},

\displaystyle\forall i\in[m],\,\quad\frac{\sqrt{P}}{3\sqrt{d}}\leq\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|}{\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|}\leq\frac{3\sqrt{P}}{\sqrt{d}}.

Proof.

The first two bounds comes directly from Lemma
A.6
and
Lemma
A.7

and the fact that we use symmetric initialization. By Lemma
A.11
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\left|\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|-\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|\right|\geq\sqrt{d}/2\right)\leq 2e^{-d/8},

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\left|\left\|\bm{Z}_{\leq P}\right\|-\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\|\bm{Z}_{\leq P}\right\|\right|\geq\sqrt{P}/2\right)\leq 2e^{-P/8}.

As a result, for any
{\bm{v}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, we have with probability at least
1-4e^{-P/8}
that

\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|}{\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|}\overset{d}{=}\frac{\left\|\bm{Z}_{\leq P}\right\|}{\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|}=\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\|\bm{Z}_{\leq P}\right\|\pm\sqrt{P}/2}{\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|\pm\sqrt{d}/2}=[1/3,3]\times\sqrt{\frac{P}{d}}.

Since we assume
P\geq\log^{C^{\prime}}d
for a large
C^{\prime}
, we have
4e^{-P/8}\leq\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}/m
. This gives the third
bound.
∎

A.3
Concentration and anti-concentration of Gaussian

Lemma A.9
.

Let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
be independent
\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
variables. Let
Y_{1},Y_{2}
be the largest and second largest
of
|Z_{1}|,\dots,|Z_{d}|
. For any
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\in(0,1)
, we have

\frac{Y_{1}}{Y_{2}}\geq 1+\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{12\log\left(12d/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\right)}\quad\text{with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}$.}

Proof.

The following proof is adapted from this MathOverflow answer
[
26
]
.
Let
f
and
F
denote the PDF and CDF of
|Z|
with
Z\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
, respectively. We have

f(y)=2\phi(y)\mathbbm{1}\{y\geq 0\}
and
F(y)=\mathrm{erf}(y/\sqrt{2})\mathbbm{1}\{y\geq 0\}
, where
\phi
is the PDF
of
\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
and
\mathrm{erf}
is the error function.
We will use the following formula for the joint PDF of two order statistics:

f_{Y_{1},Y_{2}}(y_{1},y_{2})=d(d-1)F^{d-2}(y_{2})f(y_{2})f(y_{1})\mathbbm{1}\{0<y_{2}<y_{1}\}.

Consider small
s>0
. We compute

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\frac{Y_{1}}{Y_{2}}\geq 1+s\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}\{y_{1}\geq(1+s)y_{2}\}f_{Y_{1},Y_{2}}(y_{1},y_{2})\,\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}y_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{Y_{1},Y_{2}}((1+s)y_{2}+r,y_{2})\,\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=d(d-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}F^{d-2}(y_{2})f(y_{2})\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}f((1+s)y_{2}+r)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\,\mathrm{d}y_{2}

\displaystyle=d(d-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}F^{d-2}(y_{2})f(y_{2})\left(1-F((1+s)y_{2})\right)\,\mathrm{d}y_{2}.

Let
G=F^{-1}
. With the change-of-variables
u=F(y_{2})
,
y_{2}=G(u)
, we can rewrite the above as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\frac{Y_{1}}{Y_{2}}\geq 1+s\right)

\displaystyle=d(d-1)\int_{0}^{1}u^{d-2}\left(1-F((1+s)G(u))\right)f(G(u))\,\mathrm{d}G(u)

\displaystyle=d(d-1)\int_{0}^{1}u^{d-2}\left(1-F((1+s)G(u))\right)\cancel{f(G(u))\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(G(u))}}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=d(d-1)\int_{0}^{1}u^{d-2}\left(1-F((1+s)G(u))\right)\,\mathrm{d}u.

Now, we analyze the last integral. We will use the following expansion of the (complementary) error function:

1-F(y)=1-\mathrm{erf}\left(y/\sqrt{2}\right)=\frac{e^{-y^{2}/2}}{y\sqrt{\pi/2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{y}^{\infty}r^{2}e^{-r^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}r\right).

For notational simplicity, put
w=G(u)
. Then, we have

\displaystyle 1-F((1+s)w)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(1+s)^{2}w^{2}/2}}{(1+s)w\sqrt{\pi/2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{(1+s)w}^{\infty}r^{2}e^{-r^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-(s+s^{2}/2)w^{2}\right)}{1+s}\frac{e^{-w^{2}/2}}{w\sqrt{\pi/2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{(1+s)w}^{\infty}r^{2}e^{-r^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-(s+s^{2}/2)w^{2}\right)}{1+s}\left(1-F(w)\right)\frac{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{(1+s)w}^{\infty}r^{2}e^{-r^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}r}{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{w}^{\infty}r^{2}e^{-r^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}r}.

Note that the last factor is at least
1
and
F(w)=F(G(u))=F(F^{-1}(u))=u
. Therefore,

1-F((1+s)w)\geq\frac{\exp\left(-(s+s^{2}/2)w^{2}\right)}{1+s}(1-u).

As a result, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\frac{Y_{1}}{Y_{2}}\geq 1+s\right)

\displaystyle\geq d(d-1)\int_{0}^{1}u^{d-2}(1-u)\frac{\exp\left(-(s+s^{2}/2)G^{2}(u)\right)}{1+s}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\geq d(d-1)\int_{0}^{1-\varepsilon}u^{d-2}(1-u)\frac{\exp\left(-(s+s^{2}/2)G^{2}(u)\right)}{1+s}\,\mathrm{d}u,

where
\varepsilon>0
is a parameter to be chosen later. By the next lemma, when
u\leq 1-\varepsilon
, we have

G^{2}(u)\leq 2\log(2/\varepsilon)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\frac{Y_{1}}{Y_{2}}\geq 1+s\right)

\displaystyle\geq d(d-1)\int_{0}^{1-\varepsilon}u^{d-2}(1-u)\,\mathrm{d}u\frac{\exp\left(-(2s+s^{2})\log(2/\varepsilon)\right)}{1+s}

\displaystyle\geq d(d-1)\int_{0}^{1-\varepsilon}u^{d-2}(1-u)\,\mathrm{d}u\frac{1}{1+s}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{4s}

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)^{d}\left(1+\frac{d\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\right)\frac{1}{1+s}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{4s}.

Let
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\in(0,1)
be our target failure probability. We choose

(1-\varepsilon)^{d}\left(1+\frac{d\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\right)\geq 1-\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{3}\quad\Leftarrow\quad\varepsilon=\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{6d}.

With this choice of
\varepsilon
, we compute

\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{4s}\geq 1-\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{3}\quad\Leftarrow\quad\left(\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{12d}\right)^{4s}\geq 1-\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{3}\quad\Leftarrow\quad 4s\log\left(\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{12d}\right)\geq-\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{3}\quad\Leftarrow\quad s\leq\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{12\log\left(\frac{12d}{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}\right)}.

Note that for
s
satisfying this condition, we automatically have
1/(1+s)\geq 1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}/3
.
Thus, we have

\frac{Y_{1}}{Y_{2}}\geq 1+\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{12\log\left(\frac{8d}{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}\right)}\quad\text{with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}$.}

∎

Lemma A.10
.

Let
F(y)=\mathrm{erf}(y/\sqrt{2})\mathbbm{1}\{y\geq 0\}
be the CDF of
|\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)|
, respectively.
Let
G=F^{-1}
. If
u\leq 1-1/(d\log d)
, then
G(u)\leq\sqrt{2\log(2/\varepsilon)}
.

Proof.

Note that

G(u)\leq M
iff
u\leq F(M)
iff
1-u\geq 1-F(M)
iff
1-u\geq\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}(|Z|\geq M)
. In other words,
our goal here is to find the smallest
M
such that
\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}(|Z|\geq M)\leq\varepsilon
. By the standard Gaussian
concentration, we have
\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}(|Z|\geq M)\leq 2\exp(-M^{2}/2)
. For the RHS to be upper bounded by

\varepsilon
, it suffices to choose
M\geq\sqrt{2\log(2/\varepsilon)}
.
∎

Lemma A.11
.

Let
{\bm{z}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{z}}_{m}
be independent
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\bm{I}_{d}\right)
random vectors. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\forall k\in[m],\left|\frac{\left\|{\bm{z}}_{k}\right\|}{\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\|{\bm{z}}_{1}\right\|}-1\right|\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-2me^{-\varepsilon^{2}d/3}.

Proof.

It is well-known that any
1
-Lipschitz function of
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\bm{I}_{d}\right)
is
1
-subgaussian (see, for example,
Theorem 5.2.2 of
[
29
]
). Hence, for any
s>0
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\max_{k\in[m]}\left|\left\|{\bm{z}}_{k}\right\|-\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\|{\bm{z}}_{k}\right\|\right|\geq s\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\left|\left\|{\bm{z}}_{k}\right\|-\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\|{\bm{z}}_{k}\right\|\right|\geq s\right)\leq 2me^{-s^{2}/2}.

Set
s=\varepsilon\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\|{\bm{z}}_{k}\right\|
and the above becomes

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\forall k\in[m],\left|\frac{\left\|{\bm{z}}_{k}\right\|}{\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\|{\bm{z}}_{1}\right\|}-1\right|\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-2me^{-\varepsilon^{2}(\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\|{\bm{z}}_{1}\right\|)^{2}/2}.

To complete the proof, it suffices to note that
\left\|{\bm{z}}_{1}\right\|
follows the
\chi_{d}
distribution,
and therefore we have
\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left\|{\bm{z}}_{1}\right\|\geq\sqrt{d}(1-2/d)
.
∎

Corollary A.12
.

Let
{\bm{v}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
denote the largest and second largest of
|v_{1}|,\dots,|v_{P}|
.
Suppose that
\frac{d}{\log d}\gtrsim\frac{\log^{2}(P/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})}{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}^{2}}
and

\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\in(e^{-\log^{C}d},1)
. Then, we have

\frac{w_{1}}{w_{2}}\geq 1+\frac{\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}}{100\log(24P/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})}\quad\text{with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}$}.

Proof.

Let

Lemma A.13

(Upper tail for the maximum)
.

Let

be independent. We have the upper tail

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\max_{i\in[d]}|Z_{i}|\geq\sqrt{2\log d}+s\right)\leq 2e^{-s^{2}/2},\quad\forall s\geq 0.

Proof.

For notational simplicity, put

.
By union bound and the Chernoff bound, we have for each

,

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}(Z^{*}\geq s)=\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(\bigvee_{i=1}^{d}Z_{i}\geq s\right)\leq d\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}(Z_{1}\geq s)\leq d\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}e^{\theta Z_{1}}}{e^{\theta s}}=de^{\theta^{2}/2-\theta s}.

Choose

to minimize the RHS, and we obtain

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}(Z^{*}\geq s)\leq e^{\log d-s^{2}/2}.

Replace

and this becomes

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(Z^{*}\geq\sqrt{2\log d}+s\right)\leq\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left(Z^{*}\geq\sqrt{2\log d+s^{2}}\right)\leq e^{-s^{2}/2}.

Use the fact

and we complete the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\qquad\pm 20\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{3}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{3}}\eta\hat{M}_{Z}O_{\phi}(\eta\hat{M}_{Z})\pm 4\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|}\eta\hat{M}_{Z}O_{\phi}(\eta\hat{M}_{Z})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\frac{1+4\eta\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}-2\eta\rho+2\eta\varepsilon_{v}}{1-2\eta\rho}\frac{2\eta\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{>P},\bm{Z}_{>P}\right\rangle}{(1-2\eta\rho)\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}+\frac{2\eta\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{\leq P},\bm{Z}_{\leq P}\right\rangle}{(1-2\eta\rho)\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\pm O_{\phi}\left(\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|}\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\right).

Finally, consider the third term

\textnormal{{T}}_{3}:=-\frac{\left(1+4\eta\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}-2\eta\rho+2\eta\varepsilon_{v}\right)\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left(1-2\eta\rho\right)\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\frac{\xi_{>P}}{\left\|\hat{\bm{v}}_{t+1,>P}\right\|^{2}}+\frac{\xi_{\leq P}}{\left\|\hat{\bm{v}}_{t+1,>P}\right\|^{2}}.

By our previous bounds on
\xi
, we have

|\textnormal{{T}}_{3}|\leq 64\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\frac{C_{\phi}^{4}\eta^{2}d\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}}{\left\|\hat{\bm{v}}_{t+1,>P}\right\|^{2}}+32C_{\phi}^{4}\eta^{2}P\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\leq 100C_{\phi}^{4}\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\left(P\vee\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}d\right).

Combine the above bounds, and we get

\displaystyle\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{t+1,\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{t+1,>P}\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\left(1+4\eta\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}+2\eta\varepsilon_{v}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\frac{1+4\eta\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}-2\eta\rho+2\eta\varepsilon_{v}}{1-2\eta\rho}\frac{2\eta\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{>P},\bm{Z}_{>P}\right\rangle}{(1-2\eta\rho)\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}+\frac{2\eta\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{\leq P},\bm{Z}_{\leq P}\right\rangle}{(1-2\eta\rho)\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\pm\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}30C_{\phi}^{4}\eta^{2}\pm 1000C_{\phi}^{4}\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|}\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\left(1\vee\eta d\hat{M}_{Z}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\pm 100C_{\phi}^{4}\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\left(P\vee\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}d\right).

Let
H_{t+1}
denote the second line and
\xi_{t+1}
denote the last two lines. Recall our induction
hypothesis
\left\|{\bm{v}}_{t,\leq P}\right\|^{2}/\left\|{\bm{v}}_{t,>P}\right\|^{2}=\Theta(1)(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}\left\|{\bm{v}}_{0,\leq P}\right\|^{2}/\left\|{\bm{v}}_{0,>P}\right\|^{2}=\Theta((1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}P/d)
. Meanwhile,
note that
\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|}\leq\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{d}{P}}

Then, we compute

\displaystyle|\xi_{t+1}|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\phi}(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}\frac{P}{d}\eta^{2}+(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}\frac{P}{d}\sqrt{\frac{d}{P}}\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\left(1\vee\eta d\hat{M}_{Z}\right)+\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}P

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\phi}(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\left(\sqrt{Pd}\eta\hat{M}_{Z}\vee P\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\phi}(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}P

Then, consider
H_{t+1}
. We have

\displaystyle|H_{t+1}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\frac{1+4\eta\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}-2\eta\rho+2\eta\varepsilon_{v}}{1-2\eta\rho}\frac{2\eta\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{>P},\bm{Z}_{>P}\right\rangle}{(1-2\eta\rho)\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\right|+\left|\frac{2\eta\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{\leq P},\bm{Z}_{\leq P}\right\rangle}{(1-2\eta\rho)\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}\frac{P}{d}\eta\left|\left\langle\overline{{\bm{v}}_{>P}},\bm{Z}_{>P}\right\rangle\right|+\left((1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}\frac{P}{d}\right)^{1/2}\eta\left|\left\langle\overline{{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}},\bm{Z}_{\leq P}\right\rangle\right|.

Since both
\left|\left\langle\overline{{\bm{v}}_{>P}},\bm{Z}_{>P}\right\rangle\right|
and
\left|\left\langle\overline{{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}},\bm{Z}_{\leq P}\right\rangle\right|

are conditionally
(P,\theta)
-subweibull,
H_{t+1}
is conditionally

\left(O_{\phi}((1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}\frac{P}{d}),\theta\right)
-subweibull.
∎

With the above formula, we can now use Lemma
4.1
to analyze the dynamics
of the ratio of the norms.

Lemma B.4

(Learning the subspace)
.

Suppose that

P\gg_{\phi}\log^{2}d\quad\text{and}\quad\eta\lesssim_{\phi}\frac{1}{dP\log d}\left(\frac{P^{2}}{\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}}\wedge\frac{P}{M_{Z}^{2}\log^{\theta+1}(d/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})}\right)=\tilde{\Theta}_{\phi}\left(\frac{1}{dP}\right).

Then, throughout Stage 1.1, we have

\frac{(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}}{2}\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{0,\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{0,>P}\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{3(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}}{2}\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{0,\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{0,>P}\right\|^{2}},

and Stage 1.1 takes at most
(1+o(1))(4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{-1}\log\left(d/P\right)=\tilde{O}_{\phi}\left(dP\right)
iterations.
For this result to hold for the
P
good neurons (satisfying (
9
)), it suffices to replace

\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
with
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}/P
.

Proof.

First, by Lemma
B.3
, we have for any
t\leq T
,

\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{t+1,\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{t+1,>P}\right\|^{2}}=\frac{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}}\left(1+4\eta\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}+2\eta\varepsilon_{v}\right)+H_{t+1}+\xi_{t+1},

where
\varepsilon_{v}:=\sum_{l\geq L}l\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|_{l}^{l}/\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}
, where

(H_{t+1})_{t}
is a martingale difference sequence that is conditionally

\left(O_{\phi}((1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}\frac{P}{d}),\theta\right)
-subweibull, and

(\xi_{t})_{t}
is an adapted process with

|\xi_{t+1}|\lesssim_{\phi}(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta)^{t}\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}P
for all
t\in[T]

with probability at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
.
By our induction hypothesis
v_{p}^{2}\leq\log^{2}d/P
, we have

0\leq\varepsilon_{v}:=\frac{1}{\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}}\sum_{l\geq L}l\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{P}v_{k}^{l}\leq\sum_{l\geq L}l\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|_{\infty}^{l-2}\leq\frac{C_{\phi}^{2}\log^{L-2}d}{P^{L/2-1}}=:\delta_{v}.

In particular, note that
\delta_{v}
does not depend on
t
and is
o(1)
. For notational simplicity, let

X_{t}:=\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\leq P}\right\|^{2}/\left\|{\bm{v}}_{>P}\right\|^{2}
,
x_{t}^{-}=(1+4\eta)^{t}x_{0}
and

x_{t}^{+}=(1+4\eta(1+\delta_{v}))^{t}x_{0}
.

x^{\pm}
will serve as the
lower and upper bounds for the deterministic counterpart of
X
, since

\displaystyle\left(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta\right)X_{t}+\xi_{t+1}+H_{t+1}\leq X_{t+1}\leq\left(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta(1+\delta_{v})\right)X_{t}+\xi_{t+1}+H_{t+1}.

Moreover, note that for any
t\leq T
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{x^{+}_{t}}{x^{-}_{t}}=\left(\frac{1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta(1+\delta_{v})}{1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta}\right)^{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(1+4\eta(1+\delta_{v})\right)\left(1-4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta\pm O_{\phi}(\eta^{2})\right)\right)^{t}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+4\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta\delta_{v}\pm O_{\phi}(\eta^{2})\right)^{t}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(O_{\phi}(1)\eta T\left(\delta_{v}+\eta\right)\right).

Since
T\lesssim_{\phi}\log d/\eta
, the above implies

1\leq\frac{x^{+}_{t}}{x^{-}_{t}}\leq\exp\left(O_{\phi}(1)\log d\left(\delta_{v}+\eta\right)\right)\leq 1+O_{\phi}(1)\log d\left(\delta_{v}+\eta\right)=1+o(1),

where the last (approximate) identity holds whenever

\delta_{v}\ll\frac{1}{\log d}\quad\Leftarrow\quad\frac{C_{\phi}^{2}\log^{L-2}d}{P^{L/2-1}}\ll\frac{1}{\log d}\quad\Leftarrow\quad P\gg_{\phi}\log^{2}d.

In particular, this implies that the (multiplicative) difference between
x^{+}_{t}
and
x^{-}_{t}
is small.
Now, we apply Lemma
4.1
to
X_{t}
. In our case, we have

\Xi\lesssim_{\phi}\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}P,\quad\sigma_{Z}^{2}\lesssim_{\phi}\eta^{2}\frac{P}{d}M_{Z}^{2},

\alpha=4(1+o(1))\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}\eta
and
X_{0}=\Theta(P/d)
. Recall that
T\lesssim_{\phi}\log d/\eta
.
Hence, to meet the conditions of Lemma
4.1
, it suffices to choose

\displaystyle\eta^{2}P\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\lesssim_{\phi}\frac{X_{0}}{T}

\displaystyle\quad\Leftarrow\quad\eta\lesssim_{\phi}\frac{1}{d\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\log d},

\displaystyle\eta^{2}\frac{P}{d}M_{Z}^{2}\lesssim_{\phi}\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{T\log^{\theta+1}(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})}

\displaystyle\quad\Leftarrow\quad\eta\lesssim_{\phi}\frac{P}{dM_{Z}^{2}\log d\log^{\theta+1}(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})}.

Then, by Lemma
4.1
,
we have, with probability at least
1-\Theta(\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})
,
0.5x^{-}_{t}\leq X_{t}\leq 1.5x^{+}_{t}
. Since
x^{+}_{t}=(1+o(1))x^{-}_{t}
, this implies
0.5x_{t}\leq X_{t}\leq 2x_{t}
. To complete the proof, it suffices to note that
for
x_{t}
to grow from
\Theta(P/d)
to
1
, the number of iterations needed is bounded by

(1+o(1))(4\phi_{2}^{2}\eta)^{-1}\log\left(d/P\right)
.
∎

B.1.2
Preservation of the gap

Now, we show that the gap between the largest coordinate and the second-largest coordinate can be preserved
in Stage 1.1. Let
p=\argmax_{i\in[P]}v_{i}^{2}(0)
and consider the ratio
v_{q}^{2}/v_{p}^{2}
, where
q\in[P]
is
arbitrary. The proof is conceptually very similar to the previous one, except that we will use
Lemma
E.3
instead of Lemma
4.1
.

Lemma B.5
.

For
p=\argmax_{i\in[P]}v_{i}^{2}(0)
and any
q\in[P]
,
we have

\frac{v_{t+1,q}^{2}}{v_{t+1,p}^{2}}\leq\frac{v_{t,q}^{2}}{v_{t,p}^{2}}+H_{t+1}+\xi_{t+1},

where
(H_{t+1})_{t}
is a martingale difference sequence that is conditionally

\left(O_{\phi}(\eta^{2}dM_{Z}^{2}),\theta\right)
-subweibull,
and
(\xi_{t})_{t}
is an adapted process that is uniformly bounded by
O_{\phi}(\eta^{2}d\hat{M}_{Z}^{2})
with probability
at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
.

Proof.

For notational simplicity, define

\rho_{t,q/p}:=v_{t,q}^{2}/v_{t,p}^{2},

Our goal is to upper bound
\rho_{t,q/p}
.
Recall from Lemma
B.1
that for any
k\leq P
, we have

\hat{v}_{t+1,k}^{2}=v_{t,k}^{2}+2\eta\gamma_{t,k}v_{t,k}^{2}+2\eta v_{t,k}Z_{t+1,k}+\underbrace{\eta^{2}\gamma_{t,k}^{2}v_{t,k}^{2}+\eta^{2}Z_{t+1,k}^{2}+2\eta^{2}\gamma_{t,k}^{2}v_{t,k}Z_{t+1,k}}_{=:\;\xi_{t+1,k}},

where
\gamma_{k,t}:=2\hat{\phi}_{2}^{2}+L\hat{\phi}_{L}^{2}v_{k}^{L-2}+\sum_{l>L}l\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}v_{k}^{l-2}-\rho
.
Then, we compute

\displaystyle\rho_{t+1,q/p}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,q}\right)v_{t,q}^{2}+2\eta v_{t,q}Z_{t+1,q}+\xi_{t+1,q}}{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\right)v_{t,p}^{2}+2\eta v_{t,p}Z_{t+1,p}+\xi_{t+1,p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,q}\right)v_{t,q}^{2}}{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\right)v_{t,p}^{2}}-\frac{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,q}\right)v_{t,q}^{2}}{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\right)v_{t,p}^{2}}\frac{2\eta v_{t,p}Z_{t+1,p}}{\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}}+\frac{2\eta v_{t,q}Z_{t+1,q}}{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\right)v_{t,p}^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\xi_{t+1,q}}{\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}}-\frac{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,q}\right)v_{t,q}^{2}}{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\right)v_{t,p}^{2}}\frac{\xi_{t+1,p}}{\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}}-\frac{2\eta v_{t,q}Z_{t+1,q}}{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\right)v_{t,p}^{2}}\frac{2\eta v_{t,p}Z_{t+1,p}+\xi_{t+1,p}}{\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}}

\displaystyle=:\textnormal{{T}}_{1}(\rho_{t+1,q/p})+\textnormal{{T}}_{2}(\rho_{t+1,q/p})+\textnormal{{T}}_{3}(\rho_{t+1,q/p}),

where
\textnormal{{T}}_{1}
contains the first term (signal term),
\textnormal{{T}}_{2}
contains the next two terms (approximate martingale difference terms),
and
\textnormal{{T}}_{3}
contains the last line (higher order error terms).

First, for the first term, we compute

\displaystyle\textnormal{{T}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\rho_{t,q/p}\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,q}\right)\left(1-2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\left(1-\frac{2\eta\gamma_{t,p}}{1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\rho_{t,q/p}\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,q}\right)\left(1-2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\pm 5\eta^{2}\gamma_{t,p}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho_{t,q/p}\left(1+2\eta\left(\gamma_{t,q}-\gamma_{t,p}\right)\pm 20\eta^{2}\left(\gamma_{t,p}^{2}\vee\gamma_{t,q}^{2}\right)\right).

Recall that
|\gamma_{t,k}|\leq 2C_{\phi}^{2}
and note that

\gamma_{t,q}-\gamma_{t,p}=\sum_{l\geq L}l\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}v_{q}^{l-2}-\sum_{l\geq L}l\hat{\phi}_{l}^{2}v_{p}^{l-2}\leq 0.

Hence,

\textnormal{{T}}_{1}\leq\rho_{t,q/p}\left(1+80C_{\phi}^{4}\eta^{2}\right).

Now, consider the martingale difference term

\textnormal{{T}}_{2}:=-\rho_{t,q/p}\frac{1+2\eta\gamma_{t,q}}{1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}}\frac{2\eta v_{t,p}Z_{t+1,p}}{\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}}+\frac{2\eta v_{t,q}Z_{t+1,q}}{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\right)v_{t,p}^{2}}.

We rewrite the denominator as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v_{t,p}^{2}+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}v_{t,p}^{2}+2\eta v_{t,p}Z_{t+1,p}+\xi_{t+1,p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v_{t,p}^{2}+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}v_{t,p}^{2}}-\frac{1}{v_{t,p}^{2}+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}v_{t,p}^{2}}\frac{2\eta v_{t,p}Z_{t+1,p}+\xi_{t+1,p}}{\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}}.

By (
12
), with probability at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}/d^{C}
, we have

|\xi_{p,t+1}|\lesssim_{\phi}\eta^{2}v_{t,p}^{2}+\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}+\eta^{2}|v_{t,p}|\hat{M}_{Z}\lesssim_{\phi}\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}.

Therefore,

\frac{1}{\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}}=\frac{1}{v_{t,p}^{2}+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}v_{t,p}^{2}}\pm O_{\phi}\left(\frac{1}{v_{t,p}^{2}}\frac{\eta\hat{M}_{Z}}{v_{t,p}}\right).

Then, we can rewrite
\textnormal{{T}}_{2}
as

\displaystyle\textnormal{{T}}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\rho_{t,q/p}\frac{1+2\eta\gamma_{t,q}}{1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}}\frac{2\eta v_{t,p}Z_{t+1,p}}{v_{t,p}^{2}+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}v_{t,p}^{2}}+\frac{2\eta v_{t,q}Z_{t+1,q}}{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\right)v_{t,p}^{2}}\pm O_{\phi}\left(\eta^{2}\frac{\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}}{v_{t,p}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=:H_{t+1}\pm O_{\phi}\left(\eta^{2}d\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\right).

Note that
H_{t+1}
is a martingale difference term with

|H_{t+1}|\lesssim_{\phi}\frac{\eta|Z_{t+1,p}|}{v_{t,p}}+\frac{\eta v_{t,q}|Z_{t+1,q}|}{v_{t,p}^{2}}\lesssim_{\phi}\eta\sqrt{d}\left(|Z_{t+1,p}|+|Z_{t+1,q}|\right).

Since
Z_{t+1,p}
and
Z_{t+1,q}
are both conditionally
(M_{Z}^{2},\theta)
-subweibull,
H_{t+1}
is
conditionally
\left(O_{\phi}(\eta^{2}dM_{Z}^{2}),\theta\right)
-subweibull.
Finally, consider

\textnormal{{T}}_{3}:=\frac{\xi_{t+1,q}}{\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}}-\frac{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,q}\right)v_{t,q}^{2}}{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\right)v_{t,p}^{2}}\frac{\xi_{t+1,p}}{\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}}-\frac{2\eta v_{t,q}Z_{t+1,q}}{\left(1+2\eta\gamma_{t,p}\right)v_{t,p}^{2}}\frac{2\eta v_{t,p}Z_{t+1,p}+\xi_{t+1,p}}{\hat{v}_{t+1,p}^{2}}.

Since
|\xi_{q,t+1}|\vee|\xi_{p,t+1}|\leq\eta^{2}\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}
and

|Z_{t+1,p}|\vee|Z_{t+1,q}|\leq\hat{M}_{Z}
, we have

|\textnormal{{T}}_{3}|\lesssim_{\phi}\eta^{2}d\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}.

Combining the above bounds, we get

\rho_{t+1,q/p}\leq\rho_{t,q/p}\left(1+80C_{\phi}^{4}\eta^{2}\right)+H_{t+1}+O_{\phi}\left(\eta^{2}d\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\right)=\rho_{t,q/p}+H_{t+1}+O_{\phi}\left(\eta^{2}d\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\right).

∎

Lemma B.6

(Preservation of the gap)
.

Consider
\delta_{c}\in(0,1)
,
p=\argmax_{i\in[P]}v_{i}^{2}(0)
and any
q\in[P]
.
Suppose that

\eta\lesssim_{\phi}\frac{\delta_{c}^{2}}{dP\log d}\left(\frac{P}{\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}}\wedge\frac{P}{M_{Z}^{2}\log^{\theta+1}(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})}\right)=\tilde{\Theta}_{\phi}\left(\frac{\delta_{c}}{dP}\right).

Then, we have

\sup_{t\leq T}\left(\frac{v_{t,q}^{2}}{v_{t,p}^{2}}-\frac{v_{0,q}^{2}}{v_{0,p}^{2}}\right)\leq\delta_{c}\quad\text{with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}$.}

Proof.

By Lemma
B.5
, we have

\frac{v_{t+1,q}^{2}}{v_{t+1,p}^{2}}\leq\frac{v_{t,q}^{2}}{v_{t,p}^{2}}+H_{t+1}+\xi_{t+1},

where
(H_{t+1})_{t}
is a martingale difference sequence that is conditionally

\left(O_{\phi}(\eta^{2}dM_{Z}^{2}),\theta\right)
-subweibull,
and
(\xi_{t})_{t}
is an adapted process that is uniformly bounded by
O_{\phi}(\eta^{2}d\hat{M}_{Z}^{2})
with probability
at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
. Hence, by Lemma
E.3
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\leq T}\left(\frac{v_{t,q}^{2}}{v_{t,p}^{2}}-\frac{v_{0,q}^{2}}{v_{0,p}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\phi}T\eta^{2}d\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}+\sqrt{\eta^{2}dM_{Z}^{2}T\log^{\theta+1}(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\phi}\eta d\hat{M}_{Z}^{2}\log d+\sqrt{\eta dM_{Z}^{2}\log^{\theta+1}(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})\log d}.

\displaystyle\sqrt{\eta dM_{Z}^{2}\log^{\theta+1}(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})\log d}.\lesssim_{\phi}\delta_{c}

\displaystyle\quad\Leftarrow\quad\eta\lesssim_{\phi}\frac{\delta_{c}^{2}}{dM_{Z}^{2}\log^{\theta+1}(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})\log d}.

B.1.3
Other induction hypotheses

In this subsection, we verify the induction hypothesis:

for all

. This condition is used
to ensure the influence of the higher-order term is small compared to the influence of the second-order terms.

Lemma B.7

(Upper bound on

)
.

Suppose that

First, by Lemma

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\eta^{2}\gamma_{t,p}^{2}v_{t,p}^{2}+\eta^{2}Z_{t+1,p}^{2}+2\eta^{2}\gamma_{t,p}^{2}v_{t,p}Z_{t+1,p}}_{=:\xi_{t+1}}

.

Under this induction hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle\quad\Leftarrow\quad\eta\lesssim_{\phi}\frac{\log d}{dM_{Z}^{2}\log^{\theta+1}(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})},

Lemma C.1
.

Suppose that for each

{\bm{v}}_{i_{p}}

Choose one

p\in[P]

Then, we set the

\displaystyle\left(f_{*}({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{x}};\bm{a}_{*},\bm{V})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{P}\left(\phi(x_{k})-\phi({\bm{v}}_{i_{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=1}^{P}\left(\phi(x_{k})-\phi({\bm{v}}_{i_{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\right)\left(\phi(x_{l})-\phi({\bm{v}}_{i_{l}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\right).

Hence, for
k=l

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left(\phi(x_{k})-\phi({\bm{v}}_{i_{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\phi^{2}(x_{k})+\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\phi^{2}({\bm{v}}_{i_{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}})-2\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\phi(x_{k})\phi({\bm{v}}_{i_{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{i}^{2}\left(1-\left\langle\bm{e}_{k},{\bm{v}}_{i_{k}}\right\rangle^{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{\phi}^{2}\varepsilon_{v}.

Meanwhile, for
k\neq l

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left(\phi(x_{k})-\phi({\bm{v}}_{i_{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\right)\left(\phi(x_{l})-\phi({\bm{v}}_{i_{l}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\phi(x_{k})\phi(x_{l})+\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\phi({\bm{v}}_{i_{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\phi({\bm{v}}_{i_{l}}\cdot{\bm{x}})-\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\phi(x_{k})\phi({\bm{v}}_{i_{l}}\cdot{\bm{x}})-\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\phi({\bm{v}}_{i_{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}})\phi(x_{l})

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{i}^{2}\left(\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{i_{k}},{\bm{v}}_{i_{l}}\right\rangle^{i}-v_{i_{l},k}^{i}-v_{i_{k},l}^{i}\right).

Lemma C.2

Suppose that for each

Proof.

For notational simplicity, let

\hat{D}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{{\bm{x}}_{T+n}}
denote the empirical
distribution of the samples we use in Stage 2. In addition, we write
f_{\bm{a}}
for

Let

\hat{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

Choose this

be our hypothesis

\displaystyle\left\|f_{\hat{\bm{a}}}-f_{*}\right\|_{L^{1}(D)}

\displaystyle=\left(\left\|f_{\hat{\bm{a}}}-f_{*}\right\|_{L^{1}(D)}-\left\|f_{\hat{\bm{a}}}-f_{*}\right\|_{L^{1}(\hat{D})}\right)+\left\|f_{\hat{\bm{a}}}-f_{*}\right\|_{L^{1}(\hat{D})}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\bm{a}\,:\,\left\|\bm{a}\right\|\leq\left\|\bm{a}_{*}\right\|}\left(\left\|f_{\bm{a}}-f_{*}\right\|_{L^{1}(D)}-\left\|f_{\bm{a}}-f_{*}\right\|_{L^{1}(\hat{D})}\right)+\left\|f_{\bm{a}_{*}}-f_{*}\right\|_{L^{2}(\hat{D})},

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}_{\bm{X}}\left[\sup_{\bm{a}\,:\,\left\|\bm{a}\right\|\leq\left\|\bm{a}_{*}\right\|}\left(\left\|f_{\bm{a}}-f_{*}\right\|_{L^{1}(D)}-\left\|f_{\bm{a}}-f_{*}\right\|_{L^{1}(\hat{D})}\right)\right]

Appendix E
Stochastic Induction

Our proof is essentially a large induction: When certain properties hold, we know how to analyze the dynamics and
can show certain quantities are bounded with high probability. Meanwhile, certain properties hold as long as those
quantities are still well-controlled. In the deterministic setting, this seemingly looped argument can be made
formal by either mathematical induction (in discrete time) or the continuity argument (in continuous time).
In this subsection, we show the same can also be done in the presence of randomness and derive a stochastic
version of Gronwall’s lemma and its generalizations.

We start with an example where Doob’s submartingale inequality can be directly used.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t},\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}})
be our filtered probability space and
(Z_{t})_{t}
be a martingale difference sequence.
Suppose that
\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Z_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
is uniformly bounded by
\sigma_{Z}^{2}
. Then, by Doob’s submartingale inequality,
for any
M>0
and
T>0
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left[\sup_{t\leq T}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right|\geq M\right]\leq M^{-2}\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{T}Z_{s}\right)^{2}=\frac{T\sigma_{Z}^{2}}{M^{2}}.

In particular, this implies that when
M=\omega(\sigma_{Z}\sqrt{T})
, we have

\sup_{t\leq T}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right|\leq M
with high probability.

Note that there is no need to do any kind of “induction” in the above example because of the unconditional uniform
bound on
\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Z_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
.
However, things become subtle if instead of assuming
\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Z_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
is always bounded by
\sigma_{Z}^{2}
, we
assume it to be bounded by
\sigma_{Z}^{2}
when
\sup_{s\leq t}\left|\sum_{r=1}^{s}Z_{r}\right|\leq M
. Intuitively, since

M
is chosen so that
\sup_{t\leq T}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right|\leq M
holds with high probability, the bounds

\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Z_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq\sigma_{Z}^{2}
should also hold with high probability and we can still use
Doob’s submartingale inequality as before. Now, we formalize this argument.

Lemma E.1
.

Let
(Z_{t})_{t}
be a martingale difference sequence. Suppose that there exists
M,\sigma_{Z}>0
such that
if
\sup_{s\leq t}\left|\sum_{r=1}^{s}Z_{s}\right|\leq M
, then we have
\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Z_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq\sigma_{Z}^{2}
.
Then, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left[\sup_{t\leq T}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right|>M\right]\leq\frac{T\sigma_{Z}^{2}}{M^{2}}.

Note that this bound is the same as the one we obtained with the assumption that

\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Z_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq\sigma_{Z}^{2}
always holds.

Proof.

Consider the stopping time
\tau:=\inf\{t\geq 0\;:\;\left|\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right|>M\}
.
By definition, we have
\sup_{s\leq t}\left|\sum_{r=1}^{s}Z_{s}\right|\leq M
for all
t\leq\tau
.
Then, we define
Y_{t+1}=Z_{t+1}\mathbbm{1}\{t<\tau\}
. Note that
(Y_{t})
is a martingale difference sequence
with
\mathop{\mathbb{E\/}}[Y_{t+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq\sigma_{Z}^{2}
. As a result, by Doob’s submartingale inequality, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left[\sup_{t\leq T}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{t}Y_{s}\right|>M\right]\leq T\sigma_{Z}^{2}/M^{2}.

To relate it to
(Z_{t})_{t}
, we compute

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left[\sup_{t\leq T}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right|>M\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left[\sup_{t\leq T}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right|>M\wedge\tau\leq T\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left[\left|\sum_{s=1}^{\tau}Z_{s}\right|>M\wedge\tau\leq T\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}\left[\left|\sum_{s=1}^{\tau}Y_{s}\right|>M\wedge\tau\leq T\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T\sigma_{Z}^{2}}{M^{2}},

where the first and second identities comes from the definition of
\tau
and the third from the fact
Z_{t}=Y_{t}

for all
t\leq\tau
.
∎

Now, we consider a more complicated case, where the process of interest is not a pure martingale. Suppose that the
process
(X_{t})_{t}
satisfies

X_{t+1}=(1+\alpha)X_{t}+\xi_{t+1}+Z_{t+1},\quad X_{0}=x_{0}>0,

where the signal growth rate
\alpha>0
and initialization
x_{0}>0
are given and fixed,
(\xi_{t})_{t}
is an
adapted process, and
(Z_{t})_{t}
is a martingale difference sequence. In most cases,
(\xi_{t})_{t}
will represent the
higher-order error terms.

Our goal is control the difference between
X_{t}
and its deterministic counterpart

x_{t}=(1+\alpha)^{t}x_{0}
.
To this end, we recursively expand the RHS to obtain

\displaystyle X_{t+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\alpha)^{2}X_{t-1}+(1+\alpha)\xi_{t}+\xi_{t+1}+(1+\alpha)Z_{t}+Z_{t+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\alpha)^{t+1}x_{0}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1+\alpha)^{t-s}\xi_{s+1}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1+\alpha)^{t-s}Z_{s+1}.

Divide both sides with
(1+\alpha)^{t+1}
and replace
t+1
with
t
. Then, the above becomes

X_{t}(1+\alpha)^{-t}=x_{0}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1+\alpha)^{-s}\xi_{s}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1+\alpha)^{-s}Z_{s}.

Note that
\left((1+\alpha)^{-t}Z_{t}\right)_{t}
is still a martingale difference sequence. Ideally,
|\xi_{t}|
should
be small as it represents the higher-order error terms, and we have bounds on the conditional variance of

Z_{t}
so that we can apply Doob’s submartingale inequality to the last term. Unfortunately, in many cases,
since
\xi_{t+1}
and
Z_{t+1}
, particularly their maximum and (conditional) variance, can potentially depend on

(X_{s})_{s\leq t}
, we may only be able to assume
|\xi_{t+1}|\leq(1+\alpha)^{t}\Xi
with probability at least
1-\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}},\xi}

(for each

that is bounded by
2M
and has conditional variance bounded by
C_{\theta}
for some
C_{\theta}>0
. Therefore,
by Bernstein’s inequality, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}(|\textnormal{{T}}_{1}|\geq K)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(K/\sqrt{T})^{2}}{C_{\theta}+2M}\right).

For the RHS to be bounded by
\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
, it suffices to require

K\geq\sqrt{T}\sqrt{\left(C_{\theta}+2M\right)\log\left(2/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\right)}

Finally, combining the above analysis, we obtain

\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\right|\leq 4CMT\exp\left(-M^{1/\theta}/2\right)+\sqrt{T}\sqrt{\left(C_{\theta}+2M\right)\log\left(2/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\right)},

with probability at least
1-2\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}
, where
M\geq\log^{\theta}\left(CT/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\right)
and

M/\log^{\theta}M\geq\left(\frac{2(\theta-1)}{\theta}\right)^{\theta}
.
Now, we simplify the RHS as follows.
Note that

4CMT\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}M^{1/\theta}\right)\leq\sqrt{T}\sqrt{2M\log\left(2/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\right)}\\
\Leftarrow\quad\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\log M-\frac{1}{2}M^{1/\theta}\right)\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\log\left(2/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\right)}}{4C\sqrt{T}}\\
\Leftarrow\quad\frac{M}{\log^{\theta}M}\geq 2^{\theta},\quad M\geq 4^{\theta}\log^{\theta}\left(\frac{8C^{2}T}{\log\left(2/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\right)}\right).

In other words, we can choose

M=\Theta_{\theta}\left(\log^{\theta}(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}})\right),

and obtain

\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\right|\lesssim_{\theta}\sqrt{T\log^{\theta+1}\left(T/\delta_{\mathop{\mathbb{P\/}}}\right)}.

Finally, for general
\sigma>0
, it suffices to note that if
X
is
(\sigma^{2},\theta)
-subweibull, then
X/\sigma
is

(1,\theta)
-subweibull.

∎

Proof of Lemma
E.4
.

First, we consider the upper bound on
T
. We compute

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{x_{t+1}-x_{t}}{x_{t}^{p}}=\frac{x_{t+1}^{p}}{x_{t}^{p}}\frac{x_{t+1}-x_{t}}{x_{t+1}^{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{t+1}^{p}}{x_{t}^{p}}\int_{x_{t}}^{x_{t+1}}\frac{1}{x_{t+1}^{p}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{x_{t+1}^{p}}{x_{t}^{p}}\int_{x_{t}}^{x_{t+1}}\frac{1}{y^{p}}\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{x_{t+1}^{p}}{x_{t}^{p}}\frac{1}{p-1}\left(\frac{1}{x_{t}^{p-1}}-\frac{1}{x_{t+1}^{p-1}}\right).

In addition, note that

x_{t+1}^{p}/x_{t}^{p}=\left(1+\alpha x_{t}^{p-1}\right)^{p}\leq\left(1+\alpha\right)^{p}\leq e^{\alpha p}.

Therefore,

\alpha\leq\frac{e^{\alpha p}}{p-1}\left(\frac{1}{x_{t}^{p-1}}-\frac{1}{x_{t+1}^{p-1}}\right)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{x_{t+1}^{p-1}}\leq\frac{1}{x_{t}^{p-1}}-\frac{(p-1)\alpha}{e^{\alpha p}}.

Sum both sides from
0
to
t-1
and we get

\frac{1}{x_{t}^{p-1}}\leq\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}-\frac{t(p-1)\alpha}{e^{\alpha p}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad x_{t}\geq\left(\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}-e^{-\alpha p}(p-1)\alpha t\right)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}.

In particular, this implies

T\leq\left(\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}-1\right)\frac{e^{\alpha p}}{(p-1)\alpha}.

Now, we consider the upper bound on
\sum_{t}x_{t}
. Let
(\tilde{x}_{h})_{h}
be the solution to the continuous-time ODE

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\tilde{x}_{h}=\tilde{x}_{h}^{p}
with
\tilde{x}_{0}=x_{0}
. Note that
\tilde{x}
is increasing and
therefore

\tilde{x}_{(t+1)\alpha}=\tilde{x}_{t\alpha}+\int_{0}^{\alpha}\tilde{x}_{t\alpha+r}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}r\geq\tilde{x}_{t\alpha}+\alpha a\tilde{x}_{t\alpha}^{p}.

Hence, by induction, we have
\tilde{x}_{t\alpha}\geq x_{t}
for all
t
.
In addition,
\tilde{x}_{h}
has the closed-form formula:

\tilde{x}_{h}=\left(\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}-(p-1)h\right)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}x_{t}\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{x}_{t\alpha}\leq\alpha^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\int_{t\alpha}^{(t+1)\alpha}\tilde{x}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{T\alpha}\tilde{x}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}h

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{T\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}-(p-1)h\right)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\,\mathrm{d}h.

When
p=2
, we have

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}x_{t}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{T\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{x_{0}}-h\right)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}h=\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-x_{0}T\alpha}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-e^{2\alpha}+x_{0}e^{2\alpha}}\right)\leq\frac{2}{\alpha},

as long as
\alpha\lesssim x_{0}
so that
\tilde{x}_{T\alpha}=O(1)
.
When
p>2
, to have
\tilde{x}_{T\alpha}\leq 2
, it suffices to have

\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}-(p-1)\alpha T\geq\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}\quad\Leftarrow\quad e^{\alpha p}-\frac{e^{\alpha p}-1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}\geq\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}\quad\Leftarrow\quad\alpha\lesssim x_{0}^{p-1}/p.

Let
\tilde{T}
be the time
\tilde{x}_{h}
reaches
2
. We have
\tilde{T}\leq\frac{1}{(p-1)x_{0}^{p-1}}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}x_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\tilde{T}}\left(\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}-(p-1)h\right)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\,\mathrm{d}h

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{1}{p-2}\left(\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}-(p-1)\tilde{T}\right)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left((p-1)\tilde{T}+\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}\left(-1+\left(\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\left(\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-1}}-(p-1)T\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{(p-2)\alpha}\frac{1}{x_{0}^{p-2}}.

∎
