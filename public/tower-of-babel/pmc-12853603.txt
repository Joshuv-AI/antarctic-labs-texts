Improving body image in female Chinese social media users with eating disorder symptoms: a randomized controlled trial of two online self-guided single-session interventions
Authors: Yunyi Cheng, Yuhan Chen, Wesley R. Barnhart, Chun Chen, See Heng Yim, Jason M. Nagata, Feng Ji, Jinbo He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial media use is a risk factor for eating and body image disturbances. The overlap between social media and eating and body image disturbances is particularly concerning in the Chinese context given an estimated billion active social media users in China, especially among females. This highlights the need for scalable, culturally adapted prevention and intervention strategies. This study developed and evaluated two online, self-guided, single-session interventions (SSIs), including a Media Literacy Intervention (MLI) and a Body Functionality-focused expressive writing Intervention (BFI), alongside waitlist controls, and examined their ability to improve body image among adult female Chinese social media users with eating disorder (ED) symptoms.MethodsA total of 204 female social media users with ED symptoms were recruited via Xiaohongshu (Little Red Note) and randomized to the MLI (n = 68), BFI (n = 68), or waitlist control group (n = 68). Primary outcomes included measures of negative and positive body image. Secondary outcomes included a range of measures including ED psychopathology and psychological distress. Assessments were conducted at baseline, 1-week post-intervention, and 4 weeks after baseline.ResultsBoth MLI and BFI interventions significantly outperformed the waitlist control on primary and secondary outcomes. The two interventions demonstrated comparable efficacy across most domains, except for eating flexibility, where BFI yielded greater improvements. Intervention uptake was high (93%), and most participants (95%) reported they would recommend the intervention to others.ConclusionsSSIs show promise as accessible, acceptable, and effective tools for improving body image and reducing ED symptoms among Chinese female social media users with ED symptoms. Future research should conduct larger-scale studies to examine their effectiveness and long-term impact.

Introduction

Eating disorders (EDs), such as anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge-eating disorder, are severe mental health conditions characterized by disturbed eating behaviors and excessive preoccupation with body weight and/or body shape [1]. EDs represent a significant public health concern globally, with females being disproportionately affected [2, 3], including within the Chinese context [4]. Particularly, large-scale epidemiological surveys in China report concerning prevalence estimates of approximately 7% of women meeting cut-offs for probable EDs [5, 6]. EDs are often chronic and impairing, commonly co-occurring with anxiety, depression, and suicidality, and are associated with increased risk of medical complications and mortality [7, 8]. Thus, there is an urgent need for effective, scalable interventions that address modifiable risk factors to prevent EDs.

One such factor is body image disturbance, which plays a critical role in both the development and maintenance of EDs [9]. Interventions that improve body image have demonstrated efficacy in reducing ED symptoms, making this a promising target for preventive efforts [10]. Also, social media has emerged as both a risk factor for body image disturbance and EDs and a potential platform for delivering preventive interventions [11, 12]. However, evidence-based programs specifically tailored for Chinese female social media users remain scarce, and there is also limited research comparing the effectiveness of different types of digital interventions for eating and body image disturbances. To address these gaps, the present study developed and evaluated two online, self-guided, single-session interventions (SSIs): one focused on media literacy and the other on body functionality appreciation.

Media literacy interventions

Media literacy interventions (MLI) are educational programs designed to help individuals critically evaluate media content and develop media creation skills, thereby reducing their susceptibility to harmful media influences on body image [9, 13, 14]. The theoretical basis of MLI derives from the Tripartite Influence Model (TIM), which identifies media, peers, and family as primary sources of appearance-based pressures that promote body dissatisfaction through appearance comparisons and internalization of unrealistic beauty ideals [15, 16]. Systematic reviews substantiate this model by confirming that media literacy interventions directly counteract these mechanisms. Specifically, MLI deconstruct idealized media representations to reduce internalization of thin/muscular ideals and mitigate engagement in upward appearance comparisons (i.e., self-evaluation against conventionally attractive/body ideal-aligned targets), thereby diminishing susceptibility to body dissatisfaction and disordered eating [17]. Within social media contexts, these interventions typically include components that foster critical thinking and focus on understanding motivations behind peer and celebrity content curation, including selective self-presentation and image modification practices. Concurrently, peer-focused elements address the peer environment as a risk factor for body dissatisfaction by targeting appearance-related social norms and reducing pressures to conform to unrealistic body image ideals (e.g [9, 14]).

MLI, targeting the reduction of the thin-ideal and promotion of critical engagement with appearance-focused content, have been implemented with diverse populations. For example, previous research [13] conducted a randomized study comparing two active MLI for female undergraduates: an externally oriented (i.e., feminist sociocultural) MLI and an internally oriented (i.e., cognitive) MLI. Both interventions were compared with a video-only control (“Slim Hopes”) and a non-intervention group. Although these interventions produced immediate increases in media skepticism, neither active program yielded significant shifts in body dissatisfaction [13]. Irving and Berel speculated that this disconnect might stem from media literacy primarily fostering cognitive changes (e.g., critical analysis skills) without sufficiently addressing affective body image schemas or core self-evaluative beliefs underlying dissatisfaction [13]. Another illustration of this can be found in research [18] evaluating a school-based, body-image-focused MLI for Indian adolescent females, delivered over four teacher-led modules. This program combined didactic lessons on media influence with interactive activities challenging appearance ideals. Participants demonstrated significant improvements in body satisfaction and media literacy (i.e., greater critical evaluation of media messages) following the intervention, with these gains largely maintained at a three-month follow-up.

Expanding to social media contexts, other research has [19] piloted a three-lesson social MLI for early adolescent girls, integrating a critical analysis of peer and celebrity social media posts alongside components specifically targeting peer-based appearance pressures to dismantle unrealistic beauty standards. Intervention participants showed small to medium improvements in body esteem, dietary restraint, and realism skepticism one-week post-intervention compared to classes-as-usual. Despite these encouraging findings, existing interventions share notable limitations. Primarily, all interventions rely on in-person sessions facilitated by trained professionals or teachers, inhibiting scalability. Furthermore, none of these interventions utilize a self-guided, online, or single-session format, which restricts their accessibility and misaligns with contemporary digital media consumption preferences [13, 18, 19].

Body functionality–focused interventions

Body functionality–focused interventions explicitly shift attention away from appearance and redirect it toward the ways in which the body enables meaningful action, pleasure, and health via functionality. By cultivating appreciation for bodily capabilities (e.g., strength, mobility, sensory experience), these programs aim to counteract appearance-based self-evaluation and promote a more holistic body image [20]. Specifically, body functionality–focused interventions are grounded in self-objectification theory [21], which asserts that a pervasive cultural emphasis on appearance encourages individuals to internalize an observer’s perspective of their bodies, leading to habitual body monitoring and diminished well-being. By shifting attention away from external appearance and redirecting toward intrinsic capacities and experiences, functionality interventions seek to interrupt this objectifying gaze. Positive psychology frameworks [22] further inform these programs by leveraging strengths‑based processes (e.g., gratitude or mindfulness) to foster a more integrated mind–body connection. For example, the “Expand Your Horizon” programme [20] is a cornerstone intervention in this domain, including a one‑week, three‑task online writing intervention prompting women to elaborate on different facets of body functionality (cognitive, emotional, physical). Participants reported significantly greater increases in functional satisfaction, body appreciation, and perceived body complexity, alongside decreases in self-objectification at one-week post-intervention and four-week follow-up, compared to an active control condition.

Building on this functional approach but incorporating a broader positive psychology framework, previous research [23] has developed a brief, single-session intervention combining positive functionality reflections with positive appearance reflections. Participants first contemplated valued or neutral appearance features and then reflected on bodily capabilities (e.g., strength, sensory experiences). While this combined approach yielded immediate, significant boosts in state body satisfaction compared to a control group, a critical limitation emerged: these gains were significantly diminished following exposure to idealized media images (e.g., thin-ideal advertisements). To address this challenge, Mulgrew et al. [24] further developed and evaluated a more intensive multi-session program. This expanded intervention incorporated both functional and aesthetic reflections but also added critical components: media literacy training (deconstructing idealized images and identifying manipulative techniques, such as photo editing) and cognitive restructuring exercises (challenging appearance comparisons and internalized ideals). Participants practiced applying these skills to real-world media across sessions. Although idealized media exposure still had a negative impact on body satisfaction post-intervention, the magnitude of this effect was significantly reduced for participants in the multi-session program compared to the control group.

Despite their demonstrated efficacy, these interventions exhibit limitations that constrain scalability and applicability. Crucially, previous interventions required trained personnel (e.g., researchers or facilitators) to deliver guided sessions, creating implementation barriers related to resource allocation and specialist availability [20, 24]. The multi-session formats, while showing greater resilience in the case of Mulgrew et al. [24], inherently struggle with participant attrition, potentially excluding those most vulnerable to body image disturbances who may lack sustained motivation.

Online self-guided single-session interventions

To address the limitations of traditional interventions, particularly the notable participant burden and attrition rates associated with multi-session formats (e.g [19, 24]), single-session interventions (SSIs) have emerged as a promising alternative, delivering targeted, evidence-based components through condensed therapeutic engagements [25]. These brief yet intensive protocols demonstrate implementation versatility, functioning effectively both as autonomous interventions and adjuncts to comprehensive treatments [26]. Also, interventions delivered via digital means (the Internet, apps, etc.) are low-cost and enable individuals to engage with content at their own pace, provided by online self-guided interventions, making them an appealing option for those who may feel uncomfortable in seeking traditional therapy [27]. In the domain of body image, SSIs often incorporate media-literacy education, functionality-focused exercises, and cognitive restructuring to shift attention away from appearance and redirect it toward the body’s capabilities [20, 28]. Social media platforms, with their visual and interactive affordances, are ideally suited to host these micro-interventions, embedding them directly into digital environments where users already spend significant time, thereby enhancing access and engagement.

Recent investigations have demonstrated the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of SSIs explicitly designed to address negative body image (e.g [11, 29]). In one illustration of this [11], researchers implemented a concise, 7–8-minute SSI delivered via social media platforms, which instructed participants in strategies to mitigate upward social comparisons, regulate maladaptive social media engagement, and resist internalization of the thin ideal, with results showing statistically significant enhancements in body-image satisfaction following intervention completion. Other research [29] evaluated Project Body Neutrality, a 30-minute online SSI comprising self-reflection tasks, psychoeducational content, behavioral activation components, and cognitive restructuring exercises. Results from 75 adolescent participants indicated over 80% program acceptability, with small to moderate improvements in body appreciation alongside reductions in hopelessness. Furthermore, cognitive dissonance–based interventions, most notably the Body Project, have consistently demonstrated efficacy in decreasing thin-ideal internalization and body dissatisfaction [30]. Critically, this intervention paradigm has also been adapted into brief, virtual formats [31, 32]. For example, Stice et al. [31] reported that an Internet-adapted version of the Body Project produced risk-factor reductions comparable to those of the original in-person program. Evidence from subsequent trials conducted across diverse populations and global contexts has further supported the effectiveness and generalizability of these adaptations [33]. These findings suggest that brief, digitally-delivered interventions can meaningfully reduce body dissatisfaction and related risk factors.

Based on this research, there is promise in integrating the core mechanisms of MLI and BFI within the efficient framework of SSIs. However, to our knowledge, culturally adapted SSIs targeting body image remain virtually absent in mainland China despite the documented urgency of eating pathology and body dissatisfaction among its vast population of female social media users. China’s unique sociocultural context and distinct appearance standards necessitate interventions beyond direct translations of Western programs. Existing MLIs and BFIs were developed and validated primarily in Western cohorts (e.g [19, 20]), focusing on thin- and muscular‐ideals prevalent in images on Instagram and Facebook, thereby limiting their applicability to Chinese users who interact with culturally specific content. Chinese users encounter culturally specific appearance pressures, such as the “delicate white skin, palm-sized face” aesthetics, which have proliferated on Douyin and Xiaohongshu (social media sites), and may be uniquely related to body image concerns among Chinese women [34, 35]. Consequently, developing culturally adapted interventions tailored to the Chinese population is both urgent and essential.

The present study

This study adapted and implemented two brief, online, self-guided interventions that have demonstrated efficacy in prior research: the Body Functionality-focused expressive writing Intervention (BFI) and the Media Literacy training Intervention (MLI). Both interventions are grounded in well-established psychological frameworks and share a common goal: to improve body image. The two interventions had comparable session durations (i.e., approximately 40 min). While BFI targets self-perception, MLI addresses external influences. We tested whether these two interventions outperform a waitlist control group in enhancing body image and related outcomes (e.g., eating behaviors). We also compared the relative effectiveness of the two interventions to determine if one approach yields superior outcomes. Finally, we investigated the feasibility and acceptability of the interventions.

Primary hypotheses

H₁: Participants in both intervention groups will report significantly greater improvements in body image (i.e., body dissatisfaction, body appreciation, functionality appreciation, and body image flexibility) at 1-week post-intervention (T1) and 4-week follow-up after baseline (T2) compared to the waitlist control group.

Secondary hypothesis

H2: Improvements in body image will correlate with reduced ED psychopathology, appearance comparisons, sociocultural attitudes towards appearance, objectified body consciousness, and psychological distress, as well as improved eating flexibility and intuitive eating at T1 and T2.

Furthermore, given the limited research that directly compares self-guided single-session media literacy and body functionality interventions, the comparisons in the efficacy of these interventions on both primary and secondary outcomes were exploratory. However, we anticipated that the MLI group would demonstrate greater reductions in perceived media pressure than the BFI group, while the BFI group would show stronger improvements in functionality appreciation compared to the MLI group, based on the focus of the two interventions.

Method

Design

The study employed a three-arm randomized controlled trial (RCT) design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: (a) MLI, (b) BFI, or (c) a waitlist control group. Randomization was implemented in R using a computer-generated allocation sequence that assigned the 204 participant IDs to the three groups in a 1:1:1 ratio (68 per group). Assessments occurred at baseline (T0), 1-week post-intervention (T1), and 4-week follow-up (T2). Participants allocated to the control group were placed on a waitlist and given access to both interventions after the 4-week follow-up. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Applied Psychology) of The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen (Approval No. EF20241018001). The trial was not prospectively registered (see Limitations and Future Research Directions for a discussion of this limitation).

Study sample and recruitment

A total of 204 Chinese female adults (Mage = 22.35 years, SD = 1.97), who reported elevated disordered eating concerns and regular social media use, were recruited via advertisements on the social media platform Xiaohongshu (Little Red Note). To identify “regular” users, respondents first answered the screening question, “Do you regularly use (browse or post information) social media platforms (e.g., Xiaohongshu, Weibo)?” [36]. Those interested in participating completed a brief screening measure to determine their eligibility. Based on prior similar studies [11, 37, 38], eligibility criteria included: (a) female sex (b) age ≥ 18 years; (c) an Eating Disorder Examination–Questionnaire Short Form (EDE-QS [39]) score ≥ 15 (probable ED cutoff per Chinese validation standards); (d) BMI ≥ 18 (excluding acute underweight status to prioritize intervention safety); and (e) daily access to the internet. Exclusion criteria were: (a) age < 18 years; (b) EDE-QS score < 15; (c) BMI < 18; (d) a current diagnosis of an eating disorder; (e) current substance-use disorder; and (f) active suicidal ideation or behaviors. Body functionality–focused expressive writing intervention

(BFI). Adapted from Alleva et al.’s [20] Expand Your Horizon framework, this self-guided single-session online intervention emphasized expressive writing tasks to shift focus from aesthetic evaluation to body functionality. The intervention aimed to reduce self-objectification and promote holistic body appreciation by encouraging participants to reflect on their body’s capabilities (e.g., physical strength, sensory experiences). Table 1 presents an overview of the content of BFI. Both interventions were hosted on a WeChat mini-program, accessible for 24 h post-baseline.

Control group

Participants in the waitlist control group received no active intervention during the trial but were provided access to both interventions after completing the 4-week follow-up assessment (T2). Following baseline, they viewed a static webpage stating: “Your program will unlock in eight weeks.” This design ensured ethical parity while allowing for comparison of immediate versus delayed intervention effects.

Measures

Sample characteristics

Demographic characteristics were collected at baseline, including age, sex, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), education level, occupation, and monthly income. Participants also reported their sexual orientation and gender identity.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome was body image, including both negative and positive body image. Specifically, we included a measure of negative body image (i.e., body dissatisfaction) and three measures of positive body image (i.e., body appreciation, functionality appreciation, and body image flexibility).

Body dissatisfaction

Body dissatisfaction was measured by the 9-item Body Dissatisfaction subscale of the Chinese version (translated and validated in Chinese women) of the Eating Disorder Inventory (EDI-BD [43]). Items of the EDI-BD are rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 6 (always). Five items were reverse-coded. Items are summed to produce a total score, with higher scores indicating greater body dissatisfaction. McDonald’s omega of the EDI-BD was acceptable at baseline (ω = 0.84), 1-week post-intervention (ω = 0.87), and 4-week follow-up (ω = 0.91).

Body appreciation

Body appreciation was measured using the 10-item Chinese version (translated and validated in Chinese women) of the Body Appreciation Scale-2 (Mandarin Chinese BAS‐2 [44]). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Scores were then averaged, with a higher score indicating greater body appreciation. McDonald’s omega was acceptable at baseline (ω = 0.85), 1-week post-intervention (ω = 0.88), and 4-week follow-up (ω = 0.92).

Functionality appreciation

Functionality appreciation was measured using the 7-item Chinese version (translated and validated in Chinese women) of the Functionality Appreciation Scale (FAS [45]). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Scores were then averaged, with higher scores indicating a greater appreciation of functionality. McDonald’s omega was acceptable at baseline (ω = 0.85), 1-week post-intervention (ω = 0.88), and 4-week follow-up (ω = 0.92).

Body image flexibility

Body image inflexibility was measured using the 5-item Chinese version (translated and validated in Chinese women) of the Body Image Acceptance and Action Questionnaire (BI-AAQ-5 [46]). The items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (always). Items were reverse-coded. Scores were then averaged, with higher average scores indicating greater body image flexibility. McDonald’s omega was acceptable at baseline (ω = 0.85), 1-week post-intervention (ω = 0.88), and 4-week follow-up (ω = 0.92).

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes included ED symptoms, appearance comparisons, sociocultural attitudes towards appearance (body image internalizations: thin/low body fat and muscular/athletic; appearance pressures: media, family, and peers), eating flexibility, intuitive eating, objectified body consciousness, and psychological distress.

Eating disorder symptomatology

Eating disorder symptomatology was measured using the 12-item Chinese version (translated and validated in Chinese women) of the Eating Disorder Examination-Questionnaire short form (EDE-QS [39]). Items were assessed in relation to the past week and are rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (0 days/not at all) to 3 (6–7 days/markedly). Items are summed to produce a total score, with higher total scores indicating higher levels of eating disorder symptomatology. McDonald’s omega was acceptable at baseline (ω = 0.83), 1-week post-intervention (ω = 0.86), and 4-week follow-up (ω = 0.90).

Appearance comparison

Appearance comparison was measured using the Chinese version (translated and validated in Chinese women) of the Physical Appearance Comparison Scale (PACS [47]). It had five items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Items are summed to produce a total score, with higher scores indicating higher levels of general appearance comparison. McDonald’s omega was acceptable at baseline (ω = 0.84), 1-week post-intervention (ω = 0.87), and 4-week follow-up (ω = 0.91).

Sociocultural attitudes towards appearance

Sociocultural attitudes toward appearance were measured using the 19-item Chinese version (translated and validated in Chinese women) of the Sociocultural Attitudes Towards Appearance Questionnaire-4-Female (Mandarin Chinese SATAQ-4-Female [48]). The Mandarin Chinese SATAQ-4-Female includes four subscales to assess the effects of sociocultural factors: internalization: thin/low body fat (five items), internalization: muscular/athletic (five items), family and peer pressures (five items), media pressures (four items). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). In each subscale, items are summed to produce a total score, with higher scores indicating higher levels of influences and pressures from each source that respondents feel or experience. McDonald’s omega was acceptable at baseline (thin/low body fat: ω = 0.85; muscular/athletic: ω = 0.85; family and peers: ω = 0.85; media: ω = 0.85), 1-week post intervention (thin/low body fat: ω = 0.88; muscular/athletic: ω = 0.88; family and peers: ω = 0.88; media: ω = 0.88), and 4-week follow-up (thin/low body fat: ω = 0.91; muscular/athletic: ω = 0.91; family and peers: ω = 0.91; media: ω = 0.91).

Eating flexibility

Eating flexibility was measured using the 11-item Chinese version (translated and validated in Chinese women) of the Inflexible Eating Questionnaire (IEQ [49]). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The items are summed to produce a total score, with a higher score reflecting more severe inflexible eating rules. McDonald’s omega was acceptable at baseline (ω = 0.84), 1-week post-intervention (ω = 0.86), and 4-week follow-up (ω = 0.91).

Intuitive eating

Intuitive eating was measured using the 23-item Chinese version (translated and validated in Chinese women) of the Intuitive Eating Scale-2 (IES-2 [44]). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Seven items were reverse scored. Scores were then averaged, with higher scores indicating more intuitive eating behavior. McDonald’s omega was acceptable at baseline (ω = 0.85), 1-week post-intervention (ω = 0.88), and 4-week follow-up (ω = 0.91).

Objectified body consciousness

Objectified body consciousness was measured using the Body Surveillance Subscale and Body Shame Subscale of the Chinese version (translated and validated in Chinese women) of the Objectified Body Consciousness (OBCS [50]). Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Six items of the Body Surveillance Subscale were scored in reverse. In each subscale, scores were averaged, with higher scores indicating higher levels of surveillance or habitual body monitoring and thinking of one’s body in terms of how it looks rather than how one feels. McDonald’s omega was acceptable at baseline (body surveillance ω = 0.85; body shame ω = 0.81), 1-week post-intervention (body surveillance: ω = 0.89; body shame: ω = 0.84), and 4-week follow-up (body surveillance: ω = 0.91; body shame: ω = 0.89).

Psychological distress

Psychological distress was measured using the Chinese version (translated and validated in Chinese women) of the 6-item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6 [51]). Items are assessed in relation to the past month and are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (none of the time) to 5 (all of the time). The items are summed to produce a total score, with higher scores indicating greater psychological distress. McDonald’s omega was acceptable at baseline (ω = 0.84) and the 4-week follow-up (ω = 0.91).

Intervention acceptability

Intervention acceptability was assessed through three quantitative metrics and one behavioral engagement indicator. Quantitative metrics assessing intervention acceptability included (1) whether participants would recommend this intervention to others, (2) perceived usefulness of this intervention, rated along a 10-point scale (not at all useful to extremely useful), and (3) level of satisfaction with this intervention, rated along a 10-point scale (extremely unsatisfied to extremely satisfied).

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was determined using a priori power analysis with the following assumptions: a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.50) for between-group differences on the primary outcome, an alpha level set to 0.05 (two-tailed), and a statistical power set to 0.80. Using the pwr package (Champely, 2020), the calculation indicated a need for 64 participants per group for the experimental and control conditions. Thus, the sample size of 204, 68 per condition, was adequate to detect large and medium effects, but remains underpowered for reliably identifying small effect sizes.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 [52], with the lme4 [53] and lmerTest [54] packages employed for linear mixed-effects modeling, which was used to evaluate the efficacy of the intervention for both primary and secondary outcomes. Following intention-to-treat principles, participant data were analyzed according to the groups in which individuals were allocated at baseline. Linear mixed-effects models were used to evaluate the efficacy of the interventions in terms of primary and secondary outcomes. Time was treated as a categorical variable coded as 0 (baseline), 1 (1-week post-intervention), and 2 (4-week follow-up), with baseline serving as the reference category. The group variable was also treated as a categorical variable and coded as 0 (waitlist), 1 (BFI), and 2 (MLI), with the waitlist group serving as the reference category. To test intervention efficacy, the study focused on the group × time interaction terms. A significant interaction indicates that the trajectory of change over time differs between the intervention and waitlist groups, and that BFI or MLI produced greater improvements on the outcome than the waitlist. The study examined the interaction coefficients, their 95% confidence intervals, and corresponding p-values to determine statistical significance [55]. For standardized mean differences, values of 0.20, 0.50, and 0.80 represent small, moderate, and large effects, respectively [56].

Attrition

Attrition rates were comparable across groups. Of the 204 participants, 197 (96.6%) completed the 1-week post-intervention assessment (T1), and 190 (93.1%) completed the 4-week follow-up (T2). The MLI group had an attrition rate of 5.9% (4 of 68 participants) from baseline to T2, the BFI group had an 8.8% attrition rate (6 of 68 participants), and the waitlist control group had a 5.9% attrition rate (4 of 68 participants).

Primary outcomes

Body dissatisfaction

As shown in Table 4, both interventions resulted in significant reductions in body dissatisfaction, as measured by the EDI-BD subscale. Participants in MLI revealed significant interaction effects at T1 (β = −3.938, SE = 1.059, p <.001) and T2 (β = −4.672, SE = 1.059, p <.001), with participants showing decreased body dissatisfaction at 1-week post-intervention with sustained improvements at the 4-week follow-up compared to controls. Similarly, the BFI demonstrated significant interactions at T1 (β = −3.628, SE = 1.068, p <.001) and T2 (β = −4.897, SE = 1.068, p <.001). Participants in the BFI group exhibited immediate reductions in body dissatisfaction at 1-week post-intervention with sustained improvements at the 4-week follow-up compared to the controls.

Body appreciation

Significant time × group interactions were observed for both intervention conditions. Participants in MLI showed significant interactions at T1 (β = 0.425, SE = 0.096, p <.001) and T2 (β = 0.367, SE = 0.096, p <.001), with participants demonstrating improvements in body appreciation at 1-week post-intervention that were sustained at 4-week follow-up compared to controls. For the BFI, significant interaction effects were found at T1 (β = 0.415, SE = 0.097, p <.001) and T2 (β = 0.487, SE = 0.097, p <.001). Participants in the BFI group exhibited immediate improvements in body appreciation 1-week post-intervention, with sustained improvements at the 4-week follow-up compared to the controls.

Functionality appreciation

Significant time × group interactions emerged for both intervention conditions. For the MLI, significant interactions were found at T1 (β = 0.241, SE = 0.098, p =.014) and T2 (β = 0.342, SE = 0.098, p <.001), indicating that participants in the MLI group showed immediate improvements in functionality appreciation at 1-week post-intervention, with effects sustained at the 4-week follow-up compared to controls. The BFI also demonstrated significant interaction effects, with significant interactions at T1 (β = 0.367, SE = 0.099, p <.001) and T2 (β = 0.409, SE = 0.099, p <.001). Participants in the BFI group showed immediate improvements in functionality appreciation at 1-week post-intervention, with effects sustained at the 4-week follow-up compared to the control group.

Body image flexibility

Significant time × group interactions were found for both interventions. Participants in MLI demonstrated significant interaction effects at T1 (β = 0.613, SE = 0.157, p <.001) and T2 (β = 0.803, SE = 0.157, p <.001), indicating that participants showed increased body image flexibility at 1-week post-intervention, with effects sustained through the 4-week follow-up compared to controls. Participants in BFI also showed significant interactions at T1 (β = 0.573, SE = 0.159, p <.001) and T2 (β = 0.781, SE = 0.159, p <.001). Participants in the BFI group demonstrated immediate improvements in body image flexibility at 1-week post-intervention, with sustained benefits maintained at the 4-week follow-up compared to controls.

Secondary outcomes

Eating disorder symptomatology

Significant time × group interactions emerged for both intervention conditions. Participants in the MLI demonstrated significant interaction effects at T1 (β = −3.875, SE = 0.960, p <.001) and T2 (β = −5.047, SE = 0.960, p <.001), indicating that participants showed reduced ED symptomatology at 1-week post-intervention, with effects sustained at the 4-week follow-up compared to controls. Participants in the BFI also showed significant interactions at T1 (β = −3.924, SE = 0.968, p <.001) and T2 (β = −4.770, SE = 0.968, p <.001); they exhibited immediate reductions in ED symptoms at 1-week post-intervention, with sustained improvements maintained at the 4-week follow-up compared to the controls.

Appearance comparison

Significant time × group interactions emerged for both intervention conditions. Participants in the MLI demonstrated significant interaction effects at T1 (β = −1.766, SE = 0.611, p =.004) and T2 (β = −1.469, SE = 0.611, p =.017), indicating that participants showed reduced appearance comparison tendencies at 1-week post-intervention, with effects maintained at the 4-week follow-up compared to controls. Participants in the BFI also showed significant interactions at T1 (β = −1.504, SE = 0.616, p =.015) and T2 (β = −1.931, SE = 0.616, p =.002). Participants in the BFI group exhibited immediate reductions in appearance comparison at 1-week post-intervention, with effects sustained at the 4-week follow-up compared to controls.

Sociocultural attitudes towards appearance

For family and peer pressures, participants in the MLI demonstrated a significant interaction effect only at T2, 4-week follow-up from baseline (β = −0.356, SE = 0.120, p =.003), with a non-significant trend at T1, 1-week post-intervention (β = −0.191, SE = 0.120, p =.112). Participants in the BFI showed non significant trends at both time points compared to controls: T1 (β = −0.232, SE = 0.121, p =.055) and T2 (β = −0.228, SE = 0.121, p =.060), suggesting we may have been underpowered to detect significant reductions in perceived family and peer pressures.

For media pressures, participants in the MLI showed significant interaction effects at T1 (β = −0.305, SE = 0.127, p =.017) and T2 (β = −0.383, SE = 0.127, p =.003), indicating reduced perceived media pressures that were maintained over time compared to controls. However, participants in the BFI demonstrated non-significant interactions at both T1 (β = −0.165, SE = 0.128, p =.199) and T2 (β = −0.150, SE = 0.128, p =.244).

For thin/low body fat internalization, both interventions produced significant reductions. Participants in the MLI showed significant interaction effects at T1 (β = −0.406, SE = 0.125, p =.001) and T2 (β = −0.688, SE = 0.125, p <.001), with participants demonstrating decreased internalization of thin ideals that were maintained over time compared to controls. Participants in the BFI also demonstrated significant interactions at T1 (β = −0.374, SE = 0.126, p =.003) and T2 (β = −0.628, SE = 0.126, p <.001), showing similar patterns of reduced thin-ideal internalization over time compared to controls.

For muscular/athletic internalization, the time × group interactions were not statistically significant for either intervention. For instance, participants in the MLI showed non-significant effects at T1 (β = 0.034, SE = 0.129, p =.790) and T2 (β = −0.009, SE = 0.129, p =.942), and participants in the BFI similarly demonstrated non-significant interactions at T1 (β = 0.058, SE = 0.130, p =.655) and T2 (β = 0.043, SE = 0.130, p =.739).

Eating flexibility

Significant time × group interactions were observed for both intervention conditions. Participants in the MLI demonstrated significant interaction effects at T1 (β = −3.609, SE = 1.201, p =.003) and T2 (β = −5.813, SE = 1.201, p <.001), indicating that participants showed increased eating flexibility at 1-week post-intervention, with effects maintained at the 4-week follow-up compared to controls. Participants in the BFI showed a non significant interaction at T1 (β = −2.146, SE = 1.211, p =.077) but a significant interaction at T2 (β = −2.989, SE = 1.211, p =.014). Participants in the BFI group demonstrated improvements in eating flexibility during the 4-week follow-up period compared to controls.

Intuitive eating

Significant time × group interactions emerged for both intervention conditions. Participants in the MLI showed significant interaction effects at T1 (β = 0.186, SE = 0.070, p =.008) and T2 (β = 0.251, SE = 0.070, p <.001), indicating that participants demonstrated increased intuitive eating behaviors at 1-week post-intervention, with effects maintained at the 4-week follow-up compared to controls. Participants in the BFI also demonstrated significant interactions at T1 (β = 0.276, SE = 0.071, p <.001) and T2 (β = 0.215, SE = 0.071, p =.002). Participants in the BFI group exhibited immediate improvements in intuitive eating at 1-week post-intervention, with sustained benefits maintained at the 4-week follow-up compared to controls.

Objectified body consciousness

For the body surveillance subscale, significant time × group interactions emerged for both intervention conditions. The MLI demonstrated significant interaction effects at T1 (β = −3.797, SE = 1.165, p =.001) and T2 (β = −6.516, SE = 1.165, p <.001), indicating that participants showed reduced body surveillance at 1-week post-intervention, with effects maintained at the 4-week follow-up compared to controls. Participants in the BFI also showed significant interactions at T1 (β = −3.681, SE = 1.175, p =.002) and T2 (β = −5.234, SE = 1.175, p <.001). Participants in the BFI group exhibited immediate reductions in body surveillance at 1-week post-intervention, with sustained improvements maintained at the 4-week follow-up compared to controls.

For the body shame subscale, both interventions also produced significant effects. Participants in the MLI showed significant interaction effects at T1 (β = −4.453, SE = 1.267, p <.001) and T2 (β = −5.266, SE = 1.267, p <.001), with participants demonstrating decreased body shame that was sustained across both time points compared to the control group. Similarly, the participants in the BFI demonstrated significant interactions at T1 (β = −4.109, SE = 1.277, p =.001) and T2 (β = −5.107, SE = 1.277, p <.001), indicating reduced body shame that persisted through the follow-up period compared to the control group.

Psychological distress

Significant time × group interactions were observed for both intervention conditions at the 4-week follow-up assessment. Participants in the MLI demonstrated a significant interaction effect at T2 (β = −1.984, SE = 0.790, p =.013), indicating that participants showed reduced psychological distress at the 4-week follow-up compared to the control group. Participants in the BFI also showed a significant interaction at T2 (β = −3.099, SE = 0.796, p <.001). Participants in the BFI group exhibited decreased psychological distress at the 4-week follow-up compared to the control group.

Acceptability

For participants in the MLI, there were 64 completed items assessing intervention acceptability. Of these, 61 (95%) stated that they would recommend the program to others in need. The mean usefulness and satisfaction ratings for the intervention was 7.64 (SD = 1.48) and 8.08 (SD = 1.53), respectively. For participants in the BFI, 62 completed items assessing intervention acceptability. Of these, 59 (95%) stated that they would recommend the program to others in need. The mean usefulness and satisfaction ratings for the intervention was 7.35 (SD = 1.73) and 8.02 (SD = 1.56), respectively.

Comparisons between the two interventions

As shown in Table 5, direct comparisons between the MLI and BFI groups revealed that the interventions performed similarly across most outcome measures. Of all outcome variables examined, only one significant difference in eating flexibility emerged between the two interventions. Specifically, participants in the BFI showed a significantly greater improvement in eating flexibility compared to participants in the MLI at the 4-week follow-up assessment (β = 2.823, SE = 1.284, p =.029). Discussion

The present study evaluated the efficacy and acceptability of two culturally adapted, online, self-guided, single-session interventions (SSIs), Body Functionality–Focused Expressive Writing Intervention (BFI) and Media Literacy Intervention (MLI). Relative to a waitlist control, both interventions demonstrated statistically significant and meaningful improvements across multiple symptom domains.

Efficacy of the media literacy intervention

The MLI demonstrated significant efficacy in primary and secondary outcomes compared to a waitlist control. Specifically, regarding primary outcomes, MLI participants showed statistically significant improvements in body appreciation (BAS-2), functionality appreciation (FAS), and body image flexibility (BI-AAQ-5), alongside reduced body fat dissatisfaction (EDI-BD). These gains align with the intervention’s focus on deconstructing appearance ideals through interactive critical appraisal of peer and celebrity content, which directly targets internalization processes [16]. Crucially, whereas Irving and Berel [13] found no significant changes in body dissatisfaction despite increased media skepticism in college women, our MLI achieved clinically meaningful reductions in body dissatisfaction. This divergence likely stems from the MLI’s emphasis on culturally salient, real-time examples (e.g., raw vs. filtered selfies), which may more effectively disrupt appearance comparisons than the abstract media critiques used in earlier work. Regarding secondary outcomes, the MLI significantly reduced thin-ideal internalization (SATAQ-4 Internalization-Thin), perceived media pressures (SATAQ-4 Pressures-Media), appearance comparisons (PACS), psychological distress (K6), and ED psychopathology (EDE-QS). These findings reflect the intervention’s success in weakening the link between media exposure and body-related distress by fostering realism scepticism [17]. In contrast, McLean et al. [19] found reductions in dietary restraint and Dhillon and Deepak [18] reported improved body esteem in adolescents, whereas the current MLI demonstrated a broader impact on ED psychopathology (EDE-QS) and psychological distress (K6). Interestingly, while significant reductions in thin-internalization were observed at both follow-ups, no statistically significant effects emerged for muscular/athletic internalization. This pattern aligns with the intervention’s design, which explicitly targeted thinness-oriented sociocultural pressures prevalent among Chinese women [57–59] but did not address muscularity-focused ideals. This focus was justified given well-documented thinness concerns in this population and the intervention’s cultural adaptation to local platforms like Douyin. However, emerging research suggests muscularity ideals are gaining traction among Chinese women, particularly in fitness and athletic contexts [60, 61]. Future iterations of culturally adapted MLIs should incorporate content that critically addresses these evolving ideals (e.g., deconstructing idealized fitness influencer content). This is essential in evaluating whether adding such muscularity-focused components enhances the intervention’s breadth and relevance for contemporary Chinese beauty standards.

Efficacy of the body functionality–focused expressive writing intervention

The BFI significantly improved both primary and secondary outcomes relative to the waitlist control, demonstrating efficacy for Chinese female social media users with ED symptoms. These findings align with theoretical mechanisms positing that redirecting attention from appearance-based to functionality-based body cognitions disrupts self-objectification cycles [21]. Notably, despite condensing Alleva et al.‘s [20] one-week writing intervention into a single 40-minute self-guided intervention, our BFI achieved comparable effect sizes on core body image outcomes, demonstrating its cross-cultural adaptability and scalability advantage.

Regarding primary outcomes, BFI participants showed significant improvements in body appreciation (BAS‑2), reductions in body dissatisfaction (EDI‑BD), and increases in functionality appreciation (FAS). These changes align with cognitive‑behavioral frameworks, suggesting that expressive writing facilitates cognitive restructuring of maladaptive appearance beliefs [62, 63]. Moreover, body image flexibility (BI‑AAQ‑5) improved markedly, indicating greater willingness to experience and defuse from body‑related thoughts, which is a key protective factor against disordered eating [64]. Although the intervention of Alleva et al. [20] and Mulgrew et al. [24] yielded gains on body appreciation, our single‑session BFI achieved similar magnitude changes, suggesting that concentrated reflective writing can match the efficacy of more protracted formats. Furthermore, prior studies measured functionality satisfaction [20, 23, 24], each of which reported small‑to‑moderate body functionality satisfaction improvements. In comparison, our BFI elicited moderate‑to‑large gains in functionality appreciation in a single session, indicating that focused writing prompts may more efficiently cultivate appreciation of bodily capabilities.

For secondary outcomes, BFI reduced psychological distress (K6) and objectified body consciousness (OBCS-Shame). This broader impact extends beyond the primary outcomes assessed in Alleva et al.’s [20] functionality‑focused programme (which focused on body appreciation and self‑objectification), demonstrating significant improvements in affective domains. In our study, BFI additionally yielded notable improvements in affective domains, suggesting that the self-guided expressive‑writing intervention may be especially potent for reducing internalized stigma and negative affect. Both studies of Alleva et al. [20] and Mulgrew et al. [24] assessed self‑objectification and reported moderate decreases after interventions. Our single‑session BFI achieved similar medium‑sized reductions in self‑objectification, confirming that even single-session, functionality‑focused writing can disrupt objectifying cognitions. Like the MLI findings, the BFI demonstrated no statistically significant effects on muscular/athletic internalization. This absence of effect for both interventions is consistent with their shared focus on thinness ideals prevalent in the target population, rather than muscularity concerns.

Comparative effectiveness of BFI and MLI

Direct comparisons between the BFI and the MLI revealed largely equivalent efficacy across most outcome measures. Of the 19 primary and secondary outcomes examined, both interventions produced statistically comparable improvements in body image constructs (e.g., body appreciation, functionality appreciation), eating disorder symptomatology, objectified body consciousness, psychological distress, and sociocultural attitudinal factors at 1- and 4-week follow-ups. This overall equivalence underscores the robustness of both approaches in addressing core manifestations of body image disturbance and disordered eating symptoms among Chinese female social media users.

However, one statistically significant differential effect emerged: participants in the BFI group demonstrated greater improvement in eating flexibility (as measured by the Inflexible Eating Questionnaire, IEQ) relative to the MLI group at the 4-week follow-up. This effect was not observed at the 1-week post-intervention, suggesting that the BFI’s benefits for flexible eating behaviors might manifest over time and be maintained at the 4-week follow-up. This delayed divergence may reflect the distinct therapeutic mechanism of expressive writing focused on body functionality. By repeatedly engaging participants in structured reflections about their body’s capabilities (e.g., “My legs allow me to hike with friends”; “My hands enable me to create art”), the BFI may progressively weaken rigid cognitive schemas linking self-worth to dietary control [20]. In contrast, while the MLI effectively reduced thin-ideal internalization and media pressures, its focus on external sociocultural influences may not directly target deeply ingrained eating (in)flexibility.

In addition to the differential content focus, it is instructive to consider the broader distinction between “educational” and “therapy‑based” intervention models. The MLI functions primarily as a structured psychoeducational program, teaching critical skills for deconstructing media messages and fostering media literacy competencies. By contrast, the BFI aligns more closely with brief therapeutic approaches, employing expressive writing to facilitate cognitive restructuring and emotional processing, which are techniques akin to those used in psychotherapeutic contexts [20, 62]. Thus, these findings may suggest that while therapy‑based interventions such as the BFI may offer more direct pathways to symptom remission, educational programs like the MLI can serve as scalable, cost‑effective alternatives or adjuncts, particularly in contexts where access to formal psychotherapy is limited.

Implications

The current findings offer strong support for the utility of brief, online, self-guided interventions to enhance body image among Chinese adults with ED symptoms. Intervention acceptability was high (over 95% of participants would recommend them), suggesting these formats could scale widely in Chinese digital environments. Consequently, we infer that these programs are culturally relevant, given their adaptation to Chinese social media imagery and language. Given the low cost and high scalability of single-session interventions embedded in digital platforms, these approaches may help bridge treatment gaps for body image concerns among underserved populations. Tailoring interventions to focus either on internal embodiment or external sociocultural critique allows for flexibility in meeting diverse user needs and preferences. Furthermore, the results provide evidence that body image interventions can also yield secondary benefits across psychological well-being (e.g., reduced distress), intuitive eating, and flexible eating, suggesting the broader impact of single-session interventions. Embedding these SSIs into low-bandwidth, mobile-first channels (e.g., WeChat mini-programs) can maximize reach in both urban and rural areas, while simple, self-guided formats reduce reliance on clinician time, supporting scalable digital mental health resources.

Limitations and future research directions

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, although the study used a randomized controlled design, the follow-up period was limited to eight weeks, restricting inferences about long-term intervention effects, particularly for outcomes showing delayed improvement (e.g., BFI’s eating flexibility effects at T2). Given evidence that SSI effects often diminish after 3 months [65], future trials should extend follow-up assessments to 6–12 months. Second, limited sample diversity restricts generalizability. Participants were predominantly urban, educated, cisgender Han Chinese women, rendering findings potentially inapplicable to rural, low-socioeconomic-status (SES), and gender-diverse populations (e.g., transgender or non-binary individuals) [66]. Future research should therefore recruit stratified samples across geographic, SES, and gender identity, using community-based participatory methods to adapt interventions for cultural subgroups (e.g., collaborating with rural health clinics and LGBTQ+ organizations).

Third, this study focused specifically on thinness-oriented body image concerns, which are particularly salient among Chinese women [57, 58]. As such, neither the MLI nor the BFI included content explicitly addressed muscularity-oriented concerns. Moreover, our inclusion criteria were based solely on the EDE-QS, which primarily assesses thinness-oriented ED psychopathology. That said, both the sample and the interventions were tailored toward individuals with concerns driven by thin ideals, which limits the generalizability of the findings to those with muscularity-oriented body image disturbances. However, emerging evidence suggests that muscularity has become an increasingly prominent body ideal for women [61], including in China [60]. Fourth, although the study received ethical approval prior to interventions, the trial was not prospectively registered. This lack of registration may limit comparability with other randomized trials, and future studies of similar interventions should be prospectively registered.

Finally, it is also important to note that individuals presenting with clinically significant ED symptoms require specialized, individualized treatment (e.g., cognitive-behavioral therapy) and appropriate medical care, beyond the scope of brief SSIs. Future research should both (a) adapt the MLI and BFI to incorporate culturally relevant components addressing muscularity-focused concerns (e.g., interactive critiques of exercise and fitness content) and (b) examine the interventions’ efficacy across varying levels of social-media engagement (e.g., low-level users versus those exhibiting problematic use patterns). Such refinements will help to evaluate whether such enhancements can provide additional benefits for body image and ED prevention in Chinese women.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial provides empirical evidence supporting the efficacy and acceptability of two culturally adapted, single-session online interventions, MLI and BFI, for improving body image and reducing ED symptoms among female Chinese social media users, with effects maintained at 4‑week follow‑up. Both interventions achieved high acceptability (≥ 95% recommendation rate) and low attrition (93% retention), highlighting their feasibility and potential for broad dissemination in digital contexts.
