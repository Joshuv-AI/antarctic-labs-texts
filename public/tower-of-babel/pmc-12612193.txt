Screening and analysis of malt pentapeptide DPP-IV inhibitory activity
Authors: Yi He, Yan Zhang, Pengying Zhang, Jiaying Li, Wannan Li, Weiwei Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) poses a significant global health burden, with dipeptidyl peptidase-IV (DPP-IV) inhibitors being critical therapeutic targets for its management. Malt, a natural medicinal food, shows promise for T2DM treatment, yet its active compounds need further exploration. This study integrates molecular docking (Libdock, Autodock Vina), deep learning models (ConPlex, KPGT), molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and network pharmacology to identify and validate DPP-IV inhibitors from malt. The pentapeptide YPQPQ, derived from malt hordein, exhibited potent DPP-IV inhibition (IC50: 23.87 μM) in vitro. Network pharmacology identified MAPK1 and MAPK3 (ERK1 and ERK2) as key targets, with YPQPQ significantly reducing ERK phosphorylation. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations revealed YPQPQ’s binding similarity to Diprotin A. These findings establish YPQPQ as a novel natural DPP-IV inhibitor, modulating the ERK signaling pathway, offering potential for T2DM therapy. Subject terms: Biophysics, Chemical biology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)1 is a metabolic disease and a major health issue in modern society. T2DM is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance, impaired insulin secretion, and hyperglycemia2. It has become a significant public health concern globally due to its increasing prevalence and association with severe complications such as cardiovascular diseases3, neuropathy4, and nephropathy5. One of the key therapeutic targets in T2DM management is dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-IV), a type II transmembrane protein that inactivates incretin hormones, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), by cleaving their N-terminal dipeptides6. Incretins play a vital role in glucose homeostasis by stimulating insulin secretion and inhibiting glucagon release7. Therefore, inhibiting DPP-IV can prolong the activity of GLP-1, thereby improving glycemic control and offering a strategic intervention for T2DM treatment.

While several synthetic DPP-IV inhibitors8, such as the widely used gliptins, have been developed and shown to be effective in clinical settings, concerns about potential side effects and long-term safety have driven the exploration of alternative therapeutic options9. Natural products, especially plant-based compounds10, are gaining attention as a source of safer and more sustainable DPP-IV inhibitors. Among these, grains such as malt, known for their dual role as food and traditional medicinal herbs, are of particular interest. Malt, as a food and medicinal herb, has been found to contain active ingredients that inhibit DPP-IV11,12. However, systematic identification and validation of these compounds remain insufficient, necessitating further research to uncover their therapeutic potential.

The extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)13 signaling pathway plays a critical role in regulating cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis14. Studies have suggested a potential link between dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-IV) and ERK signaling15, particularly in the context of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Beyond its enzymatic activity, DPP-IV also functions as a signaling molecule16, interacting with various intracellular pathways, including ERK. DPP-IV inhibitors can enhance glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) levels17, subsequently activating protein kinase A (PKA) and the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathway18,19, leading to downstream phosphorylation of ERK. Under hyperglycemic conditions, ERK activation exerts a protective effect on pancreatic β-cells by promoting cell proliferation and reducing apoptosis20. Furthermore, ERK signaling is involved in the regulation of inflammation21 and oxidative stress22, both of which are key contributors to insulin resistance.

In recent years, computational methods (in silico) have played an increasingly important role in biological fields such as disease mechanism elucidation, drug screening, and enzyme engineering. For example, studies using molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations found that two common mutations (E542K and E545K) in the oncogene PI3KCA disrupt the interaction between the catalytic subunit p110α and the regulatory subunit p85α, leading to abnormal cell proliferation and promoting tumor development19. Similarly, computational simulations targeting the mammalian PLK1 kinase showed that certain mutations (such as W414F) reduce the binding affinity between its Polo box domain and substrates and decrease protein stability, thereby affecting proper regulation of mitosis23. Additionally, integrated in silico analysis pipelines combining multiple computational methods have been widely applied to identify functional single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in regulatory regions (such as miRNA binding sites) that may affect gene expression. Relevant research established systematic screening workflows to identify multiple high-confidence SNPs potentially influencing post-transcriptional regulation of the CEP gene and evaluated their effects on the cell cycle and centrosome function through expression profiling and network analysis24.

Computational methods also play a crucial role in high-throughput screening of small-molecule inhibitors and bioactive peptides. For example, studies on the enzyme target PDE10A utilized molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and MM-PBSA binding free energy calculations to screen triazole compounds 3 h and 3 j, which demonstrated better binding stability and free energy compared to known ligands, showing promising potential as drug candidates25. In recent years, machine learning approaches have emerged as a complementary tool, exhibiting efficient and accurate predictive capabilities in virtual screening pipelines. For instance, dozens of classification and regression algorithms have been developed for the DPP-IV target, including regression models such as genetic algorithm feature selection combined with linear regression (GA_LR)26, random forest regression combined with support vector regression (RFR_SVR)27, and time-varying binary gravitational search algorithm combined with linear regression (TVBGSA_LR)28, which have achieved good performance in predicting the IC50 of DPP-IV inhibitors. However, these methods are mostly ligand-driven models with limited generalizability and struggle to precisely capture the complex structural interaction features between ligands and receptors. Therefore, integrating receptor-based deep learning screening models with traditional molecular docking screening methods is expected to become a more powerful screening tool.

In this study, we integrated traditional molecular docking with deep learning-based models to identify potential DPP-IV inhibitors in malt. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed to elucidate the binding mechanisms of the most promising inhibitors, while network pharmacology was employed to investigate the downstream molecular pathways affected by DPP-IV inhibition. Experimental validation confirmed the inhibitory activity of the identified compounds and their regulatory effects on relevant signaling pathways. By combining computational and experimental approaches, this work provides a comprehensive framework for the discovery of natural DPP-IV inhibitors and highlights the therapeutic potential of malt-derived bioactive compounds for T2DM treatment.

Results

Virtual screen of DPP-IV inhibitors

As shown in Fig. S1 and Table S2, we evaluated the performance of six regression models in predicting the IC50 values of DPP-IV inhibitors based on five-fold cross-validation. Among them, KPGT is a novel pre-trained molecular graph language model that integrates molecular fingerprints and descriptors as knowledge guidance. GA_LR, TVBGSA_LR, and RFR_SVR are three mainstream IC50 prediction models for DPP-IV, while GCN29 (Graph Convolutional Network) and MorganFP_MLP30 (a multilayer perceptron using molecular Morgan fingerprints as input features) are commonly used in molecular property prediction tasks such as taste classification, solubility estimation, and quantum chemical property prediction.

According to the cumulative five-fold cross-validation results (Table S2), the KPGT model achieved the best performance, with the highest coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.67), the lowest root mean square error (RMSE = 0.76), and the highest Pearson correlation coefficient (r = 0.82). This superior performance can be attributed to KPGT’s integration of graph neural networks and knowledge-guided pretraining, which effectively captures complex structural and functional features of molecules. In contrast, traditional machine learning models such as GA_LR and TVBGSA_LR performed poorly, with R² values of only 0.38 and RMSEs exceeding 1.0, indicating limited ability to learn complex molecular descriptors of DPP-IV inhibitors. RFR_SVR (R2 = 0.54) and GCN (R2 = 0.49) showed moderate performance, while MorganFP_MLP slightly outperformed GCN (R2 = 0.47, RMSE = 0.96). Moreover, statistical hypothesis testing (Figure S1G) revealed that the KPGT model was significantly better than the others in terms of RMSE (p < 0.001). The 95% confidence intervals from paired t-tests did not cross zero, further supporting the statistical significance and reliability of the observed differences.

Although the KPGT model showed superior performance on the five-fold cross-validation of the ChEMBL-based DPP-IV IC50 dataset, we further conducted a blind evaluation using an external test set consisting of 22 rationally designed DPP-IV inhibitors with known IC50 values31, which were not used in model training or validation. The average predicted IC50 values for these 22 compounds by the six models are shown in Table S3, and the corresponding performance evaluation is provided in Table S4. KPGT again outperformed the others across multiple metrics, achieving the lowest RMSE (0.68), MAE (0.59), and MAPE (0.18), along with the highest Pearson correlation coefficient (0.42). In comparison, all other models had RMSE values greater than 0.9 and negative R2 values, indicating poorer generalization ability relative to KPGT.

Next, we applied the previously trained ligand-based KPGT DPP-IV IC50 prediction model, molecular docking tools LibDock and Vina, and the receptor-based deep learning tool ConPlex to perform an average ranking of 64 malt components from the TCMSP database. Detailed results are shown in Table S5. Among them, the peptide YPQPQ derived from gliadin obtained the highest average ranking. This molecule’s Vina affinity to DPP-IV was −8.5 kcal/mol, ranking 14th; the ConPlex score was 0.0726, ranking 15th; the LibDock score was 177.638, ranking 2nd; and the KPGT predicted IC50 was 724.49 nM, ranking 6th. The average rank of these four indicators was 9.25, higher than the second-ranked compound hordatine B with 13.5. The differences in rankings among the indicators suggest that various computational methods evaluate molecular activity differently. Considering multiple scoring metrics comprehensively helps better assess binding performance and inhibitory effects, improving screening accuracy and reliability. Since YPQPQ showed the most outstanding average ranking among the four indicators, this study focuses on it for further experimental and computational investigations.

YPQPQ, also known as alpha-Gliadin (43–47), is a short peptide from wheat recorded in many databases such as FOODB: FDB012968, PubChem CID: 17787981, and TCMSP: MOL010848. YPQPQ appears four times in the alpha-gliadin (UniProt ID: P18573) protein sequence at positions 63–67, 88–92, 95–99, and 102–106. Previous studies suggest that YPQPQ may affect immune cell function through opioid-like pathways32; however, its production has been limited to chemical synthesis, with no reports on enzymatic digestion.

Assessment of YPQPQ inhibitory activity and mechanism against DPP-IV

The inhibitory activity of the inhibitor YPQPQ against DPP-IV was evaluated. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, five concentrations of YPQPQ (10, 20, 25, 50, and 100 μM) were prepared, and their respective inhibitory rates against DPP-IV were determined. The results demonstrated that YPQPQ exhibited significant inhibitory effects on DPP-IV, with inhibition rates of 67.81% and 89.55% observed at concentrations of 50 μM and 100 μM, respectively. Based on the observed inhibition rates, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of YPQPQ was calculated, as shown in Fig. 1B. The IC50 value was determined to be 23.87 μM, indicating that YPQPQ effectively inhibits DPP-IV activity in a concentration-dependent manner. These findings confirm the potent inhibitory effect of YPQPQ on DPP-IV and highlight its potential for further development as a DPP-IV inhibitor.

The inhibitory mechanism of YPQPQ on DPP-IV was determined using a Lineweaver-Burk double-reciprocal plot. As illustrated in Fig. 1C, the Lineweaver-Burk plot confirmed that YPQPQ functions as a competitive inhibitor of DPP-IV. This was demonstrated by an increase in the slope with rising concentrations of YPQPQ, corresponding to an increased Km value. However, changes in concentration did not alter the maximum reaction rate (Vmax), as evidenced by the convergence of reaction rates at a single point on the y-axis, indicating that Vmax remained constant.

The inhibition constant (Ki), a key parameter reflecting the strength of the interaction between a protein and its ligand, was calculated. Slopes (representing Km values) at varying concentrations of YPQPQ were obtained from the Lineweaver-Burk plot and analyzed via linear regression to determine the Ki value, as shown in Fig. 1D. The results indicated that YPQPQ has a Ki value of 54.24 μM.

Network pharmacology exploring of targets affected by DPP-IV in T2DM

From DISGENET, DRUGBANK, GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD databases, we found 350, 185, 9269, 698, and 99 T2DM-related targets, respectively, which after deduplication resulted in 9834 unique targets (Table S6). Meanwhile, we retrieved 100 DPP-IV-related targets from PubMed (Table S7). The intersection of these two sets yielded 70 overlapping genes (Fig. 2A). We performed KEGG enrichment analysis on these 70 genes (Fig. 2B), which revealed a complex set of pathways, some of which are closely related to diabetes, such as the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications. DPP-IV plays a crucial role in diabetes-related diseases, impacting blood glucose regulation, insulin secretion, and inflammatory responses through the AGE-RAGE pathway.

Given that DPP-IV affects multiple pathways, we visualized the KEGG enrichment results using Cytoscape to explore which genes are most likely impacted by DPP-IV. As shown in Fig. 2C, the network topology of genes and pathways indicates that MAPK1 and MAPK3 have the highest Degree Centrality, each being linked to 113 pathways. Therefore, MAPK1 and MAPK3 are considered to be important targets through which DPP-IV influences T2DM. Further experiments will be conducted to measure the expression of these genes in T2DM. But further detailed studies are needed to elucidate how DPP-IV influences the MAPK signaling pathway. Current reports indicate that, in addition to degrading GLP-1/GIP to enhance insulin secretion, DPP-IV can also activate the MAPK and NF-κB pathways through non-enzymatic mechanisms, such as interacting with caveolin-133. Moreover, studies have shown that soluble DPP-IV (sDPP-IV) can directly activate the MAPK pathway (including ERK1/2, i.e., MAPK3/MAPK1) and the NF-κB pathway by stimulating the PAR2 receptor, thereby inducing cytokine production and inflammatory responses34.

We conducted predictive analyses using Swiss Target Prediction and Pharmmapper (Tables S8, S9). The results suggest that YPQPQ may interact with off-targets, including opioid receptors, HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR), kinases, and proteases, potentially disrupting physiological processes such as pain modulation, lipid metabolism, signal transduction, and blood coagulation. Further experimental validation is planned to confirm these findings and enhance the specificity ofYPQPQ.

Protein Expression in Caco-2 cells

Exponentially growing Caco-2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with gradient concentrations of the inhibitor YPQPQ (10, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 μM) for 2 hours and 4 hours. As shown in Fig. 1E, the cell viability remained above 75% at all tested concentrations. At YPQPQ concentrations below 25 μM, the cell viability was 92.07% and 88.28% for 2 hours and 4 hours treatments, respectively, indicating no cytotoxic effects at the IC50 concentration. Therefore, subsequent experiments were conducted using YPQPQ at its IC50 concentration to stimulate Caco-2 cells.

The expression of DPP-IV protein in Caco-2 cells was further investigated. As depicted in Fig. 3A, Caco-2 cells were treated with YPQPQ at its IC50 concentration for 2 hours and 4 hours. Compared to the control group, the expression level of DPP-IV protein in YPQPQ-treated cells was significantly reduced, demonstrating that YPQPQ effectively inhibits DPP-IV expression. Based on pathway screening results, the expression of ERK (extracellular signal-regulated kinase) in Caco-2 cells was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 3B, treatment with YPQPQ at its IC50 concentration for 2 and 4 hours did not cause a notable change in total ERK protein levels. However, the phosphorylation level of ERK (p-ERK) was markedly decreased.

Inhibitor YPQPQ was evaluated for its effect on protein expression in Caco-2 cells, incubated over varying time periods.

The observed reduction in p-ERK levels may result from the direct inhibition of DPP-IV enzymatic activity by YPQPQ. DPP-IV inhibitors enhance the activity of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) by prolonging its half-life. GLP-1 signaling has been shown to suppress protein kinase C (PKC) and other upstream signaling molecules, thereby indirectly inhibiting ERK phosphorylation. This regulatory mechanism is likely mediated through the GLP-1 receptor signaling pathway, particularly in metabolically active cells. The downregulation of ERK phosphorylation suggests that DPP-IV inhibitors, such as YPQPQ, may influence key cellular processes, including proliferation, inflammatory responses, and metabolic homeostasis.

MAPK1/3 (ERK1/2) signaling plays a pivotal role in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) by modulating insulin resistance and β-cell function. Hyperglycemia and advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) activate the AGE-RAGE axis, enhancing ERK phosphorylation, which promotes inflammation and oxidative stress—key drivers of insulin resistance35. Excessive ERK activation can also impair β-cell survival, exacerbating T2DM progression. YPQPQ inhibits DPP-IV, increasing GLP-1 bioavailability and activating GLP-1 receptor signaling. This stimulates downstream pathways, including protein kinase A (PKA) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase/Akt (PI3K/Akt), which suppress ERK phosphorylation by inhibiting upstream regulators such as protein kinase C (PKC).

The reduction in p-ERK levels may attenuate inflammation and oxidative stress, potentially mitigating AGE-RAGE-mediated effects in T2DM. Additionally, DPP-IV’s non-enzymatic role as a signaling molecule can activate ERK through interactions with membrane proteins. By inhibiting DPP-IV, YPQPQ likely disrupts these interactions, further reducing p-ERK levels. These dual mechanisms highlight its therapeutic potential for T2DM management.

In conclusion, this study provides the first evidence that YPQPQ, a bioactive compound extracted from malt, not only inhibits DPP-IV expression but also reduces ERK phosphorylation levels in cells. These findings highlight the potential of YPQPQ as a novel natural DPP-IV inhibitor and underscore its possible therapeutic significance in managing chronic inflammation, metabolic disorders, and cancer. Further studies are required to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these effects.

Molecular docking

As shown in Fig. 4, molecular interaction analysis further elucidates the mechanism underlying the stability of ligand binding. Both Diprotin A (positive control) and the ligand YPQPQ are embedded within the hydrophobic core region of the protein and form stable hydrogen bonds, π-π stacking, and electrostatic interactions with key residues. Diprotin A forms multiple stable hydrogen bonds with residues such as Tyr631 and Arg125, and establishes π interactions with Tyr666, Tyr662, and Tyr547. In the YPQPQ system, residues Arg152, Lys554, W629, and Y752 stabilize the ligand through hydrogen bonds. Polar residues E205 and E206 form electrostatic interactions with the ligand, while Val711 and Tyr666 participate in hydrophobic interactions. These interactions not only restrict the local flexibility of the binding pocket region but also enhance ligand binding affinity, thereby assisting YPQPQ in achieving competitive inhibition of the substrate.

Binding interactions between the protein and ligands illustrating key interactions: deep blue represents salt bridges, red indicates electrostatic attractions, pink represents conventional hydrogen bonds, purple indicates π interactions, and green represents carbon-hydrogen bonds.

A The interaction between Diprotin A (positive control) and DPP-IV. B The Interaction between YPQPQ and DPP-IV.

In addition, we constructed receptor–ligand interaction maps for six complexes: DPP-IV–YPQPQ and positive control DPP-IV–Diprotin A obtained by ADCP, VINA, LibDock, and AlphaFold3 docking methods, as well as the FDA-approved drug Sitagliptin bound to DPP-IV (Fig. S2). Overall, the interaction maps show inhibitory characteristics; for example, residues Arg125, Glu205, Glu206, Tyr547, and Tyr662 are involved in ligand binding across all six structures, indicating consistent binding modes among the docking results. However, calculations revealed considerable RMSD differences among the four docking methods for the DPP-IV–YPQPQ complexes (Fig. S3). Given the previous benchmark assessment for protein–peptide docking36 showing that ADCP has the highest accuracy, we selected the ADCP docking results for subsequent molecular dynamics simulations.

Molecular dynamics analysis

As shown in Fig. 5, the RMSD analysis of the APO system indicates that it only stabilizes after 200 ns, with RMSD fluctuating around 4.0 Å, suggesting significant conformational flexibility and instability in the absence of ligand constraints. In contrast, the Diprotin A system stabilizes after 50 ns, with RMSD fluctuating near 2.5 Å. Similarly, the YPQPQ system reaches a stable state within 50 ns, maintaining an RMSD of approximately 2.0 Å, but it rises after 300 ns and fluctuates around 3.0 Å. The RMSD frequency distribution reflects the same trend: the APO system’s slow stabilization results in a broad RMSD range that ultimately centers around 3.7 Å, while the Diprotin A system shows a more concentrated RMSD around 2.6 Å. The YPQPQ system exhibits two distinct peaks at approximately 1.7 Å and 3.0 Å. Initial comparisons of RMSD suggest that binding of the two peptide inhibitors reduces DPP-IV’s flexibility and tends to stabilize the protein structure. RMSF analysis shows that the propeller loop region (residues 235–255) is the most flexible region in all three systems; however, compared to APO and Diprotin A, the YPQPQ-bound system exhibits higher flexibility there. The APO system also displays greater flexibility near residues 335, 520, and 715–735. Rg analysis reveals differences in overall structural compactness among the systems. The APO and YPQPQ systems exhibit slightly larger Rg fluctuations than the Diprotin A system. SASA analysis shows that binding of both YPQPQ and Diprotin A significantly reduces the solvent-accessible surface area of the protein, enhancing its thermodynamic stability. The SASA values stabilize at approximately 31,500 Å² and 31,000 Å² for the Diprotin A and YPQPQ systems, respectively. The APO system shows a higher SASA value (~33,700 Å²) with larger fluctuations, indicating that hydrophobic regions are frequently exposed to the solvent.

We focused on four key regions of DPP-IV residues: 235–255, 510–521, 710–727, and 200–209. Although three of these regions, except 200–209, do not directly participate in ligand interactions, they are likely influenced by ligand binding or mutation events, thereby affecting DPP-IV activity. The β-propeller loop spanning residues 235–255, also called the flap, has been shown by previous computational studies to regulate DPP-IV activity by controlling the frequency of its movement toward the catalytic triad core (Ser630, Asp708, His740), acting as an “open-close” gate37. The β-propeller loop at residues 510–521, referred to here as the S2-subsite loop, is closely related to the open state of the S2 binding pocket and side channel, as revealed by structural comparisons between DPP-IV and DPP8/9. Orthogonal mode analysis of molecular dynamics (MD) trajectories indicates that the C-terminal α-helix region (around residues 710–727) participates in twisting motions between two DPP-IV chains, facilitating exposure of the active site and substrate entry38. Next, we calculated covariance matrices and performed principal component analysis (PCA) on trajectories from the three systems, followed by free energy landscape mapping. As shown in Fig. 6, the APO system exhibits significant conformational flexibility, with representative conformations from two energy wells showing a shortened C-terminal α-helix and a flap lacking β-sheet structure. In contrast, the ligand-bound systems display more intact structures in key regions, such as β-sheet formation in the flap and a short α-helix in the 200–209 region, reflecting the stabilizing effect of ligand binding. In the Diprotin A system, β-sheet folding in the S2-subsite loop is shortened at 493.0 ns, forming a representative conformation of another energy well. The YPQPQ system provides the strongest stability, maintaining secondary structures robustly in all four key regions and significantly reducing conformational flexibility. However, YPQPQ exhibits two energy well conformations corresponding to the two RMSD stages in Fig. 5A or the two RMSD peaks in Fig. 5B. Structural comparison reveals that at 442.9 ns, the flap region of DPP-IV moves away from the core region, and the ligand approaches the α-helix formed by residues 200–209 more closely. These two conformations are further supported by dynamic cross-correlation matrices (DCCM, Fig. S4) and secondary structure analysis (Fig. S5). For example, in both Diprotin A and YPQPQ systems, key regions maintain more stable secondary structures, with significantly enhanced residue correlations internally. In particular, the YPQPQ system shows almost unchanged α-helix content and notably reduced β-sheet fluctuations.

We performed hydrogen bond analysis on the molecular dynamics simulation trajectories of DPP-IV–YPQPQ and DPP-IV–Diprotin A using MDAnalysis (https://www.mdanalysis.org/), setting the maximum hydrogen–acceptor atom distance to 3 Å and the donor–hydrogen–acceptor angle threshold to 150°. As shown in Fig. S6, the time-dependent hydrogen bond count comparison reveals that YPQPQ forms significantly more hydrogen bonds with DPP-IV than Diprotin A does. The hydrogen bond between Tyr and Ala654 of YPQPQ accounts for 41% of the total ligand–receptor hydrogen bonds in the DPP-IV–YPQPQ trajectory, whereas in the DPP-IV–Diprotin A system, the hydrogen bond formed between Arg471 of DPP-IV and Ile3 of Diprotin A accounts for 41% of the total hydrogen bonds. Regarding hydrogen bond stability, the average hydrogen–acceptor atom distances for hydrogen bonds formed in both DPP-IV–Diprotin A and DPP-IV–YPQPQ systems are approximately 2.8 Å, but the bond angles in the DPP-IV–YPQPQ system are closer to 180° and thus more ideal than those in the DPP-IV–Diprotin A system. Overall, YPQPQ stabilizes the local structure by forming a tight hydrogen bond network with DPP-IV.

We performed MM/PBSA binding free energy calculations for the Diprotin A and YPQPQ systems. The results showed that the total binding free energy (ΔG_total) of the Diprotin A system was −8.30 ± 1.58 kcal/mol, while that of the YPQPQ system was −14.73 ± 0.85 kcal/mol, indicating stronger binding stability between YPQPQ and the protein. The binding free energy was further decomposed into individual energy contributions. In the Diprotin A system, the van der Waals contribution (VDWAALS) was −13.58 kcal/mol, the electrostatic interaction energy (EEL) was −122.61 kcal/mol, and the polar solvation energy (EPB) was +127.89 kcal/mol, resulting in a gas-phase energy of −136.19 kcal/mol. In comparison, the YPQPQ system showed VDWAALS of −40.94 kcal/mol, EEL of −176.17 kcal/mol, and EPB of +202.38 kcal/mol, leading to a gas-phase energy of −217.11 kcal/mol. Furthermore, we performed residue-level ΔG energy decomposition for the DPP-IV–Diprotin A and DPP-IV–YPQPQ systems. As shown in Fig. S7, residues favoring binding in the YPQPQ system include Ala654, Asn710, Phe357, Tyr631, and Tyr666, while in the Diprotin A system, the key residues are Arg471 and Trp629. This is consistent with the hydrogen bond occupancy analysis; for example, Ala654, which ranks first in energy contribution in the DPP-IV–YPQPQ system, also forms the hydrogen bond with the highest occupancy, whereas Arg471 is the major contributor in the DPP-IV–Diprotin A system (Fig. S6).

Overall, the MD analysis indicates that YPQPQ forms more hydrogen bonds with the protein through multiple interactions, exhibits a more favorable (more negative) binding energy, and enhances the stability of secondary structures in key regions. The increased interactions and more negative binding energy in the complex facilitate tight binding of YPQPQ to DPP-IV, thereby competitively inhibiting the substrate. Moreover, YPQPQ restricts the conformational freedom of key regions in DPP-IV and stabilizes local secondary structures, which may weaken its functional activity or substrate-binding capability. These findings provide strong support for YPQPQ as a potential drug candidate and lay a solid theoretical foundation for subsequent molecular optimization and experimental validation.

Discussion

This study identifies YPQPQ, a malt-derived pentapeptide, as a potent natural inhibitor of dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-IV) with significant potential for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) management. By integrating computational approaches—molecular docking, deep learning models (KPGT, ConPlex), molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and network pharmacology—with experimental validation, we confirmed YPQPQ’s strong inhibitory activity (IC50: 23.87 μM) and its competitive inhibition mechanism, as evidenced by Lineweaver-Burk analysis (Ki: 54.24 μM). The KPGT model’s superior performance (R2 = 0.67, RMSE = 0.76) in predicting IC50 values underscores the efficacy of knowledge-guided deep learning in screening bioactive compounds, outperforming traditional models like GA_LR and RFR_SVR. This highlights the value of advanced computational tools in enhancing the precision of virtual screening for natural inhibitors.

YPQPQ’s binding to DPP-IV, stabilized by hydrogen bonds, π-π stacking, and electrostatic interactions with key residues (e.g., Arg152, Lys554, Tyr666), mirrors the binding profile of Diprotin A, as revealed by MD simulations. The lower binding free energy of YPQPQ (–14.73 kcal/mol) compared to Diprotin A (–8.30 kcal/mol) indicates stronger binding affinity, supported by a robust hydrogen bond network, particularly with Ala654. MD analysis further demonstrated that YPQPQ reduces DPP-IV’s conformational flexibility, stabilizing key regions like the β-propeller loop (residues 235–255), which regulates substrate access to the catalytic site. This stabilization likely contributes to YPQPQ’s competitive inhibition, limiting DPP-IV’s enzymatic activity.

Network pharmacology analysis identified MAPK1 and MAPK3 (ERK1/2) as critical DPP-IV-related targets in T2DM, with 70 overlapping genes linked to pathways like AGE-RAGE signaling, known to drive inflammation and insulin resistance39. Experimental results confirmed that YPQPQ significantly reduces ERK phosphorylation in Caco-2 cells without altering total ERK levels, suggesting modulation of the ERK signaling pathway. This effect likely stems from enhanced GLP-1 bioavailability due to DPP-IV inhibition, which activates PKA and PI3K/Akt pathways, suppressing upstream regulators like PKC40,41. Reduced p-ERK levels may mitigate inflammation and oxidative stress, key contributors to T2DM progression, highlighting YPQPQ’s dual role in enzymatic and non-enzymatic DPP-IV functions.

The absence of cytotoxicity in Caco-2 cells at the IC50 concentration (92.07% viability at 25 μM) supports YPQPQ’s safety for further development. However, its potential off-target interactions with opioid receptors and HMG-CoA reductase, as predicted by Swiss Target Prediction and Pharmmapper, warrant further investigation to ensure specificity. Future studies should employ primary cells or metabolically relevant T2DM models to validate YPQPQ’s efficacy and explore its pharmacokinetics. Additionally, optimizing YPQPQ’s structure could enhance its binding affinity and specificity, paving the way for novel T2DM therapies derived from natural sources.

Methods

Workflow

Firstly, we retrieved malt molecules from the TCMSP database42. Using four deep learning models or molecular docking, we predicted their potential as DPP-IV inhibitors. The top-ranked molecule based on average rankings underwent experimental determination of its IC50 against DPP-IV. Once inhibitory effects were confirmed, MD simulations were conducted to explore the inhibition mechanism. Simultaneously, network pharmacology was employed to identify downstream pathways through which the inhibitor might influence T2D by inhibiting DPP-IV, followed by cell experiments to validate the findings.

Materials

YPQPQ (a peptide fragment derived from Gliadin) and Gly-Pro-p-nitroanilide (Gly-Pro-pNA) were purchased from Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology (Shanghai, China). DPP-Ⅳ enzyme was purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). The antibodies against DPP-IV, ERK, and p-ERK were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (CST).

Screening of DPP-IV inhibitors

The screening strategy consists of four main parts:

Traditional molecular docking was performed using the LibDock43 module in Discovery Studio, with the receptor being DPP-IV (PDB code: 1X7044) and the ligands taken from the TCMSP malt component molecular dataset. The evaluation metric used was the LibDock score.

Traditional molecular docking was carried out using AutoDock Vina45, with the same receptor and ligands as those used in LibDock. The ligands were converted from SMILES to mol2 format using OpenBabel46.

The ConPlex tool47, a protein language model and molecular affinity prediction model, was used. By inputting protein sequences and molecular SMILES into the pre-trained ConPlex model, the binding affinity between the protein receptor and small molecules was predicted.

The IC50 values of small molecules for DPP-IV were predicted. KPGT (Knowledge-guided Pretrained Graph Transformer)48 is a novel graph transformer model that uses molecular fingerprints and descriptors as pre-guiding information for training. It has demonstrated excellent molecular prediction performance on multiple public datasets. For this study, the training strategy involved fine-tuning a pre-trained KPGT model (trained on 1 million molecules) using default parameters. We compared the KPGT model with TVBGSA_LR28, GA_LR26, and RFR_SVR27 models. These comparative methods were implemented in Python and covered molecular descriptor calculation, multicollinearity elimination, descriptor normalization, feature selection, and IC50 regression. The training data for all models was sourced from a meticulously curated dataset of 4,156 DPP-IV inhibitors’ IC50 values from the ChEMBL database (see Table S1). Regression tasks were evaluated using the following metrics: R-squared (R2), mean absolute error (MAE), mean squared error (MSE), and root mean squared error (RMSE). Results from five-fold cross-validation showed that the KPGT model performed the best on metrics such as R2, MAE, and MSE, demonstrating its exceptional ability to accurately predict the IC50 of DPP-IV inhibitors during secondary screening.

Finally, the malt component molecules were ranked based on their LibDock scores, ConPlex affinity, Vina affinity, and IC50 values predicted by the KPGT model.

To avoid biases from any single scoring metric, we ranked the malt components separately based on four computational indicators. Specifically, according to the nature of each metric, molecules with lower IC50 values predicted by the deep learning method KPGT were ranked higher; molecules with more negative binding affinities from the docking method Vina were ranked higher; and for the deep learning method ConPlex predicted affinity and the docking method LibDock scores, higher scores corresponded to higher rankings. For each molecule, the ranking positions from these four indicators were obtained, and their arithmetic mean was calculated as the molecule’s “average ranking score.” Finally, all malt components were comprehensively ranked based on this score, with higher-ranked molecules considered to have better binding potential and inhibitory activity. No additional weights were assigned to the indicators in this screening; all four scores contributed equally to the calculation.

Network pharmacology screening of targets affected by DPP-IV in T2DM

We searched for T2DM targets using keywords such as “Type 2 diabetes”, “T2DM”, and “Type 2 diabetes mellitus” in DISGENET (https://disgenet.com), DRUGBANK (https://go.drugbank.com/), GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM (https://www.omim.org/), and TTD (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/). We also searched for papers related to the DPP-IV pathway in PubMed and extracted 100 genes affected by DPP-IV. Since different databases may use varying gene names or IDs, we standardized all gene identifiers using Gene Symbols. For disease-related targets, DISGENET, DRUGBANK, GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD all provided Gene Symbols. In cases where databases such as TTD used gene aliases or non-standard names, we employed BioMart (https://www.ensembl.org/biomart) to convert them into standardized Gene Symbols. All results were then merged and deduplicated using Excel. For DPP-IV-regulated targets obtained from literature, we manually converted them to Gene Symbols using the UniProt database, followed by deduplication using Excel as well. Afterward, we used the VENNY website (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) to find the intersection, identifying 70 overlapping genes as potential targets that DPP-IV affects in T2DM. We then performed KEGG enrichment analysis on these potential genes using R.

Effect of the inhibitor YPQPQ on the viability of Caco-2 cells

Caco-2 cells, derived from human colorectal carcinoma, differentiate into a monolayer structure closely resembling small intestinal epithelial cells49. Given the high expression of DPP-IV in intestinal epithelial cells, Caco-2 cells were selected as an appropriate model for investigating DPP-IV inhibitors in this study50. Caco-2 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Exponentially growing Caco-2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 48 hours. Western Blot analysis

Exponentially growing Caco-2 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 48 hours. After removing the culture medium, cells in the sample group were treated with the inhibitor YPQPQ at its IC50 concentration for 2 hours and 4 hours, respectively, while the control group received no treatment.

The cells were lysed using RIPA buffer for 5 minutes, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C to collect total protein. The protein concentration was quantified using the BCA assay under different treatment conditions. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 37 °C for 40 minutes to minimize nonspecific binding. Primary antibodies against DPP-IV (1:1000), ERK (1:1000), p-ERK (1:1000), and β-actin (1:20,000) were incubated with the membrane overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated with a secondary antibody (1:2000) at 37 °C for 40 minutes, followed by additional washes with TBST. Subsequently, a chemiluminescent substrate was applied to the membrane, and protein bands were visualized using an imaging system.

Molecular docking

The structure of the YPQPQ was constructed using AlphaFold351. The crystal structure of DPP-IV (PDB ID: 1X70) was processed using AutoDockTools, saving the single chain and removing water molecules and other impurities. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock CrankPep (ADCP)52.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Diprotin A (Ile-Pro-Ile) is a well-known DPP-IV inhibitory peptide that has been widely reported as a positive experimental control in various studies, with inhibitory activities such as an IC50 of 13.10 μM53, 6.36 μM54, and 6.6 μM55, generally around the 10 μM level. More importantly, its crystal structure has been resolved (PDB code: 1NU8), making it a suitable reference for computational studies as well56. Therefore, in this study, Diprotin A was selected as a positive control for computational comparison with the peptide YPQPQ. In addition, an apo (ligand-free) protein system was used as a negative control, resulting in a total of three systems for analysis.

All molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using AMBER 2457, employing standard MD simulation protocols. Before the simulations, the systems were solvated, and ions were added to maintain charge neutrality. Specifically, the target protein-ligand complexes were loaded into the simulation system and parameterized using the AMBER ff19SB force field58 nd the OPC water model59. The systems were solvated within a cubic periodic box with a 10 Å buffer of water molecules around the complex. Initial energy minimization was performed to remove any potential steric clashes or geometric strain.

The MD simulations included a heating phase, during which the system temperature was gradually increased from 0 K to 310 K under the NVT ensemble. This was followed by an NPT simulation phase to maintain a constant pressure of 1 atm. During the simulations, a time step of 1 fs was used for integration, and SHAKE constraints were applied to fix all bond lengths involving hydrogen atoms. Long-range electrostatics were treated using the particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method60. Each system underwent a 500 ns production run, with atomic coordinates saved every 10 ps for subsequent analysis.

Trajectory data from the MD simulations were analyzed using various metrics to evaluate the stability, rigidity, and compactness of the protein structures. Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) was used to assess the overall structural stability of the protein-ligand complexes, while root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) quantified residue-specific flexibility. The radius of gyration (Rg) was calculated to evaluate the compactness of the systems, and the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) was measured to assess changes in solvent exposure due to ligand binding.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to analyze conformational sampling and to explore the impact of ligand binding on protein conformational freedom. Dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) and secondary structure analyses were performed to investigate the influence of ligand binding on protein dynamics and secondary structure stability.

Binding free energy calculations were conducted using the MM/PBSA method to evaluate the contribution of ligand binding to protein stability. The calculations were based on 5000 frames sampled every 50 frames from a 500-nanosecond molecular dynamics simulation trajectory. The total binding free energy was decomposed into electrostatic, van der Waals, and solvation energy components to provide a detailed understanding of the interaction. Entropy correction was not performed in these calculations to reduce computational cost.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation of three parallel sets of experiments. All statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and processed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. The significance level for statistical analysis was set at p < 0.05. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001.
