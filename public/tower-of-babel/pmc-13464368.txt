Double pylorus: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Frank Enoch Gyamfi, Rita Agyei Boakye, Joseph Yorke, Francis Opoku Twene, Daniel Adjei, Charles Bankah, Stephen Tabiri, Tonnies Abeku Buckman, Michael Ofoe Adinku, Dennis Yorke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDouble pylorus (DP), or duplication of the pylorus, is an uncommon condition that can be either congenital or acquired. Acquired DP (ADP) occurs when a peptic ulcer erodes and creates a fistula between the duodenal bulb and the distal stomach. There are a few case reports of this condition worldwide with limited data in countries with low resource settings including Ghana. Patients having acquired double pylorus have symptoms related to the underlying cause, such as peptic ulcer disease or other conditions like gastric cancer. In congenital cases, most are asymptomatic.Case PresentationWe present two cases of a 72-year-old Akan woman from Berekum in the Bono region and 15-year-old girl from Kumasi in the Ashanti region both Ghanaians who presented symptoms suggestive of peptic ulcer disease diagnosed incidentally at endoscopy, who had relief following medical treatment. The 72-year-old woman had symptoms suggestive of peptic ulcer disease over the last 5 months. She had a history of high blood pressure and took medications for both diseases. She denied the intake of alcohol and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). The 15-year-old girl had been complaining of abdominal pain for the past 6 years and had been seen at several facilities without a firm diagnosis. A biopsy of the ulcer for both patients was taken for histopathology, which later confirmed a gastric ulcer with no malignant features. The Helicobacter pylori quick test proved positive using a qualitative rapid urease test to detect the organism in gastric mucosa biopsy taken during endoscopy and the patients received triple therapy that consists of Metronidazole 400 mg three times daily, Amoxicillin 1 g twice daily, and Omeprazole 20 mg twice daily all for 10 days. This will provide more details in managing double pylorus in the aged and young populations.ConclusionDouble pylorus is a rare condition caused primarily by peptic ulcer disease. It may be congenital or acquired. Acquired cases are more prevalent and typically more symptomatic. Endoscopy is a reliable diagnostic modality.

Introduction

Double pylorus or duplication of the pylorus is a very unusual endoscopic finding that has been reported only in 0.001% to 0.4% of upper gastrointestinal endoscopies and consists of two channels connecting the prepyloric antrum to the duodenal bulb [1]. It can be characterized as a fistula that occurs between the lesser curvature of the stomach antrum and the upper wall of the duodenal bulb and with nonspecific dyspeptic complaints, such as epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting, and bloating. Double pylorus is usually found incidentally, and most of the patients do not have clinical complaints and remain hidden for a long time; the true incidence is unknown, but the recent study shows the incidence in males 2 times higher than in females [2, 3]. Although its pathogenesis is not yet fully understood, the presence of H. pylori in some systemic diseases, long-term use of drugs that delay ulcer healing has been implicated in the disease’s etiology.

Case presentation 1

We present a 72-year-old Akan woman from Berekum in the Bono region of Ghana who presented at the outpatient clinic with symptoms suggestive of peptic ulcer disease over the last 5 months. She had a history of high blood pressure and took medications for both diseases. She denied the intake of alcohol and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Physical examination revealed only mild upper abdominal tenderness and the blood tests showed a hemoglobin level of 11.4 g/l. At endoscopy, the oesophageal mucosa appeared grossly normal. The stomach had normal ruggae and the appearance of mucosa in fundus, body, and antrum. There was a double pylorus and a clean base ulcer adjacent to the exit of the accessory channel. Both channels easily admitted the endoscope (Fig. 1). A biopsy of the ulcer was taken for histopathology which later confirmed peptic ulcer with no malignant features. The test for H. pylori was positive, and the patient received triple therapy (Metronidazole 400 mg three times daily, Amoxicillin 1 g twice daily, and Omeprazole 20 mg twice daily all for 10 days). The patient, however, did not return for the scheduled repeat endoscopy and upon follow-up, she said she was no longer symptomatic and did not want further endoscopy.Fig. 1Endoscopic view showing main channel and accessory channel (arrowed blue) separated by a bridge of tissue

Case presentation 2

We present a case of a 15-year-old girl from Kumasi in the Ashanti region who had been complaining of abdominal pain for the past 6 years and has been seen at several facilities without a firm diagnosis. She was finally referred to Legacy Hospital for an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. The blood parameters were unremarkable (HB 9.6 g/dl). Physical examination was unremarkable except for mild epigastric tenderness. The pylori test was positive. The abdominopelvic ultrasound was normal. Later, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was done to reveal double pylorus and multiple antral polyps (Fig. 2). The patient was treated with H. pylori eradication therapy (Omeprazole 400 mg, Ciprofloxacin 500 mg, and Tramadazole 500 mg twice a day for 10 days) and had been monitored for the past 6 months. The symptoms were eliminated after a few weeks from the inception of therapy and the hemoglobin was normal after 5 months with no sign of relapse.Fig. 2Endoscopic view from the antrum demonstrates double pylorus. Discussion

Double pylorus is said to be present when two separate openings connect the antrum of the stomach and the duodenal bulb, with the first case reported in 1971 [4]. The etiology of congenital double pylorus has been attributed to a tubular duplication of the pylorus and this uncommon condition have a reported endoscopic incidence between 0.02 and 0.08% at endoscopy in European and African countries, even though the estimated actual incidence is 0.01% to 0.4% even though the actual incidence of this abnormality is estimated to be between 0.06 and 0.4% [4–6]. Interestingly, the incidence in Ghana is still unknown. Congenital double pylorus is a very rare type of the condition, which is twice as common in males as in females. This defect occurs during embryological development when there is a flaw in the canalization of the pylorus and mostly asymptomatic and may be an incidental finding. Patients usually do not present with pyrosis or any autonomic-associated symptoms [6].

Congenital DP is often found in children and young adult with no prior history of peptic ulcer or NSAID use. The finding is mostly coincidental as they are usually asymptomatic with associated anomalies like malrotation and pancreas divisum [2]. Diagnosis is made accidentally in patients who undergo upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. Findings at endoscopy may show an inflamed mucosa or ulceration of the antrum. A number of times, patients may have an ulcer over the accessory channel, even though in some patients the mucosa may also appear normal [7]. Although congenital double pylorus does not require any therapy or extensive investigation, endoscopic treatment, using a biliary sphincterotome for dividing the bridge tissue, is feasible to be performed [3]. Furthermore, surgical intervention should be considered when endoscopic treatment has failed, or a peptic ulcer persists despite the maximum medical therapy due to the contribution of double pylorus to bile acid reflux or in the case of free perforation.

Conversely, acquired double pylorus is found in adults and elderly with a prior history of peptic ulcer disease or NSAID use. The accessory channel is mostly located on the lesser curvature of the natrum. The bridge between the two channels may look irregular or fibrotic. Differentiating between the two is essential as congenital DP requires no treatment as it is a stable anatomic variant. The accessory channel of the acquired DP however lacks the protective structural integrity of normal pylorus [2, 8]. Moreover, the accessory channel may form as a result of a complication of a gastric or duodenal ulcer associated with NSAIDs use or Helicobacter pylori infection. Complications from gastric malignancy have also been implicated [7]. The affected patients may present with risk factors or symptoms of peptic ulceration, but there is no pathognomonic sign. Our patients, therefore, are likely to have an acquired type of double pylorus. The 71-year-old presented with dyspeptic symptoms, chronic NSAIDs use and had evidence of ulcers at endoscopy. This should be differentiated from a gastric diverticulum, which is a saccular outpouching without the accessory channel communicating with the pylorus [2, 7].

Treatment is initially medical in eradicating Helicobacter pylori, which, according to some reports correlates with double pylorus. It is also essential to also discontinue any medications, steroids, or NSAIDs [8]. If symptoms persist despite adequate medical treatment, then endoscopic or surgical intervention may be warranted.

Conclusion

Double pylorus is an unusual endoscopic finding and has been linked with Helicobacter pylori, congenital abnormalities, comorbidities, and recurrent delivery of ulcerogenic medications. In acquired double pylorus, treatment with anti-ulcer medications should be performed. We recommend that endoscopists should be aware of the double pylorus to avoid complications during side-view endoscopy, such longitudinal EUS.
