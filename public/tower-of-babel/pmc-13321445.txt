Thirty-five years of dermatology workforce inequality and centralization in Austria
Authors: Aleksandar Josipovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe equitable distribution of medical specialists is critical for ensuring timely access to dermatologic care. However, the long-term patterns of dermatologist allocation have not been systematically examined in Austria. This paper addresses the research question: How has the dermatology workforce in Austria been distributed across its nine federal states from 1990 to 2025, and what factors contribute to geographic inequalities in access to dermatologic care? The study presents the first 35-year longitudinal analysis of the distribution of the dermatology workforce in Austria. Using annual dermatologist counts and population data, we calculate dermatologist density per 100,000 inhabitants, evaluate geographic inequality using the Gini coefficient, the Coefficient of Variation (CV), and the Theil index, and assess metropolitan dominance using Location Quotients (LQ). Additionally, we forecast the national workforce supply to 2030 using ARIMA modeling. The results reveal a substantial increase in the national dermatology workforce, reflected in a consistent rise in dermatologist density across all states, and a significant reduction in geographic inequality. The Gini coefficient decreased from 0.319 to 0.159, the CV from 0.684 to 0.315, and the Theil Index from 0.187 to 0.045. Despite these improvements, Vienna remains the primary dermatologic hub, with its dermatologist density rising from 9.23 to 17.01 per 100,000 inhabitants and LQ values consistently exceeding 3.0. The ARIMA(1,1,1) forecast predicts continued workforce growth to over 2,130 dermatologists by 2030, yet no natural redistribution toward underserved states is projected. These findings are interpreted through the lenses of the Inverse Care Law, the WHO Human Resources for Health framework, urban centralization theory, and path dependency. While Austria’s dermatology workforce is expanding, it remains structurally imbalanced and unlikely to self-correct. Introduction

The distribution of medical specialists is crucial for ensuring timely access to care, improving health system performance, and promoting equity at the population level. Dermatology, in particular, plays a critical role in addressing conditions such as melanoma, chronic inflammatory diseases, and suspicious lesions, which require prompt specialist intervention. However, geographic disparities in access to dermatologic services often limit care for rural and underserved populations.

Across Europe, the maldistribution of dermatology professionals is well documented, and Austria mirrors this trend. Vienna, as the country’s primary dermatologic hub, is home to most clinics, university hospitals, and private practices. At the same time, federal states such as Burgenland, Kärnten, Tirol, and Vorarlberg continue to have comparatively limited resources. Despite the importance of addressing these disparities, no previous longitudinal study has examined Austria’s dermatology workforce using a consistent multi-decade dataset. This study seeks to answer the following research question: How has the dermatology workforce in Austria been distributed across its nine federal states from 1990 to 2025, and what factors contribute to geographic inequalities in access to dermatologic care?

This study fills this gap by providing the first 35-year longitudinal analysis (1990–2025) of dermatology workforce distribution across all nine federal states in Austria. The study’s primary objective is to assess the national growth, regional inequality, metropolitan concentration, and future workforce projections. By analyzing annual dermatologist counts and population data, this paper offers insight into the structural inequalities within the dermatology workforce in Austria.

The findings are interpreted through established frameworks, including the Inverse Care Law, the WHO Human Resources for Health (HRH) model, urban centralization theory, and path dependency. Pittman et al. [16] emphasize the importance of understanding how workforce distribution affects access to healthcare, a critical factor for designing equitable policies to address shortages and over-concentration in specific regions.

Theoretical framework

Inverse care law

Hart’s [8] Inverse Care Law posits that those with the greatest need for care often have the least access to it. This principle is particularly relevant in dermatology, where rural and aging populations face significant barriers to accessing specialist care. In Austria, these geographic disparities highlight the unequal distribution of dermatologic services, particularly in peripheral regions. Recent studies reaffirm this, with Cookson et al., [5] noting that healthcare access remains inversely related to need, especially in underserved areas.

WHO Human Resources for Health (HRH) framework

The WHO HRH framework emphasizes that equitable distribution is essential for ensuring an adequate healthcare workforce [21]. While a growing national workforce can be beneficial in total numbers, its value is limited if it is not distributed in alignment with the population’s needs. This framework underscores the need for targeted interventions to address regional workforce imbalances in Austria. Recent global estimates of health workforce distribution show persistent inequities between regions and populations, threatening the achievement of equity and universal health coverage unless addressed through deliberate workforce planning and policy [4].

Urban centralization theory

Urban Centralization Theory explains the tendency for specialists to cluster in metropolitan areas, driven by factors such as access to academic institutions, professional networks, private-sector opportunities, and higher income potential [17]. In Austria, Vienna exemplifies this dynamic, housing the majority of dermatology services and facilities, reinforcing its status as the country’s dominant dermatologic center.

The evidence from this study strongly supports the theory, as Vienna’s dermatologist density consistently exceeds 3.0 per 100,000 inhabitants, with Location Quotient (LQ) values showing extreme centralization from 1990 to 2025 (LQ = 4.19 in 1990 and 3.20 in 2025).

Despite the overall increase in dermatologist numbers across Austria, this study reveals a persistent imbalance in the distribution of the workforce. Smaller regions, such as Burgenland and Vorarlberg, continue to lag behind, with a significantly lower density of dermatologists. The results from this study further align with the theory by demonstrating that workforce expansion has disproportionately benefited metropolitan areas, particularly Vienna. Additionally, access to dermatological care remains hindered in rural areas, not only by the concentration of specialists in urban areas but also by factors such as health literacy and transportation barriers [9].

While Vienna’s dermatologist density has risen significantly (from 9.23 to 17.01 per 100,000 inhabitants between 1990 and 2025), the peripheral states have seen slower growth, reinforcing the self-reinforcing nature of urban centralization. This pattern is consistent with other healthcare sectors globally, where urban areas attract more professionals due to the concentration of resources and opportunities, leaving rural areas underserved [4].

Path dependency

Path Dependency theory suggests that historical processes, such as Vienna’s early establishment as a dermatology hub, shape and often lock in future workforce distribution patterns [15]. Once centralization is established, it becomes self-reinforcing, making it difficult to reverse the trend without targeted intervention. This concept is key to understanding why Vienna continues to dominate the dermatology workforce despite national efforts to expand it. Recent studies also emphasize the role of historical processes in shaping health workforce distribution, particularly in relation to migration and the movement of health workers [18].

Literature review

International evidence consistently demonstrates significant geographic imbalances in the distribution of medical specialists across high-income health systems [13]. Recent OECD reports show widening gaps between metropolitan and peripheral regions, underscoring the structural nature of specialist centralization [14].

Similar trends are observed in Germany, where regional deprivation is a key factor influencing both the utilization and need for specialists [10], and in Switzerland, where long-term studies reveal a stable concentration of physicians in urban areas [19]. In England, inequities in the availability of specialist services are evident, with a disproportionate concentration in urban centers [12].

Dermatology-specific evidence from outside Europe further supports these findings: the United States continues to face widening gaps between metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas [3], and countries such as Saudi Arabia report persistent maldistribution within their dermatology workforce [1]. These international studies reinforce the structural challenges associated with the geographic maldistribution of healthcare professionals.

In Austria, most existing workforce analyses focus on general practitioner (GP) shortages or the overall physician supply, with limited attention to dermatology-specific trends [20]. This study addresses that gap by conducting a comprehensive 35-year analysis of dermatology workforce trends, inequality, and centralization across all nine federal states in Austria. By focusing on the dermatology workforce specifically, this research provides a clearer understanding of regional disparities and metropolitan centralization in Austria’s healthcare system.

Methodology

This study employs a quantitative, longitudinal research design to analyze the distribution of the dermatology workforce across Austria’s nine federal states from 1990 to 2025. The analysis integrates multiple components, including absolute dermatologist counts, population-adjusted dermatologist densities, inequality indicators, centralization measures, and workforce forecasting models.

Data sources

All dermatologist workforce data were obtained directly from the Österreichische Ärztekammer (Austrian Medical Chamber), the official national authority maintaining the registry of licensed physicians in Austria. These data represent verified annual totals of board-certified dermatologists by state. Population figures were sourced from Statistik Austria. No imputation or secondary data compilation was applied, ensuring that all calculations are based on original administrative data.

Data structure

The dataset covers the nine federal states: Vienna, Burgenland, Kärnten, Niederösterreich, Oberösterreich, Salzburg, Steiermark, Tirol, and Vorarlberg. The year 1998 is missing in the source file and was excluded without interpolation to prevent artificial trend distortion.

Given that the 1998 dataset was incomplete, we opted to exclude that year from the analysis rather than impute the missing data. This decision was made to avoid introducing potential biases or artificial trends in the dataset. The univariate ARIMA(1,1,1) model uses the available data points to make projections. The model does not incorporate external factors such as changes in policy or demographics, which may influence the future distribution of dermatologists.

Location Quotient (LQ)

To assess Vienna’s dominance in the dermatology workforce, the Location Quotient (LQ) was employed as a measure of regional overrepresentation:

LQ = (Dermatologists in Vienna / National Total) ÷ (1/9).

Here, 1/9 represents the expected share of dermatologists if the distribution were perfectly equal across all nine federal states.

An LQ > 1 indicates overconcentration, and LQ > 3 is interpreted as extreme centralization, which raises concerns about equitable access to care.

Forecasting future workforce supply (2026–2030)

The model-based projections indicate that Austria’s dermatology workforce will continue to expand over the next five years. An ARIMA(1,1,1) model was selected for forecasting, based on the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values among all candidate models. The model’s residuals were evaluated using the Ljung–Box test, which showed no significant autocorrelation (Q = 0.757, p = 0.9999), confirming that the model appropriately captured the underlying data-generating process.

The forecast predicts sustained national workforce growth through 2030 (see Table 4). A visualization of the forecast, including 95% confidence intervals, is shown in Fig. 2. These confidence intervals reflect the range of uncertainty associated with the forecasted values and provide an estimate of the potential variability in the projections. The total number of dermatologists is expected to exceed 2,130 in the absence of significant structural or policy changes.

However, because the model is univariate and relies solely on historical trends, it does not account for potential reforms in specialist training, shifts in migration patterns, demographic changes, or future constraints in dermatology residency capacity. While the ARIMA model provides robust projections based on historical data, it is essential to acknowledge that structural changes, such as changes in specialist training or demographic shifts, could alter these projections.

Similar forecasting analyses in the United States have projected widening gaps in dermatology workforce adequacy, particularly between metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas [3]. This study’s projections, shown in Fig. 2 and complemented by the numerical results in Table 4, offer insights into Austria’s future dermatology workforce dynamics.

Discussion

This 35-year analysis provides strong evidence of long-term structural inequality in Austria’s dermatology workforce. While the total number of dermatologists increased substantially across all federal states, the distribution remained uneven. Population-adjusted data confirm that Vienna consistently maintained the highest dermatologist density, while regions such as Burgenland and Vorarlberg remained persistently underserved. Beyond geographic availability, access to specialist care is also influenced by factors such as accessibility, accommodation, and acceptability, as outlined in healthcare access models [6]. It is important to note that the workforce data used in this study is based on headcount, without differentiation between full-time and part-time professionals. Given the increasing trend toward part-time work, particularly in rural regions, the headcount measure may underestimate the actual availability of dermatologists in these areas. Future research should consider incorporating full-time equivalent (FTE) data to provide a more accurate picture of workforce capacity.

Inequality indicators demonstrate measurable improvement over time, with significant declines in the Gini coefficient, CV, and Theil index. However, full parity was not achieved. Vienna’s Location Quotient (LQ) remained consistently above 3.0, highlighting persistent metropolitan centralization despite national workforce growth. These findings align with earlier evidence from England and Wales, which also shows enduring spatial disparities in the distribution of general practitioners [7].

These national trends are mirrored in other European health systems, where sustained specialist growth has not led to spatial equalization [10, 12, 19]. In addition, countries such as Portugal and France have implemented regional incentive programs and decentralization of training programs in underserved areas, which have been shown to promote more equitable distribution of healthcare professionals, including dermatologists. For instance, Portugal’s incentive packages, as detailed by Alidina et al., [2], have been effective in encouraging physicians to practice in rural and underserved regions. Similarly, France has taken steps to tackle medical deserts through mandatory policies aimed at redistributing healthcare workers, as outlined by Montmartin et al., [11]. Building on the success of these policies in Portugal and France, Austria could consider implementing similar regional incentive programs and decentralizing medical education in underserved regions to attract dermatologists to rural areas. Moreover, targeted financial incentives, such as housing allowances or loan forgiveness, could further motivate dermatologists to relocate outside Vienna and enhance access to care in peripheral regions.

Dermatology-specific studies outside Europe also highlight similar urban-rural divides, particularly in the United States and Saudi Arabia [1, 3]. These parallels suggest that the maldistribution of dermatologists in Austria reflects broader structural challenges in specialist workforce planning rather than isolated national factors.

These findings are consistent with Hart’s (1971) Inverse Care Law, which posits that populations with the greatest healthcare needs—often rural, older, or geographically peripheral—tend to have the least access to specialists. The WHO HRH framework further underscores that workforce adequacy depends not only on total supply but also on the equitable geographic distribution of professionals [21]. Urban centralization theory explains why Vienna continues to attract specialists [17], while path dependency illustrates how early advantages, such as Vienna’s established dermatology infrastructure, become self-reinforcing, making redistribution unlikely without targeted intervention [15]. As the Path Dependency theory suggests, once a city like Vienna has become the dominant center for dermatology services, it becomes self-reinforcing over time, making redistribution difficult without targeted intervention.

Policy implications

To address the entrenched maldistribution of dermatologists in Austria, multi-level and evidence-based policy strategies are required:

Decentralized training

Expanding dermatology residency positions in cities such as Graz, Linz, and Innsbruck would help alleviate Vienna’s dominance and incentivize the redistribution of specialists. A feasible approach could include creating 2–3 additional residency positions per cycle in each of these locations, aligned with regional service demand.

Regional incentive packages

Targeted incentives, including financial compensation, housing allowances, loan forgiveness, and multi-year contracts, could attract dermatologists to underserved regions like Burgenland, Kärnten, and Vorarlberg.

Strengthening regional dermatology units

Investing in outpatient clinics, hospital-based dermatology services, and regional diagnostic capacity would establish stable professional hubs, reducing reliance on Vienna-based care.

National teledermatology system

A coordinated teledermatology program can help overcome access barriers, especially in rural areas, by providing triage, follow-up, and initial assessments of suspicious lesions.

Annual monitoring

Regular publication of key national workforce indicators (e.g., Gini, CV, Theil, LQ, and dermatologist-to-population ratios) would facilitate continuous evaluation and adaptive workforce planning.

Conclusion

Austria’s dermatology workforce has expanded significantly over the past 35 years; however, its spatial distribution remains structurally imbalanced. While inequality metrics show measurable improvement, the persistent gaps between Vienna and peripheral states continue to limit equitable access to dermatologic care. Vienna’s enduring overrepresentation and the stability of its LQ values indicate that national workforce growth alone does not ensure geographic balance.

Achieving equity in dermatology care will require more than increasing the number of specialists. Without targeted reforms, such as decentralized training pathways, regionally tailored incentive schemes, and expanded dermatology capacity outside Vienna, the workforce will grow without addressing regional imbalances. Structural imbalance, rather than workforce scarcity, remains the primary barrier to equitable dermatologic care in Austria.

Future research

Future studies should integrate age, morbidity, and socioeconomic factors to more accurately estimate regional demand for dermatologic care. Comparative qualitative research exploring dermatologists’ motivations for selecting practice locations could provide insights into the drivers of metropolitan clustering.

Further research could also examine the public-private segmentation of the dermatology workforce, cross-specialty comparisons, and the role of teledermatology in addressing geographic disparities.

Finally, advanced forecasting models incorporating demographic projections, training capacity, and policy scenarios would strengthen long-term workforce planning for Austria.
