Structural schemes for Hamiltonian systems
Stéphane Clain, Emmanuel Franck, Victor Michel-Dansac

Abstract
We present an adaptation of the so-called structural method [ 6 ] for Hamiltonian systems, and redesign the method for this specific context, which involves two coupled differential systems. Structural schemes decompose the problem into two sets of equations: the physical equations, which describe the local dynamics of the system, and the structural equations, which only involve the discretization of a very compact stencil. They have desirable properties, such as unconditional stability and high-order accuracy [ 7 ] . We first give a general description of the scheme for the one-body case (which corresponds to e.g. spring-mass interactions or pendulum motion), before extending the technique to the multi-body case (treating e.g. the n n -body system). The scheme is also written in the case of a non-separable system (e.g. a charged particle in an electromagnetic field). We prove that the method is exactly energy-preserving for quadratic Hamiltonians, and we give numerical evidence of the method’s efficiency, its capacity to preserve invariant quantities such as the total energy, and draw comparisons with the traditional symplectic methods.



Article

1
Introduction

Hamiltonian systems, a class of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) that arise from Hamiltonian mechanics, play a fundamental role in the mathematical modelling of physical systems with conserved quantities, such as total energy for a closed system. The dynamics of such systems are described by the principle of stationary action, leading to the Euler-Lagrange equations and the associated Lagrangian function
[
18
]
.
Derived from the Hamiltonian function, Hamiltonian systems govern the time evolution of state variables such as positions and momenta.
Hamiltonian systems are ubiquitous in various fields, including celestial mechanics, where they describe planetary orbits, or plasma physics, capturing the behavior of charged particles. Their inherent structure, characterized by symplectic geometry and energy preservation, has to be considered in their numerical approximation.

Due to their rich structure, the numerical approximation of Hamiltonian systems presents several challenges. One of the main issues is the preservation of geometric features, such as symplecticity and conserved quantities like energy or angular momentum, which are integral to the physical behavior of the system.
Standard numerical methods, even of very high order, such as explicit or implicit Runge-Kutta schemes, often fail to maintain these properties during simulations, generating error accumulations for long run simulations that can become critical and totally spoil the quality of the solution
[
2
]
. Additionally, the typically oscillatory nature of the solutions to such systems, particularly in applications like plasma physics, require specific methods to enable of handling multiple time scales without compromising accuracy or stability. On the other hand, the high-dimensional phase spaces involved in many problems further exacerbate computational costs, requiring efficient algorithms that balance precision with practicality.

These challenges have motivated the development of structure-preserving methods, such as symplectic integrators and geometric numerical schemes
[
9
]
, that ensure robust long-term stability by providing a good approximation of the total energy or other invariants. The design of schemes that preserve geometric structures for ODEs provide accurate and stable numerical methods. Some classes of partial differential equations, after well-adapted spatial discretization, are written as high-dimensional Hamiltonian ODEs. As examples, we mention the works of
[
16
,
19
]
in plasma physics or
[
26
]
in climatology where these approaches, using geometric structure-preserving time discretization, have shown their superiority.

Hamiltonian systems typically involve a system of two coupled ODEs, one describing the time evolution of a position in phase space, and the second one governing the time evolution of the momentum (or velocity). The simplest symplectic algorithm is a semi-implicit Euler integrator, where the first equation is integrated explicitly, and the second one implicitly. Therefore, it requires solving a (potentially non-linear) implicit equation at each time step.
However, this integrator is only first-order accurate. In fact, one of the most well-known symplectic algorithms for Hamiltonian systems is the
Störmer
-Verlet method, see
[
28
,
10
]
, which can be seen as a second-order accurate version of the semi-implicit Euler scheme.
From this second-order algorithm, it is relatively easy to construct high-order methods thanks to the composition strategy, introduced in
[
27
,
29
,
21
]
and summarized in
[
11
]
. Nevertheless, one drawback of this approach is that the number of function evaluations increases exponentially with the order of accuracy. Other examples of high-order schemes are provided in
[
20
,
24
]
, and other composition schemes include the ones from
[
15
]
, which still
suffer
from the same drawback in terms of number of function evaluations.

The structural method was recently introduced, in
[
6
,
7
]
, to solve ODE systems. It provides a systematic way of constructing implicit schemes with a highly compact stencil, which have an arbitrarily high order of accuracy together with unconditional stability. The technique relies on separating the physical part of the problem, i.e., the system of ODEs, from the discretization, which only involves the unknown quantities associated with the grid. In practice, the data is gathered into blocks, corresponding to several time steps to be solved simultaneously. This methodology was also adapted to one-dimensional boundary-valued problems in
[
8
]
. In this case, the structural method achieves a sixth-order accurate approximations using a tiny stencil of three points.
Very recently, a complete study of the structural method for ODE systems
[
7
]
has been carried out where linear stability, convergence, numerical errors and spectral resolution
were analyzed
together with a wide spectrum of benchmarks to assess the accuracy and stability and the ability to handle stiff problems.

In this paper, we develop and adapt the structural method to Hamiltonian systems, considering the specificities of such systems, namely the conservation of several invariants, and the two-variable nature of the problem. The paper is organized as follows. First, we present the structural method for ordinary differential equations in
Section

2
, introducing two formulations, the first using only the ODE and the second adding information related to the time derivative of the ODE. Then, the structural method is applied to Hamiltonian
one-body
problems in
Section

3
and to general
multi-body
Hamiltonian systems in
Section

5
, thus constructing high-order accurate and stable schemes for Hamiltonian systems. Finally, we present numerical results in
Section

6
to demonstrate the efficiency and accuracy of the proposed schemes. Several Hamiltonian systems are considered, from simple separable examples to more complex non-separable ones, to illustrate the performance of the structural method in various contexts.

2
The structural method for ODEs

We provide a short review of the structural method based on the so-called Physical and Structural Equations (denoted
PE
and
SE
respectively) detailed in
[
6
,
7
]
. To this end, let us consider the Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) for
t\in[0,T]

\dot{x}=f(x),\hskip 20.00003ptx(0)=x_{0}.

(1)

Traditional schemes blend the discretization with the physical equation, that is, the function that describe the dynamic of the physical system. For example, the popular Crank-
Nicolson
scheme reads

\frac{x_{n+1}-x_{n}}{\Delta t}=\frac{f(x_{n})+f(x_{n+1})}{2}

where the left-hand side is the time derivative discretization, while the right-hand side represents the physics (i.e., the function
f
that characterizes the physical problem). Obviously, one can split the scheme into two equations, namely

D_{n+1}=f(Z_{n+1})\text{\hskip 20.00003ptand \hskip 20.00003pt}2(Z_{n+1}-Z_{n})-\Delta t(D_{n}+D_{n+1})=0,

where
Z_{n}
and
D_{n}
are approximations of the Zeroth-order derivative
x(t_{n})
and the first-order Derivative
\dot{x}(t_{n})
, applying the same notations for
Z_{n+1}
and
D_{n+1}
at the time
t_{n+1}
.
The left relation is called the Physical Equation since it only involves the physics of the problem and not the discretization; the right one is called the Structural Equation since it only depends on the grid structure, and involves no physics.

An extension relies on taking the time derivative of the ODE, which reads
\ddot{x}=f^{\prime}(x)\dot{x}
. Denoting by
S_{n}
an approximation of the Second derivative
\ddot{x}(t_{n})
, we now obtain two physical equations, respectively denoted by
{\texttt{PE}}[1]
and
{\texttt{PE}}[2]
:

D_{n+1}=f(Z_{n+1})\text{\hskip 20.00003ptand \hskip 20.00003pt}S_{n+1}=f^{\prime}(Z_{n+1})D_{n+1}.

Since we have three unknowns
(Z_{n+1},D_{n+1},S_{n+1})
, we need one additional equation that represents the discretization.
We choose the following relation, exact for polynomial functions up to degree 4, and called the structural equation
{\texttt{SE}}[1]
:

12(Z_{n+1}-Z_{n})-6\Delta t(D_{n+1}+D_{n})+\Delta t^{2}(S_{n+1}-S_{n})=0.

Given the values
(Z_{n+1},D_{n+1},S_{n+1})
at a time
t_{n}
, we seek solutions at
t_{n+1}
of
{\texttt{PE}}[1]
,
{\texttt{PE}}[2]
, and
{\texttt{SE}}[1]
. Note that the problem is fully implicit and requires solving a small nonlinear system as soon as
f
is nonlinear.

Following the example, the idea of structural method consists in splitting the problem with, on the one hand, the Physical Equations and,
on
the other hand, the Structural Equations. The two sets of equations involve the unknowns over a block of size
R
steps corresponding to the time step
t_{n+1}
until
t_{n+R}
, given the initial configuration at the time
t_{n}
. We obtain a non-linear system combining the function approximations together with the derivatives for all the intermediate steps.

We now detail the structural method for the scalar ODE (
1
) in the
ZD
and
ZDS
formulations, respectively involving the zeroth- and first-order derivatives in
Section

2.1
, and the zeroth-, first- and second-order derivatives in
Section

2.2
.
The complete algorithm is then given, for each formulation, in
Section

2.3
.

2.1
ZD
equations

We first tackle the simple version called the
ZD
scheme, where one only uses implicit combinations of the approximate function and first derivative as unknowns. The generic
ZD
structural equation then reads

\sum_{r=0}^{R}a_{r,0}Z_{n+r}+a_{r,1}\Delta t\,D_{n+r}=0,

(2)

where
(a_{r,s})_{r\in\{0,\dots,R\},s\in\{0,1\}}
are the coefficients of the
SE
, independent of
n
by assuming a uniform time discretization with time step
\Delta t
. It is important to remark that
r=0
corresponds to the time
t_{n}
where all the variables are given. In total,
2(R+1)
coefficients characterize the structural equation. They can be reshaped as a vector

{\boldsymbol{a}}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0,0},&a_{1,0},&\dots,&a_{R,0},&a_{0,1},&a_{1,1},&\dots,&a_{R,1}\end{bmatrix}.

On the other hand, the implicit problem involves
2R
unknowns with
R
physical equations
{\texttt{PE}}[1],\dots,{\texttt{PE}}[R]
corresponding to the relations

D_{n+r}=f(Z_{n+r}),\text{\hskip 20.00003ptfor all }r\in\{1,\dots,R\}.

Hence, the structural method requires
R
linearly independent structural equations, i.e.
R
vectors,
({\boldsymbol{a}}^{m})_{m\in\{1,\dots,R\}}
whose components are denoted by
(a^{m}_{r,s})_{r,s,m}
, to close the system of size
2R
.

We wish to provide this set of
R
structural equations
by ensuring that the resulting scheme is high-order accurate.
To this end, for any vector
{\boldsymbol{a}}
and function
\phi
, we define the functional

E({\boldsymbol{a}},\phi)=\sum_{r=0}^{R}a_{r,0}\phi(t_{r})+a_{r,1}\Delta t\,\phi^{\prime}(t_{r}).

(3)

Taking the particular case of polynomial functions
\displaystyle\pi_{\ell}(t)=\left(\frac{t}{\Delta t}\right)^{\ell-1}
for
\ell\in\{1,\dots,2(R+1)\}
, we consider the linear system
E({\boldsymbol{a}},\pi_{\ell})=0
, which rewrites as the
2(R+1)
equations

\displaystyle\forall\ell\in\{1,\dots,2(R+1)\},\hskip 10.00002ptE(a;\pi_{\ell})=\sum_{r=0}^{R}a_{r,0}\pi_{\ell}(r\Delta t)+a_{r,1}\Delta t\,\pi_{\ell}^{\prime}(r\Delta t)=0.

We obtain a
2(R+1)\times 2(R+1)
non-singular linear system given by
M{\boldsymbol{a}}=0
, where the matrix
M
gathers all the coefficients

\pi_{\ell}(r\Delta t)
and
\pi_{\ell}^{\prime}(r\Delta t)
,
for all
\ell\in\{1,\dots,2(R+1)\}
and
r\in\{0,\dots,R\}
.

Remark
:

Notice that by construction the entries of matrix
M
do not depend on the time step
\Delta t
, therefore the vectors of the kernel are independent of
\Delta t
.

To determine the
R
structural equations, we simply withdraw the
R
last lines of the matrix
M
, leading to a reduced matrix whose kernel has dimension
R
.
To build the structural equations,
we select an orthogonal basis of this kernel
and place it in the vector
({\boldsymbol{a}}^{m})_{m\in\{1,\dots,R\}}
.
Thanks to this procedure, the obtained relations

\sum_{r=0}^{R}a^{m}_{r,0}Z_{n+r}+a^{m}_{r,1}\Delta t\,D_{n+r}=0,\hskip 10.00002ptm=1,\cdots,R,

(4)

remain exact for polynomials up to degree
R+1
.

Remark
:

The relation (
4
) does not correspond to a multistep method. Indeed, only
Z_{n}
and
D_{n}
are given while
(Z_{n+r},D_{n+r})
,
r=1,\dots,R
are the unknowns. To close the system, we impose the
R
physical equations at the time steps
t_{n+r}
,
r=1,\dots,R
.

Remark
:

For small values of
R
(say
R=2
), we derive an explicit analytic expression for the structural equation.
However, this becomes intractable for larger
R>4
, and so we compute the kernel and determine an orthogonal basis to automatically provide the
R
structural equations (see
[
6
,
7
]
for details).

At the end of the day, we get a set of
R
structural equations
{\texttt{SE}}[1],\dots,{\texttt{SE}}[R]
, linearly independent, exact for polynomials up to degree
R+1
.

2.2
ZDS
equations

The
ZD
scheme is quite effective and provides accurate solutions, but one can
improve the accuracy of
the method by introducing the second derivatives.
Indeed, differentiating the original physical equation provides a second physical equation. Therefore, the scheme is more compact and accurate for the same block size.
More precisely, the
ZDS
scheme involves the zeroth-, first- and second-order derivatives as unknowns, with the structural equation reading

\sum_{r=0}^{R}a_{r,0}Z_{n+r}+a_{r,1}\Delta t\,D_{n+r}+a_{r,2}\Delta t^{2}\,S_{n+r}=0,

(5)

with
{\boldsymbol{a}}=(a_{r,s})_{r,s}
its coefficients, independent of the time index
n
, the block number
r
and the derivation order
s
.

The
ZDS
method involves the
3R
unknowns
(Z_{n+r},D_{n+r},S_{n+r})
with
r\in\{1,\dots,R\}
.
The physical equations provide
2R
relations between these unknowns:

\forall r\in\{1,\dots,R\},\hskip 20.00003ptD_{n+r}=f(Z_{n+r})\text{\hskip 10.00002ptand \hskip 10.00002pt}S_{n+r}=f^{\prime}(Z_{n+r})D_{n+r}.

To close the system, we need
R
linearly independent structural equations of type (
5
) with respective coefficients
({\boldsymbol{a}}^{m})_{m\in\{1,\dots,R\}}
,
whose entries are
a^{m}_{r,d}
with
r\in\{0,\dots,R\}
and
d\in\{0,1,2\}
.

To this end, we introduce the functional

E({\boldsymbol{a}},\phi)=\sum_{r=0}^{R}a_{r,0}\phi(r\Delta t)+a_{r,1}\Delta t\,\phi^{\prime}(r\Delta t)+a_{r,2}\Delta t^{2}\,\phi^{\prime\prime}(r\Delta t)

and recall the set of polynomial functions
\pi_{\ell}=t^{\ell-1}
. Then the
3(R+1)(R+1)
linear system
E({\boldsymbol{a}},\pi_{\ell})=0
is non-singular and reads
M{\boldsymbol{a}}=0
where
M
is an invertible matrix.

Remark
:

Once again the entries of matrix
M
do not depend on
\Delta t
, hence the vectors of the kernel are independent of
\Delta t
.

Eliminating the
R
last rows of the matrix
M
, we get a kernel of dimension
R
with an orthogonal basis
({\boldsymbol{a}}^{m})_{m\in\{1,\dots,R\}}
that provides the
R
structural equation
{\texttt{PE}}[1],\dots,{\texttt{PE}}[R]
. Note that the relations

\sum_{r=0}^{R}a^{m}_{r,0}Z_{n+r}+a^{m}_{r,1}\Delta t\,D_{n+r}+a^{m}_{r,2}\Delta t^{2}\,S_{n+r}=0\text{\hskip 20.00003ptfor }m\in\{1,\dots,R\}

(6)

are exact for all polynomials of degree lower than
2(R+1)
. The solution of the
2R
physical equations together with the
R
structural equations provide an approximation of the
3R
variables.

Remark
:

The relations (
6
) do not correspond to a two-derivative multistep method in the sense of the book of Butcher
[
5
, Chapter 4]
, since only
(Z_{n},D_{n},S_{n})
are given whereas the other variables are unknowns of the problem. We use the expression
R
-block to highlight that we solve a non-linear system that involves all the unknowns from
t_{n+1}
until
t_{n+R}
.

2.3
Structural method for a scalar ODE

Equipped with the physical and structural equations in the
ZD
and
ZDS
formulations, we now detail the full algorithm to compute a full
R
-block of solutions with each of these formulations.

2.3.1
ZD
formulation

Assume that we know the solution
(Z_{n},D_{n})
at the time
t_{n}
. We seek the solution of the nonlinear problem with
2R
unknowns

\forall m\in\{1,\dots,R\},\hskip 20.00003ptD_{n+m}=f(Z_{n+m})\text{\hskip 10.00002ptand \hskip 10.00002pt}\sum_{r=0}^{R}a^{m}_{r,0}Z_{n+r}+a^{m}_{r,1}\Delta t\,D_{n+r}=0.

(7)

Let
\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}=(Z_{n+r})_{r\in\{1,\dots,R\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
and
\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}_{n}=(D_{n+r})_{r\in\{1,\dots,R\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
be the
R
-blocks for the approximations of
x
and
\dot{x}
at time
t_{n+r}
, for each
r\in\{1,\dots,R\}
. We rewrite the structural equations under a matrix form

A_{z}\,{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}+\Delta t\,A_{d}\,{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}_{n}+Z_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{z}+\Delta t\,D_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{d}=0,

(8)

with

\displaystyle A_{z}=(a^{m}_{r,0})_{m,r},

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt{\boldsymbol{a}}_{z}=\big(a^{1}_{0,0},a^{2}_{0,0},\cdots,a^{R}_{0,0}\big)^{\textrm{t}},

\displaystyle A_{d}=(a^{m}_{r,1})_{m,r},

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt{\boldsymbol{a}}_{d}=\big(a^{1}_{0,1},a^{2}_{0,1},\cdots,a^{R}_{0,1}\big)^{\textrm{t}}.

Assuming the matrix
A_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
is non-singular, we rewrite the system as

{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}+\Delta t\,B_{d}\,{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}_{n}+Z_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{z}+\Delta t\,D_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{d}=0

with
B_{d}=(A_{z})^{-1}A_{d}
,
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{z}=(A_{z})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{z}
,
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{d}=(A_{z})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{d}
.

To solve problem (
7
), we proceed with a fixed-point method by producing a sequence
({\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}^{(k)},{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}_{n}^{(k)})
that converges to the solution. We sketch the algorithm hereafter.

•

Initialization.
To build
{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}^{(0)}
and
{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}_{n}^{(0)}
, we set for
r\in\{1,\dots,R\}

Z^{(0)}_{n+r}=Z^{(0)}_{n+r-1}+\Delta tD^{(0)}_{n+r-1},\hskip 10.00002ptD^{(0)}_{n+r}=f(Z^{(0)}_{n+r}),

with
Z^{(0)}_{n}=Z_{n}
,
D^{(0)}_{n}=D_{n}
.

•

Iteration.
We first compute a new approximation for the solution
{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}
using the structural equations:

{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}^{(k+1)}=-\Big(Z_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{z}+\Delta t\,D_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{d}+\Delta t\,B_{d}\,{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}_{n}^{(k)}\Big)

Then, we update the first derivative with the physical equations:

\forall r\in\{1,\dots,R\},\hskip 20.00003ptD^{(k+1)}_{n+r}=f(Z^{(k+1)}_{n+r}).

•

Stopping criterion.
We end the fixed-point when two successive solutions are close enough, according to the tolerance parameter
tol
, that is
\lVert{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}^{(k+1)}-{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}^{(k)}\rVert\leq\texttt{tol}
.

Remark
:

Notice that
R=2
provides a 4th-order unconditionally stable scheme while
R=4
reaches sixth-order accuracy. Methods with
R=6
and
R=8
will also be experimented in the numerical section.∎

Remark
:

The non-linear part of the problem does not require some local linearization, hence the computation of the derivative is straightforward.
∎

When dealing with a uniform grid, analytic expressions of the structural equations are available, and given in
Table

18
(obtained from the coefficients in
[
7
, appendix A]
). The benchmarks we provide in the numerical section do not use these analytic expressions, but rather the coefficients that are automatically derived from the kernel for any
R
.

2.3.2
ZDS
formulation

Assume that we knew the solution
(Z_{n},D_{n},S_{n})
at the time
t_{n}
. We seek the solution of the following nonlinear problem with
3R
unknowns:
for all
m\in\{1,\dots,R\}
,

D_{n+m}=f(Z_{n+m})\text{, }\hskip 10.00002ptS_{n+m}=f^{\prime}(Z_{n+m})D_{n+m}\text{ and \hskip 10.00002pt}\sum_{r=0}^{R}a^{m}_{r,0}Z_{n+r}+a^{m}_{r,1}\Delta t\,D_{n+r}+a^{m}_{r,2}\Delta t^{2}S_{n+r}=0.

(9)

Denoting by
{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}_{n}
the
R
-block of the second derivative approximations, we rewrite the structural equations under a matrix form

A_{z}\,{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}+\Delta t\,A_{d}\,{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}_{n}+\Delta t^{2}\,A_{s}\,{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}_{n}+Z_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{z}+\Delta t\,D_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{d}+\Delta t^{2}\,S_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{s}=0,

(10)

with

\displaystyle A_{z}=(a^{m}_{r,0})_{m,r},

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt{\boldsymbol{a}}_{z}=\big(a^{1}_{0,0},a^{2}_{0,0},\cdots,a^{R}_{0,0}\big)^{\textrm{t}},

\displaystyle A_{d}=(a^{m}_{r,1})_{m,r},

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt{\boldsymbol{a}}_{d}=\big(a^{1}_{0,1},a^{2}_{0,1},\cdots,a^{R}_{0,1}\big)^{\textrm{t}},

\displaystyle A_{s}=(a^{m}_{r,2})_{m,r},

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt{\boldsymbol{a}}_{s}=\big(a^{1}_{0,2},a^{2}_{0,2},\cdots,a^{R}_{0,2}\big)^{\textrm{t}}.

Remark
:

For the sake of simplicity, we still use the notation
A_{z}
,
A_{d}
,
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{z}
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{d}
introduced in the
ZD
formulation
. Of course, the matrix entries are different from the ones corresponding to the
ZD
method.

Assuming the matrix
A_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
is non-singular, we rewrite the system as

{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}+\Delta t\,B_{d}\,{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}_{n}+\Delta t^{2}\,B_{s}\,{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}_{n}+Z_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{z}+\Delta t\,D_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{d}+\Delta t^{2}\,S_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{s}=0,

(11)

with
B_{d}=(A_{z})^{-1}A_{d}
,
B_{s}=(A_{z})^{-1}A_{s}
,
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{z}=(A_{z})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{z}
,
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{d}=(A_{z})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{d}
,
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{s}=(A_{z})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{s}
.

A fixed-point procedure is applied to solve the system (
9
), by producing a sequence
({\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}^{(k)},{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}_{n}^{(k)},{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}_{n}^{(k)})
that converges to the solution. We give the algorithm for the
ZDS
case.

•

Initialization.
To build
{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}^{(0)}
,
{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}_{n}^{(0)}
,
{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}_{n}^{(0)}
, we set for
r\in\{1,\dots,R\}

Z^{(0)}_{n+r}=Z^{(0)}_{n+r-1}+\Delta tD^{(0)}_{n+r-1}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}S^{(0)}_{n+r-1},\hskip 10.00002ptD^{(0)}_{n+r}=f(Z^{(0)}_{n+r}),\hskip 10.00002ptS^{(0)}_{n+r}=f^{\prime}(Z^{(0)}_{n+r})D^{(0)}_{n+r},

with
Z^{(0)}_{n}=Z_{n}
,
D^{(0)}_{n}=D_{n}
,
S^{(0)}_{n}=S_{n}
.

•

Iteration.
We first compute a new approximation for the solution using the structural equations:

{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}^{(k+1)}=-\Big(Z_{n}\,b_{z}+\Delta t\,D_{n}\,b_{d}+\Delta t^{2}\,S_{n}\,b_{s}+\Delta t\,B_{d}\,{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}_{n}^{(k)}+\Delta t^{2}\,B_{s}\,{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}_{n}^{(k)}\Big).

Then, we update the first and second derivatives with the physical equations, by computing

\forall r\in\{1,\dots,R\},\hskip 20.00003ptD^{(k+1)}_{n+r}=f(Z^{(k+1)}_{n+r})\text{\hskip 10.00002ptand \hskip 10.00002pt}S^{(k+1)}_{n+r}=f^{\prime}(Z^{(k+1)}_{n+r})D^{(k+1)}_{n+r}.

•

Stopping criterion.
We end the fixed-point when two successive solutions are close enough according to the tolerance parameter
tol
, that is
\|{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}^{(k+1)}-{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}_{n}^{(k)}\|\leq\texttt{tol}
.

Remark
:

It has been shown
in
[
7
]
that
R=1
provides a 4th-order unconditionally stable scheme while
R=2
reaches sixth-order accuracy. Methods with
R=3
and
R=4
are also investigated in the numerical section.∎

Remark
:

Given a grid, the structural equations’ coefficients are computed independently of the problem, and their evaluations may be obtained in a pre-processing process or be stored with the grid points. ∎

Once again, analytic expressions of the structural equations are available for uniform grids. We reproduce the results of
[
7
]
in
Table

19
. As mentioned above, we only use the kernel method to compute the coefficients, making it possible to carry out the simulation for any
R
.

Remark
:

The authors showed in
[
7
]
that the structural method is equivalent to a multi-derivative Runge-Kutta (MDRK) method as long as we use all the available physical equations. Note that the resulting MDKT is very specific since we guarantee that all the intermediate steps enjoy the same order of accuracy than the last step. Such a property is not true in general for the MDRK.

3
Structural method for Hamiltonian problems: one-body systems

We reach the main novelty of the present work by adapting the structural method to Hamiltonian systems. The main difference is the introduction of a second variable
p
, that requires to handle both the approximations of
t\to x(t)\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\to p(t)\in\mathbb{R}
, coupled through Hamilton’s equations.
This section is dedicated to
one-body
Hamiltonian equations, while
multi-body
systems will be considered in the next section.
We first introduce the physical equations for the
one-body
Hamiltonian problem in
Section

3.1
, and then detail the structural equations and the algorithm to solve the coupled physical and structural equations in
Section

3.2
.

3.1
Physical equations

Consider a smooth function
\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
that takes as input
the position
x
and the momentum
p
of some body subject to some exterior forces
.
We seek solutions
x=x(t)
and
p=p(t)
such that
{\mathcal{H}\left({x(t)},{p(t)}\right)}
is constant. Differentiating in time gives
\partial_{x}{\mathcal{H}\left({x},{p}\right)}\dot{x}+\partial_{p}{\mathcal{H}\left({x},{p}\right)}\dot{p}=0
, and we define the trajectories as the solution of the ODE

\dot{x}=\partial_{p}{\mathcal{H}\left({x},{p}\right)}\text{\hskip 10.00002ptand \hskip 10.00002pt}\dot{p}=-\partial_{x}{\mathcal{H}\left({x},{p}\right)},

with the initial condition
x(0)=x_{0}
,
p(0)=p_{0}
.
This ODE makes up the first physical equations
{\texttt{PE}}[1]
.
We reformulate the problem within the
Z
,
D
framework by denoting by
{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}
and
{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}
the approximations of
x
and
\dot{x}
, and adopt similar notations for
{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}
and
{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}
. The physical equations then read

\displaystyle{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{p}{\mathcal{H}\left({{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}},{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}}\right)},

(12)

\displaystyle{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}{\mathcal{H}\left({{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}},{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}}\right)}.

(13)

For each time step, we have
2
physical equations, with
4
unknowns in total, and so we need
2
structural equations to close the system.

In the
ZDS
case, to provide the second physical system
{\texttt{PE}}[2]
, we differentiate in time the first physical equations, and we obtain

\ddot{x}=\partial_{xp}{\mathcal{H}\left({x},{p}\right)}\dot{x}+\partial_{pp}{\mathcal{H}\left({x},{p}\right)}\dot{p}\text{\hskip 20.00003ptand \hskip 20.00003pt}\ddot{p}=-\partial_{xx}{\mathcal{H}\left({x},{p}\right)}\dot{x}-\partial_{xp}{\mathcal{H}\left({x},{p}\right)}\dot{p}.

Reformulating the problem within the
Z
,
D
,
S
framework yields the second physical equations
{\texttt{PE}}[2]
:

\displaystyle{S\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{xp}{\mathcal{H}\left({{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}},{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}}\right)}{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}+\partial_{pp}{\mathcal{H}\left({{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}},{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}}\right)}{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}},

(14)

\displaystyle{S\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{xx}{\mathcal{H}\left({{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}},{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}}\right)}{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}-\partial_{xp}{\mathcal{H}\left({{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}},{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}}\right)}{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}.

(15)

Note that we have
4
physical equations, with
6
unknowns in total, and so
2
structural equations are required to close the system for each time step.

3.2
Structural equations and algorithms

Equipped with the physical and structural equations in the
ZD
and
ZDS
formulations, coupling
x
and
p
, we now give the algorithm to solve them in both cases.

3.2.1
ZD
schemes

Approximations of the function
x
and the derivative
\dot{x}
are connected via the structural equations (
4
), and similarly for the function
p
and its derivative.
Hence, the structural equations read

\forall m\in\{1,\dots,R\},\hskip 20.00003pt\begin{cases}\sum_{r=0}^{R}a^{m}_{r,0}{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n+r}+a^{m}_{r,1}\Delta t\,{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n+r}=0,\\
\sum_{r=0}^{R}a^{m}_{r,0}{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n+r}+a^{m}_{r,1}\Delta t\,{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n+r}=0.\end{cases}

It is important to note that the
x
and
p
use the
same
structural equations (the same coefficients
a^{m}_{r,s}
) and
only differ
through the physical ones. Let denote by

{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n}=\big({Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n+1},{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n+2},\dots,{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n+R}\big)^{\textrm{t}}\text{\hskip 20.00003ptand \hskip 20.00003pt}{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n}=\big({Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n+1},{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n+2},\dots,{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n+R}\big)^{\textrm{t}},

the respective
R
-block vectors for the functions
x
and
p
. Similarly, the
R
-block vectors for the first derivatives are denoted by
{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n}
and
{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n}
. The structural equations for the
ZD
scheme of size
R
then read

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle={{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n}+B_{d}\,\Delta t\,{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n}+{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{z}+{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n}\,\Delta t\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{d},

(16)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle={{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n}+B_{d}\,\Delta t\,{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n}+{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n}\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{z}+{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n}\,\Delta t\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{d}.

(17)

Remark
:

These two linear systems are independent and should be treated in parallel.

To solve the ODEs
stemming
from the Hamiltonian, we proceed in a very similar way as in
Section

2.3.1
. We produce two sequences
\Big({{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n}^{(k)},{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}_{n}^{(k)}\Big)
and
\Big({{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n}^{(k)},{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}_{n}^{(k)}\Big)
that converge to the solution. The fixed-point algorithm is then given by the following iterative procedure.

•

To build

{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.84995pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-0.92505pt\textrm{x}}_{n}^{(k+1)}
and
{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.84995pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-0.92505pt\textrm{p}}_{n}^{(k+1)}

Remark
:

We have just considered a very basic fixed-point method for the sake of simplicity, but any accelerating techniques could be exploited. In practice, the predictor is good enough and very few iterations (3 to 12 in practice, depending on the tolerance and order of the method) are required to provide the accurate approximation. Of course, for stiff problems, a more sophisticated fixed-point procedure may be implemented.∎

4
Conservation properties of the structural schemes

Before moving on to multi-body systems, we first check a few conservation properties of the
ZD
and
ZDS
schemes applied to one-body Hamiltonian problems.
Namely, we discuss their (the lack of) symplecticity in
Section

4.1
, and their exact preservation of quadratic Hamiltonians in
Section

4.2
.

4.1
Structural scheme and symplecticity

The symplecticity is the ability to exactly preserve geometric properties of the flow. In
[
17
,
25
]
, a set of conditions for the Butcher tableau coefficients is provided, corresponding to
R(R+1)/2
additional constraints on the coefficients given by

\alpha_{R+1,m}\alpha_{m,s}+\alpha_{R+1,s}\alpha_{s,m}=\alpha_{R+1,m}\alpha_{R+1,s},\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq m,s\leq R+1,

taking into account that
\beta_{m}=\alpha_{R+1,m}
in the context of the structural equations.

In conclusion, we have
R(R+1)/2
additional constraints on RK matrix
A
and vector
b
that have already been defined by the
R+2
accuracy conditions (to be exact for polynomials up to degree
R+1
) together with the mandatory condition that the kernel is at least of dimension
R
to provide
R
linearly independent structural equations. Consequently, except one very particular case (
R=1
), the
R(R+1)/2
conditions cannot be satisfied, hence the symplecticity is not achieved.

Moreover, Hairer
et al.

[
12
]
prove that there is no two-derivative symplectic RK scheme. Consequently, the
ZDS
scheme can not be symplectic.

4.2
Exact conservation of quadratic Hamiltonians

In 2006, Ernst Hairer
[
13
]
shows that exact energy conservation and symplecticity for Hamiltonian systems cannot be obtained simultaneously. Moreover, symplectic integrators are not necessarily the main objective and researchers promote the energy or momentum conservation strategy for the long-time simulations
[
4
]
. A first quantity that we expect to preserve at the numerical level is the Hamiltonian. We prove in this section that the structural schemes
ZD
or
ZDS
with
R
blocks exactly preserve quadratic Hamiltonians every
R
steps.

4.2.1
The quadratic Hamiltonian

Let us consider the quadratic Hamiltonian of the form

{\mathcal{H}\left({x},{p}\right)}=\frac{1}{2}p^{2}+\gamma xp+\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}x^{2},

(20)

with
\gamma,\omega\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, the associated ODEs read

\dot{x}=\gamma x+p,\text{\hskip 20.00003ptand \hskip 20.00003pt}\dot{p}=-\omega^{2}x-\gamma p,

or in the compact form

{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}=\gamma{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}+{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}},\text{\hskip 20.00003ptand \hskip 20.00003pt}{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}=-\omega^{2}{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}-\gamma{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}.

We also consider the ODEs satisfied by the second derivatives

\ddot{x}=(\gamma^{2}-\omega^{2})x,\text{\hskip 20.00003ptand \hskip 20.00003pt}\ddot{p}=(\gamma^{2}-\omega^{2})p,

which we rewrite under the form

{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}=(\gamma^{2}-\omega^{2}){{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}},\text{\hskip 20.00003ptand \hskip 20.00003pt}{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}=(\gamma^{2}-\omega^{2}){{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}},

to design the
ZDS
scheme.

For the sake of simplicity, we deal with the separable case, taking
\gamma=0
, and assume
\omega=1
. Then, the equations reduce to

{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}={{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}},\hskip 10.00002pt{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}=-{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}},\hskip 10.00002pt{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}=-{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}},\hskip 10.00002pt{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}=-{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}.

(21)

5
Structural method for Hamiltonian problems: multi-body systems

We now generalize the method to the situation where the Hamiltonian system involves
K
vector-valued bodies. Such problems arise when modelling planetary systems, charged particles in electromagnetic fields, multiple coupled mass-damped-spring systems, among many other cases. The main issue of this extension lies in handling the technical difficulties arising when discretizing such complex systems. Overcoming these difficulties requires new notation and specific operators, which we introduce in
Section

5.1
. The structural algorithm is then recast using this notation, in
Section

5.2
.

5.1
Notation

The Hamiltonian system involves
K
distinct bodies characterized by their positions
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
and momenta
{\boldsymbol{p}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
. We gather the
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{p}}
in the “body space”
{\mathfrak{B}}=\mathbb{R}^{I\times K}
, and we introduce the matrix notation

X=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{1}&x_{1}^{2}&\cdots&x_{1}^{K}\\
x_{2}^{1}&x_{2}^{2}&\cdots&x_{2}^{K}\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
x_{I}^{1}&x_{I}^{2}&&x_{I}^{K}\\
\end{bmatrix}\text{\hskip 20.00003ptand \hskip 20.00003pt}P=\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}^{1}&p_{1}^{2}&\cdots&p_{1}^{K}\\
p_{2}^{1}&p_{2}^{2}&\cdots&p_{2}^{K}\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
p_{I}^{1}&p_{I}^{2}&&p_{I}^{K}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Let
X,P\in{\mathfrak{B}}
and
{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}\in\mathbb{R}
be the Hamiltonian. We adopt the following notation.

•

The gradients
\nabla_{X}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}\in{\mathfrak{B}}
and
\nabla_{P}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}\in{\mathfrak{B}}
are given by

\nabla_{X}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}=\left[\partial_{x_{i}^{k}}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}\right]_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\},i\in\{1,\dots,I\}}.

Note that the gradient does not output a vector but is reshaped to provide an element in
{\mathfrak{B}}
.

•

The second derivatives
\nabla_{XX}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}
,
\nabla_{PP}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}
,
\nabla_{XP}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}=\nabla_{X}\left(\nabla_{P}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}\right)
, and
\nabla_{PX}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}=\nabla_{P}\left(\nabla_{X}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}\right)
belong to
{\mathfrak{B}}^{2}
. Note that the order
\nabla_{XP}
and
\nabla_{PX}
are, in general, different.
We introduce the following computation rules, using Einstein summation:

\displaystyle\nabla_{PX}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}\cdot\dot{p}

\displaystyle=

Initialization.
To initialize the

{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n}^{(0)}

,
{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}_{n}^{(0)}
,

for all

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r}={\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r-1}+\Delta t\,{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r-1}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\,{\boldsymbol{S}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r-1},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(0)}_{n+r}={\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(0)}_{n+r-1}+\Delta t\,{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(0)}_{n+r-1}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\,{\boldsymbol{S}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(0)}_{n+r-1},

where we compute the first and second derivatives through the physical equations

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r}=\nabla_{P}{\mathcal{H}\left({{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r}},{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(0)}_{n+r}}\right)},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{S}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r}=\nabla_{XP}{\mathcal{H}\left({{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r}},{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(0)}_{n+r}}\right)}{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r}+\nabla_{PP}{\mathcal{H}\left({{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r}},{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(0)}_{n+r}}\right)}{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}^{(0)}_{n+r},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{S}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(0)}_{n+r}=-\nabla_{XX}{\mathcal{H}\left({{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r}},{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(0)}_{n+r}}\right)}{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r}-\nabla_{PX}{\mathcal{H}\left({{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n+r}},{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(0)}_{n+r}}\right)}{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(0)}_{n+r}

{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(0)}_{n}={\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n}
,

,

.

Iteration.
We first compute new
R
-size blocks
{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n}^{(k+1)}
and
{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}_{n}^{(k+1)}
, approximating the positions and momenta, using the system of structural equations:

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Big(b_{z}\otimes{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n}+b_{d}\otimes{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n}+b_{s}\otimes{\boldsymbol{S}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n}+B_{d}\times{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n}^{(k)}+B_{s}\times{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n}^{(k)}\Big),

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.99997pt\boldsymbol{Z}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}_{n}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Big(b_{z}\otimes{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}_{n}+b_{d}\otimes{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}_{n}+b_{s}\otimes{\boldsymbol{S}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}_{n}+B_{d}\times{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{D}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}_{n}^{(k)}+B_{s}\times{{\boldsymbol{\flat}\hskip-1.49994pt\boldsymbol{S}}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}_{n}^{(k)}\Big).

Then, for each
r\in\{1,\dots,R\}
, we update the first and second derivatives at each time step

by setting

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}=\nabla_{P}{\mathcal{H}\left({{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}},{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}}\right)},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}=-\nabla_{X}{\mathcal{H}\left({{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}},{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}}\right)},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{S}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}=\nabla_{XP}{\mathcal{H}\left({{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}},{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}}\right)}{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}+\nabla_{PP}{\mathcal{H}\left({{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}},{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}}\right)}{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{S}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}=-\nabla_{XX}{\mathcal{H}\left({{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}},{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}}\right)}{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}-\nabla_{PX}{\mathcal{H}\left({{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}},{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}}\right)}{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}^{(k+1)}_{n+r}.

•

Stopping criterion.
We end the fixed-point scheme once two successive solutions are close enough, according to the tolerance parameter

\|{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n+R}^{(k+1)}-{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n+R}^{(k)}\|\leq\texttt{tol}
.

6.2
One-dimensional pendulum problem

We turn to a nonlinear
one-body
problem, and consider the one-dimensional pendulum system governed by the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}(x,p)=\frac{p^{2}}{2m\ell^{2}}+mg\ell(1-\cos(x))

(33)

where
m
is the mass,
\ell
the length of the pendulum and
g
the gravity.

6.2.1
Physical equations

The differential system
derived
from Hamilton’s equations reads

\dot{x}=\frac{p}{m},\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{p}=-mg\ell\sin(x)

(34)

The solution is a periodic solution
[
1
]
with a period given by the elliptic integral

T_{p}=4\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{mg}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-\omega^{2}\sin^{2}(u)}},

with
\omega=\sin(\pi/8)
.
Then, differentiating the first physical equation (
34
) with respect to time provides relations involving the second-order derivative, namely

\ddot{x}=\frac{\dot{p}}{m},\hskip 20.00003pt\ddot{p}=-mg\ell\cos(x)\dot{x}.

We then deduce the first set of physical equations
{\texttt{PE}}[1]
, given by

{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}={Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}/m,\hskip 20.00003pt{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}=-mg\ell\sin({Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}),

whereas the second set of physical equations
{\texttt{PE}}[2]
reads

{S\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}={D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}/m,\hskip 20.00003pt{S\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{p}}=-mg\ell\cos({Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}){D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{x}}.

6.2.2
Numerical tests

The numerical applications have been carried out with
m=1
,
g=1
,
\ell=1
, and the initial conditions
x(0)=\pi/4
and
p(0)=0
. A numerical approximation of the period is
T_{p}=6.53
. In
[
1
]
, an analytical solution is derived, whose expression, not reported here, involves a Jacobi elliptic function. The exact position and momentum at final time
T=100
are given by

x(t=100)=-0.2633498226088722\text{\hskip 20.00003ptand \hskip 20.00003pt}p(t=100)=-0.7189111241830892

in
double
precision (since the Jacobi functions are not implemented with arbitrary precision). Hence, schemes with very high accuracy will reach machine error with a comparatively low number of points.

6.3
Two-dimensional Kepler problem

The two-dimensional Kepler system consists in a particle moving around a fixed point. It is characterized by the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})=\displaystyle\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{p}}\|^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|},

(35)

corresponding to the total mechanical energy.
The particle position
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the momentum
{\boldsymbol{p}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
describe a quadratic curve (elliptic, parabolic or hyperbolic) depending on the initial total energy.

6.3.1
Physical equations and invariants

We first derive the dynamical system associated to the Hamiltonian (
35
)

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{p}},\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}=-\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{3}},

and, differentiating with respect to time, we obtain the relations with the second derivatives:

\ddot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\dot{\boldsymbol{p}},\hskip 20.00003pt\ddot{\boldsymbol{p}}=-\frac{{\boldsymbol{p}}}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{3}}+3{\boldsymbol{x}}\frac{{\boldsymbol{p}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{5}}.

We deduce the first group of physical equations
PE
[1], given by

{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}={Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}},\hskip 20.00003pt{D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}=-\frac{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}}{\|{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}\|^{3}},

while the second group of physical equation
PE
[2] reads

{S\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}={D\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}},\hskip 20.00003pt{S\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}=-\frac{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}}{\|{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}\|^{3}}+3{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}\frac{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}\cdot{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}}{\|{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}\|^{5}}.

In addition to the Hamiltonian, there are two other invariant quantities for the Kepler system, namely:

1.

the angular momentum

\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})={\boldsymbol{x}}\times{\boldsymbol{p}}=p_{2}x_{1}-p_{1}x_{2};

2.

the Laplace-Runge-Lenz (LRL) vector

\mathcal{A}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})=\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}}){\boldsymbol{p}}^{\perp}+\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\text{, \hskip 20.00003ptwith }\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|},

from which we extract a one-dimensional invariant by summing the two components of
\mathcal{A}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})
, yielding

\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})=(p_{2}x_{1}-p_{1}x_{2})(p_{1}-p_{2})+\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|}.

6.3.3
Projection on the third invariant manifold

Since the last invariant is not fully preserved, we revisit the scheme by adding a projection onto the third invariant manifold characterized by the equation

\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})=\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{p}}_{0})=\mathcal{R}_{0}.

In short, we provide new vectors
\widetilde{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}}_{n}
and
\widetilde{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}}_{n}
close to
{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}_{n}
and
{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}_{n}
such that
\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}}_{n},\widetilde{{Z\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}}_{n})=\mathcal{R}_{0}
.

To this end, given
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{p}}
assumed to be close to the manifold, we consider the optimization problem: find
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}
that minimize

\mathcal{J}=\frac{1}{2}\Big\|[\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}]^{\textrm{t}}-[{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}}]^{\textrm{t}}\Big\|^{2},\text{ \hskip 10.00002ptunder the constraint }\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}})=\mathcal{R}_{0}.

We obtain an update
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}
given by

\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{x}_{1}\\
\widetilde{x}_{2}\\
\widetilde{p}_{1}\\
\widetilde{p}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
p_{1}\\
p_{2}\end{bmatrix}-\lambda\displaystyle\nabla_{x,p}\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}),\textrm{ with }\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}})=\mathcal{R}_{0},

where

\nabla_{x,p}\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})=\begin{bmatrix}+p_{2}(p_{2}-p_{1})-\dfrac{(x_{2})^{2}}{||x||^{3}}\\
-p_{1}(p_{2}-p_{1})-\dfrac{(x_{1})^{2}}{||x||^{3}}\\
2x_{2}p_{1}-x_{1}p_{2}-x_{2}p_{2}\vphantom{\dfrac{1}{2}}\\
2x_{1}p_{2}-x_{1}p_{1}-x_{2}p_{1}\vphantom{\dfrac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

We then use the following approximation

0=\mathcal{R}\Big({\boldsymbol{x}}-\lambda\nabla_{x}\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}})\,,\,{\boldsymbol{p}}-\lambda\nabla_{p}\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}})\Big)-\mathcal{R}_{0}\approx\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})-\mathcal{R}_{0}-\lambda\Big<\nabla_{x,p}\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}),\nabla_{x,p}\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})\Big>.

Substituting
\nabla_{x,p}\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}})
with
\nabla_{x,p}\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})
in the inner product, and we get the approximation

\lambda=\frac{\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})-\mathcal{R}_{0}}{\|\nabla_{x,p}\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})\|^{2}}.

Thus, the approximation of the projection onto the third manifold is given by

\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\\
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{x}}\\
{\boldsymbol{p}}\end{bmatrix}-\frac{\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})-\mathcal{R}_{0}}{\|\nabla_{x,p}\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})\|^{2}}\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{x}\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})\\[3.00003pt]
\nabla_{p}\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})\end{bmatrix}.

The three-dimensional
K
-body system is characterized by the position
X[k]={\boldsymbol{x}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the momentum
P[k]={\boldsymbol{p}}^{k}=m_{k}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
of each body, where matrices
X,P\in{\mathfrak{B}}
are given by
X_{i,k}={\boldsymbol{x}}^{k}_{i}
and
P_{i,k}={\boldsymbol{p}}^{k}_{i}
, for all
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
and
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. The Hamiltonian corresponds to the time-invariant total mechanical energy

\mathcal{H}(X,P)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{2m_{k}}\|{\boldsymbol{p}}^{k}\|^{2}-\sum_{k\neq\ell}\frac{Gm_{k}m_{\ell}}{2\|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{k}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\ell}\|}.

6.5.1
Physical equations and invariants

Hamilton’s equations
\dot{X}=\nabla_{P}\mathcal{H}(X,P)
and
\dot{P}=-\nabla_{X}\mathcal{H}(X,P)
give the first set of physical equations:

\text{for all }k\in\{1,\dots,K\},\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}^{k}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{p}}^{k}}{m_{k}},\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}^{k}=-\sum_{\ell\neq k}Gm_{k}m_{\ell}\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{k}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\ell}}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{k}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\ell}\|^{3}}.

Hence, the first set of physical equations
{\texttt{PE}}[1]
given by relations (
36
) – (
37
) derives from Hamilton’s principle:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]=\frac{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}[k]}{m_{k}},

(36)

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}[k]=-\sum_{\ell\neq k}Gm_{k}m_{\ell}\frac{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[\ell]-{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]}{\big\|{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[\ell]-{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]\big\|^{3}}.

(37)

Differentiating the dynamical system once more provides the second set of physical equations
{\texttt{PE}}[2]
, given by relations (
38
) – (
39
):

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{S}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}[k]

\displaystyle=\frac{{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}[k]}{m_{k}},

(38)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{S}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf p}}[k]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\ell\neq k}Gm_{k}m_{\ell}\Bigg(\frac{{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[\ell]-{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]}{\big\|{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[\ell]-{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]\big\|^{3}}-3\Big\langle{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]-{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[\ell],{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]-{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[\ell]\Big\rangle\,\frac{{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]-{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[\ell]}{\big\|{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]-{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[\ell]\big\|^{5}}\Bigg),

(39)

with
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the usual inner product, while
{\boldsymbol{Z}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]
,
{\boldsymbol{D}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]
and
{\boldsymbol{S}\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}[k]
represent the position, the velocity and the acceleration of body
k
.
A second invariant of the
K
-body system is the angular momentum
\mathcal{L}(X,P)

\mathcal{L}(X,P)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{L}_{k}(X[k],P[k])=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{k}\times{\boldsymbol{p}}^{k}.

6.6
Motion of a particle in a 3D electromagnetic field

We consider a charged particle subjected to electromagnetic forces, characterized by the electric potential
\varphi=\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}})\in\mathbb{R}
and the magnetic potential
A=A({\boldsymbol{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
This situation is governed by the following Hamiltonian:

\mathcal{H}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{p}})=\frac{1}{2m}\|{\boldsymbol{p}}-eA({\boldsymbol{x}})\|^{2}+e\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}),

with
m
and
e
the mass and electric charge of the particle.
We denote by
A({\boldsymbol{x}})=[A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}]^{\textrm{t}}
the components of the magnetic potential.

This is an example of a non-separable Hamiltonian, i.e., a problem where
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\mathcal{H}
depends on both
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{p}}
.
The classical schemes already used in the previous section and implemented in
julia
failed to solve such a problem.
Hence, we have implemented the second-order symplectic
Störmer
-Verlet schemes
[
10
]
. The composition method
[

make it possible to reach the fourth, sixth and eighth orders of accuracy, to provide comparisons with the structural method.

\displaystyle\frac{m}{e}{S\hskip-1.00006pt\textrm{\bf x}}

6.7.1

6.7.2

Function calls

On the one hand, the
ZD
and
ZDS
schemes solve the system using blocks of size
R
, while the total number of steps is denoted by
N
. We simply call
nb_iter_avg
the ratio between the total number of iterations, including the fixed-point solves, and the total number of steps
N
. Conversely, since one iteration over the block corresponds to
R
evaluations of the Physical Equations (PE), the average number of “PE function calls”
nb_call_avg
is given by the ratio between the total number of calls to PE and the number of steps
N
, that is
nb_call_avg = R × nb_iter_avg ​​​.

The classical schemes, on the other hand, compose several semi-implicit steps, with one linear and potentially one nonlinear solve for every semi-implicit step. The steps alternate between solving for
\nabla_{X}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}
and
\nabla_{P}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}
.
In the separable case, we have two linear solves; in the non-separable one, a linear and a non-linear solve are carried out.
Note that, in the specific case of the separable particle problem from
Section

6.6.3
,
\nabla_{P}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}
is linear in
P
, and the non-linear solve for
\nabla_{P}{\mathcal{H}\left({X},{P}\right)}
is not needed, which reduces the total number of non-linear solves.

Based on the preceding analysis, we can summarize the key conclusions as follows:

•

One of the key benefits of the structural method is its unconditional stability.
Indeed, one can take very large time steps, which leads to a significant reduction in the number of function calls without loss in accuracy.

•

In the separable case, both approaches allow for large time steps.
For high orders of accuracy and large number
N
of time steps, the structural schemes are faster because n
{}^{\text{ZD}}_{\text{it}}
and n
{}^{\text{ZDS}}_{\text{it}}
remain small. At lower orders and small values of
N
, the structural method is slower, but its precision remains superior. As a result, we maintain a better speed-to-accuracy ratio overall.

•

In the non-separable case, regardless of
N
, our method has a smaller complexity if a similar amount of iterations in the non-linear solver is required. Even when the number of iterations is larger, the structural method still has a significant advantage, namely its unconditional stability. In the particle case (where the number of iterations was comparable), the classical method required time steps about
20
times smaller than the structural method to ensure stability.

In conclusion, the structural method should, in general, be slightly faster than classical methods in the separable case, and significantly faster in the non-separable case. To make sure this is truly the case, in the next section, we compare the runtime of both methods in the two cases.

6.8

Computation time

This last section is dedicated to the comparison of the computation time required by the structural schemes and the classical ones. We tackle a separable setting (the outer Solar system problem) and a non-separable one (the particle in a 3D electromagnetic field). To provide a fair comparison, they were coded in the same framework, using the same representation of floating-point numbers. Since the ZD and ZDS schemes are more stable, we additionally choose the lowest time step imposed by the classical symplectic schemes. The tests were run on a personal computer with an AMD Ryzen 7 5800H @ 4.46 GHz and 16 GB of RAM with only one thread.

7

Conclusions

In this paper, evidence was brought forward of the ability of the structural method to provide stable and accurate solutions in the specific context of Hamiltonian systems. We have detailed the design of the method, and provided numerical experiments with different classical Hamiltonian systems, systematically comparing the results to well-known symplectic solvers. These experiments showed that our method is quite efficient and fast, and, in the
ZDS
case, provides a solution of excellent quality. We also tackled a non-separable system to prove the superiority of the structural method with
respect
to other schemes. In short, we have provided a general framework for the numerical approximation of Hamiltonian systems, that can easily be adapted to large classes of problems.

We would like to highlight that our method is quite versatile (e.g. by adapting the block size to fix the convergence rate), very easy to implement, and with a low memory consumption. Unconditional stability is also a desirable property, since it makes it possible to reduce the computational effort involved in dealing with long-time simulations by taking large time steps. Additionally, we also mention the simplicity of the fixed point method to solve non-linear problems where all the matrices and inverse matrices associated to the structural equations are computed once in a pre-processing stage, that dramatically reduces runtime. At last, we achieve very high accuracy that requires quad-precision (even octuple precision) when we use the
ZDS
scheme with
R>2
.

A final remark is the ability to produce more physical equations by differentiating the physical equations twice (or more),
and by including
the third-order (or higher-order) derivatives in the structural equations. That way, extreme accuracy (more than one hundred correct digits) would be obtained, which could be useful for applications requiring very long simulation times and very accurate approximations (planet or satellite positioning, for instance).
