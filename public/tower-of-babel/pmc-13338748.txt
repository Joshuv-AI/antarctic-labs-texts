Contribution of cone-beam computed tomography to the evaluation of lesions resulting from multiple myeloma and to the early diagnosis of the disease: A scoping review
Authors: Thaiza Gonçalves Rocha, Carla Barros de Oliveira, Raphael dos Santos Alves Martins Veiga, Eduardo Murad Villoria, Roberto José Pessoa de Magalhães Filho, Angelo Maiolino, Lucianne Cople Maia, Sandra Regina Torres, Maria Augusta Visconti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo analyze the available evidence regarding the contribution of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) to the evaluation of osteolytic lesions resulting from multiple myeloma (MM) and to the early diagnosis of the disease.Materials and MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, Embase, LILACS, Google Scholar, and OpenGrey through November 2025, without restrictions on language or publication date. Studies that identified or described MM-related lesions using CBCT were included, whereas those involving patients with medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaws were excluded.ResultsSeventeen studies met the inclusion criteria, including 11 case reports, 2 case series, and 4 cross-sectional studies. In most case reports (9 studies), the initial clinical suspicion arose from oral alterations and/or the identification of osteolytic lesions on CBCT, prompting medical referral. The mandible was the most frequently affected site (13 studies). Various tomographic patterns were described, most commonly extensive lesions with irregular contours and ill-defined margins (10 studies) and punched-out lesions (5 studies).ConclusionCBCT demonstrates potential as a valuable tool for the early identification and characterization of MM-related lesions in the head and neck region. However, the available literature remains limited and is predominantly composed of isolated case reports, indicating a substantial knowledge gap. These findings underscore the need for further research and for the training of dental professionals—particularly radiologists—to adopt a more sensitive and multidisciplinary diagnostic approach.Keywords: Multiple Myeloma, Cone-Beam Computed Tomography, Diagnostic Imaging, Early Diagnosis

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematological neoplasm characterized by the clonal proliferation of plasma cells in the bone marrow,1,2 with a higher prevalence among males aged 50–80 years.3 The most common clinical manifestations include anemia, fatigue, hemostatic disorders, immunodeficiency, weight loss, hypercalcemia, persistent bone pain, and osteolytic lesions.4 These lesions arise from the activation of osteoclast precursors by neoplastic plasma cells, resulting in dysregulation of the balance between bone resorption and formation.5

Studies indicate that 80%–90% of patients with MM develop osteolytic lesions, with a predilection for the vertebrae, skull, pelvis, ribs, and long bones.6,7,8 The gnathic bones may also be affected in 30%–100% of cases, with greater involvement of the mandible.3,9,10,11,12,13 Oral manifestations include osteolytic lesions, pathological fractures, tissue enlargement, gingival hemorrhage, ulcerations, and amyloidosis.14,15

Early detection of these bone changes through imaging is essential for the diagnosis, follow-up, and prognosis of MM.16 Although 2-dimensional imaging modalities are still widely used, 3-dimensional techniques—such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT)—are increasingly adopted because they enable detailed visualization of the head and neck region, ranging from localized fields to the skullcap and cervical vertebrae.17,18,19 Recent studies have reported cases in which osteolytic lesions were incidentally identified on CBCT scans performed for dental purposes, leading to referral for specialized medical evaluation.10,11,20

Osteolytic lesions associated with MM may exhibit diverse tomographic patterns, including diffuse or infiltrative lesions,9 small and multiple well-defined decorticated lesions (punched-out pattern),10,11 and focal unilocular or multilocular lesions.12,13,21 Given the clinical relevance of these lesions in the head and neck region, the advantages of CBCT, and the limited number of studies systematically describing these findings, this scoping review aimed to map the available scientific evidence on the contribution of CBCT to the evaluation of MM-related lesions and to the early diagnosis of the disease, while also identifying directions for future research.

Materials and Methods

Protocol and registration

This scoping review sought to answer the following questions: What is the contribution of CBCT to the evaluation and identification of MM lesions? What is the role of CBCT and the oral radiologist in the early diagnosis of MM? Which anatomical sites in the head and neck region are most affected by bone destruction resulting from MM? What are the most frequent tomographic patterns of MM lesions in the head and neck region? This study was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines.22 The main topics explored were osteolytic lesions caused by MM and CBCT. The protocol for this scoping review was published in the Open Science Framework (osf.io/kr5uz) under the registration https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/DTC6G.

This review was not intended to compare CBCT with other imaging modalities, but rather to specifically map the evidence related to CBCT findings in MM.

Eligibility criteria

Considering the Population (P), Concept (C), and Context (C) framework recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute,23 studies involving patients diagnosed with MM, either before or after the proposed evaluation (P), who underwent CBCT (C) for analysis of bone structures in the head and/or neck region (C) were included.

Studies with the following designs were included: case reports, case series, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, and diagnostic studies. In addition, completed systematic reviews and meta-analyses were also considered eligible to support and strengthen the present scoping review.

Studies in which patients already diagnosed with MM underwent CBCT because they developed medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaws were excluded. Incomplete texts, conference abstracts, letters to the editor, editorials, and experimental animal studies were also excluded. No restrictions were applied regarding sex, age, language, or date of publication.

Sources of information

Systematic searches were conducted in 6 databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane Library, and LILACS (Latin American and Caribbean Literature in Health Sciences). In a second stage, other sources, including OpenGrey and Google Scholar, were searched. In addition, a manual search of the reference lists of the selected articles was performed. Experts were identified through the Expertscape website (https://expertscape.com) and contacted by e-mail to obtain additional data and/or information on ongoing studies. To keep the search strategy up to date, alerts were established for each database. The searches were updated through November 2025.

Search strategies

The search strategies were developed using controlled vocabulary (MeSH), entry terms, and free-text terms related to MM and CBCT. Initially, a search strategy was developed for PubMed and was subsequently adapted for the other databases according to their respective syntax requirements. All terms were combined using the Boolean operators “OR” and “AND” to construct the search strategies. Table 1 presents the search strategy used for each database.

Selection of articles

The references were exported to EndNote (https://access.clarivate.com/login?app=endnote), where duplicates were removed automatically. The remaining duplicates were then removed manually using Rayyan (https://www.rayyan.ai). Next, the studies were selected in 2 stages by 2 independent reviewers. In the first stage, titles and abstracts were screened according to the eligibility criteria. In the second stage, all eligible and potentially eligible articles were read in full. During title and abstract screening, studies broadly addressing oral manifestations of MM were not excluded when the imaging modality was not explicitly stated, in order to avoid premature exclusion. These records were subsequently assessed during the full-text eligibility stage and excluded when the absence of CBCT was confirmed. Disagreements at any stage were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. Articles retrieved in languages unknown to the reviewers were translated using Google Translate (https://translate.google.com).

Data extraction and synthesis

Data were extracted using a standardized spreadsheet that included the following information: 1) study characteristics (authors, year of publication, country, journal, design, and objective); 2) sample characteristics (sex, age, oral manifestations, and time of diagnosis); 3) CBCT characteristics, including device and brand, as well as acquisition protocol (kVp/mAs, field of view, and voxel size [mm]); 4) results and/or tomographic description of the lesions; 5) conclusions; and 6) information on funding and conflicts of interest. The 2 reviewers independently read the full texts and extracted the data. After extraction, the reviewers’ spreadsheets were compared, and disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. All information extracted from each article was summarized in a table.

Data related to study design, patient sex and age, reported oral manifestations, and the temporal relationship between CBCT and the timing of MM diagnosis were compiled. Graphs were prepared to present the percentages of available data from the included studies in figure format. In addition, illustrative figures were created to synthesize the reported data on the country in which each study was conducted, the anatomical sites affected, and the tomographic patterns of the lesions, based on the descriptions of bone destruction resulting from MM visualized on CBCT images.

The gray-literature search yielded 631 references, including 300 from Google Scholar (the first 300 listings, accessed on 07/15/2025) and 331 identified from the reference lists of previously included studies. Of these, 62 references were selected for initial screening because they appeared potentially relevant to the inclusion criteria. After exclusion of 21 duplicates, 41 studies were evaluated. Of these, 12 were excluded because they addressed cases of osteonecrosis of the jaws, 9 because they did not use CBCT as an imaging modality, and 12 because they were conference abstracts or editorials. Ultimately, 8 additional articles from the gray literature were included.10,12,13,15,28,29,30,31 Thus, the total number of studies included in this scoping review was 17.

Characteristics of the articles included and summary of the data

All studies were published within the last 17 years, with the earliest published in 200925 and the most recent in 2025.26 The studies were conducted in the following countries: Brazil,9,26,27,28,29 India,10,13,30,31 the United States,15,25,32,33,34 Australia,11,12 and Saudi Arabia.20 A figure was prepared to illustrate the country in which each study was conducted on a world map (Fig. 2). Of the 17 articles, 4 were cross-sectional observational studies,9,26,27,28 2 were case series,12,15 and 11 were case reports.10,11,13,20,25,29,30,31,32,33,34 The articles were published in journals focused on maxillofacial pathology, diagnostic imaging, and oncology, and most were published in dentistry journals.

Countries in which the included studies were conducted: Saudi Arabia (n=1), Australia (n=2), India (n=4), Brazil (n=5), and the United States (n=5).

In general, the case reports and case series aimed to describe manifestations of MM in the head and neck region, using CBCT to evaluate bone involvement, particularly in the gnathic bones.10,11,12,13,15,20,25,26,29,30,31 Two case reports described the use of CBCT as an imaging modality to guide vertebroplasty procedures in cervical vertebrae affected by osteolytic lesions caused by MM.32,34 The cross-sectional studies aimed to investigate the characteristics of bone trabeculae,27 tomographic patterns of bone destruction in patients with MM,9,26 or differences between CBCT images and panoramic radiographs in patients with MM.28

The 17 included studies collectively involved 135 patients. All case reports described a single patient. Among the 5 patients included in 1 case series, only 1 underwent CBCT to evaluate bone involvement in the gnathic region.15 In the other case series, which included 6 patients, only 2 underwent CBCT imaging.12 The number of patients included in the cross-sectional studies ranged from 2726 to 33.9,27

Among the 11 case reports, 7 involved male patients10,11,25,31,32,33,34 and 4 involved female patients.13,20,29,30 The only patient who underwent CBCT in 1 of the case series was male,15 whereas in the other case series, in which 2 patients underwent this examination, 1 patient was male and 1 was female.12 In 2 cross-sectional studies,26,27 the proportion of male participants was slightly higher (approximately 55%). The ages of the patients included in the studies ranged from 25 years15 to 80 years,25,26 with most patients affected in the sixth and seventh decades of life. One study27 reported only the mean age of the 33 patients in its sample, which was 60 years. Two studies did not report either the sex or age of the patients.9,28 Among the 41 patients whose ages were reported, 23 were younger than 60 years10,12,15,26,29,31,32,33 and 18 were older than 60 years.11,13,20,25,26,30,34

Eight of the 11 studies that reported oral manifestations described intraoral volume enlargement.10,13,15,20,25,29,30,31 The term “intraoral volume enlargement” was used as a descriptive category to encompass clinically reported enlargement of intraoral soft and/or hard tissues, including soft tissue swelling and bone expansion associated with osteolytic lesions, as described in the included studies. In 5 cases, the authors reported that the patient complained of pain in the affected region,10,12,13,29,33 whereas in 3 cases, the patient reported decreased sensation or paresthesia.25,29,30 In 1 case, the patient presented with macroglossia due to amyloidosis of the tongue.12 In 9 of the 17 studies,10,11,13,20,25,29,30,31,33 the patient was referred for specialized medical evaluation based on findings from the clinical oral examination and/or the identification of osteolytic lesions on CBCT. In the remaining 8 studies, patients had already been diagnosed with MM. In 1 case, the patient was completely asymptomatic, and MM began to be investigated after osteolytic lesions in the mandible and cervical vertebrae were identified on CBCT scans obtained for dental implant planning.11 In this context, the contribution of CBCT to early diagnosis was inferred from cases in which CBCT findings preceded the diagnosis of MM or prompted referral for medical investigation. To provide a visual summary of the distribution of available data across the included studies regarding study design, patient sex and age, reported oral manifestations, and the temporal relationship between CBCT and MM diagnosis, a figure was created containing percentage-based graphs and identifiers for the articles from which the data were obtained (Fig. 3).

Only 3 studies reported the characteristics of the CBCT scanner and the image acquisition protocol.9,26,27 The mandible was the most frequently affected anatomical structure, and osteolytic lesions at this site were reported in 13 of the 17 articles included in this review.9,10,11,12,13,15,25,26,28,29,30,31,33 In 5 studies,9,12,20,26,28 osteolytic lesions in the maxilla were identified and described. Osteolytic lesions in the skull were reported in 4 of the 17 studies,10,11,20,31 and 5 studies described lesions in the cervical vertebrae.11,20,32,33,34 Ten studies9,10,12,13,15,20,25,26,28,33 described the lesions as osteolytic, relatively extensive, irregular, and poorly defined. Five studies10,11,20,29,31 reported punched-out lesions, characterized by multiple small hypoattenuating areas with well-defined, decorticated borders. Three articles30,32,34 did not mention either of these patterns and instead described the osteolytic lesions as extensive bone destruction with defined margins. Involvement of the cortical bone, including expansion, thinning, or rupture, was the most frequently observed effect and was reported in 8 studies,9,10,13,20,25,29,30,33 as was dental involvement in 5 studies.9,12,15,25,29 In 1 case report,20 involvement of the maxillary sinus, frontal sinus, and maxillary tuberosity was also observed. In addition, 1 study mentioned involvement of the mandibular canal in the tomographic description of the lesion.10

Most studies concluded that recognition of the oral manifestations and tomographic characteristics of osteolytic lesions is essential for the early diagnosis of MM, underscoring the need to consider MM in the differential diagnosis, particularly in older adults. Discussion

This scoping review demonstrated that CBCT frequently reveals extensive, ill-defined osteolytic lesions in the gnathic bones, particularly in the mandible, and often represents the initial imaging finding leading to medical referral and diagnosis of MM.

Recent studies that analyzed osteolytic lesions in the head and neck region of patients with MM using CBCT9,11,12,20,26 have reported a considerable prevalence of these lesions in this anatomical region, particularly in the mandible, and/or have described their identification as the first sign of MM.11,20,25 The present scoping review mapped the available literature on the contribution of CBCT to the identification and evaluation of MM-related lesions and to the early diagnosis of the disease, especially in light of the absence of previously published systematic or scoping reviews on this topic. Based on the findings presented, it was possible to observe that, despite the excellent diagnostic performance of CBCT in detecting bone lesions and its widespread use in dental practice, few studies have evaluated and characterized the tomographic patterns of lesions resulting from MM. Furthermore, most studies described only a single case, highlighting the need for future investigations with larger cohorts and more rigorous methodological designs to generate stronger evidence.

Numerous studies have examined the gnathic bones of patients with MM using radiographic examinations, particularly panoramic radiography,35,36,37 which remains the most commonly requested and widely used imaging modality in dentistry. However, panoramic radiography, like other 2-dimensional techniques, has limited accuracy and low specificity for the detection of bone alterations.38,39 Moreover, variability in lesion patterns and size can make diagnosis based on radiographic examinations particularly challenging, especially in the early stages of lesion development. Early diagnosis is essential to avoid the need for invasive treatment and to improve patient prognosis, particularly in cases of malignant neoplasms that may progress rapidly and metastasize.

CBCT is a diagnostic imaging modality with high spatial resolution for the evaluation of hard tissues and, compared with other tomographic techniques, is associated with lower radiation exposure17 and lower cost.40 Compared with panoramic radiography, recent studies have demonstrated the superiority of CBCT for the diagnosis of various conditions affecting the head and neck region, with greater clinical relevance for early diagnosis and treatment planning.41,42,43,44

Five included studies10,11,20,29,31 described the lesions on tomographic images as multiple small hypoattenuating areas with well-defined, decorticated margins, corresponding to the punched-out pattern commonly observed on 2-dimensional examinations. In contrast, volumetric analysis did not demonstrate a true punched-out pattern; instead, a diffuse osteoporotic destruction pattern involving both medullary and cortical bone was predominant. The absence of the punched-out pattern was attributed to the 3-dimensional visualization provided by CBCT, which eliminates structural overlap. Because of the partial-volume effect inherent to tomographic imaging, sections obtained through affected cortical bone or thicker reconstructions may falsely suggest a punched-out pattern.9,26 This may explain why some studies reported lesions with this appearance. These findings are consistent with the premise that oral radiology continues to undergo technological advancement, leading to improved image quality and the recognition of new patterns of bone-lesion behavior on volumetric imaging, particularly CBCT.45 This heterogeneity in lesion description reinforces the diagnostic challenge, as the different tomographic findings may raise several differential diagnostic considerations involving gnathic bone lesions, such as brown tumors, solitary plasmacytomas, and even maxillary osteomyelitis.

In addition, some studies10,11,20,31 reported the detection of osteolytic lesions in the skull by CBCT, corroborating the findings of a systematic review46 that concluded that computed tomography has greater capacity than magnetic resonance imaging to detect MM-related lesions in the skull. These findings suggest that CBCT may represent an efficient, less expensive alternative associated with lower radiation exposure than other tomographic modalities.

Notably, in most studies, patients were referred for specialized medical evaluation on the basis of alterations observed during the clinical oral examination and/or the identification of osteolytic lesions on CBCT. Accordingly, most authors10,11,12,15,20,25,26,29,30,31,33 emphasized the role of the dental surgeon (particularly the oral radiologist), which extends beyond clinical support to play a fundamental role in the diagnostic process.47,48 A multidisciplinary team approach is essential for the management of hematological malignancies. Radiologists play a key role in translating imaging findings into clinically meaningful information and are often at the forefront of diagnosis, staging, monitoring, and treatment evaluation.49,50

This relevance stems from the fact that bone alterations resulting from systemic diseases such as MM are often identified in the oral cavity during routine dental examinations. Further studies on osteolytic lesions caused by MM are needed to expand current knowledge and to help establish a diagnostic pathway for dental practice, thereby encouraging early diagnosis of the disease given the importance of the dental surgeon in this context.

The findings of this review indicate that the literature on this topic remains scarce and is dominated by single-case reports, highlighting a substantial knowledge gap. For this reason, there is currently insufficient scientific evidence to support the routine use of CBCT for screening patients diagnosed with or suspected of having MM. However, the analyzed data suggest that CBCT performs sufficiently well to justify further, more robust studies and the training of dental professionals—especially oral and maxillofacial radiologists—to adopt a more sensitive and multidisciplinary diagnostic approach.
