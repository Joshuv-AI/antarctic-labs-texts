Leveraging Data Science for Conducting Observational Studies: Highlighting Advantages and Limitations Throughout the Evaluation of a Use Case
Authors: Jorge A Rios-Duarte, Taylor L Pick, Trece N Robson, Raymond J Lin, Austin Todd, Santiago Romero-Brufau, Nahid Y Vidal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop a data science pipeline for data extraction and collection to conduct an observational study using institutional medical informatics and artificial intelligence tools.Patients and MethodsSkin excision cases exposed to intra-incisional clindamycin (from February 5 to October 14, 2025) and nonexposed (from January 1 to December 31, 2022) were defined as our cohorts of interest. The outcome of our study was surgical site infection (SSI) 30 days after surgery. A large language model (LLM)-supported data science pipeline was used for cohort identification, case screening, and extraction of procedural information. LLM screening and data extraction were validated in 300 random cases. A generalized estimating equation model was used to analyze the effect of intra-incisional clindamycin on SSI.ResultsThe LLM achieved high accuracy for screening cases, with accurate identification of procedures done in the head (accuracy, 99.0%; 95% CI, 97.1%-99.8%) and skin excisions (97.0%; 95% CI, 94.4%-98.6%). In addition, it achieved remarkable performance for extraction of clinical information, with the highest accuracy observed for extraction of anatomical location associated with the procedure (accuracy, 100.0%; 95% CI, 98.8%-100.0%). The final dataset included 2247 skin excision cases (594 exposed and 1653 nonexposed) from 1923 patients. Cases that received intra-incisional clindamycin had lower odds of SSI; however, this association was not statistically significant (aOR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.31-1.07).ConclusionThe implementation of medical informatics tools and robust artificial intelligence algorithms will enable the conduct of end-to-end research pipelines. However, it is important to always consider humans in the loop, as oversight ensures reliability, accuracy, and robustness.

Electronic health records (EHRs) have evolved into indispensable infrastructure for clinical research, enabling large-scale observational studies.1 Widespread EHR adoption has enabled more efficient feasibility assessment, recruitment, screening, and data collection for clinical research.2, 3, 4, 5 Despite challenges regarding data quality and completeness, security, interoperability across diverse systems, and infrastructure maintenance,6, 7, 8 EHRs have transformed data to real-world evidence clinical research when used with appropriate methodological rigor.9

Manual chart review remains the gold standard for extraction from EHRs but is labor-intensive and poorly scalable, especially for the vast majority of data in clinical reports comprised of unstructured information.10, 11, 12, 13 As clinical research relies heavily on narrative EHR text, the need for standardized, efficient data extraction methods has become urgent as databases expand.14,15 The exponential growth of health data, reflecting developing understandings of disease complexity and personalized medicine, will require robust and scalable data strategies.16

Natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning offer the potential to automate information extraction from clinical notes with accuracy approaching or exceeding human-level performance.17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 However, traditional rule-based NLP models often lack generalizability, while machine learning-based NLP models require substantial manual annotation.23, 24, 25, 26 These limitations restrict research that relies on specialized knowledge, confidentiality, or diverse datasets at scale. Large language models (LLMs) employ deep learning models pretrained on large quantities of text, capable of zero-shot and few-shot learning EHR extraction.27, 28, 29 Our institutional informatics tool for data retrieval for research purposes, the Mayo Data Explorer, was recently upgraded with a LLM for text analysis on clinical documents (ACCRUE-L). Herein, we describe the development of a data science pipeline to conduct an observational study using institutional medical informatics and artificial intelligence.

Methods

Case Study: Intra-Incisional Clindamycin for Surgical Site Infection Prevention: A Practice Change

Starting February 5, 2025, our division of dermatologic surgery implemented a practice change for surgical site infection (SSI) prevention at our resident’s excision clinic, consisting in the use of intra-incisional clindamycin (600 mcg/mL clindamycin in 1% lidocaine with epinephrine 1:100,000) during local anesthesia for skin excisions on nonhead anatomical locations. The implementation of this practice change was supported by considerable evidence on the efficacy of intra-incisional clindamycin for SSI prevention in our field. However, there is a relative scarcity of evidence on the effectiveness of this intervention in realistic, noncontrolled scenarios.30,31

Our research question was defined as “What is the effectiveness of intra-incisional clindamycin for SSI prevention following skin excisions?”. To assess the effectiveness of our practice change, we defined 2 cohorts: (1) the exposed cohort, skin excisions on nonhead anatomical locations from February 5 2025, to October 14, 2025, with documented intra-incisional clindamycin application and (2) the nonexposed cohort, composed of skin excisions on nonhead anatomical locations from January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2022. Cases from patients who died within the 45 days after surgery, had an associated diagnosis of infection or hidradenitis suppurativa at the time of surgery, or had incomplete data were excluded from the final study dataset. SSI was defined as documentation of the event in the patient EHRs within 30 days after surgery.

Two types of statistical analyses were performed. A descriptive statistical analysis was conducted to provide meaningful summaries of our recorded variables such as infection rate, surgical characteristics, and demographic characteristics. In addition, an inferential statistical analysis was performed to estimate the effect of intra-incisional clindamycin on SSIs by comparing exposed and nonexposed cohorts. A generalized estimating equation model was used given the presence of repeated measures (patients might contribute more than one case during study time). Two models were implemented: a simple model with no covariates and a second model adjusted for potential measured confounders. P values <.05 were considered significant. For statistical analysis, we used the R programming language.

For planning, we determined definitive factors to narrow our data search, including the definition of our final cohort of interest (dates of case retrieval), selection of variables (eg, demographic characteristics, comorbidities, etc.), definition of variables (eg, history of diabetes defined as the identification of specific diagnostic codes the year before the procedure), and selection of data sources (eg, access to specific sections of our data lake). The planning stage ended with the development of a search strategy for the identification of our cases of interest (exposed and nonexposed cohort). Both exposed and nonexposed cases were identified and retrieved using metadata embedded in our clinical documents (eg, procedural notes from our department), but an additional filter was included for our exposed cohort to only include notes associated with documentation of intra-incisional clindamycin use. A list of the final included variables and their definition is provided in the Supplementary Table, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/) from the Supplementary Appendix.

Access to our institutional data lake and data collection were performed using one of our internal medical informatics tools developed for researchers at our institution. This specialized tool allows accessing to specific sections of our data lake through well-defined tables (eg, diagnoses, orders, etc.), enabling the use of different variables for filtering on the data tables (eg, sex, age, etc.). To identify potential SSIs, we retrieved metadata from orders, medications administered, and diagnostic codes generated during the 45 days after surgery to screen patients for posterior manual chart review and confirmation of SSI within 30 days after surgery. Additional information on the screening process for SSI is provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Researchers at our institution (led by coauthor SRB) implemented an internal LLM fine-tuned for extraction of data and Q&A from clinical documents (the ACCRUE-L project) accessible through the medical informatics tool. Use of this internal artificial intelligence tool enabled the automatic extraction of specific information from procedure notes (procedure characteristics) as well as automatic screening of surgical cases based on inclusion/exclusion criteria using a set of prompts (more information in the Supplementary Appendix). To validate data extraction and screening using LLM, we randomly selected 300 cases for manual validation by estimating the accuracy of extraction and screening based on EHRs.

Data were preprocessed using R programming. This included multiple steps of table joining, filtering based on conditionals, and producing structured data from semistructured data (derived from LLM extraction) to generate a final dataset including our cohorts of interest as well as the specified variables. The final unit of analysis was the individual skin excision cases. Figure 2 illustrates the process of generating our final dataset, showing the preprocessing steps performed.

Results

LLM Data Extraction

The LLM successfully assisted our screening process for selection of eligible surgical cases by using Q&A capabilities based on our inclusion/exclusion criteria. The model correctly identified whether the case note described a procedure done on the head 99.0% (95% CI, 97.1%-99.8%) of the time. In addition, the model correctly identified that the procedure described was a skin excision 97.0% of the time (95% CI, 94.4%-98.6%).

Our institutional LLM also had a remarkable performance for the extraction of relevant clinical information from unstructured clinical notes for dataset development in a semistructured format. The highest accuracy was observed for extraction of anatomical location associated with the procedure (accuracy, 100.0%; 95% CI, [98.8%-100.0%]), followed by preoperative size (99.7%; 95% CI, 98.2%-100.0%), and both diagnosis associated with the procedure and surgical reconstruction, which achieved the same accuracy (99.3%; 95% CI, 97.6%-99.9%).

Clindamycin Practice Change Effectiveness

The final dataset included 2247 skin excision cases from 1923 patients. A total of 594 cases were included in the exposed group and 1653 in the nonexposed group. A diagram showing the data flow through our designed pipeline is shown in Figure 3. Regarding case-level demographic characteristics, 56.1% of the cases were performed in female patients with a mean age of 61.9 (SD, 16.6). Regarding specific surgical characteristics, the most common diagnosis was melanoma (20.3%, n=457/2247) followed by any nevus (19.7%, n=442/2247). Most cases were performed on the trunk/abdomen/anogenital area (53.0%, n=1192/2247), and were closed using primary closure (97.7%, n=2195/2247). In terms of outcomes, SSI was noted on only 3.0% (n=67/2247), with 55/1653 (3.3%) cases documented in the nonexposed cohort and 12/594 (2.0%) cases in the exposed cohort. Table 1 displays in detail the demographic characteristics and surgical characteristics at the case level. Cases that received intra-incisional clindamycin had lower odds of SSI; however, this association was not statistically significant (OR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.31-1.11). When modeling the effect of intra-incisional clindamycin adjusted by measured confounders, we found a similar effect, but this remained nonsignificant (aOR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.31-1.07). Discussion

Although EHRs contain vast quantities of data, reliance on manual chart review has limited scalability for large observational studies. Despite limitations and considerations, LLMs perform well in NLP tasks, offering a potential solution for extracting unstructured data from the growing quantity of medical documents. Our case study reported the development and feasibility of a data science pipeline to conduct an observational study, leveraging a LLM for Q&A and data extraction.

Scope of Our Method and Limitations

Several technical and contextual limitations challenge the generalizability of our pipeline. Substantial data science expertise was required, as LLMs only assist in data extraction. Most LLMs lack intrinsic reliability with the potential to generate factually incorrect outputs, making rigorous validation essential before considering clinical application.32,33 The present model cannot independently perform preprocessing, cohort identification, filtering, or data manipulation, all of which require human effort.

In addition, the performance of LLMs for medical Q&A and data extraction might differ depending on the primary source of data (eg, procedural notes, radiology reports, etc.). Surgical notes within our institution utilize standardized note templates. In clinical settings with less standardization across EHRs, the LLM text analysis process might be more complex. Task-dependent performance remains an important consideration, as LLMs report higher accuracy for structured extraction tasks (such as surgical sites or defect sizes) compared with complex interpretive tasks (such as differential diagnoses).34

Documentation practices vary across providers even with the use of standardized templates, as physicians write notes for their intended clinical audience, not for researchers. Although surgical sites or defect sizes may be described in a consistent, explicit format across EHRs, diagnoses may take on forms that reflect subjective clinical reasoning. Prompt engineering introduces further complexity, as relevant and accurate responses require structuring and optimization.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge that the practice change evaluation encompassed 2 different periods (2025 vs 2022). This makes our research and results prone to bias given some unmeasurable or inherent differences related to time that are either complicated to account for or nearly impossible to identify. However, at our division of dermatologic surgery, SSI prevention measures have been stable during the last years with the adoption of standard protocols for the preparation of the operative site as well as SSI prophylaxis.

Lessons Learned

Collaboration between the authors, information technology experts, and the team behind ACCRUE-L was pivotal for the development of our pipeline and validation of uncommon or confusing model outputs. This multidisciplinary approach aligns with established best practices with EHR-based research, forming teams that include clinicians, informaticians, and data scientists.35,36

Furthermore, statistical sampling for manual chart review and confirmation of SSI data provided dual validation, assessing LLM case screening and extraction accuracy from clinical notes and verifying clinical conclusions through complete chart review for SSIs. This approach facilitated both technical and clinical validation. Without institutional information technology infrastructure support, multidisciplinary collaboration, and dedicated resources for ongoing data validation and monitoring, applications of these tools may be restricted.

The secondary use of EHR data for research presents fundamental challenges intrinsic to their original purpose as EHRs were designed for clinical documentation, billing, and medical-legal purposes. This difference creates inherent mismatches between clinical data and research needs.37 Understanding clinical metadata requires institutional knowledge and clinical expertise, increasing personnel requirements and highlighting the importance of collaboration. Although EHR systems store vast quantities of data, the lack of standardization, completeness, and structure, limits research applications, especially when attempting novel studies. Institutions must invest in data infrastructure to support data-driven strategies, as this will enable robust research and quality-improving measurements as a secondary use case while benefiting patient care. This includes implementation of security filters, adequate information technology staffing, and mechanisms for continuous data quality monitoring.

Conclusion

The implementation of medical informatics tools in combination with the deployment of robust artificial intelligence algorithms will enable the conduct of end-to-end research pipelines in dermatology and other medical specialties. However, it is important to always consider pipelines that keep humans in the loop, as human oversight ensures reliability and accuracy while enhancing trust and robustness.
