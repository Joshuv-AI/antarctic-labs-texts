Multi-Domain Security for 6G ISAC: Challenges and Opportunities in Transportation
Musa Furkan Keskin, Muralikrishnan Srinivasan, Onur Günlü, Hui Chen, Panagiotis Papadimitratos, Magnus Almgren, Zhongxia Simon He, and Henk Wymeersch

Abstract
Integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) will be central to 6G-enabled transportation, providing both seamless connectivity and high-precision sensing. However, this tight integration exposes attack points not encountered in pure sensing and communication systems. In this article, we identify unique ISAC-induced security challenges and opportunities in three interrelated domains: cyber-physical (where manipulation of sensors and actuators can mislead perception and control), physical-layer (where over-the-air signals are vulnerable to spoofing and jamming) and protocol (where complex cryptographic protocols cannot detect lower-layer attacks). Building on these insights, we put forward a multi-domain security vision for 6G transportation and propose an integrated security framework that unifies protection across domains.



I
Introduction

The emergence of 6G wireless systems is redefining network design by fusing communication and sensing into a unified framework known as integrated sensing and communication (ISAC). ISAC enables simultaneous data exchange and environmental perception through shared spectrum and hardware
[
9
]
.
Such dual use of resources enhances spectral efficiency, latency and situational awareness, with major impact on transportation by supporting autonomous driving and intelligent traffic management
[
3
]
. However, with this innovation comes a radical shift in security considerations, leading to new vulnerabilities across the protocol stack that cannot be addressed by conventional data and network security mechanisms. Security in this context involves two interdependent aspects:
communication security
, which protects the authenticity, integrity and confidentiality of transmitted data, and
sensing security
, which safeguards the privacy of sensed entities and ensures the trustworthiness of sensing results against attacks
[
15
]
.

Prior research on secure positioning has largely focused on authenticated peers and protected message exchanges
[
10
]
while ISAC extends the challenge to
uncooperative and passive targets
, shifting part of the attack surface into the environment itself. As a result, adversaries can exploit unencrypted over-the-air features, such as range, Doppler and angle, for unauthorized sensing and manipulate echoes to create ghost objects
[
12
,
1
]
. In addition, due to joint use of waveforms for sensing and communications, cross-layer coupling becomes tighter in ISAC, resulting in faster error propagation across layers (e.g., sensing spoofing modifies beam selection, which degrades link reliability). These characteristics distinguish secure ISAC from classical secure positioning and demand a broader multi-domain defense tailored to unique threat surfaces introduced in ISAC, spanning
(i) the cyber-physical domain
(sensor/actuator integrity and control-loop safety),
(ii) the physical-layer domain
(robustness to spoofing/jamming/interference and environmental manipulation) and
(iii) the protocol domain
(cryptographic measures to ensure authenticity, integrity and confidentiality for both data and sensing information).

ISAC-induced vulnerabilities in transportation systems span the cyber-physical, physical-layer and protocol domains. The cyber-physical domain forms the interface between physical environment and digital decision-making, where sensors (such as cameras and radar) observe the physical environment and provide input to autonomous decision systems, while actuators execute these decisions (e.g., steering). The physical-layer governs the generation, propagation and reception of waveforms that simultaneously support communication and environmental sensing. The protocol domain provides cryptographic protocols to ensure authenticity, integrity and confidentiality of user data and sensing information. A security breach in any domain can cascade into system-wide failures, calling for a coordinated multi-domain defense strategy.

Existing works on the intersection of ISAC and security either focus primarily on physical-layer security (PLS) techniques

or examine different layers in isolation

(ii)

While our framework is application-agnostic, we emphasize transportation use cases where the consequences of insecure ISAC are particularly critical. II
Security Risks and Enhancements by ISAC

In contrast to traditional systems, where sensing and communication operate independently, the dual functionality of ISAC creates tightly coupled dependencies: a security compromise of one functionality can propagate into the other (see Fig.
1
). This section systematically analyzes ISAC-induced risks and enhancements across the three domains, with a focus on ISAC-specific features that distinguish it from pure sensing and pure communication systems, as shown in Table
I
. We categorize attacks according to the domain of the legitimate entity being targeted rather than the attacker’s domain (i.e., the victim defines the domain).

II-A

Domain 1: Cyber-Physical

II-A
1
Security Risks by ISAC

In traditional systems, onboard sensors are self-contained and operate independently of the communication infrastructure. They can be targeted through local spoofing and tampering, but adversaries typically need to be physically close to influence their behavior. Here, ISAC introduces two new attack surfaces. First, ISAC enables external sensing by infrastructure such as base stations (BSs) and roadside units (RSUs), whose measurements may feed directly into onboard perception and control algorithms. This effectively extends the sensor boundary beyond the vehicle, introducing two vulnerabilities:
(i)
malicious RSUs/BSs (internal adversaries) and
(ii)
attacks on their ISAC functionality by external adversaries through spoofing and jamming
[
2
]
. In both cases, corrupted infrastructure data can mislead onboard systems that lack cross-validation with local sensors, which may cause unsafe actuation (e.g., emergency braking triggered by phantom obstacles). Although such signal-level manipulation originates in the physical-layer, its impact manifests as a cyber-physical domain failure. Second, ISAC communication can rely on sensing results (e.g., for beam alignment) so that attacks against onboard sensors may compromise communication performance (e.g., reliability and latency)
[
5
]
.

II-A
2
Security Enhancements by ISAC

While bringing new security risks, ISAC also offers opportunities to improve resilience at the cyber-physical domain. First, one of its strengths lies in enabling independent, infrastructure-based sensing that allows for cross-verification of onboard sensor data. If, for instance, a vehicle’s radar is spoofed to hide a nearby obstacle, an ISAC-enabled BS might still detect it and notify the vehicle. This external input can help resolve conflicting observations. Infrastructure-based ISAC sensing also operates at elevated viewpoints over wide areas, providing a broad and complementary view of the environment. These perspectives make it possible to detect inconsistencies, such as a mismatch between a predicted trajectory and range-Doppler observations, and raise security flags. Second, ISAC can act as a fallback when onboard sensing fails. Infrastructure-based sensing may provide sufficient situational awareness to keep the system functioning safely. Moreover, inputs from ISAC-enabled RSUs/BSs can help vehicles validate claims by other vehicles (e.g., their geostamps), which can prevent attacks by malicious entities from impacting actuator decisions. Overall, the enhancement at the cyber-physical domain comes from a strengthened infrastructure with sensing in the sense that it can have a broad perception of the physical aspects of the system, enabling more resilient sensor/actuator loops.

II-B

Domain 2: Physical-Layer

II-B
1
Security Risks by ISAC

The dual-functionality of ISAC systems expands the attack surface at the physical layer in various ways and exposes both functionalities to new threats not arising in pure sensing and communication systems. The first threat concerns sensing information leakage and privacy violations. As ISAC signals propagate through the environment, they reflect off objects like vehicles and pedestrians, and thus directly embed geometric information such as position and velocity (i.e., through range, angle and Doppler measurements). Therefore, unlike traditional communication systems where the outcome of sensing is transmitted via secured data (e.g., exchange of sensory data via V2X communications for cooperative perception), ISAC allows external adversarial receivers to extract environmental information directly from the received waveform (e.g., using unencrypted, known pilots) without, for example, decrypting the payload. As an example, to undermine location privacy in spite of pseudonymity, unlinkability or even encryption that could be applied even on safety beacons and conceal location data, an eavesdropper can passively intercept Wi-Fi signals and estimate object trajectories
[
2
]
.

In addition to the threats resulting from passive attacks, which fall under the umbrella of
exploratory attacks

[
4
]
, malicious entities can also launch active attacks against ISAC systems. Adversaries may inject false channel state information (CSI) via pilot contamination attacks, distort channel feedback and perform coordinated jamming, all of which degrade sensing accuracy and communication performance (e.g., CSI-based precoding)
[
4
]
. The structured nature of ISAC waveforms (e.g., standardized pilots in Wi-Fi sensing
[
2
]
) makes them vulnerable to spoofing attacks, where adversaries inject false reflections to create phantom targets. These active signal manipulations can mislead both sensing (e.g., object detection) and communication (e.g., beam alignment, user tracking), leading to more severe consequences in ISAC than in pure sensing and communication. Beyond conventional threats, emerging technologies integrated into ISAC, such as reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RISs) and machine learning, introduce new attack vectors. An adversarial RIS can manipulate the propagation environment by altering reflection angles to distort received signals
[
1
]
. Similarly, learning-based ISAC functions (e.g., for channel charting and beam prediction) are vulnerable to adversarial attacks (e.g., data poisoning during training and RF fingerprint spoofing during inference). Here, even minor perturbations in channel features can induce large errors in localization.

II-B
2
Security Enhancements by ISAC

ISAC brings security enhancements at the physical layer in numerous aspects. First, coding-theoretic methods can enable secure communication rates to exceed the secrecy capacity of ISAC systems with the help of sensing feedback
[
5
]
. Similarly, joint coding and beamforming strategies can be developed for sensing-assisted secure communication. Moreover, integrated communications and private state sensing can be achieved for ISAC channels that can be modeled as state-dependent wiretap channels. Relative to pure secure communications, the enhancement arises because ISAC provides an implicit, geometry-coupled sensing feedback via echoes. This causal feedback can be exploited to surpass no-feedback secrecy limits and to adapt codebooks/beamforming under fast V2X mobility without explicit CSI overhead. Second, an ISAC BS can leverage backscattered downlink signals to sense both the UE and surrounding objects, and optimize its beamforming to focus energy on the UE while suppressing leakage to the directions of the objects, considered as potential adversarial receivers
[
11
]
. Following a similar spirit to sensing-aided beamforming, ISAC also enables adaptive artificial noise (AN) injection in both spatial and temporal domains. Specifically, AN can be directed towards adversarial regions to degrade their SNR while preserving a required signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) at the legitimate UE
[
11
]
. Sensing-aided AN injection not only strengthens
data confidentiality
(by protecting user data from eavesdropping) but also improves
sensing privacy
(by distorting pilots and thus impairing the ability of adversaries to perform sensing of UEs and objects in the environment).

II-C

Domain 3: Protocol

II-C
1
Security Risks by ISAC

As discussed in Sec.
II-B
, in ISAC systems the attackers can sidestep encryption entirely by exploiting the analog sensing channel, extracting private information without violating digital payload confidentiality. Moreover, ISAC systems often rely on real-time feedback and cross-layer optimization, introducing tight coupling between layers. An adversary who manipulates lower-layer behavior (e.g., spoofed CSI, injected delay) can trigger protocol-level decisions such as handovers, route changes and beam tracking updates based on corrupted sensing inputs.
A key challenge is to distinguish malicious manipulations from benign new nodes, for example, by checking consistency in transmit power, spatial alignment and timing. This kind of control-plane poisoning is amplified by the vertical integration of ISAC. In addition, post-quantum cryptography (PQC) and its resource demands pose new concerns for ISAC. The large key sizes and processing delays of PQC schemes may be incompatible with low-latency and real-time sensing requirements of ISAC, especially in safety-critical domains like automated driving and drone navigation.

II-C
2
Security Enhancements by ISAC

Despite these risks, ISAC also opens up novel paths for strengthening higher-layer security through context-aware cryptography and sensing-informed trust mechanisms. ISAC sensing can support location-based authentication and context-aware key generation. For example, a shared key may be derived not only from the wireless channel but also from aligned sensing events, e.g., by detecting the same target and confirming co-presence at a location. This approach serves as a source of secrecy based on information (i.e., environmental signatures) that are not easy to guess for attackers. As a further enhancement at higher layers, sensing information can improve trust management and anomaly detection. To create a hybrid trust model that combines digital authentication with physical evidence, devices whose trajectories, RF fingerprints and Doppler histories deviate from expected patterns can be flagged as suspicious even when their cryptographic credentials remain valid. ISAC further enables upper-layer systems to cross-check communication integrity using physical-layer cues, such as abrupt beam misalignment and inconsistent signal strength (which may indicate tampering/spoofing). In this way, cryptographic security is extended into the physical domain to improve resilience against attacks that manipulate real-world signal behavior.

III
Integrated Multi-Domain Approach to Secure ISAC

Sec.
II
outlines defenses within individual domains, often designed to operate in isolation. In reality, threats in ISAC-enabled transportation rarely stay confined to a single domain. For instance, control decisions in the cyber-physical domain can be detected at the physical layer
[
6
]
, while sensing data leakage can undermine cryptographic guarantees at higher layers. To address this interdependence, we introduce four cross-domain approaches that together form a security cycle:
(i)
authentication,
(ii)
key generation,
(iii)
anomaly detection and
(iv)
security adaptation (see Fig.
2
), ensuring coordination rather than replacement of domain-specific measures.
Authentication fusion
combines physical-layer fingerprints with cryptographic methods, countering impersonation even when devices evade cryptographic encryption. The generation of
cross-layer keys
transforms the randomness of the physical layer into a secret key, mitigating risks such as pilot contamination.
Cross-layer anomaly detection
correlates sensing dynamics with protocol layer reports to determine inconsistencies caused by RIS manipulation, AI poisoning, remote device spoofing, etc. Finally,
dynamic security adaptation
ensures that once anomalies are detected, the response mechanism is robust. Therefore, unlike Sec.
II
, where domain-internal risks and enhancements are discussed, Sec.
III
introduces cross-domain defenses that explicitly fuse evidence (e.g., physical fingerprints and cryptographic credentials) and route signals (e.g., from sensing features to key derivation) across domains.

III-A

Authentication Fusion

ISAC-enabled physical layer authentication (PLA) can leverage channel‐specific fingerprints, such as channel impulse response (CIR), angle and Doppler, that are difficult to forge over the millisecond time scales relevant to transportation. In an ISAC transceiver, pilots already transmitted for joint radar/communication naturally expose these fingerprints, eliminating the need for additional signaling. The verifier builds the features of the legitimate node during the offline acquisition/training phase and compares it with the ones freshly measured for every ISAC frame during the online authentication phase. To sharpen discrimination, ISAC hardware can actively shape the channel in real time by, e.g., randomly selecting a RIS pattern or switching to a pseudo-random beam. The goal is to ensure that only a prover physically present at the same location will exhibit channel variations consistent with the controlled perturbations of the verifier
[
13
]
. PLA is fast and context-aware, but by itself does not offer long-term key binding. Cross-layer fusion of PLA and protocol-domain cryptography can run both checks within a single ISAC frame:
(i)
the transmitter appends a lightweight cryptographic key, generated using popular cryptographic algorithms, and
(ii)
the verifier performs the PLA test on the induced feature. The packet is accepted only if both the digital token and the physical signature match. Cross-layer challenge-response designs of this form have been shown to significantly reduce the probabilities of impersonation and replay success, compared to the use of either layer alone, while adding negligible airtime overhead
[
8
]
. In transportation scenarios such as intersection crossing and platooning, ISAC-enabled sensing features (e.g., Doppler, angle-of-arrival (AoA), or angle-of-departure (AoD)) provide richer fingerprints than conventional communication-only signals, making PLA-cryptography fusion especially effective. Through dual-factor handshakes, the run-time efficiency and quantum-resilience of PLA can be combined with the established robustness of cryptographic methods to yield a scalable authentication framework for multi-domain secure ISAC, which can limit the impact of adversaries attempting to impersonate control/sensing nodes to inject false data.

III-B

Cross-Layer Key Generation

To turn intra-domain features into cross-domain keys suitable for session establishment and rekeying, we can bind physical/sensing context to cryptographic material by building on protocol domain enhancements in Sec.
II-C
. Beyond conventional bidirectional key exchanges, ISAC systems can derive secret keys directly from physical observables using location-dependent randomness as cryptographic material. While authentication fusion leverages these features to verify identity, cross-layer key generation transforms them into stable keys for ongoing secure communication. Since these features are tied to the environment and difficult to reproduce, they provide resilience against impersonation and replay attacks, similar to physical unclonable functions (PUFs) that exploit inherent manufacturing randomness. ISAC-based key generation operates through a layered pipeline that transforms physical randomness and cryptographic material into a unified key, as shown in Fig.
2
. At the
physical layer
, devices such as vehicles or RSUs extract features from channel responses, AoAs/AoDs and multipath signatures, then quantize them into bit strings
[
7
]
.
Reconciliation corrects mismatches across peers and privacy amplification compresses the aligned bits into uniformly random keys resilient to inference attacks. In parallel, the
protocol layer
performs conventional cryptographic operations: authenticating devices, executing key agreement schemes and deriving session keys for secure communication. The two outputs are then fused through
context binding
either via concatenation followed by hashing or by incorporating physical features such as CIR into the key derivation process. The result is a context bound key that integrates the unpredictability of the wireless environment with the structure of standard cryptographic protocols, remaining compatible with existing security infrastructures.

Case study:

A simulation demonstrates the reliability-entropy trade-off, as shown in Fig.
4
. In particular, shorter keys (3 bits) converge at lower SNR while longer ones (6 bits) yield higher entropy but require larger SNR to converge. Even in a challenging adversarial geometry where Eve shares the same angular cluster with Bob but differs in one weak path, her agreement probability remains lower, confirming the robustness of ISAC-derived keys. While the extracted 3-6 physical bits are not intended to replace cryptographic keys, they serve as channel-bound secrets that gate access to heavier higher-layer keying/authentication. Even a few bits of uncontestable entropy from the wireless channel drastically cut down adversarial attempts.

III-C

Cross-Layer Anomaly Detection

In addition to secure key generation, ISAC also enables real-time cross-layer anomaly detection by correlating observables across the physical, protocol and cyber-physical domains, as shown in Fig.
2
. A unified detection engine can expose threats along three complementary dimensions. First,
cross-layer consistency checker
aligns physical features such as AoA and Doppler with protocol metadata and control reports to reveal anomalies, for example, a Doppler-inferred velocity that contradicts a reported trajectory. Second,
spatio-temporal analyzer
evaluates the natural evolution of multipath and Doppler features in temporal and spatial domains, flagging abrupt discontinuities and replayed patterns indicative of spoofing or RIS manipulation.
Finally,
context correlator
validates whether reflections and mobility states are consistent with environmental constraints such as road geometry and traffic flow. Here, mismatches across devices and with the map expose localized forgeries. Since most attacks perturb only one layer at a time, these complementary dimensions strengthen anomaly detection across layers and raise the bar for an adversary attempting to remain undetected.

III-D

Dynamic Security Adaptation

Following anomaly detection, ISAC systems must also react quickly to contain threats before they propagate across domains. Dynamic security adaptation closes the loop by allowing the network to reconfigure itself in real time when inconsistencies appear. At the physical-layer, nodes may sharpen beam patterns, randomize RIS phases and improve radar resolution to reduce exposure toward suspicious directions and enhance situational awareness. The protocol domain can respond by escalating authentication, adding multi-factor checks and switching to alternative observables for key generation when an existing observable becomes unreliable. At the cyber-physical domain, systems can raise trust thresholds and request confirmation from nearby sensors. These measures work best when coordinated. For instance, RSUs can gather anomaly reports and issue updated trust metrics and beam policies while infrastructure-less networks such as UAV swarms and V2V clusters adapt collaboratively through consensus and federated learning.

IV
Open Research Challenges and Future Directions

We outline three emerging security challenges that may not be addressed by current multi-domain approaches, along with future research directions.

IV-A

Security of Distributed and Near-Field ISAC

Distributed ISAC (DISAC) emerges as a technological leap from single-node ISAC systems by enabling multi-view sensing, wider coverage and improved robustness against blockages. In DISAC networks, the requirement for coordination among heterogeneous nodes due to exchange of CSI, sensing results and synchronization signals can significantly enlarge the attack surface, and amplify latency and privacy risks. A promising direction to explore in future studies is the design of cooperative, privacy-preserving multi-domain defense schemes and lightweight trust mechanisms that maintain sensing accuracy and communication reliability across distributed nodes (e.g., resilience against few compromised nodes). Similar to DISAC, near-field ISAC with extremely large MIMO arrays introduces unique security risks due to spherical wavefronts and range-angle coupling. Adversaries can exploit focal-zone hot spots induced by finite-depth near-field beamforming to capture concentrated signal energy (passive attack) and perturb the focus point away from targets/UEs (active attack). Possible mitigations include robust near-field beamforming under security and privacy constraints, accounting for focal point perturbations and side-lobe leakage.

IV-B

Role of AI in Multi-Domain ISAC Security

Through its crucial roles in beam management, waveform design, cooperative perception and decision-making in transportation systems, AI is rapidly becoming a core architectural component of ISAC pipelines. Traditional security assumptions can thus be fundamentally disrupted since vulnerabilities in AI models can cascade across sensing, communication and control. For example, to simultaneously degrade connectivity and situational awareness, adversaries may exploit AI-driven beam prediction in a platoon of vehicles by introducing small perturbations that misalign beams. AI also enables attackers to learn device-specific signatures and design adaptive spoofing and jamming strategies. Conversely, via its support in anomaly detection, distribution-shift monitoring and rapid policy adaptation, AI can also serve as a powerful defense mechanism. Future AI paradigms (such as large-scale foundation models for multi-modal ISAC tasks and federated or distributed training in V2X networks) introduce both opportunities and new risks (e.g., through model leakage, poisoning and synchronization vulnerabilities). More broadly, as software stacks (many of which are now generated and maintained with AI) grow in complexity, software vulnerabilities will inevitably arise that affect sensing and communication in intricate and non-obvious ways.
Future research should therefore treat AI not as a single tool but as an architectural layer in ISAC security design, requiring new threat models (including software-level ones), robust training pipelines and privacy-preserving learning strategies.

IV-C

Post-Quantum Cryptography and Non-Repudiation in ISAC

PQC introduces fundamental challenges for ISAC systems. The large key sizes, heavy computation and extended handshake latencies of PQC conflict with the sub-millisecond timing constraints of vehicular ISAC operations. These limitations call for new co-design principles where cryptographic exchanges are embedded within the ISAC frame structure rather than treated as separate network-layer processes. Key challenges include the allocation of compute and latency budgets across sensing, communication and security tasks, the design of lightweight post-quantum primitives suited for short coherence times, and the synchronization of key management with adaptive waveform scheduling. Beyond confidentiality and authenticity, transportation also demands non-repudiation to ensure post-event accountability. Controlled-access distributed ledgers can record cryptographic hashes of ISAC transactions asynchronously (i.e., without real-time constraints), which enables verifiable accountability among vehicles, infrastructure and control entities without interfering with the real-time loop. The combined design of PQC, PHY-based key generation and distributed ledger auditing thus defines a new security-latency frontier for post-quantum ISAC systems, linking cryptographic assurance to temporal and spatial constraints inherent in 6G vehicular networks.
