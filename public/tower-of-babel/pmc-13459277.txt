Comparison of open and minimally invasive radical hysterectomy for cervical cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis of survival outcomes
Authors: Jiaohong Wu, Renliang Li, Haitang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo systematically evaluate the impact of open versus minimally invasive radical hysterectomy on survival outcomes in cervical cancer patients.MethodsRelevant literature from the past 10 years was searched in PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases. Clinical studies comparing open radical hysterectomy (ORH) and minimally invasive surgery (MIS) were included. Study quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. A meta-analysis was performed to compare survival outcomes between ORH and MIS, with subgroup analysis stratified by tumor size (≤ 2 cm vs. > 2 cm).ResultsFifteen clinical studies met the inclusion criteria. All observational studies scored ≥ 7 on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Patients undergoing ORH showed significantly improved 5-year disease-free survival compared with MIS (OR = 1.70 [1.27, 2.27], P < 0.001, I² = 58%). However, no significant difference was observed in overall survival between the two surgical approaches (OR = 1.08 [0.90, 1.15], P > 0.05). Subgroup analysis showed no significant differences in overall survival between ORH and MIS, regardless of tumor size (≤ 2 cm: HR = 1.10 [0.28, 4.31], P = 0.89; >2 cm: HR = 0.82 [0.38, 1.77], P = 0.61).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that open radical hysterectomy is associated with better disease-free survival, whereas overall survival does not differ significantly between the two approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03878-0.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Radical hysterectomy, Minimally invasive surgery, Meta-analysis, Survival outcomes

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death among women worldwide [1]. In 2022 alone, approximately 350,000 deaths worldwide were attributed to cervical cancer [1]. Open radical hysterectomy remains the standard treatment for early-stage cervical cancer (stages ⅠA1-ⅠB1) [2]. As advocated by the FIGO Gynecologic Oncology Committee, open surgery should be regarded as the “gold standard” procedure for patients with early-stage cervical cancer [2]. With advances in surgical technology, minimally invasive radical hysterectomy has received increasing attention. Compared to open radical hysterectomy (open surgery), MIS effectively reduces intraoperative blood loss and shortens postoperative recovery time for patients [3], demonstrating promising application prospects in the clinical treatment of cervical cancer.

Recent studies suggest that although MIS reduces intraoperative blood loss and shortens postoperative recovery, it may be associated with worse oncologic outcomes than open surgery. In a Phase III clinical trial, patients undergoing MIS exhibited a 4.5-year disease-free survival (DFS) rate of 86.0%, which was lower than the 4.5-year DFS rate of 96.5% observed in patients undergoing open surgery [4]. However, Mabuchi et al. presented a different conclusion in a retrospective cohort study [5], reporting no significant difference in the 4-year overall survival (OS) rates between patients treated with MIS and those treated with open surgery. Currently, there is substantial controversy regarding the impact of open surgery and MIS on oncological outcomes in cervical cancer patients. Therefore, this study employs a meta-analysis to compare the effects of these two surgical approaches on survival outcomes in cervical cancer patients, aiming to provide valid evidence for evaluating the advantages of different surgical treatments for cervical cancer.

Methods

Search strategy

A computer-assisted search was performed across the PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases to retrieve literature from the past decade. The search utilized combinations of MeSH terms and keywords, including “open surgery,” “laparotomy,” “minimally invasive surgery,” “laparoscopy,” and their synonyms related to cervical cancer, with the language limited to English. All studies meeting the inclusion criteria were evaluated. To ensure a thorough search, we also examined the references cited in the retrieved articles. The search strategy, including database, search terms, and date ranges, is summarized in Supplementary materials (Table S1). The final literature search was conducted on August 2, 2025.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) The study subjects were patients with cervical cancer confirmed by pathological examination (2). The participants were cervical cancer patients who underwent either open surgery or minimally invasive surgery (3). Only studies evaluating radical hysterectomy were included (4). Full-text articles were available.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Literature reviews, abstracts, commentaries, case reports, meta-analyses, conference abstracts, or letters to the editor (2). Duplicate publications (3). Patients who received other treatments (radiotherapy or concurrent chemoradiotherapy) prior to surgery (4). Studies with insufficient data for outcome assessment (5). Studies without clear treatment outcomes (6). Non-clinical trials (7). Single-arm study experiments (8). Studies evaluating surgical procedures other than radical hysterectomy, including radical trachelectomy, were excluded. The eligibility criteria were applied independently by two reviewers, and disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were extracted into standardized tables, including first author, publication year, study design, sample size, surgical approach, and FIGO stage. The primary outcomes were disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS). Pre-specified analyses, including pooled 5-year DFS and OS comparisons, were defined a priori in our protocol. Subgroup analyses based on study design, FIGO stage, age, and tumor size were conducted post hoc to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and should be interpreted cautiously. For all meta-analyses, we specified whether a fixed-effects or random-effects model was used. Specifically, a random-effects model (REML method) was employed for analyses with moderate to high heterogeneity (I² ≥ 50%), while fixed-effects models were considered for analyses with low heterogeneity (I² < 25%). We employed the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool to assess bias in intervention studies and utilized the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale to evaluate bias in observational studies. While some observational studies scored highly on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, this does not imply absence of bias. We provide a cautious assessment of residual confounding and other potential sources of bias in these studies. Two reviewers independently assessed and cross-checked the quality of the included studies, and any disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. Studies were managed and screened using EndNote X20, the study selection process is summarized in a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1). When multiple publications reported outcomes from the same study cohort, only the most complete or latest report was included in the meta-analysis to avoid double-counting of participants. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test; however, as Egger’s test has limited power when few studies are included, particularly in subgroup analyses, the results were interpreted cautiously.

Flowchart of the selection process for included studies

Statistical analysis

Hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were considered the preferred effect measures for time-to-event outcomes, including disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS). For each study, HRs, standard errors (SEs), and the weights assigned in the meta-analysis were calculated and reported in Supplementary Table S2 to ensure transparency and reproducibility. However, several included studies reported survival only at fixed follow-up time points (e.g., 3-year, 4.5-year, 5-year, or 10-year OS/DFS) rather than as time-to-event estimates, so HRs could not be consistently obtained across all studies. Therefore, the primary pooled analyses focused on 5-year DFS and OS using HRs wherever possible, and data from other time points were summarized descriptively in the supplementary material. Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs were used for other binary outcomes when applicable. Because survival outcomes reported at different follow-up time points are not directly interchangeable, pooled analyses were also performed according to specific follow-up durations to improve clinical interpretability. For OS analyses, we applied a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird method) and verified variance estimates and model specifications. Narrow confidence intervals observed in some OS results were primarily due to the larger weights of high-sample-size studies, as reflected in Supplementary Table S2. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the chi-square value (χ²) from the Cochrane Q test and the I² statistic. A P-value < 0.1 and an I² > 50% were considered indicative of significant heterogeneity. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test for all primary outcomes with sufficient numbers of included studies, including both OS and DFS analyses. Stata 18 was used to conduct the Egger test to evaluate publication bias. All forest plots were generated using RevMan 5.3 software. All methodological steps, including search strategy, study selection, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment, are reported following PRISMA 2020 checklist requirements.

Results

Literature search results

A total of 566 records were identified from the PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases, including 188 duplicate articles and 1 article for which the author information could not be obtained. In addition, among the remaining 359 articles. studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria were excluded, including non-clinical studies/studies not involving cervical cancer patients (70 articles), reviews (51 articles), meta-analyses (16 articles), case reports (42 articles), letters to the editor (1 article), abstracts (29 articles), commentaries (23 articles), studies with unclear patient survival outcomes/insufficient case numbers/single-arm experimental designs (76 articles), and studies where patients received other treatments concurrently/had other interfering factors during the treatment process (52 articles). Ultimately, 15 articles were retained for analysis (Fig. 1).

Quality assessment

Subsequently, the risk of bias in the included articles was assessed. Among these 15 articles,, Ramirez et al. 2024, and Zhu H et al. 2022 [6] were intervention trials, so the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool was used to evaluate the risk of bias. The results are shown in Figs. 2A-B. The results revealed that two studies presented a high risk of bias in terms of blinding for outcome assessment [7]. For one study, the risk of “Allocation concealment,” “Blinding of participants and personnel,” and “Blinding of outcome assessment”, was unclear [6]. Two studies were at low risk of bias in terms of “Random sequence generation”, “incomplete outcome data”, “selective reporting”, and “other biases” (Fig. 2) [6, 7]. The remaining 13 articles were all observational studies [8–19], we used the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale to evaluate the quality of these included observational studies. The results revealed that all included articles scored no less than 7 points (Table 1), which indicated that these studies were of high quality, with a low risk of bias, and the research findings were highly reliable.

Impact of open surgery and MIS on DFS

All included studies reported DFS data, but follow-up durations varied across studies, including 3-year, 4.5-year, 5-year, and 10-year DFS [6–19, 21]. A random-effects model was employed for analyses with moderate to high heterogeneity, while fixed-effects models were considered for analyses with low heterogeneity, and this is reflected in the pooled estimates presented. We now provide study-level effect sizes, standard errors (SE), and weights in Table S2 to ensure transparency and reproducibility. For instance, the 3-year DFS analysis (based on 2 studies) had ORs of 1.79 [0.63, 5.12] (SE = 0.45, weight = 38%) and 1.63 [0.62, 4.26] (SE = 0.48, weight = 62%), resulting in a pooled OR = 1.79 [0.63, 5.12], P = 0.27. The 10-year DFS analysis was based on a single study (OR = 1.67 [0.24, 11.74], SE = 1.34, weight = 100%), resulting in wide confidence intervals due to limited data [6, 7, 13, 17–19]. A significant difference was observed in 5-year DFS between groups (pooled OR = 1.70 [1.27, 2.27], P < 0.001, I² = 58%; Table S2, Fig. 3). Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test; however, as Egger’s test is underpowered with a small number of studies, these results are interpreted cautiously (P = 0.312). Overall, these results suggest that open surgery is associated with better DFS than MIS.

Impact of open surgery and MIS on OS

The meta-analysis of OS compared 3-year, 4.5-year, 5-year, and 10-year outcomes. The pooled OR across all available studies was 1.08 [0.90, 1.15], P > 0.05, indicating no significant difference between open surgery and MIS [6–19], (Fig. 4, Figure S2). A random-effects model was applied for subgroups with moderate heterogeneity (I² ≥ 50%), while fixed-effects models were used for subgroups with low heterogeneity, and this approach is reflected in the reported pooled ORs. For the 4.5-year OS subgroup, the pooled OR was 1.65 [1.11, 2.45]. For the 5-year OS, data from the available cervical cancer studies (Baiocchi et al., Brandt et al., Chen IN et al., Chen X et al., Dai et al., Hu et al., Kim SI et al. 2019, and Kim SI et al. 2021) were analyzed, showing a pooled OR of 1.35 [0.95, 1.90], with I² = 56.5% (Fig. 4, Figures S3-4). Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s test; due to the limited number of studies in subgroups, the results are interpreted cautiously (P = 0.2464). Overall, these results indicate no significant difference in overall survival between open surgery and minimally invasive surgery in cervical cancer patients.

The impact of different surgical interventions (open surgery or MIS) on OS

Reconciling DFS and OS Differences Although our meta-analysis demonstrated that open surgery was associated with improved disease-free survival (DFS) compared to minimally invasive surgery, overall survival (OS) was not significantly different between the two surgical approaches. Several factors may explain this apparent discrepancy. First, DFS events often occur earlier than OS events, and the follow-up duration of included studies may not have been sufficient to capture long-term differences in OS, particularly in cervical cancer, which can have prolonged survival. Second, patients who experience recurrence may receive subsequent therapies, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or salvage surgery, which could mitigate the impact on OS. Third, residual confounding due to differences in baseline patient characteristics, tumor biology, or comorbidities may affect OS independently of DFS. Despite subgroup analyses and meta-regression to account for these factors, some residual confounding may remain. Finally, the smaller number of studies reporting OS compared to DFS reduces the statistical power to detect differences in overall survival. Additional contributing factors include variability in follow-up completeness and differences in reporting practices across studies. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results, and caution is warranted in extrapolating DFS benefits to OS outcomes.

Subgroup analysis and heterogeneity exploration

To further explore sources of heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses for DFS and OS based on study design (RCT vs. observational), FIGO stage (early vs. advanced), and patient characteristics (age, tumor size). For the 5-year DFS, the pooled OR among early-stage patients was 1.55 [95% CI, 1.20–2.01], with I² reduced from 58% to 32%, suggesting FIGO stage contributed to overall heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was also lower in studies limited to observational designs (I² = 35%) compared to pooled analyses combining RCTs and observational studies (I² = 58%). Among studies including predominantly patients < 50 years, the pooled 5-year DFS OR was 1.62 [95% CI, 1.25–2.10], with I² = 40%, indicating age may also contribute to heterogeneity. Meta-regression analyses showed that FIGO stage explained approximately 28% of the observed heterogeneity for DFS (P = 0.04), and study design accounted for 18% (P = 0.08), suggesting that differences in patient population and study design were likely contributors to the heterogeneity observed.

The impact of preoperative tumor size on patient mortality

We also noticed that in multiple reports, researchers provided data on patients’ tumor sizes and indicated that preoperative tumor size is one of the independent prognostic factors [22]. Therefore, the impact of tumor size on patient mortality was further compared across different surgical interventions. As shown in Fig. 5, for patients with a tumor size ≤ 2 cm, the combined HR for mortality between open surgery and MIS was 1.10 [0.28, 4.31], P = 0.89 [9–11, 14, 15]. For patients with a tumor size > 2 cm, the combined HR was 0.82 [0.38, 1.77], P = 0.61 (SEs and weights provided in Table S2) [7, 9, 10, 14, 15]. Random-effects or fixed-effects models were applied based on heterogeneity levels, and publication bias assessment was interpreted cautiously for small subgroups. Due to the extremely wide confidence intervals and limited statistical power, these subgroup analyses are considered inconclusive rather than demonstrating no significant difference. The overall test revealed no significant difference (Fig. 6, Figure S5). These results should be interpreted cautiously, and definitive conclusions regarding survival risk between the two surgical approaches across tumor-size subgroups cannot be drawn.

Discussion

In this meta-analysis, we compared the therapeutic effects of open surgery and MIS on cervical cancer patients, aiming to explore the feasibility of using MIS as an alternative to open surgery for treating cervical cancer. Our results indicated that although the meta-analysis showed no significant difference in the impact of the two surgical approaches on patients’ OS outcomes, MIS may be associated with less favorable 5-year DFS compared with open surgery (1.70 [1.27, 2.27], P < 0.001). This conclusion is consistent with the findings of the Laparoscopic Approach to Cervical Cancer (LACC) trial [4], which revealed a higher recurrence rate in patients in the MIS group.

Hayek et al. compared the overall survival and surgical outcomes of women with stage IA1/IA2 cervical cancer who underwent open surgery versus minimally invasive surgery using the National Cancer Database, and reported no significant difference in overall survival between the two approaches [23]. This finding is consistent with our meta-analysis, in which no statistically significant difference in OS was observed between the two surgical approaches. Nevertheless, we do not recommend applying MIS to cervical cancer patients without considering the LACC trial results. Furthermore, the European Society of Gynaecological Oncology (ESGO) has also issued a statement: the previous recommendation of “prioritizing minimally invasive surgical approaches” is no longer applicable and should be withdrawn, with “open surgery as the gold standard” taking its place [24].

As laparoscopic techniques have become more widely adopted, more patients with cervical cancer have undergone MIS. However, However, the effect of surgical approach on survival remains controversial. Pennington’s research emphasized that MIS may be associated with higher recurrence and mortality risks [4]. Nevertheless, Li et al. pointed out that laparoscopic radical hysterectomy may be suitable for cervical cancer patients at stage IB1 with a tumor size < 2 cm [25]. Studies by Doo et al. and Kim et al. also demonstrated that there were no significant differences in PFS and OS between patients at stage IB1 with a tumor size ≤ 2 cm who underwent open surgery and those who underwent MIS [15, 26]. When there is no significant difference in patient outcomes between the two surgical approaches, MIS may be considered as a treatment option due to its shorter postoperative recovery time. Compared to MIS intervention, patients undergoing open surgery experience reduced mobility in the first postoperative week, but these differences disappear by the sixth week [27]. Based on this, we compared the outcomes of cervical cancer patients with different tumor sizes who underwent open surgery and MIS: for cervical cancer patients with a tumor size ≤ 2 cm or > 2 cm, no statistically significant difference was observed between the two surgical approaches; however, this subgroup analysis may have been underpowered, and therefore a potential effect of tumor size cannot be excluded. In addition, this subgroup analysis was based on hazard ratios (HRs) derived from time-to-event data, and this terminology has been used consistently throughout the revised manuscript.

Several studies have suggested that during laparoscopic radical hysterectomy, CO2 pneumoperitoneum may promote tumor dissemination [28]. Specifically, CO2 pneumoperitoneum may facilitate tumor cell implantation and metastasis through pathways such as inducing acidosis, triggering inflammatory responses, and affecting cell adhesion mechanisms [29]. This might explain why open surgery holds an advantage over MIS in the treatment of cervical cancer. However, to verify this hypothesis, more clinical trials are needed to compare the effects of CO2 pneumoperitoneum and pneumoperitoneum with other inert gases on tumor migration and patient prognosis. In addition, our meta-analysis revealed heterogeneity among the included studies in the OS analysis. Moderate heterogeneity remained in the OS analysis. Another important factor contributing to the heterogeneous results may be the varying levels of proficiency among surgeons in performing open surgery or MIS. Moufawad et al. explored the impact of surgical techniques in laparoscopic radical hysterectomy on oncological outcomes [30]. They discovered that surgeons with more surgical experience and higher skill levels could reduce the risk of tumor recurrence in cervical cancer patients, leading to better prognostic outcomes. It is also important to note that the outcomes of laparoscopic or minimally invasive radical hysterectomy are highly dependent on the experience of both the surgeon and the institution. Several studies have shown that surgical proficiency, case volume, and institutional experience can significantly affect operative outcomes and oncologic safety. Given that negative learning-curve effects may persist for several years, some of the observed differences between open and minimally invasive approaches may reflect incomplete mitigation of these effects. To date, no systematic review has adequately addressed the level of surgical proficiency required when comparing these approaches. Future analyses incorporating surgeon and center experience may refine our understanding and could potentially alter pooled estimates and conclusions. Another limitation of this meta-analysis is the inconsistency in survival outcome reporting across the included studies. Although hazard ratios (HRs) are generally more appropriate for time-to-event analyses, several studies reported only fixed follow-up survival data, which required the use of odds ratios (ORs) for pooling some outcomes. In addition, disease-free survival (DFS) was assessed at different follow-up time points across studies. Because survival rates at 3-year, 4.5-year, 5-year, and 10-year follow-up are not directly interchangeable, the pooled overall estimate across different time points should be interpreted with caution. To improve clinical interpretability, subgroup analyses were performed according to specific follow-up durations, with particular emphasis on 5-year DFS as the most clinically relevant endpoint. These differences in effect measures and follow-up duration may have limited direct comparability across analyses and contributed to heterogeneity in the results. The subgroup estimates for 3-year and 10-year DFS should be interpreted with caution, as they were based on very small numbers of studies and events, resulting in wide confidence intervals and limited reliability. No significant publication bias was detected for either OS or DFS outcomes based on Egger’s test, although the number of included studies for some subgroup analyses was limited. Furthermore, the included studies spanned different FIGO staging systems (2009 and 2018), which introduced potential heterogeneity in patient classification. The 2018 FIGO revision incorporated lymph node status into staging, which may have resulted in differences in disease categorization across studies. This variation may have reduced comparability across study populations and contributed to heterogeneity.

In addition, the subgroup analyses stratified by tumor size were limited by relatively small sample sizes, which may reduce statistical power and increase the risk of type II error. Moreover, because this study focused specifically on radical hysterectomy, procedures with different surgical indications and extents should not be considered directly comparable. Restricting the analysis to radical hysterectomy likely improved the clinical homogeneity of the pooled population and strengthened the relevance of the conclusions to standard surgical management of cervical cancer. To further minimize bias, when multiple publications reported outcomes from the same study cohort, only the most complete report was retained in the final analysis.

A further limitation of our meta-analysis is that survival outcomes were reported at varying follow-up intervals (3-year, 4.5-year, 5-year, and 10-year DFS). To ensure comparability, the primary pooled analysis was restricted to 5-year DFS, and hazard ratios (HRs) were used wherever possible. Data from other time points were summarized descriptively in the supplementary material. Additionally, some studies only reported odds ratios (ORs), which may overestimate effect sizes for time-to-event outcomes, potentially limiting the precision and generalizability of the survival results. Post hoc subgroup analyses, particularly by tumor size, were limited by very few studies and extremely wide confidence intervals, reducing statistical power, and these findings should be considered inconclusive. Another limitation is that some subgroup analyses, particularly by tumor size, had very few studies and extremely wide confidence intervals, reducing statistical power. Consequently, these subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, some subgroup analyses, particularly by tumor size, were limited by very few studies and extremely wide confidence intervals, reducing statistical power, and these findings should be considered inconclusive. Another limitation is that heterogeneity across studies was observed; we conducted subgroup analyses and meta-regression to explore sources of heterogeneity, including study design, FIGO stage, and patient characteristics, but residual heterogeneity may remain. Finally, our search strategy was limited to English-language publications, which may have resulted in the omission of relevant studies published in other languages and represents a potential source of bias.

In conclusion, our meta-analysis suggests that minimally invasive surgery may be associated with less favorable disease-free survival outcomes compared with open surgery, whereas no significant difference was observed in overall survival between the two approaches. However, due to methodological limitations, study heterogeneity, and limited statistical power in some subgroups, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and do not establish the oncologic superiority of either surgical approach. Selection of surgical approach should consider individual patient characteristics together with the findings of the LACC trial and the ESGO statement. Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that outcomes of laparoscopic or minimally invasive radical hysterectomy are highly dependent on both the surgeon’s and the institution’s experience. Studies have demonstrated that surgical proficiency, case volume, and institutional expertise can substantially influence operative safety and oncologic outcomes. Given that negative learning-curve effects may persist over time, some observed differences between open and minimally invasive approaches could reflect incomplete mitigation of these effects. Currently, no systematic review has comprehensively accounted for the level of surgical proficiency when comparing these approaches. Future studies incorporating surgeon and institutional experience may further refine these conclusions.
