Clinical outcomes of subtalar arthrodesis for calcaneal fracture malunion and their association with preoperative anxiety and depression: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hang Zhao, Jun Lu, Yi Li, Dong Hu, JunKui Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Patients with foot and ankle disorders often experience preoperative anxiety / depression, which can adversely affect surgical outcomes. This study aimed to explore the factors influencing preoperative mental health status in patients with malunited calcaneal fractures and to evaluate the potential impact of preoperative mental health on prognosis following subtalar arthrodesis (STA).Methods This study retrospectively enrolled 45 patients with malunited calcaneal fractures who underwent subtalar arthrodesis from January 2015 to May 2023. Demographic data were collected, and assessments using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) ankle-hindfoot score, and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) were performed. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare preoperative and final follow-up VAS and AOFAS scores. Spearman correlation was used to analyze the associations between preoperative HADS scores and preoperative VAS/AOFAS scores, and the associations between preoperative HADS scores and final follow-up VAS/AOFAS scores. A HADS-A or HADS-D score ≥ 8 was defined as indicating anxiety/depressive symptoms.Results A total of 39 patients (32 male, 7 female) completed follow-up. Sixteen patients (41.03%) had preoperative HADS-A or HADS-D scores ≥ 8, indicating anxiety/depressive symptoms. Significant improvements in VAS and AOFAS scores were observed at final follow-up compared to preoperative assessments (P < 0.001). Preoperative VAS scores showed significant positive correlations with both anxiety and depressive symptoms (P < 0.05), while preoperative AOFAS scores showed a significant negative correlation with anxiety (P < 0.05) but not with depression (P > 0.05). No significant correlations were found between preoperative anxiety/depressive symptoms and baseline characteristics (gender, age, BMI, disease duration; all P > 0.05). Notably, preoperative HADS scores were not significantly correlated with final follow-up VAS or AOFAS scores (all P > 0.05).Conclusions STA effectively treats malunited calcaneal fractures, significantly improving clinical outcomes. Preoperative pain levels correlated with both anxiety and depressive symptoms, whereas functional status correlated only with anxiety. No statistically significant association was identified between preoperative anxiety/depressive symptoms and final follow-up pain relief or functional recovery in this cohort. Given the modest sample size, these null findings should be interpreted cautiously. Clinicians should prioritize preoperative pain management to alleviate psychological distress and optimize perioperative care.Keywords: Calcaneal fracture malunion, STA, Anxiety, Depression

Introduction

Calcaneal fractures account for approximately 2% of all fractures and more than 60% of tarsal fractures [1, 2], with a higher incidence in adults sustaining high-energy trauma. If surgical intervention is delayed due to inadequate reduction with conservative treatment, inappropriate treatment selection, or delayed patient presentation, malunion often develops, leading to persistent pain, abnormal gait, and reduced quality of life [3–8].

Chronic musculoskeletal pain is often accompanied by anxiety and depression, with a significantly higher prevalence than in the general population [9]. The fear-avoidance model suggests that pain catastrophizing leads to activity avoidance, which in turn contributes to functional decline and depression [10, 11]; negative emotions such as anxiety and depression further reduce pain tolerance and increase pain perception, creating a vicious cycle [12]. Currently, subtalar arthrodesis (STA) is widely recognized as the most commonly used and relatively effective surgical approach for managing calcaneal fracture malunion, which can alleviate pain, restore hindfoot alignment, and improve function to some extent [13, 14]. However, this procedure still has limitations including incision-related complications, restricted joint range of motion, and long-term adjacent joint degeneration, necessitating optimization of perioperative management.

With the increasing sophistication of surgical techniques, preoperative mental health status has been recognized as an important factor influencing functional recovery in fracture patients. The incidence of anxiety and depression increases significantly after orthopedic trauma [15, 16]. Patients with calcaneal fractures are twice as likely to have a history of mental health disorders [17]. Researchers have attempted to identify risk factors for this phenomenon: anxiety and depression are not associated with patient gender but are significantly correlated with age [18]; preoperative anxiety and depression are not directly related to disease duration [19]; underweight elderly patients are at higher risk of developing anxiety and depressive symptoms [20]; and preoperative pain is positively correlated with anxiety and depressive symptoms [10]. Furthermore, multiple studies have confirmed that preoperative mental health status significantly affects postoperative recovery: preoperative depression is significantly negatively correlated with postoperative pain (VAS) and functional scores (AOFAS) [21]; in joint replacement surgery, patients with anxiety and depression experience longer recovery periods [22]; in patients with lower extremity fractures, anxiety and depression exacerbate postoperative pain and increase the risk of complications [23]; and in total knee and total hip arthroplasty, preoperative anxiety and depression are closely associated with poorer prognosis and lower satisfaction [24, 25]. Recent studies in foot and ankle surgery have also confirmed that preoperative anxiety/depression is associated with worse outcomes after total ankle arthroplasty [26] and ankle arthrodesis [27]. Collectively, these findings suggest that preoperative mental health status may be an important factor influencing outcomes in foot and ankle surgery.

However, no study has specifically investigated whether preoperative anxiety/depressive symptoms affect the postoperative outcomes of subtalar arthrodesis for calcaneal fracture malunion. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of STA for calcaneal fracture malunion, analyze factors associated with preoperative anxiety/depression, and explore their correlation with postoperative pain relief and functional recovery. We hypothesized that preoperative pain severity and functional status would be significantly correlated with anxiety/depressive symptoms, and that patients with more severe preoperative anxiety/depressive symptoms would have worse postoperative outcomes.

Materials and methods

General information

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Approval No: 2025-KY-072-01). We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent STA at the Comprehensive Foot and Ankle Surgical Unit of Xi’an Honghui Hospital between January 2015 and May 2023. A total of 45 patients were enrolled, of whom 39 completed full follow-up. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Delayed surgical intervention (> 3 weeks post-fracture) due to treatment delay; (3) Unilateral calcaneal fracture malunion classified as Stephens-Sanders type II or higher; (4) Underwent STA; (5) Capable of independently completing questionnaires with adequate language comprehension and cognitive ability. Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients with multiple fractures; (2) Previous traumatic surgery on the affected ankle; (3) Comorbidities including severe osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, or ankle joint infections; (4) Chronic systemic diseases (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypertension, malignant tumors, hepatic or renal insufficiency); (5) Pre-existing psychiatric disorders prior to fracture (including diagnosed anxiety disorder, depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, etc.); (6) current use of antidepressants or anxiolytics.

Diagnosis of calcaneal fracture malunion was based on clinical symptoms (persistent heel pain, difficulty walking) combined with radiographic findings (lateral X-ray showing Böhler angle < 20°, loss of calcaneal height, and irregularity of the subtalar joint). All patients had confirmed subtalar arthritis on preoperative CT. The Stephens-Sanders classification was retrospectively applied by two independent foot and ankle surgeons based on preoperative CT images; any disagreement was resolved by consensus. In this cohort, 16 patients were type II and 23 were type III.

Method

This study collected baseline patient characteristics including gender, age, body mass index (BMI), and disease duration. The primary objectives were to analyze factors associated with preoperative mental health status, and examine the impact of preoperative mental health status on clinical outcomes. Accordingly, upon hospital admission, the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was used to assess patients’ mental health status. The validated Chinese version of the HADS was used, and patients completed the questionnaire by themselves. Meanwhile, the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and the American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) ankle-hindfoot score were used to evaluate pain symptoms and foot function preoperatively and at the final follow-up. The final follow-up was conducted in December 2024.

The HADS consists of two subscales: the Anxiety subscale (HADS-A) and Depression subscale (HADS-D), each subscale comprising 7 items measure the severity of anxiety and depressive symptoms, with each item scored from 0 to 3. Total scores range from 0 to 21 for each subscale, where higher scores indicate greater symptom severity [28]. The VAS score utilizes a 100-mm horizontal line marked with the descriptors “no pain” (0 mm) and “worst imaginable pain” (100 mm). Patients mark their current pain level on the line, yielding a score ranging from 0 to 100, where higher scores indicate greater pain intensity [29]. The AOFAS scoring system has a maximum of 100 points, comprising three domains: subjective pain assessment (40 points), objective functional evaluation (50 points), and alignment (10 points). Widely adopted for functional assessment of ankle disorders, the postoperative scoring range following STA is 0–94 points, as the 6 points allocated for subtalar motion are excluded. Higher scores indicate better ankle-hindfoot function [30]. The reliability and validity of the HADS, VAS, and AOFAS in calcaneal fracture patients have been previously established in our earlier work [31].

All surgical procedures were performed by the same team of senior foot and ankle orthopedic specialists to maintain technical consistency and standardization. Postoperatively, all patients received uniform rehabilitation protocol guidance to ensure treatment consistency, thereby providing a reliable basis for objective outcome comparison.

Surgery and rehabilitation

This study employed general anesthesia combined with nerve block for STA in the treatment of malunited calcaneal fractures. At the beginning of the surgery, an L-shaped incision (approximately 15 cm in length) was made laterally to the calcaneus. The skin, subcutaneous tissue, and periosteum were sequentially incised to expose the lateral wall of the calcaneus and the subtalar joint. Intraoperative findings revealed malunion of the calcaneus with significant lateral wall expansion, rendering the original fracture line indistinguishable. Subsequently, an oscillating saw was utilized to remodel the lateral wall of the calcaneus, further exposing the subtalar joint. Severe disorganization of the subtalar articular surface was observed, accompanied by substantial cartilage damage and extensive scar tissue formation. The procedure continued with debridement of the subtalar articular cartilage and surrounding tissues, followed by transverse osteotomy at Gissane’s angle to remove the collapsed articular surface, thereby correcting the varus and depression deformities of the calcaneus. During the procedure, allogeneic bone grafting was performed according to the extent of subtalar joint bone defect. Fixation was achieved using a bone plate with screws, supplemented by two cannulated screws for subtalar joint stabilization.Intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy demonstrated satisfactory osteotomy correction and optimal bone graft positioning, with all internal fixation devices properly placed and complete correction of the hindfoot varus deformity. The wound was then irrigated, the fascial flap was trimmed, and closure was performed in layers.

Postoperatively, the affected ankle was immobilized with a brace, with the limb elevated and moderate ice packs applied to control swelling. Avoid weight-bearing on the affected limb or maintain a hanging position. An 8-week non-weight-bearing period was enforced, during which patients were prohibited from applying any load to the affected extremity. Gradual transition to partial weight-bearing commenced after 8 weeks, progressing to full weight-bearing status. All patients underwent scheduled follow-up evaluations at 1, 2, and 3 months postoperatively and annually thereafter, with rehabilitation guidance provided and postoperative recovery closely monitored.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess data normality. Parametric tests were applied for normally distributed data, while nonparametric tests were employed for non-normally distributed data.The results indicated that the data indicated non-normal distribution. Therefore, nonparametric tests were employed for subsequent analyses to ensure result accuracy. All descriptive data are presented as median and interquartile range (M [P25, P75]). Intervariable correlations were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Comparisons of preoperative and final follow-up VAS and AOFAS scores were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. With statistical significance set at p < 0.05. For paired comparisons of preoperative and final follow-up VAS and AOFAS scores, the median difference and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated using the Hodges-Lehmann method, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed to assess statistical significance.

Results

General condition of the patient

This study enrolled 45 eligible patients. Among them, 39 completed full follow-up, and 6 were lost to follow-up (3 due to change of contact information, 2 refused participation, and 1 died of an unrelated cause). The cohort comprised 32 males and 7 females. Sixteen patients (41.03%) had preoperative HADS-A or HADS-D scores ≥ 8, indicating anxiety/depressive symptoms. Median values were: age 48.00 years (IQR 35.00, 56.00), disease duration 18.00 months (IQR 7.00, 84.00), and follow-up period 44.00 months (IQR 39.00, 62.00), ranging from 19 to 119 months. All 39 patients achieved solid subtalar fusion on X-ray at 6–9 months postoperatively, as evidenced by trabecular bone bridging across the joint. The median VAS score decreased from 80.0 (IQR 70.0, 80.0) preoperatively to 10.0 (IQR 4.0, 20.0) at final follow-up. The Hodges-Lehmann estimate of the median difference was − 63.0 (95% CI: -66.0 to -58.0). Discussion

Of note, this is a retrospective, exploratory study. Given the small sample size and the absence of multivariable adjustment, the associations identified should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating rather than causal, and should be interpreted in the context of the study’s limitations.

Current research on STA for calcaneal fracture malunion has primarily focused on surgical techniques, graft selection, and clinical outcome assessments [13, 14, 32–34]. However, there remains a paucity of studies investigating the potential impact of patients’ preoperative mental health status, particularly anxiety and depression, on postoperative outcomes. Existing evidence demonstrates that orthopedic trauma patients frequently experience anxiety and depression, which may significantly impair postoperative recovery and consequently compromise surgical outcomes [10, 23, 35, 36]. Furthermore, extensive research in orthopedic surgery has established that patients with preoperative anxiety and depressive symptoms typically experience poorer postoperative recovery and lower satisfaction rates [24, 37, 38]. Consequently, focusing on and intervening in preoperative mental health status, particularly anxiety and depressive symptoms, has become a key factor in improving surgical outcomes and enhancing patient prognosis.

In this study, the 39 patients who completed follow-up showed significant improvements in VAS and AOFAS scores at final follow-up compared with preoperative assessments, and overall satisfaction was high. Calcaneal malunion is often accompanied by subtalar arthritis, arch collapse, loss of calcaneal height, and hindfoot varus/valgus deformity. These pathological changes lead to severe pain, activity limitation, and even disability [32, 39, 40], significantly reducing quality of life. We believe that subtalar arthrodesis fundamentally prevents further progression of subtalar arthritis, significantly alleviates pain, restores partial hindfoot function, and improves hindfoot morphology. These positive clinical outcomes help patients return to work and social roles, consistent with previous studies [32, 40–43].

This study further investigated factors associated with patients’ preoperative mental health status. The results showed that preoperative VAS scores were significantly positively correlated with HADS-A and HADS-D scores, indicating that higher preoperative pain levels were associated with more severe anxiety and depressive symptoms. This finding is highly consistent with psychological research in the field of pain. Castillo et al. demonstrated that pain is a significant predictor of anxiety and depression in the early stages after severe lower extremity trauma [10]. Gureje et al. reported that persistent pain increases the risk of anxiety and depressive disorders, and that exacerbation of these negative emotions is correlated with pain severity [44]. Voshaar also pointed out that pain is an independent risk factor for depression in patients with hip fractures [45]. From a psychological mechanism perspective, the fear-avoidance model explains this association: pain catastrophizing leads to excessive fear of pain, which in turn causes activity avoidance, resulting in functional decline, social isolation, and emotional distress [10, 11]; simultaneously, anxiety and depression can reduce pain tolerance and enhance pain perception, creating a vicious cycle [12].

Further analysis showed that preoperative AOFAS scores were significantly negatively correlated with HADS-A scores, but not with HADS-D scores (P = 0.06, approaching significance). This indicates that poorer preoperative functional status is associated with more severe anxiety symptoms. Giannoudis et al. found that the severity of anxiety symptoms was correlated with the degree of lower extremity injury [46]. Several studies have reported that anxiety is common and progressively develops after various types of orthopedic trauma, sports-related injuries, and falls [47–50]. In addition, a study of college students showed that appearance evaluation was negatively correlated with anxiety [51]. These findings are consistent with our results and further support the negative correlation between AOFAS scores and anxiety symptoms. We believe that foot dysfunction not only limits daily activities but may also damage self-image, thereby exacerbating anxiety. In contrast, the development of depression depends more on multiple factors such as emotional burden and reduced quality of life, with functional limitations having a relatively indirect effect [45].

This study did not find a significant association between preoperative AOFAS scores and depressive symptoms (P = 0.06), nor were statistically significant correlations observed between preoperative HADS scores and final follow-up outcomes (all P > 0.05). Notably, the P value for the correlation between AOFAS and HADS-D approached the significance threshold, and the sample size was relatively small (n = 39). These null findings may be attributable to insufficient statistical power (type II error) rather than a true absence of association. Goh et al. similarly found no significant impact of preoperative mental health status on two-year postoperative VAS and AOFAS scores in patients undergoing hallux valgus surgery [52], and studies on knee arthroplasty have also shown that preoperative psychological distress is not associated with postoperative outcomes [53]. However, these null findings should not be overinterpreted. In the field of foot and ankle surgery, Wong et al. found that preoperative anxiety/depression was associated with worse outcomes after total ankle arthroplasty [26], and Zhang et al. also reported that preoperative psychological distress was significantly associated with prognosis after ankle arthrodesis [27]. These findings are consistent with our earlier work in a cohort of calcaneal fracture patients, which suggested that preoperative mental health status was associated with postoperative clinical outcomes, with poorer psychological health predicting a worse prognosis [31]. Therefore, the null findings of this study may be population-specific and require further validation. The lack of statistically significant associations does not exclude the possibility of clinically meaningful effects, which may have been undetected due to limited statistical power.

This study also analyzed the influence of baseline patient characteristics (gender, age, disease duration, BMI) on preoperative mental health status. The results showed no significant correlations between gender, age, BMI, or disease duration and preoperative HADS scores (all P > 0.05). Although gender and age are often considered important factors influencing mental health, they did not show significant associations in this study. Ye et al. also found that age and gender had no significant impact on mental health status in patients awaiting heart transplantation, and disease duration did not significantly affect anxiety symptoms [54]. In the present study, patients with calcaneal fracture malunion had a long disease duration, but its effect on mental health was more indirect compared with pain and functional limitation. Similarly, a study on hip arthroscopy for femoroacetabular impingement found that preoperative anxiety and depression were not associated with age or symptom duration [19]. Furthermore, no significant correlation was found between BMI and anxiety or depressive symptoms, consistent with a study of obese patients after kidney transplantation [55]. Therefore, we believe that pain and functional activity were the main factors influencing preoperative mental health status in patients with calcaneal fracture malunion in this study, while gender, age, BMI, and disease duration played a weaker role.

Based on the findings of this study, we propose the following recommendations: routine preoperative screening for anxiety and depressive symptoms using the HADS to identify at-risk patients; for patients with significant psychological distress, referral to psychological services or provision of cognitive-behavioral therapy may be considered, as previous studies have shown that psychological interventions can improve outcomes following orthopedic surgery [35, 56]; and strengthening perioperative pain management, given that pain is a major driver of psychological distress. Future studies should investigate whether preoperative psychological interventions can further optimize postoperative outcomes after STA.

This study not only evaluated the clinical outcomes of subtalar arthrodesis for calcaneal fracture malunion, but also analyzed factors associated with preoperative mental health status and their impact on patient prognosis, providing valuable references for comprehensive treatment strategies. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 39), and no a priori power analysis was performed, which may limit statistical power; therefore, some null findings should be interpreted cautiously. Additionally, excluding patients with pre-existing psychiatric disorders may have reduced the variability of psychological scores and introduced selection bias. Second, as a single-center retrospective study, the generalizability of the conclusions is limited. Third, multivariate regression analysis was not conducted to adjust for potential confounders (e.g., severity of deformity, rehabilitation adherence), and residual bias may exist. Fourth, anatomical parameters were not included, nor were quality of life measures (e.g., SF-36) or other patient-reported outcome measures, which affects the comprehensiveness of the study. Fifth, only preoperative mental health status was assessed; postoperative HADS data were not collected, precluding analysis of the trajectory of psychological changes and their interaction with functional recovery. Additionally, short-term postoperative functional scores (e.g., VAS and AOFAS at 1, 2, and 3 months) were not systematically collected, limiting the assessment of recovery trajectories. Nevertheless, this study provides preliminary evidence for the relationship between preoperative mental health and surgical recovery in this population. Sixth, the loss to follow-up rate was 13.3% (6/45). Although reasons were provided, potential selection bias cannot be fully excluded, though the low rate suggests limited impact. Finally, although this study found no significant association between preoperative mental health status and long-term surgical outcomes, this does not negate the importance of psychological factors. It is possible that the marked surgical efficacy (VAS decreased from 80 to 10) masked the modest impact of psychological factors, or that the null findings resulted from type II error due to the small sample size. Future large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies incorporating postoperative psychological assessments and multivariate regression analyses are needed to further elucidate the role of preoperative mental health status in the prognosis of STA.

Conclusion

Subtalar arthrodesis effectively improves pain and function in patients with calcaneal fracture malunion. More importantly, preoperative pain severity is significantly correlated with anxiety and depressive symptoms, while foot function is correlated only with preoperative anxiety symptoms, suggesting that effective perioperative pain management may help alleviate psychological distress. In this study, no statistically significant association was identified between preoperative anxiety/depressive symptoms and final follow-up outcomes in this cohort; however, given the modest sample size, these null findings should be interpreted cautiously. Therefore, clinicians should incorporate preoperative pain management and mental health status assessment into comprehensive treatment strategies to optimize overall patient prognosis. Future studies should further investigate the impact of preoperative mental health interventions on patient outcomes.
