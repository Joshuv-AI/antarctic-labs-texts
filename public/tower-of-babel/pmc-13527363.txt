Congenital melanocytic nevus of the clitoral hood: A case report
Authors: Daniel Sastoque, Julian Rojas, Angie Rachel Mora Guerrero, Cindy Morales, Alfredo Mejia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pigmented lesions of the vulva span a broad spectrum, from benign melanocytic nevi to melanoma. Within this region, the clitoris and clitoral hood are of particular surgical importance because of their sensory and functional roles. Congenital melanocytic nevi (CMN) of the clitoral hood are exceedingly rare, and their optimal management is not well defined. We report a 12-year-old girl with a CMN of the clitoral hood measuring 17 × 13 mm. Because of persistent local pruritus, the difficulty of reliable dermoscopic surveillance at this site, and the family’s inability to guarantee periodic follow-up, an elliptical excision with 3-mm margins and immediate reconstruction using local mucocutaneous advancement flaps from the labia minora was performed. Histopathology confirmed a benign compound melanocytic nevus with clear margins. At one-month follow-up the wound had healed with preserved clitoral sensation, no scar contracture or dyschromia, and no functional impairment. This case illustrates that small CMN of the clitoral hood carry a low risk of malignant transformation and that, when excision is chosen for symptomatic or surveillance-related reasons, a conservative plastic-surgical approach can achieve complete removal while preserving genital form and function.

Introduction

Pigmented lesions of the female genital region are uncommon and represent a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. 1 They include melanocytic nevi (with or without architectural disorder), lentigines, dysplastic nevi, atypical genital-type melanocytic nevi, and melanoma. 2

The prevalence of vulvar pigmented lesions in adult women is estimated at 10–12%, with vulvar nevi accounting for approximately 2%. 3 In the pediatric population the prevalence of genital nevi is around 3.5%, with an anatomical distribution favoring the labia majora (50%), labia minora (22.2%), clitoral hood (16.7%), and clitoris (11.1%). 4 Most are noted at birth (36.3%) or within the first two years of life (27.3%) and are therefore classified as congenital or early-onset. 4

Clinically, vulvar nevi present as well-defined, symmetrical macules or papules with uniform pigmentation ranging from pink to brown or bluish tones, and are frequently asymptomatic, although some cause pruritus or discomfort. 3 Their evaluation is complicated by the overlap of clinical and histopathological features between benign and malignant lesions, which may lead to unnecessary procedures. 3

Importantly, the absolute risk of malignant transformation of a congenital melanocytic nevus (CMN) is low, particularly for small lesions. The cumulative lifetime risk of melanoma associated with CMN up to 80 years of age has been estimated at 0.03% in men and 0.009% in women, 5 and small CMN (<1.5 cm) carry a very low risk. 6 This low baseline risk should be weighed carefully against the functional and psychological implications of surgery in the anogenital region.

Management of CMN in the vulvar region must therefore be individualized. From a reconstructive standpoint, surgical planning should aim to preserve sexual function and genital anatomy, using local mucocutaneous flaps to minimize postoperative morbidity. Reports of CMN specifically involving the clitoral hood, and of their surgical management, are exceptionally scarce, which is the basis for the present report.

Case presentation

The patient was admitted to the Subred Integrada de Servicios de Salud Norte, Sede Simón Bolívar (Bogotá, Colombia), in March 2025. A 12-year-old girl was referred with a pigmented lesion of the clitoral hood (clitoral prepuce) that had been present since early childhood, consistent with a clinical and dermoscopic diagnosis of congenital melanocytic nevus (CMN).

On examination, a well-circumscribed pigmented papular lesion measuring 17 × 13 mm was identified on the clitoral hood, with regular, well-defined borders, homogeneous dark-brown pigmentation, and a mobile base suggestive of a superficial origin (Figure 1(a)). Dermoscopy revealed a multicomponent pattern with peripheral globules and a central pseudonetwork, consistent with a benign melanocytic nevus. Nevertheless, the mucosal location and the recognized difficulty of dermoscopic interpretation at genital sites warranted careful consideration and follow-up.

The decision to proceed with excision was based on the persistent local pruritus, the difficulty of reliable long-term dermoscopic surveillance at this anatomically concealed mucosal site, and the family’s inability to guarantee attendance at periodic follow-up visits; it was not driven primarily by concern for malignant transformation, given the small size and benign features of the lesion. The rationale, the alternative of clinical observation, and the reconstructive plan were discussed with the patient and her mother, who preferred definitive excision.

Surgery was performed under local anesthesia with sedation and lasted approximately 40 minutes. An elliptical excision of the lesion including the involved clitoral hood was carried out with 3-mm safety margins (Figure 1(b) and (c)). The mucocutaneous tissues of the vulva, particularly the labia minora and clitoral hood, have marked elasticity and laxity, which facilitates primary closure of sizeable defects with minimal tension and optimal healing.

Immediate reconstruction was performed using advancement of local mucocutaneous flaps raised from the adjacent labia minora (Figure 1(d); schematic in Figure 2). The flaps were designed as random-pattern advancement flaps supplied by the abundant subdermal vascular plexus of the labial mucosa. Dissection was kept superficial and lateral to avoid the dorsal neurovascular supply to the glans clitoris, thereby preserving clitoral sensation, and the natural laxity of the tissue was used to redistribute tension and achieve a tension-free closure. Absorbable monofilament sutures (poliglecaprone 25, Monocryl) were used. Postoperative analgesia consisted of oral acetaminophen. No dressing was required after the first postoperative day; local care thereafter consisted of hygiene with soap and water, avoiding creams or perfumed products.

The patient was reviewed at 15 days and at 1 month. At 15 days the wound was healing well with an immature scar; by 1 month the scar was mature and supple, without contracture, dyspigmentation, or recurrence, with preserved clitoral sensation and a normal genital contour. Both the patient and her mother reported satisfaction with the functional and cosmetic result.

Discussion

CMN are benign congenital melanocytic tumors (hamartomas) that may arise on any cutaneous or mucocutaneous surface, including the female genital region. 7 They result from a clonal proliferation of melanocytes and are typically uniform and symmetrical, with homogeneous driver mutations. 5

The risk of malignant transformation of CMN is closely related to lesion size. For small congenital nevi (<1.5 cm) the lifetime risk of melanoma is low (<1%); the risk rises with projected adult size, reaching up to approximately 5% for lesions larger than 20 cm and is highest in giant nevi (>40 cm) and in the presence of numerous satellite nevi. 6 Our patient’s lesion was small (17 × 13 mm) and therefore fell within the lowest-risk category; consequently, surgery was undertaken for symptomatic and surveillance-related reasons rather than because of a high oncological risk. Although melanoma of the genital region is rare, its prognosis is poor owing to delayed diagnosis, 1 which reinforces the value of either reliable surveillance or, when this is not feasible, definitive excision.

Diagnosis rests on careful clinical evaluation of symmetry, borders, color, and diameter, 3 complemented by dermoscopy for pattern recognition; benign lesions typically display symmetry, uniform color, and regular structures, whereas asymmetry, multiple colors, and irregular pigment distribution raise concern. 8 In genital skin, however, dermoscopic interpretation is more difficult because of physiological pigmentation and anatomical variability. 9 Genital nevi may also change during puberty under hormonal influence, and such changes can complicate clinical assessment without necessarily indicating malignancy. 10 When malignancy is suspected, excisional or incisional biopsy is preferred over shave biopsy, which may compromise assessment of tumor thickness.3,7

A particular diagnostic pitfall concerns the histopathology of genital melanocytic lesions. Benign nevi are distinguished from melanoma by their symmetry and circumscription, the maturation of melanocytes with increasing dermal depth, the absence of pagetoid spread, and the absence of deep or atypical mitoses and marked cytological atypia. However, melanocytic nevi of “special sites,” including the genitalia, frequently exhibit atypical histological features—such as large and confluent junctional nests, dyscohesion, cytological atypia, and retraction artifact—that may mimic melanoma despite an entirely benign clinical course.2,11,12 These atypical genital-type nevi are well documented and can lead to overdiagnosis if the pathologist is unaware of the anatomical context; long-term cohorts confirm a low rate of progression to melanoma. 13 Correlation of the clinical, dermoscopic, and histopathological findings, ideally by a pathologist experienced with genital specimens, is therefore essential.

Management of vulvar CMN should be individualized. Asymptomatic nevi with benign features can be monitored without intervention. 4 Excision is reasonable when the lesion is symptomatic, shows suspicious change, or when reliable follow-up cannot be ensured.3,4 In children, lesion growth may be physiological and proportional to overall body growth, so observation is valid when follow-up is feasible; excision is indicated for progressive or atypical change, local symptoms, or when surveillance is not possible. 4 In the present case the lesion had not changed in size or color, but the combination of persistent pruritus and the impossibility of guaranteeing dermoscopic follow-up at a concealed mucosal site supported definitive excision.

From a reconstructive perspective, the inherent elasticity of the vulvar mucocutaneous tissues allows adequate resection with primary closure using local flaps. Local advancement flaps from the labia minora are well suited to defects of the clitoral hood: they are random-pattern flaps nourished by the abundant subdermal plexus of the labial mucosa, they can be advanced with minimal tension by exploiting the natural laxity of the tissue, and, when dissection is kept superficial and lateral to the midline dorsal neurovascular bundle, they preserve clitoral sensation and anatomy. Techniques described for labial and clitoral-hood reconstruction, including clitoral hood flaps and Y-V and wedge advancement flaps, provide a versatile toolkit for this region. 14 An operative schematic of the excision and flap advancement is provided (Figure 2) to illustrate these principles. Current literature nonetheless lacks specific guidance on the maximal defect size amenable to primary local-flap closure in this region, and further study is warranted to define safe resection limits without compromising long-term function or aesthetics.

Reports of CMN specifically involving the clitoris or clitoral hood are exceptionally rare; most available data derive from broader series of genital or vulvar nevi (Table 1). This scarcity, together with the functional importance of the site, underscores the value of the present case in informing conservative, function-preserving management. Conclusions

CMN of the clitoral hood are rare, and small lesions carry a very low risk of malignant transformation. When excision is chosen—for symptomatic relief, patient and family preference, or because reliable surveillance cannot be ensured—a conservative plastic-surgical approach using local mucocutaneous advancement flaps from the labia minora allows complete removal of the lesion while preserving clitoral sensation, genital anatomy, and cosmetic appearance. Treatment should be individualized, balancing the low oncological risk of small CMN against the functional and psychological considerations specific to this anatomical region.
