Fair Vertex Problems Parameterized by Cluster Vertex Deletion
Tomáš Masařík, Jędrzej Olkowski, Anna Zych-Pawlewicz

Abstract
In this paper we study fair variants of 𝖬𝖲𝖮 1 \mathsf{MSO}_{1} definable problems parameterized by cluster vertex deletion number, i.e., the smallest number of vertices required to be removed from the graph such that what remains is a collection of cliques. While typical graph problems seek the smallest set of vertices satisfying some property, their fair variants seek such a set that does not contain too many vertices in any neighborhood of any vertex. Formally, the task is to find a set X ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) X\subseteq V(G) satisfying some 𝖬𝖲𝖮 1 \mathsf{MSO}_{1} definable property, whose fair cost is at most k k , i.e., such that for all v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G) it holds that | X ∩ N ⁡ ( v ) | ≤ k |X\cap N(v)|\leq k , where N ⁡ ( v ) N(v) stands for an open neighborhood of a vertex v v . Recently, Knop, Masařík, and Toufar [MFCS 2019] showed that all fair 𝖬𝖲𝖮 1 \mathsf{MSO}_{1} definable problems can be solved in 𝖥𝖯𝖳 \mathsf{FPT} time parameterized by the twin cover of a graph. They asked whether such a statement can be achieved for a more general parameterization by cluster vertex deletion number. In this paper, we prove that in full generality this is not possible by demonstrating 𝖶 ⁡ [ 𝟣 ] \mathsf{W[1]} -hardness. On the other hand, we give a sufficient condition under which a fair 𝖬𝖲𝖮 1 \mathsf{MSO}_{1} definable problem admits an 𝖥𝖯𝖳 \mathsf{FPT} algorithm parameterized by the cluster vertex deletion number. Our algorithm is general enough to capture the fair variant of many natural graph problems such as the Fair Feedback Vertex Set problem, the Fair Vertex Cover problem, the Fair Dominating Set problem, the Fair Odd Cycle Transversal problem, as well as connected variants thereof. Moreover, we solve the Fair [ σ , ρ ] [\sigma,\rho] -Domination problem for σ \sigma finite, or when both σ \sigma and ρ \rho are cofinite. That is, given finite or cofinite ρ , σ ⊆ ℕ \rho,\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{N} , the task is to find set of vertices X ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) X\subseteq V(G) of fair cost at most k k such that for all v ∈ X v\in X , | N ⁡ ( v ) ∩ X | ∈ σ |N(v)\cap X|\in\sigma and for all v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) ∖ X v\in V(G)\setminus X , | N ⁡ ( v ) ∩ X | ∈ ρ |N(v)\cap X|\in\rho .

Introduction

We investigate the parameterized complexity of a rich class of graph problems under structural parameterization.
In particular, we study fair problems that are becoming more popular in recent years, although their history can be traced back to the 1980s
[
23
]
.
The paradigm of the
fair problems
is that we seek a solution, usually represented as a subset of some universe, which is well-distributed within the given underlying structure but does not necessarily have the smallest possible size.
The standard formulation of the idea above, which is tailored to graph problems, uses the fair cost function as a measure of well-distributedness.
Given a graph
G
, we define the
fair cost
(
\mathsf{fc}
) of a set
X\subseteq V(G)
as
\mathsf{fc}(X)\coloneqq\max_{v\in V(G)}|N(v)\cap X|
, where
N(v)
is the open neighborhood of a vertex
v
.
Then the
fair vertex problems
on graphs ask for a set
X\subseteq V(G)
of the smallest fair cost among all vertex subsets satisfying some property.
As we wish to solve a large collection of problems, we need to formally characterize what kind of properties we work with.
For that, we use the concept of graph logic which is a standard tool that allows us to specify a collection of problems
definable using some particular graph logic.
For example, in the
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
logic
1
1

1

The logic
\mathsf{FO}
allows quantifications only over elements (vertices or edges).
A more powerful logic
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
extends
\mathsf{FO}
by permitting quantification over sets of vertices (but not sets of edges).
For more details consult
[
3
]
.

, for which we design our main
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm, we can define many standard graph properties such as
k
-colorability, acyclicity, and even the connectivity of a set.
Following
[
16
]
, we provide two definitions of fair problems, the
second one being more general.

In this paper, we study the fair vertex problems under structural parameterization.

The cluster vertex deletion number is a well-known optimization problem; see e.g.,

Related Results.

There was also some interest in exploring fair
problems under parameterizations other than structural.
Parameterization by the solution size was studied in
[
10
,
12
]
.
Papers
[
8
,
10
,
2
]
studied an
extension of the fair concept to sets, specifically the fair hitting set problem under various parameterizations.
In particular,
[
2
]
shows that the Fair Vertex
Cover problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete for any
k\geq 4
even on planar
graphs, where
k
is the fair cost of the solution.

The concept of Fair Problems has been generalized using the notion of
local cardinality constraints
, as introduced by Szeider
[
26
]
.
Given a graph
G
with cardinality constraints
\alpha(v)\subseteq[n]
for all
v\in V(G)
,
a solution is a subset
W\subseteq V(G)
such that
|W\cap N(v)|\in\alpha(v)
for all
v\in V(G)
. A notable special case, introduced in
[
14
]
, is called
local linear cardinality constraints
, where all
\alpha(v)
form an interval.
Local linear problems were further studied in
[
13
]
.
Observe that the fair vertex problems with fair cost
f
represent the simplest local cardinality constraints, where
\alpha(v)=[0,f]
for all
v\in V(G)
.

1.1
Overview of Our Results

Here, we give an overview of results.
First, we state our hardness theorem that answers a general case of a question posted in
[
16
]
. Notice that the hardness is for the more restricted deletion variant where only a sentence in
\mathsf{FO}
logic is used after the deletion of a set.

Theorem 1

(Hardness)
.

The Fair Vertex
\mathsf{FO}
Deletion is
\mathsf{W[1]}
-hard parameterized by the size of the formula and the cluster vertex deletion number, for any cluster vertex deletion number greater than 3.

To state the positive result formally, we need to specify
some conditions under which it holds, as by Theorem
1
it cannot hold in full generality.
For that we need to
introduce more model-checking background and tools which we do
in
Section

4
.
Here, for the sake of simplicity, we give an overview,
intuition, and a less formal statement.
We also explain the main idea behind the methods of our proof.
We conclude with the list of fair problems which, by the power
of our positive result,
are solvable in
\mathsf{FPT}
time parameterized by the cluster vertex deletion number.

A big-picture strategy of our proof can be summarized as follows.
First, we assign a
shape
to every pair
(G,X)

where
G
is a graph and
X\subseteq V(G)
represents
a potential solution to our problem
(See Section
3.3
for more details).
The shapes can be viewed as equivalence classes from the perspective of the formula.
That means we can check the truthfulness of the formula on the shape.
A desired outcome of this part of the proof is that the number of
possible shapes is bounded by the parameters: the formula size and the size of a modulator.
Therefore, all the shapes can be enumerated in
\mathsf{FPT}
time.
A drawback of this strategy is that the fair cost of the potential solution
X
cannot be easily mapped to the shapes and has to be computed.
In
Section

5
we show how to compute the actual solution of some
particular shape with
a small fair cost.
More precisely, given a shape we are able to formulate an
integer linear program (ILP) to recover the solution of minimal fair cost, which belongs to the equivalence class of this shape.
Moreover, such an ILP has the number of variables bounded by our parameters,
therefore we utilize the following algorithm originally proposed by Lenstra
[
22
]
.

Theorem 2

(
[
22
]
with an improved running time by Reis and Rothvoss
[
25
]
)
.

There is an algorithm that, given an ILP (Integer Linear Program) with
p
variables and
m
constraints, finds an optimal solution in time
{(\log p)}^{\mathcal{O}(p)}\cdot(m\log L)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, where
L
is the maximum absolute value of the coefficients.

Hence, the approach above leads to a solution in
\mathsf{FPT}
time.
This general approach was used in both
[
16
,
24
]
to
obtain similar general results for fair problems
under different parameterization such as twin cover.

Although the general approach to the problem is similar,
parameterization by cluster vertex deletion poses a number of
new challenges. First of all, the vertices in
G-D
(where
D

is the modulator) may have different neighborhoods in
D
,
whereas under the parameterization by twin cover all vertices in the same clique of

G-D
are twins. Second of all, as mentioned before,
parameterization via cluster vertex deletion is

\mathsf{W[1]}
-hard. Hence, we need to apply careful analysis to find
an assumption, as
general as possible, that makes the problem
\mathsf{FPT}
.

For our
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm to work we need a somewhat consistent behavior of a fair cost solution on cliques in
G-D
. An example of such behavior is the necessity to include all but one vertex in the solution from each clique, as in the Fair Vertex Cover problem. Another example is the existence of a low fair cost solution that contains only a bounded (in terms of parameters) number of vertices from each clique.
We now (somewhat informally) explain the general assumption that we
propose. When working with a modulator
D
it is convenient to
partition all cliques in
G-D
into
clique types
. We divide cliques into types depending on their neighborhoods in
D
. To be more precise, a clique type is a vector indexed with subsets
S\subseteq D
, storing a number for each
S\subseteq D
. This number describes how many vertices of the clique have neighborhood
S
in
D
. Since we want to enumerate all possible clique types,
storing the exact number of such vertices is prohibitively
expensive. Thus, the clique type stores the precise number only
up to a certain threshold
\alpha
. Above this threshold we only
know from the clique type that there is more than
\alpha
vertices
in the clique with neighborhood
S
.
In this way, we can partition all cliques in
G-D
into a bounded number of clique types.

We consider some solution
X\subseteq V(G)
to our
problem that we wish to find. Since we can enumerate all
possible candidates for
X\cap D
, we focus on finding
X

within the cliques of
G-D
. As each clique type can be treated
independently, it is safe to assume for now
that all the cliques in the graph admit the same clique type.
We say that set
S\subseteq D
is
\alpha
-
bounded
(for
the clique
type under consideration) if the number of vertices with
neighborhood
S
in
D
is stored explicitly by the clique
type, i.e., all cliques within this clique type have the same
amount of vertices with neighborhood
S
in
D
and this amount
is at most
\alpha
. Moreover, we say that
S
is

\delta
-
thin
with regard to the considered clique type and
the considered solution
X
if for every clique
C
within
this clique type we have

\left|\{v\in V(C)\mid N(v)\cap D=S\}\cap X\right|\leq\delta
, that is, the
size of the intersection of the set of vertices with neighborhood
S
and
X
is bounded above by the parameter
\delta
. Finally, we will say that

S
is
\delta
-
thick
if for every clique
C
within this clique type we have

\left|\{v\in V(C)\mid N(v)\cap D=S\}\setminus X\right|\leq\delta
, that is,
the size of the intersection of the set of vertices with neighborhood
S
and

X
is upper-bounded by the parameter
\delta
. We will say that a pair

(G,X)
has an
\alpha
-coherent shape (with regard to the considered
clique type) if each set
S\subseteq D
is either

\alpha
-bounded,
\alpha/2
-thin,
or
\alpha/2
-thick.
If a pair
(G,X)
has an
\alpha
-coherent shape with regard to all
non-empty
clique types, then we just say that
(G,X)
has an
\alpha
-coherent
shape. We illustrate the coherence property in
Figure

5
. The
\alpha
-coherence for large enough alpha is precisely what we need to make our
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm work.

(G,X)

.

In the following theorem, we assume the optimum modulator is given on the input as there is an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm parameterized by the solution size that finds it. Currently, the best bound is
1.7549^{|D_{G}|}\cdot\text{poly}(|V(G)|)

[
28
]
.

Theorem 3

(Main informal algorithmic
\mathsf{FPT}
statement; see
Theorem

24
for a formal version)
.

Let
\varphi(\aleph)
be any
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
formula with one free
variable, let
G
be a graph and let
D_{G}
be a minimum
modulator
of
G
.
If there exists a solution of the minimal fair cost whose
shape is
\alpha
-coherent for sufficiently large value
of
\alpha
dependent purely on
|\varphi|
and
|D_{G}|
,
then we can solve the respective
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
-FairVE

problem in
\mathsf{FPT}
time parameterized by
|\varphi|
and
|D_{G}|
.

The assumptions of the above theorem are
properties of the problem,
and we can verify if a problem satisfies them.
We provide such verification for a variety of problems
in
Section

6
.
Here, we demonstrate this for
the Fair Vertex Cover problem.
Note that from each clique we take either all or all
but one vertices to the vertex cover.
Therefore, each solution leads to a coherent shape, as
for every clique type every subset of the modulator is
1
-thick.

We exhibit several fundamental graph vertex problems that are covered by
Theorem

3
.
We provide only very concise formulation here, for more details see
Section

6
.
Given a graph
G
and integer
d
in the input,
we describe the problems by expressing how
G-X
is constrained.
Note that in addition to that we are looking for
X
with fair cost at most
d
.
The
Fair Feedback Vertex Set
problem requires
G-X
to be a forest.
The
Fair Odd Cycle Transversal
problem requires
G-X
to be a bipartite graph.
Of course, we can impose additional
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
expressible constraints on
X
in all the problems above.
In particular, the connectivity constraint on
X
leads to variants of the problems above that have been well studied in the non-fair setting; see e.g., a recent study of those problems parameterized by clique-width
[
7
]
.
We also define the Fair
[\sigma,\rho]
-Domination problem for
\sigma,\rho
finite or cofinite.
The original
[\sigma,\rho]
-Domination
problems have been coined by Telle in
[
27
]
.
The task is to find a set of vertices
X\subseteq V(G)
such that for all
v\in X
,
|N(v)\cap X|\in\sigma
and for all
v\in V(G)\setminus X
,
|N(v)\cap X|\in\rho
.
An important and classical member of the family of problems above is the
Dominating Set
problem, where
\sigma=\mathbb{N},\rho=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
.
We conclude with a list of problems that are implied by
Theorem

3
.

Corollary 4
.

We can solve the following problems in
\mathsf{FPT}
time parameterized by the cluster vertex deletion:

•

Fair Vertex Cover,

•

Fair Feedback Vertex Set,

•

Fair Odd Cycle Transversal,

•

Fair Dominating Set,

•

Connected variants of these problems,

•

and Fair
[\sigma,\rho]
-Domination problem for
\sigma
finite, or when both
\sigma,\rho
are cofinite.

Preliminaries

We denote
[n]\coloneqq\{0,1,2,\ldots,n\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{\text{odd}}\coloneqq\{2n+1:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
For a graph
G
and a set
X\subseteq V(G)
, we
denote the complement of
X
as

\overline{X}\coloneqq V(G)\setminus X
, the subgraph induced by

X
as
G[{X}]
, while
G-X
denotes
G[{\overline{X}}]
.
For a vertex
v\in V(G)
, we denote by
N(v)
the open
neighborhood of
v
, while
N[v]
denotes the closed
neighborhood of
v
. For a set
X\subseteq V(G)
and a
vertex
v\in V(G)
, we denote
N_{X}(v)\coloneqq N(v)\cap X
, i.e., the neighbors of
v
in
X
.
A
modulator

D\subseteq V(G)
of
G
is a set
such that
G-D
is a collection of
vertex-disjoint cliques.
For every graph
G
, we denote by
D_{G}
any fixed modulator of the
smallest size.
For a graph
G
and its modulator
D_{G}
, let
\mathcal{C}_{G}

denote the collection of cliques in
G-D_{G}
. The
cluster
vertex deletion number
of a graph
G
, also denoted as
\mathop{\mathrm{cvd}}(G)
,
is the size of
D_{G}
.

The formulas of
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
logic are those that can be constructed using vertex variables, denoted usually by
x_{i},y_{i},\dots
, set variables denoted usually by
X_{i},Y_{i},\dots
,
label classes
denoted by
L_{i}
, the predicates
E(x_{i},x_{j})
,
x_{i}\in L_{j}
, and
x_{i}=x_{j}
operating on vertex variables, standard propositional connectives, and the quantifiers
\exists,\forall
operating on vertex and set variables. The semantics is defined in the usual way, with the predicate
E(x_{i},x_{j})
being true if and only if
(x_{i},x_{j})\in E
and labels being interpreted as sets of vertices.

We will also consider two more standard logics in graph theory. The logic
\mathsf{FO}
allows quantifications only over elements (vertices or edges).
A more powerful logic,
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
, extends
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
by permitting
quantification over sets of edges.
It is well known that
\mathsf{FO}

\subsetneq

\mathsf{MSO}_{1}

\subsetneq

\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
, where connectivity and the Hamiltonian cycle are properties demonstrating strict inclusions, respectively.
For more details consult
[
3
]
.
Let
\varphi(\aleph)
be a formula with one free variable.
The
size
of
\varphi(\aleph)
(denoted by
|\varphi|
) is defined as the number of vertex and set quantifiers.

3
Shapes

In this section, we work towards compact representations of
solutions to a particular
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
-FairVE problem that we
wish to solve. In
Section

3.1
, we introduce clique types,
which compactly represent the relevant information about
cliques of the input graph.
Then, we define compliance of sets of vertices
(
Section

3.2
), a property that allows us to represent
a solution set in a compact form. To justify this definition,
we show that we can assume compliance without any loss in either
the fair cost or the logical value of the formula.
After that, we give a formal definition of a shape
(
Section

3.3
). A shape provides a compact description of the
solution and contains all the information relevant for the
truthfulness of the formula.

3.1
Clique Types

To define the clique types,
we assume from now on that a set
D_{G}
has a fixed ordering.
We denote all binary vectors of length
d
by
\mathsf{NT}^{d}=\{0,1\}^{d}
, and we call them
neighborhood types
.
As we mostly use
\mathsf{NT}^{d}
for
d=|D_{G}|
, we write
\mathsf{NT}
when it is clear from the context.
Since the vertices in
D_{G}
are ordered,
there is a natural bijection between all subsets of
D_{G}
and
vectors in
\mathsf{NT}^{|D_{G}|}
.
For each
C\in\mathcal{C}_{G}
and for each
\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}^{|D_{G}|}
,
we let
C_{\mathsf{nT}}\coloneqq\{v\in V(C):N_{D_{G}}(v)=\mathsf{nT}\}
be
the set of vertices in
C
whose neighborhood in
D_{G}
is
\mathsf{nT}
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{\text{odd}}
2
2

2

For technical ease of notation that will be apparent later, we insist on
\alpha
being odd. At this point, this assumption is not necessary, but we prefer to make it consistent and prepare the reader from the start that
\alpha
takes only odd values.
,
we define an
\alpha
-clique type
as vector

\mathsf{cT}
indexed by neighborhood types
\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}
, with entries taking values from
[\alpha]
.
We say that a clique
C\in\mathcal{C}_{G}
is of
\alpha
-clique
type
\mathsf{cT}
if
\mathsf{cT}[\mathsf{nT}]=\min(|C_{\mathsf{nT}}|,\alpha)
for each

\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}
.
We denote as
\mathsf{CT}^{(\alpha,|D_{G}|)}\coloneqq[\alpha]^{\mathsf{NT}^{|D_{G}|}}
the set of all
\alpha
-clique types.
Clearly,
|\mathsf{CT}^{(\alpha,|D_{G}|)}|=(\alpha+1)^{|\mathsf{NT}^{|D_{G}|}|}=(\alpha+1)^{2^{|D_{G}|}}
.
We will use the shortened notation
\mathsf{CT}
for
\mathsf{CT}^{(\alpha,|D_{G}|)}
whenever it is clear from the context.

3.2
Compliant Sets

The following definition captures which sets of vertices can be
compactly represented by our approach.

Definition 5

(
\alpha
-compliant sets)
.

Let
\alpha
be a positive integer and
G
be a graph.
Recall that
D_{G}
is the modulator of
G
and
\mathcal{C}_{G}
is the
collection of cliques in
G-D_{G}
.

We say that
X\subseteq V(G)
is
\alpha
-compliant
if
for each
C\in\mathcal{C}_{G}
and for each
\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}

\text{either }|X\cap C_{\mathsf{nT}}|\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\text{ or }|\overline{X}\cap C_{\mathsf{nT}}|\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}.

From now on we will insist on the solution to our problem to be

\alpha
-compliant for some parameter
\alpha
. To justify that
it is reasonable to expect
\alpha
-compliance and reveal for
which parameter
\alpha
it is needed, we provide the lemma below.

Lemma 6

(Compliant solution of capped fair cost)
.

There is a computable function
{f:\mathbb{N}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}}
such that for any
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
formula
\varphi(\aleph)
with one free variable, for any graph
G
, and for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{\text{odd}},\alpha>f(|D_{G}|,|\varphi|)
, the following is true. For each
X\subseteq V(G)
such that
G\models\varphi(X)
, there exist
X^{\prime}\subseteq V(G)
such that:

•

X^{\prime}
is
\alpha
-compliant,

•

G\models\varphi(X^{\prime})
, and

•

\mathsf{fc}(X^{\prime})\leq\mathsf{fc}(X)
.

Note that
\alpha
-compliance is a weaker
property than
\alpha
-coherence introduced in
Section
1.1
. In particular,

\alpha
-compliance is necessary for
\alpha
-coherence.
We postpone the proof as we first need to introduce more model-checking tools before we can prove it.

3.3
Solution Patterns and Shapes

Throughout this section, we work with
\alpha
-compliant sets,
for some fixed
\alpha
that depends only on
the formula and the size of the modulator.

Moreover, we use

(\alpha+1)
-clique types in order to
distinguish if for some particular clique
C\in\mathcal{C}_{G}
and

\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}
the size
of
C_{\mathsf{nT}}
is greater than
\alpha
or not. We say that
a pair
(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT})
, where
\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}
and

\mathsf{cT}\in\mathsf{CT}^{(\alpha+1,|D_{G}|)}

is
bounded
if
\mathsf{cT}[\mathsf{nT}]\leq\alpha
.
Otherwise (i.e.,
\mathsf{cT}[\mathsf{nT}]=\alpha+1
),
(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT})
is
unbounded
.

Now we describe how an
\alpha
-compliant set
X\subseteq V(G)

can be represented.
To do so, we introduce the concept of shapes and solution patterns.
For some given parameters
d
and
\alpha
, we define a
solution pattern
as
a vector indexed by
neighborhood types
\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}^{d}
and with values
from
[\alpha]
. We denote all
solution patterns
as

\mathsf{SP}^{(\alpha,d)}\coloneqq[\alpha]^{\mathsf{NT}^{d}}
.
We use shorthand notation
\mathsf{SP}
for

\mathsf{SP}^{(\alpha,|D_{G}|)}
when it is clear from the context.
Intuitively, every solution pattern
\mathsf{sP}\in\mathsf{SP}^{(\alpha,|D_{G}|)}
describes
how some potential solution
X
is distributed over vertices
with a
specific neighborhood type
\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}^{|D_{G}|}
in a
particular clique
C\in\mathcal{C}_{G}
.
To be more precise, if
\mathsf{cT}
is the clique type of a clique

C
,
and
\mathsf{cT}[\mathsf{nT}]\leq\alpha
, we can easily describe the exact
amount of vertices from
X
in
C_{\mathsf{nT}}
by a solution pattern
which can hold all possible values up to
\alpha
. If, on the
other hand,
\mathsf{cT}[\mathsf{nT}]=\alpha+1
, assuming
\alpha
-compliance
of
X
, we can describe
X\cap C_{\mathsf{nT}}
by either storing

\mathsf{sP}[\mathsf{nT}]=|X\cap C_{\mathsf{nT}}|
if
|X\cap C_{\mathsf{nT}}|\leq\alpha/2
, or
by storing
\mathsf{sP}[\mathsf{nT}]=\alpha-|\overline{X}\cap C_{\mathsf{nT}}|
if

|\overline{X}\cap C_{\mathsf{nT}}|\leq\alpha/2
. In other words, the
solution pattern encodes either the cardinality of the intersection of
C_{\mathsf{nT}}

and
X
, or it encodes the cardinality of
C_{\mathsf{nT}}\setminus X
, depending on which
of these two values is smaller than
\alpha/2
. This is
summarized in the following definition.

Definition 7
.

For a graph
G
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{\text{odd}}
and a clique
C\in\mathcal{C}_{G}
whose

(\alpha+1)
-clique type is
\mathsf{cT}
, we say that a set
X\subseteq V(G)

matches
a solution pattern

\mathsf{sP}\in\mathsf{SP}^{(\alpha,|D_{G}|)}
on clique
C

if for each
\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}^{|D_{G}|}
it holds
that

|X\cap C_{\mathsf{nT}}|=\begin{cases}\mathsf{sP}[\mathsf{nT}]&if $(\mathsf{cT}[\mathsf{nT}]\leq\alpha)$ or $(\mathsf{cT}[\mathsf{nT}]=\alpha+1$ and $\mathsf{sP}[\mathsf{nT}]<\alpha/2)$\\
|C_{\mathsf{nT}}|-(\alpha-\mathsf{sP}[\mathsf{nT}])&if $\mathsf{cT}[\mathsf{nT}]=\alpha+1$ and $\mathsf{sP}[\mathsf{nT}]>\alpha/2$\end{cases}

The above definition naturally partitions the triplets

(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT},\mathsf{sP})
where
\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}
,

\mathsf{cT}\in\mathsf{CT}^{(\alpha+1,|D_{G}|)}
, and

\mathsf{sP}\in\mathsf{SP}^{(\alpha,|D_{G}|)}
into the following types:

bounded:

(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT},\mathsf{sP})
is
bounded
if

(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT})
is bounded (otherwise
(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT},\mathsf{sP})
is
unbounded
)

thin:

(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT},\mathsf{sP})
is
thin

if it is unbounded and
\mathsf{sP}[\mathsf{nT}]<\alpha/2
,

thick:

(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT},\mathsf{sP})
is
thick

if it is unbounded and
\mathsf{sP}[\mathsf{nT}]>\alpha/2
.

Note that as
\alpha
is odd
(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT},\mathsf{sP})
is either thin, thick, or bounded.

Having defined solution patterns and their meaning, we move on to
defining the most important combinatorial object of the paper.

Definition 8

(Shapes)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{\text{odd}}
and
\gamma,d\in\mathbb{N}
.
We define
\mathsf{Shapes}^{(\alpha,\gamma,d)}
as a set of all tuples
(\mathsf{nT}^{*},\mathsf{M})
, where:

1)

\mathsf{nT}^{*}\in\mathsf{NT}^{d}
,

2)

\mathsf{M}
is a
|\mathsf{SP}^{(\alpha,d)}|\times|\mathsf{CT}^{(\alpha+1,d)}|
matrix
whose entries are from
[\gamma]
, and

3)

for each
\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}
,
\mathsf{cT}\in\mathsf{CT}^{(\alpha+1,d)}
, and
\mathsf{sP}\in\mathsf{SP}^{(\alpha,d)}
such that
\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{sP},\mathsf{cT}]>0
and
\mathsf{cT}[\mathsf{nT}]\leq\alpha
, we have
\mathsf{sP}[\mathsf{nT}]\leq\mathsf{cT}[\mathsf{nT}]
.

We refer to an element
\mathsf{shp}\in\mathsf{Shapes}^{(\alpha,\gamma,d)}
as a
shape
.

We note that Condition
3)
in
Definition

8
is
necessary, as it ensures that whenever the triplet
(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT},\mathsf{sP})

is bounded,
the solution pattern
\mathsf{sP}
does not describe a solution containing
more vertices than are present in a corresponding subset
C_{\mathsf{nT}}

of a clique
C
of type
\mathsf{cT}
.

We now provide some intuition behind the shapes. First, we briefly
describe the meaning of the parameters
\alpha,\gamma
. Roughly
speaking,
\alpha
corresponds to the maximum number of vertices
of the same neighborhood within a single clique that the formula
can distinguish. In other words, once there are more than
\alpha

such vertices, increasing their number further makes no difference
to the formula. On the other hand,
\gamma
corresponds to the
maximum number of cliques of the same
(\alpha+1)
-clique type the
formula can distinguish. We will formalize this intuition
in
Corollary

19
and
Corollary

22
. Thus,
we restrict ourselves to shapes with values bounded by these
parameters.
In other words, given
G
and
\varphi(\aleph)
, the shapes in

\mathsf{Shapes}^{(\alpha,\gamma,|D_{G}|)}
describe all
the information about the solution that is relevant for the formula,
as
\alpha
and
\gamma
are computed from
\varphi(\aleph)
and
D_{G}
.
Let us now intuitively describe how the shapes are interpreted further.
Each such shape, denoted as
\mathsf{shp}
, consists of two parts that
are supposed to guess some potential solution
X
.
First, the shape guesses the portion of the potential solution
set
X
in the
modulator
D_{G}
. More precisely,
X\cap D_{G}
forms the first part
of
\mathsf{shp}
, denoted
\mathsf{nT}^{*}
. The second part is a matrix, that
guesses how the solution
X
is distributed over the cliques and
their respective neighborhood parts. More precisely,
the entry
\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{sP},\mathsf{cT}]
represents the minimum of two values:
parameter
\gamma
and the number of cliques
C
whose

(\alpha+1)
-clique
type is
\mathsf{cT}
and such that
X
matches
\mathsf{sP}
on
C
.
Notice that the number of all possible shapes is bounded in terms of
\alpha,\gamma,|D_{G}|
, so we can enumerate all of them, and maintain an
\mathsf{FPT}
complexity.

Definition 9

(The shape of a graph and a set)
.

For a graph
G
and an
\alpha
-compliant set

X\subseteq V(G)
we define a shape of
X
in
G
,
denoted as
\mathsf{Shape}^{(\alpha,\gamma)}(G,X)\in\mathsf{Shapes}^{(\alpha,\gamma,|D_{G}|)}
,
as follows:

•

\mathsf{nT}^{*}=X\cap D_{G}

•

M[\mathsf{sP},\mathsf{cT}]=\min(\gamma,|\{C\in\mathcal{C}_{G}:C\text{ is
of type }\mathsf{cT}\text{ and }X\text{ matches }\mathsf{sP}\text{ on }C\}|)

for all

\mathsf{sP}\in\mathsf{SP}^{(\alpha,|D_{G}|)}
and
\mathsf{cT}\in\mathsf{CT}^{(\alpha+1,|D_{G}|)}

We already described how to assign a shape to a pair
(G,X)
.
It is also possible, given a shape
\mathsf{shp}
, to produce a
canonical pair
(G^{\prime},X^{\prime})
whose shape is
\mathsf{shp}
.

Definition 10

(The graph and the set associated with a shape)
.

Given
(\mathsf{nT}^{*},\mathsf{M})=\mathsf{shp}\in\mathsf{Shapes}^{(\alpha,\gamma,d)}
,
we define the graph
G^{\prime}
and the set
X^{\prime}\subseteq V(G^{\prime})

associated
with
\mathsf{shp}
as follows:

1)

G^{\prime}
has
d
vertices in the modulator, and
exactly those represented by vector
\mathsf{nT}^{*}
are in
X^{\prime}
;

2)

For each
\mathsf{cT}\in\mathsf{CT}^{(\alpha+1,d)}
,
G^{\prime}
contains exactly

\sum_{\mathsf{sP}\in\mathsf{SP}^{(\alpha,d)}}\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{sP},\mathsf{cT}]

cliques of clique type
\mathsf{cT}
, as described by the column indexed by
\mathsf{cT}

(each clique contains exactly
\mathsf{cT}[\mathsf{nT}]
vertices of neighborhood type
\mathsf{nT}

for each
\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}
).
Moreover, for each
\mathsf{sP}\in\mathsf{SP}^{(\alpha,d)}
, the set
X^{\prime}
matches the
solution pattern
\mathsf{sP}
on exactly
\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{sP},\mathsf{cT}]
cliques of clique type
\mathsf{cT}
.

We established that a shape can be interpreted as
a pair
(G^{\prime},X^{\prime})
, which is a canonical instance
corresponding to this shape. This allows us to evaluate a formula
on
G^{\prime}
, where
X^{\prime}
is substituted for the free variable.
We can view this process as evaluating the formula on the shape
directly. This is formalized in the following definition.

Definition 11

(Evaluation of a shape)
.

Let
\varphi(\aleph)
be a fixed formula with one free variable.
For each
\mathsf{shp}\in\mathsf{Shapes}^{(\alpha,\gamma,d)}
, we define whether
\mathsf{shp}
is
true
or
false
for the formula
\varphi(\aleph)
(denoted as
\models\varphi(\mathsf{shp})
).
Let
G^{\prime},X^{\prime}
be the graph and the set associated with
\mathsf{shp}
.
Then
\models\varphi(\mathsf{shp})
if and only if
G^{\prime}\models\varphi(X^{\prime})
.

We proceed with the definition of coherent shapes.
Our final
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm presented in Section
5
is
only able to find
\alpha
-compliant solutions whose shape is
coherent.

Definition 12

(Coherent shape)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{\text{odd}}
and
\gamma,d\in\mathbb{N}
.
We say that
(\mathsf{nT}^{*},\mathsf{M})=\mathsf{shp}\in\mathsf{Shapes}^{(\alpha,\gamma,d)}
is
coherent
if, for each
\mathsf{nT}\in\mathsf{NT}
and
\mathsf{cT}\in\mathsf{CT}^{(\alpha+1,d)}
such that
(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT})
is unbounded, the elements of the set

\{(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT},\mathsf{sP})\mid\mathsf{sP}\in\mathsf{SP}^{(\alpha,d)}\text{ and }\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{sP},\mathsf{cT}]>0\}

are either all thin or all thick. In this case, we say that
(\mathsf{nT},\mathsf{cT})
is
thin
or
thick
, respectively.

4
Main Tools and Model-checking Machinery

The purpose of this section is to confirm that the truthfulness
of the formula on an
\alpha
-compliant set is equivalent to
the truthfulness on its shape.

Lemma 13
.

There is a computable function
{f:\mathbb{N}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}}
such that, for any
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
formula
\varphi(\aleph)
with one free variable, for any graph
G
, and for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{\text{odd}}
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha,\gamma>f(|D_{G}|,|\varphi|)
, the following holds.
If
X\subseteq V(G)
is
\alpha
-compliant
then
\models\varphi(\mathsf{Shape}^{(\alpha,\gamma)}(G,X))
if and only if
G\models\varphi(X)
.

Before we prove
Lemma

13

in
Section

4.1
we need to introduce model-checking tools and notation.
In the remainder of this section, we introduce known logical tools
useful for proving Lemma
13
, which is
crucial for the
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm in
Section

5
.

We will consider labelled graphs,
which are more convenient for proving some properties of the formula
on a graph. Recall that a label is simply a subset of vertices.

Definition 14
.

For a labelled graph
G
, we say that vertices
u
and
v
have the
same labelled type if they have the same labels and they have the same
closed neighborhoods, i.e.,
N[u]=N[v]
.

We will follow the approach of irrelevant vertices and irrelevant cliques, as it was done in
[
16
]
and
[
20
]
.

Definition 15
.

For a given labelled graph
G
, a set
X\subseteq V(G)
, and a
formula
\varphi(\aleph)
, we define a sentence
\varphi_{X}
in the
following way.
We introduce a new label
\ell_{X}
and assign
\ell_{X}
to all vertices in

X
, thus obtaining the labelled graph
G_{X}
. We create the sentence

\varphi_{X}
from
\varphi(\aleph)
by replacing every occurrence of

\aleph
with
\ell_{X}
.

We observe that
\varphi_{X}
is a valid expression, since
\ell_{X}
represents a set of vertices, as
X
does.

Observation 16
.

Let
G
be a labelled graph,
X\subseteq V(G)
, and

\varphi(\aleph)
be a formula. Then
G\models\varphi(X)\iff G_{X}\models\varphi_{X}.

We now state a theorem that will be useful for proving
Corollary

19
,
which is a crucial tool that allows us to maintain the truthfulness of
the formula under graph trimming operations.

Theorem 17

(Reformulation of
[
20
, Lemma 5]
)
.

Let
G
be a labelled graph and
\varphi
be an
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
sentence with
q_{S}
set quantifiers and
q_{v}
vertex quantifiers. Let
T\subseteq V(G)
consist of vertices of the same labelled type. Also let
Q
be any subset of
T
such that
|T\setminus Q|\geq 2^{q_{S}}q_{v}
. Then
G\models\varphi
if and only if
G-Q\models\varphi
.

Theorem
17
allows us to trim the graph without
changing the logical value of the formula, given that there
are many vertices of the same labelled type. For our
purpose, we want to
be able to trim graphs and their subsets of vertices without
changing the logical value of a formula on the particular
subset. This can be achieved with the trimming set defined below.

Definition 18

(Trimming set)
.

Let
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
G
be a labelled graph,

X\subseteq V(G)
be any subset of vertices and

T\subseteq V(G)
be a subset of vertices of the same
labelled type such that
|T|\geq\tau
.
We define a
\tau
-trimming set

Q
of
(G,X,T)
depending
on
|X\cap T|
as follows.

Case 1:

|X\cap T|\leq\frac{\tau}{2}
. We set
Q
to be any subset of
T\setminus X
of size
|T|-\tau
.

Case 2:

{\frac{\tau}{2}<|X\cap T|\leq|T|-\frac{\tau}{2}}
.
Let
Q_{1}
be any subset of
X\cap T
of size

\mathsf{FO}
formula:

\mathsf{modul}(v)\coloneqq\exists_{a,b,c\in N(v)}\ a\neq b\wedge a\neq c\wedge b\neq c\wedge\neg e(a,b)\wedge\neg e(b,c)\wedge\neg e(c,a).

We then present the final formula, which is satisfied if and only if, in every clique
C^{i}
, we remove at least one set
C^{i}_{k}
for some
k\in[d]
, while leaving the modulator unchanged.
Observe that the above is true if and only if there are
no

d
vertices outside the modulator that lie in one clique and each of them is a neighbor of a different vertex of the modulator.
We encode this by the following
\mathsf{FO}
sentence:

\begin{gathered}\varphi\coloneqq\exists_{m_{1},\dots,m_{d}}\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d]\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}m_{i}\neq m_{j}\ \wedge\ \bigwedge_{i\in[d]}\mathsf{modul}(m_{i})\ \wedge\\
\neg\Bigg(\exists_{v_{1},\dots,v_{d}}\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d]\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}v_{i}\neq v_{j}\ \wedge\ \bigwedge_{i\in[d]}\neg\mathsf{modul}(v_{i})\ \wedge\ \bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d]\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}e(v_{i},v_{j})\ \wedge\ \bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d]\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\Big(\exists u:\mathsf{modul}(u)\wedge e(u,v_{i})\wedge\neg e(u,v_{j})\Big)\Bigg).\end{gathered}

Finally, we set the value of the fair-cost threshold in
Fair Vertex
\mathsf{FO}
Deletion
to be
k\coloneqq b
.

We now analyze the correctness of the reduction.
Since entries of
Unary
d
-Tuple
are encoded in unary, the number of vertices in
\bigcup_{i}C^{i}
is linear in the sum of all tuple entries, and the private cliques contribute only
3d(b+1)
additional vertices (which is comparable to the unary encoded items due to preprocessing of the trivial instances); hence
|G|
is polynomial in the input size.
The size of
\varphi
depends only on
|D_{G}|
.

Finally, we show the completeness and soundness of the reduction.
Assume that
Unary
d
-Tuple
is a YES-instance, and let
f
be a witness of that.
Then the set
X
such that
\forall_{i\in[n]}X\cap C^{i}=C^{i}_{f(i)}
is a witness for a YES-instance of
Fair Vertex
\mathsf{FO}
Deletion
.

Now, assume that
Fair Vertex
\mathsf{FO}
Deletion
is a YES-instance and let
X\subseteq V(G)
be a witness of that.
Note that
X\cap\mathop{\mathrm{cvd}}(G)=\emptyset
.
Moreover, for every
i\in[n]
there exists some
k\in[d]
such that
C^{i}_{k}\subseteq X
, since
X
satisfies
\varphi
.
Let
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
be such that for every
i\in[n]
we have
X^{\prime}\cap C^{i}=C^{i}_{k}
for some
k\in[d]
with
C^{i}_{k}\subseteq X
.
Observe that
X^{\prime}
still satisfies
\varphi
and
\mathsf{fc}(X^{\prime})\leq\mathsf{fc}(X)\leq k
.
Let
f
be such that for every
i\in[n]
,
f(i)
is equal to
k\in[d]
if
X^{\prime}\cap C^{i}=C^{i}_{k}
.
Then
f
is a witness that the instance of
Unary
d
-Tuple
is a YES-instance.
∎

8
Conclusions

In this paper, we resolved an open question posed in
[
16
]
.
We showed that, despite the general problem being
\mathsf{W[1]}
-hard, we are able to solve many graph vertex problems in
\mathsf{FPT}
time.
It would be interesting to see whether we can extend (some of) the
\mathsf{FPT}
results to denser graph parameters.
In particular,
[
16
]
shows that the Fair Vertex Cover problem is solvable in
\mathsf{FPT}
time parameterized by the modular-width.
We would like to emphasize their question of whether
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
-FairVE is
\mathsf{FPT}
parameterized by the modular-width and the size of the formula.

While
Theorem

24
covers a large number of natural graph vertex problems, it would be interesting to know whether all cases of Fair
[\sigma,\rho]
-Domination problems are
\mathsf{FPT}
parameterized by the cluster vertex deletion; namely, the unresolved case where
\sigma
is cofinite and
\rho
is finite.

More generally, our intuition is that the tractable cases of fair vertex problems parameterized by the cluster vertex deletion are close to
Theorem

24
.
We leave it as an open and challenging question where the dichotomy is drawn.

Finally, we propose the previously overlooked question of whether
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
-FairVE problems are in
\mathsf{XP}
parameterized by the clique-width and the size of the formula.
For the
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
-FairVE problem,
[
17
]
showed an
\mathsf{XP}
algorithm parameterized by the treewidth and the size of the formula, as observed by
[
24
]
.
This result was later implied by a more general result of
[
14
]
.
However, to the best of our knowledge, an
\mathsf{XP}
algorithm parameterized by clique-width is missing.
