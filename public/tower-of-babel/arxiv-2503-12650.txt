Probing superconductivity with tunneling spectroscopy in rhombohedral graphene
Denis Sedov, Mathias S. Scheurer

Abstract
Motivated by experiments on rhombohedral tetralayer graphene showing signs of superconductivity emerging from a valley-polarized normal state, we here analyze theoretically how scanning tunneling spectroscopy can be used to probe the superconducting order parameter of the system. To describe different pairing scenarios on equal footing, we develop a microscopic tunneling approach that can capture arbitrary, including finite-momentum, superconducting order parameters and low-symmetry normal-state Hamiltonians. Our analysis shows that the broken time-reversal symmetry in a single valley leads to unique features in the weak-tunneling regime that are different for commensurate and incommensurate Cooper pair momenta. We further uncover an unconventional spatial dependence of the Andreev conductance, allowing to distinguish between three topologically distinct classes of single- 𝐪 \mathbf{q} pairing states in the system, and compute the signatures of a competing translational-symmetry breaking three- 𝐪 \mathbf{q} “moiré superconductor”.

General formalism
—To model STS experiments in RTG systematically, we incorporate and generalize the Hamiltonian approach to tunneling based on the Keldysh formalism
[
45
]
to the case with arbitrary momentum dependent superconducting (SC) order parameters
\Delta_{\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
, coupling electronic states at in general non-zero net momentum,
\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\neq 0
, and without TRS in the multi-component normal-state Bloch Hamiltonian
h_{\mathbf{k}}
. More explicitly, the target system, here RTG, is described by
\hat{H}_{c}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}h_{\mathbf{k}}c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}-c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}\cdot h_{-\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{T}}\cdot c^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow})+\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}\cdot\Delta_{\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\cdot c^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\downarrow}+\mathrm{H.c.})
, where
h_{\mathbf{k}}
is the continuum 8-band model
[
46
,
47
]
of RTG and
c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}
is a creation operator of electrons in the
\mathbf{K}
-valley with momentum
\mathbf{k}
and spin
\sigma
. We assume that the tunneling is local with respect to the lead degrees of freedom,
\hat{H}_{t}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}f^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(0)t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}+\mathrm{H.c.}
, where
f_{\sigma}^{\dagger}(0)
creates an electron at the tip of the lead and
t_{\mathbf{k}}
are the tunneling matrix elements. We also assume that both systems are kept at the same temperature and the difference between their chemical potentials is controlled by the STS bias voltage
eV
. Thus, we neglect any effects of the distribution function renormalization.

The tunneling current can be divided into four distinct terms with different physical interpretations (
[
45
]
, SI). While we always include all of them in our numerics, for our discussion here, only two of them are crucial. First, in the weak-tunneling regime, when tunneling amplitudes are much smaller than characteristic energies of the systems (e.g. bandwidth, hopping constants), the current is mostly determined by the famous expression,
I_{\text{weak}}\sim\int d\omega\rho_{f,\text{pp}}(\omega-eV)\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\rho_{c,\text{pp}}(\omega,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})t_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\left[n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega-eV)-n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega)\right]+o(\|t\|^{2})
, where
\rho_{f/c,pp}
are bare particle spectral functions. The corresponding tunneling conductance,
G(eV)=\partial I/\partial(eV)
, is then proportional to the local density of states (LDOS). Therefore, weak-tunneling allows to probe spectral features of the Bogoliubov excitations. Second, when tunneling amplitudes become large enough, the Andreev contribution to the current become non-negligible. It is proportional to the SC order parameter
\Delta_{\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
and reads as

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{A}}=\frac{4\pi e}{\hbar}\int d\omega\biggl|\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}G_{cc,\mathrm{ph}}^{r}(\omega,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})t^{*}_{-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\biggr|^{2}

(1)

\displaystyle\times\rho_{f}^{\mathrm{pp}}(\omega-eV)\rho_{f}^{\mathrm{hh}}(\omega+eV)\left[n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega-eV)-n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega+eV)\right],

where
G_{cc,\mathrm{ph}}^{r}\propto\Delta_{\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
is the particle-hole (anomalous) part of the retarded Greens function of the RTG in the Nambu basis. The Andreev current is generated in the process in which electrons tunnel from the lead to the SC, then by the Andreev scattering are converted to a hole tunneling back to the lead. This is the only contribution to the current that does not generally vanish when
|eV|
lies within the gap of the Bogoliubov excitations. This feature along with its proportionality to the SC order parameter makes the strong tunneling regime a useful tool to extract information about the symmetry of the SC from STS experiments.

Weak tunneling
—We begin our analysis in the weak-tunneling regime, where the current is dominated by
I_{\text{weak}}
following the LDOS. We further first focus on
\mathbf{q}=0
intravalley pairing (commensurate momentum
2\mathbf{K}
) in valley-polarized RTG and only keep the band crossing the Fermi level. Then superconductivity is described by
H_{1\text{b}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\xi_{\mathbf{k}}d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}d^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}+H_{\Delta}

H_{\Delta}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}(d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}d^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}+\mathrm{H.c.})
and
d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow\downarrow}

We note that additional spin polarization in the normal state does not change these conclusions.

—The weak-tunneling regime gives access to unique features associated with broken TRS in RTG, but it is not sensitive to the phase of
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
and, thus, the IR of the order parameter. This is different in the strong-tunneling regime, where

\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\to\Delta_{C_{3z}^{-1}\mathbf{k}}=w\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
, with IRs

, respectively

—Our work demonstrates that the broken TRS in valley-imbalanced superconductors leads to a variety of features in tunneling spectroscopy that are different from pairing states emerging out of normal states with TRS. \displaystyle\hat{H}=\psi^{\dagger}\left(H_{0}+H_{t}\right)\psi,

where

,
c^{\dagger}
denote the full set of creation operators of the two systems; matrices
H_{0},H_{t}
describe uncoupled systems and tunneling between them, respectively, and are given as

\displaystyle H_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{f}&0\\
0&H_{c}\end{pmatrix},\quad H_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}0&T_{t}^{\dagger}\\
T_{t}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

We assume that both systems are kept at the same temperature and the difference between their chemical potentials is given by a bias voltage,
\mu_{f}-\mu_{c}=eV
. Then the steady-state tunneling current reads

\displaystyle j(\tau)=-\sum_{i}e_{f,i}\braket{\dot{n}_{f,i}(\tau)}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{i,j}\left[e_{f,i}(T^{\dagger}_{t})_{ij}\braket{f_{i}^{\dagger}(\tau)c_{j}(\tau)}-e_{f,i}T_{t,ij}\braket{c_{j}^{\dagger}(\tau)f_{i}(\tau)}\right],

(4)

where
e_{f,i}=\pm e
is the charge corresponding to the
i
-th degree of freedom of the system-
f
, and the average of the operators are given by the Green function on the Keldysh contour,
G^{+-}_{ij}(\tau,\tau)=i\braket{\psi_{j}^{\dagger}(\tau)\psi_{i}(\tau)}
. The tunneling current can be conveniently rewritten in the following form

\displaystyle j=\frac{e}{2\pi\hbar}\int d\omega\mathrm{Tr}\left[JG^{+-}(\omega)\right],\quad J=\begin{pmatrix}0&Q_{f}T_{t}^{\dagger}\\
-T_{t}Q_{f}&0\end{pmatrix},

(5)

where matrix
Q_{ij}^{f}=\delta_{ij}e_{f,j}
is the charge matrix of the
f
-system. The bare
+-
Green’s function
g^{+-}(\omega)=2\pi i\,\mathrm{diag}\left[\rho_{f}(\omega)n_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}(\omega),\rho_{c}(\omega)n_{\mathrm{F}}^{c}(\omega)\right]
, where
\rho_{c,f}(\omega)
are spectral functions of the systems, and
n_{\mathrm{F}}^{c,f}(\omega)=n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega-Q_{c,f}\mu_{c,f})
, their thermal Fermi-Dirac distribution functions. The
G^{+-}
is connected to
g^{+-}
in the following way

\displaystyle G^{+-}=(1+G^{r}H_{t})g^{+-}(1+H_{t}G^{a})

(6)

where advanced and retarded Green functions
G^{a,r}
satisfy a Dyson equation,

\displaystyle G^{a,r}=(1+G^{a,r}H_{t})g^{a,r}=g^{a,r}(1+H_{t}G^{a,r})

(7)

with
g^{r,a}(\omega)=(\omega\pm i\eta-H_{0})^{-1}
and
\eta
being a finite lifetime broadening.

This approach implicitly assumes that changes in the chemical potentials (and temperature) due to the tunneling are negligible, so that characteristic time scales of charging are much smaller than the time scales associated with
H_{t}
.

This formalism can be applied to a wide range of tunneling problems, herein we focus on the system modeling STS experiment, comprising a one-dimensional lead (its degrees of freedom are denoted by the
f
operators) and superconductor (SC) of interest (
c
operators). A general mean-field Hamiltonian of the system with opposite-spin pairing superconductivity can be written as follows

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{c}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}h_{\mathbf{k}}c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}-c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}\cdot h_{-\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{T}}\cdot c^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow})+\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}\cdot\Delta_{\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\cdot c^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\downarrow}+\mathrm{H.c.}),

(8)

where
h_{\mathbf{k}}
is a spin-independent Hamiltonian matrix describing the (multiband) structure of the system, and
\Delta_{\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
is a superconducting (SC) order parameter matrix. We further simplify our analysis by considering a local tunneling approximation with respect to the lead degrees of freedom, the tunneling Hamiltonian then reads

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{t}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}f^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(z=0)t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}+\mathrm{H.c.}

(9)

where
f^{\dagger}(z=0)
creates an electron at the tip of the lead,
t_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
is a row-vector with the same number of component as in
c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}
. We also consider tunneling that preserves spin-rotational symmetry,
t_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}=t_{\mathbf{k},\downarrow}=t_{\mathbf{k}}
. In order to incorporate the approach described earlier, we move to Nambu space and introduce particle and hole operators,

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathrm{p}}=c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow},\ c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathrm{h}}=c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow},\ f^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{p}}=f^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}(z=0),\ f^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{h}}=f_{\downarrow}(z=0).

(10)

The structure of the Dyson equation (
7
) and expression for the tunneling current (
5
) allow to work with the Green function projected onto the subspace
\mathrm{im}H_{t}
. In this subspace, we arrange operators in the following manner,
f^{\dagger}=(f^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{p}},f^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{h}})
,
c^{\dagger}=(\ldots,c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathrm{p}},\ldots,c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathrm{h}},\ldots)
, where
\ldots
denotes going through all indices/operators in
\mathbf{k}
-space. Therefore,
Q_{f}=\sigma_{z}
,
Q_{c}=\sigma_{z}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\dim\mathcal{H}_{c,\uparrow}}
, and finally the tunneling matrix reads

\displaystyle T_{t}^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}\ldots t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\ldots&0\\
0&\ldots-t^{\mathrm{T}}_{-\mathbf{k}}\ldots\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

Moreover, in this subspace, we set
\rho_{f}(\omega)=\mathrm{diag}(\rho_{f,\mathrm{pp}}(\omega-eV),\rho_{f,\mathrm{hh}}(\omega-eV))
, where
\rho_{f,\mathrm{pp/hh}}(\omega\mp eV)
is a bare particle (hole) local density of states at the tip of the lead. It is defined in such a way that
\rho_{f,\mathrm{pp}}(\omega)
corresponds to the LDOS at
\mu_{f}
. Finally, let us clarify the notation for the Green functions matrix elements,

\displaystyle G_{cc,\alpha\beta}(\omega,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-i\Braket{c_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}(\omega)c_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\beta}^{\dagger}(\omega)},

(12)

\displaystyle G_{ff,\alpha\beta}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-i\Braket{f_{\alpha}(\omega)f_{\beta}^{\dagger}(\omega)},

(13)

\displaystyle G_{cf,\alpha\beta}(\omega,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=-i\Braket{c_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}(\omega)f_{\beta}^{\dagger}(\omega)},

(14)

where
\alpha=\mathrm{p,h};\beta=\mathrm{p,h}
.

In general, the tunneling current can be splited into four distinct terms with different physical interpretations,
I=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{\mathrm{A}}
,

\displaystyle I_{1}=\frac{4\pi e}{\hbar}\int d\omega\left|1+\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}G^{r}_{cf,\mathrm{pp}}(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{1})\right|^{2}\rho_{f,\mathrm{pp}}(\omega-eV)\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\rho_{f,\mathrm{pp}}(\omega)t_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\left[n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega-eV)-n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega)\right],

(15a)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} I_{2}=-\frac{8\pi e}{h}\int d\omega&\mathrm{Re}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}G_{fc,\mathrm{hp}}^{a}(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{1})t^{*}_{-\mathbf{k}_{1}}\left(1+\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}G^{r}_{cf,\mathrm{pp}}(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{2})\right)\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}t^{\mathrm{\dagger}}_{\mathbf{k}}\rho_{c,\mathrm{ph}}(\omega,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})t_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right]\\
&\times\rho_{f,\mathrm{pp}}(\omega-eV)\left[n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega-eV)-n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega)\right],\end{aligned}

(15b)

\displaystyle I_{3}=\frac{4\pi e}{\hbar}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}(G^{r}_{cf,\mathrm{ph}}(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{1})\right|^{2}\rho_{f,\mathrm{pp}}(\omega-eV)\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}t^{\mathrm{T}}_{-\mathbf{k}}\rho_{c,\mathrm{hh}}(\omega,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})t^{*}_{-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\left[n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega-eV)-n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega)\right],

(15c)

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{A}}=\frac{4\pi e}{\hbar}\int d\omega\left|\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}t^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}}G_{cc,\mathrm{ph}}^{r}(\omega,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})t^{*}_{-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\rho_{f,\mathrm{pp}}(\omega-eV)\rho_{f,\mathrm{hh}}(\omega+eV)\left[n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega-eV)-n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega+eV)\right].

(15d)

We are primarily interested in two terms that allow one to extract most of the information about the spectral properties of the Bogoliubov excitations and symmetry of superconductivity in the weak and strong tunneling regimes. The weak tunneling, when tunneling amplitudes are much smaller than characteristic energies of the systems (e.g. bandwidth, hopping constants), is mostly determined by the local density of states (LDOS),
I_{\text{weak}}\sim\int d\omega\rho_{f,\text{pp}}(\omega-eV)\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\rho_{c,\text{pp}}(\omega,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})t_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\left[n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega-eV)-n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega)\right]+o(\|t\|^{2})
, it describes the transfer of an electron from lead to a superconductor. In the strong tunneling, when the tunneling amplitudes are comparable, the Andreev current
I_{\mathrm{A}}
can become non-negligible. Andreev current is generated by the process in which an electron tunnels from the lead to the SC, then by the Andreev scattering is converted to the hole tunneling back to the lead. This is the only that does not vanish when
|eV|
lies with the gap of the Bogoliubov excitations, and as we show later it can be useful for probing the symmetry of the SC order parameter.

With some slight modifications, this approach can also be applied to study the tunneling current in the spin-polarized system. The difference is that we should rewrite the problem in the extended Majorana basis,

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2}\Psi^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}\Psi,\quad\Psi=(\psi,\psi^{\dagger})^{\mathrm{T}}.

(16)

The dynamics of the systems is then described by the retarded (advanced) Green function,

\displaystyle G^{r,a}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega\pm i\eta-\mathcal{H}}.

(17)

G^{+-}
is then constructed in the same way as described earlier. Once again identifying particle and hole operators (which are not independent now),
c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathrm{p}}=c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}},\ c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathrm{h}}=c_{-\mathbf{k}},\ f^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{p}}=f^{\dagger}(0),\ f^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{h}}=f(0)
, one get the same expression for the tunneling current up to an additional factor
1/2
.

In our numerical calculations, to remove all possible effects associated with the band structure of the lead, we assume that its DOS in the tip is constant,
\rho_{f,\mathrm{pp}}(\omega)=\rho_{f,\mathrm{hh}}(\omega)=\mathrm{const}(\omega)
.

u_{\mathbf{k},+}=u_{-\mathbf{k},-}^{*}
. Then the expression for the Andreev current is given by the same expression as before,

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{A}}\propto\int d\omega\left|\sum_{\mathbf{k}}(t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\ t^{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathbf{k}})G^{r}_{cc,\mathrm{ph}}(\omega,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})\begin{pmatrix}t_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}\\
t_{\mathbf{k}}\end{pmatrix}\right|^{2}\left[n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega-eV)+n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega+eV)\right].

(39)

Let us again look at the bare weight factor,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}(t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\ t^{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathbf{k}})g_{cc,\mathrm{ph}}(\omega,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})\begin{pmatrix}t_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}\\
t_{\mathbf{k}}\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{1}{(\omega-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})(\omega+\xi_{\mathbf{k}})-|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}}(t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\ t^{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathbf{k}})\begin{pmatrix}0&\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\\
-\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}u_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{T}}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}t_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}\\
t_{\mathbf{k}}\end{pmatrix}.

(40)

Since

\displaystyle(t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\ t^{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathbf{k}})\begin{pmatrix}0&\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\\
-\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}u_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{T}}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}t_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}\\
t_{\mathbf{k}}\end{pmatrix}=\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\left[t_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}u_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}t_{\mathbf{k}}-t_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{T}}u_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}u^{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathbf{k}}t^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}\right]=0.

(41)

the Andreev current vanishes for an arbitrary order parameter. This is a direct consequence of the TRS.

D.2
Intravalley spin-polarzed SC

Let us consider an intraband spin-polarized SC, its Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{1b}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\xi_{\mathbf{k}}d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}d_{\mathbf{k}}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left[\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}d_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}d_{-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}+\mathrm{H.c.}\right],

(42)

where
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=-\Delta_{-\mathbf{k}}
,
d_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}=c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}
, the spin index is omitted. We again assume that tunneling preserves spin, and therefore, the whole system can be treated as spin-polarized. Then the tunneling Hamiltonian is given as follows

\displaystyle H_{t}=f^{\dagger}(0)\sum_{\mathbf{k}}t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}c_{\mathbf{k}}+\mathrm{H.c.}

(43)

In this case, as noted in the Appendix
A
, it is convenient to work in the extended Majorana basis. Then the the Andreev current is given by the same expression as before,

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{A}}\propto\int d\omega\left|\sum_{\mathbf{k}}t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}G_{cc,\mathrm{ph}}^{r}(\omega,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})t^{*}_{-\mathbf{k}}\right|^{2}\left[n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega-eV)+n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega+eV)\right].

(44)

Let us analyze the weight factor using bare Green function,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}t^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}g_{cc,\mathrm{ph}}(\omega,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})t_{-\mathbf{k}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{1}{(\omega-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})(\omega+\xi_{-\mathbf{k}})-|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}t_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}u_{\mathbf{k}}t_{-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}u_{-\mathbf{k}}

(45)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}t_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}u_{\mathbf{k}}t_{-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}u_{-\mathbf{k}}\left[\frac{1}{(\omega-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})(\omega+\xi_{-\mathbf{k}})-|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}}-(\mathbf{k}\to-\mathbf{k})\right].

(46)

In contrast to the spin-polarizaed inter-valley pairing discussed above, this expresion and hence the Andreev current does not vanish since the TRS is broken,
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\neq\xi_{-\mathbf{k}}
. However, it vanishes at
\omega=0
for an arbitrary
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
. This suggests that in the spin-polarized case, the Andreev part of the tunneling conductance is generally smaller than in the spin-singlet SC.

Appendix E
Topology and gauge choice

In this appendix, we will provide more details on the different contributions to the Berry curvature stated in the main text. To keep it general for now, let us consider a multiband Bloch Hamiltonian
h_{\mathbf{k}}
(with arbitrary number of bands, internal degrees of freedom etc.) and assume superconductivity with center of mass momentum
\mathbf{Q}
emerges in one of its bands, with energies
\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}
and Bloch states
\ket{u_{\mathbf{k}}}
. On the mean-field level, this can be described by the multi-band model

H_{\text{MB}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{h}_{\text{BdG}}(\mathbf{k})\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\phantom{\dagger}},\quad\hat{h}_{\text{BdG}}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}h_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}&\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}P\\
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}P^{\dagger}&-h^{*}_{-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2},\end{pmatrix},\quad\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2,\uparrow}\\
c^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2,\downarrow}\end{pmatrix},

(47)

where
c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}
are the electronic annihilation operators,
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{C}
the superconducting order parameter, and
P=\ket{u_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}}\bra{u^{*}_{-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}}
ensures that pairing only takes place in the aforementioned band. Our goal is to evaluate the Chern number of the occupied superconducting BdG band. From Eq. (
47
), we find that the associated wave function can be written as

\ket{\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}}=\sum_{p=\pm}a_{\mathbf{k},p}\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k},p}}\otimes\ket{p}_{\tau},\quad\text{with}\quad\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k},+}}=\ket{u_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}},\,\,\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k},-}}=\ket{u^{*}_{-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}},

(48)

where
\ket{p=+}_{\tau}=(1,0)^{T}
and
\ket{p=-}_{\tau}=(0,1)^{T}
are basis vectors in Nambu space. Furthermore,
\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{k}}=(a_{\mathbf{k},+},a_{\mathbf{k},-})^{T}
is the lower eigenvector of the effective one-band BdG Hamiltonian

h_{\text{BdG}}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}&\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\\
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}&-\xi_{-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}\end{pmatrix}=E_{0}(\mathbf{k})\tau_{0}+\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\tau_{x},\tau_{y},\tau_{z})^{T},

(49)

which we also expanded in Pauli matrices
\tau_{j}
for later reference. After straightforward algebra, one finds that the Berry curvature of the BdG wavefunctions in Eq. (
48
) can be written as (
\partial_{j}=\partial/\partial{k_{j}}
)

\Omega(\mathbf{k})\equiv-2\,\text{Im}\braket{\partial_{x}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}|\partial_{y}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}}=\Omega_{\text{SC}}(\mathbf{k})+\Omega_{\text{b}}(\mathbf{k})+\delta\Omega(\mathbf{k}),

(50a)

where

\Omega_{\text{SC}}(\mathbf{k})=-2\,\text{Im}[(\partial_{x}\mathbf{a}^{*}_{\mathbf{k}})\cdot\partial_{y}\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{k}}]=\frac{1}{2|\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}}|^{3}}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\partial_{x}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\times\partial_{y}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}})

(50b)

is the Berry curvature associated with the effective BdG Hamiltonian in Eq. (
49
),

\Omega_{\text{b}}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{p=\pm}|a_{\mathbf{k},p}|^{2}p\,\Omega_{u}(p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2),\quad\Omega_{u}(\mathbf{k})=-2\,\text{Im}\braket{\partial_{x}u_{\mathbf{k}}|\partial_{y}u_{\mathbf{k}}}

(50c)

is the (particle and hole) contribution from the Berry curvature
\Omega_{u}(\mathbf{k})
of the bands hosting superconductivity, and

\delta\Omega(\mathbf{k})=-2\,\text{Im}\left[\sum_{p=\pm}(\partial_{x}a^{*}_{\mathbf{k},p})a_{\mathbf{k},p}\braket{\phi_{\mathbf{k},p}|\partial_{y}\phi_{\mathbf{k},p}}-(x\leftrightarrow y)\right]

(50d)

is a mixing term. Before proceeding further with the application to RTG, a few comments are in order: as immediately follows from its definition, the total Berry curvature
\Omega(\mathbf{k})
is invariant under
\mathbf{k}
-dependent U(1) gauge transformations of
\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{k}}
and of the Bloch states we express superconductivity in; formally, these correspond to

\displaystyle U_{a}(1):\,\,\,\,\quad\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{k}}\quad

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\quad e^{i\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}}\,\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{k}},

(51)

\displaystyle U_{u}(1):\quad\ket{u_{\mathbf{k}}}\quad

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\quad e^{i\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}}\,\ket{u_{\mathbf{k}}},

(52)

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{k},p}\quad

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\quad e^{-ip\alpha_{p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}}a_{\mathbf{k},p},

(53)

respectively. While all three terms in Eq. (
50a
) are separately invariant under
U_{a}(1)
, only
\Omega_{\text{b}}(\mathbf{k})
is invariant under
U_{u}(1)
. In this sense,
\delta\Omega(\mathbf{k})
is a necessary additional term accompanying
\Omega_{\text{SC}}(\mathbf{k})
to ensure gauge invariance. This can be made more explicit by writing

\Omega_{\text{SC}}(\mathbf{k})+\delta\Omega(\mathbf{k})=-2\,\text{Im}[(D_{x}\mathbf{a}^{*}_{\mathbf{k}})\cdot D_{y}\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{k}}],\quad D_{j}=\tau_{0}\partial_{j}+\begin{pmatrix}\braket{\phi_{\mathbf{k},+}|\partial_{y}\phi_{\mathbf{k},+}}&0\\
0&\braket{\phi_{\mathbf{k},-}|\partial_{y}\phi_{\mathbf{k},-}}\end{pmatrix},

(54)

where
D_{j}
can be thought of as the gauge co-variant derivative with respect to
U_{u}(1)
.

It is also instructive to consider limiting cases. First, if
h_{\mathbf{k}}
is just a trivial one-band model, i.e.,
h_{\mathbf{k}}=\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}
, the Bloch states have no internal structure and we get
\ket{\partial_{j}u_{\mathbf{k}}}=0
such that
\Omega(\mathbf{k})=\Omega_{\text{SC}}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{2|\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}}|^{3}}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\partial_{x}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\times\partial_{y}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}})
, recovering the well-known standard expression. Another simple limit is
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}<0
with
|\xi_{\mathbf{k}}|\gg|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|
for all momenta, corresponding to a fully filled band where superconductivity plays no role. It then holds
\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{k}}\sim(1,0)^{T}
and, thus,
\Omega(\mathbf{k})=\Omega_{u}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2)
, as it should be.

In the intravalley pairing regime of RTG discussed in the main text, we have
\mathbf{Q}=2\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{q}
(with
|\mathbf{q}|\ll|\mathbf{K}|
or simply
\mathbf{q}=0
). The Chern number of superconducting band is then given by
C=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\text{BZ}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\,\Omega(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\text{LE}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\,\Omega(\mathbf{k})+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\text{BZ}\setminus\text{LE}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\,\Omega(\mathbf{k})
, where we split the integral into the low-energy (LE) part, where the continuum model of the active valley used in this work applies (small
\xi_{\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{q}/2\pm\mathbf{k}}
), and its complement. As we expect no superconductivity in the complement, we conclude from Eq. (
50d
) that
\Omega(\mathbf{k})=-\Omega_{u}(-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{q}/2)
for all
\mathbf{k}\in\text{BZ}\setminus\text{LE}
. Assuming that valley polarization only leads to an energetic deformation of the band but not to a topological phase transition in the normal state itself, TRS implies
\int_{\text{BZ}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\,\Omega_{u}(-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{q}/2)=0
and we, thus, get
\int_{\text{BZ}\setminus\text{LE}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\,\Omega(\mathbf{k})=\int_{\text{LE}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\,\Omega_{u}(-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{q}/2)
. Taken together, we can write the superconducting Chern number entirely as a low-energy expression,

C=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\text{LE}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\left[\Omega_{\text{SC}}(\mathbf{k})+\Omega_{\text{b}}(\mathbf{k})+\delta\Omega(\mathbf{k})+\Omega_{u}(-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{q}/2)\right],

(55)

which we can evaluate in the continuum model. To do this, let us for simplicity consider the minimal, isotropic two-band model of Eq. (
18
) where only the dominant degrees of freedom—the
A_{1}
(upper component of Hamiltonian) and
B_{4}
(lower component)—are kept
[
47
]
,

h_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{K}}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{0}-\mu&w_{0}(k_{x}+ik_{y})^{4}\\
w_{0}(k_{x}+ik_{y})^{4}&-u_{0}-\mu\end{pmatrix}.

(56)

A simple possible gauge choice for the wave functions in the upper band is given by

\ket{u_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{K}}}=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}+w_{0}\mathbf{k}^{8}}+u_{0}\\
w_{0}(k_{x}+ik_{y})^{4}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}>0.

(57)

In this gauge,
C_{3z}^{A_{1}}
acts trivially, i.e., without an additional
\mathbf{k}
dependent phase, on the electronic band creation operators
d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}
. Let us now assume
\mathbf{q}=0
and take an order parameter
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta
, which thus transforms under
A
of
C_{3z}^{A_{1}}
. Using the wavefunctions in Eq. (
57
), one can finds

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\text{LE}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\left[\Omega_{\text{b}}(\mathbf{k})+\delta\Omega(\mathbf{k})+\Omega_{u}(-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{q}/2)\right]=0.

(58)

In fact, this still holds if we generalize to
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta e^{in\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, transforming under
E
(for
n=3m-1
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
),
E^{*}
(for
n=3m+1
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
), or
A
(
n=3m

\mathbf{a}^{(n)}_{\mathbf{k}}
of the superconductor with
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta e^{in\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}

a^{(n)}_{\mathbf{k},+}=a^{(0)}_{\mathbf{k},+}
and
a^{(n)}_{\mathbf{k},-}=e^{-in\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}a^{(0)}_{\mathbf{k},-}

\mathbf{k}

55

C=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\text{LE}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\,\Omega_{\text{SC}}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\text{LE}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\,\frac{1}{|\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}}|^{3}}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\partial_{x}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\times\partial_{y}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{k}})

(59)

in this gauge, as stated in the main text. Although we derived this relation using constant
|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|

\mathbf{k}
