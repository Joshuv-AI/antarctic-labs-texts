Integrated effects of furrow irrigation systems and irrigation regimes on water productivity of onion (Allium cepa L.) crop in a semi-arid area
Authors: Getachew Wandifra Boru, Tigabie Setu Birhan, Terhas Legese Beyene
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDwindling water resources with inept field water management impends sustainable agricultural production in semi-arid areas of Ethiopia. Thus, the adaption and adoption of improved irrigation water running lines are indispensable and imperative to recuperate water productivity in areas where water resources are inadequate. The ground experiments were pointed to elucidate the weight of conventional, alternate, and fixed furrow irrigation systems under irrigation levels of 100 %, 70 %, and 40 % of crop evapotranspiration (ETc) on onion growth, yield, water productivity, and economic return in Alage, Central, Ethiopia. The field trials were conducted at nine treatments arranged in a randomized complete block design with three replications and water was managed at fixed intervals with variable depth approach. The result illustrated that furrow systems in conjunction with irrigation levels had a weighty pertain (P < 0.05) on onion growth, yield, water productivity, and economic return. The highest yield of 30.75 ton ha−1 was achieved at conventional furrow irrigation with 100%ETc dictated to zero water saving subsequently, the crop and irrigation water productivity dropped with the values of 6.86 and 5.61 kg m−3 respectively. The diminished yield of 6.39 ton ha−1 obtained at fixed furrow irrigation with 40%ETc conquered the saved water output lastly, the crop and irrigation water productivities demoted with values of 6.87 and 5.70 kg m−3 respectively. Nevertheless, 24.93ton ha−1 of ideal yield was obtained under alternative furrow irrigation with 100%ETc and the 50 % hoarded water was higher than the yield decline. Consequently, the crop and irrigation water productivities rose with values of 10.96 and 9.09 kg m−3 respectively. Eventually, the saved water opened for 0.03ha extra irrigable area that will onward to the highest total expected yield of 37.40 ton ha−1 as compared with all other treatments. Under conventional furrow irrigation with 100%ETc, the obtained yield dominated the wastewater accordingly, the highest net return of 668484ETB ha−1 and benefit-cost ratio of 3.63 was achieved. Besides, the unbearable yield penalty under fixed furrow with 40 % headed to negative net return and lowest befit cost ratio as related with other treatments. However, further economic results confirmed that the 50 % saved water under alternative furrow with 100 % can recompense for the net return reduction that will make this trial combination preferable as compared with other treatments. Other results also recommended that, under water-limiting and inefficient field water governing conditions, alternative furrow irrigation with 100%ETc can be the ideal blend to enhance water productivity in semi-arid regions of Ethiopia and elsewhere that share similar agroclimatic conditions.Keywords: Furrow irrigation, Deficit irrigation, Water productivity, Onion, Optimal irrigation

Introduction

Irrigation plays a vital role in agriculture to cope with the rapidly increasing population that clues to exhaustive increment of withdrawal demands for water use in the world [1] and the ever-increasing world population has limited the water supply for irrigation [2]. Moreover, with the rapid economic growth and living conditions improvement, high amounts of water sources have become rundown, leading to water has become one of the scarce resources globally [3] and this scarce resource becomes a source of demand conflict [4,5]. Likewise, the demand of agricultural sectors accounts for more than 70–72 % of the total world freshwater withdrawals [6,7], this highest agricultural withdrawal makes agricultural irrigation a source of water shortage [6], and afterward, it is also a victim [3]. Even though this sector is a reason for water threat and waste, contributes more than 40–45 % of the total food production worldwide and irrigated agriculture is vitally important for arid and semi-arid areas of the world because more than 40 % of the global productive land is concentrated in these areas characterized by less rainfall [6,8]. As a consequence rather than hesitating in this sector, the development of improved management options is trouble for optimal use of the restrained water in the areas of irrigated agriculture [6,8,9].

Agriculture is the mainstay of Ethiopia’s economy, contributing more than 40 % to the gross domestic product (GDP), and almost 90 % of export earnings [10] and the livelihood of more than 85 % of the population is dependent on this backbone sector [11,12]. Ethiopia is gifted in water resources with 12 river basins with annual outputs of 122Bm3 surface sources and 2.6 to 2.65Bm3 subsurface which are ready for potential 30 to 70 million ha cultivable land [10]. However, less than 15 million ha are under cultivation, and less than 6 % use available current technologies [4,10]. Besides, less than 2 %–5 % has been utilized to the near present for irrigated agriculture which has been 98 % dependent on erratic seasonal rainfall [4]. Less efficient old-style surface irrigation (97.8 % share of furrow system) up to date is practiced due to limited extension, lack of awareness, economic status, and the absence of multiple improved approaches [13]. These constraints are more intense in arid and semi-arid regions of the country, where the amount and distribution of rainfall are not sufficient for crop cultivation [4,[14], [15], [16], [17]] with nearly 62 % irrigable land concentrated [18].

Among the old-style surface irrigation of basin and border, the furrow irrigation method is a partial surface watering that precedes an increase in water productivity, and no more effort is required to keep improving its management and efficiency [8,19]. Hence, the enhancement of the furrow irrigation system in Ethiopia is generally important [20] and more ideal in arid and semi-arid areas because smallholder farmers and the majority of commercial and state farms are familiar with this application system [4]. Also, furrow irrigation can be developed at the farm level with minimal investment and entail low energy [21], and less than 50 % of the irrigated area is covered by water leading to more than 30 % water saved compared with other surface methods [6]. These benefits are more important in arid and semi-arid regions of Ethiopia, which are highly faced with uncertainties associated with climate change, and limited and erratic rainfall distribution [15,16]. In most parts of Ethiopia, the conventional furrow irrigation system is commonly practiced in irrigated agriculture ascribed to enormous water losses in the tailwater and deep percolation later on endorsed to waterlogging, salinity problems, and leaching of valuable nutrients [4]. Thus, the development of improved irrigation water management approaches under furrow irrigation for arid and semiarid water-scarce areas is vitally important [16,22,23]. Ideally, devising and tailoring environmentally friendly and easily adopted water management options by the users is important for saving excessive water use and loss [24], and amendment and implementation of improved irrigation technologies are important to assure water productivity and sustainability [25]. As a result, an attempt was made to evaluate three furrow irrigation systems, viz, conventional furrow irrigation (CFI) every furrow is irrigated during each irrigation event, alternate furrow irrigation (AFI), half out of the furrows in each plot is irrigated in each irrigation event turn by turn and fixed furrow irrigation (FFI), half out of the total furrows in each plot is irrigated throughout the crop growth stages [1]. Several options integrated with furrow irrigation systems can improve water productivity such as; increasing harvest index, water-saving irrigation, employing organic mulching, and deficit irrigation [6,26].

Among these, deficit irrigation offers more water savings that become popular in arid and semi-arid regions [27] and this irrigation practice provides a means of reducing water consumption while minimizing adversarial effects on yield [28]. The basic information needed to apply this approach is the response of crops to water deficit [29] and investigations show that this practice deliberately supplies water to the crop less than the optimal requirement either part [30] or throughout the cropping season [23,31,32], for different crops like maize [30,33], and onion [22,23,32]. Further, deficit irrigation is an indispensable practice where essential resources such as capital, energy, and/or labour are limited factors for crop cultivation [6]. Ref. [29] verified the importance of deficit irrigation, 50 % of the full irrigation supply reasons a yield bargain of only 10–15 %, and water productivity increased by 33 % on the wheat crop. Moreover, with reduced irrigation up to half of the optimal crop water use under conventional furrow irrigation, the yield reduction was subjected to saved water [33].

The impact of deficit irrigation on yield reduction can be evaluated by correlating relative water deficit with relative yield reduction or nominated by crop yield response factor (ky) [34]. The application of yield response factor (ky) allows water supply quantification for irrigation planning, design, and operation [34] and is a viable tool in irrigation design and scheduling [35]. Moreover, deficit irrigation can be examined using economic analysis by associating the involved production and opportunity costs [36]. Financial analysis offers valuable insights to determine the relative monetary gains or economic advantage under well and deficit conditions [6]. It provides constructive information to make irrigation management decisions and to search out multiple alternatives [28] and the economic return to irrigation often involves more than the value of crop yield [36].

Onion (Allium Cepa L.) is the second most commonly produced and consumed vegetable worldwide due to its economic, nutritional, and medicinal benefits [37]. It is widely produced by small farmers and commercial cultivators throughout the year for local use and export markets [22] and more interestingly, onion is one of the most significant and commonly used as a constituent in Ethiopian day-to-day recipes [16]. The crop is considered sensitive to water stress [38] but, can be grown in different irrigation management conditions in the semi-arid region of Ethiopia [16,22,31,39]. However, the water productivity for onion production is highly faced by rapidly declining water resources due to climate change, competition for water among different users, and more importantly experience of inefficient management systems. Nevertheless, extended experimental investigations for the improvement of irrigation water management through formulating reasonable irrigation strategies in semi-arid areas of Ethiopia are rarely explored, and vanished and the issue remains. From these general thoughts, the present study quested to evaluate the combined effect of conventional, alternate, and fixed furrow irrigation systems with irrigation regimes of 100 %,70 %, and 40%ETc on onion production and water productivity.

Materials and methods

Description of experimental area and climate

The field tryouts were carried out in 2020/21 and 2021/22 on onion crops at Alage Agricultural Technical and Vocational Educational and Training College in the Central Ethiopia, Kembata Zone, Alage, Ethiopia. As described in Fig. 1, geographically, the area was located at about 7°65' North latitude and 38°56' East longitude with an average elevation of 1600 m above mean sea level. The area was situated at about a distance of 217 km from the south of the country's capital city Addis Ababa and distant by 211 km from the regional capital city of Hosaena.

Location map of the study area.

The area is found in the dry plateau agro-ecology zone of the southern part of the Ethiopian Central Rift Valley which is a semi-arid region characterized by a bimodal rainfall pattern from season April and May and September to November. The mean annual rainfall of the experimental area is below 750 mm with annual mean minimum and maximum temperatures are 16 and 32 °C, respectively. The historical weather data painted that there was a longer duration of insignificant rainfall distribution spanning from the start of October to the end of February thus, irrigation is necessary for onion production and had insignificant effects, particularly on deficit irrigation treatments, and generally less inhibited deficit irrigation practice.

Soil properties analysis

Soil physical properties which are the main input for the experiment were collected and examined in the two trial periods before and at the time of test. For these, three composite soil samples were collected from different plots diagonally using a cylindrical core sampler up to 60 cm with 20 cm vertical intervals of 0–20 cm, 20–40 cm, and 40–60 cm.

The soil bulk density (ρb) was determined by taking undisturbed soil samples using core samples from the given interval and oven drying for 24 h at a temperature of 105 °C and then computed by dividing dry soil mass by the volume of the core sampler. For soil texture, the hydrometric method was employed to quantify the amount of soil units in a given soil mass and after that, the final parting was conducted by USDA textural triangle.

The infiltration rate of the soil was measured using a double-ring infiltrometer at the in-situ level [40]. During the test, the ground surface was leveled, and internal and external rings were driven into the soil up to a depth of 15 cm by using a hammer. The water filling was done on both external and internal rings up to the same water level. The time was recorded using a stopwatch. The drop in water level in the inner ring was measured at every interval of the time. The readings continued until the water level drop became almost constant. In each filling, the water level of the outside ring was kept the same as the water level of the inner ring. Reading was taken at shorter time intervals in the beginning and longer time intervals later.

The field capacity (FC) and permanent wilting point (PWP) were determined using a pressure plate apparatus and then the total available water (TAW) was calculated by deducting permanent wilting from the field capacity of the test soil. The gravimetric method corrected time domain reflector meter (TDR) was used to routinely monitor soil moisture content before and after irrigation. During the correlation of TDR readings with the gravimetric measurement, soil moisture content measured using the gravimetric method was converted to the volumetric moisture content in each equivalent root zone using Equations 1, 2) [6] whereas, no need for conversion of the TDR readings because it directly provides volumetric soil moisture content.

For soil chemical properties analysis, the soil pH was determined from a soil solution of 1:2.5 ratios (soil to water) with a pH meter, and the electrical conductivity (EC) of the soil was determined using an electrical conductivity meter.

The experiments were carried out with a two-factorial combination of conventional furrow irrigation (CFI), alternate furrow irrigation (AFI), and fixed furrow irrigation (FFI) with three irrigation levels of 100 %, 70 %, and 40%ETc. The design of the experimental plot was arranged in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications having nine treatments in each three replications. There was a buffer zone of 1 and 1.5 m between the plot and replications respectively (Fig. 2). The size of individual plots was 12.4 m × 3 m with 27 total plots then the total experimental area size was 1641.60 m2 (43.2 m × 38 m).

Onion (Allium cepa L.) Bombay red seeds variety was selected as a trial onion crop variety because it was cultivated by local farmers and different studies portrayed that this onion variety stands against water stress in semi-arid regions of Ethiopia, yielding optimum yield production with improved water productivity [16,22,39]. A seed rate of 5 kg ha−1 was sowed on six raising beds of 2 by 8 m size, mulched with grass for 8 days up to fully raised. After 50 days, transplanting of the normal seedlings was carried out in 2020/21 on November 15, 2020/21 and on November 07, 2021/22 on a plot with 10 and 60 cm plants and furrow spacing of 40 plants per planting ridge. According to Alage agronomic practice and onion nature, fertilizer was managed at rates of 200 kg ha−1 and 100 kg ha−1 of DAP (di-ammonium phosphate), and urea respectively. DAP was only applied at the time of sowing, while urea was applied twice during the vegetative and blooming periods. From the total five planting ridges in each plot, the central two ridges of ten plants were considered for data collection. All agronomic management cultures were common, the same, and similar to the local culture for all experimental treatments in two experimental seasons except for depth of application variations.

Crop evapotranspiration, irrigation scheduling, and irrigation water management

Daily meteorological data of maximum and minimum temperature, rainfall, wind speed, solar radiation, and relative humidity were collected from a weather station of Alage Vocational and Training College situated less than 500 m from the experimental plot. The daily reference evapotranspiration (ETo) was determined using the Penman-Monteith method. In the study area crop coefficient (kc) of onion has not yet been studied as a result, crop coefficients of 0.7, 1.05, and 0.75 at the initial, mid, and late onion growth stages respectively [26]. The intermediate values (Kc) between the end of the initial up to the start of mid and between the end of mid and up to the start of late crop growth stages were interpolated using Penman-Monteith [26].

The net irrigation requirement for onion crops was estimated by deducting effective rainfall from computed crop evapotranspiration however, in the experimental seasons the dropped rainfall was almost nil making the net irrigation requirement equal to crop evapotranspiration. When, how much, and how to apply irrigation water to the irrigation field was taken into account during the irrigation schedule, irrigation watering was initiated for all treatments whenever the soil moisture in the control treatment within the root depth reached a management-allowed deficit of 30 % at initial,45 % at mid and 50 % at late onion growth stage. Besides, although irrigation took place on the same day to all treatments as fully watered plots, the length of irrigation applications was less by 0.4 and 0.7 than the control treatment, and the number of irrigated furrows was reduced by half for alternative and fixed furrows. The adopted root depth of the onion was 25 and 60 cm for the initial and mid-growth stages respectively. Thus, 542.88 and 553.99 mm depth of irrigation water was applied for 1st and 2nd experimental seasons respectively, under fixed intervals (every six days) and variable application depth (fill up to field capacity). This irrigation scheduling was developed by considering experimental soil type, crop variety, previous experiences, and local cultural practice [16,32].

Furrow application efficiency (Ea) was estimated to account for irrigation water losses during application in the furrows, evaporation, or distribution and was assessed before the start of the experiment in 2020/21. During Ea estimation soil moisture was taken before water application and after 24 h known volume of water from three selected closed-ended furrows at the head, mid, and tail of the furrows up to 60 cm. The difference in soil moisture content before and after irrigation in the crop root zone was used for the calculation of the amount of water stored in the crop root zone. Finally, the estimated application efficiency value was 83 % obtained by dividing volumes of water stored in the crop root zone (m3) by applied water to the field (m3). Ultimately, the gross depth of applied water for control and/or all treatments was computed as the ratio of the net irrigation requirement to the application efficiency. However, the computed application value of the present study was in disagreement with the furrow irrigation system application efficiency varies from 45 to 60 % stages [26]. This might be the fact that furrows were short in length, close-ended, and water application was in a more controlled way. However, the present study was limited to a small experimental plot, thus, for more understanding of the application depth, flow rate, and infiltration characteristics the furrow size should be maximized.

After computing crop evapotranspiration and adjusting irrigation scheduling, then the coming activity was irrigation water application adjustment and management. Surface water source from the Gido River was brought upstream of the field under gravity through the earthen canal and introduced in each irrigation plot by measuring using a calibrated siphon tube. The discharge out of the siphon tube was 1.5 L s−1, from the adjusted hydraulic head of 10 cm and siphon tube size of 1.5 cm in diameter. Flow available from the siphon tube was less than the maximum non-erosive discharge of 2.7 L s−1 determined using a 0.22 % average slope of the experimental plot along the furrow for clay-to-clay loam soil (0.05–0.25).

Performance evaluation of furrow irrigation systems under different field data

Onion agronomy, yield parameters, water productivity, yield response, and economic return

The measured agronomic data were plant height, leaf length, and leaf number and the yield components were bulb diameter, marketable, unmarketable, and total bulb yield. Plant height was measured from the ground surface to the peak of the leaf using a graduated ruler. Leaf number was manually counted from selected plants and leaf length was measured from uninstructive leaf of the plant steam to the peak of the leaf. The marketable yield was a yield of non-diseased, which is not under-sized free from physiological disordered, shaped, and healthy onion (bulb diameter >3 cm). The unmarketable yield was the yield of diseased, decayed, and under-sized onion (bulb diameter < 3 cm), and the total bulb yield was the sum of marketable and unmarketable yields [41] and the bulb diameter was measured using a Varner caliper. The agronomic data of plant height, leaf length, and leaf number were measured and collected in fifteen days intervals throughout the plant growth stages and yield components were collected at the time of crop harvest.

The other performance indicator was water productivity, expressed as the ratio of crop yield to water used or water applied and expressed as units of kg m−3. Economic analysis for different treatments was also an integral part of the study because provides valuable information to make ultimate irrigation management judgments and to explore desirable alternatives. During analysis, the input costs included the cost of expenses for farm equipment, irrigation water, labour, fertilizer, insecticide, and onion seed and the output was onion yield and saved water. Then the net return from each treatment was computed by deducting total cost (TC) from gross return (GR) and the benefit-cost ratio, (BCR) was calculated with the ratio of gross return (GR) to total cost (TC) [43]. The prevailing market price of onion in the local market at the time of crop harvest in 2020/21 and 2021/22 was 27 and 30 Ethiopian Birr (ETB) kg−1 and the irrigation water price was taken as 3 ETB per 1000 m3 for Awash River [11].

Statistical data analysis

The statistical data analysis for different treatments was analyzed using the R 4.4.0 programming software [44]. The mean separation between treatments was carried out using Duncan’s multiple range test at a 5 % significance level.

Results and discussions

Physiochemical characterization of the experimental soil

As presented in Table 2, soil physical properties show a noticeable variation down to vertically, this might be attached to increasing overburden and/or compaction from overlying. The average soil bulk density was 1.20 gm cm−3 and the average textural class of the experimental soil was clay. The value bulk density was increased as the soil depth increased from the ground surface this indicates that the soil compaction increased with the soil depth increased. This clearly shows the water-holding capacity was better for the bottom layer and that the deep percolation was minimized. The value of bulk density was found between 0.7 and 1.9 g cm−3 [45] for normal soil for good plant root development, aeration, water movement within the soil, and ease of uptake of nutrients by plants. The average soil moisture contents at field capacity and permanent wilting point were 35.44 and 20.61 %, with 14.83 % of the total available water that was ranged for clay soil from 14 to 18 % [40].

These values were below the threshold value of soil EC for onion yield reduction, i.e. 1.7 dsm−1. The decline in yield of onion crops starts at a threshold value of EC of 1.7 ds m−1 [46]. Effects of furrow irrigation systems and irrigation regimes on onion agronomy, yield parameters, water productivity, yield response, and economic return

Effects on growth parameters

The pooled values of plant height, leaf length, and leaf number for different treatments in two experimental seasons are presented in Fig. 3. In the outlook of furrow irrigation systems, onion agronomic parameters were improved by conventional furrow followed by alternative and fixed furrow irrigation systems and corresponding irrigation regimes, onion growth parameters were enhanced by 100%ETc followed by 70%ETc and 40%ETc. Moreover, the plant height at the late stage was improved by 31.71 % and 16.10 % at conventional furrow irrigation compared with fixed and alternative furrow irrigation systems respectively. Irrigation level of 100%ETc improved plant height at the late stage by 15.68 and 18.52 % as compared with 70 % and 40%ETc respectively. For analysis of leaf number and length, the values were taken from the late growth stage, as a result, the maximum values were observed at conventional furrow irrigation with 100%ETc and the minimum was recorded at fixed furrow irrigation with 40%ETc. This might be credited to; the under conventional furrow irrigation, all crop rows get water in each irrigation event creating a favorable environment for plants. But, under fixed furrow irrigation half of the plant rows get water throughout the plant growth season but half parts are in a state of dryness during the whole growth season which may cause the existence of an adverse environment for plants. The values in alternative furrow irrigation were intermediate, this might be attributed to turn-by-turn irrigation making a favorable environment for the plant relative to fixed furrow irrigation. Meanwhile, the result revealed significant difference in plant height in different irrigation treatments was noticeable at the plant development and mid-growth stages, this might be attributed to plants being more sensitive to water stress as compared to initial and late growth stages.

The maximum plant height of 43.74 cm was recorded by conventional furrow irrigation followed by 43.32 cm in alternative and 42.84 cm by fixed furrow irrigation which was in agreement with [31]. The result also proves the findings of [32] that an increased level of irrigation in turn increases the height of the onion crop ultimately increasing yield. Refs. [18,39] described that water stress lowers transpiration, photosynthesis, biomass accumulation, and growth rates ultimately decreasing bulb yield and attributing to more unmarketable yield production.

As described in Table 4, the onion agronomic parameters had a significant impact at all irrigation levels and furrow irrigation systems at (P < 0.05). Moreover, the statistical values revealed that the plant height at the initial stage for more than 40 % of the treatments achieved statistically insignificant values, which might be attributed that, initially all plots were fully saturated to enhance good onion establishment and the other contributing reason is the less sensitive nature of onion to water stress at this particular stage. Besides, at the time of transplanting both deficit and optimal plots were optimally irrigated finally, compensated deficit treatments and contributed to statistical insignificance. Afterward, in the rest of the growth stages similarity rarely existed, which might be attributed to the combined effect of crop sensitivity, large deficit gap and furrow factors made statistical significance. In line with this, different investigations revealed that onion crops are sensitive to water deficit at development and mid-growth stages [1,18,39] causing statistical significance for most of the treatments in the present study. The combined effects of treatment, except for unmarketable yield other yield parameters were better in conventional furrow followed by alternative and fixed furrow irrigation systems (Fig. 4). Besides, the unmarketable yield was increased by 33.78 % as the irrigation levels decreased from 100 % to 40%ETc and the marketable yield improved by 68.96 % at conventional furrow compared with fixed furrow and by 37.47 % as compared with alternative furrow. Eventually, the total bulb yield was enhanced by 30.72 % as compared with the alternative furrow and by 53.23 % with that of the fixed furrow. Moreover, conventional furrow irrigation with 100%ETc reduced unmarketable by 55.64 % and improved marketable yield by 96.82 % as compared with fixed furrow irrigation with 40%ETc. Likewise, on a pooled basis, conventional furrow irrigation improved bulb diameter by 9.21 % relative to alternative furrow and by 47.47 % relative to fixed furrow irrigation. Conventional furrow irrigation with 100%ETc improved the bulb diameter by 30.33 % as compared to the fixed furrow with 40%ETc. The stated results might clarify that under less stressed treatments and continued watering in conventional furrow irrigation, the marketable yield was more overhead than the unmarketable yield. Contrariwise, the minimum values obtained at fixed furrow irrigation are approved that half of the watercourse furrows are permanently dry throughout the crop growth periods leading to less wetted area directed to inhibition of plant physiological activity and biomass accumulation finally heading for to the increases of undersized or unmarketable yield production and worsen total bulb yield. However, treatments that achieved intermediate yield parameters might be due to the comparable contribution of furrow irrigation systems and irrigation levels.

Effects on water productivity indicators

In light of escalating furrow irrigation systems, the maximum crop and irrigation water productivity values of 10.66 &10.65 kg m−3 and 8.84 & 8.81 kg m−3 were recorded at alternative furrow irrigation in two trial seasons respectively. The intermediate values 8.23 & 8.44 kg m−3 and 6.78 & 7.00 kg m−3 were attained at conventional furrow irrigation system and the minimum values of 7.23 & 7.05 kg m−3 and 6.08 &5.85 kg m−3 were achieved at fixed furrow irrigation system (Fig. 5). Thus, alternative furrow irrigation systems improved water productivity by 21.84 % more than conventional and by 32.72 % more than fixed furrows. This might be the reason that at the alternative furrow irrigation system, the yield production was optimal from optimal water application, at conventional furrow irrigation the yield production was not comparable with water supplied and at fixed furrow irrigation the predominate yield reduction sheds light on water productivity enhancement.

Effect of furrow irrigation systems and irrigation levels on water productivity indicators (CWP = crop water productivity, and IWP = irrigation water productivity).

Contend to the common trend, water productivity consistently increased with deficit level boosted, but in the present study, these values were varied periodically. On an average basis under alternative furrow irrigation with 100%ETc, the maximum crop and irrigation water productivity values of 10.96 & 9.09 kg m−3 were observed. The exacerbated yield gaps caused the minimum crop and irrigation water productivity values of 6.22 & 5.61 kg m−3 under fixed furrow irrigation with 40%ETc, which confirmed that yield reduction reduced water productivity even if the supply level was reduced by 80 % (Table 9). Ref. [48] also investigated that a large yield penalty value of 5.9 tons ha−1 from a minimum depth of application was ultimately caused by a minimum water productivity value of 1.35 kg m−3 for maize crops. Ref. [49] stated that crop and irrigation water productivity values increased as soil moisture deficit levels increased. Ref. [50] reported that more stressed maize crops are directed to the minimum yield production ultimately water productivity becomes minimal. Ref. [51] claimed that to improve water productivity for enhanced water utilization under water-stressed situations irrigation depth should be kept at 50 to 70%ETc. The present result also revealed that not only deficit levels but also furrow irrigation systems equally contributed to water productivity improvement or decline. However, achieving the two opposing conditions of yield and water productivity increment at the same time is difficult so, it should be concerned about optimal yield reduction and maximum water productivity. Besides, crop water productivity was greater than irrigation water productivity in two growing seasons, the reason might be that the crop evapotranspiration was less than the depth of water applied and all water requirement was nourished only by irrigation, there was no other contributing source in the two experimental seasons. Furthermore, the values of crop and irrigation water productivity were varied across two trial seasons, this might have occurred due to the variation of climate that caused to depth of water application variation, and yield output. Additionally, onion agronomy, yield parameters and water productivity indicators showed distinct variation among treatments. This was allowed for easily identification of the contribution of irrigation levels and application systems to analysis parameters. However, if the deficit level gap in between treatments is small, like by10 %,20 % and 25 % makes biases for analysis and optimal application system and irrigation level selection. Thus, it is better to increased deficit level gaps for better result.

Effects on yield response factor (ky)

The relative evapotranspiration deficit with the corresponding relative yield reduction treatment-wise is presented in Table 7. The observed yield response factor (ky) values varied from 0.55 to 1.11 and 0.57 to 1.10 in the first and second growing seasons respectively and the value was increased as the deficit level increased and yield decreased. Thus, the maximum ky values of 1.11 and 1.10 were observed under fixed furrow irrigation with 40%ETc due to intolerable yield reduction and the minimum ky values of 0.55 and 0.57 were observed under alternative furrow irrigation with 100%ETc due to optimal yield reduction. Moreover, the trend of the result exhibited that the relative yield reduction was increased as the relative evapotranspiration deficit increased corresponding to all furrow irrigation systems. However, with the same amount of water deficit, the yield response factor (ky) was higher for fixed furrow irrigation as compared with alternative furrow irrigation. This points out that at the same irrigation regimes, furrow irrigation systems evocatively affected the onion yield response factor. As the ky represented the response of the crop to water deficit, in the alternative furrow irrigation system, the application of deficit irrigation was advisable because yield reduction was optimal for water savings. However, in the case of fixed furrow irrigation, deficit irrigation was impractical because yield reduction was significant relative to water savings and in conventional furrow irrigation the yield increment was less than water savings. Thus, the yield response values revealed that in water-limited areas, alternative furrow irrigation with 100%ETc can be desirable to other treatments.

Effects on economic parameters

Virtually, a weighty variation of production cost was not observed among different treatments in two trial seasons which trends linearly (Fig. 7). The reason is that the use of labour forces (excluding irrigation water), farm equipment, fertilizer, insecticide, and onion seed costs almost the same as those irrigated treatments. Unlikely, as the opportunity cost in different treatments varied, the pattern of the net return function did not behave like that of the cost function. The highest net return and benefit-cost ratio values of 668484 ETB ha−1 and 3.63 were observed in treatments received 100%ETc under conventional furrow and followed by alternative furrow with 100%ETc.This means that the gain from higher yield was greater than the associated production cost. Treatments revived 40%ETc under alternative and fixed furrow irrigation systems showed minimum and negative net balance respectively, implying that the cost of production is higher than the gross return and as the gross return is directly associated with the yield and saved water, the negative net return is the reason for such low yield. Except for the cost curve, other economic parameter curves show relative variations, implying that the combined effect of furrow systems and irrigation regimes varied in the response yield.

Relationship of economic analysis of onion production and different furrow irrigation systems (TC = total cost, GR = gross return, NR = net return, and BCR = benefit-cost ratio).

Onion production is often constrained by a shortage of irrigation water. In such water-limiting conditions, the saved water through deficit irrigation of one piece of land might be used to irrigate additional land, thus increasing farm income. As a result, the total onion production in the alternative furrow with 100%ETc increased because the yield from the increased 0.03ha land under production will be more than counterbalanced for the reduced yield from half deficit and this treatment is more preferred for such conditions. Ref. [28] also confirmed that reduced yield and monetary gains under deficit irrigation especially in water-limiting situations may be compensated by increased production from the additional irrigated area with the water saved by deficit irrigation. Besides, in line with the present study, Refs. [28,33,57] stated that the net return was maximum from maximum yield while, treatments under more water stress showed negative net balance, implying that the cost of production is higher than the gross return and the saved water cannot counterbalance the monetary losses.

The fusion impact of furrow irrigation systems and irrigation levels on economic parameters was significant at (P < 0.05). The statistical analysis revealed that treatments under alternative and fixed furrow irrigation systems with the corresponding irrigation levels do not show a significant difference in total cost, due to the same depth of water application, however, significant variation was observed in gross return, net return, and benefit-cost ratio, this might be due to variation of yield produced (Table 8). Conventional furrow irrigation is also statistically superior as compared with alternative and fixed furrow irrigation in different economic parameters. Thus, the finding showed that irrigation capacity and profitability are the most important decision-making components in irrigated agriculture [6,58]. Although yield per hectare is reduced due to a 50 % deficit at alternative furrow compared to full irrigation, the reduction in irrigation cost and the opportunity cost of water more than compensates for the lower yield. Consequently, the result of the present study can provide a solid basis and clues for making irrigation management decisions, screening, and the development of scientific irrigation strategies.

Comparison of water saved with yield reduction

The effects of two factors on onion growth, yield, and water productivity exclusively had nothing to do with pinout fitting combinations rather the other screening was conducted by associating potential water saved and yield reduction (Table 9). At the time of relating water saved with yield reduction, the value of yield obtained from optimal irrigation level was the basis for the comparison of other treatments and the values are the average of two seasons. The pooled result showed that relative minimum yield reduction was observed under alternative furrow irrigation with 100%ETc correspondingly 50 % water savings leading to 0.03ha further irrigable area and 12464.89 kg ha−1 additional yield, ultimately causing 37394.89 kg ha−1 total yield productions. On the other hand, treatments received 40%ETc under alternative and fixed furrow irrigation systems saved 80 % water for a further 0.06ha irrigable area, but the percentage intolerable yield reduction was impractical as that of saved water. The results also clearly show that the water saved and yield reduction was relatively minimal in conventional furrow irrigation followed by alternative and fixed furrow irrigation systems. Relating the extent of water saved and yield reduction is important for screening potential combinations of application systems and irrigation regimes [1,31,33,39]. Thus, it can be decided that treatments received 100%ETc under alternative furrow irrigation was an optimal unity that had ideal actual yield production with 50 % water saving and maximum water productivity.

Conclusions and recommendations

Increasing demands for water use team up with poor management in irrigated agriculture menacing the paucity of water in semiarid regions of Ethiopia. Hence, enhancement of water productivity through thoroughly management is vital for honest water allocation and maintaining water use sustainability. This study underscores the influence of different furrow irrigation systems incorporated with dissimilar irrigation regimes on onion agronomy, yield, water productivity, and economic return. Except for bulb diameter, the mutual interference of furrow irrigation systems and irrigation levels on onion agronomy, yield parameters, water productivity, and economic return was meaningfully impacted at (p < 0.05). Accordingly, excluding unmarketable yield, the agronomic, yield parameters and economic benefit were improved as the level of deficit decreased. The water productivity indicators were better as the level of irrigation dwindled apart from treatments that were exposed to intolerable yield tradeoffs. Regardless of unmarketable yield, other reliant parameters were augmented in conventional furrow irrigation and later alternative and fixed furrow irrigation systems. Moreover, the extreme values of onion agronomy, yield parameters, and economic return with intermediate water productivity values were recorded at conventional furrow irrigation with 100%ETc. The minimum values of onion agronomy, yield production, and water productivity were observed under fixed furrow irrigation with 40%ETc. The optimal vegetative growth and yield parameters with maximum water productivity values were noticed under alternative furrow irrigation with 100%ETc. The study confirmed that the highest net return was observed under conventional furrow with 100%ETc after that alternative furrow with 100%ETc and the negative net return was obtained under fixed furrow irrigation with 40%ETc. However, in water-limiting conditions, treatments received 100%ETc at alternative furrow irrigation can counterbalance the net return reduction from 50 % saved water. Besides, onion agronomic, yield parameters and water productivity indicators confirmed that alternative furrow irrigation with 100%ETc can be apt and upholds to attain optimum crop production with improved water productivity in semiarid regions of Ethiopia and areas whose agroclimatic situations are analogous. However, the experiment was carried out under a specific agroclimatic and irrigation management condition in a small experimental plot hence, further investigations are required in different agro-climatic regions under a large experimental area.
