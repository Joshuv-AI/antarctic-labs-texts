Altered functional brain network in adolescents with non-suicidal self-injury: a resting-state EEG study
Authors: Liqin Zheng, Na Du, Shusheng Bao, Yufeng Deng, Zedong Wang, Linqi Chen, Zhanjiang Sun, Chunya Li, Ya’nan Li, Yu Xiao, Jia Chen, Yunge Li, Kai Chen, Tao Zhang, Hesong Wang, Tao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) has a huge impact on the physical and mental health of adolescents, and the changes in the brain network functional connectivity pattern of NSSI are still unclear. Electroencephalogram (EEG) has been widely used in the study of brain function in mental disorders due to its high temporal resolution. Thus, it is of great significance to investigate the brain network functional connectivity (FC) and network characteristics changes in NSSI adolescents. Fifty NSSI adolescents with depressive episodes and 25 healthy controls (HCs) were assessed using eye-open resting state EEG signals and clinical scales. Phase locking value (PLV), weighted Phase Lag Index (wPLI), and graph theory analysis and machine learning analysis were combined to explore the brain functional connectivity and topological properties of adolescents with NSSI. Compared with HCs, adolescents with NSSI revealed significantly decreased connectivity in the alpha band based on the wPLI/PLV-derived network, and significantly increased connectivity and network efficiency in the theta bands in PLV-derived brain network. Reduced characteristic path length and enhanced clustering coefficient/global efficiency/local efficiency were found in the delta/theta band for PLV-derived network, which related to the clinical scale score (i.e. Toronto Alexithymia Scale). The best classification performance was achieved using comprehensive features (combined PLV- and wPLI-derived) and logistic regression classifier. Overall, these findings present novel evidence to understand NSSI neuropathological mechanisms and provide potential targets for NSSI treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08019-8.

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) constitutes the deliberate, direct destruction of body tissue without suicidal intent, frequently employed by individuals to alleviate distressing emotions and attain sensory relief [1, 2]. This behavior exhibits high concealment, with affected individuals rarely seeking treatment voluntarily. Without timely clinical intervention, the transient emotional regulation benefits of NSSI may inadvertently reinforce behavioral escalation toward increasingly severe self-injurious acts. In extreme manifestations, this progression may culminate in suicidal ideation or behaviors, posing significant life-threatening risks [3, 4]. The early definition of NSSI was not clear, and it has been considered as one of the typical symptoms of borderline personality disorder (BPD) [5, 6]. However, expanding clinical observations and epidemiological investigations across diverse populations have demonstrated that NSSI prevalence substantially exceeds that of BPD. Notably, most hospitalized NSSI patients do not satisfy personality disorder diagnostic criteria [7]. In the latest Diagnostic classification, NSSI attained recognition as a distinct psychiatric condition within the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [8]. Contemporary clinical studies in domestic settings further document progressively rising NSSI incidence rates among adolescent and young adult populations [9, 10]. Thus, NSSI is now acknowledged as an emerging important public health problem.

Multiple risk factors contribute to NSSI, with family and social psychological factors being particularly significant. For example, Wei and his colleagues indicated that abnormal peer relationships may be linked to NSSI through depressive symptoms [9]. Wu et al. also came to a similar conclusion and found that the bullying experience of adolescent patients was associated with more severe NSSI behaviors through the indirect pathway of depressive symptoms [10]. Meanwhile, patient age and gender represent potential influences, younger females often exhibit heightened symptom severity, and the lifetime prevalence rate may be underestimated in previous understanding [11, 12]. Several investigations have sought to identify unique metabolic and neural characteristics in NSSI patients. Wang et al. showed that compared with healthy controls (HCs), the methylation level of CpG5 locus of SIRT1 gene in NSSI patients was significantly increased, and the plasma SIRT1 protein concentration was significantly decreased, suggesting that the methylation level and mRNA expression of this gene may be used as an in vivo marker for adolescents with NSSI [13]. Auerbach et al. argued that although some studies had reported the volume atrophy of the anterior cingulate gyrus and the weakened activation of the striatum associated with NSSI, remained lacking of clear and recognized conclusions on the neural markers of adolescents with NSSI [14]. Consequently, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying adolescent non-suicidal self-injury require further elucidation to advance effective treatments.

The collective firing activity of neurons in the human brain can be recorded by head surface electrodes, namely Electroencephalography (EEG), which is widely used to evaluate various neurological impairments and explore the key biomarkers of mental diseases with the advantage of high temporal resolution [15]. For instance, Feldmann et al. measured frontal cortical activity asymmetry in adolescent depression subtypes, revealing that adolescents with acute depression and comorbid anxiety disorder exhibited greater left-sided than right-sided EEG alpha power compared to HCs, which indicated heightened right-frontal cortical activity relative to left-frontal regions [16]. Research utilizing neurophysiological measures reveals distinctive neural patterns associated with NSSI. Tsypes and colleagues employed feedback negativity (FN) event-related potentials in children aged 7–11 years. Children with NSSI histories demonstrated significantly more negative ΔFN (defined as FN response to loss minus FN response to gain) than non-NSSI peers, which was related to the increase in impulsiveness [17]. Complementary evidence indicated that under negative emotional stimuli, depressed adolescents with NSSI exhibit enhanced P300 amplitudes compared to both without NSSI depressed individuals and HCs. Concurrent increases in delta/theta event-related synchronization (ERS) and theta/alpha desynchronization (ERD) further indicated impaired response inhibition—a potential biomarker of NSSI vulnerability [18]. Notably, hemispheric asymmetry patterns differentiated NSSI subgroups: those with suicide attempts display left-hemisphere spectral power dominance, whereas non-attempters show right-hemispheric predominance. This neural divergence positioned EEG as a promising tool for quantifying neurofunctional impairment and stratifying suicide risk among NSSI adolescents [19].

The human brain constitutes a structurally and functionally complex network system, a functionally coordinated brain network enables efficient segregation and integration of information processing [20]. Graph theory-based network modeling and topological analysis provide powerful frameworks for elucidating brain organization principles [21]. Recent neuroimaging studies further reveal distinct alterations in neural circuitry among NSSI patients relative to controls, particularly within affective processing circuits. Compared with healthy adolescents, those with NSSI history exhibited diminished FC between the amygdala and medial prefrontal cortex. Meanwhile, enhanced negative coupling between amygdala and prefrontal regions correlated with greater reductions in NSSI behaviors following treatment [22]. Linear regression models revealed that greater NSSI severity correlated with smaller bilateral putamen volume. The putamen’s involvement in NSSI was reflected in weaker connectivity between the left putamen and bilateral temporoparietal junction, and between the right putamen and left temporoparietal junction. Furthermore, heightened psychopathological symptoms correlated with more severe NSSI among those exhibiting reduced putamen volume or weaker connectivity [23]. Compared to without NSSI depressed individuals and HCs, depressed adolescents with NSSI displayed increased connectivity between the central executive network (CEN) and default mode network (DMN), and decreased coherence in the anterior DMN and insular salience networks [24]. Nevertheless, consistent patterns of brain network connectivity alterations in NSSI adolescents remain undefined.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate abnormalities in brain functional networks by analyzing resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) data from adolescents with NSSI and co-occurring depressive episodes and from healthy controls (HCs). Based on the view that the neural mechanisms of NSSI involve both emotional and cognitive circuits, we propose the following hypotheses: (1) Adolescents with NSSI may show abnormalities in functional connectivity synchronization and network efficiency within specific frequency bands, such as theta and alpha, particularly over frontal-parietal related scalp regions; (2) These network features will be associated with specific clinical symptoms and can effectively distinguish individuals, with functional connectivity edges likely contributing the most. To achieve this, the study uses both the Phase Locking Value (PLV) and the weighted Phase Lag Index (wPLI) to calculate whole-brain connectivity, distinguishing between different types of neural synchronization. Next, the graph-theoretical properties of the networks are analyzed. Finally, significant connectivity and network features are linked with clinical symptoms, and their ability to classify individuals is evaluated using multiple classification models. The core contribution of this study is that it is the first in NSSI research to integrate the dual metrics of PLV/wPLI, graph theory, and machine learning analysis. This approach aims not only to reveal network abnormalities at the group level but also to preliminarily distinguish neural features with different explanatory power. This provides new evidence for understanding NSSI mechanisms and for identifying objective assessment targets.

The structure of the paper is as follows: the Methods section describes the participants, EEG preprocessing, functional connectivity and graph theory analysis, and the machine learning process. The Results section reports between-group differences, clinical correlations, and classification results. The Discussion section explains the neurobiological meaning of the findings, limitations, and future directions.

Materials and methods

Participants

Per the Declaration of Helsinki, this study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China, China (approved number: 106142022081023952). All participants and their parents provided written informed consent. The non-suicidal self-injury patients were recruited from inpatients in Chengdu Fourth People’s Hospital, China, and were examined and diagnosed by trained psychiatric physicians. NSSI patients were required to meet the following criteria: (1) between 12 and 25 years of age, normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity; (2) meet the diagnostic criteria for non-suicidal self-harm in the DSM-5; (3) patients who have been taking antidepressant medication do not need to discontinue it, but must maintain their dosing frequency and dosage within one month prior to the trial; and (4) no transcranial magnetic stimulation treatment one month prior to the trial.

The HCs were recruited from surrounding communities and to meet the following criteria: (1) between 12 and 25 years of age, normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity; (2) no medical history of neurological or psychiatric disorders; (3) no history of psychotropic medication use; and (4) no severe systemic conditions (e.g., cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction).

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of severe traumatic brain injury or neurodevelopmental disorders; (2) the presence of serious somatic illnesses (e.g., cancer, etc.); (3) presence of metallic material in the body (e.g., cardiac pacemakers, stents, etc.); (4) history of abuse of alcohol/drug.

A detailed description of the participant selection process is as follows: Initially, a total of 123 potentially eligible NSSI patients and 48 HCs were selected. To minimize the impact of medication on EEG signals, 16 individuals were excluded due to the use of psychiatric medications within one month before EEG recorded. Another 29 were excluded for other clinical reasons (e.g., comorbidities other than depression). Additionally, 28 participants were excluded due to poor EEG data quality. Similarly, in the HCs group, 13 were excluded due to age mismatch with the NSSI group, and 10 were excluded for not providing written informed consent. Following this rigorous screening process, the final analytical cohort consisted of 75 participants (50 NSSI patients and 25 HCs). A corresponding flowchart is provided in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S1).

Clinical assessment

The study procedure was performed in a quiet, temperature-controlled, well-ventilated room. Before EEG acquisition, each participant completed the demographic questionnaire including general information such as age, sex, race, height, weight, education, anamnesis, history of smoking, drinking and probiotics, etc. Clinical symptoms were evaluated using the Adolescent Non-suicidal Self-injury Assessment Questionnaire (ANSSIQ) [25], 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) [26], Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA) [27], Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) [28], and Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) [29]. Additionally, Chinese revised version of Barratt Impulsiveness Scale 11th (BIS-11) [30], Chinese revised version of Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (SES) [31], and the Chinese Version of the 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20-C; adapted and validated by Zhu et al. from the original TAS-20) [32–36] were used to assess the severity of impulsiveness, self-esteem, and alexithymia traits in each subject in both groups.

EEG data acquisition

Prior to EEG recording, participants were instructed to sit comfortably on a backrest chair and to ensure they felt no discomfort with the environment, to minimize potential EEG artifacts. EEG signals were recorded using a BrainAmp EEG amplifier (BrainAmp MR plus64, Brain Products, Germany) and BrainVision Recorder 2.0 software (https://www.brainproducts.com/solutions/), through a TMS-compatible flexible cap fitted with 62-channel Ag/AgCl electrodes arranged according to the international 10/20 system. During the recording of EEG signals, the sampling rate was set as 1000 Hz, the reference electrode and the ground electrode were positioned at the AFz and FCz, and the impedances of the electrodes were maintained below 10 kΩ. During EEG acquisition, participants were instructed to keep their eyes open naturally and focusing on a “+” symbol on the screen (Fig S2), refrain from head or body movements to minimize artifacts, and remain relaxed and awake. They were asked to avoid cognitive activities such as thinking, calculating, or recalling. Any limb movements during the recording were also noted for later EEG data quality control. Open-eye resting-state EEG data were recorded for four minutes.

EEG data preprocessing

Figure 1 shows the detailed analysis procedure of resting-state EEG datasets. Specifically, the EEG signals were preprocessed using the EEGLAB toolbox (EEGLAB v2024.2, https://sccn.ucsd.edu/eeglab). The average reference method was used to the signals of 62 channels. The bandpass filters of the data were set to 1–48 Hz, and a notch filter of 48–52 Hz was used to eliminate the 50 Hz power frequency interference. Continuous resting-state data were down-sampled to 500 Hz and segmented into epochs of 2 s [37]. Subsequently, independent component analysis (ICA, total components = 62) was used to remove eye blink and eye movement artifacts, the average number of rejected ICA components was 9.24. Following ICA, artifact rejection was performed through visual inspection and threshold-based control to remove epochs of poor quality. Artifact rejection employed a ± 100 µV threshold per channel per epoch across all datasets. Additionally, in accordance with previous studies, participants with fewer than 50 remaining epochs [38] after this procedure were excluded from further analysis. Finally, the average number of retained epochs were 108.14 ± 15.61 for the NSSI group and 108.08 ± 16.70 for the HCs. To reduce the effect of volume conduction on EEG networks, sparse electrodes, that was, 21 channels that cover the main brain regions were selected (i.e., Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, C3, C4, P3, P4, O1, O2, F7, F8, T7, T8, P7, P8, Fz, Cz, Pz, Fpz, Oz), were used in present study [15].

Functional brain networks

In EEG brain network analysis, typically, EEG electrodes were regarded as network nodes, and the measurements between paired electrodes were regarded as network edges. Thus, in the present study, 21 electrodes were set as nodes, and the functional connections (FCs) of all paired-electrode were set as edges. FC was assessed using the PLV, a widely established metric for quantifying phase synchronization between neural signals. PLV was selected for its high sensitivity in detecting stable phase-locking relationships, which is particularly advantageous for studies with small to medium sample sizes, and it is well-supported in the literature [39, 40]. For each participant, four characteristic indicators (i.e. network properties) were calculated using the brain connectivity toolbox (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/bct/), including characteristic path length (CPL), clustering coefficient (CC), global efficiency (GE), local efficiency (LE) [15, 45–47].

Classification

Functional connectivity (FC) and network properties showing significant differences between self-harming patients and healthy controls were extracted. This included metrics derived from both wPLI and PLV. Four distinct feature sets were then constructed for classification: (1) alpha-band FC from wPLI only; (2) alpha-band FC from wPLI plus its corresponding graph theory metrics; (3) alpha-band FC from wPLI combined with theta- and alpha-band FC from PLV; and (4) all significant FC and graph theory metrics from both wPLI and PLV. For classifiers, support vector machine (SVM with radial basis function (RBF) and linear kernels), random forest and logistic regression were employed. To mitigate classifier performance bias induced by data imbalance between the NSSI and HC groups (HC: n = 25), class weighting was applied during model training. Leave-one-subject-out cross-validation (LOSOCV) method was utilized to measure the classification performance [47, 48]. To be specific, a total of 75 participants in this study were included to train the models. In LOSOCV, 74 participants were set as the training dataset and 1 participant was set as the test dataset. And conducted t-tests again in each fold to re-select features, with a statistical threshold of p < 0.05, to ensure that feature selection was independently conducted for the training set in each CV fold to prevent data leakage. This procedure was iterated 75 times until all participants were tested and the average across all iterations was represented as final results. The model classification performance was evaluated by the accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), and area under the curve (AUC).

Statistical and correlation analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 8 and IBM SPSS 26.0. For demographic information and clinical scales, count data were expressed as the number of cases or frequencies, and compared using the χ2 test; Measurement data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and compared using a two-tailed independent samples t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test. The network-based statistic (NBS) method was used to determined the significant FCs difference. The t-value threshold was set at 3.0 and the permutation test calculations were set to 5000. The significance threshold for the FWER-corrected p-value of the permutation test was set at p < 0.05. The network properties differences in the two groups were assessed by a two-tailed independent samples t-test. Spearman’s correlation analysis was conducted to examine associations between scales and network metrics; False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was employed for multiple comparison correction. Generally, a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics and clinical assessment

Following screening against inclusion/exclusion criteria, 75 adolescents were enrolled, comprising 50 NSSI patients with depressive episodes (14 male) and 25 HCs (12 male) without psychiatric history. With the exception of education (NSSI: 9.24 ± 1.84 years vs. HC: 10.44 ± 2.73 years; p = 0.029), other demographic characteristics showed no significant differences in age (NSSI: 15.06 ± 1.71 years vs. HC: 15.48 ± 2.12 years; p > 0.05), gender distribution, body mass index (BMI), or handedness (p > 0.05 for all). The NSSI group exhibited significantly higher frequencies of self-injurious behaviors compared to controls (p < 0.001). Group differences in resting-state EEG functional network connectivity

Using clean EEG segments from the NSSI and HC groups, we performed whole-brain weighted functional connectivity analysis with two synchronization metrics: PLV and wPLI. Group comparisons were conducted for the full frequency band (1–48 Hz) and for standard sub-bands. All analyses controlled for the potential confounding effects of gender and education level through statistical regression. First, the PLV- and wPLI-derived brain network functional connectivity analysis across the full frequency band revealed no significant differences between the NSSI and HC groups following two-sample t-tests with NBS correction for multiple comparisons (Fig. S3a and S4a).

Subsequent frequency-specific analyses demonstrated distinct patterns between the two groups. Figures 2 and 3 display the group-averaged PLV/wPLI-derived FC matrices for the δ, θ, α, β, and γ frequency bands, alongside the between-group difference matrices (t-statistic values), respectively. Stars denote connections with statistically significant group differences (p < 0.05, NBS-corrected). Notably, for PLV, significant alterations were found in the θ and α bands. In the θ band, seven connections were significantly enhanced (Fp1–O2, F3–P4, F8–O1, F7–O2, F8–O2, F7–Oz, F8–P7). In the α band, one connection was enhanced (P3–P7), while four connections were weakened (P3–P8, P8–O1, Cz–P8, P8–Oz). In the wPLI α band, multiple connections showed significant weakening (Fp2–P3, Fp2–P4, P4–Pz, Fpz–P4, Pz–O2, F7–Pz, Pz–P8, Fpz–Pz, Pz–Oz). Figure 4a and b visually present the spatial topography of the discovered significant PLV- and wPLI-derived FCs on EEG scalp maps, respectively.

Consistent with the topological analysis results, network properties of the full frequency band (1–48 Hz) revealed no statistically significant differences between NSSI and HC groups (Fig. S3b and S4b). After controlling for the effects of gender and education level, significant differences in graph-theoretical metrics were observed between the NSSI group and healthy controls. The specific results are as follows: For PLV-derived networks, in the delta band (Fig. 5a), CPL showed a marginally significant decrease (p = 0.0505), while CC, LE, and GE were significantly increased (all p < 0.05); In the theta band (Fig. 5b), CPL was significantly decreased, and CC, LE, and GE were significantly increased (all p < 0.01); In the beta band (Fig. 5d), CPL was significantly increased, and CC, LE, and GE were significantly decreased (all p < 0.05).

Correlations between resting-state network characteristics and clinical characteristics

Based on previous FC and network properties analysis, significantly altered FCs (i.e. network edges) and network properties were performed Spearman’s correlation analysis with clinical scale scores, with controlling for the effects of gender and education level. The PLV-derived Characteristic Path Length (CPL) in the delta band, which showed a trend toward significance between groups (p = 0.0505), was also included in the correlation analysis with clinical measures due to the comprehensiveness of network metrics. The FDR method was applied to correct for multiple comparisons. As shown in Fig. 7 and Fig. S5, after multiple comparison correction, only the PLV-derived graph-theoretical measures in the delta and theta bands showed significant correlations with the TAS-20-C score. Among them, CPL was negatively correlated with TAS-20-C (delta: r = -0.420, p = 0.031; theta: r = -0.499, p = 0.012), whereas the CC, GE, LE all showed positive correlations with TAS-20-C (delta: r = 0.417, p = 0.031; r = 0.421, p = 0.031; r = 0.417, p = 0.031, respectively; theta: r = 0.473, p = 0.012; r = 0.496, p = 0.012; r = 0.473, p = 0.012, respectively).

Overall classification performance

Functional connectivity and graph-theoretic network properties that differed significantly between groups were extracted, encompassing measures from both wPLI and PLV. Subsequently, four specific feature combinations were established for analysis, progressing from a focused set (e.g., wPLI alpha-band FC alone) to a comprehensive one (all significant wPLI- and PLV-derived features). The predictive power of these feature sets was assessed by building 16 classification models using four different classifiers. Table 3 shows the classification performance. LOSOCV was used to evaluate the performance of the classifiers. Models leveraging the combined feature set from wPLI and PLV demonstrated superior classification performance compared to those based solely on wPLI features, with gains in both accuracy and sensitivity. Notably, a logistic regression model trained on the complete set of significant wPLI and PLV features yielded the best results (AUC = 0.906, ACC = 0.813, SEN = 0.800, SPE = 0.840). Additionally, Fig. 8 illustrates the highest-accuracy ROC curve alongside the importance ranking of the top 10 contributing features. Notably, feature importance analysis revealed that the top 10 most contributory features were exclusively functional connectivity edges, not graph metrics. This indicates that while the overall feature set is a hybrid, the discriminative power is predominantly anchored in the detailed synchronization patterns captured at the edge level. Graph metrics, although less weighted individually, may contribute by summarizing global network topology and enhancing the model’s stability when integrated with edge-level data.

Discussion

Investigating brain network patterns and identifying clinically relevant network biomarkers holds significant value for elucidating the neural mechanisms and neural regulation strategies in NSSI. This study leveraged resting-state EEG data to construct brain functional networks using PLV and wPLI connectivity, combining graph theory and machine learning analysis, the study systematically examined the brain functional connectivity and topological properties of adolescents with NSSI who were experiencing depressive episodes. Compared with HCs, network abnormalities in NSSI patients showed significant specificity in terms of frequency bands and indicators. In the α frequency band, there was a widespread reduction in functional connectivity and a decrease in global efficiency across both PLV and wPLI methods; while in the θ frequency band, only in the PLV indicator, enhanced connections and increased network efficiency were observed, and this over-integration pattern was potential related to the severity of the patient’s mood dysregulation. Then, the machine learning model that integrated multi-frequency bands and multiple indicators could effectively distinguish NSSI at the individual level, and its discriminative power mainly stemmed from specific long-range functional connection patterns such as those in the frontal-parietal/occipital regions. The association between network characteristics and clinical symptoms is highly specific, and only alexithymia was associated with low-frequency network properties with a robust correlation. These findings depict a multi-level, feature-separated picture of brain network abnormalities, providing new evidence of band- and computational-level specificity for understanding the complex neural mechanism of NSSI.

EEG signals have a high temporal resolution on the order of milliseconds and enable to capture of fast neural oscillations in the brain [42]. Research suggested that the non-linear properties of EEG signals are crucial for understanding brain functional states, and broadband phase analysis holds the potential to capture such cross-frequency interactions [49]. In this study, investigating the signal characteristics across the 1–48 Hz wider frequency domain, to analyze the overall characteristics of brain activity and to explore whether NSSI involves complex, non-linear interactions across multiple sub-bands. In the current study, we first observed the FC patterns of NSSI adolescents and HCs in the full frequency band. There was no significant difference between the two groups, indicating the frequency-specific [50, 51] alterations of the NSSI group in the brain network pattern.

Subsequently, our analysis within the five sub-bands corroborated these findings, revealing that NSSI-related alterations in brain networks exhibit distinct frequency specificity, which in itself carries significant neurobiological implications. The most robust findings were concentrated in the alpha band. Alpha oscillations represent the dominant rhythm in the adult brain during resting state and are believed to play a central role in sensory gating, the maintenance of endogenous attention, and the functional coordination of large-scale cortical networks [52, 53]. This study found that, regardless of whether using PLV—which is relatively sensitive to volume conduction effects—or wPLI, which is robust against them [41], both metrics consistently indicated a significant weakening of long-range functional connectivity (e.g., fronto-parietal Fp2-P4/P3, parieto-occipital Pz-O2/Oz) in the NSSI group within the alpha band. Furthermore, wPLI-derived network global efficiency and clustering coefficient were also reduced. The fronto-parietal network is a core circuit for cognitive control and emotion regulation [54], and its connectivity with the occipital sensory cortex is crucial for the affective integration of sensory information. Therefore, the widespread weakening of network integration function in the alpha band may reflect fundamental neural deficits in inhibitory control and the disintegration of large-scale cortical network coordination in adolescents with NSSI. This aligns with the core clinical manifestations of high impulsivity and emotional dysregulation in this population [55]. A recent quantitative EEG study on depressed adolescents with NSSI supports this view, reporting specific alpha power abnormalities in the prefrontal regions of the NSSI group [56].

Unlike the low-integration seen in the α frequency band network, the θ frequency band showed enhanced connectivity in the NSSI group—but only in the PLV index. This was accompanied by increased global efficiency and a higher clustering coefficient of the network. The discrepancy between findings from PLV and other measures may stem from differences in calculation methods and should be interpreted with caution given their technical distinctions. Although PLV is more sensitive to volume conduction, its use in EEG classification has been shown to improve accuracy [57]—a pattern also observed in our study, suggesting that combining methods can offer a more complete pattern of brain connectivity. The enhanced θ-band connectivity and elevated network efficiency observed with PLV in this study may reflect an over-synchronized state within distributed circuits involved in emotion and motivation processing. Notably, these strengthened connections (e.g., F7/F8–O1/O2) largely span frontal and occipital regions. The fronto-occipital circuit plays a key role in emotional stimulus processing, motivated attention, and cognitive control [58]. This over-integration in low-frequency networks could be interpreted in two ways: as a compensatory response to imbalance in emotion-regulation circuits, or more likely, as a pathological “dedifferentiation” or maladaptive state [59, 60]. Although Xiao et al. observed altered frontal activity in adolescents with borderline personality disorder (often accompanied by self-harm)—with increased activation and connectivity viewed as compensatory [61]—we lean toward the latter explanation. The rigid, poorly differentiated integration pattern we observed aligns with the alexithymia symptoms found in this group, offering a potential neural-computational explanation for patients’ difficulty in recognizing and distinguishing subtle emotions. These findings provide broader clues for understanding the neural mechanisms of NSSI. That said, PLV-based analyses still require careful consideration of volume conduction, especially in future high-density EEG studies with limited spatial resolution—though we employed a sparse-channel strategy to minimize such effects.

Furthermore, no widespread significant inter-group differences were found in any other sub-bands such as the γ frequency band (30–48 Hz). This might suggest that the abnormal resting-state brain network of NSSI is mainly anchored in low-frequency oscillations (such as α and θ) that are involved in large-scale coordination, rather than γ oscillations which are more related to advanced cognitive functions such as local micro-circuit processing [62]. This frequency-specific pattern highlights the necessity of conducting frequency-resolved analysis when analyzing the neural mechanisms of NSSI.

Understanding the association between brain network characteristics and clinical symptoms is crucial for elucidating the mechanism of NSSI. This study reveals a highly selective pattern of association: after controlling for multiple comparisons, only the global efficiency of the low-frequency band (δ/θ) network derived from PLV is significantly positively correlated with the alexithymia, while there is no such correlation with symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and impulsivity. This specificity suggests that different clinical symptom dimensions of NSSI may correspond to distinct neural substrates. The alexithymia (inability to recognize and regulate emotions) as the core psychological feature of NSSI [63] may have a particularly neural basis related to the “stiffness” of the synchronization of large-scale networks in the low-frequency band. While emotional symptoms such as depression and anxiety may be more associated with other neural mechanisms (such as circuits involving the limbic system or other frequency bands’ oscillations), some fMRI studies also support that NSSI involves functional changes in multiple large-scale networks [14, 55, 64]. In this study, an increase in the efficiency of the θ frequency band network is associated with more severe alexithymia, further supporting that this over-integration of the network is not an adaptive compensation, but more likely a pathological “dedifferentiation” or maladaptive state, directly coupling with the core clinical phenotype of patients’ emotional cognitive deficits.

The search for biomarkers that can be used for objective assessment is one of the important goals in understanding abnormal brain networks [65]. This study found that a machine learning model based on the fusion of multiple indicator features can effectively distinguish NSSI at the individual level, and the core driving factor of classification is the specific functional connection edges distributed in the theta and alpha frequency bands (such as PLV-theta’s F7-Oz, PLV-alpha’s P3-P7, etc.), rather than general network properties. These specific connection edges can serve as potential diagnostic discriminative markers, providing specific targets for the development of clinical tools. This discovery forms a meaningful separation and complementation with the results at the population level: what is stable and related to clinical symptoms (emotional dysregulation) is the graph-theoretical attribute reflecting the overall integration level of the network, which is more like a “state association marker” to understand the severity variation of symptoms within the group. This separation of “diagnostic markers” and “state markers” may reflect the hierarchical nature of the neural mechanism of NSSI. That is to say, macroscopic changes in network efficiency may be associated with specific core clinical symptoms (such as emotional dysregulation), while the basis for disease diagnosis may be another set of more microscopic and specific disordered connection patterns [59, 66]. This strongly suggests that different clinical symptom dimensions of NSSI may correspond to different types of neural substrates, reflecting its inherent neural heterogeneity. Future research may need to stratify based on different symptom dimensions (such as high impulsivity vs. high emotional dysregulation) to find more specific biomarkers.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, all the NSSI participants were accompanied by depressive episodes, and there was no control group without NSSI for depression, thus the observed brain network abnormalities might partly reflect the common neural characteristics of adolescent depression. These findings provide clues for understanding the neural mechanism of NSSI, but its specificity needs to be further verified in future studies. The sample size of the healthy control group was small (n = 25), which might limit the statistical power and generalization. Secondly, the spatial resolution of scalp EEG is limited, and it cannot directly locate to deep brain regions (such as the amygdala, nucleus accumbens). Therefore, all neurobiological inferences in this study were strictly limited to the “large-scale cortical network synchrony” level and avoided direct association with specific subcortical structures’ functions. Future studies need to combine source reconstruction, high-density EEG or integrate with fMRI methods to improve spatial localization accuracy. Moreover, to avoid threshold selection bias, this study used a fully connected weighted network, but thus did not conduct normalization analysis for random networks. Future research can conduct sensitivity analysis at multiple sparse levels and calculate normalization indicators to verify the robustness of topological properties. Finally, although the classification model performed well in internal validation, its generalization ability still needs to be further verified in independent, larger multi-center clinical samples.

Conclusion

This study systematically revealed the multi-level abnormalities in the resting-state brain functional networks of adolescents with NSSI who also have depressive episodes through the integration of PLV/wPLI synchronous analysis, graph theory, and machine learning. The main features include: the weakened network integration function in the α frequency band, which may constitute the neural basis of their inhibitory control deficits; the θ frequency band shows an indicator-dependent potential “dedifferentiation” integration pattern and is related to the symptoms of emotional dysregulation. On this basis, specific functional connections combined with graph theory indicators show good potential for distinguishing NSSI at the individual level. These findings not only provide new evidence for understanding the frequency-specific neural mechanisms related to NSSI, but also offers new ideas and targets for developing more explanatory and predictive objective assessment tools in the future.
