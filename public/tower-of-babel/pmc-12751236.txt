Efficacy of 10 days high-dose and double-dose rabeprazole-based concomitant therapy for Helicobacter pylori eradication among Lebanese population: a pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Marc Elias, Charbel Yazbeck, Anthony Zaarour, Vanessa Sleiman, Nancy Sakr, Georges Raad, Bassem Akiki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study is to optimize Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) eradication by increasing the proton pump inhibitor (PPI) dose and reducing the treatment duration of antibiotics without compromising the treatment’s safety and efficacy.MethodsThis pilot randomized controlled trial included 120 patients from Notre Dame des Secours University Hospital in Byblos, Lebanon, between February 2023 and December 2023. Participants were Lebanese adults with H. pylori infection confirmed by upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy or nursing, malignancy or gastric mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma, allergy or contraindications to study drugs, active upper gastrointestinal bleeding, prior gastric surgeries, recent use of PPIs or antibiotics, celiac disease, previous H. pylori treatment, or refusal to consent. Eligible patients (n = 113) were randomly assigned into three groups: Group A (n = 38) received a standard-dose of rabeprazole 20 mg twice daily for 14 days with concomitant therapy, Group B (n = 37) received a double-dose of rabeprazole (20 mg twice per day) for 10 days with concomitant therapy, and Group C (n = 38) received a high-dose of rabeprazole (20 mg three times per day) for 10 days with concomitant therapy. Eradication was assessed by urea breath test six weeks after treatment completion.ResultsOut of 120 participants, 101 patients were considered for analysis, while 19 patients were excluded (15.8%). Of these exclusions, 7 patients were excluded before randomization due to drug allergy and opted for alternative treatment (5.8%), while 12 were excluded after randomization due to loss to follow-up (12%). The results indicated that the high-dose PPI group C had the highest percentage of negative breath tests (100%), followed by the standard treatment group A with 94.7%, and then group B with 83.3%. However, given the pilot nature and sample size of this study, these results, particularly the 100% rate in Group C, should be interpreted as preliminary and require confirmation in a larger trial.ConclusionThe use of high-dose PPI with 10 days of concomitant therapy (Group C) demonstrated the highest eradication rate among the study groups. This promising finding suggests a potential benefit of the high-dose PPI regimen with 10-day concomitant therapy but must be interpreted with caution; it warrants further investigation in a larger and adequately powered randomized controlled trial to confirm its efficacy and generalizability, given the pilot nature of this study.Name of the registryLebanon clinical trial registry;Trail registration numberLBCTR2025075755;Date of registration in primary registry14/08/2025.

Background

Helicobacter pylori or H.pylori, is a flagellated, gram-negative bacteria, that resides in the lining of the stomach [1], approximately 50% of the global population is affected by H.pylori[2]. This infection can lead to various gastrointestinal disorders, including peptic ulcers and gastritis. H pylori infection may remain asymptomatic for an extended period [3]. Various treatment regimens for H.Pylori eradication exist [4]. In countries with minimal clarithromycin resistance, the traditional triple therapy is still considered a 1 st line therapy. However, the Maastricht VI criteria recommend the first line treatment for H.pylori eradication to be the use of concomitant or bismuth quadruple therapies[5]. Concomitant therapy or quadruple regimen involves a combination of three antibiotics: amoxicillin 1000 mg, clarithromycin 500 mg, and metronidazole 500 mg, administered 2 times per day in association with a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) twice daily [6].

The escalating global antibiotic resistance observed recently poses a significant challenge to the efficacy of first-line treatments for H. pylori infection, especially in patients resistant to clarithromycin or dual resistance to clarithromycin and metronidazole [7]. A high resistance to metronidazole and clarithromycin was proved by 2 studies conducted in Lebanon[8, 9]. Given the high or unknown rate of clarithromycin resistance in Lebanon, the Maastricht VI/Florence consensus recommends against conventional triple therapy and suggests the use of concomitant or bismuth quadruple therapy as a 1^st^ line empirical treatment in our region[5] that being the case, it is preferable to use the concomitant or bismuth therapy as a 1 st line treatment is our area.

To optimize treatment plans, various therapeutic techniques, including adjustments in treatment durations, PPI dosages, and the administration of pre-treatment doses or pre-medications such as probiotics, have been explored in this domain [10].

Of particular interest, a systematic review showed that the administration of the highest PPI dose is necessary[11]. In parallel, a meta-analysis published in 2014, which involved 11 randomized controlled trials with 3500 patients, aimed to assess the effectiveness of sequential, concomitant, and hybrid therapy in eradicating H. pylori, concluded that there was no significant difference among sequential therapy, concomitant therapy, and hybrid therapy in treating H. pylori infection [12]. In addition, another randomized clinical trial conducted in Taiwan in 2019, involved 240 patients, compared a 14-day regimen of esomeprazole (PPI) and amoxicillin in a high dose dual therapy (esomeprazole 40 mg three times daily and amoxicillin 750 mg four times daily for 14 days) to a 7-day concomitant therapy. The trial demonstrated eradication rates of 91.7% and 86.7%, respectively, with fewer reported adverse events [13].

On the other hand, several studies suggested that H.pylori eradication could be affected by high gastric pH levels by promoting the efficacy of antimicrobials [14]. In vitro, PPIs have shown direct antibacterial efficacy against H.pylori [15]. Thus, in vivo, they inhibit the bacteria rather than eradicate it[16]. This suppression may be enough to encounter the role of co-prescribing antibiotics in order to eradicate H.pylori.

Rabeprazole was selected as the PPI for this study due to its pharmacokinetic profile. It has a higher pKa (5.0) compared to other PPIs, leading to more rapid activation and accumulation in the parietal cell, and it is metabolized through a non-enzymatic pathway to a significant extent, making its acid-suppressive effect less variable and less influenced by CYP2C19 polymorphisms, which is advantageous in a genetically diverse population. [17].

Furthermore, a randomized controlled trial from Hamamatsu University School of Medicine Japan showed that 10 mg rabeprazole 4 times per day and amoxicillin500 mg 4 times daily for 14 days appears useful after failure of the usual triple proton pump inhibitor/amoxicillin/clarithromycin therapy in H. pylori eradication.[18].

Moreover, concomitant therapy taken twice daily showed greater adherence to treatment compared to bismuth quadruple therapy. Curiously, some patients may not tolerate a 14-day course of antibiotics due to side effects, which may require a reduction in the duration of treatment. To the best of our knowledge, a treatment regimen using a high dose PPI-rabeprazole 20 mg three times daily with concomitant therapy for 10 days has never been investigated.

That being the case, this study was designed to optimize H. pylori eradication by increasing the PPI dose and reducing the treatment duration without compromising its safety and efficacy among Lebanese Population.

Materials and methods

Trial design, approval, and registration

This is a single-center, pilot randomized open-label controlled trial conducted at 3 gastroenterologist clinics at CHU-NDS University Hospital in Byblos, Lebanon, from February 2023 till December 2023. This study was registered with the Lebanese ministry of public health (MOPH) on the Lebanese clinical trials registry (LBCTR) platform under the number (LBCTR2025075755) and received approval from the institutional review board (IRB) at CHU-NDS University Hospital to conduct this study over a 10-month period as a pilot study. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. It was a randomized study but not using a blinded technique because each treatment arm had a unique regimen (different pill appearances, doses and duration). Participants

The trial included Lebanese adults visiting our clinics after undergoing gastro-intestinal endoscopy (n = 120). In each clinic, exclusively adult (> 18 years old) patients, who did upper Gastro-intestinal (GI) endoscopy with postivie H.pylori infection based on Giemsa stained histological findings, were invited to participate at their routine histological assessment after taking their consent.

Exclusion criteria were: 1) Pregnancy or nursing, 2) Malignancy or gastric MALT lymphoma, 3) Allergy or contra-indications to study drugs, 4) Active upper gastrointestinal bleeding, 5) Previous gastric surgeries, 6) Patients taking PPIs or antibiotics in the previous month, 7) Celiac disease, 8) Prior H.pylori treatment, 9) Refusal to consent.

It should be noted that 19 patients were excluded from which 7 patients before randomization due to drug allergy and they took alternative treatment, and 12 after randomization due to loss to follow up.

Randomization and blinding

Patients who met the inclusion criteria (n = 113) were randomly assigned using the sealed opaque envelope technique. In each clinic, there were 6 sealed opaque envelopes (AABBCC), with each envelope containing one letter. The included patients were allocated into groups of 6 for randomization. The first patient must choose one envelope, which will be excluded from the second patient, and so on, in order to ensure that from each set of 6 participants, there are 2 patients in each group (2A, 2B, 2 C), thus ensuring that the number of patients in study groups matches at baseline. The present study was not blinded because, after selection, the treating physician must clearly explain the treatment regimen to ensure good adherence to treatment and minimize side effects. Each group received a different PPI dose, duration of treatment, and a combination of three antibiotics, making it challenging to provide all patients with identical drug pills (in terms of shape, color, and size) for the same treatment duration (14 days). To reduce potential bias from the open-label design, patient-reported outcomes (gastrointestinal symptoms and medication adherence) were collected using validated instruments (GSRS and LMAS) administered at standardized time points. Furthermore, the primary outcome of this study, H. pylori eradication was assessed using the objective urea breath test, which is not susceptible to observer bias.

Intervention

After obtaining signed informed consent, 113 participants who met the inclusion criteria were allocated into three groups. Group A (n = 38) received a standard-dose of rabeprazole 20 mg twice daily for 14 days with concomitant therapy. Group B (n = 37) received a double-dose of rabeprazole (20 mg twice daily) for 10 days with concomitant therapy. Group C (n = 38) received a high-dose of rabeprazole (20 mg three times daily) for 10 days with concomitant therapy.

Outcome

To measure the study outcome, six weeks after the treatment, and after stopping proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) for at least 2 weeks, each patient was asked to undergo a urea breath test either at the hospital’s laboratory or externally and then return with the results to determine the eradication rate. The urea breath test is an important noninvasive test used for post-eradication follow up, offering safety, reliability, ease of performance and a high accuracy up to 96% according to a meta-analysis results posted in “world journal of gastroenterology” [19].

Data collection

Data collection commenced with a questionnaire in clinic with patients who met inclusion criteria (n = 113). The questionnaire encompassed personal, demographic, and medical history inquiries, including the study group, date of visit, date of birth, age, sex, marital status, level of education, smoking status, caffeine and alcohol consumption, BMI, previous medical and surgical history, symptoms prior to endoscopy, endoscopy indication, endoscopy findings, histological findings confirming H. pylori infection. The included patients answered the validated Arabic version of the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale which is a revised version of the GSRS scale also known as "VERSION USED IN THE GSRS-QOL” [20] (GSRS) before starting any treatment, then they were randomly assigned using the sealed opaque envelope technique into 3 groups. A Urea Breath Test (UBT) was performed after 6 weeks of the cure and after stopping PPI for 14 days. A phone call interview was conducted more than 6 weeks post-treatment for each patient to evaluate the safety and the efficacy of the treatment, by answering for the second time the GSRS score, in addition to the Lebanese Medication Adherence Scale (LMAS) [20, 21], and if they experienced any side effects to the study medications.

Lebanese medication adherence scale

It is a validated scale in Lebanon which consists of 14 questions to monitor in our study the adherence of the patient to the medications answered by yes or no [20, 21].

Gastrointestinal symptom rating scale

It is also a validated scale in Lebanon, it is the revised version of the GSRS also known as the "VERSION USED IN THE GSRS-QOL” which consists of 15 questions asked before and after the treatment which leads to 3 scores: reflux score, abdominal pain score and indigestion score before and after the treatment to monitor the patient’s safety in our study. Numerical rating was done on each item from 1 to 7, on a scale of severity that vary from 0:no discomfort at all to 7: very severe. We also asked each patients if he/she had any unusual side effects during its treatment[20].

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS 25 software was employed for statistical analysis, where descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis, were calculated for each symptom score before and after treatment within each treatment group and overall. Given significant skewness and kurtosis values for several variables, non-parametric tests were deemed more appropriate for further analysis. These tests included the Wilcoxon Signed-Ranks Test, conducted to compare within-group changes in symptom scores (pre-treatment vs. post-treatment) for each symptom within each treatment group. The Kruskal–Wallis Test was utilized to assess potential differences in treatment efficacy across the groups. Additionally, the Chi-Square Test was applied to analyze relationships between categorical variables such as Sex, Marital status, Education status, Address, Habits, Indications, and treatment groups, determining whether significant associations existed. A significance level of p < 0.05 was employed for all statistical tests. To assess whether the treatment effect on eradication rates was independent of baseline characteristics, a post-hoc multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed with urea breath test result (negative vs. positive) as the dependent variable and treatment group, age, and BMI as covariates. As this was a pilot study focused on feasibility and generating preliminary data, a formal sample size calculation was not performed.

Lebanese medication adherence scale

All three treatment groups had high average LMAS[20, 21] scores, ranging from 97.62% to 99.25%, indicating good adherence to the medication regimen. The overall average LMAS score was 98.37%, suggesting a generally high level of adherence across all patients. There was a small standard deviation (5.52) across all groups, indicating a relatively consistent level of adherence within each group. The Kruskal–Wallis test showed no statistically significant differences in LMAS scores between the three treatment groups (p-value = 0.608). This suggests that all three treatment regimens were equally effective in promoting medication adherence.

Eradication rates

A statistically significant relation was shown between the urea breath test result (positive/negative) and the treatment groups (A, B, C) (p = 0.030) based on chi-square test. This suggests that the proportion of individuals with positive and negative breath tests differs significantly across the three groups. Particularly, double-dose rabeprazole group had the highest percentage of positive urea breath tests (16.7%), followed by standard treatment group with 5.3% and then high-dose PPI group with 0%. This suggests that Group B might have had less successful eradication of H. pylori compared to the other two groups. To determine whether this relationship was confounded by baseline differences in age and BMI between the groups, we performed a multivariate logistic regression analysis. After adjustment for age and BMI, treatment group remained a significant independent predictor of eradication success (p = 0.035). In the adjusted model, Group C (high-dose PPI) maintained a significantly higher odds of eradication compared to Group B (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 8.4, 95% CI: 1.3–55.6, p = 0.027). The difference between Group C and Group A was not statistically significant in the adjusted analysis (aOR = 4.2, 95% CI: 0.3–53.1, p = 0.27), though the point estimate still favored Group C. The high-dose PPI group had the highest percentage of negative breath tests (100%), followed by the standard treatment group with 94.7% and then double-dose rabeprazole group with 83.3%. The eradication rates with their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were: Group A: 94.7% (95% CI: 82.7%—98.5%), Group B: 83.3% (95% CI: 66.4%—92.7%), and Group C: 100% (95% CI: 89.6%—100%). The wide confidence interval for Group C reflects the statistical uncertainty associated with the small sample size and indicates that the true eradication rate for this regimen in the population may be lower. This suggests that Group C may be the most effective treatment in eradicating H. pylori based on negative urea breath test results (Fig. 3). In Addition, to assess the potential impact of loss to follow-up on our results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis. Under a worst-case scenario where all 12 patients lost to follow-up (7 from Group B, 5 from Group C) were considered treatment failures, the intention-to-treat eradication rates would be: Group A: 94.7% (36/38), Group B: 67.6% (25/37), and Group C: 86.8% (33/38). Under a best-case scenario where all lost patients were considered cured (clinically plausible given their asymptomatic status), the rates would be: Group A: 94.7% (36/38), Group B: 86.5% (32/37), and Group C: 100% (38/38).

The documented adverse effects after treatment were mild, such as taste disturbance or nausea. No serious side effects (this includes effects that were life-threatening, required hospitalization, resulted in significant disability, or led to therapy discontinuation) were documented.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study aimed to optimize H.pylori eradication by reducing the duration of treatment, increasing the PPI dose without reducing the safety and the efficacy of the treatment. This pilot randomized controlled trial showed that Group C: 10 days high dose rabeprazole (20 mg 3 times per day) with concomitant therapy, achieved a promising eradication rate of 100% based on Urea Breath Test, though this must be interpreted with caution due to the sample size.

Interpretation

There are many published papers (RCT, meta-analysis…) aiming to find the optimal treatment for h.pylori eradication that became a major problem worldwide, according to the Maastricht criteria, the recommended first line treatment for H.pylori eradication is either concomitant or bismuth quadruple therapies. Despite the availability of multiple therapeutic regimens, the eradication rate of H.pylori is decreasing due to several factors, but the main reasons of failure remains the antibiotic resistance and the poor compliance to treatment[22, 23]. Since the eradication rate has decreased steadily in the last years, there are conflicting findings on the duration of the optimal treatment for H.pylori eradication. European guidelines indicate that 1 week of the triple therapy is acceptable[24] whereas, in the US, they prefer 10 to 14 days of treatment. [24, 25] The variation in eradication rates among countries can be attributed to numerous factors, including environmental conditions, bacterial virulence, excessive antibiotic intake, and improper administration practices, all of which contribute to the escalation of bacterial resistance to antibiotics[26]. In order to find the best H pylori treatment, regional eradication rates and antimicrobial resistance should be taken into account.

There is a lack of data about the prevalence of Hpylori and its antimicrobial resistance (specially clarithromycin and metronidazole) among Lebanese population. A Lebanese study included 54 H.pylori isolated cultures from gastric biopsies at the American University of Beirut Medical Center tested the prevalence of H.pylori resistance to antibiotics in Lebanon revealed a resistance of 29.5% to metronidazole, 4% to clarithromycin, 2% to tetracycline and 0% resistance to amoxicillin in vitro.[8] Another study conducted in Lebanon in 2014 showed a 38.1% rate of resistance to clarithromycin[9]. In line with the Maastricht VI consensus for areas of high or unknown resistance, our study employed concomitant therapy as the empirical first-line regimen. However, a key limitation is that routine antibiotic susceptibility testing is not available in clinical practice in Lebanon[5]. No Lebanese data or consensus about the best treatment regimen to be used for our patients. To the best of our knowledge, a treatment regimen using a high dose PPI-rabeprazole 20 mg three times daily with concomitant therapy for 10 days (group C) has never been investigated. The whole sample eradication rate described in this study is 93.04%, which matches the rate of concomitant-based H.pylori eradication that ranges between 77%[27] and 94.4%[28]. These results align with Zullo A[29], where the overall eradication rate was 87.5% in a controlled trial to test the importance of high dose PPI (esomeprazole 40 mg three times daily) with amoxicillin 1 g twice daily for 10 days which aligns with our study that 10 days treatment regimen of antibiotics might be enough. The potentially superior efficacy of rabeprazole may be attributed to its pharmacodynamic advantages. Rabeprazole has a higher pKa (5.0) than esomeprazole (4.0), which allows for faster activation and more efficient proton pump inhibition, especially in the less acidic environment created by antibiotic therapy. This more consistent and potent acid suppression is crucial for enhancing the efficacy of concomitant antibiotics, particularly in the face of rising resistance.[17].

In addition, this study tends to assume that Group C (the study group to monitor the role of high dose PPI of 20 mg rabeprazole 3 times per day with the 10 days of concomitant treatment regimen) has the highest percentage of negative breath tests (100%), followed by Group A (the control group) with 94.7% and then Group B (the group to monitor if only the 10 days treatment regimen without high dose PPI is enough) with 83.3%. This suggests that Group C may be the most effective treatment in eradicating H. pylori based on negative test results. Given the small sample size and the resulting statistical imprecision, this result should be considered a strong preliminary signal rather than a definitive conclusion.

These results align with the findings of several studies that high gastric pH enhance the efficacy of antibiotics[14], and the study that showed the role of PPIs to encounter the role of antibiotics to eradicate H.pylori.[15] These results align with the findings of Laine et al.'s study[29] which demonstrated that after 7 days of triple therapy, the eradication rate of H. pylori was 86%, while 10 and 14 days of treatment yielded eradication rates of 90% and 92%, respectively. So, even after 14 days of treatment, some patients were still infected with H.pylori and are considered resistant to treatment, and needed another cure, so more antibiotics, more adverse effects and may lead to more drug resistance. This was another reason for trying to find the optimal treatment duration. The documented adverse events were mild such as taste disturbance or nausea, no serious side effects (this includes effects that were life-threatening, required hospitalization, resulted in significant disability, or led to therapy discontinuation) were documented and there was no statistically significant difference in adverse effects between study groups. Gastrointestinal side effects were measured using the GSRS[20] score, and there was statistically significant difference of GSRS score before and after the cure in the 3 groups. Treating Physicians played a crucial role in instructing patients to adhere strictly to medication regimens, aiming to minimize the risk of antibiotic resistance—a significant factor contributing to treatment failure in H. pylori infections[30]. The Lebanese Medical Adherence Scale indicated high levels of patient adherence, underscoring the effectiveness of physicians who dedicated time to explain the importance of treatment compliance.

Limitations and biases

This pilot-randomized clinical trial comprised 113 participants and yielded promising results. The primary limitation is the small sample size. The study was not powered to detect anything but very large differences between groups, and the point estimate of 100% eradication in Group C is associated with a wide confidence interval. Therefore, the observed superior efficacy of the high-dose PPI regimen should be considered preliminary and require validation in a larger, adequately powered trial to ensure its representativeness for the Lebanese population.

Furthermore, the trial was registered after patient’s enrollment had already begun. We acknowledge that this delay may raise concerns about potential reporting bias. However, the postponement was due to exceptional circumstances: Lebanon’s severe economic crisis since 2019, which coincided with a prolonged technical failure of the national trials’ registry (LBCTR platform), making prospective registration impossible despite our best efforts. Once the LBCTR became operational, registration was completed. Importantly, the study protocol was finalized before the trial commenced, and all pre-specified outcomes have been fully reported.

The open-label design of this study presents a notable limitation, as the lack of blinding for both patients and investigators could have introduced bias. Awareness of treatment allocation may have influenced patient-reported outcomes; for example, patients receiving the high-dose regimen (Group C) might have experienced placebo effects that affected their reports on the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS) or their adherence, as measured by the Lebanese Medication Adherence Scale (LMAS). Similarly, investigators' knowledge of group assignments could have unintentionally affected their interactions with patients or the documentation of adverse events. To mitigate this, we utilized validated questionnaires and standardized instructions for all participants. The high adherence levels and significant symptom improvements observed across all groups suggest that substantial bias is unlikely. Most importantly, the primary outcome: H. pylori eradication rate, was assessed using the objective urea breath test (UBT), which is unaffected by subjective influences, thereby supporting the reliability of our main findings.

This study also had non-response from 12 patients who took their medications after their routine histological clinical visit post endoscopy due to loss of follow up, thus they were excluded, and this loss to follow up may pose a potential source of bias that these patients results could influence our study results, further studies with larger sample size are indicated.

A significant limitation is the absence of antibiotic susceptibility testing for clarithromycin and metronidazole. As this testing is not routinely available in Lebanon, we could not determine the specific resistance profiles of the infecting H. pylori strains. Consequently, we cannot definitively conclude whether the superior eradication rate in the high-dose PPI group (C) was due to the regimen overcoming antimicrobial resistance or a potentially lower, uneven prevalence of resistant strains within that group. This underscores the need for future studies conducted in settings where susceptibility testing is feasible to directly assess the regimen's efficacy against confirmed resistant infections.

In this study, there was selection bias, because most of the patients live near the hospital, our results has showed that 86.8% of Group A, 80% of group B and 69.7% of group C live in Mount Lebanon Governorate.

There is also information bias, primarily an issue in randomized clinical trials where patients are enrolled sequentially rather than all at once. Additionally, bias due to noncompliance of the 12 excluded patients who were lost to follow-up. Another limitation was the follow-up method that was done via phone calls.

Conclusion

In Conclusion, this pilot clinical trial suggests that adding a high-dose PPI to a 10 days concomitant therapy regimen achieved a promising 100% eradication rate while maintaining a favorable safety profile. However, given the small sample size and the statistical imprecision inherent in this preliminary investigation, these findings must be interpreted with caution. The potential superiority of this high-dose PPI regimen requires confirmation through larger, adequately powered randomized controlled trials before any definitive clinical recommendations.
