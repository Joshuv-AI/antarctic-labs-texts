What drives participation? A look at personal influences on Tanzanian nurses in project ECHO
Authors: Godfrey Kacholi, Bahati Mfaki, Idda Lyatonga Swai, Mackfallen G. Anasel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe Project Extension for Community Health Care Outcomes (Project ECHO) is a renowned collaborative model of medical education and care management. This model enhances the capabilities of primary care providers through telementoring, providing a promising and cost-effective approach to augmenting healthcare professionals' knowledge. Participation in weekly ECHO sessions often falls short of expectations despite its benefits. This study examined the personal factors that influence Tanzanian nurses' engagement with Project ECHO.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study involving 450 nurses from 42 primary healthcare facilities in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. A standardised questionnaire served as the data collection tool. Descriptive analysis was performed to calculate means, frequencies and percentages for socio-demographic variables and the level of participation of nurses. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to test the association between the level of participation of nurses in ECHO sessions and their socio-demographic factors. ResultsOnly 36% (n = 162) of 450 nurses attended at least two ECHO sessions (median 2, IQR 1–2). Paediatric ECHO sessions had the highest attendance (43.8%, n = 71). Ease of computer use (AOR = 1.95, p = 0.015) and ICT self-motivation (AOR = 2.08, p = 0.003) significantly increased attendance. A majority (58%) lacked personal computers. Surprisingly, adequate ICT skills lowered the likelihood of attending at least two sessions (AOR = 0.37, p < 0.001).ConclusionThe study found low participation of nurses in ECHO sessions, with paediatric ECHO sessions showing the highest engagement. Comfort with online format and ease of instructions motivated attendance, while computer proficiency and ICT motivation were positive predictors. Surprisingly, adequate ICT skills correlated with lower participation. Enhancing basic computer literacy and ICT motivation is crucial for improved engagement. Future research should explore reasons for lower participation among digitally skilled nurses and tailor session content accordingly.

Introduction

The healthcare system in Tanzania is faced with significant constraints that limit its ability to provide quality healthcare services [1]. These restraints include a lack of financial resources, a geographically dispersed population, and a shortage of qualified healthcare professionals, particularly in rural areas [2, 3]. These challenges pose a considerable obstacle to ensuring access to high-quality healthcare services, thereby exacerbating ongoing health disparities throughout the country [4, 5]. In this context, innovative strategies are vital to boost the knowledge, skillsets, and capacity of healthcare workers to deliver effective, efficient, quality, and timely care [6]. One such innovative approach is Project ECHO (Extension for Community Healthcare Outcomes), which has emerged as a promising and effective method to address these constraints [7].

Project ECHO, launched in 2003 at the University of New Mexico in the United States of America, is a telementoring model that fosters collaborative learning among healthcare professionals [8]. ECHO sessions feature a multidisciplinary panel of experts from leading institutions who present complex patient cases and engage in real-time discussions with participants [8–12]. Healthcare providers, often in geographically dispersed locations, share their own cases, ask questions, and receive guidance from both the expert panel and their peers [13]. This collaborative approach facilitates knowledge exchange, builds capacity for evidence-based decision-making, and promotes continuous professional development [13–15]. The interactive nature of ECHO sessions provides a unique opportunity for healthcare professionals to interact with experts from diverse fields, fostering a culture of collaboration and continuous learning [13, 16, 17]. Overall, Project ECHO has become a powerful tool for advancing clinical practice, improving patient outcomes, and enhancing healthcare delivery [12].

Project ECHO presents significant promise for the Tanzanian healthcare system by leveraging technology to surmount geographical barriers [18]. Through ECHO sessions, remote nurses can connect with specialist experts, promoting knowledge sharing and enhancing clinical practice [19]. Numerous studies have shown that Project ECHO can enhance the knowledge, self-efficacy, and clinical skills of healthcare professionals in various medical specialities [8, 11, 13].

Nurses, who constitute approximately 38.6% of Tanzania's healthcare workforce and are crucial to healthcare delivery [20], are vital participants in Project ECHO's weekly sessions aimed at improving care; however, iECHO data from 2022 indicates a low national participation rate of only 5% among nurses [21]. To address this significant gap and optimise the program's effectiveness in empowering these essential professionals through knowledge sharing and collaboration, this study investigates the individual factors influencing nurses'participation in Project ECHO sessions within Dar es Salaam's primary healthcare facilities. Understanding these factors is critical to maximising the impact of Project ECHO on patient care across Tanzania.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania's largest and most populated city, to explore how nurses participate in Project ECHO across its five municipal councils: Temeke, Kinondoni, Dar es Salaam City, Ilala, and Kigamboni. Among these councils, Ilala, Temeke, and Ubungo hold the largest populations, while Kinondoni and Kigamboni have fewer residents. As of 2022, Dar es Salaam had 868 operational primary healthcare facilities. Of these facilities, 306 (35.3%) are installed with the necessary technology, such as Zoom software, dual televisions, and a camera, that allows healthcare workers to participate in weekly virtual mentorship sessions offered by Project ECHO.

Sample size and sampling procedures

To ensure our findings were statistically robust, we calculated a sample size using Cochran's formula (22). This formula factored in a 95% confidence interval and a 5% margin of error. To account for potential non-responses, we increased the sample size by 15%, resulting in a final target of 450 participants. We utilised a multistage cluster sampling method to select primary healthcare facilities. These facilities were grouped into district hospitals, health centres, and dispensaries. We then randomly selected a specific number from each group: 4 district hospitals, 11 health centres, and 27 dispensaries.

To participate in the study, nurses had to meet the following criteria: provide written informed consent, have at least one year of work experience (full-time or part-time), and be familiar with Project ECHO. We used purposive sampling to select participants from eligible primary healthcare facilities where nurses had been involved in Project ECHO's weekly tele-mentorship sessions for at least one year before the study. The recruitment and data collection ran from October to November 2023.

Data collection instrument and procedures

A standardised questionnaire was administered to the study participants to collect data about effective participation in Project ECHO sessions. The questionnaire was structured to include (1) socio-demographic information of participants, (2) participation in Project ECHO sessions, and (3) individual factors influencing participation in ECHO sessions (such as easiness to the computer, comfortability with the online experience, access to the ECHO session centre/room, adequacy of ICT skills and knowledge, possession of computer, motivation to use ICT, and interest in career exploration).

To ensure the questionnaire accurately measured ECHO session participation, we used Cronbach's alpha (α = 0.73) to assess internal reliability. The questionnaire was pre-tested in six facilities, which included one district-level hospital, two health centres, and three dispensaries. The results from this pre-testing were excluded from the final analysis. To enhance understanding, the questionnaire was translated into Kiswahili, a widely spoken national language. A well-trained research assistant, who also participated in the pre-testing, administered the questionnaires to the nurses under the guidance of the principal investigator (first author).

Variables and measurements

Our study aimed to determine the participation of nurses in ECHO sessions, which we defined as the act of joining and/or attending Project ECHO sessions to learn, discuss, and share ideas or questions about a particular topic. While we recognise that our definition may not cover all possible interpretations of participation, it was necessary to standardise the definition for our study and to inform future research. To measure participation, we asked nurses whether they had attended ECHO sessions in the last 12 months and recorded their responses as either Yes or No. We categorised our independent variables into two groups: socio-demographic variables and individual factors that could affect nurses'participation in ECHO sessions. The socio-demographic variables we considered relevant to the study included age, gender, place of residence, marital status, level within the nursing cadre, education level, working experience, and nature of employment. The individual variables were ease of computer use, online comfort, ease of online instructions, accessibility of session centre, ICT skills and knowledge, personal computer availability, self-motivation for ICT use, and career exploration interest.

Data analysis

The data gathered from the questionnaire was entered into Microsoft Excel for cleaning and analysis. We then transferred it to STATA version 18 for further analysis. To understand the socio-demographic variables and individual factors affecting participation in ECHO sessions, we conducted a descriptive analysis using frequency, percentages, and measures of central tendency or dispersion (median, standard deviation, and range). Logistic regression analysis was employed to assess the relationship between the outcome variable, which is participation in ECHO sessions, and the independent variables comprising individual factors. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to identify the specific individual factors that affect the participation of nurses in Project ECHO in primary health centres in the Dar es Salaam Region of Tanzania. The study is part of a larger study that examined the factors that influence nurses'participation in ECHO sessions across Tanzania.

This study has revealed that the nurses'participation level in the ECHO project sessions was significantly low. This could be attributed to the limited access to personal computers, as stated by the nurses. It is well-established that attending sessions during work hours can be challenging for nurses without dedicated devices, especially when considering shared workstations and other pressing responsibilities. Based on the findings of the 2016 assessment of the Namibian ECHO Pilot, a significant proportion (73.6%) of the respondents indicated that they utilised the clinic or hospital computer to access the Project ECHO sessions. In contrast, a mere 13.2% reported using their personal computer or laptop for the same purpose [9]. These results indicate that nurses have limited access to personal devices for educational sessions, highlighting the significance of ensuring reliable computer access within healthcare facilities to facilitate the participation of nurses in professional development programmes.

The observed significantly low participation of nurses in the ECHO project sessions likely stems from limited access to personal computers, a challenge echoed in the 2016 Namibian ECHO pilot assessment where a substantial of respondents relied on clinic or hospital computers for session access, contrasting sharply with the mere less than 40% using personal devices [9]. This reliance on shared workstations, coupled with competing work priorities, inherently creates barriers to consistent session attendance during work hours [23]. Reinforcing this, our finding that nurses reporting ease of computer use were nearly twice as likely to attend ECHO sessions underscores the critical role of basic digital literacy in engaging with telementoring programmes. Although a majority reported computer ease, the significant association with attendance highlights the necessity of addressing the digital literacy needs of the remaining less than forty per cent of the participants to enhance overall participation in professional development initiatives like Project ECHO by ensuring reliable computer access within healthcare facilities and potentially implementing targeted computer skills training [24].

The seemingly counterintuitive finding that nurses with adequate ICT skills and knowledge were significantly less likely to attend ECHO sessions demands closer scrutiny. One plausible interpretation is that nurses who already perceive themselves as proficient in ICT may not recognise the immediate benefit or relevance of the ECHO session content to their existing skillset. This interpretation may contradict a study on health workers'knowledge and attitudes towards computer applications in rural African health facilities, including those in Tanzania. In that study, over 70% of health workers reported having no basic training in computers, and more than 60% were found to be computer illiterate [25]. Perhaps, this negative association could point towards unmeasured confounding factors. For instance, nurses with strong ICT skills might be assigned to roles with heavier technological demands, leaving them with less time or flexibility to participate in additional online sessions. Given that a considerable proportion (61.1%) of nurses reported adequate ICT skills, understanding the reasons behind this inverse relationship is crucial for optimising ECHO programme engagement and ensuring its relevance across all levels of digital literacy within the nursing workforce. Future research could explore the perceived needs and expectations of nurses with varying ICT skill levels regarding telementoring content and delivery.

Although participation in ECHO sessions was limited; however, the nurses who attended reported feeling at ease with computer use during these sessions. This comfort may be attributed to the extensive exposure to computers that graduate nurses typically receive throughout their academic studies and prior work experiences. Such familiarity likely facilitated their ease of engagement during the sessions, enabling them to develop the requisite skills to effectively navigate the ECHO platform. This may also be facilitated by the structure of the ECHO sessions, in which participants are typically provided with clear instructions on how to join the virtual videoconference using Zoom software[26]. This approach is likely to increase nurses'comfort level during ECHO sessions.

While a substantial majority of nurses reported comfort with the online experience (73.8%) and found online instructions easy to use (66.2%) (Table 3), these factors did not significantly predict ECHO attendance in the adjusted analysis, suggesting they might be necessary but not primary drivers of participation. In contrast, the findings indicate that active participants in ECHO sessions generally possess good ICT proficiency, likely fueled by their intrinsic motivation, enabling their confident engagement. This aligns with a retrospective evaluation of an HIV/TB ECHO programme that highlighted the user-friendliness of the Zoom platform, allowing even those with limited tech expertise to adapt and participate with minimal difficulty [27]. This suggests that while ease of use is important for accessibility, inherent motivation and potentially pre-existing ICT comfort levels may play a more significant role in driving active participation in ECHO sessions.

The findings of the recent study contradict those of previous studies. The results indicate that the participation of nurses in ECHO sessions did not lead to career development exploration [7, 9, 18, 28, 29]. The current study's findings challenge the assumption that participating in educational programmes like ECHO sessions automatically translates to nurses exploring career advancement. These results suggest that ECHO sessions, in their current format, might not directly prompt nurses to consider career development. Perhaps other factors, such as one-to-one mentorship and coaching programmes, are more important for motivating nurses to explore career opportunities beyond attending ECHO sessions virtually. Based on a previous study, it has been found that the e-learning programme can improve the knowledge and skills of healthcare practitioners. As a result, it can lead to better care for patients. However, to provide a significant practical advantage, the programme should consider the local context and needs [28].

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study possesses several strengths. Firstly, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, it is the first study of its kind to be conducted in Tanzania to examine individual factors affecting nurses'participation in ECHO sessions. Secondly, participants were selected from three primary tiers of health service provision, namely district hospitals, health centres, and dispensaries. Nevertheless, the study's reliance solely on quantitative methods, its cross-sectional design, and its confinement to Dar es Salaam may limit the generalisability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study examining the participation of nurses in Project ECHO sessions within the Dar es Salaam region of Tanzania reveals a concerning overall participation rate, with only 36% of nurses attending at least two sessions over the past year. Attendance levels varied across different ECHO sessions, with the paediatric session demonstrating the highest engagement. Factors that motivated participation included comfort with the online format and the ease of following instructions. Additionally, proficiency in computer use and intrinsic motivation for ICT emerged as significant positive predictors of attendance. Conversely, the presence of adequate ICT skills and knowledge was associated with a decreased likelihood of participation, highlighting a need for further investigation into this phenomenon. To improve engagement in telementoring initiatives such as Project ECHO, it is critical to implement interventions aimed at enhancing basic computer literacy and fostering motivation for ICT utilisation. Future research should also explore the underlying reasons for the lower participation rates among nurses who self-report having adequate ICT skills, as well as investigate strategies to better align ECHO session content with the needs and perceived value for nurses across varying levels of digital proficiency. Addressing these issues, along with ensuring dependable access to technology, has the potential to significantly elevate the reach and effectiveness of Project ECHO in facilitating the professional development of nurses in Tanzania.
