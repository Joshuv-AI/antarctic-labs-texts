Plasma cell-free DNA methylation-based prognosis in metastatic castrate-resistant prostate cancer
Authors: Jodie Wong, Yijun Tian, Manishkumar S. Patel, Kapil Avasthi, Claire Hanson, Matt Larsen, Enos Ampaw, Rebekah Gutowski, Muhammad Z. H. Fadlullah, Joseph Finkelstein, Aik Choon Tan, Jong Park, Brandon J. Manley, Chiang-Ching Huang, Manish Kohli, Liang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Molecular prognostication in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) remains a challenge due to the lack of validated biomarkers. This study developed a plasma cell-free DNA (cfDNA) methylation-based prognostic model in mCRPC. Targeted cfDNA methylation sequencing in 96 prostate cancer patients across different states of cancer progression revealed 77 methylation haplotype blocks (MHBs) differentially methylated from organ-confined prostate cancer to mCRPC states. Among these 77 MHBs, the top 20 MHBs were associated with mCRPC overall survival and most MHB methylation levels positively correlated with predicted circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) fraction. By integrating the MHB-based risk score with currently available prognostic clinical variables and ctDNA fraction, a prognostic nomogram was developed which showed high predictive performance for mCRPC survival (AUC = 0.99 for 6 months, AUC = 0.84 for 1 year, and AUC = 0.82 for 2 years). These findings demonstrate potential of cfDNA methylation as a molecular biology-driven biomarker for mCRPC prognosis.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most common cancer in men globally and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with over 396,000 deaths annually worldwide1. Despite significant advancements in therapeutic strategies, survival for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) remains poor, with a median survival time of 30 to 34 months2, underscoring the need for improved prognostication methods. Since 2015, the management of metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) has rapidly evolved, with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT)-based combinations emerging as the new standard of care3. Several of these novel treatments target the androgen receptor-testosterone pathway, utilizing agents such as Androgen Receptor Pathway Inhibitors that were initially developed for mCRPC. Despite the rapid adoption of these intensified mHSPC drug combinations, progression to mCRPC is inevitable.

Currently, prognostication in mCRPC relies primarily on clinical variables, either with circulating tumor cell number (>5 per 7.5 milliliters blood)4 or with non-specific blood-based proteins such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), albumin, and hemoglobin levels5–7. Clinical factors such as the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, opioid use for pain management, and presence of visceral metastases are also included to derive prognostic risk groups in mCRPC2. While these markers offer the advantages of being validated in prospective randomized clinical trials and are easily measured in blood samples or clinical history, they are not mCRPC tumor biology-specific. Consequently, it is challenging to leverage prognostic risk groups developed from these markers to develop targeted therapeutic interventions in mCRPC patients with poor prognostic risk.

To address these limitations, efforts to integrate blood molecular profiling with the non-specific blood-based biomarkers into prognostication have emerged in the last decade. Plasma cell-free DNA (cfDNA) alterations, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), and specific genomic aberrations such as TP53 mutations, AR amplifications, and RB1 loss have shown promise as prognostic classifiers in metastatic prostate cancer8–13. While many of these genomic alterations are truncal and present in the primary tumor, their detection in ctDNA can be limited by low tumor DNA shedding in localized prostate cancer14,15. Even within the mCRPC state, clonal evolution and tumor heterogeneity can make genomic profiling challenging16. In contrast, DNA methylation has emerged as a promising biomarker due to its enhanced sensitivity and stability17,18. It is well known that DNA methylation changes arise early in tumorigenesis and span broad genomics regions that can be detected even at low tumor fractions19. Additionally, DNA methylation patterns are tissue-specific which provide insights into tissue of origin and improve cancer discrimination20.

In this study, we examined plasma cfDNA methylation profiles in 96 prostate cancer patients from the localized, organ-confined states to the metastatic states of prostate cancer progression. We employed methylation haplotype blocks (MHBs) to identify fragment level co-methylation signatures specific to the state of prostate cancer progression and then developed epigenetic prognostic classifiers for mCRPC lethality by integrating clinical and epigenetic markers. Our goal was to establish a clinical-molecular tool specific to mCRPC-biology that enhances prognostication and potentially guides personalized targeted treatment strategies based on molecular pathways associated with the aggressive mCRPC state.

Mapping characteristics of targeted methylation sequencing

We performed Enzymatic Methyl-Seq (EM-Seq) to identify 5-methylcytosine (5-mC) at CpG sites in 437 target regions selected from peer-reviewed studies, TCGA data, and prostate cancer related oncogene and tumor-suppressor gene regions (Supplementary Data 2). Across patient samples, we achieved a mean mapping efficiency of 77.4% (ranging 48.4–80.3%). Among mappable reads, 60.5% (range: 41–68%) were aligned to the target regions, and 1.9% (range: 1.1–2.7%) were duplicate alignments (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among the unique alignments in the target regions, we found that 99.3% had coverage greater than 10x and overall mean coverage was 96x (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Distribution of methylation haplotype blocks

Following alignment and methylation calling with Bismark21, we utilized mHapSuite22 to conduct an exploration of MHBs, which we defined as genomic regions containing at least three bases and a linkage disequilibrium value (r2) greater than 0.3, across patients representing different states of prostate cancer. This analysis identified a total of 3525 MHBs in the 437 target regions across all patient samples. After we excluded MHBs with less than 3 CpGs and median read counts below 50, 1530 MHBs remained for further analysis. Among these, 130 MHBs were located within positive control regions, while 16 MHBs were within negative control regions (Supplementary Fig. 3). The median methylation was 84.6% among the positive control regions and 0.3% among the negative control regions (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Among the 437 target regions, 366 were involved in genes implicated in cancers, of which 263 were linked to prostate cancer based on prior peer-reviewed studies and known oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes (Supplementary Data 2). These 366 cancer-associated regions were represented by 1194 MHBs (Supplementary Fig. 3). The mean length of these MHBs was 127 base pairs (bp) (ranging from 5 to 2100 bp) and the mean number of CpGs per MHB was 11 CpGs (ranging from 3 to 137 CpGs) (Supplementary Fig. 5). Since MHBs are defined based on linkage disequilibrium and span contiguous genomic regions, they can overlap multiple functional elements and be categorized into more than one CpG or genic feature. The 1194 MHBs were primarily located in CpG islands (76.3% of MHBs) and CpG shores (30% of MHBs). Furthermore, these regions largely consisted of promoters (47.3% of MHBs), introns (68.6% of MHBs), exons (50.3% of MHBs), and genic regions 1 to 5 Kb from transcription start sites (TSS) (53.2% of MHBs) (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Differential methylation haplotype analysis reveals progressive methylation changes throughout progression of prostate cancer

To investigate methylation changes across progressive prostate cancer states, we calculated successive CpG methylation at varying fragment lengths by determining the methylation haplotype load (MHL) across the 1194 MHBs (Supplementary Data 3)22,23. This analysis demonstrated increasing MHL values as the disease progressed from localized organ-confined prostate cancer to the metastatic states (Fig. 1A, B). To identify differentially methylated regions (DMRs) among localized prostate cancer, mHSPC, and mCRPC states, we excluded any MHBs with missing values (n = 71 MHBs). From the remaining 1123 MHBs, we partitioned 203 MHBs with MHL > 0.05 in localized prostate cancer for separate analysis. The rationale behind the filtering criteria is the low ctDNA fraction found in localized prostate cancer14,15 which could lead to low cancer-specific methylation detection in the localized state. High methylation level found at the localized state may represent contributions from non-tumor cells such as lymphocytes. From the 920 MHBs, we identified 81 DMRs across localized prostate cancer and mHSPC (two-tailed Welch’s unpaired t-test, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Data 4). When comparing mHSPC with mCRPC, we identified 699 DMRs (two-tailed Welch’s unpaired t-test, p< 0.05), of which 77 DMRs were found in common with the analysis comparing localized prostate cancer with mHSPC (two-tailed Welch’s unpaired t-test, p < 0.05, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.1) (Fig. 2B, C, Supplementary Data 5). These DMRs all demonstrated a consistent positive shift in MHL values as the disease progressed, suggesting progressive hypermethylation within target regions.

Target region methylation and clinical biomarkers are associated with overall survival

To investigate the prognostic potential of the identified DMRs with overall survival in mCRPC, we selected the top 20 of the 77 common DMRs identified in the comparative analysis between localized, mHSPC, and mCRPC states for further evaluation. Among the 49 mCRPC patients, 34 died during follow-up and had a median survival time of 17.5 months (Supplementary Data 1). Univariate Cox proportional hazards survival analyses showed that all selected 20 DMRs were significantly associated with overall survival (p < 0.05) (Table 2). To reduce redundancy and avoid overrepresentation of signals from a single gene, we excluded DMRs associated with the same gene, as these regions were highly correlated (Supplementary Fig. 7). The resulting 15 DMRs were used to generate an MHB-based composite prognostic risk score for mCRPC survival analysis. A univariate Cox analysis of the MHB composite risk score showed significant association with mCRPC survival (p < 0.05, hazard ratio (HR) = 3.8) (Fig. 3A). Patients with risk scores above the median risk score were classified as high-risk, while those with risk scores below the median were classified as low-risk. We found that mCRPC patients with high-risk scores had significantly worse overall survival. When examining the prognostic potential of the known clinical biomarkers (PSA, LDH, ALP, albumin, and hemoglobin), univariate Cox analyses showed that increased PSA, LDH, and ALP levels were associated with worse overall survival in mCRPC patients (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). In a multivariable Cox model combining PSA, LDH, and ALP, the median survival times were 16.8 months for high-risk patients and 42.8 months for low-risk patients (log-rank test, p = 0.001) (Fig. 3C).

To assess the potential to enhance mCRPC prognostication, the MHB-based composite score was integrated with prognostic clinical biomarkers. Upon multivariable analysis, integrating the MHB-based composite score with significant clinical prognostic factors from univariate Cox analyses (PSA, LDH, ALP), the MHB-based score remained significantly associated with mCRPC survival (p = 0.00369) (Fig. 3D). This multivariable model demonstrated that high-risk mCRPC patients had significantly worse overall survival than low-risk patients, with median survival times of 16.8 vs. 39.7 months respectively (log-rank test, p = 0.0022). (Fig. 3E).

Nontumor-derived methylation regions also show prognostic potential

In addition to MHBs with low methylation, we also examined the 203 MHBs with high successive methylation at localized prostate cancers (MHL > 0.05). Although these methylation signals were unlikely derived from tumor cells, the methylation changes in cfDNA could reflect tumor microenvironment and immune responses. Comparative analysis identified 37 DMRs between localized prostate cancer and mHSPC, of which 5 remained significant when comparing mHSPC with mCRPC (two-tailed Welch’s unpaired t-test, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 8). We further examined the prognostic potential of these MHBs and observed that 4 of the 5 MHBs were significantly associated with mCRPC survival in univariate Cox analysis (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Data 6). The MHB-based composite risk score based on the top 3 DMRs associated with unique gene regions also significantly distinguished mCRPC patients with different survival risks (Supplementary Fig. 9A). Combining the 3-MHB composite score with significant clinical prognostic factors also demonstrated the MHB-based score remained significantly associated with mCRPC survival and the multivariable model stratified high-risk and low-risk mCRPC patients (Supplementary Fig. 9B, C).

Methylation markers correlate with ctDNA fraction

To explore whether ctDNA fraction might influence survival predictions in this patient cohort, we utilized the online ctDNA fraction prediction tool in mCRPC state (https://www.ctdna.org)24. Predicted ctDNA fraction of patient samples was performed using the concentrations of plasma cfDNA, PSA, LDH, and ALP (Supplementary Data 1). Considering that ctDNA fraction can reflect tumor burden and disease progression25–27, we assessed whether its variation among patients would be reflected in survival predictions. In line with previous studies in mCRPC12,28,29 demonstrating that high ctDNA fraction was observed to be associated with poor prognosis, we observed that the estimated ctDNA fraction was highly associated with mCRPC survival (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A). Patients with low ctDNA fraction (<5%) had a median survival time of 42.8 months and patients with high ctDNA fraction (≥5%) had a median survival time of 14.4 months (log-rank test, p = 0.00058) (Fig. 4B). We further evaluated whether MHL values in the MHBs correlated with the predicted ctDNA fraction. Among the MHBs which were significantly associated with mCRPC survival (Cox p < 0.05) and patients with ctDNA fraction at or above 1% (n = 35), we found that the MHL values in 780 MHBs positively correlate with ctDNA fraction (Pearson r > 0.5). (Supplementary Data 7, Supplementary Fig. 10A). Interestingly beyond these MHBs, 12 MHBS were not correlated (Pearson −0.2 < r < 0.2) and 43 MHBs were inversely correlated (Pearson r < 0) with ctDNA fraction (Supplementary Data 7, Supplementary Fig. 10B, C). cfDNA methylation is an independent predictor for survival in a multi-modal risk model

Clinical variables, ctDNA fraction, and cfDNA methylation observed to be associated with survival were integrated into a single multi-modal model for prognostic performance. We first combined the ctDNA fraction and the 15 MHB-based composite score into a multivariable composite risk score. Multivariable analysis demonstrated that both factors were independently associated with overall survival (Fig. 4C). A nested model comparison using ANOVA showed that adding the 15 MHB-based composite score significantly improved fit over ctDNA alone (Likelihood ratio test = 15.451, p-value = 0.000085). Of note, in a correlation analysis between the 15 MHB-based composite score and ctDNA fraction there were 8 outlier cases which exhibited poor correlation (residual > ±1) suggesting that the MHB-based score may capture prognostic information not reflected by ctDNA alone (Supplementary Fig. 11). Together, the combined model efficiently stratified low-risk and high-risk patients, with a median survival time of 42.8 months in low-risk patients and 16.8 months in high-risk patients (log-rank test, p = 0.00043) (Fig. 4D). We then incorporated the clinical prognostic biomarkers of PSA, LDH, and ALP into the multivariable composite score to establish a multi-modal risk score. Among all survival-associated variables, the 15 MHB-based composite score remained significantly predictive of mCRPC survival (p = 0.00318) (Supplementary Fig. 12A). By integrating ctDNA fraction, methylation, and clinical biomarkers, the multi-modal risk score classified survival with the median survival time for the low-risk and the high-risk groups observed to be 42.8 months and 15.5 months, respectively (log-rank test, p = 0.00025) (Supplementary Fig. 12B). These findings suggest that methylation markers correlated with ctDNA fraction hold potential as complementary biomarkers for survival prediction in mCRPC.

Functional enrichment analysis reveals pathways associated with prostate cancer progression

To investigate the potential biological significance of the 77 DMRs identified across the stages of prostate cancer, we performed functional enrichment using GREAT (great.stanford.edu)30. This analysis revealed a significant enrichment for the Gene Ontology (GO) biological process regulation of transmembrane receptor protein serine/threonine kinase signaling pathway (GO:0090092; p = 4.27E-7, FDR = 5.62E-3).

To further dissect the epigenetic landscape associated with late-stage metastatic prostate cancer, we focused on the 272 most significant DMRs (two-tailed Welch’s unpaired t-test, p < 0.01) from the 622 DMRs identified (Fig. 2C) as uniquely associated with late-stage disease. GREAT analysis of these top mCRPC-associated DMRs revealed enrichment for the GO biological process cell fate commitment (GO:0045165; p = 1.19E-0, FDR = 1.56E-5). Additionally, we observed significant enrichment for the GO molecular functions transcriptional repressor activity, RNA polymerase II core promoter proximal region sequence-specific binding (GO:0001078; p = 1.07E-9, FDR = 4.51E-6) and steroid hormone receptor activity (GO:0003707; p = 7.02E-8, FDR = 1.48E-3).

Discussion

In this study, we profiled tumor-specific cfDNA methylation patterns to identify the DMRs that provide insights into prostate cancer progression, survival prognosis, and risk stratification. The findings reveal that a set of MHBs undergo epigenetic changes across the prostate cancer states and in advanced prostate cancer states. The enrichment of the serine/threonine kinase signaling pathway from the DMRs identified across prostate cancer states suggests that the DMRs may contribute to key mechanisms underlying disease progression as this is a pathway implicated in prostate cancer proliferation, metastasis, and treatment resistance outcomes31–34. The enrichment of cell fate commitment biological process from the DMRs associated with advanced prostate cancer may indicate the epigenetic regulation of lineage plasticity facilitating tumor adaptation and treatment resistance in advanced disease35,36. Besides the biological processes, the transcriptional repressor activity and steroid hormone receptor activity enriched among the molecular functions suggest that epigenetic alterations in mCRPC may influence transcriptional dysregulation and androgen receptor signal which are both critical in driving prostate cancer progression and therapeutic resistance37–39. Furthermore, the MHB-based composite risk score generated from the identified DMRs showed a significant association with mCRPC survival. We also observed that cfDNA methylation in the majority of target regions were correlated positively with predicted ctDNA fraction, suggesting that methylation could partially reflect tumor burden. However, we also identified a subset of regions which were not correlated or inversely correlated with ctDNA fraction. Interestingly, while the positively correlated and non-correlated MHBs had low MHL (<0.05) at localized PC, the inversely correlated MHBs had elevated MHL (>0.05) at localized PC which may be derived from tumor-associated cells such as immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. Finally, we integrated current prognostic clinical variables into the MHB-based risk score to develop patient-centric prognostic nomograms for individual mCRPC patients.

Methylation changes occur across large genomic regions, often affecting multiple CpG sites within a locus and generating a stronger cumulative signal that enhances detection sensitivity and provides insights into tumor biology40. These changes occur early in tumorigenesis41,42 and can change to reflect environmental and treatment exposures43,44, while still expressing tissue-specific or tumor-specific patterns that allow for identification of the tissue of origin20,45. This makes methylation profiling an attractive biomarker strategy particularly when ctDNA fractions are low during early-stage or minimal residual disease and fall below the detection limits of conventional ctDNA quantification methods, leading to false negatives. These quantification methods have been well reported using copy number variations (CNVs)46–48 and specific mutations49–51 but have limitations as they may lack sensitivity (CNV-based) or require high sequencing depth to capture rare mutant alleles52 because of variability of mutations across tumor types and disease states53. Furthermore, methylation patterns can capture inter-patient and intra-tumor heterogeneity that may not be reflected in ctDNA fraction alone, as samples with similar ctDNA levels can still exhibit distinct methylation profiles. Identifying methylation markers can therefore provide valuable insights into dynamic tumor biology, complementing established biomarkers to enhance risk stratification, guide therapeutic decisions, and uncover potential drug targets. This translational potential is exemplified by two regions included in the 15 MHB-based score—associated with ALOX5 and CDKN2A—which are currently under investigation as direct or indirect targets for prostate cancer in preclinical studies and a clinical trial (NCT05113537)54,55.

Exploring the potential for clinical translation, this study developed prognostic nomograms as patient-centric tools to predict mCRPC survival. Prognostic risk groups can classify patients into broad groups based on categorical variables by simplifying continuous data into categories. While this is helpful, it does not account for the unique combination of non-specific clinical variables present in each patient and potentially overlooks patient-level and tumor-biology based nuances. Unlike categorical risk-grouping, a nomogram-based approach handles categorical and continuous variables more efficiently. Nomograms offer a visual, individualized risk prediction approach that integrates multiple prognostic variables, making them more interpretable and clinically actionable than standard risk models56,57. Although nomograms can be built using clinical variables alone, as indicated by their strong prognostic value in this study, the inclusion of tumor-specific methylation markers obtained through non-invasive blood draws enhances the model’s prognostic accuracy. Furthermore, since clinical biomarkers such as ALP and LDH are not specific to tumor biology they lack the molecular context that tumor-specific methylation can provide. Methylation profiling can provide insights into potential disease mechanisms and inform treatment strategies.

The potential of cfDNA methylation as a critical biomarker in assessing treatment response and clinical outcomes cfDNA methylation in prostate cancer detection, prognosis, and monitoring58–61 has been reported. In one study dynamic methylated changes associated with disease progression and treatment response in metastatic prostate cancer58 were observed. An independent study demonstrated that methylation patterns can serve as a proxy for tumor fraction in metastatic prostate cancer59. A tumor tissue-informed approach showed that limited methylation markers are capable of differentiating treatment-responsive from non-responsive mCRPC patients and predicting progression-free survival in mCRPC patients60. Lastly, a study employing a genome-wide approach identified methylation signatures that could distinguish between localized and metastatic cancer. Furthermore, methylation level at a gene promoter region was associated with progression-free survival and overall survival61. Building on these findings, this study incorporated cfDNA methylation into an integrative prognostic model to explore a tumor biology-based patient-centric application for cfDNA methylation in mCRPC prognostication. Going beyond traditional non-specific clinical variables, cfDNA methylation reflects altered tumor biology-based features that may provide future opportunities for therapeutic targeting in the mCRPC state.

There are several advantages to using a targeted rather than a whole genome methylation sequencing approach. First, the targeted EM-seq is more sensitive, cost-effective, and avoids DNA degradation associated with bisulfite-based sequencing approaches62,63. This enhanced sensitivity allows for the detection of subtle methylation differences that may be missed by traditional methods. Second, prior studies have typically analyzed mean methylation levels across sequencing reads within predefined genomic windows or specific target regions59,60,64. This approach may overlook the heterogeneity of methylation patterns within individual DNA fragments, further masking variations in methylation haplotypes that may have functional relevance in cancer progression. In this study, we leveraged MHBs which captured the coordinated methylation patterns across multiple CpGs within the same DNA fragment23 and in different states of cancer progression. By considering read-level methylation haplotypes and including samples collected from different progressive cancer states, a more granular view of regions undergoing tumor-specific epigenetic alterations in mCRPC could be obtained since the MHBs also consider interactions between CpG sites. Third, we generated a patient-centric prognostic nomogram, a methodology which has not commonly employed in prior cfDNA methylation studies, providing an intuitive, clinically actionable framework for survival prediction.

This study highlights promising findings as a proof-of-principle but has several limitations. The small sample size limits the generalizability of the results, and future validation in larger, independent cohorts is necessary to confirm the study approach and the robustness of the prognostic models. Additionally, the detection of methylation signals in cfDNA may be influenced by ctDNA levels, as regions with low ctDNA abundance may fall below the detection threshold, potentially biasing the observed methylation patterns toward those more readily detectable in advanced disease. While we attempted to account for this by analyzing the correlation between methylation and predicted ctDNA fraction, this study relied on predicted rather than directly measured ctDNA levels, which may introduce uncertainty. A direct ctDNA quantification alongside methylation profiling could provide a better understanding of their relationship. Nonetheless, the findings in this study highlight the potential of cfDNA methylation profiling as a clinically relevant tool for prognostication and disease monitoring in prostate cancer. These cfDNA methylation signatures could complement existing prognostic models to offer tumor-biology-based prognostic tools. Since cfDNA-based testing is non-invasive, scalable, and allows for longitudinal monitoring without the need for tissue biopsies, if validated this approach will aid in the development of patient-centric, biology-informed nomograms for individualized risk stratification.

Methods

Patient cohort

This study used plasma samples from a prospective, clinically annotated, real-world patient biobank. To avoid preanalytical variations, the sample collection followed uniform standard operating procedures (SOPs). All SOPs include a uniform blood-based collection of specimens in patients visiting Huntsman Cancer Institute (HCI). Prostate cancer patients receiving treatments are approached under institutional biospecimen collection Institutional Review Board (IRB)-approved study of “Total Cancer Care” (IRB# 00089989; IRB# 00139755) and written informed consent is obtained. In addition, the study (#MCC 20563) also received approval by the Moffitt Cancer Center IRB. Collection of blood is performed under Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) supervision of the Biorepository and Molecular Pathology (BMP) Core facility at HCI and routed to the investigator’s research lab for uniform processing. All collections used in this study are performed in EDTA tubes and plasma generated from platelet-poor fractions used for proteomic analyses and processing is completed within two hours of collection and banked in −80 °C and with no freeze-thaws. Granular clinical annotation is obtained from EMRs with data dictionaries.

Blood collection and cfDNA extraction

Whole blood collected in 4.5 mL EDTA tubes from all patients in the biobank was subjected to centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 min and a second centrifugation of the supernatant at 3000 rpm for 10 min to make platelet-poor plasma. 0.5–1 mL fractions of the plasma samples were stored at −80 °C until use. 1–2 mL of plasma was used for automated cfDNA extraction with the Maxwell RSC ccfDNA Plasma Kit (Promega, #AS1480). cfDNA quantification was performed using Qubit.

Capture probe design

437 targeted methylation regions were selected from three major sources, including (1) Literature search identified 184 DMRs associated with prostate cancer65–69; (2) TCGA-PRAD20 data showed 103 DMRs when compared to normal controls. The selection criteria used were a difference in mean methylation β value of > 0.4 between prostate tumor tissues and normal tissues, and a mean methylation β value < 0.05 in plasma cfDNA from healthy controls; and (3) Prostate cancer-related oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes (n = 79) (Supplementary Data 2). We also included 29 positive and 42 negative control regions. The positive and negative control regions were selected based on regions that had consistently shown >95% methylation level and <0.5% methylation level in cfDNA from healthy controls20, respectively. Overall, we selected 437 targeted regions, including 366 prostate cancer-related regions, 29 positive control, and 42 negative control regions. The selected regions covered all CpG islands of targeted genes and +/-5Kb flanking sequences. All capture probes were designed by Twist Custom Design Service.

EM-seq library preparation and target capture

10–20 ng of cfDNA was subjected to Enzymatic Methyl-seq (EM-seq) (NEB, #E7120S). Briefly, the cfDNA was first ligated with methylated adaptors, followed by oxidation using TET2 reaction and deamination using APOBEC reaction. Indexed primers were then used for 8–10 cycles of amplification. To enrich regions of interest, whole genome sequencing libraries were captured with a custom-made Twist targeted methylome panel covering 3.44 Mbp of genome. The captured libraries were further amplified for an additional 5 cycles before performing 150 bp paired-end (PE) sequencing in an Illumina sequencer.

Data preprocessing and methylation calling

Quality control checks were performed with fastqc (v0.11.9) and multiqc (v1.12) on the FASTQ files. Illumina adapter sequences were trimmed with cutadapt (v2.8), with a filter applied to discard reads shorter than 20 bp. Alignment to the human reference genome hg38/GRCh38 and methylation calling were performed on the processed reads using Bismark (v0.22.3) with bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1)21. Alignment parameters were set to a multi-seed length of 22 bp with 1 mismatch (-L 11, -N 1). Deduplication to remove alignments at the same position in the mapped genome was performed with Bismark (deduplicate_bismark). Mappable reads and duplicate alignments were extracted from Bismark alignment reports. To evaluate sequencing coverage in target regions, mosdepth (v0.3.3) was utilized to generate coverage profiles for each sample.

Methylation haplotype blocks (MHBs) identification and methylation haplotype load (MHL) analysis

Methylation haplotypes were extracted by converting deduplicated BAM files into mHAP format with mHapSuite (v2.1) (convert), available at https://github.com/yoyoong/mHapSuite22. MHBs were then identified with mHapSuite (MHBDiscovery) within the target regions (±5Kb) across all patients, based on the parameters of a core window of 3, and r2 cutoff of 0.3, and p-value cutoff of 0.05. Identification of differential MHBs

Prior to identifying which MHBs are differentially methylated, MHBs missing MHL values were excluded. Prediction of ctDNA fraction and correlation with MHL

Predicted plasma ctDNA fractions for mCRPC patients (n = 49) were determined by inputting cfDNA, PSA, LDH, ALP, ECOG Performance Score, liver metastasis, and lung metastasis information for each patient into the online tool https://www.ctdna.org24. Patients with <1% predicted ctDNA fraction (n = 14) were excluded from correlation analyses. Among the remaining mCRPC patients (n = 35) Pearson correlation was performed between the predicted ctDNA fraction and the 15 MHB-based composite score, as well as with the MHL of each MHB that had no missing values. MHBs with Pearson r > 0.5 were identified as positively correlated with predicted ctDNA fraction. MHBs with Pearson r > −0.2 and < 0.2 were identified as not correlated with predicted ctDNA fraction. MHBs with Pearson r < 0 were identified as inversely correlated with predicted ctDNA fraction. The plots depicting the correlation between ctDNA fraction and MHBs were generated with the tidyverse and ggpubr (v0.6.0) R package.

Nomogram development

To estimate individual survival probabilities, survival nomograms were built based on univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. These models quantified the relationship between survival outcomes and multiple predictors. The Cox coefficients derived from each model were used to assign point values, allowing for the calculation of a total score that reflects an individual’s overall survival risk profile. Model performance was evaluated using 100x repeated 5-fold cross validation. Mean time-dependent Receiver Operating Characteristic Area on the Curve (ROC AUC) and standard deviation were computed across repetitions.

The nomograms were developed based on the identified predictors for predicting the 0.5-, 1- and 2-year survival, and each predictor was represented on a separate axis in the nomogram. 0.5-, 1- or 2-year survival probability is acquired by calculating the total score of all predictors. For a given predictor, the number of points can be determined by drawing a vertical line from the measured predictor value to the ‘points’ scale at the top of the nomogram. The sum of the points from each predictor is equal to the total points score. After determining the total points score, survival probability can be determined by drawing a vertical line from the total points score in the ‘total points’ scale to the survival probability scales at the bottom of the nomogram. Intersection with a survival probability scale (0.5-, 1-, 2-year) indicates the patient’s predicted probability of survival (0–0.9). Nomograms were constructed with the rms (6.8.0) R package. Time-dependent ROC curves were determined and plotted with the timeROC R (0.4) package.
