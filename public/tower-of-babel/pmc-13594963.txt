Association of circulating miR-210 with post-stroke seizures and clinical outcome: A preliminary study
Authors: Yasaman Enayati, Reza Daneshvar kakhki, Mohammad Kazemi, Akram Yazdani, Hossein Motedayyen, Mahyar Noorbakhsh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-stroke seizure (PSS) is characterized by sudden, uncontrolled electrical activity in the brain following a stroke. Currently, reliable biomarkers for PSS are lacking. This study aimed to investigate serum levels of microRNA-210 (miR-210) as potential diagnostic and prognostic markers for PSS.MethodsSerum samples (2 mL) were collected from 40 patients with ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke (IS and HS) upon admission, along with 20 healthy controls. Serum miR-210 levels were measured using real-time PCR. Diagnostic and prognostic accuracy was assessed for miR-210 and the PoSERS model. Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, stroke type, and stroke location, were recorded. The laboratory parameters, including white blood cells (WBCs), C-reactive protein (CRP), and erythrocyte sediment rate (ESR), were also assessed.ResultsSignificant differences were observed in stroke types (IS vs. HS) and stroke locations (cortical vs. subcortical) between patients with and without PSS (P < 0.05). Additionally, triglyceride (TG), HbA1C, and cholesterol levels differed significantly between these groups (P < 0.05). Serum miR-210 levels were significantly lower in stroke patients compared to healthy controls (P < 0.05). After one year of follow-up, miR-210 levels significantly differed between patients with and without PSS, as well as between survivors and non-survivors (P < 0.05). A negative correlation was found between circulating miR-210 and total cholesterol levels in stroke patients (r = -0.338, P < 0.05). However, miR-210 showed limited predictive value for PSS (AUC = 0.48), whereas the PoSERS model demonstrated good predictive performance (AUC = 0.77).ConclusionsAlthough circulating miR-210 was significantly associated with PSS and disease outcome, it alone does not serve as a reliable biomarker for early diagnosis of PSS. Further studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to validate these findings.Keywords: MiR-210, Stroke, Post-stroke seizure, PoSERS model

Introduction

Post-stroke seizure (PSS) refers to a sudden, uncontrolled burst of electrical activity in the brain that occurs in individuals without a prior history of seizures. PSS accounts for nearly 30–50% of newly diagnosed seizures, and the risk of developing seizures during the first year after stroke is estimated to be about 23 times higher than in the general population (Tanaka and Ihara, 2017, Strzelczyk et al., 2010a). Early seizures occur within the first week—most commonly within 24 h—whereas late seizures appear after the first week, with the highest incidence between 6 and 12 months post stroke (Strzelczyk et al., 2010a). Because approximately 90% of affected patients are at risk of recurrence (Tanaka and Ihara, 2017), early identification of patients at higher risk is essential for timely management and preventing seizure‑associated complications.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), small non‑coding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally (Motedayyen et al., 2015, Sattari et al., 2021, Arefnezhad et al., 2024, Goleij et al., 2024a, Fahimi et al., 2025), have emerged as promising non-invasive biomarkers in neurological disorders (Ghotloo et al., 2019, Jafari et al., 2024, Chahardehi et al., 2024, Goleij et al., 2024b).Among them, miR‑210—a hypoxia-induced miRNA—has been extensively studied for its role in ischemic brain injury, angiogenesis, cellular metabolism, and neuroinflammatory responses (Lou et al., 2012, Aderinto et al., 2024). Experimental studies have suggested that dysregulation of miR‑210 may influence pathways involved in neuronal survival, inflammation, and epileptogenesis (Zhou et al., 2022, Huang et al., 2018, Chen et al., 2016), making it a potential candidate biomarker in post-stroke conditions.

Although several clinical prediction models exist for estimating the risk of developing PSS after ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke(IS and HS)—including post-stroke epilepsy risk score (PoSERS), PSEiCARe, SeLECT, CAVE, and LANE (Tanaka et al., 2024). PoSERS is a useful tool to predict the risk of seizure within the first few days after a stroke (Strzelczyk et al., 2010b). It is superior to other models in specificity and sensitivity in the early prediction of seizures caused by IS and HS stroke (Zou and Chen, 2020). However, no gold standard model has been yet proposed for predicting and diagnosing seizures after stroke. However, the currently available prediction models have only moderate predictive performance and none has been universally accepted for routine clinical use. There is a lack of established, sensitive, and specific non-invasive approaches such as serum biomarkers which can help to diagnose and predict PSS.

Given the involvement of miR‑210 in hypoxia-related molecular processes and neuronal injury, the present study aimed to assess whether serum miR‑210 levels differ between patients with IS, HS, and healthy individuals, and to explore its potential utility as a biomarker for early prediction of PSS.

Materials and methods

Study populations

The study population consisted of 40 subjects with stroke and 20 healthy subjects. The diagnosis of stroke was confirmed by a neurologist using diagnostic criteria (Powers et al., 2019). Inclusion criteria were: 1) subjects with stroke diagnosis; and 2) the absence of health problems, malignancy, and other disorders affecting the expression level of miR-210 in patients with stroke. Exclusion criteria included: 1) the use of anti-epileptic drugs; 2) history of epilepsy and/or seizure before stroke; 3) the presence of other risk factors related to seizure in patients with stroke; and 4) the presence of health problems and other abnormalities influencing the expression level of miR-210 in healthy volunteers. The diagnosis of PSS was established by experienced neurologists during scheduled follow-up visits based on clinical history, witness descriptions, neurological examination, and available medical records. EEG was performed whenever clinically indicated but was not available for all patients. Demographic data, including participants’ age, gender, and ethnicity, were obtained through a standardized questionnaire. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kashan University of Medical Sciences (IR.KAUMS.MEDNT.REC.1402.218) which was by the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants or legally authorized representatives before taking part in the study.

Sample collection and preparation

Peripheral venous blood (5 mL) was collected from each participant using sterile plain serum collection tubes containing a clot activator (red-top or yellow-top tubes) without anticoagulants and separation gel. The samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min and were subsequently centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min to separate the serum. The serum was carefully transferred into RNase-free microcentrifuge tubes, aliquoted to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles, and stored at −80°C until RNA extraction.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

For miR-210 expression analysis, total RNAs were isolated from the serum samples using a blood RNA isolation kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Parstous, Iran, Catalog number: A101231). The yield and purity of RNA were determined using a microplate reader (Biotek Epoch, USA). Afterward, complementary deoxyribonucleic acid (cDNA) was synthesized using the Bon-Mir RT kit (Bonyakhteh, Tehran, Iran, Catalog number: BON001027SL) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. qPCR assay was carried out using an ABI7700 machine (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA) and the SYBR Green master mix (Parstous, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each qPCR run was done as follows: initial 2 min of incubation at 95 °C followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. All analyses were performed in duplicate. The threshold cycle (Ct) was calculated by the Applied Biosystems software and melting curves were automatically generated at the end of each run. U6 RNA expression level, as an endogenous control, was employed to normalize the miR-210 expression level of each sample. The PCR cycling conditions for U6 RNA were identical to those used for miR-210, consisting of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min followed by 40 amplification cycles at 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. The comparative ΔΔCt method was used to analyze the quantitative expression level of the miR-210 gene and 2-ΔΔCt was employed to show fold change in gene expression (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

The assessment of laboratory parameters

The white blood cells (WBCs) were counted using a BioRad TC20 Automated Cell Counter (Hercules, CA, USA) within several hours after collecting EDTA-treated blood samples (5 mL). An immunoturbidimetric assay kit (PishtazTeb Zaman, Iran) was used to determine the serum concentration of C-reactive protein (CRP) using an automated biochemistry analyzer (Mindray BS-800, China). The values of erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were determined by an ESR analyzer (ParsianTeb, Iran).

Follow-up

A total of 40 subjects with stroke or their families were followed up by telephone and visited every three months, lasting for one year.

Statistical analysis

The results were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software, USA) and are represented as mean±standard deviation (SD). The normal distribution of the data was evaluated using the D′Agostino-Pearson test. According to the non-normal distribution of data, the Mann–Whitney test was employed to compare the two groups. The Spearman’s test was used to determine the correlation coefficients of the data. Multivariate analyses were performed using multiple binary logistic regression models. The correlations were studied by the Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) area under the curve (AUC) was used to determine the specificity, sensitivity, negative predictive value (NPV), and positive predictive value (PPV) of miR-210and PoSERS model in the early diagnosis of PSS. P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All seizure events occurred at least one week after stroke onset and were therefore classified as late PSS. No significant difference was observed in age and gender between patient and healthy groups (Table 3). Significant differences were observed between patients with and without PSS regarding lesion location, triglyceride (TG), HbA1c, and cholesterol levels (P < 0.05, Table 3). Discussion

Neuroinflammation is increasingly recognized as a central mechanism underlying both stroke progression and epileptogenesis. Consequently, the identification of reliable biomarkers capable of reflecting neuroinflammatory activity may facilitate early risk stratification, optimize the timing of anti-inflammatory interventions, and improve the identification of patients at increased risk of developing PSS. A growing body of evidence indicates that circulating biomarkers provide a minimally invasive approach for monitoring the dynamic molecular changes associated with stroke and seizure, thereby facilitating the translation of experimental findings into clinical practice (Bustamante et al., 2016). Against this background, the present study investigated whether circulating miR-210, a hypoxia-responsive microRNA, could serve as a biomarker for the early prediction of PSS in patients with IS and HS.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that cortical stroke is associated with a substantially higher risk of seizure than subcortical stroke (Zelano et al., 2020, Phan et al., 2022). Consistent with these observations, all patients who developed PSS in the present study had cortical lesions. Although the cerebral cortex is traditionally regarded as the primary site of seizure initiation, accumulating evidence suggests that epileptic activity involves a complex network of interactions between cortical and subcortical structures that contribute to seizure propagation and behavioral manifestations (Norden and Blumenfeld, 2002, Badawy et al., 2013, Streng and Krook-Magnuson, 2021). Cortical lesions may increase seizure susceptibility because of their high neuronal density, extensive synaptic connectivity, and greater propensity for excitatory neurotransmission (Zelano et al., 2020, Phan et al., 2022, Kammersgaard and Olsen, 2005). Furthermore, anatomical and pathophysiological differences between cortical and subcortical regions may explain the greater epileptogenic potential of cortical infarcts (Daniele et al., 1996). We also observed a higher frequency of seizures among patients with HS than among those with IS, which is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that intracerebral hemorrhage carries a greater risk of PSS than ischemic stroke (Zhang et al., 2014, Bladin et al., 2000, Galovic et al., 2021). This increased susceptibility has been attributed to blood-brain barrier disruption, blood degradation products, oxidative stress, and exaggerated neuroinflammatory responses following hemorrhage. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of stroke subtype and lesion location in determining seizure risk and further support the need for reliable, non-invasive biomarkers capable of identifying patients at increased risk of developing PSS.

Because miR-210 is a hypoxia-inducible microRNA involved in oxidative stress regulation, angiogenesis, apoptosis, and neuroprotection, we next investigated its circulating expression in patients with stroke (McCormick et al., 2013, Ivan and Huang, 2013, Jiang et al., 2015). Our results demonstrated that serum miR-210 expression was significantly reduced in patients with both IS and HS compared with healthy controls. This finding agrees with previous clinical studies reporting decreased circulating miR-210 levels in patients with ischemic stroke (Zeng et al., 2011, Rahmati et al., 2021). Moreover, downregulation of miR-210 may contribute to stroke severity through dysregulation of TP53-mediated cellular responses involved in DNA repair and apoptosis (Wang et al., 2016). In contrast, several experimental studies have reported increased miR-210 expression following cerebral ischemia or hypoxia (Aderinto et al., 2024, Ma, 2018). For example, inhibition of miR-210 has been shown to attenuate post-stroke neuroinflammation, reduce cerebral injury, and improve long-term neurological recovery in experimental stroke models (Huang et al., 2018). The apparent discrepancy between clinical and experimental findings is likely attributable to differences in study design, biological specimens, disease stage, and species. Whereas animal studies generally quantify miR-210 expression directly in ischemic brain tissue under acute hypoxic conditions, clinical investigations, including the present study, measure circulating miR-210 in peripheral blood, which may reflect different biological processes and temporal patterns of expression.

An additional methodological consideration concerns the selection of an appropriate endogenous reference gene for circulating miRNA quantification. In the present study, U6 RNA was selected as the internal control because it demonstrated consistent amplification across all analyzed samples and has been widely used as an endogenous reference in numerous previous studies investigating circulating miRNAs, including studies of stroke. The use of U6 also facilitates direct comparison of our findings with the existing literature. Nevertheless, increasing evidence suggests that U6 expression may not be sufficiently stable in extracellular biofluids, potentially introducing variability into circulating miRNA measurements. Therefore, although our normalization strategy is consistent with many previously published studies (Fahimi et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2010, Grigolashvili et al., 2025, Xiang et al., 2014), future investigations should validate more stable endogenous reference genes or incorporate exogenous spike-in controls, such as cel-miR-39, to further improve the accuracy, robustness, and reproducibility of circulating miRNA quantification.

The association between circulating miR-210 expression and the development of PSS was further explored during the one-year follow-up period. Serum miR-210 levels were significantly lower in patients who subsequently developed PSS than in those who remained seizure-free. However, ROC curve analysis demonstrated only modest diagnostic performance, indicating that circulating miR-210 alone is insufficient to reliably predict PSS in the early post-stroke period. This apparent discrepancy most likely reflects the extremely small number of patients who developed PSS during follow-up, which may produce statistically significant group differences while limiting discrimination in ROC analysis. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the limited sample size substantially reduced statistical power and increased the risk of type II error. Consequently, the estimated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and AUC should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution rather than as definitive measures of diagnostic performance. All three patients who developed PSS experienced their first seizure at least one week after stroke onset and were therefore classified as having late PSS. Therefore, the limited diagnostic performance observed in the present study should not be interpreted as definitive evidence against a potential predictive role of circulating miR-210 but rather as an indication that additional studies with larger sample sizes are required.

Interestingly, patients who developed PSS exhibited significantly higher inflammatory indices, including elevated WBC count, neutrophil count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), than patients without seizures. These findings are consistent with the growing body of evidence supporting a critical role of neuroinflammation in epileptogenesis following stroke. Activation of resident microglia and astrocytes, together with infiltration of peripheral immune cells, promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, resulting in blood-brain barrier disruption, neuronal hyperexcitability, and enhanced seizure susceptibility (Vannucci, 2000, Ma et al., 2016, Li et al., 2023, Lekoubou et al., 2021). Accordingly, systemic inflammatory markers may complement circulating miRNAs in identifying patients at increased risk of developing PSS.

In addition to seizure occurrence, circulating miR-210 expression was associated with clinical outcome. Non-survivors exhibited significantly higher circulating miR-210 levels than survivors. This finding may reflect a compensatory upregulation of miR-210 in response to more severe hypoxic and inflammatory brain injury rather than a direct pathogenic effect of the molecule itself. Given that miR-210 is activated under hypoxic conditions and participates in adaptive pathways involved in angiogenesis, mitochondrial metabolism, oxidative stress regulation, and apoptosis (McCormick et al., 2013, Ivan and Huang, 2013, Jiang et al., 2015, Rahmati et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2016, Ma, 2018), its increased expression may represent an endogenous protective response to extensive neuronal damage. Nevertheless, despite this apparent compensatory activation, the magnitude of brain injury in patients with poor outcomes may exceed the protective capacity of miR-210. Therefore, elevated circulating miR-210 may reflect the severity of hypoxic brain injury rather than indicate successful neuroprotection. This association should, however, be interpreted cautiously until confirmed in larger prospective studies.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, only three patients developed PSS during the one-year follow-up period, substantially limiting the statistical power and reducing the generalizability of the findings regarding the predictive performance of circulating miR-210. Second, the relatively small number of patients with HS limited subgroup analyses, particularly because HS is associated with a higher risk of PSS than IS. Third, although U6 RNA was selected as the endogenous reference because it demonstrated reproducible amplification across all analyzed samples and has been widely used in previous studies investigating circulating miRNAs, including studies of stroke, its expression may not be sufficiently stable in extracellular biofluids. Therefore, future studies should validate more robust endogenous reference genes or incorporate exogenous spike-in controls, such as cel-miR-39, to improve the reliability of circulating miRNA quantification. In addition, electroencephalographic (EEG) confirmation was not available for all patients with suspected PSS. Consequently, seizure diagnosis relied primarily on clinical assessment by neurologists during routine follow-up, and the possibility of seizure misclassification cannot be completely excluded. Finally, although the one-year follow-up period captured the time interval during which the majority of PSS are expected to occur, with the incidence declining substantially thereafter, a longer follow-up might still identify the small proportion of patients who develop late-onset seizures beyond the first year after stroke. Therefore, larger multicenter prospective studies with extended follow-up, standardized EEG confirmation, validated normalization strategies for circulating miRNA analysis, and a greater representation of patients with HS are warranted to establish the clinical utility of circulating miR-210 as a biomarker for post-stroke seizures.

Conclusion

Taken together, the present findings provide preliminary evidence that circulating miR-210 is associated with stroke severity, neuroinflammatory responses, and clinical outcome but does not demonstrate sufficient diagnostic performance to serve as a reliable standalone biomarker for the early prediction of post-stroke seizures. Given the limited number of patients who developed PSS, these findings should be considered exploratory and require validation in larger prospective multicenter cohorts. Future studies incorporating standardized EEG confirmation, optimized normalization strategies for circulating miRNA analysis, and a greater proportion of patients with hemorrhagic stroke will be essential to clarify the diagnostic and prognostic value of circulating miR-210 in post-stroke epilepsy.
