Introduction

Cancer remains a leading contributor to premature mortality globally, with escalating incidence rates posing substantial public health challenges [1–3 ] Radiotherapy serves as a cornerstone non-invasive intervention for solid tumors, administered to approximately 50% of cancer patients as adjuvant or radical treatment. While technological advancements in treatment precision have been achieved, radiation-induced toxicities persist as an unavoidable consequence, with radiation dermatitis (RD) representing the most prevalent complication [4–6]. Clinical studies document that over 90% of radiotherapy recipients develop varying degrees of RD [7], a condition that progressively intensifies during treatment and exerts detrimental impacts on quality of life, necessitating vigilant monitoring throughout therapeutic regimens [5, 8, 9].

The pathophysiological cascade of RD initiates with cellular damage at treatment commencement, though clinical manifestations typically emerge 1–4 weeks post-radiation initiation. Subsequent radiation fractions often exacerbate symptoms, with complete resolution generally occurring 2–4 weeks post-treatment [10–12]. Radiation-induced compromise of cutaneous barrier function enhances dermal susceptibility to cumulative damage, potentially expanding both the affected surface area and severity grade [13]. Clinical presentations range from erythema and edema to desquamation, with severe cases progressing to ulcerative or necrotic lesions that may mandate treatment suspension while substantially impairing patient wellbeing. Studies indicate that the incidence of severe RD is 34.8%, with 13% of affected patients discontinuing radiation therapy as a result [14]. A meta-analysis of five randomized controlled trials, involving 218 patients, demonstrated that glutamine can reduce the overall risk of radiation-induced dermatitis (relative risk [RR] = 0.90, p = 0.05). This effect was even more pronounced in preventing moderate to severe cases (RR = 0.49, p = 0.001). Further analysis revealed that a daily dose of 20–30 g of glutamine offers the most effective prevention (RR = 0.60), with highly consistent results across studies [15]. These findings underscore the importance of timely intervention to slow the progression of RD and improve patient outcomes.

Proactive management strategies demonstrate capacity to mitigate symptom burden, arrest pathological progression, and minimize infection risks. Effective dermatological intervention not only alleviates physical discomfort but also reduces psychological distress while reinforcing therapeutic adherence [16–19]. These considerations underscore the critical need for precise assessment and timely intervention in RD management [8, 12, 20]. Current clinical practice predominantly employs visual assessment protocols dependent on clinician expertise for RD grading. This subjective methodology introduces substantial inter-observer variability influenced by diagnostic experience and evaluation criteria, potentially compromising grading accuracy and subsequent clinical decision-making [21, 22]. The establishment of standardized, objective assessment frameworks therefore represents an urgent unmet need in radiation oncology.

Emerging artificial intelligence (AI) technologies present transformative potential for dermatological evaluation, particularly in radiation-induced skin toxicity [23–25]. Unlike the subjectivity and inconsistency inherent in human assessments, AI can eliminate these issues to enable accurate and efficient evaluation of RD. Moreover, machine learning (ML) algorithms demonstrate particular promise in predictive analytics, integrating radiotherapy dosimetry, and clinical parameters to identify high-risk patients and predict dermatitis progression. Such predictive modeling enables preemptive, personalized interventions that may reduce severe toxicity incidence while optimizing treatment continuity and quality of life.

This review critically evaluates the limitations of conventional assessment methods while demonstrating the clinical applicability of AI in automated RD evaluation through cross-dermatosis comparative analyses. Furthermore, it pioneers the novel application of AI-powered predictive analytics for radiation toxicity endpoints, providing scientific foundations for personalized therapeutic decision-making. By identifying current knowledge gaps and methodological constraints, this work delineates strategic research priorities to advance intelligent RD management systems.

Method

As this is a narrative review, we conducted a targeted literature search to synthesize representative evidence on AI applications for RD. Literature was retrieved from PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases (search period: January 2018–January 2025) using keyword combinations: [“artificial intelligence” OR “machine learning” OR “deep learning”] AND [“radiation dermatitis” OR “radiation-induced skin toxicity”].

We prioritized studies developing or validating AI models for RD assessment (e.g., automated grading, severity prediction) using clinical images or integrating clinical variables (e.g., radiotherapy dose, patient demographics) for personalized management, as well as those reporting clinical application data (e.g., diagnostic accuracy vs. clinicians, workflow efficiency improvements). Relevant predictive models were also included to provide a broader context. Studies were excluded if they focused solely on algorithm development without clinical validation or lacked RD-specific application scenarios (e.g., general dermatological AI models not validated for RD).

Overview of radiation dermatitis

RD is an inflammatory skin reaction triggered by exposure to ionizing radiation, including β-rays, γ-rays, and X-rays [26, 27]. Clinically categorized by temporal progression, acute RD manifests within 90 days post-irradiation, whereas chronic RD develops beyond this timeframe [5, 7, 26]. This review focuses on acute RD due to its higher prevalence and clinical urgency.

Radiation exposure directly damages skin cells [26] ionizing radiation induces direct DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) via secondary electron interactions, while also causing indirect oxidative damage through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [7, 26, 28, 29]. Cumulative radiation fractions trigger progressive inflammatory infiltration, disrupting tissue architecture and compromising barrier integrity [30]. Notably, basal keratinocytes and activated hair follicle stem cells exhibit heightened radiosensitivity due to their proliferative activity, whereas melanocytes are susceptible to radiation-induced oxidative damage [26, 27, 30]. Radiation-triggered apoptosis in these cells critically impairs epidermal regenerative capacity. Repeated radiation exposure overwhelms cutaneous repair mechanisms, culminating in dermatitis progression. Dermal vascular injury precipitates capillary dilation and histamine-mediated erythema persisting 3–4 weeks [7, 31]. Differences in radiation dose also significantly influence the severity of skin reactions. At moderate doses, the skin initiates compensatory repair mechanisms through accelerated basal layer keratinocyte proliferation, leading to epidermal thickening and dry desquamation [32]. Conversely, at high doses, the basal layer undergoes irreversible regenerative failure, manifesting as moist desquamation with exudate formation that exacerbates inflammatory cascades [27, 31–34].

Current management strategies, per MASCC guidelines in The Lancet Oncology, emphasize preventive barriers (Mepitel/Hydro films) for breast cancer patients and anti-inflammatory interventions (topical corticosteroids, photobiomodulation) [35]. The Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) grading system categorizes RD severity levels to direct clinical interventions [8] (Table 1). Grade 1 RD (RTOG criteria, Fig. 1), requires standard skincare with emollients and topical corticosteroids. Grades 2–3 RD necessitate infection prophylaxis through desquamated area dressings; while silver sulfadiazine demonstrates variable efficacy, systemic antibiotics remain restricted to confirmed infections. To quantify the severity of RD, the most commonly used toxicity scoring systems are the Common Toxicity Criteria Adverse Event (CTCAE) and RTOG toxicity scoring system [22]. CTCAE emphasizes pan-body toxicity evaluation, whereas RTOG criteria – the radiation oncology specialty standard – concentrate on tissue-specific pathological alterations to optimize therapeutic guidance [36]. However, these rating systems rely on visual assessment, which is susceptible to substantial subjectivity and inter-assessor variability—largely stemming from inconsistent interpretation of clinical symptoms and application of grading criteria [22].

The scoring system relies on visual assessment, and factors such as the assessor’s experience, sensitivity to symptoms, timing of assessment, and individual patient differences can lead to variability in scoring outcomes [36]. Additionally, ambiguities in defining erythema/desquamation severity thresholds and inconsistent grading criteria application further compound these discrepancies. These limitations have catalyzed emerging AI applications for RD assessment, aiming to standardize grading precision and predictive performance.

Overview of AI technologies

AI techniques, particularly deep learning (DL) methods, have demonstrated transformative potential in automated assessment of dermatologic conditions through computer vision and image processing [24, 37–40]. AI automatically extracts features from large-scale skin image data using computer vision and image processing technologies, enabling early screening, grading, and monitoring of disease progression, while significantly improving diagnostic objectivity and accuracy [24, 25, 37, 39, 41, 42]. For example, in diagnosing melanoma, psoriasis, and other common skin conditions, AI has demonstrated high efficiency and accuracy, with diagnostic performance in certain areas approaching or even surpassing that of some dermatologists [24, 37, 41–43]. In studies on melanoma identification, DL algorithms have achieved a 91% accuracy rate, surpassing dermatologists’ average accuracy of 84.6% [37].The integration of artificial intelligence into dermatologic practice has enhanced diagnostic precision and clinical decision-making through systematic data analysis. AI-powered systems streamline lesion evaluation by automating image interpretation and cross-referencing clinical data, thereby reducing subjective diagnostic variability while maintaining alignment with evidence-based diagnostic criteria. This technological advancement demonstrates dual utility in supporting clinician workflow efficiency and reinforcing diagnostic rigor through standardized analytical protocols.

As a pivotal subset of ML, DL distinguishes itself from conventional ML approaches by its ability to autonomously extract features from raw data—an attribute that confers distinct advantages in medical image analysis [44]. This capability proves particularly impactful in dermatological applications, where DL demonstrates enhanced efficiency in recognizing and classifying cutaneous disorders with complex morphological presentations, including melanoma, psoriasis, and RD [45, 46]. Among DL architectures, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have emerged as the predominant framework for medical image processing. CNNs employ hierarchical convolutional operations to discern subtle lesion characteristics from dermoscopic images, achieving diagnostic accuracy comparable to dermatologists in early skin cancer detection [47, 48].

Although CNNs have performed well in automated skin disease assessment, they still face limitations when dealing with long-range dependencies in the time dimension [49]. To address this issue, the Transformer model introduces a ‘self-attention mechanism’ that processes the input token (a combination of an image block and temporal position encoding), calculates its relationship with all other tokens, and computes the attention weight for the token [50, 51].

Current AI applications in dermatology primarily focus on classification tasks, as exemplified by the estimation of melanoma probability and the grading of RD severity [37, 52]. As illustrated in Fig. 2, a standard CNN pipeline processes individual dermatitis images to output graded severity assessments. This automated approach mitigates subjectivity inherent in manual grading, particularly for RD features like erythema intensity and distribution [25]. Such systems not only reduce clinician workload but also provide decision support for junior practitioners through standardized references. Despite the clinical prevalence of RD, AI-driven solutions for its management remain underdeveloped compared to other dermatoses like melanoma and vitiligo. Application of artificial intelligence in radiation dermatitis assessment

Automatic grading of radiation dermatitis based on deep Learning

Currently, deep learning has demonstrated excellent performance in image assessment scenarios for various skin diseases such as eczema and psoriasis. However, in the field of RD, research on automatic grading based on this technology remains relatively scarce. Considering the urgent clinical demand for standardized and objective RD grading, such technological exploration undoubtedly holds significant importance.

RD erythema assessment analysis using an ensemble CNN model

Researchers from SCARLETRED Holding GmbH and the Medical University of Graz developed a quantitative framework for erythema assessment using CIELAB color space transformation [25]. High-fidelity skin images were captured with a proprietary imaging system, and CIELAB conversion separated luminance (L*) from color components (a*: red-green axis; b*: yellow-blue axis). An ensemble CNN model was built using bagging techniques, combining models trained on different subsets of data. The model used 2263 images (86.1% of which were breast cancer cases) for preprocessing. The RD images were categorized according to CTCAE grades (0–2). The results showed that the ensemble CNN outperformed all other traditional models in both binary classification tasks (accuracy 87%, sensitivity 90%, specificity 82%) and ternary classification tasks (accuracy 66%). Notably, this approach only requires a smartphone installed with the company’s application and calibrated stickers to facilitate image acquisition. After images are uploaded to the cloud, the system automatically performs color correction, SEV* calculation, and AI grading without any manual intervention, resulting in low operational costs.

Clinically, this model’s smartphone compatibility addresses the need for convenient RD assessment in outpatient follow-ups. Key limitations remain: most patients had Fitzpatrick skin types I–III (93.3%), leaving the model’s performance on darker skin tones untested [25]. Future iterations should prioritize diverse skin tone data to ensure equitable performance across patient groups. The study claims that the AI has the potential to save physicians’ time, but it does not quantify the time required for AI analysis (including the total duration of image upload, processing, and result delivery), nor does it compare the time cost differences with physicians’ subjective assessments. Furthermore, the model only provides a severity grade and does not offer corresponding clinical recommendations based on the classification, making it difficult to directly translate the research findings into a clinical decision support tool.

Multi-angle RD analysis using U-Net and DenseNet-121 fusion

Although the aforementioned erythema assessment framework based on the ensemble CNN has achieved low-cost automatic grading of RD, it still has shortcomings in multi-angle adaptation and quantitative verification of clinical efficiency. A fusion model proposed by The Hong Kong Polytechnic University in 2022 has just been developed to address these issues [52]. Their study developed a framework integrating U-Net-based neck segmentation with DenseNet-121 classification, enabling multi-view analysis of nasopharyngeal carcinoma [52]. Utilizing multi-center data from 1205 RD images, this approach achieved 83.0% accuracy in four-category classification (Grade 0, 1, 2, and above 2) based on RTOG criteria. Computational efficiency analysis revealed a 0.02 s/image processing time versus clinicians’ 30 s–2 min per evaluation, representing a 1500–6000-fold temporal improvement, and thus significantly reduces workload in busy radiotherapy departments.

Furthermore, it is worth noting that a methodological innovation of this study lies in its adoption of a “majority voting system” to establish the ground truth. Specifically, five assessors collectively participated in the labeling process, and the grade with the highest consensus was used as the final label for model training and validation. The results demonstrated that the diagnostic performance of the DL model showed no significant difference from that of three assessors (p > 0.05) and was significantly superior to that of one less experienced assessor (p < 0.001). Only one assessor significantly outperformed the model (p = 0.039), albeit by a narrow margin in accuracy. These findings support the conclusion that the model’s performance is comparable to that of human experts. This approach contrasts markedly with the conventional paradigm of using “single-expert evaluation” as an absolute ground truth, more effectively highlighting the potential differentiated auxiliary role that AI models may play among physicians with varying levels of clinical experience.

While the proposed model demonstrates relatively robust performance and represents the first attempt to test clinical applicability, several limitations remain that could be addressed in future improvements. The dataset suffers from a significant class imbalance, with severe dermatitis cases (RTOG Grade ≥3) constituting only 4.48% of the total samples. This raises concerns about the model’s generalization capability in identifying severe radiation dermatitis. Furthermore, the study uses single images as the unit of analysis without incorporating key clinical variables such as patient baseline data (e.g., smoking history, BMI), radiation therapy protocols, or tumor stage—all of which have been demonstrated to be strongly correlated with the development and progression of radiation dermatitis. The failure to integrate these clinical factors and reliance solely on image features limits the model’s ability to provide a comprehensive “RD risk + grading” assessment for individual patients, thereby diminishing its value as a clinical decision support tool [52].

Precise RD analysis based on dose distribution and CIELAB-RGB data fusion

Despite the significant breakthroughs of the multi-angle fusion model in grading accuracy and efficiency, it, similar to the ensemble CNN model, relies solely on the color and texture features of skin images while ignoring skin dose distribution during radiotherapy—a core factor influencing the occurrence and progression of RD. The study by Yonsei University has filled this gap by introducing dose data [57]. They innovatively established a pixel-level correspondence between skin dose distribution maps and CIELAB-RGB color data through spatial alignment. The fused seven-channel data (R, G, B, L*, a*, b*, dose) was utilized for network training, leading to a significant improvement in segmentation performance. Specifically, the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) for RGB-only input was 0.50 ± 0.21 for faint RD segmentation and 0.40 ± 0.22 for severe RD segmentation, whereas the RGBLAB_D fusion achieved a DSC of 0.62 ± 0.21 for faint RD and 0.69 ± 0.21 for severe RD. These findings demonstrate that the dose-dependent prediction model effectively enhances the accuracy of RD segmentation.

Incorporating skin dose distribution—a key clinical determinant of RD severity—enhances the model’s relevance to radiotherapy workflows. However, reliance on 3D cameras limits deployment in primary care settings. Developing compatible tools for standard smartphones would broaden its clinical accessibility, enabling real-time dose-correlated RD assessment during treatment. It must be pointed out that this study included only 73 skin images, all from a single institution, resulting in significant sample size bias and geographical homogeneity. Furthermore, it classified RD only into “faint” and “severe” categories instead of using standardized grading systems such as CTCAE or RTOG. Additionally, since the development of RD is a dose-dependent dynamic process, the model can only perform single-time evaluation and cannot meet the clinical need for dynamic monitoring and early intervention, limiting its application scenario to one-time snapshot assessment. The images in this study were captured using a 3D camera under standardized lighting conditions in the treatment room. However, in primary care settings, specialized 3D cameras may be unavailable. Images with low resolution or color deviations could lead to a sharp decline in model performance. Moreover, the robustness of the model has not been evaluated, casting doubt on its generalizability.

Prediction of radiation dermatitis severity using machine learning and deep learning

All the studies in Section Automatic Grading of Radiation Dermatitis Based on Deep Learning mentioned above focus on the “retrospective grading of already developed RD”, which refers to determining the severity of existing skin reactions. However, in clinical practice, if the severity of RD can be predictively assessed prospectively before its onset, it will be more conducive to formulating intervention plans in advance and reducing the risk of treatment interruption. Therefore, research on RD prediction based on machine learning and deep learning has become a new key direction.

RD risk prediction using machine learning models

The 2024 study conducted by Chang Geng Memorial Hospital represents a paradigm shift toward pre-intervention prediction of RD [58]. This study focused on developing a risk prediction model for RD following proton radiotherapy for head and neck cancer. Data from 57 patients were analyzed, integrating 11 clinical factors and 9 dosimetric parameters. To identify six key predictors, including smoking history and N-stage, highly correlated variables were first removed using Pearson correlation analysis, followed by feature selection via Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. During model construction, XGBoost (AUC = 0.890) significantly outperformed Random Forest (AUC = 0.670). Interestingly, when a reduced feature set was applied to the XGBoost model, its performance declined (XGBoost_LASSO AUC = 0.820), suggesting that not all models are suited to learning from simplified feature spaces. Furthermore, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) value analysis revealed that variables such as smoking history and AJCC stage contributed positively to the prediction of RD, providing an interpretable basis for the model’s decisions.

This experiment demonstrates that the XGBoost model (AUC = 0.890) holds potential as a tool for RD risk screening. For instance, it could identify patients at high risk of developing Grade ≥2 RD, enabling proactive interventions—such as steroid administration or enhanced skin care—to reduce treatment interruption rates. Clinically, this translates to improved treatment continuity and patient quality of life. Integrating SHAP value interpretation helps clinicians understand the model’s decision-making (e.g., smoking history as a key predictor), increasing trust in AI-assisted risk stratification.

In addition, Reddy et al. used classical ML models including random forest and logistic regression to analyze data from over 2000 patients and predicted acute radiation toxicities such as Grade ≥2 dermatitis, achieving AUCs above 0.80 [59].

Prediction of RD severity using deep learning techniques

Current methods for predicting RD include reflection confocal microscopy for early lesion detection [60], skin dose distribution analysis [61], ultrasound-based tissue characterization [62], quantitative thermal imaging biomarkers [63], and spectrophotometry-derived indices such as melanin (IM) and erythema (IE) to quantify skin pigmentation [64]. Despite these advancements, clinical adoption remains limited. Challenges persist across all approaches: insufficient sample sizes in validation studies, suboptimal predictive accuracy, patient discomfort from contact-based imaging (e.g., ultrasound gel or probe pressure), technically complex operational protocols, and the economic burden of specialized equipment. Even non-contact spectrophotometric measurements, while less invasive, still require standardized calibration, underscoring the need for more robust, accessible predictive frameworks.

Traditional machine learning models have shown good performance in RD risk prediction, but they are mostly limited to conducting risk analysis based on structured features (such as dose parameters and clinical indicators) and struggle to effectively interpret dynamic sequence images. In contrast, deep learning, leveraging its advantage in efficiently processing unstructured data, is expected to provide more compelling predictions of endpoint severity through RD images, yet it still faces numerous challenges in clinical implementation at present.

AI-driven image-based early prediction of RD remains in its infancy, with no mature approaches yet reported in the literature. Unlike traditional grading tasks that focus on retrospective assessment, predictive modeling requires longitudinal skin imaging data spanning pre-treatment, on-treatment, and post-treatment phases (Fig 3). Current challenges and future directions

Data limitations: insufficient scale and population representation bias

Existing RD repositories face dual constraints of scale insufficiency and population underrepresentation. The largest published dataset contains fewer than 2300 images - orders of magnitude below requirements for robust deep learning applications [25]. Compounding this limitation, current collections exhibit pronounced racial homogeneity, with over 93% of samples originating from Fitzpatrick skin types I-III [25]. This sampling bias introduces critical clinical risks, as Melanin-related masking of erythema may lead to delayed diagnosis in darker-skinned populations (Fitzpatrick IV-VI) [65].

The incidence and severity of RD in patients after intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) and hypofractionation were generally lower compared to patients who received conventional radiotherapy techniques. The incidence of severe RD also decreased, resulting in an imbalanced dataset with significantly more samples of mild RD than severe RD. This imbalance notably limited the models’ performance in detecting severe dermatitis. Although techniques such as data augmentation and Dropout were employed to balance the data, their effect was ultimately limited [66].

To support accurate grading and progression prediction AI models, it is crucial to establish a high-quality dataset that includes diverse ethnicities, skin types, and radiotherapy dose distributions. Special emphasis should be placed on collecting weekly skin imaging data from patients during radiotherapy to comprehensively assess the dynamic changes of RD throughout the treatment. Furthermore, multicenter collaboration and data sharing should be encouraged to expand the dataset’s size and diversity, ultimately improving the model’s generalizability and clinical applicability.

Toward longitudinal analysis: integrating time-series data for RD prediction

Current AI frameworks predominantly rely on static image analysis, overlooking the temporal progression of RD. Critical biomarkers—such as pigmentation changes during the pre-erythema phase, desquamation patterns associated with cumulative radiation dose, and post-treatment healing trajectories—are not effectively integrated into predictive models due to the absence of time-series modeling. This limitation prevents existing approaches from leveraging weekly treatment images routinely collected in clinical practice, thereby hindering the ability to predict RD progression trajectories or optimize adaptive radiotherapy strategies.

From a clinical perspective, dynamic RD monitoring (weekly during radiotherapy) is essential for timely intervention. Time-series models that integrate longitudinal images with treatment plans can predict RD progression before visible symptoms emerge, enabling preemptive care—a key unmet need in current clinical practice. Thus, the integration of time-series modeling is essential to addressing this limitation. The Transformer model’s self-attention mechanism captures the dynamics of RD over time, specifically how dermatitis images from different weeks influence future predictions. Through the global self-attention mechanism, the Transformer is able to capture long-term dependencies in the time series, providing a more accurate representation of the evolution of dermatitis in the prediction. Ultimately, by leveraging these temporal relationships, the model facilitates the prediction of RD endpoint severity [51, 67].

Dual interpretability challenges: bridging biological mechanisms and algorithmic transparency

The application of AI in RD assessment remains constrained by a lack of interpretability, primarily evident in two key areas. First, the issue of biological interpretability: current AI models often fail to provide a clear biological basis when predicting RD. For instance, while a model may identify high-risk patients, it cannot explain how changes in skin structure or cellular processes contribute to the development of RD, which undermines clinicians’ confidence in its predictions. Second, the challenge of model interpretability persists due to the “black-box” nature of many deep learning models, particularly CNNs [68]. These models extract high-dimensional features through millions of parameters but struggle to provide intuitive causal explanations, conflicting with the pathology- and physiology-driven diagnostic reasoning used in clinical practice.

Clinicians require transparent AI predictions to justify interventions. To address these limitations, future efforts could focus on enhancing biological interpretability by integrating multi-modal biomarkers, such as histological images, inflammatory markers, and skin temperature variations, to uncover the underlying biological mechanisms of RD. For improving model interpretability, visualization tools can be employed to highlight specific regions of interest in images, thereby increasing clinicians’ trust and understanding of AI systems. For example, SHAP can quantify the contribution of each feature to the model’s prediction, providing a more transparent and actionable foundation for decision-making [69].

Multimodal fusion strategies: synergistic optimization of imaging grading and risk prediction

Current RD research predominantly focuses on imaging-based grading, evaluating severity through features such as erythema, desquamation, and ulceration. However, relying solely on imaging grading limits the ability to predict which patients are at higher risk of progressing to severe RD, leading to inadequate early intervention for high-risk individuals. While existing risk assessment methods—such as confocal microscopy, ultrasound tissue imaging, and thermography—offer additional insights, their utility is constrained by small sample sizes, limited detection accuracy, and high costs, hindering broad clinical adoption.

Combining imaging features with clinical variables (e.g., age, comorbidities, radiation dose) aligns with personalized medicine principles. To address these limitations, an integrated AI model combining imaging grading with risk prediction could be developed, leveraging deep learning for automated RD image classification and incorporating clinical data (e.g., skin type, radiation dose, genetic markers) to predict progression risk. By employing ensemble learning or multi-task learning frameworks, such a model could synergistically enhance both imaging analysis and risk prediction, improving overall performance [70]. For instance, integrating clinical variables like smoking history or tumor N-stage via XGBoost or deep learning architectures could boost early RD detection accuracy. This approach not only refines diagnostic precision but also enables early identification of high-risk patients during radiation therapy, facilitating optimized dose adjustments and personalized care strategies to ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Regulatory and ethical considerations for clinical translation

To achieve clinical translation, AI-driven radiodermatitis assessment tools must undergo stringent regulatory approval and meet ethical standards, in addition to technical performance such as fairness and interpretability. Classified as Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), these systems typically require Class II certification (e.g., FDA 510(k) or China NMPA registration), with a focus on demonstrating clinical validity in real-world settings rather than retrospective algorithmic accuracy [71, 72]. Deployment must clarify accountability in human-AI collaboration, emphasizing that AI serves an assistive role—not replacing clinicians—to preserve final decision-making authority, mitigate automation bias, and ensure patient safety [73].

Thus, future efforts should extend beyond algorithm refinement to prioritize multicenter prospective trials compliant with regulatory standards and develop clinician-centered ethical deployment frameworks to advance practical clinical integration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, AI-based automated assessment and prediction of radiation dermatitis hold significant promise for improving patient outcomes in radiotherapy. Clinically, these tools address critical unmet needs: standardizing grading to reduce inter-observer variability, accelerating assessment efficiency to alleviate clinician workload, and enabling early identification of high-risk patients for preemptive intervention. However, several key challenges persist, including limitations in dataset scale and diversity, deficits in temporal modeling, and barriers to clinical translation. Future research should focus on developing multimodal data ecosystems, incorporating temporal relationships, and optimizing clinical translation through explainable AI. Addressing these challenges will enable AI models to attain higher predictive accuracy and greater clinical applicability, ultimately optimizing RD management and improving the quality of life for cancer patients receiving radiotherapy.
