A Polynomial Space Lower Bound for Diameter Estimation in Dynamic Streams
Sanjeev Khanna, Ashwin Padaki, Krish Singal, Erik Waingarten

Abstract
We study the space complexity of estimating the diameter of a subset of points in an arbitrary metric space in the dynamic (turnstile) streaming model. The input is given as a stream of updates to a frequency vector x ∈ ℤ ≥ 0 n x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n} , where the support of x x defines a multiset of points in a fixed metric space ℳ = ( [ n ] , 𝖽 ) \mathcal{M}=([n],\mathsf{d}) . The goal is to estimate the diameter of this multiset, defined as max ⁡ { 𝖽 ⁡ ( i , j ) : x i , x j > 0 } \max\{\mathsf{d}(i,j)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{i},x_{j}>0\} , to a specified approximation factor while using as little space as possible. In insertion-only streams, a simple O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) -space algorithm achieves a 2-approximation. In sharp contrast to this, we show that in the dynamic streaming model, any algorithm achieving a constant-factor approximation to diameter requires polynomial space. Specifically, we prove that a c c -approximation to the diameter requires n Ω ⁡ ( 1 / c ) n^{\Omega(1/c)} space. Our lower bound relies on two conceptual contributions: (1) a new connection between dynamic streaming algorithms and linear sketches for scale-invariant functions, a class that includes diameter estimation, and (2) a connection between linear sketches for diameter and the minrank of graphs, a notion previously studied in index coding. We complement our lower bound with a nearly matching upper bound, which gives a c c -approximation to the diameter in general metrics using n O ⁡ ( 1 / c ) n^{O(1/c)} space.

Introduction

We study streaming algorithms for estimating the diameter of a set of points in a metric space. Let
\mathcal{M}=([n],\mathsf{d})
be a metric space on
n
elements, where
\mathsf{d}
is a distance function. We encode (multi-)subsets of
\mathcal{M}
as vectors
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
, where the entry
x_{i}
denotes the multiplicity of element
i\in[n]
in the subset. The goal is to estimate the diameter of the subset encoded by
x
in a streaming model, where the vector
x
is updated dynamically through insertions and deletions (i.e., increments and decrements to its coordinates), and the diameter of the set represented by
x
is defined as:

\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)=\max\left\{\mathsf{d}(i,j)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{i},x_{j}>0\right\}.

We assume oracle access to the metric
\mathsf{d}
, without limiting the number of distance queries. In particular, we allow algorithms to depend arbitrarily on the structure of the metric. The central objective is to minimize the space complexity required to output a
c
-approximation to
\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)
: a number
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)/c<\boldsymbol{\eta}\leq\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)

with high probability.
1
1

1

The fact that a
c
-approximation must be
strictly
larger than the diameter over
c
is non-standard (usually, an output which is exactly diameter over
c
is considered a
c
-approximation). We opt for this notion of approximation, since it will make the upper and lower bounds slightly cleaner to state.

In insertion-only streams (where updates only increment
x
), there is a simple
O(\log n)
-space algorithm achieving a
c
-approximation for any
c>2
: store the first inserted element
i^{*}\in[n]
and maintain the maximum distance
\mathsf{d}(i^{*},j)
over all subsequently inserted elements
j
. This quantity corresponds to the radius of the smallest enclosing ball centered at
i^{*}
, which is within a factor
2
of the diameter.
2
2

2

By a reduction from the indexing problem in communication complexity, there exist metric spaces
\mathcal{M}
where achieving a
c
-approximation for
c\leq 2
requires
\Omega(n)
bits of space, even in insertion-only streams.

However, this approach breaks down in the presence of deletions: if
i^{*}
is later removed from the set, the algorithm loses all relevant information. This motivates the following fundamental question:

Do there exist small-space streaming algorithms that can approximate the diameter in the dynamic (turnstile) model, where both insertions and deletions are allowed?

Note that, given
prior knowledge
of some
i\in[n]
with
x_{i}\neq 0
in the final vector, one could reduce the problem to maintaining distances
\mathsf{d}(i,j)
for updated elements
j
, and use
\ell_{0}
-sampling
[
FIS05
,
JST11
]
to estimate the radius around
i
under insertions and deletions. However, identifying such an
i

during
the stream is challenging: while an
\ell_{0}
-sampler can recover such an index in logarithmic space, its output only becomes available at the end of the stream—too late to maintain relevant distances involving
i
. Determining the space-complexity of streaming the diameter of a turnstile stream of points in a high-dimensional Euclidean space was asked by Krauthgamer
[
Kra22
]
. While the current manuscript does not resolve that problem (which currently remains open), it is a necessary first step.

1.1
Our Results

We establish nearly tight space-approximation tradeoffs for estimating the diameter of general metrics in dynamic streams. Our main result is a lower bound that shows any randomized
c
-approximation streaming algorithm which succeeds with high-constant probability must use polynomial space (as a function of
n
and
c
), demonstrating a strong separation from the logarithmic-space 2-approximation in insertion-only streams.

Theorem 1
.

For any
c\geq 1
, there exists a metric
\mathcal{M}=([n],\mathsf{d})
such that any dynamic streaming algorithm that computes a
c
-approximation to
\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)
for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
must use
\tilde{\Omega}(n^{1/(2\lceil{c}\rceil-1)})
bits of space.

As we discuss in Section
1.3
, the proof of Theorem
1
relies on two important conceptual developments. The first is further developing the connection between dynamic streaming algorithms and linear sketches
[
Gan08
,
LNW14
,
KP20
]
. We consider problems defined on partial functions
g\colon\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\{0,1,*\}
that are
scale-invariant
(i.e.
g(ax)=g(x)
for any scaling
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
of a vector
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
)—note that
\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)
satisfies this property. We show that for this class of problems, dynamic streaming algorithms can be made linear sketches without any overhead in complexity. In contrast, the space complexity of the sketch in
[
LNW14
]
increases logarithmically in the maximum entry of
x
, which is necessary for general problems but would render the second part of our argument meaningless.

The second is a connection between linear sketches for the diameter and the minrank of a particular graph associated with the metric
\mathcal{M}
. The minrank of a graph was developed in
[
BYBJK06
]
for the problem of index coding with side information, and we use a bound from
[
ABG+20
]
on the minrank of a random graph in order to lower bound the dimension of linear sketches. Complementing this, we give a matching upper bound that achieves a
c
-approximation in
n^{O(1/c)}
space for general metrics with polynomially bounded aspect ratio:

Theorem 2
.

For any metric space
\mathcal{M}=([n],\mathsf{d})
and any
c>6
, there is a dynamic streaming algorithm that computes a
c
-approximation to
\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)
with
\tilde{O}(n^{1/\lfloor(c-2)/4\rfloor})
bits of space.
3
3

3

We assume, for convenience, that final frequency vectors have entries with magnitude at most
\mathrm{poly}(n)
(to support frequency vectors of magnitude
m
, we pay a multiplicative
O(\log m)
in the bit-complexity). Furthermore, we assume for convenience, that the metric
\mathcal{M}
has aspect ratio
\mathrm{poly}(n)
(to support aspect ratio
\Delta
, we pay an additive
\mathrm{polylog}(n\Delta)
factor in the bit-complexity).

The proof of Theorem
2
makes use of low-dimensional
\ell_{\infty}
-embeddings and
\ell_{0}
-sampling. Specifically, a result of
[
Mat96
]
shows that any metric on
n
points admits a distortion-
(2k+1)
embedding into
\ell_{\infty}^{d}
with dimension
d=O(kn^{1/(k+1)}\log n)
. We show that for dynamic pointsets in
\ell_{\infty}^{d}
, a constant factor approximation to the diameter be accomplished in
\tilde{O}(d)
space, by maintaining a constant number of
\ell_{0}
-samplers per coordinate.

Our techniques also yield new embedding lower bounds. We show that the same random metrics used in Theorem
1
require large dimension when embedded into
\ell_{\infty}
with bounded distortion:

Theorem 3
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
c<2k+1
, let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}
be the shortest path metric on a random bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}(n,n,p)
with
p=n^{-1+1/(2k-1)}/\mathrm{polylog}(n)
. Then with high probability, any embedding of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}
into
\ell_{\infty}
with distortion
c
requires dimension
\tilde{\Omega}(n^{1/(2k-1)})
.

The benefit of Theorem
3
is that it gives precise constants for embedding a simple-to-describe metric into low-dimensional
\ell_{\infty}
. To best of our knowledge, comparable theorems for dimension-distortion tradeoffs for embeddings into normed spaces use one of two routes. They show that subsets of low-dimensional norms cannot host shortest path metrics on expanders (of the form in
[
Mat97
,
LMN05
,
Nao17
]
, which are lossy with respect to constant-factors in the dimension-distortion tradeoffs), or use a counting argument in Matousek
[
Mat96
]
(attributed to
[
Bou85
,
JLS87
]
) which relates the dimension-distortion tradeoff to existence of dense graphs with no short cycles. Even though the counting argument in
[
Mat96
]
gives precise constants and can obtain a better tradeoff than Theorem
3
when instantiated with the currently-known densest cycle-free graphs, the appeal of Theorem
3
is the simplicity of the hard metric, and the technique, which identifies the minrank of a graph associated with the metric as the relevant quantity in dimension-distortion tradeoffs.

\Omega(\min\{n,e^{d^{1/3}}\})
space.

4

4

.

1.3
Technical Overview

Communication Game and the Minrank.

A natural approach to lower bounding the space complexity of a streaming algorithm for diameter is to consider the following one-way communication game. There is a fixed public metric
\mathcal{M}
, which in our case is the shortest path metric on a random bipartite graph
\mathbf{G}=(U,V,\mathbf{E})\sim\mathcal{G}(n,n,p)
(Definition
4.1
), along with a public source of randomness.

•

Alice receives a uniformly random vector
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}
encoding a subset of
U
, by associating the
n
coordinates with the elements
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in U
.

•

Bob receives a uniformly random index
\boldsymbol{i}\in[n]
corresponding to a vertex
v_{\boldsymbol{i}}\in V
, as well as a vector
\boldsymbol{y}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
for all
u_{j}\notin N(v_{\boldsymbol{i}})\cup\{u_{\boldsymbol{i}}\}
, and
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}=0
otherwise (here,
N(v_{\boldsymbol{i}})
denotes the neighborhood of
v_{\boldsymbol{i}}
).

Both Alice and Bob have oracle access to the metric
\mathcal{M}
, and Alice must send a message that enables Bob to recover
\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
with high probability. Assuming the existence of a dynamic streaming algorithm for approximating diameter, Alice simulates insertions of every
u_{j}
with
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}=1
and sends the algorithm’s state to Bob. Bob then simulates deletions of all
u_{j}
with
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}=1
and queries the streaming algorithm. We set
p\lesssim n^{1/(2k-1)}/n
, so that
\mathsf{d}(u_{i},v_{i})\geq 2k+1
with high probability. Hence, if
\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=0
, the vector
\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}
is supported entirely within
N(v_{\boldsymbol{i}})
and the diameter is at most 2; if
\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=1
, the resulting set has diameter at least
2k
(except for the unlikely case every
u_{j}\in N(v_{\boldsymbol{j}})
has
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}=0
). For any
c\leq k
, a
c
-approximation to
\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{M}}(\cdot)
can thus distinguish between these two cases. This setup corresponds to an instance of
index coding
with side information
[
BYBJK06
]
, where Bob knows almost everything about Alice’s subset (all but the membership of elements in
N(v_{\boldsymbol{i}})\cup\{u_{\boldsymbol{i}}\}
). The communication complexity of this problem remains open in general, but progress has been made under the restriction to linear protocols over finite fields. Over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, the complexity is characterized by the
minrank
of the knowledge graph (i.e., the index
i\in[n]
has directed edges to everything except
N(v_{i})
). The minrank is the minimum rank of an
n\times n
matrix that is nonzero on the diagonal and zero at every
(i,j)
that is not an edge of the knowledge graph. For linear protocols over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, the message length (i.e. the sketch dimension) must be at least the minrank, which is
\tilde{\Omega}(n^{1/(2k-1)})
for our distribution over graphs
[
ABG+20
]
. This completes the lower bound for linear sketches over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and motivates our approach to general streaming lower bounds via reductions to linear sketches
[
Gan08
,
LNW14
,
KP20
]
, which bypasses the need to analyze the communication problem in full generality.

General Streaming Lower Bounds.

A subtlety arises when generalizing to a field
\mathbb{F}
beyond
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, since there are two things to generalize. First, the communication problem may consider inputs
\boldsymbol{x}
drawn from
\mathbb{F}^{n}
instead of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}=\{0,1\}^{n}
; second, linear protocols may be over
\mathbb{F}
instead of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. If the domain of the communication game and linear protocols use the same field
\mathbb{F}
, bounds easily generalize.
5
5

5

See Remark 2.3 in
[
LS09
]
.
However, in a general streaming lower bound, we must first fix an input distribution (which is tied to the domain of the communication game), and then allow for a streaming algorithm to be tailored to that distribution.

This issue appears concretely in
[
LNW14
]
: the reduction to linear sketches produces a sketch over
\mathbb{R}
whose dimension and entries grow with the magnitude of the final frequency vectors (see
m
in Theorem 16 of
[
LNW14
]
). Consider the following faulty approach toward a streaming lower bound. Fix an input distribution and apply
[
LNW14
]
to obtain an
s\times n
sketching matrix. Then, correctness under the distribution implies that frequency vectors
x
that “fool” the sketch (e.g., those supported only on
N(v_{i})\cup\{u_{i}\}
with
x_{i}\neq 0
) must not sketch to
0
. A duality argument (Lemma
4.6
) then yields a rank-
s
matrix satisfying the minrank conditions. The minrank lower bound
[
ABG+20
]
(which holds for arbitrary fields, including
\mathbb{R}
) implies a lower bound on
s
.

However, the quantitative parameters in
[
LNW14
]
introduce a circular dependency that leads to the downfall of this approach. The duality argument requires the kernel of the sketch to avoid the convex hull of fooling inputs. Since streaming algorithms operate on integer vectors, a “continuous-to-discrete” argument shows that the convex hull intersection must contain an integer point (Lemma
4.6
). However, the coordinates of this point, which is the solution to a system of
s
linear equations, may be as large as
\exp(s)
. To apply the duality argument and rule out these integer-valued fooling vectors, the input distribution must support vectors with entries up to
\exp(s)
. But the logarithmic loss in
[
LNW14
]
means the dimensionality of the sketch,
s
, will suffer a loss least logarithmic in
\exp(s)
, which leads to the circularity and gives a vacuous lower bound.

Revisiting the Reduction in
[
LNW14
]
.

The reduction from streaming algorithms to linear sketches proceeds in two steps. The first step reduces dynamic streaming algorithms to
path-independent
algorithms
[
Gan08
,
LNW14
]
, whose memory contents depend only on the final frequency vector, not the update sequence. This step is lossless (Theorem
2.3
) and is the starting point of our lower bound argument. Path-independent algorithms have a natural linear structure over
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
: the
kernel

K
(the set of frequency vectors mapping to the initial state) is closed under addition, i.e.
x,y\in K\Rightarrow x+y\in K
.
6
6

6

If
x,y\in K
, they individually map the algorithm to the initial state, then
x+y
can first update
x
(resulting in the initial state) and then update
y
(again, going back to initial state), so
x+y\in K
.

While this linearity does not imply that path-independent algorithms are linear sketches, the second step in
[
LNW14
]
shows how to simulate them using linear sketches, at the cost of a logarithmic overhead in sketch dimension, and sketch entries that depend on the magnitude of the final frequency vectors.

Our first main technical result (Theorem
1.3
, proved in Section
3
) eliminates the logarithmic loss incurred in
[
LNW14
]
(and therefore avoids the aforementioned circularity) for a class of problems we call
scale-invariant decision problems
. These are partial functions
g\colon\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\{0,1,*\}
satisfying
g(ax)=g(x)
for all scalings
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
(Definition
3.1
). The diameter decision problem falls into this class, since scaling a frequency vector does not change its support. Our second main result (Theorem
1.3
, proved in Section
4
) gives a lower bound on the sketch dimension required to approximate the diameter on random bipartite graph metrics. The statement of Theorem
1
follows directly from combining Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.3
, both of which we outline below.

Overview of Theorem
1.3
: Streaming to Sketching for Scale-Invariant Problems.

In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.3
, which gives a reduction from dynamic streaming algorithms to linear sketches for scale-invariant problems. The dimensionality of the resulting sketch is at most
\mathcal{S}^{+}(\mathcal{A},1)
, the final space used by the streaming algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on frequency vectors in
\{0,1\}^{n}
(see Definition
2.8
).

{restatable*}

theoremlinearsketchthm
Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is a randomized streaming algorithm that computes a scale-invariant function
g
over all dynamic streams with probability at least
1-\delta
. Then, for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
supported on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap g^{-1}(\{0,1\})

•

•

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathrm{Dec}(T\boldsymbol{x})=g(\boldsymbol{x})\right]\geq 1-O(\delta).

(1)

The novelty of Theorem

T
(i.e. its dimensionality and the size of its entries) is completely independent of the magnitude of the vectors in the support of

[
Gan08

LNW14

(\mathrm{Enc},\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime})

\mathrm{Enc}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\mathcal{W}
maps frequency vectors to a state

7

7

The function
\mathrm{Enc}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\mathcal{W}

produces the output (we use
\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}

\mathrm{Dec}

\mathbb{R}^{n}

. However, submodules of

\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
. This alone does not work, but we have yet to take advantage of scale-invariance. By drawing a random scaling
\mathbf{Z}

\mathbb{Z}_{+}

Overview of Theorem
1.3

Randomized Streaming Algorithm.

We follow the standard formalization of (randomized) streaming algorithms from

Definition 2.5

(Dynamic Stream)
.

A dynamic stream

Definition 2.6

(Randomized Streaming Algorithm)
.

A randomized streaming algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is specified by a Turing machine with state space
S
and two distinguished states
s,t\in S
denoting the start and end of an update’s processing. The machine has access to three tapes:

•

: A read-only, one-way tape containing a dynamic stream
\sigma
.

•

: A two-way tape storing memory from
\mathcal{W}\subset\{0,1\}^{*}
.

•

Random Tape
: A read-only, two-way tape containing a fixed string
\rho\in\{0,1\}^{r}
of random bits. The parameter
r
is the randomness complexity of
\mathcal{A}
(see Definition
2.9
).

A configuration after processing an update is a tuple
(w,\rho)
, where
w\in\mathcal{W}
is the working tape and
\rho
is the random tape.

Output Function:
There is a function
\mathrm{Dec}\colon\mathcal{W}\times\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}
that produces the output from the final configuration.

Correctness, Space Complexity, and Randomness Complexity.

We now define the correctness, the space complexity of randomized streaming algorithms (that which we will lower bound), and the randomness complexity of a streaming algorithm. The computation starts with the input tape initialized to a dynamic stream
\sigma
of length
u

\rho
is filled with
r

q
is initialized to
s
.
The algorithm proceeds in

•

The machine begins in state

\sigma
.

•

It updates
w

q
, while
\rho

•

When it reaches state

Each update defines a transition from
(w,\rho)
to

]

Proof.

We note that we may decompose the vector

3
Streaming Algorithms to Sketching Matrices

Our first main theorem shows how to extract a low-complexity sketching matrix from a randomized streaming algorithm, for the class of
scale-invariant
promise problems (i.e., problems whose answer does not change if one rescales the frequency vector).

Definition 3.1
.

A problem specified by a partial function
g\colon\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\{0,1,*\}
is scale invariant if
g(ax)=g(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

\linearsketchthm

The matrix may be used as a linear sketch for solving instances sampled from
\mathcal{D}
with high probability.
We note that in a crucial contrast to a similar transformation given in
[
LNW14
]
, the complexity of the matrix
T
we find (i.e., the dimensionality and granularity of the entries of

) does not depend on the support of the frequency vectors in
\mathcal{D}
. The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of above theorem.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We will now prove Theorem
1.3
by showing that any streaming algorithm for computing a scale-invariant function can be simulated by a linear sketch of comparable dimension that satisfies the guarantees of the theorem. At a high-level, our proof proceeds by first lifting the given input distribution via random scaling and then applying Theorem
2.3
to obtain a path-independent algorithm. We then construct a sketching matrix using the basis obtained from Lemma
2.12
, and this ensures that the kernel of the matrix contains the zero-set (Definition
2.11
). The main technical step is defining a decoder that operates on the image of the sketch and essentially maintains the correctness guarantee of the streaming algorithm. Towards this end, we define a suitable majority-based decoder, and utilize scale-invariance and a coupling argument to prove that the error probability of our sketch is similar to that of the original streaming algorithm.

We start by defining a randomized distribution over scalings of frequency vectors, which allows us to leverage scale-invariance in the proof. Let
\mathsf{R},\mathsf{L}\in\mathbb{N}
be parameters (which should be read as very large numbers) to be set later. Let
P\subset\mathbb{N}
denote the set of all primes less than
\mathsf{R}
. Define the distribution
\mathcal{Z}
over
\mathbb{Z}
as follows:

•

For each
p\in P
, sample
\boldsymbol{\eta}(p)\sim\{0,\dots,\mathsf{L}\}
independently;

•

Define
\mathbf{Z}=\prod_{p\in P}p^{\boldsymbol{\eta}(p)}
.

Let
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
be the distribution over
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
obtained by sampling
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
and
\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{Z}
, and setting
\boldsymbol{z}=\mathbf{Z}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}
. Furthermore, up to an (negligible) additional error probability, we may assume that
\mathcal{D}
is finitely supported and let

such that
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})\subset\llbracket-m_{0},m_{0}\rrbracket^{n}

\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})

m_{0}
so as to ensure a
(1-\delta)

\delta

\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
does not lie in
\llbracket-m_{0},m_{0}\rrbracket^{n}

Apply Theorem
2.3
to the scale-invariant function

, for any large enough bound

with a large enough bound

such that:

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\boldsymbol{z}))=g(\boldsymbol{z})]\geq 1-8\delta,

(2)

and
\mathcal{S}^{+}(\mathcal{B},\ell)\leq\mathcal{S}^{+}(\mathcal{A},\ell)

\mathcal{S}^{+}(\mathcal{B},1)\leq\mathcal{S}^{+}(\mathcal{A},1)

By Lemma

In other words,

\gamma=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{Y}}\left[\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}\mid T\boldsymbol{x}=y}[\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{y})\neq g(\boldsymbol{x})]\right],

To analyze

•

For each

•

For at least one choice of

Using this coupling, and noting that the marginal distribution of

\gamma=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{Y}}\left[\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{((\mathbf{Z}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}),(\mathbf{Z}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}))\sim\mathcal{C}(y)}[\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}(y)\neq g(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})]\right]=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{Y}}\left[\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{((\mathbf{Z}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}),(\mathbf{Z}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}))\sim\mathcal{C}(y)}[\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}(y)\neq g(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})]\right];

where the second equality uses the fact that

is scale-invariant. Now whenever
((\mathbf{Z}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}),(\mathbf{Z}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}))\sim\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{y})

•

Case
(a):
\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}))=\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{y})
, which means
\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}))\neq g(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})
.

•

Case
(b):
\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}))=g(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\neq\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{y})

\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{y})\neq g(\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})

\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}))=\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{y})

\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}))\neq g(\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
.

•

Case
(c):
\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}))=g(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\neq\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{y})

\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}))=\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{y})=g(\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
, and this means that
g(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\neq g(\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
.

Thus, at least one of the three cases occurs with probability at least
\gamma/3
.

(Easier) Cases (a) and (b).

In both cases, we observe that one of
\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
or
\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
is a failure point for the deterministic algorithm
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{W},\mathrm{Enc},\mathrm{Dec},\oplus)
on an input drawn from
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. In particular, if case (a) occurs with probability
\geq\gamma/3
, we have:

\frac{\gamma}{3}\leq\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\boldsymbol{z}))\neq g(\boldsymbol{z})]\leq 8\delta,

by the guarantee of Theorem
2.3
.

Similarly, suppose that case (b) occurs with probability
\geq\gamma/3
. We again find that
\boldsymbol{z}=\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is a point where
\mathcal{B}
errs, so the same bound applies:

\frac{\gamma}{3}\leq\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\boldsymbol{z}))\neq g(\boldsymbol{z})]\leq 8\delta.

Combining both, we get
\gamma\leq 24\delta
if either case (a) or (b) occur with probability
\geq\gamma/3
.

(Harder) Case (c).

In this case, we have:

\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}))=g(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}),\quad\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}))=g(\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}),\quad\text{but }g(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\neq g(\mathbf{Z}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}).

Since
T\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=T\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
, it follows that
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\ker(T)=M
, the kernel of
T
, which is spanned by
K(1)
. So
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
can be written as a (real) linear combination
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}b_{i}
of the

basis vectors of
M
(Definition
2.11
), and recall these are all in
\llbracket-1,1\rrbracket^{n}
and the encoding function evaluates each
\mathrm{Enc}(b_{i})=0
. In particular, letting
W
be the

matrix whose columns are

\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=W\alpha

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{t}

Furthermore,
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
are integer-valued and all entries of

\llbracket-1,1\rrbracket^{n}

be a

t

(recall,

\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\llbracket-m_{0},m_{0}\rrbracket^{n}

Using Lemma
2.13
, this has the consequence that for any

where
|a|\leq(m-1)/(2t\cdot m_{0}t!)

\mathrm{Enc}(a\mathsf{D}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}))=\mathrm{Enc}(0).

As long as

,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Enc}(a\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Enc}(a\mathsf{D}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}))\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{|a\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{1j}|}\mathrm{sign}(a\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{1j})e_{j}\right)

\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(a\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}))\neq g(a\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{3}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{Z}}\left[\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim\mathcal{Y}\\

(3)

3
). The difficulty is that
\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{z}
does not follow the same distribution as
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, since
\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
implies
\boldsymbol{z}=\mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{x}
for
\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{Z}
and
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
, whereas here we are analyzing
\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{z}=\mathsf{D}\mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{x}
. However, this is precisely where the design of the distribution
\mathcal{Z}
allows us to control the discrepancy between these two distributions. In particular, we will show that for appropriate choice of the parameters
\mathsf{L},\mathsf{R}
, we can ensure that the scaling by
\mathsf{D}
maps almost all of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
back into itself, modulo a small

of
O(\delta)
.

Now to bound this source of error, let us consider the prime factorization of
\mathsf{D}=\prod_{p\in P(\mathsf{D})}p^{\pi(p)}
. Then for
\mathsf{D}\mathbf{Z}
to lie within the support of
\mathcal{Z}
, it suffices that, for each
p\in P(\mathsf{D})
, the sampled exponent
\boldsymbol{\eta}(p)
in
\mathbf{Z}
satisfies
\boldsymbol{\eta}(p)\leq\mathsf{L}-\pi(p)
. The probability that this fails is at most:

\sum_{p\in P(\mathsf{D})}\frac{\pi(p)}{\mathsf{L}},

since each
\boldsymbol{\eta}(p)
is drawn uniformly from
\{0,\dots,\mathsf{L}\}
. Each
\pi(p)\leq O(\log\mathsf{D})
and
|P(\mathsf{D})|\leq O(\log\mathsf{D})
as well, so the total failure probability is at most
O(\log^{2}\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{L})
.

In these boundary cases, we can no longer ensure that
\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{z}
lies in the support of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, and this is the only source of error in connecting the two distributions. We now choose
\mathsf{L}=\Omega(n^{2}\log^{2}n/\delta)
, and

\mathsf{R}\geq n!\geq\mathsf{D}
, to achieve an additive error of
O(\delta)
, as desired. This yields:

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{z}))\neq g(\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{z})]\leq\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{Enc}(\boldsymbol{z}))\neq g(\boldsymbol{z})]+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}\mathsf{D}}{\mathsf{L}}\right).

By the earlier guarantee on
\mathcal{B}
from Theorem
2.3
, the first term is at most
8\delta
, and by our choice of
\mathsf{L}
and
\mathsf{R}
, the second term is at most
O(\delta)
. Hence we conclude that case (c) also satisfies:

\gamma\leq 3\cdot O(\delta)=O(\delta),

completing the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

The real minrank of a graph on
m
vertices is the smallest rank of an matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
with nonzero entries on the diagonal, and
M_{ij}=0
whenever
(i,j)
is not an edge of the graph.

To obtain our lower bound, it will be sufficient for the following properties to hold in our graph
\mathbf{G}
. For the remainder of the section, it will be useful to fix an integer
k=\lceil c\rceil\leq\log n/(20\log\log n)
. Notice that for larger values of
c\geq\log n/(20\log\log n)
, the desired lower bound becomes vacuous. We will set a suitable
p=p(n,k)
to derive the desired lower bound on graph metrics
\mathbf{G}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
k\leq\log n/(20\log\log n)
, set
p=n^{-1+1/(2k-1)}/\log n
and let
\mathbf{G}\sim\mathcal{G}(n,n,p)
. Then, the following three properties hold with probability
1-o(1)
:

•

The set
I^{*}(\mathbf{G})
of coordinates
i\in[n]
where
\mathsf{d}_{\mathbf{G}}(u_{i},v_{i})\geq 2k+1
has size
|I^{*}(\mathbf{G})|\geq 0.9n
.

•

For every
i\in[n]
, the neighborhood
\mathbf{N}(v_{i})\neq\emptyset
.

•

Letting
\tilde{\mathbf{G}}=(I^{*}(\mathbf{G}),\tilde{\mathbf{E}})
be the (directed) graph where
(i,j)\in\tilde{\mathbf{E}}
whenever
(u_{j},v_{i})\notin\mathbf{E}
, we have
\mathrm{minrank}(\tilde{\mathbf{G}})\geq\tilde{\Omega}(np)
.

Proof.

For the first bullet point, we first upper bound the probability that
\mathsf{d}_{\mathbf{G}}(u_{i},v_{i})\leq 2k-1
for any fixed
i\in[n]
using a union bound; then, we lower bound
|I^{*}(\mathbf{G})|
by Markov’s inequality. In particular, fix
i
and note that in order for
\mathsf{d}_{\mathbf{G}}(u_{i},v_{i})
to have a path of length at most
2k-1
, there must be some
\ell\leq k-1
and two sequences
u^{(1)},\dots,u^{(\ell+1)}
and
v^{(1)},\dots,v^{(\ell+1)}
where
u^{(1)}=u_{i}
and
v^{(\ell+1)}=v_{i}
such that an edge connecting
u^{(j)}
to
v^{(j)}
always exists, and an edge connecting
v^{(j)}
to
u^{(j+1)}
always exists. Hence, we can upper bound the probability that such a path exists by:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}n^{2\ell}\cdot p^{2\ell+1}=p\cdot\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}(np)^{2\ell}\leq p\cdot\frac{(np)^{2k-2}}{1-(1/np)^{2}}\leq 2n^{2k-2}p^{2k-1}\leq\frac{2}{\log n}.

Hence,
I^{*}(\mathbf{G})
is expected to have size at least
n-o(n)
, and we obtain the desired bound by Markov’s inequality. The second bullet point follows from a simpler bound. The probability that there exists some
i\in[n]
where
\mathbf{N}(v_{i})=\emptyset
is at most
n(1-p)^{n}\leq 1/n
whenever
p\geq 2\log n/n
(note, our setting of
p
is significantly larger, so we expect
\mathbf{N}(v_{i})
to have size
\Omega(n^{1/(2k-1)})
).

The final bullet point has its proof in Appendix
A
and uses an argument of
[
ABG+20
]
. Roughly speaking,
[
ABG+20
]
lower bounds the minrank of a random (directed) graph
\mathcal{G}(m,q)
by
\Omega(m\log(1/q)/\log m)
. Their lower bound proceeds by a union bound over sub-matrices with a special structure (which they show are very unlikely to appear). For us, the only step is showing the argument can incorporate the fact we consider a sub-graph defined by vertices in
I^{*}(\mathbf{G})
whose has size
m
is at least
\Omega(n)
, and we set
q=1-p
, so
\log(1/q)\geq p
.
∎

Description of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{G})
.

We now describe the hard distribution
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{G})
referred to in Theorem
1.3
, and we will show that a linear sketch cannot correctly compute
\mathrm{diam}^{2,k}_{\mathbf{G}}(\boldsymbol{x})
where
\boldsymbol{x}
is drawn from this distribution. We first define
\mathcal{D}(G)
for any fixed bipartite graph
G=(U,V,E)
with
|U|=|V|=n
, and then pick
\mathbf{G}
satisfying the conditions of Lemma
4.3
. A draw from
\mathcal{D}(G)
is an input
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2n}
where each coordinate corresponds to a point in
G
.
As it will turn out, it suffices to consider frequency vectors supported entirely on the first
n
coordinates corresponding to the
n
vertices
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in U
. Specifically, for two large parameters
\mathsf{P},\mathsf{U}\in\mathbb{N}
(which we specify later), an input
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}(G)
is drawn according to the following procedure:

1.

We draw
\boldsymbol{i}\sim I^{*}(G)
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=0
with probability
1/2
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=\mathsf{P}
with probability
1/2
.

2.

Every
j\in[n]
such that
(u_{j},v_{\boldsymbol{i}})\in E
has
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\sim[\mathsf{P}\cdot\mathsf{U}]
, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}=0
if
(u_{j},v_{\boldsymbol{i}})\notin E
.

3.

The final
n
coordinates
\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2n}
are all set to
0
.

It is also useful to introduce the notation
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{i})\sim\mathcal{D}(G)
which reveals the index
\boldsymbol{i}
sampled in Step
1
, and this allows one to connect our desired function
\mathrm{diam}^{2,k}_{G}(\cdot)
to the distribution.

Claim 4.4
.

Let
G=(U,V,E)
satisfy conditions of Lemma
4.3
. Then, for any
(x,i)
sampled from
\mathcal{D}(G)
,
\mathrm{diam}_{G}^{2,k}(x)={\bf 1}\{x_{i}>0\}
.

Proof.

Fix any
(x,i)
drawn from
\mathcal{D}(G)
and let
X\subset[2n]
denote the support of the vector
x
. If
x_{i}=0
, then
X=N(v_{i})
and therefore has diameter at most
2
. If
x_{i}>0
, then
X=N(v_{i})\cup\{u_{i}\}
. Choosing
u^{\prime}\in N(v_{i})
arbitrarily, the diameter of
X
is at least
\mathsf{d}_{G}(u_{i},u^{\prime})\geq\mathsf{d}_{G}(u_{i},v_{i})-\mathsf{d}_{G}(v_{i},u^{\prime})\geq 2k
, where we used the fact that
i\in I^{\ast}(G)
.
∎

Given
\mathcal{D}(G)
, we let
(T,\mathrm{Dec})
be an integer
s\times 2n
matrix with
\|T\|_{\infty}\leq O((2n)^{n})
, and
\mathrm{Dec}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{s}\to\{0,1\}
which we assume computes
\mathrm{diam}_{G}^{2,k}(\boldsymbol{x})
with probability at least
1-1/(100n)
over
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}(G)
. Note that vectors
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D}(G))\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2n}
are always zero in the last
n
coordinates (those corresponding to vertices in
V
), so it suffices to consider the first
n
columns of
T
, and we henceforth refer to
T
as an
s\times n
matrix.

Claim 4.5
.

Suppose
G
satisfies the condition in Lemma
4.3
. Let
\mathsf{Q}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathsf{P}\cdot O(s^{2}(2n)^{2n})^{s+1}
and for every
i\in I^{\ast}
, we define
Z_{i}(\mathsf{P})
to be the set of vectors
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
satisfying:

(i)

z_{i}=\mathsf{P}

(ii)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}_{\infty}\leq\mathsf{Q}

(iii)

z_{j}=0
for all
j\in[n]
for which
(u_{j},v_{i})\notin E
.

When
\mathsf{U}=\omega(n(s^{2}(2n)^{2n})^{s+1})
, we have that
\ker(T)\cap Z_{i}(\mathsf{P})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Fix an
i\in I^{\ast}(G)
and let
z\in Z_{i}(\mathsf{P})
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction that
Tz=0
. We will lower bound the failure probability of
(T,\mathrm{Dec})
. We consider a draw of
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}(G)
conditioned on
\boldsymbol{i}=i
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=0
in Step
1
(which occurs with probability
1/(2n)
). Whenever
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\in\{\mathsf{Q}+1,\dots,\mathsf{P}\mathsf{U}-\mathsf{Q}\}
for all
j\in[n]
with
(u_{j},v_{i})\in E
, the fact
\|z\|_{\infty}\leq\mathsf{Q}
implies
\boldsymbol{x}+z
lies in the support of
\mathcal{D}(G)
. Thus,
\boldsymbol{x}+z
is drawn from
\mathcal{D}(G)
with the same probability as
\boldsymbol{x}
is. Namely,
i
is chosen and
(\boldsymbol{x}+z)_{i}=\mathsf{P}
with probability
1/(2n)
, and the distribution is uniform over its support on the remaining coordinates. However,
Tz=0
implies
T(\boldsymbol{x}+z)=T\boldsymbol{x}
, which means that
\mathrm{Dec}(T(\boldsymbol{x}+z))=\mathrm{Dec}(T\boldsymbol{x})
. By Claim
4.4
,
\mathrm{diam}_{G}^{2,k}(\boldsymbol{x})\neq\mathrm{diam}_{G}^{2,k}(\boldsymbol{x}+z)
, meaning
(\mathrm{Dec},T)
is incorrect on one of
\{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+z\}
. In other words,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}(G)}\left[\mathrm{Dec}(T\boldsymbol{x})\neq\mathrm{diam}_{G}^{2,k}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]\geq\frac{1}{2n}\cdot\left(1-\frac{2\cdot\mathsf{Q}}{\mathsf{P}\cdot\mathsf{U}}\right)^{n}\geq\frac{1}{50n}

whenever
\mathsf{U}\gg n\cdot(s^{2}(2n)^{2n})^{s+1}
.
∎

Crucially, the fact that
\ker(T)\cap Z_{i}(\mathsf{P})=\emptyset
for all
i\in I^{\ast}
allows us to construct a low-rank matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
which satisfies the conditions of the minrank of
\tilde{G}
(the third bullet point in Lemma
4.3
).

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
T
be an integer
s\times n
matrix satisfying
\|T\|_{\infty}\leq O((2n)^{n})
such that
\ker(T)\cap Z_{i}(\mathsf{P})=\emptyset
for all
i\in I^{\ast}
. If
\mathsf{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(O(s^{2}(2n)^{2n})^{s})!
, there exists an
n\times n
matrix
M
of rank at most
s
with ones on the diagonal and
M_{ij}=0
whenever
i\in I^{*}(G)
and
(u_{j},v_{i})\in E
.

Proof.

We will let
M=HT
where we describe the
n\times s
matrix
H
by describing each of its rows
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
for
i\in[n]
. If
i\notin I^{*}(G)
, we let
h_{i}
be the
T^{(i)}/\|T^{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}
, where
T^{(i)}
is the
i
-column of
T
, which satisfies the constraint that
M_{ii}=1
.

On the other hand, suppose
i\in I^{*}(G)
, and let
A
be the
s\times|N(v_{i})|
submatrix of
T
which considers the columns
j\in[n]
lying in the neighborhood
u_{j}\in N(v_{i})
, and
W
be the
s\times k
matrix with
k\leq\min\{s,|N(v_{i})|\}
which contains a maximal set of linearly independent columns of
A
. We will first show that there are no vectors
w\in\mathbb{R}^{|N(v_{i})|}
with
Aw=T^{(i)}
. If such a vector
w
existed, there would also exist
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
which satisfies
Wy=T^{(i)}
, and because
T^{(i)}
and
W
have integer coordinates of magnitude at most
O((2n)^{n})
, there exists an integer vector
\tilde{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
which satisfies
W\tilde{y}=\mathsf{D}\cdot T^{(i)}
, where
\mathsf{D}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\det(W^{\intercal}W)
is an integer of magnitude at most
O(s^{2}(2n)^{2n})^{s}
, and
\|\tilde{y}\|_{\infty}\leq\mathsf{D}\cdot O(s^{2}(2n)^{2n})^{s+1}
. Thus, by setting of
\mathsf{P}=(O(s^{2}(2n)^{2n})^{s})!
, the fact
\tilde{y}
exists means
\mathsf{P}/\mathsf{D}
is an integer, and
\tilde{y}^{\prime}=\mathsf{P}/\mathsf{D}\cdot\tilde{y}
satisfies
W\tilde{y}^{\prime}=\mathsf{P}\cdot T^{(i)}
and
\|\tilde{y}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\mathsf{P}\cdot O(s^{2}(2n)^{2n})^{s+1}
. Let
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be defined as
z_{i}=\mathsf{P}
and
z_{j}=-\tilde{y}_{j^{\prime}}^{\prime}
for each
j
such that
u_{j}\in N(v_{i})
, where
\tilde{y}_{j^{\prime}}^{\prime}
is the coordinate of
\tilde{y}^{\prime}
associated with the column of
T
indexed by
u_{j}
.
This would imply the existence of a
z\in\ker(T)
that is also in
Z_{i}(\mathsf{P})
, which we have ruled out by Claim
4.5
.
Since no such vectors exists, this means that

\left\{T^{(i)}-Aw\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}w\in\mathbb{R}^{|N(v_{i})|}\right\},

is a convex set that does not contain
0
. Therefore, there is a vector
\tilde{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
which satisfies
\langle\tilde{h},T^{(i)}\rangle-\langle\tilde{h},Aw\rangle>0
for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{|N(v_{i})|}
. This implies, in particular, that
\langle\tilde{h},T^{(i)}\rangle>\langle\tilde{h},T^{(j)}\rangle\cdot\mathsf{W}
, for all
j\in N(v_{i})
and all
\mathsf{W}\in\mathbb{R}
. Note this can only happen if
\langle\tilde{h},T^{(j)}\rangle=0
. Rescaling
\tilde{h}
by
1/\langle\tilde{h},T^{(i)}\rangle
gives the desired vector
h_{i}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Fix
k=\lceil{c}\rceil\leq\log n/(20\log\log n)
and take
p=n^{-1+1/(2k-1)}/\log n
(recall, if
c\geq\log n/(8\log\log n)
then the lower bound holds vacuously), and a draw
\mathbf{G}\sim\mathcal{G}(n,n,p)
satisfying conditions of Lemma
4.3
which hold with probability
1-o(1)
. Observe that in the graph
G
, all paths between
U
-side vertices have even length, since paths alternate between vertices in
U
and
V
.
So any
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{G}))
which satisfies
\mathrm{diam}_{\mathbf{G}}(x)\geq 2c
will also satisfy
\mathrm{diam}_{\mathbf{G}}(x)\geq 2k
, and this means that the linear sketch
(T,\mathrm{Dec})
must satisfy
\mathrm{Dec}(T\boldsymbol{x})=\mathrm{diam}^{2,k}_{\mathbf{G}}(\boldsymbol{x})
with probability
1-1/(100n)
. Therefore, we can apply Claim
4.5
and Lemma
4.6
and obtain an
n\times n
matrix
M
of rank at most
s
, with ones on the diagonal and which satisfies
M_{ij}=0
whenever
i\in I^{*}(\mathbf{G})
and
(u_{j},v_{i})\in\mathbf{E}
. Consider the sub-matrix
M^{\prime}
which considers rows and columns in
I^{*}(\mathbf{G})
; and note this satisfies conditions of the minrank on the knowledge graph
\tilde{\mathbf{G}}
. The matrix
M^{\prime}
has rank at most
s
(since it is a sub-matrix of
M
), and satisfies
M^{\prime}_{ij}=0
whenever
(i,j)\notin\tilde{\mathbf{E}}
(equivalent to
(u_{j},v_{i})\in\mathbf{E}
). By the third bullet point of Lemma
4.3
,
s
is at least
\tilde{\Omega}(np)
, and we obtain the desired bound.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
G
satisfy the condition of Theorem
1.3
and let
\mathcal{A}
be a dynamic streaming algorithm computing a
c
-approximation for
\mathrm{diam}_{G}
with success probability
1-o(1/n)
(note that we can afford to lose a
O(\log n)
multiplicative factor to ensure the high success probability). The algorithm
\mathcal{A}
computes
\mathrm{diam}^{2,c}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)
for any stream with frequency vector
x
with probability at least
1-o(1/n)
(over the internal randomness of the algorithm), where
\mathcal{M}
is the shortest path metric induced by
G
. Applying Theorem
1.3
on the distribution
\mathcal{D}(G)
, which is supported on
(\mathrm{diam}^{2,c}_{G})^{-1}(\{0,1\})
by Claim

, we obtain a linear sketch
(T,\mathrm{Dec})
of dimension

satisfying the conditions of Theorem

, which implies

\mathcal{S}^{+}(\mathcal{A},1)\geq s\geq\tilde{\Omega}(n^{1/(2\lceil{c}\rceil-1)}).

∎

4.2
Linear Sketching Lower Bound for
AFN

Theorem
1.3
proves a lower bound on the dimension of any linear sketch estimating the diameter on random graph metrics. We now show that, as a consequence, a similar lower bound holds for the related problem of approximate furthest neighbor.

We will show lower bounds for computing
\textup{{AFN}}_{1,c}^{\mathcal{M}}
(Definition
2.2
) that hold for the same random graph metric
\mathbf{G}=(U,V,\mathbf{E})\sim\mathcal{G}(n,n,p)
given in Definition
4.1
, albeit with a slightly different setting of
p
. We will require two additional properties of
\mathbf{G}
.

Claim 4.7
.

For
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\leq\frac{\log n}{20\log\log n}
, set
p=n^{-1+1/(2k-1)}/\log^{5}n
. With probability
1-o(1)
over the draw of
\mathbf{G}=(U,V,\mathbf{E})\sim\mathcal{G}(n,n,p)
, the following properties hold:

(i)

|\mathbf{N}(v_{i})|\geq pn/2
for all
i\in[n]

(ii)

|\mathbf{N}(v_{i})\cap\mathbf{N}(v_{j})|\leq\log n\cdot\max\{np^{2},1\}
for all distinct
i,j\in[n]

Proof.

We will show that each property holds with probability
1-o(1)
. Notice that for any fixed
i\in[n]
, the probability that
|\mathbf{N}(v_{i})|\leq pn/2
is at most
e^{-pn/8}\leq 1/n^{2}
by a Chernoff bound (this holds whenever
p\geq 16\log n/n
, which is true for our setting of
k
). Union bounding over
i\in[n]
, we conclude that (i) holds with high probability. Next, take any distinct
i,j\in[n]
, and observe that the distribution of
|\mathbf{N}(v_{i})\cap\mathbf{N}(v_{j})|
is precisely
\operatorname{Bin}(n,p^{2})
. If
k=1
, then
np^{2}=\tilde{\Omega}(n)
and standard concentration bounds imply
|\mathbf{N}(v_{i})\cap\mathbf{N}(v_{j})|\leq np^{2}\log n
except with probability
1/n^{10}
. If
k\geq 2
, then
np^{2}\ll n^{-0.1}
and it can be directly shown that

\sum_{\ell=\log n}^{n}\binom{n}{\ell}\cdot p^{2\ell}\cdot(1-p^{2})^{n-\ell}<\sum_{\ell=\log n}^{n}(np^{2})^{\ell}=n^{-\omega(1)}.

In either case, taking a union bound over all pairs
i,j\in[n]
concludes (ii).
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Fix
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
c\leq 2k+1
. For
\mathbf{G}\sim\mathcal{G}(n,n,p)
with
p=n^{1/(2k-1)-1}/\log^{5}n
, and
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{G})
(from Subsection
4.1
), the following holds with probability
1-o(1)
: any linear sketch
(T,\mathrm{Dec})
which satisfies

•

The matrix
T\in\mathbb{Z}^{s\times 2n}
has
\|T\|_{\infty}\leq O((2n)^{n})
, and

•

\mathrm{Dec}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{s}\times[2n]\to\{0,1\}
is an output function such that the probability over
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
that there exists
q\in[2n]
satisfying
\mathrm{Dec}(T\boldsymbol{x},q)\in\{0,1\}\setminus\{\textup{{AFN}}^{1,c}_{\mathbf{G}}(\boldsymbol{x},q)\}
is at most
1/(200n)
,

must have
s\geq\tilde{\Omega}(n^{1/(2k-1)})
.

Proof.

Notice that the lower bound is vacuous when
k=\Omega(\log n/\log\log n)
. Thus, for the remainder of the proof, we may assume
k\leq\log n/20\log\log n
.

The proof is via a direct reduction to Theorem
1.3
using
\ell_{0}
-samplers (Lemma
2.4
) and Claim
4.7
. In particular, we design a linear sketch
(T^{\prime},\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime})
which will satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1.3
with the approximation factor
c^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=k
. The sketch
(T^{\prime},\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime})
does the following:

•

It initializes an assumed sketch
(T,\mathrm{Dec})
from the hypothesis of this lemma.

•

It initializes
L
independent
\ell_{0}
-samplers from Lemma
2.4
for vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{2n}
and
\delta=0.1
, for a parameter
L
(which will be set to
100b\log n
for
b\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\log n\cdot\max\{np^{2},1\}=o(np)
).

The matrix
T^{\prime}
is obtained by stacking the
s
rows of
T
, as well as the
L\cdot O(\log n)
rows of the
\ell_{0}
-samplers, for a total dimensionality of
s+O(L\log n)
. Note that the bound
\|T^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq O((2n)^{n})
follows from the fact
\|T\|_{\infty}\leq O((2n)^{n})
, and the
\ell_{0}
-samplers have entries in
\{-1,0,1\}
(from Lemma
2.4
). The decoding function
\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
proceeds as follows:

1.

Extract from the
L
many
\ell_{0}
-samplers, coordinates
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\ell}_{L}\in[2n]\cup\{\bot\}
such that any non-failing index
t\in[L]
(i.e., one whose
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{t}\neq\bot
) satisfies
\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{t}}\neq 0
.

2.

Second, we find the unique
i\in[n]
such that
u_{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}},\dots,u_{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{L}}\in N(v_{i})\cup\{u_{i}\}
, which we show exists as long as
|\{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\ell}_{L}\}|\geq b+2
from the description of
\mathcal{D}
and Claim
4.7
. We output
\mathrm{Dec}(T\boldsymbol{x},v_{i})
.

We now show that
\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
1.3
, that is, that the probability over
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
that
\mathrm{Dec}^{\prime}(T^{\prime}\boldsymbol{x})=\mathrm{diam}_{\mathbf{G}}^{2,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is at least
1-1/(100n)
. In particular, we consider any draw of
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
which was generated with
\boldsymbol{i}
, and we first claim that, as long as
L=100b\log n
, the
\ell_{0}
-samplers return satisfy
|\{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\ell}_{L}\}|\geq b+2
with probability
1-o(1/n)
. This occurs for two reasons. First, (i) each
\ell_{0}
-sampler fails independently with probability at most
0.1
, so by a standard Chernoff bound, there are at most
L/2

\ell_{0}
-samplers which fail. Second, (ii) whenever at least
L/2
many
\ell_{0}
-samplers succeed, each is distributed uniformly among indices
j\in[n]
where
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\neq 0
; if

Appendix B
Reduction to Path-Independent Algorithm

The goal of this section is to provide a self-contained proof that, given any randomized streaming algorithm
\mathcal{A}
computing some function

which computes

over any distribution

supported on finitely many frequency vectors. Reductions of this form were introduced in
[
Gan08
,
LNW14
]
as a core component of strengthening lower bounds for linear sketches into lower bounds for general streaming algorithms. The main theorem, relating general randomized streaming algorithms
\mathcal{A}
to path-independent algorithms
\mathcal{B}
, is given below and corresponds to Theorem 5 of
[
LNW14
]
—the crucial definitions of randomized streaming algorithms and path-independent algorithms, appear in Definition
2.6
and Definition
2.10
.

For
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a\leq b
, we will use the notation
\llbracket a,b\rrbracket\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}
. We will also use
\log x
to denote the base-
2
logarithm of
x
.

\lnwthm

To prove Theorem
2.3
, we will first generate a graph corresponding to possible working tapes of the streaming algorithm
\mathcal{A}
. In a streaming algorithm, the number of possible configurations of working tapes may be infinite (as the algorithm is meant to support arbitrarily long streams); however, it will be helpful for us to define a finite graph, which is where we will use the fact that we have a finite bound. Namely, the fact that distribution
\mathcal{D}
is finitely-supported means there is a large enough
m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})\subset\llbracket-m^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rrbracket^{n}
. Then, our construction will be parametrized by any fixed bound
m\geq m^{\prime}
. As mentioned earlier, while the fact

is finite is important for the construction, it will not affect the complexity analysis, as we have the space complexity bound
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{B},\ell)\leq\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A},\ell)
and
\mathcal{S}^{+}(\mathcal{B},\ell)\leq\mathcal{S}^{+}(\mathcal{A},\ell)
for all
\ell\leq m
. The subsequent sections give the construction of
\mathcal{B}
; we note that multiple intermediate lemmas and definitions must be established to ensure the objects are well-defined and that they satisfy the desired bounds on space complexity and failure probability. The overview of the proof, which solely connects the various lemmas and sections, appears in Subsection
B.4
.

B.3

and Correctness

In order to complete the construction of the deterministic, path-independent algorithm

Lemma B.13

(Finite Collection of Zero-Frequency Streams)
.

For any randomized streaming algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with finite randomness complexity

, there exists a finite collection

and any

(o_{1},o_{2})\text{ is an edge of }G(\rho)\iff\exists\sigma\in S\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\begin{array}[]{c}\text{transitioning $\mathcal{A}$ from $o_{1}$ with randomness tape $\rho$}\\
\text{and input stream $\sigma$ gives working tape $o_{2}$.}\end{array}

Proof.

We use the finite bound on

and

to obtain a finite bound on the length of streams needed to transition edges in

. First, for any

and any edge

of

\text{stream $\sigma$ with randomness tape $\rho$ gives $o_{2}$}\end{array}\right\}.

The above is well-defined, since lengths of zero-frequency streams are positive integers, and the fact

is an edge of

means at least one such zero-frequency stream exists. Now, using the fact there are finitely many

be the maximum over all

depends solely on

, so we will drop the expectation over

whenever the expectation over

, and therefore,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathrm{Dec}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime};\rho,v)\in\{0,1\}\setminus\{g(\boldsymbol{x})\}\right]\leq 2\cdot\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{o}^{\prime}}\left[\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{o}^{\prime},\rho)\in\{0,1\}\setminus\{g(\boldsymbol{x})\}\right]\right]\leq 8\delta.

∎

Appendix C
A Basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
from the Path-Independent Algorithm

In this section, we consider a fixed deterministic and path-independent algorithm
\mathcal{B}
, which is specified by
(\mathcal{W},\mathrm{Enc},\mathrm{Dec},\oplus)
, and the goal of this section is to extract a special
s\times n
matrix
T
whose complexity (i.e., the number of rows
s
and the magnitude of entries in
T
) depends on the space complexity of the algorithm
\mathcal{B}
. The discussion follows the works of
[
Gan08
,
LNW14
]
which show that the so-called “kernel” of the encoding function
\mathrm{Enc}(\cdot)
of a path-independent algorithm can be used to define a sub-module of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Because we have imposed the condition that
\mathrm{Enc}(\cdot)
satisfies the path-independence property to bound
m
, and because the function
\mathrm{Enc}(\cdot)
is only defined on vectors
\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{n}
, we provide the arguments. For the rest of the section, we consider a fixed setting of a large enough bound
m\in\mathbb{N}
, as well as a fixed
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{W},\mathrm{Enc},\mathrm{Dec},\oplus)
, and we refer the reader to Definition
2.11
for the definition of the zero-set of
\mathrm{Enc}(\cdot)
, as well as the zero-set subspace.

Note, we defined the zero-set as a discrete subset of
\{-\ell,\dots,\ell\}^{n}
and the zero-set subspace as a subspace over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We avoid the discussion of modules (and the fact that an extension of
K(\ell)
is a sub-module of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
) so as to not confuse notions of linear independence over
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{R}
. In particular, we will always work with subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so that linear combinations are always taken with respect to real numbers.

Definition C.1

(Equivalence Classes)
.

For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
\ell<m/2
and
x\in\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{n}
, the equivalence class
C(x;\ell)
is

C(x;\ell)=\left\{y\in\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}y-x\in K(2\ell)\right\}.

The fact that we restrict
\ell<m/2
in Definition
C.1
is due to the subsequent lemma, where we use the fact
\mathcal{B}
is path-independent with bound
m
to connect the encoding map
\mathrm{Enc}(\cdot)
with the equivalence classes
C(x;\ell)
defined above. Since path-independence with bound
m
(in Definition
2.10
) requires us to restrict the underlying vectors to those within
\{-(m-1),\dots,m-1\}^{n}
, the condition
\ell<m/2
enables us to conclude that any
x,y\in\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{n}
has
x-y\in\llbracket-(m-1),m-1\rrbracket^{n}
.

Lemma C.2
.

For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
\ell<m/2
and any two
x,y\in\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{n}
,

\mathrm{Enc}(x)=\mathrm{Enc}(y)\iff y\in C(x;\ell)\iff x\in C(y;\ell).

Proof.

Let
0^{n}=p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{h}=x
denote a shortest path in a graph whose vertices are
\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{n}
, and there is an edge connecting
(u,v)
whenever
u-v=\xi e_{i}
for some
\xi\in\{-1,1\}
and
i\in[n]
. Notice that for all
k\in[h]
, we have
x-p_{k}\in\llbracket-2\ell,2\ell\rrbracket^{n}
and
y-p_{k}\in\llbracket-2\ell,2\ell\rrbracket^{n}
since
x,y,p_{k}\in\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{n}
since we selected a shortest path. By the setting of
\ell<m/2
, we always have
x-p_{k},y-p_{k}\in\llbracket-(m-1),m-1\rrbracket^{n}
, and hence we can always apply the path-independence property (Definition
2.10
) to conclude, if
(p_{k},p_{k+1})
is the edge with
p_{k}-p_{k+1}=\xi e_{i}
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Enc}(x-p_{k})\oplus\xi e_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Enc}(x-p_{k}+\xi e_{i})=\mathrm{Enc}(x-p_{k+1}),\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Enc}(y-p_{k})\oplus\xi e_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Enc}(y-p_{k}+\xi e_{i})=\mathrm{Enc}(y-p_{k+1}).

Therefore, a simple induction on all
k\in[h]
, we have

\mathrm{Enc}(x-p_{k})=\mathrm{Enc}(y-p_{k})\iff\mathrm{Enc}(x)=\mathrm{Enc}(y)

Setting
k=h
implies
\mathrm{Enc}(0)=\mathrm{Enc}(y-x)
, i.e.,
y-x\in K(2\ell)
and
y\in C(x;\ell)
, iff
\mathrm{Enc}(x)=\mathrm{Enc}(y)
. The final equivalence is because the choice of
x
and
y
is symmetric.
∎

We now show that the size of the equivalence classes
C(x;\ell)
is related to the dimensionality of the zero-set subspace
M(\ell)
. We will then use the lemma below in order to conclude that small-space streaming algorithms must have zero-sets whose subspace has dimensionality close to
n
.

Lemma C.3
.

For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
\ell<m/2
, any
x\in\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{n}
satisfies
|C(x;\ell)|\leq(\ell+1)^{t(2\ell)}
.

Proof.

Let
B
be the
n\times t(2\ell)
matrix whose columns correspond to the vectors
b_{1},\dots,b_{t(2\ell)}\in K(2\ell)
. The matrix
B
has rank
t(2\ell)
by definition, and therefore there exists a set of
t(2\ell)
rows
R\subset[n]
whose columns are linearly independent. Let
T
be the
t(2\ell)\times n
matrix with a single
1
in each row such that
TB
is the
t(2\ell)\times t(2\ell)
full-rank matrix which selects the rows of
B
. Consider the map
f\colon C(x;\ell)\to\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{t(2\ell)}
given by
y\in C(x;\ell)\mapsto Ty\in\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{t(2\ell)}
, whose image has size
(\ell+1)^{t(2\ell)}
. We now argue that the map is injective as follows: suppose
y,y^{\prime}\in C(x;\ell)
with
Ty=Ty^{\prime}
, then
y-y^{\prime}\in K
, so there exists a unique
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{t(2\ell)}
where
y-y^{\prime}=B\alpha
. Then, we have
0=T(y-y^{\prime})=TB\alpha
, but since
TB
is full-rank,
\alpha=0
and hence
y-y^{\prime}=0
.
∎

Lemma C.4
.

For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
\ell<m/2
,
\mathcal{S}^{+}(\mathcal{B},\ell)\geq(n-t(2\ell))\log_{2}(\ell+1)
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{S}^{+}(\mathcal{B},\ell)
is the logarithm of the distinct number of encoded states resulting from non-negative vectors. By Lemma
C.2
,

\displaystyle\left|\left\{\mathrm{Enc}(x)\in\mathcal{W}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x\in\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{n}\right\}\right|=\left|\left\{C(x;\ell)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x\in\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{n}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\ell+1)^{n}}{\max\{|C(x;\ell)|\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x\in\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket^{n}\}}

\displaystyle\geq(\ell+1)^{n-t(2\ell)},

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
C.3
.
∎

Proof of Lemma

.

We let
\ell=1
, so that

and
\mathcal{S}^{+}(\mathcal{B},1)\geq n-t
from Lemma

. We let

B
be the
t\times n
matrix whose rows are

, and note that
x\in M^{\perp}
if and only if
Bx=0
. Since
B

t

which are linearly independent, and hence consider the
t\times t
sub-matrix
B^{\prime}
given by the columns of
B
in
C
, since

is a full-rank

(here, we index the coordinates of

and by Cramer’s rule, each

x^{(j)}_{j}=-\det(B^{\prime})\qquad\text{and}\qquad x^{(j)}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&i\in[n]\setminus C\setminus\{j\}\\
\alpha_{i}\cdot\det(B^{\prime})&i\in C\end{array}\right..
