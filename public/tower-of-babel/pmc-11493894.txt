Simultaneous segmentation and classification of 99mTc-DMSA renal scintigraphic images with a deep learning approach
Authors: Jiayi Wang, Mingyan Wu, Xiemei Ruan, Jiaying Zhang, Zhengguo Chen, Yihui Zhai, Hong Xu, Ha Wu, Jeff L Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground99mTc-DMSA scan plays an important role in assessing functional abnormalities in kidneys. As a promising network for deep learning (DL), Mask R-CNN has the capability of simultaneously segmenting and classifying objects in images. In this study, we tested the feasibility and accuracy of Mask R-CNN in diagnosing acute pyelonephritis (APN) and segmenting kidneys in 99mTc-DMSA scintigraphic images. Two hundred and sixty patients with suspected APN were recruited for DMSA scan, of which 358 kidneys were diagnosed as APN. Of the recruited patients, 210 were randomly selected for training and validating Mask R-CNN, and the other 50 patients’ images were used for model testing. Accuracy of the results was assessed by comparing against references from human experts.ResultsIn the validation phase, the trained model provided segmentation masks with intersection over union (mask IoU, for segmentation accuracy) of 86.6%, and classifications with mean average precision at the bounding box IoU ≥ 50% (mAP50, for classification accuracy) of 86.2%. In testing with the 50 independent patients, mask IoU of the model’s segmentation was 90.3%±2.2%, and in classifying the kidneys for APN, the trained model showed accuracy of 89.0%, sensitivity of 84.8% and specificity of 97.0%. In identifying patients with any APN kidney, 3 out of 50 patients were mis-diagnosed, achieving accuracy of 94.0%.ConclusionsMask R-CNN, designed to perform both segmentation and classification for images, showed much promise in analyzing 99mTc-DMSA images for both accurate diagnosis of APN and kidney segmentation.Keywords: 99mTc-DMSA, Deep learning, Acute pyelonephritis, Image classification, Image segmentation

Background

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is common among children (Korbel et al. 2017), with prevalence rate of 7.0% in infants of 24 months or younger (Shaikh et al. 2008). Repeated infection of urinary tracts can cause acute pyelonephritis (APN), which if neglected or mistreated may progress to permanent renal damage. Therefore, in clinical practice, it is recommended for pediatric patients with UTI to routinely perform kidney imaging, most commonly renal scintigraphy with Technetium-99m dimercaptosuccinic acid (99mTc-DMSA), to identify potential abnormality in renal parenchyma (Nammalwar et al. 2005; Verboven et al. 1990; Riccabona 2016; Kwatra et al. 2013; Nguyen et al. 2010).

In 99mTc-DMSA images, cortex of heathy kidney would show timely and homogeneous enhancement as the tracer accumulates; with APN, the enhancement would often be lower, leading to indistinct margin of a kidney, or in other cases, tracer enhancement decreases in only part of renal cortex (Mandell et al. 1997). Currently, diagnosis of APN is based on visual inspection of the above-mentioned features in the 99mTc-DMSA images. In addition, the same images are also used in clinical practice to compute kidney volume to estimate renal function, or further derive relative renal function based on tracer uptake of the two kidneys. Therefore, segmentation of kidneys in the images is also a routine step of image processing. With more complicated patterns of enhancement in kidneys of APN, segmentation of kidneys with APN can be more challenging than that of heathy ones.

In the past years, multiple promising methods have been proposed for either the diagnosis (or classification, a more frequently used term in image processing) of APN and/or kidney segmentation in 99mTc-DMSA images. In previous studies, the two tasks were often implemented separately with different methods (Levtchenko et al. 2001; Yen et al. 1996; Rombolotti et al. 2022; Cao et al. 2012), making its implementation cumbersome. (Sampedroa et al. 2017) proposed a framework that combines both automatic segmentation of kidney and further assessment of renal damage using radiomics analysis. (Wright et al. 2014) classified healthy versus APN kidneys using image features extracted by principal component analysis and heuristic feature method. Deep learning (DL) has advanced rapidly in the past few years, showing much promise in performing multiple image-processing tasks without the need for manual selection of image features. (Lin et al. 2023) trained a convolutional neural network (CNN) to automatically extract features and classify scarred kidneys from healthy ones with accuracy of more than 90%.

Recently, a deep-learning network termed as Mask R-CNN was proposed (He et al. 2017), with the capability of simultaneously classifying and segmenting images. We realized that the network could be used to process 99mTc-DMSA images of kidneys, to perform diagnostic classification of APN and at the same time segment kidneys. This study aims to build and train Mask R-CNN, and to assess its accuracy in diagnosing APN and in segmenting kidney in 99mTc-DMSA images.

Methods

In the following, we first describe how the 99mTc-DMSA images were acquired, followed by introduction of Mask R-CNN. Then we introduce how the images were processed to prepare for the training and testing of the network.

99m Tc-DMSA renal scintigraphy

This study was approved by the Ethics Board of the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University (CHFU) and by the IRB committee of ShanghaiTech University. As a retrospective study, we included 260 patients (153 males; age 1.3 ∼ 184.7 months) who had been diagnosed with UTI and underwent 99mTc-DMSA scintigraphy for screening of APN, between January 2016 and December 2018.

In a clinical single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) system (Siemens Medical Solution, Hoffman Estates, IL), the patient was positioned supine and scanned with a standard clinical protocol. Specifically, 1.85 MBq/kg of 99mTc-DMSA was intravenously injected, with a minimum dose of 18.5 MBq and a maximum of 185 MBq. About 2–3 h after tracer injection, images covering the two kidneys were acquired with the following parameter values: energy peak 140 keV, window width 20%, acquisition matrix 256 × 256, and acquisition count 300,000. The acquired images were transferred to a Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS) station, where the images were inspected by an experienced nuclear-medicine physician to detect hypoactive areas that would cause diminished edges along part or entire contour of a kidney, or partial or entire kidney regions getting enlarged due to defects (Mandell et al. 1997).

Mask R-CNN for image classification and segmentation

Mask R-CNN was originally proposed for detecting and then classifying objects in an image, and when implemented, outputs the segmentation mask of the object of interest as well (He et al. 2017). In the following, we introduce how Mask R-CNN was customized and trained to classify and segment kidneys in 99mTc-DMSA images.

The architecture of Mask R-CNN is shown in Fig. 1. As the first component of the network, the ResNet50-FPN backbone (He et al. 2016; Lin et al. 2017) extracted the features of the input image, obtaining the feature maps. In the backbone, ResNet or residual network consisted of multiple residual blocks, in which the outputs were added to the inputs. FPN or feature pyramid network merged the bottom-up features with top-down information across multiple scales using lateral connection of 1 × 1 convolution layer, followed by 3 × 3 convolutional layer to produce feature maps of various scales (Lin et al. 2017). The incorporation of FPN into ResNet enriched the model’s perceptual range to preserve more original information. As the second component of the network, RPN or region proposal network (Ren et al. 2015) produced a large number of anchors of different size for each pixel in the feature map, and used the non-maximum suppression (NMS) method (Neubeck and Gool 2006) to eliminate the anchors that do not contain object of interest. Then at the step of ROI Align (He et al. 2017), the selected anchor boxes generated from the resized feature maps were adjusted so that their size and position fitted for the original image. As the final component of the network, the regions of interest (ROIs) from RPN were fed into two parallel pathways: a fully convolutional network (FCN) for kidney segmentation, and a set of fully connected layers (FC) for classification of kidneys with and without APN (Long et al. 2015). For more details of Mask R-CNN, readers can refer to its original paper (He et al. 2017).

Architecture of Mask R-CNN model for analyzing 99mTc-DMSA images. The network contains 4 components: ResNet50-FPN as backbone, RPN, ROI Align, and FCN or FC for outputting segmentation or classification outcome. ResNet50-FPN is to extract features and generate feature maps. RPN first produces different-size anchors for each point in the feature map, then splits into two branches: classification of object from its background, and adjustment of each anchor’s position. In the step of ROI Align, the sizes of the anchors are adjusted. In the final step, segmentation mask of kidney and classification labels are generated

Image processing and annotation

For the recruited 260 patients, an experienced nuclear-medicine physician made diagnosis by visual inspection of the acquired 99mTc-DMSA images, identifying 358 kidneys with APN (or abnormal) and 162 normal kidneys. These diagnosis results were used as the annotations of classification for the kidneys in model training and testing. For implementation of Mask R-CNN, the other two types of annotations for the images were also needed, including detection box and segmentation mask. Using the LabelMe software (Russell et al. 2008), segmentation mask for each kidney was manually delineated by an expert with 10 years’ experience in renal image processing. Based on the mask of each kidney, we generated the smallest upright rectangular box that covered the entire mask, and regarded it as the kidney’s detection box. All the masks and detection boxes were independently checked by two experts with 3–10 years’ experience in renal image processing.

Of the recruited 260 patients, 210 were randomly selected for the training of Mask R-CNN, consisting of 292 abnormal and 128 normal kidneys; the other 50 patients for model testing contained 66 abnormal and 34 normal kidneys. To better match the ratio of abnormal vs. normal cases in the training and testing groups, we increased the number of normal kidneys in the training group by simple transformations. Specifically, for each of the 6 patients with two normal kidneys in the training group, we transformed the image by horizontal flip, vertical flip, and 180-degree rotation (Shorten and Khoshgoftaar 2019), generating 3 more images. As a result, the training group contained 228 images, with 292 abnormal and 146 normal kidneys, and both the training group and the testing group had the ratio of abnormal vs. normal cases of about 2:1.

Model training

For the ResNet50-FPN backbone in the network, we used its pre-trained weights based on the ImageNet dataset (Deng et al. 2009). Our training group of 228 images were used to train the parameters of the other components of the network. Of the 228 images, 90% or 205 randomly selected images were used for model training, and the other 10% for model validation. The random distribution was repeated for three times for three rounds of training and validation, to reduce potential randomization error. Each training data fed into the training process included one image and its corresponding annotations: classification label (abnormal or normal), segmentation mask and detection box for each of the two kidneys, which was evaluated with loss function of log loss, binary cross-entropy loss and smooth L1 loss, respectively. The three loss functions were summed up as the loss for optimization. The total training epoch was 50, with a batch size of 2, a momentum of 0.9, a weight decay of 1e-4, and an optimizer of Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD). The learning rate was initially set at 0.04, decreased to 0.004 and 0.0004 at the 10th and 20th epoch, respectively. The parameter “mask_roi_pool” was set at 34. In the training, the loss converged at about the 40th epoch, to a value of 0.13 ∼ 0.14.

Performance of the trained model

Performance of the trained Mask R-CNN model was assessed in two steps: with the datasets for validation and then in testing with 50 independent data. For the validation dataset (randomly selected 10% of the training data), we recorded values for two metrics: mask intersection over union (mask IoU) and mean average precision at bounding box IoU ≥ 50% (mAP50), which are the standard metrics for assessing Mask R-CNN’s performance. For evaluating segmentation’s accuracy, mask IoU was computed as the overlapped area between the model’s predicted mask and the reference mask, divided by the union area of the two masks. The metric mAP50 quantifies the model’s accuracy in classifying the kidneys as abnormal versus normal. In computing mAP50, precision-recall curve for either normal or abnormal kidney was generated by computing precision and recall rates using bounding box with IoU equal or larger than 50%; mean of the areas under the curves for both normal- and abnormal-kidney classes was denoted as mAP50.

With the 50 independent datasets, we further tested the trained model’s performance in kidney segmentation and classification. Mask IoU and Dice coefficient were computed for evaluating kidney segmentation. The Dice coefficient was calculated as two times of the pixel number of the intersection of the model’s predicted mask and the reference mask, divided by sum of the two masks’ pixel numbers. For diagnostic classification, we computed the accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of the classification outcomes. Here, accuracy was computed as the ratio of the number of correctly classified normal and abnormal kidneys over the entire number of kidneys, sensitivity as the ratio of the number of correctly predicted abnormal kidneys over the number of true abnormal kidneys, and specificity as the ratio of the number of correctly predicted normal kidneys over the number of true normal kidneys. In clinical practice, diagnosis of abnormality in either of the two kidneys for a patient would often result in diagnosis of abnormality for the patient. Therefore, we also computed the diagnosis accuracy as the percentage of correctly diagnosed abnormal patients over the entire group of 50 patients.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the Mask R-CNN’s performance in segmenting kidneys and diagnosing APN as assessed in the model-validation phase. Three rounds of training and then validation were performed, so Table 1 lists three sets of results. Parameter mAP50 for assessing classification accuracy ranged from 85.0 to 86.9%, with average of 86.2%, and mask IoU for assessing segmentation accuracy ranged from 86.5 to 86.7%, with average of 86.6%. These results demonstrate high performance of the network in both classification and segmentation, and also indicate that the potential randomization error in the 9:1 data distribution was low.

Figure 2 shows the result of classification and segmentation by the trained Mask R-CNN for a representative example in the testing datasets. The two kidneys, one normal and the other abnormal, were correctly classified, and the segmentation masks were comparable to the masks delineated by a human operator. For all the 50 independent data that consisted of 66 abnormal and 34 normal kidneys, the trained network achieved diagnosis with accuracy of 89.0% (89/100), sensitivity of 84.8% (56/66) and specificity of 97.0% (33/34). In terms of number of correctly diagnosed patients, 3 out of the 50 patients were mis-diagnosed, resulting in diagnosis accuracy of 94.0%. For kidney segmentation, mask IoU averaged for the 100 kidneys was 90.3%±2.2%, and Dice coefficient was 94.9%±1.2%.

Discussion

By this study we explored the feasibility of using Mask R-CNN to analyze 99mTc-DMSA renal images. Previously, (Lin et al. 2020) demonstrated the network’s promise in segmenting thorax bone SPECT images. The design of Mask R-CNN makes it perfect for fulfilling the clinical needs for analyzing 99mTc-DMSA renal images. First, one primary function of the network is to detect objects in an image. While the object-detection function was not explicitly employed in this study, it made the detection of two kidneys in an image fully automatic; in other words, with the method, we can process the image without the need of first cropping the kidneys, as most other methods do. Second, following object detection, two secondary functions of the network are to classify the objects and to accurately delineate their contours or masks. This procedure is exactly how the renal images are clinically analyzed, i.e. localize the two kidneys, determine whether they are abnormal, and draw their contour for estimating their sizes.

The trained network in this study showed excellent accuracy for both the classification and segmentation tasks. As a comparison, Wright et al. in a pioneer study (Wright et al. 2014) achieved classification accuracy of 93.3% in diagnosing APN, but their manual selection of image features require additional time cost and is usually difficult to generalize to new data. The first work (Lin et al. 2023) using CNN for the classification managed to achieve automatic extraction of features, and resulting in an accuracy of 85.0% and a sensitivity of 75.0%. In this current study, Mask R-CNN performed both kidney classification and segmentation, with accuracy of about 90%.

The performance of the trained network can be improved further. In checking the mis-classified cases in the testing group, we found one representative example (Fig. 3), in which the classification had been made based on mostly the contour but less other features of the kidney. In Fig. 3a, the right kidney, which is pointed by a red arrow, was diagnosed as APN, based on observation that even though the kidney had regular pea-like shape, it showed relatively lower and non-uniform enhancement of tracer. However, as shown in Fig. 3b, the kidney was falsely classified by the network as normal. We speculated that the network could rely too much on a kidney’s contour for classification. For comparison, in Fig. 3c and d, we show a correctly classified normal kidney (pointed by a green arrow), which displays contour of similar shape but more intense and uniform enhancement of tracer. Therefore, we believe that the network can be further improved to learn more features of kidneys, including but not limited to tracer enhancement level, enhancement distribution, and even possibly contrast with the contralateral kidney. In future study, this improvement will be explored in either adjusting the structure of the network, simply increasing the number of training data, or both. Furthermore, patient’s medical history, which is often considered in interpreting images by physicians, should be somehow incorporated into the network’s classification in future work.

For comparison, we showed a different case (c and d), the right kidney (pointed by green arrow) of which was diagnosed as normal (c) and was correctly classified by the network as normal (d). The two kidneys in discussion had contours of similar shape, but the correctly classified one (c) showed higher and more uniform enhancement of tracer

The study has a few limitations. First, the datasets used in this study were collected from a same hospital using the same data acquisition protocol. Considering that different hospitals’ protocol can be different, the trained network’s performance on different data could be compromised. This will be evaluated in future with data collected differently. Second, this study included pediatric patients from a wide range of age, from 1 to 184 months. Renal structure and function develop substantially during this period, so that characteristics of the imaged kidneys can be quite complex even if they were all normal. A thorough approach of study would be to recruit patients of similar age and thus similar stage of kidney development, and then study how APN impacts a kidney. Third, the proposed network used 99mTc-DMSA image only for APN diagnosis, so ignores other potentially useful information of patient, such as blood test, urine tests and medical history.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the trained Mask R-CNN demonstrated excellent performance in diagnosing APN and segmenting kidneys based on 99mTc-DMSA images. The results provided by the method could be incorporated into clinical practice to assist and facilitate the reporting process of nuclear-medicine physicians. In future work, the method can be further improved for higher accuracy, and similar approach can be extended to the diagnosis of other renal diseases.
