The fast rate of convergence of the smooth adapted Wasserstein distance
Martin Larsson, Jonghwa Park, Johannes Wiesel

Abstract
Estimating a d d -dimensional distribution μ \mu by the empirical measure μ ^ n \accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n} of its samples is an important task in probability theory, statistics and machine learning. It is well known that 𝔼 [ 𝒲 p ( μ ^ n , μ ) ] ≲ n − 1 / d \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)]\lesssim n^{-1/d} for d > 2 ​ p d>2p , where 𝒲 p \mathcal{W}_{p} denotes the p p -Wasserstein metric. An effective tool to combat this curse of dimensionality is the smooth Wasserstein distance 𝒲 p ( σ ) \mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p} , which measures the distance between two probability measures after having convolved them with isotropic Gaussian noise 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , σ 2 ​ I ) \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\text{I}) . In this paper we apply this smoothing technique to the adapted Wasserstein distance. We show that the smooth adapted Wasserstein distance 𝒜 ​ 𝒲 p ( σ ) \mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)} achieves the fast rate of convergence 𝔼 [ 𝒜 𝒲 p ( σ ) ( μ ^ n , μ ) ] ≲ n − 1 / 2 \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)]\lesssim n^{-1/2} , if μ \mu is subgaussian. This result follows from the surprising fact, that any subgaussian measure μ \mu convolved with a Gaussian distribution has locally Lipschitz kernels. Keywords: empirical measure, (smooth, adapted) Wasserstein distance, fast rate, curse of dimensionality, Lipschitz kernels

Introduction

Let the Borel probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
be the laws of two stochastic processes
X=(X_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
and
Y=(Y_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
on the path space
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
, where
d\geq 1
and
T\geq 2
. Let furthermore
\mathcal{P}_{p}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})
denote the set of all Borel probability measures on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
with finite
p
-moments, where
1\leq p<\infty
is fixed. The weak topology on
\mathcal{P}_{p}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})
is metrized by the
p
-Wasserstein distance

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\left(\inf_{\pi\in\cpl(\mu,\nu)}\int\left|x-y\right|^{p}\pi(dx,dy)\right)^{1/p}.

(1)

Here,
\left|\cdot\right|
denotes the
\ell^{2}
-norm on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
and
\cpl(\mu,\nu)
is the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
; see
[
37
,
36
,
35
]
for a general overview of optimal transport theory and the Wasserstein distance.

For computational as well as estimation purposes,
\mu
is often approximated by its empirical distribution
\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{X^{(j)}}
where
X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(n)}
are i.i.d samples from
\mu
. By the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem,
\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}
converges weakly to
\mu
almost surely as the sample size
n
approaches infinity. As a consequence,
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)
vanishes with probability one. However, this convergence is severely impeded by an exponential dependence on the dimension
dT
of the path space, posing a challenge for computational efficiency. In fact,
[
15
]
shows the sharp
curse of dimensionality (COD)
convergence rates
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)]\leq Cn^{-1/(dT)}
whenever
dT>2p
and
\mu
is supported on
([0,1]^{d})^{T}
.

In order to improve these convergence rates, the
smooth
p
-Wasserstein distance

\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
was recently studied
[
18
,
20
,
19
,
29
,
34
,
21
,
22
]
.

Definition 1

(Smooth Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})
. The smooth
p
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
with smoothing parameter
\sigma>0
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma},\nu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}).

(2)

Here
\ast
denotes the convolution operator and
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\text{I}_{dT})
is an isotropic Gaussian measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
.

Smoothing
\mathcal{W}_{p}
in this way leads to several interesting results. In particular, the expected
\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
-distance between
\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}
and
\mu
exhibits dimension-free convergence rates; this clearly improves upon the classical Wasserstein convergence rates discussed above. The following list gives a general overview of known results for
\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
:

(1)

Topological equivalence.
The two metrics
\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
and
\mathcal{W}_{p}
generate the same topology on
\mathcal{P}_{p}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})
. See
[
18
,
29
]
.

(2)

Stability.
As
\sigma\to 0
,
\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}\to\mathcal{W}_{p}
and the metric
\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
serves as a proxy for the original metric
\mathcal{W}_{p}
. In particular,
[
29
]
shows that for some
C>0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu,\nu)\leq\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)\leq\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu,\nu)+C\sigma.

(3)

(3)

Slow rate.
In
[
20
,
29
]
it is shown that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)^{p}]\leq Cn^{-1/2}
holds when
\mu
has a finite
q
-moment for some
q>2(dT+p)
. This result implies the
slow rate
of convergence, i.e., for some
C>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)]\leq Cn^{-1/(2p)}.

(4)

(4)

Fast rate.
If
\int e^{\beta\left|x\right|^{2}}\mu(dx)<\infty
for some
\beta>(p-1)/\sigma^{2}
(requiring
\mu
to be subgaussian), this can be improved to the
fast rate
of convergence, i.e., for some
C>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)]\leq Cn^{-1/2};

(5)

see
[
29
,
12
]
. Moreover,
[
20
,
12
]
together with
[
21
]
suggest that subgaussianity is necessary for the fast rate to hold.

(5)

Distributional limit.
Under the same assumption as in
(4)
,
\sqrt{n}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)
converges weakly to a supremum of a tight Gaussian process. Details can be found in
[
19
,
34
]
for
p=1
and
[
21
,
22
]
for
p>1
.

Taken together, the first two items show that the smoothed distance
\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
provides a good proxy for
\mathcal{W}_{p}
. For small values of
\sigma
, the smoothed distance remains close to the original Wasserstein distance. The third and fourth items highlight the improved statistical convergence rates achieved by the smoothed distance
\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
, which makes it particularly appealing for statistical applications.

When the dimension is sufficiently small, the slow rate
n^{-1/(2p)}
coincides with the classical Wasserstein rates for
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}_{p}(\widehat{\mu}_{n},\mu)]
; see
[
15
]
. Smoothing ensures that the rate
n^{-1/(2p)}
remain valid even in high dimensions.

When
p>1
, the fast rate
n^{-1/2}
implies strictly faster convergence than the slow rate
n^{-1/(2p)}
. Going back to the seminal work of Ajtai–Komlós–Tusnády
[
4
]
, it is well-known that the fast rate
n^{-1/2}
is achievable only if the support of
\mu
is regular enough or topologically connected. In our setting, convolving
\mu
with a Gaussian measure inherently ensures this connectivity by spreading mass across the entire space, and thereby facilitating the rate
n^{-1/2}
. While establishing the slow rate
(3)
under minimal assumptions is of independent interest, the distributional limit
(5)
suggests that the fast rate
(4)
is sharp in general. As a simple example, take
\mu=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{a}+\delta_{b})
for
a\neq b
. Similar to
[
15
, Example (a) on page 2]
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)]\geq Cn^{-1/2}
(see Appendix
B
for details).

While smoothing offers statistical advantages, it also introduces certain computational challenges. The convolution approach yields on optimal transport problem of Gaussian mixtures. To the best of our knowledge, efficient algorithms that exploit this mixture structure are currently unknown. One possible approach is to approximate the convolution by sampling from the Gaussian kernel, although this may partially offset the statistical advantages of the smoothed distance.

In this paper, we extend the smoothing technique for
\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)}
to the
adapted Wasserstein distance

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
and study the statistical properties of its smoothed counterpart
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)}
, the
smooth adapted Wasserstein distance
. The adapted Wasserstein distance was introduced to address the following issue:

For many time-dependent operators
F:\mathcal{P}_{p}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})\to\mathbb{R}
,
\liminf_{n\to\infty}|F(\mu_{n})-F(\mu)|>0
even if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu_{n},\mu)=0
.

Examples of such operators
F
are the value functions of optimal stopping problems, the Doob decomposition, superhedging problems, utility maximization, stochastic programming and risk measurements
[
30
,
31
,
17
,
2
,
8
]
; these commonly account for the time-structure of
\mu
and
\nu
. As we describe in more detail below,
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
generates the coarsest topology, which makes such operators continuous
[
9
]
. Having introduced
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)}
, the main result of this paper, presented in Theorem
4
, can be summarized as follows:

If
\mu
is subgaussian, then
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu,\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n})]\lesssim 1/\sqrt{n}
, i.e., the fast rate of convergence holds for the smooth adapted Wasserstein distance.

Before discussing Theorem
4
in Section
1.4
in more detail, we first recall basic facts about
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
in Section
1.1
and review existing results on finite-sample guarantees for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
and
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
in Section
1.2
and Section
1.3
, respectively.

1.1
Adapted distances

To illustrate the fact that the usual Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{p}
is inadequate for time-dependent optimization problems, take
T=2,d=1
and consider the laws
\mu=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{(0,1)}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{(0,-1)}
and
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{(\varepsilon,1)}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{(-\varepsilon,-1)}
for
\varepsilon>0
. Figure
1
illustrates their sample paths. It is evident that
\mu_{\varepsilon}
is close to
\mu
in Wasserstein distance for small
\varepsilon
; in fact,
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu_{\varepsilon},\mu)=\varepsilon
. However, the process
X^{\varepsilon}\sim\mu^{\epsilon}
differs significantly from
X\sim\mu
as its values at time
2
are already determined at time
1
. To see this, note that for
X=(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mu
and
X^{\varepsilon}=(X^{\varepsilon}_{1},X^{\varepsilon}_{2})\sim\mu_{\varepsilon}
,

\displaystyle\law(X_{2}|X_{1})=\left(\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-1}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{1}=0\}},\quad\law(X^{\varepsilon}_{2}\,|\,X^{\varepsilon}_{1})=\delta_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{\varepsilon}_{1}=\varepsilon\}}+\delta_{-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{\varepsilon}_{1}=-\varepsilon\}}.

(6)

As a consequence, the values of utility maximization problems and optimal stopping problems for
\mu^{\epsilon}
do not converge to the corresponding values for
\mu
.

Figure 1
:
\mu=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{(0,1)}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{(0,-1)}
on the left and
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{(\varepsilon,1)}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{(-\varepsilon,-1)}
on the right.

To take the flow of information formalized through the natural filtration of a stochastic process into account, several variants of the weak topology have been independently developed by different communities, e.g.,
[
26
,
5
,
24
,
33
,
23
,
27
,
1
,
13
]
. Notably,
[
9
]
demonstrates that all these seemingly different variants of topologies coincide and are generated by the adapted Wasserstein distance. In order to define it formally, we first introduce the notion of
bicausal couplings
.

Definition 2

(Bicausal coupling)
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
. A coupling
\pi\in\cpl(\mu,\nu)
is bicausal if for
(X,Y)\sim\pi
and
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T-1\}
,

\displaystyle(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{t})\text{ and }(X_{t+1},\ldots,X_{T})\text{ are conditionally independent given }X_{1},\ldots,X_{t},

(7)

and

\displaystyle(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t})\text{ and }(Y_{t+1},\ldots,Y_{T})\text{ are conditionally independent given }Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{t}.

(8)

The set of all bicausal couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
is denoted by
\cplbc(\mu,\nu)
.

Definition 3

(The adapted Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})
. The adapted
p
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\left(\inf_{\pi\in\cplbc(\mu,\nu)}\int\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|x_{t}-y_{t}\right|^{p}\pi(dx,dy)\right)^{1/p}.

(9)

Similarly to (
2
), the smooth adapted
p
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
with smoothing parameter
\sigma>0
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma},\nu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}).

(10)

As mentioned above, the adapted Wasserstein distance induces the coarsest topology that makes optimal stopping problems continuous
[
9
]
. This topology is finer than the weak topology.

1.2
Approximations in adapted distances

Unlike the Wasserstein case, it is well-known (e.g.,
[
32
,
6
]
) that the empirical measure
\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}
does not converge to
\mu
in adapted Wasserstein distance as
n\to\infty
. To ensure approximation of
\mu
by empirical data in adapted Wasserstein sense,
[
6
]
devise the so-called
adapted empirical measure

\boldsymbol{\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}}
as an alternative to the empirical measure
\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}
. It is defined as a projection of
\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}
onto a refining grid and satisfies
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}},\mu)=0
. However the convergence rate obtained for
\boldsymbol{\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}}
is essentially the same as the Wasserstein COD rates for
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)]
. In fact, it was shown in
[
6
,
3
]
that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}},\mu)]\leq Cn^{-1/(dT)}
for some
C>0
when
d\geq 3
and
\mu
is a probability measure on
([0,1]^{d})^{T}
that has Lipschitz kernels. As
\mathcal{W}_{p}\leq C\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
for some constant
C
that depends only on
d,T
, the rates for
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}},\mu)]
are sharp.

Motivated by the non-adapted counterpart
\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
, smoothing techniques have been introduced to achieve dimension-free adapted Wasserstein approximations. One of the earliest works in this direction is
[
32
]
, which states that
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}\ast\eta_{\sigma_{n}})
converges to zero in probability under arguably restrictive assumptions on
\mu
. Here
\eta_{\sigma_{n}}
are non-Gaussian smoothing kernels converging weakly to the Dirac distribution
\delta_{0}
for
\sigma_{n}\to 0
. Precise statements can be found in
[
32
, Theorem
4
]
. On the contrary, we keep
\sigma>0
fixed throughout this work.

1.3
Prior results for the smooth adapted Wasserstein distance

Paralleling our list for
\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)}
above, let us now provide an overview of known results for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)}
. It seems natural to conjecture that items (1)-(5) still hold when replacing
\mathcal{W}_{p}
by
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
and
\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)}
by
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)}
. Perhaps surprisingly, it turns out that this is not the case, as already the first item on the list fails. Here is the corresponding list of facts for the adapted Wasserstein distance:

(1)

Topology.
The two metrics
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
and
\mathcal{W}_{p}
generate the same topology on on
\mathcal{P}_{p}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})

[
10
]
. Note that this is
not
the same topology as the one generated by
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
.

(2)

Stability.
For small values of
\sigma
,
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
closely approximates
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
. More specifically,
[
10
]
establishes that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu,\nu)-\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)\right|\leq C(\omega_{\mu}(\sigma)+\omega_{\nu}(\sigma))

(11)

where
\omega_{\mu}
quantifies the regularity of the kernels of
\mu
. The precise definition of
\omega_{\mu}
is given in
[
10
, Theorem
26
]
. Let us emphasize that
\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\omega_{\mu}(\sigma)=0
. In particular, if
\mu
has Lipschitz kernels,
\omega_{\mu}(\sigma)\leq C\sigma
in (
11
).

(3)

Slow rate.
For
p=1
,
[
25
]
shows that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{1}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)]\leq Cn^{-1/2}
for compactly supported
\mu
.
For general
p\geq 1
,
[
10
]
shows that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)^{p}]\leq Cn^{-1/2+p/(2q)}
if
\int\left|x\right|^{q}\mu(dx)<\infty
for some
q>p\vee(dT+2)
. In particular, if
\mu
has a compact support, we can take
q
arbitrarily large, resulting in the bound
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)^{p}]\leq Cn^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
and a constant
C>0
depending on
\varepsilon
. This recovers the slow rate
(3)
for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
, up to a loss of
\varepsilon
.

(4)

Fast rate.
To the best of our knowledge, there is no existing result for the fast rate for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
. Our paper fills this gap.

(5)

Distributional limit.
To the best of our knowledge, there is no result for the limiting distribution of
\sqrt{n}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)
. We plan to address this question in future research.

While the first result suggests that the smooth variant does not preserve the topological structure induced by the metric
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
, the stability bound in the second item
(2)
demonstrates that
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
still provides a quantitative proxy for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
. In particular, when the smoothing parameter
\sigma
is small, the gap between
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
and
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
can be controlled quantitatively. From a statistical point of view, this makes
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
a natural surrogate for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
, in the same spirit that
\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
is often used as a surrogate for the classical Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{p}
.

Furthermore, the estimator
\widehat{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n}
is consistent for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
and its rate of convergence can be quantified via the rates for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
, as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\widehat{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n},\mu)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\widehat{\mu}_{n},\mu)]+\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{\sigma},\mu)

(12)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}n^{-1/2}+C_{2}\omega_{\mu}(\sigma).

(13)

For a fixed value of
\sigma
, the number of samples needed to approximate
\mu
at a precision of
\epsilon
is thus of the dimension free order
n^{1/2}/\epsilon
.

Regarding the fast rate for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
, the closest paper we could find is
[
11
]
, where the authors introduce the so-called
smoothed empirical martingale projection distance

\text{MPD}^{\ast\xi}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},p)
. It is defined as the minimal
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{p}_{p}
-value between
\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}\ast\xi
for a smoothing distribution
\xi
and the set of martingale measures (i.e., the set of measures
\nu
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[X_{2}|X_{1}]=X_{1}
if
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\nu
).
\text{MPD}^{\ast\xi}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},p)
is used to construct a test for the martingale property of
\mu
. When
\mu
is a martingale measure,
[
11
]
shows that
n^{p/2}\text{MPD}^{\ast\xi}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},p)
has a weak limit. Although
\xi
is not necessarily Gaussian and their setting differs from ours, it reflects the spirit of the fast rate we aim to explore. This is why we will examine this example in more detail in Section
1.5
.

1.4
Main results

We are now in a position to state our main result: the fast rate for the smooth adapted
p
-Wasserstein distance and subgaussian measures
\mu
.

Theorem 4

(Fast rate)
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
\sigma>0
. Suppose that
\mu
is a probability measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
, where
d\geq 1
and
T\geq 2
, such that
\int e^{q\left|x\right|^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}\mu(dx)<\infty
for
q>8p(2p-1)(T+9)
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
that depends only on
p,q,d,T,\sigma
and
\int e^{q\left|x\right|^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}\mu(dx)
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)]\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}.

(14)

Using the fact that
\mathcal{W}_{p}\leq C\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
for some constant
C
that depends only on
d,T
, Theorem
4
is sharp. Furthermore, recall from
(4)
for
\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
that the fast rate
n^{-1/2}
essentially holds only if
\mu
is subgaussian. Theorem
4
shows that the converse is true.

Remark 5

(Dependence of
C
on
\sigma
)
.

While the convergence rate
n^{-1/2}
is dimension-free, the prefactor
C
may grow exponentially with the dimension, especially when
\sigma
is small. In the special case where
p=1
,
[
25
, Theorem
4.1
]
shows that for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{1}
, the dependence of
C
on small values of
\sigma
is
\sigma^{-dT/2}
, which becomes dominant in high dimensions. As pointed out in
[
29
, Remark
3
]
, a similar phenomenon occurs for the non-adapted counterpart
\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}
. Indeed, when
p=1
,
[
20
, Proposition
1
]
shows that the corresponding dependence also scales as
\sigma^{-dT/2}
(for an underlying
dT
-dimensional space). For higher values of
p>1
, one may expect the dependence on
\sigma
to deteriorate further. Our proof focuses on establishing the sharp rate
n^{-1/2}
in the case
p>1
and does not track this dependence explicitly.

We detail the proof of Theorem
4
in Section
2
. We also remark that the moment assumption
q>8p(2p-1)(T+9)
can be relaxed. In fact, we will see in Section
2
, that (
14
) holds if
q>\inf_{\beta}q^{*}(p,T,\beta)
, where the infimum is taken over all parameters
\beta
in the set (
P
). We refer to (
Q
) for a precise definition of
q^{*}(p,T,\beta)
.

The proof of Theorem
4
combines the dynamic programming principle for the adapted Wasserstein distance (see Proposition
12
) with the following rather surprising result, stating that
any
compactly supported measure convolved with Gaussian noise automatically has Lipschitz kernels:

Proposition 6

(Smoothed measures have Lipschitz kernels; exact statement in Proposition
7
)
.

Suppose
\mu
is a compactly supported probability measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
that depends only on
d,p,\sigma,\supp(\mu)
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma})_{x_{1:t}},(\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma})_{y_{1:t}})\leq C\left|x_{1:t}-y_{1:t}\right|

(15)

for all
x,y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
and
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T-1\}
. Here,
x_{1:t}
denotes the first
t
coordinates of
x
and
(\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma})_{x_{1:t}}
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined via

\displaystyle(\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma})_{x_{1:t}}(dx_{t+1}):=\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}\in dx_{t+1}\,|\,X_{1}=x_{1},\cdots,X_{t}=x_{t})

(16)

for
X\sim\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
.

In Proposition
7
below we extend this result to subgaussian measures, showing that a smoothed subgaussian measure has
locally
Lipschitz kernels. Since Lipschitz kernels naturally arise in many applications and are well-studied
[
6
,
3
,
10
,
14
]
, we believe that Proposition
7
is of independent interest.

1.5
Applications

We now present a concrete application of the fast rate (
14
), which is taken from
[
11
]
. Consider a probability measure
\mu
on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}
. Recall that we call
\mu
a martingale measure, if
\mathbb{E}[X_{2}|X_{1}]=X_{1}
holds for
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mu
. Closely following
[
11
]
, let us define the
smoothed martingale projection distance (SMPD)
via

\displaystyle\text{SMPD}(\mu,p):=\inf\big\{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu\ast\xi,\nu)\,|\,\nu\text{ is a martingale measure}\big\}.

(17)

Here we define
\xi
as the law of
(Z_{1},Z_{1}+Z_{2})
, where
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
are two independent Gaussian random variables with mean
0
and covariance matrix
\text{I}_{d}
.
\text{SMPD}(\mu,p)
measures the
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
-distance between
\mu\ast\xi
and the space of martingale measures, and is used to test whether
\mu
is a martingale measure. While
[
11
]
offers an in-depth analysis of this distance, let us emphasize here that
\mu
is a martingale if and only if
\text{SMPD}(\mu,p)=0
. In particular, to test the martingale hypothesis using i.i.d samples, we aim to bound the probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\text{SMPD}(\mu,p)-\text{SMPD}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},p)\right|>\alpha\right)

(18)

for
\alpha>0
. By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\text{SMPD}(\mu,p)-\text{SMPD}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},p)\right|\leq\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu\ast\xi,\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}\ast\xi).

(19)

Combined with the Markov inequality leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\text{SMPD}(\mu,p)-\text{SMPD}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},p)\right|>\alpha\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu\ast\xi,\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}\ast\xi)]}{\alpha}.

(20)

The fast rate (
14
) applied to the right hand side of (
20
) establishes the convergence rate
n^{-1/2}
, which is independent of the dimension
d
.

1.6
Notation and preparations

We close this section by setting up notation in Section
1.6
. As mentioned above,
\left|\cdot\right|
is the Euclidean norm, and we denote the scalar (dot) product by
\cdot
. Throughout the paper,
T\geq 2
is the number of time steps and
d\geq 1
is the dimension of the state space. The Hölder conjugate of
p
is denoted by
p^{\prime}
, i.e.,
1/p+1/p^{\prime}=1
.

For any Borel set
A
in Euclidean space, the set of all Borel probability measures on
A
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(A)
. For
1\leq p<\infty
,
\mathcal{P}_{p}(A)
is the set of all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(A)
that have finite
p
-moments, i.e.,
\int\left|x\right|^{p}\mu(dx)<\infty
. For a measure
\mu
, we denote the pushforward measure of
\mu
under a Borel function
T
by
T_{\#}\mu
, i.e.,
T_{\#}\mu(A)=\mu(\{x:T(x)\in A\})
for all Borel sets
A
.

Given
X\sim\mu\in\mathcal{P}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})
, we denote the mean of
X
by
\mean(\mu):=\int x\mu(dx)
. Also, the trace of the covariance matrix of
X
is denoted by
\var(\mu):=\int\left|x-\mean(\mu)\right|^{2}\mu(dx)
. For
r>0
, we define
M_{r}(\mu):=\int\left|x\right|^{r}\mu(dx)
and
\mathcal{E}_{r}(\mu):=\int e^{r\left|x\right|^{2}}\mu(dx)
.

For
x\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
and
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T\}
, we use the shorthand notation
x_{t}
to denote the
t
-coordinate of
x
and
x_{1:t}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
. In particular,
x_{1:T}=x
. Similarly, given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})
, we write
\mu_{t}
for the projection of
\mu
onto the
t
-coordinate and
\mu_{1:t}
for the projection of
\mu
onto the first
t
-coordinates. Precisely speaking,
\mu_{t}
=
P^{t}_{\#}\mu
and
\mu_{1:t}=P^{1:t}_{\#}\mu
for
P^{t}(x)=x_{t}
and
P^{1:t}(x)=x_{1:t}
.

Given
x\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})
, recall that a disintegration(or kernel) of
\mu
is a measure
\mu_{x_{1:t}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T\}
which is defined via
\mu_{x_{1:t}}(dx_{t+1})=\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}\in dx_{t+1}\;|\;X_{1:t}=x_{1:t})
for
X\sim\mu\in\mathcal{P}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})
.

Given
\sigma>0
, we write
\varphi_{\sigma}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}\to\mathbb{R}
for the Gaussian density, i.e.,
\varphi_{\sigma}(x)=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-dT/2}e^{-\left|x\right|^{2}/2}
. It is the density of the centered Gaussian measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
with covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}\text{I}_{dT}
, which is denoted by
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
. Given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})
,
\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
is a convolution of
\mu
and
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
, i.e.,
\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(A)=\int\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(A-x)\mu(dx)
for all measurable
A
. Note that
\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
has density
x\mapsto\varphi_{\sigma}\ast\mu(x)=\int\varphi_{\sigma}(x-y)\mu(dy)
. We use the shorthand notation
\mu^{\sigma}:=\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
. For
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T\}
, we abuse notation and write
\varphi_{\sigma}(x_{1:t})=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-dt/2}e^{-\left|x_{1:t}\right|^{2}/2}
. Similarly,
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
can mean a centered Gaussian measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{t}
with covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}\text{I}_{dt}
depending on the context. Following the same reasoning, the density of
(P^{1:t}_{\#}\mu)^{\sigma}=(P^{1:t}_{\#}\mu)\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
is denoted by
x_{1:t}\mapsto\varphi_{\sigma}\ast\mu(x_{1:t})=\int\varphi_{\sigma}(x_{1:t}-y_{1:t})\mu(dy_{1:t})
.

For a probability measure
\mu
and i.i.d samples
X^{(1)},X^{(2)},\ldots,X^{(n)}
of
\mu
, we define the empirical measure of
\mu
via
\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{X^{(j)}}
. Note that this is a measure-valued random variable. Adopting the same notation as above, we write
\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n}
for
\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(A)
for some Borel set
A
in a Euclidean space. For
1\leq p<\infty
,
L^{p}(\mu;\mathbb{R}^{k})
is the set of all functions
f:A\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mu;\mathbb{R}^{k})}:=(\int_{A}\left|f\right|^{p}d\mu)^{1/p}<\infty
. When
k=1
, we often write
L^{p}(\mu)\>=L^{p}(\mu;\mathbb{R}^{k})
. We denote by
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
the set of all smooth functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
with compact support. For the multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write
\left|\alpha\right|=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}
and denote the
\alpha
-th derivative of
h
by
\partial^{\alpha}h(x)=\frac{\partial^{\alpha_{1}}}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}}\frac{\partial^{\alpha_{2}}}{\partial x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}}\cdots\frac{\partial^{\alpha_{N}}}{\partial x_{N}^{\alpha_{N}}}h(x)
.

If
\gamma
is a finite signed measure on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\mathcal{F}
is a class of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we identify
\gamma
with the linear functional
g\mapsto\gamma(g):=\int gd\gamma
on
\mathcal{F}
and denote its
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})
-norm by
\left\|\gamma\right\|_{\mathcal{F}}:=\sup\{\gamma(g):g\in\mathcal{F}\}
.

2
Proof of Theorem
4

Unless otherwise stated, the parameters
1<p<\infty
and
\sigma>0
are fixed throughout the remainder of this note. Furthermore we always assume that
T\geq 2
and
d\geq 1
.

2.1
Outline of the proof

The proof of Theorem
4
is divided into three parts: Section
2.2
, Section
2.3
and Section
2.4
. Before we proceed with a rigorous proof, let us sketch the main steps. For simplicity of exposition, we assume that
T=2
and that
\mu
is compactly supported (only) in this section.

Step 1:
We show in Proposition
7
in Section
2.2
, that smoothed measures have locally Lipschitz kernels. In particular, when
T=2
and
\mu
is compactly supported, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{x_{1}},(\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}})\leq C\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|\text{ for all }x_{1},y_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(21)

Proposition
7
follows from combining Lemma
10
and Lemma
11
: Lemma
10
shows that if a measure
\gamma
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{x_{1}},\gamma)\leq C\left\|(\mu^{\sigma})_{x_{1}}-\gamma\right\|_{\mathcal{F}},

(22)

where
\mathcal{F}
is a class of test functions specified in Lemma
10
. Setting
\gamma=(\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}}
, this implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{x_{1}},(\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}})\leq C\left\|(\mu^{\sigma})_{x_{1}}-(\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}}\right\|_{\mathcal{F}}.

(23)

In Lemma
11
, we apply a Taylor expansion to show that

\displaystyle\left\|(\mu^{\sigma})_{x_{1}}-(\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}}\right\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leq C\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|.

(24)

Step 2:
In Section
2.3
, we first recall the dynamic programming principle (DPP) for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}_{p}
in Proposition
12
. When
T=2
, it can be stated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu,\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n})^{p}=\inf_{\gamma\in\cpl((\mu^{\sigma})_{1},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n})_{1})}\int\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|^{p}+\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{x_{1}},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n})_{y_{1}})^{p}\gamma(dx_{1},dy_{1}).

(25)

Using (
21
) together with the triangle inequality, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{x_{1}},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n})_{y_{1}})^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{x_{1}},(\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}})^{p}+C\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n})_{y_{1}})^{p}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq C\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|^{p}+C\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n})_{y_{1}})^{p}.

(27)

Plugging (
27
) into (
25
) and choosing
\gamma
as an optimal coupling for
\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\sigma)}(\mu_{1},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n})_{1})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu,\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n})\leq C\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu_{1},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n})_{1})+C\left(\int\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n})_{y_{1}})^{p}\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n}(dy)\right)^{1/p}.

(28)

Section
2.3
is devoted to establishing Lemma
13
, which is an analogue of (
28
) for subgaussian measures
\mu
that are not necessarily compactly supported. Let us emphasize here that if
\mu
is not compactly supported, the kernel of
\mu^{\sigma}
is
locally
Lipschitz. Consequently, the estimate (
21
) may fail and estimates (
27
) and (
28
) have to be modified accordingly.

Step 3:
In Section
2.4
, we complete the proof of Theorem
4
. The main idea is to partition
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into disjoint sets
(E_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
and apply empirical process theory (see Lemma
15
). By taking expectations in (
28
) and using (
22
), we can show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu,\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n})]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu_{1},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n})_{1})]+C\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n})_{y_{1}})^{p}\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n}(dy)\right)^{1/p}\right]

(29)

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu_{1},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n})_{1})]+C\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int\left\|(\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}}-(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n})_{y_{1}}\right\|^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n}(dy)\right)^{1/p}\right]

(30)

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu_{1},(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n})_{1})]+C\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{E_{k}}\left\|(\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1}}-(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n})_{y_{1}}\right\|^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n}(dy)\right)^{1/p}\right].

(31)

In Lemma
16
, we further refine this estimate and show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\mu,\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n})]\leq Cn^{-1/2}+C\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f(k)\mathbb{E}[\left\|\mu-\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}]

(32)

for some class of functions
\mathcal{H}_{k}
and a function
f
. In Lemma
18
, we apply empirical process theory to (

) to obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left\|\mu-\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}]\leq Cn^{-1/2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}g(k,\ell)

(33)

for some function
g
. As a final step, we show how the moment condition of
\mu
implies that the series

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f(k)\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}g(k,\ell)

(34)

converges.

To improve readability, technical lemmas involving computations or estimates of Gaussian kernels are deferred to Appendix

.

2.2
Step 1: Regularity of kernels of smoothed measures

This section is mainly devoted to the proof of Proposition
7
. As mentioned in Section
1
, a subgaussian measure convolved with Gaussian noise has locally Lipschitz kernels. Moreover, if the measure is compactly supported, then its kernels are Lipschitz.

Proposition 7

(Kernels of a smoothed measure)
.

Let
0<\beta<1/p^{\prime}
. Suppose that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})
satisfies
\mathcal{E}_{q/(2\sigma^{2})}(\mu)<\infty
for
q>2(p-1)/\beta
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
that depends only on
d,p,\sigma,q,\beta,\mathcal{E}_{q/(2\sigma^{2})}(\mu)
such that for all
x,y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}
and
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T-1\}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{x_{1:t}},(\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1:t}})\leq Ce^{\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\left|x_{1:t}-\mean(\mu_{1:t})\right|\vee\left|y_{1:t}-\mean(\mu_{1:t})\right|\right)^{2}}\left|x_{1:t}-y_{1:t}\right|.

(35)

In particular, if
\mu
is compactly supported,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}((\mu^{\sigma})_{x_{1:t}},(\mu^{\sigma})_{y_{1:t}})\leq C\left|x_{1:t}-y_{1:t}\right|

(36)

for some constant
C>0
that depends only on
d,p,\sigma,\supp(\mu)
.

The proof of Proposition
7
is deferred to the end of this section. The proof is based on two lemmas: Lemma
10
and Lemma
11
. We first recall
[
21
, Proposition
2.1
]
which shows that the Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{p}
can be bounded above by the dual Sobolev norm.

Proposition 8

(Proposition
2.1
in
[
21
]
)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and suppose that
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\ll\rho
for some reference measure
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Denote their respective densities by
f_{i}=d\mu_{i}/d\rho
,
i=0,1
. If
f_{0}
or
f_{1}
is bounded from below by some

\displaystyle 2^{-dT/2}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[e^{\frac{\left|X\right|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right]\right)^{-1}\sup\left\{(\mu^{\sigma}-\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n})(\varphi):\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T}),\left\|\nabla\varphi\right\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathcal{N}_{\sqrt{2}\sigma};(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{T})}\leq 1\right\}.

(208)

Here,
\mu^{\sigma}:=\mu\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
and
\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n}:=\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n}\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
. Denoting by
Z_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{(j)}=a\}}
, it is easy to compute that

\displaystyle\mu^{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(a,\sigma^{2}\text{I}_{dT})+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(b,\sigma^{2}\text{I}_{dT}),\quad\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n}=Z_{n}\mathcal{N}(a,\sigma^{2}\text{I}_{dT})+(1-Z_{n})\mathcal{N}(b,\sigma^{2}\text{I}_{dT}).

(209)

A similar computation was used in
[
15
, Example (a) on page 2]
. The above implies

\displaystyle(\mu^{\sigma}-\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n})(\varphi)=\int\varphi d\mu^{\sigma}-\int\varphi d\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}^{\sigma}_{n}=\left(1/2-Z_{n}\right)\left(\int\varphi d\mathcal{N}(a,\sigma^{2}\text{I}_{dT})-\int\varphi d\mathcal{N}(b,\sigma^{2}\text{I}_{dT})\right).

(210)

In particular, we obtain from (
208
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(\sigma)}_{p}(\accentset{\smash{\raisebox{-3.25499pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\phantom{x}}$}}}}{\mu}_{n},\mu)\geq C\left|Z_{n}-1/2\right|

(211)

for some positive
C
. This concludes the proof since
\mathbb{E}[\left|Z_{n}-1/2\right|]
is of order
n^{-1/2}
.
