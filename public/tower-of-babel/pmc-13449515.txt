Sphingomonas incarnata sp. nov., an endophytic bacterium with bioactive potential isolated from passionfruit leaves
Authors: Luis Gabriel Cueva-Yesquén, Kariny Marley de Castro Martins, Kelly Johanna Hidalgo-Martinez, Adilson Sartoratto, Adriana da Silva Santos de Oliveira, Itamar Soares de Melo, Fabiana Fantinatti-Garboggini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe genus Sphingomonas encompasses metabolically versatile bacteria found in diverse environments; however, species associated with the leaf endosphere of medicinal plants remain poorly characterized. Here we describe a novel species isolated from the leaf endosphere of Passiflora incarnata, a medicinal plant recognized for its rich content of flavonoids and phenolic compounds, collected in São Paulo, Brazil.ResultsA Gram-negative, aerobic, rod-shaped, motile bacterium, designated strain EP61.1T, was characterized through a polyphasic taxonomic approach. Genome-based indices confirmed species-level distinctiveness, with average nucleotide identity values ranging from 85.50 to 92.37% and digital DNA-DNA hybridization values from 22.8 to 45.8%, both below the accepted thresholds for prokaryotic species delineation. The genome comprises 4.82 Mbp with a guanine-cytosine content of 67.83%, encoding 4,479 protein-coding sequences. The predominant respiratory quinone is ubiquinone Q-10; the major cellular fatty acids are summed feature 8 (C18:1 ω7c/C18:1 ω6c) and C16:0; and the polar lipid profile includes diphosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidyldimethylethanolamine, sphingoglycolipid, glycolipids, phosphoaminolipids, and two unidentified lipids. Biosynthetic gene clusters such as zeaxanthin (a photoprotective carotenoid) and siderophores (iron-chelating compounds) were predicted from the genome. Additionally, volatile organic compounds produced by strain EP61.1T promoted plant growth and induced flavonoid accumulation in Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings under controlled conditions.ConclusionsBased on polyphasic taxonomic evidence, strain EP61.1T represents a novel species of the genus Sphingomonas, for which the name Sphingomonas incarnata sp. nov. is proposed. The predicted biosynthetic gene clusters suggest ecological advantages underlying its endophytic lifestyle, while the capacity to modulate plant secondary metabolism through interkingdom volatile signaling points to broader relevance for plant health and biotechnological applications. The type strain is EP61.1T (= CBMAI 2297T = ICMP 24843T = MUM 23.02T).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05210-5.Keywords: Sphingomonas, Endophyte, Passiflora incarnate

Introduction

The genus Sphingomonas, first proposed by Yabuuchi et al. [1], was established following the reclassification of Pseudomonas paucimobilis into Sphingomonas paucimobilis. In the same study, four additional species were also described: Sphingomonas parapaucimobilis, Sphingomonas yanoikuyae, Sphingomonas adhaesiva, and Sphingomonas capsulata. Members of this genus are currently classified within the class Alphaproteobacteria and the family Sphingomonadaceae. As of March 2026, more than 214 species have been validly published in the genus Sphingomonas according to the List of Prokaryotic Names with Standing in Nomenclature (LPSN), highlighting the remarkable taxonomic diversity of the genus.

Sphingomonas have been isolated from a wide range of ecological niches, including Antarctic tundra soils (S. antarctica), alpine environments (S. alpina), desert biological soil crusts (S. mucosissima), forest soils (S. changbaiensis), atmospheric air (S. aerophila), aquatic environments (S. wittichii) and agricultural soils (S. kyungheensis) [2–8]. Members of the genus encompass Gram-negative, strictly aerobic, rod-shaped bacteria that typically produce yellow, orange, or off-white pigments. Furthermore, they are characterized chemotaxonomically by the presence of ubiquinone-10 (Q-10) as the major respiratory quinone, the occurrence of 2-hydroxymyristic acid (C14:0 2-OH) and other 2-hydroxy fatty acids, and sym-homospermidine as the predominant polyamine [9].

Several species of Sphingomonas have been reported from plant-associated environments [10]. Sphingomonas endophytica and Sphingomonas jatrophae were isolated from internal tissues [11, 12]. While other members, such as Sphingomonas melonis and Sphingomonas phyllosphaerae, were found inhabiting the phyllosphere [13, 14], suggesting that species of this genus are also adapted to surface compartments.

The genus Passiflora, belonging to the family Passifloraceae, comprises more than 600 species distributed mainly in tropical and subtropical regions. Several species are widely recognized for their pharmacological importance due to the presence of diverse secondary metabolites. Among them, Passiflora incarnata and Passiflora edulis are the most used species in pharmacological and medicinal applications [15]. The aerial parts of P. incarnata contain a wide range of bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, β-carboline alkaloids, and phenolic compounds. Major flavonoids characterized include vitexin, isovitexin, orientin, isoorientin, apigenin, and chrysin, which are associated with antioxidant, sedative, and anxiolytic activities [16]. Plant secondary metabolites such as phenolic compounds, including flavonoids and phenolic acids, are known to influence the structure and function of plant-associated microbial communities [17]. In turn, the metabolization of phenolic compounds was characterized in Sphingomonas strains, even in plant-associated environments [18]. These metabolic capabilities may provide ecological advantages for Sphingomonas in colonizing plant tissues rich in phenolic metabolites, including those produced by Passiflora species. Despite the increasing number of species described within the genus Sphingomonas, the diversity of strains associated with medicinal plants, particularly in the leaf endosphere, remains poorly explored.

In this study, we propose a novel species of the genus Sphingomonas, designated EP61.1T, isolated from the leaf endosphere of Passiflora incarnata collected in São Paulo, Brazil. A polyphasic taxonomic approach combining phenotypic characterization, chemotaxonomic analyses, and whole-genome sequencing was employed to determine its taxonomic affiliation. Genome-based analyses further revealed the metabolic potential of strain EP61.1T, including biosynthetic gene clusters associated with carotenoid production and siderophore biosynthesis, which may contribute to its adaptation to plant-associated environments.

Material and methods

Isolation and culture conditions

The bacterial strain was obtained from P. incarnata leaves in a study by Goulart et al. [19]. Briefly, plants of P. incarnata in vegetative stage (VS) from the Centroflora Group agricultural fields located in Botucatu, São Paulo, Brazil (23.93 S, 48.56 W) were randomly selected and their leaves were detached and transported to the laboratory, where surface sterilization was performed (100% ethanol for 3 min, followed by 2% sodium hypochlorite for 2 min, and 70% ethanol for 3 min). Surface-sterilized leaves were ground with mortars in phosphate-buffered saline (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 1.8 mM KH2PO4, distilled water 1,000 ml, pH 7.4), and the resulting suspension was spread onto Gause’s synthetic agar [20]. Plates were incubated at 28 ± 2 °C for up to 30 days. Based on colony morphology, a lightly yellow-pigmented, circular, smooth and convex colony with entire margins was selected, purified, and preserved as a glycerol stock at − 80 °C. This isolate was designated as EP61.1T and grown on trypticase soy agar (TSA, Difco) at 28 °C overnight for subsequent analyses.

Morphological and physiological characterization

Cell morphology of strain EP61.1T was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The strain EP61.1T was cultured in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) overnight at 28 °C, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and fixed in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (sodium cacodylate, (CH3)2AsO2Na·3H2O) supplemented with 2% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, cells were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (20, 40, 60, 80, 95, and 100%), and critical-point dried using CO2. Strain EP61.1T was grown in trypticase soy broth (TSB, Difco) medium at 28 °C for 48 h to characterize the colony morphology by Gram staining. Growth was determined by measuring OD600 ≥ 0.1. The catalase activity was assessed by applying a 3% (v/v) hydrogen peroxide solution directly onto cell biomass from colony and vigorous bubble formation was recorded as a positive result. Aerobic requirements were evaluated by inoculating a colony into thioglycolate broth. A colony was inoculated in semi-solid TSA (0,7% agar) and incubated overnight at 28 °C to validate the motility. Growth was investigated at different temperatures (4–50 °C), pH values (4.0–10.0), and NaCl concentrations (1–10%, w/v) in TSB. The pH was adjusted before sterilization with 0.1 M citric acid/0.1 M sodium citrate (pH 4.0–5.0), 0.1 M KH2PO4/0.1 M NaOH (pH 6.0–8.0), and 0.1 M NaHCO3/0.1 M Na2CO3 (pH 9.0–10.0). Substrate utilization, enzyme activities, and acid production from carbohydrates were analyzed using the API 20NE and API 50CH kits (bioMérieux) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All strips were read after 24 h and 48 h.

Chemotaxonomic analysis

The chemotaxonomic characterization of strain EP61.1T included analyses of quinones, fatty acids, and polar lipids. Cellular fatty acid composition was analyzed by extracting the fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs). Fatty acids were extracted, methylated and quantified by gas chromatography (Agilent 6850 GC). The FAMEs were identified with the TSBA6 database (version 6.10) of the Sherlock Microbial Identification System (MIDI). Respiratory quinones and polar lipids were analyzed according to the method of Minnikin et al. [21]. Briefly, ubiquinones were extracted from 50 mg dry cell biomass with methanol-saline (0.3% w/v NaCl: methanol, 1:10 v/v) and petroleum ether. The purified ubiquinones were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Polar lipids were extracted from the same biomass with chloroform–methanol–0.3% saline solution (9:10:3, v/v) and separated by two-dimensional thin-layer chromatography (TLC). Total lipids were detected with 10% ethanolic molybdophosphoric acids.

Phylogenetic analysis based on the 16S rRNA gene sequence

The strain EP61.1T was cultured in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) overnight at 28 °C, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and its genomic DNA was extracted following the modified protocol of Van Soolingen et al. [22]. The nearly complete 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using the universal primers 10 F (5′-GAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1525R (5′-AAGGAGGTGWTCCARCC-3′) as described by Lane [23]. PCR amplification consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 4 min, followed by 32 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 55 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 3 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

PCR products were purified using the PCR GFX™ kit (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) and sequenced by the Sanger method using the BigDye™ Terminator v3.1 kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on an ABI 3500XL Genetic Analyzer with primers 10F, 1525R, 530R, and 968F. The resulting sequences were assembled using BioEdit was submitted to the Identify service of the web-based EzBioCloud platform [24]. EzBioCloud use a curated database of 16S rRNA gene sequences and whole-genome assemblies (WGA) primarily from type material.

The 16S rRNA gene sequence of EP61.1T and those recovered from EzBiocloud analysis were aligned in the software MEGA11 [25] usign the aligner CLUSTAL W and the potitions no aligned were trimmed. The best substitution model was determined using the option Find Best DNA/Protein Models. The phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using Maximum Parsimony (MP) and Maximum Likelihood (ML). For this latter method, the model Tamura 3-parameter with Gamma distribution (+ G) were applied. The robustness of the tree topology was evaluated with 1000 replications.

Genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation

The strain EP61.1T was cultured in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) overnight at 28 °C, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and total DNA was extracted using the PowerMax Soil DNA kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit™ 3.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen), and purity was assessed by the A260/A280 ratio using a NanoDrop™ 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The sequencing library was prepared using the Nextera XT kit, and whole-genome sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform generating, paired-end reads (2 × 250 bp). Raw reads were evaluated using FastQC v0.11.9 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/), and quality trimming and adapter removal were performed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [26]. Reads shorter than 100 bp or with a Phred quality score lower than 30 were discarded prior to assembly. Genome assembly was performed de novo using SPAdes v3.13 [27] with multiple k-mer sizes ranging from 21 to 127. Assembly quality was evaluated using QUAST v5.0.2 [28], and genome completeness and contamination were assessed using CheckM v1.1.3 [29]. Genome annotation was performed using the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP) [30–32]. Gene prediction was also conducted using Prodigal v2.6.3 [33] and functional annotation was obtained using the eggNOG-mapper v2 web server [34]. The circular genome map was generated using GenoVi v0.4.3 [35], which integrates Circos [36], GC-analysis.py [37], and skewIT [38] to visualize genome structure, GC content and GC skew. Protein coding sequences were classified into clusters of orthologous groups (COGs) using DeepNOG [39]. Secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters were predicted using the antiSMASH platform [40].

Determination of overall genome-relatedness indices (OGRI)

To determine the taxonomic position of strain EP61.1T, overall genome relatedness indices (OGRI) were calculated with the genomes of the closest phylogenetic relatives. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) values were obtained using the JSpeciesWS server [41] (ANIb and ANIm) and the ANI calculator available in EzBioCloud (ANIu). Digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) values were estimated using the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC) [42] with BLAST + as the alignment algorithm. Average amino acid identity (AAI) values were calculated using EzAAI v1.2.4 [43] with MMseqs2 as the alignment method.

Phylogenomic analysis

Phylogenomic analysis was performed using the PhyloPhlAn 3.0 pipeline [44]. This approach identifies a set of conserved phylogenetic markers (n = 400) across query genomes using DIAMOND v0.9.21 [45]. The identified sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.487 [46] and concatenated for phylogenetic reconstruction with FastTree v2.1.11 [47] using the Jukes-Cantor + CAT model by default. The resulting phylogenomic tree was visualized and edited using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) tool [48].

Role of Sphingomonas in the flavonoid production

Strain EP61.1T was isolated from the leaf endosphere, a microenvironment rich in phenolic compounds, particularly in P. incarnata, and was therefore previously investigated for genes potentially related to flavonoid metabolism [49]. In that study, a DNA region displaying high similarity to a flavonol synthase-like sequence was amplified and sequenced. Nevertheless, flavonoid biosynthesis involves a complex pathway that is well established in plants and is not typically considered a native bacterial metabolic pathway [50]. Considering that Passiflora leaves accumulate substantial amounts of flavonoids, a hypothesis is that EP61.1T might modulates flavonoid biosynthesis in the host through interkingdom signaling. To explore this possibility, a VOC-based communication assay in a model host was proposed, since recent studies indicate that microbial volatiles can induce flavonoid biosynthesis in plants [51].

Plant material and growth conditions

Seeds of the wild-type ecotype Arabidopsis thaliana (Col-0) were surface-disinfected (95% ethanol for 1 min, 2.5% sodium hypochlorite for 10 min, and 5 washes with sterile distilled water) and subjected to vernalization in the dark at 4 °C for 48 h. After vernalization, the seeds were plated on Petri dishes containing 0.5 × Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium [52] supplemented with 0.5% sucrose, 0.05% MES, and 0.8% agar. The plants were grown in a growth chamber under controlled light conditions (12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod), at 23 ± 2 °C.

Treatment of A. thaliana with bacterial VOCs and growth promotion

Cells of strain EP61.1T were grown in TSB for 24 h at 28 °C and 250 rpm. Divided Petri dishes (I-plates) containing 0.5 × MS medium were prepared, and seeds were placed on one side of the plate (six seeds per plate). The opposite compartment was prepared with TSA and inoculated with 5 μL of the EP61.1T suspension which was deposited onto the medium. Two control treatments were included: 5 μL of sterile TSB medium and 5 μL of a suspension of Pseudomonas syringae pv. passiflorae LMG 5185. The strain LMG 5185 was provided by CBMAI (The Brazilian Collection of Microorganisms from Environment and Industry). All treatments were applied to the non-plant compartment of the I-plates. Plates were sealed with PVC film and incubated vertically for 14 days in a growth chamber as previously described.

Total flavonoid content determination

Total flavonoid content (TFC) was determined using the aluminum chloride method as described by Huang et al. [53], with minor modifications, to evaluate flavonoid accumulation in Arabidopsis thaliana plants wild-type Columbia-0 (Col-0) exposed or not to bacterial volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Dry aerial tissues of A. thaliana were ground in 1 mL of 80% methanol using a tissue homogenizer, and the homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. One hundred microliters of the supernatant were transferred to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube containing 300 μL of methanol, and the mixture was vortexed. Subsequently, 20 μL of 10% (w/v) aluminum chloride and 20 μL of 1 M potassium acetate were added, and the mixture was mixed thoroughly. The reaction mixture was diluted with 700 μL of methanol and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. To correct the intrinsic absorbance of the extracts, a blank was prepared by diluting 100 μL of supernatant with 1 mL of methanol, without adding reagents. After incubation, 150 μL of each reaction (samples, blanks, or standards) were transferred to a clear 96-well microplate, and absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a microplate reader. All measurements were performed in technical triplicate. TFC was expressed as milligrams of quercetin equivalents per milliliter (mg QE mL-1) based on a standard calibration curve prepared with quercetin solutions ranging from 0 to 1 mg mL-1.

Results and discussion

Morphological, physiological and biochemical characteristics

Colonies of strain EP61.1T grown on TSA were light yellow-pigmented, circular, smooth, and convex with entire margins. The cells were Gram-stain-negative, rod-shaped with approximately 0.4 µm wide and 2.5 µm long (Fig. 1). Strain EP61.1T grew at pH 6.0–9.0 (optimum pH 7.0), at temperatures ranging from 15 to 37 °C (optimum 28–30 °C), and in the presence of 0–4% (w/v) NaCl, with optimal growth in the absence of NaCl. Growth was observed throughout the entire semi-solid medium, indicating that strain EP61.1T is motile. The differential biochemical characteristics of strain EP61.1T and its closest relatives, based on ANI analysis, are summarized in Table 1. Strain EP61.1T could be distinguished from Sphingomonas leidyi, Sphingomonas kyeonggiense, and Sphingomonas trueperi by the absence of L-arabinose and D-mannose assimilation. In addition, strain EP61.1T did not hydrolyze gelatin and lacked β-galactosidase activity, differentiating it from S. trueperi. Differences were also observed in carbohydrate utilization patterns, since strain EP61.1T showed weak acid production from D-glucose but was negative for acid production from L-rhamnose and D-cellobiose, whereas these reactions were positive in S. leidyi and S. trueperi. All physiological and biochemical characteristics tested in the 20NE and 50CH systems for the strain EP61.1T are shown in Table S2. The strain is Gram-staining negative, rod-shaped and catalase-positive. According to API 20NE tests, all strains were negative for nitrate reduction, indole production, arginine dihydrolase and urease, and positive for esculin hydrolysis and assimilation of D-glucose, N-acetyl-glucosamine and D-maltose. All strains were also negative for assimilation of D-mannitol, potassium gluconate, adipic acid and phenylacetic acid.

Chemotaxonomic characteristics

The predominant respiratory quinone of strain EP61.1T was ubiquinone with 10 isoprenoid units in the side chain (Q-10), which is characteristic of members of the genus Sphingomonas. The major cellular fatty acids (> 5%) detected in strain EP61.1T were summed feature 8 (C18:1 ω7c/C18:1 ω6c) (62.16%) and C16:0 (16.75%). Additional fatty acids detected in smaller proportions (< 5%) included C14:0 2-OH, C17:1 ω6c, C18:0, C18:1 ω5c, 11-methyl C18:1 ω7c and C16:0 2-OH. The occurrence of the hydroxy fatty acid C14:0 2-OH is consistent with chemotaxonomic features commonly reported for species of the genus Sphingomonas. Overall, the fatty acid composition of strain EP61.1T showed a pattern comparable to those reported for the closely related species (Table 2). The polar lipid profile of strain EP61.1T consisted of diphosphatidylglycerol (DPG), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidyldimethylethanolamine (PDME), sphingoglycolipid (SGL), glycolipids (GL), phosphoaminolipids (PNL), and two unidentified lipids (L1 and L2), as revealed by two-dimensional thin-layer chromatography (Fig. S2). The strain EP61.1T shares DPG, PE, PG, PC and SGL with all three closest relatives. However, it differs from S. kyeonggiense THG-DT81T by the presence of PDME and PNL, which were absent in that species [55]. Additionally, PME, present in both S. leidyi DSM 4733T and S. trueperi DSM 7225T [54, 56], was not detected in strain EP61.1T.

Phylogenetic analysis based on 16S ribosomal RNA sequences

The nearly complete 16S rRNA gene sequence of strain EP61.1T was obtained by Sanger sequencing (MG778757.2) and submitted to the EzBioCloud’s identification service. The similarity-based searches (Table S1) showed closeness with Sphingomonas leidyi ATCC 15260T (99.15%), Sphingomonas mali NBRC 15500 (97.94%), and Sphingomonas pruni NBRC 15498T (97.80%). Considering the novel proposed thresholds of 90.1–99% for prokaryotic genus delineation and 97.2–100% for species [57], strain EP61.1T is positioned within the genus Sphingomonas; however, additional genome-based analyses are required to define its species-level taxonomic status. The phylogenetic analysis based on ML grouped EP61.1T in a monophyletic clade with Sphingomonas leidyi ATCC 15260T, suggesting an evolutionary relationship with this latter species and confirming that it belongs to the Sphingomonas genus (Fig. 2). The MP-based tree showed the same topology and clusterization for EP61.1T (Fig. S1).

Comparative genomics

The whole-genome sequencing by Illumina yielded 1,736,617 paired-end reads (250 × 2 bp). The assembly using SPAdes generated a draft genome of 4,817,922 bp distributed in 33 scaffolds. The Quast evaluation calculated a N50 of 228,585 bp, a GC content of 67.84% and a coverage of 90 ×. The CheckM analysis determined 99.3% of completeness and 2.39% of contamination, confirming that the genome assembly meets the quality standards recommended for the use of genome sequence data in prokaryotic taxonomy [58]. The genome has been deposited in GenBank under accession number JAPDIR000000000.1 and the unique copy annotated of the 16S rRNA gene (locus tag OMP43_22790; 1486 bp) showed 100% of identity with the 16S rRNA gene sequence recovered by Sanger (1484 bp).

In the phylogenomic reconstruction using orthologous protein alignments, EP61.1T clustered again with S. leidyi ATCC 15260T (Fig. 3), confirming it as the closest relative. Nevertheless, when the EP61.1T genome was pairwise aligned to calculate OGRI, the differences with S. leidyi and other relatives increased and are below the widely accepted thresholds of ANI (95–96%) and dDDH (70%) for delimiting prokaryotic species [59, 60]. Specifically, when strain EP61.1T was compared with its three closest relatives, ANI values ranged from 92.37 to 85.50%, and dDDH values from 45.8 to 22.8% (Table 3). The AAI values ranged from 92.77 to 74.05%, confirming that all comparisons remain within the genus Sphingomonas as the AAI threshold for genus-delineation is 60–65% [58]. These metrics, together with phylogenetic reconstructions, indicate that strain EP61.1T represents a novel species within the genus Sphingomonas. Comparative genome data also justify the use of S. leidyi, S. kyeonggiensis, and S. trueperi as references for this polyphasic study.

Functional insights based on the genome

The genome of strain EP61.1T consists of 4.82 Mbp, with a G + C content of 67.83%. The chromosome contained 4,564 genes, including 49 tRNAs, 3 complete rRNAs, and 4,479 protein-coding sequences (CDS), which were classified into 25 functional groups and 4 categories of orthologous groups. The major categories comprised general function prediction only (R, 8.8%), cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis (M, 7.5%), transcription (K, 7.4%), signal transduction mechanism (T, 6.8%), unknown function (S, 6.2%), inorganic ion transport and metabolism (P, 5.9%), amino acid transport and metabolism (E, 5.52%), carbohydrate transport and metabolism (G, 5.4%) and lipid transport and metabolism (I, 5.3%) (Fig. 4).

Secondary metabolite prediction analysis using the antiSMASH platform identified seven gene clusters in the EP61.1T genome (Table 4, Fig S3-9). These included two non-ribosomal peptide synthases (NRPSs), one terpene, one RiPP, one NAPAA, one chemical hybrid (NRPS-betalactone), and one homoserinelactone. Cluster 1 (0.75) matched with the zeaxanthin gene cluster from Xanthobacter autotrophicus Py2. Specifically, three key enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of this carotenoid were detected: phytoene synthase (locus tag, OMP43_00575), phytoene desaturase (OMP43_00565) and lycopene cyclase (locus tag, OMP43_00560), which confirms its potential as a zeaxanthin producer. This carotenoid compound is known as a critical photoprotective agent, produced by plants, algae and some bacterial groups [61]. A recent study demonstrated that zeaxanthin-producing bacteria can mitigate the oxidative damage induced by light stress in coral endosymbionts [62]. These findings suggest a potential role for EP61.1T in plant systems, particularly considering that this strain was originally isolated from leaf tissues.

A gene cluster predicted with medium confidence (0.62) was ferrichrome. The adenylation (locus tag, OMP43_19545) and peptidyl carrier (locus tag, OMP43_19570) domains from the NRPS module were detected in the EP61.1T genome. Although this cluster is commonly found in fungi, it has also been detected in Lactobacillus casei [63]. Ferrichrome production not only contributes to iron uptake under starvation conditions, but also triggers induced systemic resistance, which induces the synthesis of phenolic compounds in plant hosts [64]. Particularly, these compounds are relevant in P. incarnata leaves [65]. The genomic potential for synthesizing bioactive compounds supports the endophytic association of EP61.1T with a plant with phyto-pharmacological properties and might explain its occurrence in a compartment (leaf) rich in phenolic phytoconstituents.

Production of flavonoids and plant-growth promotion

Exposure to VOCs emitted by strain EP61.1T promoted the growth of Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings in split-plate assays, whereas exposure to Pseudomonas syringae pv. passiflorae severely impaired the plant development (Fig. 5A). After 14 days of cultivation, seedlings exposed to EP61.1T showed a significant increase in fresh mass compared with the control, whereas seedlings exposed to P. syringae showed a significant reduction (Fig. 5B). In relation to the control, EP61.1T increased fresh mass by approximately 98%, while P. syringae reduced it by approximately 77%. A similar trend was observed for dry mass, with pooled tissues reaching 35.2 mg in the EP61.1T treatment, compared with 14.0 mg in the control and 5.1 mg in the P. syringae treatment (Fig. 5C). These results highlight the contrasting effects of the volatile blends produced by the two bacterial strains, indicating a growth-promoting effect of EP61.1T and an inhibitory effect of P. syringae on A. thaliana seedlings under the experimental conditions tested.

In addition to the effects on plant growth, exposure to EP61.1T VOCs was associated with higher total flavonoid content in tissues. Flavonoid quantification by the aluminum chloride method indicated an increase from 0.594 to 1.563 µg QE mg-1 dry mass in seedlings exposed to EP61.1T relative to the control (Fig. 5D). Additional information on the standard curve and flavonoid content calculation is provided in Tables S3 and S4. Although this analysis is presented as a descriptive measurement due to the limited biomass obtained in the split-plate system, the data suggests that the beneficial effects of EP61.1T VOCs may extend beyond growth promotion and involve changes in plant secondary metabolism. This result matches with recent evidence showing that microbial VOCs can induce flavonoid biosynthesis in plants and contribute to enhanced resistance against pathogen infection [51, 66]. Given the roles of flavonoids in plant development, redox balance, and defense-related responses, the increase observed here may reflect not only a growth-associated metabolic shift, but also a broader physiological adjustment associated with responses to potential pathogen attack [67]. To determine the VOC-driven mechanism involved in the increased phenotype for flavonoid production a GC–MS-based profiling sould be performed. Furthermore, these results were obtained using Arabidopsis thaliana as a model system, which, despite its widespread use in plant–microbe interaction studies, does not replicate the biochemical and ecological context of the native host Passiflora incarnata, where flavonoid accumulation is substantially higher and likely subject to distinct regulatory mechanisms.

Conclusion

Based on a polyphasic taxonomic approach integrating phenotypic, chemotaxonomic, and genomic evidence, strain EP61.1T represents a novel species of the genus Sphingomonas, for which the name Sphingomonas incarnata sp. nov. is proposed. Genome analysis revealed biosynthetic gene clusters potentially involved in carotenoid and siderophore production, suggesting that these metabolic capacities may confer ecological advantages underlying its endophytic occurrence in Passiflora incarnata. VOC-mediated assays further suggest a potential role in promoting plant growth and influencing flavonoid accumulation, although additional mechanistic studies are required.

Description of Sphingomonas incarnata sp. nov.

Sphingomonas incarnata (in.car.na’ta. L. fem. adj. incarnata, referring to the host plant Passiflora incarnata, from which the type strain was isolated). Cells are Gram-negative, aerobic, motile rods. Colonies grown on TSA are light yellow, circular, smooth, and convex with entire margins. Growth occurs at 15–37 °C (optimum 28–30 °C), at pH 6.0–9.0 (optimum pH 7.0), and in the presence of 0–4% (w/v) NaCl, with optimal growth in the absence of NaCl. Positive for esculin hydrolysis and for assimilation of D-glucose, N-acetyl-glucosamine, D-maltose, and malic acid. Negative for nitrate reduction, indole production, arginine dihydrolase, urease, gelatin hydrolysis, β-galactosidase activity, and assimilation of L-arabinose, D-mannose, D-mannitol, potassium gluconate, capric acid, adipic acid, trisodium citrate, and phenylacetic acid. Acid is weakly produced from D-glucose, N-acetyl-glucosamine, D-maltose, D-trehalose, gentibiose, and D-lyxose, but not from L-arabinose, D-mannose, L-rhamnose, or D-cellobiose. The predominant respiratory quinone is ubiquinone Q-10. The major cellular fatty acids (> 5%) are summed feature 8 (C18:1 ω7c/C18:1 ω6c) (62.16%) and C16:0 (16.75%). The polar lipid profile consists of diphosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidyldimethylethanolamine, sphingoglycolipid, glycolipids, phosphoaminolipids, and two unidentified lipids. The DNA G + C content of the type strain is 67.84%, and the genome size is 4.82 Mbp. The type strain is EP61.1T (= CBMAI 2297T = ICMP 24843T = MUM 23.02T), isolated from leaf tissues of Passiflora incarnata collected in São Paulo, Brazil. The 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequence and the whole-genome shotgun project of the type strain have been deposited in GenBank under accession numbers MG778757.2 and JAPDIR000000000.1
