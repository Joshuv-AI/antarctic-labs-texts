24-hour movement behaviors and internalizing and externalizing problems among Japanese preschool children: A compositional data analysis
Authors: Pin Zhang, Jiameng Ma, Zan Huang, Cain C.T. Clark, Sitong Chen, Hyunshik Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/objectiveBecause 24-h movement behaviors share a finite time budget, treating them as independent exposures may obscure their joint associations with mental health. This study examined associations of the 24-h movement behavior composition with internalizing and externalizing problem scores among Japanese preschool children using compositional data analysis (CoDA) and compositional isotemporal substitution.MethodsA total of 469 Japanese preschool children aged 3–5 years wore a triaxial accelerometer (Active Style Pro HJA-750C) to assess sedentary behavior (SB), light physical activity (LPA), and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA). Nighttime sleep duration and Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire outcomes were assessed by parent report. Isometric log-ratio regression and compositional isotemporal substitution models were adjusted for age, sex, and continuous body mass index.ResultsA greater relative allocation to MVPA, compared with the geometric mean of sleep, SB, and LPA, was associated with a lower internalizing problem score (unstandardized B = −1.25, 95% confidence interval, −2.40 to −0.10; p = 0.03). No first-pivot estimate was statistically significant for externalizing problem scores. In model-based 15-min/day reallocation scenarios, reallocating time from SB to MVPA corresponded to a 0.27-point lower predicted internalizing score, whereas the reverse reallocation corresponded to a 0.36-point higher score. Similar inverse associations were observed in the full-sample waking-time composition and residual-adjusted models, but not in the residual-restricted samples.ConclusionThese cross-sectional findings indicate that a greater relative allocation of time to MVPA was associated with lower internalizing problem scores, but they do not establish a protective or causal effect.

Introduction

Growing international attention has been directed toward 24-h movement behaviors in young children, comprising physical activity (PA), sedentary behavior, and sleep, given their critical role in shaping physical, cognitive, and socio-emotional development. 1, 2, 3 These behaviors are inherently interdependent within the fixed 24-h day; consequently, allocating more time to one behavior reduces time available for others. Despite this inherent time-use constraint, much of the existing literature has treated these behaviors as independent predictors, failing to account for their complementary and compensatory relationships and, consequently, overlooking the collective influence of the 24-h movement behavior composition on young children's health and well-being.4,5 These limitations have been increasingly addressed through the application of compositional data analysis (CoDA), which offers distinct methodological advantages for examining the closed and inherently relative nature of 24-h movement behavior data. 6, 7, 8.

Globally, an estimated 10–20% of children and adolescents experience clinically relevant behavioral difficulties, including internalizing problems (e.g., emotional symptoms, anxiety, social withdrawal) and externalizing problems (e.g., conduct issues, hyperactivity, attention difficulties). 9, 10, 11 Previous studies have shown that early internalizing difficulties increase the risk of depression and anxiety disorders later, with internalizing symptoms in adolescence associated with a two-to threefold increase in the likelihood of developing depressive and anxiety disorders in early adulthood.11,12 Furthermore, externalizing problems are closely associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including poor academic achievement and difficulties in peer relationships.13,14 Studies conducted in Japan during the COVID-19 pandemic reported increases in clinically relevant emotional and behavioral difficulties among children, including increased hyperactivity and reduced prosocial behavior among preschool children.15,16 Because these observations were made during an exceptional social and public health context, they should not be interpreted as evidence of long-term trends beyond the pandemic period. Nevertheless, they highlight the importance of examining behavioral and emotional problems during the formative preschool years.

Recently, accumulating international evidence has increasingly highlighted the 24-h movement behavior composition as an important correlate of behavioral and socio-emotional health in early childhood. Higher levels of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) have been associated with fewer internalizing problems and better emotional regulation.17 Likewise, sufficient sleep duration has been shown to substantially reduce the risk of behavioral problems in early childhood, whereas shorter sleep duration is consistently linked to greater aggression, hyperactivity, and attentional difficulties. 18,19 In contrast, excessive screen time, as a common form of sedentary behavior, has been consistently associated with a broad range of emotional and behavioral difficulties, including heightened hyperactivity, poorer peer relationships, and reduced prosocial behavior.20,21 However, many previous studies have modeled PA, sedentary behavior, and sleep duration as independent predictors, without accounting for their inherent interdependence. Such traditional multiple regression approaches to time-use data are fundamentally multi-collinear, compromising the stability of parameter estimates and limiting the interpretability of the findings.5,7 Moreover, traditional analytic approaches typically yield broad and non-specific recommendations, such as increasing PA or reducing sedentary behavior, without providing actionable evidence on how time should be reallocated among behaviors to promote health within the finite 24-h day.

CoDA addresses these limitations by modeling the 24-h movement behavior composition as an inherently interdependent composition, thereby allowing the relative contribution of each behavior to be quantified. Building on this approach, compositional regression and isotemporal substitution techniques offer methodological advantages for estimating the health implications of reallocating time from one behavior to another.7,22 Nevertheless, existing CoDA research involving children and adolescents has predominantly focused on obesity and body composition,23 cardiometabolic health indicators,24 biological markers,25 fundamental movement skills,26 and physical fitness27 or cardiorespiratory fitness,28 while evidence on socio-emotional or behavioral outcomes remains limited, particularly among preschool children. Recent studies have begun to examine these relationships in preschool children. A guideline-adherence study reported that meeting the physical activity guideline was associated with greater prosocial behavior, whereas meeting the screen time and sleep guidelines was associated with lower externalizing problem scores.29 A compositional study found that greater relative MVPA was associated with lower Total Difficulties and Internalizing Problems scores.30 In contrast, another study found no significant association between the 24-h movement behavior composition and social-emotional development.31 Collectively, the emerging evidence remains mixed across behavioral measurements, outcome definitions, and analytical approaches.

In Japan, recent evidence indicates marked shifts in preschool children's 24-h movement behavior patterns. Recent data further show that daily MVPA among Japanese preschool children has declined significantly,16,32 while sedentary behavior and screen time have increased,33,34 and approximately 30% of children fail to meet the recommended sleep guidelines.33 However, to our knowledge, no previous study has used CoDA to examine the associations of the 24-h movement behavior composition with internalizing and externalizing problems among Japanese preschool children. Addressing this gap may clarify whether the relative distribution of accelerometer-derived waking behaviors and parent-reported nighttime sleep is associated with these behavioral outcomes in this population.

The present study aimed to examine how the 24-h movement behavior composition was associated with internalizing and externalizing behavioral problems among Japanese preschool children using CoDA. First, we examine the associations between the relative time distribution of PA, sedentary behavior, and sleep and internalizing and externalizing problem scores derived from the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) using CoDA-based regression models. Second, we apply an isotemporal substitution approach to estimate the predicted changes in behavioral problem scores when time is reallocated from one behavior to another, thereby characterizing the predicted differences associated with specific time-reallocation scenarios.

Method

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study used data collected from October to the end of November 2021 as part of the “24-h Movement and Mental Health of East Asian Children” project.35 This period corresponds to autumn in Miyagi Prefecture, northeastern Japan. The quasi-emergency measures applied to Miyagi Prefecture ended on September 30, 2021; therefore, the prefecture was not under a state of emergency or quasi-emergency designation during data collection. However, a prefectural “rebound prevention period” remained in effect from October 1 to October 31, during which residents were asked to avoid crowded places and unnecessary interprefectural travel, gather in small groups, and continue basic infection-prevention practices, including mask wearing and hand hygiene.36 Routine infection-prevention practices continued during the remainder of the data-collection period. In collaboration with five kindergartens in the region, assessments were conducted using a combination of objective accelerometer measurements and subjective questionnaire-based evaluations. Before data collection, parents or legal guardians received written information describing the study procedures and provided written informed consent for their child's participation. The study procedures were also explained to the children in simple, age-appropriate language, and verbal assent was sought. Children were not asked to read or sign a written consent form. If a child expressed unwillingness to participate or showed discomfort with any procedure, participation was not initiated or was discontinued, irrespective of parental consent. The participating kindergartens provided institutional permission and assisted with implementation of the study. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 683 preschool children were invited to participate in the study. Of these, 39 declined the invitation and 127 did not respond, resulting in 517 children who agreed to participate.

Eligibility criteria included enrollment in one of the participating kindergartens, an age of 3–5 years at assessment, and written informed consent provided by a parent or legal guardian. In addition, inclusion in the analytical sample required valid accelerometer data (≥4 days with ≥10 h/day of wear time) and completion of the parent questionnaire.37 After excluding 8 children who did not meet the inclusion criteria, 509 children were eligible for data collection. Subsequently, 26 children were excluded due to missing accelerometer data and 14 due to missing questionnaire data (Fig. 1). Measures and instrumentation

24-hour movement behaviors

PA and SB were assessed using a triaxial accelerometer (Active Style Pro HJA-750C, Omron Healthcare Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan). A laboratory calibration study in Japanese children evaluated a prototype of the HJA-750C against energy expenditure measured by indirect calorimetry and developed child-specific equations for estimating physical activity intensity.38 Parents received standardized instructions to attach the device near the iliac crest using an elastic belt. Side placement was assigned according to handedness as an operational field standardization procedure rather than being freely chosen: right-handed children wore the device on the right side of the waist, whereas left-handed children wore it on the left side. Parents were instructed to attach the accelerometer after the child woke in the morning and to remove it at bedtime. The device was therefore worn for one week during waking hours, typically from approximately 7:00 a.m. to 9:00 p.m. This time range represented the typical waking-wear period rather than a fixed monitoring window. The device was also removed during water-based activities, such as bathing and swimming. The HJA-750C initially provides MET values derived from adult prediction equations. In the present study, the device-output MET values were corrected at the 10-s epoch level using the first-order equations based on the calibration study of Tanaka et al.38 and the processing guidance provided by the Japan Physical Activity Research Platform.39 Let X denote the MET value provided by the HJA-750C. The HJA-750C classification of each epoch as non-locomotive or locomotive activity was used to select the corresponding correction equation.

For non-locomotive activity when X≤1.9:CorrectedMETs=0.0103×(X−0.88230.0351)+0.9

For non-locomotive activity when X≥2.0:CorrectedMETs=0.0103×(X−1.34350.0196)+0.9

For locomotive activity:CorrectedMETs=1.0012+0.0037×(X−1.11280.0086)

Tanaka et al. presented both a first-order equation with a fixed intercept of 0.9 and a quadratic equation for non-locomotive activity; the quadratic equation provided a slightly better fit. In accordance with the processing procedure used in the present study, we applied the first-order equations shown above. The corrected MET values were then classified as SB (≤1.5 METs), LPA (1.6–2.9 METs), MPA (3.0–5.9 METs), and VPA (≥6.0 METs); MVPA was calculated as the sum of MPA and VPA. These thresholds were used as operational intensity categories to maintain comparability with previous HJA-750C studies in preschool children.33,35 However, the exact thresholds have not been independently validated as preschool-specific HJA-750C cut points under free-living conditions.

Parents completed a daily log documenting their child's bedtime and wake-up time, as well as the duration and reasons for device removal during the intended waking-wear period. Nighttime sleep duration was estimated independently from parent-reported bedtime and wake-up times, which were recorded separately for weekdays and weekends.40,41 The nighttime interval between the reported bedtime and wake-up time was not treated as accelerometer non-wear during waking hours. Naps were not recorded separately; therefore, daytime sleep occurring while the accelerometer was worn may have been classified as SB because of the low movement intensity, whereas potential sleep occurring during accelerometer non-wear remained unclassified. Accordingly, the parent-reported nighttime sleep interval was not included in the accelerometer-derived SB, LPA, or MVPA components. During the intended waking-wear period, accelerometer non-wear was defined as at least 20 consecutive minutes of zero values. These periods were excluded from the classification of SB, LPA, and MVPA and were not automatically classified as sleep.

Internalizing and externalizing behavioral problems

Behavioral and emotional problems were assessed using the parent-reported Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ).42 The SDQ is a brief behavioral screening instrument with established psychometric properties.43 The SDQ is available in more than 75 languages, reflecting its broad applicability across cultural contexts, and multiple validated versions have been developed for use in research, clinical practice, and educational settings.42 The instrument consists of 25 items rated on a 3-point Likert scale (0 = Not True, 1 = Somewhat True, 2 = Certainly True), yielding five subscale scores, Emotional Symptoms, Conduct Problems, Hyperactivity/Inattention, Peer Problems, and Prosocial Behavior, each ranging from 0 to 10. Consistent with established scoring procedures, Internalizing Problems was defined as the sum of the Emotional Symptoms and Peer Problems subscales (range: 0–20), while Externalizing Problems was calculated as the sum of the Conduct Problems and Hyperactivity/Inattention subscales (range: 0–20). The Total Difficulties score represented the combined sum of all four problem subscales (Internalizing + Externalizing), with a total possible range of 0–40. The Japanese version of the SDQ has demonstrated high reliability and validity, with previous studies in Japanese child populations reporting acceptable to good psychometric properties.44,45 Parents completed the SDQ during the same 7-day accelerometer monitoring period and returned it together with the accelerometer and daily log at the end of monitoring.

Covariates

Child demographic and anthropometric information was obtained using a parent-completed questionnaire during the same 7-day accelerometer monitoring period. The completed questionnaire was returned together with the accelerometer and daily log at the end of monitoring. Parents reported their child's age, sex, height in centimeters, and weight in kilograms. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). BMI was entered into the adjusted regression models as a continuous variable, together with age and sex, to account for potential confounding in the association between the 24-h movement behavior composition and internalizing and externalizing problem scores. Height and weight were used only to calculate BMI and were not entered separately into the regression models. BMI was not categorized using adult or child weight-status cutoffs because it was included for covariate adjustment rather than weight-status classification.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with the compositions and robCompositions packages used for handling and analyzing compositional data. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize participant characteristics, including means and standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The mean 24-h time-use composition of the cohort was also derived and reported.

CoDA was used for the primary analysis to examine the association between the 24-h movement behavior composition and the primary behavioral outcomes (Internalizing Problems, Externalizing Problems).4,5,7 CoDA provides a robust statistical framework that overcomes multicollinearity of co-dependent components of a finite total (24 h or 1440 min) by analyzing the log-ratios between the components, thereby preserving the relative nature of the time-use data.46 For each child, the four observed components—sleep, SB, LPA, and MVPA—were closed to 1440 min/day using the following formula: closed componentᵢ = 1440 × componentᵢ/(sleep + SB + LPA + MVPA). This closure proportionally rescaled the four observed components but did not behaviorally classify unobserved time. Before closure, residual time was calculated as 1440 − (sleep + SB + LPA + MVPA). Positive residual values represented unclassified time, whereas negative values indicated that the summed reported and measured components exceeded 1440 min/day. The primary movement behavior composition consisted of four parts: sleep, SB, LPA, and MVPA. After closure to 1440 min/day, the four-part composition was transformed into three orthonormal isometric log-ratio (ilr) coordinates. A pivot-coordinate approach was used to facilitate interpretation. For an ordered composition (x1,x2,x3,x4), the three coordinates were defined as:z1=34ln⁡[x1(x2x3x4)1/3],z2=23ln⁡[x2(x3x4)1/2],z3=12ln⁡(x3x4).

Each behavior was sequentially placed in x1, so that the first coordinate represented that behavior relative to the geometric mean of the remaining three behaviors. For each pivot orientation, all three ilr coordinates were included in the regression model:SDQ=β0+β1z1+β2z2+β3z3+β4Age+β5Sex+β6BMI+ϵ.

Age and BMI were entered into the regression models as continuous variables, while sex was entered as a categorical variable. The coefficient of the first pivot coordinate was reported for interpretation. Separate, mathematically equivalent pivot orientations were generated for sleep, SB, LPA, and MVPA. These four first-coordinate estimates were not entered simultaneously in a single model.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the influence of residual time and the mixed measurement sources. First, the four-part analysis was repeated after restricting the sample to children with residual time ≤180 min/day and ≤240 min/day. These thresholds were pragmatic and were not based on externally validated criteria. Second, SB, LPA, and MVPA were analyzed as a three-part waking-time composition and were therefore transformed into two ilr coordinates, with parent-reported nighttime sleep duration included as a separate covariate. This three-part, two-coordinate specification was used only in the waking-time sensitivity analysis. Third, the original four-part model was adjusted for centered signed residual time. Finally, the model was adjusted for centered absolute residual time, representing the magnitude of deviation from 1440 min/day regardless of direction. The residual covariates were expressed per 60 min. To translate the regression coefficients from the ilr models into practically meaningful and interpretable results, isotemporal substitution analysis was performed.22 The isotemporal substitution estimates were derived from the same covariate-adjusted compositional regression models. This analytical technique uses the model parameters to estimate the predicted change in an outcome associated with reallocating a fixed amount of time, set at 15 min/day, from one behavior to another, while holding the time in the remaining behaviors constant. This allows for a direct examination of the relative importance of different behaviors. Results are presented as estimated changes in SDQ points with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Sensitivity analyses

Before closure, the mean residual time was 155.29 min/day. Residual time exceeded 180 min/day in 193 children (41.2%) and 240 min/day in 70 children (14.9%), while 29 children (6.2%) had negative residual values. The relative MVPA coefficient remained negative but was not statistically significant in the residual-restricted samples (≤180 min/day: B = −0.573, 95% CI −2.158 to 1.013, p = 0.478, n = 276; ≤240 min/day: B = −0.954, 95% CI −2.248 to 0.340, p = 0.148, n = 399). However, greater relative MVPA remained associated with lower internalizing problem scores in the three-part waking-time composition (B = −1.350, 95% CI −2.567 to −0.134, p = 0.030) and in four-part models adjusted for signed residual time (B = −1.203, 95% CI −2.350 to −0.056, p = 0.040) and absolute residual time (B = −1.242, 95% CI −2.389 to −0.095, p = 0.034). No relative MVPA coefficient was statistically significant for externalizing problems across these alternative specifications. Sensitivity-analysis estimates for internalizing and externalizing problem scores are presented in Supplementary Table S3 and S4, respectively.

Isotemporal substitution analysis

In the fully adjusted isotemporal substitution models, the model-estimated reallocation of 15 min/day from SB to MVPA was associated with a 0.27-point decrease in internalizing problem scores, whereas reallocation from MVPA to SB was associated with a 0.36-point increase. Reallocation from sleep to MVPA was associated with a 0.28-point decrease, whereas reallocation from MVPA to sleep was associated with a 0.36-point increase. Other reallocation estimates were not statistically significant. No statistically significant reallocation estimates were observed for externalizing problems (Fig. 3). Discussion

This study used CoDA and isotemporal substitution modeling to examine associations between the 24-h movement behavior composition and internalizing and externalizing problems among Japanese preschool children. A greater relative allocation of time to MVPA was associated with lower internalizing problem scores in the primary analysis. Similar inverse associations were observed in the full-sample waking-time composition analysis and the residual-adjusted four-part models, but the association was attenuated and was not statistically significant in the residual-restricted analyses.

This pattern was broadly consistent with a CoDA study in Chinese preschool children that associated greater relative MVPA with lower Total Difficulties and Internalizing Problems scores.30 The absence of significant externalizing estimates differed from a guideline-adherence study reporting lower scores among children meeting screen time and sleep recommendations.29 Another study found no association with social-emotional development, although it assessed a different outcome from the present study.31 These mixed findings may reflect differences in measurement, outcomes, analytical approaches, and cultural contexts. Early childhood is a critical period for the development of emotion regulation, and internalizing problems may be particularly sensitive to daily experiences during this stage. MVPA may support socio-emotional well-being through biological and psychosocial pathways, including stress regulation, executive functioning, and interactive play experiences.47,48 Thus, the inverse association between MVPA and internalizing problems observed in this study may reflect the combined influence of these mechanisms. From a compositional perspective, the relative allocation of time to MVPA within the finite 24-h day may be important for children's socio-emotional well-being.

The isotemporal substitution estimates were asymmetrical: reallocating time from SB or sleep to MVPA was associated with small decreases in predicted internalizing problem scores, whereas reallocating time away from MVPA was associated with somewhat larger increases. These model-based associations should not be interpreted as evidence of a protective effect or a minimum threshold of MVPA. Rather, they indicate that predicted internalizing problem scores varied according to the direction and source behavior of the reallocation. Because the data were cross-sectional, the analysis cannot establish whether changing MVPA would cause changes in internalizing problems. These findings describe average model-predicted differences at the group level and do not imply that individual children with relatively low MVPA will necessarily experience internalizing problems, or that those with relatively high MVPA will not. Previous evidence linking sleep duration and regularity to behavioral difficulties further underscores the importance of considering movement behaviors jointly within the 24-h day.18,19 Excessive screen time has been associated with adverse socio-emotional outcomes, including elevated hyperactivity, poorer peer relationships, and lower prosocial behavior.20,21 However, the present study measured accelerometer-derived SB rather than screen time. These constructs are not synonymous: SB encompasses both screen-based and non-screen-based sedentary activities, whereas accelerometry cannot identify the behavioral context of sedentary periods. Therefore, our findings for SB should not be interpreted as findings concerning screen time. The compositional approach applied in the present study evaluated the relative balance among accelerometer-derived SB, LPA, and MVPA and parent-reported nighttime sleep.

We found no statistically significant associations between the 24-h movement behavior composition and externalizing problems. This null finding may reflect the multifactorial etiology of externalizing problems, which are shaped by a broad range of influences, including temperamental characteristics, parenting practices, and the home environment.49,50 In addition, because the SDQ was assessed via parent report, internalizing difficulties may be captured with greater sensitivity, whereas externalizing behaviors may be more contingent on situational contexts and thus more variable across settings. Finally, externalizing problems in the preschool years often show substantial normative developmental variability, which may further attenuate detectable associations with daily movement behavior patterns. In addition, internalizing and externalizing problems may differ in their sensitivity to everyday environmental inputs. Internalizing difficulties often reflect more subtle disturbances in mood and emotion regulation and may therefore be more directly shaped by daily behavior patterns, whereas externalizing problems may be more strongly influenced by interpersonal dynamics and the caregiving context. Although the proportion of explained variance (R2) in our models was modest, small effects should be interpreted in relation to their context and potential practical significance.51,52 Child mental health is shaped by a complex combination of biological, psychological, social, and environmental factors; movement behaviors therefore represent only one set of potential influences and are not expected to explain a large proportion of the outcome variance. Modest associations involving modifiable movement behaviors may nevertheless be relevant at the population level. However, longitudinal and intervention studies are needed before specific behavioral or policy recommendations can be made. This study has several strengths, including the use of accelerometer-based objective measurement, well-validated instruments for assessing behavioral outcomes, and the application of compositional data analysis. Given the limited number of compositional studies involving preschool-aged children and the limited comparable evidence from Japan, the present study contributes context-specific evidence to this emerging literature.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the primary four-part composition combined accelerometer-derived waking behaviors with parent-reported nighttime sleep, which was subject to potential reporting error. Naps were not recorded separately; therefore, daytime sleep occurring during accelerometer wear may have been classified as SB, whereas potential sleep occurring during non-wear remained unclassified. Moreover, closure to 1440 min/day proportionally rescaled the observed components but did not provide a behavioral classification of residual time. Although the inverse association between relative MVPA and internalizing problem scores was supported by the full-sample waking-time composition analysis and the residual-adjusted four-part models, it was attenuated and was not statistically significant in the residual-restricted samples. The findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Second, while accelerometers provide an objective assessment of PA, they do not directly capture posture; consequently, static behaviors such as standing or lying may be misclassified as LPA or SB, leading to potential over- or underestimation of specific activity categories. Accordingly, PA-related estimates in this study should be interpreted in light of these measurement constraints. In addition, side placement was standardized according to handedness as an operational field procedure rather than on the basis of a validated side-specific calibration. Although the device was positioned near the iliac crest on either side, minor differences between right- and left-side placement may have introduced additional variability in the estimated MET values. Furthermore, although the child-specific correction equations were derived from a laboratory calibration study of Japanese children, the exact intensity thresholds used to distinguish SB, LPA, MPA, and VPA have not been independently validated for the HJA-750C under free-living conditions in preschool children. Potential intensity misclassification may therefore have redistributed time among SB, LPA, and MVPA, thereby affecting the relative movement behavior composition, the resulting ilr coordinates, and the estimated associations. The direction and magnitude of this potential bias cannot be determined from the present data.

Third, internalizing and externalizing problems were assessed using the parent-reported SDQ. Parents' perceptions and knowledge of their child's behavior may have influenced the ratings, and children's behavior may differ between home and kindergarten settings. In addition, height and weight were reported by parents rather than measured by trained research staff. Reporting error in either measure may have affected the calculated BMI and may have resulted in residual confounding in the adjusted analyses. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the observed associations between SB, intensity-specific PA, and mental health. Future longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal ordering, and where feasible, intervention studies should test whether changes in specific behaviors (e.g., reducing SB or increasing MVPA) causally influence mental health outcomes and clarify the direction of these effects. Fifth, because the sample was drawn from a limited area in northeastern Japan, generalizability to the broader population of Japanese preschool children may be restricted. Movement opportunities and lifestyle patterns may vary by regional socioeconomic and environmental conditions; in particular, rural or socioeconomically disadvantaged groups may face constraints related to facilities, space, and equipment, as well as fewer opportunities to engage in organized activities (e.g., community sports programs), which could contribute to differences in daily activity levels and socio-emotional development. Therefore, future multi-site studies including geographically and socioeconomically diverse samples are warranted to evaluate the reproducibility of our findings and strengthen their external validity. Because data were collected only during autumn, seasonal variation related to temperature and daylight availability could not be assessed, and the observed PA, SB, and sleep patterns may not represent patterns during other seasons. Sixth, data were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic. Although neither a state of emergency nor quasi-emergency measures were in force in Miyagi Prefecture during data collection, a prefectural rebound prevention period remained in effect during October 2021. The broader pandemic context and associated infection-prevention practices may have influenced kindergarten routines, family activities, social interactions, opportunities for active play, and parent-reported behavioral outcomes. Because pre-pandemic comparison data and participant-level information regarding pandemic-related behavioral changes were unavailable, the extent and direction of these influences could not be determined. The findings may therefore not fully represent movement behavior and behavioral outcome patterns outside the pandemic period. Finally, although additional dose-response plots were generated for reallocations of up to 30 min/day, the primary isotemporal substitution estimates reported in the text were based on a fixed reallocation of 15 min/day. Larger reallocations should therefore be interpreted cautiously, as they may represent counterfactual compositions that are less feasible for some participants.

Conclusion

This study found that a greater relative allocation of time to MVPA was associated with lower internalizing problem scores in the primary analysis. Similar inverse associations were observed in the full-sample waking-time composition analysis and the residual-adjusted four-part models, but not in the residual-restricted samples. The model-estimated differences under the time-reallocation scenarios were small and asymmetrical. No statistically significant associations were observed for externalizing problems. Therefore, these cross-sectional findings are consistent with a potential beneficial role of MVPA but do not establish a protective or causal effect. Longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether changes in movement behavior composition influence internalizing problems.
