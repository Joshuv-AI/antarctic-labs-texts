Free-ranging hedgehogs as sentinels of environmental extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales
Authors: Tal Zilberman Daniels, David Eshar, Yael Aharon, Sharon Amit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance (AMR), particularly among extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (ESBL-PE), represents a major challenge at the human-animal-environment interface. We conducted a nationwide study assessing faecal carriage of ESBL-PE in free-ranging hedgehogs, a synanthropic species well adapted to urban environments. Between December 2022 and September 2023, 246 hedgehogs rescued across Israel and admitted to the country's sole wildlife hospital were screened on admission, with all isolates undergoing antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Overall, 71% of hedgehogs carried at least one ESBL-PE isolate, and 2% harboured carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales. Escherichia coli was the predominant species, followed by Citrobacter, Klebsiella, and Enterobacter spp. High resistance rates were observed to quinolones (83%) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (46%). Carriage prevalence varied over time, with higher rates observed during months associated with holiday periods and increased outdoor recreational activity.These findings suggest that free-ranging hedgehogs may serve as a complementary sentinel species for monitoring AMR patterns in One Health contexts.Keywords: Hedgehogs, Antimicrobial resistance, Extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (ESBL-PE), Sentinel

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a natural evolutionary phenomenon that has been markedly accelerated by selective pressures associated with human activity [1], [2]. Recognised as a major public health threat, AMR poses significant challenges to both treatment and infection control [3]. A substantial proportion of significant difficult-to-treat resistant bacteria are extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (ESBL-PE), which are associated with poor prognosis [4], [5].

Antimicrobial use in human and veterinary medicine, as well as in agriculture, is a key driver of the spread of AMR [6], [7]. While substantial progress has been made in understanding AMR in humans, One Health frameworks developed over recent decades have broadened investigation to interconnected drivers across humans, animals, and the environment [8]. These have guided intensive research into the role of farm animals and wastewater in the broader transmission of AMR through ecosystems and food chains. However, the contribution of wildlife to AMR ecology and transmission remains less well characterised in many settings [9].

Hedgehogs are small, omnivorous mammals that have rapidly adapted to urban environments due to human encroachment on their natural habitats. This adaptation has led to the creation of a unique human-animal interface, making them a relevant model for studying AMR transmission. Hedgehogs have already been identified as a potential reservoir of the mecC gene, which confers methicillin resistance in Staphylococcus aureus [10], and ESBL-PE carriage has also been detected in a small European study [11].

In Israel, rapid population growth, high human density, and accelerating urbanisation have markedly transformed hedgehog habitats. Hedgehogs are now frequently observed in streets, lawns, and gardens of towns and villages. This widespread urban encroachment, combined with nationally accessible, centrally coordinated and free-of-charge wildlife veterinary services, creates a distinctive opportunity to investigate the role of hedgehogs in harbouring and potentially contributing to the dissemination of antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The Wildlife Hospital of Israel (WHIL), located in the Safari Zoological Park in Ramat Gan, is the primary centre for treating sick and injured hedgehogs, most of which are rescued by concerned citizens from across the country. Between December 2022 and September 2023, faecal samples were collected from newly admitted hedgehogs. Upon arrival, each hedgehog was placed in a clean cage with fresh disposable nylon tarp bedding to prevent sample cross-contamination. The geographical origin, species, weight, and presenting conditions of each hedgehog were documented.

Laboratory methods

Fresh faecal samples were collected upon the hedgehog's arrival and enriched overnight in brain-heart infusion broth (BHI, Hylabs, Israel). The following day, samples were plated onto selective culture media (Chromagar ESBL, Hylabs, Israel) to identify ESBL-PE. Following overnight incubation, the plates were examined for visible growth, and suspected colonies were identified using MALDI-TOF MS (Bruker Daltonics, USA). All confirmed Enterobacterales isolates underwent antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) using the VITEK2 system (N-395 card, bioMérieux, France). To confirm the resistance mechanisms, the modified double disc synergy test was employed [12], accompanied by the VITEK2 expert system. Carbapenemase production was confirmed using a lateral flow immunoassay (CARBA-5, NG Biotech, France), a qualitative assay that detects the five most prevalent carbapenemases (NDM, IMP, VIM, OXA-48-like, and KPC).

Statistical analysis

The Chi-square test was used to assess temporal trends in carriage rates.

Ethical considerations

The study was authorised by the Israel Nature and Parks Authority, permit no. 2023–43,256.

Results

Between December 2022 and September 2023, a total of 246 faecal samples were collected from hedgehogs admitted to the Wildlife Hospital of Israel (WHIL). The majority of the hedgehogs were Erinaceus concolor (N = 241), the most common species in Israel, while five were Hemiechinus auratus. Although also native to Israel, the desert-dwelling Paraechinus aethiopicus was not represented in the cohort.

Of the 246 hedgehogs sampled, 71% (N = 175) carried at least one ESBL-PE isolate, yielding a total of 274 individual ESBL-PE isolates. The most prevalent species was Escherichia coli, followed by Citrobacter spp., Klebsiella spp., Enterobacter spp., and other less common Enterobacterales (Fig. 1). No associations were observed between carriage rates and physical or clinical parameters (weight, sex, primary diagnoses). Similarly, carriage rates were consistent across geographic districts.

Distribution of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales species isolated from hedgehogs.

Faecal samples from hedgehogs were screened for ESBL-producing Enterobacterales following overnight enrichment in liquid media and isolation on selective agar. A total of 274 ESBL-producing Enterobacterales isolates were recovered from 246 hedgehogs between December 2022 and September 2023.

Temporal analysis revealed variation in carriage rates across the study period, with lower rates observed in January (57%) and a tendency towards higher rates in months characterised by major public or school holiday periods, which are typically associated with increased recreational outdoor activity. Specifically, ESBL-PE carriage rates were 78%, 81% and 78% in April, July and September, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Notably, six hedgehogs (2%) carried carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales. CARBA-5 was positive for OXA-48-like carbapenemase in two isolates and for NDM in one isolate; three isolates were CARBA-5-negative.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was available for 240 of 274 isolates and revealed very high resistance rates to commonly used antimicrobials: 83% of isolates were resistant to ciprofloxacin, 60% to chloramphenicol, and 46% to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrates a high prevalence of extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (ESBL-PE) carriage among free-ranging hedgehogs in Israel. The observed carriage rate of 71% appears higher than previously reported in European hedgehogs [11] and other wildlife species [13], [14], and higher than rates reported in the general human population in Israel [15], [16]. Direct comparisons between studies should be interpreted cautiously, given differences in host populations, sampling strategies, and laboratory methodologies.

To our knowledge, NDM-producing carbapenem-resistant Escherichia coli has not previously been reported in hedgehogs. Although detected infrequently, the presence of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales in a free-ranging wildlife species is epidemiologically noteworthy, given the critical clinical importance of these resistance mechanisms. Even sporadic detection of carbapenem resistance in wildlife underscores the environmental dissemination of clinically relevant resistance determinants beyond healthcare and agricultural settings.

In addition, ESBL-PE isolates commonly exhibited resistance to ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, highlighting the extent of multidrug resistance in this population. Such resistance profiles are not uncommon among ESBL-producing Enterobacterales and mirror patterns frequently reported in human clinical and environmental contexts [17].

ESBL-PE carriage showed temporal variation, with a tendency towards higher rates in months characterised by major public or school holiday periods and increased recreational outdoor activity. This pattern may reflect greater hedgehog exposure to human-associated waste, contaminated food sources or domestic animals during such periods. However, this temporal pattern did not reach statistical significance and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Wastewater and broader urban environmental contamination represent plausible exposure pathways [18]. However, no environmental sampling was performed, and the study design does not allow attribution of specific sources.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the study population consisted of hedgehogs admitted to a single wildlife hospital, this centre is the only wildlife hospital in the country and receives animals from across the country, providing broad geographic coverage. Nevertheless, hedgehogs brought for veterinary care may not fully represent the healthy free-ranging hedgehog population, and the conditions leading to admission may themselves influence intestinal carriage of resistant bacteria. This should be distinguished from hospital-associated acquisition or sampling artefact. The animals were rescued from the community and had not been treated in another veterinary facility before admission. Samples were collected on admission, after placement in a clean cage with fresh disposable nylon tarp bedding to minimise cross-contamination, and before hospital treatment could reasonably have affected faecal carriage. Second, while carriage of resistant bacteria was documented, no molecular typing or whole-genome sequencing was performed. Therefore, the study cannot determine genetic relatedness, define resistance mechanisms in detail, or infer transmission pathways between hedgehogs, other animals, humans and the environment. Future studies incorporating genomic analysis will be needed to address these questions.

In conclusion, free-ranging hedgehogs may represent a complementary bioindicator species for AMR circulation in urban ecosystems. Their synanthropic behaviour and close proximity to human activity position them at the human-animal-environment interface, supporting their inclusion in integrated AMR surveillance frameworks within a One Health perspective.
