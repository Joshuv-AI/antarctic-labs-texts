Thermal Performance Enhancement of a Biomimetic Fish-Fin Latent Heat Storage Unit Using CNTs-COOH/Erythritol Composite Phase Change Material
Authors: Shiping Liu, Zhenggui Li, Baozhu Han, Yanxiong Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phase change energy storage is a key technology for mitigating the volatility and intermittency of renewable energy and plays an important role in improving the efficiency, stability, and reliability of energy systems. To enhance the thermal conductivity of erythritol (Ery), this study introduces carboxylated carbon nanotubes (CNTs-COOH) as a high-thermal-conductivity filler into the Ery matrix to prepare a composite phase change material (CPCM). Inspired by biomimetic principles, a biomimetic fish-fin latent heat storage structure was designed. A series of characterizations were conducted on the CPCM, and the effects of fin eccentricity (FE) and fin inclination angle on the thermal storage performance were investigated through numerical simulation. The results show that the CPCM with 3 wt % CNTs-COOH exhibits thermal conductivities of 0.531 and 0.433 W/(m·K) at 80 and 150 °C, respectively, representing increases of 14% and 20% compared with pure Ery. CNTs-COOH effectively inhibit nanoparticle aggregation. Eccentric fins alleviate the slow heat transfer issue at the bottom of the thermal storage unit and improve phase change material (PCM) temperature uniformity. The configuration with FE = 5 mm exhibits the best thermal performance, reducing the complete melting time by 22% compared with FE = 0 mm and increasing both heat storage capacity and heat storage power by 2.4%. The effect of fin angle on the melting process follows a trend of “initially slow and subsequently rapid”. At 2160 s, compared with the 45° configuration, the 90° and 130° configurations significantly enhance convective heat transfer. Among them, the 130° configuration increases heat storage capacity by 2.93% and reduces the complete melting time by 10%.

Introduction

Background

Latent heat thermal energy storage technology, as an important approach for improving energy utilization efficiency and promoting renewable energy applications, has attracted increasing attention in recent years. Phase change materials (PCMs) can absorb or release large amounts of latent heat during phase transitions and achieve energy storage and release at nearly constant temperatures, showing great application potential in fields such as solar energy utilization, industrial waste heat recovery, and building energy conservation. According to their working principles, thermal energy storage technologies can be mainly classified into sensible heat storage (SHS), latent heat storage (LHS), and thermochemical storage (TCS). , Among them, LHS exhibits advantages including high energy storage density, nearly isothermal heat storage, and good operational stability. Based on their material composition, PCMs can be classified into three categories: inorganic, organic, and eutectic materials. Inorganic PCMs generally exhibit high thermal conductivity and latent heat; however, they are prone to phase separation and subcooling issues. Organic PCMs possess excellent chemical stability and cycling performance, making them more suitable for engineering applications, especially in the medium-temperature thermal energy storage field (100–300 °C). However, most PCMs suffer from inherently low thermal conductivity, resulting in slow heat storage and release rates, which limits their application under high-power-density thermal storage conditions. − To address these limitations, traditional studies have mainly focused on material modification strategies, where high-thermal-conductivity fillers, such as metal particles, graphite, and carbon nanotubes (CNTs), are incorporated into composite PCMs (CPCMs) to enhance thermal conductivity and improve heat transfer performance. −

Previous studies have demonstrated that the thermal storage performance of phase-change systems depends not only on the properties of the PCM itself but also on the thermal storage structure. Rational structural designs, such as fin enhancement, porous frameworks, and composite thermal conductive networks, can effectively enhance heat transfer, optimize temperature distribution, and accelerate the movement of the phase change interface, thereby improving the overall thermal response performance of the system. Therefore, integrating material modification with structural design to achieve synergistic regulation of heat conduction and convection has become an important research direction for enhancing the performance of phase-change thermal energy storage systems. ,

Progress in Composite Phase Change Materials

Han et al. and Abdeali et al. , noted that pristine PCMs suffer from drawbacks such as low thermal conductivity and significant supercooling. To enhance their performance, nanoenhanced CPCMs were prepared by incorporating nanoadditives, thereby improving thermal conductivity and latent heat of phase change while suppressing supercooling. Li et al. proposed a modification method for CNTs involving ethylene grafting and silica coating; these modified CNTs enhanced the thermal conductivity of paraffin, resulting in an 84.6% increase. Using molecular dynamics simulations, Peng incorporated CNTs of three different diameters into PCMs; they found that reducing the CNT diameter strengthened the interfacial bonding between the CNTs and the PCM, leading to a progressive increase in the thermal conductivity of the CPCM as the CNT diameter decreased.

However, due to density differences, interfacial interactions, and gravitational effects, nanofillers are prone to agglomeration and sedimentation during the solid–liquid phase transition of PCMs, resulting in nonuniform distribution and deterioration of thermophysical properties. This phenomenon limits the long-term cyclic stability of carbon-based nanoparticle-enhanced PCMs. Williams et al. pointed out that most current studies on nanoenhanced PCMs mainly focus on improving thermal conductivity and reducing charging/discharging times, while insufficient attention has been paid to enhancing latent heat capacity and improving long-term stability to mitigate issues such as nanoparticle sedimentation. Therefore, several researchers have conducted valuable investigations into the stability of nanoenhanced PCMs. Li et al. conducted a critical review of nanocomposite PCMs and indicated that nanoparticle addition generally reduces the heat capacity and increases viscosity. Although nanoparticles exhibit great potential for enhancing thermal conductivity, the mechanisms associated with Brownian motion and semisolid layer formation remain controversial. The review emphasized that uniform nanoparticle dispersion and long-term stability are essential for maintaining consistent thermal performance. He et al. demonstrated that encapsulating copper foam/paraffin wax composites with a low-melting-point alloy effectively suppressed leakage during phase transition and significantly improved shape stability. Bharathiraja et al. dispersed graphene nanoplatelets and nano-SiO2 into paraffin wax and found that hybrid nanoparticles increased thermal conductivity by 38%, 42%, and 50% under different nanoparticle concentrations while maintaining uniform dispersion within the paraffin matrix.

Research Progress on Thermal Energy Storage Structures

The thermal storage performance of thermal energy storage (TES) systems is strongly influenced by the melting rate of PCMs. In addition to optimizing the thermophysical properties of PCMs, reasonable structural design of thermal storage components can effectively shorten the melting time of PCMs and improve the heat transfer performance of the system. Therefore, extensive research has been conducted on fin-enhanced heat transfer. Hosseini et al. analyzed the effects of geometric parameters of longitudinal rectangular fins on the charging performance of shell-and-tube latent heat thermal storage units. The results demonstrated that fins can effectively shorten the complete melting time and promote heat transfer toward the deep region of the PCM. Chen et al. combined topology optimization with fin eccentricity and found that, at an eccentricity of 5 mm, the optimized fin structure increased the heat storage rate by 36.43% and the heat release rate by 15.53% with the objective of minimizing temperature differences. Alnakeeb et al. designed a thermal energy storage unit based on an elliptical tube and systematically analyzed the effects of eccentricity, aspect ratio, orientation angle, and inclination angle on thermal performance. Kumar et al. further investigated the coupled effects of eccentricity and fin angle. Among the three fin configurations, the configuration with a fin angle of 60° improved the melting performance by 6% and 7% compared with those with fin angles of 120° and 180°, respectively. In addition, the eccentric arrangement enhanced the melting performance by 7% compared with the concentric configuration.

In recent years, the design of thermal energy storage structures has gradually shifted from traditional regular fins to more complex biomimetic topological structures. Saini et al. proposed a multiscale synergistic design integrating biomimetic fins, structures, and materials based on biological prototypes, which significantly enhanced the heat transfer performance of latent heat storage units (LHSUs). Zheng et al. developed biomimetic tree-shaped fins that could regulate the evolution of the solidification front and significantly improve the solid fraction. The fourth-order treelike fins increased the solidification efficiency by 53% compared with conventional longitudinal fins. Liu et al. addressed the insufficient heat transfer caused by the low thermal conductivity of PCMs and incomplete melting at the bottom of shell-and-tube thermal energy storage systems by designing a novel biomimetic spider-web fin structure. The effects of different fin arrangements and radial angles on thermal storage performance were analyzed, and the results demonstrated that the proposed biomimetic spider-web fins could significantly accelerate PCM melting at the bottom and improve temperature uniformity.

Research Objectives

Overall, current research on improving the comprehensive performance of PCMs mainly focuses on solving problems such as shape stability, thermal cycling stability, and chemical stability. The core strategies include physical encapsulation, material composites, and chemical modification. Among these, the mainstream methods for improving thermal conductivity can be summarized into four categories: adding high-thermal-conductivity fillers, constructing three-dimensional thermally conductive networks, macroscopic encapsulation optimization, and microstructural modification. This study systematically investigated the dispersibility, interfacial thermal resistance, and undercooling suppression behavior of carboxylated CNTs (CNTs-COOH) in erythritol (Ery). Ery, as a matrix PCM, has a phase transition temperature in the intermediate temperature range of 100–300 °C, has high latent heat, and is nontoxic, showing broad application prospects in solar thermal storage and industrial waste heat recovery. However, its low thermal conductivity and significant undercooling limit its practical applications. As a nanofiller, CNTs-COOH can enhance the compatibility and dispersion stability of CNTs in a polar erythritol matrix through hydrogen bonding via their carboxyl functional groups, which are expected to reduce interfacial thermal resistance and improve overall thermal conductivity.

On the basis of the above research on heat storage structures, this work designs a biomimetic fish-fin fin structure. The configuration selection is based on the following considerations: the biomimetic fish-fin structure can lessen thermal resistance through radial distribution, construct multilevel heat conduction channels through hierarchical branching, guide convection and suppress vortices through curved profiles, and improve temperature field uniformity through periodic arrangement. This is the first time that a CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCM has been incorporated into a biomimetic fish-fin structure. The purpose of this combination is to further investigate the applicability of structural optimization in composite material systems and systematically examine the effects of eccentricity and tilt angle on the thermal storage performance of CPCMs, which differs from previous studies that focused on only a single parameter or material. It is expected that at the material level, the thermal conductivity can be improved by reducing interfacial thermal resistance; at the structural level, the temperature gradient can be weakened by increasing the heat transfer area, thereby significantly enhancing the melting rate and temperature uniformity. This study provides an integrated solution combining material modification and structural optimization for medium-temperature thermal storage systems and promotes the application of phase-change energy storage technology in fields such as solar thermal utilization and industrial waste heat recovery.

Experimental Procedures

Preparation of CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCMs

To prepare 1 wt % CNTs-COOH/Ery, 39.6 g of erythritol was weighed into a beaker and heated to a constant temperature of 150 °C using a ring heater to melt Ery. A mechanical stirrer was used to maintain a stirring speed of 500 rpm. During this process, 0.4 g of CNTs-COOH was slowly added, and the mixture was stirred for 30 min to promote the uniform distribution of CNTs-COOH in Ery. The preparation process is shown in Figure . Based on this process, 1, 2, and 3 wt % CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCMs, as well as a 1 wt % Ery/CNTs CPCM containing un-carboxylated CNTs, were prepared. After stirring, the CPCM was poured into test tubes and placed in a thermostatic incubator at 140 °C to maintain a liquid state for easy observation of sedimentation stability. Figure shows that the 1 wt % CNTs/Ery CPCM exhibited significant sedimentation on the first day, whereas 1 and 3 wt % CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCMs showed no obvious sedimentation after 1 week of standing. Un-carboxylated CNTs tend to aggregate due to their chemically inert and hydrophobic surface, as well as strong van der Waals interactions. In addition, their poor compatibility with polar erythritol leads to weak interfacial interactions, resulting in low dispersion stability and eventual sedimentation in the composite system. In contrast, carboxylation can introduce polar functional groups onto the surface of CNTs, which are capable of forming intermolecular hydrogen bonds with erythritol, thereby improving the stability of CNTs-COOH/Ery composites.

Characterization of CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCMs

To systematically characterize the thermal stability, physical properties, and microstructure of carboxylated carbon nanotube-reinforced erythritol composite phase-change materials, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), simultaneous thermal analysis (TG-DSC), thermal conductivity measurements, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were performed on the samples.

Fourier Transform Infrared Analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to analyze the functional group structures of CNTs-COOH and 1 wt % CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCM. The recorded spectra underwent baseline correction and moderate smoothing to eliminate background drift and reduce noise; the results are presented in Figure . In the spectrum of CNTs-COOH, the absorption peak at 3437.975 cm–1 is attributed to −OH stretching vibrations, indicating the introduction of oxygen-containing functional groups onto the carbon nanotube surface following carboxylation. The peak at 1631.001 cm–1 corresponds to the CC skeletal vibration of the sp2-hybridized carbon structure; shifts in this peak position are associated with structural defects induced during carboxylation and alterations to the carbon structure caused by oxygen-containing functional groups. The absorption peak at 597.43 cm–1 is related to carbon skeletal vibrations. In addition, the CO stretching vibration in carboxyl groups is usually located around 1730 cm–1, but no obvious absorption peak was observed in this study, which is related to the low carboxyl content on the CNT surface and the weak absorption intensity of the carbonyl group. For the 1 wt % CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCM, the broad peak at 3210.416 cm–1 is primarily attributed to the −OH stretching vibration of Ery molecules, while also being influenced by the oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface of CNTs-COOH. Compared with CNTs-COOH, the O–H absorption peak in the composite material shifts toward lower wavenumbers, indicating enhanced interactions between hydroxyl groups associated with hydrogen bond formation. Additionally, the peak at 2909.575 cm–1 corresponds to C–H stretching vibrations; the peaks at 1413.566 cm–1 and 1267.967 cm–1 are associated with C–H bending vibrations and C–O-related vibrations, respectively; the strong absorption peak at 1049.568 cm–1 is attributed to C–O stretching vibrations; and the peaks at 726.55 cm–1 and 855.76 cm–1 are related to molecular skeleton vibrations. In summary, the FTIR results show that CNTs-COOH are successfully dispersed in the Ery matrix, and the interfacial bonding between them is mainly formed through noncovalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces, without the formation of new covalent bonds.

Simultaneous Thermal Analysis

The phase transition stability and thermal decomposition behavior of the samples were investigated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and the corresponding TGA curves are shown in Figure (a). To quantitatively assess thermal stability, the temperature corresponding to a 5% mass loss (T5%) was used as the unified criterion for the onset of thermal decomposition. The T5% of Ery was approximately 255 °C, and the remaining mass of pure Ery at 350 °C was approximately 4.07%. This result indicates that the thermal decomposition of Ery starts earlier and proceeds rapidly. The T5% of the 1 wt % CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCM was approximately 265 °C. Compared with Ery, the 1 wt % CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCM exhibited a higher mass retention rate within the same temperature range, especially in the high-temperature range of 350–500 °C. For example, at 500 °C, the remaining mass of the 1 wt % CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCM sample was approximately 4.28%, while that of Ery was only about 1.49%. This indicates that the addition of CNTs-COOH slows the thermal decomposition process of the material, thereby improving the thermal stability of the CPCM.

(a) TGA curves of Ery and CPCMs at different mass fractions; (b) DSC curves of Ery and CPCMs at different mass fractions.

As shown in the DSC curves in Figure (b), the addition of CNTs-COOH reduces the Ery content, leading to a gradual decrease in the phase transition enthalpy of CPCMs with different mass fractions. Within the test temperature range of 50–80 °C, all samples exhibited a single endothermic melting peak at 120 °C, corresponding to the solid–liquid phase transition of Ery. The melting point of the CPCMs is essentially the same as that of Ery, 120 °C, indicating that the introduction of CNTs-COOH does not significantly alter the crystal structure of the matrix. As the content of CNTs-COOH increases, the melting enthalpy of the composite phase change materials gradually decreases. This phenomenon is not solely caused by the mass dilution effect resulting from the substitution of Ery by CNTs-COOH but is also attributed to the combined effects of mass dilution and interface-induced changes in crystallization behavior. On the one hand, as a non-phase-change component, CNTs-COOH reduce the effective mass fraction of Ery in the composite material, decreasing the amount of Ery per unit mass that can participate in the solid–liquid phase transition and thereby reducing the heat storage capacity. On the other hand, the oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface of CNTs-COOH can generate interfacial noncovalent interactions with the hydroxyl groups of Ery molecules. These interactions may restrict the migration and rearrangement of Ery molecules during the solidification process, reduce the degree of ordering during crystal growth, and lower the effective crystallization enthalpy. It should be noted that this latent heat loss is not an inevitable consequence of adding CNTs but is related to the loading amount of CNTs and the strength of interfacial interactions. This effect can be optimized by adjusting the formulation, thereby achieving a balance between improving thermal conductivity and preserving latent heat.

Thermal Conductivity Measurement

A Hot Disk thermal constants analyzer based on the transient plane source method was employed to characterize the thermal conductivity of the samples. The tested materials included pure Ery and CPCMs containing 1, 2, and 3 wt % CNTs-COOH. For each test, approximately 5 g of powder sample was evenly divided into two portions and sequentially loaded into a 30 mm mold; the samples were then pressed at 20 MPa to form two flat circular discs. During testing, a spiral nickel-foil sensor insulated with polyimide was sandwiched between the two sample discs. After applying pressure to ensure intimate contact, the testing power and duration were calibrated and stabilized for data acquisition. As shown in Figure , the thermal conductivity of the CPCMs increased at both 80 and 150 °C with increasing mass fractions of CNTs-COOH. Specifically, the thermal conductivity of the 3 wt % CPCM was 1.14 and 1.20 times higher than those of pure Ery at 80 and 150 °C, respectively. This enhancement is primarily attributed to the formation of an efficient thermally conductive network by CNTs within the Ery matrix. Additionally, potential interfacial hydrogen-bonding interactions between CNTs-COOH and erythritol may contribute to a synergistic effect.

Thermal conductivity of CPCMs at different mass fractions: (a) Thermal conductivity at 80 °C; (b) Thermal conductivity at 150 °C.

The combined results of FTIR, DSC, and thermal conductivity tests indicate that the CNTs-COOH/Ery interfacial interaction is a key factor affecting the composite material’s performance. The oxygen-containing functional groups on the CNTs-COOH surface enhance the interfacial bonding with the Ery matrix, improve the dispersion stability of CNTs, and promote the formation of continuous heat transfer pathways, thereby improving the composite material’s thermal conductivity. However, this interfacial interaction simultaneously restricts the free migration and ordered arrangement of some Ery molecules, reducing the degree of crystal formation perfection and affecting latent heat storage capacity. Therefore, CNTs-COOH have a typical “dual effect” on Ery-based composite phase change materials: enhancing heat transfer performance while simultaneously resulting in a certain loss of phase change heat storage capacity.

Figure (b) shows a localized SEM image of the CPCM; as highlighted by the red circle, CNTs-COOH are interwoven within the Ery matrix as individual filaments and small bundles, forming a three-dimensional network structure that contrasts sharply with the smooth surface of pristine Ery. This indicates that carboxylation modification effectively improves the interfacial compatibility between CNTs-COOH and Ery. Furthermore, the absence of significant large aggregates demonstrates that CNTs-COOH exhibit good dispersion within the Ery matrix.

Melting Experiment with CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCMs

As shown in Figure , the geometric structure of the thermal storage tank mainly consists of a tank body and a lid, and the material used is quartz glass. As shown in Figure , the red dashed line indicates the position of the solid–liquid interface between the solid and liquid regions during the melting process, and its variation can be used to intuitively characterize the melting behavior of the material. During the initial stage of 0–40 min, heat transfer in the Ery system is mainly dominated by conduction, with heat transferred primarily from top to bottom; as time progresses, the liquid-phase region gradually expands. However, during the 100–120 min period, due to restricted internal flow, the solid–liquid interface remains almost unchanged, resulting in a relatively low melting rate. In contrast, the Ery/CNTs-COOH-1 system exhibits obvious interface fluctuations during the 20–80 min period, indicating significantly enhanced natural convection and improved heat transfer efficiency. In particular, during the 60–120 min period, the residual solid phase at the bottom is gradually eroded and completely melts at 120 min, which is over 20 min earlier than in the pure Ery system. This difference is mainly attributed to the three-dimensional thermally conductive network formed by CNTs-COOH in the matrix. On the one hand, this network reduces the overall thermal resistance of the system, enabling heat to be rapidly transferred from the heating surface to the interior. On the other hand, the enhanced local temperature gradient promotes the formation of density differences within the fluid, thereby strengthening natural convection.

Design of a Biomimetic Fish-Fin Shell-and-Tube Latent Heat Thermal Energy Storage Unit

Physical Model

A biomimetic fish-fin finned shell-and-tube TES unit was designed in this study. The system consists of a heat transfer tube, biomimetic fish-fin fins, a shell, and the CPCM. During operation, heat is transferred from the heat-transfer tube to the CPCM through the tube wall. The CPCM absorbs heat and undergoes a phase change, thereby achieving thermal energy storage. As shown in Figure (a), a schematic diagram of the biomimetic fish-fin finned thermal energy storage structure is presented, while Figure (b) illustrates its geometric dimensions. The thermal energy storage unit adopted a horizontal configuration. The outer shell is a cylindrical structure, and the interior is filled with the PCM. The main PCM region is a cylinder with a diameter of 118 mm and a length of 160 mm. A straight circular heat-transfer tube with a diameter of 20 mm is installed inside the unit. Biomimetic fish-fin fins are welded onto the outer surface of the tube, with a fin length of 150 mm and a width of 35 mm. To further enhance the thermal energy storage performance, two optimization strategies are proposed in this study, including fin eccentricity design and fin inclination angle design. These optimization designs aim to enhance heat transfer efficiency, reduce energy losses, and improve the overall performance of the thermal energy storage system. To improve computational efficiency while ensuring solution accuracy, the three-dimensional model was reasonably simplified into a two-dimensional radial cross-sectional model for analysis.

Boundary Conditions

This study uses ANSYS software for numerical simulation. A Realizable k–ε turbulence model is selected, and the near-wall region is simulated using an enhanced wall treatment method. The pressure–velocity coupling is solved using the SIMPLE algorithm, and the pressure correction equations are solved using PRESTO!. , The continuity equation, momentum equation, and energy equation are discretized using a second-order upwind scheme, and the absolute convergence criteria are set to 10–3, 10–3, and 10–6, respectively. Initially, the ambient temperature of the entire computational domain was set to 25 °C. Mesh Generation and Model Validation

As shown in Figure (a), the computational domain is discretized using a predominantly quadrilateral unstructured mesh, which achieves a balance between computational efficiency and numerical accuracy. Five layers of boundary layer meshes are generated near the wall, with a growth rate of 1.2. The mesh lines are aligned as closely as possible with the flow direction to reduce the numerical diffusion.

(a) Computational domain grid; (b) Comparison of numerical simulation and experimental results.

In the melting experiments conducted in section , temperature variation data were obtained at three axial positions, points A, B, and C, along the thermal storage tank. A numerical model with the same physical configuration as that of the experimental setup was established for validation. As shown in Figure (b), the numerical results are generally higher than the experimental data, which can be attributed to the fact that heat losses to the surroundings and the volumetric change of the phase change material are not considered in the simulations. The average absolute errors between the experimental and numerical results at points A, B, and C are 4.5%, 2.02%, and 6.28%, respectively, indicating that the proposed numerical model has good reliability.

Time Step and Grid Independence Verification

To simultaneously achieve both computational efficiency and solution accuracy, we used five different grids and step sizes for independent verification. The mesh numbers are 21 504, 25 723, 29 256, 33 480, and 38 211, respectively, and the corresponding time steps are 0.05, 0.01, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 s, respectively. The results of the independence study are presented in Figure . As shown in Figure (a), the variation trends of the liquid fraction of the PCM under different mesh numbers are generally consistent. When the mesh number is increased to 29 256 and above, the curves nearly overlap, indicating that further mesh refinement has a negligible influence on the numerical results. It is thus demonstrated that the simulation results have essentially satisfied the requirements of mesh independence. In contrast, as shown in Figure (b), the time step has a certain influence on the evolution of the liquid fraction of the PCM. A relatively large time step of 0.5 s leads to noticeable deviations during the late-melting stage. When the time step is reduced to less than 0.1 s, the curves gradually converge and become consistent. Considering both computational accuracy and efficiency, a mesh count of 29 256 and a time step of 0.1 s were ultimately selected as the parameters for the numerical simulation, effectively reducing computational costs while ensuring high solution accuracy.

Effect of Fin Eccentricity on Heat Transfer

While keeping the outer shell size, heat exchange tube diameter, and total number of fins constant, different eccentric fin arrangements were achieved by changing the position of the fins relative to the central axis. The fin eccentricities were set to 1 mm, 3 mm, and 5 mm. Figure (a) shows that during the initial melting stage at 720 s, the liquid phase was mainly concentrated near the heating tubes and fins, with a relatively small overall area. At 2160 s, the liquid-phase area expanded rapidly, and the melting range increased more significantly with increasing eccentricity. When FE = 5 mm, the bottom region melted first due to the downward movement of the heating tubes, showing a melting trend from bottom to top. At 2880 s, melting was basically completed except for FE = 1 mm. Figure (b) shows that at 1440 s, the high-temperature region gradually expanded outward. When FE = 0 and 1 mm, the upper region exhibited a higher temperature due to buoyancy, whereas under the conditions of FE = 3 and 5 mm, the high-temperature region shifted downward because of the downward movement of the heating tubes. At the end of melting (2880 s), the high-temperature region further expanded and gradually covered the entire area and the temperature distribution tended to become uniform. Simultaneously, with increasing eccentricity, internal heat diffusion and natural convection were further enhanced, causing the temperature difference between the liquid and solid phases to decrease rapidly and resulting in a faster temperature homogenization process. Figure (c) shows that at 720 s, the fluid velocity inside the system was close to zero, indicating that heat conduction dominated at this stage and natural convection had not yet been established. At 1440 s, driven by temperature distribution differences, buoyancy gradually developed within the system and the velocity field began to circulate along the fins. When the time reached 2160 s, the flow velocity increased significantly and multiple stable circulating vortex structures formed inside the system. The high-speed flow region was mainly distributed near the liquid–solid interface, with the maximum velocity reaching 2.0 m/s at FE = 5 mm and 1.4 m/s at FE = 0 mm. With an increase in eccentricity, both the flow range and intensity increased significantly.

Effect of Fin Inclination Angle on Heat Transfer

Based on the fin structure, the installation angle of the fins relative to the radial direction was further changed, with fin inclination angles of 45°, 90°, and 130°, to investigate the effect of fin angle on heat transfer enhancement. As shown in the liquid phase fraction cloud diagram in Figure (a), significant differences in melting behavior were observed under different inclination angles. At 720 s, the liquid phase region was the largest under the 45° condition, while it decreased successively under the 90° and 130° conditions, indicating that melting occurred more rapidly at smaller inclination angles. By 1440 s, the liquid phase region under the 130° condition exhibited obvious fragmentation characteristics, with the originally continuous liquid phase being divided into multiple discrete regions by the solid phase. In contrast, the solid phase under the 45° and 90° conditions was still mainly concentrated in the central region, indicating that larger inclination angles are more conducive to the dispersion and advancement of the solid–liquid interface during the middle stage. When the time reached 2880 s, the residual solid phase under the 130° condition had completely melted, while solid material remained at the bottom under the 45° condition. As shown in the temperature field distribution in Figure (b), during the 720 and 1440 s stages, the overall temperature level under the 130° condition was relatively low, approaching 120 °C, exhibiting a certain start-up delay characteristic. However, as time progressed to 2160 s, the overall temperature reached 154 °C, with the maximum temperature reaching 183 °C, essentially approaching those under the 45° and 90° conditions. In the later stage at 2880 s, the high-temperature region further expanded in the central area, which is consistent with the evolution trend of the liquid fraction, indicating that the 130° condition exhibited stronger heat transport capacity after 2160 s. Under the 130° condition, the initial thermal response delay is attributed to the large inclination angle hindering early stage melting. However, as the solid PCM gradually melts, the exposed heat transfer surface enhances natural convection, thereby accelerating the temperature rise during the later stage. As shown in Figure (c), during the initial stage at 720 s, all three conditions were in the heat conduction-dominated period, with extremely low flow velocities. After 2160 s, the peak velocity at 45° was approximately 2.0 m/s, that at 90° was 1.8 m/s, while that at 130° was only 1.6 m/s. After 2880 s, convection under the 130° condition was fully developed, and the overall velocity level was close to those under the 45° and 90° conditions. Although the peak velocity at 130° did not exceed those of the other conditions, the enhanced interfacial disturbance caused by the fragmented solid phase distribution, along with the increased exposed fin area during the later stages, jointly improved the local heat transfer coefficient and effective heat transfer area. In addition, the continuous promotion of interfacial heat transfer by early stage fragmentation resulted in the cumulative melting effect surpassing that of the 45° condition at 2880 s, and the slightly lower velocity did not affect the final performance.

Conclusions

This work proposes a method to improve the thermal conductivity of Ery by incorporating high-thermal-conductivity carbon nanotubes as fillers. Simultaneously, a biomimetic fish-fin-like latent heat storage structure was designed by mimicking the shape of fish fins. Its branching characteristics can both increase the effective heat-transfer area and enhance local natural convection. Based on this, the modified material and biomimetic structure were combined to systematically investigate the effects of fin eccentricity and inclination angle on heat storage performance. The main conclusions of this work are as follows:(1)To improve the thermal conductivity of Ery, a high-thermal-conductivity filler, CNTs-COOH, was introduced into the Ery matrix. The thermal conductivity of the resulting CPCM increased with increasing CNT mass fraction. At 3 wt %, the thermal conductivity of the CPCM was 1.14 times and 1.2 times that of pure Ery at 80 and 150 °C, respectively. Moreover, the carboxylation treatment of CNTs effectively alleviated the agglomeration issue of nanoparticles in the phase-change material.(2)The fins are eccentrically arranged along the direction of gravity, providing more flow space for the PCM melting at the bottom of the LHSU, promoting more extensive convective heat transfer and significantly reducing the melting time. Among them, the configuration with fins eccentrically arranged by 5 mm exhibits the best heat-transfer performance, shortening the complete melting time by 22% and increasing the heat-storage capacity and heat-storage power by 2.4%.(3)The effect of fin angle on the PCM melting process exhibits a clear “slow-at-first, then-fast” characteristic. In the initial melting phase (0–720 s) at a 130° inclination angle, the heat transfer process is relatively delayed due to the low thermal conductivity of the solid PCM and the large contact thermal resistance between the fins and the PCM. As the solid–liquid interface gradually advances (1440–2160 s), the solid phase region breaks down and decreases, increasing the exposed fin area, which expands the effective heat exchange area, reduces thermal resistance, and gradually increases the melting rate. When the liquid phase fraction reaches approximately 50%, natural convection fully develops and further promotes heat transfer, continuously accelerating the later melting process. Finally, the heat storage power under the 130° inclination condition is increased by 2.93%, the complete melting time is shortened by 10%, and its cumulative melting effect exceeds that of the 45° inclination condition.(4)Combining material modification and structural optimization, the final optimized scheme is the 3 wt % CNTs-COOH/Ery CPCM with a biomimetic fish-fin structure optimized with an eccentricity of 5 mm and a 130° inclination angle. In this scheme, material modification increased the thermal conductivity by approximately 1.2 times; a 5 mm eccentricity shortened the complete melting time by 22% and increased both the heat storage capacity and heat storage power by 2.4%; the 130° inclination angle further increased the heat storage power by 2.93% and shortened the melting time by 10%. Based on the above, the biomimetic design provides additional heat transfer enhancement by increasing the effective heat exchange area and inducing local convection enhancement.

This study has certain limitations. In terms of structure, the proposed biomimetic fin structure has currently mainly been evaluated based on numerical simulations, and further experimental studies are needed to verify its actual enhancement effect. In practical applications, factors such as idealized boundary conditions, homogeneous material assumptions, manufacturing errors, surface roughness, long-term operational stability, and scale-up effects can all affect heat transfer performance. In terms of materials, the introduction of CNTs-COOH can effectively improve the heat transfer capacity and dispersion stability of composite phase-change materials; however, its interfacial effect may affect the crystallization arrangement of Ery molecules, thus leading to a certain degree of latent heat loss.

Based on the above research, the follow-up work will be carried out in the following two aspects. In terms of materials, (1) the specific heat capacity of the CPCM will be measured using the DSC sapphire method to establish a complete thermophysical property database; (2) more than 500 accelerated thermal cycling experiments will be conducted to evaluate long-term service reliability; (3) a further balance between thermal conductivity enhancement and thermal storage performance will be achieved by optimizing the mass ratio of CNTs-COOH to Ery, controlling the intensity of interfacial interactions, and constructing a multicomponent synergistic system. In terms of structure, (1) the length and number of biomimetic fins will be systematically optimized through numerical simulations; (2) the eccentricity, inclination angle, and biomimetic parameters will be jointly optimized; and (3) the feasibility will be improved by comparing experimental and simulation results. Finally, cross-scale synergistic optimization of material modification and structural design will be achieved.
