Nerve anastomoses in the upper limb: an up-to-date meta-analysis
Authors: Dawid Plutecki, Michał Bonczar, Jerzy Walocha, Weronika Chaba-Karnaś, Antonina Nowak, Tomasz Kozioł, Kacper Bąk, Marcel Frankiewicz, Mariusz Trystuła, Mateusz Koziej
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThere are numerous communicating branches (CB) between the nerves of the brachial plexus. Therefore, the objective of this study was to provide comprehensive data on the prevalence, morphometry, and anatomical classifications of upper extremity anastomoses such as: Martin–Gruber anastomosis (MGA), Marinacci anastomosis (MA), Riche-Cannieu anastomosis (CRA), Berrettini anastomosis (BA), median and musculocutaneous nerves anastomoses (MMA), or radial and lateral cutaneous nerve of forearm anastomosis (RLCA).Material and methodsMajor online medical databases such as PubMed, Embase, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, SciELO, BIOSIS, Current Content Connect and Korean Journal Database were searched to gather all relevant studies regarding the anatomical characteristics of these structures. Finally, a total of 189 studies were included in this meta-analysis, based on 23,572 upper extremities.ResultsThe prevalence of MGA was 20.74% (cadaveric studies), 18.93% (fetal studies), 16.65% (healthy subjects) and 20.37% (patients with carpal tunnel syndrom). Introduction

There are numerous communicating branches (CB) between the nerves of the brachial plexus. The typical brachial plexus is formed by the union of fibers arising from the anterior rami of the lower five cervical (C5–C8) and the first thoracic (T1) spinal nerves. These roots join in a characteristic pattern to form the superior, middle, and inferior trunks, each of which divides into anterior and posterior divisions. The divisions then reorganize to form the lateral, medial, and posterior cords, which continue distally as the terminal branches of the plexus [20, 22, 153]. Communications usually occur between anterior divisions of nerves sharing similar spinal origins, such as the musculocutaneous nerve (MCN) with the median nerve (MN), or the MN with the ulnar nerve (UN) in the forearm and hand. Although less common, connections between posterior division nerves have also been reported, such as the communication between the superficial branch of the radial nerve (SBRN) with the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm (LCNF) [197].

Interconnections between the MN and UN commonly occur in the brachial plexus, forearm, or hand [129]. In 1763, the Swedish anatomist Martin first described the morphological characteristics of a transfer of nerve fascicles in the forearm from the proximal median or anterior interosseous nerve to the distal ulnar nerve. This description was later complemented by Gruber in 1870, resulting in the term Martin–Gruber anastomosis (MGA) [39, 54, 161, 164, 218]. In 1964, Marinacci, using electrodiagnostic studies, described a patient who, following trauma to the median nerve in the forearm, exhibited denervation of the forearm flexor muscles but retained function of all median-innervated hand muscles. The Marinacci communication (MA), also known as the „reverse MGA”, carries motor fibers from the proximal ulnar to the distal median nerve in the forearm [127, 135, 161]. Another notable interconnection is the Riche–Cannieu anastomosis (CRA), also known as the „deep palmar communication”. Originally described in two separate anatomical studies by Riche and Cannieu in 1896, this anastomosis represents a connection between the deep branch of the ulnar nerve and the recurrent branch of the median nerve. It results in variable innervation of the thenar muscles by the ulnar nerve [161, 167, 218]. The Berrettini anastomosis (BA), also known as the „superficial palmar communication”, is a sensory connection between the third common palmar digital nerve from the median nerve and the fourth common palmar digital nerve from the superficial branch of the ulnar nerve in the palm. It was named after the Italian painter Pietro Berrettini da Cortona, who first illustrated this communication in 1741[161, 167, 218]. The most common variation of the MCN involves an anastomotic branch with the MN. Anatomical variations of the communication between the MCN and MN are relatively common and are thought to result from developmental differences during embryogenesis [18, 181].

Anatomical variations of peripheral nerve anastomoses are clinically important, as they may lead to diagnostic errors and, consequently, surgical complications due to inaccurate preoperative planning. Additionally, anatomical nerve connections may become compressed or entrapped by muscles or ligaments, leading to focal mononeuropathies [173]. Therefore, the objective of this study was to provide comprehensive data on the prevalence, morphometry, and anatomical classifications of upper extremity anastomoses. To accomplish this, an up-to-date systematic literature search and meta-analysis were performed.

Methods and materials

Search strategy

Major online medical databases such as PubMed, Embase, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, SciELO, BIOSIS, Current Content Connect, and Korean Journal Database, were used to gather all relevant studies on upper limb nerve anastomoses (Supplementary Table 1). The study collection ended in September 2025. In agreement with the Boolean technique, the following search terms were employed: (median nerve anastomosis OR median nerve communication OR ulnar nerve anastomosis OR ulnar nerve communication OR Martin Gruber OR Marinacci OR Riche Cannieu OR deep palmar anastomosis OR deep palmar communication OR Berrettini OR Superficial palmar anastomosis OR Superficial palmar communication OR Musculocutaneous nerve anastomosis OR Musculocutaneous nerve communication OR Medial brachial cutaneous nerve anastomosis OR Medial brachial cutaneous nerve communication OR medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve anastomosis OR medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve communication OR Radial nerve anastomosis OR radial nerve communication). The search terms were individually adapted to each database to minimize potential bias. Neither date, language, article type, nor text availability conditions were applied. An additional search was conducted through the references of the identified studies at the end of the search stage to ensure the accuracy of the process. During the study, the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were followed. Furthermore, the Critical Appraisal Tool for Anatomical Meta-analysis (CATAM) and Anatomical Quality Assessment Tool (AQUA) were used to provide the highest quality findings [49, 50, 79, 200, 219].

Eligibility assessment

The database search and the manual search identified a total of 13 442 studies that were initially evaluated by two independent reviewers. After removing duplicates and irrelevant records, a total of 368 articles were qualified for a full-text evaluation. To minimize potential bias and maintain accurate statistical methodology, articles such as case reports, case series, conference reports, reviews, letters to editors, and studies that provided incomplete or irrelevant data were excluded. The inclusion criteria consisted of original studies with extractable numerical data regarding the topic of this study. Data extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted data from qualified studies (DP, TK). Qualitative data, such as year of publication, country and continent of origin, data collection methodology, and information on nerve anastomose in the studied groups, were collected. Quantitative data, such as sample size, numerical data on anatomical characteristics, morphometry, and classifications of the nerve anastomoses, were also gathered. Any discrepancies between the studies identified by the two reviewers were resolved by contacting the authors of the original studies wherever possible or by consensus with a third reviewer (JW).

Statistical analysis

To perform the meta-analyses, MetaXL version 5.3 software (EpiGear International Pty Ltd, Wilston, Queensland, Australia), and Comprehensive Meta-analysis version 3.0 software (Biostat Inc., Englewood, NJ, USA) were used. A random-effects model was used in all analyses. The heterogeneity among the studies was evaluated using both the Chi-squared test and the I-squared statistic [80]. The I-squared statistic was interpreted as follows: 0–40% as “might not be important”; 30–60% as “may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50–90% as “may represent substantial heterogeneity”; 75–100% as “may represent considerable heterogeneity”. P-value of <0.05 and the confidence intervals (95% CI) were used to find statistically significant differences between the studied groups. In cases of overlapping confidence intervals, the differences were considered statistically insignificant.

Results

Search results

Initially, a total of 13 442 (13 364 through records identified from databases and 78 through additional search) articles were identified in the databases. A total of 12 770 records were removed because they were duplicates or articles irrelevant to the studied topic. Furthermore, 672 records were screened. Following another evaluation, an additional 304 records were excluded due to a lack of data or irrelevance to the present study. Subsequently, a total of 368 studies were qualified for a full-text assessment, during which 63 studies were removed because they were case reports or case series, 96 articles were excluded due to lack of data, 19 papers were excluded because they were review articles, and 1 studies were excluded because it was an animal study. Finally, a total of 189 studies were included in this meta-analysis, based on 23 572 upper extremities. The overall data collection process is presented in Fig. 1. Martin Gruber anastomosis

The pooled prevalence of MGA was 20.74% (95% CI 16.43–25.42%) for cadaveric was 18.93% (95% CI 9.76%–30.14%) for fetal studies, based on 2891 and 612 upper limbs. Divided by region, the MGA was found most often in North America–37.64% (95% CI 30.71%–44.82%, with 181 specimens). Although, for electrophysiological studies, the pooled prevalence of MGA was 16.65% (95% CI 13.47–20.09%) for healthy subjects and 20.37% (95% CI 8.36–35.61%) for adults with carpal tunnel syndrome, according to 4407 and 2852 upper extremities. In both subgropus, the most prevalent MGA was in Europe—25.10% (95% CI 17.13–33.99%, based on 727 specimens) and 38.93% (95% CI 0.00–100%, depend on 1906 specimens). The analysis of the Oh et al. classification of MGA was established, considering a total of 589 upper limbs (electrophysiological studies). The most prevalent was type II, communication innervating the first dorsal interosseus muscle, occurring in 58.01% (95% CI 47.92–67.78%). However, according to Rodríguez-Niedenführ et al. classification, based on 319 specimens (cadaveric & fetal studies), proved that, the most common was pattern I (single communication), which result in 96.07% (95% CI 92.13%—98.76%). To be more precise, I can be divided into 3 subgroups, the most typical was type Ic (communication arose from the anterior interosseous nerve) at 75.16% (95% CI 65.17%—83.97%). The mean MGA diameter was set at 7.94 mm (SE=1.28), and the length was 44.62 mm (SE=7.59). All results mentioned before and more detailed ones are gathered in Table 2 and Figs. 2, 3.

Berrettini anastomosis

The pooled prevalence of BA was 67.97% (95% CI 56.62%—78.35%) for cadaveric, and 29.07% (95% CI 24.06%—34.34%) for fetal studies, based on 2243 and 300 upper limbs. Divided by region, the BA was found most often in South America—87.72% (95% CI 65.36%—100%, with 162 specimens). Discussion

Embryological basis of upper limb innervation and anatomical variants

The nerve fibres extend into the myotomes at stage 13 of embryological development [176]. Following the vascularization by a single central artery, the nerves of spinal levels C5 to T1 connect and form a brachial plexus at stages 14–16 [7, 118, 176]. At stage 16, the brachial plexus differentiates, allowing for its short branches to be distinguished. Simultaneously, the median, ulnar and radial nerves enter into hand plate. Subsequently, as the forearm skeleton and musculature develop, the UN and MN separate into divisions innervating the forearm muscles. By stages 20–21, the pattern of upper limb innervation closely resembles that of the adult.

Incomplete separation and aberrant distribution of nerve fibers during embryological development are considered the basis of inter-nerve communications. The fibers of one peripheral nerve may transiently course with another nerve trunk and subsequently separate distally, thereby forming neural anastomoses [187]. Such connections most commonly occur between MCN and MN, MN and UN, or SBRN and LCNF and may be found in the arm, forearm and hand.

Anatomical variants of upper limb innervation may coexist with arterial variations. Antonopoulos et al. described a case of type I BA with concomitant incomplete superficial palmar arch [11]. In that report, the superficial palmar arch was formed solely by superficial branch of the ulnar artery, whereas the neural anastomosis linked the third and fourth common digital nerves. Furthermore, BA may exhibit a plexiform pattern, with the superficial palmar arch perforating the anastomotic network Neural anastomosis may also coexist. Nimma et al., in an electrophysiological study, reported a case of bilateral MGA accompanying reverse MGA (known also as MA) in the same patient Additionally, Natsis et al. depicted an atypical ulnar-median nerve communication connecting palmar cutaneous branch of the UN with the third common palmar digital nerve, coexisting with four distinct recurrent motor branches of the MN. Finally, Tsakotos et al. described a case of MMA coexisting with bilateral symmetric accessory coracobrachialis muscle. They implied that the impaired muscle development may contribute to abnormal innervation [143, 144, 180, 203].

Not a novel concept, but the most comprehensive data

The present study investigates in detail the CB between the nerves derived from the brachial plexus. It is not the only one to summarize neural anastomoses in the upper limb; however, it is based on the greatest sample size and offers the most rigorous analysis to date. Notably, the current research comprises 189 studies, evaluating not only MN and UN connections but also anastomoses involving the radial, musculocutaneous, and medial cutaneous nerves of the arm and forearm. Moreover, this is the first systematic review to analyze occuring of MGA, BA, and MMA in fetal subgroup.

The prevalence of anastomoses was established separately for the two study types. The first group consisted of cadaveric, and fetal studies, further categorized into morphological types, whereas the second group comprised electrophysiological studies. This stratified approach was adopted due to heterogeneity in the presence of intranervous connections when both groups were analyzed together. The median and ulnar nerve anastomoses have been previously assessed by Roy et al., based on 58 studies included in their analysis [167]. Both studies systematically searched several databases; the present study screened nine, in comparison to five assessed in the second paper.

Remarkably, the present study applied the AQUA guidelines to ensure a thorough and standardized assessment of the methodological quality of the included studies [200]. In contrast, the compared review did not employ any objective tool for quality appraisal. Furthermore, our search strategy incorporated broader search terms to enhance sensitivity. However, Roy et al. imposed no restrictions regarding language, date range, or article type and additionally screened reference lists to maximize the completeness of their results. Moreover, the present study involved a search for additional anastomoses.

The first anastomosis analyzed in both studies was MGA. In the present paper, its prevalence was higher in the morphological study types, reaching 20.74% (cadaveric studies) and 18.93% (fetal studies), and lower in the electrophysiological group at 16.65% (healthy subjects) and 20.37% (patients with carpal tunnel syndrom). Moreover, MGA presence was most frequent in North America regarding the former group. Roy et al. did not divide geographical distributions by study type and noted that North America was the most common region for MGA prevalence. The mean length of MGA was 44.62 mm 土 7.59 mm.

The least frequent anastomosis was the MA, known also as „reverse MGA”. Its prevalence was reported exclusively based on electrophysiological studies, reaching 2.04%, compared with 0.8% in the second review.

Another analysed variant was the connection between the ulnar and the median nerves on the palmar surface of the hand, known as BA. Roy et al. reported an overall prevalence of 60.9%, whereas in the present study, the BA was found in 67.97% in cadaveric studies, 29.07% in fetal studies and 11.88% in electrophysiological studies ones. For the classification of this anastomosis, the system proposed by Sulamain et al. was applied. Type I was the most common variant, accounting for 74.22% of cases in our study, which is comparable to the referenced study reporting such type prevalence of 86.2%. The mean length of the BA did not differ substantially between the studies, measuring approximately 19–20 mm in both. Finally, the prevalence of CRA observed in the present study was comparable to that reported by Roy et al. (50.98% vs. 55.5%). Similar discrepancies in connection frequency between morphological and electrophysiological studies were also noted.

In recent days, a new systematic review with meta-analysis [91] has been published addressing communications between the MN and UN in the forearm and hand. While this study provides valuable insights into the prevalence and clinical implications of MGA, its methodological approach appears to rely on relatively narrowly defined search terms. As a consequence, the number of included studies (20 out of 879 studies in contrast to 189 studies of 13 442 records in our meta-analysis) is limited, reflecting a highly restrictive selection process. Additionally, it should be noted that the time frame of database searching in the published study was similar to that applied in our analysis (September 2025). Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the prevalence of MGA was greater among healthy individuals (approximately 25% vs 16.65% in our meta-analysis) than in patients with carpal tunnel syndrome (around 8–10% vs 20.37% in our meta-analysis). Rajadurai et al. stated that compression of the median nerve interferes with the electrophysiological identification of MGA, and cadaver-based studies showed lower detection rates (approximately 6% vs 20.74%), reflecting methodological differences. Our findings do not reveal any such discrepancies.

Importantly, this is the first systematic review to analyze MMA and RLCA. The first anastomosis constitutes a connection between median and musculocutaneous nerves. Its pooled prevalence was relatively high and reached 23.90% (cadaveric studies) and 10.30% (fetal studies). The most common communication pattern was Type I, located proximal to the point of entry into the coracobrachial muscle. The second variant connects radial nerve with lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm. The pooled prevalence of RLCA, reported solely from cadaveric studies, amounted to 40.98%.

Neural anastomoses are less frequent in fetuses (MGA, BA, and MMA) than in cadaveric group, primarily due to the immaturity of the nervous system, as many connections develop and stabilize later during postnatal growth and functional maturation. Additionally, limited sensory and motor activity in utero, along with the absence of environmental and adaptive stimulations, reduces the formation and persistence of such connections compared to adults.

Clinical connotations

The upper limb nervous anastomoses are not indicators for intervention; their presence may pose a risk for atypical manifestations of musculoskeletal pathologies, such as carpal tunnel syndrome, neuropathies and cubital tunnel syndrome [182].

In regard to MGA, excluding this variant prior to establishing a diagnosis of ulnar neuropathy at the elbow is essential [61]. Maya-González et al. outlined the case of a 70-year-old patient with bilateral median and ulnar nerve involvement [133]. The patient presented with progressive pain and paresthesia in both hands over a 4-month period. Nerve conduction examination revealed the coexistence of a bilateral MGA and MA. Additionally, mildly prolonged distal sensory responses of both median nerves and absent sensory response of the right ulnar nerve to the little finger indicated bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome and ulnar neuropathy at Guyon’s canal. Recognition of these anatomical variants was essential for establishing the correct diagnosis.

Furthermore, with MGA presence, there is a risk of iatrogenic injury that may produce unexpected ulnar sensory deficits. Riechers et al. described a case in which, after repair of a ruptured biceps tendon, injury to ulnar sensory fibers resulted in paresthesia of the fourth and fifth digits despite preserved hand motor function [160].

Considering BA, it can alter the expected sensory distribution in the hand by allowing the UN to innervate digits typically attributed to the MN. As a consequence, median nerve injury may present with preserved sensation on the hand, and the results of a conduction trial can be misinterpreted, provided the anastomosis is not recognized. Wakode et al. reported a case of a patient who sustained a laceration of the median nerve [215]. However, the patient did not exhibit significant sensory deficits, as subsequent nerve conduction studies demonstrated BA, with ulnar sensory fibers supplying all digits except the thumb.

Lastly, Bölükbaşi et al. report a case of a patient with bilateral RCA [204]. Upon median nerve stimulation compound muscle potential from the abductor pollicis brevis and flexor pollicis muscles was noted from ulnar, rather than median nerve, with normal sensory responses, thereby diagnosis of RCA was stated. Additionally, the patient experienced dull pain in the neck region, with no radiation to upper extremities for five years. Indisputably, the cause of neck pain was cervical spondylosis, however the authors outline that the presence of an ulnar-median nerve anastomosis can lead to the misinterpretation of a complete median nerve lesion as partial.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of our study lies in the abundance of analyzed upper limbs’anasomoses, with regard to type of study, geographical region and morphological features, such as the mean length and diameter of neural connection. Furthermore, variant classifications were systematically reported. Overall, the present study provides a comprehensive analysis of upper limb innervation variants, adding innovative contributions to the current anatomical knowledge and providing valuable practical information for clinical practice. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant addressing. Firstly, substantial heterogeneity was observed among the included studies, reflected by the wide range of confidence intervals. Although electrophysiological studies were not combined with cadaveric, fetal and intraoperative ones, the heterogeneity persisted. This might have appeared due to use of various classification systems in the included studies or discrepancies in the electrophysiological procedures themselves. Secondly, different places of measurement of nerves’ length and diameter may have introduced bias. Finally, regarding classification systems, only a single system was chosen for each anastomosis. Incorporating more of them could provide additional detail; however, it would likely introduce more complexity, potentially reducing the clarity of the analysis.

Future directions

There is a need to unify various classification systems of the aforementioned anastomoses. For instance, the MGA has at least five categorization schemes, depending on the site of connection [31, 54], the number of branches forming the anastomosis [96], the muscle innervated [78] or the nerve of origin [167]. Consequently, no consensus has been reached, which may lead to misunderstandings and reluctance to apply these classifications in clinical practice. Furthermore, the coexistence of different anastomoses should be addressed in future studies. These connections are not uncommon and, as demonstrated in the case reports, they may occur in pairs. Original studies exploring this pattern would be beneficial for clinicians, as they could clarify the authentic prevalence of such configurations, improve anatomical awareness during diagnostic and surgical procedures, ultimately leading to reduced risk of misinterpretation or iatrogenic injury.

Conclusions

The nerve anastomoses exhibit a high degree of variability regarding its anatomy, especially their classification types. The prevalence of MGA was 20.74% (cadaveric studies), 18.93% (fetal studies), 16.65% (healthy subjects) and 20.37% (patients with carpal tunnel syndrom). The most prevalent was type II of the Oh et al. classification of MGA, communication innervating the first dorsal interosseus muscle, occurring in 58.01%, and pattern I (single communication) of Rodríguez-Niedenführ et al. classification, which resulted in 96.07%. The BA was found in 67.97% (cadaveric studies), 29.07% (fetal studies) and 11.88% (electrophysiological studies). Based on BA classification—the most common was type I of the Sulamain et al. classification—74.62%. The pooled prevalence of MMA was 23.90% (cadaveric studies) and 10.30% (fetal studies), with type I—communications proximal to the point of entry of the MCN into the coracobrachialis, at 80.76%, based on Loukas et al. classification. CRA was established at 50.98% (cadaveric studies), and 62.68% (electrophysiological studies), nevertheless the pooled prevalence of MA was 2.04%.
