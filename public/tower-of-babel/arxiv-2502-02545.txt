Optimal Spectral Transitions in High-Dimensional Multi-Index Models
Leonardo Defilippis, Yatin Dandi, Pierre Mergny, Florent Krzakala, Bruno Loureiro

Abstract
We consider the problem of how many samples from a Gaussian multi-index model are required to weakly reconstruct the relevant index subspace. Despite its increasing popularity as a testbed for investigating the computational complexity of neural networks, results beyond the single-index setting remain elusive. In this work, we introduce spectral algorithms based on the linearization of a message passing scheme tailored to this problem. Our main contribution is to show that the proposed methods achieve the optimal reconstruction threshold. Leveraging a high-dimensional characterization of the algorithms, we show that above the critical threshold the leading eigenvector correlates with the relevant index subspace, a phenomenon reminiscent of the Baik–Ben Arous–Peche (BBP) transition in spiked models arising in random matrix theory. Supported by numerical experiments and a rigorous theoretical framework, our work bridges critical gaps in the computational limits of weak learnability in multi-index model.

Introduction

A popular model to study learning problems in statistics, computer science and machine learning is the
multi-index model
, where the target function depends on a low-dimensional subspace of the covariates. In this problem, one aims at identifying the
p
-dimensional linear subspace spanned by a family of orthonormal vectors
{\bm{w}}_{\star,1},\dots,{\bm{w}}_{\star,p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from
n
independent observations
({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})
from the model:

\displaystyle y_{i}=g\left(\langle{\bm{w}}_{\star,1},{\bm{x}}_{i}\rangle,\dots,\langle{\bm{w}}_{\star,p},{\bm{x}}_{i}\rangle\right),\qquad i\in\llbracket n\rrbracket.

(1)

This formulation encompasses several fundamental problems in machine learning, signal processing, and theoretical computer science, including:
(i) Linear estimation, where
p=1
and
g(z)=z
, (ii) Phase retrieval, where
p=1
and
g(z)=|z|
.
(iii) Learning Two-layer neural networks, where
p
is the width and
{g({\bm{z}})=\sum_{j\in\llbracket p\rrbracket}a_{j}\sigma(z_{j})}
for some non-linear activation function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, or (iv) learning Sparse parity functions, where
g({\bm{z}})=\text{sign}(\prod_{j\in\llbracket p\rrbracket}z_{j})
.

A classical problem in statistics
(
Friedman and Stuetzle, 1981
;
Yuan, 2011
;
Babichev and Bach, 2018
)
, the multi-index model has recently gained in popularity in the machine learning community as a generative model for supervised learning data where the labels only depend on an underlying low-dimensional latent subspace of the covariates
(
Aubin et al., 2018
;
Damian et al., 2022
;
Dandi et al., 2024a
)
.

Of particular interest to this work are spectral methods, which play a fundamental role in machine learning by offering an efficient and computationally tractable approach to extracting meaningful structure from high-dimensional noisy data. A paradigmatic example is the Baik–Ben Arous–Péché (BBP) transition
(
Baik et al., 2005
)
, where the leading eigenvalue of a matrix correlates with the hidden signal — a phenomenon that is ubiquitous in machine learning theory. Beyond their practical utility, spectral methods often serve as a starting point for more advanced approaches, including iterative and nonlinear techniques. This leads us to the central question of this paper:

Can one design optimal spectral methods that minimizes the amount of data required for identifying the hidden subspace in multi-index models?

While the optimal spectral method for single-index models are well understood
(
Luo et al., 2019
;
Mondelli and Montanari, 2018
;
Maillard et al., 2022
;
Mondelli et al., 2022
)
, and their optimality in terms of weak recovery threshold established
(
Barbier et al., 2019
;
Mondelli and Montanari, 2018
)
their counterparts for multi-index models remain largely unexplored. This gap is particularly important, as multi-index models serve as a natural testbed for studying the computational complexity of feature learning in modern machine learning, and have attracted much attention recently
(
Abbe et al., 2023
;
Damian et al., 2022
;
Dandi et al., 2024a
;
Arnaboldi et al., 2023
;
Collins-Woodfin et al., 2024
;
Berthier et al., 2024
;
Simsek et al., 2024
)
.

Main contributions —

In this work, we step up to this challenge by constructing optimal spectral methods for multi-index models. We introduce and analyze spectral algorithms based on a linearized message-passing framework, specifically tailored to this setting. Our main contribution is to present two such constructions, establish the reconstruction threshold for these methods and to show that they achieve the provably optimal threshold for weak recovery among efficient algorithms
(
Troiani et al., 2025
)
.

Other Related works –

Recently, multi-index models have become a proxy model for studying non-convex optimization

Relevant to this work is the class of approximate message passing (AMP) algorithms, which have garnered significant interest in the high-dimensional statistics and machine learning communities over the past decade

. AMP for multi-index models was discussed in

. Spectral initialization for AMP in the context of single-index models has been studied by

The interplay between AMP and spectral methods has been extensively studied in the literature, see for example

;

;

;

)
.

During the finalization of this work, an independent paper analysing a family of spectral estimators indexed by a pre-processing function

5
Conclusion and Perspectives

In this work, we tackled weak recovery in high-dimensional multi-index models via spectral methods, deriving two estimators inspired by a linearization of AMP. We showed they achieve the optimal reconstruction threshold, closing a key gap in prior approaches that required additional side information. Our analysis establishes that above the critical sample complexity, the leading eigenvectors of the proposed spectral operators align with the ground-truth subspace, echoing the BBP transition in random matrix theory. This work advances our understanding of weak subspace recovery in multi-index models and provides a principled framework for designing optimal spectral estimators. It bridges ideas from random matrix theory, approximate message passing, and neural feature learning.

Several directions remain open. A random matrix theory analysis of our spectral analysis — which requires a challenging control of the spectral norms — could be used to prove the two conjectures. Extending our AMP linearization to higher-order schemes, such as the Kikuchi hierarchy, may unlock insights into harder generative exponent problems, including the notorious sparse parity function. We hope this work sparks further research at the intersection of spectral methods and high-dimensional inference.

{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p}\setminus\{{\bm{0}}\}
. Moreover, for such
a
, the largest eigenvalue of
{\mathcal{F}}(\;\cdot\;;a,{\bm{V}})
is larger than
\alpha^{-1}
, hence the uninformed fixed point is unstable for
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
. This can be shown repeating a similar argument as the one we have applied in eq. (
91
). Since the GAMP convergence equations correspond to a generalized power iteration of
\bm{T}
, normalized by the eigenvalue
a\gamma
, the instability of the uninformed fixed point implies that it is associated to an eigenvector smaller than
\lambda_{s}
defined in Theorem
3.8
.

C.2
Sketch of derivation for Conjecture
3.10

These results for the fixed points of the state evolution justify Conjecture
3.10
on the weak recovery properties of the symmetric spectral estimator. The following argument is analogous to the one given for the asymmetric spectral method given in Appendix
D
.
As a consequence of eq.
71
, the Algorithm in Definition
3.6
behaves as the power-iteration on the matrix
\bm{T}
.
Suppose that
\lambda_{1}(\bm{T})-\lambda_{2}(\bm{T})=\omega(d^{-\kappa})
for some
\kappa>0
. Then, we obtain that
{\bm{w}}^{t}=(a\gamma)^{-1}{\bm{T}}{\bm{w}}^{t-1}
converges to the top-most eigenvector of
\frac{1}{a\gamma}\bm{T}
in
\mathcal{O}(\log d)
iterations.

Moreover, based on extensive
confirmations in the literature
[
Rush and Venkataramanan, 2018
,
Li and Wei, 2022
,
Li et al., 2023
]
, we conjecture that the asymetric spectral method is described by the state-evolution equations up to
\mathcal{O}(\log d)
iterations.

Theorem
3.8
predicts the convergence of the state evolution iterate
\bm{M}^{t}
to a fixed point corresponding to
\bm{M}\neq 0
for
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
. Since the fixed point conditions for the algorithm stipulate that
{\bm{w}}^{t}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{s}}\bm{T}{\bm{w}}^{t}
, we obtain under the validity of the state-evolution description:

\frac{1}{d}\norm{\bw^t - \frac{1}{\lambda_s} \mat{T} \bw^t}\xrightarrow[P]{d\rightarrow\infty}0,

(93)

and consequently

\frac{1}{\norm{\bw^t}^{2}}{\bm{w}}^{t}\bm{L}{\bm{w}}^{t}\xrightarrow[P]{d\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{s}.

(94)

On the other hand, the equivalence to power-iteration implies that the LHS must converge to the top-most eigenvalue of
\bm{T}
. We thus conclude that:

\lambda_{1}(\bm{T})\xrightarrow[P]{d\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{s}.

(95)

Random Matrix Theory analysis for the case where

(y)

In this section, we will consider the setting of Thm.

simplified in that case and then sketch the outline of the proof using random matrix theory tools in Sec.

C.3.1
Introduction to the model

For
l\in\llbracket p\rrbracket
we denote by
{\mathrm{z}}_{l}=\lambda_{l}({\mathrm{y}})/(\lambda_{l}({\mathrm{y}})+1)
where
{\mathrm{y}}\sim\mathsf{Z}
and for
y\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mathsf{Z})
,
\lambda_{l}(y)\equiv\lambda_{l}({\bm{G}}(y))
is the
l
-th eigenvalue of
{\bm{G}}(y)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\bm{I}_{p})}[\bm{\mathbf{z}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{T}-{\bm{I}}|{\mathrm{y}}=y]
. Given the sample
({\mathrm{x}},{\mathrm{y}}_{i})_{i\in\llbracket n\rrbracket}
, we let
{\bm{D}}_{l}=\mathrm{Diag}(\{\lambda_{l}({\mathrm{y}}_{i})(\lambda_{l}({\mathrm{y}}_{i})+1)^{-1}\}_{i\in\llbracket n\rrbracket})
for
l\in\llbracket p\rrbracket
. In the rest of this section we will restrict to a particular setting for the model introduced in the main text. We first recall the definition.

Definition C.1

(Jointly diagonalizable)
.

Let
\bm{M}(.):\mathbb{R}\supset I\ni t\mapsto\bm{M}(t)
be a symmetric matrix-valued function. We say that
\bm{M}(.)
is
jointly diagonalizable
if for all
t\in I
, we have
\bm{M}(t)={\bm{U}}\bm{\Lambda}(t){\bm{U}}^{T}
, with
\bm{\Lambda}(t)
diagonal and the orthogonal matrix
{\bm{U}}
is constant with respect to
t
.

We will restrict to a subclass of our model such that one has

Assumption C.2
.

{\bm{G}}(.):\mathrm{Supp}(\mathsf{Z})\ni y\mapsto{\bm{G}}(y)
is jointly diagonalizable.

Note that this subset of problems includes many cases of interest, including the ones considered in this manuscript, such as the monomial
g({\bm{z}})=\prod_{k\in\llbracket p\rrbracket}z_{k}
, the norm
g({\bm{z}})=p^{-1}\norm{\bz}^{2}
and the embedded sparse parity
g(z_{1},z_{2})=\operatorname{sign}(z_{1},z_{2})
.

By rotationally invariance of the hidden directions (
{\bm{W}}_{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}{\bm{O}}{\bm{W}}_{\star}
for any orthogonal matrix
\bm{O}
) two jointly diagonalizable multi-index models specified by the conditional distributions
\mathsf{P}(\cdot|{\bm{z}})
and
\mathsf{P}_{{\bm{O}}}(\cdot|{\bm{z}}):=\mathsf{P}(\cdot|{\bm{O}}{\bm{z}})
are equivalent up to a change basis and in particular share the same
\alpha_{c}
. Indeed with

\mathsf{Z}_{{\bm{O}}}(y):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\bm{I}_{p})}\mathsf{P}_{{\bm{O}}}(y|{\bm{z}})
one can immediately check that
\mathsf{Z}_{{\bm{O}}}(y)=\mathsf{Z}(y)
and

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\bm{I}_{p})}[\bm{\mathbf{z}}\mathsf{P}_{{\bm{O}}}(y|\bm{\mathbf{z}})]={\bm{O}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\bm{I}_{p})}[\bm{\mathbf{z}}{\mathsf{P}}(y|\bm{\mathbf{z}})]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}[\bm{\mathbf{z}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{T}\mathsf{P}_{{\bm{O}}}(y|\bm{\mathbf{z}})]={\bm{O}}^{T}\mathrm{Cov}[\bm{\mathbf{z}}\big|y]{\bm{O}}
. As a consequence, we can set
{\bm{G}}(y)
to be diagonal without loss of generality.

Next, we describe how the constants in Conjecture
3.10
simplifies under this jointly diagonalizable setting. To this end, we introduce the convex functions

\displaystyle\psi_{\alpha,l}(a)=a\left(1+\alpha\,\mathbb{E}_{{\mathrm{z}}_{l}}\frac{{\mathrm{z}}_{l}}{a-{\mathrm{z}}_{l}}\right)\quad l\in\llbracket p\rrbracket\,,

(96)

and let
\overline{a}_{\alpha,l}=\argmin\psi_{\alpha,l}(a)
be the minima, that is
\overline{a}_{\alpha,l}
solves

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{{\mathrm{z}}_{l}}{\overline{a}_{\alpha,l}-{\mathrm{z}}_{l}}\right)^{2}\right]=\alpha^{-1}\quad l\in\llbracket p\rrbracket\,.

(97)

For later use, we also define

\displaystyle\zeta_{\alpha,l}(a)=\psi_{\alpha,l}\big(\max(a,\overline{a}_{\alpha,l})\big)\,\quad l\in\llbracket p\rrbracket\,.

(98)

we have the following result.

Lemma C.3
.

Under Assumption
C.2
, the constants
(\alpha_{c},\lambda_{b},\lambda_{c})
in Conjecture
3.10
further simplifies to

(i)

\alpha_{c}=\min_{l\in\llbracket p\rrbracket}1/(\mathbb{E}\lambda_{l}({\mathrm{y}})^{2})
;

(ii)

\lambda_{s}=1
,

(iii)

\lambda_{b}=\max_{l\in\llbracket p\rrbracket}\psi_{\alpha,l}(\overline{a}_{\alpha,l})

Proof.

For Part-(i) since
{\bm{G}}(y)
is diagonal, the critical threshold described in Lem.
2.2
is maximized over rank-one matrices, that is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{c}}=\sup_{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathrm{y}}\sim\mathsf{Z}}\langle\bm{u},\mathrm{Diag}\big(\{\lambda_{l}({\mathrm{y}})^{2}\}_{l\in\llbracket p\rrbracket}\big)\bm{u}\rangle\,,

(99)

which gives by Courant-Fisher theorem the desired result for the threshold.

We next turn to the analysis of SE of Prop.
3.2
under the jointly diagonalizable assumption
C.2

\mathcal{T}_{l}({\mathrm{y}}):=\lambda_{l}({\mathrm{y}})(\lambda_{l}({\mathrm{y}})+1)^{-1}

\displaystyle M_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\alpha M_{\mu\nu}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathrm{y}}}\left[{\mathcal{T}}_{\mu}(a/V_{\mu}^{2}-{\mathcal{T}}_{\mu}({\mathrm{y}}))^{-1}\lambda_{\nu}({\mathrm{y}})\right]

(100)

\displaystyle Q_{\mu\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu\in\llbracket p\rrbracket}M_{\mu\nu}^{2}+\alpha\left({\mathcal{G}}_{\mu\mu}({\bm{M}};a,{\bm{V}})+Q_{\mu\mu}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathrm{y}}}\left[\left({\mathcal{T}}_{\mu}({\mathrm{y}})(a/V_{\mu}^{2}-{\mathcal{T}}_{\mu}({\mathrm{y}}))^{-1}\right)^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\gamma V_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle=1+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{{\mathrm{y}}}\left[{\mathcal{T}}_{\mu}({\mathrm{y}})(a/V_{\mu}^{2}-{\mathcal{T}}_{\mu}({\mathrm{y}}))^{-1}\right]

Note that, for

\displaystyle=1

C.3.2
Outline of the proof using RMT

We give a proof of Conjecture
3.10
with the values for the threshold
\alpha_{c}
, the top outlier
\lambda_{s}=1
and the edge
\lambda_{b}
computed in Lem.
C.3
.

Assuming
C.2
, the symmetric spectral estimator introduced in Eq. (
13
) has the block-diagonal structure

{\bm{T}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\bm{D}_{1}\bm{\mathbf{X}}&\bm{0}_{d\times d}&\dots&\bm{0}_{d\times d}\\
\bm{0}_{d\times d}&\bm{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\bm{D}_{2}\bm{\mathbf{X}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\bm{0}_{d\times d}\\
\bm{0}_{d\times d}&\dots&\bm{0}_{d\times d}&\bm{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\bm{D}_{p}\bm{\mathbf{X}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(116)

and thus its spectral properties can be immediately obtained from its diagonal block since we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Spec}(\bm{T})=\bigcup_{l\in\llbracket p\rrbracket}\mathrm{Spec}(\bm{\tilde{T}}_{l})\qquad\mbox{where}\quad\bm{\tilde{T}}_{l}:=\bm{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\bm{D}_{l}\bm{\mathbf{X}}\,.

(117)

In particular, the top eigenvalue of
\bm{T}
appearing in Conjecture
3.10
is simply the max of the top eigenvalue of each block
\bm{\tilde{T}}_{l}
and one can restrict to the study of the spectral properties of the
\bm{\tilde{T}}_{l}
, following closely the derivation of
Lu and Li [2019]
which tackles the case
p=1
.

For each
\bm{\tilde{T}}_{l}
, we will partition the column of the sensing vector
\bm{x}_{i}
into parts that align with the hidden components
\bm{w}_{\star}
and part that lives in the orthogonal complement, the only difference with the setting considered in
Lu and Li [2019]
being in that now one has to deal with
p
hidden directions instead of one. To do so, we first use the Gram-Schmidt decomposition of the hidden matrix
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\bm{W}_{\star}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\bm{W}_{\star}=\bm{V}\bm{R}

(118)

where
\bm{V}=(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{p})
is a semi-orthogonal matrix of dimension
(d\times p)
and
R_{ij}=\langle\bm{u}_{i},\bm{w}_{j})\delta_{i\leq j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is upper triangular. Note that as
\bm{w}_{i,\star}
are iid standard Gaussian, as
d\to\infty
we have
R_{ii}\to 1
and
R_{ij}\to 0
for
i\neq j
, exponentially fast. By rotationally invariance one has
\bm{\mathbf{X}}\overset{(d)}{=}\bm{O}\bm{\mathbf{X}}
for any
\bm{O}
orthogonal matrix, hence one can fix
\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{e}_{i}
(the
i
-th canonical vector) without loss of generality. We decompose each vectors
\bm{x}_{i}
in this basis as follows

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=(\bm{\kappa}_{i}\quad\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i})^{T}\;\mbox{with}\,\bm{\kappa}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\,\mbox{and}\,\bm{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-p}\quad

(119)

or equivalently

\displaystyle\quad\bm{\mathbf{X}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{K}\\
\bm{\mathbf{U}}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\mbox{with}\,\bm{K}:=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\kappa}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\kappa}_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}\,\mbox{and}\,\bm{\mathbf{U}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d-p)\times n}\,

(120)

where
\bm{K}
is by construction a matrix with iid Gaussian entries. From this partition, we can re-write each
\bm{\tilde{T}}_{l}
as

\displaystyle\bm{\tilde{T}}_{l}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{R}_{l}&\bm{Q}_{l}^{T}\\
\bm{Q}_{l}&\bm{\mathbf{P}}_{l}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\begin{cases}\bm{R}_{l}&:=\frac{1}{d}\bm{K}^{T}\bm{D}_{l}\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},\\
\bm{Q}_{l}&:=\frac{1}{d}\bm{\mathbf{U}}(\bm{D}_{l}\bm{K}^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{(d-p)\times p},\\
\bm{\mathbf{P}}_{l}&:=\frac{1}{d}\bm{\mathbf{U}}^{T}\bm{D}_{l}\bm{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d-p)\times(d-p)}\,.\end{cases}

(121)

Lemma C.4

(Edge and existence of outliers)
.

As
d\to\infty
, the empirical spectral distribution of
\bm{T}_{l}
converges weakly almost surely to a distribution
\mu_{l}
with rightmost edge
\tau_{l}:=\psi_{\alpha,l}(\overline{a}_{\alpha,l})
. Furthermore, for each
l\in\llbracket p\rrbracket
, there exists up to
p
outliers in the spectrum of
\bm{T}_{l}
above this edge
\tau_{l}
.

Proof.

This follows from the same proof of Proposition 3.1 in
Lu and Li [2019]
(see Appendix A.4): by eigenvalue interlacing theorem, if put the eigenvalues in increasing order, we have for any
i\in\llbracket(d-p)p\rrbracket
:
\lambda_{i}^{(\nearrow)}(\bm{\tilde{T}}_{l})\leq\lambda_{i}^{(\nearrow)}(\bm{P}_{l})\leq\lambda^{(\nearrow)}_{i+p}(\bm{\tilde{T}}_{l})
. Furthermore the empirical distribution of
\bm{P}_{l}
converges
strongly
to a limiting distribution
\mu_{l}
with rightmost edge
\tau_{l}
, since all but the top
p
eigenvalues of
\bm{T}_{l}
are trapped between eigenvalues of
\bm{P}_{l}
, one gets the desired result.
∎

From Eq. (
117

\bm{T}
converges weakly almost surely to the distribution
\mu:=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{l}\mu_{l}
whose rightmost edge is given by
\lambda_{b}
in Lem.
C.3
. Next, following again
Lu and Li [2019]
, we map the position of the top outliers in each block

to an additive spiked matrix model.

Lemma C.5
.

Let
\bm{\tilde{P}}_{l}(\mu)=\bm{P}_{l}+\bm{Q}_{l}(\bm{R}_{l}-\mu\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{Q}_{l}^{T}
denotes the family of spiked matrices indexed by a parameter

This follows from the determinant lemma in the same proof of Proposition 3.1 of

(see Section 3.2) with their

\bm{R}_{l}
.
∎

\bm{\tilde{P}}_{l}(\mu)
