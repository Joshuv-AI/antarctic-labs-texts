Synergistic enhancement of recycled aggregate concrete using cement slurry-treated aggregates and graphite nano/micro platelets
Authors: Suniti Suparp, Burachat Chatveera, Panumas Saingam, Muhammad Fahad, Rana Faisal Tufail, Adnan Nawaz, Preeda Chaimawhan, Qudeer Hussain, Afaq Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of recycled coarse aggregates (RCA) in concrete is an effective strategy for reducing construction and demolition waste; however, their inferior quality often leads to reduced mechanical and durability performance. This study investigates the synergistic effects of cement slurry-treated RCA and graphite nano/micro platelets (GNMPs) on the fresh, mechanical, durability, and microstructural properties of concrete. RCA replacement levels of 0%, 30%, 60%, and 100% were combined with GNMP dosages of 0.1%, 0.3%, and 0.5% by weight of cement. Results indicate that untreated 100% RCA reduced compressive strength and increased water absorption by more than 25–30% compared to natural aggregate concrete, whereas cement slurry treatment significantly improved aggregate quality and partially restored performance. The incorporation of GNMPs led to compressive strength increases of up to ~ 20–25%, along with corresponding improvements in split tensile and flexural strengths. Durability performance was also enhanced, with reductions in water absorption (~ 15–20%), sorptivity (~ 15–20%), and chloride ion permeability (shift from “low” to “very low” category). Freeze–thaw resistance improved significantly, with mass loss reduced by up to ~ 30–40% in GNMP-modified mixes. Mixes containing 30% RCA and 0.5% GNMPs exhibited the most balanced performance, achieving strength and durability comparable to or exceeding conventional concrete, while maintaining reduced permeability and refined microstructure. Microstructural analysis confirmed improved matrix densification and interfacial transition zone (ITZ) characteristics due to the combined action of slurry-treated RCA and GNMPs. The findings demonstrate that the synergistic use of treated RCA and GNMPs provides an effective pathway for producing high-performance and sustainable concrete suitable for structural applications.Keywords: Recycled aggregate concrete, Graphite nano/micro platelets, Cement slurry treatment, Durability properties, Microstructural properties, Sustainable materials, Buildings, Sustainable Development, Green InfrastructureSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Annual production of concrete has exceeded 10 billion tons making it the most widely used man-made material. Concrete demand is increasing manifolds due to continued urbanization and infrastructure expansion1,2. In order to meet the concrete demand, natural aggregates (NA) extraction is extensively required because 70–80% volume of concrete is covered by NA. Extraction of virgin aggregates is estimated at tens of billions of tons per year which is directly linked to habitat degradation, resource depletion, and landscape disturbance3. At the same time, the construction sector generates huge volumes of construction and demolition waste (C&DW) each year. Major part of this C&DW consists of concrete rubble which is frequently dumped in an uncontrolled manner. These issues highlight the urgency of developing sustainable concrete technologies that simultaneously conserve NA and divert C&DW from disposal4.

Although recycled aggregate concrete (RAC) offers clear environmental advantages but its widespread structural application remains limited due to its inferior mechanical and durability performance as compared to natural aggregate concrete (NAC)5–7. Environmental assessments have shown that the partial replacement of NA with recycled coarse aggregates (RCA) can reduce NA consumption, landfill dumping, and carbon footprint of concrete production. RCA typically contains adhered mortar, leading to higher porosity and water absorption (often 2–3 times higher than natural aggregates), along with lower density. As a result, recycled aggregate concrete (RAC) commonly exhibits reductions in compressive strength of approximately 10–30% and increases in water absorption and permeability of up to 20–50% compared to natural aggregate concrete, depending on replacement level and aggregate quality. These deficiencies are primarily associated with a weaker interfacial transition zone (ITZ) and increased pore connectivity, which adversely affect both mechanical performance and long-term durability8,9. As a result, RAC frequently shows reduced compressive and flexural strengths and higher transport coefficients, which in turn translate into poorer long‑term durability, especially under chloride exposure, freeze–thaw cycles, or other aggressive conditions. Addressing these material‑level weaknesses is therefore one of the main obstacles that must be overcome before RAC can be widely accepted for structural applications10,11.

Numerous pre-treatment techniques have been developed to improve RCA quality before its utilization for concrete production12–16. Mechanical abrasion, natural or forced washing, and thermal or chemical treatments have been shown to remove part of the adhered mortar and contaminants, thereby reducing water absorption and enhancing density17,18. More recently, slurry-coating methods using cementitious or pozzolanic suspensions have attracted attention because they can both fill microcracks and pores in the old mortar and strengthen the aggregate surface19,20. Studies on cement or pozzolan slurries report notable gains in compressive strength, along with measurable reductions in chloride penetration of RAC, particularly when high RCA replacement levels are combined with optimized soaking time and slurry composition21–23. Nevertheless, present work still indicates that aggregate treatment alone may not fully restore RAC performance to that of comparable natural aggregate concrete, especially with respect to permeability-controlled durability indicators24,25.

In parallel, the incorporation of nano- and micro-scale carbon-based additives has emerged as an effective strategy for refining the microstructure of cementitious composites26–28. Graphite nano/micro platelets (GNMPs) are graphene‑based particles with a thin, flake‑like geometry and high aspect ratio that have emerged as versatile nano‑scale reinforcements for cementitious materials29,30. When properly dispersed, GNMPs can act as nucleation sites for hydration products, fill nano scale voids, bridge microcracks, leading to denser matrices, improved ITZ quality, and enhanced mechanical and durability properties31. Experimental studies on conventional and self-compacting concretes have reported significant improvements in compressive, tensile, and flexural strengths, as well as reductions in water absorption and freeze–thaw damage, for mixes containing optimally dosed GNMPs32–34. However, these benefits are highly sensitive to dosage and dispersion technique; excessive GNMP contents tend to promote particle agglomeration, generate weak planes, and ultimately reduce performance35–37.

Despite growing evidence on RCA pre-treatment and GNMP-modified concretes, existing literature largely considers these approaches in isolation. Most RAC studies focus on upgrading RCA through mechanical, chemical, or slurry-based treatments and then assess strength and durability relative to untreated RAC or NAC38,39. Conversely, investigations on GNMPs typically employ NA and do not account for the more porous and heterogeneous substrate presented by RA40. As a result, relatively limited information is available on the combined or synergistic action of treated RCA and GNMPs in a single RAC system, particularly in terms of how a slurry-enhanced aggregate skeleton interacts with a nano-reinforced cement matrix. Systematic experimental data addressing mechanical behavior, transport properties, freeze–thaw response, and microstructural evolution of RAC with both cement slurry-treated RCA and GNMPs remain scarce, and optimal GNMP dosage ranges for such composite systems are not yet well established. Previous studies38,39 have reported that cementitious or pozzolanic slurry treatments can improve compressive strength of RAC by approximately 10–20% and reduce chloride permeability by 15–30%, depending on treatment conditions and RCA quality. However, even with such improvements, the performance of treated RAC often remains lower than that of natural aggregate concrete, particularly at high replacement levels.

The present study is designed to address this gap by investigating the synergistic enhancement of RAC through the dual strategy of cement slurry treatment of RCA and GNMP incorporation in the cement matrix. An extensive experimental program is undertaken involving 16 concrete mixes with varying RCA replacement levels and GNMP dosages, where RCA were pre-treated by washing or cement slurry coating during the screening phase, and cement slurry-treated RCA were subsequently used in GNMP-modified mixes. During this study, each mix was evaluated for fresh and hardened concrete response along with durability and microstructural analysis. Durability was assessed through water absorption, sorptivity, chloride ion penetration, freeze–thaw, and rate of water absorption. Microstructural investigation was conducted on optimum and control mix to study the effects of slurry‑treated RCA and GNMPs on internal pore structure and ITZ densification.

This study aims to identify a concrete mix which utilizes RCA and attains performance comparable to conventional concrete. Findings of this study are expected to contribute to understanding the effects of pre-treatment techniques on RCA and interaction with GNMPs in a concrete mix. The results are expected to provide guidance on optimum dosage of GNMPs and RCA content for modified mix that supports sustainable construction practices.

Experimental program

Materials

Ordinary Portland cement (OPC) in accordance to ASTM C150 Type I41 was used as binder in this study. The chemical composition and loss on ignition values of OPC complied with the limits specified in ASTM C150 as shown in Table 1. Natural coarse aggregates (NCA) were crushed stone from a nearby quarry, with a nominal maximum size of 20 mm and a grading envelope compatible with ASTM C3342. Recycled coarse aggregates (RCA) were obtained by crushing concrete debris from a local C&DW site which was crushed and screened to match the size range of the NCA. The particle size distribution curves of the fine aggregates (FA), RCA, and NCA are presented in Fig. 1. The key physical properties of NCA and RCA, including specific gravity, water absorption, and bulk density, are listed in Table 2. Compared to NCA, RCA showed lower specific gravity and higher water absorption, primarily due to the presence of adhered mortar and increased porosity. River sand was used as FA having maximum particle size of 4.75 mm and grading falling within Zone II of ASTM C33 limits. The sand satisfied the cleanliness and silt content requirements specified in the relevant standards, and its physical properties are also reported in Table 2. Potable tap water free from deleterious impurities was used for both concrete mixing and curing, in accordance with ASTM C160243.

GNMPs were incorporated as a nano-scale additive within the cement matrix. The GNMPs were supplied in dry powder form with a flake-like platelet morphology, average lateral dimensions in the micro-scale, and thickness in the nano- to submicron range, similar to materials used in recent GNMP-modified concrete studies29,31. For dispersion, GNMPs were initially mixed into an aqueous solution containing acacia gum and subsequently introduced into the concrete during batching. Acacia gum was used as a dispersing agent to assist GNMP distribution during mixing, following procedures reported in previous nanomaterial–cement composite research. Acacia gum was selected as a dispersing agent due to its ability to act as a natural surfactant and stabilizing agent for carbon-based nano/micro materials in aqueous suspensions. Previous studies44 have shown that biopolymer-based dispersants can effectively reduce particle agglomeration and improve the distribution of nanomaterials in cementitious matrices by providing steric stabilization. The use of acacia gum in this study was intended to facilitate uniform dispersion of GNMPs during mixing and to minimize clustering effects that could otherwise adversely affect mechanical and durability performance. Similar approaches have been reported in nanomaterial-modified cement composites, where proper dispersion is critical for achieving performance enhancement.

Recycled aggregate pre-treatment methods

RCA were subjected to pre-treatment prior to concrete production in order to modify their surface condition and reduce the influence of adhered old mortar. Two treatment approaches were adopted, namely washing treatment and cement slurry treatment. These treatments aimed to reduce surface contaminants, partially remove adhered mortar, and improve the quality of the aggregate–paste interface in recycled aggregate concrete. Untreated RCAs were also retained for comparison. All treated aggregates were brought to a saturated surface-dry condition before use in concrete mixing.

Washing treatment of RCA

For the washing treatment, RCA obtained from the demolition source was first air-dried to a surface-dry condition and then thoroughly washed with clean water to remove loose particles, dust, and weakly adhered mortar. The aggregates were placed in a washing container, repeatedly agitated under a continuous flow of water, and then drained until the effluent became visibly clear, indicating the removal of suspended fines and surface impurities. Following washing, the aggregates were spread in a single layer and allowed to dry under ambient laboratory conditions to achieve a saturated-surface-dry (SSD) or near-SSD state before batching. This treatment was intended to improve the cleanliness and surface condition of RCA without significantly altering its internal pore structure, and it served as a baseline pre-treatment against which the cement slurry coating method could be compared. Washing treatment served as a baseline pre-treatment against which the cement slurry coating method was compared. A visual representation of the water washing process is provided in Fig. 2.

Pretreatment of RCA by water washing.

Cement slurry treatment of RCA

For the cement slurry treatment, RCA was first pre-wetted and then immersed in a freshly prepared cement slurry to coat the aggregate surfaces with a thin cementitious layer. The slurry was produced by mixing OPC and water at a specified water-to-cement ratio to obtain a fluid, uniform suspension capable of penetrating surface pores and microcracks in the adhered mortar. The RCA was added to the slurry container and gently stirred to ensure uniform coating of all particles, and the aggregates were kept in suspension for a predetermined contact period to promote adequate slurry adherence. After coating, the slurry-treated aggregates were removed from the container, allowed to drain, and then spread on a flat surface for drying under laboratory conditions until a stable, non-tacky surface was obtained. This process resulted in the formation of a new cementitious shell around the RCA, resulting in the formation of a cementitious layer around the RCA particles. The main steps of the cement slurry coating procedure are shown in Fig. 3. The two pre-treatment methods were subsequently evaluated in Phase I of the experimental program by comparing the mechanical performance of concrete mixes made with untreated RCA, water-washed RCA, and cement slurry-coated RCA against NAC.

Specimen preparation and strengthening procedure

The concrete mixtures were designed to investigate the combined influence of RCA pre-treatment and GNMPs on the mechanical and durability performance of RAC. A constant water-to-binder ratio of 0.45 was adopted for all mixes, and the total binder content and aggregate gradings were kept identical across the experimental program to isolate the effects of RCA replacement level, pre-treatment method, and GNMP dosage. The experimental program was organized into sequential phases and groups, as summarized in Table 3. In Phase 1, four mixes were prepared to assess the influence of RCA pre-treatment alone: a natural aggregate control mix (NAC1-Control), a mix with 100% untreated RCA (UT100RCA0G), a mix with 100% water-washed RCA (WT100RCA0G), and a mix with 100% cement slurry-coated RCA (CST100RCA0G). All Phase 1 mixes contained 0% GNMPs and 0 kg/m³ acacia gum, enabling a direct comparison of mechanical performance as a function of aggregate condition only. Phase 2 focused on the combined effect of GNMP dosage and RCA replacement level using cement slurry-coated RCA as the recycled aggregate type. A natural aggregate control without GNMPs (NAC2-Control) was first established, followed by mixes in which GNMPs were added at 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5% by weight of cement while keeping the coarse aggregate as 100% natural (RCA0G0.1, RCA0G0.3, RCA0G0.5).

Test methods

Fresh properties were evaluated using the slump test in accordance with ASTM C14345 to assess workability and consistency of all mixes. Slump measurements were taken immediately after mixing, providing a basis to compare the influence of RCA replacement level, pre-treatment, and GNMP dosage on fresh concrete behavior. Mechanical performance was measured by compressive strength, splitting tensile strength, flexural strength, and ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) tests. Compressive strength testing was performed on 100 mm × 200 mm cylinders at 14- and 28-days according to ASTM C3946, while splitting tensile strength was measured at 28 days only as per ASTM C49647. Flexural strength testing was performed on beam specimens of 100 mm × 100 mm × 500 mm according to ASTM C7848. UPV testing was conducted to examine internal uniformity and possible defects which is a widely recognized technique. For durability assessment, water absorption and indices related to density and voids were determined using ASTM C64249. Sorptivity and rate of water absorption tests were conducted on disk specimens following capillary absorption procedures similar to ASTM C158550. Chloride penetration was evaluated with a rapid chloride penetration test setup following the rating scheme of ASTM C120251. Freeze–thaw resistance was quantified by monitoring mass loss and visible scaling after cyclic exposure in a controlled freezing chamber following CEN/TS 12,390‑9. For microstructural analysis, selected specimens were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to study the ITZ and the degree of matrix densification. Fragments taken from broken compressive strength cylinders were dried, prepared, and gold‑coated before imaging so that pore structure, crack patterns, and the distribution of GNMPs around the aggregates could be observed in detail.

Results and discussion

Phase 1: effects of RCA pre-treatment

Experimental work was performed in two phases. In Phase 1, RCA pretreatment was performed to select the most appropriate technique for phase II testing. Phase 1 testing compared three types of pre-treated RCA when NA were 100% replaced with pre-treated RCA to select the best pre-treatment technique for phase II testing. Figure 5 shows the compressive, splitting tensile, and flexural strengths of natural aggregate control (NAC1-Control), untreated RCA (UT100RCA0G), water-washed RCA (WT100RCA0G), and cement slurry–coated RCA (CST100RCA0G). The use of untreated RCA led to noticeable reductions in all strength parameters relative to the control, confirming the detrimental effect of adhered mortar, higher porosity, and weaker ITZ associated with recycled aggregates. Compared with NAC1-Control, UT100RCA0G showed lower compressive, split tensile, and flexural strengths, consistent with previous reports on RAC produced with unmodified RCA. Washing treatment produced a modest improvement over untreated RCA, with small gains in compressive and tensile strengths, reflecting the removal of loose fines and weak surface mortar while leaving the internal pore structure largely unchanged. Cement slurry coating provided the most significant enhancement among the RCA treatments, giving higher compressive, splitting tensile, and flexural strengths than both untreated and washed RCA mixes, while narrowing the gap to the natural aggregate control. This improvement can be attributed to the slurry’s ability to fill surface pores and microcracks in the adhered mortar, forming a denser, more continuous cementitious shell around the RCA and a stronger aggregate–paste interface in the hardened concrete. The resulting refinement of the interfacial transition zone reduces local stress concentrations and improves stress transfer, which benefits both compressive and tensile behavior. On the basis of these Phase 1 findings, cement slurry–treated RCA was selected for all Phase 2 mixtures to isolate and examine the combined effects of RCA replacement level and GNMP dosage on fresh, mechanical, durability, and microstructural properties.

Effect of RCA pre-treatment on compressive, split tensile and flexural strength.

Fresh concrete properties of GNMP-modified mixes

The slump values of GNMP‑modified mixes with different RCA replacement levels are presented in Fig. 6. The natural aggregate control (NAC2‑Control) exhibited the highest slump of 88 mm, while the incorporation of GNMPs at 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5% by cement mass progressively reduced workability to 60, 55, and 50 mm, respectively, even when only natural coarse aggregate was used (RCA0G0.1–RCA0G0.5). This reduction is consistent with previous studies on graphene‑based nanomaterials, where the high specific surface area and tendency to form flocculated clusters increased internal friction and water demand, thereby lowering slump52–54. When 100% cement slurry‑treated RCA was used (RCA100G0), the slump reduced to 45 mm relative to NAC2‑Control, reflecting the higher water absorption and angularity of RCA despite the slurry coating. Increasing GNMP dosage at 100% RCA further decreased slump to 40, 39, and 35 mm for 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5% GNMPs, respectively, indicating a combined stiffening effect of RCA and GNMPs on the fresh mix. The reduction in strength was observed in mixes containing 30% and 60% RCA, but slump value was slightly higher than 100% RCA mixes. The control mixes of group 4 and 5 such as RCA30G0 and RCA60G0 had slumps of about 52 mm and 49 mm, and the values dropped progressively with addition of GNMPs reaching up to 35 mm at 0.5% GNMP dosage. These observations align with earlier findings that GNMPs causes reduced workability in cementitious systems, even at low dosages, unless compensated by increased superplasticizer or optimized dispersion strategies55,56. Experimental results confirm that increasing RCA content and GNMP dosage both reduce workability, with the most pronounced slump loss occurring at 100% RCA and 0.5% GNMPs.

Hardened concrete properties

Compressive strength

Figure 7 shows the compressive strength results for all Phase II mixes tested on 14 and 28 days. The results clearly indicate that the incorporation of GNMPs enhanced the compressive strength of both NAC and RAC across all RCA replacement levels. For mixes without RCA (Group 2), the addition of GNMPs resulted in a progressive increase in compressive strength with the increase in its dosage from 0.1% to 0.5%. The highest improvement in group 2 specimens was observed at 0.5% GNMP dosage. This increase in strength is attributed to the micro-filler effect of GNMPs and densification of cement matrix by filling micro-voids and refining the pore structure. Previous studies have reported similar results that addition of graphene-based additives improves the hydration kinetics and reduces porosity contributing to higher compressive strength26,30. RCA mixes naturally came out weaker than the NAC mixes, which makes sense given the old mortar and extra porosity in recycled material. The inclusion of GNMPs in RCA mixes significantly mitigated this strength reduction issue. The strength gain was more pronounced in group 4 and 5 specimens having 30% and 60% RCA content from 0.1% to 0.5% GNMP dosage. The increase in strength was more at higher dosages of GNMPs which indicates a beneficial interaction between cement slurry-treated RCA and GNMPs. Even at 100% RCA replacement, effect of GNMPs addition was noticeable as compared to control mix of same group without GNMPs. Overall, the compressive strength results demonstrate that GNMPs effectively compensate for the inherent drawbacks of RCA, especially when pre-treated RCA are used.

Split tensile strength

Split tensile test results are summarized in Table 5 for all mixes tested in phase II. The results show a consistent increase in split tensile strength with the addition of GNMPs like compressive strength results. For group 2 specimens having 0% RCA, tensile strength increased gradually from 3.96 MPa to 4.92 MPa with 0% to 0.5% GNMP dosage. These results highlight improved resistance to crack initiation and propagation. The specimens of group 3, 4 and 5 having 0% GNMP dosage show lower values of split tensile strength due to addition of RCA and weaker microstructure. Addition of GNMPs in these groups showed significant improvement in tensile strength with highest improvements observed at higher GNMP dosages. The improvement in properties is due to the crack-bridging capability of GNMPs as already reported in previous studies53. These experimental results clearly show the effect of GNMPs addition which is more pronounced in RAC mixes. Addition of GNMPs improves concrete matrix by bridging the microcracks and ITZ.

Flexural strength

Flexural strength results of phase II mixes are also shown in Table 4. These results indicate improvements in flexural strength values due to the addition of GNMPs in concrete mixes irrespective of RCA replacement level as already highlighted in compressive as well as split tensile strength results. For group 2 specimens, flexural strength increased gradually from 17.08 MPa to 23.39 MPa with increase in GNMP dosage from 0% to 0.5%. These results highlight the improvement in crack resistance under bending loads with increase in GNMP content in the mix. Reduction in flexural strength is observed with the increase in RCA content which shows the porous microstructure of RAC mixes. However, addition of GNMPs in RAC mixes effectively mitigated the negative effects of RCA content. The specimens of group 4 and 5, having 30% & 60% RCA content, showed greater strength recovery especially at higher GNMP dosages. Moreover, effect of GNMPs was also significant for group 3 mixes at 100% RCA replacement. The improvement in flexural, compressive and split tensile strength is due to the combined effects of matrix densification and improved stress redistribution provided by GNMPs. The plate-like morphology of GNMPs enables crack deflection and energy dissipation of concrete mixes under the applied loads. Experimental results confirm that GNMPs play a critical role in enhancing the post-cracking behavior of RAC.

Ultrasonic pulse velocity

The ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) results are presented in Fig. 8. Figure 8 shows that the UPV values generally decrease with increase in RCA content. However, addition of GNMPs shows an increase in UPV values irrespective of RCA replacements. For NAC mixes, GNMP addition shows higher UPV values highlighting the improvement in internal compactness and reduction in voids. The increase in UPV values was more pronounced in RCA mixes after the addition of GNMPs by mitigating the negative effect of RCA addition. The experimental results indicate that addition of GNMPs improves the quality of the mix and minimizes the effect of RCA content. These results complement the results of compressive, flexural and split tensile strength testing. The hardened concrete properties confirm that GNMP incorporation contributes to overall matrix densification and improved structural integrity. The consistent increase in UPV values further supports the effectiveness of combining cement slurry-treated RCA with GNMPs to produce RAC with improved mechanical performance.

Durability properties

Water absorption

The water absorption results of Phase II mixes are summarized in Table 6. The results show an increase in water absorption with the increase in RCA percentage. This increase in water absorption is attributed to the porous nature of adhered mortar and microcracks present in RCA. This effect was most pronounced for the control mixes containing 100% RCA without GNMPs. The inclusion of GNMPs resulted in a consistent reduction in water absorption across all mix groups. The water absorption of all mixes was reduced with increase in GNMP dosages across all groups. This reduction indicates effective pore refinement and densification of the cementitious matrix induced by GNMP incorporation. The plate-like morphology of GNMPs promotes micro-filling of capillary pores and improved packing density, thereby limiting water ingress. The 30% and 60% RCA mixes actually showed a slightly bigger relative drop with GNMPs than 100% RCA mixes. These results shows that the slurry coating and platelets reinforce each other towards better mix. These results prove that GNMPs can partially offset the negative durability effects associated with RCA. The obtained water absorption values for GNMP-modified RAC mixes (2.86–3.56%) fall within or below the range typically reported for recycled aggregate concrete with similar replacement levels (generally 3–6% for 30–100% RCA). The relatively lower values observed in the present study, particularly for mixes with 30% and 60% RCA, indicate the effectiveness of combined cement slurry treatment and GNMP incorporation in reducing pore connectivity. The superior performance of 30–60% RCA mixes compared to 100% RCA can be attributed to the presence of natural aggregates, which provide a denser and less porous skeleton, thereby limiting overall permeability. At 100% RCA replacement, the higher volume of adhered mortar increases porosity and interconnected voids, which cannot be fully compensated even with GNMP-induced densification.

Sorptivity

Sorptivity results are also shown in Table 5 which highlight that the trend is similar to water absorption results. Increase in RCA content increases the sorptivity values which reflects enhanced capillary suction due to higher pore connectivity in RAC. However, the addition of GNMPs significantly reduced sorptivity for all RCA replacement levels. Sorptivity values decreased steadily with increase in GNMP dosages highlighting improved resistance to capillary water uptake. The improvement in sorptivity results is because of refinement of the pore network and interruption of capillary channels by GNMPs. Previous studies have also reported that graphene-based additives reduce sorptivity and improves microstructural compactness and ITZ. These results confirm that GNMP incorporation effectively improves the resistance of RAC against capillary-driven moisture transport. The measured sorptivity values (0.86–1.21 mm) are comparable to or lower than those reported in previous studies on RAC, where values typically range from 1.0 to 1.5 mm for high RCA contents. The reduction achieved with GNMP addition highlights the effectiveness of nano-scale fillers in interrupting capillary pore networks.

Mixes with 30% and 60% RCA demonstrated comparatively lower sorptivity than 100% RCA mixes due to a more favorable balance between natural and recycled aggregates. The inclusion of natural aggregates reduces the continuity of capillary channels, while GNMPs further refine the pore structure, resulting in improved resistance to capillary suction.

Rate of water absorption

Figure 9 plots how the rate of water absorption changed over time for the Phase II mixes. Every mix started with a sharp uptake in the first few hours, then the curves flattened out as expected for cement paste systems. RCA mixes with 0% GNMP dosage showed higher absorption rates throughout the test duration compared to NAC which indicates increased permeability of mixes. The incorporation of GNMPs reduced the rate of water absorption across all groups. The increase in GNMP dosage caused reduction in rate of water absorption and reached saturation more gradually, demonstrating improved resistance to moisture ingress. This effect was more evident in mixes with partial RCA replacement, where GNMPs significantly moderated the absorption kinetics. The improvement in results is attributed to pore refinement and densification of cement matrix. GNMPs act as effective micro-fillers, reducing the connectivity of capillary pores and slowing down water transport. These results further support the beneficial role of GNMPs in enhancing the durability performance of RAC.

Mass loss

Freeze-thaw resistance results are shown in Table 7. The results show that the mixes having 0% GNMP dosage exhibited higher mass loss compared to NAC-Control. These results indicate increased vulnerability to freeze–thaw damage due to higher porosity and weaker ITZ due to addition of RCA. Whereas incorporation of GNMPs notably reduced mass loss across all RCA replacement levels in RAC. Increase in GNMP dosage progressively lowered mass loss across all groups which shows enhanced resistance to freeze–thaw deterioration due to GNMPs. This improvement in freeze-thaw resistance is due to the densification of the cement matrix and reduced ingress of water into the pore structure. The improved ITZ of concrete mixes limits internal stress development during freezing cycles. The results demonstrate that GNMPs effectively enhance the freeze–thaw durability of RAC, particularly when combined with cement slurry-treated RCA. The mass loss values observed in this study are lower than those typically reported for untreated RAC under freeze–thaw exposure, where values often exceed 1.0% for high RCA contents. The improved performance of GNMP-modified mixes, particularly at 30–60% RCA, indicates enhanced resistance to internal damage caused by cyclic freezing. The relatively better performance of partial RCA mixes can be attributed to reduced internal stress development due to lower pore connectivity and improved ITZ characteristics, as compared to 100% RCA mixes where higher porosity facilitates water ingress and subsequent damage during freezing cycles.

Chloride ion penetration

Resistance to chloride ion penetration results is also summarized in Table 7. Mixes having RCA percentages showed that higher charge passed values as compared to NAC-control which indicates higher chloride permeability in RAC mixes. This effect was effectively countered with the addition of GNMPs in RAC mixes. The results showed substantial reduction in charge passed for all mixes containing GNMPs. The chloride ion penetration was consistently reduced with increase in GNMP dosage across all groups of phase II testing. In results, most GNMP-modified mixes are classified under the very low permeability category. The improvement in resistance to chloride ion penetration is observed due to refined pore structure and reduced connectivity of transport pathways caused by GNMP incorporation. The reduction in the results was more pronounced for mixes having more RCA content in group 3 and group 5 specimens. These results show the effectiveness of GNMPs content in mitigating the adverse effects of RCA related to durability properties. These results highlight the potential of GNMP-modified RAC applications where resistance to chloride ingress and permeability is required. Durability results of all these tests confirms that the addition of GNMPs significantly improves moisture resistance, freeze–thaw durability, and chloride penetration resistance of RAC mixes. The improvement in durability results is in line with the findings presented in hardened concrete properties due to the addition of RCA and GNMPs. The charge passed values for GNMP-modified mixes fall within the “very low” permeability range (< 1000 coulombs), which is significantly lower than typical values reported for RAC with high RCA content (commonly 1000–3000 coulombs). This demonstrates the substantial improvement in resistance to chloride ingress achieved in this study. The enhanced performance of 30–60% RCA mixes compared to 100% RCA can be explained by the combined effect of a denser aggregate skeleton and improved matrix quality. While GNMPs effectively refine the pore structure, the presence of natural aggregates in partial replacement mixes reduces the overall permeability more efficiently than in fully recycled systems.

Microstructural investigation

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed on selected mixes to better understand the material-level changes which improves the mechanical and durability performance of GNMP-modified RAC. The NAC2-Control mix was taken as reference and RCA30G0.5 mix was selected for comparison due to its superior overall performance among the RAC mixes. RCA30G0.5 mix shows an optimum balance between RCA replacement level and GNMP dosage which shows consistent improvements in strength, durability, and workability results. Therefore, this sample was used for detailed microstructural investigation and its comparison with NAC. Figure 10 presents the SEM images of NAC2-Control and RCA30G0.5 mixes at 28 days of curing. The control specimen displayed a comparatively non-uniform microstructure, with loosely arranged hydration products which are interspersed with visible micro-voids. Although the presence of calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel is evident, the matrix lacks compactness as discontinuities are observed which serve as preferential paths for crack initiation and moisture penetration. These characteristics are commonly observed in conventional concrete which is aligned with the moderate strength and durability performance recorded for the control mix.

SEM image of NAC2-control and RCA30G0.5.

In contrast, the RCA30G0.5 mix exhibited a remarkably denser and more homogeneous microstructure. The matrix appeared more continuous with finer and less interconnected pores and uniform distribution of hydration products. The improvement in ITZ can be observed due to negligible boundary between aggregates and the surrounding paste. This refinement is attributed to the incorporation of GNMPs, which contributes through micro-filling effects and by providing additional nucleation sites for hydration products. This denser microstructure leads to a more tightly packed C–S–H network. The improvement observed in the microstructure of RCA30G0.5 mix provides a a clear explanation for the improvements in compressive, split tensile, and flexural strengths discussed earlier. Moreover, the reduced pore connectivity and refined capillary structure are consistent with the observed reductions in water absorption, sorptivity, rate of water absorption, and chloride ion penetration. By limiting the ingress of moisture and aggressive ions and reducing stress concentrations within the matrix, these microstructural improvements contribute directly to enhanced durability. SEM analysis confirms that the synergistic use of cement slurry-treated RCA and GNMPs leads to meaningful microstructural refinement. These microstructural observations translate into improved macro-scale performance of RAC and support the effectiveness of proposed modification strategy. Although direct quantitative techniques for evaluating dispersion quality (such as zeta potential or spectroscopic methods) were not employed in this study, the observed improvements in microstructure, including a denser matrix and reduced pore connectivity, provide indirect evidence of effective GNMP distribution. Nevertheless, future work should include dedicated dispersion characterization to further validate the effectiveness of dispersing agents and compare alternative approaches.

Conclusions

This study was conducted to investigate the synergistic effects of pre–treated RCA and GNMP dosages on the overall performance of RAC. The experimental program was designed to evaluate fresh, mechanical, durability, and microstructural properties with an aim to identify the optimum mix. The experimental results showed effectiveness of GNMPs in order to mitigate the inherent drawbacks of RCA in GNMP-modified RAC mix. The conclusions drawn from this study are as follows:

Cement slurry treatment significantly improved RCA quality, resulting in noticeable recovery of mechanical performance compared to untreated RCA, primarily through pore filling and ITZ strengthening.

Research limitations

This study primarily focused on elucidating the synergistic effects of cement slurry–treated RCA and GNMPs on the fresh, mechanical, durability, and microstructural behavior of concrete. Although UPV was measured to provide qualitative insight into internal concrete condition, a quantitative correlation between compressive strength and UPV was not developed. Such correlation requires a larger experimental dataset and additional statistical calibration to build a reliable predictive model, which was beyond the scope of the present investigation.
