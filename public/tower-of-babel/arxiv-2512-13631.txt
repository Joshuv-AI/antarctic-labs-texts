Torus Time-Spectral Method for Quasi-Periodic Problems
Sicheng He, Hang Li, Kivanc Ekici

Abstract
Quasi-periodic trajectories with two or more incommensurate frequencies are ubiquitous in nonlinear dynamics, yet the classical Fourier-based time-spectral method is tied to strictly periodic responses. We introduce a torus time-spectral method that lifts the governing equations to an extended angular phase space, applies double-Fourier collocation on the invariant torus, and solves for the state. The formulation exhibits spectral convergence for quasi-periodic problem which we give a rigorous mathematical proof and also verify numerically. We demonstrate the approach on Duffing oscillators and a nonlinear Klein–Gordon system, documenting spectral error decay on the torus and tight agreement with time-accurate integrations while using modest frequency grids. The method extends naturally to higher-dimensional tori and offers a computationally efficient framework for analyzing quasi-periodic phenomena in fluid mechanics, plasma physics, celestial mechanics, and other domains where multi-frequency dynamics arise.

In contrast, traditional harmonic balance implementations require a frequency-domain reformulation that couples Fourier modes through convolution sums or alternating frequency–time (AFT) procedures, complicating code maintenance and limiting modularity.

However, it is likely that such problem can be formulated with additional equations following Thomas et al.’s work that extends TSM to TSM LCO equation

2
Periodic and quasi-periodic dynamics

The time-spectral method was developed with significant success for periodic problems in the CFD community, often yielding an order-of-magnitude speed-up compared with time-accurate integration.
Applications range from forced periodic responses to limit-cycle oscillations (LCO)
[
11
,
12
,
25
]
.

However, the original form of TSM cannot be used to capture quasi-periodic regimes whose Fourier spectra contain incommensurate peaks.
Simple rational approximations
[
41
]
introduce “beat” phenomena when the base frequencies are nearly commensurate, motivating the torus lifting adopted in this work.
By recognizing that quasi-periodic trajectories densely fill an
n
-torus without repetition, we reformulate the problem within a higher-dimensional periodic space.
We now revisit the lifting strategy in a compact notation that distinguishes temporal and angular variables throughout the remainder of the paper.

Consider a nonlinear dynamical system governed by

\dot{\mathbf{q}}(t)=\mathbf{f}\!\big(\mathbf{q}(t),t\big),

(1)

where
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the state vector,
\mathbf{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the forcing term, and
n
is the dimension of the state space.
The autonomous system
\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})
is a special case.

2.1
Periodic solutions

A solution
\mathbf{q}(t)
is called
periodic
with period
T>0
if
\mathbf{q}(t+T)=\mathbf{q}(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Equivalently, the solution admits a representation

\mathbf{q}(t)=\mathbf{q}(\theta),\qquad\theta=\omega t,\quad\omega=\frac{2\pi}{T},

(2)

where
\mathbf{q}(\theta)
is
2\pi
-periodic in the angular variable
\theta
.
Periodic trajectories lie on a one-dimensional invariant manifold (a circle
\mathbb{T}^{1}
).
Differentiating
eq.

2
with respect to time yields the
invariance equation
on
\mathbb{T}^{1}
:

\omega\,\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}(\theta)=\mathbf{f}\!\big(\mathbf{q}(\theta),\theta\big),

(3)

which must hold for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
.
Solving
eq.

3
for
\mathbf{q}(\theta)
and
\omega
simultaneously recovers the periodic orbit without time-marching, and this is the basis of the classical time-spectral method.

2.2
Quasi-periodic solutions

A solution
\mathbf{q}(t)
is called
quasi-periodic
with
m
basic frequencies
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m}
if it admits a representation

\mathbf{q}(t)=\mathbf{q}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m}),\qquad\theta_{j}=\omega_{j}t,\quad j=1,\ldots,m,

(4)

where
\mathbf{q}(\bm{\theta})
is a smooth
2\pi
-periodic function on the
m
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{m}=[0,2\pi)^{m}
, and the frequency vector
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m})^{\intercal}
is
rationally independent
, meaning that

\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{j}\omega_{j}=0\quad\text{for integers}\quad k_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k_{1}=\cdots=k_{m}=0.

(5)

When the frequencies are rationally independent, the trajectory
\{\mathbf{q}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}t\in\mathbb{R}\}
densely fills the invariant torus
\{\mathbf{q}(\bm{\theta})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{T}^{m}\}
and never repeats.
Periodic solutions are the special case of
m=1
.

For the remainder of this work we focus on the two-frequency case (
m=2
), although the formulation extends naturally to arbitrary
m
.
Differentiating
eq.

4
with respect to time and applying the chain rule yields the
invariance equation
on the two-torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
:

\omega_{1}\,\partial_{\theta_{1}}\mathbf{q}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})+\omega_{2}\,\partial_{\theta_{2}}\mathbf{q}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\mathbf{f}\!\big(\mathbf{q}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}),\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\big),

(6)

which must hold for all
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
This equation states that the vector field
\mathbf{f}
evaluated on the torus must be tangent to the torus, ensuring that trajectories remain on the invariant manifold for all time.
Solving
eq.

6
for
\mathbf{q}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
and
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
simultaneously recovers the quasi-periodic solution without integrating the original time-dependent system
eq.

1
.

Example

Consider the scalar differential equation

\dot{q}(t)=\sin(\omega_{1}t)+\cos(\omega_{2}t),

(7)

with rationally independent frequencies
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}>0
.
Writing
q(t)=q(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
with
\theta_{j}=\omega_{j}t
, the invariance equation, i.e.,
eq.

6
, becomes

\omega_{1}\,\partial_{\theta_{1}}q(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})+\omega_{2}\,\partial_{\theta_{2}}q(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\sin(\theta_{1})+\cos(\theta_{2}),

(8)

which is a first-order partial differential equation (PDE) on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
rather than an ordinary differential equation (ODE) in time.
This
lifting
from the temporal variable
t
to the angular coordinates
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
is the foundation of the torus time-spectral method.

3
Classic time-spectral method

The classical time-spectral method leverages the spectral convergence of the Fourier transform to represent a periodic signal. The key feature of this method is the conversion of a periodic time signal to the frequency space for differentiation before casting it back to the time domain. These operations can be efficiently performed in one step using the spectral differentiation operator
\mathbf{D}
defined as follows:

\mathbf{D}[j,k]=\begin{cases}0,&j=k,\\
\dfrac{(-1)^{\,j-k}}{2}\,\csc\!\left(\dfrac{(j-k)\pi}{n_{\text{TS}}}\right),&j\neq k,\end{cases}.

(9)

For a periodic problem with a known fundamental frequency
\omega
, the classical TSM formulation discretizes the periodic orbit at
n_{\text{TS}}
equally spaced collocation points within one period
T=2\pi/\omega
.
Let
\theta_{(i)}=2\pi i/n_{\text{TS}}
for
i=0,\ldots,n_{\text{TS}}-1
denote the phase instances, and let
\mathbf{q}_{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the state vector at phase
\theta_{(i)}
.
The discrete residual at each collocation point balances the spectral phase derivative with the forcing term:

\mathbf{r}^{(n_{\text{TS}})}_{\mathrm{TS}}(\mathbf{q}^{(n_{\text{TS}})})=\,\omega\left(\mathbf{D}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)\,\mathbf{q}^{(n_{\text{TS}})}-\mathbf{f}^{(n_{\text{TS}})}\left(\mathbf{q}^{(n_{\text{TS}})},\bm{\theta}^{(n_{\text{TS}})}\right),

(10)

where the stacked vectors are defined as

\bm{\theta}^{(n_{\text{TS}})}=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{(0)}\\
\theta_{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\theta_{(n_{\text{TS}}-1)}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{q}^{(n_{\text{TS}})}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{(0)}\\
\mathbf{q}_{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{q}_{(n_{\text{TS}}-1)}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{f}^{(n_{\text{TS}})}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{(0)}\\
\mathbf{f}_{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{f}_{(n_{\text{TS}}-1)}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{r}^{(n_{\text{TS}})}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{r}_{(0)}\\
\mathbf{r}_{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{r}_{(n_{\text{TS}}-1)}\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

Here
\mathbf{f}_{(i)}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}_{(i)},\theta_{(i)})
evaluates the forcing at the
i
-th collocation point, and the tensor product
\mathbf{D}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n}
applies the spectral differentiation matrix to each component of the state vector.
Solving
\mathbf{r}^{(n_{\text{TS}})}_{\mathrm{TS}}=0
yields the periodic solution at all collocation nodes simultaneously, bypassing the need for long-time integration.

When approximating a quasi-periodic problem using the classical time-spectral method, spectral convergence can be achieved locally.
However, this is not the case over a dense torus.
This limitation motivates the development of the TTSM method.

4
Torus Time-Spectral Method (TTSM)

4.1
Nodal equation

Using the torus coordinates
\theta_{1}=\omega_{1}t,\;\theta_{2}=\omega_{2}t
,
the invariance equation (
6
) on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
becomes our starting point.
We discretize each angle over
n_{\text{TS},1}
and
n_{\text{TS},2}
equally-spaced points respectively:

\theta_{1}^{(\ell)}=\frac{2\pi\ell}{n_{\text{TS},1}},\quad\ell=0,\dots,n_{\text{TS},1}-1,\qquad\theta_{2}^{(m)}=\frac{2\pi m}{n_{\text{TS},2}},\quad m=0,\dots,n_{\text{TS},2}-1.

(12)

At each discrete point
(\ell,m)
in the angular space, the nodal unknowns are denoted by

\mathbf{q}_{(\ell,m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
whereas
the Fourier spectral differentiation matrices in
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are denoted by

\mathbf{D}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{TS},1}\times n_{\text{TS},1}}
and

\mathbf{D}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{TS},2}\times n_{\text{TS},2}}
, each constructed via
eq.

9
with the corresponding grid size.
The equation at each node then becomes

\mathbf{r}_{\ell,m}=\omega_{1}\sum_{p=0}^{n_{\text{TS},1}-1}(\mathbf{D}_{1})_{\ell p}\,\mathbf{q}_{(p,m)}+\omega_{2}\sum_{q=0}^{n_{\text{TS},2}-1}(\mathbf{D}_{2})_{mq}\,\mathbf{q}_{(\ell,q)}-\mathbf{f}\!\big(\mathbf{q}_{(\ell,m)},\theta_{1}^{(\ell)},\theta_{2}^{(m)}\big)=0,

(13)

which couples each nodal state to its neighbors through the spectral stencil depicted by the example of
fig.

1
.

4.2
Stacked equation

For global Newton–Krylov solvers, we work with a stacked representation.
Let
N=n\times n_{\text{TS},1}\times n_{\text{TS},2}
denote the total number of unknowns.
To construct the global differentiation operators, we extend the spectral matrices
\mathbf{D}_{1}
and
\mathbf{D}_{2}
to the full tensor-product grid and then to the state dimension via

{\mathcal{D}}_{1}=(\mathbf{I}_{n_{\text{TS},2}}\otimes\mathbf{D}_{1})\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\qquad{\mathcal{D}}_{2}=(\mathbf{D}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n_{\text{TS},1}})\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}.

(14)

The discrete residual can then be written as

\mathbf{r}^{(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})}_{\mathrm{TTSM}}(\mathbf{q}^{(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})})=\Big(\omega_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{1}+\omega_{2}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}\Big)\mathbf{q}^{(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})}-\mathbf{f}^{(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})}(\mathbf{q}^{(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})},\bm{\theta}^{(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})}),

(15)

where the stacked vectors are defined as

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\theta}_{(0,0)}\\
\bm{\theta}_{(1,0)}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\theta}_{(n_{\text{TS},1}-1,n_{\text{TS},2}-1)}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{q}^{(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{(0,0)}\\
\mathbf{q}_{(1,0)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{q}_{(n_{\text{TS},1}-1,n_{\text{TS},2}-1)}\end{bmatrix},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{(0,0)}\\
\mathbf{f}_{(1,0)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{f}_{(n_{\text{TS},1}-1,n_{\text{TS},2}-1)}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{r}^{(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{r}_{(0,0)}\\
\mathbf{r}_{(1,0)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{r}_{(n_{\text{TS},1}-1,n_{\text{TS},2}-1)}\end{bmatrix}.

Here
\bm{\theta}_{(\ell,m)}=(\theta_{1}^{(\ell)},\theta_{2}^{(m)})
denotes the angular grid point,
\mathbf{f}_{(\ell,m)}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}_{(\ell,m)},\theta_{1}^{(\ell)},\theta_{2}^{(m)})
is the forcing at the
(\ell,m)
-th collocation point, and the tensor products
{\mathcal{D}}_{j}
apply the spectral differentiation matrices to each component of the state vector.
Solving for
\mathbf{r}^{(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})}_{\mathrm{TTSM}}=0
yields the quasi-periodic solution at all collocation nodes simultaneously.

For simplicity, we define the compact form by

\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{TTSM}}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})=\Big(\omega_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{1}+\omega_{2}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}\Big)\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{f}(\hat{\mathbf{q}},\bm{\theta}),

(17)

where
\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
denotes the stacked discrete nodal vector.

4.3
Rank deficiency

When the governing dynamics contain a neutral direction—for example, the forced linear oscillator with no restoring term shown in
fig.

3
—the collocation system inherits the resulting gauge freedom: any constant shift of
\hat{\mathbf{q}}
leaves
eq.

13
unchanged and the discrete Jacobian loses rank.
In such cases we regularize by enforcing a
nodal anchor
, replacing one row of
eq.

13
with a physical constraint such as
\mathbf{q}_{(0,0)}=\mathbf{q}(0)
or by prescribing the torus average; this replacement removes the nullspace that would otherwise render the linearized system singular.
For the nonlinear and damped examples considered later (Duffing oscillator, Klein–Gordon equation), the combination of nonlinear saturation and dissipation determines a unique amplitude, so no additional constraint is needed.

4.4
Extension to
k
-torus systems

The proposed framework extends naturally to quasi-periodic problems with
k
frequency components.
Let
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k})
with
\theta_{j}=\omega_{j}t
parameterize the
k
-torus
\mathbb{T}^{k}
, and discretize each angle with
n_{\text{TS},j}
collocation points.
The resulting residual maintains the Kronecker-sum structure

\mathbf{r}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\omega_{j}\mathcal{D}_{j}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{f}(\hat{\mathbf{q}},\bm{\theta}),

(18)

where
\mathcal{D}_{j}=(\cdots\otimes\mathbf{I}_{M_{j+1}}\otimes\mathbf{D}_{j}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{M_{j-1}}\otimes\cdots)\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n}
is the lifted differentiation matrix in direction
\theta_{j}
.
All ingredients of the two-frequency solver carry over: the Jacobian remains block-circulant with respect to each angular index, the preconditioner is assembled from one-dimensional TSM solves, and the convergence results in
section

5
generalize by replacing the bivariate interpolant with its multivariate counterpart.
Finally, padding can be applied to suppress aliasing for problems with more than two incommensurate frequencies.

4.5
Solution method

We solve the nonlinear residual given in
eq.

15
using a damped Newton–Krylov method with the base frequencies
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
held fixed.
At iteration
k
the torus state
\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}
is updated by solving

\mathbf{J}^{(k)}\,\Delta\hat{\mathbf{q}}=-\,\mathbf{r}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}),

(19)

where
\mathbf{J}^{(k)}
is the Jacobian of the residual.
The Jacobian exhibits a block-sparse structure arising from the global coupling of the Fourier spectral stencil, as illustrated in
fig.

1
.
As mentioned earlier, if a nodal anchor or linear phase constraint is enforced, the corresponding row replaces one equation so that
eq.

19
remains well posed.
Restarted GMRES technique
[
43
]
is used to solve the linear system, and a backtracking line search on the Newton update ensures a monotonic reduction of the nonlinear residual.

5
Convergence analysis

Next, we analyze the discrete residual of the TTSM.
Recall that
n
denotes the state-space dimension and let
N=n\times n_{\text{TS},1}\times n_{\text{TS},2}
be the total number of unknowns.
Let
\mathbf{q}^{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the exact (continuous) torus solution of
eq.

6
.
We discretize on an
n_{\text{TS},1}\times n_{\text{TS},2}
collocation grid with nodes
(\theta_{1}^{(\ell)},\theta_{2}^{(m)})
for
\ell=0,\ldots,n_{\text{TS},1}-1
and
m=0,\ldots,n_{\text{TS},2}-1
.
The discrete state vector
\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
collects the nodal values, and the discrete residual is

\mathbf{r}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})=(\omega_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{1}+\omega_{2}{\mathcal{D}}_{2})\,\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{f}(\hat{\mathbf{q}},\bm{\theta}),

(20)

where
\mathcal{D}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
are the Fourier spectral differentiation matrices acting on the nodal values.

Recall that
\mathbb{T}^{2}=[0,2\pi)^{2}
denotes the two-torus.
Let
\mathbb{T}_{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}}
denote the space of trigonometric polynomials on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with bounded wave numbers,

\mathbb{T}_{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}}=\operatorname{span}\!\left\{e^{\mathrm{i}(k_{1}\theta_{1}+k_{2}\theta_{2})}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}|k_{1}|\leq K_{1},|k_{2}|\leq K_{2}\right\},\qquad K_{j}=\lfloor(n_{\text{TS},j}-1)/2\rfloor,

(21)

Denote by
\mathbf{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
the sampling operator that evaluates a continuous function at the collocation nodes and stacks the values into a vector.
Let
\mathbf{v}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the unique vector-valued trigonometric polynomial in
(\mathbb{T}_{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}})^{n}
whose nodal values coincide with the samples of
\mathbf{q}^{\star}
at the collocation grid, and write
\hat{\mathbf{v}}\coloneq\mathbf{P}\mathbf{q}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
for these nodal values.

Lemma 5.1

(Truncation error and consistency)
.

Assume
\mathbf{q}^{\star}\in C^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
s>1
, and that
\mathbf{f}(\cdot,\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
is
C^{1}
in
\mathbf{q}
uniformly in
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
.
Then the discrete operator

(\omega_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{1}+\omega_{2}{\mathcal{D}}_{2})

is consistent with the continuous operator

(\omega_{1}\partial_{\theta_{1}}+\omega_{2}\partial_{\theta_{2}})

on
(\mathbb{T}_{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}})^{n}
, and the nodal truncation error

\mathbf{t}_{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}}\coloneq(\omega_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{1}+\omega_{2}{\mathcal{D}}_{2})\,\hat{\mathbf{v}}-\mathbf{f}(\hat{\mathbf{v}},\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

(22)

satisfies

\|\mathbf{t}_{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}}\|_{\infty}\leq C\big(n_{\text{TS},1}^{1-s}+n_{\text{TS},2}^{1-s}\big),\qquad s>1,

(23)

where
C
depends on
\|\mathbf{q}^{\star}\|_{C^{s}}
and the Lipschitz constant of
\mathbf{f}
.
If
\mathbf{q}^{\star}
and
\mathbf{f}
are analytic in
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
on a complex strip of half-width
\rho>0
, then

\|\mathbf{t}_{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}}\|_{\infty}\leq C\,e^{-\rho\,\min\{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}\}},

(24)

where
C
depends on the analytic norm of
\mathbf{q}^{\star}
on the strip.

Proof.

The interpolant
\mathbf{v}
of
\mathbf{q}^{\star}
belongs to the trigonometric polynomial space
\mathbb{T}_{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}}
, so it admits the expansion

\mathbf{v}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\sum_{|k_{1}|\leq K_{1}}\sum_{|k_{2}|\leq K_{2}}\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k_{1},k_{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}(k_{1}\theta_{1}+k_{2}\theta_{2})}.

(25)

By construction, the differentiation matrices
{\mathcal{D}}_{j}
reproduce the action of
\partial_{\theta_{j}}
exactly on each Fourier mode in this span; therefore the discrete and continuous derivatives coincide at the collocation nodes,

(\omega_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{1}+\omega_{2}{\mathcal{D}}_{2})\hat{\mathbf{v}}=\bigl[(\omega_{1}\partial_{\theta_{1}}+\omega_{2}\partial_{\theta_{2}})\mathbf{v}\bigr]\big|_{\text{nodes}}.

All unresolved Fourier content of
\mathbf{q}^{\star}
, therefore, enters only through the interpolation defect
\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{q}^{\star}
.
Evaluating the residual of the interpolant and subtracting the continuous invariance equation for
\mathbf{q}^{\star}
yields, pointwise at the nodes,

\mathbf{t}_{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}}=\bigl[(\omega_{1}\partial_{\theta_{1}}+\omega_{2}\partial_{\theta_{2}})(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{q}^{\star})\bigr]\big|_{\text{nodes}}\\
-\bigl[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{v},\bm{\theta})-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}^{\star},\bm{\theta})\bigr]\big|_{\text{nodes}}.

(26)

Applying standard Fourier interpolation estimates on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
yields

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{q}^{\star}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C(n_{\text{TS},1}^{-s}+n_{\text{TS},2}^{-s}),

(27)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{\theta_{j}}(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{q}^{\star})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C(n_{\text{TS},1}^{1-s}+n_{\text{TS},2}^{1-s}),

(28)

and by the mean-value theorem for
\mathbf{f}
,

\|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{v})-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}^{\star})\|\leq L_{f}\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{q}^{\star}\|.

Combining these bounds yields (
23
); for analytic data, exponential decay of Fourier coefficients implies (
24
).
∎

Theorem 5.2

(Convergence of the TTSM solution for non-autonomous systems)
.

Consider a non-autonomous system where the frequencies
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
are prescribed.
Assume the hypotheses of Lemma
5.1
and that the linearized operator
\mathcal{J}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,

\mathcal{J}^{\star}\coloneq\omega_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{1}+\omega_{2}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}-\operatorname{blkdiag}\{\partial_{\mathbf{q}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}^{\star}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}),\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\},

(29)

where each
\mathcal{D}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and the block-diagonal matrix has
n_{\text{TS},1}\times n_{\text{TS},2}
blocks of size
n\times n
, satisfies a uniform inf–sup (stability) condition:
there exists
c_{0}>0
such that for all grid sizes sufficiently large,

\|\mathcal{J}^{\star}\mathbf{z}\|_{\infty}\geq c_{0}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\infty}\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(30)

Then for sufficiently large
(n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2})
there exists a unique discrete solution

\hat{\mathbf{q}}
of
\mathbf{r}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})=0
such that

\|\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\hat{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\infty}\leq C\big(n_{\text{TS},1}^{1-s}+n_{\text{TS},2}^{1-s}\big),

(31)

for
C^{s}
data, or

\|\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\hat{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\infty}\leq C\,e^{-\rho\,\min\{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}\}},

(32)

for analytic data.

Proof.

Evaluating
\mathbf{r}(\hat{\mathbf{v}})
gives
the truncation error
\mathbf{t}_{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}}
from Lemma
5.1
.
Since
\hat{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{q}^{\star}
, the Jacobian at
\hat{\mathbf{v}}
coincides with
\mathcal{J}^{\star}
, which is invertible with
\|\mathcal{J}^{\star-1}\|_{\infty}\leq 1/c_{0}
by the stability assumption.
A standard linearization argument then yields

\|\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\hat{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathcal{J}^{\star-1}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{r}(\hat{\mathbf{v}})\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{c_{0}}\|\mathbf{t}_{n_{\text{TS},1},n_{\text{TS},2}}\|_{\infty},

which gives (
31
)–(
32
).
∎

Remark 5.3

(Stability assumption)
.

The inf–sup condition on
\mathcal{J}^{\star}
is a discrete stability hypothesis that parallels the continuous non-resonance condition for the linearized flow on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

6.3
Nonlinear Klein–Gordon equation

The model problem considered here is the nonlinear Klein–Gordon equation in one spatial dimension,

q_{tt}-q_{xx}+q+\varepsilon q^{3}+\gamma q_{t}=g\sin(x)\,\bigl(\cos(\omega_{1}t)+\cos(\omega_{2}t)\bigr),

(38)

where
q(x,t)
denotes the field variable.
The parameter
\gamma
introduces damping,
\varepsilon
controls the strength of nonlinearity, and
g
sets the amplitude of an external spatially varying forcing.
The right-hand side includes driving terms at two incommensurate frequencies
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}

Introducing the velocity

38

\begin{bmatrix}q_{t}\\
v_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}v\\

(39)

For these computations, the spatial direction
x

A direct time-integration of this system produces a high fidelity time-accurate reference solution.
In parallel, the problem is reformulated in relation to the torus defined by the two forcing frequencies by introducing the angular coordinates
\theta_{1}=\omega_{1}t
and
\theta_{2}=\omega_{2}t

The lifted state
\mathbf{q}=[q,\,v]^{\intercal}

\omega_{1}\,\partial_{\theta_{1}}\mathbf{q}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})+\omega_{2}\,\partial_{\theta_{2}}\mathbf{q}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\begin{bmatrix}v\\
q_{xx}-q-\varepsilon q^{3}-\gamma v+g\sin(x)\,\bigl(\cos\theta_{1}+\cos\theta_{2}\bigr)\end{bmatrix},

(40)

posed on the torus
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in[0,2\pi)^{2}
with
\theta_{j}=\omega_{j}t
.
In this time-spectral approach, the governing equations are enforced at collocation points on the two-dimensional torus, and the unknown field is recovered by solving a nonlinear algebraic system for its Fourier coefficients.
We use the parameter set
\omega_{1}=1.0
,
\omega_{2}=\sqrt{2}

\gamma=0.2
,
\varepsilon=0.5

7
Conclusion

This paper introduced the torus time-spectral method (TTSM) for quasi-periodic dynamical systems driven by two incommensurate frequencies.
By reformulating the governing equations on a two-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and applying double-Fourier collocation, we transform the infinite-horizon quasi-periodic problem into a finite nonlinear algebraic system that can be solved with standard Newton–Krylov techniques.

The key contributions of this work are twofold.
First, we developed a time-domain torus collocation formulation that enables direct reuse of existing residual routines from steady-state or single-frequency time-spectral solvers, and showed that the TTSM bypasses the transient decay phase inherent to time-accurate integration by directly computing the attracting invariant torus.
Second, we established a rigorous convergence analysis (
theorem

5.2
) guaranteeing spectral accuracy for analytic solutions, and verified through numerical experiments that modest tensor grids—as few as
3\times 3
for the Duffing oscillator and
5\times 5
for the spatially discretized Klein–Gordon equation—suffice to achieve engineering accuracy, with solution errors decaying exponentially at rates of approximately
0.55
–
0.85
per grid point.

The method does have limitations.
The frequencies
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
must be known
a priori
, which restricts applicability to problems where the forcing frequencies are prescribed or can be identified from preliminary analysis; autonomous LCOs, whose frequency emerges from the dynamics itself rather than from external forcing, would require an augmented formulation that solves for the unknown period alongside the torus solution.
Additionally, the non-resonance condition underlying
theorem

5.2
may be violated for certain frequency ratios or parameter regimes, leading to ill-conditioned Jacobians.
For problems with neutral directions (e.g., undamped linear oscillators), a nodal anchor or similar regularization is required to remove the resulting gauge freedom.
