Iatrogenic Pseudoaneurysm After Arthroscopic Knee Procedures: A Systematic Review of Presenting Features, Injured Vessels, Management, and Outcomes
Authors: Ethan J Sack, Max Deneroff, Zubair Mohammed, Austin Khau, Chadi Ellaham, Jared W Nichols
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pseudoaneurysm after arthroscopic knee procedures is uncommon but clinically important because it can initially resemble routine postoperative swelling, hematoma, recurrent hemarthrosis, deep vein thrombosis, infection, or compartment syndrome. PubMed, Embase, and Scopus were searched through July 9, 2026. The original database pathway yielded 312 records and 54 included reports representing 60 patient cases. During peer-review citation audit, three additional potentially eligible reports were identified; two full texts were retrieved and included, while one report could not be retrieved and was not used for patient-level inference. The final synthesis therefore included 56 reports and 62 patient cases. Using a mutually exclusive primary-procedure classification, meniscal procedures accounted for 25/62 cases (40.3%) and anterior cruciate ligament (ACL)-related procedures for 19/62 (30.6%). The popliteal artery was the most frequent injured-vessel group (26/62, 41.9%), followed by medial/inferior genicular branches (9/62, 14.5%). Among 49 cases with an explicitly quantifiable interval to vascular diagnosis or recognition, the median was 14 days (range: 1-365 days); 13 cases had missing or non-quantifiable timing. Primary treatment was open vascular surgery in 30 cases, endovascular embolization/coiling in 14, ultrasound-guided thrombin injection in six, covered-stent or other endovascular repair in five, conservative observation in two, an unspecified vascular approach in four, and no definitive pseudoaneurysm-directed treatment was reported in one. Fasciotomy and other adjunctive procedures were coded separately. Final outcome categories were favorable recovery/resolution without persistent morbidity in 42 cases, persistent neurologic/functional morbidity or incomplete recovery in four, limited or short-term follow-up in 10, and no outcome reported in six. These frequencies describe published cases only and cannot estimate incidence, risk, or comparative treatment effectiveness. Recurrent or progressive postoperative symptoms, a pulsatile finding, neurologic change, or symptoms out of proportion to the expected course should prompt consideration of vascular imaging.

Introduction and background

Knee arthroscopy is widely used for meniscal, ligamentous, synovial, and diagnostic pathology. Early postoperative pain, bruising, stiffness, and swelling are common and can obscure an arterial injury. A pseudoaneurysm forms when an arterial wall defect remains in communication with the parent vessel but is contained by surrounding tissue; after arthroscopy, it may present later as recurrent hemarthrosis, hematoma, focal swelling, or symptoms that mimic venous thrombosis or infection [1-4].

The anatomy around the knee creates risk to both the popliteal artery and smaller periarticular branches. Posterior meniscal work, posterior capsular instrumentation, portal placement, synovectomy, cyst procedures, and adjacent soft-tissue dissection have all been linked to injuries involving the popliteal, genicular, descending genicular, sural, or nearby muscular branches [5-11].

Ligament reconstruction adds procedure-specific mechanisms such as hamstring harvest, tibial or femoral tunnel drilling, fixation hardware, outside-in trajectories, revision fixation, and combined cruciate reconstruction. The reported anterior cruciate ligament (ACL)-related cases therefore span both main-vessel and branch-vessel injury patterns rather than a single mechanism [12-20].

Clinical presentation is equally variable. Some patients have a classic pulsatile mass, bruit, or thrill, whereas others first develop persistent pain, recurrent hemarthrosis, calf swelling, neurologic deficit, suspected deep vein thrombosis, or a compartment-syndrome-like picture [21-24]. Distal pulses may remain present in branch-vessel injury or with preserved collateral flow, so a normal pulse examination does not exclude pseudoaneurysm.

Imaging is central when a vascular complication is suspected. Duplex or Doppler ultrasound can identify arterial flow in accessible lesions, CT angiography can define deeper anatomy and competing diagnoses, and catheter angiography can confirm the injured vessel while permitting embolization in selected cases [25-29]. These modalities play different clinical roles and should not be interpreted as interchangeable first-line tests in every patient.

Management in the published cases includes open resection, ligation, primary repair, patch angioplasty, interposition grafting, and bypass for selected injuries [30-37]. Branch-vessel pseudoaneurysms have also been managed with coil or liquid embolization and ultrasound-guided thrombin injection, while selected main-vessel injuries have been treated endovascularly [38-42]. The case literature is too heterogeneous to establish a single best treatment or a validated treatment hierarchy.

The knowledge gap is therefore not whether pseudoaneurysm can occur after knee arthroscopy, but how the scattered case literature can be organized into clinically useful patterns without implying population-level incidence or comparative effectiveness. This review summarizes the procedures involved, injured vessels, warning signs, diagnostic pathways, treatments, and outcomes while explicitly separating mutually exclusive from overlapping descriptive categories.

Review

Methods

Study Design and Protocol

We conducted a descriptive systematic review of published case reports and small case series describing pseudoaneurysm after arthroscopic knee procedures, reported in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidance [43]. A formal review protocol was not prospectively registered or publicly deposited. The clinical review question, eligibility criteria, patient-level extraction approach, and descriptive synthesis were established before the final synthesis. Revisions made after peer review consisted of a targeted citation audit, inclusion of two newly retrieved eligible reports, design-matched appraisal, and recoding of procedure, vessel, treatment, imaging-role, and outcome variables for clarity; the clinical eligibility question was not changed.

Deduplication and Study Selection

Records were consolidated in an Excel-compatible screening spreadsheet. No dedicated automated deduplication program was used. Exact DOI matches and exact-title matches were removed first; near-duplicates were then adjudicated manually using title, author, publication year, journal, and article content when necessary. The high duplicate proportion reflects deliberate overlap among broad PubMed, Embase, and Scopus searches. Six reports removed at the full-text stage represented duplicate patient reports or duplicate full-text copies that could only be recognized reliably after article-level comparison; they were therefore distinct from the record-level duplicates removed before title/abstract screening. Two reviewers screened titles/abstracts and independently assessed potentially eligible full texts, resolving disagreements by discussion and consensus.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible publications were human case reports or case series with extractable patient-level data describing a pseudoaneurysm that followed an arthroscopic knee procedure. The operational category of “other therapeutic knee arthroscopy” required an intra-articular or portal-based arthroscopic intervention temporally preceding the vascular lesion; it included arthroscopic synovectomy, irrigation/debridement, cyst procedures, and similar therapeutic arthroscopy. Procedures that were primarily open, arthroplasty-based, trauma-only, non-knee, or non-arthroscopic were excluded. Associated arteriovenous fistula was allowed when a pseudoaneurysm was also present. Data Extraction, Coding, and Synthesis

Two reviewers independently extracted patient-level data using a standardized spreadsheet, with one row per patient whenever cases could be separated. Extracted variables included demographics, comorbidity/coagulopathy, procedure, surgical details, injured vessel, presentation, timing, imaging, treatment, outcome, recurrence, neurologic or ischemic sequelae, and other complications. Unreported fields were coded as “Not reported” rather than inferred.

Procedure was recoded into one mutually exclusive primary category per case: meniscal; isolated ACL-related; combined ACL and meniscal; PCL/combined cruciate; synovectomy/debridement/irrigation/cyst procedure; or other diagnostic/therapeutic arthroscopy. Raw vessel wording was preserved in the extraction dataset, while a standardized vessel group was assigned for synthesis. When a source used unusual terminology, such as “superior middle genicular artery” reported by Becher et al. [4], the original wording was retained and grouped under a broader superior medial/middle genicular category rather than silently rewritten.

One mutually exclusive primary vascular-treatment category was assigned per case; adjunctive procedures such as fasciotomy, hematoma evacuation, and nerve reconstruction were coded separately. Outcomes were recoded into four mutually exclusive final-status categories: favorable recovery/resolution without persistent morbidity documented; persistent neurologic/functional morbidity or incomplete recovery; limited/short-term follow-up in which definitive recovery was not established; and outcome not reported. Imaging was retained at the patient level with role notes. Diagnostic vascular ultrasound was distinguished from ultrasound used only for aspiration, procedural guidance, or follow-up, and catheter angiography was distinguished conceptually from noninvasive initial imaging because it was frequently confirmatory, treatment-planning, or treatment-associated.

For time-to-diagnosis analysis, an interval in days was coded only when the source provided an explicit postoperative interval to vascular diagnosis/recognition. When the exact diagnosis date was not separately stated, the first explicit recognition of the pseudoaneurysm was used. Vague intervals such as “several weeks” or “several months” were excluded from the numerical calculation rather than converted. Methodological Appraisal

Methodological appraisal was matched to publication design using JBI critical appraisal tools [44]. Single-patient reports were considered with the JBI Case Reports checklist, while the six multi-patient publications were considered with the JBI Case Series checklist. Responses were retained as Yes, No, Unclear, or Not applicable. No numerical quality score was calculated, appraisal did not determine eligibility, and “Unclear” or “Not applicable” responses were not collapsed into “No.” The revised study-selection flow diagram was prepared in a PRISMA 2020-compatible format [45]. Statistical Approach

Because the evidence consisted of case reports and small case series without comparator groups, standardized follow-up, or uniform outcome definitions, meta-analysis and comparative effectiveness testing were not appropriate. Findings are reported with counts, percentages, medians, and ranges. Wilson confidence intervals were removed because the published cases are a selected, publication-biased sample and interval estimates could imply population-level precision that the design cannot support. No P-values, subgroup hypothesis tests, or historical-period comparisons were performed.

Results

Study Selection

The three database searches identified 312 raw records. After 237 record-level duplicates were removed, 75 records underwent title/abstract screening; 14 were excluded. Sixty-one full-text reports were assessed, of which seven were excluded: six duplicate patient reports or duplicate full-text copies and one trauma-only/non-arthroscopic report. The original database pathway therefore contributed 54 reports representing 60 patient cases. The original corpus included both older and contemporary case-based reports across meniscal, ligament, synovial, and other arthroscopic settings [46-57]. Study Characteristics and Demographics

Age was reported in 60/62 patients. Exact numeric age was available in 59/62 and was used for the age summary; the median was 31 years (range, 8-84 years). One additional report described age only as being in the patient's 20s and was not assigned a numeric value. Sex was reported in 60/62 patients: 46 were male and 14 were female. The review spans several decades of case literature, but no era-stratified statistical comparison was performed and no temporal treatment trend is inferred. Clinical Presentation and Timing

Presentation categories were descriptive and could overlap within a patient. Swelling, effusion, edema, or hemarthrosis was documented in 42/62 cases (67.7%), pain in 35/62 (56.5%), and a pulsatile mass, bruit, or thrill in 33/62 (53.2%). Bleeding/hematoma/ecchymosis or recurrent hemarthrosis was documented in 15/62 (24.2%), neurologic findings in 10/62 (16.1%), a DVT-like presentation in 4/62 (6.5%), and compartment-syndrome/ischemic features in 7/62 (11.3%).

An explicitly quantifiable interval from the index arthroscopic procedure to vascular diagnosis or first definite pseudoaneurysm recognition was available in 49/62 cases. The median interval was 14 days, with a range of 1 to 365 days. Thirteen cases had missing or non-quantifiable timing and were not converted from vague wording.

Imaging and Diagnosis

The included reports frequently used more than one imaging modality, so imaging categories are not mutually exclusive. Noninvasive vascular ultrasound was counted as diagnostic only when it was used to evaluate the vascular lesion; ultrasound used solely for aspiration guidance, treatment guidance, or follow-up was not counted as diagnostic vascular ultrasound. Catheter angiography was often performed after ultrasound, CT angiography, or MR angiography, or in conjunction with embolization. It is therefore described in the patient-level dataset by clinical role rather than interpreted as a uniform initial diagnostic test. The newly added Milankov case illustrates this sequence: Doppler and ultrasound preceded angiographic confirmation [59]. In the Armato case, color Doppler was performed after digital subtraction angiography for comparison and was not classified as the initial diagnostic test [58].

Treatment and Outcomes

Primary definitive treatment was assigned once per case. Open surgical vascular treatment was the primary approach in 30/62 cases (48.4%); endovascular embolization/coiling in 14/62 (22.6%); ultrasound-guided thrombin injection in 6/62 (9.7%); covered stent or other endovascular repair in 5/62 (8.1%); conservative observation in 2/62 (3.2%); vascular treatment with approach not reported in 4/62 (6.5%); and no definitive pseudoaneurysm-directed treatment was reported in 1/62 (1.6%). Adjunctive procedures were not mixed into these categories. Three patients underwent fasciotomy/decompression, and hematoma drainage/evacuation or nerve procedures were separately recorded when reported.

Outcome categories were mutually exclusive. Favorable recovery or vascular resolution without persistent morbidity was documented in 42/62 cases (67.7%). Persistent neurologic/functional morbidity or incomplete recovery was documented in 4/62 (6.5%). Ten cases (16.1%) had only limited or short-term follow-up that did not establish definitive recovery, and 6/62 (9.7%) did not report an outcome. These categories replace the prior composite “favorable outcome” measure. Discussion

The main clinical message is not that one procedure or one vessel defines this complication, but that vascular injury can emerge from several arthroscopic mechanisms and can initially resemble common postoperative problems. A mutually exclusive procedure classification clarifies that meniscal and ACL-related procedures account for the largest shares of the published cases without double-counting combined procedures. The vessel analysis likewise shows a substantial burden of genicular and other branch-vessel injuries in addition to popliteal lesions.

Diagnostic delay remains a practical concern. The median quantifiable interval was two weeks, but the range extended to one year. The case literature shows why: distal pulses can remain present, nonvascular diagnoses are frequently considered first, and early ultrasound may be negative or may be performed for another purpose. These observations support careful reassessment when postoperative swelling, hemarthrosis, focal pain, a new pulsatile finding, neurologic deficit, or ischemic features recur or progress rather than resolving as expected.

The revised imaging analysis also avoids treating angiography and ultrasound as single-role variables. Duplex ultrasound may be the first vascular test in an accessible lesion, CT angiography may map deeper anatomy or clarify a competing diagnosis, and catheter angiography can serve as confirmation, treatment planning, or treatment itself. A report that mentions angiography therefore does not imply that angiography was the first diagnostic study, and ultrasound used only for aspiration or follow-up does not represent diagnostic vascular ultrasound.

Treatment should similarly be interpreted as anatomy- and severity-dependent rather than as a historical contest between open and endovascular techniques. The included reports describe both open vascular reconstruction/ligation and less invasive embolization, thrombin injection, or stent-based treatment. The available evidence does not support formal comparisons among these approaches because case selection, vessel caliber, rupture, arteriovenous fistula, neurologic compression, and local expertise differ substantially across reports. The prior manuscript wording suggesting a temporal treatment shift has therefore been softened rather than converted into an unsupported era comparison.

The two newly included reports reinforce the need for branch-vessel awareness. Armato and Czarnecki described an inferior lateral geniculate pseudoaneurysm after partial lateral synovectomy, with MRI and digital subtraction angiography establishing the diagnosis [58]. Milankov et al. described a medial inferior genicular pseudoaneurysm after hamstring-autograft ACL reconstruction; distal pulses remained present, noninvasive imaging preceded angiographic confirmation, and ligation led to full functional recovery at six months [59]. The third report identified during review could not be retrieved, so it was not used to infer patients, vessels, treatments, or outcomes. Limitations

This review is limited by the underlying evidence base. Case reports and small case series are highly susceptible to publication bias and selective reporting; severe, unusual, technically interesting, or successfully treated cases are more likely to be published, while mild, spontaneously thrombosing, conservatively managed, or unrecognized cases are likely underrepresented. Therefore, the observed frequencies cannot estimate incidence, risk, or comparative treatment effectiveness.

Reporting quality and follow-up were inconsistent, and some older reports did not provide sufficient detail to classify timing, imaging sequence, or final functional outcome. The literature also spans different arthroscopic techniques and eras without a common denominator. A formal protocol was not prospectively registered. The initial reference-list search was not count-logged separately, so a retrospective hand-search count was not invented; the peer-review citation audit is transparently reported as an add-on pathway. One peer-review-identified publication could not be retrieved and was not included.

Although no full-text report was excluded solely for language, requiring sufficient patient-level information for reliable extraction can still introduce language-related selection bias. No translation was required among the full texts ultimately assessed, but non-English or difficult-to-access reports may remain underrepresented. Finally, the design-matched JBI appraisal is descriptive and does not overcome the intrinsic selection and reporting limitations of case-based literature.

Conclusions

Iatrogenic pseudoaneurysm after arthroscopic knee procedures is rare but clinically important because it can resemble routine postoperative complications and may be diagnosed days to months after surgery. Published cases involve both the popliteal artery and multiple peri-genicular branches. Recurrent or progressive swelling, hemarthrosis, focal pulsatility, neurologic change, ischemic features, or symptoms out of proportion to the expected course should prompt consideration of vascular imaging. Treatment must be individualized to the injured vessel, lesion characteristics, associated complications, and local expertise. Because the evidence is exclusively case-based, these findings should be used as a literature-informed warning framework rather than as estimates of incidence or as a validated diagnostic or treatment algorithm.
