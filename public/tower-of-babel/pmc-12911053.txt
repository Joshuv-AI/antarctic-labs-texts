Assessing health service satisfaction among beneficiaries in Cameroon: a cross-sectional study to inform healthcare improvement
Authors: Justin Ndié, Jean-Pierre Yves Awono Noah, Francis Ateba Ndongo, Rogacien Kana Dongmo, Joseph Nelson Siewe Fodjo, Fanny Mayoh, Caroline Teh Monteh, Carolle Dongmo Temgoua, Yembe Wepnyu Njamnshi, Guy Christophe Eloundou Onguene, Tatiana Avang Nkoa Palisson, Félicité Naah Tabala, Christian Noël Bayiha, Basile Keugoung, Jérôme Ateudjieu, Anne Cécile Zoung-Kanyi Bissek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient satisfaction is a key indicator of healthcare quality and an important determinant of service utilisation and adherence. In Cameroon, routine monitoring of patient experience is limited and existing evidence remains fragmented.ObjectiveTo assess satisfaction with health services among users of Cameroonian health facilities and identify associated factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2022 to June 2023 in 69 health facilities across eight regions. Participants ≥ 21 years old were recruited through voluntary sampling. Satisfaction was measured using a culturally adapted PSQ-18. Descriptive analyses summarised the seven subscales. Group differences were examined using Welch’s ANOVA, and determinants of satisfaction were assessed through multivariable robust Gamma regression.ResultsA total of 559 participants were enrolled (median age 30 years; 76.9% women). Overall satisfaction was moderate (mean 3.49/5; 62.3/100). Communication, technical quality and interpersonal manners had the highest scores, whereas financial aspects and accessibility were the lowest. Significant disparities were found by facility type, facility category and region. In the adjusted model, satisfaction was about 10% lower in public facilities compared with private facilities (expβ = 0.90; 95% CI: 0.85–0.96). Compared with district hospitals, satisfaction was 14% higher in Regional Hospitals (expβ = 1.14; 95% CI: 1.06–1.21) and 20% higher in Integrated Health Centres (expβ = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.13–1.28). Regionally, satisfaction was 10–20% lower in Adamawa, East, Far North and Douala/Littoral compared with the Centre region. Sociodemographic variables—including age, sex, education, income, and residence—were not associated with satisfaction.ConclusionPatient satisfaction in Cameroon is moderate and shaped primarily by structural and organisational factors, with substantial disparities across facility types, facility categories and regions. Targeted improvements in district-level services, financial accessibility, and public facility organisation could meaningfully enhance patient experience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13959-0.Keywords: Patient satisfaction, Quality of health care, Health facilities, PSQ-18, Cross-sectional studies, Cameroon

Background

Patient satisfaction is an important component of the evaluation of healthcare quality and of the quality improvement process within health facilities. It reflects the extent to which patients perceive that obtaining and using a healthcare service generates positive feelings [1–4]. It remains a key indicator for measuring the quality of healthcare services and one of the critical determinants of success in healthcare delivery [5].

Over the past decades, healthcare systems have increasingly shifted toward a model in which patients, as beneficiaries of these services, are regarded as clients whose needs must be satisfied. Health facilities are therefore expected to deliver high-quality, efficient, equitable, timely and patient-centred care grounded in scientific evidence [6, 7]. In this context, the availability, accessibility and quality of healthcare services are essential determinants of beneficiaries’ satisfaction. High levels of satisfaction are closely associated with patient well-being, increased utilisation of services and better treatment adherence [8]. Studies conducted in various settings have reported wide variations in satisfaction levels, ranging from 50% to 94% [2, 3, 8–14]. Recent evidence from neighbouring West and Central African settings demonstrates that patient satisfaction remains a key but heterogeneous indicator across low- and middle-income countries. Recent evidence from neighbouring African countries also shows substantial variability in patient satisfaction across health systems. A systematic review from Nigeria has highlighted marked differences in satisfaction levels and underscored the influence of waiting time, staff attitude and accessibility on patient experience [15]. In Rwanda, a recent 2024 study conducted at Remera Rukoma District Hospital similarly reported heterogeneous satisfaction levels, with 52.6% of patients being highly satisfied and notable dissatisfaction related to wait times, communication gaps and delays in service delivery [16]. These findings mirror patterns observed in other LMICs and illustrate how service organisation, communication practices and operational efficiency continue to influence patient experience across the region.

In Cameroon, as in several African countries, there is no national system for routine monitoring of patient satisfaction, nor any regulatory framework requiring its measurement at the level of health facilities [2]. The few existing studies provide useful insights but remain limited in scope. For instance, a 2020 local study reported that 85% of users were satisfied with the overall quality of health services, while the study by Moussa found satisfaction levels of 59.1% for access to care and 60.8% for patient reception [12, 13]. However, these studies were conducted in isolated settings, often relied on non-validated tools and did not allow for broader interpretation across regions or facility types.

Overall, the evidence available in Cameroon remains fragmented, facility-specific and methodologically heterogeneous, leaving a critical gap in understanding patient satisfaction at a national or multi-regional level. No study has yet examined satisfaction across several regions and levels of health facilities using a standardised and validated measurement instrument.

The present study therefore aimed to assess patients’ satisfaction with healthcare services in Cameroon and to identify factors associated with this satisfaction across different regions, levels and types of health facilities.

Methods

Study design and

A cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2022 to June 2023 in 69 health facilities of the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, and 6th categories across eight regions of Cameroon.

Study population

The study population consisted of health service users aged 21 and over who had consented to participate.

Sampling and sample size

Sampling

Sampling was conducted at three levels: regions, health facilities, and individual participants.

Regional selection: Eight regions (Adamawa, Centre, East, Far North, Littoral, North, West and South) were conveniently included based on feasible and secure access for data collection. Yaoundé and Douala, the country’s two largest urban centres, were retained because their demographic and epidemiological profiles closely reflect national patterns. The North-West and South-West regions were excluded due to ongoing sociopolitical instability and security constraints that prevented safe and standardised data collection.

Health facility selection: Within each selected region, health facilities were chosen conveniently according to feasibility and service volume, prioritising those reporting at least 100 outpatient visits per month. Facilities of various ownership types (public, private and faith-based) and categories were included to reflect the diversity of service delivery contexts commonly used by the population.

Participant recruitment: Eligible health service users were recruited through voluntary sampling [17, 18]. The study was presented to individuals exiting outpatient services, and inclusion occurred only if they actively chose to participate after receiving information about the study. The opportunistic nature of patient attendance in outpatient departments, the absence of a structured sampling frame, and the sensitivity of evaluating satisfaction with healthcare services made it impractical to identify or summon randomly selected individuals without potentially influencing their interaction with health personnel. Recruiting only those who were already present and willing to participate reduced the risk of discomfort or perceived pressure and ensured that participation remained entirely voluntary.

Data collection tool

Patient satisfaction was assessed using a structured questionnaire (Additional file 2) comprising two sections. The first captured sociodemographic and economic characteristics, as well as self-rated health on a 0–100 visual analogue scale. The second consisted of a contextualised version of the 18-item Patient Satisfaction Questionnaire Short Form (PSQ-18) [20–22]. The original English PSQ-18 was translated into French, back-translated, and reviewed by a panel of clinicians and a methodologist to ensure conceptual equivalence. A pre-final version was piloted among health service users to assess clarity and acceptability, leading to minor wording adjustments. The adapted PSQ-18 assessed communication, technical competence, interpersonal manner, financial accessibility and overall satisfaction on a five-point Likert scale. The internal consistency of subscale and overall scale were assessed. This process ensured both contextual relevance and psychometric robustness for evaluating patient satisfaction in Cameroonian health facilities.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected by 32 interviewers (staff from the central, regional, and others facilities levels) who were trained on the study methodology and in administering the questionnaire using Kobo Collect on smartphones. During the recruitment of each participant, the data collectors introduced themselves and explained the purpose of the study. Information regarding the participant’s eligibility for the survey was also obtained. The information notice was provided, informed consent was obtained, and the questionnaire was administered.

Data management and analysis

Data management

The variable of interest, “beneficiaries’ satisfaction with health services”, measured with the PSQ-18 tool, was grouped into seven subscales, allowing for an assessment of different dimensions of beneficiaries’ satisfaction with health services [20, 21], (Additional file 3, Table A). Some PSQ-18 items were coded so that agreement reflected satisfaction with health services, while other items were coded so that agreement reflected dissatisfaction with health services [20, 21]. All items were scored so that higher scores reflected high satisfaction with health services [20, 21] (Additional file 3, Table B).

Independent variables included sociodemographic, economic and facility-level characteristics. Age was analysed as continuous variables. Sex (female/male), responsibility for healthcare costs (self/other), education (not in school, primary, secondary, superior), marital status (single, married, cohabiting, divorced, widow(er)), monthly income (no income; <50,000; 50,000–149,000; 150,000–250,000; >250,000 XAF), area of residence (rural, semi-urban, urban) and region (eight study regions) were treated as categorical variables. Facility-level characteristics included ownership (public, private secular, private confessional) and statutory category. In Cameroon, statutory categories range from 1st to 6th, with higher categories (1st–3rd) corresponding to specialised referral hospitals (General, Central and Regional Hospitals) and lower categories (4th–6th) to district hospital, medical subdivision hospital and integrated health centres providing primary-level care.

For regression analyses, all categorical variables were dummy coded to allow explicit control over the choice of reference categories [23] and to enhance interpretability of the exponentiated coefficients. Reference groups were selected based on contextual and analytical relevance: <50,000 XAF for monthly income (corresponding to the threshold just above the national minimum wage), urban residence for area of residence (representing the setting with the highest concentration of healthcare resources), Yaoundé/Centre for region (political capital with a high density of higher-category hospitals and specialised staff), ‘primary or less’ for education level (lowest formal schooling), married or cohabiting for marital status (individuals living in a union), self-payment for responsibility for healthcare costs, and 4th-category facilities for health facility category, as district hospitals in this category are expected to manage the majority of common conditions according to national health sector policy.

Data analysis

Descriptives statistics

Continuous variables were described using the median with the interquartile range (IQR) or mean with standard deviation and categorical variables were described using frequencies and proportions.

The overall average satisfaction of health service users and each of his dimensions was measured by a score ranging from 1 to 5. The closer the score was to 5, the higher the level of satisfaction, and the further the score was from 5, the lower the level of satisfaction. In order to facilitate interpretation and allow comparison with studies using standardised metrics, all satisfaction scores originally measured on a 1–5 Likert scale were linearly transformed to a 0–100 scale using the following formula:

This transformation maps the minimum and maximum possible scores to 0 and 100 respectively.

For internal consistency, McDonald’s Omega (ω) was preferred over Cronbach’s Alpha (α) for subscales with three or more items because ω provides a more accurate estimate of reliability under realistic psychometric conditions. Unlike α, which assumes tau-equivalence and equal item contributions, ω is based on the actual factor loadings and is therefore considered a more robust and theoretically sound indicator of internal consistency [24–26]. For two-item subscales, ω could not be estimated and reliability was assessed using inter-item correlations (Spearman correlations) and the Spearman–Brown coefficient [27, 28]. Confidence intervals for Spearman correlations were computed using the Bonett and Wright variance estimator, which provides more accurate and robust interval estimates for rank-based correlations [29].

Univariate analysis

For each of the 7 dimensions measured, satisfaction levels were compared by region, by type of facility (public or private) and by level of health facility using one-factor Robust ANOVA with post-hoc Games–Howell correction. Classical ANOVA assumes normality and homogeneity of variances, assumptions that are often violated by Likert-derived scores. Testing these assumptions in large samples frequently produces statistically significant deviations that are not practically meaningful. Robust ANOVA methods such as Welch’s test, which do not rely on these assumptions and provide more reliable control of Type I error under heteroscedasticity and non-normality, were therefore used directly [30–33].

Multivariable analysis

Since the scores were asymmetric to the right, a Robust Gamma regression model was used to identify factors associated with satisfaction among health service users. Aggregated PSQ-18 subscale and overall satisfaction scores were treated as continuous variables. Distributional diagnostics showed that all scores were strictly positive and exhibited marked right-skewness. Histograms with density curves for each subscale and the overall score are presented in additional file 4 (Figure A). Because ordinary least squares regression requires normally distributed residuals and homoscedasticity, assumptions violated in our dataset, a generalized linear model with a Gamma family and log-link was applied. This model is appropriate for asymmetrical, positive continuous outcomes and allows multiplicative interpretation of effects on mean satisfaction scores.

To assess robustness, sensitivity analyses were conducted using robust OLS, beta regression, and ordinal logistic regression models (Additional file 4, Table C). All models yielded consistent effect estimates, but the Robust Gamma specification demonstrated superior overall fit, further supporting its use as the primary analytic approach. In Gamma regression with a log link, coefficients describe multiplicative effects on the expected mean of the outcome. Exponentiated coefficients (exp(β)) are therefore interpreted as mean ratios, representing proportional increases or decreases in the satisfaction score relative to the reference category [34, 35].

Given the use of robust Gamma regression, only a limited number of assumptions required verification. Linearity for the continuous predictor (age) was assessed using the Box–Tidwell procedure by testing the age × ln(age) interaction [36, 37], which showed no evidence of non-linearity (Additional file 5, Table D). Independence of observations was ensured by the cross-sectional study design. Multicollinearity was examined using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), with values < 5 considered acceptable and the tolerance statistics are well above 0.2 [37, 38]. All VIFs were below the commonly accepted threshold of 5, and tolerance values were above 0.2, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. The predictors were therefore retained in the regression models without adjustment (Additional file 5, Table E).

All tests were two tailed and the regression coefficients and their 95% confidence intervals were presented. Data analysis was performed using XLStat 2019 and SPSS 27.

Results

Socio-demographic and facility-related characteristics of study participants

Participants were distributed across all eight study regions, with the largest proportion recruited in Douala (37.4%), followed by Yaoundé (16.8%) and the South region (13.4%). Most respondents were enrolled in public health facilities (64.6%), while 15.6% and 19.9% attended private secular and private confessional facilities, respectively. Regarding statutory categories, participants were mainly recruited from 3rd-category (35.4%) and 4th-category (31.8%) health facilities, with smaller proportions from 2nd-, 5th- and 6th-category facilities (Table 1).

PSQ-18 satisfaction sub-category and overall score and reliability

Reliability estimates varied across subscales. For two-item subscales, Spearman–Brown coefficients ranged from acceptable to good for General Satisfaction (0.65), Interpersonal Manner (0.56), Communication (0.60) and Time Spent with Clinician (0.52), supported by moderate inter-item correlations. In contrast, the Financial Aspects subscale showed very low coherence (Spearman–Brown = 0.11; r = 0.07), indicating limited internal consistency. Among multi-item subscales, Technical Quality (ω = 0.75) and Accessibility and Convenience (ω = 0.61) demonstrated adequate reliability. The overall satisfaction scale showed excellent internal consistency (ω = 0.92), confirming strong coherence across all 18 items (Table 3).

Overall satisfaction had a mean score of 3.49 out of 5. Subscale means ranged from 3.17 to 3.63, with the highest scores observed for Communication (3.63), Technical Quality (3.62) and Interpersonal Manner (3.60). General Satisfaction averaged 3.53, while Time Spent with Clinician (3.47) and Accessibility & Convenience (3.42) indicated moderate satisfaction. Financial Aspects had the lowest mean score (3.17), suggesting that cost-related elements of care were perceived less favourably. When rescaled to a 0–100 metric for ease of interpretation, overall satisfaction reached 62.3 points. Subscale scores ranged from 54.2 points for Financial Aspects to 65.8 points for Communication, with Technical Quality (65.5 points) and Interpersonal Manner (65.0 points) also showing relatively higher satisfaction compared with other dimensions (Table 3).

Robust comparison of satisfaction scores across groups using Welch ANOVA

Figure 1 displays the estimated mean satisfaction scores across the seven PSQ-18 subscales for each of the eight study regions. Yaoundé and the West region consistently showed the highest satisfaction levels across multiple subscales, with mean scores generally ranging between 3.7 and 4.1, particularly for communication, technical quality, and general satisfaction. In contrast, the Adamawa and East regions recorded lower satisfaction levels (≈ 3.0–3.4) across most dimensions, especially for financial aspects and time spent with clinician. The Far North, North, and South regions showed intermediate but variable patterns, with mean scores generally between 3.2 and 3.7, depending on the dimension (Fig. 1).

Variation in patient satisfaction across regions for the Seven PSQ-18 subscales (Robust ANOVA with Welch Test)

Welch’s ANOVA results (Additional file 6, Table F) confirmed that these regional differences were statistically significant for all seven satisfaction dimensions (all p < 0.001). Post-hoc Games–Howell comparisons (Additional file 6, Table G) further revealed that Yaoundé scored significantly higher than Adamawa, East, and Littoral on several subscales, most notably communication (differences ranging from + 0.72 to + 1.00, p < 0.05), interpersonal manner (+ 0.73 to + 0.80, p < 0.05), and financial aspects (+ 0.60, p = 0.007). Conversely, Adamawa scored significantly lower than Centre/Yaoundé on general satisfaction (–0.56, p = 0.047), and East scored significantly lower than Yaoundé and West on several domains including interpersonal manner, communication, and accessibility (all p < 0.05).

These statistical results align with the graphical trends in Fig. 1, where the Centre (Yaoundé) and West regions visibly cluster at the upper range of satisfaction across subscales, in contrast to Adamawa and East, which consistently occupy the lower end of the satisfaction spectrum.

Figure 2 presents the estimated mean satisfaction scores across the seven PSQ-18 subscales for each category of health facilities. Integrated Health Centres (means ranging from 3.50 to 4.00) and Regional Hospitals (3.30 to 3.90) consistently demonstrated the highest levels of satisfaction, particularly for communication (IHC ≈ 4.0; RH ≈ 3.9), interpersonal manners (IHC ≈ 3.9; RH ≈ 3.7) and technical quality (IHC ≈ 4.0; RH ≈ 3.9). In contrast, Medical Subdivisional Centres showed the lowest satisfaction levels across nearly all subscales (≈ 2.7–3.2). District Hospitals displayed intermediate values (≈ 3.3–3.6), whereas Central Hospitals showed more variable performance, with notably lower scores on technical quality and accessibility and convenience (Fig. 2).

Variation in patient satisfaction across facility categories for the seven PSQ-18 subscales (Robust ANOVA with Welch Test)

Welch’s ANOVA (Additional file 7, Table H) confirmed that these differences across facility categories were statistically significant for all seven dimensions (all p < 0.01). Post-hoc Games–Howell comparisons (Additional file 7, Table I) further indicated that Integrated Health Centres and Regional Hospitals scored significantly higher than District Hospitals and Medical Subdivisional Centres on several key subscales, including communication, interpersonal manner, and technical quality (all p < 0.05). These statistical results are fully consistent with the graphical patterns observed in Fig. 2, where Integrated Health Centres and Regional Hospitals visibly cluster at the upper end of the satisfaction scale.

Figure 3 illustrates the estimated mean satisfaction scores across the seven PSQ-18 subscales according to the type of health facility. Overall, Lay Private and Confessional Private facilities consistently showed higher satisfaction levels than public facilities across most dimensions. Lay Private facilities displayed the highest means for general satisfaction, technical quality, communication, and time spent with clinician (≈ 3.7–3.8), while Confessional Private facilities followed closely with similar patterns but slightly lower scores on financial aspects. In contrast, Public facilities showed the lowest satisfaction levels across all subscales, with particularly marked gaps in financial aspects (≈ 3.1) and in accessibility and convenience (≈ 3.3). Differences between facility types were most apparent for financial-related dimensions, whereas interpersonal manner and communication showed comparatively smaller variations.

Variation in patient satisfaction across public, lay private, and confessional facilities for the seven PSQ-18 subscales (Robust ANOVA with Welch Test)

These visual patterns were supported by the Welch ANOVA results (Additional file 8, Table J), which confirmed statistically significant overall differences between facility types for general satisfaction (F = 6.47, p = 0.002), technical quality (F = 3.42, p = 0.035), financial aspects (F = 8.10, p < 0.001), and accessibility and convenience (F = 8.28, p < 0.001). No significant overall differences were observed for interpersonal manner, communication, or time spent with clinician (Additional file 8, Table K). For technical quality, although the Welch test indicated a significant global difference, Games–Howell post-hoc comparisons (Additional file 8, Table K) did not identify any pairwise contrasts that remained significant after correction for multiple testing, indicating that the differences between individual facility types were small in magnitude despite a detectable overall effect.

Taken together, these findings suggest that satisfaction varies meaningfully by facility type for domains linked to structural quality, financial accessibility, and logistical convenience, whereas interpersonal and communication-related experiences appear relatively similar across facility types.

Discussion

Overview of the main findings

This multi-regional study offers a detailed assessment of patient satisfaction in Cameroon using the validated PSQ-18. Overall satisfaction was moderate (3.49/5; 62.3/100). The highest scores were observed for communication (65.8/100), technical quality (65.5/100) and interpersonal manner (65.0/100), underscoring the strength of provider–patient interactions. In contrast, financial aspects (54.2/100) and accessibility and convenience (60.5/100) performed least well, reflecting persistent economic and organisational constraints within the health system. Significant disparities emerged across facility types, statutory categories, and regions. Satisfaction was 10% lower in public facilities compared with private ones (expβ = 0.90) and markedly higher in Regional Hospitals (+ 14%) and Integrated Health Centres (+ 20%) compared with District Hospitals. Regional differences were also pronounced, with satisfaction 10–20% lower in Adamawa, East, Far North, and Douala/Littoral relative to the Centre region. The absence of associations with sociodemographic factors further indicates that organisational and structural characteristics of care environments play a greater role in shaping patient experience than individual attributes.

Explaining the observed satisfaction patterns

The relatively high scores for communication, interpersonal manner and technical quality suggest strong relational and clinical engagement by frontline staff. In contrast, the low score for financial aspects (54.2/100) reflects structural limitations in a system still dominated by out-of-pocket payments, which account for over 70% of total health expenditure in Cameroon and remain a major barrier to equitable access, consistent with evidence from Sub-Saharan Africa [39–42]. The very low internal consistency of this subscale (Spearman–Brown = 0.11) indicates substantial heterogeneity in how respondents perceive the financial dimension of care, likely reflecting differences in service fees, medicine availability and facility type. As the study did not directly measure pricing or stock-outs, these mechanisms should be viewed as plausible hypotheses rather than definitive explanations. Differences across facility categories likewise reflect underlying organisational structures [43]. District hospitals, which serve as the first referral level within district health systems and manage a wider range of clinical conditions than primary care facilities, often handle more complex case-mix and substantial patient loads [44]. In many low- and middle-income settings, these hospitals face persistent staffing challenges and heavy workloads due to limited human resources and the need to provide both general and referral services [45]. Furthermore, health supply chains in such contexts are known to be fragmented and prone to disruption, affecting the reliable availability of essential medicines and supplies across facility levels. In our study, integrated Health Centres (6th category) and Regional Hospitals (3rd category) performed better than District Hospitals (4th category).

Regional disparities align with well-documented structural differences in Cameroon. ECAM household surveys show that poverty levels are highest in the Far North, North and North-West, while Yaoundé, Douala, the Centre and the Littoral present the lowest deprivation [46], affecting households’ ability to seek and sustain access to care [47]. Likewise, other national studies highlight regional inequalities in service access and availability of qualified health personnel, with the Centre, Littoral and West regions better supplied than Adamawa, the East and the Far North. Similar patterns are seen in the distribution and density of health facilities [43, 48]. These combined socioeconomic and structural disparities provide plausible explanations for the lower satisfaction observed in Adamawa, the East, the Far North and the Littoral compared with the Centre region.

Importantly, the absence of associations between satisfaction and sociodemographic characteristics in our study is fully consistent with the conceptual foundations of the PSQ-18. The instrument was explicitly designed by RAND to capture patients’ evaluations of organisational, interpersonal and systemic attributes of care, such as technical quality, communication, accessibility, and financial arrangements, rather than characteristics of the patients themselves [21]. In this framework, satisfaction is expected to vary primarily as a function of how services are organised and delivered, which aligns with the pattern observed in our data.

Challenges in comparing patient satisfaction across heterogeneous health-system studies

The satisfaction profile observed in this study differs markedly from the extremely high satisfaction levels (> 75–90%) reported in several African studies [1–57]. However, direct comparison across studies is inherently problematic because of substantial methodological heterogeneity. Most prior studies relied on satisfaction instruments other than the PSQ-18 and assessed a wide range of services, including physical medicine, oncology, surgery, antenatal care, HIV services, treatment outcomes, health insurance schemes, post-abortion care, inpatient care, general health services, cardiology, and family planning. In addition, analytical approaches varied considerably across studies.

Among the few African studies that employed the PSQ-18 [58, 59], satisfaction was typically reported as a crude “percentage satisfaction” based on categorical scoring, a practice that may inflate satisfaction estimates. In contrast, our study applied a linear rescaling of scores to a standardised 0–100 metric, in line with established methodological recommendations [20, 21]. This approach situates respondents along a continuous satisfaction continuum rather than estimating the proportion of users who are satisfied. Consequently, values such as 65.8/100 for communication, 54.2/100 for financial aspects, and 62.3/100 for overall satisfaction should not be interpreted as percentages. Linear rescaling preserves interval-level measurement and yields more conservative, precise, and comparable estimates [21, 60, 61]. Accordingly, the apparent discrepancy between our overall satisfaction score (62.3/100) and satisfaction levels exceeding 90% in previous studies is more plausibly attributable to differences in measurement quality and scoring practices than to genuine variation in patient experience.

Our findings can also be discussed in relation to non-African studies that used the PSQ-18, notably those conducted in Bhutan [62] and Nepal [63], although differences in study scope and health-system organisation limit direct comparability. In the Bhutanese study by Dorgi et al., conducted in two regional referral hospitals, overall patient satisfaction exceeded 4.0 on the 5-point Likert scale, with financial aspects rated highest and accessibility and convenience rated lowest. By contrast, our study included 69 health facilities across multiple levels of care and reported a more moderate overall satisfaction score (mean = 3.49/5), with subscale means ranging from 3.17 to 3.63. Notably, financial aspects were the lowest-rated domain (3.17) in our analysis, indicating stronger perceived financial barriers in the Cameroonian context.

Similarly, Poudel et al., using the PSQ-18, reported relatively high mean satisfaction scores across domains, with interpersonal manner, communication, and overall satisfaction ranking among the highest dimensions. While the relative ordering of domains closely mirrors our findings, absolute satisfaction levels were lower in our study, where these domains scored between 3.60 and 3.63 on the 5-point scale. This suggests that broader facility coverage and greater heterogeneity in service delivery may contribute to more moderate average satisfaction scores.

Beyond descriptive comparisons, determinants of satisfaction also differed. In the Bhutanese study, satisfaction varied between the two referral hospitals and was associated with individual-level characteristics, whereas the Nepalese study was conducted in a single referral hospital. In contrast, our analysis showed that satisfaction was driven primarily by facility-level and regional factors, with no meaningful sociodemographic effects. Taken together, these findings suggest that PSQ-18–based studies restricted to a limited number of referral hospitals may overestimate satisfaction and fail to capture system-wide variability. Finally, the strong psychometric performance of our instrument (ω = 0.92) supports the robustness of our estimates and reinforces the validity of our findings, while underscoring the need for caution when interpreting cross-study differences in reported satisfaction levels.

Implications for health system improvement

These findings have important implications for health system strengthening in Cameroon. The predominance of facility-level and regional determinants of patient satisfaction underscores the need to prioritise organisational and structural reforms over individual-level interventions. In particular, strengthening the organisation of care in District Hospitals, through improved workflow, patient flow management, and the reliable availability of essential supplies, should be a central focus of quality improvement efforts.

The consistently low performance of the financial dimension highlights the urgency of expanding financial protection mechanisms, including accelerated progress towards Universal Health Coverage, to reduce out-of-pocket expenditures and improve equity in access to care. At the same time, the relatively strong performance observed in interpersonal domains suggests an opportunity to consolidate existing strengths through institutionalised training in patient-centred communication and respectful care, particularly in public facilities and lower-performing regions.

Given the methodological challenges identified in comparing satisfaction estimates across settings, establishing a national patient-experience monitoring system based on validated and standardised instruments such as the PSQ-18 would enable more accurate benchmarking, strengthen accountability, and support continuous quality improvement across facility levels and regions. Finally, future research should adopt longitudinal and mixed-methods designs to better capture temporal changes in patient experience and to elucidate contextual drivers of dissatisfaction, particularly in persistently low-scoring regions such as Adamawa and the East.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, data were not collected in the North-West and South-West regions due to insecurity, limiting national representativeness and preventing assessment of satisfaction in conflict-affected settings. Second, voluntary sampling may have introduced selection bias, with more engaged or more satisfied users potentially overrepresented. Third, satisfaction was self-reported, raising the possibility of information and social desirability biases. The financial aspects subscale also showed low internal consistency, indicating less stable responses and warranting cautious interpretation. These factors may affect the generalisability and precision of some estimates.

Nonetheless, the study presents important strengths. It uses a large, multi-regional sample spanning diverse facility types and statutory categories, providing a broad picture of patient experience in Cameroon. The culturally adapted and validated PSQ-18 strengthens the reliability of most dimensions, and the use of robust analytic approaches—including Welch ANOVA and Gamma regression with heteroscedasticity-consistent estimators, enhances methodological validity. Together, these strengths support the credibility of the findings and their relevance for health system improvement.

Conclusion

This study shows that overall satisfaction with health services in Cameroon is moderate, with substantial disparities across regions and types of health facilities. Structural and organisational features, particularly facility type, service accessibility, and financial barriers, were the main determinants of satisfaction, whereas sociodemographic characteristics had little influence.

Improving financial accessibility, strengthening infrastructure and human resources, and enhancing provider communication and patient-centred care are critical levers for improving the user experience. These findings can inform the Ministry of Public Health in designing a national patient satisfaction monitoring system and guiding targeted quality-improvement interventions, especially in lower-performing regions and public facilities.

Future research should incorporate longitudinal or mixed methods approaches to track satisfaction over time, explore patient experiences more deeply, and evaluate the impact of reforms aimed at improving the quality of care. Strengthening governance and promoting regional equity remain essential to building public trust and advancing quality within Cameroon’s health system.
