Hydrothermal Synthesis of MnO2 Microspheres and Their Degradation of Toluene
Authors: Meijuan Lu, Yulian Ma, Danping Li, Min Jiang, Chenglong Yu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractVarious urchin-like MnO2 materials were obtained with a facile hydrothermal method through controlling the Mn precursor, reaction time, and reaction temperature. The property of MnO2 materials was characterized by scanning electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction, and H2 temperature-programmed reduction. The results showed that the Mn precursor could significantly impact the morphology of as-prepared MnO2. When the precursor was Mn(CH3COO)2·4H2O, the MnO2 morphology consisted of tennis-like microspheres assembled by nanorods. While the precursor was MnCl2·4H2O, the sample morphology was a chestnut shell, and the samples were sea urchin microspheres, as the precursor was MnSO4·H2O. At the same time, the morphology of MnO2 was affected by hydrothermal time and temperature. The nanoneedles on the microsphere surface gradually lengthened with increasing hydrothermal time and hydrothermal temperature, until nanowires were formed. MnO2 crystallinity was also influenced by hydrothermal temperature. It was γ-MnO2 as the temperature was 50 and 80 °C while evolved to be α-MnO2 and β-MnO2 when the temperature increased to 140 °C. As MnO2 (MnO2-1 h, MnO2-2 h, MnO2-4 h, and MnO2-6 h) was prepared to degrade toluene, all the samples could completely catalyze toluene at the temperature of 225 °C. However, the MnO2-4 h showed the best catalytic effect at a lower temperature.

Introduction

Toluene is one of the important basic chemicals and has been extensively used in the process of industrial products, such as plastics, solvents, pesticides, dyes, adhesives, etc.1 However, it could be harmful to our respiratory system and nervous system with long-term exposure.2 Toluene is also one of the main contributors of photochemical smog and urban haze.3 Hence, its elimination is an urgent environmental concern.

Among the various techniques for toluene elimination, catalytic degradation has enjoyed wide application for its easy operation, energy conservation, high efficiency, and less secondary contamination.4 A catalyst plays a vital role in the application of this technique.5 Hence, it is essential to develop a new catalyst with higher activity, lower cost, and better stabilization performance. In this regard, MnO2 has been widely used in VOC clearance for its unique properties, such as multiple valence of manganese (Mn2+, Mn3+, and Mn4+),6,7 strong oxidizability, low cost, and high environmental compatibility.8 In addition, Mn oxide is a typical metal oxide that contains numerous electron centers, which could adsorb O2 forming electron-rich species, and gives abundant lattice oxygen to promote the catalytic reaction.9

Various morphologies of MnO2, such as 0D tunnel structure nanoparticles,10 1D tunnel structure nanowires, nanorods, nanotubes, nanobelts,11,12 2D layer structure,13 and 3D mesh structure,14,15 have been synthesized and used to degrade VOCs. Among those different morphology MnO2 nanomaterials, hierarchical MnO2 assembled by 1D/2D subunits (such as the sea urchin-like shape) has received immense attention in recent years for its large surface area and pore volume.16 In addition, the unique edge and tip structure could effectively promote electron transfer in the catalytic reaction and show excellent performance in heterogeneous catalytic oxidation.17 For the synthesis method, coprecipitation,10 the sol–gel method,18 and the hydrothermal method19 have been reported to be used for the preparation of hierarchical 3D MnO2 materials. Among these methods, the hydrothermal method has been widely applied owing to its no-template and mild hydrothermal conditions in the synthesis process.8,20

In addition, Ye et al. found that MnO2-CeO2 synthesized using the hydrothermal method had higher Hg0 removal efficiency at lower temperatures.21 The work reported by Yao et al. showed that the MnOx-CeO2 catalyst synthesized using the hydrothermal method achieved the excellent catalytic performance for NO at low temperatures.22 Therefore, the hydrothermal method is an advantageous method in catalyst synthesis. However, the preparation conditions including Mn(II) precursors, reaction time, temperature, etc. could affect the structure and morphology of MnO2. Lyu. et al.23 found that catalysts prepared with different Mn(II) precursors had different toluene catalytic activation, and MnO2-Ac showed the best activity because of its specific surface area, oxygen vacancy concentration, and content of adsorbed oxygen. In addition, Yang et al. found that the hydrothermal reaction temperature showed a significant effect on the MnO2 phase and morphology. When the temperature was 90 °C, MnO2 was urchin-like while transformed into a flower-ball at the temperature of 160 °C.14 On the other hand, when the reaction time was 1 h, MnO2 was the urchin γ-MnO2 microspheres, while hollow β-MnO2 microspheres formed as the reaction time prolonged to 9 h.16 These studies indicated that the morphology and crystallinity of MnO2 could be affected by the Mn(II) precursor, reaction temperature, and reaction time, which further influenced the catalytic activity of VOCs.23,24 Unfortunately, the formation pathway about sea urchin-like shaped MnO2 was still unclear, and there were still few reports about the impact of sea urchin MnO2 prepared at different hydrothermal times on VOC catalytic degradation.

Herein, we synthesized MnO2 nanomaterials with the hydrothermal method using MnCl2·4H2O, Mn(CH3COO)2·4H2O, and MnSO4·H2O as precursors. Then, the formation process of MnO2 was studied by changing the hydrothermal temperature and the hydrothermal time, and the formation mechanism of nanoneedles on the surface of sea urchin MnO2 was discussed. Moreover, the toluene catalytic activity of the MnO2 preparation at different hydrothermal times was also evaluated.

Experiments and Materials

MnO2 Nanomaterials Synthesis

For the effect of the Mn(II) precursor, different MnO2 samples were synthesized separately using MnCl2·4H2O, Mn(CH3COO)2, and MnSO4·H2O as precursors. Briefly, 3.1633 g of MnCl2·4H2O, 3.9142 g of Mn(CH3COO)2, and 2.7 g of MnSO4·H2O were separately added into 13.185 g of (NH3)2S2O8 and then were respectively dissolved in some deionized water to obtain 80.0 mL of solution; the solution was then placed into a 100 mL Teflon autoclave and was heated at 80 °C for 4 h. The obtained solid products were washed to neutral conditions using deionized water. Finally, the samples were dried at 80 °C until they were completely dry. The obtained catalysts were respectively named MnO2-Cl (MnCl2·4H2O as the precursor), MnO2-Ac (Mn(CH3COO)2 as the precursor), and MnO2-S (MnSO4·H2O as the precursor).

Based on these results, we found that the morphology of MnO2 was sea urchin microspheres when MnSO4·H2O was used as the precursor, and the shape of nanoneedles located on the microsphere surface was very regular and more orderly. Therefore, we chose MnSO4·H2O as the precursor in the experiment for the effect of hydrothermal reaction temperature and the effect of hydrothermal reaction time.

For the effect of hydrothermal reaction temperature, 2.7 g of MnSO4·H2O and 13.185 g of (NH3)2S2O8 were dissolved into some deionized water to obtain 80 mL of solution, which was placed into a 100 mL Teflon autoclave, and then was put into an oven for 4 h at different temperatures (50, 80, 110, and 140 °C). The obtained solid products were washed using deionized water until they were neutral and were dried in an oven at 80 °C. The synthesized products were labeled as MnO2-50 (the hydrothermal temperature was 50 °C), MnO2-80 (the hydrothermal temperature was 80 °C), MnO2-110 (the hydrothermal temperature was 110 °C), and MnO2-140 (the hydrothermal temperature was 140 °C).

For the effect of hydrothermal reaction time, 2.7 g of MnSO4·H2O and 13.185 g of (NH3)2S2O8 were dissolved into some deionized water to obtain 80 mL of solution, which was placed in a 100 mL Teflon autoclave, and was put in an oven at 80 °C for different periods of times (1, 2, 4, and 6 h). The resulting solid samples were washed using deionized water until they were neutral, and they were dried in an oven at 80 °C. The synthesized products were respectively labeled as MnO2-1 h (the hydrothermal time was 1 h), MnO2-2 h (the hydrothermal time was 2 h), MnO2-4 h (the hydrothermal time was 4 h), and MnO2-6 h (the hydrothermal time was 6 h).

Catalyst Characterization

The morphologies of samples were examined by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S-3400N, Hitachi, Japan).

X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) patterns were measured using an X-ray diffractometer (ADVANCE D8, Bruker, Germany).

H2-TPR profiles were measured with a Micromeritics AutoChemII 2920 instrument. First, 45 mg samples were purged with high-purity helium for 60 min at the temperature of 300 °C and then cooled down to 60 °C. Then, a 10% H2–90% Ar mixture was opened. The samples were heated to 600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The H2 consumption was recorded with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).

Catalyst Activity Measurement

The catalytic oxidation of toluene was performed in a U-shaped quartz tubular fixed-bed reactor with an outer diameter of 10 mm and an inner diameter of 8 mm. The toluene vapor was obtained with the bubbling method. Toluene vapor was diluted to 2259 mg·cm–3 in a mixing chamber. This mixed gas at a flow rate of 200 mL·min–1 was introduced into the U-shaped quartz tubular fixed-bed reactor, which was packed with 0.065 g of the catalyst (40–60 mesh) and 0.26 g of silica sand (40–60 mesh). Toluene concentrations in the inlet and outlet reactors were measured using an online GC-MS system (GC-7890B MS-5977B, Agilent) with a TG-BOND Q pillar capillary column (30 m, 0.32 mm). The temperature of the column box was controlled at 60 °C.

Results and Discussion

Effect of the Precursor on the MnO2 Morphology

MnO2 samples were synthesized with the hydrothermal method using MnCl2·4H2O, Mn(CH3COO)2·4H2O, and MnSO4·H2O as precursors at the temperature of 80 °C. The SEM images of these MnO2 samples are displayed in Figure 1. As shown in Figure 1, these samples revealed different morphologies. MnO2-Ac (Figure 1A) displayed a tennis-like microsphere consisting of nanorods, and the microsphere diameter was about 2.05–3.38 μm (mean, 2.58 μm). MnO2-Cl (Figure 1B) appeared in the shape of a chestnut shell with a diameter of approximately 0.85–3.62 μm (mean, 1.57 μm). MnO2-S (Figure 1C) displayed sea urchin microspheres with a diameter of about 2.44–7.91 μm (mean, 4.85 μm). Based on the results, we also found that the shape of nanoneedles located on the microsphere surface was also affected by the Mn precursor. It was more orderly as the precursor was MnSO4·H2O. This was mainly due to the influence of anions in the precursor solution because SO42– could promote the nanoneedle formation in the hydrothermal reaction.25 The SEM images indicated that the Mn(II) precursors unequivocally determined the morphology of MnO2.

Microstructure of MnO2 microspheres obtained by different precursors. (A) MnO2-Ac, (B) MnO2-Cl, and (C) MnO2-S.

Effect of Hydrothermal Time on the Sea Urchin MnO2 Structure

Hydrothermal time, as an important factor of the hydrothermal reaction, would impact the catalyst structure and its performance for VOC clearance. Therefore, we explored the influence of hydrothermal time on the MnO2 morphology and crystalline structure.

The shapes of synthetic MnO2 were characterized by SEM, and the results are shown in Figure 2. Sample MnO2-1 h demonstrated a ball-like microsphere assembled by nanorods, the diameter of which was about 2.05–3.38 μm (mean, 2.58 μm). With the water bath time increasing from 1 to 2 h, the nanorods of the sample surface changed into nanoneedles, forming urchin-like microspheres. The increase of bath time to 4 h led to a better morphology since the nanoneedles became more ordered. With a further increase of bath time to 6 h, nanoneedles on the surface of urchin-like microspheres became shorter and disordered, forming tennis-like microspheres. These results indicated that the growth of nanoneedles on the surface of microspheres was a process of self-templating, and 4 h was an appropriate reaction time for sea urchin MnO2 microspheres.

SEM images of MnO2 synthesized under different hydrothermal times. (A) 1, (B) 2, (C) 4, and (D) 6 h.

The powder XRD patterns of samples are exhibited in Figure 3. For all of the samples, the diffraction peaks matched with pure γ-MnO2 (JCPDS no. 14-0644), and the intensity was not notably different. Lin et al. found that a large number of MnO2 crystal nuclei were rapidly formed as the hydrothermal reaction started. However, MnO2 preferentially grew along the c-axis because there were many defects on the MnO2 surface.26 Then, numerous nanoneedles were generated with hydrothermal reaction time extension, finally forming MnO2 of urchin-like microspheres. We also found that the intensity of the diffraction peak was weak when the hydrothermal reaction time was 1 and 2 h, which might be caused by the low crystallinity of MnO2. However, no significant improvement was noticed as the bath time increased to 4 and 8 h. This might be explained by the thin nanoneedles on the surface of the microspheres, resulting in the failed detection of the sample with the instrument.

XRD patterns of MnO2 synthesized under different hydrothermal times.

Effect of Synthesis Temperature on MnO2 Microspheres

Generally, the hydrothermal temperature is beneficial for the formation of sea urchin-like microspheres.16 Herein, the influence of the hydrothermal temperature on morphology was explored. The synthesized MnO2 samples were characterized by SEM and XRD, and the results are shown in Figures 4 and 5.

SEM images of MnO2 prepared at temperatures of (A) 50, (B) 80, (C) 110, and (D) 140 °C.

XRD patterns of MnO2 synthesized under different hydrothermal temperatures.

The morphology of samples prepared at 50 °C showed the microsphere, which was assembled by nanofibers, and the diameter was about 2.85–5.52 μm. As the temperature increased to 80 °C, nanoneedles aggregated on the surface of microspheres and formed the urchin-like microspheres with a diameter of about 2.44–7.91 μm (Figure 4B). When the reaction temperature increased to 110 °C, the length of nanoneedles increased and the morphologies were rambutan-like microspheres (Figure 4C). When the reaction temperature further increased to 140 °C, yarn-like microsphere MnO2 appeared. These results indicated that the formation process of urchin-like MnO2 contained two aspects: one was the process of MnO2 nuclei's rapid formation; another was the nanoneedle self-assembly on the amorphous spheres.

In order to understand the correlation between crystallinity and hydrothermal temperature, the samples obtained at different temperatures were tested by XRD, and the results are shown in Figure 5. Samples prepared at 50 and 80 °C could be assigned to the tetragonal γ-MnO2 phase (JCPDS no. 14-0644). However, when the temperature increased, the 2θ moved to the high angle and the positions of diffraction peaks appeared at 37.5 and 56.7°, respectively, at 110 and 140 °C, which belong to α-MnO2 (JCPDS no. 44-0141) and β-MnO2 (JCPDS no. 24-0735), respectively. These results indicated that the MnO2 phase changed from the γ type to the α and β type with an increase in the hydrothermal reaction temperature.

Growth Mechanism of Urchin-like Microspheres of MnO2

Based on the results of SEM and XRD, the MnO2 microsphere formation mechanism was hypothesized. Its evolution process could be briefly explained as rapid formation of small nuclei and Ostwald ripening of nuclei followed by nanoneedle self-assembly on the amorphous spheres and transformation to the morphology of urchin-like microspheres (Figure 6).

Formation process of the MnO2 urchin-like microspheres.

At the beginning, there were abundant MnO2 colloids as a result of MnSO4 and (NH4)2S2O8 blended in deionized water under hydrothermal conditions (R1–R3).27,28

This MnO2 colloid could act as the nuclei of the MnO2 nanocrystal. With the increase of hydrothermal time, the radioactive nanoneedles appeared around nuclei and formed ball-like microspheres of γ-MnO2. Since there were many defect sites on the γ-MnO2 surface, MnO2 preferentially grew along the c-axis. Nanoneedles changed into nanowires when hydrothermal treatment continued and formed urchin-like γ-MnO2. On the other hand, γ-MnO2 was semistable metastable under the acidic condition, while α- or β-MnO2 was more stable than γ-MnO2.16 Therefore, it changed to α- or β-MnO2 as the hydrothermal temperature increased to 110 °C.

Catalytic Activity for Foluene

The toluene removal performance was conducted in the temperature range of 150–250 °C, and the results are shown in Figure 7. The toluene removal efficiency was improved with the increase of catalytic temperature, and 100% removal efficiency was achieved at a temperature of 225 °C for all the as-prepared MnO2 samples. However, the performance of toluene removal efficiency between different samples showed a significant difference at the low temperature. MnO2-1 h showed 22, 14, and 53% toluene removal efficiencies at 150, 175, and 200 °C, respectively. MnO2-2 h showed 30 and 33% toluene removal efficiencies at 150 and 175 °C, respectively. MnO2-4 h showed 74, 66, and 89% toluene removal efficiencies at 150, 175, and 200 °C, respectively. MnO2-6 h showed 5, 5, and 59% toluene removal efficiencies at 150, 175, and 200 °C, respectively. These results demonstrated that the sample of MnO2-4 h had the highest toluene removal efficiency at a lower temperature. This might be due to the structure of nanoneedles on the sea urchin MnO2 microspheres. For the reason, it may be because of the Gibbs free energy existing on the surface of nanomaterials. The Gibbs free energy gradually increased with the size of nanomaterial decreased,29 resulting in the energy consumption decline during the process of catalytic material activation.30 Therefore, the chemical reactions were more likely to occur on the surface of sea urchin MnO2 microspheres because there was a large number of nanoneedles with a very small size. According to the results of SEM images (Figure 2), the length and the number of nanoneedles gradually increased with extending hydrothermal time, and the nanoneedles reached the optimal state at a 4 h hydrothermal time. Therefore, the MnO2-4 h sample showed the highest removal efficiency for toluene.

Toluene removal on the MnO2 obtained under different hydrothermal times.

H2-TPR

The reduction capacity of the MnO2 sample was evaluated with H2-TPR (Figure 8). All the samples exhibited two strong reduction peaks occurring at 200–300 and 300–450 °C, which were owing to stepwise reduction of MnO2 to Mn3O4 and Mn3O4 to MnO, respectively.31 For the prepared samples at the different hydrothermal times, the first reduction peak shifted to low temperatures with the increase of hydrothermal time, reached the lowest reduction temperature for the sample of MnO2-4 h, and then shifted to higher temperatures. The sequence followed was MnO2-4 h (266 °C)<MnO2-2 h (270 °C) < MnO2-1 h (273 °C) < MnO2-6 h (279 °C). At the same time, the sample of MnO2-4 h also had the lowest temperature at the second reduction peak, which was consistent with the performance of the toluene conversion (Figure 8). These results revealed that the sample of MnO2-4 h had a better performance of low-temperature reducibility, which was beneficial for toluene conversion.

H2-TPR profiles of MnO2 samples obtained under different hydrothermal times.

Conclusions

MnO2 nanomaterials were prepared via a hydrothermal method using MnCl2·4H2O, Mn(CH3COO)2·4H2O, and MnSO4·H2O as precursors. The Mn(II) precursors could have an impact on the MnO2 morphology. The morphology of MnO2-Ac, MnO2-Cl, and MnO2-S respectively displayed tennis-like microspheres, chestnut shell microspheres, and sea urchin microspheres. At the same time, the sea urchin MnO2 morphology and crystallinity were also affected by hydrothermal time and hydrothermal temperature. The nanoneedles gradually lengthened with the increase of reaction time and reaction temperature, and crystalline MnO2 changed from γ-MnO2 to α- and β-MnO2 as the hydrothermal temperature rose. When the prepared MnO2 degraded toluene, 100% conversion efficiency was achieved at the temperature of 250 °C for all the MnO2 catalysts. However, MnO2-4 h displayed the highest catalytic activity for toluene under low temperatures, which was attributed to the nanoneedles on the surface of sea urchin MnO2 microspheres and better low-temperature reduction performance of MnO2 microspheres.
