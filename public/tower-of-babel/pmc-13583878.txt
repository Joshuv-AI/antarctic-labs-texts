Identification and functional characterisation of SCO1 as a mitochondria-related biomarker in lupus nephritis
Authors: Haiping Xie, Jianwen Liu, Juanjuan He, Yunfeng Dai, Fei Gao, Yanfang Wu, Zhihan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLupus nephritis (LN) is a severe complication of systemic lupus erythematosus. Mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation are key contributors to its pathogenesis, yet the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood and reliable biomarkers are limited. This study investigates mitochondrial-related genes (MRGs) in LN and evaluates their potential as biomarkers.MethodsDifferentially expressed genes (DEGs) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database and MRGs were derived from the MitoCarta3.0 dataset. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis identified key modules, followed by Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses of mitochondrial DEGs (MitoDEGs). Protein-protein interaction network analysis and random forest modelling were applied to identify hub MitoDEGs. Their diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves. Immune cell infiltration was estimated using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis and correlations with hub MitoDEGs were analysed. Immunofluorescence staining and functional assays in HK-2 cells were performed to validate the expression and biological roles of the hub genes.ResultsAmong the 36 MitoDEGs, synthesis of cytochrome c oxidase 1 (SCO1) was identified. SCO1 exhibited strong discriminatory power in both the training and validation sets. After false discovery rate correction, SCO1 showed significant positive correlations with CD8+T cells, naive CD4+T cells and resting memory CD4+T cells. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that SCO1 was predominantly expressed in renal tubules and was significantly upregulated in patients with LN compared with healthy controls. Functional validation demonstrated that SCO1 overexpression enhanced cell viability, whereas its knockdown reduced cell viability, increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen apecies and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, without affecting ATP levels. SCO1 modulation altered P62 expression but did not significantly affect PARKIN, PINK1, or LC3B levels.ConclusionSCO1 showed elevated expression in renal tubules and strong discriminatory ability. It may help preserve tubular cell viability and mitochondrial homeostasis under inflammatory stress, highlighting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker and a candidate for further mechanistic investigation in LN.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic multisystem autoimmune disorder that affects the skin, musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, pulmonary and gastrointestinal systems and remains a major cause of global morbidity and mortality.1 Among its organ manifestations, lupus nephritis (LN) is both common, occurring in approximately 50% of patients, and severe, representing a leading cause of renal failure and death in SLE.1 Despite advances in immunosuppressive therapies, the long-term prognosis of LN remains unsatisfactory. This is largely attributable to its incompletely understood complex pathogenesis and a continuing lack of effective, specific biomarkers for early diagnosis or prognostic assessment.2

Mounting evidence indicates that mitochondrial dysfunction and immune-mediated inflammation act in concert to drive renal injury in LN.2 3 Hallmarks of mitochondrial dysfunction include loss of membrane potential, reduced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, excessive reactive oxygen species generation and activation of intrinsic apoptotic pathways.3 4 Functionally, these alterations exacerbate renal inflammation, potentiate type I interferon signalling and amplify podocyte injury, thereby accelerating glomerular damage.4 5 Pharmacological restoration of mitochondrial homeostasis, for example, using the mitophagy inducer ubiquitin mitochondria inhibitor-77 or the mitochondrial uncoupler niclosamide ethanolamine salt, attenuates proteinuria and histological injury in experimental LN, underscoring the therapeutic relevance of mitochondrial health.6 7

At the molecular level, dysregulation spans mitochondrial dynamics, DNA integrity, metabolic flux and quality-control pathways. Upregulated fission mediators, including dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) and fission protein 1 (Fis1), act synergistically to damage podocytes and aggravate proteinuria.8 Aberrant expression of mitophagy genes such as BNIP3 and Parkin, alongside pathogenic mutations in the mitochondrial D-loop, further compromises bioenergetic capacity in LN.9 10 The complement receptor C5aR1 modulates mitochondrial dynamics by influencing Drp1/Fis1 activity, while nucleic acid-sensing Toll-like receptors 7 (TLR7) and TLR9 propagate intrarenal inflammation and secondary mitochondrial injury through immune complex deposition.11–13 Collectively, these observations underscore the pivotal yet underexplored contribution of mitochondrial-related genes (MRGs) to LN pathogenesis.

Nevertheless, a systematic investigation of MRGs in human LN, including their diagnostic utility and their interaction with the renal immune microenvironment, is still lacking. In this study, we integrate transcriptomic datasets with curated mitochondrial gene inventories to identify differentially expressed mitochondrial genes in LN, delineate their associations with immune cells and assess their potential as predictive or diagnostic biomarkers. By elucidating the genetic-level connections between mitochondrial dysfunction and immune dysregulation, we aim to provide mechanistic insights and a rational basis for novel therapeutic strategies in LN.

Methods and materials

Data sources

Gene expression microarray datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/),14 a public repository hosting extensive high-throughput sequencing and microarray expression data. The keyword “lupus nephritis” was used to search for relevant datasets, and those derived from non-human specimens were excluded. To investigate differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between LN and healthy controls (HC), two datasets, GSE112943 and GSE60861, were chosen. Specifically, dataset GSE112943,15 generated using the GPL10558 platform, comprises 14 kidney biopsy samples from LN patients and seven samples from HC. Dataset GSE60861,16 based on the GPL13497 platform, was used as a validation set to evaluate diagnostic performance and includes 11 LN samples and 10 HC samples. Patients included in these datasets were clinically diagnosed with LN according to either (1) the revised 1997 American College of Rheumatology criteria for SLE17 or (2) histological evidence from renal biopsy demonstrating immune complex-mediated glomerulonephritis consistent with LN, as classified by the 2003 International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society system (figure 1).18

Con, control; DEGs, differentially expressed genes; HC, healthy controls; GO, Gene Ontology; KEGG, Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes; LN, lupus nephritis; MitoDEGs, mitochondrial-related differentially expressed genes; PPI, protein-protein interaction; ROC, receiver operating characteristic; WGCNA, weighted gene co-expression network analysis.

Identification of DEGs

DEGs in the GSE112943 dataset were identified using the ‘limma’ R package. Genes with a p value of <0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2 FC|) >2 were considered statistically significant. The resulting DEGs were visualised using heatmap and volcano plots. DEGs were obtained from the MitoCarta3.0 database (http://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta).19 Overlapping genes between the DEGs and MRGs, designated mitochondrial-related DEGs (MitoDEGs), were identified and visualised using Venn diagrams.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Wweighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), a systems biology method designed to identify biologically meaningful co-expression modules and their relationships with clinical traits,20 was applied to the GSE112943 dataset. Outlier samples were excluded by hierarchical clustering using the ‘hclust’ function in R. A suitable soft-thresholding power (β) was selected from values between 1 and 20 to attain a scale-free network topology, with a criterion of R2>0.8. Gene co-expression modules were identified via hierarchical clustering with a minimum size of 50 genes, and highly similar modules were merged using a cut-off height of 0.25. Module eigengenes were calculated, and associations between modules and clinical traits were assessed. Modules showing strong correlations with LN were identified, and their constituent genes were selected for further investigation.

Functional enrichment analysis of MRGs

Genes from key modules showing strong association with LN, along with candidate MitoDEGs, were analysed using Venn diagrams to identify common MitoDEGs. To explore the biological functions and signalling pathways of these overlapping genes, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the ‘clusterProfiler’ R package. Terms and pathways with a significance level of p value <0.05 were considered statistically enriched and were visualised using bar and bubble plots.

Selection of hub MitoDEGs

A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the online STRING database (V.10.0; https://string-db.org/),21 considering only interactions with a combined confidence score >0.4 as statistically significant. The resulting network was visualised using Cytoscape software (V.3.7.2). The CytoHubba plugin was employed to rank genes through maximal clique centrality (MCC) and the top 10 genes were selected. Subsequently, random forest analysis was performed with the ‘randomForest’ R package to identify the most discriminative genes. A random forest (RF) model consisting of 100 decision trees was constructed using the discovery cohort, and the optimal number of trees was determined through cross-validation error analysis. Genes were ranked according to their importance scores, and the 10 most influential genes were identified. Finally, the candidate genes derived from the CytoHubba and random forest analyses were intersected using a Venn diagram, and the overlapping genes were defined as hub MitoDEGs.

Evaluation of the diagnostic capability of hub MitoDEGs

The diagnostic performance of the hub MitoDEGs in distinguishing LN from control groups was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves. Area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated and visualised for both the discovery dataset (GSE112943) and an independent external validation dataset (GSE60861) using the ‘ROCR’ R package (v1.0-11). Additionally, boxplots illustrating differential gene expression between LN and control groups were generated with the ‘ggplot2’ R package. To address potential batch effects arising from the use of different microarray platforms (GSE112943 on GPL10558; GSE60861 on GPL13497) and to further validate the diagnostic performance of synthesis of cytochrome c oxidase 1 (SCO1) and ferredoxin reductase (FDXR) in an independent same-platform cohort, we performed batch-effect correction using the empirical Bayes method ComBat.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

Immune cell infiltration was estimated using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), which evaluates the enrichment of immune cell-specific gene sets in RNA-seq data. Briefly, gene sets representing 22 immune cell types were curated from the LM22 signature, including B cells, T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, mast cells, eosinophils and neutrophils. The ssGSEA algorithm ranks all genes in each sample by expression, then calculates an enrichment score for each immune cell type based on the rank positions of its marker genes.

The ssGSEA scores were calculated for all samples in the GSE112943 dataset. The correlation between hub gene (SCO1) expression and immune cell ssGSEA scores was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. To correct for multiple testing, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to all 44 correlation tests (22 immune cell types×2 hub genes). Only correlations with FDR <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Human subjects

Patients diagnosed with SLE according to established diagnostic criteria were recruited from Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital. Age-matched and gender-matched HCs were also enrolled. The study protocol was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital (IACUC-FPH-SL-20231020[0215]). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Immunofluorescence double staining on paraffin sections

Paraffin-embedded renal tissue sections were subjected to immunofluorescence double labelling. Sections were baked at 37°C and subsequently fixed in methanol, followed by antigen retrieval under citrate buffer conditions. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and non-specific binding sites were blocked with appropriate serum (10% rabbit serum for goat primary antibodies or 3% BSA for others). The first primary antibody was applied and incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with a species-specific horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody and tyramide signal amplification (TSA) development. After microwave treatment to remove bound antibodies, a second round of blocking, primary antibody incubation, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody application and TSA development were performed sequentially. Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). To minimise background signal, autofluorescence quenching was conducted, and sections were mounted with antifade mounting medium. Fluorescence images were captured using filter sets or spectral detection configurations appropriate for the excitation and emission wavelengths of the fluorophores employed.

Cell lines

The human proximal tubular epithelial cell line HK-2 was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (HyClone) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (containing 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. Subculture was performed at 70–80% confluence using 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25 200–072), and cells were reseeded at specified densities. All cultures were routinely screened for mycoplasma contamination, and only mycoplasma-free cells were used for experiments. To establish the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced cellular injury model, cells were treated with increasing concentrations of LPS (0, 10, 20 and 40 µg/mL) for 24 hours or 48 hours, and cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay.

Transient transfection

Cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 0.5–2×105 cells per well and cultured at 37°C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 until attachment and reaching 60–70% confluence, at which point transient transfection was performed. Two mixtures were prepared using serum-reduced transfection medium (Opti-MEM). Tube A contained 20 pmol of microRNA in 25µL of medium. Tube B contained 1.5µL of Lipofectamine 3000 diluted in 25µL of medium. The diluted Lipofectamine 3000 from tube B was added dropwise to the microRNA solution in tube A, mixed gently and incubated at room temperature for 5 min to facilitate complex formation. Separately, 10µg of plasmid DNA was diluted in 1000µL of Opti-MEM and mixed gently. Then, 20µL of Turbofect was added, mixed and the solution incubated at room temperature for 15 min to form DNA-reagent complexes. For both transfection modalities, the respective complexes were added dropwise to the culture medium of the monolayer cells according to experimental groupings, and the plates were gently rocked to ensure even distribution. Cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. After 8–12 hours, the transfection medium was replaced with pre-warmed complete growth medium containing serum, and the cells were cultured further until downstream analysis.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3000 cells per well. After cell attachment, the designated treatments were administered. Following 48 hours of treatment, the culture medium was removed and replaced with a CCK-8 working solution according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance at 450 nm (OD450) was measured using a microplate reader within 2 hours of adding the reagent. Cell viability and proliferation were assessed based on the recorded OD450 values.

Flow cytometry

A 5,5′,6,6′-Tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) working solution (MedChemExpress, HY-15534) was prepared by diluting 50µL of JC-1 (200×) in 8mL of ultrapure water. The mixture was vortexed vigorously until fully dissolved, after which 2mL of 5×JC-1 staining buffer was added and mixed thoroughly. For cells cultured in six-well plates, the existing medium was removed and, where necessary, the cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Next, 1mL of fresh complete medium was introduced, followed by 1mL of the JC-1 working solution. The cells were then incubated at 37°C for 20 min. Concurrently, a 1×JC-1 buffer was prepared on ice by combining one part 5×JC-1 buffer with four parts distilled water. Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with the ice-cold 1×JC-1 buffer and replenished with 2mL of complete medium. Subsequently, the cells were detached using trypsin, collected via centrifugation at 1200rpm for 5 min and resuspended in 200µL of 1×binding buffer per tube. Immediate flow cytometric analysis was performed, with JC-1 monomers detected in the green fluorescence channel and aggregates detected in the red channel.

A MitoSOX Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator (MitoSOX Red) working solution (5 µM) was prepared by diluting the 5mM stock solution 1:1000 in PBS (MCE, HY-D1055). For cells in each well of a six-well plate, the culture medium was removed and, optionally, the cells were rinsed once with PBS. Then, 1 mL of the working solution was added, and the cells were incubated at 37°C for 10–60 min. Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS, detached and collected by centrifugation at 1200 rpm for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in 200 µL of 1×binding buffer per tube and immediately analysed by flow cytometry, with fluorescence emission collected in the phycoerythrin (PE)/Texas Red-equivalent channel. Reagent volumes were adjusted proportionally for other plate formats.

Western blot analysis

Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, harvested by trypsinisation and lysed on ice for 30 min using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and protease inhibitors. The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 15 000× g for 15 min at 4°C, and protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay (Pierce, NCI3225CH). Equal amounts of protein were mixed with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer, denatured at 95–100 °C for 10 min, separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) on 12% resolving and 5% stacking gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes activated with methanol. The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies (SCO1, LC3, P62, PINK1, PRAK1, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH); diluted 1:1000) either at 37°C for 1 hour or at 4°C overnight. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies (diluted 1:5000) for 1 hour at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualised using enhanced chemiluminescence and captured with a digital imaging system.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and visualisations were performed using R software V.4.2.1. Differences in gene expression levels or immune cell proportions between groups were assessed using two-sided Wilcoxon tests. Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman’s rank correlation. For cell-based assays and immunofluorescence analysis of human renal tissues, data were analysed with GraphPad Prism V.9.0. Results are expressed as mean±SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are denoted as follows: *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001 and ****p<0.0001.

Results

Identification of DEGs and MitoDEGs in LN

Analysis of the GSE112943 dataset using the ‘limma’ R package identified 5311 DEGs, consisting of 4554 upregulated and 757 downregulated genes. The distribution of DEGs was visualised through a heatmap and volcano plot (figure 2A and B). MitoDEGs were identified by intersecting the DEGs with the MitoCarta gene inventory, resulting in 354 candidate MitoDEGs for subsequent analysis (figure 2C).

12 gene modules were identified in the GSE112943 dataset through WGCNA. The soft-thresholding power analysis revealed that gene associations conformed strongly to a scale-free topology. This was particularly evident at a power of β=3, where the scale-free topology fit index (R2) reached 0.9. A heatmap of module-trait relationships, based on Spearman’s correlation coefficient, was generated to evaluate associations between each module and the clinical phenotype. Among the 12 modules, the MEblack module displayed the strongest positive correlation with LN (r=0.63, p=0.002), whereas the MEturquoise module exhibited the strongest negative correlation (r=−0.85, p=8×10-7) (figure 2D, E and F).

Analysis of the MRGs and functional enrichment

To identify biologically significant MRGs in LN, upregulated candidate MitoDEGs (logFC>0) were intersected with genes from the black module, while downregulated candidate MitoDEGs (logFC<0) were intersected with genes from the turquoise module. This procedure yielded 36 common MitoDEGs, comprising 12 upregulated and 24 downregulated genes (figure 3A). To explore the biological functions of these MitoDEGs, functional enrichment analysis was conducted (figure 3B). The results indicated that these genes are primarily associated with the maintenance of mitochondrial integrity and function, redox processes and critical metabolic pathways. In particular, significant enrichment was observed in mitochondrial respiratory chain complex assembly, electron transfer activity and pyruvate metabolism, implying their potential importance in mitochondrial dysfunction related to LN.

Hub MitoDEGs associated with LN

To evaluate the relative importance of the 36 MitoDEGs, a PPI network was constructed (figure 3C). In this network, node colour intensity corresponds to the magnitude of differential expression, as measured by fold-change values derived from the GSE112943 dataset. MCC were employed to identify hub genes within the network. Subsequently, RF analysis was conducted to rank the 36 MitoDEGs by relative importance. A total of 11 genes tied with a score of 2 using the MCC algorithm. Because these tied genes could not be ranked by importance, they were all removed (online supplemental table 1). The top six genes with an MCC score of ≥3 were then selected to find their intersection with the top 10 RF genes. An intersection analysis of the results revealed two common hub MitoDEGs: SCO1 and FDXR (figure 3D). These were identified as potential biomarkers significantly associated with LN.

Exploration of the diagnostic capability of hub MitoDEGs

After ComBat correction, the inter-platform expression distributions of GSE112943 and GSE60861 became more comparable. SCO1 showed consistent upregulation in LN across both the training cohort (GSE112943, AUC=0.86, 95% CI 0.50 to 1.00) and the validation cohort (GSE60861, AUC=0.79, 95% CI 0.63 to 0.92), with both values preserved after ComBat correction. FDXR showed downregulation in LN in the training cohort (GSE112943, AUC=1.00, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.00) but upregulation in LN in GSE60861 (AUC=0.84, 95% CI 0.74 to 0.93). This opposite direction of regulation in GSE60861 indicates that, despite a high AUC, FDXR is not a robust biomarker across cohorts and may be sensitive to cohort composition, sample handling, or platform-specific measurement issues (online supplemental table 2).

Correlation of SCO1 expression with immune cell infiltration characteristics in LN patients

All correlation tests were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (FDR <0.05 threshold). Before correction, 22 of 44 correlations were significant. After FDR correction, six of 44 correlations remained significant. After FDR correction (FDR <0.05), SCO1 expression showed significant positive correlations with CD8+T cells (r=0.662, FDR=0.0125), naive CD4+T cells (r=0.722, FDR=0.0048) and resting memory CD4+T cells (r=0.581, FDR=0.0368). SCO1 also showed significant negative correlations with M0 macrophages (r=−0.779, FDR=0.0020), M2 macrophages (r=−0.671, FDR=0.0125) and neutrophils (r=−0.730, FDR=0.0048) (figure 4A).

The expression of SCO1 was enhanced in LN patients

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on kidney tissue samples from patients with LN and HCs to evaluate the expression of SCO1 (green), synaptopodin (red) and nuclei (DAPI, blue). In the control group, synaptopodin exhibited distinct and well-defined expression within glomeruli, whereas SCO1 staining appeared relatively uniform and was detected mainly in extraglomerular renal structures surrounding the glomeruli. Renal tissues from LN patients showed markedly increased and more widespread SCO1 expression, accompanied by disrupted or diminished synaptopodin staining in glomeruli (figure 5A and B).

Impact of SCO1 on cell viability and mitochondrial homeostasis

The expression of SCO1 was successfully modulated in HK-2 cells, as confirmed by quantitativePCR (qPCR) and Western blot analyses. These results demonstrated robust overexpression in the OE-SCO1 group and efficient depletion in the si-SCO1 group compared with their respective controls (online supplemental figure 1). LPS treatment reduced cell viability in a concentration-dependent and time-dependent manner. At 24 hours, cell viability was modestly but significantly decreased, whereas a more pronounced reduction was observed after 48 hours, particularly at 20 µg/mL and 40 µg/mL. Therefore, 40 µg/mL LPS for 48 hours was selected for the establishment of the subsequent cellular injury model (online supplemental figure 1 A and B). Functionally, CCK-8 assays showed that SCO1 overexpression significantly increased cell viability compared with the overexpression negative (OE-NC) group, whereas SCO1 knockdown significantly reduced cell viability compared with the si-NC group (figure 6A). MitoSOX Red analysis revealed that SCO1 overexpression did not significantly affect mitochondrial superoxide production; however, SCO1 knockdown markedly increased mitochondrial ROS levels (figure 6B). Consistently, JC-1 staining showed no significant difference in the PE/fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) ratio between the OE-SCO1 and OE-NC groups, whereas SCO1 knockdown significantly decreased the PE/FITC ratio, indicating mitochondrial membrane depolarisation (figure 6C). Intracellular ATP levels were not significantly altered by either SCO1 overexpression or knockdown (data not shown). Representative phase-contrast images showed qualitative alterations in cell morphology and confluence following SCO1 overexpression or knockdown under LPS stimulation (figure 6D). Western blot analysis showed that SCO1 overexpression significantly decreased P62 protein expression, whereas SCO1 knockdown significantly increased P62 expression compared with the corresponding negative controls. In contrast, the expression levels of PRAK1, PINK1 and LC3B were not significantly altered by either SCO1 overexpression or knockdown (figure 6E and F).

Discussion

In this study, mitochondria-related biomarkers in LN were identified through integrated transcriptomic, network and machine-learning analyses. Among 36 MitoDEGs, SCO1 emerged as a diagnostic candidate with good discriminatory performance across the training and validation cohorts. SCO1 showed significant associations with immune cell infiltration, although these findings, derived from computational deconvolution, should be regarded as exploratory. Immunofluorescence confirmed increased SCO1 expression predominantly in renal tubular structures from LN patients, and functional assays in HK-2 cells demonstrated that SCO1 overexpression enhanced cell viability, whereas its knockdown reduced cell viability, increased mitochondrial ROS and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential. Together, these results suggest that SCO1 may help preserve tubular cell viability and mitochondrial homeostasis under inflammatory stress, supporting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker and a candidate regulator of tubular mitochondrial function in LN.

Unlike previous research that has primarily focused on generalised mitochondrial abnormalities and systemic immune dysregulation in LN,4 the present study identifies SCO1 as a specific mitochondrial-related hub gene and evaluates its diagnostic potential and possible role in tubular mitochondrial homeostasis. Earlier reviews have described mitochondrial dysfunction as a contributor to LN progression via mechanisms such as enhanced type I interferon production, defective mitophagy and renal fibrosis4; however, few investigations have identified specific gene targets. Although SCO1 is known to function as a mitochondrial copper chaperone essential for cytochrome c oxidase assembly22 thereby supporting mitochondrial respiratory function, its association with immune cell infiltration and tubular injury in LN represents a novel finding. Thus, these findings not only support the broader concept that mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to LN pathogenesis, but also pinpoint SCO1 as specific factors connecting mitochondrial impairment to immune dysregulation and tubular damage, thereby extending the conceptual framework established in earlier literature.

The expression levels of SCO1 showed a consistent upregulation trend across different datasets, although some variations were observed. This variability highlights the necessity for further investigation to elucidate the precise role of SCO1 in LN and to comprehensively evaluate its utility as a biomarker. Although FDXR demonstrated a perfect AUC value in the training cohort, its expression direction was inconsistent across datasets. This discrepancy may reflect the limited sample size, cohort heterogeneity, platform-related effects or potential overfitting; therefore, validation in larger independent cohorts is warranted. Given its limited cross-cohort robustness, FDXR was not prioritised for subsequent experimental validation, and further analyses focused on SCO1. Immunofluorescence staining of renal biopsy specimens further confirmed increased SCO1 expression, predominantly in renal tubular structures, in patients with LN compared with HCs.

Building on these findings, a plausible mechanistic framework can be proposed that connects SCO1 to mitochondrial homeostasis and tubular responses to inflammatory stress in LN. SCO1 encodes a mitochondrial copper chaperone that is essential for the assembly of mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase complex IV and thus plays a key role in maintaining mitochondrial respiratory integrity. Impaired SCO1 function may compromise complex IV activity and oxidative phosphorylation, potentially leading to mitochondrial oxidative stress and membrane depolarisation. Consistent with this possibility, SCO1 knockdown in HK-2 cells increased mitochondrial ROS and reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, although intracellular ATP levels remained unchanged, suggesting that cellular energy homeostasis may have been maintained through compensatory mechanisms. In LN, mitochondrial dysfunction is further aggravated by oxidative stress, defective mitophagy and mitochondrial DNA release—factors known to promote type I interferon responses and immune activation in SLE. However, the present findings do not establish that SCO1-dependent mitochondrial alterations directly drive immune-cell infiltration or tubular injury. The predominant upregulation of SCO1 in renal tubular structures may instead reflect a cell-type-specific adaptive response aimed at preserving mitochondrial function under inflammatory stress. Future studies should determine whether SCO1 dysregulation affects mitochondrial copper homeostasis, respiratory-chain activity, ROS production or mitophagic flux in renal tubular cells. Overall, the proposed immune-mitochondrial relationship remains supported primarily by computational correlations and indirect in vitro evidence and should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Studies using tubular-immune co-culture systems and in vivo models are required to determine whether SCO1-mediated mitochondrial changes directly influence renal inflammation and LN progression.

The identification of SCO1 as a mitochondrial-related biomarker not only enhances the mechanistic understanding of LN but also holds potential clinical implications. Tubular injury is increasingly recognised as a critical determinant of renal outcomes in LN.1 Our findings demonstrated elevated SCO1 expression predominantly in renal tubules, while functional experiments showed that SCO1 overexpression enhanced tubular cell viability and its knockdown increased mitochondrial ROS and induced mitochondrial membrane depolarisation. These results suggest that increased SCO1 expression may reflect an adaptive response to tubular mitochondrial stress, supporting its potential utility as a diagnostic biomarker of LN-associated tubular alterations. However, its relationship with disease activity, histological severity and long-term renal outcomes remains to be established in larger clinical cohorts. Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly regarded as a promising therapeutic target in SLE and LN. Interventions such as N-acetylcysteine, metformin, coenzyme Q10 and mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors have been shown to restore mitochondrial homeostasis, reduce oxidative stress and ameliorate disease activity in both preclinical and certain clinical models of SLE.23 24

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample sizes of both the transcriptomic datasets and clinical specimens may limit the generalisability of our findings. Second, although correlations were observed between SCO1 expression and immune cell infiltration, these associations are observational and do not imply causality. Third, while our study design suggests a potential link between mitochondrial dysfunction and immune dysregulation in LN, the in vitro experiments mainly focused on mitochondrial-related functional readouts and did not include direct assessment of immune-related mechanisms; therefore, the proposed immune-mitochondrial crosstalk should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Fourth, the limited amount of renal biopsy tissue precluded co-staining of SCO1 with tubular or immune-cell markers, restricting detailed spatial characterisation of its cellular distribution. Fifth, functional validation was confined to HK-2 cells, and CCK-8 assays reflected overall cell viability or metabolic activity rather than directly measuring cell proliferation or death. In addition, although SCO1 modulation altered P62 expression, mitophagic flux was not directly assessed using lysosomal inhibitors or mitochondrial-specific flux assays; therefore, no definitive conclusion regarding mitophagy can be drawn. Downstream signalling pathways, respiratory-chain activity, mitochondrial copper homeostasis and metabolic adaptations associated with SCO1 dysregulation also remain unresolved. Future studies should include larger and more diverse LN cohorts with longitudinal follow-up to validate the diagnostic and potential prognostic value of SCO1 for disease progression and treatment response. Renal tubular cell-specific knockout or overexpression models, together with tubular-immune cell co-culture systems, will be essential to establish causal relationships between SCO1 dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction and LN pathology. Moreover, multi-omics approaches integrating transcriptomic, metabolomic and single-cell sequencing data may provide deeper insights into the interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and immune dysregulation in LN and help determine whether SCO1 therapeutic relevance beyond its potential role as a biomarker.

Conclusion

This study identified SCO1 as a mitochondrial-related biomarker with good discriminatory performance for LN. SCO1 expression was associated with immune-cell infiltration and was predominantly increased in renal tubular structures, although these findings do not establish a direct causal relationship with immune dysregulation or tubular injury. Functional experiments further demonstrated that SCO1 overexpression enhanced HK-2 cell viability, whereas SCO1 knockdown increased mitochondrial ROS and reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, suggesting that SCO1 may help preserve tubular cell viability and mitochondrial homeostasis under inflammatory stress. These findings provide new insights into mitochondrial alterations in LN and support further investigation of SCO1 as a potential diagnostic biomarker and regulator of tubular mitochondrial function.
