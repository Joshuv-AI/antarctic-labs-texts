Democratizing News Recommenders: Modeling Multiple Perspectives for News Candidate Generation with VQ-VAE
Hardy, Sebastian Padó, Amelie Wührl, Tanise Ceron

Abstract
Current News Recommender Systems based on past clicks are designed for engagement, but come at the cost of limiting diversity in the suggested content. While diversity-aware algorithms exist, they suffer from two major limitations. First, they fail to account for normative diversity, which requires fair access to a broad range of perspectives. Second, they typically apply diversity late in the system’s pipeline, after a lot of content has already been filtered out. Both limitations confine their effectiveness and prevent them from promoting true normative diversity in news recommendations. We propose Aspect-Aware Candidate Generation ( A2CG ) to address these limitations. Our framework introduces diversity into the earliest pipeline stage and uses a configurable mechanism to align diversity with specific democratic goals. A2CG represents each news article using multiple aspects of perspectives (e.g., sentiment, political leaning, frame) and uses a Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE) to create a discrete, multi-faceted representation. A decoder-only model then learns user preferences over these aspect codes. We then inject diversity directly by reversing the sign on some of the query vector’s aspects during the candidate retrieval process, ensuring a more diverse set of candidates. Our method, evaluated on the MIND dataset, enables a flexible trade-off between personalization and diversity early in the recommendation pipeline. It also generates more novel, diverse, and serendipitous candidates while effectively taking into account aspects that strengthen democratic values. These empirical results make it a promising approach for downstream democratized news recommendation systems.



1
Introduction

News Recommender Systems
(NRS) are widely implemented in online news outlets, where they offer personalized news selections to users
[
Thurman and Schifferes 2012
,
Bastian et al. 2020
]
and reduce information overload
[
Resnick et al. 1994
]
. Such systems are primarily designed for improving click-through rates and user engagement
[
Li et al. 2010
]
by predicting news articles that are interesting to the users
[
Thurman and Schifferes 2012
,
Bastian et al. 2020
]
. This objective has the potential to reinforce selective exposure
[
Frey 1986
,
Hart et al. 2009
]
and amplify echo chambers
[
Nguyen 2020
]
because they recommend content that already aligns with their views. To address these concerns, research on NRS
[
Gharahighehi and Vens 2023
,
Iana et al. 2024
,
Qi et al. 2021a
]
proposes a design of diversity-aware NRS.

Existing approaches, however, have two limitations concerning the implementation of diversity in NRS. The first concerns the multifaceted nature of the diversity problem. In practice, the operationalization of diversity in NRS implementations is often limited. Many systems rely on simplified or proxy measures – such as topic variety or keyword differences
[
Wang et al. 2018a
,
Lee et al. 2020
]
.
In theory, diversity has many definitions, as it can be seen as diversity of sources, narrators, or perspectives in terms of viewpoints
[
Loecherbach et al. 2020
]
. These properties of diversity entail several definitions and metrics, often leading to competing optimization goals
[
Wu et al. 2024
]
because of conflicting interests, for example, showing a wider variety of topics instead of different viewpoints on the same topic.
Helberger 2019
conceptualizes how NRS should behave based on four key democratic models
[
Karppinen 2012
]
:
Liberal
(individual autonomy),
Participatory
(common good),
Deliberative
(debate and diverse viewpoints), and
Critical
(challenging the status quo). However,
progress remains mainly at the conceptual or evaluation level, and no operationalization of democratic NRS have been proposed.

In our work, we use the definition of normative diversity as playing a specific role in a democratic system
[
Karppinen 2012
,
Helberger et al. 2018
,
Vrijenhoek et al. 2024a
]
. We focus on the need that NRS expose users to a representation of diverse perspectives or viewpoints, creating
a space where opinions coexist.

The second limitation concerns the architecture of the NRS. In many NRS
[
Wang et al. 2018b
]
, particularly those at scale
[
Okura et al. 2017
]
, achieving high normative diversity is difficult because the ranking process occurs after the initial candidate generation has already significantly narrowed the pool of potential recommendations. This two-stage architecture
[
Li et al. 2011
,
Qi et al. 2021b
,
Song et al. 2022
]
is a design choice for two main reasons: computational efficiency and scalability. The last stage, or the ranking model, is typically made for high accuracy and relevance, but it is often too complex and costly to run on the many new articles published every day. We address it by proposing an efficient approach that addresses diversity at an earlier stage of the recommendation pipeline, while remaining computationally efficient.

For that, we propose a framework called Aspect-Aware Candidate Generation (
A2CG
), as shown in Figure
1
,
that tackles the issue of diversity before the recommendation stage. By introducing diversity constraints at this early stage, we can ensure that a broader and more representative set of news articles is considered for recommendation.
A2CG
’s main idea is to represent article content as a bundle of distinct, manageable “aspects” of perspectives (e.g., political leaning, category, sentiment) which are proxies for identifying perspectives in texts. We then use these aspects to drive the candidate generation process. We utilize Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoders (VQ-VAE)
[
Oord et al. 2018
]
to create aspect-specific discrete latent spaces, referred to as
codebooks
, which store a finite set of learnable embedding vectors used to represent each aspect. This approach enables a decoder-only model
[
Touvron et al. 2023
]
to learn user interests over a discrete, multi-faceted representation of articles and generate novel candidates by predicting a new sequence of unseen aspects. This architecture enriches the article representations for an efficient retrieval process while still effectively capturing user history. By mapping these rich, quantized representations to a vector space and using a fast nearest-neighbor search algorithm, we can quickly and effectively retrieve a diverse set of candidates. While nearest-neighbor search is a standard technique,
A2CG
allows us to go beyond simple item similarity and retrieve a broader, more diverse set of articles in an efficient offline framework.

We conduct a comprehensive analysis to investigate the impact of our approach by formulating the following research questions:

1.

To what extent does
A2CG
improve traditional and normative diversity over the baselines?

We benchmark vanilla
A2CG
against various baselines, including those optimized for specific traditional and normative diversity metrics. An analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of these baselines sets the stage for a comparison that demonstrates how vanilla
A2CG
improves upon existing methods.

2.

How does
A2CG
’s multi-aspect approach balance the trade-off between diversity and personalization?

We investigate how
A2CG
’s diversity injection mechanism influences the trade-off between personalization and diversity. Our analysis examines the trends and specific metrics that are affected by this process.

3.

How does
A2CG
address the different requirements of the four democratic models as conceptualized by
Karppinen 2012
,
Helberger 2019
?

We evaluate the performance of
A2CG
and baseline methods across four theoretically grounded models of democracy. Our analysis focuses on how
A2CG
’s tunability provides the necessary flexibility to address the distinct requirements of each model, a key advantage over the more rigid baselines.

Our key contributions are:

1.

A novel multi-aspect framework for diverse candidate generation. Our approach injects diversity into the multi-aspect representation itself, enabling a flexible trade-off between personalization and diversity early in the recommendation pipeline. This addresses the limitations of methods that only apply diversity constraints at the ranking stage.

2.

A generative approach with a VQ-VAE and a decoder-only model to create novel and unseen bundles of aspects. These generated bundles serve as a unique representation of the next article a user is likely to read.

3.

The operationalization of four models of democracy
[
Karppinen 2012
,
Helberger 2019
,
Vrijenhoek et al. 2024b
]
as a theoretical framework for our model analysis.

4.

The development of a new set of strong baselines and followed by a comprehensive analysis using traditional and normative diversity metrics (
RADio

[
Vrijenhoek et al. 2024b
]
).

2
Related Work

News Recommender Systems (NRS) have been widely adopted to help users navigate the vast amount of online news, but a large body of research has highlighted their potential to create echo chambers or by increasing selective exposure recommending content that aligns with a user’s past preferences
[
Hart et al. 2009
,
Nguyen 2020
]
. This has led to a growing interest in incorporating diversity as an objective in NRS design. However, definition and implementation of diversity vary widely.

2.1
Diversity in Recommender Systems

Much of the early work on diversity in recommender systems, particularly in other domains like e-commerce and music, has focused on intra-list diversity, which measures the dissimilarity of items within a single recommendation list
[
Loecherbach et al. 2020
]
. These approaches often use simplified metrics like topic variety or keyword differences to promote a broader range of content. However, the academic discourse, particularly in the context of news, has shifted towards a more nuanced understanding of diversity, often referred to as normative diversity. This concept is rooted in democratic theory and focuses on the societal role of journalism, advocating for the exposure of users to a representative range of viewpoints and perspectives, not just different topics
[
Helberger 2019
,
Karppinen 2012
]
. The RADio framework
[
Vrijenhoek et al. 2024b
]
is one such recent contribution that provides a set of metrics to evaluate normative diversity, bridging the gap between theoretical frameworks and technical implementations.

2.2
Two-Stage Recommendation Architecture

The majority of large-scale recommender systems, including many NRS, operate on a two-stage architecture to balance computational efficiency with recommendation quality
[
Li et al. 2011
,
Okura et al. 2017
]
. The first stage, candidate generation, quickly narrows down a massive corpus of items to a smaller, more manageable set of potential recommendations. The second stage, ranking, then refines this set to produce the final, personalized list. The current paper identifies a key limitation with this approach: if the initial candidate generation stage does not prioritize diversity, the ranking model, no matter how sophisticated, can only re-order an already-homogeneous set of articles. This is a recognized challenge in the field, where efforts to inject diversity have traditionally been concentrated in the ranking stage
[
Gharahighehi and Vens 2023
,
Iana et al. 2024
]
. The proposed
A2CG
framework addresses this by introducing diversity at the candidate generation stage, a less-explored but critical area for achieving meaningful diversity.

3
Methods

3.1
Candidate Generation

We consider the candidate generation problem with a large set of possible news article candidates,
N=\{n_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|N|}
, as the starting point for our formulation. Each user,
u
, has a history of previously read articles,
H_{u}\subset N
. The objective is to retrieve candidate articles,
C\subset N
with
|C|\ll|N|
such that
C
is both relevant to the user’s history, while also being diverse.

Our proposed method,
A2CG
, centers on learning a representation of what the user is likely to read next,
q_{u}
, which serves as a query for retrieving
C
. However, due to the inherently discrete nature of news items and the vast space of possible candidates, it is not possible to directly predict the target article. To address this, we instead focus on an approach that generates the
abstract concept
of the article by combining different aspects.

3.1.1
Multi-Aspects Embedding of Article

In the context of news articles, an
aspect
, denoted by
\mathcal{A}
, represents a specific characteristic or dimension of the article’s content that can be used to differentiate its viewpoint. Formally, let
\mathcal{A}=\{\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{K}\}
denote the set of all aspects considered, where each
\mathcal{A}_{k}
corresponds to a particular attribute (e.g., sentiment, political leaning, or framing). In our case, we focus on five distinct aspects: article content, sentiment, category, political leaning, and frames. However, the advantage of our framework is that it can be extended to any aspect.

Given a news article
n_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
, where
\mathcal{D}
is the
MIND
dataset
[
Wu et al. 2020
]
, each annotated with labels for four of the five aspects (sentiment, category, political leaning, and frames), our goal is to learn an aspect-specific embedding function. For the
article content
aspect, the embedding function directly processes the raw text of the title and abstract of the article without a specific label. We use an adapted Sentence Transformer model
[
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
]
with ModernBERT
[
Warner et al. 2024
]
as the base encoder to embed the
article content
aspect.

For the other four aspects, we further fine-tune each by using the article content aspect as the base embedding, with supervised contrastive learning
[
Khosla et al. 2021
]
. Let
i\in I
be the index set of our training dataset, and let
P(i)
be the set of
positive
samples sharing the same label
y_{k}
, and
A(i)
be the set of comparison samples (positives and negatives). Let
z_{i}
denote the embedding of article
n_{i}
. Then the contrastive loss (using the notation from
Khosla et al. 2021
) for aspect
k
is:

\mathcal{L}^{(k)}=\sum_{i\in I}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{(k)}=\sum_{i\in I}\frac{-1}{|P(i)|}\sum_{p\in P(i)}\log\frac{\exp\big(z_{i}\cdot z_{p}/\tau\big)}{\sum\limits_{a\in A(i)}\exp\big(z_{i}\cdot z_{a}/\tau\big)},

where
\tau>0
is a temperature parameter. This objective pulls embeddings of articles sharing the same aspect label closer while pushing apart those with different labels.

3.1.2
Article Quantization

For each aspect
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, we train a separate Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE)
[
Oord et al. 2018
]
to discretize its continuous space. The VQ-VAE uses vector quantization to map a continuous, high-dimensional input into a discrete set of learned codebook vectors, preserving the aspect information.

Given the aspect-specific embedding,
z_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, of aspect
k
, the encoder
E_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{e}}
outputs a single latent vector,
h
, which is quantized to the nearest entry in the aspect-specific codebook
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\{e_{j}^{(k)}\}_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{C}_{k}|}
:

i_{k}=\arg\min_{j\in\{1,\dots,|\mathcal{C}_{k}|\}}\|h-e_{j}^{(k)}\|_{2}
,
where
i_{k}
is the chosen codebook index for aspect
k
and subsequently
\phi_{k}(x)=e_{i_{k}}^{(k)}
is the quantized representation for aspect
k
.

The VQ-VAE is trained to reconstruct the original aspect embedding
z_{k}
from its quantized form while also learning an effective codebook. The training loss has three components:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VQ}}^{(k)}=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\|z_{k}-D_{k}(\phi_{k}(x))\|_{2}}_{\text{Reconstruction loss: ensure decoded output matches $z_{k}$}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\|\mathrm{sg}[E_{k}(z_{k})]-\phi_{k}(x)\|_{2}}_{\text{Codebook loss: updates codebook to match encoder outputs}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\beta\|E_{k}(z_{k})-\mathrm{sg}[\phi_{k}(x)]\|_{2}}_{\text{Commitment loss{ encourage encoder outputs to stay near chosen codes}}},

where
D_{k}
is the decoder for aspect
k
,
\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]
is the stop-gradient operator, and
\beta
is the commitment weight.

Finally, the full representation of an article
x
is the sequence of the
K
aspect-wise quantized codebook indices,

\Phi(x)=\big[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{K}\big]
.

3.1.3
Decoder-Only Modeling over Quantized Aspect Codes

The final step in our candidate generation framework involves modeling user history over the quantized multi-aspect representation of news articles. Formally, we use a LLaMA
[
Touvron et al. 2023
]
model, which is a Transformer-based decoder-only
[
Vaswani et al. 2017
]
, to learn the conditional probability of the next quantized article representation, given a user’s reading history. For the full formulation of the Transformer’s architecture, please refer to
Vaswani et al. 2017
.

Given a user
u
with a history of articles,
H_{u}=(\Phi(n_{1}),\cdots,\Phi(n_{k}))
, the model is trained to predict the next article’s representation,
\Phi(n_{k+1})
. To do this, we first unroll the history into a single, flattened sequence of indices.

Let
S
be the flattened sequence of indices from the user’s history:

\begin{split}S&=[\Phi(n_{1}),\Phi(n_{2}),\dots,\Phi(n_{k})]\\
&=[i_{1,1},\dots,i_{1,K},i_{2,1},\dots,i_{2,K},\dots,i_{k,1},\dots,i_{k,K}]\end{split}

(1)

The model is then trained to maximize the conditional probability of next token, using a cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hist}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{|S|-1}\log P\big(S_{t+1}\,\mid\,[\mathrm{BoS},S_{\leq t}]).

During inference, we condition on the entire history to predict the next
K
aspect indices.

P(\Phi(n_{k+1})|S)=\prod_{j=1}^{K}P(\Phi(n_{k+1})_{j}|S,\Phi(n_{k+1})_{<j})

3.1.4
Retrieval via Quantized Codebook Embeddings

Once the decoder-only model has been trained to predict quantized codes, we perform candidate retrieval by mapping articles into a vector database using their codebook embeddings.

Storage and Query Formulation.

For each article
n_{i}
in the set
N
, we embed it
K
times using the fine-tuned aspect models to obtain
(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{K})
. We then apply Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to reduce the dimensionality of each embedding. These reduced vectors are then concatenated and stored in a vector database.

At query time, we use the decoder-only model to predict the next
K
aspect indices. We then retrieve the corresponding codebook embeddings from the
quantizer, followed by decoding each embedding into its aspect representation:

q(H_{u})=f(\Phi(n_{k+1}))=\big(e^{(1)}_{j_{1}},e^{(2)}_{j_{2}},\dots,e^{(K)}_{j_{K}}\big),

where
f(\cdot)
is a lookup function that maps each predicted index to its codebook embedding
e^{(m)}_{j_{m}}
for aspect
m
and then decodes it into the final aspect vector.

To promote diversity in candidate representations, we introduce a
diversity injection
step (cf. Algorithm
1
). Given the predicted aspect indices
\Phi(n_{k+1})=(j_{1},\dots,j_{K})
, we decode and reduce each aspect vector, and with probability
p
randomly select
n
aspects to have their vectors inverted (multiplied by
-1
) before concatenation. This operation encourages exploration by inverting the direction of selected aspect vectors.

Nearest Neighbor Retrieval.

Using
q
as a query vector, we perform nearest neighbor search over the database of concatenated article embeddings. Specifically, we employ the Hierarchical Navigable Small-World (HNSW) algorithm
[
Malkov and Yashunin 2020
]
to retrieve the top-
k
most similar article vectors,

C=\mathrm{HNSW}\big(q,\{E(n_{i})\}_{i=1}^{|N|},k\big)
,
where
C\subset N
contains the
k

time. These retrieved articles form the candidate set for downstream ranking.

3.2
Evaluation

We evaluate our method’s performance on traditional and normative diversity metrics. Accuracy and ranking metrics were not used because our approach is designed solely for candidate generation, not ranking, which is the function of the downstream NRS.

3.2.1
Traditional Metrics

Intra-List Diversity.

We measured intra-list diversity using average pairwise dissimilarity,
D_{\text{avg}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}}\left[1-\cos(\mathbf{f}_{i},\mathbf{f}_{j})\right]
,
where
\mathbf{f}_{i}
and
\mathbf{f}_{j}
denote feature vectors of candidate articles, and
\mathcal{P}
is the set of all article pairs.

Serendipity.

Serendipity measures the average dissimilarity of the candidate sets from a user’s past clicks. It uses a continuous measure (cosine similarity) to see how far the candidate sets are from the user’s previously liked content. It is computed as

S=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left[1-\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\cos(\mathbf{f}_{c},\mathbf{f}_{h})\right]
,
where
\mathcal{C}
is the candidate set and
\mathcal{H}
is the set of article in the user history.

Novelty.

Novelty is a simpler, binary measure than serendipity. Instead of a continuous score, it counts the proportion of candidates that are “novel” based on a strict threshold. An article is considered novel if its maximum similarity to any previously clicked item is below a certain threshold
\theta
:

\text{Novelty}=\left|\left\{c\in\mathcal{R}\,\middle|\,\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\cos(\mathbf{f}_{c},\mathbf{f}_{h})<\theta\right\}\right|/|\mathcal{C}|
,
where we set
\theta=0.65
.

Similarity.

Similarity measures the average similarity between candidate sets and user histories:

\text{Similarity}=\frac{1}{|I|\cdot|J|}\sum_{i=1}^{|I|}\sum_{j=1}^{|J|}\frac{\vec{c}_{i}\cdot\vec{h}_{j}}{\|\vec{c}_{i}\|\cdot\|\vec{h}_{j}\|}

where
\vec{c}_{i}
and
\vec{h}_{j}
are the component of aspect vectors of candidate set and history, respectively.

3.2.2
RADio
Metrics.

To further evaluate candidates diversity, we use the
RADio
framework
[
Vrijenhoek et al. 2024b
]
, which offers a unified approach to normative diversity metrics
with optional rank-awareness. In our setting, we disable the rank-aware component, which applies a positional discount to candidates, as our focus is on diversity irrespective of ranking position. We use category distribution for Calibration and subcategory for Fragmentation, sentiment for Activation, and frame for Representation and Alternative Voices. Metric scores range from 0 (no divergence) to 1 (maximum divergence), without an inherent interpretation as “good” or “bad.”

The
RADio
metrics include:

•

Calibration:
Alignment of topical distribution between candidates and the user’s history. Lower values indicate greater similarity.

•

Fragmentation:
Divergence of candidates across users. Lower values indicate more overlap between users.

•

Activation:
Sentiment-based emotional polarization between candidates and the user’s history. Lower values indicate a more similar emotional tone.

•

Representation:
Diversity of frames in candidates relative to the entire corpus. Lower values indicate frames are represented proportionally to the dataset.

•

Alternative Voices:
Diversity of frames in candidates relative to the user’s history. Lower values indicate greater similarity to the user’s history.

Additionally, in order to answer RQ3 on how appropriate
A2CG
is for the four models of democracy
[
Karppinen 2012
,
Helberger 2019
]
:

1.

The Liberal model
prioritizes a user’s personal preferences and autonomy.

2.

The Participatory model
focuses on the common good to help users be active citizens.

3.

The Deliberative model
fosters debate by neutrally presenting diverse viewpoints.

4.

The Critical
model challenges the status quo by amplifying unheard voices to inspire action against injustice.

To quantitatively assess them, we operationalize the qualitative model from
Vrijenhoek et al. 2024b
into a continuous scoring function. The higher the score for each model, the greater the degree of alignment of the candidate generator model with the democratic model. We define the score as

where
n
is the number of metrics (Calibration etc.) associated with the democratic model.

3.2.3
Baselines

Our experimental evaluation includes a set of candidate generators designed to benchmark the effectiveness of our
A2CG
framework. These baselines represent common strategies for candidate generation, ranging from simple random selection to more sophisticated methods presented in this order.

Uniform Random:

This baseline selects a set of candidates completely at random from the entire available articles, without considering user history or article aspects. It serves as a control to measure the baseline level of diversity and relevance, as it has no explicit mechanism for either.

History Average:

This relevance-based baseline calculates a single query vector by averaging the embedding vectors of all articles in a user’s reading history. It then uses this average vector to perform a nearest-neighbor search in the vector database to retrieve the most similar articles. This method prioritizes relevance to a user’s overall past interests but has no built-in mechanism for diversity.

K-Means Random:

This baseline aims for a balance between diversity and relevance by first clustering (50 clusters) the entire article corpus using K-Means and then randomly selecting one article from each cluster to form a diverse candidate set. This ensures that the selected candidates span the major aspects present in the data.

K-Means Greedy:

This diversity-focused baseline is an iterative algorithm that builds a diverse set of candidates within a K-Means cluster (50 clusters). It starts by picking one random article. In each subsequent step, it calculates the dissimilarity of all other articles to the current set of chosen candidates and selects the next article based on a probability distribution weighted by this dissimilarity. This ensures that each article adds a new perspective to the candidate set.

MIND
’s Candidate Generator:

We used the impressions recorded in the
MIND
dataset’s behavior logs as the output of its proprietary candidate generation algorithm. To differentiate between the
MIND
dataset and its candidate generation, we will use the term
the
MIND
baseline
to indicate its algorithm for generating impressions.

4
Experimental Setup

To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed
A2CG
framework, we conduct a series of experiments on the
MIND
dataset.

4.1
Dataset

The Microsoft News Dataset (
MIND
)
[
Wu et al. 2020
]
is a widely used, large-scale news corpus for NRS. Its nature as a news aggregate portal makes it well-suited for our candidate generation framework.
For our experiments, we used the
MIND
-large version, which contains a more extensive set of user sessions and articles. We followed the common practice
[
Iana et al. 2024
]
of re-splitting the dataset, fine-tuning our model on the validation set, and evaluating it on the test set. For each test sample, we used all 6,997 articles from the test set as possible candidates, assuming the goal is to filter this large, daily pool down to a smaller, more relevant number of recommendations. We set the number of candidates to 50, which is approximately the average number of impressions in the training dataset.

We augment the
MIND
dataset with additional aspects: sentiment, political leaning, and frames. This augmentation is performed using pre-trained classifiers using title and abstract text as input:

Sentiment:

We use a fine-tuned DistilBERT model
[
Sanh et al. 2020
,
tabularisai et al. 2025
]
trained on a large sentiment analysis corpus to classify each article’s sentiment into five sentiment categories (Very Negative, Negative, Neutral, Positive, and Very Positive).

Political Leaning:

We employ a two-stage process to classify the political leaning of articles. Initially, we use the DEBATE model
[
Burnham et al. 2024
]
to classify articles as either political or non-political. For articles classified as political, we then use
LLAMA3.1-70B
model
[
AI@Meta 2024
]
to further classify their specific leaning as left, center, right, or neutral.

Media Frames:

According to
Entman 1993
, frames highlight or emphasize certain aspects of a debate, thus providing a rich representation of perspective in news articles. We use a fine-tuned XLMRoberta-based framing classifier
[
Daffara et al. 2025
,
Conneau et al. 2019
]

to identify the dominant communication frames present in each article (e.g., economic consequences, human interest, morality). This allows us to capture a more nuanced form of diversity beyond simple topics.
These augmented labels are treated as ground truth for training the aspect-specific embedding functions in our framework (§
3.1.1
).

4.2
Implementation Details

The following details each component of
A2CG
.

4.2.1
Aspect-Specific Embeddings

We use a ModernBERT-base model from the Hugging Face Transformers library as the encoder for our Sentence Transformer. We fine-tune four separate models, each corresponding to one of the four aspects: sentiment, political leaning, category (from
MIND
), and frames. The supervised contrastive learning objective is optimized using the AdamW optimizer. We train each model until it converges. The temperature parameter
\tau
for the contrastive loss is set to 0.1.

4.2.2
Article Quantization with VQ-VAE

For each of the four aspects, we train an independent VQ-VAE. The encoder and decoder networks are implemented as simple feed-forward networks. The input dimension for each VQ-VAE is the embedding size of the Sentence Transformer (d=768). We use Optuna to optimize the hyperparameters for each VQ-VAE model.

4.2.3
Decoder-Only Modeling

We utilize a decoder-only transformer model based on the Llama 3 architecture
[
AI@Meta 2024
]
, which we train from scratch. The input to this model is the concatenated sequence of quantized aspect codes. The training objective is the cross-entropy loss over the predicted next code in the sequence.

4.2.4
Retrieval and Evaluation

For candidate retrieval, we use Milvus, a high-dimensional vector database, to store the concatenated embeddings of all articles in the
MIND
test set. To improve search speed, we reduced the dimensionality of each aspect to 128 using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). We then used the Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) algorithm to perform an efficient nearest-neighbor search. To get 50 candidate articles, we retrieve the two most similar articles for each of the top 25 predictions from the beam search (beam width 25).

4.2.5
Evaluation Protocol

We use the metrics detailed in Sections
3.2.1
and
3.2.2
to assess the diversity of the generated candidate sets. We compare our approach to the baseline candidate generation mechanism of the
MIND
dataset, which is not explicitly designed for diversity. Our analysis will specifically focus on how
A2CG
, with different aspect-biasing configurations, affects these diversity metrics and their trade-off with relevance.

5.1
RQ1: Impact of
A2CG
on traditional and normative diversity

First, we establish the performance of the baseline methods. We analyze their inherent strengths and weaknesses, which serve as a foundation for a comparative discussion with our model,
A2CG
. It’s important to note that a metric’s score doesn’t necessarily indicate good or bad performance, but rather highlights a model’s inclination toward a specific characteristic, such as diversity or personalization. We use three scales for comparison: low (0–0.33), medium (0.33–0.66), and high (0.66–1) during the analysis.

MIND
baseline:

In traditional diversity metrics, the
MIND
baseline demonstrates high
Intra-List Diversity
score (0.6397), but medium
Serendipity
and
Novelty
scores (0.3426 and 0.4177, respectively) suggesting that, while the candidates are diverse, they are neither particularly unexpected nor unfamiliar to the user. In
RADio
metrics, among the baselines, the
MIND
baseline has the highest
Calibration
,
Fragmentation
,
Representation
, and
Alternative Voices
(0.5667, 0.7316, 0.6611, and 0.5705, respectively). While its candidates demonstrate topical novelty, personalization, and greater divergence from the user history/dataset’s typical framing choices, the black box nature of the model prevents us from drawing any conclusions on why it achieves this performance.

History Average:

Among the baselines, the method exhibits the lowest
Intra-list Diversity
(0.4167) and
Similarity
(0.3085), yet the highest
Novelty
(0.5165). This is because it does not use a diversification strategy. Instead, it retrieves the top 50 most similar results using a single query vector, which is the average of a user’s past articles. This vector averaging process, however, also decreases the ability to personalize candidates. While the centroid vector is near the center of the user’s historical article space, it loses the specificity of any individual article. This can lead to the retrieval of off-topic or generic content that, while close to the average, is dissimilar to any single item the user has previously engaged with.

The method also demonstrates the lowest
Calibration
score (0.4898), which is also supported by the lowest
Alternative Voices
score (0.5044), suggesting high personalization with framing choice similar to the user history. It shows a medium score in
Representation
(0.5786), which indicates that the method framing choice diverges from the dataset.

K-Means Random and K-Means Greedy:

While both K-Means-based baselines demonstrate an ability to diversify, they obtain different results. The greedy version has medium
Novelty
(0.5034), the highest
Intra-List Diversity
(0.6472) and
Serendipity
(0.3755), surpassing
MIND
baseline. This is due to its two-layered diversity retrieval: first, through clustering, and second, by maximizing the distance between items within each cluster. Conversely, the random version, despite a medium
Intra-List Diversity
(0.6008), exhibits the lowest
Serendipity
(0.3306) and
Novelty
(0.3818) among all methods.
It also shows the highest
Similarity
(0.3820), indicating a limited capacity to introduce new content to the user. Both the random and greedy versions have very low
Activation
scores (0.0890 and 0.1551, respectively), which shows that the sentiment of the candidates is similar to that of the overall dataset, characteristic of the use of clustering. The 0 score of the
Fragmentation
means there is no divergence: all users receive the same proportion of topics.

The two K-Means strategies reveal a clear trade-off: random selection achieves lower scores in
Representation
(0.3039) and
Alternative Voices
(0.5158), indicating closer alignment with corpus and user history, while greedy selection, which maximizes distance, yields higher scores (0.4849 and 0.5565), reflecting greater divergence.

Uniform Random:

The Uniform Random method offers a compelling trade-off, with its metrics consistently positioned between those of the K-Means Random and History Average baselines. By sampling from the entire dataset, it has a higher probability of selecting articles outside of a user’s typical interests, resulting in higher
Serendipity
(0.3560) and
Novelty
(0.4583) scores. Its low
Activation
score (0.1386) suggests that the candidates’ overall sentiment is more similar to the dataset distribution. Furthermore, this method has a medium
Representation
score (0.4853), which shows that the frame distribution is slightly similar to the whole dataset. Both outcomes are expected of a random strategy.

Vanilla
A2CG
:

Despite having the lowest
Intra-List Diversity
scores (0.5643), the vanilla
A2CG
shows a high level of personalization, reflected in its high
Similarity
(0.3716) score close to
MIND
’s candidate generator.
This is an expected outcome, as it retrieves similar articles to the candidate articles generated based on a user’s past behavior. Unlike the History Average method, which relies on averaging user history,
A2CG
optimizes the user’s history for the next article prediction. This allows it to generate genuine candidates with new content while staying closer to individual articles in the user’s history. This is evidenced by its
Serendipity
(0.3650) and
Novelty
(0.4740) scores, which are higher than the
MIND
baseline (0.3426 and 0.4177, respectively).

The vanilla
A2CG
model has a higher score than
MIND
baseline on all
RADio
metrics except for
Representative
. While many of these differences are marginal,
A2CG
’s significantly higher Activation score (0.3263) is notable. This indicates that its candidates offer greater sentiment diversity by differing more in emotional tone from the user’s reading history. Overall, this suggests that
A2CG
Vanilla’s content-based approach is competitive to
MIND
baseline.

5.2
RQ2: Impact of the multi-aspect
A2CG
on diversity–personalization trade-off

The introduction of diversity injection into the
A2CG
framework provides a granular control mechanism for navigating the trade-offs between personalization and various forms of diversity.

As both the number of flipped aspects (
n
) and the flipping probability increase, a clear pattern emerges across traditional diversity metrics:
Intra-List Diversity
,
Serendipity
, and
Novelty
consistently rise, while
Similarity
declines. This demonstrates the framework’s direct effectiveness in promoting varied content over repetition. For instance, the most aggressive configuration (
n
=5, 100%) achieves the highest scores for
Serendipity
(0.4492) and
Novelty
(0.6294), higher than all other baselines. The
Intra-List Diversity
, however, has a reversal at the end, initially increasing from 0.5643 to 0.6964 as new topics are explored, but decreasing sharply to 0.5904 under full flipping. This counterintuitive fall occurs because fully inverted aspects push the system toward a narrower subset of opposite aspects, making the set internally more homogeneous.

Several
RADio
metrics,
Calibration
,
Activation
, and
Representation
, exhibit a U-shaped trend as flipping increases. When only a few aspects are flipped, most candidate vectors remain aligned with the user’s history, leading to small declines:
Calibration
drops from 0.5922 to 0.5632, while
Activation
and
Representation
also drop slightly, reflecting reduced emotional alignment and increased frame alignment with the dataset. As more aspects are flipped, new opposite topics and frames enter the mix, reversing the trend. With full flipping, candidate vectors are completely inverted, producing a sharp increase in all three metrics—
Calibration
rises to 0.6157, and
Activation
and
Representation
rise to 0.3188 and 0.5856, respectively.

In contrast,
Fragmentation
steadily decreases (0.7433 to 0.5546) as flipping increases. Since the same flipping process is applied to all users, the candidate set, while more diverse for each individual, becomes more similar across users, reducing cross-user diversity.

The
Alternative Voices
metric increases steadily with more aggressive flipping, reaching its highest value across all experiments at 0.7441 for the most extreme setting (
n
=5, 100%). This represents a substantial improvement over both the
MIND
baseline (0.5705) and the vanilla
A2CG
(0.5880). In practical terms, this means users are exposed to a wider range of frames and viewpoints that differ from their historical preferences.

[

,

]
(for operationalization see Section 3) to evaluate how diversity injection affects the democratic functions of candidate generation.

) scores for each democratic framework. The results demonstrate how various
A2CG

The baseline methods each exhibit a strong, inherent alignment with different democratic ideals, highlighting their inflexibility. The History Average method, which focuses purely on a user’s history, excels at the
Liberal
model (0.8210), which prioritizes individual preference. It also, perhaps counter-intuitively, scores highest on the
Critical
model (0.7965). This suggests that by averaging a user’s history, the resulting candidates are generic enough to challenge the specific dominant narratives of any single article. Conversely, it is the lowest on
Deliberative
model (0.5905), which emphasizes reasoned debate and exposure to diverse perspectives. This indicates that simply averaging a user’s historical preferences does little to foster engagement with opposing viewpoints or promote thoughtful deliberation across different frames.

In contrast, random or cluster-based baselines, e.g., K-Means Random, achieve higher scores on
Participatory
(0.8971) and
Deliberative
(0.9666) models, reflecting their greater ability to introduce novel content and encourage collective engagement, at the cost of reduced alignment with individual preferences (
Liberal
: 0.3494).

Our
A2CG
framework, however, demonstrates remarkable flexibility, capable of being tuned to prioritize any of the four democratic models. This adaptability addresses the core limitation of the baselines. For the
Liberal
model, a moderate configuration (
n
=3, 50%) achieves a score of 0.7893, rivaling the top-performing History Average and
MIND
baselines by preserving a high degree of personalization. Predictably, the most aggressive setting (
n
=5, 100%) has the lowest score on this metric, confirming that excessive diversity injection undermines individual preference.

To align with
Participatory
and
Deliberative
models, more aggressive flipping is required. The
n
=4, 100% configuration significantly boosts these scores, making it competitive with the K-Means methods while maintaining a much stronger
Liberal
score (0.7513). This shows
A2CG
can broaden perspectives without completely abandoning personalization.

For the
Critical
model, which aims to challenge dominant narratives, the most aggressive configuration (
n
=5, 100%) is the most effective. It achieves the highest scores among
A2CG
variants for both frame (0.7550) and political (0.7772) aspects of the
Critical
model. This demonstrates that aggressive diversity injection (higher n values and flipping probability) is a powerful tool for surfacing alternative and oppositional viewpoints.

In conclusion, unlike the static baselines, which are locked into a single normative approach,
A2CG
offers the opportunity for versatile control. By adjusting the flipping parameters, a system can be deliberately configured to align with specific democratic objectives such as fostering personal development (
Liberal
), building a shared understanding (
Participatory/Deliberative
), or questioning the status quo (
Critical
). This tunability offers a practical path for our model to navigate the inherent tensions between these often-competing democratic ideals.

6
Discussion

Our findings indicate that a single model cannot effectively serve all four democratic models. No model consistently scored high across every category, demonstrating that there is not a one-size-fits-all solution. Instead of trying to create a universal model, the key is to fine-tune a model to align with a specific democratic goal. The advantage of
A2CG
is that it allows news outlets to tune the system to match the model that best reflects their vision
[
Helberger 2019
]
. Moreover, it provides a tuning mechanism directly to users, enabling them to make their own adjustments and promoting greater transparency in recommendation systems.

Baseline models like K-Means and History Average can be optimized for particular democratic models –
Participatory/Deliberative
and
Calibration
, respectively. However, these baselines have inherent flaws. K-Means relies heavily on the overall data distribution, while History Average is over-reliant on a user’s past behavior. These limitations prevent them from achieving the flexibility needed to navigate the complex trade-offs between different democratic ideals. Furthermore, they are not ideal for the downstream NRS, which requires a degree of personalization and diversity.

Our
A2CG
, however, provides the necessary flexibility to address these limitations. By acting as a “control panel” with its aspect-flipping mechanism,
A2CG
can be tuned to prioritize any of the four democratic models while still maintaining a strong balance of personalization and diversity. For instance, while a moderate configuration can rival the History Average model in serving the
Liberal
ideal, a more aggressive setting can effectively help the representation of all perspectives (
Participatory/Deliberative
), or even changing the narratives (
Critical
) or the tone (
Activation
) outperforming most baselines in the
Critical
model. It can also be tuned to mirror the performance of
MIND
’s candidate generator. This ability to adjust parameters allows for a deliberate and responsible approach to news curation, enabling a single framework to meet diverse and often competing societal objectives.

A key limitation of this work is the lack of a formal evaluation of the aspect classifier on the
MIND
dataset. We acknowledge that our framework’s effectiveness relies on this component, but the intensive process of creating a human-annotated dataset was beyond our current scope. The results of our downstream analysis, however, serve as a strong indirect validation of the classifier’s performance. For future work, we will create a gold-standard test set for evaluation and fine-tuning. Alternatively, we will develop a model to jointly learn aspect representations and user preferences, which would eliminate the need for a separate classifier.

7
Conclusion and Future Works

In this paper, we propose Aspect-Aware Candidate Generation (
A2CG
) to democratize news recommender systems, grounding our work in a theoretical model of democracy. Our evaluation addresses three key research questions, demonstrating
A2CG
’s effectiveness and flexibility.

First, we find that the vanilla
A2CG
model performs competitively against both baselines and the proprietary
MIND
algorithm. Second, by integrating a mechanism for diversity injection,
A2CG
becomes a versatile and tunable solution for balancing personalization with normative diversity. Finally, we demonstrated that while baseline methods are inherently limited to a single democratic model,
A2CG
can be precisely configured to target specific models of democracy, offering a more adaptable and responsible approach to candidate generation.

For future work, we plan to integrate
A2CG
into a comprehensive simulation model. This simulation environment will provide a controlled and efficient way to test and optimize NRS, offering insights that would be difficult to obtain through real-world deployment.

Ethical Considerations

Our approach,
A2CG
, if implemented maliciously, may prioritize the candidate set of only one perspective, potentially leading to selective exposure of information
[
Hart et al. 2009
]
.

Another factor to take into account is the quality of the aspect classifiers. We need to ensure that they are not misclassifying news articles excessively. For example, in the context of political leaning, the diversification would be affected and one side of the political spectrum could be overemphasized even though the objective of
A2CG
is to diversify content.

At a general level, when implemented for real-world use cases, the pipeline should ensure it includes trusted sources to avoid spreading misinformation.
