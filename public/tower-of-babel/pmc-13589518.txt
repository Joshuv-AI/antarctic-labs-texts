Ultrasound-Guided Acupotomy for Degenerative Cervical Radiculopathy: Considerations on Effect Estimates, Feasibility, and Safety Reporting [Letter]
Authors: Wenjie Xu, Junjie Yao, Lijun Wang
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with interest the pilot randomized controlled trial by Cho et al comparing ultrasound-guided acupotomy (UA) with conventional physical therapy (CPT) in patients with degenerative cervical radiculopathy.1 The study provides useful preliminary data on an image-guided intervention for which direct comparative evidence remains limited, reporting high treatment adherence, between-group differences favoring UA for several pain and functional outcomes, and no intervention-related serious adverse event during the study period. Because these findings are intended to inform a future confirmatory trial, three analytical clarifications may help define the treatment effect, feasibility, and safety estimates more precisely.

First, the exact treatment contrast underlying the longitudinal clinical analyses would benefit from clarification. The Methods state that between-group differences in mean change from baseline to weeks 4, 7, and 11 were evaluated, whereas the Statistical Methods describe analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with baseline score as a covariate, and Table 3 reports adjusted mean differences. For neck pain, baseline visual analog scale (VAS) means were 75.2 mm in the UA group and 63.6 mm in the CPT group; at week 4, the corresponding observed means were 30.9 and 48.4 mm. Thus, the unadjusted difference between the observed week-4 means is 17.5 mm, whereas Table 3 reports an adjusted contrast of 25.7 mm. ANCOVA is a well-established approach for baseline-adjusted comparisons in randomized trials,2 but this difference makes the model specification relevant to interpretation. Could the authors clarify whether the ANCOVA modeled the follow-up score or change from baseline, and whether baseline was included as a repeated time point in the repeated-measures ANOVA? The Statistical Methods also state that missing values were handled using last observation carried forward. Would the authors consider a sensitivity analysis using, for example, a mixed model for repeated measures based on available follow-up observations to assess whether visit-specific treatment contrasts are consistent with the reported estimates?

Second, the interpretation of feasibility would be strengthened by clarifying how the observed feasibility measures were intended to inform progression to a definitive trial. Feasibility outcomes were designated as the primary outcomes, and the reported attrition rate of 3.1%, protocol adherence of 96.9%, UA treatment adherence of 100%, and data completeness of 96.9% are encouraging. However, the published article does not report prespecified progression criteria linking these measures to a decision about whether, or under what modifications, the study should proceed to a larger confirmatory trial. Guidance for randomized pilot and feasibility trials recommends relating feasibility outcomes to progression decisions and making the rationale for the pilot sample size explicit in relation to its objectives.3 Could the authors clarify whether progression criteria for recruitment, attrition, adherence, or data completeness were specified in the registered protocol and, if so, how the observed estimates compared with those criteria? If formal thresholds were not prespecified, confidence intervals for the principal feasibility proportions and a brief explanation of how these estimates will inform recruitment assumptions, attrition allowances, and other design parameters would help clarify the basis for progression.

Third, clarification of the denominator and reporting metric for treatment-related harms would improve the clinical interpretation of the safety findings. In the UA group, nine adverse events (AEs) were reported among five participants, including five treatment-related AEs across 96 treatment sessions; Table 2 notes that no intervention-related serious AE was observed. Because the 16 UA participants contributed 96 treatment sessions, the reported 5/96 session-level proportion is not equivalent to the proportion of patients who experienced at least one treatment-related AE during the six-session course. Harms-reporting guidance recommends distinguishing participant-level risks from event rates when recurrent events are possible.4 Could the authors report the number and proportion of unique UA participants who experienced at least one treatment-related AE, with an exact confidence interval, and clarify whether any treatment-related events recurred within the same participant? It would also be useful to show the uncertainty surrounding the observation of zero intervention-related serious AEs in the 16 UA-treated participants, because observing zero events in a small cohort provides limited precision for estimating the underlying risk.5

These clarifications would help distinguish what was directly observed in this pilot trial from what can be inferred about longitudinal treatment effects, progression to a definitive study, and patient-level safety. We would welcome the authors’ clarification of these points and, where feasible, the corresponding supplementary estimates.
