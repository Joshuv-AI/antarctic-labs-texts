SME-TEAM: Leveraging Trust and Ethics for Secure and Responsible Use of AI and LLMs in SMEs
Iqbal H. Sarker, Helge Janicke, Ahmad Mohsin, Leandros Maglaras

Abstract
Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Large Language Models (LLMs) are reshaping today’s business practices, however, their adoption within small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) raises significant technical, ethical and trust issues. This paper proposes a structured, multi-phased framework designed to embed trust and ethical principles throughout the AI lifecycle for their secure and responsible use in SMEs. Structured around four pillars, i.e., Data, Algorithms, Human oversight, and Model Architecture, the framework bridges theoretical ethical principles with operational practice, enhancing AI capabilities in diverse SME applications. Ultimately, this paper offers a structured roadmap for responsible AI adoption, framing trust and ethics as a catalyst for resilience, competitiveness, and sustainable innovation in SMEs. Keywords: SMEs, Digital transformation, Ethical AI, Trust, Cybersecurity, Data-driven, Human-AI, Sustainable development.

The rapid integration of AI and LLMs into business operations introduces a complex array of risk factors and cybersecurity challenges

AI systems are highly susceptible to adversarial attacks, where carefully manipulated inputs distort outputs and lead to flawed or harmful decisions. Beyond these technical vulnerabilities, AI adoption magnifies broader challenges including algorithmic bias, opacity in decision-making, and over-reliance on automation, all of which can erode accountability and stakeholder trust. SMEs with their comparatively limited cybersecurity resources and governance mechanisms, are particularly vulnerable. Still many SMEs are not ready to adopt AI due to these risk factors and ethical issues
[
41
]
. This underscores the crucial importance of embedding secure-by-design principles, robust data governance, continuous monitoring, and structured human oversight across the AI lifecycle.

Conceptual Foundation for the Framework

3.1
Trust and Ethical AI Principles

The adoption of AI in SMEs must be guided by a set of core trust and ethical principles that serve as both a moral foundation and an operational compass. Drawing on existing studies

43

44

, we outline the following key principles, which are interlinked and mutually reinforcing, to ensure that AI adoption promotes secure, responsible, and sustainable outcomes. These are:
Fairness and non-discrimination
ensuring AI systems avoid bias and treat all groups equitably;
Accountability and responsibility
which require clear mechanisms of oversight, liability, and redress for AI-driven outcomes;
Transparency and explainability
which make decision pathways interpretable for both technical and non-technical stakeholders;
Privacy and data protection
safeguard personal information and ensure compliance with regulatory standards;
Safety and security
embedding resilience against adversarial attacks, misuse, and system vulnerabilities;
Human-centredness and oversight
emphasise the necessity of retaining meaningful human control, especially in high-stakes contexts;
Inclusivity and accessibility
ensure that AI serves diverse users and helps bridge digital divides;
Trustworthiness and reliability
highlight the importance of delivering consistent, dependable results that inspire confidence;
Contestability
guarantees that individuals, groups, or communities impacted by AI systems have timely avenues to challenge outcomes or decisions; and finally,
Human, societal, and environmental wellbeing
reinforces the principle that AI adoption should advance not only business efficiency but also the broader interests of society and the environment. Together, these principles provide SMEs with actionable guidance to integrate AI and LLMs responsibly, balancing innovation with accountability and ensuring that digital transformation contributes to long-term resilience and sustainable growth.

4
The Framework: SME-TEAM

To enable secure and responsible adoption of AI in SMEs, this paper introduces a structured, multi-phased framework “SME-TEAM" consisting of seven layers, grounded in trust, ethics, and AI modeling, as shown in Figure
5
. The framework provides SMEs with a structured, scalable roadmap that integrates trust and ethical principles across the AI lifecycle. It is designed to be adaptable, offering SMEs guidance that balances innovation with risk mitigation, ensuring systems are both operationally effective and socially responsible. In the following, we discuss the components of the framework, structured across three phases.

Figure 5:
An illustration of SME-TEAM framework.

Pre-Modelling Phase:
The pre-modelling phase establishes the foundation for secure and responsible AI adoption in SMEs by embedding governance, ethics, and data stewardship before any technical integration begins. In contexts where resources are limited but risks can be disproportionately high, SMEs must first develop ethical and trust foundations through lightweight but explicit guidelines that emphasize fairness, inclusivity, accountability, and transparency, ensuring alignment with sectoral expectations and regulatory requirements. At the same time, ensuring data integrity and provenance becomes essential, with mechanisms such as metadata tagging, bias detection, anonymisation, and privacy compliance safeguarding the authenticity and reliability of input datasets. By addressing vulnerabilities such as data poisoning, manipulation, and hidden skewness early, this phase creates a credible baseline of trust and integrity. Collectively, the pre-modelling practices ensure that SMEs approach AI adoption with the necessary ethical, legal, and contextual readiness, avoiding premature experimentation and fostering the resilience required for trustworthy AI systems.

In-Modelling Phase:
The in-modelling phase forms the technical core of AI adoption within the knowledge value chain
[
48
]
, embedding resilience, transparency, and contextual alignment into model design and deployment. Secure-by-design practices strengthen robustness against adversarial threats, anomaly detection failures, and injection attacks, thereby safeguarding SMEs from vulnerabilities that could undermine reliability. Simultaneously, explainability and interpretability mechanisms address the “black-box” challenge
[
5
]
of AI and LLMs by offering human-centred transparency through decision pathways, counterfactual reasoning, and intuitive visualisation dashboards that enable non-technical stakeholders to validate results. In parallel, context-aware alignment integrates ethical and sector-specific guardrails, ensuring outputs remain responsible, compliant, and relevant across sensitive domains such as finance, healthcare, and manufacturing, while minimising unintended consequences. Collectively, these measures transform modelling from a purely technical exercise into a secure, interpretable, and ethically aligned process that strengthens trust and operational credibility for SMEs adopting AI.

Post-Modelling Phase:
The post-modelling phase secures the long-term trust, accountability, and adaptability of AI systems in SMEs by embedding continuous governance and oversight into day-to-day operations. Human–AI collaboration plays a central role, with decision-making protocols formalising human-in-the-loop mechanisms that balance automation with expert judgement, ensuring clear escalation pathways in high-risk domains such as finance, healthcare, and safety-critical industries. Alongside this, continuous monitoring and ethical auditing introduce adaptive safeguards, including bias re-evaluation, drift detection, compliance checks, and performance tracking, enabling SMEs to adjust dynamically to shifting ethical standards, regulatory requirements, and operational environments. Collectively, these practices shift deployment from a static endpoint to a proactive, ongoing lifecycle, ensuring that AI systems remain transparent, accountable, and trustworthy well beyond their initial rollout.

Together, these seven layers form a holistic, phased framework for AI adoption in SMEs. By embedding trust, ethics, and AI modeling across the entire lifecycle, the framework equips SMEs to manage operational and ethical challenges while harnessing AI as a catalyst for innovation, competitiveness, and sustainable growth.

6
Implications for Research

The framework highlights the importance of developing AI technologies that are not only technically robust but also contextually practical for SMEs, which often operate with constrained budgets, limited computational resources, and smaller technical teams compared to large enterprises. Many existing approaches to explainability, bias detection, and adversarial robustness are tailored to enterprise-scale environments, requiring significant infrastructure and expertise that SMEs cannot realistically sustain. Addressing this gap demands research into lightweight, resource-efficient approaches, such as simplified explainability tools that produce human-readable insights, bias detection algorithms tailored to SME datasets, and robustness mechanisms capable of functioning on standard SME-level infrastructure. By adapting innovations to the SME context, barriers to adoption can be reduced, ensuring that responsible and trustworthy AI becomes accessible beyond large corporations and contributes broadly to the economic ecosystem.

Beyond efficiency, there is an urgent need to develop sector-specific interpretability frameworks that translate AI outputs into insights meaningful within their application context. While general-purpose interpretability methods offer algorithmic transparency, they often fail to address the ethical and operational realities of specific domains. In finance, this requires risk models to be explainable in terms of fairness and compliance with lending regulations; in healthcare, interpretability must align with clinical protocols and patient safety standards; in manufacturing, explanations should emphasise implications for quality assurance and operational efficiency. Advancing research into domain-specific interpretability ensures that AI outputs are not only transparent but also actionable and ethically grounded, enabling SME stakeholders to adopt AI-driven decision-making with greater confidence.

Another promising avenue is the development of adaptive auditing and drift detection methods tailored to SME environments. Unlike static compliance checks, adaptive auditing supports continuous monitoring, enabling SMEs to stay aligned with evolving ethical standards and regulatory requirements without relying on constant external oversight. Research can advance this by designing lightweight systems that detect data drift, concept drift, and ethical risks in real time, allowing vulnerabilities to be addressed before they escalate into systemic failures. Beyond the technical dimension, interdisciplinary collaboration is equally vital. Insights from social sciences, business studies, and ethics can help uncover how organisational culture, employee trust, and stakeholder values influence AI adoption, ensuring that technological innovations evolve into socially responsible solutions that reflect the practical realities of SMEs.

7
Discussion and Final Remarks

7.1
Theoretical Contributions

This paper contributes to the expanding body of research on secure and responsible AI by proposing a structured framework that embeds trust and ethical principles throughout the AI lifecycle in the SME context. The framework advances theory in four key ways. First, it bridges the gap between abstract ethical principles and their operationalisation, illustrating how values such as fairness, transparency, and accountability can be translated into actionable layers of AI adoption. Second, it introduces four conceptual pillars - Data, Algorithms, Human Oversight, and Model Architecture, as theoretical anchors that connect ethical values with practical implementation, offering a robust lens for balancing technical, organisational, and ethical dimensions. Third, it adopts a multi-phased perspective encompassing pre-modelling, in-modelling, and post-modelling stages, thereby providing a holistic understanding of ethical AI deployment across the full pipeline rather than in isolation. Finally, it extends AI governance debates by positioning trust and ethics not as constraints but as enablers of sustainable innovation, resilience, and competitiveness. Collectively, these contributions establish a novel foundation for understanding how SMEs can integrate AI responsibly while ensuring long-term growth and societal value.

7.2
Practical Implications

Beyond its theoretical significance, the framework provides SMEs with a practical roadmap for adopting AI securely and responsibly. It enables organisations to leverage automation, predictive insights, and personalisation while maintaining data integrity, protecting privacy, and ensuring regulatory compliance. For business leaders, it offers actionable guidance on enhancing productivity, expanding market opportunities, mitigating cyber risks, and driving sustainable innovation. Crucially, it emphasises digital market expansion alongside new employment opportunities, ensuring that transformation supports workforce development rather than displacement. For policymakers, the framework serves as a scalable blueprint for shaping SME-focused digital strategies, funding schemes, and regulatory interventions. Collectively, it illustrates how trust and ethics centred AI adoption can position SMEs as resilient, innovative, and competitive actors in the digital economy.
