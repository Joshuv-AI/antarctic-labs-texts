The Complexity of Pure Strategy Relevant Equilibria in Concurrent Games
Purandar Bhaduri

Abstract
We study rational synthesis problems for concurrent games with ω \omega -regular objectives. Our model of rationality considers only pure strategy Nash equilibria that satisfy either a social welfare or Pareto optimality condition with respect to an ω \omega -regular objective for each agent. This extends earlier work on equilibria in concurrent games, without consideration about their quality. Our results show that the existence of Nash equilibria satisfying social welfare conditions can be computed as efficiently as the constrained Nash equilibrium existence problem. On the other hand, the existence of Nash equilibria satisfying the Pareto optimality condition possibly involves a higher upper bound, except in the case of Büchi and Muller games, for which all three problems are in the classes 𝖯 \mathsf{P} and 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -complete, respectively.

Introduction

Infinite games on finite graphs play a fundamental role in the automated synthesis of reactive systems from their specification
[
7
,
3
]
. The goal of reactive synthesis is to design a system that meets its specification in all possible environments. This problem can be modelled as a zero-sum game between the system and the environment, where a winning strategy for the system yields the design of a correct-by-construction controller.

In many situations involving autonomous agents, such as robots, drones, and
autonomous vehicles, a purely adversarial view of the environment of a system is not appropriate. Instead, each agent should be viewed as trying to satisfy her own objective rather than preventing the other agents from meeting theirs. This gives rise to the notion of
rational synthesis

[
8
,
12
]
, where we restrict our attention to agent behaviours that arise from game-theoretic equilibria. The most widely studied equilibrium is the Nash equilibrium (NE)
[
13
]
, a strategy profile for which no agent has the incentive to unilaterally deviate from her strategy.

In this paper we investigate the complexity of computing certain desirable Nash
equilibria in concurrent games played on finite graphs. We call these equilibria

relevant
after
[
5
]
. Our games have multiple
agents (or players), each with an
\omega
-regular objective. The games are concurrent, where the agents make their moves simultaneously. Each agent tries to fulfil her own objective while being subject to the decisions of the other agents. Such games provide a common framework for modelling systems with multiple, distributed, and autonomous agents.

It is well known that for such games pure strategy Nash equilibria may not exist and may not be unique when they do. Some equilibria may not be optimal, where only a few or none of the agents meet their objectives. We focus on two measures of the
quality of equilibria – social welfare and Pareto optimality.

Intuitively, the social welfare criterion considers the number of agents who
meet their objectives in a given equilibrium. Pareto optimal equilibria, on the
other hand, consider equilibria which are maximal in the sense that no additional
agent can meet her objective in any behaviour of the system, whether it is the
result of an equilibrium or not.

The specific problems which we are interested in are (i) the Constrained NE Existence Problem, asking whether there is an equilibrium satisfying a given lower and upper threshold for the payoff of each agent, (ii) the Social Welfare Decision Problem (
\mathsf{SWDP}
), asking whether there is an equilibrium where the number of agents meeting their objectives is above a given threshold,
and (iii) the Pareto Optimal Decision Problem (
\mathsf{PODP}
), where the problem is to decide whether there is an equilibrium such that no other strategy profile, whether an equilibrium or not, results in a strict superset of agents meeting their objectives. The complexity of the Constrained NE Existence Problem for different
\omega
-regular objectives was extensively studied by Bouyer
et al.

[
4
]
. We mention them here as a point of reference and also because many of our results depend on them.

\mathsf{SWDP}
and

However, a Pareto optimality condition seems to entail a higher upper bound, except for Büchi and Muller objectives, for which all three problems are in the classes
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete, respectively.

1.1
Related Work

Concurrent game structures were introduced by Alur
et al.

[
2
]

for modelling the behaviour of open systems containing both system and environment components, called players or agents. Every state transition in such a game is determined by a choice of move by each player and models their synchronous composition.

The seminal work related to problems on Nash equilibria for concurrent games is
by Bouyer
et al.

[
4
]
. This paper studies Nash equilibria for
pure-strategy multiplayer concurrent deterministic games for various

\omega
-regular objectives and their generalisations. It gives
comprehensive results for the Value Problem, the NE Existence Problem and the
Constrained NE Existence Problem for all these objectives. The present work is
an extension where we also study the existence of desirable equilibria,
namely the existence of NE with lower bounds on social welfare and those satisfying
Pareto optimality.

The term ‘relevant equilibria’ was introduced by Brihaye
et al.

[
5
]

to refer to desirable equilibria such as those with high social welfare and those that are Pareto optimal. This paper was in the context of turn-based multiplayer games with quantitative reachability objectives. In contrast, our work is about concurrent qualitative games with the
\omega
-regular objectives specified above.

Another line of work that deals with infinite concurrent games on finite graphs
with both qualitative and quantitative objectives is by Gutierrez

et al.

[
10
]
. In contrast to our work, their focus is on

\mathsf{LTL}
and
\mathsf{GR(1)}
objectives, a setting where the input sizes can
be exponentially more succinct and the expressive power somewhat restricted.

Condurache
et al.

[
6
]
have also investigated rational synthesis problems for concurrent games for the same objectives that we consider in this paper. However, their interest lies in
non-cooperative
rational synthesis, where the aim is to synthesise a strategy for agent
0
such that with respect to
all
NE involving the rest of the agents, agent
0
’s payoff is always
1
. Computationally, this is a harder problem than the
cooperative
rational synthesis problem in our work, where we are interested in the existence of
at least one
NE satisfying some constraints. Moreover,
[
6
]
does not consider the quality of NEs, in contrast to the present work.

2.2
Concurrent Game Structures

Definition 2.1
.

A
concurrent game structure
(CGS) is a tuple
\mathcal{G}=(\mathsf{St},\mathsf{Agt},\mathsf{Act},\mathsf{avb},\mathsf{tr})
where
\mathsf{St}
is a finite non-empty set of states,
\mathsf{Agt}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
is the set of agents (or players),
\mathsf{Act}
is a finite set of actions, the map
\mathsf{avb}:\mathsf{St}\times\mathsf{Agt}\rightarrow 2^{\mathsf{Act}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
indicates the actions available in a given state for a given agent, and
\mathsf{tr}:\mathsf{St}\times\mathsf{Act}^{n}\rightarrow\mathsf{St}
is the transition function.

An
action profile
or
move

\bar{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathsf{Act}^{n}
is just an
n
-tuple of actions. Here,
a_{i}
is the action taken by
agent
i
. We often write
\bar{a}(i)
for
a_{i}
. We say
\bar{a}
is

legal
at a state
s
if
a_{i}\in\mathsf{avb}(s,i)
for all
i\in\mathsf{Agt}
.
We call a CGS
turn-based
if, for each state, the set of available moves
is a singleton for all but at most one player; such a player is said to
own

the state.
1
1

1

We prefer the term player to agent when referring to
turn-based games.

Definition 2.2
.

A
play
in the CGS
\mathcal{G}
is a sequence of states

\rho=s_{0}s_{1}s_{2}\ldots
such that
s_{j+1}=\mathsf{tr}(s_{j},\bar{a}_{j})
for some legal move
\bar{a}_{j}
in state

s_{j}
, for all
j\geq 0
.

Let
\mathsf{Plays}(\mathcal{G})
be the set of all plays in the CGS
\mathcal{G}
. A
history

h

is any finite prefix of a play. Let
\mathsf{Hist}(\mathcal{G})
be the set of
all histories in
\mathcal{G}
. We often drop
\mathcal{G}
when referring to plays and
histories when it is clear from the context. The last element of a history
h
is denoted by
\mathsf{last}(h)
.

Definition 2.3
.

A strategy for agent
i
is a map
\sigma_{i}:\mathsf{Hist}\rightarrow\mathsf{Act}
such that
\sigma_{i}(h)\in\mathsf{avb}(\mathsf{last}(h),i)
. A

strategy profile
is a tuple
\bar{\sigma}=\langle\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}\rangle
of strategies, one for each agent.

By convention,
\bar{\sigma}_{-i}
is the tuple of strategies excluding that of
agent
i
and
\langle\bar{\sigma}_{-i},\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\rangle
is obtained from the
profile
\bar{\sigma}
by substituting agent
i
’s strategy
\sigma_{i}

by
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
. Note that our strategies are
pure
,
i.e.
, they do not involve any randomisation.

We say that a play
\rho
is
compatible
with a strategy
\sigma_{i}
of agent
i
if for every prefix

\rho[0,k]
of
\rho
with
k\geq 0
we have
\sigma_{i}(\rho[0,k])=a_{i_{k}}
and
\mathsf{tr}(\rho[k],\langle a_{-i_{k}},a_{i_{k}}\rangle)=\rho[k+1]
, for some action profile
a_{-i_{k}}
of the other agents making
\langle a_{-i_{k}},a_{i_{k}}\rangle
legal in
\rho[k]
. We can define compatibility between a history and an agent
i
strategy in a similar way. For a coalition
P\subseteq\mathsf{Agt}
, and a tuple
\sigma_{P}
of strategies for the agents in
P
, we write
\mathsf{Out}_{\mathcal{G}}(\sigma_{P})
for the set of plays (called
outcomes
) in
\mathcal{G}
that are compatible with strategy
\sigma_{i}
for every
i\in P
. Note that a strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}
and an initial state
s
uniquely define a play
\mathsf{Out}(s,\bar{\sigma})
, referred to as its
outcome
.

Remark
.

We assume that the transition function
\mathsf{tr}
is represented explicitly as a table when
\mathcal{G}
is an input to an algorithm. Its size
|\mathsf{tr}|
is
\sum_{s\in\mathsf{St}}\prod_{i\in\mathsf{Agt}}\mathsf{avb}(s,i)\cdot\lceil\log(|\mathsf{St}|)\rceil
and this can be exponential in the number
n
of agents.

2.3
Concurrent Games and Solution Concepts

Omega-regular Games

A concurrent (or multiplayer) game is a pair
\langle\mathcal{G},(\mathsf{Obj}_{i})_{i\in\mathsf{Agt}}\rangle
where
\mathcal{G}
is a CGS and
\mathsf{Obj}_{i}\subseteq\mathsf{St}^{\omega}
is the

objective
for agent
i
. Thus, an objective is a set of infinite sequences
of states in
\mathcal{G}
. For us, an objective can be any one of safety,
reachability, Büchi, coBüchi, parity or Muller, defined below.
For a play
\rho
in a CGS
\mathcal{G}
we write
\mathit{occ}(\rho)
for the states that occur
in
\rho
and
\mathit{inf}(\rho)
for the states that occur infinitely often
in
\rho
,
i.e.
,
\mathit{occ}(\rho)=\{s\in\mathsf{St}\mid\exists j\geq 0.\;s=\rho[j]\}
and
\mathit{inf}(\rho)=\{s\in\mathsf{St}\mid\forall j\geq 0.\;\exists k\geq j.\;s=\rho[k]\}
. Then, we consider the following objectives:

1.

Reachability:
Given a set
F\subseteq\mathsf{St}
of
target states
,
\text{{Reach}}(F)=\{\rho\in\mathsf{St}^{\omega}\mid\mathit{occ}(\rho)\cap F\neq\emptyset\}
;

2.

Safety:
Given a set
F\subseteq\mathsf{St}
of
unsafe states
,
\text{{Safe}}(F)=\{\rho\in\mathsf{St}^{\omega}\mid\mathit{occ}(\rho)\cap F=\emptyset\}
;

3.

Büchi:
Given a set
F\subseteq\mathsf{St}
of
accept states
,
\text{{B{\"{u}}chi}}(F)=\{\rho\in\mathsf{St}^{\omega}\mid\mathit{inf}(\rho)\cap F\neq\emptyset\}
;

4.

coBüchi:
Given a set
F\subseteq\mathsf{St}
of
reject states
,
\text{{coB{\"{u}}chi}}(F)=\{\rho\in\mathsf{St}^{\omega}\mid\mathit{inf}(\rho)\cap F=\emptyset\}
;

5.

Parity:
For a given
priority function

p:\mathsf{St}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
,
\text{{Parity}}(p)=\{\rho\in\mathsf{St}^{\omega}\mid\min\{p(s)\mid s\in\mathit{inf}(\rho)\}\text{ is even}\}
;

6.

Muller:
For a given finite set
C
of
colours
, a colouring function
c:\mathsf{St}\rightarrow C
and a set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{C}
,
\text{{Muller}}(\varphi)=\{\rho\in\mathsf{St}^{\omega}\mid\mathit{inf}(c(\rho))\in\mathcal{F}\}
. Here
c(\rho)
is the infinite sequence of colours of the states in the sequence
\rho
and
\mathit{inf}(c(\rho))
is the set of colours appearing infinitely often in the sequence
c(\rho)
.

For a given play
\rho
, the
payoff
of
\rho
, denoted
\mathsf{Payoff}(\rho)
, is given by the tuple
\langle\mathsf{Payoff}_{i}(\rho)\rangle_{i\in\mathsf{Agt}}
. Here,
\mathsf{Payoff}_{i}(\rho)\in\{0,1\}
, the payoff of agent
i
, is defined by
\mathsf{Payoff}_{i}(\rho)=1\Leftrightarrow\rho\in\mathsf{Obj}_{i}
. We say agent
i
wins the play
\rho
if her payoff is
1
and she loses
\rho
otherwise. For a given state
s
in
\mathcal{G}
, we write
\mathsf{Payoff}(s,\bar{\sigma})
(respectively,
\mathsf{Payoff}_{i}(s,\bar{\sigma})
) for the payoff (respectively, payoff of agent
i
) for the unique play
\rho
that is the outcome of the strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}
starting from state
s
.

A two-player concurrent game is
zero-sum
if for any play
\rho
, Player
1
wins
\rho
if, and only if, Player
2
loses it. This is a purely adversarial setting, where one player loses if the other wins and there are no ties. In a zero-sum game, we are interested in finding a winning strategy for a player from a given state if it exists. Such a strategy allows the player to win no matter how the other player moves. In non-zero-sum games, where each player has her own objective and is not necessarily trying to play spoilsport, winning strategies are too restrictive and seldom exist. Instead, the notion of an equilibrium, a strategy profile that is best possible for each player
given the strategies of the other players in the profile, is the key concept. The most celebrated equilibrium in the literature is that of Nash equilibrium, defined below.

Nash Equilibrium and Relevant Equilibria

The solution concept for games we consider in this paper is the Nash equilibrium
[
13
]
, a strategy profile in which no agent has the incentive to unilaterally change her strategy.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a concurrent game and let
s
be a state of
\mathcal{G}
. A strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}
is a
Nash equilibrium
(NE) of
\mathcal{G}
from
s
if for every agent
i
and every strategy
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}
of agent
i
, it is the case that

\mathsf{Payoff}_{i}(s,\bar{\sigma})\geq\mathsf{Payoff}_{i}(s,\langle\bar{\sigma}_{-i},\sigma_{i}^{\prime}\rangle)
.

In the kind of games we consider, the payoffs of agents for a given play
\rho
only depend on the set of states that are visited and the set of states that are visited infinitely often in
\rho
. For such games, the outcomes of Nash equilibria can be taken to be ultimately periodic sequences as shown in
[
4
]
(Proposition 3.1). We restate the result here.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is a concurrent game where for any pair
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
of plays,
\mathit{occ}(\rho)=\mathit{occ}(\rho^{\prime})
and
\inf(\rho)=\inf(\rho^{\prime})
imply
\mathsf{Payoff}(\rho)=\mathsf{Payoff}(\rho^{\prime})
. If
\rho
is an outcome of an NE in
\mathcal{G}
then there is an NE with outcome
\rho^{\prime}
of the form
\alpha_{1}.\alpha_{2}^{\omega}
such that
\mathsf{Payoff}(\rho)=\mathsf{Payoff}(\rho^{\prime})
, where the lengths
|\alpha_{1}|
and
|\alpha_{2}|
have an upper bound of
|\mathsf{St}|^{2}
.

In concurrent games, pure strategy Nash equilibria may not exist, or multiple
equilibria may exist. We identify the equilibria that are desirable and call
them
relevant
after
[
5
]
. For example, an equilibrium in which the number of players who meet their objectives is maximum among all equilibria (
i.e.
, one that maximises the
social welfare
defined below) would be considered a relevant one.

The relevant equilibria we consider are based on social welfare and Pareto
optimality. Given a play
\rho
in a concurrent
game
\langle\mathcal{G},(\mathsf{Obj})_{i}\rangle
, we denote by
\mathsf{Win}(\rho)
the set of
agents who meet their objective along
\rho
,
i.e.
,
\mathsf{Win}(\rho)=\{i\in\mathsf{Agt}\mid\rho\in\mathsf{Obj}_{i}\}
. The
social welfare

\mathsf{sw}(\rho)
of
\rho
is

|\mathsf{Win}(\rho)|
,
i.e.
, the number of agents who meet their objectives in

\rho
. For a given state
s
in
\mathcal{G}
and a strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}
,
we write
\mathsf{sw}(s,\bar{\sigma})
for
\mathsf{sw}(\rho)
where
\rho=\mathsf{Out}(s,\bar{\sigma})
.

To define Pareto optimality in a game, let
P=\{\langle w_{i}(\rho)\rangle_{i\in\mathsf{Agt}}\mid\rho\in\mathsf{Plays}(\mathcal{G})\}
, where
w_{i}(\rho)=1
if agent
i
wins

\rho
and
0
otherwise,
i.e.
, the set
P
is the set of winner profiles for all plays in
\mathcal{G}
. Then a winner profile
p\in P
is
Pareto optimal
if it is maximal in
P
with respect to the componentwise ordering
\leq_{P}
on
P
where
0<1
in each component. This means no other agent can be added to the set of winners in
p
,
i.e.
,
p
represents a maximal set of winners along any play.

2.4
Rational Synthesis Problems

The rational synthesis problem generalises the synthesis problem to multiagent
systems. The aim is to synthesise a game-theoretic equilibrium, a
Nash equilibrium in our case, that satisfies additional desirable properties.
The problems we study are detailed below.

For completeness, we start by referring to the
Constrained NE Problem

solved by Bouyer
et al.

[
4
]
. This problem asks whether there is an NE in a given game whose payoff profile satisfies both an upper and a lower threshold.
2
2

2

The original formulation in
[
4
]
was stated in terms of a preference relation
\lesssim_{A}
for each agent
A
as follows: Given two plays
\rho_{A}^{\ell}
and
\rho_{A}^{u}
for each agent
A
, is there a Nash equilibrium
\bar{\sigma}
which satisfies
\rho_{A}^{\ell}\lesssim_{A}\mathsf{Out}(\bar{\sigma})\lesssim_{A}\rho_{A}^{u}
for all
A\in\mathsf{Agt}
? Since for
\omega
-regular objectives a threshold play
\rho
is encoded by the pair
(\mathit{occ}(\rho),\mathit{inf}(\rho))
in
[
4
]
, the way we state the problem is polynomially equivalent.

Problem 1

(
Constrained NE Existence Problem).

Given a game
\mathcal{G}
, a state
s
in
\mathcal{G}
and
threshold tuples
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\!\in\!\mathbb{B}^{n}
, decide whether there
exists an NE
\bar{\sigma}
such that
\mathbf{v}\leq(\mathsf{Payoff}(s,\bar{\sigma}))\leq\mathbf{u}
,
where the ordering is defined componentwise.

Bouyer
et al.
showed that the problem is
\mathsf{P}
-complete for Büchi objectives,

\mathsf{NP}
-complete for safety, reachability and co-Büchi objectives, and

\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete for Muller objectives; the complexity class for parity
objectives is
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{NP}}_{\parallel}
-complete, where
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{NP}}_{\parallel}
is the class of problems
that can be solved by a deterministic Turing machine in polynomial time with an
access to an oracle for solving
\mathsf{NP}
problems and such that the oracle can be queried only
once with a set of queries.

We now come to the relevant equilibrium problems that we explore in this paper.
First, we define the social welfare problem as placing a lower bound on the social
welfare,
i.e.
, the number of winning players, of an equilibrium.

Problem 2

(
Social Welfare Decision Problem (
\mathsf{SWDP}
)).

Given a game
\mathcal{G}
, a state
s
in
\mathcal{G}
and a threshold value
v\in\mathbb{N}
, decide whether
there exists
an NE
\bar{\sigma}
such that
\mathsf{sw}(s,\bar{\sigma})\geq v
.

The second problem we consider is the Pareto optimality decision problem for rational synthesis.

Problem 3

(
Pareto Optimal Decision Problem (
\mathsf{PODP}
)).

Given a game
\mathcal{G}
and a state
s
in
\mathcal{G}
, decide whether
there exists
an NE
\bar{\sigma}
such that
\mathsf{Payoff}(s,\bar{\sigma})
is Pareto optimal.

Notice that the problems above are about the
existence
of NE satisfying
some conditions. Hence, it is a
cooperative
setting in which we assume that all agents will cooperate when presented with an NE.

2.5
The Suspect Game

Many results from Bouyer
et al.

[
4
]
that we use below rely on a key construction. The idea is based on a correspondence between Nash equilibria in a concurrent game
\mathcal{G}
and winning strategies in a two-player zero-sum game
\mathcal{H}
derived from
\mathcal{G}
, called the suspect game. The game
\mathcal{H}
is played between
Eve
and
Adam
. Intuitively,
Eve
’s goal is to prove that the sequence of moves proposed by her results from a Nash equilibrium in
\mathcal{G}
, while
Adam
’s task is to foil her attempt by exhibiting that some agent has a profitable deviation from the strategy suggested by
Eve
. Here we recall the basic definitions and results from
[
4
]
.

Given two states
s
and
s^{\prime}
and a move
\bar{a}
in a concurrent game
\mathcal{G}
, the set of
suspect agents
for
(s,s^{\prime})
and
\bar{a}
is the set

\mathsf{Susp}((s,s^{\prime}),\bar{a})=\{i\in\mathsf{Agt}\mid\exists a^{\prime}\in\mathsf{avb}(s,i).\ \mathsf{tr}(s,\langle\bar{a}_{-i},a^{\prime}\rangle)=s^{\prime}\}.

Note that if
\mathsf{tr}(s,\bar{a})=s^{\prime}
then
\mathsf{Susp}((s,s^{\prime}),\bar{a})=\mathsf{Agt}
,
i.e.
, every agent is suspect if there is no deviation from the suggested move.
For a play
\rho
and a strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}
, the set of suspect agents for
\rho
and
\bar{\sigma}
is given by the set of suspect agents along each transition of
\rho
:

\mathsf{Susp}(\rho,\bar{\sigma})=\{i\in\mathsf{Agt}\mid\forall j\in\mathbb{N}.\ i\in\mathsf{Susp}((\rho[j],\rho[j+1]),\bar{\sigma}(\rho[0,j])\}.

The idea is that agent
i
is a suspect for a pair
(s,s^{\prime})
and move
\bar{a}
if she can unilaterally deviate from her action
a_{i}
in
\bar{a}
to trigger the transition
(s,s^{\prime})
. It follows from the above definitions that agent
i
is in
\mathsf{Susp}(\rho,\bar{\sigma})
if, and only if, there is a strategy
\sigma^{\prime}
for agent
i
such that
\mathsf{Out}(\langle\bar{\sigma}_{-i},\sigma^{\prime}\rangle)=\rho
.

For a fixed play
\pi
in a concurrent game
\mathcal{G}
, we build the
suspect game

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
, a two-player turn-based zero-sum game between
Eve
and
Adam
as follows. The set of states of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
is the disjoint union of the states
V_{\exists}\subseteq\mathsf{St}\times 2^{\mathsf{Agt}}
owned by
Eve
, and the set
V_{\forall}\subset\mathsf{St}\times 2^{\mathsf{Agt}}\times\mathsf{Act}^{\mathsf{Agt}}
owned by
Adam
. The game proceeds in the following way: from a state
(s,P)
in
V_{\exists}
,
Eve
chooses a legal move
\bar{a}
from
s
in
\mathcal{G}
, resulting in the new state
(s,P,\bar{a})
in
V_{\forall}
.
Adam
then chooses a move
\bar{a}^{\prime}
that will actually apply in
\mathcal{G}
leading to a state
s^{\prime}
in
\mathsf{St}
; the resulting state in
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
is
(s^{\prime},P\cap\mathsf{Susp}((s,s^{\prime}),\bar{a}))
. In the special case when the state chosen by
Adam
is such that
s^{\prime}=\mathsf{tr}(s,\bar{a})
, we say that
Adam
obeys
Eve
. In this case, the new state is given by
(s^{\prime},P)
.

We define the two projections
\mathit{proj}_{1}
and
\mathit{proj}_{2}
from
V_{\exists}
to
\mathsf{St}
and
2^{\mathsf{Agt}}
, respectively, by
\mathit{proj}_{1}(s,P)=s
and
\mathit{proj}_{2}(s,P)=P
. These projections are extended to plays in
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
in a natural way but only using
Eve
’s states – for example,
\mathit{proj}_{1}((s_{0},P_{0})(s_{0},P_{0},\bar{a})(s_{1},P_{1}))\cdots=s_{0}s_{1}\cdots
. For any play
\rho
in
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
,
\mathit{proj}_{2}(\rho)
, which is a sequence of sets of agents in
\mathcal{G}
, is non-increasing, and hence its limit
\lambda(\rho)
is well defined. Note that if
\lambda(\rho)\neq\emptyset
then
\mathit{proj}_{1}(\rho)
is a play in
\mathcal{G}
. A play
\rho
is winning for
Eve
, if for all
i\in\lambda(\rho)
the play
\pi
is as good as or better than
\mathit{proj}_{1}(\rho)
for agent
i
in
\mathcal{G}
,
i.e.
.,
\mathsf{Payoff}_{i}(\pi)\geq\mathsf{Payoff}_{i}(\mathit{proj}_{1}(\rho))
. The
winning region

W(\mathcal{G},\pi)
is the set of states of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
from which
Eve
has a winning strategy.

The correctness of the suspect game construction is captured by the following theorem from
[
4
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a concurrent game,
s
a state of
\mathcal{G}
and
\pi
a play in
\mathcal{G}
. Then the following two conditions are equivalent.

1.

There is an NE
\bar{\sigma}
from
s
in
\mathcal{G}
whose outcome is
\pi
.

2.

There is a play
\rho
from
(s,\mathsf{Agt})
in
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
satisfying

(a)

Adam
obeys
Eve
along
\rho
,

(b)

\mathit{proj}_{1}(\rho)=\pi
, and

(c)

for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a strategy
\sigma_{\exists}^{i}
for
Eve
, for which any play in
\rho[0,i]\cdot\mathsf{Out}(\rho[i],\sigma_{\exists}^{i})
is winning for
Eve
.

The following theorem from
[
4
]
asserts that not only is the suspect game construction correct, but it does not result in an exponential blow-up in size as well. Note that the infinite play
\pi
is specified by the pair of finite sets
(\mathit{occ}(\pi),\mathit{inf}(\pi))
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a concurrent game and
\pi
a play in
\mathcal{G}
. The number of reachable states from
\mathsf{St}\times\mathsf{Agt}
in
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
is polynomial in the size of
\mathcal{G}
.

Note that for two plays
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
in a concurrent game
\mathcal{G}
the suspect games

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
and
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi^{\prime})
have identical CGSs and differ only in the winning conditions. Thus, the CGS
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{G})
of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
depends solely on
\mathcal{G}
and is polynomial in size. Also, if we denote the set of losing agents in
\pi
by
\mathrm{Los}(\pi)
, the winning condition for
Eve
in
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
can be stated as follows: for every
i\in\lambda(\rho)\cap\mathrm{Los}(\pi)
, agent
i
loses
\mathit{proj}_{1}(\rho)
in
\mathcal{G}
. Thus, the winning condition depends only on
\mathrm{Los}(\pi)
and not the exact sequence
\pi
. Henceforth we denote the suspect game by
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},L)
, where
L\subseteq\mathsf{Agt}
and
Eve
wins the play
\rho
if for every
i\in\lambda(\rho)\cap L
, agent
i
loses the play
\mathit{proj}_{1}(\rho)
in
\mathcal{G}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

\mathsf{SWDP}
for reachability objectives is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

Proof.

We reduce
\mathsf{SAT}
to
\mathsf{SWDP}
with reachability objectives. Let
\phi=C_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge C_{m}
be an instance of
\mathsf{SAT}
with

over a set of variables

construct the
(m+1)
-player turn-based reachability game as shown in
Fig.
1
. In this game Player
0
owns all the states, shown as
circles in the figure. The target set for Player
0
includes all the states,

, Player
0

for the social welfare
function in the

Clearly, the game has an NE with a payoff of

for each player if, and only if,

\phi
is satisfiable. If

to

and to

We show that
\mathsf{SWDP}
for reachability objectives is in

\mathsf{NP}
by mirroring the Algorithm in Section 5.1.2 in
[
4
]

with a small modification for adapting the solution for the constrained NE existence problem to

\mathsf{SWDP}
. The algorithm shown below makes essential use of the suspect game
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},L)
and its reduction
to the safety game
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{G})
with safety objective
\Omega_{L}
. See
[
4
]
for the technical details.

1.

Given a value
v\in[0,n]
, first guess a lasso-shaped play
\rho=\alpha_{1}\cdot\alpha_{2}^{\omega}
where
|\alpha_{i}|^{2}\leq 2|\mathsf{St}|^{2}

in
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{G})
such that
Adam
obeys
Eve
along
\rho
, and the play
\pi=\mathit{proj}_{1}(\rho)
in
\mathcal{G}
satisfies the constraint that at least
v
players are winning in it. This condition on the number of winning players is the only change from Section 5.1.2 in
[
4
]
.

2.

Then compute the set

of the winning states for
Eve
in the suspect game
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{Los}(\pi))
, where
\mathrm{Los}(\pi)
is the set of losing players along
\pi
.

3.

Finally, check that
\rho
always stays in

.

We refer to
[
4
]
for the proof that this nondeterministic algorithm runs in polynomial time.

∎

Pareto Optimal Decision Problem

It is clear that the construction in the proof of Lemma
3.1

yields an NE that is Pareto-optimal if, and only if, the formula
\phi
is
satisfiable. Hence the Pareto Optimal Decision Problem for reachability objectives

\mathsf{NP}
-hard using the same reduction as in the lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

\mathsf{PODP}
for reachability objectives is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

For the upper bound, we show that
\mathsf{PODP}
for reachability objectives is in
the class
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{NP}}=\mathsf{\Delta}^{p}_{2}
in the polynomial hierarchy as follows.
First, use binary search for the threshold value
v
in the range
[0,n]
and the
\mathsf{NP}
oracle for deciding
\mathsf{SWDP}
in the proof of Lemma
3.5
above to determine the maximum value
m
of
v
for which the procedure returns yes. Then, to check if there is a run
\rho
where more than
m
players are winners, search for any strongly connected component (SCC)

reachable from the initial state

in the underlying CGS that satisfies the following condition: there are more than
m

F_{1},\ldots,F_{n}
with a nonempty intersection with

. Since this can be done in polynomial time using Tarjan’s algorithm for finding all SCCs in a directed graph
[
14
]
, the entire procedure runs in

time. However, we leave the question of whether
\mathsf{PODP}
for reachability objectives is
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard open.

We show that
\mathsf{SWDP}

Lemma 3.3
.

\mathsf{SWDP}
for safety objectives is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

Proof.

We reduce
\mathsf{SAT}
to
\mathsf{SWDP}
with safety objectives for a turn-based game.
Consider an instance
\phi=C_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge C_{m}
of
\mathsf{SAT}
with
C_{j}=\ell_{j,1}\vee\ell_{j,2}\vee\ell_{j,3}
over a set of variables

\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. We associate a
(2n+1)
-player turn-based
game
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
with
\phi
. The set of states is given by the union of

\displaystyle V_{0}=\{s\}\text{ where }s\text{ is the initial state in the $\mathsf{SWDP}$ instance,}

\displaystyle V_{k}=\{x_{k},\neg x_{k}\}\text{ for each }1\leq k\leq n,\text{ and }

\displaystyle V_{n+j}=\{\ell_{j,1},\ell_{j,2},\ell_{j,3}\}\text{ for each }1\leq j\leq m.

We add a transition from each state in
V_{i}
to each state in
V_{i+1}
for
0\leq i\leq n+m
, assuming
V_{n+m+1}=V_{0}
. The game has
2n+1
players

P_{0},\ldots,P_{2n}
.
P_{0}
owns all the states and her safety objective is given
by the unsafe set
F_{0}=\emptyset
,
i.e.
, she always wins. This implies that all
strategy profiles are NE, as the other players have no choice at any state. For

1\leq k\leq n
,
P_{2k-1}
and
P_{2k}
have safety objectives given by the

\displaystyle F_{2k-1}=\{x_{k}\}\cup\{\ell_{j,p}\mid\ell_{j,p}\text{ is the literal }x_{k}\}

\displaystyle F_{2k}=\{\neg x_{k}\}\cup\{\ell_{j,p}\mid\ell_{j,p}\text{ is the literal }\neg x_{k}\}

At least
n
of these sets
F_{1},\ldots,F_{2n}
will be visited along any
infinite play and thus at least
n
of these
2n
players
P_{1},\ldots,P_{2n}

will always lose. We show that
\phi
is satisfiable if, and only if, there exists
an NE for which at most (and hence exactly)
n
of these
2n
sets

F_{1},\ldots,F_{2n}
are visited,
i.e.
, at least
n+1
players win. In other

\phi
is satisfiable if, and only if, there is an NE
\sigma
in

\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
with
\mathsf{sw}(\sigma)\geq n+1
.

Assume
\phi
is satisfiable and let
\tau
be a satisfying valuation. The

\sigma_{0}
for
P_{0}
simply follows
\tau
,
i.e.
, for states in

V_{k-1}
for
1\leq k\leq n

if

and it chooses
\neg x_{k}
otherwise. From a state in
V_{n+k-1}
for
1\leq k\leq m
, it chooses one of the
\ell_{j,p}
that evaluates to
\mathsf{true}

under
\tau
, say the one with the least index. As a result, the number of sets in

F_{1},\ldots,F_{n}
that are visited at least once is
n
. Since
P_{0}
owns all
the states and wins using
\sigma_{0}
and the strategies of all the other players
are empty, we have an NE
\sigma
where
n+1
players win.

Conversely, pick a play in
\mathcal{G}
resulting from a strategy
\sigma_{0}
of
P_{0}

such that at most (hence exactly)
n
of the sets

are
visited at least once. In particular, this play never visits one of
x_{k}
and

\neg x_{k}
for any
1\leq k\leq n
. Clearly, such a strategy is part of an NE

\sigma
(since all strategy profiles are) in which the number of winning
players is
n+1
. One can define a truth valuation
\tau
over

\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
from the play – simply set

to
\mathsf{true}
if
x_{k}

is visited at least once and to
\mathsf{false}
otherwise. Also, any state of

with

that is visited at least once must correspond to a
literal that is assigned the value
\mathsf{true}
by
\tau
, otherwise there would be
more than
n
states among
F_{1},\ldots,F_{n}
visited at least once. Hence, each
clause of
\phi
evaluates to
\mathsf{true}
under
\tau
, and therefore
\phi
is
satisfiable.

\mathsf{SWDP}
for safety objectives is in

1.

Given a value
v\in[0,n]
, first guess a lasso-shaped play
\rho=\alpha_{1}\cdot\alpha_{2}^{\omega}
where
|\alpha_{i}|^{2}\leq|\mathsf{St}|^{2}
in
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{G})

Adam

Eve
along
\rho
, and the play

Then check that any deviation by

\rho

3.3
Büchi Games

Social Welfare Problem

We show that the social welfare problem for Büchi objectives can be solved in polynomial time by giving a polynomial time algorithm that invokes the procedure for Constrained NE Existence Problem from
[

. A polynomial-time algorithm for the latter was presented in

Lemma 3.5
.

\mathsf{SWDP}
for Büchi objectives is in
\mathsf{P}
.

Proof.

The following is a polynomial-time algorithm for deciding whether there is a Nash equilibrium with
v

or more winners in a given concurrent game with Büchi objectives, starting from a state
s
.

1.

Find all the reachable SCCs in the underlying digraph of
\mathcal{G}
using Tarjan’s algorithm
[
14
]
. Call the number of agents that meet their Büchi objectives in the SCC
C
the
rank of

C
. Agent
i
meets her objective in the SCC
C
if
F_{i}\cap C\neq\emptyset
.

2.

Sort the SCCs in non-increasing order according to their rank.

3.

For each rank
r
starting from the highest down to
v
, check whether there is an NE with
r
winners using the
algorithm for constrained NE Existence from
[
4
]
by setting both the lower and upper thresholds
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{u}
to the winner profile of each SCC
C
of rank
r

∎

The correctness of the algorithm depends on Proposition
2.1
on the lasso characterization of
NEs in concurrent games.

Pareto Optimal Decision Problem

Lemma 3.6
.

\mathsf{PODP}
for Büchi objectives is in
\mathsf{P}
.

Proof.

A small modification in the algorithm in the proof of Lemma
3.5
gives a polynomial-time algorithm for deciding whether a Pareto-optimal NE exists. After sorting the SCCs in non-increasing order according to their rank, starting from the highest rank we check if any of the SCCs with the given rank is an NE. If a rank r is found for which all SCCs are non-NEs, then return ‘no’. Otherwise, if an SCC corresponding to an NE is found, then return ‘yes’.
∎

4
Conclusion

In this work, we have extended the complexity results for rational synthesis problems for concurrent games to the case of relevant equilibria. We restrict ourselves to pure strategy
Nash equilibria satisfying a social welfare or a Pareto optimality condition.

This work can be extended in many possible directions. One can consider solution concepts other than Nash equilibria, such as subgame perfect equilibria and admissible strategy profiles. It will also be fruitful to extend these results to the case of quantitative games such as those with mean payoff and discounted-sum objectives. Finally, the case of mixed rather than pure strategy equilibria would be an interesting extension.
