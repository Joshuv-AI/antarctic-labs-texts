The long arc of scientists in spaceflight: scientists flying with their experiments
Authors: Robert J Ferl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent developments in commercial suborbital spaceflight have expanded opportunities for researchers to fly with their experiments. In an accompanying paper in npj Microgravity, we report results from a researcher-tended experiment aboard Blue Origin New Shepard flight NS-261. This Perspective examines the historically rare role of scientists flying to conduct their own research and argues that firsthand participation in spaceflight enriches scientific observation, interpretation, and discovery.Subject terms: Astronomy and planetary science, Biophysics, Neuroscience, Physics

Introduction

Human spaceflight has always been intertwined with science, from the earliest physiological observations made during initial ventures beyond Earth to increasingly sophisticated experiments conducted aboard orbital platforms. Over time, the methods by which space science is performed have evolved, often favoring automation or the delegation of experimental tasks to career astronauts trained to operate a wide array of investigations. While this operational model has produced extraordinary results, it necessarily distances the scientist from the moment‑to‑moment unfolding of the experiment and from the physical and sensory context in which those measurements occur. In many scientific disciplines, firsthand observation and intimate familiarity with the experimental environment are understood to shape both interpretation and discovery, yet these dimensions have been largely unavailable to space researchers.

Since the earliest days of human spaceflight, science has traveled alongside astronauts, yet scientists flying to conduct their own experiments have remained rare throughout this history. This Perspective places the author’s flight experience within the long arc of scientists in spaceflight, from early physiological measurements and the emergence of scientist-astronauts to contemporary commercial suborbital missions. The researcher tending the experiment discussed here was the author, an academic space biologist who proposed the science, was awarded a flight through NASA’s Flight Opportunities Program, and ultimately flew with the experiment.

The space science community now finds itself at a moment where this long-standing pattern is beginning to shift. Developments in suborbital spacecraft and the broader emergence of commercial spaceflight capabilities have expanded opportunities for researchers not only to send experiments to space, but to fly with them, bearing direct witness to their execution and to the spaceflight environment itself.

My flight on New Shepard NS-26 had its unique aspects. Some firsts, certainly. But nothing wildly unforeseen or unexpected when viewed against the history of human spaceflight and the trajectory of science within it. From the earliest human ventures that brushed the edge of space, physiological measurements and experimental observations have been gathered from those who flew and by those who flew. In that sense, there was nothing inherently novel about a scientist being present to capture scientific data during flight.

And yet, there was. While science has always accompanied human spaceflight, science as the primary reason for an individual to fly, to go to space specifically in order to conduct one’s own experiment, has been rare. Again, not new, but rare. The NS-26 flight was notable not because it departed from precedent, but because it made visible a long-standing yet infrequently realized role in space science: the scientist flying to conduct their own research, rather than delegating that role to a professional astronaut.

Short history and heritage of scientist-astronauts

Harrison Schmitt of Apollo 17 is often regarded as the first US astronaut to fly who was primarily a scientist. While the Soviet space program had demonstrated the value of scientific specialists in space with physician Boris Yegorov and engineer Konstantin Feoktistov aboard Voskhod 1 in 19642, Schmitt was a discipline-specific scientist-astronaut who went to space and to the Moon because that is where the science needed doing, even though his formal crew role was Lunar Module Pilot. Schmidt’s astronaut class, Group 4, selected in 1965, was the first NASA class to feature scientists and physicians, not all or only test pilots3. The NASA Skylab era, with its emphasis on science and long-duration space research, more formally introduced the position of scientist-astronaut. Several members of the Group 4 NASA class of Scientist-Astronauts flew Skylab missions with a crew member designation as Science Pilot, specifically on Skylab 2, 3, and 4. The Space Shuttle era saw entire missions dedicated to science and further amplified the concept of scientist-astronaut in the formal crew designation of Payload Specialist, people who were trained as astronauts but were not career astronauts. Two prominent, almost bookend examples (among many), especially for biologists, are Spacelab Life Sciences SLS-1 on STS-40 with Millie Hughes-Fulford4, and Neurolab on STS-90 with Jay Buckey and Jim Pawelczyk5; all practicing scientists who flew as scientist-astronauts in the crew member designation of Payload Specialist.

The era of the International Space Station tilted back toward career astronauts flying even if the operational focus for the expeditions was science and the astronauts themselves were scientifically skilled. It is simply true that every astronaut that our lab has ever worked with has been fabulously skilled and deeply interested in the science that was put before them to do. They have all been great at learning what needed to be done in the hundreds of experiments that needed doing. But the notion of the scientist-first-astronaut-second or scientist-flying-for-a-particular-experiment was diminished in the ISS era. In many cases, scientists with experiments on the ISS currently are not given the opportunity to train, in person, the astronauts working on their experiments. This has left most of us practicing space scientists with our nose pressed against the window, or more accurately the computer screen, looking in and watching others do our work for us.

Not so anymore – most certainly now in the suborbital realm and maybe soon in orbit during the era of increased access to ISS and commercial LEO destinations.

New era of scientist-astronauts

There are two primary developments that have been trending to enable scientists to be potential astronauts for the purpose of doing their science. The first development is a national policy-derived programmatic effort at NASA. The second is a national cultural drive from the space science community itself, a drive that is fundamentally enabled by the development of commercial space flight providers.

The Flight Opportunities Program was established in 2011 by NASA within the NASA Space Technology Mission Directorate in response to the National Academies 2010 report “Revitalizing NASA’s Suborbital Program: Advancing Science, Driving Innovation and Developing Workforce”6. Since its inception, the Flight Opportunities Program has been instrumental in advancing technology for space exploration and has championed the inclusion of scientists in the flight environments for NASA7. The history of Flight Opportunities exemplifies a policy of strategic integration with and between researchers and flight providers, beginning with what was the Commercial Reusable Suborbital Research Program. The Flight Opportunities Program set the tone for and worked on the many aspects of pulling together flight providers and scientists to the point where proposals for scientists to fly to space were eventually entertained and funded.

Contemporaneous with the development of the National Academies 2010 report and the establishment of NASA Flight Opportunities, the Suborbital Applications Research Group (SARG) was established in 2009 as an activity of the Commercial Spaceflight Federation (CSF). The efforts of the SARG have had a direct impact on how NASA viewed suborbital vehicles as viable research platforms and in enabling researchers to conduct their own experiments in suborbital vehicles. The Next-Generation Suborbital Researchers Conference emerged from SARG, the Southwest Research Institute (SwRI), and the CSF to become the primary conference bringing together suborbital researchers, commercial flight providers, NASA, and other agencies from the US and around the world for the purpose of advancing and advocating for suborbital science. Out of those meetings, which started in 2010 and continue to occur about every 18 months, has come a continued evolution, growth, and development of research community interest, technology development, and flight provider awareness of the tremendous range and importance of the science that can be done in commercial vehicles, including human-rated vehicles capable of suborbital spaceflight.

Before this flight on NS-26 as a NASA-funded scientist to do science in space, the door for scientists flying on suborbital spaceships was truly cracked open by privately funded science training missions8, Flight Opportunities funding for commercial crew to do NASA science9, and the science-based flight of Virgin Galactic 01 for the Italian Air Force10. It is also worth noting that both Virgin Galactic and Blue Origin have flown many autonomous payload flights, thus bringing scientists to the suborbital flight lines and thereby creating an operational familiarity with the vehicles and processes of suborbital flights.

The experience of suborbital science experiments

The science to be done in suborbital space is real and worth the trip. There are large numbers of concepts that can be better understood with three minutes of quality microgravity for experimentation. Some of these experiments are for technology or science development to buy down risks of going all the way to orbit or the ISS. Yet there is also tremendous intrinsic value in the suborbital realm itself, from observations that can be made through the windows to assays involving, in our example, rapid changes in biology during various phases of the spaceflight, especially the first few minutes of being in space. In the research article accompanying this Perspective, we demonstrate the deployment of KSC Fixation Tubes (KFTs) as a technology for safely sampling biology at different phases of the flight. KFTs have a long legacy of science in spaceflight, with hundreds of successful orbital samplings in the Space Shuttle and the ISS. This suborbital deployment of KFTs therefore shows, in essence, the reverse directionality for what is usually considered the technology development path, where here a proven orbital technology now enables science in the suborbital realm. Indeed, the data in the accompanying paper, data that came from using the KFTs in suborbital spaceflight and employing the analytical pathways developed with on-orbit samples, also demonstrate the intrinsic value of suborbital spaceflight to understand the initial effects on biology of leaving Earth for space and the relationship of those responses to the long-term effects of living in space. Now that KFTs are fully proven within suborbital spacecraft, any biology that can fit inside a KFT and be preserved with a fixative can be assayed during a flight.

Putting the personal researcher into the loop creates better science and better scientists. The better science comes from observation and witnessing of the experiment process. Notes from personal researcher observation of fluid flow, sample coverage, biological behavior, mechanical stresses, as examples, clearly enhance the science return and broaden appreciation of the factors affecting the science. For example, during my flight I was able to note the coverage of the samples by the preservative, make adjustments in the movement of the KFTs to ensure rapid fixation, and create context-informed audible notes about the timing and quality of each sampling activity. The better scientist comes from the fact that the experience places the scientist in the moment of the experiment and deeply within the context of the environment, bearing firsthand witness. Nobel Prize-winning geneticist Barbara McClintock famously expounded a philosophy that emphasized deep empathy for the systems she studied: that a scientist must develop a nuanced, intimate relationship with that which is under study. This philosophy is captured by the title of her biography, A Feeling for the Organism11, reflecting the ethos that one needs a close relationship with what one studies. There is no question that going to space puts one in touch with space in ways that remote observation simply cannot replicate.

The spaceflight experience is indeed profound. The experience must be managed and trained, but it is doable. That I have extensive flight and flight operations experience may have made it possible or easier for NASA, the flight provider, and the university to get comfortable with the idea of me flying to space. However, none of those previous flight activities were, in hindsight, absolute requirements for the implementation of this flight. The act of flying to space in the current suborbital platforms is manageable by a wide range of persons. Extensive flight experience is simply not required.

Current status of researcher spaceflights

Scientists have learned and are still learning how to think differently about science and science experimentation in the suborbital realm. Taking an experiment with you is very different from handing an experiment off to automation or some other person to perform. The rocket companies have learned and are still learning how best to enable science in their spaceships. For both Virgin Galactic and Blue Origin the principal vehicle design is primarily for accommodation of people simply for the experience, yet both companies recognize the value of flying equipment with those people for the purposes of science. This co-development enhances and informs what we all in the science community can propose for suborbital flights, as well as flights in the next phase of space exploration science in low Earth orbit and beyond. Indeed, there are already more scientists in the queue for flying experiments in suborbital spaceships, including universities buying entire research flights12. Surely more opportunities for science will continue to be made available by NASA, other agencies, academia, and the commercial science ecosystem, as scientists adapt to the notion of flying with their experiments and flight providers understand the business case and intrinsic value of science flights.

Situational reflection

There is certainly a brand of adventure to be had by participation in remote and autonomous science, especially science in space. But the satisfaction, and even the sudden emptiness, of a science adventure complete are intense and intangible human concepts, and the argument can be made that scientists cannot fully bear witness to their science unless they experience it firsthand, completely enmeshed in the totality of its environment. Science gains meaning not only from data, but from context, perception, and above all, presence13,14.

Seen in this broader context, the NS-26 flight does not stand apart from the history of space science so much as it settles back into it. The researcher space experience reflects a long-standing, if infrequently realized, mode of inquiry in which the space scientist is present with the experiment, bearing direct witness to both the system under study and the environment in which it unfolds. Such opportunities remain uncommon, but they are not anomalous; rather, they represent a recurring thread in the fabric of human spaceflight that reappears when access, capability, and intent align15. As suborbital and Low Earth Orbit platforms continue to mature, the question may no longer be whether scientists can fly with their experiments, but how often, as this role will again become visible along the long arc of spaceflight science.
