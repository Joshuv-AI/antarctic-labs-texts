Effect of evening dexmedetomidine nasal spray on postoperative sleep disturbance in patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy: protocol for a multicentre, prospective, randomised, double-blind, controlled trial

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative sleep disturbance (PSD) is a common complication following laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC). It is associated with delirium, cognitive decline and delayed recovery. Effective preventive strategies are currently lacking. Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α₂-adrenergic receptor agonist, offered in a nasal spray formulation with high bioavailability and convenient administration, may present a novel approach for PSD prevention.ObjectivesThe primary objective is to evaluate the effect of evening dexmedetomidine nasal spray on the incidence of PSD on the first postoperative day in LC patients. Introduction

Postsurgical sleep disturbance (PSD) is a frequent and clinically significant complication after surgery, with reported incidence ranging from 15% to 72%.1 Older patients, women, those experiencing severe postoperative pain and individuals with preoperative anxiety are at particularly high risk.2 PSD not only compromises patient comfort but also predisposes to postoperative delirium and cognitive dysfunction, ultimately delaying functional recovery and hospital discharge. General anaesthesia itself has been identified in multiple studies as an independent risk factor for PSD.3 Despite its clinical importance, no standardised preventive strategy has been widely adopted.

Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α₂-adrenergic receptor agonist, produces sedation that closely resembles natural non-rapid eye movement (REM) sleep by activating receptors in the locus coeruleus. It provides both sedative and analgesic effects while maintaining arousability.4 Low-dose intravenous administration has been shown to improve postoperative sleep quality in intensive care and general ward settings2 5; however, the requirement for continuous infusion limits its broader application. The intranasal spray formulation of dexmedetomidine offers high bioavailability (83.8%) and convenient administration.6–8 Studies in paediatric populations have demonstrated its safety and efficacy as a premedication.9 Recent adult studies also suggest that a dose of 1–1.5 µg/kg significantly improves postoperative sleep architecture, prolongs N2 and N3 sleep stages, reduces awakenings and lowers Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores.10–12

Beyond its direct modulation of sleep architecture, dexmedetomidine’s role in alleviating preoperative negative psychological states is gaining increasing attention. Anxiety and depression are strong predictors of PSD, potentially exacerbating postoperative sleep disruptions by overactivating the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system, thereby disturbing circadian rhythms. Dexmedetomidine exerts anxiolytic and antisympathetic effects by centrally inhibiting norepinephrine release.13 14 Consequently, we hypothesise that dexmedetomidine may improve PSD through a dual-pathway mechanism: first, by directly simulating physiological sleep, and second, by mitigating pre-existing anxiety/depression, thereby reducing their negative impact on sleep quality at the source. This provides a novel interventional perspective for PSD prevention.

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) requires rapid postoperative recovery and early discharge, yet PSD remains common in this population and contributes to unplanned readmission and delayed return to daily activity. There is thus a compelling need for a simple, evidence-based prophylactic strategy.

This multicentre, prospective, randomised, double-blind, controlled trial will evaluate whether a fixed 50 µg dose of intranasal dexmedetomidine administered in the evening after LC reduces the incidence of PSD and improves objective sleep architecture.

Objective monitoring: Wristwatch-type wearable device (Huawei Watch D2. The medical device registration certificate number for the product is: Guangdong Device Approval 20242071034) will record total sleep time, REM duration, light (N1–N2) and deep (N3) sleep, and number of nocturnal awakenings during the first postoperative night.15

Subjective evaluation: Validated questionnaires will be administered, including the PSQI for global sleep quality, the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS) for insomnia symptoms, and the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) for postoperative pain, which may confound sleep outcomes.

We hypothesise that compared with placebo, a single 50 µg dose of intranasal dexmedetomidine administered on the evening of surgery will not only improve sleep architecture through its direct sedative effects but may also further reduce the incidence of PSD via the amelioration of preoperative negative emotional states, thereby significantly enhancing both objective and subjective sleep quality while maintaining a favourable safety profile.

Study design

This study is a multicentre, prospective, randomised, double-blind, controlled trial designed to evaluate the effect of evening dexmedetomidine nasal spray on the incidence of postoperative sleep disturbance in patients undergoing LC. To maintain blinding, the trial medications (dexmedetomidine nasal spray and saline placebo) are identical in appearance. Only the drug administrator will be aware of the group assignments, whereas participants, outcome assessors, and data analysts will remain blinded to the treatment allocation.

Participants

One day before surgery, the research team will screen patients in strict accordance with the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. If the procedure is scheduled for a Monday, screening will be completed on the preceding Friday. All eligible participants who voluntarily agree to participate will provide written informed consent before formal enrolment (online supplemental file 1). This trial enrolled patients who met all the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18–65 years; (2) scheduled to undergo LC under general anaesthesia; and (3) agreed to participate and provided signed informed consent. Patients were excluded from participation for any of the following reasons: (1) refusal to participate in this study; (2) pre-existing sick sinus syndrome, severe sinus bradycardia (heart rate <50 beats/min), second-degree or higher atrioventricular block without a pacemaker implantation, congenital heart disease accompanied by any arrhythmia or other severe cardiovascular diseases with cardiac function classification ≥class III; (3) preoperative diagnosis of sleep apnoea or Snoring, Tired, Observed apnoea, Pressure, BMI, Age, Neck, Gender (STOP-Bang) score ≥3 with HCO₃⁻≥28 mmol/L; (4) conditions unsuitable for intranasal administration, such as severe rhinitis or nasal deformities; (5) preoperative PSQI score >7; (6) use of sedatives or hypnotic drugs within 1 month prior to surgery; (7) known allergy to dexmedetomidine; (8) body mass index (BMI) >30 kg/m²; (9) severe liver dysfunction (Child-Pugh Class C) or severe renal dysfunction requiring preoperative dialysis; (10) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status≥IV; (11) inability to comprehend visual analogue scales or presence of language communication barriers; (12) pregnancy or lactation; (13) current participation in other interventional clinical studies; or (14) other conditions deemed by the investigator or supervising physician as unsuitable for study participation. Participants were withdrawn from the study if any of the following occurred: (1) the participant voluntarily requested to withdraw from the study, (2) loss to follow-up, (3) occurrence of a serious adverse event (AE) deemed related to the study intervention, (4) occurrence of a serious postoperative complication that, in the judgement of the investigator or the Ethics Committee, made it inappropriate for the participant to continue in the study (eg, transfer to ICU, delayed discharge), (5) any protocol violation that substantially affected the primary outcome assessment. For all withdrawn participants, the reason for withdrawal was documented in detail. The last available observation or assessment prior to withdrawal was carried forward for the intention-to-treat analysis (table 1).

Randomisation, allocation concealment and blinding

This trial uses a block randomisation method. A random sequence will be generated in SAS V.9.4 or a later version with predefined seed numbers and block lengths, allocating participants to the experimental or control group in a 1:1 ratio. Sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed randomisation cards will then be prepared. These cards will be managed and distributed by authorised personnel who are not involved in participant screening or treatment. After enrolment, the investigator or an authorised staff member will request the allocation from the card administrator. The administrator will retrieve the corresponding card in sequential order, record the participant’s screening number and initials, sign the card and note the unsealing date to complete the allocation process. Both dexmedetomidine and saline solutions are colourless and transparent. Preparation of trial medications is performed by a designated unblinded investigator who is not involved in participant enrolment, anaesthesia administration or postoperative follow-up. The study coordinator will collect the assigned medications based on the allocation results within 1 hour before administration, deliver them to the relevant researchers and record the details in a blinded log. All blinding records will be securely maintained by the principal investigator.

Interventions

Participants were randomised to either the dexmedetomidine group or the saline control group. Patients assigned to the dexmedetomidine group received intranasal administration (one spray per nostril, 25 µg per spray) between 20:00 and 22:00 on both the evening before surgery and the evening of surgery. The saline control group received an equivalent volume of saline nasal spray at the same time points and in the same manner. The dexmedetomidine nasal spray device used in this study is designed to deliver a fixed, precise dose per spray, ensuring consistent bioavailability and simplifying administration for both healthcare providers and potential future out-of-hospital use. A fixed dose eliminates the complexities and potential errors associated with weight-based calculations in a postoperative ward setting, enhancing clinical utility and patient safety. The study enrolled patients with normal preoperative sleep quality (PSQI<7). For this specific population, our primary goal was to explore the efficacy of a low dose in preventing PSD, thereby prioritising an excellent safety profile. A 50 µg dose represents a conservative yet potentially effective starting point, minimising the risk of haemodynamic side effects, such as bradycardia or hypotension, which are more dose dependent. While intravenous, weight-based intraoperative dosing is common, evidence supports the efficacy of low, fixed-dose intranasal dexmedetomidine in the postoperative period.16 In summary, the 50 µg fixed dose was chosen to balance proven efficacy from our preliminary data with the highest possible safety, while also aligning with the practical advantages of the nasal spray delivery system.

For anaesthesia management, all patients adhered to routine preoperative fasting for 6–8 hours. On entering the operating room, standard monitoring was established, including heart rate, non-invasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry, Bispectral Index (BIS) and nasopharyngeal temperature, with invasive arterial pressure monitoring performed when necessary. Anaesthesia was induced with midazolam (0.015 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3–0.5 µg/kg) and propofol. Maintenance during surgery was achieved with remifentanil (0.2–0.4 µg/kg/min) and propofol, supplemented with non-depolarising muscle relaxants. Bilateral transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block with 20 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine was performed to enhance analgesia. Prior to induction, dexamethasone (5 mg) was administered to prevent postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV). The use of dexmedetomidine, nitrous oxide, scopolamine and penehyclidine was prohibited during anaesthesia. All patients underwent endotracheal intubation for airway management.

Intraoperative management targeted a BIS of 40–60, blood pressure fluctuations within 20% of baseline values, and body temperature maintained between 36°C and 37°C. The use of vasoactive drugs was recorded. If intraoperative analgesia was considered insufficient, supplemental sufentanil (5–10 µg/dose) was administered. Before the end of surgery, a 5-HT₃ receptor antagonist and oxycodone could be given to prevent PONV and to provide adjunct analgesia. Routine reversal of neuromuscular blockade was achieved with sugammadex, and all administered medications were meticulously documented. After surgery, patients were transferred to the postanaesthesia care unit (PACU) for extubation, where an anaesthesiologist assessed readiness for PACU discharge.

Data collection and management

Authorised research personnel (physicians or nurses) thoroughly familiar with the study protocol will conduct patient visits in the surgical ward. These visits will take place in a quiet environment, preferably with the patient’s family members present. The researcher will provide a comprehensive explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential benefits and risks to the patient and/or their legal representative. It will be clearly communicated that postoperative telephone follow-ups will be performed. All questions from the patient and family will be addressed in detail, allowing sufficient time for consideration. Written informed consent will be obtained from the patient and/or their representative only after confirming understanding and voluntary participation.

Data will be systematically collected in three main categories:

Preoperative baseline data: demographic information (name, gender, date of birth, education level, years of education and BMI), medical history (comorbidities, medication history, smoking and alcohol use, allergies, surgical and anaesthesia history) and disease severity assessments (New York Heart Association (NYHA) cardiac function classification, Charlson Comorbidity Index and ASA physical status limited to classes I–III). Baseline assessments using the QoR-15, MMSE, PSQI, NRS and HADS scales will evaluate quality of recovery, cognitive function, sleep quality, pain and anxiety/depression levels, respectively.

Intraoperative monitoring data: details on anaesthetic drug dosages, induction and maintenance protocols, surgical specifics (site, approach and duration), mechanical ventilation parameters (including lowest SpO₂), oxygenation and circulatory metrics (using the average values from the 5 min immediately before induction and the moment of induction as baseline), instances of blood pressure fluctuations >20% from baseline and SpO₂ <90%, usage of vasoactive drugs, fluid balance metrics (blood loss, transfusion details, intake and output) and AEs such as emergence agitation, defined by a Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) score ≥+1.

Postoperative follow-up data: assessments will be performed at specific time points—on PACU arrival, the evening of surgery, the morning and evening of postoperative days 1 and 3, and at 30 days postsurgery. Data will include recovery-related AEs (nausea/vomiting, hypoxemia, haemodynamic instability, delirium, etc), blood pressure monitoring results, QoR-15 scores, frequency and severity of nausea/vomiting, resting and movement-evoked pain NRS scores, sleep quality (using AIS, NRS and PSQI), HADS scores, complication rates, hospital readmission rates and tracking until the endpoint event (all-cause mortality).

A proprietary medical data platform will be used for multicentre data collection and synchronisation (https://www.laowaitalk.com/admin/). This platform incorporates automated quality checks to minimise errors and ensure high data integrity.

Study outcomes

Before initiating statistical analysis, the analysis datasets will be clearly defined. This study will use the following datasets:

Full analysis set (FAS): subjects determined according to the intention-to-treat principle, comprising all randomised participants who received the study drug at least once.

Per-protocol set (PPS): a subset of the FAS that includes all participants who completed the trial without major protocol deviations.

Safety set (SS): all participants who received at least one dose of the study treatment and underwent at least one safety assessment; this set will serve as the primary population for safety analyses.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome is the incidence of sleep disturbance on the first postoperative day. We defined the occurrence of sleep disturbance on the first postoperative day as an AIS score ≥6 or an NRS score ≥6. Analyses will be conducted using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. The primary analysis will be performed in both the FAS and PPS populations to assess differences in incidence between the trial and control groups.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes will be evaluated in the FAS and PPS populations. Statistical comparisons will use appropriate tests based on data type: continuous variables will be analysed with paired t-tests or paired Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for within-group comparisons (pre vs post-treatment) and independent t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for between-group comparisons; categorical variables will be analysed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test; and ordinal variables will be assessed using rank-sum tests.

Safety outcomes

Safety analysis will be based on the SS. The number of occurrences and incidence rates of AEs will be calculated. Between-group comparisons of AE incidence will be performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Detailed descriptions of all AEs observed in the trial will be presented in listings. The χ² test will be used to compare the incidence rates of specific AEs—including hypertension, hypotension, bradycardia, hypoxemia, tachycardia and other AEs—between the two groups.

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

This study is designed as a superiority trial. Participants in the dexmedetomidine group will receive intranasal dexmedetomidine spray, whereas those in the control group will receive intranasal saline spray. The primary endpoint is the incidence of sleep disturbance. Based on previous literature, it is expected that intranasal dexmedetomidine will reduce the incidence of sleep disturbance on the first postoperative day by 40%.17 Assuming a power (1-β) of 0.8, a two-sided type I error (α) of 0.05, and a 1:1 allocation ratio, sample size calculations performed using PASS V.15.0 software indicate that at least 117 subjects per group are required. Handling of missing or implausible data

Within the FAS, missing primary outcome data (sleep quality NRS and AIS scores at 24 hours postoperatively) will be imputed using the Last Observation Carried Forward method. No imputation will be applied to other missing outcomes. Logical checks will be performed during data management, and any implausible entries will be queried with investigators. Corrections will be made based on written responses before the database is locked. Analyses will be conducted using SAS software (V.9.4 or higher). Descriptive statistics: continuous variables will be summarised with mean, SD, median, IQR, minimum and maximum. Categorical variables will be summarised as frequencies and percentages. Primary efficacy outcome: the incidence of sleep disturbance on postoperative day 1 will be compared between groups using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables: within-group comparisons (pre vs post-treatment) will use paired t-tests or paired Wilcoxon rank-sum tests; between-group comparisons will use independent t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Between-group comparisons over time will be analysed using a repeated-measures mixed-effects model. Categorical variables: χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Ordinal variables: rank-sum tests. Safety analysis: AEs will be summarised by number and incidence rate, with between-group comparisons using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Unless otherwise specified for the primary outcome, all statistical tests will be two sided, and p <0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Study monitoring

An independent monitoring mechanism will be established for this study. Certified monitors will conduct regular on-site and remote audits of all participating research centres to ensure strict adherence to the study protocol and standard operating procedures. Monitoring activities will include, but are not limited to, verifying the compliance of the informed-consent process, the timeliness and accuracy of data recording, the proper implementation of interventions and the reporting and handling of AEs. All data collection and management procedures will comply with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines and the relevant regulations issued by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) of China, ensuring the scientific integrity of the research process and the authenticity and reliability of the data. A data-safety monitoring plan will be established according to the level of risk associated with the clinical study. All AEs will be documented in detail, appropriately managed and followed until proper resolution or stabilisation of the condition. Serious AEs and unanticipated incidents will be promptly reported to the Ethics Committee, regulatory authorities and the sponsor as required. The principal investigator will conduct regular cumulative reviews of all AEs to evaluate the study’s risk–benefit profile. In double-blind trials, emergency unblinding may be performed when necessary to ensure subject safety and rights. Studies involving more than minimal risk will assign monitors to audit the research data, while high-risk studies will establish an independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board to review cumulative safety and efficacy data and to provide recommendations regarding the continuation of the study.

Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α₂-adrenergic receptor agonist, demonstrates a favourable safety profile in its nasal-spray formulation, characterised by high bioavailability (83.8%). Previous studies have indicated good tolerability for both preoperative medication and sleep improvement in paediatric and adult populations. The dosage selected for this study is 50 µg (approximately equivalent to 0.5–1.5 µg/kg), which falls within a recognised safe and effective range. Nonetheless, all patients will undergo comprehensive intraoperative and postoperative monitoring of vital signs, including blood pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation and BIS. An experienced team of anaesthesiologists will be responsible for overall management throughout the process to minimise potential risks such as haemodynamic fluctuations, hypoxemia or excessive sedation.

Written informed consent will be obtained from each participant or their legal representative prior to enrolment, after they are fully informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks and rights. Participants retain the right to withdraw from the study unconditionally at any stage. Data from withdrawn participants will be excluded from the final analysis, and all personally identifiable information will be anonymised during data processing to strictly protect privacy and confidentiality. Collected data will be used solely for the purposes of this research. All electronic and paper records will be stored in locked facilities or encrypted systems with strictly controlled access permissions.

Discussion

This randomised controlled trial aims to investigate the effect of evening administration of dexmedetomidine nasal spray on the incidence of PSD in patients undergoing LC. Its significance lies in providing a feasible, non-invasive preventive strategy to improve postoperative sleep quality and overall recovery. With the widespread adoption of laparoscopic techniques,18 LC has become a common procedure, underscoring the importance of rapid recovery and early safe discharge. PSD, however, exhibits a high incidence rate (15%–72%) and is closely associated with delirium, cognitive decline, exacerbated pain and delayed recovery, while effective preventive measures remain lacking. Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α₂-adrenergic receptor agonist, offers multiple benefits, including the induction of a near-physiological sleep state, anxiolysis and analgesia. Notably, its mechanism for improving postoperative sleep may extend beyond direct modulation of sleep centres to include intervention in preoperative negative emotional states, such as anxiety and depression.19 Preoperative anxiety and depression are independent risk factors for PSD, potentially exacerbating sleep disturbances through disruption of the stress axis and circadian rhythms. By centrally inhibiting norepinephrine release, dexmedetomidine produces significant anxiolytic and sedative effects. Consequently, we hypothesise that dexmedetomidine may act via a dual-pathway mechanism targeting both ‘emotion and sleep’: directly simulating natural sleep and optimising sleep architecture, while concurrently alleviating pre-existing preoperative anxiety/depression to mitigate their negative impact on postoperative sleep, thereby potentially preventing PSD more effectively. The intranasal spray formulation enhances administration convenience and patient compliance. Utilising a multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled design, this study systematically evaluates the preventive efficacy of this intervention against PSD.

Should this study demonstrate that evening dexmedetomidine nasal spray significantly reduces the incidence of PSD, it would hold substantial clinical translational value. The implications extend beyond offering a novel strategy for managing sleep disorders to optimising the overall perioperative management pathway for surgical patients. The non-invasive intranasal route eliminates the dependency on infusion pumps required for intravenous administration, rendering it particularly suitable for general wards and potentially aligning with trends towards early discharge and home-based recovery. Furthermore, concomitant with sleep improvement, dexmedetomidine’s synergistic anxiolytic and analgesic properties may promote multidimensional postoperative recovery, aligning closely with the core principles of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery protocols, which emphasise comprehensive physiological and psychological recuperation.20 21

Although dexmedetomidine has shown potential for improving sleep quality in ICU and general ward settings,22 high-level evidence regarding its intranasal formulation for PSD prevention remains scarce. This study features a rigorous design, employing standardised assessment tools such as the PSQI, AIS and NRS for multidimensional sleep evaluation. Incorporating secondary endpoints including the QoR-15 scale, HADS and pain scores allows for a comprehensive assessment of the intervention’s impact on recovery quality and psychological state. The specific inclusion of HADS aims to directly test the hypothesis that improvement in emotional state indirectly facilitates sleep. Objective sleep architecture recording via wearable devices further enhances the reliability and persuasiveness of the findings.

This study also has several limitations. First, while the multicentre design improves the generalisability of results, consistency in protocol implementation across different centres requires attention. Second, although subjective sleep assessments are supplemented by objective monitoring, future research could incorporate more precise methods like polysomnography (PSG) for deeper analysis of sleep architecture changes.23 The follow-up period primarily focuses on the first 30 postoperative days; longer term sleep quality changes warrant further investigation. Finally, although we assessed changes in anxiety and depression scores (HADS), mediation analysis exploring their relationship with sleep improvement might be limited by the study design. Future studies could incorporate more frequent assessment timepoints to elucidate the causal pathway better.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This study offers several methodological and practical strengths. The multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled design minimises implementation and measurement biases, generating high-level evidence for evaluating the preventive effect of intranasal dexmedetomidine on PSD. The intervention—evening nasal spray administration—is highly feasible and promotes patient adherence, avoiding the need for specialised equipment or continuous monitoring associated with intravenous infusion, making it particularly suitable for day-surgery and general-ward settings.

The protocol does not impose excessive restrictions on intraoperative anaesthesia management, allowing adjustments according to routine clinical practice at participating centres. This flexibility enhances the generalisability and external validity of the findings, ensuring that the results reflect real-world applicability.

Furthermore, the study endpoints extend beyond the primary outcome of PSD incidence to encompass comprehensive evaluation of sleep architecture (N2, N3 stage duration), HADS, QoR-15 and pain levels. This multidimensional assessment, particularly the focus on psychological state, provides a robust dataset to understand the potential clinical benefits of dexmedetomidine via the hypothesised dual-pathway mechanism involving both ‘emotion and sleep’, contributing to a comprehensive understanding of its mode of action. An additional key methodological feature of this study lies in its focus on a homogeneous population undergoing LC. This selection offers significant advantages: LC is a highly standardised surgical procedure, and postoperative pain can be effectively managed via multimodal analgesic regimens, such as the TAP block employed in this study. This homogeneity and controllability of pain create ideal conditions for isolating the major confounding factor of severe pain, thereby enabling a more precise assessment of the direct sleep-promoting effects of dexmedetomidine. Consequently, the significant improvement in sleep quality we observed provides stronger evidence for the independent efficacy of intranasal dexmedetomidine spray in enhancing postoperative sleep.

Limitations

Concurrently, several limitations deserve mention. Although intraoperative anaesthesia management generally followed the protocol, variations in specific drug choices and management details among centres and anaesthesiologists might have occurred. While this flexibility enhances external validity, it could introduce uncontrolled confounding factors, potentially affecting the precise estimation of dexmedetomidine’s independent effect.

The study population is confined to patients undergoing LC, necessitating caution when extrapolating findings to other surgical procedures, especially major operations involving greater physiological stress or longer durations. Moreover, although sleep quality was assessed using subjective scales (AIS, NRS, PSQI) complemented by objective monitoring (wearable devices), the non-application of gold-standard PSG24 25 may somewhat limit the depth of analysis regarding sleep architecture changes and their association with emotional improvement. Finally, although the sample size was calculated with an allowance for dropouts, some subgroup analyses might remain underpowered.

Nevertheless, through stratified randomisation, rigorous blinding procedures, multicentre participation and standardised assessment tools, we endeavoured to minimise potential biases and enhance findings’ reliability. The results will provide important evidence regarding using intranasal dexmedetomidine for PSD prevention and help lay the groundwork for future interventional studies involving broader patient populations and diverse surgical settings.
