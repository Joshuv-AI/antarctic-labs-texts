Community change agents and disaster preparedness among women in coastal areas
Authors: D Rajeswari, R Jayaraman, D Celina, Kanchana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnhancing disaster preparedness among women in vulnerable coastal regions is critical for reducing the impact of emergencies. Leveraging community members as change agents helps bridge knowledge gaps, shift attitudes, and improve emergency skills in resource-limited settings.ObjectiveThis study assessed the effectiveness of trained community change agents (CCAs) in improving disaster-related knowledge, attitudes, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) skills among women.Materials and methodsA quasi-experimental study involved 414 women from four coastal villages in Tamil Nadu, with 206 in the experimental group and 208 in the control group. Community change agents were trained in disaster-related knowledge, attitudes, and Cardio Pulmonary Resuscitation skills using the Coastal Disaster Readiness Package (CDRP). They then provided similar training to women in their communities, focusing on home disaster preparedness. Data was collected using a structured questionnaire and an observational checklist.ResultsThe results indicated that women trained by Community Change Agents exhibited significant enhancements in disaster preparedness knowledge score relative to the control group, from pre-test 7.52 ± 3.71 to at 6-month post-test 16.43 ± 3.67, reflecting a 44.6% knowledge score gain. In contrast, the control group showed a minimal increase from 7.75 ± 3.66 to 9.01 ± 4.80. Attitude ratings were enhanced by 39.4% in the experimental group compared to a mere 0.6% in the control group. The experimental group observed significant gains in skills, rising scores from 13.44 at one month to 15.35 at six months (mean difference = 1.91, F = 45.95, p = 0.001). The control group, on the other hand, did not see any noteworthy improvements. Correlation analysis showed that there were moderate positive relationships between knowledge and attitude (r = 0.52, p = 0.001), knowledge and skill (r = 0.54, p = 0.001), and attitude and skill (r = 0.49, p = 0.001).ConclusionThe study concludes that trained community change agents significantly improved disaster-related knowledge, attitudes, and CPR skills among women in coastal areas. The intervention effectively bridged preparedness gaps in a vulnerable population. Empowering local agents proved to be a practical and impactful strategy for enhancing community resilience.Clinical trial numberNot Applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-025-01393-8.Keywords: Behavior change, Cardio pulmonary resuscitation, Community health workers, Disaster planning, Women's participation

Introduction

Disasters can strike at any time and occur daily across the world, posing serious public health threats and causing significant impacts in terms of death, injury, infrastructure damage, suffering, and loss of livelihoods. Disasters, whether natural or human-made, lead to loss of life and property, and their impact varies by region, culture, and available resources [1, 2].

While natural disasters are beyond our control, we can mitigate their impact and increase our resilience through effective strategies. These events often cause widespread loss of life and property, and disrupt communities beyond their capacity to recover alone [3, 4]. Active community participation is crucial for disaster risk reduction, with the role of women increasingly emphasized [5, 6]. Women and girls are more vulnerable to natural disasters, with nearly 14 times higher death rates [7, 8].

India’s geographical location plays a significant role in making it highly vulnerable to natural disasters. Being surrounded by the sea on three sides, India is frequently affected by events such as floods, storms, cyclones, and devastating tsunamis. India is among the most flood-prone countries in the world. Coastal populations are facing particularly high levels of risk and exposure. The Tsunami affected 2,260 km of India’s coastal belt. Tamil Nadu was the worst-hit state, with the most affected regions being the coastal districts of Chennai, Kanchipuram, Cuddalore, Nagapattinam, and Kanyakumari. In total, 13 districts in Tamil Nadu were impacted by this catastrophic event [9]. Disaster management training ensures disaster preparedness, response, and recovery. It educates people and organizations on risk assessment, emergency planning, and response and recovery [10, 11].

Evidence indicates that effectively structured training programs can substantially improve knowledge, practical skills, and attitudes regarding disaster preparedness. For instance, an intervention in Iran showed that volunteers’ functional skills (F = 3.75), knowledge (F = 166.50), and attitude (F = 1.74) all improved at p = 0.001. The effects lasted over time for functional skills (F = 1.83) and knowledge (F = 18.04) (p < 0.05) [12]. In the same way, A study in disaster mitigation through community participation in Kochi residents following the Kerala floods of 2018 and 2019 found that people who actively took part in community events were 23% more likely to take steps to protect themselves, such as making emergency plans, keeping up with disaster news, and making friends with people in their neighborhood [13]. Other research has demonstrated that participatory learning and action (PLA) models facilitated by community health workers enhance preparedness knowledge, resilience metrics, and self-efficacy, particularly when training encompasses first aid, early warning systems, and psychosocial support [14]. Disaster management training helps people and organizations handle post-disaster issues.

Most disaster preparedness programs focus on giving people information instead of teaching them how to use it, and lack community participation. Therefore, this study involved CCAs and women from at-risk rural coastal villages to evaluate the efficacy of CCAs in enhancing women’s knowledge, attitudes, and disaster preparedness skills.

Research methodology

Research design

An experimental study utilizing a quasi-experimental design was employed to assess the effectiveness of Community change agents among women on disaster preparedness in coastal communities. The study involved an experimental group and a control group, with pre- and post-intervention assessments conducted to measure changes in knowledge, attitude, and skills.

Study subjects

The study was conducted in four geographically separated coastal villages to prevent contamination between groups. In 1st phase, the Community change agents were identified with the support of local leaders and public health nurses (10–12 women per village). The inclusion criteria for the CCAs were Women aged 25–30 years, studied up to the 8th standard, permanent residents of the selected villages, able to read and write Tamil, have good communication skills, willing to undergo first responder training, and able to spend 1–2 hours daily for training to women in the community. Women with prior emergency training were excluded. The sample size for the study was calculated based on a prior study in which disaster preparedness knowledge significantly improved following the intervention [15]. The Sample size estimation and power of the study were calculated as: n = (Z1 − α/2​+Z1 − β​)2⋅[p1​(1 − p1​) + p2​(1 − p2​)]​/(P1-P2)2. Where: Z1 − α/2​ = 2.576 (for 99% CI), Z1 − β​ = 1.282 (for 90% power), p1​ = 0.56 (baseline knowledge score), p2​ = 0.75 (post-intervention score). Plug in the values n = (2.576 + 1.282)2⋅ [0.56(0.44) + 0.75(0.25)] ​/ (0.56.-0.75)2=178.8 = 179. With 20% attrition women per group was 215.

In the second phase, the researcher identified 430 women (in the experimental group: 215, and in the control group: 215) with the help of community change agents. A total of 414 women participated in the study: 206 in the experimental group and 208 in the control group (post-attrition). Women aged between 18 and 50 years who can understand and respond to Tamil and are residing near the coastal areas were included in the study, while Women with physical and mental illness and with prior emergency training were excluded. Non-probability convenience sampling was used for participant recruitment. While the non-random approach may limit the generalizability of the findings, steps were taken to minimize potential bias. All participants received the same instructions, data collection methods, and assessment tools. Blinding in community-based interventions presents intrinsic challenges; nevertheless, measures were implemented to mitigate bias. To avoid contamination effects, the women in the control group were not told what the experimental group was learning. Strategies were used to control for possible confounding variables. Villages that were far apart (12 km distance in between) from each other were chosen to lower the chance that information would spread between groups. We gathered basic information about the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics (like age, education, socioeconomic status, previous disaster exposure, etc.) to account for things that could affect knowledge, attitudes, and skills without the intervention. The control group did not undergo any structured training during the study period, thereby ensuring that enhancements could be predominantly ascribed to the CCA-led intervention. Statistical adjustments were made during the analysis when they were needed to account for differences between the groups at the start.

Ethical approval was granted by the International Centre for Collaborative Research (ICCR) at Omayal Achi College of Nursing, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Interventions

The intervention was based on a prior pilot study conducted in 2018 by the authors, which demonstrated the feasibility and effectiveness of using community change agents to improve disaster preparedness among women in coastal regions [16].

The intervention components included: The coastal disaster readiness package consists of Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) on disaster readiness, education on social mapping, and 72-hour family disaster preparedness kit, a demonstration, and a return demonstration on Cardio-pulmonary Resuscitation (CPR) competency skills with little adult manikin by the trained CCAs along with the researchers. The IEC on Disaster preparedness, which included education on social mapping, evacuation plan, impact and health problems in natural disasters, flood safety measures, prevention of communicable diseases, women’s reproductive health care with flash cards. Preparation of 72-hour family kit (CRP) with 10 items, which included:

(i) evacuation pack, (ii) Hand hygiene pack, (iii) menstrual hygiene pack, (iv) Reproductive health care pack, (v) Personal protective device pack, (vi) communicable diseases prevention pack, vii)first aid pack, viii) medicine for emergency and chronic illness, ix) non-perishable food for 72 h, and x) cash with documents. Demonstration of adult CPR competency skills on the little Anne adult mannequin and return demonstration. All the contents were included in a handbook and given for reinforcement to all participants.

In 1st phase, the CCAs, selected with support from local leaders and public health nurses (10–12 women per village), underwent a 3-day training program at a local health centre. The three-day training for Community Change Agents (CCAs) was conducted at the Main Primary Health Centre, Sadras, following the Community Disaster Preparedness Programme (CDRP) framework. On Day 1, participants were seated in a comfortable setting, and the researcher provided a comprehensive orientation covering the study objectives, procedures, and ethical considerations. Written informed consent was obtained, and confidentiality was assured. Demographic data were collected through a structured interview, followed by a baseline assessment of knowledge and attitude on disaster preparedness using a structured questionnaire and a four-point Likert scale. Day 2 focused on core disaster preparedness training, including basic concepts of disaster and its impact on the community, social mapping and identification of high-risk zones, flood safety measures and evacuation planning, prevention of communicable diseases, reproductive health care for women during emergencies, and preparation of a 72-hour Family Disaster Kit. The session was interactive and incorporated group discussions, demonstrations, and visual aids. On Day 3, certified trainers from the SAGA (Save a Life Give a Life) Foundation, Chennai, conducted hands-on CPR and Basic Life Support (BLS) training for 23 CCAs, involving mannequin-based practice of chest compressions, rescue breathing, and emergency response protocols. Upon successful completion, participants received a BLS Trainer Certificate and a Coastal Disaster Preparedness Package containing essential reference materials and tools for community-level preparedness. A post-test assessment of knowledge and attitude was conducted on the seventh day following completion of the training programme.

In the second phase, the intervention group received a structured Coastal Disaster Readiness Package (CDRP), delivered through Community Change Agents (CCAs) trained volunteers, along with the researchers.

Data collection tools

The structured questionnaire, attitude scale, and CPR observational checklist were developed and validated by the authors in an earlier study [16]. These tools were used in this study with updated validation by content experts. It includes a Knowledge Questionnaire consisting of 20 multiple-choice items on disaster preparedness, with a scoring system of 1 point per correct answer (maximum score: 20). The level of knowledge is categorized as inadequate (< 50%), moderately adequate (50–75%), and adequate (≥ 75%). The Attitude Scale comprised 20 items on a modified 4-point Likert scale (10 positive and 10 negative statements) covering five domains: familiarity, preparedness, adaptation, awareness, and risk perception. Attitude scores were interpreted as unfavorable (< 50%), moderately favorable (50–75%), and favorable (≥ 75%). CPR skills were assessed using a 17-step observational checklist performed on the Little Anne mannequin, with 1 point awarded for each correctly executed step (maximum score: 17); competency was rated as poor (< 50%), moderate (50–75%), and good (≥ 75%). All tools were validated by experts in medicine, nursing, statistics, and representatives from the SAGA (AHA), with reported reliability coefficients of 0.78 for knowledge, 0.82 for attitude, and 0.85 for skills.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using both descriptive and inferential statistics. Descriptive Statistics, Frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation (SD) were used to summarize demographic data and baseline characteristics. Inferential Statistics,

Paired t-test and Repeated Measures ANOVA were used for within-group comparisons (pre- and post-intervention). An unpaired t-test was used for between-group comparisons. Chi-square test assessed homogeneity and associations with demographic variables. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to examine relationships between knowledge, attitude, and skill scores.

Discussion

The results of this quasi-experimental study demonstrate the significance of trained Community Change Agents (CCAs) in preparing women in vulnerable coastal communities for disasters. The intervention significantly improved participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and skills related to disaster response, particularly in CPR, thereby illustrating the efficacy of the Coastal Disaster Readiness Package (CDRP).

The study found that women assisted by Community Change Agents significantly improved their disaster preparedness knowledge over a six-month follow-up, demonstrating the effectiveness of participatory, community-driven strategies in teaching useful skills. The findings suggest that ongoing local engagement is more likely to result in enduring preparedness behaviors. This finding aligns with recent evidence demonstrating that community health workers and locally recruited volunteers are effective at delivering health and risk preparedness information when training is context-specific and reinforced over time. These reviews emphasize that CHW/volunteer interventions are especially effective where formal services are limited and when training includes repeated contacts and content is culturally tailored [17].

This also aligns with prior studies, which demonstrated that community disaster education programs significantly enhanced awareness and retention of information among individuals in comparable high-risk groups [10].

The sustained retention of knowledge, particularly the notable improvement observed at the six-month mark, suggests that community-based strategies may possess greater longevity than isolated interventions. Also, the fact that the women were taught by people they knew from their communities probably made them more trustworthy and useful [18].

The study found that women assisted by Community Change Agents showed significant enhancements in disaster-related attitudes, including familiarity with coastal disasters, preparedness, adaptation, awareness, and risk perception. These improvements occurred immediately and grew over time, indicating that the CCA-led approach encourages long-lasting and progressive changes in attitudes. The rise in disaster awareness emphasizes the importance of integrating local facilitators into preparedness programs to contextualize information, foster trust, and promote community ownership of risk reduction strategies.

Studies of gender and preparedness report that women’s participation in locally-led programs often increases perceived self-efficacy and intent to prepare, because interventions that use trusted community members overcome social and informational barriers that otherwise blunt attitudinal change [19]. Research has shown that participatory disaster education fosters a proactive mindset and enhances risk perception and preparedness behaviours, which is in line with our findings [20].

Skill acquisition, particularly in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), also improved markedly in the experimental group, with statistically significant differences at the 1st, 3rd, and 6th month post-tests. These findings are consistent with systematic reviews showing that brief, community-based practical training, especially with repeated refreshers or blended approaches (hands-on plus digital refreshers), improves both immediate CPR skill and medium-term retention [21]. The American Heart Association likewise recommends targeting and tailoring layperson CPR training to community subgroups and emphasizing practical, repeated skill practice to increase bystander CPR [22].

One of the best things about this study is that it gives local women a chance to be CCAs. This model, which is led by peers, not only makes health messages easier to understand and accept but also promotes community ownership and long-term success. A study demonstrated that the presence of trusted local volunteers significantly enhances participation and the adoption of behaviours that facilitate disaster preparedness [23].

In our study, a synergistic relationship between knowledge, attitude, and skills in women’s disaster preparedness was found. As knowledge increased, so did positive attitudes, enhancing practical skills. This highlights the importance of integrated educational strategies, cognitive comprehension, and attitudinal transformation. The study suggests that disaster preparedness programs should focus on building motivation, confidence, and practical skills through community-based activities, rather than just providing information. Recent community resilience literature underlines that interventions addressing all three domains, especially when delivered by community organizations or CHWs, show stronger practical outcomes (actual preparedness behaviors and skills) than single-domain interventions [24,25].

This study emphasises the imperative of decentralizing disaster preparedness education and integrating it into conventional public health outreach from a nursing and community health perspective. The CCAs model can help nurses and health educators give important health and safety information to people who are at risk or on the edge. Empowering women can make families and communities stronger because they are often the main caregivers [26, 27].

Limitations

The study yielded substantial results; however, it had some limitations. The study was limited to women and a non-probability sampling method from specific coastal communities, which may affect generalizability. Although groups were similar at baseline, factors like personal motivation or prior informal training may have influenced results. The follow-up period was six months; while gains were sustained during this time, the durability of knowledge, attitude, and skill changes beyond this period remains unknown. There might have been a bias in the self-reporting attitude results. CPR skills were assessed in simulated settings, not real emergencies. Because the program was given in a certain cultural and social setting, it may need to be changed before it can be used in other places. In the selected areas, women from two streets initially refused to participate in the training. To address this, the researcher met with the local religious leader, after which the women agreed to take part. The resuscitation training for CCAs was conducted with the support of the American Heart Association, while all other expenses for arrangements were covered by the researchers.

Recommendations

Based on these results, future studies should look into whether people can retain what they learn and how well they can do it after six months. Comparative studies could look at how well CCAs work compared to other delivery models, like digital or school-based interventions, in reaching different groups in the community. Qualitative studies could also show how participants’ real-life experiences, views on risk, and social dynamics affect their willingness to be prepared. Adding men, young people, and other vulnerable groups to the study would help figure out how widely the CCAs model can be used. Finally, including CCAs in official disaster management systems and measuring their effects during real emergency responses would be very important for making this approach a permanent part of the system.

Conclusion

The study reveals that Community Change Agents (CCAs) significantly enhance participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and life-saving skills through culturally relevant training. This peer-led model has potential for enduring community resilience, especially in resource-constrained environments. Implementing CCAs in current disaster risk reduction systems could bridge the gap between policy and household readiness. The study emphasizes the need for involvement of local Community Change agents and ongoing disaster education or training for women in coastal villages to sustain effective preparedness measures over time.
