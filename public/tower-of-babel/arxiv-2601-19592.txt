Torsion groups and the Bienvenu–Geroldinger conjecture
Salvatore Tringali, Weihao Yan

Abstract
. Let M M be a monoid (written multiplicatively). Equipped with the operation of setwise multiplication induced by M M on its parts, the collection of all finite subsets of M M containing the identity element is itself a monoid, denoted by 𝒫 fin , 1 ​ ( M ) \mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},1}(M) and called the reduced finitary power monoid of M M . One is naturally led to ask whether, for all H H and K K in a given class of monoids, 𝒫 fin , 1 ​ ( H ) \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H) and 𝒫 fin , 1 ​ ( K ) \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K) are isomorphic if and only if H H and K K are. The problem originates from a conjecture of Bienvenu and Geroldinger [Israel J. Math., 2025] that was recently settled by the authors [Proc. AMS, 2025]. Here, we provide a positive answer to the problem in the case where H H and K K are cancellative monoids, one of which is torsion. In particular, the answer is in the affirmative when H H and K K are torsion groups. Whether the conclusion extends to arbitrary groups remains open.

1.
Introduction

Let
S
be a semigroup (see the end of this section for notation and terminology). Equipped with the (binary) operation of setwise multiplication defined on the power set of
S
by

(X,Y)\mapsto\{xy:x\in X,\kern 1.00006pty\in Y\},

the collection of all non-empty subsets of
S
is itself a semigroup, denoted by

\mathcal{P}(S)
and referred to as the
large power semigroup
of
S
. Moreover, the family of all non-empty
finite
subsets of
S
is a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{P}(S)
, denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(S)
and called the
finitary power semigroup
of
S
.

The systematic investigation of these structures began in the late 1960s and was continued quite intensively by semigroup theorists and computer scientists through the 1980s and 1990s. A catalyst of these early developments has been the role that power semigroups play in the study of formal languages and automata.
A milestone in the history of the subject was marked by a 1967 paper of Tamura and Shafer
[
30
]
that has eventually led to the following questions, where a semigroup
H
is said to be
globally isomorphic
to a semigroup
K
if there is a
global isomorphism
from
H
to
K
, that is, an isomorphism from
\mathcal{P}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}(K)
.

Questions 1.1
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of semigroups. Given
H,K\in\mathscr{C}
, is it true that

(a)

\mathcal{P}(H)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}(K)
if and only if
H
is isomorphic to
K
?

(b)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(H)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(K)
if and only if
H
is isomorphic to
K
?

The interesting aspect of these questions lies in the “only if ” direction. In fact, if
f
is an isomorphism from a semigroup
H
to a semigroup
K
, then its
augmentation

f^{\ast}\colon\mathcal{P}(H)\to\mathcal{P}(K)\colon X\mapsto f[X]:=\{f(x)\colon x\in X\}

(1)

is a global isomorphism from
H
to
K
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(H)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(K)
via the restriction of
f^{\ast}
to the non-empty finite subsets of
H
, since
f^{\ast}(X)\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(K)
for every
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(H)
(see
[
31
, Remark 4]
and
[
10
, Sect. 1]
for further details).

The answer to Question
1.1
(a)
is negative for the class of all semigroups
[
21
]
; is positive for groups
[
26
]
, semilattices
[
19
, p. 218]
, completely
0
-simple semigroups and completely simple semigroups
[
28
, Theorems 5.9 and 6.8]
, Clifford semigroups
[
9
, Theorem 4.7]
, and cancellative commutative semigroups
[
31
, Corollary 1]
;
and is open for finite semigroups
[
15
, p. 5]
, despite some authors having claimed the opposite based on results announced in
[
29
]
but never proved.

As for Question
1.1
(b)
, very little is known outside the case where
\mathscr{C}
is a class of
finite
semigroups contained in any of the classes that are already addressed by the positive results reviewed in the previous paragraph (note that the large and finitary power semigroups of a semigroup

coincide if and only if

is finite). More precisely, Bienvenu and Geroldinger have established in
[
2
, Theorem 3.2(3)]
that the problem has an affirmative answer for numerical monoids, and the conclusion has been subsequently generalized to cancellative commutative semigroups in
[
31
, Corollary 1]
. Here, a
numerical monoid
is a submonoid of the additive monoid of non-negative integers with finite complement in
\mathbb{N}
.

Now suppose that
M
is a monoid with identity
1_{M}
. Both
\mathcal{P}(M)
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(M)
are then monoids too, their identity being the singleton
\{1_{M}\}
. Moreover, the family of all finite subsets of
M
containing
1_{M}
is a submonoid of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(M)
, herein denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(M)
and called the
reduced finitary power monoid
of

. Introduced by Fan and Tringali in
[

]
and further investigated in
[

,
2
,
10
]
and
[
33
, Sect. 4.2]
,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(M)
is one of the most basic objects in an intricate web of structures, generically dubbed as

.

Power monoids provide an intrinsic algebraic framework for a number of problems in additive number theory and related fields, including Sárközy’s conjecture on the “additive irreducibility” of the set of [non-zero] quadratic residues of a finite field of prime order
[
25
, Conjecture 1.6]
and Ostmann’s conjecture
[
23
, p. 13]
on the “asymptotic additive irreducibility” of the set of (positive rational) primes.
In addition, the arithmetic of power monoids — particularly with regard to the possibility (or impossibility) of writing certain sets as a finite product of other sets that are, in a suitable sense, irreducible — is a rich topic in itself, and it has been playing a pivotal role in the development of a “unifying theory of factorization”
[
33
,
32
,
3
,
4
,
6
]
that reaches far beyond the scope of the classical theory
[
11
,
12
]
.

With these ideas in mind, one is naturally led to ask the following, as a complement to Questions
1.1
:

Question 1.2
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of monoids. Given
H,K\in\mathscr{C}
, is it true that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
if and only if
H
is isomorphic to
K
?

Again, the core of the question lies in proving the “only if ” direction. In fact, let
f
be a semigroup isomorphism from a monoid
H
to a monoid
K
, and let
f^{\ast}
be the augmentation of
f
, as defined by Eq. (
1
). Then,
f
maps the identity of
H
to the identity of
K
(see, e.g., the last lines of
[
31
, Sect. 2]
), and hence
f(u)\in K^{\times}
for all
u\in H^{\times}
. Since

is a finite subset of
K
for every
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
, it is then clear that
f^{\ast}
restricts to an isomorphism from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
(cf.
[
35

). Consequently, Question

provides yet another example of the functorial isomorphism problem discussed at the outset of this section.

In
[
35
, Theorem 2.5]
, Tringali and Yan answer
Question
1.2
in the affirmative for the class of

rational Puiseux monoids
, that is, submonoids of the non-negative rational numbers
under addition. This confirms a conjecture of Bienvenu and
Geroldinger
[
2
, Conjecture 4.7]
concerning numerical monoids.
In contrast, Rago has recently shown in
[
24
, Corollary 3]
that the answer to the same
question is negative for the class of cancellative commutative monoids — and, more generally,
for any class of monoids that contains non-isomorphic cancellative valuation monoids
[
14
, Theorem and Definition 2.6.4.2]
with trivial unit groups and isomorphic groups of fractions.

In the present work, we contribute to this line of research by proving that Question
1.2
admits an affirmative answer for torsion groups (Corollary
5.2
). One major step requires showing — and this is a fact of independent
interest — that, for every isomorphism
f
from the reduced finitary power monoid of a monoid
H
to the reduced finitary power
monoid of a monoid
K
, there exists a bijection

g\colon H\to K
, called the pullback of
f
,
such that
f(\{1_{H},x\})=\{1_{K},g(x)\}
for all
x\in H
(Corollary

3.3
and Definition
3.4
). Our main result states that if
H
and
K
are cancellative monoids and one of them is torsion (and hence a group), then the pullback of an isomorphism from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
is an isomorphism from
H
to
K
(Theorem
5.1
). Question
1.2
remains open in the case of arbitrary groups.

Generalities.

If not explicitly specified, we write all semigroups (and monoids) multiplicatively, and all morphisms are
semigroup
homomorphisms (even if their domain and codomain are both monoids).

We denote by
\mathbb{N}
the (set of) non-negative integers, by
\mathbb{N}^{+}
the positive integers, by
\mathbb{Z}
the integers, by
|X|
the cardinality of a set
X
, and by
f^{-1}
the (functional) inverse of a bijection
f
.
Unless otherwise stated, we reserve the letters
m
and
n
for positive integers, and the letters
i
,
j
, and
k
for non-negative integers.
If
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
\llbracket a,b\rrbracket:=\{x\in\allowbreak\mathbb{N}\colon\allowbreak a\leq x\leq b\}
be the (
discrete
)
interval
from
a
to
b
.

An element
a
in a semigroup
S
is said to be
torsion
if the set
\{a^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\}
is finite, and

cancellative
if left multiplication and right multiplication by
a
are both injective functions on
S
. The semigroup itself is torsion (respectively, cancellative) if each
of its elements is.

Suppose now that
M
is a monoid. The
order

{\rm ord}_{M}(a)
of an element
a\in M
is the
size
of the
cyclic submonoid

\langle a\rangle_{M}:=\{a^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
generated by
a
: if
\langle a\rangle_{M}
is infinite, then
{\rm ord}_{M}(a):=\infty
; otherwise,
{\rm ord}_{M}(a)
is the number of elements in
\langle a\rangle_{M}
. It is a basic fact — and we will often used it later without further comment — that every cancellative torsion element
a\in M
is a unit, and its order is the smallest
n\geq 1

such that
a^{n}=1_{M}
. Here, a
unit
is an element
u\in M
for which there exists a
(necessarily unique) element
v\in M
, denoted by
u^{-1}
and called the
inverse
of
u
, such that

uv=vu=1_{M}
.

Additional notation and terminology, if not explained when first used, are standard or should be clear from the context. In particular, we refer to Howie’s monograph
[
16
]
for the basics of semigroup theory.

2.
Preliminaries on the order of an element in a monoid

Below, we collect some fundamental properties of the order of an element in a monoid
M
, with focus on the case where
M
is cancellative. These properties are closely related to specific “structural features” of the finitary power monoid
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(M)
of
M
and will play a key role in the proofs of Sect.
4
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
M
be a monoid. A torsion element
z\in M
has order
n
if and only if
n
is the smallest integer
k\geq 1
such that
\{1_{M},z\}^{k}=\{1_{M},z\}^{k-1}
.

Proof.

Fix
z\in M
. By
[
16
, Theorem 1.2.2(2)]
,
z
has order
n
if and only if
1_{M},z,\ldots,z^{n-1}
are pairwise distinct and there exists
m\in\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket
such that
z^{n}=z^{m}
. This implies, on the one hand, that

\{1_{M},z\}^{n}=\{1_{M},z,\ldots,z^{n}\}=\{1_{M},z,\ldots,z^{n-1}\}=\{1_{M},z\}^{n-1};

(2)

and, on the other hand, that

z^{k}\notin\{1_{M},z,\ldots,z^{k-1}\}=\{1_{M},z\}^{k-1},\qquad\text{for all }k\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket.

(3)

In particular, Eq. (
3
) shows that
\{1_{M},z\}^{k}\neq\{1_{M},z\}^{k-1}
for every
k\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
, which, together with Eq. (
2
), is enough to complete the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
M
be a monoid, and fix
z\in M
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. If
r\geq\ell-1
is an integer, then

\{1_{M},z^{\ell}\}\{1_{M},z\}^{r}=\{1_{M},z\}^{\ell+r}.

If, in addition,
z
is cancellative and
\ell
is no larger than the order of
z
in
M
, then

\{1_{M},z^{\ell}\}\{1_{M},z\}^{r}\neq\{1_{M},z\}^{s},\qquad\text{for all }r,s\in\mathbb{N}\text{ with }r<\ell-1\leq s.

Proof.

The first part is straightforward: for any integer
r\geq\ell-1
, we have

\{1_{M},z^{\ell}\}\{1_{M},z\}^{r}=\{1_{M},\ldots,z^{r}\}\cup\{z^{\ell},\ldots,z^{\ell+r}\}=\{1_{M},z,\ldots,z^{\ell+r}\}=\{1_{M},z\}^{\ell+r},

As for the second part, suppose that
z
is a cancellative element and
\ell
is no larger than
n:={\rm ord}_{M}(z)
. Assume for a contradiction that there exist

r,s\in\mathbb{N}
with

r<\ell-1\leq s
such that

\{1_{M},z\}^{s}=\{1_{M},z^{\ell}\}\{1_{M},z\}^{r}=\{1_{M},z\}^{r}\cup z^{\ell}\{1_{M},z\}^{r}.

(4)

Since
1\leq\ell\leq n
, it is then clear that

z^{\ell-1}\notin\{1_{M},\allowbreak z\}^{r}\quad\text{and}\quad z^{\ell-1}\in\{1_{M},\allowbreak z\}^{s}.

It thus follows
from Eq. (
4
) that
z^{\ell-1}=z^{\ell+i}
for some

i\in\llbracket 0,r\rrbracket
. By the cancellativity of
z
, this implies
z^{i+1}=1_{M}
. In
particular,
z
is a unit and generates a finite cyclic subgroup of the unit group of
M
of order
n
. Therefore, we conclude from the elementary properties of cyclic groups

[
18
, Theorem I.3.4(iv)]
that
n\mid i+\allowbreak 1
, which is impossible since

1\leq i+1\leq\allowbreak r+\allowbreak 1\leq\allowbreak\ell-1<n
.
∎

Example 2.3
.

Let
M
be a cyclic monoid of order
4
and index
2
, meaning that
M
is generated by an element
z
of order
4
such that
z^{2}=z^{4}
(see
[
16
, Sect. 1.2]
for terminology). We have

\{1_{M},z^{3}\}\{1_{M},z\}=\{1_{M},z\}^{3+1},

which shows that Lemma
2.2
fails to be true with
\ell=\allowbreak 3
: otherwise, we would have
\{1_{M},z^{3}\}\{1_{M},z\}^{r}\neq\{1_{M},z\}^{3+r}
for each
r\in\{0,1\}
. In other words, Lemma
2.2
need not be true if
z
is not cancellative.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
M
be a monoid. If
x,y\in M
and
x^{r}=y^{s}
for some
r,s\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, then

\{1_{M},x\}^{r-1}\{1_{M},xy\}\{1_{M},y\}^{s}=\{1_{M},x\}^{r}\{1_{M},y\}^{s+1}

(5)

and

\{1_{M},x\}^{r}\{1_{M},xy\}\{1_{M},y\}^{s-1}=\{1_{M},x\}^{r+1}\{1_{M},y\}^{s}.

(6)

Proof.

We focus on the first identity, as the second follows by symmetry.

Denote by
L
and
R
the left-hand side and the right-hand side of Eq. (
5
), respectively. Given
z\in M
, it is clear that
z\in L
if and only if either
z=x^{i}y^{j}
or
z=x^{i+1}y^{j+1}
for some
i\in\llbracket 0,r-1\rrbracket
and
j\in\llbracket 0,s\rrbracket
, while
z\in R
if and only if
z=x^{i}y^{j}
for some
i\in\llbracket 0,r\rrbracket
and
j\in\llbracket 0,s+1\rrbracket
. Consequently, we have

R\smallsetminus\{x^{r},y^{s+1}\}\subseteq L\subseteq R.

It remains to check that
x^{r},y^{s+1}\in L
, and this is immediate. Indeed, we have
x^{r}=\allowbreak y^{s}
(by hypothesis), and hence
y^{s+1}=x^{r}y
. Since
y^{s},x^{r}y\in L
(as noted above), we are done.
∎

Lemma
2.4
applies, in particular, to torsion
groups, because in a torsion group every element has a positive power equal to
the identity. We conclude the section with the following:

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
M
be a monoid, and let
x,y\in M
be cancellative torsion elements. Then
x
and
y
are units, and there exist
r,u\in\llbracket 1,{\rm ord}_{M}(x)\rrbracket
and
s,v\in\llbracket 1,{\rm ord}_{M}(y)\rrbracket
such that

\text{(i) }x^{r}=y^{s}\text{ and }x^{u}=y^{v};\qquad\text{(ii) if }x^{c}=y^{d}\text{ for some }c,d\in\mathbb{Z},\text{ then }r\mid c\text{ and }v\mid d.

Proof.

Set
m:={\rm ord}_{M}(x)
and
n:={\rm ord}_{M}(y)
. Every cancellative torsion element
z\in M
is a unit and its order is the smallest integer
k\geq 1
such that
z^{k}=1_{M}
. Accordingly, we have
x^{m}=y^{n}=1_{M}
, which, by the well-ordering principle, implies the existence of integers
r,u\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
and
s,v\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
such that

\text{(I) }x^{r}=y^{s}\text{ and }x^{u}=y^{v};\qquad\text{(II) if }c,d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\text{ and }x^{c}=y^{d},\text{ then }r\leq c\text{ and }v\leq d.

(7)

With that said, we focus on proving part (ii) of the statement for
r
, as the case for
v
is symmetric.

First, assume
x^{c}=y^{d}
with
c\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
d\in\mathbb{Z}
. By the division algorithm, there exist
q\in\mathbb{Z}
and
k\in\allowbreak\llbracket 0,\allowbreak n-1\rrbracket
such that
d=nq+k
. If
k=0
, then
x^{c}=\allowbreak(y^{n})^{q}=1_{M}
, and hence, by the elementary properties of cyclic groups

[
18
, Theorem I.3.4(iv)]
,
m\mid c
. Otherwise,
x^{c}=\allowbreak y^{nq+k}=\allowbreak(y^{n})^{q}y^{k}=y^{k}
, which, by Eq. (
7
) and the positivity of
k
, yields
r\leq\allowbreak c
. In any case (recall that
r\leq m
), this shows that

\text{if }x^{c}=y^{d}\text{ for some }c\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\text{ and }d\in\mathbb{Z},\text{ then }r\leq c.

(8)

Next, suppose that
x^{c}=y^{d}
with
c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
. Similarly as before, there exist
q\in\mathbb{Z}
and
k\in\llbracket 0,r-1\rrbracket
such that
c=rq+k
, with the result that
y^{d}=x^{rq}x^{k}=y^{sq}x^{k}
and hence
x^{k}=y^{d-sq}
. By Eq. (
8
), this is only possible if
k=0
, which implies that
r\mid c
and completes the proof.
∎

3.
The two-to-two property

Let
H
and
K
be monoids. In this short section, we show that all isomorphisms from the reduced finitary power monoid
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
of
H
to the reduced finitary power monoid of
K
have a somewhat surprising property in common: they bijectively map sets of the form
\{1_{H},x\}\subseteq H
to sets of the form
\{1_{K},\allowbreak y\}\subseteq\allowbreak K
, regardless of any hypotheses on
H
and
K
(Corollary
3.3
).
The proof is essentially combinatorial, as the main step (Theorem
3.2
) reduces to counting and bounding the number of solutions to certain equations involving product sets.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
M
be a monoid,
S
be a subset of
M
containing the identity
1_{M}
, and
n
be an integer
\geq 3
. If
1_{M}\notin T\subseteq S
, then
(S^{n-1}\smallsetminus T)S=S^{n}
. In particular, the equation
AS=S^{n}
has at least
2^{|S|-1}
solutions
A
over the large power monoid
\mathcal{P}(M)
of
M
.

Proof.

Let
T
be a subset of
S\smallsetminus\{1_{M}\}
, and define
Q:=S^{n-1}\smallsetminus T
. It is obvious that
QS\subseteq\allowbreak S^{n-1}S=\allowbreak S^{n}
, so we only need to check that
S^{n}\subseteq QS
.
To this end, fix
z\in S^{n}
, and let
k
be the smallest non-negative integer such that
z\in S^{k}
(it is clear that
0\leq k\leq n
; and since
1_{M}\in S
, we have
S\subseteq S^{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S^{n}
). Our goal is to show that
z\in QS
, and we distinguish three cases.

•

Case 1:

k=0
or
k=1
. If
k=0
, then
z\in S^{0}=\{1_{M}\}
and thus
z=1_{M}
. It follows that, regardless of whether
k=0
or
k=1
,
z\in S
and hence
z\in 1_{M}S\subseteq QS
(note that
1_{M}\in Q
).

•

Case 2:

2\leq k\leq n-1
. We have
z\notin T
, or else
z\in S
(by the fact that
T\subseteq S
), contradicting the definition itself of
k
(which guarantees that
z\notin S^{i}
for every non-negative integer
i<k
). It follows that
z\in S^{k}\smallsetminus T\subseteq Q
(by the fact that
S^{k}\subseteq S^{n-1}
), and hence
z\in Q1_{M}\subseteq QS
.

•

Case 3:

k=n
. By the definition of
k
, we have that
z\notin S^{i}
for each
i\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
. On the other hand,
z=s_{1}\cdots s_{n}
for some
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in S
. It follows that
z^{\prime}:=s_{1}\cdots s_{n-1}\notin T
, or else
z=z^{\prime}s_{n}\in S^{2}
, which is a contradiction because
2<3\leq n
. Therefore,
z^{\prime}\in S^{n-1}\smallsetminus T
and hence
z=z^{\prime}s_{n}\in QS
.

As for the “In particular” part of the statement, we have already observed that
S\subseteq S^{n-1}
. So, if
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are distinct subsets of
S\smallsetminus\{1_{M}\}
, then the sets
S^{n-1}\smallsetminus T_{1}
and
S^{n-1}\smallsetminus T_{2}
are likewise distinct. As a result, the equation
AS=S^{n}
has at least as many solutions
A
over
\mathcal{P}(M)
as there are subsets of
S\smallsetminus\{1_{M}\}
; namely, it has at least
2^{|S|-1}
solutions.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

If
H
and
K
are monoids and
f
is an isomorphism from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
, then
f(X)
is a
2
-element set for every
2
-element set
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
.

Proof.

The claim is vacuously true if
H
is a trivial monoid. Otherwise, fix a non-identity element
x\in H
and set
X:=\{1_{H},x\}
,
Y:=f(X)
, and
k:=|Y|-1
. Considering that
1_{K}\in Y
and
Y\neq\allowbreak f(\{1_{H}\})=\allowbreak\{1_{K}\}
(by the injectivity of
f
), it is clear that
k
is a
positive
integer. We need to prove that
k=1
. For, put

\mathcal{C}_{X}:=\{A\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)\colon AX=X^{3}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{D}_{Y}:=\{B\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)\colon BY=Y^{3}\}.

Since
1_{H}\in X
, it is clear that
A\in\mathcal{C}_{X}
yields
A\subseteq X^{3}
and
f(A)Y=Y^{3}
. In a similar way,
B\in\mathcal{D}_{Y}
yields
B\subseteq Y^{3}
and
f^{-1}(B)X=X^{3}
. We thus see that
d:=\allowbreak|\mathcal{C}_{X}|=\allowbreak|\mathcal{D}_{Y}|\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, for
X^{3}
is a finite set and the restriction of
f
to
\mathcal{C}_{X}
establishes a bijection
\mathcal{C}_{X}\to\mathcal{D}_{Y}
.

With these preliminaries in place, suppose for a contradiction that
k\geq 2
. We then get from the above and Lemma
3.1
(applied with
S=Y
and
n=3
) that
d=|\mathcal{D}_{Y}|\geq 4
. Let
m
be the order of the submonoid
\{1_{H},x,x^{2},\ldots\}
of
H
generated by
x
; note that
m\geq 2
(by the fact that
x\neq 1_{H}
). We will distinguish four cases, depending on whether
m=2
,
m=3
,
m=4
, or
m\geq 5
.

Case 1:

m=2
. We have
x^{2}\in X
and hence
X^{3}=X
. Consequently,
A\in\mathcal{C}_{X}
only if
A\subseteq X
, which shows in turn that
d=|\mathcal{C}_{X}|=2
and contradicts our previous finding (that
d\geq 4
).

Case 2:

m=3
.
We have
x^{3}\in X^{2}
and
x^{2}\notin X
, with the result that
X^{3}=X^{2}\neq X
and, hence,
A\in C_{X}
only if
\{1_{H}\}\subsetneq A\subseteq X^{3}=\{1_{H},x,x^{2}\}
. It follows that
d=|\mathcal{C}_{X}|=3
, which is again absurd.

Case 3:

m=4
. We have
x^{4}\in X^{3}
and
x^{3}\notin X^{2}
(or else
m\leq 3
), implying that
X^{2}\neq X^{3}=X^{4}
and hence
Y^{2}\neq Y^{3}=Y^{4}
. Since
Y\subseteq Y^{2}\subseteq Y^{3}
, it follows that
Y^{2}\smallsetminus S
and
Y^{3}\smallsetminus T
differ from each other for all subsets
S
and
T
of
Y
. So, we gather from Lemma
3.1
(applied first with
S=Y
and
n=3
, then with
S=Y
and
n=4
) that
d=|\mathcal{D}_{Y}|\geq 2^{k}+2^{k}\geq 8
. If, on the other hand,
A\in\allowbreak\mathcal{C}_{X}
, then
\{1_{H}\}\neq\allowbreak A\neq\allowbreak\{1_{H},x\}
(or else
AX\subseteq X^{2}\subsetneq X^{3}
). To wit,
A\in\mathcal{C}_{X}
only if
A=\allowbreak\{1_{H}\}\cup\allowbreak A^{\prime}
for some
A^{\prime}\subseteq\allowbreak X^{3}\smallsetminus\{1_{H}\}=\allowbreak\{x,x^{2},x^{3}\}
with
\varnothing\neq A^{\prime}\neq\{x\}
. But this means that
8\leq d=\allowbreak|\mathcal{C}_{X}|\leq 2^{3}-1=7
, which is still a contradiction.

Case 4:

m\geq 5
. We have
x^{4}\notin X^{3}
(or else
m\leq 4
) and
x^{3}\notin X^{2}
(or else
m\leq 3
). So, if
A\in\mathcal{C}_{X}
, then
x^{3}\notin A
and hence
A\subseteq X^{2}
; moreover,
A\neq\{1_{H}\}
(or else
AX=X\neq X^{3})
and
x^{2}\in A
(or else
A\subseteq X
and hence
AX\subseteq X^{2}\subsetneq X^{3}
). It follows that
A\in\mathcal{C}_{X}
if and only if
A=\{1_{H},x^{2}\}
or
A=X^{2}
, with the result that
d=|\mathcal{C}_{X}|=2
. However, this is once more impossible and finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
H
and
K
be monoids, and let
f
be an isomorphism
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
. There is then a (uniquely determined) bijection
g\colon H\to K
such that
f(\{1_{H},\allowbreak x\})=\allowbreak\{1_{K},g(x)\}
for each
x\in H
. In particular,
g(1_{H})=1_{K}
.

Proof.

Since
f
maps
\{1_{H}\}
to
\{1_{K}\}
, we gather from Theorem
3.2
that, for all
x\in H
, there is a (unique) element
y\in K
with the property that
f(\{1_{H},x\})=\{1_{K},y\}
. Namely, there exists a function
g\colon H\to K
such that
g(1_{H})=1_{K}
and
f(\{1_{H},x\})=\{1_{K},g(x)\}
for all
x\in H
. It remains to see that
g
is bijective.

To begin, fix
u,v\in H
with
u\neq v
. We have from the above and the injectivity of
f
that

\{1_{K},g(u)\}=\allowbreak f(\{1_{H},u\})\neq f(\{1_{K},v\}=\{1_{K},g(v)\},

which is only possible if
g(u)\neq g(v)
. Consequently,
g
is itself injective.

Next, let
y\in K
. Since
f
is an isomorphism
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
, its inverse
f^{-1}
is an isomorphism from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. So, again from Theorem
3.2
, we have that
f^{-1}(\{1_{K},y\})=\allowbreak\{1_{H},x\}
for some
x\in H
. It follows that
\{1_{K},y\}=f(\{1_{H},x\})=\{1_{H},g(x)\}
and hence
g(x)=y
, which shows that
g
is also surjective and completes the proof.
∎

It is an open question whether Corollary
3.3
can be generalized to show that, for arbitrary monoids
H
and
K
, every isomorphism from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
is cardinality-preserving (this remains unclear even in the cancellative case). Nevertheless, the result simplifies the proof of
[
34
, Theorem 3.2]
— making it independent of the Fundamental Theorem of Additive Number Theory
[
22
, Theorem 1.1]
— and justifies the following definition, which plays a crucial role in this work.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
H
and
K
be monoids. Based on Corollary
3.3
, we let the
pullback
of an isomorphism
f\colon\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
be the unique bijection
H\to K
such that
f(\{1_{H},x\})=\{1_{K},g(x)\}
for all
x\in H
.

We devote the next section to studying some of the properties of the pullback. The ultimate goal is to prove that, under certain circumstances, the pullback is actually an isomorphism (Proposition
4.7
).

4.
The pullback and its properties

Some of the results proved in this section are valid for arbitrary monoids. However,
most of them rely on cancellativity, and it would be interesting to understand if they extend to more general settings.

We first show that pullbacks (Definition
3.4
) are order-preserving. We then establish that, at least in the cancellative setting, they map
k
-powers to
k
-powers for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
(Proposition
4.3
).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
H
and
K
be monoids, and let
g
be the pullback of an isomorphism
f
from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
. Then
\mathrm{ord}_{H}(x)=\mathrm{ord}_{K}(g(x))
for every
x\in H
.

Proof.

Fix an element
x\in H
, and denote by
n
its order. Our objective is to show that
m:=\mathrm{ord}_{K}(g(x))=\allowbreak n
. We distinguish two cases, depending on whether
n
is finite or not.

Case 1:

n<\infty
. By Lemma
2.1
,
n
is the smallest integer
k\geq 1
such that

\{1_{H},x\}^{k}=\{1_{H},x\}^{k-1}.

Since, for all
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
, the equality
X^{r}=X^{r-1}
holds if and only if
f(X)^{r}=f(X)^{r-1}
, it follows that
n
is also the smallest integer
k\geq 1
for which

\{1_{K},g(x)\}^{k}=f(\{1_{H},x\})^{k}=f(\{1_{H},x\})^{k-1}=\{1_{K},g(x)\}^{k-1}.

Therefore, by reapplying Lemma
2.1
, we conclude that
m=n
.

Case 2:

n=\infty
. Given
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
with
i<j
, we infer from the basic properties of cyclic semigroups
[
16
, Theorem 1.2.2(1)]
that

x^{i}\neq x^{j}
. This in turn implies

\{1_{H},x\}^{i}\subsetneq\{1_{H},x\}^{j}
, and consequently

\{1_{K},g(x)\}^{i}=f\bigl(\{1_{H},x\}^{i}\bigr)\neq f\bigl(\{1_{H},x\}^{j}\bigr)=\{1_{K},g(x)\}^{j}.

It follows that
m=\infty=n
, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
g
be the pullback of an isomorphism
f\colon\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
, where
H
and
K
are monoids with
K
cancellative. If
x\in H
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then
g(x^{k})=g(x)^{\ell}
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
\ell\leq k
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in H
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and set
y:=g(x)
. We need to show that
g(x^{k})=y^{\ell}
for some
\ell\in\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket
.

To start with, we have from Corollary
3.3
and Proposition
4.1
that

g(x^{0})=g(1_{H})=1_{K}=y^{0}\quad\text{and}\quad n:={\rm ord}_{H}(x)={\rm ord}_{K}(y).

(9)

Since
x^{k}=x^{i}
for some
i\in\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket
(including the case
n=\infty
, where
\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket=\mathbb{N}
and
i=k
),
we will therefore assume in the remainder that
1\leq k<n
. By the first part of Lemma
2.2
, this yields

\{1_{H},x^{k}\}\{1_{H},x\}^{k-1}=\{1_{H},x\}^{2k-1},

which, by taking the image of both sides under
f
, leads to

g(x^{k})\in\{1_{K},g(x^{k})\}\{1_{K},y\}^{k-1}=\{1_{K},y\}^{2k-1}.

It follows that
g(x^{k})=y^{\ell}
for some
\ell\in\llbracket 0,2k-1\rrbracket
, and consequently

\{1_{K},y^{\ell}\}\{1_{K},y\}^{k-1}=\{1_{K},y\}^{2k-1}.

(10)

Considering that
g
is injective and
1\leq k<n
, we may actually assume by Eq. (
9
) that

1\leq\ell\leq\min(n-1,2k-1);

in particular,
n
being the order of
y
in
K
guarantees that
y^{\ell}=y^{j}
for some
j\in\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket
. Since
K
is, by hypothesis, a cancellative monoid (and hence
z
is a cancellative element in
K
), it is then straightforward from Eq. (
10
) and the second part of Lemma
2.2
(applied with
M=K
,
z=y
,
r=\allowbreak k-\allowbreak 1
, and
s=\allowbreak 2k-\allowbreak 1
) that
\ell-1\leq\allowbreak k-\allowbreak 1
, namely,
\ell\leq k
(as desired).
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

If
H
and
K
are cancellative monoids and
g
is the pullback of an isomorphism
f
from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
, then
g(x^{k})=g(x)^{k}
for all
x\in H
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in H
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The inverse
f^{-1}
of
f
is an isomorphism
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. Accordingly, let
h
denote the pullback of
f^{-1}
, and set
y:=g(x)
and
z:=h(y)
.

First, observe that
x^{k}=x^{r}
for some
r\in\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket
, where

n:={\rm ord}_{H}(x)
. Since
H
is cancellative, we additionally have
k\equiv r\bmod n
,
with the convention that this congruence reduces to
k=r
when
n=\infty
.
On the other hand, Lemma
4.2
guarantees that there exist
\ell,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
m\leq\ell\leq r
such that
g(x^{r})=y^{\ell}

and
h(y^{\ell})=z^{m}
; and it is actually clear from our definitions that
h=g^{-1}
and hence
z=x
, where
g^{-1}
denotes the inverse of
g
(recall from Corollary
3.3
that
g
is a bijection).

Thus,
x^{r}=g^{-1}(y^{\ell})=x^{m}
. Since
0\leq m\leq r<n={\rm ord}_{H}(x)
, it follows that
r=m
. Consequently, we gather from the above that
\ell=r

and
g(x^{k})=g(x^{r})=y^{r}
. Now, considering that
K
is also a cancellative monoid and, by Proposition
4.1
, the order of
y
in
K
is the same as the order of
x
in
H
, we conclude from the congruence
k\equiv r\bmod n
that
g(x^{k})=y^{r}=y^{k}=g(x)^{k}
.
∎

Example 4.4
.

Let
H
be a cyclic group of order
2
, and
K
be an idempotent monoid of order
2
(note that
H
and
K
are unique up to isomorphism). The function
f
from

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
that maps
\{1_{H}\}
to
\{1_{K}\}
and
H
to
K
is a (monoid) isomorphism, whose pullback is the function
g\colon H\to K
that sends
1_{H}
to
1_{K}
and the unique non-identity element
x\in H
to the unique non-identity element
y\in K
. However, we have
x^{2}=1_{H}
, and hence
1_{K}=g(1_{H})=g(x^{2})\neq g(x)^{2}=y
. This proves that Proposition
4.3
need not be true when the monoids in its statement are not
both
cancellative.

We continue with a series of lemmas that will eventually allow us to prove that, in the
cancellative setting, pullbacks behave like semigroup homomorphisms when restricted
to the product of two torsion elements (Proposition
4.7
).

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
H
and
K
be cancellative monoids, and let
g
be the pullback of an isomorphism
f
from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
. If
x,y\in H
are torsion elements, then either
g(xy)=g(x)g(y)
or
x^{2}y^{2}=1_{H}
.

Proof.

Set
m:={\rm ord}_{H}(x)
and
n:={\rm ord}_{H}(y)
, and let
a:=g(x)
and
b:=g(y)
. We will often use without further comment that
g
is an injective function and each of
H
and
K
is a cancellative monoid.

By the hypothesis that
x
and
y
are torsion elements,
m
and
n
are positive integers. Furthermore, we know from Proposition
2.5
that
x,y\in H^{\times}
, and there exist
r,\allowbreak u\in\allowbreak\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
and
s,v\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
such that

\text{(i) }x^{r}=y^{s}\text{ and }x^{u}=y^{v};\qquad\text{(ii) if }x^{c}=y^{d}\text{ for some }c,d\in\mathbb{Z},\text{ then }r\mid c\text{ and }v\mid d.

(11)

We henceforth assume that
g(xy)\neq g(x)g(y)
, that is,
g(xy)\neq ab
; otherwise, there is nothing to do.
To facilitate the presentation, the remainder of the proof is structured as a sequence of claims.

Claim A:

y\neq x^{c}
and
x\neq y^{d}
for all
c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof of
Claim

A
.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
y=x^{c}
for some
c\in\mathbb{Z}
(the other case is similar).
Using that
x^{m}=1_{H}
and applying the division algorithm to write
c=\allowbreak nq+k
with

q\in\allowbreak\mathbb{Z}
and
k\in\allowbreak\llbracket 0,m-1\rrbracket
, we get that
x^{c}=\allowbreak(x^{m})^{q}x^{k}=\allowbreak x^{k}
.
It is then clear by
Proposition
4.3
that

ab\neq g(xy)=g(x^{c+1})=g(x^{k+1})=a^{k+1}=a\cdot a^{k}=ag(x^{k})=ag(x^{c})=ag(y)=ab,

which is impossible and implies that
y\neq x^{c}
for all
c\in\mathbb{Z}
.

∎

Claim B:

r\geq 2
and
v\geq 2
.

Proof of
Claim

B
.

Upon considering that
r
and
v
are positive integers, the conclusion is straightforward from Eq. (
11
)(i) and
Claim

A
.
∎

Claim C:

There exist
j,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
2\leq j<n
and
2\leq\ell<m
such that
g(xy)=ab^{j}=a^{\ell}b
.

Proof of
Claim

C
.

We focus on proving that
g(xy)=ab^{j}
for some
j\in\llbracket 2,n-1\rrbracket
, as the second half of the statement can be established from Eq. (
11
) and Lemma
2.4
by a symmetric argument.

To start with, we gather from Eq. (
11
)(i) and the first part of Lemma
2.4
that

\{1_{H},x\}^{r-1}\{1_{H},xy\}\{1_{H},y\}^{s}=\{1_{H},x\}^{r}\{1_{H},y\}^{s+1}.

(12)

By applying
f
to Eq. (
12
), it follows that

g(xy)\in\{1_{K},a\}^{r-1}\{1_{K},g(xy)\}\{1_{K},b\}^{s}=\{1_{K},a\}^{r}\{1_{K},b\}^{s+1}.

(13)

Therefore,
g(xy)=a^{i}b^{j}
for some
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
with
i\leq r
and
j\leq s+1
. Let us check that
i=1
.

Proposition
4.1
ensures that
\mathrm{ord}_{K}(b)=n
. Since
K
is a cancellative monoid,
b
is then a unit of order
n
in
K
, and hence
b^{z}\{1_{K},b\}^{n}=\{1_{K},b\}^{n}
for all

. As a result,

\{1_{K},a^{i}\}\{1_{K},b\}^{n}=\{1_{K},b\}^{n}\cup a^{i}\{1_{K},b\}^{n}=\{1_{K},b\}^{n}\cup a^{i}b^{j}\{1_{K},b\}^{n}=\{1_{K},a^{i}b^{j}\}\{1_{K},b\}^{n},

which, by the injectivity of
f
(and omitting some routine steps), leads to

x^{i}\in\{1_{H},x^{i}\}\{1_{H},y\}^{n}=\{1_{H},xy\}\{1_{H},y\}^{n}.

Thus, either
x^{i}=y^{k}
or
x^{i}=xy^{k+1}
for some
k\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket
.
However, the first possibility must be ruled out. Indeed, we proved above that
i
is a
positive
integer no larger than
r
.
So, if
x^{i}=y^{k}
, then by Eq. (
11
)(ii), we can only have

.
This, however, entails by Proposition
4.3
that

g(xy)=a^{r}b^{j}=g(x^{r})\kern 1.00006ptb^{j}=g(y^{s})\kern 1.00006ptb^{j}=b^{s}b^{j}=b^{s+j}=g(y^{s+j}),

which yields

and thus

, contradicting
Claim

A
.

To sum up, we have found that
x^{i}=xy^{k+1}
, and hence

x^{i-1}=y^{k+1}
. By Eq. (
11
)(ii), this proves that

. Since

(
Claim

B
) and
-1\leq\allowbreak i-1<r
, it is then clear that

and, consequently,
g(xy)=ab^{j}
.
It remains to see that, without loss of generality,
2\leq j<n
.

For, we may of course assume that
1\leq\allowbreak j\leq n
, as we have already noted that
b
is a unit of order

in

. Moreover, the condition

ensures that

, and thus
2\leq j\leq n
. Finally, suppose for a contradiction that

. Then

g(xy)=\allowbreak ab^{n}=\allowbreak a=g(x)
,
and hence

.
Since
g(1_{H})=1_{K}
, it follows that
ab\neq\allowbreak g(xy)=\allowbreak g(x)=\allowbreak ag(1_{H})=ab
, which is absurd.

∎

Claim D:

s\leq n-2
and

.

Proof of
Claim

D
.

We verify that

, as the other inequality can be treated analogously.

We already know that

. By

(and the cancellativity of

), there exist

, this guarantees that

)(i), we have

. It follows that

, and hence

; in fact, if

, then

, and thus

If, on the other hand,

, then by Eq. (

)(i) we have

, and hence

, which is impossible by

. Therefore,

.

∎

Claim E:

s=n-2
and

.

Proof of
Claim

E
.

We will show that

, as the equality

can be proved similarly.

To begin, we infer from Eq. (

) and
Claim

that there exists
j\in\llbracket 2,n-1\rrbracket
such that

ab^{j}\{1_{K},b\}^{s}\subseteq\{1_{K},a\}^{r-1}\{1_{K},ab^{j}\}\{1_{K},b\}^{s}=\{1_{K},a\}^{r}\{1_{K},b\}^{s+1}.

(14)

If
ab^{p}=a^{k}b^{q}
for some
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
and
k\in\llbracket 0,r\rrbracket
, then

a^{k-1}=b^{p-q}
, which, by Eq. (
11
)(ii), is only possible if
r\mid k-1
. So,
k=1
(as we have
r\geq 2
by
Claim

B
),
and we conclude from Eq. (
14
) that

ab^{j}\{1_{K},b\}^{s}\subseteq a\{1_{K},b\}^{s+1}.

By cancelling
a
on the left, the last display gives in turn that

b^{j+s}\in b^{j}\{1_{K},b\}^{s}\subseteq\{1_{K},b\}^{s+1}.

(15)

Now,
Claim

C
yields
2\leq j\leq n-1
,
and thus
s+1<j+s
. Consequently, for Eq. (
15
) to hold, it is
necessary that
j+s\geq n
(recall that
b
is a unit of order
n
in
K
). It follows that

j+k=n
for some
k\in\llbracket 1,s\rrbracket
, which, again by Eq. (
15
),
implies that
b^{n-1}=b^{j+k-1}=b^{t}

for a certain
t\in\llbracket 0,\allowbreak s+\allowbreak 1\rrbracket
. However, the latter is only possible if
s+1\geq n-1
, and so,
by
Claim

D
,
s=n-2
.

∎

We are ready to finish the proof of Lemma
4.5
. In fact, since
y
is a unit of order
n
in
H
, we get from
Claim

E
that
x^{r}=y^{n-2}=y^{-2}
. Likewise,

y^{v}=x^{m-2}=x^{-2}
. Therefore,
x^{2}=\allowbreak y^{-v}
and
y^{2}=x^{-r}
. By
Claim

B
and Eq. (
11
)(ii), this shows that
r=v=2
. As a result,
x^{2}=y^{-2}
, that is,

x^{2}y^{2}=1_{H}
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
H
and
K
be cancellative monoids, and let
g
be the pullback of an isomorphism
f
from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
. If
x,y\in H
are torsion elements with
x^{2}=1_{H}
or
y^{2}=1_{H}
, then
g(xy)=g(x)g(y)
.

Proof.

Assume to the contrary that
g(xy)\neq ab
, where
a:=g(x)
and
b:=g(y)
. By Lemma
4.5
, we have
x^{2}y^{2}=1_{H}
; and by the hypothesis that
x^{2}=1_{H}
or
y^{2}=1_{H}
, this entails
x^{2}=y^{2}=1_{H}
.
Consequently,

\{1_{H},x\}\{1_{H},xy\}\{1_{H},y\}=\{1_{H},x,y,xy,x^{2}y,xy^{2},x^{2}y^{2}\}=\{1_{H},x,y,xy\}=\{1_{H},x\}\{1_{H},y\}.

It follows, by applying
f
to the last display (and omitting some routine details), that

ab\neq g(xy)\in\{1_{K},a\}\{1_{K},g(xy)\}\{1_{K},b\}=\{1_{K},a\}\{1_{K},b\}=\{1_{K},a,b,ab\}.

(16)

By the injectivity of
g
, we gather from
Eq. (
16
) that
xy\in\{1_{H},x,y\}
, which, by the cancellativity of
H
,
yields either
xy=1_{H}
, or
x=1_{H}
, or
y=1_{H}
. However, if
x=1_{H}
or
y=1_{H}
, then
g(xy)=ab
, which is absurd.
So, the only possibility is that
xy=1_{H}
, and since
x^{2}=1_{H}
(as noted above), we find that

y=\allowbreak x^{2}y=x(xy)=x1_{H}=x
.
Accordingly, we are guaranteed by Proposition
4.3
that

g(xy)=g(x^{2})=g(x)^{2}=g(x)g(y)=ab,

which is again a contradiction and completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
H
and
K
be cancellative monoids, and let
g
be the pullback of an isomorphism
f
from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
. If
x,y\in H
are torsion elements, then
g(xy)=g(x)g(y)
.

Proof.

Suppose for a contradiction that
g(xy)\neq g(x)g(y)
. Then, by Lemmas
4.5
and
4.6
,

\text{(i) }x^{2}y^{2}=1_{H}\quad\text{and}\quad\text{(ii) }x^{2}\neq 1_{H}\neq y^{2}.

(17)

It follows that
x^{4}y^{2}=x^{2}(x^{2}y^{2})=x^{2}\neq 1_{H}
. Thus, we infer from Lemma
4.5
and Proposition
4.3
that

g(x^{2}y)=g(x^{2})g(y)=g(x)^{2}g(y)\neq g(x)g(xy),

(18)

where the inequality at the far right stems from the cancellativity of
K
and the assumption that
g(xy)\neq g(x)g(y)
.
Then, another call to Lemma
4.5
, together with Eqs. (
17
)(i) and (
18
), yields

x^{2}y^{2}=1_{H}=x^{2}(xy)^{2}=x^{3}yxy,

and hence
xyx=y
(by the cancellativity of
H
). Accordingly, we find that

\{1_{H},xy\}\{1_{H},x\}=\{1_{H},xy,x,xyx\}=\{1_{H},x,y,xy\}=\{1_{H},x\}\{1_{H},y\},

which, by taking the image of the left-most and right-most sides under
f
, leads to

g(x)g(y)\neq g(xy)\in\{1_{K},g(xy)\}\{1_{K},g(x)\}=\{1_{K},g(x)\}\{1_{K},g(y)\}=\{1_{K},g(x),g(y),g(x)g(y)\}.

(19)

Now,
n:={\rm ord}_{H}(x)
is a positive integer, since
x
is a torsion element in
H
. Similarly as in the last part of the proof of Lemma
4.6
, it is then straightforward from Eqs. (
17
)(ii) and (
19
) that
xy=1_{H}
, and hence
y=x^{-1}=x^{n-1}
. As a result, we conclude from Proposition
4.3
that

g(xy)=g(x^{n})=g(x)^{n}=g(x)g(x)^{n-1}=g(x)g(x^{n-1})=g(x)g(y),

which is however a contradiction and completes the proof.
∎

5.
Main result

We are now in a position to prove the main result of the paper (Theorem
5.1
) and thereby
show that Question
1.2
has a positive answer for the class of torsion groups (Corollary
5.2
).

Theorem 5.1
.

If
H
and
K
are cancellative monoids and one of them is torsion, then
H
and
K
are both groups and the pullback of an isomorphism from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
is an isomorphism from
H
to
K
.

Proof.

Let
g
be the pullback of an isomorphism

f\colon\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
.
By Proposition
4.1
and the bijectivity of
g
(Corollary
3.3
),
H
is torsion if and only if
K
is. On the other hand, any cancellative torsion monoid is a group. Therefore,
H
and
K
are both torsion groups, and it follows from
Proposition
4.7
that
g(xy)=g(x)g(y)
for all
x,y\in H
. That is,
g
is in fact an isomorphism from
H
to
K
.
∎

The following corollary is now an obvious consequence of Theorem
5.1
.

Corollary 5.2
.

If
H
and
K
are groups, one of which is torsion, and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
, then
H
is isomorphic to
K
.

It remains open whether
Corollary
5.2
extends to arbitrary groups, while it is known from
[
24
]
that
Theorem
5.1
does not carry over to arbitrary cancellative monoids, even in the commutative setting.
