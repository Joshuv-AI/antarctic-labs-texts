An open bone marrow megakaryocyte dataset for automated morphologic studies
Authors: Linghao Zhuang, Ying Zhang, Xingyue Zhao, Haoyu Zhao, Peiqi Li, Zhiping Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecise classification of megakaryocyte subtypes in bone marrow examination is crucial for the diagnosis and research of various hematological disorders, including Myelodysplastic Syndromes (MDS) and other platelet-production related diseases. While deep learning (DL) has demonstrated remarkable success in medical image classification, its application to megakaryocyte classification has been hindered by the scarcity of high-quality, openly licensed datasets. Therefore, we present MK-11, a dataset comprising 7,204 Wright-Giemsa stained single-cell images across 11 clinically relevant megakaryocyte subtypes. All images were annotated by two experienced hematopathologists with consensus review to ensure annotation quality, following standardized diagnostic criteria. Several state-of-the-art neural networks, including convolutional and transformer-based models, were evaluated on this benchmark, establishing strong baseline performance for megakaryocyte classification. To ensure reproducibility, we provide standardized five-fold cross-validation partitions along with all original images, annotations, partitioning schemes, and evaluation scripts under open licenses. In conclusion, this work presents the first public megakaryocyte subtype classification dataset for automatic morphological assessment development and evaluation, serving as a benchmark for future research.Subject terms: Super-resolution microscopy, Optical imaging

Background & Summary

Bone marrow cell morphological assessment remains the reference standard for diagnosing Myelodysplastic Syndromes (MDS) and other clonal hematopoietic disorders1. In clinical practice, this process begins with bone marrow aspiration through posterior superior iliac spine puncture, followed by Wright-Giemsa staining and microscopic evaluation by pathologists2. Taking MDS as a representative example, the assessment requires counting at least 500 nucleated cells with particular attention to megakaryocyte morphology, as approximately 89% of newly diagnosed cases exhibit megakaryocyte dysplasia3,4. According to the 2022 International Working Group on Morphology standards, a megakaryocyte abnormality proportion of 10% or more constitutes a significant criterion for confirming MDS1,4. The clinical significance of megakaryocyte evaluation extends beyond MDS diagnosis: different megakaryocyte subtypes are closely associated with various hematological disorders, disease progression and prognosis. For instance, elevated proportions of primitive and immature megakaryocytes often indicate increased risk of transformation to acute myeloid leukemia, while micromegakaryocytes and naked-nucleus megakaryocytes are highly specific indicators of MDS associated with poor prognosis5.

However, current manual assessment faces several challenges: it is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and highly subjective, with limited inter-observer consistency and significant diagnostic variability between hospitals and physicians, severely impacting treatment decisions. While computer-aided diagnostic systems could potentially address these issues, the development of such systems has been hindered by the extreme scarcity of open, standardized, and well-annotated bone marrow image resources, particularly for megakaryocytes, which represent only approximately 0.05% of nucleated marrow cells6. The establishment of automated recognition systems could standardize this process, not only reducing clinical workload but also significantly improving diagnostic accuracy and consistency, ultimately benefiting patient care through more reliable prognosis and treatment decisions.

The critical need for automated megakaryocyte analysis is currently unmet by existing public datasets, which either lack megakaryocyte samples entirely or provide insufficient annotation detail for subtype classification. Several resources have advanced automated morphological research on other blood cell types, yet none adequately addresses megakaryocyte classification. For instance, BloodMNIST7 (17,092 peripheral blood white blood cells, 8 categories) and Raabin-WBC8 (40,000 white blood cells, 8 categories) focus exclusively on circulating leukocytes, containing no bone marrow cells. The TCIA Bone marrow-Cytomorphology repository9, despite providing whole slide images from 945 patients, contains very few megakaryocytes and lacks single-cell annotations necessary for subtype classification research. A smaller dataset on Roboflow10 (1,928 masks) primarily addresses megakaryocyte segmentation rather than subtype differentiation. These existing resources present two critical limitations: (i) extremely few or completely absent megakaryocyte samples; (ii) lack of subtype annotations, making them inadequate for supporting fine-grained phenotypic recognition in hematological disorders. Based on this classification scheme, we constructed MK-11, the first publicly available image resource with annotated megakaryocyte subtypes. The dataset comprises 7,204 single-cell images cropped from Wright-Giemsa-stained bone marrow smears of 70 patients (Fig. 1). Two board-certified hematopathologists independently performed the initial annotations, with discrepancies in 4.7% of cases resolved through consensus review by a senior expert. The resulting label distribution exhibits a natural long-tail pattern reflecting clinical observations. To ensure reproducibility and facilitate fair comparisons, we provide standardized five-fold cross-validation splits along with reference implementations and baseline results using five common deep learning architectures — ConvNeXt12, EfficientNet13, ResNet14, VGG15, and ViT16. These baseline evaluations establish performance benchmarks and highlight potential directions for future research.

Methods

The construction of the MK-11 dataset followed a rigorous four-stage process: data collection and preprocessing, data filtering, expert annotation and data organization (Fig. 2). The key steps of each stage are detailed below.

Overview of the MK-11 dataset construction workflow. The four-stage process includes: (a) data collection and preprocessing from bone marrow specimens, (b) stringent quality filtering of whole slide images, (c) expert annotation and single-cell cropping by certified hematopathologists, and (d) standardized data organization with five-fold cross-validation for baseline model training and evaluation.

Data collection and preprocessing

We collected bone marrow aspiration specimens from 70 patients clinically diagnosed with MDS, ITP, acute megakaryoblastic leukemia, and healthy donors according to WHO-2022 criteria17. Following standard operating procedures, bone marrow particulates were smeared onto high-quality 45 mm × 23 mm glass slides within 30 minutes of collection. The slides were air-dried at room temperature, fixed with methanol, and stained using a Wright-Giemsa staining system according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. The age range was 14–86 years (mean ± SD: 52.89 ± 17.15; median 56.50, IQR 44.00–65.75). The sex distribution was 33 female (47.1%) and 37 male (52.9%).

All smear specimens were digitized using a KF-P BL05 panoramic digital pathology scanner (Ningbo Kfbio Technology Co., Ltd.) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 40 × /0.75 NA objective and a high-precision brightfield linear array sensor. The scanning resolution achieved 0.253 μm per pixel, with images saved as.kfb format files at 24-bit RGB color depth. Each digitized bone marrow smear (45 mm × 23 mm) generated an ultra-high-resolution image of approximately 180,000 × 93,184 pixels, with a file size of about 4.05 GiB and an average scanning time of 8 minutes and 22 seconds. To ensure consistent image quality, we performed strict color calibration using the manufacturer’s standard white board and employed the built-in dynamic refocusing module (6 focus points per square millimeter) for automatic focusing, guaranteeing imaging clarity across the entire field of view.

Following digital slide acquisition, an experienced hematopathology researcher examined the whole slide images at native 40 × resolution to precisely locate and mark regions containing megakaryocytes. These target regions were then exported in lossless PNG format, preserving the original image characteristics for subsequent expert annotation and analysis, as shown in Fig. 2(a).

Data filtering

To ensure dataset quality, we implemented a stringent quality control process for the digital microscopy images prior to expert annotation. The process employed a multi-stage screening strategy evaluating key technical and biological indicators, including image quality, staining effects, and specimen integrity, with detailed criteria provided in Table 3. From an initial collection of 6,281 microscopy images scanned at 40 × magnification, comprehensive quality control screening identified and removed 217 images that failed to meet one or more quality metrics. The remaining 6,064 high-quality images provided the foundation for extracting 7,204 megakaryocyte patches for subsequent annotation.

Expert annotation

The annotation process was conducted by two board-certified hematopathologists (J.Z.P. and Z.Y.) using the LabelImg open-source annotation tool, which supports precise annotation and classification of high-resolution pathological images. Each expert independently examined images at 40 × magnification, delineated individual megakaryocytes, and assigned labels according to an 11-category classification system based on the International Working Group on Morphology (IWGM) standards. For subsequent crop generation, we expanded the tight bounding box by a 10–20 px margin to preserve local context, and all crops retained their native physical resolution without resampling.

To minimize subjective bias, annotations strictly followed a “one cell, one label” principle, with the two experts working independently. The system automatically identified cases where the experts’ classifications differed, revealing discrepancies in 4.7% of cells. These controversial cases underwent consensus review chaired by a senior hematopathology expert to establish definitive labels.

To quantitatively assess inter-observer reliability prior to consensus, we calculated Cohen’s κ coefficient between the two annotators’ independent labels. The overall Cohen’s κ was 0.83, indicating almost perfect agreement according to the Landis and Koch interpretation18. Class-wise κ values ranged from 0.72 to 0.89, suggesting consistently high labeling consistency across the 11 megakaryocyte subtypes. This objective metric provides strong evidence of annotation quality and reduces concerns regarding label noise.

This rigorous multi-stage annotation process yielded 7,204 high-quality megakaryocyte images with verified classification labels, forming the official MK-11 dataset.

Data organization

To ensure reproducibility and enable comprehensive evaluation of deep learning models, we organized the MK-11 dataset using a standardized five-fold cross-validation scheme19. The data processing workflow began with a global shuffle of the entire image collection using a fixed random seed to ensure controllable randomization. Subsequently, each megakaryocyte subtype was evenly distributed across five non-overlapping subsets while maintaining class balance, forming the basis for five-fold validation.

Importantly, each patient contributes exactly one whole-slide image (WSI), and all cropped single-cell images derived from the same WSI are kept within the same fold. This ensures that no images from the same patient appear in both training and test sets simultaneously. In other words, the split is effectively at the patient level rather than purely at the image level, thereby minimizing the risk of data leakage that could arise from intra-patient similarity.

For each validation round k (k ∈ [0,4]), the kth fold served as the independent test set, while the remaining four folds were combined and further stratified into training and validation sets with an 8:2 ratio, maintaining class distribution. This two-stage stratified sampling strategy ensures that: (i) all images from a single patient remain in one fold only, (ii) class distributions remain consistent across all data splits, and (iii) no data leakage occurs between training and evaluation sets within each experimental round.

The dataset is organized following standard deep learning conventions, with each cross-validation round containing separate train, val and test directories. Images are arranged in an ImageNet-style layout, organized by class labels in corresponding folders. We provide a Python script that can either reproduce our standard partitions using the original random seed or generate custom splits based on specific research requirements. In addition, we release a machine-readable metadata.csv that records, for each crop, the anonymized patient ID, WSI ID, subtype label, fold/split assignment, and magnification. Baseline model performance using this standardized organization is presented in the “Technical Validation” section. We believe this patient-level splitting strategy provides a fairer evaluation protocol and enhances the robustness of reported performance.

Technical Validation

To rigorously validate the dataset and evaluate its utility for deep learning applications, we benchmarked five mainstream architectures — ConvNeXt12, EfficientNet13, ResNet14, VGG15, and ViT16 — using standardized five-fold cross-validation. All images underwent uniform preprocessing: resizing to 224 × 224 pixels using bicubic interpolation and normalization according to ImageNet specifications.

To enhance model generalization and simulate clinical variability, we implemented comprehensive data augmentation including: AutoAugment policy rand-m9-mstd0.5-inc1 for optical and geometric transformations, Random Erasing (probability = 0.25) to simulate occlusions, and label smoothing (ε = 0.1) to address long-tail distribution challenges. Random cropping was applied during training to increase sample diversity, while center cropping was used during validation for consistent evaluation.

The experimental results revealed an average accuracy of approximately 74% even with optimal configurations, reflecting the task’s inherent challenges due to the long-tail distribution and subtle morphological differences between subtypes. The best-performing model misclassified approximately 1,800 of 7,204 images across all validation rounds.

To analyze classification errors and further validate annotation quality, we conducted a detailed review of 200 stratified randomly sampled misclassified cases by senior hematopathologists who were not involved in the initial annotation. Approximately 93% of misclassifications were attributed to two factors: boundary cases with similar morphological features along the cell development spectrum, and highly similar subtypes. Importantly, the review confirmed the accuracy of original annotations, validating the dataset’s reliability.

Given the complexity of megakaryocyte morphology and the significant long-tail distribution of our dataset, future research directions could focus on: (i) developing specialized techniques for handling class imbalance in fine-grained visual classification, (ii) exploring few-shot learning approaches for rare subtypes with limited samples, and (iii) investigating more sophisticated feature extraction methods for distinguishing subtle morphological differences between similar subtypes. These advances would enhance model robustness and practical utility in real-world clinical scenarios, where rare subtypes often carry significant diagnostic importance.
