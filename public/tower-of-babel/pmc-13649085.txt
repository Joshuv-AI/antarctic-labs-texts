Report of Adverse Events Associated With Cutaneous Procedures: Update From the CAPER Registry
Authors: Sabrina S. Smith, Michael D. Yi, Elizabeth Zhang, Joycie Chang, Thanvi Gullapalli, Doroteja Dragovic, Vanessa J. Lazaro‐Camp, Ashish C. Bhatia, Arisa Ortiz, Murad Alam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo systematically evaluate submissions to the CAPER database by analyzing procedural context, characteristics of reported adverse events (AEs), and provider involvement in order to identify patterns with potential implications for patient safety.MethodsA retrospective review of all reports submitted to the CAPER database from March 2021 to August 2025 was conducted, categorizing cases by procedure type, reported AE, and provider involved.ResultsIn total, 199 reports describing 298 AEs across 35 various cutaneous procedures were submitted to the CAPER registry from March 2021 to August 2025. Dermatologists performed 62% of reported procedures, while non‐dermatologist physicians, non‐physicians, and unknown providers accounted for 38%. Filler injections, ablative laser resurfacing, and non‐ablative laser treatments were the most frequently reported procedures. Although dermatologists performed most procedures represented in the registry, 51% of reported AEs were associated with procedures performed by non‐dermatology providers. Erythema and scarring were among the most frequently reported AEs, while rare but serious events, including vascular compromise and necrosis, were reported following injectable and energy‐based procedures performed by a variety of provider types, including non‐physicians.ConclusionsThe CAPER registry provides valuable insight into AEs associated with cutaneous procedures and may help identify emerging safety concerns related to new devices or techniques. Broader engagement with AE reporting systems may inform best practices and improve patient safety, particularly as cutaneous procedures increasingly occur outside traditional clinical settings.

Introduction

Adverse event (AE) registries address procedural safety needs given the limitations of clinical trials and case series, such as detection of rare, delayed, or unexpected AEs. Registries provide post‐market surveillance, signal generation, and hypothesis formation for AEs associated with new or evolving devices or techniques, off‐label use, or combination procedures that may not be captured elsewhere. Registries are of great use in medicine, as rapid innovation, a predominance of outpatient procedures, and operator variability may lead to unintended outcomes. One such registry is the FDA Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) database, which has been used to characterize medical device‐related AEs such as those involving cosmetic injectable soft‐tissue fillers and events related to laser and energy‐based devices [1, 2].

The Cutaneous Procedures Adverse Events Reporting (CAPER) registry (https://caper.net/) serves as a nationwide, voluntary database that monitors AEs specific to dermatology [3, 4, 5]. Developed by a steering committee from the American Society for Dermatologic Surgery Association (ASDSA) and Northwestern University, CAPER collects outcomes from surgical, laser, and cosmetic treatments performed by dermatologists, other physicians, and non‐physician clinicians. The CAPER registry was initially only promoted to members of the ASDSA but has since expanded to accept reports of dermatologic AEs from all relevant providers. We sought to systematically evaluate submissions to the CAPER database by examining procedural context, reported AE characteristics, and provider involvement to identify patterns with potential patient safety implications.

Methods

AE reports submitted to the CAPER database include procedural details (including indication, device or product used, treatment parameters, and who performed the procedure) and AE description (including type, severity, and duration of the AE, timeline from initial detection, response offered for the AE, course of action, and outcome) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1).

We conducted a retrospective review of all reports submitted to the CAPER database from March 2021 to August 2025. Reports were reviewed and categorized according to the type of procedure performed, the type of reported AE, and the type of provider involved in the procedure.

Results

From March 2021 to August 2025, 199 reports itemizing 298 AEs pertaining to 35 different types of cutaneous procedures were received (Tables 1 and 2). Of all cases reported, 62% of procedures were performed by dermatologists and 38% by non‐dermatologist physicians, non‐physicians, and unknown providers. Filler injections, ablative laser resurfacing treatments, and non‐ablative laser treatments were the most commonly reported procedures at 18%, 11.5%, and 6%, respectively. Most cases of these three types were performed by dermatologists (Table 1).

Adverse events reported by dermatologists versus non‐dermatologists.

Although dermatologists performed most procedures reported in the CAPER registry, a substantial number of reports were associated with procedures performed by non‐dermatologists (Table 2). The most frequently reported AEs were erythema and scarring. Of the reported cases of erythema and scarring in the registry, 63% and 77%, respectively, were associated with procedures performed by non‐dermatology providers (Table 2). The most concerning AEs reported included vascular compromise and necrosis, although the frequency of these was much lower. Cases that resulted in vascular compromise included calcium hydroxyapatite and hyaluronic acid filler injections of the cheek, a non‐FDA approved pharmaceutical injection containing hyaluronic acid, perioral and lip hyaluronic acid filler injections, nasolabial fold hyaluronic acid filler injections, and temple hyaluronic acid filler injections. In all of these cases, the vascular compromise resolved spontaneously or with hyaluronidase treatment. Cases that resulted in necrosis included partial graft failure on the scalp with necrotic skin, necrosis of the fascia and muscle of the forearm following skin cancer excision, slight sloughing of the mucosal lip after hyaluronic acid filler injection, open ulcerations with frank impetiginization following plasma resurfacing, ulceration of the shin and calf following sclerotherapy for leg veins, complete graft failure and ulceration on the calf following Mohs surgery, and severe burns and skin sloughing following intense pulsed light (IPL) treatment. These cases resolved with treatments such as skin substitutes, biologic dressings, antibiotics, or hyaluronidase administration.

Certain procedures, including “unspecified injections,” ink extractions, and IPL, were more commonly associated with concerning AEs. The injections involved unknown fillers, an “antiox facial solution” intended only for topical use, and a polynucleotide (PN) injectable derived from marine DNA marketed for periorbital rejuvenation. The unspecified injections were administered by either unidentified providers or non‐physicians in med‐spas and associated with erythema, nodules, and induration. The ink extraction procedures were performed by tattoo artists using a non‐laser removal method (Tatt2Away) and were associated with permanent scarring (Supporting Information S2: Table 1). Cases associated with light treatment, for example, IPL, were more frequently performed by non‐physicians, which was not unexpected as IPL treatments are typically performed in med‐spas, and improper treatment often causes striping, burning, or blistering [6, 7].

Discussion

Given the very large number of cutaneous procedures performed per year in the United States, the modest number of reports in the CAPER database reflects the number of events submitted to this voluntary reporting system and does not allow estimation of the true incidence of AEs. Notwithstanding the voluntary nature of the reporting system, the presented data also highlight several concerning events which appear to arise in nontraditional clinical environments, such as med‐spas or settings involving non‐physician providers. While most complications were mild and self‐remitting, reports of serious AEs such as vascular compromise and necrosis are particularly concerning and may represent sentinel events that underestimate their true prevalence.

Registries such as CAPER are useful tools for tracking events outside of the expected clinical course, including rare or unusual complications and delayed AEs. Red‐flag events may result in severe, progressive, or prolonged adverse outcomes; functional or cosmetic morbidity; or infectious, vascular, neurologic, or systemic involvement. Timely submission after event recognition is imperative so that safety concerns are recognized and addressed promptly. Ongoing surveillance through structured reporting systems is therefore essential to identify emerging safety patterns and inform targeted quality improvement efforts.

One limitation of the CAPER database is the disproportionately high reporting from dermatologists, who may be more aware of the registry given that it has been co‐managed by and was originally promoted to the ASDSA, a professional society representing dermatologic surgeons. Although the registry has since expanded to accept dermatologic AE reports from all relevant providers, this may still introduce reporting bias. AEs associated with other provider groups are likely underreported in the CAPER registry [4, 5]. Additionally, the registry does not provide denominator data for the total number of procedures performed by each provider type, making comparisons of complication frequency unreliable.

Broader awareness of the CAPER registry may help mitigate this bias and yield more complete data that would help further improve patient safety. Registry reporting should be emphasized as non‐punitive, but rather quality improvement focused, to maximize reporting practices across provider groups. Future efforts may focus on integration into routine clinical and institutional workflows to encourage broad participation and data completeness.

Conclusion

The CAPER registry is useful as reports of AEs associated with new procedures or devices may lead to prompt attention by physicians, manufacturers, and regulators to address underlying safety concerns. Reports to the CAPER registry reveal that AEs following cutaneous procedures remain an important patient safety issue, particularly for procedures performed in nontraditional clinical settings. Finally, broader engagement with AE registries like CAPER may guide best practices and enhance patient safety as cutaneous procedures continue to evolve.
