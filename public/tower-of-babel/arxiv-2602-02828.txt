A Single Revision Step Improves Token-Efficient LLM Reasoning
Yingchuan Zhang, Terry Ma, Wenxuan Zhong, Ping Ma

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) achieve higher accuracy on challenging reasoning tasks by scaling test-time compute through multiple trajectory sampling. However, standard aggregation methods like majority voting or individual confidence-based filtering face a fundamental "blind spot": they evaluate each trace in isolation. As problems scale in difficulty, models often generate hallucinated paths that exhibit misleadingly high confidence, causing the true solution to be suppressed by a narrow margin in traditional voting. We ask: can we enable traces to "peer-review" each other to resolve these near-miss errors? We introduce Packet-Conditioned Revision (PACER) , a training-free, inference-only framework that enables reasoning traces to revise their conclusions through a structured coordination step. After a preliminary screening of generated traces, PACER constructs a compact consensus packet containing (i) unique candidate answers, (ii) their aggregated confidence scores, and (iii) representative reasoning summaries for each candidate answer. Individual traces then perform a targeted self-review conditioned on this packet, allowing them to identify specific logical junctions where they diverged from the broader consensus and pivot if their original reasoning is found to be flawed. Final predictions are obtained via confidence-weighted voting over these revised trajectories. On challenging competitive math benchmarks such as AIME and BRUMO, PACER matches or exceeds the accuracy of 256-sample majority voting, significantly outperforming raw ensemble baselines by transforming simple consensus into a collaborative logical refinement process.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) can solve substantially harder problems when we allocate more computation at inference time.
A particularly effective family of techniques samples multiple reasoning traces (a.k.a. chains-of-thought or solution trajectories) and aggregates their final answers.
Majority voting (MV), also called self-consistency, instantiates this idea by generating many independent traces and returning the most frequent answer
(
19
)
.
This simple ensemble often improves accuracy on challenging reasoning benchmarks, including math and logic tasks where single-sample decoding is brittle
(
20
)
.

The problem: accuracy gains are tied to expensive, redundant tokens.

The main drawback of MV is cost.
Generating
N
full traces costs roughly
N
times the tokens (and latency) of a single run, which quickly becomes prohibitive in real deployments.
Moreover, MV treats all traces as equally informative: a trace that is internally inconsistent, hallucinated, or off-distribution contributes one full vote just like a coherent trace.
This is especially wasteful because many errors manifest early in decoding (e.g., unstable token probabilities, oscillating intermediate values), yet MV pays for the entire completion before it can discard the result.

Why existing efficiency methods are still limited.

Recent work improves
token efficiency
by allocating compute adaptively rather than uniformly.
DeepConf
(
6
)
monitors token-level uncertainty during decoding and early-stops trajectories that appear unreliable, substantially reducing wasted generation.
Adaptive stopping rules based on answer-frequency estimates provide complementary routes to efficiency
(
7
)
.
However, these methods largely preserve a key structural assumption of self-consistency:
traces are generated and evaluated in isolation.

Once a trace commits to an answer, that trace does not get to reconsider after observing what other traces concluded.
This independence is especially problematic in hard math problems where a model can be ’confidently wrong,’ generating high-confidence hallucinations that pass through existing filters like DeepConf but could be easily identified when contrasted with alternative logical steps.
In practice, ensembles frequently contain (i) a plurality of mutually consistent but wrong answers supported by similar flawed reasoning patterns, and (ii) a smaller set of correct answers supported by a qualitatively different rationale.
If traces cannot condition on set-level evidence, the system must rely on the raw confidence signal alone, which can be fragile under tight token budgets.

A coordination gap at test time.

There is thus a gap between two extremes:
(1)
Self-consistency/MV
aggregates many full traces and benefits from diversity, but pays a large token cost
(
19
)
;
(2)
Confidence-guided early stopping
reduces cost, but produces a smaller, screened set of traces that are still treated as independent, leaving potential accuracy improvements unrealized
(
6
)
.
A natural response is to let traces interact through refinement or debate.
But iterative refinement methods typically re-check a
single
trace without access to peer evidence (e.g., self-refinement) or require multiple long rounds of interaction (e.g., debate), increasing latency and tokens
(
13
;
18
;
5
;
9
)
.
This motivates a focused question:

Can we introduce a minimal, training-free coordination step that enables revision using peer evidence, while preserving the token savings of confidence-guided sampling?

Our approach: a single revision step with low-bandwidth peer evidence.

We propose
Packet-Conditioned Revision (PACER)
, a lightweight coordination layer that sits on top of token-efficient sampling (we instantiate it with DeepConf-Online
(
6
)
).
PACER operates in three stages:
(1) generate a pool of
completed, screened
traces under an inference budget;
(2) summarize the pool into a compact
consensus packet
that contains only low-bandwidth set-level evidence—top distinct answers, their support counts, and a short representative rationale per answer;
(3) prompt each trace to perform a brief self-review conditioned on this packet and optionally revise its final answer.
The final prediction is then obtained by confidence-weighted vote over the revised answers.
Intuitively, the packet provides just enough information for a trace to notice when its conclusion is weakly supported relative to alternatives, or when another answer is backed by a more coherent rationale—without exposing the model to the full (token-heavy) set of traces.

How PACER differs from prior work.

PACER is
not
another way to stop early; it is a post-hoc coordination step that can be appended to existing pipelines.
Compared to DeepConf
(
6
)
, PACER does not compete as a stopping rule; instead, it targets the ’residual errors’—traces that successfully ’fool’ the stopping criterion with high local confidence but collapse under global peer scrutiny. By introducing peer evidence, PACER enables these high-confidence but incorrect traces to self-correct.
Compared to self-consistency/MV
(
19
)
, PACER aims to recover ensemble-like robustness under a smaller effective pool by enabling
repair
of wrong traces rather than merely counting them.
Compared to self-refinement methods
(
13
;
18
)
, PACER’s novelty is that revision is driven by a structured summary of
peer
outcomes (counts + representative rationales), not only by introspection on one’s own trace.
Compared to multi-agent debate
(
5
;
9
)
, PACER uses a single short revision step (no multi-round interaction), keeping overhead small and predictable.

Contributions.

Our contributions are:

1.

Coordination primitive:
We formalize the
consensus packet
, a compact representation of an ensemble that exposes answer support counts and representative rationales, enabling set-level conditioning with overhead that depends on
N
(top answers) rather than the number of traces.

2.

Training-free single-round revision:
We introduce PACER, an inference-only procedure that plugs into confidence-guided sampling (e.g., DeepConf-Online) and performs a single short consensus-conditioned self-review per trace.

3.

Theory:
We provide a simple repair-vs-damage condition under which a revision step improves expected trace accuracy (Section
5
), clarifying when consensus conditioning is beneficial.

4.

Measurable gains under a fixed attempt budget:
On competitive math benchmarks (AIME 2024/2025, HMMT 2025, BRUMO 2025), PACER consistently improves the accuracy–token tradeoff, matching or exceeding MV@256 while using far fewer generated tokens. For example, on
HMMT 2025
, PACER with GPT-OSS improves over the strong DeepConf-Online baseline by
+10.0
absolute percentage points (28/30 vs 25/30) (Table
2
).

Related Work

We review prior work on (i)
parallel
test-time scaling via sampling-and-aggregation, (ii)
token-efficient
variants that reduce wasted inference compute, and (iii)
sequential
refinement/interaction methods. We then position PACER as a lightweight coordination step that bridges parallel scaling with a single round of revision, without training.

Parallel test-time scaling via sampling and aggregation.

A standard way to improve LLM reasoning at inference time is to sample multiple solution traces and aggregate them.
Self-consistency / majority voting (MV)
(
19
)
is a widely used instantiation: it reduces stochastic errors by returning the most frequent extracted answer across sampled chains of thought.
Related approaches include best-of-
N
selection with verifiers or reward models
(
4
)
, as well as search-based decoding procedures (e.g., tree search over intermediate thoughts) that expand multiple branches under a fixed model
(
22
)
.
These methods can deliver large accuracy gains, but their cost typically scales with the number (and length) of sampled traces.

Token-efficient parallel scaling and adaptive stopping.

A growing line of work targets the inefficiency of fixed-size ensembles by
adapting
either (a) how many samples to draw, or (b) how long to let each trace unfold.
Adaptive-consistency-style methods stop sampling once the leading answer appears sufficiently dominant
(
7
)
.
Early-stopping self-consistency (ESC) serializes sampling into windows and halts once the answer distribution concentrates, reducing the number of required samples
(
8
)
.
More recently, methods exploit
confidence/uncertainty signals
to decide whether to continue: DeepConf monitors token-level uncertainty and terminates low-quality trajectories early
(
6
)
;
CGES frames stopping as a Bayesian decision rule using scalar confidence signals
(
2
)
;
ES-CoT stops within a single chain by detecting convergence of intermediate “step answers”
(
14
)
;
and entropy-based signals have also been proposed for early stopping in reasoning traces
(
16
)
.
Complementary empirical studies analyze test-time scaling and compute-optimal inference trade-offs across model size and inference strategy
(
21
;
11
)
.

Limitation shared by these approaches:
even when they adaptively allocate compute, they still generate and evaluate traces largely
in isolation
, without allowing a trace to revise after seeing what the
set
of traces supports.

Set-level aggregation beyond voting.

Several works go beyond heuristic voting by making aggregation itself more expressive.
Universal Self-Consistency (USC) prompts an LLM to
select
the most consistent candidate among multiple samples, avoiding brittle answer-extraction heuristics
(
3
)
.
Sample Set Aggregator (SSA) trains a separate compact model to reason over multiple parallel samples and output a final answer
(
15
)
, improving aggregation at the cost of additional training.
Other directions add external evaluators/verifiers and scale their number or diversity (e.g., multi-agent verification)
(
10
)
.

PACER differs
in that it remains
training-free
and uses no additional verifier model; instead, it injects a minimal coordination signal (a compact consensus packet) and performs only a
single
revision step per trace before a final vote.

Sequential refinement, self-critique, and multi-agent interaction.

Sequential test-time scaling improves an initial output through iterative revision and feedback.
Self-Refine alternates between generating feedback and refining the answer, repeatedly
(
12
)
;
Reflexion uses verbal feedback stored in memory to improve subsequent trials in agentic settings
(
17
)
;
and multi-agent debate frameworks exchange arguments over multiple rounds to improve factuality and reasoning
(
5
;
9
)
.
These methods can be powerful but often incur multiple long rounds of generation, increasing latency and token cost.

Positioning PACER.

PACER occupies a middle ground between parallel ensembling and sequential refinement.
Like self-consistency and DeepConf-style screening, PACER starts from a pool of sampled traces; unlike them, PACER adds
one
low-token coordination round by summarizing the pool into a consensus packet (answers, counts, representative rationales) and letting each trace
revise
conditioned on that shared evidence.
Compared to self-refinement, PACER’s revision is
consensus-conditioned
(set-aware) rather than purely self-contained; compared to USC/SSA, PACER does not replace aggregation with a trained selector/aggregator, but instead improves the trace set
before
a simple majority vote.

3
Preliminaries and Problem Formulation

We study training-free test-time scaling for reasoning tasks under a fixed inference-token budget.
Let
x
be a prompt (e.g., a math problem) and let an LLM with parameters
\theta
generate a token sequence (a reasoning trace)
\tau=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{T})
.
We assume an
answer extractor

\textsc{Extract}(\tau)\mapsto a
that maps a completed trace to a discrete final answer
a
(e.g., by parsing the content inside
\boxed{}
).
We write
a=\textsc{Extract}(\tau)
and denote the ground-truth answer by
y^{*}
.

Inference-time sampling and aggregation.

A standard approach samples multiple traces
\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
independently and aggregates extracted answers
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
by majority vote:

\hat{a}_{\text{MV}}=\arg\max_{a}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}[a_{i}=a].

(1)

While effective, MV requires generating many full traces, so its token cost scales approximately linearly with
n
.

3.1
Token-Level Uncertainty Signals

During decoding, the model produces a next-token distribution
P_{\theta}(v\mid x,w_{<t})
at step
t
.
Following confidence-guided sampling methods
(
6
)
, we use token-level uncertainty as a proxy for trace instability.
Concretely, we define a top-
k
negative log-probability statistic

U_{t}\;=\;-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log P_{\theta}\!\left(v_{t}^{(j)}\mid x,w_{<t}\right),

(2)

where
v_{t}^{(j)}
is the
j
-th most likely token at step
t
.
Larger
U_{t}
indicates greater local uncertainty (less stable decoding).

To reduce noise, we use a windowed average with window size
W
:

\bar{U}_{t}\;=\;\frac{1}{W}\sum_{r=\max(1,t-W+1)}^{t}U_{r}.

(3)

3.2
A Reproducible DeepConf-Online Variant

DeepConf-style online early stopping terminates a trace once its uncertainty becomes unusually high for the current prompt
(
6
)
.
For reproducibility, we specify a concrete stopping rule based on the
prefix maximum
of windowed uncertainty.

Prefix instability and stability.

Define the
prefix instability
up to step
t
as

I_{t}(\tau)\;=\;\max_{1\leq r\leq t}\bar{U}_{r},

(4)

and define the corresponding
prefix stability
as

S_{t}(\tau)\;=\;-I_{t}(\tau).

(5)

By construction,
S_{t}(\tau)
is non-increasing in
t
(once a high-uncertainty region appears, the maximum cannot decrease).
For a completed trace
\tau
, we write
S(\tau)=S_{T}(\tau)
.

Warmup threshold estimation.

We first sample
N_{\text{init}}

full
warmup traces without early stopping:

\mathcal{T}_{\text{warm}}=\{\tau_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\text{init}}}
.
We compute
S(\tau_{j})
for each warmup trace and set a screening threshold as a tail percentile:

s\;=\;\textsc{Percentile}\Big(\{S(\tau_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N_{\text{init}}},\ 100-\eta\Big),

(6)

so that “Top-
\eta\%
” screening corresponds to keeping traces with
S(\tau)\geq s
.

Online early stopping.

For each subsequent sampling attempt, we decode token-by-token and maintain
S_{t}(\tau)
online.
We
early-stop and discard
the attempt at the first time
t
such that

S_{t}(\tau)<s.

(7)

Because
S_{t}
is non-increasing, once the trace falls below
s
it cannot recover, making this rule compute-efficient.

Budgets and terminology.

To avoid ambiguity between “attempts” and “completed traces,” we distinguish:

•

N_{\text{try}}
: the number of sampling
attempts
(some attempts may be early-stopped).

•

B
: the number of
completed traces
retained for downstream aggregation/coordination.

In our main setting, we fix
N_{\text{try}}
(e.g.,
256
total attempts including warmup) and let the number of completed online traces be data-dependent.

3.3
Stable Pool Construction and Answer Extraction

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\text{surv}}
be the set of
completed
online traces that were not early-stopped.
PACER coordinates over a stable pool of completed traces:

\mathcal{T}\;=\;\{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{warm}}\mid S(\tau)\geq s\}\;\cup\;\mathcal{T}_{\text{surv}}.

(8)

For each
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
we extract an answer
a=\textsc{Extract}(\tau)
and form pairs
(\tau,a)
.

Practical answer extraction.

In all experiments we prompt the model to place the final answer in
\boxed{}
and define
\textsc{Extract}(\tau)
as the substring inside the last occurrence of
\boxed{}
.
If parsing fails, we fall back to a simple heuristic (e.g., last numeric token span).
We report extraction-failure rates in the appendix when applicable.

Top-
N
Candidate Selection.
Given the stable pool
\mathcal{T}
, we aggregate extracted answers using confidence-weighted sums:
V(a)=\sum_{(\tau,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T},a^{\prime}=a}S(\tau)
. We then select the top
N
answers
\mathcal{A}_{\text{top}}=\{a^{(1)},\dots,a^{(N)}\}
with the highest
V(a)
as candidates for the subsequent coordination phase.

Representative Trace Selection.
For each candidate answer
a\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{top}}
, we identify a single
representative trace

\tau^{*}_{a}
from the stable pool that provides the most stable justification for that answer. We select the trace with the maximum prefix stability:

\tau^{*}_{a}\;=\;\arg\max_{\{\tau\in\mathcal{T}\mid\textsc{Extract}(\tau)=a\}}S(\tau).

(9)

The rationale (text sequence) of
\tau^{*}_{a}
is then extracted to form the qualitative evidence in the
consensus packet
for Phase II.

Confidence-Weighted Voting (CWV).

While standard MV (Eq.
1
) treats all traces as equally informative, we employ Confidence-Weighted Voting (CWV) to prioritize logically stable reasoning paths. Each trace’s contribution is weighted by its prefix stability
S(\tau)
, and the final prediction is the answer with the highest aggregate support
V(a)
:

\hat{a}_{\text{CWV}}(\mathcal{T})=\arg\max_{a}V(a).

(10)

In our experimental setup, the
Online
baseline is defined as applying CWV directly to the initial stable pool
\mathcal{T}
. In contrast, our proposed PACER method applies the same CWV rule to the reasoning traces
after
they have undergone the consensus-conditioned revision process described in Section
4
.

3.4
Token Accounting

We measure inference cost as the total number of generated tokens, including:
(i) warmup full traces,
(ii) online attempts (counting tokens generated before early stopping),
and, for PACER , (iii) revision tokens and (iv) any packet-construction tokens if summarization uses the model.
Formally,

Tokens

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{warm}}}|\tau|+\sum_{\text{attempt }i}|\tau_{i}^{\text{(attempt)}}|+\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}|\tau^{\text{(review)}}|

\displaystyle+\textsc{PacketTokens},

(11)

where
PacketTokens
represents the potential overhead of LLM-based summarization. In our implementation, the consensus packet is constructed via deterministic truncation of existing traces. Consequently,
\textsc{PacketTokens}=0
, meaning the coordination signal is generated with zero token overhead. The only additional cost arises from the short revision step (
\tau^{\text{(review)}}
).

4
Packet-Conditioned Revision
(PACER)

Consensus-Conditioned Revision (PACER) is a training-free coordination layer that augments token-efficient sampling with a single, low-token revision step.
PACER is designed to plug into confidence-guided inference (we instantiate it with a reproducible DeepConf-Online variant in Sec.
3.2
).
The key idea is to expose each completed trace to a compact summary of
set-level evidence
—which answers are most supported and what representative rationales look like—then allow a brief self-review that can correct errors without generating many additional full traces.

4.1
Setup and Trace Pool Construction

Given a prompt
x
, we first run DeepConf-Online with
N_{\text{try}}
total sampling attempts, including
N_{\text{init}}
warmup traces decoded to completion. The warmup traces are used to estimate a stability threshold
s
(Eq. (
6
)) and subsequent online attempts are early-stopped and discarded if their online stability
S_{t}(\tau)
drops below
s
(Eq. (
7
)).

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\text{warm}}
denote the set of warmup full traces and
\mathcal{T}_{\text{surv}}
denote the set of completed online traces that survive early stopping. PACER coordinates over a stable completed pool
\mathcal{T}
, which is constructed by retaining only those traces that meet the stability requirement:

\mathcal{T}\;=\;\{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{warm}}\mid S(\tau)\geq s\}\;\cup\;\mathcal{T}_{\text{surv}},

(12)

where
S(\tau)
is the completed-trace stability (Sec.
3.2
). For each
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
, we extract an answer
a=\textsc{Extract}(\tau)
. We write
B=|\mathcal{T}|
for the realized number of completed traces;
B
is data-dependent even when
N_{\text{try}}
is fixed.

Dual Filtering: Online Screening and Top-
N
Selection.
To ensure the coordination signal is both locally robust and globally representative, we implement a dual-filtering mechanism. Following the initial online screening that forms
\mathcal{T}
, we perform a second filtering step by selecting the Top-
N
candidate answers
\mathcal{A}_{\text{top}}
. For each unique answer
a
in the pool, we compute its confidence-weighted support
V(a)=\sum S(\tau_{i})
. We then filter the distribution to retain only the
N
answers with the highest weighted support:

\mathcal{A}_{\text{top}}=\{a^{(1)},\dots,a^{(N)}\}\text{ s.t. }V(a^{(1)})\geq\dots\geq V(a^{(N)}).

(13)

This two-stage approach ensures that the
consensus packet
constructed in the next step focuses only on the most promising logical directions identified by the ensemble.

4.2
Consensus Packet

The consensus packet is a bounded-length text summary of the pool
\mathcal{T}
that provides low-bandwidth peer evidence.
It consists of the Top-
N
distinct extracted answers, their support counts in the pool, and one representative compressed rationale per answer.

Answer support.

Following the definition in Sec.
3.3
, we calculate the confidence-weighted support for each answer:

V(a)\;=\;\sum_{(\tau_{i},a_{i})\in\mathcal{T}}S(\tau_{i})\cdot\mathbb{I}[a_{i}=a]
.
We then select the set
\mathcal{A}_{\text{top}}
consisting of the
N
answers with the largest
V(a)
.

Representative trace selection.

For each
a\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{top}}
, we identify the representative trace
\tau_{a}
as the most stable justification (Eq. (
9
)).
This choice ensures that the rationale provided in the packet for each candidate reflects the highest-confidence reasoning available in the pool.

Rationale compression and bounded size.

To keep packet length independent of
B
, we compress each representative rationale to a fixed budget
L_{\text{sum}}
tokens.
In the main experiments we use deterministic truncation
\mathrm{summ}_{a}=\textsc{Trunc}(\tau_{a};L_{\text{sum}})
, and we ablate alternative compression schemes.
The consensus packet is then

\mathcal{P}\;=\;\big\{(a,\ V(a),\ R_{a})\big\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{top}}}.

(14)

Because
|\mathcal{P}|=N
and each summary is bounded, packet overhead scales as
O(N\cdot L_{\text{sum}})
rather than
O(B)
.

4.3
Single-Round Consensus-Conditioned Revision

Given a completed trace
(\tau_{i},a_{i})\in\mathcal{T}
, PACER performs a short review that conditions on the packet and outputs a revised answer
a_{i}^{\prime}
:

a_{i}^{\prime}\leftarrow\textsc{Review}(x,\tau_{i},a_{i},\mathcal{P};\ L_{\text{rev}})
.

We enforce a strict review budget
L_{\text{rev}}
tokens to keep the additional cost predictable.
The review prompt includes (i) the original problem, (ii) the trace’s original solution and extracted answer, and (iii) the consensus packet.
The model is instructed to either keep the answer if confident or revise it if peer evidence suggests an error.

Design rationale.

PACER uses a
single
revision step rather than multi-round interaction.
This choice keeps overhead small while enabling two error-correction mechanisms that are unavailable under independent sampling:
(i)
support awareness
: a trace can reconsider when its answer has low support relative to alternatives; and
(ii)
rationale contrast
: a trace can detect missing steps or contradictions by comparing its own reasoning against a representative rationale.

4.4
Aggregation

PACER produces the final prediction by performing a confidence-weighted vote over the revised answers
\{a_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{B}
.
Each revised answer is weighted by the prefix stability
S(\tau_{i})
of its original trace:

\hat{a}=\arg\max_{a}\sum_{i=1}^{B}S(\tau_{i})\cdot\mathbb{I}[a_{i}^{\prime}=a].

(15)

By weighting the revised answers with their initial stability scores, we ensure that the final decision prioritizes revisions made by traces that were originally robust, while allowing those same traces to update their conclusions based on the global peer evidence presented in the consensus packet.

4.5
Complexity and Token Cost

Compared to DeepConf-Online without revision, PACER adds:
(i) packet construction overhead, which is
O(N\cdot L_{\text{sum}})
tokens if summaries are produced by the model and near-zero if summaries are obtained by truncation; and
(ii) one review completion per trace with budget
L_{\text{rev}}
, for a total of at most
B\cdot L_{\text{rev}}
additional generated tokens.
Thus,PACER’s additional cost is bounded and typically small relative to generating hundreds of full traces in MV@256.

5
Theoretical Analysis

This section provides simple, checkable conditions under which a
single
consensus-conditioned revision step improves accuracy, and explains why this improvement can be token-efficient relative to generating additional full traces.

5.1
Revision as Repair vs. Damage

Consider a trace drawn from the stable pool
\mathcal{T}
(Eq. (
12
)) with extracted pre-review answer
A
and post-review answer
A^{\prime}
after applying
\textsc{Review}(\cdot)
conditioned on the consensus packet
\mathcal{P}
(Eq. (
14
)).
Let
y^{*}
be the ground-truth answer for prompt
x
and define the pre-review correctness probability

p\;=\;\Pr(A=y^{*}).

We summarize the effect of revision by:

•

Repair rate

\alpha=\Pr(A^{\prime}=y^{*}\mid A\neq y^{*})
,

•

Damage rate

\beta=\Pr(A^{\prime}\neq y^{*}\mid A=y^{*})
.

Assumption 1

(Stabilizing revision)
.

Revision is stabilizing if the prevalence-adjusted mass of repairs dominates damages:

(1-p)\alpha\;\geq\;p\beta.

(16)

Proposition 1

(Per-trace accuracy gain)
.

Under Assumption
1
,

\Pr(A^{\prime}=y^{*})\;\geq\;\Pr(A=y^{*}),

with strict inequality when (
16
) is strict.

Why this is useful.

Proposition
1
is intentionally minimal, but by itself can feel tautological.
Next we tie
\alpha,\beta
to
observable packet statistics
(support counts and margins), yielding predictions that are directly testable in ablations.

5.2
Consensus Margin and Revision Dynamics

Let
V(a)
be the confidence-weighted support of answer
a
in the stable pool
\mathcal{T}
(Eq. 2.9), and let

a^{(1)}\;=\;\arg\max_{a}V(a)

denote the leading answer in the pool.
Define the
confidence-weighted margin

\Delta\;=\;V(a^{(1)})-\max_{a\neq a^{(1)}}V(a),

(17)

which measures the concentration of ensemble evidence on its leader.

We consider repair and damage rates
conditioned on the ensemble evidence summarized by
\Delta
:

\alpha(\Delta)=\Pr(A^{\prime}=y^{*}\mid A\neq y^{*},\Delta)
,

\beta(\Delta)=\Pr(A^{\prime}\neq y^{*}\mid A=y^{*},\Delta)
.
Intuitively, larger margins indicate clearer peer evidence and should make revision more robust.

Assumption 2

(Margin-monotone consensus influence)
.

There exist nondecreasing
\underline{\alpha}(\Delta)
and nonincreasing
\overline{\beta}(\Delta)
such that for all
\Delta
,

\alpha(\Delta)\geq\underline{\alpha}(\Delta),\beta(\Delta)\leq\overline{\beta}(\Delta).

Moreover, for margins above a threshold
\Delta_{0}
, the ensemble evidence is sufficiently strong that

(1-p)\,\underline{\alpha}(\Delta)\;\geq\;p\,\overline{\beta}(\Delta)\quad\text{for all }\Delta\geq\Delta_{0}.

(18)

Assumption
2
is
empirically checkable
: we can estimate
\alpha(\Delta)
and
\beta(\Delta)
from flip statistics grouped by observed margins (Section
6
).

Corollary 1

(Margin-conditioned per-trace improvement)
.

Under Assumption
2
, for all
\Delta\geq\Delta_{0}
,

\Pr(A^{\prime}=y^{*}\mid\Delta)\;\geq\;\Pr(A=y^{*}\mid\Delta).

Interpretation.

Corollary
1
formalizes a key PACER intuition: revision is most reliably beneficial when the pool exhibits a clear leader (large
\Delta
), allowing the coordination process to effectively repair erroneous traces while minimizing damage to correct ones.

PACER’s final prediction is CWV over revised answers (Eq. (

exceeds
1/2
.Let
B=|\mathcal{T}|
be the number of completed traces in the pool and let

p^{\prime}(\Delta)\;=\;\Pr(A^{\prime}=y^{*}\mid\Delta).

For analysis, we adopt a standard conditional-independence approximation:
conditional on the ensemble evidence (summarized by the margin
\Delta

.

Assume conditional independence given
\Delta

p^{\prime}(\Delta)

5.4
Why One Revision Can Be Token-Efficient

We now relate accuracy gains to token cost.
Let
L
be the expected token length of a full trace, and let
L_{\text{rev}}\ll L
be the enforced review budget (Sec.
4.3
).
Suppose we can either:
(i) spend
\Delta T
tokens generating additional full traces for MV, or
(ii) spend
\Delta T
tokens performing reviews for PACER.

A key regime for test-time scaling is where MV exhibits diminishing returns as
B
grows (the self-consistency curve flattens), while a brief review can increase per-trace correctness by a roughly constant amount on instances with informative margins (Corollary
1
).

Corollary 2

(A simple value-of-information comparison)
.

Fix a margin
\Delta\geq\Delta_{0}
and suppose review increases per-trace correctness from
p(\Delta)
to
p^{\prime}(\Delta)=p(\Delta)+\delta(\Delta)
with
\delta(\Delta)>0
and
p^{\prime}(\Delta)>\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Under Proposition
2
, for a fixed pool size
B
, the exponent in (
19
) improves by

\displaystyle 2B\Big((p(\Delta)+\delta(\Delta)-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}-(p(\Delta)-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\;=\;2B\,\delta(\Delta)\,(2p(\Delta)-1+\delta(\Delta)).

Thus, when
L_{\text{rev}}\ll L
, spending tokens on a single review per trace can increase the majority-vote exponent more per token than adding a small number of extra full traces, especially in the diminishing-returns regime where increasing
B
yields only modest gains.

Practical implication.

Corollary
2
motivates PACER’s design choice: a bounded-cost revision step can be a better use of tokens than further increasing sample count once MV gains saturate.
In experiments, we instantiate this comparison by reporting accuracy vs. total generated tokens (Eq. (
11
)) and by measuring
\alpha(\Delta),\beta(\Delta)
and flip behavior as functions of
\Delta

Discussion.

While DeepConf effectively removes noise often matching MV, it hits a ceiling on "hard" errors where models converge on plausible distractors. PACER breaks this ceiling. By exposing survivors to divergent peer rationales, it prompts the model to switch to the correct path, yielding consistent gains (+2–3 problems) on difficult benchmarks. These results hold across architectures, suggesting Consensus-Conditioned Revision is a robust inference scaling mechanism.

Impact Statement

This work studies inference-time coordination mechanisms that improve LLM reasoning accuracy under a fixed token budget.
Such methods can reduce compute requirements for legitimate applications (e.g., education and scientific assistance), but improved reasoning capability may also increase the effectiveness of misuse in domains where strong reasoning is harmful.
PACER does not introduce new training data or model weights; it is a prompting and aggregation technique.
We encourage practitioners to pair more capable inference-time methods with appropriate safety mitigations.
