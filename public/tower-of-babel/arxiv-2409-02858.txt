Revisiting ILP Models for Exact Crossing Minimization in Storyline Drawings
Alexander Dobler, Michael Jünger, Paul J. Jünger, Julian Meffert, Petra Mutzel, Martin Nöllenburg

Abstract
Storyline drawings are a popular visualization of interactions of a set of characters over time, e.g., to show participants of scenes in a book or movie. Characters are represented as x x -monotone curves that converge vertically for interactions and diverge otherwise. Combinatorially, the task of computing storyline drawings reduces to finding a sequence of permutations of the character curves for the different time points, with the primary objective being crossing minimization of the induced character trajectories. In this paper, we revisit exact integer linear programming (ILP) approaches for this 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard problem. By enriching previous formulations with additional problem-specific insights and new heuristics, we obtain exact solutions for an extended new benchmark set of larger and more complex instances than had been used before. Our experiments show that our enriched formulations lead to better performing algorithms when compared to state-of-the–art modelling techniques. In particular, our best algorithms are on average 2.6–3.2 times faster than the state-of-the-art and succeed in solving complex instances that could not be solved before within the given time limit. Further, we show in an ablation study that our enrichment components contribute considerably to the performance of the new ILP formulation.

Storylines for the first Harry Potter movie. Interactions are shown as vertical gray bars.

Storyline drawings are a well-studied visualization style for complex event-based temporal interaction data and have been popularized by the xkcd comic “Movie Narrative Charts” in 2009
[
17
]
.
They show a set of characters, e.g., from the plot of a movie or book, and how they interact or co-occur in a sequence of events over time, e.g., by participating in the same scene or conversation of the evolving story.
A storyline drawing is a two-dimensional diagram, where the
x
-axis represents time and the
y
-dimension is used for the vertical grouping of characters according to their interaction sequence. The exact temporal distance of interactions is usually not depicted, only their order.
Each character is represented as an
x
-monotone curve, and interactions are represented by vertically grouping the curves of the participating characters at the
x
-coordinate corresponding to the interaction time.
Characters that are not participating in an interaction at any specific point in time are vertically separated from each other.

Figure

1
shows an example of a storyline drawing.
Due to their popularity and the intuitive data encoding, they are well suited for visual storytelling and have since been used as visual metaphors for representing a variety of different event-based data sets beyond the original book and movie plots
[
17
,
26
]
, e.g., for software projects
[
25
,
18
]
, newspaper articles
[
1
]
, political debates on social media
[
16
]
, visual summaries of meeting contents
[
21
]
, scientific collaborations
[
12
]
, sports commentary
[
32
]
, and gameplay data
[
31
]
.

There is one main degree of freedom when computing and optimizing storyline drawings: the (vertical) linear order and positioning of the characters at each discrete time steps.
The only hard constraint is that all characters participating in the same interaction must be consecutive as a group.
This degree of freedom can thus be used to minimize the number of crossings between character curves, their wiggles (i.e. the amount of vertical movement of character lines in the visualization), and any excessive white space in the diagram, which are the three major objectives that have been identified for computing storyline drawings
[
26
,
25
]
.
While the number of crossings is determined purely combinatorially by the sequence of character permutations, wiggles and white space depend on the actual
y
-coordinates assigned to each character curve at each point in time.

In this paper, our interest is the combinatorial crossing minimization problem.
It is the primary objective in practical storyline optimization pipelines
[
16
]
, where it forms the input to the subsequent steps of reducing wiggles and white space while maintaining the character order.
Additionally, crossing minimization is one of the most fundamental graph drawing problems
[
20
,
2
]
and it is well known that graph drawings with fewer crossings increase readability
[
19
]
.
Unfortunately, crossing minimization in storyline drawings is an
\mathsf{NP}
-hard problem
[
15
,
7
]
and hence practical approaches for storyline visualization usually resort to heuristics, even though they cannot guarantee optimal solutions.

We consider the crossing minimization problem from the opposite side and revisit exact integer linear programming (ILP) approaches
[
9
]
for computing provably optimal solutions. Such approaches often lead to practical exact algorithms.
Our goal is to improve on the runtime performance of such exact methods by enriching the models with both new problem-specific insights and better heuristics. Faster exact algorithms for crossing minimization in storyline drawings are practically relevant for two reasons: firstly, solving moderately sized instances to optimality within a few seconds provides a strictly better alternative to commonly used suboptimal heuristics, and secondly, knowing optimal solutions for a large set of representative benchmark instances (even if their computations take several minutes or up to a few hours) is a prerequisite for any thorough experimental study on the performance of non-exact crossing minimization heuristics and for generating crossing-minimum stimuli in user experiments.

Related Work.

Tanahashi and Ma
[
26
]
introduced storyline drawings as an information visualization problem, provided the first visual encoding model, and defined the above-mentioned optimization criteria (crossings, wiggles, white space).
They suggested a genetic algorithm to compute storyline drawings.
Ogawa and Ma
[
18
]
used a greedy algorithm to compute storylines to depict software evolution. Due to slow computation times of previous methods, Liu et al.
[
16
]
split the layout process into a pipeline of several subproblems ordered by importance, the first one being crossing minimization.
They solved the character line ordering by an iterated application of a constrained barycenter algorithm, a classic heuristic for multi-layer crossing minimization
[
23
]
.
Their results were obtained in less than a second and had fewer crossings than those computed by the genetic algorithm
[
26
]
, which took more than a day to compute on some of the same instances.
Tanahashi et al.
[
25
]
enhanced previous methods to take into account streaming data and apply a simple sequential left-to-right sorting heuristic. Recent practical works on storyline drawings focus on other aspects, such as an interactive editor
[
28
]
or a mixed-initiative system including a reinforcement learning AI component
[
27
]
; both these systems apply a two-layer crossing minimization heuristic
[
6
]
.

Several authors focused on the combinatorial crossing minimization problem and its complexity.
Kostitsyna et al.
[
15
]
observed that the
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness of the problem follows from a similar bipartite crossing minimization problem
[
7
]
and proved fixed-parameter tractability when the number of characters is bounded by a parameter
k
.
Gronemann et al.
[
9
]
were the first to model the problem as a special type of tree-constrained multi-layer crossing minimization problem. They implemented an exact branch-and-cut approach that exploits the equivalence of the quadratic unconstrained 0/1-optimization problem with the maximum cut problem in a graph.
They managed to solve many instances with up to 20 characters and 50 interactions optimally within a few seconds.
Van Dijk et al.
[
30
,
29
]
proposed block-crossing minimization in storyline drawings, which counts grid-like blocks of crossings rather than individual crossings.
They showed
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness and proposed greedy heuristics, a fixed-parameter tractable algorithm, and an approximation algorithm.
In a follow-up work, van Dijk et al.
[
30
]
implemented and experimentally evaluated an exact approach for the block crossing minimization problem using SAT solving.
A different variation of storylines was studied by Di Giacomo et al.
[
8
]
, who considered ubiquitous characters as
x
-monotone trees with multiple branches, enabling characters to participate in multiple simultaneous interactions; they solved the crossing minimization aspect using an adaptation of the previous SAT model
[
30
]
.
Dobler et al.
[
5
]
consider time interval storylines, where additionally to the order of characters, the order of time steps in so-called time-intervals can be permuted.

The problem is also similar to crossing minimization in layered graph drawing, which was introduced by Sugiyama et al.
[
23
]
. The problem is to draw a graph with its vertices on multiple parallel lines while minimizing crossings. A notable difference to storyline crossing minimization is that vertices can have arbitrary degree and that edges can span more than one layer. For a survey of algorithms and techniques in layered graph drawing, we refer to Healy and Nikolov
[
11
]
.

Contributions.

The contributions of this paper are the following:

•

We identify structural properties of storyline drawings and prove that there exist crossing-minimum drawings satisfying them, reducing the search space of feasible solutions.

•

We propose a new ILP formulation exploiting these structural insights in order to (i) significantly reduce the number of required constraints and (ii) apply symmetry breaking constraints to strengthen the ILP model.

•

We introduce several new heuristics that support the exact solver, either as initial heuristics to improve branch-and-bound pruning or for deriving integral solutions from fractional ones during the incremental ILP solving process.

•

We have compiled a new benchmark set of storyline instances, including those of earlier studies, as well as several challenging new ones.

•

We have conducted a detailed experimental evaluation of our new ILP model using the above benchmark set. We compare its ability to solve instances with state-of-the-art ILP models. Moreover, in an ablation study, we show that our further enhancements (e.g., adding symmetry breaking constraints and novel heuristics) contributes considerably to the performance of both the new and several state-of-the-art ILP formulations.

•

We show that our ILP models are able to solve previously unsolved instances from the literature and obtain a speedup of 2.6–3.2 compared to the state of the art.

Data sets, source code, evaluation, and a visualization software are available on
https://osf.io/3bua2/
.

Preliminaries

Permutations.

Given a set
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
, a permutation
\pi
is a linear order of its elements, or equivalently, a bijective mapping from
\{1,2,\dots,|X|\}
to
X
. For
x,x^{\prime}\in X
we write
x\prec_{\pi}x^{\prime}
if
x
comes before
x^{\prime}
in
\pi
.
For
Y\subseteq X
,
\pi[Y]
is the permutation
\pi
restricted to
Y
, formally, for
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
,
y\prec_{\pi[Y]}y^{\prime}
if and only if
y\prec_{\pi}y^{\prime}
. For two permutations
\pi,\phi
of two sets
X
and
Y
with
X\cap Y=\emptyset
, we denote by
\pi\star\phi
their concatenation. Given two permutations
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
of the same set
X
, the
inversions
between
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
is the number of pairs
x,x^{\prime}\in X
such that
\pi^{-1}(x)<\pi^{-1}(x^{\prime})
and
\pi^{\prime-1}(x)>\pi^{-1}(x^{\prime})
.

Next, we define the set of all characters active in the time interval

Problem output.

Solutions to storyline instances
(T,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{I},A)
consist of a sequence of
\ell
permutations
S=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{\ell})
such that
\pi_{i}
is a permutation of
\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})
for all
i=1,\ldots,\ell
satisfying the condition that the set of characters of each interaction
I\in\mathcal{I}(t_{i})

appears consecutively.
We call
S
a

or

.

The number of crossings
\text{cr}(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1})
between two consecutive permutations
\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{i+1}
is defined as the number of inversions of the two permutations
\pi_{i}[C]
and
\pi_{i+1}[C]
, where
C=\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})\cap\operatorname{AC}(t_{i+1})
. The number of crossings in a storyline solution is
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\text{cr}(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1})
. The problem addressed in this paper is the following.

Problem 1

(Storyline Problem)
.

Given a storyline instance
(T,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{I},A)
, find a storyline drawing
S
with the minimum number of crossings.

Standard Models for the Storyline Problem

The most natural ILP formulation to solve
Problem

1
has a quadratic objective function and is based on the linear ordering model, which uses binary variables in order to encode the linear ordering at each time step. The number of crossings between two subsequent time steps is then given by the number of inversions of the two permutations.

From now on, we assume that characters
c_{u},c_{v},c_{w}
are pairwise different, even if we write for example
c_{u},c_{v}\in C
for some set
C
of characters.

Quadratic Model (QDR)

For each time step
t_{i},i=1,2,\dots,\ell
and each tuple of characters
c_{u},c_{v}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})
we introduce a binary
ordering variable

x_{i,u,v}
which is equal to 1 if and only if
c_{u}\prec_{\pi_{i}}c_{v}
.
The quadratic model QDR is given as follows:

\displaystyle\min\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{c_{u},c_{v}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i},t_{i+1})}x_{i,u,v}x_{i+1,v,u}

(QDR)

\displaystyle x_{i,u,v}=1-x_{i,v,u}

\displaystyle\qquad\textrm{for all }i=1,\ldots,\ell;c_{u},c_{v}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})\textrm{ with }u<v

(EQ)

\displaystyle x_{i,u,v}+x_{i,v,w}+x_{i,w,u}\leq 2

\displaystyle\qquad\textrm{for all }i=1,\ldots,\ell;c_{u},c_{v},c_{w}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})

(LOP)

\displaystyle x_{i,u,w}=x_{i,v,w}

\displaystyle\qquad\textrm{for all }i=1,\ldots,\ell;I\in{\cal I}(t_{i});

(TREE)

\displaystyle\qquad c_{u},c_{v}\in\operatorname{{char}}(I),u<v;c_{w}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})\setminus\operatorname{{char}}(I)

\displaystyle x_{i,u,v}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\qquad\textrm{for all }i=1,\ldots,\ell;c_{u},c_{v}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i}),

(BIN)

The character curves for
c_{u}
and
c_{v}
cross between the two layers
t_{i}
and
t_{i+1}
if and only if
one of the terms
x_{i,u,v}x_{i+1,v,u}
and
x_{i,v,u}x_{i+1,u,v}
equals
1
.
The (
LOP
) and (
EQ
) constraints ensure transitivity of the set of characters
\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})
present at time step
t_{i}
and guarantee that they define a total order.
For all interactions
I\in{\cal I}(t_{i})
the (
TREE
) constraints ensure that characters from
I
appear consecutively at the respective time step
t_{i}
.

Linearized Model (LIN)

The standard linearisation of quadratic integer programs introduces additional variables
y_{i,u,v}
that substitute the quadratic terms
x_{i,u,v}x_{i+1,v,u}
for all
t_{i}
,
i=1,2,\dots,\ell-1
and each tuple of characters
c_{u},c_{v}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i},t_{i+1})
in the objective function. In order to link the new variables with the ordering variables, we introduce the following constraints:

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad y_{i,u,v}\geq x_{i,u,v}-x_{i+1,u,v}

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{for all }i=1,\dots,\ell;c_{u},c_{v}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i},t_{i+1})

(CR)

Obviously, the variable
y_{i,u,v}
is forced to
1
, if the character
c_{u}
is before
c_{v}
at time step
t_{i}
in the solution represented by the
y
-variables, and the order of both characters is reversed at time step
t_{i+1}
.
The linearised model (
LIN
) is given as follows.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\min

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{c_{u},c_{v}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i},t_{i+1})}y_{i,u,v}

(LIN)

\displaystyle\quad y_{i,u,v},x_{i,u,v}\text{ satisfy (\ref{BIN-Con}), (\ref{EQ-Con}), (\ref{LOP-Con}), (\ref{TREE-Con}), and (\ref{CR-Con})}

Max-Cut Model (CUT)

Gronemann et al.
[
9
]
have suggested a formulation based on the transformation of the problem into a quadratic unconstrained binary program with additional (TREE) constraints, which is then solved using a maximum cut approach. Here, we omit the detour via the quadratic binary program and directly provide the corresponding maximum cut formulation.
Starting with a feasible storyline drawing
\hat{S}=(\hat{\pi}_{1},\dots,\hat{\pi}_{\ell})
, we define the graph
G_{M}=(V_{M},E_{M})
:
The vertex set
V_{M}
is given by a vertex
v^{*}
and the union of the sets
V_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,\ell)
, where
V_{i}
has a vertex
c_{uv}^{i}
for each pair
c_{u},c_{v}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})
with
c_{u}\prec_{\hat{\pi}_{i}}c_{v}
.

We introduce an edge between the vertices
c_{uv}^{i}
and
c_{pq}^{i+1}
if the corresponding characters coincide.
In the case that
c_{u}=c_{p}
and
c_{v}=c_{q}
, the (type-1) edge
e=e_{uv}^{i}
gets a weight of
w_{e}=-1
, and in the case that
c_{u}=c_{q}
and
c_{v}=c_{p}
, the (type-2) edge
e=e_{uv}^{i}
gets a weight of
w_{e}=1
.
We define the constant
K
as the number of edges of type (2).
The intention is the following:
An edge of type (1) results in a crossing if and only if it is in the cut, and an edge of type (2) results in a crossing if and only if it is not in the cut.
This construction allows for associating the maximum cut objective function values
W
to corresponding crossing numbers
K-W
.
In particular,
W=0
for the empty cut corresponds to the number of crossings
K

\hat{S}

In order to guarantee that the characters of an interaction appear consecutively, we introduce type-3 edges with weight 0 from the additional vertex
v^{*}

We introduce a binary variable
z_{e}
for every edge

The following model guarantees that every optimal solution corresponds to a constrained maximum cut in the graph
G_{M}
that provides the optimal solution to the storyline problem.
The constraints (

The correctness is provided in

9

3

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in F}z_{e}-\sum_{e\in C\setminus F}z_{e}\leq|F|-1

\displaystyle\quad\begin{aligned} &\textrm{for all }i=1,\ldots,\ell;I\in{\cal I}(t_{i});\\

4
Structural Properties of Storyline Solutions

In this section, we identify structural properties of storyline solutions that will help us to optimize the models proposed in
Section

5
, and that guide the exact optimization process.

For the results in this section, we introduce some definitions and observe some properties of the function
\operatorname{{cr}}
.
Let
\pi
be a permutation of the set
X
, and
\phi
be a permutation of the set
Y\subseteq X
.
We define the permutation
\psi=\operatorname{{assign}}(\pi,\phi)
as the permutation of the set
X
such that

\psi(i)=\begin{cases}\pi(i)\qquad\text{if }\pi(i)\in X\setminus Y\\
\phi(j)\qquad\text{if }c=\pi(i)\in Y\text{ with }j=\pi[Y]^{-1}(c)\end{cases}

In particular, all elements in
X\setminus Y
are ordered according to
\pi
, and all elements in
Y
switch their position so that they are ordered according to
\phi
.
Notice that
\operatorname{{assign}}(\pi,\phi)[Y]=\phi
.

Example 1
.

Let
\pi=(c,e,b,d,f,a,g)
and
\phi=(a,b,c)
. Then
\operatorname{{assign}}(\pi,\psi)=(a,e,b,d,f,c,g)
.

Consider three consecutive time steps
t_{i},t_{i+1},t_{i+2}
(
1\leq i\leq\ell-2)
, we observe a special type of triangle inequality.

Observation 2
.

Let
C\subseteq\operatorname{AC}(t_{i},t_{i+2})
. For any feasible solution
S
we have

\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i}[C],\pi_{i+1}[C])+\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i+1}[C],\pi_{i+2}[C])\geq\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i}[C],\pi_{i+2}[C]).

Next, consider two permutations
\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1}
of a storyline solution such that
C=\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})\cap\operatorname{AC}(t_{i+1})
.
Consider two sets
X,Y\subseteq C
. We define
\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1},X,Y)
as the amount of
(c,c^{\prime})\in X\times Y
such that either
c\prec_{\pi_{i}}c^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime}\prec_{\pi_{i+1}}c
, or
c^{\prime}\prec_{\pi_{i}}c
and
c\prec_{\pi_{i+1}}c^{\prime}
. I.e., these are the crossings between time steps
t_{i}
and
t_{i+1}
with one character in
X
and one character in
Y
. We set
\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1},X)=\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1},X,X)
.

Observation 3
.

It holds that
\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1},X)=\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i}[X],\pi_{i+1}[X])
.

Observation 4
.

Let
X,Y
be sets such that
X\dot{\cup}Y=C\subseteq\operatorname{AC}(t_{i},t_{i+1})
. It holds that

\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1},C)=\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1},X)+\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1},Y)+\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1},X,Y).

Now, we define two properties of storyline drawings.
Definition

5
captures that the relative order of characters in an interaction can be propagated backwards.

Definition 5

(Type-1 consistency)
.

Let
S=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{\ell})
be a solution to a storyline instance
(T,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{I},A)
. Let
I\in\mathcal{I}
,
t_{i}=\operatorname{{time}}(I)
and
C=\operatorname{{char}}(I)
. Let
1<j(I)\leq i
be the index of the earliest time step
t_{j(j)}
such that
C\subseteq\operatorname{AC}(t_{j(I)},t_{i})
and

\forall k\in\{j(I)+1,\dots,i\}:\operatorname{CI}(t_{k})\cap C=\emptyset\lor\exists I\in\mathcal{I}(t_{k}):C\subseteq\operatorname{{char}}(I).

We say that
S
is
I
-consistent if

\forall k\in\{j(I),j(I)+1,\dots,i\}:\pi_{k}[C]=\pi_{i}[C].

Further, we say that
S
is
type-1-consistent
if it
is
I
-consistent for all
I\in\cal{I}
.

Definition

6
defines the property that between suitable pairs of interactions with the same set of characters, these characters are kept together between the two time steps. Note that this is not implied by type-1 consistency.

Definition 6

(Type-2 consistency)
.

Let
S=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{\ell})
be a solution to a storyline instance
(T,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{I},A)
. Consider two interactions
I_{1},I_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
such that

•

\operatorname{{char}}(I_{1})=\operatorname{{char}}(I_{2})=C
,

•

i=\operatorname{{time}}(I_{1})<\operatorname{{time}}(I_{2})=j
, and

•

\forall k\in\mathbb{N}:i<k<j\Rightarrow[\operatorname{CI}(t_{k})\cap C=\emptyset\lor\exists I_{3}\in\mathcal{I}(t_{k}):C\subseteq\operatorname{{char}}(I_{3})]
.

We say that
S
is
(I_{1},I_{2})
-consistent if

\forall i<k<j:\exists\pi^{a},\pi^{b}:\pi_{k}=\pi^{a}\star\pi_{i}[C]\star\pi^{b}.

Further, we say that
S
is type-2-consistent if it is
(I_{1},I_{2})
-consistent for all such pairs
(I_{1},I_{2})
.

The following lemma shows that we can achieve type-1 consistency for storyline drawings without increasing the number of crossings. Essentially, if a storyline solution is not type-1 consistent for an interaction
I
, we can propagate the relative order of characters in that interaction forward from the time step
t_{j(I)}
from
Definition

5
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
(T,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{I},A)

S
a type-1-consistent solution

S

S^{\prime}

Let
S=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{\ell})
be a solution such that there exists
I\in\mathcal{I}
such that
S
is not
I
-consistent.
We choose such an interaction
I
that is as “late as possible”, i.e.
I\in\mathcal{I}(t_{i})
with
i
maximal.
We construct
S^{\prime}=(\pi_{1}^{\prime},\pi_{2}^{\prime},\dots,\pi_{\ell}^{\prime})
such that

[(1)]

1.

\operatorname{{cr}}(S^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{{cr}}(S)
,

2.

S^{\prime}
is
I
-consistent,

3.

for each
I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
such that
S
is
I^{\prime}
-consistent and
\operatorname{{time}}(I^{\prime})\geq\operatorname{{time}}(I)

S^{\prime}
is also
I^{\prime}
-consistent.

Let
t_{i}=\operatorname{{time}}(I)
and
C=\operatorname{{char}}(I)
and define
j(I)<i
as in
Definition

5
.
For all
1\leq k\leq\ell
such that
k\leq j(I)
or
k>i

\pi_{k}^{\prime}=\pi_{k}
.
For

\pi_{k}^{\prime}=\operatorname{{assign}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{j(I)}[C])
.
It is clear that the new solution

is
I
-consistent and is still a solution to the storyline instance (characters involved in interactions are still consecutive in the respective time steps).

We first argue that
\operatorname{{cr}}(S^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{{cr}}(S)
.
First, it is clear that
\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi^{\prime}_{k},\pi^{\prime}_{k+1})=\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1})
for
k\leq j(I)-1
and
k>i
as the involved permutations did not change.
It remains to show that

\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime},\pi_{k+1}^{\prime})\leq\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1}).

We also have that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime},\pi_{k+1}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime},\pi_{k+1}^{\prime},C)+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime},\pi_{k+1}^{\prime},\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1})\setminus C)+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime},\pi_{k+1}^{\prime},C,\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1})\setminus C),

and

\displaystyle\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1})=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1},C)+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1},\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1})\setminus C)+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1},C,\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1})\setminus C).

Thus it is enough to show that the inequality holds for the respective terms separately.

•

\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime},\pi_{k+1}^{\prime},C)\leq\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1},C)
: We have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1},C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}[C],\pi_{k+1}[C])

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}[C\cap\operatorname{AC}(t_{i+1})],\pi_{k+1}[C\cap\operatorname{AC}(t_{i+1})])

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{j(I)}[C\cap\operatorname{AC}(t_{i+1})],\pi_{i+1}[C\cap\operatorname{AC}(t_{i+1})])

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi^{\prime}_{i}[C\cap\operatorname{AC}(t_{i+1})],\pi_{i+1}^{\prime}[C\cap\operatorname{AC}(t_{i+1})])

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime},\pi_{k+1}^{\prime},C)

The first inequality holds because
C\cap\operatorname{AC}(t_{i+1})
is a subset of
C
. The second inequality holds because of
Observation

2
. The last equality holds because the only crossings between characters from
C
from
t_{j(I)}
to
t_{i+1}
appear between
t_{i}
and
t_{i+1}
as the relative order of characters from
C
in

is the same from
j(I)
to

.

•

\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime},\pi_{k+1}^{\prime},\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1})\setminus C)\leq\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1},\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1})\setminus C)

and let

\displaystyle\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime},\pi_{k+1}^{\prime},\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1})\setminus C)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime}[C_{a}],\pi_{k+1}^{\prime}[C_{a}])

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}[C_{a}],\pi_{k+1}[C_{a}])

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1},\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1})\setminus C).

The key is that

and
\pi_{k+1}[C_{a}]=\pi_{k+1}^{\prime}[C_{a}]
, as we did not change the relative order of non-interaction characters, that is, characters that are not in

.
As the equality holds for each term, it also holds for the sum.

•

\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime},\pi_{k+1}^{\prime},C,\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1})\setminus C)\leq\sum_{k=j(I)}^{i}\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1},C,\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1})\setminus C)

separately and consider two cases.

If

and
\pi_{i}^{\prime}

and
\pi_{i+1}
as
\pi_{i+1}^{\prime}=\pi_{i+1}

\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1},C,\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1)})\setminus C)=\operatorname{{cr}}(\pi_{k}^{\prime},\pi_{k+1}^{\prime},C,\operatorname{AC}(t_{k},t_{k+1})\setminus C)

In the remaining case, we have that
k\in\{j(I),j(I)+1,\dots,i-1\}
. Consider a character

. Let
\alpha
be the amount of characters from

that are crossed by

and

in

. We show that

crosses at most

characters from
C
between
t_{k}
and
t_{k+1}
in
S^{\prime}
, which is enough to show the claim.
Let
\operatorname{abv}_{k}
be the amount of

such that
c^{\prime}\prec_{\pi_{k}}c
. Define
\operatorname{abv}_{k+1}
by replacing
k
with
k+1
in the definition. Equivalently define
\operatorname{abv}_{k}^{\prime}
and
\operatorname{abv}_{k+1}^{\prime}
by replacing
\pi_{k}
by
\pi_{k}^{\prime}
and
\pi_{k+1}
by
\pi^{\prime}_{k+1}
, respectively.
By the definition of

we have that
\operatorname{abv}_{k}=\operatorname{abv}_{k}^{\prime}
and
\operatorname{abv}_{k+1}=\operatorname{abv}_{k+1}^{\prime}
. By the pigeonhole principle,
c
must cross at least
\beta=|\operatorname{abv}_{k}-\operatorname{abv}_{k+1}|
characters from

between
t_{k}
and
t_{k+1}
(for

and

). Notice that

is the exact amount of crossings for

is a lower bound. Thus

and

in

when compared to

.

Putting these inequalities together, we get

. It is easy to see that we constructed

such that it satisfies (2).

It remains to show (3), i.e. that for each
I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
such that

-consistent and

1.

\operatorname{{time}}(I^{\prime})=\operatorname{{time}}(I)

\operatorname{{char}}(I)\cap\operatorname{{char}}(I^{\prime})=\emptyset
. Hence, we did not change relative orders of character pairs from
\operatorname{{char}}(I^{\prime})
and we obtain a contradiction.

2.

\operatorname{{time}}(I^{\prime})>\operatorname{{time}}(I)=t_{i}

(a)

(b)

\operatorname{{char}}(I^{\prime})\not\subseteq\operatorname{{char}}(I)

. But we only changed orders up to

, so

is already not

-consistent, a contradiction.

Lastly, assume that
S
is type-2-consistent, we prove that
S^{\prime}
is as well. We proceed by contradiction.
Hence, let there be
(I_{1},I_{2})
as in
Definition

6

S
is
(I_{1},I_{2})
-consistent, but

is not. It follows that the interval
[t_{j(I)+1},t_{i}]
has non-empty intersection with
[\operatorname{{time}}(I_{1}),\operatorname{{time}}(I_{2})]
. It further follows that
\operatorname{{char}}(I_{1})\subseteq C
. But if

was already
(I_{1},I_{2})
-consistent, we did not change relative orders nor positions of characters in
\operatorname{{char}}(I_{1})
during the above process, a contradiction.

The proof is concluded by applying the above procedure inductively.
∎

(1)

The following is a direct consequence.

Corollary 9
.

For each storyline instance
(T,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{I},A)
there exists a crossing-minimum solution

Theorem

It shows that we can in specific cases assume the order of characters

Theorem 10

Let
(T,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{I},A)
be a storyline instance. There exists a crossing-minimum solution
S=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{\ell})
with the following property.

[(1)]

2.

if
C_{a}\subseteq\operatorname{AC}(t_{i-1},t_{i})
, then
\pi_{i}[C_{a}]=\pi_{i-1}[C_{a}]
,

3.

if
\operatorname{{char}}(I)\subseteq\operatorname{AC}(t_{i-1},t_{i})
, then
\pi_{i}[\operatorname{{char}}(I)]=\pi_{i-1}[\operatorname{{char}}(I)]
, and

4.

C_{b}\subseteq\operatorname{AC}(t_{i-1},t_{i})
, then
\pi_{i}[C_{b}]=\pi_{i-1}[C_{b}]
.

Proof.

Consider a crossing-minimum solution
S^{*}=(\pi_{1}^{*},\pi_{2}^{*},\dots,\pi_{\ell}^{*})

(T,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{I}^{\prime},A)
where
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{S^{*}}

\mathcal{I}_{S^{*}}
containing the following interactions.

|\mathcal{I}(t_{i})|=1
and
\mathcal{I}=\{I\}
, let
C_{a},C_{b}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})
such that
\pi_{i}^{*}=\pi_{i}[C_{a}]*\pi^{*}_{i}[\operatorname{{char}}(I)]\star\pi^{*}_{i}[C_{b}]
.
If

5.1
The Propagated Linear Ordering Model (PLO)

For our new formulation, we take the linearized model (
LIN
) as basis, but remove some of the (
LOP
)-constraints for time step
t_{i}
as we can make use of propagating the ordering at
t_{i-1}
by
Theorem

10
as follows.
If
\mathcal{I}(t_{i})
for
i>1
contains only one interaction
I
, and no characters outside the interaction start at
t_{i}
(i.e.,
\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})\setminus\operatorname{AC}(t_{i-1})\subseteq\operatorname{CI}(t_{i})
), we only include a part of the (
LOP
)-constraints for time step
t_{i}
using a representative character
c_{w}\in\operatorname{{char}}(I)

From the set of (
LOP
)-constraints containing at least one character in
\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})\setminus\operatorname{{char}}(I)
, we keep only those that contain exactly two characters in
\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})\setminus\operatorname{{char}}(I)
and the representative character
c_{w}\in\operatorname{{char}}(I)
.
This is sufficient, because we can define the order of the active characters in
t_{i}
relative to the order of the characters in the interaction
I
based on

10
. Hence, let

be a representative character from the set
\operatorname{{char}}(I)
, and consider a pair of characters

By
Theorem

10

and

are above or below

t_{i-1}
. Otherwise, their relative order is already given by their relative order to

\displaystyle x_{i,u,v}

\displaystyle\geq x_{i-1,u,v}+x_{i,u,w}+x_{i,v,w}-2

\displaystyle x_{i,u,v}

The two constraints ensure that

If additionally

\displaystyle x_{i,u,v}=x_{i-1,u,v}

We call this formulation

Theorem 11
.

Every optimal solution to the formulation (PLO) corresponds to a crossing minimum storyline drawing.

Proof.

We only have to show that solutions to PLO satisfy transitivity constraints for the ordering variables, as we include the constraint set (

We show this by induction on

•

If

•

Otherwise, there is exactly one interaction

6
Implementation

In this section, we discuss relevant implementation details and new heuristic-based approaches to improve our algorithms.

6.1
Initial Heuristic

Our initial heuristic follows a decomposition strategy. The original problem instance is first split into smaller subproblems, each comprising
\hat{\ell}
many consecutive time steps, which are of such a size that it is possible to quickly compute a crossing minimal solution for this subproblem.
The initial heuristic solution
S_{h}
is computed by optimally solving these subsequent “slices” of the original problem instance and piecing together a global solution. This works as follows.
First, a crossing minimal solution
S_{1}=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{\hat{\ell}})
is computed for the first
\hat{\ell}
many time steps of the original problem using the
PLO
ILP formulation. The first
\hat{s}
many layers of
S_{1}
are assigned to the initial heuristic solution
S_{h}
. In the next iteration, we compute an optimal solution for time steps
\hat{s},\dots,\hat{s}+\hat{\ell}-1
while enforcing the ordering of layer
\hat{s}
by fixing the ordering variables accordingly. Again, we fix the first
\hat{s}
many permutations of this solution as the permutations of the corresponding time steps in the heuristic solution
S_{h}
. This is continued until the heuristic solution is computed for all time steps
t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\ell}
.

We choose
\hat{s}<\hat{\ell}
in order to include as much information about the global problem into the sliced subproblem as possible (in the form of the time steps that are about to come), while achieving a good tradeoff between time needed to solve all subproblems and the number of crossings of
S_{h}
. Experimental evaluation showed that
\hat{s}=5,\hat{\ell}=30
yields a good tradeoff between runtime and solution quality.

6.2
Rounding and Local Improvement Heuristics

We propose a rounding heuristic that exploits fractional LP-solutions. Furthermore, we try to improve these solutions as well as incumbent solutions found by the solver software by proposing three local improvement heuristics
Rem-DC
,
Push-CR
, and
SL-Bary
.

Rounding Heuristic.

We propose a strategy to round fractional solutions of the ordering variables
x_{i,u,v}
to valid integer solutions corresponding to a drawing of the storyline instance.
In particular, we compute permutations
\pi_{i}
for
t_{i}
, going from
i=1
to
\ell
in this order, and convert them to integer solutions of ordering variables in the natural way. This works as follows. First, for each interaction
I\in\mathcal{I}(t)
we compute a permutation
\pi_{I}
of its characters
\operatorname{{char}}(I)
. For this we compute for each
c\in\operatorname{{char}}(I)
the value
d^{-}(c)
which is computed as
A+B
where
A
is the number of
c^{\prime}\in\operatorname{{char}}(I)
such that
x_{i,c^{\prime},c}>0.5+\epsilon
and
B
is the number of
c^{\prime}
such that
|x_{i,c,c^{\prime}}-0.5|\leq\epsilon
,
\{c,c^{\prime}\}\subseteq\operatorname{AC}(t_{i-1})
, and
c^{\prime}\prec_{\pi_{i-1}}c
1
1

1

This is useful as setting all ordering variables to
0.5
is a valid solution to the LP relaxation of most considered models and thus ordering variables often assume this value
. Clearly,
B
is only positive if
i>1
. Then
\pi_{I}
is computed by sorting
\operatorname{{char}}(I)
by their
d^{-}
-values. If the model contains symmetry breaking constraints, this sometimes leads to an infeasible ordering. In this case, we find an order
\pi_{I}
that also satisfies the symmetry breaking constraints imposed by
\pi_{i-1}
(some pairs of characters must have the same relative order in
\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{i-1}
) as follows. We construct
\pi_{I}
iteratively by always selecting the character
c\in\operatorname{{char}}(I)
that has the smallest value
d^{-}(c)
and, furthermore, there is no character
c^{\prime}
which was not selected yet and needs to precede
c
according to
\pi_{i-1}
and the symmetry constraints. This is implemented in quasi-linear time using a priority queue.

Local Improvement Heuristics.

Rem-DC (remove double crossings)

This heuristic finds pairs of characters that cross twice, and both crossings can be removed without increasing the total number of crossings. Formally, this is possible for a drawing
S
and two characters
c,c^{\prime}
if there exist
1\leq i<j\leq\ell
with
j-i>1
such that

•

c
and
c^{\prime}
cross between
t_{i}
and
t_{i+1}
, and
t_{j-1}
and
t_{j}
, and

•

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
i<k<j
,
c
and
c^{\prime}
either belong to the same interaction in
t_{k}
, or they both are in no interaction for
t_{k}
.

Then for all
k
as above we can exchange
c
and
c^{\prime}
in
\pi_{k}
. This removes the double-crossing between
c
and
c^{\prime}
and further does not introduce new crossings.

Push-CR

This heuristic proceeds from
i=2,\dots,\ell
in this order and tries to push crossings between
\pi_{i-1}
and
\pi_{i}
forward by one time step: Let
C
be a maximal set of characters such that (1) all characters in
C
appear consecutively in
\pi_{i}
, (2)
C\subseteq\operatorname{AC}(t_{i-1},t_{i})
, and (3) all characters in
C
either appear in the same interaction in
t_{i}
or no character in
C
is part of an interaction. For each such set of characters

we replace in
\pi_{i}
,
\pi_{i}[C]
by
\pi_{i-1}[C]
. By similar arguments as in
Section

4
this never increases the number of crossings.

Bary-SL

Lastly, we describe a variant of the barycenter heuristic
[
23
]
for storylines that iteratively improves a storyline drawing by updating
\pi_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq\ell
based on
\pi_{i-1}
and
\pi_{i+1}
or one of them if not both exist. It is only applied to
\pi_{i}
if
|\mathcal{I}(t_{i})|=1
. Informally, we say that a pair of characters
c,c^{\prime}
is comparable if
c
and
c^{\prime}
have the same relative order in
\pi_{i-1}
and
\pi_{i+1}
. We compute an ordering
\pi_{i}
such that most comparable pairs have the same relative order in
\pi_{i-1},\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1}
as follows. We compute the directed auxiliary graph
G_{C}
whose vertex set is a subset
S
of
\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})
and which contains an arc from
c
to
c^{\prime}
for each comparable pair
c
and
c^{\prime}
such that
c
is before
c^{\prime}
in
\pi_{i-1}
and
\pi_{i+1}
. Then, an order of
S
is built by iteratively selecting the vertex from
G_{C}
with the fewest incoming arcs. We also ensure that characters
c
that are not part of
I
are above or below the characters in
I
depending on which option leads to fewer crossings between
c
and
\operatorname{{char}}(I)
with respect to the considered time steps
t_{i-1},t_{i},t_{i+1}
. The algorithm computing the order based on the graph

is then applied to the characters in the interaction yielding
\pi_{I}
, and those not in the interaction yielding
\pi_{C}
, respectively. The ordering
\pi_{C}
is inserted into the maximum position of
\pi_{I}
such that all characters before
\pi_{C}
“prefer” being above the interaction with regard to crossings with
\operatorname{{char}}(I)
.
The new
\pi_{i}

Both
Bary-SL
and
Push-CR
are applied successively to layers
2,\dots,\ell
. This is repeated five times and applied to valid integer solutions found by the solver and the rounding heuristic described above. If enabled, the rounding heuristic is applied to every LP solution found by the solver.
Rem-DC
is applied five times to each pair of characters.

6.3
Max-Cut Implementation Details

Since the original implementation of Gronemann et al.
[
9
]
is not available, we provide our own implementation that was optimized beyond their algorithm. After reading the input, we first find an initial starting solution by applying adapted barycenter techniques as described in
[
9
]
. We start the root relaxation with the objective function and the tree constraints (
TRC
) as the only constraints, and start separating the odd cycle (
CYC
) (as suggested by Charfreitag et al.
[
4
]
) and the (
LOPC
) constraints.
The (
LOPC
) constraints are separated by complete enumeration.
Whenever a new LP solution is available, all nonbinding inequalities are eliminated, and we try to exploit the information in the (fractional) solution in order to obtain a better incumbent solution.

The root phase ends when no violated inequalities are found. Then the branch-and-cut phase is started by changing the variable types from continuous in the interval
[0,1]
to binary. In the Gurobi “MIPSOL” callbacks at branch-and-cut nodes with an integer solution, we check if the integral solution is the characteristic vector of a storyline drawing. If so and if the number of crossings is lower than the one of the incumbent solution, the latter is updated, otherwise, the exact (
CYC
) and (
LOPC
) separators are called to provide violated inequalities that are passed to Gurobi as
lazy constraints
.
In the Gurobi “MIPNODE” callbacks it is tried to exploit the fractional solution for a possible update of the incumbent solution.

6.4
Implementation of the ILP Models

The models (
QDR
), (
LIN
), and (PLO) include many symmetries regarding ordering variables and crossing variables.
For each
t_{i}\in T
and pair of characters
c_{u},c_{v}\in\operatorname{AC}(t_{i})
we only keep the ordering variables
x_{i,u,v}
and crossing variables
y_{i,u,v}
with

. The constraints are adjusted with standard projections
[
10
,

]

We take the linearized model (

We decided to always add (

Hence, we consider the following algorithms for our experimental evaluation.

•

•

•

The latter three algorithms are by default extended with the symmetry breaking constraints described in

Conclusion and Future Work

As shown in our experimental study, our new methods and algorithms dominate the state-of-the-art algorithms and are able to solve large instances to optimality, while the newly introduced improvements are beneficial towards all considered formulations. We observe two directions for future work.

•

Our new components for improvement could be implemented into the max-cut formulation. However, initial experiments have shown that the simple linearized formulation (
LIN
) performs comparably to the more complex max-cut formulation, hence we expect that this will result in a negligible or no improvement over our proposed formulations.

•

Out of our 59 instances (see
https://osf.io/3bua2/
) we were able to solve 55 when increasing the time limit. The remaining four unsolved instances should pose a challenge to engineer new exact methods for crossing minimization in storylines.
