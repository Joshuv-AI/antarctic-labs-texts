Resilience Through Escalation: A Graph-Based PACE Architecture for Satellite Threat Response
Anouar Boumeftah 1 , Sarah McKenzie-Picot 2 , Peter Klimas 2 , Gunes Karabulut Kurt 1

Abstract
Satellite systems increasingly face operational risks from jamming, cyberattacks, and electromagnetic disruptions. Traditional redundancy strategies often fail against dynamic, multi-vector threats. This paper introduces a resilience-by-design framework grounded in the PACE (Primary, Alternate, Contingency, Emergency) methodology—originally developed for tactical communications in military operations—adapting it to satellite systems through a layered state-transition model informed by threat scoring frameworks such as CVSS, DREAD, and NASA’s risk matrix. We define a dynamic resilience index to quantify system adaptability and implement three PACE variants—static, adaptive, and softmax-based decision models—to evaluate resilience under diverse disruption scenarios. The proposed approach highlights the effectiveness of lightweight, decision-aware fallback mechanisms in improving survivability and operational continuity for next-generation space assets.



I
Introduction

Satellite systems face an increasingly volatile threat landscape, shaped by both natural phenomena and deliberate interference. As reliance grows on space-based infrastructure for communications, navigation, Earth observation, and defense, orbital assets have become critical—and vulnerable—components of global networks. Threats include cyberattacks, jamming, spoofing, electromagnetic disturbances, and cascading failures across the space-ground-link chain. Recent incidents highlight these risks: the 2022 Viasat cyberattack disrupted civilian and military services across Europe
[
1
]
, while the 1962 Starfish Prime test illustrated the widespread impact of high-altitude Electromagnetic Pulse (EMP) events
[
2
]
.

I-A

Background and Related Work

Resilience in satellite systems refers to the ability to sustain essential functions in the presence of both hardware faults and malicious threats. Recent research has emphasized that while space assets are increasingly targeted by cyberattacks, many systems still lack robust, integrated resilience strategies. Pavur
et al.

[
3
]
document decades of satellite hacking incidents, underscoring the vulnerability of the sector. Falco
et al.

[
4
]
emphasize the importance of dynamic requirements based on mission objectives. Military and national space policies now stress resilience as a blend of fault tolerance and cyber defense
[
5
]
, requiring satellites to withstand both stochastic failures and deliberate attacks. In engineering, redundancy remains a prime tactic, often implemented via N+1 schemes or Triple Modular Redundancy (TMR). However, these methods are largely static, and while effective against random faults, they may fail when confronted with coordinated or adaptive attacks. Sun
et al.

[
6
]
apply Bayesian methods to assess satellite redundancy effectiveness, and O’Halloran
et al.

[
7
]
present a framework for analyzing failure propagation in Cyber-Physical Systems during early design stages, emphasizing the identification of forward, backward, and uncoupled propagation paths using graph-theoretic metrics. Xia
et al.

[
8
]
extend this with a directed graph approach for Complex electromechanical systems (CMES) under attack. Although effective for random failures, these approaches are largely static and assume passive threats, making them less suitable for adaptive or coordinated attacks. From a system-level view, the MILSATCOM DFARR initiative offers insights into designing affordable, resilient SATCOM systems
[
9
]
. Its focus on modular waveforms and flexible ground-space processing reflects a systems engineering approach to cyber and jamming resilience, aligning with emerging policies promoting layered protection against kinetic, electronic, and software-based threats.

I-B

Existing Threat Modeling Frameworks

A Threat Model is typically a formalized abstraction that defines potential adversarial and non-adverserial hazards, system vulnerabilities, and establishes the pathways through which such threats can be realized. The use of threat modeling lies not only in capturing the scope and nature of operational risks but also in its facilitation of risk-informed decision-making across system design, deployment, and in the scope of this paper, contingency planning. NASA’s risk matrix is widely used for aerospace risk assessment, classifying hazards by likelihood and severity. In cybersecurity, frameworks such as CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) and DREAD score vulnerabilities and threats. While CVSS quantifies software risks, DREAD qualitatively ranks threats across dimensions such as damage potential and exploitability. Applied to satellites, these models provide useful insights but remain under-integrated into onboard decision-making. Pavur
et al.

[
3
]
and Falco
et al.

[
4
]
call for unified threat modeling that bridges space-specific hazards and cyber risk assessment. Complementing this, Jacobsen and Marandi provide a cyber-threat taxonomy tailored to unmanned aerial systems, using STRIDE to identify vulnerabilities ranging from spoofing to privilege escalation. Their methodology offers a transferrable framework for satellite system threat modeling—especially in the growing field of autonomous or semi-autonomous space-based platforms where cyber-physical integration poses similar risks
[
10
]
.

Despite their value, existing threat models are often applied in isolation and remain disconnected from onboard decision-making systems. This limits their utility in dynamic, contested environments that demand rapid adaptation. To overcome this gap, Section
III
presents a structured, decision-aware fallback framework designed to support resilient satellite operations under diverse threat conditions.

I-C

Origins of the PACE Methodology

The PACE model—
Primary
,
Alternate
,
Contingency
, and
Emergency
—was developed by U.S. Special Operations Forces to ensure mission continuity in contested or degraded environments
[
11
]
. It promotes a hierarchy of independent fallback options, prioritized by feasibility and reliability. While originally used in tactical communications, PACE is now applied across high-reliability domains. In military command and control, it supports critical functions under disruption
[
12
]
; medical systems use it to sequence emergency responses
[
13
]
; and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) applies it to design redundant communication frameworks for national infrastructure and emergency operations
[
14
]
.

I-D

Research Gap Identification

Conventional redundancy improves fault tolerance but is static and tailored to random, independent failures. In contested environments, such designs fail against adaptive or coordinated threats, often leading to common mode failures where all redundant units are compromised
[
15
]
. They also impose overhead without guaranteeing resilience to evolving risks. Recent work advocates for context-aware redundancy. Jacobs
et al.
propose reconfigurable schemes that adapt to environmental stress
[
15
]
; George
et al.
present spacecraft architectures switching between TMR and symmetric multiprocessing
[
16
]
. Graph-based techniques, including knowledge graphs for anomaly detection, further support system-level adaptivity
[
17
,
8
]
.

These studies point to the need for multi-layered, adaptive strategies. To address this, we propose a layered architecture inspired by the PACE planning concept. Our key contributions are:

•

Adapt the PACE resilience framework—originally used in the military —for satellite operations in contested orbital environments.

•

Design a threat-driven decision tree that selects optimal PACE fallback layers based on mission utility and operational cost.

•

Formalize dynamic transitions using a softmax-based mechanism to enable traceable, reward-driven escalation and recovery strategies.

•

Build a system-level model integrating threat likelihood, operational cost, and utility, and evaluate performance via Monte Carlo simulations.

•

Define the Dynamic Redundancy Efficiency Index (DREI) to quantify the trade-off between mission performance and fallback cost across strategies.

Cascading Failure Effects Across Subsystems

Cross-segment attacks can trigger system-wide degradation. Spoofed links may mislead On-Board Computers (OBCs), while ground-based intrusions can corrupt firmware and disable safeguards. Malware propagation via inter-satellite links (ISLs) has also been demonstrated
[

III
Proposed Framework

III-A

PACE-Based Resilience Model

This model organizes operational behavior into four resilience layers:
Primary
(nominal),
Alternate
(degraded but functional),
Contingency
(survivability under stress), and
Emergency
(minimal-operation or distress signaling). Each layer reflects a trade-off between mission utility and system burden—balancing performance, risk tolerance, and resource consumption. Together, these layers form a modular, adaptive structure for graceful degradation. Transitions are guided by context-aware metrics and pre-defined thresholds to maintain continuity
[
12
]
.

III-B

Graph-Based Representation

Fig. 2
:
PACE Graph-Based Representation Diagram

As shown in Fig.
2
, we formalize the PACE-based framework as a directed multi-layer graph
G=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
, where:

•

\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}\{s_{i}^{(j)}\}
is the set of system states across all layers
L_{i}\in\{\text{P},\text{A},\text{C},\text{E}\}
,

•

\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}
is the set of transitions between states.

Each state
s\in\mathcal{S}
is associated with a utility function
\omega(s)
and a cost-adjusted recovery potential. Transitions
(s,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
are annotated with:

w(s,s^{\prime})=\left(p(s,s^{\prime}),c(s,s^{\prime})\right)

(1)

where
p(s,s^{\prime})
is the probability of transitioning from
s
to
s^{\prime}
, and
c(s,s^{\prime})
is the associated cost.

III-B
1
State Definition and Layering

Each layer
L_{i}
contains a fixed number of states
s_{i}^{(j)}
, representing degrees of operational viability. Transitions may occur within a layer (horizontal transitions) or across layers (vertical transitions). The following rules apply: horizontal transitions handle degradation or recovery within the same layer; vertical transitions represent fallback under threat or upward recovery, subject to cost and timing constraints.

III-B
2
Transition Probability and Cost Modeling

Transition weights are modeled jointly based on threat likelihood (NASA risk matrices, CVSS scores), operational cost (e.g., resource use, mission loss, delays), and system dynamics, which adapt to environmental factors such as jamming intensity, energy reserves, and mission phase. Due to page limitations, we omit the detailed numerical formulations of threat probability estimation and transition modeling, though these were developed as part of our approach.

IV
Resilience Evaluation Index

To quantify how effectively a satellite system maintains operational continuity under disruption, we introduce a single index: the
Dynamic Redundancy Efficiency Index (DREI)
. This index captures the trade-off between mission utility and the cost of fallback or recovery transitions over time.
Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of all system states in the PACE structure, and
P_{t}(s)
the probability of the system being in state
s\in\mathcal{S}
at time
t
. Each state
s
is associated with a utility value
\omega(s)
, and transitions between states
(s,s^{\prime})
incur a cost
c_{t}(s,s^{\prime})
at time
t
. We define the adjusted utility
\omega_{t}^{*}(s)
as:

\omega_{t}^{*}(s)=\begin{cases}\omega(s)\cdot\kappa,&\text{if }s=\text{P\_Nominal and }t>0\\
\omega(s),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2)

where
\kappa>1
is a resilience reward multiplier that emphasizes the importance of returning to nominal operation.

The DREI at time
t
is computed as:

\text{DREI}_{t}=\frac{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\omega_{t}^{*}(s)\cdot P_{t}(s)}{C_{t}}.

(3)

This formulation rewards resilience strategies that (1) recover high-utility states, and (2) do so without incurring excessive cost, making it suitable for benchmarking dynamic adaptation mechanisms such as energy-aware fallbacks or informed reconfiguration as presented in Section
V-B
.
High DREI values reflect configurations that maximize system utility while minimizing cost under evolving threat.

V
Methodology

V-A

Simulation Environment Description

To evaluate the proposed framework, we simulate a representative signal jamming scenario using a time-stepped, event-driven model. The satellite system evolves under operational constraints and external threats as defined in Fig.
1
. At each timestep, the environment updates energy reserves, jamming intensity
J_{t}\in[0,1]
, and threat activity, then applies fallback transitions based on the selected PACE model (static, adaptive, or reward-optimized). Each transition incurs a probabilistic cost and alters the system state. A predefined crisis at a specific timestep intensifies threats and tests recovery via layer suppression, blocked transitions, or elevated transition costs to simulate stress conditions.

V-B

Implemented PACE Variants

We implement three operational models of the PACE framework to evaluate the impact of environmental awareness and adaptive decision-making on system resilience.

V-B
1
Simple Static PACE Model

This baseline uses fixed transition weights from precomputed threat analyses. Each transition
(s,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
is defined as:

w(s,s^{\prime})=\left(p(s,s^{\prime}),\;c(s,s^{\prime})\right)

(4)

At each timestep, the system stays in state
s
with probability
p_{\text{stay}}
; otherwise, it transitions according to the static distribution
\{p(s,s^{\prime})\}
. This non-reactive model ignores environmental feedback and serves as a benchmark.

V-B
2
Environment-Aware Adaptive PACE Model

This adaptive model adjusts transitions based on real-time conditions, mainly jamming intensity
J_{t}
and recovery concurrency
C\geq 1
. For each transition
(s,s^{\prime})
, parameters evolve as:

\displaystyle p_{t}(s,s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=p(s,s^{\prime})\cdot(1+\lambda J_{t})

(5)

\displaystyle c_{t}(s,s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=c(s,s^{\prime})+\gamma J_{t}-\alpha(C-1),

(6)

with sensitivity coefficients
\lambda,\gamma,\alpha
.

Higher
J_{t}
increases likelihood of degraded states, while concurrent recovery lowers cost. Infeasible transitions are excluded, and one is sampled from the adjusted distribution at each timestep.

V-B
3
Epsilon-Greedy MDP-Inspired PACE Model

This model uses a one-step reward-based decision rule inspired by Markov Decision Processes. Each valid transition
(s,s^{\prime})
is scored by a reward
R(s,s^{\prime})=\omega(s^{\prime})-c_{t}(s,s^{\prime})
, where
\omega(s^{\prime})
is the utility of the target state and
c_{t}(s,s^{\prime})
is the environment-adjusted cost. Transitions are selected via an
\epsilon
-greedy policy: with probability
\epsilon
, a random successor
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(s)
is chosen (exploration); otherwise, the transition maximizing
R(s,s^{\prime})
is selected (exploitation):

s\rightarrow s^{\prime}\sim\begin{cases}\text{Uniform}(\mathcal{A}(s))&\text{if }\epsilon\\
\arg\max\limits_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(s)}R(s,s^{\prime})&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This approach balances exploration and exploitation, favoring high-utility, low-cost transitions while avoiding local optima.

For each PACE variant and for the jamming scenario, we perform
5000
independent Monte Carlo trials, each running up to
T=12
timesteps.

VI-B

Key Insights and Interpretation

The results highlight the value of incorporating environmental feedback into fallback strategies. While the static model struggled with dynamic disruptions, the adaptive version showed modest gains by reacting to threat levels. In contrast, the softmax model was notably more resilient—recovering utility more effectively while keeping costs lower. That said, the framework has limitations. Our threat scenarios were modeled as discrete events, which oversimplifies the continuous nature of real-world disruptions. Capturing longer-term dependencies, such as how sustained jamming affects power consumption or recovery feasibility, would improve realism. Additionally, moving beyond offline parameter tuning toward online learning could further improve real-time robustness.

VII
Conclusion

Our evaluation of three PACE variants—static, adaptive, and softmax-based—showed that traditional static redundancy fails to sustain utility during crises, often leading to degraded or failed states. The adaptive model improved resilience through environmental awareness but still struggled under stress. The softmax-based model consistently outperformed both, achieving higher recovery rates, lower costs, and the highest resilience index (DREI).

These results yield three key insights: (1) resilience requires real-time adaptation to dynamic threats; (2) lightweight, reward-based decision mechanisms can offer strong performance without heavy computation; and (3) layered degradation strategies enhance mission continuity and reduce failure risk.
