Cytogenetic and Molecular Findings in Hydrops‐Related Mirror Syndrome
Authors: Brian A Burnett, Christian M Parobek, Jessian L Munoz, Lauren E Westerfield, Kristi Poling, Cara Buskmiller, Amir A Shamshirsaz, Roopali V Donepudi, Ignatia B Van den Veyver, Magdalena Sanz Cortes, Michael A Belfort, Ahmed A Nassr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveMirror syndrome is a rare, life‐threatening condition in which maternal fluid overload mirrors fetal hydrops. Data on genetic findings in affected pregnancies are limited. We compared genetic diagnoses in hydrops cases with and without mirror syndrome.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study (2012–2023) of pregnancies with non‐immune hydrops at a single fetal center. Clinical data along with prenatal and postnatal genetic testing results were analyzed. Mirror syndrome was defined as fetal hydrops with new or worsening hypertension and significant maternal edema. Genetic testing rates and diagnostic yields were compared between the groups.ResultsOf the 192 cases, 140 (73%) underwent genetic testing. Testing rates were similar between mirror (24/31, 77%) and non‐mirror cases (116/161, 72%; p = 0.54). Abnormal findings explaining the phenotype were identified in 29% of mirror and 32% of non‐mirror cases (p = 0.79). Rates of single‐gene disorders, aneuploidy, and copy‐number variants did not differ (p > 0.05). Most single‐gene diagnoses involve cardiovascular or lymphatic pathways.ConclusionThe rates and types of genetic diagnoses were similar in hydrops cases with and without mirror syndrome. Despite limited power to detect subtle differences, these findings support a similar approach to genetic counseling and evaluation for hydrops cases with and without mirror syndrome.

Introduction

Mirror syndrome (MS), also known as Ballantyne syndrome, is a rare maternal disorder characterized by new or worsening hypertension with clinically significant generalized edema in the setting of fetal hydrops [1]. It is associated with substantial maternal morbidity and adverse perinatal outcomes and typically resolves only when the underlying fetal/placental pathology is treated or the pregnancy ends [1, 2, 3, 4]. Although it is known that mirror syndrome is more likely to occur in cases of cardiogenic hydrops, the pathophysiology of MS remains incompletely understood [5]. Proposed mechanisms include placental injury with maternal endothelial dysfunction and hemodilution mirroring the fetal edematous state [6, 7, 8].

On ultrasound, fetal hydrops is defined by pathologic fluid in at least two fetal compartments, such as ascites, pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, or skin edema, and is frequently accompanied by placental thickening and polyhydramnios [9]. Etiologies include fetal anemia, chromosomal abnormalities, congenital infections, and cardiovascular or pulmonary disorders, among others [9, 10].

Genetic causes of hydrops are common and span aneuploidy, copy‐number variants, and single‐gene conditions, including those affecting cardiovascular and lymphatic development [9, 11, 12, 13]. Establishing an etiologic diagnosis informs prognosis, guides perinatal management and candidacy for fetal therapy, and refines recurrence‐risk counseling. Contemporary evaluation often includes chromosome analysis or chromosomal microarray with reflex to gene sequencing via exome or genome sequencing, or a phenotype‐informed gene panel [9, 12, 13]. Recent studies show that exome sequencing substantially improves diagnostic yield in cases of non‐immune hydrops fetalis (NIHF) that remain unresolved after initial genetic testing, identifying pathogenic variants in roughly 29%–52% of cases, including RASopathies, inborn errors of metabolism, musculoskeletal disorders, and many other monogenic conditions [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. A systematic approach that integrates detailed phenotyping, family history, and comprehensive molecular testing is recommended to maximize diagnostic yield and to inform prognosis and recurrence risk [20, 21]. The wide spectrum of genes implicated underscores the genetic heterogeneity of NIHF and supports the use of exome or genome sequencing rather than targeted panels for comprehensive evaluation [22].

Although hydrops and MS co‐occur, it is unclear whether pregnancies complicated by MS differ from other hydrops cases in the likelihood or spectrum of identifiable genetic diagnoses. Prior reports have emphasized obstetric outcomes, with limited data directly comparing genetic testing rates and diagnostic yield between hydrops with and without MS [1, 2].

In case series, alpha thalassemia major emerged as the predominant cause accounting for most cases of hydrops complicated by mirror syndrome. This was particularly prominent in patients of Asian descent, reflecting the higher population carrier frequency for cis‐haplotype α‐globin variants [23]. Other genetic etiologies, including 18p deletion and congenital disorder of glycosylation type Ia (CDG‐Ia), have been described in isolated case reports, though the overall number of reported cases remains small [24, 25].

Although Turner syndrome (Monosomy X) is a frequent cause of NIHF, larger cohort studies suggest that Turner syndrome is rarely associated with maternal MS, indicating that the risk of maternal involvement may vary by genetic diagnosis [26]. Clarifying whether MS is associated with distinct cytogenetic or molecular findings may help refine test selection and improve counseling for affected families.

Accordingly, we conducted a retrospective cohort study of pregnancies with fetal hydrops to compare the diagnostic yield of prenatal genetic testing between cases with and without MS and to describe the distribution of cytogenetic and molecular diagnoses across groups. We also evaluated group differences in genetic testing uptake and test‐type utilization.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of pregnancies referred to our tertiary fetal center from March 2012 through July 2023 for prenatal diagnosis of fetal hydrops. The protocol was approved by the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB #H‐37494), which granted a waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligible cases were all pregnancies with non‐immune hydrops confirmed on ultrasound performed at our center. Hydrops was defined as pathologic fluid in ≥ 2 fetal compartments (any combination of ascites, pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, or skin edema ≥ 5 mm).

Ultrasound Protocol and Timing

All referrals for suspected hydrops underwent a standardized ultrasound scan that included a detailed anatomical survey, cervical length, amniotic fluid and fetal Doppler assessment. Gestational age at diagnosis was anchored to the first ultrasound at our center that confirmed hydrops.

Multidisciplinary Evaluation and Ancillary Testing

Care was coordinated through a multidisciplinary team, which in most cases included both maternal‐fetal medicine and genetic counseling services. Additional work‐up followed clinical indications and institutional pathways, and could include fetal echocardiography, targeted maternal infectious serologies (parvovirus B19, cytomegalovirus [CMV], Toxoplasma gondii, and Treponema pallidum), screening for fetal anemia, and other testing at the discretion of the care team.

Definition of Mirror Syndrome

Mirror syndrome was assigned when fetal hydrops co‐occurred with new or worsening maternal hypertension and clinically significant generalized edema (e.g., rapid onset, excessive weight gain, hemodilution, or pulmonary edema), as determined by the treating clinician [1]. Worsening hypertension was defined as an acute change from baseline blood pressure control. Because mirror syndrome and pre‐eclampsia share overlapping clinical features, each diagnosis was confirmed by independent chart review using the predefined study criteria. Two maternal–fetal medicine specialists (B.A.B. and C.M.P.) independently reviewed each chart to ensure consistent classification across the cohort. Cases requiring consensus were reviewed with the senior author (A.A.N.). Adjudicators were blinded to prenatal and postnatal genetic testing results at the time of mirror syndrome classification.

Genetic Testing Workflow

Diagnostic genetic evaluation was offered according to fetal phenotype. Testing could include karyotype, chromosomal microarray (CMA), a next‐generation sequencing (NGS) gene panel, or exome sequencing. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was added when rapid aneuploidy assessment was clinically useful. Prenatal samples were obtained from amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling. Parental samples for trio analyses were collected when indicated. In cases without prenatal diagnostic testing or non‐diagnostic but incomplete prenatal genetic testing (e.g., negative CMA but no sequencing), postnatal genetic testing was offered at the discretion of the postnatal genetics consult service, including trio exome and trio genome sequencing. Nine of twelve prenatal exomes were performed under an independent NIH‐supported institutional research protocol.

Variant Interpretation and Definition of Diagnostic Yield

All testing was performed in CAP/CLIA certified laboratories with result interpretation in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and Association for Molecular Pathology guidelines [27, 28]. For study purposes, diagnostic yield was defined as the proportion of tested cases with a sequence‐based or chromosomal pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) result that explained the hydrops phenotype.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the diagnostic yield of genetic testing. Secondary outcomes were (1) genetic testing rates by MS status and (2) the distribution of genetic diagnoses by category (aneuploidy, copy‐number variants, and single‐gene disorders).

Data Collection and Management

Demographic, clinical, sonographic, laboratory, and genetic data were abstracted from electronic medical records and managed in REDCap (Baylor College of Medicine). Analyses used available data; denominators are shown as n/N where applicable.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were compared using Pearson's chi‐square (χ 2) or Fisher's exact test and are presented as n (%). Continuous variables were assessed for normality with the Shapiro‐Wilk test; Student's t‐test (mean ± SD) or Mann‐Whitney U (median [IQR]) was applied as appropriate. Bivariate analyses compared genetic testing rates and findings between mirror and non‐mirror syndrome groups. Two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between maternal mirror syndrome and diagnostic yield of genetic testing, adjusting for whether exome sequencing was performed. Analyses were performed in R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

As this study included cases spanning 12 years, we examined the frequency of various testing modalities over time, including Karyotype, Microarray, exome/genome, and gene panels (Figure S1). When binned by era (2012–2017 vs. 2018–2023), there was a non‐significant increase in exome sequencing and genome sequencing among cases from the latter years of the study.

The primary outcome, the diagnostic yield of genetic testing, was also similar between the groups. Among those who underwent diagnostic genetic testing, abnormal genetic findings that explained the hydrops phenotype were identified in 7 of 24 (29%; 95% CI, 13%–51%) mirror syndrome cases compared with 37 of 116 (32%; 95% CI, 24%–41%) non‐mirror cases (p = 0.79). Progression to mirror syndrome was not significantly associated with a genetic diagnosis, even when accounting for the increased rate of exome testing among mirror cases aOR 0.75 (95% CI, 0.27–1.89).

Aneuploidies, copy‐number variants, and single‐gene disorders were represented in both mirror and non‐mirror cohorts (Figure 1). Among single gene disorders, RASopathies and cardiomyopathies were common, occurring in 6/17 (35%) and 5/17 (29%) of cases diagnosed with a monogenic disorder, respectively. Ciliopathies, disorders of NOTCH signaling, and overgrowth syndromes, among others, were also identified. When stratified by mirror syndrome, 3/5 (60%) of monogenic disorders among mirror cases were RASopathies (HRAS, PTPN11, RAF1), while 3/12 (25%) of monogenic disorders among non‐mirror cases were RASopathies (HRAS, PTPN11, RIT1) (p = 0.28). Overall, the types of genetic diagnoses identified were broadly similar between mirror and non‐mirror hydrops cases. Among the MS group, the following diagnoses were identified: familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (MYH7), Costello syndrome (HRAS), left‐ventricular non‐compaction (TPM1), holoprosencephaly (ZIC2), and Noonan syndrome (RAF1 and PTPN11) (Table 3). Notably, clinically relevant variants in MYH7, HRAS and PTPN11 were found in the mirror and non‐mirror groups (Table 3, Table S1).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of pregnancies complicated by fetal hydrops, the diagnostic yield of pre‐ or postnatal genetic testing was similar in cases with MS and without MS. Testing uptake did not meaningfully differ between groups, and the distribution of etiologies, including aneuploidy, pathogenic copy‐number variants, and single‐gene disorders, were comparable.

These findings support the view that MS reflects a maternal response to severe fetal and placental disease rather than a condition defined by a distinct fetal genetic mechanism. Across the entire cohort, most single‐gene diagnoses involved cardiovascular and lymphatic pathways, which is consistent with known contributors to hydrops, and this pattern did not vary by mirror syndrome status. Clinically, test selection and pretest counseling should follow standard hydrop algorithms based on fetal phenotype, irrespective of the presence of MS.

In this cohort, RASopathies were the most commonly identified monogenic disorders, followed closely by cardiomyopathies. RASopathies are commonly identified among cases of non‐immune hydrops fetalis. In a systematic review and meta‐analysis, Makhamreh et al. identified a RASopathy in 23% of non‐immune hydrops cases with a confirmed genetic diagnosis [29]. Recent case reports have linked RASopathies to mirror syndrome; however, there are few studies, if any, that systematically evaluate genetic etiologies among mirror syndrome cases [30, 31]. In this study, 60% of mirror cases with a monogenic disorder had a RASopathy, compared to 25% of non‐mirror cases with a monogenic disorder. Although these proportions were not statistically significant, possibly due to a small sample size, these findings warrant future study.

This study has several strengths, including uniform confirmation of non‐immune hydrops, an explicit operational definition of MS applied by maternal‐fetal medicine specialists, a pre‐specified primary outcome of diagnostic yield, and granular categorization of cytogenetic and single‐gene diagnoses across contemporary testing platforms.

There are also limitations. The retrospective design introduces potential selection and information bias because a subset of patients did not undergo genetic testing. Testing strategies evolved over the study period from 2012 to 2023, including clinical availability of more extensive phenotype‐driven NGS panels and prenatal exome sequencing. As all testing was performed clinically, evolving scientific evidence and society guidelines supporting molecular testing in hydrops cases affected the likelihood of obtaining insurance coverage for such testing, especially toward the latter part of the study period. These factors may have introduced era effects. Finally, as this was a retrospective study derived from a convenience sample, our study is not powered to detect small differences in the diagnostic yield between mirror and non‐mirror cases, as can be seen from the confidence intervals. A larger sample would have increased power to detect small differences in diagnostic yield.

Future work should prospectively evaluate standardized, phenotype‐driven testing pathways across centers, such as concurrent cytogenetic and molecular testing modalities or moving to genome sequencing as a first‐tier test. Studies should also incorporate consistent trio sequencing to optimize interpretation and pair prenatal genomics with maternal biomarkers of endothelial dysfunction and placental pathology to refine the mechanistic understanding. Linking prenatal results with postnatal outcomes and, when available, autopsy or placental genomic data could further clarify causal pathways.

We found no difference in diagnostic yield or in the distribution of cytogenetic and molecular findings between hydrops with and without MS. These results support a uniform, phenotype‐driven genetic evaluation for all non‐immune hydrops pregnancies, with counseling guided by fetal presentation.

Conclusion

The rates and types of genetic diagnoses were similar between non‐immune hydrops cases with and without concomitant MS. This suggests that no single molecular mechanism fully explains the pathogenesis of mirror syndrome. Despite this study's limited ability to detect subtle differences between groups, these findings support a similar approach to genetic counseling and evaluation for hydrops cases with and without mirror syndrome.
