New insights into the evolution analysis of trihelix gene family in eggplant (Solanum melongena L.) and expression analysis under abiotic stress
Authors: Yanhong Lan, Fangyi Gong, Chun Li, Feng Xia, Yifan Li, Xiaojun Liu, Duchen Liu, Genyun Liang, Chao Fang, Peng Cai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrihliex transcription factors (TFs) play crucial roles in plant growth and development, stress response, and plant hormone signaling network transmission. In order to comprehensively investigate the functions of trihliex genes in eggplant development and the abiotic stress response, we conducted an extensive analysis of the trihliex gene family in the eggplant genome.ResultsIn this study, 30 trihelix gene family members were unevenly distributed on 12 chromosomes. On the basis of their phylogenetic relationships, these genes were conserved in different plant species and could be divided into six subfamilies, with trihelix genes within the same subfamily sharing similar structures. The promoter regions of trihelix genes contained cis-acting elements related to plant growth and development, plant hormones, and abiotic stress responses, suggesting potential applications in the development of more resistant crops. Selective pressure assessments indicated that trihliex genes have undergone purifying selection pressure. Expression analysis on the basis of transcriptomic profiles revealed that SmGT18, SmGT29, SmGT6, and SmGT28 are highly expressed in roots, leaves, flowers, and fruits, respectively. Expression analysis via quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR) revealed that most trihelix genes respond to low temperature, abscisic acid (ABA), and salicylic acid (SA), with SmGT29 exhibiting significant upregulation under cold stress conditions. The SmGT29 gene was subsequently successfully cloned from eggplant, which was located in the nucleus, robust transcriptional activity, and a protein molecular weight of 74.59 kDa. On the basis of these findings, SmGT29 was postulated to be a pivotal candidate gene that actively responds to biotic stress stimuli, thereby supporting the plant’s innate stress resistance mechanisms.ConclusionIn summary, this study was the first report on trihelix genes and their potential roles in eggplant plants. These results provided valuable insights for enhancing stress resistance and quality traits in eggplant breeding, thereby serving as a crucial reference for future improvement efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10959-y.

Background

Transcription factors (TFs) are special structural proteins that regulate plant growth and development [1], and they are known as trans-acting factors. These TFs can bind to specific DNA sequences (cis-acting elements) in the upstream promoter region of target genes [2], thereby regulating their specific expression in plants under different spatial, tissue, cellular, or environmental conditions [3]. With the continuous development of molecular biology and genetics technologies, significant progress had been made in the research of transcription factors. Researchers had gradually revealed the specific mechanisms of transcription factors in plant growth and development as well as stress response through methods such as gene cloning, expression analysis, and functional identification. Currently, various TFs such as MYB [4], bHLH [5], AP2/EREBP [6], NAC [7], and WRKY [8] had been reported to play crucial regulatory roles in different plants [9]. According to research statistics, more than 60 types of TFs had been discovered in plants [10]. However, the current research on trihelix TFs in plants is still not comprehensive or sufficient.

Trihelix TFs are widely distributed in the plant kingdom and are unique TFs that are specific to plants. They also exhibit high conservation among different species and can specifically recognize the GT element on the promoter, which is also known as the GT factor [11]. Its name originated from the conserved structural domain of the unique trihelix structure (helix-loop-helix-loop-helix) specific to the trihelix gene family, and similar structural domains have also been found in the MYB protein family [12]. However, the amino acid sequences inserted between the helix structures in the structural domain of trihelix family members are longer than those in MYB, resulting in differences in the three-dimensional structure of the proteins, and the gaps between helix pairs result in different recognition sequences between GT factors and Myb/SANT-LIKE proteins [13, 14]. The first identified trihelix gene, GT-1, was found in pea (Pisum sativum L.) by Green et al. [15] and subsequently recognized and identified in Arabidopsis and tobacco [16, 17]. On the basis of the conservation of protein domains in the phylogenetic tree, trihelix TFs were divided into six subfamilies, namely GT-1, GT-2, GTγ, GT-δ, SH4, and SIP1 [18]. The GT element of trihelix genes was present in the promoters of multiple light-regulated genes and was also present in the promoters of many biotic and abiotic stress response genes [19, 20]. Additionally, there was a complex regulatory relationship between the trihelix TFs and the GT element, enabling trihelix genes to perform diverse biological functions [11].

In plants, trihelix TFs could regulate plant growth and development, as well as plant responses to various environmental stresses, by modulating the expression of related genes to increase plant stress resistance. The overexpression of the TaGT22L1D gene affected the development and growth of floral organs in wheat [21]. In addition, trihelix genes could also regulate the activity of plant meristems, affecting the morphogenesis of plants [22]. The role of trihelix TFs in the response to various stress environments such as drought, high temperature, and salt stress was particularly important. The GhGT26 protein could bind to site1-type GT cis-elements (GT-3a) as well as MYB elements MRE3 and MRE4, enhancing the tolerance of transgenic Arabidopsis plants to salt stress [23]. Furthermore, a loss-of-function analysis of GTL1 revealed intriguing differences between gtl1 mutants and wild-type plants. Specifically, the mutants presented a reduced number of stomata, leading to comparatively lower rates of water loss and consequently, greater tolerance to drought stress than their wild-type counterparts did, as documented in [24]. The expression of the FaGT-2-like gene could be suppressed by treatments with high salt, drought, low temperature, and ABA in strawberry, indicating a negative response to abiotic stress [25]. On the other hand, MdSIP1-2 promoted lateral root development, ABA sensitivity, and tolerance to drought and salt stress, suggesting that MdSIP1-2 may play a crucial role in regulating apple growth and abiotic stress responses [26]. ShCIGT, through its interaction with SnRK1, jointly regulates the expression of stress-related genes under cold and drought stress, thereby increasing the stress resistance of tomatoes [27]. Members of the Arabidopsis GT-2 subfamily, such as AtGT2L, were highly expressed in petals and leaves. These genes respond to cold stress and drought stress by increasing their expression levels. Additionally, AtGT26 was activated in Arabidopsis inflorescences and leaves and responds to cold stress, salt stress, and drought stress [28]. In terms of functional research, trihelix TFs were able to form complex regulatory networks through interactions with other TFs, signaling molecules, and proteins, precisely regulating plant growth and development. There was an interaction between AtGT2L in Arabidopsis and the regulatory protein Ca2+, which enhanced the resistance of the model plant Arabidopsis to high salt stress through their interaction [29]. Although trihelix TFs had been identified in some plant species, many questions remain that need further investigation regarding their specific functions and regulatory mechanisms, their interactions with other transcription factors, and the identification of downstream target genes.

Eggplant (Solanum melongena L.), an important vegetable crop, is widely cultivated globally, especially in China, where its cultivation area has been continuously expanding in recent years [30]. Its significance lies in its substantial contribution to farmers’ income enhancement and rural revitalization efforts. Eggplants, which are rich in nutrients, are deeply loved by consumers [31]. However, eggplant is highly susceptible to environmental factors during its growth process, particularly water stress and temperature stress, which are major causes of reduced quality and yield. In recent years, modern molecular biology methods, high-throughput sequencing, and genetic engineering technologies have led to continuous improvements in eggplant genome data, promoting the mining of excellent stress resistance genes in eggplants. The trihelix gene family had been reported in most plants, but research on this gene family in eggplant has yet to be conducted.

In the present study, the trihelix gene family was identified in eggplant. The chromosomal distributions, protein characteristics, gene structures, and conserved motif compositions of the identified trihelix genes were analyzed. We then identified orthologous relationships, analyzed gene duplication events, and constructed phylogenetic trees of the identified trihelix genes. Using public transcriptome data from eggplant tissue and our eggplant cold stress RNA-seq data, we analyzed the spatial and temporal expression and differential expression profiles of eggplant trihelix genes under abiotic stresses. In addition, our qRT‒PCR results validated the supposition that eggplant trihelix genes participate in various abiotic stress responses. Our research provides valuable clues for future functional characterization of trihelix genes in eggplant.

Materials and methods

Identification and physicochemical properties of trihelix TFs

To identify possible trihelix TFs in six Solanaceae species (S. melongena, Solanum lycopersicum, Solanum pennellii, Solanum pimpinellifolium, Solanum tuberosum, and Capsicum annuum), we downloaded all the corresponding genome sequences and protein sequences from the Solanaceae Genomics Network (SGN, https://solgenomics.net/, accessed on 6 December 2022) [32]. Arabidopsis and Oryza sativa data were downloaded from the Information Resource (TAIR, https://www.arabidopsis.org/index.jsp, accessed on 6 December 2022) and Phytozome 13 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/, accessed on 6 December 2022), respectively. The candidate trihelix TFs were identified in a local database via BLASTp. E values of 10 − 5 and > 200-bit scores were kept cut off to identify putative trihelix TFs, and the BLASTp output was tabulated [33]. The conserved domain database (CDD) (https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd, accessed on 8 January 2020) [34], Pfam (PF13837) (http://pfam.xfam.org/, accessed on 8 January 2021) [35], and SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de, accessed on 8January 2021) [36] were used to confirm the identity of trihelix proteins containing conserved domains of the trihelix family. The physical and chemical properties of the candidate proteins were analyzed via ExPASy (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/, accessed on 3 October 2021). The WoLF PSORT online software (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) was used to predict the subcellular localization of the eggplant trihelix gene [37].

Phylogenetic reconstruction and classification

The amino acid sequences of the abovementioned 251 trihelix TFs were used for phylogenetic analysis. Multiple sequence alignments of all selected trihelix amino acid sequences were performed using ClustalW with default parameters. The neighbor-joining (NJ) method was used to construct unrooted phylogenetic trees of the trihelix TFs using MEGA v11 software (https://www.megasoftware.net/) [38]. The phylogenetic trees were edited using FigTree v1.4.3 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/, accessed on 13 June 2019).

Gene structure analysis and identification of conserved motifs

The protein sequences of all the genes were imported into the MEME tool (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme; accessed on 17 July 2009) [39] to analyze the conserved motifs, considering the fourteen maximum most conserved motifs predicted for each sequence. The intron‒exon structures of the TFs were obtained on the basis of the gene annotation Gff3 files and uploaded to the Gene Structure Display Server 2.0 (http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn/; accessed on 10December 2014) [40]. 2000 bp promoter sequences upstream of the initiation codon of the trihelix genes were extracted. The PLANTCARE database (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/; accessed on 10 January 2002) [41] was used to analyze the cis-regulatory elements of the transcription factors. Finally, the graphics of the structures were drawn using TBtools software [42].

Chromosomal location, gene duplication events and synteny analysis

Chromosomal location information for every trihelix gene was obtained from the gene annotation Gff3 file. TBools software was used to construct chromosomal distribution images of the genes. The duplicated gene pairs among the plants were identified via MCScanX [43], with an E value of 1 × e − 5 in the BlastP search [33]. For the selective force analysis of a duplication gene pair, the nonsynonymous (Ka)/synonymous (Ks) substitution (Ka/Ks) rates (Ka/Ks < 1, purifying; or Ka/Ks > 1, positive) during evolution [44] were calculated via TBtools. The syntenic relationships between identified genes were visualized using TBtools [42].

Analysis of codon usage patterns of trihelix genes

The codon usage characteristics of the eggplant trihelix genes were thoroughly analyzed using the CodonW 1.4.2 software (http://codonw.sourceforge.net, accessed March 15th, 2022) [45]. To assess the base composition preference, various metrics were employed, including the frequency of optimal codons (FOP), the codon adaptation index (CAI), the overall GC content of codons, and the GC content of codons specifically coding for the same amino acid at the third position (GC3S). Additionally, the effective number of codons (ENC), GC1, GC2, and GC3 values of the eggplant trihelix family genes were computed using the CUSP and CHIP online tools available within EMBOSS (http://embossgui.sourceforge.net/demo/, accessed March 16th, 2022), further enabling the evaluation of codon usage preferences.

Gene expression analysis

Data for the tissue-specific expression of trihelix genes were obtained from Barchi et al. [46]. The raw transcriptome data were downloaded and reanalyzed to obtain an expression matrix. The detailed analysis pipeline was as follows: the read data were first aligned to the eggplant genome using STAR 2.7.9a software [47], quantified via featureCounts [48], and then normalized via DESeq2 [49]. The RNA-seq data for cold treatment were obtained from our laboratory [50]. Finally, the heatmap was plotted via TBtools [42].

Growth conditions and RNA extraction

Two S. melongena varieties, march’s early white-flowered eggplant (“E7134”) and true black eggplant (“E7145”), and eggplant varieties (two eggplant materials were preserved and provided by the Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences) were selected for cold treatment in this study. For the low-temperature treatment experiment, eggplant seedlings were subjected to 10 °C during the day and 5 °C at night, with a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod until the fourth leaves were fully expanded. Total RNA was extracted from eggplant leaves after 0, 1, 2, 4, and 7 d and then stored at − 80 °C before RNA extraction. The seedlings grown under normal conditions were used as a control, and three biological replicates were performed.

For the ABA and SA treatments, 54-day-old seedlings were sprayed with 100 µM ABA and 1 mM SA on their leaves, respectively. The leaves of the control and treated plants were sampled at 0, 6, 12, and 48 h after treatment. All the samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. Leaves from 2 varieties were pooled, and three biological replicates were performed.

The total RNA of the plantlets was extracted via TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA samples were then assessed by qRT − PCR using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara). Actin was used as an internal control gene. Three biological replicates (each containing three plants) and three technical replicates were measured for each treatment. The relative expression level of a gene was calculated according to the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers used for qRT − PCR analysis were listed in Table S1.

Transcriptional activity and protein expression analysis of SmGT29

For the transcriptional activity experiment, the coding region of SmGT29 was amplified and fused in-frame downstream of the GAL4 DNA binding domain in the pGBKT7 vector; the primers used were listed in the Table S2. The constructs were subsequently transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain AH109 (Clontech) and subsequently grown on SD/-Trp/-Leu. The transcriptional activity was examined on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade media supplemented with x-a-gal. To further demonstrate that SmGT29 was a transcription factor, subcellular localization experiments were conducted. The full-length CDS of SmGT29 was inserted into GFP, the recombinant plasmid SmGT29-GFP was transformed into tobacco leaves using the Agrobacterium strain GV3101. The fluorescence of the fusion proteins was detected via a Leica laser scanning confocal microscopy (Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

The full-length CDS of SmGT29 was inserted into the pET-28a (+) plasmid (P0023, Miaoling Bioscience & Technology Co., Ltd., China). The recombinant E. coli-pET-28a (+)-SmGT29 cells were induced with isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) at low temperature and then disrupted by ultrasonication, followed by centrifugation to obtain the supernatant. The recombinant proteins were purified by loading onto a Ni-Sepharose 6FF column (GE Healthcare, USA). The purity and molecular weight of R-SmGT29 were determined via 7% (W V1) sodium dodecyl sulfate − polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS − PAGE), with the protein bands were visualized via Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining.

Results

Genome-wide identification of trihelix genes

A total of 30 nonredundant trihelix TFs were obtained from the whole genome of eggplant, and their specific domains were confirmed via NCBI-CDD and SMART, namely, SmGT1 ~ SmGT30, all of which contained Myb_DNA-bind_4 (Fig. S1). The physical and chemical properties of the putative trihelix TFs were analyzed using EXPASy software (https://web.EXPASy.org/protparam, accessed on 8 December 2022). The lengths of the proteins ranged from 175 aa (SmGT10) to 98 4aa (SmGT20), molecular weights vary between 19.858 kDa (SmGT10) and 110.156 kDa (SmGT20), the isoelectric points ranged from 4.64 (SmGT7) to 9.95 (SmGT11), with 16 proteins having isoelectric points below 7 and being rich in acidic amino acids, and the remaining 14 proteins having isoelectric points above 7 and being rich in basic amino acids. The instability index ranged from 39.09 (SmGT26) to 75 (SmGT11), the aliphatic index ranged from 52.9 (SmGT22) to 90.85 (SmGT1), grand average of hydropathicity range from − 1.173 (SmGT7) to -0.285 (SmGT1). Subcellular localization analysis revealed that SmGT1, SmGT4, SmGT12, SmGT19, SmGT21, and SmGT26 located were specifically in the chloroplast, and the remaining 24 proteins were located in the nucleus.

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of trihelix TFs

To explore the phylogenetic relationships and evolutionary patterns of trihelix TFs among the 6 Solanaceae species, Arabidopsis, and O. sativa, an unrooted phylogenetic tree was conducted on the basis of the aligned full-length amino acid sequences of all the proteins via MEGA v11.0 (Fig. 1). The phylogenetic tree revealed that all of the trihelix genes are divided into six subfamilies, namely GT-1, GT-2, GT-γ, GT-δ, SH4, and SIP1, and revealed an uneven distribution of trihelix genes among the different subfamilies. These subfamilies contain 4, 8, 3, 1, 4, and 10 trihelix family members in eggplant, respectively. SIP1 was the largest subfamily, containing 10 eggplant trihelix members, whereas the GTδ subfamily was the smallest, containing only 1 member. These results suggested that eggplant trihelix genes are distributed unevenly among the different subfamilies.

Gene structure and motif analyses

To clarify the structural and compositional characteristics of trihelix TFs, the conserved motif and gene structure were analyzed (Fig. 2a). A total of 14 conserved motifs were identified among the trihelix TFs (E value < 10) (Fig. 2b, Fig. S2). There were significant differences in the number and types of conserved motifs among the 30 eggplant trihelix TFs. All the sequences containing motif 1 and motif 2 appeared in the GT-2 and SIP1 subfamilies, whereas motif 3 was exclusive to SIP1. Motif 4 was present in the GT-1, SH4, and GT-2 subfamilies. Motif 6 was found in all subfamilies except for SIP1. Motif 7 and motif 8 were observed in only some members of the GT-2 subfamily. Both the SH4 and GT-γ subfamilies contained motif 9, whereas the SmGT11, SmGT17, and SmGT23 included motif 10. The GT-2 subfamily presented a greater variety of motif sequences. SmGT6 had only 3 motifs: motif 6, motif 1, and motif 14, which differed significantly from the other sequences. These results indicated that genes within the same subfamily possess similar and specific motifs, while different subfamilies presented distinct conserved motifs. These findings provided a basis for the functional diversity of trihelix genes in eggplant and offer a reference for studying the functional differentiation among subfamilies.

To further understand the diversity of eggplant trihelix genes, the intron/exon structure of the trihelix genes was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 2c, the trihelix gene structure was complex, with a wide range of variations in gene length, exon number, and intron length. Among the 30 eggplant trihelix genes, the GT-1 subfamily had one intron in all the genes except SmGT15. In the GT-2 subfamily, SmGT14 and SmGT29 contained one intron, SmGT1 had 9 introns, and the other genes did not have introns. Within the GTγ and SIP1 subfamilies, SmGT5, SmGT10, and SmGT30 all lacked introns. The SH4 subfamily presented a varying numbers of introns ranging from 0 to 3, whereas the SIP1 subfamily presented 0 to 14 introns.

Analysis of promoter elements in the trihelix genes

Various cis-acting elements responsive to growth and development, hormones, and environmental signals had been identified in eggplant trihelix genes. The results revealed the existence of 19 cis-acting elements in varying numbers (Fig. 3a). Among them, the G-box element was the most abundant, with 39 instances, whereas the AuxRR motif element was the least common, with only 5 instances. On the basis of their functional roles, these elements could be roughly categorized into four types: light-responsive elements, growth- and development-related elements, hormone-related elements, and stress-related elements (Fig. 3b). Auxin-responsive elements (TCA-elements), growth- and development-responsive elements (CAT-elements), defense and stress elements (TC-rich repeats), and jasmonic acid (JA)-responsive elements (TGACG-motifs) were distributed across all subfamilies (Fig. 3c). Low-temperature responsive elements (LTRs) were present in 12 genes within the GT-2, GT-1, GTγ, SH4, and SIP1 subfamilies. MBS, which was a drought-responsive element, is found in all the subfamilies except for GTγ. Auxin responsive elements (TCA-elements) were present in 19 genes, whereas drought signal responsive elements (MBSs) were found in 11 genes. Additionally, the GT-2, SH4, and SIP1 subfamilies all presented jasmonic acid (JA)-, light-, ABA-, and SA-responsive elements. The above results suggested that eggplant trihelix genes may regulate growth, development, and stress responses in eggplant by participating in different signaling pathways.

Chromosomal locations, gene duplication and synteny analysis

The distribution and location information of trihelix genes on chromosomes provided important references for studying the evolution and functions of trihelix TFs. Genome chromosomal location analysis revealed that the 30 trihelix genes were distributed on all 12 chromosomes of eggplant (Fig. 4a). Chromosome 1 and chromosome 4 contained only one gene; by comparison, chromosomes 3, 5, 8, and 9 contained only four genes, while members of the trihelix genes were not mapped to chromosome 12. The distribution of eggplant trihelix genes on chromosomes was inhomogeneous, with a bias toward one end of the chromosomes. Additionally, the aggregation of multiple trihelix genes in close proximity to the same physical location on a chromosome suggests the occurrence of replication events among the members of the eggplant trihelix genes. To reveal the evolutionary mechanism of the trihelix genes, duplication events were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 4b, two pairs of segmental repeat events were detected in the eggplant trihelix genes, and no tandem duplication events were detected.

To further explore the evolutionary relationships among trihelix TFs across species, a synteny map between eggplant and four representative species was constructed to explore the evolution of trihelix genes in eggplant. We found that 58 trihelix genes were collinear with those of Arabidopsis, rice, tomato, and pepper, and the number of homologous pairs among these species was 15, 2, 27, and 25, respectively (Fig. 4c; Table S3). The Ka/Ks ratios of trihelix gene collinearity pairs were both greater than 0 and less than 1, indicating that these genes were subjected to significant purification selection pressure during the evolutionary process. The Ka/ Ks ratios of trihelix gene collinearity pairs between eggplant and Arabidopsis were significantly less than 0.2, substantially lower than those of other collinearity pairs (Fig. 4d). These results suggested that the trihelix genes undergo more rigorous purification selection, thereby rendering them less prone to mutational variation.

Analysis of the base number and codon usage bias of trihelix genes

The results indicated that among the trihelix genes, U3s and A3s were generally more prevalent than were G3s and C3s. Specifically, the average GC content was 43%, with GC3s accounting for 47% and AT3s accounting for 52% (Fig. 5a; Table S4). This suggested a preference for synonymous codons terminating in A/U among trihelix genes. The effective number of codons (ENC) ranged from 50.36 to 60.5, with 30 coding DNA sequences (CDS) exhibiting ENC values exceeding 50, encompassing 100% of the dataset (Fig. 5b). This finding indicated that the trihelix genes presented a relatively weak codon usage bias in encoding amino acids. The codon adaptation index (CAI) varied from 0.139 to 0.246, with an average value of 0.194, with the trihelix genes displaying the highest expression level. Furthermore, the Frequency of optimal codons (Fop) ranged from 0.298 to 0.438, with an average value of 0.379 (Fig. 5; Table S4).

To further investigate the codon usage patterns, the RSCU values of the trihelix genes were calculated separately (Fig. 5c). The results revealed that there was little difference in the RSCU values among the 30 trihelix genes. An RSCU value greater than 1 indicated greater codon usage bias, whereas a value less than 1 indicated a nonpreferred codon. Among the eggplant trihelix genes, 45.76% of the codons presented an RSCU > 1. Among them, five codons, AGA, AGG, UUG, GUU, and GCU, had RSCU values greater than 1.6, indicating that they were highly preferred (Fig. 5c). The PR2 plot analysis revealed deviations from 0.5 in the values of A3/ (A3 + T3) or G3/ (G3 + C3), indicating that the frequency of usage of the bases U, A, G, and C at the third position of the codon was unequal. This result suggested that both mutation pressure and natural selection contribute to shaping the codon preferences of trihelix genes, as depicted in Fig. 5d. The correlation between GC12 and GC3 was statistically significant (p < 0.05), suggesting that the codon preference of the walnut trihelix genes was influenced primarily by mutation pressure (Fig. 5e).

Expression profile analysis of eggplant trihelix genes

To investigate the tissue-specific expression patterns of eggplant trihelix genes, transcriptome sequencing data from the eggplant plant genome database [46] were utilized to analyze their expression profiles across four distinct tissues: roots, leaves, flowers, and fruits. After the gene expression data were normalized, a log2 value greater than 1 was considered specific gene expression, whereas a log2 value less than or equal to 1 was considered nonspecific gene expression [51]. This analysis revealed distinct expression patterns among different groups of trihelix genes, as depicted in Fig. 6. The heatmap clearly indicated that most trihelix genes are expressed in all tissues at various developmental stages, with the exception of SmGT26 and SmGT30. Nevertheless, the expression patterns of each individual gene exhibited notable variations. SmGT7, SmGT15, SmGT16, SmGT25, and SmGT28 were highly expressed in fruit stage 3 (Fig. 6a); however, their expression levels were very low in other organizations, which suggests that these genes play universal roles in the development and ripening process of fruit eggplants. SmGT2 and SmGT18 were highly expressed in only the roots; SmGT2 was highly expressed in leaves; SmGT3, SmGT6, and SmGT10 were highly expressed in the flowers. Trihelix genes from the same cluster, such as SmGT7/16, SmGT1/14, and SmGT12/13, presented similar expression patterns in different tissues.

Our research group meticulously measured the transcriptomes of cold-tolerant (“E7134”) and cold-sensitive (“E7145”) eggplant varieties and subjected them to cold stress conditions. Among the trihelix genes, genes that presented differential expression patterns in response to cold stress were identified. As shown in Fig. 6b, a total of 21 genes (comprising 70%) presented altered expression levels under cold stress. Trihelix genes exhibited distinct regulatory patterns in cold-tolerant and cold-sensitive eggplant varieties. Notably, only a subset of genes demonstrated presented increased expression levels following stress treatment. After a period of 7 days under cold stress, the number of upregulated genes among the trihelix genes outweighed the number of downregulated genes. Among these genes, SmGT29 exhibited the most prominent upregulation, indicating its significant role in the cold stress response.

To validate the expression patterns of trihelix genes under cold stress, eggplant seedlings were subjected to cold stress. A total of 10 family members were randomly selected for RT − qPCR. The results indicated that the expression levels of members of the trihelix genes (excluding SmGT16, SmGT17, SmGT25, and SmGT27) significantly increased after 1 d of cold treatment, with higher expression levels observed in the cold-tolerant variety “E7134” than in the cold-sensitive variety “E7145” (except for SmGT12 and SmGT27) (Fig. 7a). The expression of SmGT29 was significantly greater in “E7134” than in the control under cold stress, increasing nearly 10-fold. The expression patterns of SmGT1 and SmGT24 were similar, exhibiting fluctuating changes. The expression levels of these genes significantly increased after the first day but decreased significantly on 2nd and 4th days. Additionally, the transcription levels of SmGT23 were suppressed by cold stress.

To comprehensively study the expression patterns of the trihelix genes under abiotic stress, we measured the expression levels of trihelix gene members in two materials under hormone (ABA and SA) stress. Under ABA stress, all 10 trihelix genes responded to different degrees, especially SmGT13 and SmGT16, whose expression levels significantly increased at 48 h, reaching 8-fold and 4-fold greater than those of the control, respectively. However, the expression levels of SmGT23 and SmGT25 significantly decreased with ABA stress treatment, indicating that their expression was suppressed by ABA stress (Fig. 7b). The expression level of SmGT9 significantly increased after 48 h of ABA treatment, and the expression level of the cold-tolerant material SmGT29 was greater than that of the sensitive material. Under SA stress, only SmGT12 and SmGT24 reached their peak expression levels at 48 h and 12 h, respectively, and their expression levels were higher in the “E7134” material than in the “E7145” material, reaching 8-fold and 4-fold higher than those of the control, respectively. The expression levels of SmGT16, SmGT17 and SmGT27 significantly decreased with SA stress treatment, whereas those of the other genes fluctuated at different time points (Fig. 7c). In addition, the SmGT29 gene, which was highly expressed under cold stress, reached its maximum expression level after 12 h of SA treatment and responded to SA stress.

Transcriptional activity and protein expression of SmGT29

As a candidate gene for the cold response, SmGT29 was further investigated to confirm its transcriptional activity and protein expression. First, SmGT29 functions as a transcription activator mediated by its conserved GT domain (Fig. 8a), and truncated variants of SmGT29 were constructed and fused downstream of the GAL4 binding domain in the pGBKT7 vector for yeast expression. Tests on defective yeast culture media of SD/-Trp and SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade showed that yeast expressing the full-length SmGT29 and a fragment containing only amino acid residues 356–476 grew well on these plates exhibiting robust growth and a distinct blue coloration, whereas cells expressing the 1-356 and 1-430 fragments could not grow on SD/-Trp/-His/-Ade culture media (Fig. 8b; Fig. S3; Fig. S4). To further confirm that SmGT29 functions as a factor, the subcellular localization of SmGT29 was carried out. We constructed a 35 s: SmGT29-GFP fusion reporter gene and transiently expressed it in tobacco leaves. The GFP signal was detected only in the nuclei of tobacco leaves, suggesting that SmGT29 may act as a nuclear TF (Fig. 8c). These results indicated that SmGT29 can indeed function as a transcription activator and that the presumptive transcriptional activation domain required for its function is located in the C-terminal region of the protein.

The recombinant plasmid of SmGT29 with E. coli BL21 was constructed, and the purified recombinant protein SmGT29 was obtained after IPTG-induced expression at low temperature overnight, low-temperature ultrasonic disruption, and purification by Ni-column affinity chromatography. SDS − PAGE analysis revealed that the purified recombinant protein matched the predicted molecular weight of the protein (with a fusion tag size of 20 kDa) and exhibited a single protein band (Fig. 8d; Fig. S5). These results indicated that SmGT29 is expressed at the protein level and possesses functional characteristics.

Discussion

Eggplants originate from the tropical regions of Southeast Asia and are now widely cultivated worldwide. As an important vegetable crop, eggplant is rich in nutrients such as protein, various vitamins, trace elements, and alkaloids and has high edibility benefits. The eggplant reference genome was published in 2021 [52]; however, whole-genome studies of the eggplant trihelix gene family have not been published. In the present study, a total of 30 trihelix genes were identified in eggplant (Table 1), which may be related to gene duplication during the evolution of the plant genome [53]. The number of trihelix TFs varies among different plants, with 94 members in wheat [54], 41 members in rice [55], and 52 members in Brassica rapa [56]. Analysis of the physicochemical properties of the eggplant trihelix genes revealed significant differences in the amino acid sequence length, molecular weight, and isoelectric point of their encoded proteins. Subcellular localization predictions revealed that 80% of the eggplant trihelix genes are located in the nucleus, which was consistent with the function of trihelix genes as TFs regulating gene expression. Phylogenetic tree analysis revealed that eggplant trihelix TFs can be divided into six subfamilies, demonstrating the uneven distribution of trihelix genes among different subfamilies, namely, GT-1, GT-2, GT-γ, GT-δ, SH4, and SIP1 (Fig. 1) [57–59]. This finding is consistent with the results of Wang et al. regarding the classification of trihelix genes and the distribution of members in each subfamily in Arabidopsis and Setaria italia [18, 60]. Compared with the other subfamilies, the SmSIP1 group (10, 30.0%) has more members, indicating that those SmSIP1 members may have undergone stronger partial differentiation in the long-term evolutionary process.

The phylogenetic tree analysis in this study revealed the presence of homologous trihelix genes between eggplant and Arabidopsis. For example, the SmGT18 gene from the GT-2 subfamily is homologous to the AtGTL1 (AT1G33240) gene. The trihelix TF AT1G33240 had been proven to suppress glandular trichome development in Arabidopsis [22]. Additionally, AT1G33240 suppressed the expression of the SDD1 gene, increasing stomatal density (SD) in the leaf epidermis, which further affected plant water use efficiency [24]. The SmGT8 gene from the GT-1 subfamily was homologous to the AtGT-3b gene, which had been shown to respond significantly to both pathogenic and salt stress [61]. Furthermore, the SmGT27 gene from the GT-2 subfamily in eggplant was homologous to the AtGT2L (AT5G28300) gene in Arabidopsis. AtGT2L had been shown to be involved in the response to salt stress, low temperature, and abscisic acid stress. It had also been demonstrated that this gene binds to the AGAG-box to regulate the expression of stress tolerance genes, increasing proline content and the ability to scavenge reactive oxygen species. It also regulated stomatal size to reduce the water loss rate, increasing tolerance to salt and osmotic stress [29]. Therefore, these genes that are homologous between eggplant and Arabidopsis may have similar functions. In addition, on the basis of whole-genome analyses, a total of 30 trihliex genes were identified in eggplant, exhibiting a diverse range of intron counts, ranging from 1 to 14. The results revealed that a certain number of introns can increase the length of genes and the frequency of recombination between genes, which is beneficial to the evolution of species. However, intron-free genes tend to respond quickly to changes in the environment. The members belonging to the same clade usually share similar motif compositions and exon/intron structures, and they may have similar functions. In summary, our results revealed the conservation of eggplant trihelix gene evolution and provided references for further studies on the functions of trihelix genes.

The trihelix genes within the reported subfamilies may play a fundamental role in tissue development, environmental adaptation and gene evolution in dissimilar plant species [62]. The expression patterns of trihelix genes during the growth and development of different tissues revealed that members of the trihelix genes exhibit certain tissue-specific expression patterns. For example, SmGT29 was highly expressed in leaves, while the expression levels of SmGT3/12/13/26/30 were zero. On the other hand, SmGT18 was abundantly expressed in roots, which aligns with similar findings reported by Wang et al. [58]. Previous studies had shown that the GT gene family is involved in various growth and development processes such as root development and fruit maturation. Therefore, the specific expression patterns of trihelix genes in different eggplant tissues may be related to their regulatory roles in normal plant growth and development. To further explore the physiological role of the trihelix gene family in environmental adaptation, we systematically analyzed the expression of 12 eggplant trihelix genes under different stress treatments, including cold, ABA, and JA. Members of the GT2 subfamily, specifically AtTH26 and SbTH15, exhibit a remarkable similarity in their motif compositions. Previous investigations revealed that AtTH26 (designated At5G28300) can be potently induced by various stress stimuli, including NaCl, drought, cold, and abscisic acid. Furthermore, its robust expression in the inflorescences and leaves of Arabidopsis plants contributes significantly to enhancing plant resilience against adverse environmental conditions [29]. ShCIGT, a cold-inducible gene meticulously isolated from wild tomato, plays a pivotal role in enhancing the ability of tomato plants to withstand and adapt to a diverse array of abiotic stresses [63]. Similarly, under cold stress conditions, we detected the differential expression of 21 trihelix genes of the trihelix gene family via RNA-seq high-throughput sequencing technology, and the changes in the expression levels of these genes under cold stress were likely associated with the cold resistance of plants [64]. Subsequently, 10 differentially expressed trihelix genes were randomly selected from the trihelix genes for RT‒qPCR analysis. These experimental data provide detailed information regarding the expression patterns and trends of trihelix genes under cold stress. Specifically, the SmGT29 gene was significantly upregulated under cold stress, suggesting that it may function as a positive regulatory factor involved in the cold response of eggplant. This gene could respond to cold stress by activating metabolic pathways related to cold resistance, regulating cell wall composition, or enhancing antioxidant capacity. On the other hand, SmGT16 and SmGT23 were significantly downregulated under cold stress, possibly indicating that they have certain other functions under normal growth conditions but are suppressed during low-temperature stress. The functions of these genes may be related to plant growth, development, or normal metabolism. Under cold stress, to prioritize the response to adversity, plants may temporarily suppress the expression of these genes to optimize resource allocation. Furthermore, by comparing the differences in the expression of trihelix genes under cold stress in varieties with different cold tolerances, we aimed to reveal their genetic variation and potential breeding value in terms of cold resistance. For example, SmGT13/17/23/24 were more highly expressed in the cold-tolerant eggplant variety “E7134” than in the cold-sensitive variety “E7145” (Fig. 7). Cold-tolerant eggplant varieties exhibit specific expression patterns of trihelix genes, which can be used as candidate genes for improving crop cold resistance.

ABA and SA are crucial signaling molecules involved in plant physiological and morphological responses. They regulate ROS levels and activate the expression of transcription factors and resistance genes during both biotic and abiotic stress periods [65, 66]. The ABA pathway is central to plant drought resistance, and ABREs are conserved in the promoters of drought-responsive genes [67, 68]. To date, the trihelix TF had been found to respond to aba and SA in different species, but there had been no relevant experimental research on the trihelix transcription factor in eggplant. GhGT26 in cotton was widely expressed in various tissues (with the highest level in flowers) and responded to high-salt and ABA treatments at the transcriptional level [23]. The expression of most members of the trihelix gene family in wheat was downregulated after ABA treatment [56], and the TaGT gene presented a negative response to abiotic stress. cotton GhGT23 was overexpressed in Arabidopsis, and its expression level was significantly upregulated after 12 h under ABA treatment [19]. The promoters of 19 trihelix genes contained ABREs (18) (Fig. 3), and five trihelix genes were upregulated in response to ABA (Fig. 7c), indicating potential drought resistance. Trihelix genes respond differently to SA; the expression levels of SmGT16/17/27 were significantly downregulated at 12 h, whereas the expression levels of SmGT12/23/24/29 peaked at 48 h and 12 h, respectively (Fig. 7d), suggesting diverse functions. The induction of cellular signaling pathways in response to cold stress was divided into ABA-dependent and non-ABA-dependent pathways [69, 70]. It seems that trihelix genes are involved in the ABA-dependent pathway in response to low-temperature stress, although more research is needed in this field. Therefore, eggplant trihelix genes presented varying degrees of response to cold and hormonal stress. Although the molecular mechanisms underlying these responses are yet unclear, it is speculated that these genes may play important roles in abiotic stress responses and could be candidate genes for research on stress resistance in eggplant, which needs further experimental verification.

Conclusion

A comprehensive analysis of the trihelix gene family in eggplant was conducted in the present study. Thirty full-length trihelix genes were characterized and further classified into six main phylogenetic groups, and phylogenetic comparisons of trihelix genes from 7 different plant species provided valuable clues about the evolutionary characteristics of trihelix genes. Trihelix genes had a highly similar exon‒intron structure and motif compositions within the same subgroup and the regulatory functions of different subgroups were specific. Subcellular localization analysis and experiments revealed that the trihelix genes were expressed primarily within the nucleus. Compared to segmental duplication, tandem duplication had a lesser impact on trihelix genes during the genome expansion of the eggplant. The Ka/Ks ratio of collinear trihelix gene pairs was less than 0.2, indicating that eggplant trihelix genes are highly conserved evolutionarily and primarily influenced by purifying selection during evolutionary processes. SmGT19 was involved in the regulation of cold stress, SmGT13 and SmGT12 cross-regulated the response to ABA and SA, respectively. More importantly, the transcriptional activity and protein expression function of SmGT29 were validated. These findings serve as valuable resources, enabling a deeper comprehension of the biological functions of trihelix genes in eggplant. In summary, the aforementioned discoveries provided insight into the potential functional role of trihelix genes in eggplant. Our comprehensive analysis aided in the selection of candidate trihelix genes for further functional verification and genetic improvement of eggplant agronomic traits and environmental resistance. Additionally, these findings served as a theoretical basis for promoting the afforestation of Solanaceae species in various regions.
