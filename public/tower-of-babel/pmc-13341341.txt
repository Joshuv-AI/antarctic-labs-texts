Brain Serotonin Transporter Is Elevated in Obesity: Bayesian and Frequentist Meta‐Analyses
Authors: Kyoungjune Pak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThe brain plays a critical role in controlling energy balance. Previous studies have mainly focused on dopaminergic mechanisms, whereas much less attention has been paid to serotonin during the emergence of overweight and obesity. Therefore, this study investigated the association between serotonin transporter (SERT) availability and obesity using frequentist and Bayesian meta‐analyses.MethodsA systematic search was conducted for PET and SPECT studies comparing brain SERT availability between obese and non‐obese individuals. Five studies comprising 142 obese and 115 non‐obese participants were included. Bayesian models and frequentist meta‐analyses were applied. Meta‐regression was conducted to examine the potential moderators, including radiopharmaceuticals and body mass index.ResultsIn the frequentist meta‐analysis, the pooled standardised mean difference in SERT availability was significantly different between subjects with and without obesity (0.2675, 0.0075–0.5275, p = 0.0437, I 2 = 0%), suggesting that subjects with obesity had higher brain SERT availability than those without obesity. However, in the Bayesian model, the 95% credible interval for the standardised mean difference crossed zero, and no substantial differences were observed across studies, radiopharmaceuticals, and regions of interest.ConclusionsA modest elevation in brain SERT availability in subjects with obesity was supported by frequentist meta‐analysis, but this finding was not conclusively supported by Bayesian hierarchical modelling. These mixed results suggest a potential role of altered SERT dynamics in the neurobiology of obesity, but strongly underscore the need for larger, highly powered investigations to definitively establish this relationship.Keywords: neuroimaging, obesity, PET, serotonin transporter

Introduction

The global prevalence of obesity has nearly tripled since 1975, making it a major threat to public health. Obesity is a recognised risk factor for cancers of the colon [1], pancreas [2], thyroid [3], liver [4] and uterus [5] as well as for cardiovascular disease and diabetes mellitus [6].

Obesity is a complex, multifactorial disease driven by an interplay of genetic, environmental and behavioural factors that ultimately result in a chronic imbalance where long‐term energy intake surpasses energy expenditure [7]. The brain plays a critical role in controlling energy balance [7]. Feeding induces dopamine release into the striatum [8], and the dysfunction of the dopaminergic reward system can lead to overeating and obesity [9]. Previous studies have mainly focused on dopaminergic mechanisms [10, 11, 12], while much less attention has been paid to serotonin in the fields of overweight and obesity. Increased serotonin levels typically suppress appetite and food intake [13]. In contrast, lower synaptic serotonin levels may contribute to hyperphagic behaviour, impaired satiety signalling and altered mood, all of which contribute to the development and maintenance of obesity [14]. However, because direct measurement of synaptic serotonin in humans is not possible, imaging modalities such as positron emission tomography (PET) and single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) using serotonin transporter (SERT) tracers have been utilised to explore serotonergic contributions to obesity.

SERT, a presynaptic membrane protein on serotonergic axons and terminals, regulates neurotransmission through the reuptake of serotonin into neurons [15]. Several PET studies have examined the association between brain SERT availability and obesity; however, the findings have been inconsistent. Some studies reported that a higher body mass index (BMI) was associated with altered SERT function in the thalamus [16], while others observed no clear relationship [17], and one study found an inverse association in the neocortex [18]. However, previous studies included small sample sizes, discrepancies in the region‐of‐interest (ROI), and a variety of radiopharmaceuticals, leading to these inconsistent findings. Therefore, it is timely to meta‐analyse previous studies on the association between SERT availability, measured using PET or SPECT and obesity. In this study, we investigated the association between SERT availability and obesity using frequentist and Bayesian meta‐analyses.

Materials and Methods

Data Search and Study Selection

A systematic search of Embase, Medline and Web of Science was conducted from inception to April 2025 for articles published in English using the keywords ‘serotonin transporter’, ‘obesity’ and ‘positron emission tomography OR single‐photon emission computed tomography’. All the searches were limited to human studies. The inclusion criteria were neuroimaging studies that (1) compared SERT availability between obese and non‐obese subjects and (2) measured SERT availability using PET or SPECT scans. Reviews, abstracts, and editorials were excluded. Duplicate articles were excluded from analysis. If more than one study used the same set of patients, the one reporting the information most relevant to the present study was included. The sole author (K.P.) independently conducted the systematic search, screened the titles and abstracts, determined the final eligibility of the included studies and performed the data extraction. The quality of the included observational studies was assessed using the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates studies based on three domains: selection of study groups, comparability of groups and ascertainment of either exposure or outcome.

Data Extraction and Statistical Analysis

The following information was extracted from the reports: first author, year of publication, country, radiopharmaceuticals, mean BMI of subjects with and without obesity and the ROI. First, I extracted the mean and standard deviation of SERT availability and the number of subjects in each group directly from each study, if provided by the authors. If they were not provided by the publication, the original subject‐wise data matrix was used for one study [19] in which subjects with obesity were classified as having a BMI > 30 kg/m2 and subjects without obesity as having a BMI less than 25 kg/m2. Subsequently, the standardised mean difference (Hedges' g) in SERT availability was calculated for each study.

The standardised mean difference in SERT availability between obese and non‐obese subjects was first investigated using Bayesian hierarchical modelling with brms [20, 21, 22], which applies the Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling tools of RStan [23]. I set up a model with the standardised mean difference in SERT availability as a dependent variable and added study, radiopharmaceutical, and ROI as random intercepts to allow the standardised mean difference in SERT availability to vary among studies, radiopharmaceuticals, and ROIs. The Bayesian models were estimated using four Markov chains, each of which had 4000 iterations, including 1000 warm‐ups, totaling 12 000 post‐warm‐up samples with a Normal(0, 1) prior for the population‐level intercept, and a half‐Cauchy(0, 1) prior for the group‐level standard deviations. To ensure the robustness of the posterior distributions to prior selection, a sensitivity analysis was additionally conducted using default, non‐informative flat priors. The sampling parameters were modified slightly to facilitate convergence (maximum tree depth = 20). In addition, a complementary frequentist meta‐analysis was performed using Metafor [24]. Heterogeneity among the studies was assessed using Cochran's Q and I 2 statistics, as described previously [25]. The pooled effect size was estimated using a fixed‐effect model. This model was explicitly selected because no statistical heterogeneity was detected among the included studies. Meta‐regression was performed to explore the potential sources of heterogeneity due to radiopharmaceuticals and the mean BMI of the obese subjects. Publication bias was visually assessed utilising a funnel plot and statistical asymmetry was evaluated using Egger's regression test. Statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software version 4.4.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Study Characteristics

The search identified 1398 articles from Embase/Medline and 1409 from the Web of Science. After excluding animal studies (n = 304), non‐English publications (n = 37), conference abstracts (n = 280) and duplicate records (n = 541), studies were assessed for eligibility after screening the titles or abstracts. After reviewing the full text of 28 articles, five studies were eligible for inclusion in this study [19, 26, 27, 28, 29] with a total of 142 subjects with obesity and 115 subjects without obesity (Figure 1). Two studies using C11‐DASB [28, 29], two using I123‐FP‐CIT [19, 26] and one using I123‐ADAM [27] were included. Based on the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale assessment, all studies were of high quality. Brain Serotonin Transporter and Obesity: A Frequentist Meta‐Analysis

A single ROI from each study was included in the meta‐analysis. If the study reported results for more than one ROI, the mean SERT availability across the ROIs was calculated (specifically, Griebsch et al. [28] and Nam et al. [19] which reported 3 ROIs). Contrary to the Bayesian findings, the pooled standardised mean difference of SERT availability was significantly different between subjects with and without obesity (0.2675, 95% confidence interval 0.0075 to 0.5275, p = 0.0437, I 2 = 0%), suggesting that subjects with obesity had higher brain SERT availability than those without obesity (Figure 4). Meta‐regression analyses revealed no statistically significant effects of BMI or radiopharmaceutical use on the pooled effect size. Visual inspection of the funnel plot did not reveal severe asymmetry (Figure S1). Furthermore, Egger's regression test did not indicate statistically significant publication bias (p = 0.1058).

Discussion

This meta‐analysis investigated differences in brain SERT availability between obese and non‐obese subjects using both Bayesian and frequentist approaches. While the Bayesian analysis did not yield conclusive group differences, the frequentist meta‐analysis revealed a statistically significant increase in SERT availability in obese individuals compared to those without obesity. This finding suggests the potential upregulation of SERT in the brain and contributes to a growing body of evidence implicating altered serotonergic signalling in the pathophysiology of obesity.

Neurobiological Mechanisms of Serotonergic Dysfunction

Obesity results from a chronic imbalance between energy intake and expenditure [7]. The brain plays a critical role in controlling energy balance [7]. Although the dopaminergic system has historically garnered much attention in obesity research [10, 11], there is growing evidence that serotonin regulates appetite, satiety and energy balance [12]. SERT, a membrane protein located at the presynapse of serotonergic nerve terminals, clears serotonin from the synapse, thereby reducing neurotransmission [15]. Increased serotonin levels typically suppress appetite and food intake [13]. Therefore, the increase in SERT binding observed in our meta‐analysis may reflect reduced synaptic serotonin levels in individuals with obesity. Lower synaptic serotonin levels may contribute to hyperphagic behaviour, impaired satiety signalling and altered mood, all of which contribute to the development and maintenance of obesity [14]. Supporting this view, inhibition of SERT with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, such as sibutramine and fenfluramine, reduces food intake, underscoring the functional relevance of SERT in energy balance regulation [30]. This regulation of energy balance likely involves a complex interplay between central neurotransmission and peripheral metabolic signals. Recent evidence indicates that peripheral metabolic messengers, specifically ceramides, play a decisive role in systemic energy homeostasis. It has been established that elevated endogenous ceramides in obesity signal through the formylpeptide receptor 2 (FPR2) to inhibit adipocyte thermogenesis, thereby exacerbating weight gain [31].

This meta‐analytic finding reinforcing altered serotonergic signalling in the pathophysiology of obesity concurs well with the established ‘hyposerotonergic hypothesis of obesity’, which posits that obesity is associated with decreased intrasynaptic serotonin [13]. Support for this view comes from the known efficacy of anti‐obesity drugs that increase extracellular serotonin levels by inhibiting SERT reuptake, thereby promoting satiety and reducing food intake [32]. In stark contrast, neurochemical depletion of brain serotonin in animals has been shown to result in uncontrolled hyperphagia [32]. Furthermore, evidence from monozygotic within‐pair twin analyses suggests that acquired obesity, driven by environmental rather than purely genetic factors, is significantly associated with increased hypothalamic/thalamic SERT binding, potentially linked to reduced plasma tryptophan [33]. Previously, voxel‐based analyses have shown a positive correlation between BMI and SERT availability, specifically in the thalamus, suggesting that a higher BMI is associated with altered SERT regulation in this brain region [16]. However, results across studies remain inconsistent, with some reporting no relationship [17], while one study observed a negative correlation between neocortical SERT binding and BMI [18]. A closer inspection of the studies included in this meta‐analysis shows that, despite individually non‐significant findings, participants with obesity generally exhibited higher SERT binding than individuals without obesity [19, 27, 28, 29], with the exception of one study [26]. For instance, in the study by Haahr et al. [29], the mean SERT availability in the subcortex was 2.09 in the group with obesity and 1.99 in the group without obesity. Similarly, in a study by Griebsch et al. [28], the mean SERT availability in the dorsal raphe nucleus was 3.43 for the group with obesity compared to 3.1 for the group without obesity. Although individual studies showed this trend without statistical significance, this meta‐analysis identified a statistically significant increase in SERT availability in individuals with obesity compared to those without obesity. The lack of statistical significance in individual studies, despite consistent directional trends, raises the question of why our meta‐analysis detected a significant association. This discrepancy may be due to the limited statistical power of individual studies, which often have small sample sizes and thus lack the sensitivity to detect modest between‐group differences. By aggregating data across studies, the meta‐analysis enhanced the overall sample size and statistical power, enabling the identification of consistent patterns that might have been overlooked in smaller datasets. In addition, the results of our meta‐analysis are supported by a prior study of within‐pair twin analyses that showed higher hypothalamic/thalamic SERT binding in heavier cotwins, although there was no correlation between SERT binding and BMI [33].

Methodological Heterogeneity and Regional Considerations

The heterogeneity in the reported SERT findings across studies may be partly due to regional differences in SERT distribution and radioligand characteristics. Each SERT radiopharmaceutical displays distinct regional binding patterns in the brain, reflecting differences in the affinity and selectivity for SERT. Differences in radioligand sensitivity and competition with endogenous serotonin may explain these inconsistencies. For example, C11‐DASB has high affinity and selectivity for SERT (Ki = 1.1 nM), is sensitive to endogenous serotonin levels, and binds primarily to the midbrain, thalamus and striatum, with lower binding in cortical regions [34]. I123‐ADAM displays a high affinity for SERT (Ki = 0.013 nM) and is 10 000‐fold more selective for SERT than for DAT (Ki = 840 nM), with primary binding to the midbrain [35]. Both C11‐DASB and I123‐ADAM displayed highly selective binding to SERT. However, I123‐FP‐CIT exhibits moderate selectivity for SERT (Ki = 9.73 nM) and high affinity for DAT [36], with primary binding sites in the midbrain and thalamus [37, 38]. Therefore, I123‐FP‐CIT is less selective for SERT compared to other radiopharmaceuticals. These pharmacological differences may explain the heterogeneity across the studies.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the number of eligible studies was limited (n = 5), which reflects the overall paucity of neuroimaging data on SERT in obesity. Second, radioligand‐specific properties and variability in regional ROI selection may have introduced methodological heterogeneity despite efforts to adjust through Bayesian modelling and meta‐regression. Third, SERT availability does not necessarily reflect synaptic serotonin levels. For example, SERT upregulation may represent a compensatory mechanism in response to chronically low serotonin levels or altered serotonergic tone driven by environmental or metabolic signals [39]. Fourth, the literature search, study selection, and data extraction were performed by a single author. While standard guidelines for systematic reviews were followed as rigorously as possible, the lack of a second independent reviewer increases the potential risk of selection bias and human error. Finally, according to the GRADE framework, the overall certainty of the evidence presented in this meta‐analysis is classified as ‘Low’. While the individual studies demonstrated high methodological quality on NOS, the inherent observational and cross‐sectional nature of the included neuroimaging studies prevents the establishment of a definitive causal relationship between SERT availability and obesity.

Clinical Translation and Future Perspectives

A modest elevation in brain SERT availability in subjects with obesity was supported by frequentist meta‐analysis, but this finding was not conclusively supported by Bayesian hierarchical modelling. This statistical divergence highlights that while there is a directionally consistent trend across the existing literature, the current body of evidence is not entirely definitive. From a clinical perspective, SERT could eventually serve as a biomarker to tailor serotonergic treatments or monitor central responses to interventions like bariatric surgery. Future large‐scale, longitudinal research must move beyond mere diagnostic observation to utilise central SERT availability as a predictive biomarker for tailoring weight loss strategies. Evidence indicates that baseline SERT availability can serve as a predictor of treatment response, with patients exhibiting high SERT availability potentially benefiting more from surgical interventions such as Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass surgery, while those with low SERT availability may benefit more from non‐surgical dietary approaches [28]. Utilising such neurobiological profiles to fully decode how altered serotonergic neurotransmission impacts energy balance networks in human obesity will be essential for future clinical translation.
