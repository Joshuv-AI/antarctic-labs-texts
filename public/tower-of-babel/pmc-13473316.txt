Introduction

Abdominal pregnancy is a rare form of ectopic gestation where the embryo implants within the peritoneal cavity, external to the uterine cavity and fallopian tubes. It accounts for approximately 1% of all ectopic pregnancies and occurs in roughly 1 in 11,000 to 1 in 30,000 pregnancies, carrying significant maternal morbidity and mortality risk (1, 2).

A lithopedion, or “stone baby,” is an exceptionally rare complication of abdominal pregnancy. It occurs when a fetus dies after 3 months of gestation, remains sterile, and, because it is too large to be reabsorbed by the maternal body, subsequently undergoes calcification (3). The incidence of lithopedion is estimated at 0.0054% of all gestations, with fewer than 350 cases documented in over 400 years of medical literature (4, 5).

While most cases of lithopedion are discovered incidentally in postmenopausal women decades after the index pregnancy (6), the diagnosis of a lithopedion coexisting with a viable intrauterine pregnancy in a young woman is an extraordinarily rare clinical entity. Such cases pose unique diagnostic and management challenges due to the anatomical distortion and potential risks to the ongoing viable pregnancy (7). We report a case of a 27-year-old woman who presented with acute abdominal pain and was found to have a 12-week viable intrauterine pregnancy coexisting with a retained, calcified abdominal fetus corresponding to 22 weeks of gestation. This paper has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria (8).

Case report

A 27-year-old female, G2P1L1, presented to the emergency department with complaints of abdominal pain and vomiting persisting for the last 6 h. The pain was generalized but more pronounced on the left side. Her last menstrual period was 12 weeks prior to presentation. Her obstetric history was significant for one previous pregnancy, delivered by lower-segment cesarean section 26 months before the current presentation. There was no prior history of miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, or other documented pregnancy loss. During the current pregnancy, she had undergone one previous antenatal checkup, which included a dating scan that was reportedly normal.

On physical examination, her vital signs were within normal limits (blood pressure 110/70 mmHg, pulse 84 bpm, afebrile). Abdominal examination revealed a soft abdomen with the fundus of the uterus just palpable above the pubic symphysis, consistent with a 12-week gestation. Distinct tenderness was elicited in the left lumbar and iliac regions. No guarding or rigidity was noted. Laboratory investigations, including a complete blood count, liver function tests, and renal function tests, showed parameters within normal limits.

An emergent ultrasonographic evaluation of the abdomen was performed. The scan revealed a single live intrauterine fetus (Figure 1) with a crown-rump length of 55.2 mm, corresponding to a gestational age of 12 weeks and 1 day. Cardiac activity and fetal movements were normal. Coexisting with this viable pregnancy, the scan identified an extrauterine, heterogeneous mass located on the left side of the uterus (Figure 2). This mass contained calcified structures producing posterior acoustic shadowing and a dead, macerated fetus with a femur length of 3.9 cm, corresponding to a gestational age of approximately 22 weeks and 4 days. The findings were suggestive of a dead intra-abdominal pregnancy, i.e., lithopedion.

To confirm the diagnosis and rule out acute complications, the patient was managed conservatively with analgesics and observation. This initial observation was chosen because she was haemodynamically stable, had no clinical features suggestive of peritonitis, had normal laboratory parameters, and had a viable intrauterine pregnancy; it also allowed reassessment before surgery rather than proceeding immediately in the absence of acute deterioration. A repeat ultrasonography performed 3 days later corroborated the initial findings, showing no change in the status of the intrauterine pregnancy or the extrauterine mass. Following this confirmation, definitive operative removal was planned because of the retained fetus, the initial symptomatic presentation, and concern for mechanical complications as the intrauterine pregnancy progressed.

Given the size of the retained fetus and the patient’s symptoms, an exploratory laparotomy was planned to remove the dead macerated fetus and prevent potential mechanical obstruction or complications as the intrauterine pregnancy progressed. Under regional anesthesia, the abdomen was opened via a longitudinal midline incision. Intraoperative exploration revealed a calcified skeletal mass corresponding to a dead fetus located on the anterior surface of the uterus (Figure 3). Notably, the mass was not adherent to major intra-abdominal structures such as the bladder, omentum, or bowel loops. The dead fetus was carefully removed without disturbing the gravid uterus or the viable intrauterine pregnancy. There were no intraoperative complications. Estimated blood loss was 150 mL and operative duration was 130 min.

The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery. She received prophylactic antibiotics and tocolytic support (progesterone 200 mg for 7 days) to prevent preterm uterine contractions. She was discharged on the fifth postoperative day in stable condition. At the 4-week postoperative follow-up, an ultrasonography showed a progressing single intrauterine pregnancy with normal interval growth and normal amniotic fluid volume. There was no trace of any extrauterine residue. The patient remains under regular follow-up in the antenatal clinic.

Discussion

The formation of a lithopedion requires three specific conditions: the fetus must survive beyond the first trimester (usually >3 months), it must remain sterile (escaping infection that would cause abscess formation), and it must evade detection to allow time for calcification (2, 9). In this case, the extrauterine fetus corresponded to a size of 22 weeks, suggesting it had survived to the second trimester during a previous unrecorded or misdiagnosed gestation before succumbing and calcifying (Figure 4).

The coexistence of a lithopedion with a subsequent viable gestation is a phenomenon documented in fewer than a dozen cases in modern literature (7). The discrepancy in gestational ages (12 weeks viable vs. 22 weeks dead) confirms that the abdominal pregnancy was not a heterotopic twin conceived concurrently with the current pregnancy. Instead, it represents a “missed” abdominal pregnancy from an intervening conception that likely occurred between her previous cesarean section and the current pregnancy. The patient’s history of a previous cesarean section is a known risk factor for uterine scar disruption, which can predispose to secondary abdominal pregnancies (10).

Diagnosis of such cases is often delayed because symptoms can be nonspecific or attributed to the current pregnancy. As seen in this patient, abdominal pain is the most common presenting symptom, often due to mass effect or peritoneal irritation (11). Ultrasonography remains the primary diagnostic modality, characterized by the “posterior acoustic shadow” sign produced by the calcified fetal bones, which can sometimes make visualization of fetal anatomy difficult (12). Differential diagnostic considerations included a calcified uterine fibroid, ovarian teratoma, chronic ectopic pregnancy, or another calcified abdominopelvic mass. However, as the ultrasonography demonstrated a separate extrauterine mass containing recognizable calcified fetal skeletal structures with posterior acoustic shadowing and fetal biometry (femur length 3.9 cm) corresponding to approximately 22 weeks and 4 days, making lithopedion the most likely diagnosis. Additional cross-sectional imaging with CT or MRI was not pursued because ultrasonography was considered confirmatory, and a decision for surgical management had already been made on clinical grounds.

Management of a lithopedion diagnosed during pregnancy presents a dilemma. While small, asymptomatic lithopedions in non-pregnant women can sometimes be managed expectantly (13), surgical removal is generally recommended when diagnosed during pregnancy (14). The presence of a rigid, calcified mass can cause mechanical obstruction, fetal malpresentation, or dystocia during labor. Furthermore, as the gravid uterus expands, it may compress the lithopedion against bowel or bladder, leading to obstruction or fistula formation (15). In this case, an exploratory laparotomy was chosen to mitigate these risks. The lack of adhesions to the bowel or omentum, a fortunate and somewhat atypical finding for abdominal pregnancy, allowed for safe removal without compromising the ongoing pregnancy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case highlights the importance of thorough ultrasonographic evaluation in patients with persistent abdominal pain during pregnancy. It confirms that with careful surgical planning, a retained abdominal pregnancy can be successfully removed without interrupting a coexisting viable intrauterine gestation. When lithopedion is diagnosed during pregnancy, management should be individualized; symptomatic or sizeable masses warrant surgical assessment to reduce the risk of mass effect, obstruction, malpresentation, or dystocia while prioritizing preservation of the ongoing pregnancy.
