Diversity and distribution of a prevalent Microviridae group across the global oceans
Authors: Ying Wu, Zuqing Wu, Luyuan Guo, Jiabing Shao, Hang Xiao, Mingyu Yang, Chunmei Deng, Yahui Zhang, Zefeng Zhang, Yanlin Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Small single-stranded DNA phages of the Microviridae family are diverse and prevalent in oceans. Our understanding of Microviridae phages that infect the ecologically important marine Roseobacter is currently limited, comprising few isolates. Here, we report six roseophages that infect Roseobacter RCA strains. Genomic and phylogenetic analyses revealed that they were new members of the previously identified subfamily Occultatumvirinae. Additionally, 232 marine uncultivated virus genomes (UViGs) affiliated to Occultatumvirinae were obtained from environmental genome datasets. Phylogenomic analysis revealed that marine Occultatumvirinae phages could be further grouped into 11 subgroups. Moreover, meta-omics based read-mapping analysis showed that Occultatumvirinae phages were globally distributed, with two low G + C subgroups showing the most prevalent distribution. Furthermore, one phage in subgroup 2 was found to be extremely ubiquitous. Overall, this study expands our understanding of the diversity and ecology of the Occultatumvirinae microviruses in the ocean and highlights their ecological impacts.

Introduction

As the most abundant biological entities in global oceans, viruses are regarded as major players in marine ecosystems1–4. Bacteriophages numerically dominate marine viral communities, and profoundly influence the composition and function of microbial communities, and global biogeochemical cycles2–5. Most of the currently characterized marine phages are tailed phages containing a double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) genome. Owing to the methodological limitations in the detection of small-sized single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) particles and sequencing of their genome fragments, ssDNA phages are much less-studied than dsDNA phages. Despite this, along with the increasing number of available ssDNA viral genomes assembled from metagenomic studies, ssDNA phages are widely recognized as important components of marine viral communities6–8. Among them, members of the Microviridae family (microviruses) are abundant and widespread in the ocean. Within the Microviridae family, only two subfamilies, Gokushovirinae and Bullavirinae, have been defined by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), although > 20 unofficial subfamilies have been proposed in the literature9. Microviruses belonging to the subfamily Gokushovirinae has been revealed to be ubiquitous in the ocean7,10,11. The quantity of Chlamydiamicrovirus-like microviruses in Gokushovirinae has been quantified to be > 105 phages/mL based on the polony method in some marine subsurface areas11. In addition, microviruses have been isolated from several marine bacterial hosts12–14. Currently known cultivated marine microviruses have an icosahedral capsid with small diameters (20–30 nm) and consist of a circular ssDNA genome ranging from 4248 to 5581 nt12–14. A great amount of the genetic diversity within marine microviruses has been detected from various marine environments via metagenomic surveys6,7,10,15–18. A recent study identified and analyzed over 10,000 microviral genomes globally, of which 810 were from seawater, marine fish, and marine invertebrates19. This study also proposed to elevate the Microviridae to the order level, consisting 3 putative suborders and at least 19 putative families, and most of the proposed families lack cultivable representatives19. To further explore the genetic diversity of marine ssDNA microviruses, efforts are still needed to isolate and characterize more ssDNA phages, particularly those that infect ecologically important marine bacteria.

The well-known marine Roseobacter group is an abundant and important heterotrophic bacterial group20. Members of this group dominate coastal and polar waters and possess diverse metabolic capabilities, thereby playing important roles in global carbon and sulfur cycling. Within the Roseobacter group, several lineages (such as RCA, CHABI-5, SAG-O19, and NAC11-7) dominate the Roseobacter communities and account for up to 60% of the global pelagic Roseobacter group21. Members of these dominant lineages have a streamlined genome and are regarded as oligotrophs that have adapted to nutrient-deficient oceanic environments. The RCA lineage is among the most abundant marine bacterioplankton groups that dominate subtropical and polar surfaces21,22. Currently, eight available RCA isolates have been obtained from different marine samples23–25, and several dsDNA roseophages that infect RCA strains have been reported25–28.

The vast majority of the known roseophages have a dsDNA genome, only two have an ssDNA genome and belong to the Microviridae13,14. The two ssDNA roseophages were classified into two proposed subfamilies, Amoyovirinae13 and Tainavirinae14. Here, using Roseobacter RCA strains as hosts, we report the genomes of six new ssDNA roseophages. These roseophages belong to the family Microviridae and share similar genomic arrangements. Genomic analysis revealed that they all belong to previously proposed subfamily Occultatumvirinae. Moreover, over 200 new Occultatumvirinae uncultivated virus genomes (UViGs) were mined from the public marine genome datasets. We found that the marine Occultatumvirinae phages are highly diverse. We also examined the global distribution patterns of these Occultatumvirinae phages using meta-omics based read-mapping analysis.

Results

Isolation and general features of six roseophages

Using Roseobacter RCA strains FZCC0023 and FZCC0040 as hosts, roseophages were enriched from three coastal seawater samples, and genomically sequenced. After genome sequencing and assembly, we identified a total of 18 roseophage genomes (Table S1). Among these roseophages, six have a small circularly closed genome (Table S1 and Table 1). While this represents a small sample size, this suggests that microviruses may contribute to a significant proportion of roseophages. Sequencing data analysis revealed that these six roseophages were mixed with roseophages of other types. After three rounds of purification using the dilution-to-extinction method, CRPss-151 was successfully purified, but we failed to purify other five roseophages. After genome assembly, 99.86% of the clean reads (non-host reads) were assembled into one circular phage genome, suggesting that CRPss-151 is pure. Transmission electron microscopic observation showed that CRPss-151 has an icosahedral capsid with a diameter of about 25 nm (Fig. 1a). Growth curves showed that CRPss-151 caused declines in host numbers and produced phage particles (Supplementary Fig. 1). The genome sizes of these six roseophages ranged from 5409 to 5978 nt, and their G + C content ranged from 44.07% to 47.64% (Table 1). All roseophage genomes contained direct terminal repeats, indicating that the genomes were complete. Based on the intergenomic similarity calculations using VIRIDIC, four of the six roseophages were found to be highly similar to each other, sharing 82.20% – 92.60% intergenomic similarity (Fig. 1b). CRPss-154 and CRPss-251 were distinct from other roseophages, with limited genomic regions aligned with other four roseophages and intergenomic similarity values of 0.90% – 14.70% (Fig. 1b).

Genomic analysis and taxonomic classification

Gene prediction analysis identified 6 to 8 open reading frames (ORFs) in each roseophage genome (Table 1). All roseophages were similar in gene content and arrangement (Fig. 1c). Three conserved microviral proteins, major capsid protein (VP1), DNA pilot protein (VP2) and replication initiator protein (VP4), were identified in all six roseophages, suggesting their close evolutionary relationship with microviruses. Their VP1 shared 36.25–97.55% pairwise amino acid identity with each other and was most similar to those in previously reported Occultatumvirinae14 and Family 719 microviruses (25.71–51.31% amino acid identity). The closest matches of VP4 among these roseophages were also from Family 7 and Occultatumvirinae members. All six roseophages also encode homologs of the microviral DNA pilot protein (VP2), which is involved in viral ssDNA delivery from the capsid to host cytoplasm29. Furthermore, the genome-wide evolutionary ViPTree classification (Supplementary Fig. 2) and geNomad analysis classified these six roseophages into the Microviridae (Supplementary Data 1).

Phylogenetic analysis based on the concatenated alignment of VP1 and VP4 sequences showed that these roseophages fell within the Occultatumvirinae/Family 7 cluster and were located in distinct branches of the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1d). We found that the recommended taxonomic groups “Family 7”19 and Occultatumvirinae14 refer to the same group of microviruses. Since the proposed re-classification of microviruses by Kirchberger et al.19 has not been approved by ICTV yet, we tentatively classified these six roseophages into the subfamily Occultatumvirinae. Occultatumvirinae phages were firstly isolated on Rhizobium, and Rhizobium microviruses were grouped into two clusters, chaparroviruses and chicoviruses30,31. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first isolation of marine Occultatumvirinae phages.

Except for CRPss-154, the other five roseophages possessed a gene encoding an M15 family metallopeptidase. Peptidases are widely distributed in bacterial and phage genomes17,32–35, and this phage-encoded peptidase gene is likely involved in host cell lysis33,34. HNH endonuclease gene, which plays various roles in the phage lifecycle and is regarded as a key component of phage DNA packaging machines36, was only identified in CRPss-154.

Identification and classification of Occultatumvirinae UViGs

To comprehensively elucidate the diversity of Occultatumvirinae phages in marine environments and investigate their ecological functions, we recovered 232 new Occultatumvirinae UViGs from marine uncultured viral and microbial genome datasets using the proteins of known Occultatumvirinae members and the six roseophages as baits. Of the 232 UViGs, 185 had complete or nearly complete genomes (≥95% genome completeness) (Supplementary Data 2). This analysis increased the number of known Occultatumvirinae genomes from 195 to 433 (Supplementary Data 2).

To better taxonomize these UViGs and roseophages, the Microvirus Organization Pipeline Using Protein sharing (MOP-UP) pipeline developed for the discovery and classification of microviruses based on protein-sharing-network was used19. The results showed that they all clustered with previously reported Occultatumvirinae members and were well-separated from other microviral groups (Fig. 2). ViPTree analysis also confirmed that they are all new members of the subfamily Occultatumvirinae (Supplementary Fig. 2). Phylogenetic analyses of Occultatumvirinae microviruses

Phylogenetic analysis based on VP1 and VP4 was performed to explore the diversity of Occultatumvirinae microviruses. All marine Occultatumvirinae sequences identified in this study, together with previously reported marine and non-marine Occultatumvirinae sequences, were included in the analysis. The results revealed that Occultatumvirinae members were highly diverse and well-separated from their closest relatives, Tainavirinae phages (Fig. 3a). Based on the tree topology, we separated Occultatumvirinae members into at least 11 subgroups (Fig. 3a). All of the 11 subgroups contained marine-derived sequences and seven subgroups (subgroups 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 11) were composed exclusively of marine members. This suggests that marine environments harbor the vast majority of Occultatumvirinae diversity. All six RCA roseophages were grouped into subgroup 11, the largest subgroup of Occultatumvirinae. Members of this subgroup mostly originated from the oceanic and coastal regions. Four subgroups (3, 4, 5 and 10) also harbored non-marine members, most of which were affiliated with subgroup 5 (Fig. 3a). The previously reported chaparroviruses and chicoviruses infecting Rhizobium were grouped into subgroup 5 (Fig. 3a). In total, 93 Occultatumvirinae phages were identified from the bacterial genomes (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 2), most of which are non-marine phages belonging to subgroup 5.

The G + C content of all known Occultatumvirinae phage genomes varied between 36.04 and 64.26% and the genome sizes of Occultatumvirinae members with complete genomes varied between 5028 and 6716 nt (Supplementary Data 2 and Fig. 3b). Overall, the genome sizes within each subgroup were relatively consistent. The genome sizes of subgroups 2, 3, 4, 5 and 10 (median = 6042.50, 6020.00, 6123.00, 6037.00 and 6140.00; p < 0.05) were significantly larger than those of the other subgroups (Fig. 3c). Members in subgroups 8, 9 and 11 had significantly lower G + C content (median = 44.82%, 37.73%, and 40.27%, p < 0.05) (Fig. 3c) than the other subgroup members.

Genome content and variation of Occultatumvirinae phages

The ORFs identified in all Occultatumvirinae members were grouped into 162 orthogroups and 437 singletons (Supplementary Data 3). Approximately 48% proteins were annotated with putative functions, whereas the remaining were annotated as unknown proteins.

VP1, VP2 and VP4 form monophyletic groups and are considered core genes of microviruses9,17,19. All Occultatumvirinae microviruses with complete genomes encoded these three genes. The peptidase or endolysin-like protein involved in cell lysis was identified in most Occultatumvirinae genomes; however, it was absent in subgroups 6 and 7 genomes. The absence of peptidase suggests that these subgroups may rely on host genes or other unidentified lysis genes to lyse cells. Regarding accessory genes, only methyltransferase gene was identified in two subgroup 4 and one subgroup 5 genomes. Phage-encoded methyltransferases are presumed to methylate phage genomes, and therefore protecting phage genomes against host restriction endonuclease activity37.

Lysogenic life cycle of marine Occultatumvirinae phages

Despite not harboring lysogeny‑associated genes, several non-marine Occultatumvirinae phages were identified as prophages of Rhizobiales and Rhodobacterale19. These prophages were mainly distributed in subgroups 3, 5, and 10 (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 2), suggesting that lysogeny was prevalent in these subgroups. In addition, a total of six marine Occultatumvirinae prophages were identified from Roseobacter genomes (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 2). Five of them belong to subgroup 10, while the other one was identified as a singleton. Five Occultatumvirinae prophages have been reported previously19. The other Occultatumvirinae prophage (3300025057_Ga0208018_100189) was identified from a recently reported metagenome-assembled genome (MAG). This prophage was located in a Roseobacter strain genome which shared similarity with Sulfitobacter pontiacus BL28 plasmid p2 DNA sequence (71% coverage, 98.99% identity). The genome map of the six marine Occultatumvirinae prophages revealed their arrangement in the host (VP2-VP1-peptidase-VP4) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Distribution and biogeography of marine Occultatumvirinae phages

To evaluate the presence and relative abundance of marine-derived Occultatumvirinae microviruses in global oceans, a read-mapping analysis (≥95% identity, ≥50 bp aligned length, ≥80% aligned percentage) was performed via mapping environmental reads to the non-redundant marine Occultatumvirinae genome dataset (Supplementary Data 4). Considering the ssDNA phages are underestimated in most viromes, our analysis not only used reads from various marine viromes, but also included reads from marine metagenomes and metatranscriptomes.

As expected, no Occultatumvirinae members were detected in the Tara viromic datasets (Supplementary Fig. 4). This is because viromic studies are traditionally biased towards dsDNA viruses owing to sequencing library preparation methods38–40. They were only detected at some Chesapeake Bay and Delaware Bay stations with ultra-deep viromic sequencing datasets (Supplementary Fig. 4), in which the detected microvirus was assumed to have originated from the dsDNA form within the bacterial cell.

We also investigated their distribution in marine cellular metagenomes and metatranscriptomes (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 5). The microviral sequences detected from these datasets were likely from integrated prophages, microviruses in replicate dsDNA form, or transcribed microviral genes. The six roseophages reported in this study were only detected at few stations, suggesting that they were not prevalent in the ocean. Although our roseophages showed limited distribution, some of other members were widely distributed in global oceans (Fig. 4). Among the 11 subgroups, five were detected (Fig. 5a, b). Overall, the detected Occultatumvirinae members possessed similar distribution patterns in metagenomes and metatranscriptomes (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 5). Occultatumvirinae members were mostly detected in the westerlies (30° N– 60° N and 30° S– 60° S) and polar regions (Fig. 5a, c). Vertically, they were mainly present in the surface (SRF) and deep chlorophyll maximum (DCM) zones (Fig. 5d). Low G + C content subgroups 11 and 9 were the two most cosmopolitan Occultatumvirinae subgroups (Fig. 4, and Fig. 5a, b). The six roseophages isolated in this study belonged to subgroup 11, which contained 102 members, including 96 members with unknown hosts. More than 85% of the members in subgroups 11 and 9 were detected in marine metagenomes or metatranscriptomes (Fig. 5b). Most members in these two subgroups were widespread in polar regions and frequently detected at some high-latitude coastal stations (Fig. 4). Furthermore, we observed that some members of subgroup 11 showed an opposite distribution pattern (Fig. 4). They were not detected at polar stations, but were frequently detected in trade (0° N–30° N and 0° S–30° S) and westerlies biomes. This suggests that the hosts of these phages have distinct ecological niches. A small proportion of members of subgroups 2, 8, and 10 were detected in the datasets (Fig. 5b). None of the members in subgroups 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 were detected in any of the analyzed datasets (Fig. 5b), suggesting that they relatively rarely occurred in marine environments.

Discussion

ssDNA genome microviruses are ubiquitous and diverse in global oceans7,10,16,19. Previous studies on marine microviruses were based on a few isolates and environmental viral genomes7,10,12–14,16. In this study, six novel microviruses infecting Roseobacter RCA strains were reported. The genome architecture of the six roseophages displayed typical features of microviruses, including a small genome size, possession of microviral marker genes, and sequence similarity to known microviruses. These roseophages were classified as the first marine cultivated representatives of the subfamily Occultatumvirinae14. Members of Occultatumvirinae have been shown to infect Rhizobiales, Rhodobacterales and Hyphomicrobiales in Alphaproteobacteria14,19. The discovery of the six Occultatumvirinae roseophages and the identification of Occultatumvirinae prophage further indicate that Alphaproteobacteria is an important host of Occultatumvirinae phages14.

Here, we identified additional 232 Occultatumvirinae UViGs from marine environmental genome datasets. Phylogenetic analysis grouped all Occultatumvirinae members into at least 11 subgroups, five among which were novel. This suggests that this study significantly expanded the diversity of Occultatumvirinae microviruses. These novel Occultatumvirinae subgroups consisted exclusively of marine-derived microviruses, suggesting that the marine Occultatumvirinae diversity has been severely underestimated. Previously identified Occultatumvirinae members were mostly high G + C non-marine MAGs19. We found that marine Occultatumvirinae phages showed wide variations in genome size and G + C content. Notably, members in subgroups 8, 9, and 11 have a low G + C content (Fig. 3c). The G + C content of phages has been shown to be correlated with that of their bacterial hosts41,42. In marine surface environments, low G + C content and genome-streamlined bacteria dominate bacterial communities43,44. Our results imply that subgroups 8, 9, and 11 may infect dominant marine bacterial taxa.

Evidence of the integration of Occultatumvirinae genomes into bacterial chromosomes has been found in several bacterial fragments14. All currently known marine Occultatumvirinae prophages were integrated into the marine Roseobacter genomes. This suggests that roseobacters are important hosts for Occultatumvirinae phages. Nonetheless, hosts of most marine Occultatumvirinae phages still remain unknown. It has been reported that the mechanism for the genomic integration of microviruses involves the presence of ~28 bp quasi-palindromic dif-motifs flanking all microviral prophages. These motifs enable microviruses to utilize the pervasive bacterial XerCD recombinase system for integration into bacterial genomes45. However, these dif-motifs have not been identified in these Occultatumvirinae prophage genomes and their integration mechanism remains elusive.

It is not surprising that Occultatumvirinae members were not detected in TARA marine viromes, due to the inability of detecting ssDNA fragments on the Illumina sequencing platform. To characterize the global distribution and activity of Occultatumvirinae members, the TARA Ocean metagenomic and metatranscriptomic reads were used for the read-mapping analysis. The results showed that five Occultatumvirinae subgroups can be detected in both metagenomes and metatranscriptomes and they showed similar distribution patterns in these two datasets, suggesting their high transcriptional activity in marine environments. Occultatumvirinae phages were present globally and low G + C content subgroups 9 and 11 dominate the Occultatumvirinae communities. The results suggested that low G + C Occultatumvirinae members infecting dominant marine bacteria were more prevalent than the other subgroups and thus have broader ecological functions.

The subgroup 2 member IMGVR_UViG_3300034037_000063 was strikingly prevalent in global oceans. We hypothesized that the hosts of this phage were globally prevalent and had very broad ecological adaptability in global oceans. The relative abundance of this phage did not show significant variation among different biomes but was positively correlated with depth, suggesting that it was more dominant in the deeper ocean. The host and ecological importance of this novel phage requires further exploration.

Methods

Bacterial strains and growth media

Marine Roseobacter RCA strains FZCC0023 and FZCC0040 were isolated from the coastal waters of Pingtan Island in China (25°26’ N, 119°47’ E) in 201725. These two strains can’t grow on solid media and can only grow in liquid seawater-based media. They were cultured in a natural seawater-based medium with excess vitamins46, 1 mM NH4Cl, 100 μM KH2PO4, 1 μM FeCl3, mixed carbon sources47 and 50 μM taurine. RCA strains were cultured at 23 °C in the dark. A Guava EasyCyte flow cytometer (Guava Technologies, Millipore, CA, USA) was used to count bacterial cells.

Roseophage isolation and purification

Surface seawater samples for isolating roseophages were collected from three coastal marine stations (Table 1). Seawater samples were filtered through 0.1 μm-pore-size sterile syringe filters and stored in the dark at 4 °C until use. Due to the inability of performing plaque assay using these RCA strains, roseophages were isolated from the seawater samples using a liquid culture-based enrichment procedure48. A phage enrichment procedure was established via mixing filtered seawater samples with the growing RCA strains. Cell growth and potential bacterial lysis were monitored using a Guava EasyCyte flow cytometer (Guava Technologies). The dilution-to-extinction phage purified procedure was conducted to purify the roseophages49.

Transmission electron microscopic observation and growth experiments

An exponentially growing host culture was infected with CRPss-151 stock solution and incubated until host cells were lysed. The lysate of CRPss-151 was filtered through a 0.1 μm syringe filter and subsequently concentrated by ultracentrifugation (Beckman Coulter) at 50,000 g for 2 h. A drop of the concentrated CRPss-151 sample was placed on a copper grid and subsequently dried in the air. The grid was stained for 30 s in 2% phosphotungstic acid and then observed by a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope at a voltage of 80 kV.

For the growth curve of host FZCC0023, CRPss-151 was added to exponentially growing FZCC0023 cultures at a phage-to-host ratio of ~3. FZCC0023 cultures without the addition of CRPss-151 were set as controls. Cell counts were determined using a Guava EasyCyte flow cytometer. For the growth curve of CRPss-151, CRPss-151 was added to exponentially growing FZCC0023 cultures at a phage-to-host ratio of ~0.1. After the addition of the CRPss-151, an aliquot of the cell suspension was collected and filtered through 0.2-μm syringe filters every hour, and the relative abundance of CRPss-151 particles was quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR). A qPCR primer set were designed using Primer-designing tool on NCBI, targeting the VP1 sequence of CRPss-151. The primer pair used for amplification was: 5’- GCAAAGAGACATGGTGAATG-3’ (forward) and 5’-CTGTTTGATCGGCAAAAACT-3’ (reversed). qPCR reaction was performed using the Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Vazyme) with three biological replicates and three technical repeats. qPCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 30 s.

Phage DNA extraction and amplification

The phage lysate (150 mL) was filtered through 0.1 μm-pore-size polycarbonate membrane filters (Millipore) to remove cells and debris. To concentrate the phage particles, filtered lysates were concentrated to 1 ml using Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filters (30 kDa; Millipore). The concentrated phage samples were further concentrated via ultracentrifugation at 50,000 g for 2 h. The phage DNA was extracted using the Qiagen Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The ssDNA was converted to dsDNA using the Illustra GenomiPhi V2 DNA Amplification Kit (Cytiva, MA, USA). Phage DNA was sequenced using an Illumina paired-end HiSeq 2500 sequencing system (Illumina Inc., CA, USA).

Genome assembly, annotation, and comparison

Fastp 0.20.1 was used for quality filtering and adaptor trimming of the raw reads (-q 20 -l 50)50. De novo genome assembly was performed using MEGAHIT v1.2.951. After assembly, direct terminal repeats were identified by BLASTN. Putative open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted from assembled genomes using GeneMarkS (http://exon.gatech.edu/GeneMark). Putative biological functions were assigned to translated ORFs using BLASTP (amino acid identity 25%, alignment coverage ≥ 50%, and E-value ≤ 0.001) against the NCBI nonredundant (nr) database and the NCBI-RefSeq database in comparison with known proteins. The intergenomic similarities among the roseophages were calculated using VIRIDIC52. To compare gene similarities between the six RCA phages, the ORFs were compared by using BLASTP (E-value < 0.001, qcov_hsp_perc 50, outfmt 6). Representative roseophage genomes were compared and visualized using Easyfig v2.2.353.

Two core genes (VP1 and VP4) were selected for phylogenetic analysis. The two core genes of the six roseophages and other known microviruses were aligned using MUSCLE v3.8.155154 and edited using trimAL v1.4.rev1555. The alignments were concatenated and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE v2.2.0.356, with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Retrieving Occultatumvirinae phages

To retrieve marine-derived Occultatumvirinae genomes, approximately seven million marine source uncultivated viral genomes (UViGs) were downloaded from IMG/VR4. Additionally, marine metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) datasets57 were downloaded and included in the analysis. Profile hidden Markov models (HMM)58 were constructed using VP1 and VP4 sequences of the Occultatumvirinae/Family 7 microviruses14,19,30 and roseophages isolated in this study using hmmbuild with the default parameters. HMM profiles were used to query the downloaded MVGs and MAGs using hmmsearch program (E-value ≤ 0.05)19. Only matches with ≥ 30% identity of VP1 and ≥ 80% alignment length were considered. UViGs containing both the VP1 and VP4 gene homologs were retained for further analysis. UViGs were clustered using CD-HIT v4.8.1 (-c 0.95 -aS 0.8)59,60 and only the longest sequences of each species cluster were retained for read-mapping analysis. CheckV v0.8.161 was used to estimate the completeness of the Occultatumvirinae genomes. Prodigal62 was used to predict ORFs of all Occultatumvirinae genomes. OrthoFinder v2.5.263,64 was used to identify groups of orthologous genes from ORFs of Occultatumvirinae genomes based on sequence similarity (BLASTP option: E-value ≤ 10−3; ≥ 25% identity; ≥ 50% alignment length).

Protein-sharing network analysis and phylogenomic analyses

A protein-sharing network was constructed using Microvirus Organization Pipeline Using Protein Sharing (MOP-UP)19. GeNomad65 was used to identify virus sequences for quick classification.

Phylogenomic analyses were conducted to evaluate the evolutionary relationships among the Occultatumvirinae phages. The VP1 and VP4 genes of all Occultatumvirinae phages were aligned using MUSCLE v3.8.155154 and edited using trimAL v1.4.rev1555. The alignments were concatenated and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE v2.2.0.356, with 1000 bootstrap replicates. A genome-wide proteomic phylogenetic tree was constructed using ViPTree (https:// www.genome.jp/viptree). The phylogenetic trees and protein identity heatmap were visualized and annotated using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) v.5.

Recruitment of metagenomic reads and statistical analysis

The relative abundance of these Occultatumvirinae phages were evaluated using an in silico read mapping approach, as described previously. A total of 258 marine virome datasets were used for viromic read-mapping to analysis, including the Global Ocean Viromes 239., Pearl River Estuary virome66, Mariana Trench virome67, Eastern Tropical North Pacific virome68, viromes of the Delaware Bay Chesapeake Bay69, Red Sea virome70, South China Sea DNA Virome71, Pacific Ocean Virome72, Scripps Pier Virome73, and Indian Ocean Virome74. Marine metagenomes and metatranscriptomes obtained from Tara Oceans project75 were also downloaded for analysis.

Omic reads were mapped against the non-redundant Occultatumvirinae phage genomes using CoverM v0.6.1 (-p bwa-mem, –min-read-percent-identity 95, –min-read-aligned-length 50, –min-read-aligned-percent 80, https://github.com/wwood/CoverM). The relative abundance of phages was normalized to the mapped read counts per kilobase pair of genomes per million read counts in the metagenome (RPKM). Differences in the abundance of viruses were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test in R v4.3.2 and plotted using the pheatmap R package. Box plots of the detected Occultatumvirinae phages constructed using the package ggplot2 in R. The world map data was retrieved using the map_data() function and the map plot was created using the map_data() function of the ggplot2 in R.
