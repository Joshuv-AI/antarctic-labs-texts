The variability fingerprint of a warming world informing climate risk and solutions
Authors: Yunran Wang, Calum G. Turvey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Global warming has become an increasingly urgent issue that must be addressed within a limited timeframe, as the debate over the overshoot pathway intensifies. Existing climate and socio-economic models inform our understanding but are too complex for timely action and non-expert use. To address these challenges, our stochastic framework leverages a one-box Ornstein–Uhlenbeck model with colored-noise forcing to characterize variance scaling in detrended temperatures and links this behavior to an empirical Hurst coefficient. The observed weakening of anti-persistence, as summarized by this coefficient, is then used to quantify changes in temperature variance relative to the pre-industrial era. Our empirical Hurst maps reveal patterns consistent with findings from global climate models, highlighting greater changes in temperature variance in equatorial regions, such as southeastern Amazonia and western Indonesia, than in high-latitude regions. Furthermore, seasonal analyses of African temperature records reveal pronounced heterogeneity in variance changes, identifying vulnerable regions that are masked when variability is assessed at annual timescales. By linking our results to climate solutions that consider fairness in burden-sharing and unequal risk-bearing capacity, we anticipate that the empirical Hurst coefficient will support more equitable and effective climate action.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54733-4.

Introduction

The acceleration of global warming is no longer a distant warning but a present reality, reshaping ecosystems, disrupting economies, and testing societal resilience worldwide. Driven primarily by unprecedented greenhouse gas emissions and weakened natural cooling processes1, global temperatures are increasingly likely to trigger irreversible and cascading impacts. In response, the 2015 Paris Agreement set the goal of limiting warming to well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels, while pursuing efforts to constrain it to 1.5 °C2,3. Despite ongoing mitigation and adaptation efforts, progress remains insufficient, highlighting persistent challenges and deep uncertainties in achieving these targets4–6.

Given current emission trajectories and observed warming rates, overshoot pathways, defined as temporary exceedances of global temperature limits followed by a return to lower levels, are receiving increasing attention as a reflection of insufficient mitigation progress7–9. Once considered exceptional, overshoots are now embedded in mainstream mitigation pathways, with all recent IPCC scenarios consistent with the 1.5 °C target featuring multi-decadal overshoot periods3,10. This reliance has prompted growing debate over their consequences, particularly whether temporary exceedances can be managed without triggering irreversible tipping points. While some studies suggest that brief and limited overshoots may remain reversible under specific magnitudes and timescales7,9, accumulating evidence indicates that climate responses and associated risks differ substantially between overshoot and non-overshoot pathways11. Even if long-term temperatures ultimately stabilize within Paris Agreement thresholds, overshoot pathways can entail higher tipping risks than pathways that avoid overshoots altogether12, underscoring the importance of immediate and sustained emission reductions11.

Rapid emission cuts are widely recognized as essential, yet real-world progress is constrained by the difficulty of translating complex climate assessments into actionable, scalable solutions13. To support such action, it is crucial to characterize temperature variability, particularly its spatiotemporal structure and regions exhibiting amplified variability14,15. This information is central to effective climate strategy design and to reducing reliance on overshoot pathways as a default response to climate change.

However, the principal tools used to inform mitigation and adaptation are Global Climate Models (GCMs) and Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs), both of which have important limitations for timely decision making. GCMs provide a critical foundation for long-term climate projections, but their complexity and high computational costs limit their suitability for rapid analyses. Moreover, GCM-based projections are affected by substantial structural uncertainty, large internal variability at regional scales, and strong dependence on model calibration and experimental design, which cannot be fully resolved by increasing model resolution or ensemble size16–18. These limitations complicate interpretation and reduce the usability of climate information in policy and investment contexts, where uncertainty can delay the mobilization of mitigation finance13. IAMs link emissions to economic and policy outcomes using reduced-form representations of the climate system19–22, but their limited transparency and accessibility make them difficult for non-expert decision-makers or the public to engage with. In addition, research based on both GCMs and IAMs often emphasizes global or annual averages23, which can obscure sub-annual variability and region-specific seasonal vulnerabilities.

Building on these insights, we develop a simple and transparent computational method based on a one-box mean-reverting framework with coloured noise forcing, represented in a fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck form24. Stochastic representations of this type have been widely used in weather-related financial modeling, where repeated simulation of temperature evolution is required, and more broadly in climate research to characterize temperature variability and persistence across time scales25–29.

While fractional stochastic processes allow for scale-varying dependence, as examined by the climacogram in high-frequency weather applications, this study does not aim to establish long-term persistence in temperature dynamics. Instead, the stochastic framework serves as a reduced-form statistical reference to quantify variance amplification in the detrended temperature record. Supplementary Fig. S2 shows that a simple OU benchmark provides only a limited description of the observed variability structure and is included as a diagnostic comparison, motivating our emphasis on empirical variance scaling. More generally, temperature variability may reflect a mixture of short- and long-term behavior at finer temporal resolutions, such as daily or weekly intervals29,30. Accordingly, our analysis fixes the time scale and focuses on cross-grid comparisons of the same empirical parameter evaluated over an identical time window. This month-specific construction is adopted because the physical drivers of temperature variability are often seasonally heterogeneous.

Moreover, we treat the pre-industrial climate state as ergodic in the sense of ergodic theory, with stable statistical properties, such as constant variance over time31. This state serves as the baseline for measuring changes in temperature variability. Following the Industrial Revolution, temperature variability under anthropogenic forcing can be interpreted as departing from fluctuations around a fixed historical baseline3. In a forced system, internal variability generates a distribution of possible temperature trajectories. The historical temperature record, therefore, is one realization among many32. When the full ensemble of realizations is considered, the resulting variance exceeds that of the pre-industrial ergodic state, consistent with evidence from large ensemble climate simulations and stochastic climate modeling33. To quantify departures from the pre-industrial baseline, we empirically estimate the Hurst coefficient within a mean-reverting framework as a proxy for changes in temperature variance. By mapping these empirical Hurst values at a 0.5° spatial resolution across all 12 months, we identify seasonally heterogeneous patterns of variability amplification.

The resulting maps reveal continuity with earlier studies and new insights into the regional and seasonal amplification of temperature variability. For example, we observe pronounced increases in variability over equatorial regions, particularly the Amazon basin, during the boreal summer. The Amazon region is widely recognized for pronounced climate variability, including enhanced seasonal and interannual fluctuations34–36, and for being highly sensitive to extreme thermal stress and drought dynamics37,38. Previous studies have emphasized the importance of temperature variability14,39. Our framework extends this literature by providing a transparent and computationally efficient approach to characterize variability at high spatial and temporal resolution. As an analytical tool, our framework uses the empirical Hurst coefficient to establish a tractable link between stochastic features in observed climate records and policy-relevant measures of variability, informing how variability amplification can guide the sequencing and prioritization of climate solutions.

Temperature variability: a natural metric for climate change mitigation

In this section, we examine how temperature variability can be measured and interpreted as a natural metric for climate change mitigation. Efforts to limit global warming to 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels are motivated by the goal of avoiding irreversible impacts. Yet, a focus on mean temperature alone overlooks an equally important dimension of climate risk. Changes in temperature variability play a central role in shaping the socio-economic consequences of extreme events36,39,40. Moreover, the historical record shows that temperature variability exhibits pronounced regional and temporal structure, indicating that it contains information not captured by global mean temperature alone and can inform the prioritization of mitigation efforts14,23,41.

Following IPCC assessments3, we adopt the period 1850 to 1900 as the pre-industrial reference era and treat it as an ergodic reference state for baseline temperature variability31. In this context, ergodicity implies that statistical properties such as the mean and variance remain stable over time, allowing them to be inferred either from temporal observations or from an ensemble perspective. This assumption is supported by minimal anthropogenic forcing and the absence of large-scale externally forced temperature changes during the pre-industrial period41,42. Under anthropogenic forcing, the climate system departs from its pre-industrial baseline, consistent with the established conclusion that human activities are now the dominant driver of global climate change3. From a stochastic process perspective, such departures can be interpreted as excursions away from the reference state43.

Within our framework, we adopt a mean-reverting stochastic process as a reduced-form representation of temperature evolution, motivated by established stochastic temperature modeling approaches25–29. External forcing is held constant using parameters estimated from the historical temperature record, such that the spread of simulated trajectories reflects stochastic variability rather than differences in forcing. To minimize bias in measuring variability, long-term trends are removed from the temperature series. The empirically estimated Hurst coefficient then quantifies changes in temperature variance relative to the pre-industrial baseline. Although the Hurst coefficient theoretically spans values between 0 and 1, our analysis focuses on the interval from 0 to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{1}{2}$$\end{document}, corresponding to the anti-persistent and mean-reverting regime relevant here44. Higher empirical Hurst values indicate a weakening of short-term anti-persistence and greater persistence45. This interpretation remains distinct from long-memory behavior, which is not inferred in our analysis.

Since the Industrial Revolution, temperature variance has amplified unevenly across regions and seasons, reflecting the inertia of the climate system and its response to anthropogenic forcing45. In our framework, regional differences in variance amplification are captured by the empirical Hurst coefficient (Hemp), as illustrated in Fig. 1. Low-Hemp regions, such as the margins of Greenland46, exhibit gradual weakening of stability within the broader context of global warming. By contrast, high-Hemp regions, exemplified by the Amazon Basin47, display rapidly amplified variability, indicating a faster loss of stability. These contrasting cases illustrate how our framework distinguishes between gradual and rapid modes of stability loss. Without effective climate solutions, variance in high-Hemp regions continues to expand, signaling trajectories of escalating instability. If mitigation efforts succeed, temperature variability can be constrained within a managed regime characterized by lower Hemp values. High Hemp values observed across 12 months further identify regions where variance amplification is sustained throughout the year, reflecting structural instability rather than seasonal variability alone. Distinguishing between year-round amplification and seasonal variability is therefore essential for interpreting the spatial patterns revealed by our monthly analyses.

Theoretical basis

Our framework is motivated by the logic of Single-Model Initial-Condition Large Ensembles (SMILEs), which generate multiple realizations of the climate system under identical external forcing but perturbed initial conditions33. By producing ensembles of divergent trajectories, SMILEs enable a conceptual separation between the forced response and internal variability, while reducing uncertainty associated with structural differences across climate models18. As nonlinear interactions evolve, small perturbations in initial conditions amplify, leading to diverging simulations that collectively capture the stochastic nature of internal climate variability. This perspective underpins our stochastic framework, enabling us to adopt the rationale of large-ensemble approaches within a simplified, transparent statistical representation.

In this study, we adopt a stochastic mean-reverting process to characterize detrended temperature fluctuations in the historical record. From a stochastic-process perspective, many temperature trajectories are conceptually possible, each representing a distinct realization of stochastic perturbations around the observed trajectory. The observed temperature record, therefore, corresponds to one realized trajectory, while other potential realizations, if realized, would diverge from it over time under identical external forcing. Given the long-term nature of climate change and the relatively short observational record of approximately one century, our analysis focuses on quantifying temperature variability within this available data window. External forcing is treated as background information embedded in the historical record, rather than as an explicit time-varying model input. Accordingly, the Hemp index is introduced as an empirical diagnostic of changes in temperature variability over the past 121 years. This index allows us to describe variability around the single observed trajectory, rather than to generate ensembles of alternative climate realizations under prescribed forcing scenarios. Specifically, it captures a weakening of anti-persistent behavior and a corresponding variance amplification within the detrended month-specific windows. This window-specific scaling behavior does not necessitate that the full 121-year record of monthly or daily observations be represented by a single long-term dependence process.

As a preprocessing step, we detrend the temperature series by removing a deterministic linear trend estimated separately for each month using cross-year observations; details are provided in Supplementary Note 1. Because months are analyzed independently (e.g., January temperatures across years), intra-annual seasonality is held fixed and does not require explicit modeling. Empirical Hurst maps for identifying high climate-risk regions

Using global monthly temperature data from the widely used CRU TS v4.06 dataset 15,53−55, we map Hemp coefficients at 0.5° spatial resolution for each month worldwide (Fig. 2). Yellow regions indicate areas where historical temperature variance through 2021 remains close to the pre-industrial baseline, whereas purple regions highlight pronounced increases. The resulting maps reveal that some regions exhibit persistent year-round amplification, whereas others show pronounced seasonal dependence. Such heterogeneous responses of temperature variability to global warming are consistent with previous observational and modeling studies documenting strong regional and seasonal structure in variability changes56–58. While the monthly patterns alone do not identify underlying mechanisms, they provide a basis for comparison with large-ensemble climate simulations and local case studies. To address uncertainty in parameter estimation, we report grid-level statistical significance tests for the estimated mean-reversion parameter, with corresponding p-values shown in Supplementary Fig. S4.

Across all 12 maps, low-latitude regions consistently exhibit higher Hemp values than high-latitude regions throughout the year. The contrast is particularly pronounced during the boreal summer (June–September), when sea-ice dynamics strongly influence variability59. In the Arctic, rising air temperatures driven by CO₂ emissions60,61 accelerate sea-ice melt, while the associated increase in ocean heat capacity buffers temperature variability relative to equatorial regions36,62, consistent with earlier findings of attenuated variability responses at mid- and high latitudes57,63. At finer spatial scales, the margins of the Greenland ice sheet exhibit stronger variability changes along the eastern periphery than the western margin, highlighting ice-sheet boundaries as sensitive zones46,64.

While global maps reveal broad patterns, continent-level analysis highlights equatorial hotspots where temperature variability is most strongly amplified. In South America, the Amazon Basin exhibits consistently high Hemp values throughout the year, with the largest values concentrated in southeastern Amazonia during boreal summer. This spatial pattern aligns with previous findings that southeastern Amazonia has experienced more intense deforestation, warming, and dry-season moisture stress than western regions, alongside elevated fire activity and ecosystem degradation47,65,66. In Southeast Asia, higher Hemp values are consistently observed across western Indonesia throughout the year, with pronounced peaks in July, September, and November, consistent with the heightened climate sensitivity of island systems65. Identifying such high-risk regions is critical for informing targeted climate solutions, though detailed risk assessments remain necessary to ensure interventions reduce rather than amplify existing vulnerabilities.

Seasonal heterogeneity evident at continental scales highlights dynamics15 that are often obscured in annual assessments but are critical for understanding the impacts of global warming23. Africa provides a clear example, as its landmass spans both hemispheres and exhibits pronounced spatial and temporal contrasts in temperature variability. In East Africa, Hemp values increase from June to September, peaking in August, whereas West and Central Africa show comparatively lower values during the same period. From October to December, this pattern reverses, leaving the forests of West Africa and the Congo Basin with more pronounced variability. Although our monthly maps cannot directly attribute these patterns to vegetation types, recent studies suggest that elevated atmospheric CO₂ may promote woody plant growth under arid conditions, potentially enhancing resilience66,67. However, the long-term stability of these ecosystems remains uncertain, as African landscapes may undergo abrupt transitions between woodland- and grassland-dominated states68, compounded by ongoing pressures from selective logging, fire, and edge effects that have already driven substantial carbon loss69. Preventing further deforestation, therefore, remains critical, given the central role of tropical forests in regulating regional and global climate.

Extending these empirical patterns into a forward-looking perspective, we consider a scenario in which Hemp remains constant over the next century. Under this assumption, we compute the maximum Hemp for each continent across 12 months (Table 1) and the corresponding variability amplification factor over a 100-year horizon (Table 2). Among the four largest continents, maxima occur most frequently in Amazonia, highlighting its role as a global hotspot of temperature variability. By contrast, Africa and Asia exhibit shifting maxima, seasonally clustered around 0.042 and 0.047, respectively, indicating spatially and temporally varying centers of weaker anti-persistence that do not translate into year-round amplification of variability. This contrast underscores the need for sustained monitoring and continent-specific strategies to manage climate risks.

To assess sensitivity to the choice of gridded temperature product, we repeated the core mapping exercise using the Berkeley Earth70 monthly dataset with the same preprocessing and estimation procedures, and the results are shown in Supplementary Fig. S5. Despite differences in spatial resolution, the Berkeley Earth maps show substantial overlap with CRU TS v4.06 in the location of the main hotspot regions, indicating that the broad spatial pattern is robust across products. Local temperature levels and the exact extent of some regions nevertheless differ because the two products are constructed differently. CRU TS v4.06 grids monthly station anomalies on a 0.5° land grid relative to a 1961–1990 anomaly reference period and converts them to actual values using product climatologies, whereas the Berkeley Earth fields used here are based on an independent 1° reconstruction, with absolute temperatures obtained by adding a 1951–1980 monthly climatology to the anomaly fields. These methodological differences can affect local temperature magnitudes even when the dominant hotspot structure remains similar.

Decoupled pathways of emissions and variability reveal unequal climate risks

Greenhouse gas emissions are the dominant driver of global warming, but the risks that follow from rising temperatures and amplified variability are distributed unevenly. Equatorial regions have contributed relatively little to historical emissions but already face disproportionately severe impacts58. This decoupling between emissions responsibility and climate risk highlights a fundamental equity challenge, as high-emitting regions have benefited most from fossil fuel use while shifting a larger share of climate risks onto more vulnerable populations. Addressing this imbalance requires that responsibility and equity be explicitly considered when evaluating and prioritizing climate solutions.

Discussions of national responsibility in the climate literature are typically framed in terms of aggregated, country-level attribution metrics, including national contributions to observed global warming71, country-level attribution of climate extremes72, and estimates of climate-related damages73. Consistent with this approach, we present country-level Hemp values for August in Fig. 3, ranking the top 20 countries by cumulative GHG emissions74. August is selected because it exhibits the strongest amplification of temperature variability in the global maps and therefore represents an upper-bound case. Across the ranking, no clear systematic relationship emerges between total emissions and variability amplification. The four largest emitters exhibit relatively low Hemp values, whereas countries such as Brazil, Indonesia, and Saudi Arabia display higher values with wide interquartile ranges and numerous outliers. Among the lower half of the ranking, developing countries tend to exhibit higher median Hemp values and broader interquartile ranges than developed countries. This pattern indicates greater within-country heterogeneity in temperature variability amplification, whereas developed countries tend to display more tightly clustered distributions of Hemp. Such differences underscore that national averages can mask substantial internal disparities, particularly in regions already exposed to elevated climate variability.

Hence, given the limited time available to deploy climate solutions, global disparities in climate risk deserve explicit consideration. Our results show that amplified temperature variability does not align with national emission levels and that substantial heterogeneity exists both across and within countries. This mismatch reveals that climate risk is not simply a function of aggregate emissions but arises from the interplay between localized variability dynamics, ecological feedback, and societal vulnerabilities. Ignoring these interactions risks obscuring the regions where instabilities accumulate most rapidly. Recognizing this complexity is essential for moving beyond aggregate indicators and grounding climate policy in the spatial and temporal dynamics that drive risk.

Discussion

Attempts to keep warming within 1.5 °C2, 3 have been hindered by insufficient mitigation progress13, making overshoot pathways increasingly plausible7−9, 11, 12. This shift highlights the limits of relying solely on mean temperature targets to assess climate risk, as changes in temperature variability are more directly linked to the frequency and severity of extremes and their socioeconomic impacts36, 39, 40. Monitoring how variability evolves, therefore, provides a complementary perspective for identifying regions where climatic stability is eroding most rapidly and for evaluating whether mitigation efforts are sufficient to slow or contain that erosion. In this context, our empirical Hurst maps quantify changes in temperature variance relative to the pre-industrial baseline and offer a tractable lens for interpreting regional and seasonal asymmetries in climate risk that aggregate metrics often obscure15, 23.

Our global Hemp maps across 12 months reveal coherent patterns of change in temperature variability across multiple spatial scales, from localized hotspots to global contrasts. At the local scale, southeastern Amazonia, western Indonesia, and the margins of Greenland consistently emerge as centers of amplified variability. These regions are widely recognized as climate-sensitive systems, and their persistent instability highlights heightened vulnerability to ecological disruption and cascading climate feedback56−58,64−66. At the continental scale, Africa exhibits pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity in variability change, consistent with the complexity of land–climate interactions in regions characterized by mixed forest–savanna landscapes and spatially uneven human influences67, 69. Such heterogeneity underscores the difficulty of projecting long-term changes in variance using localized analyses alone. On the global scale, variance trajectories diverge between high and low latitudes, with contrasts that intensify during the boreal summer57–63. These seasonal asymmetries reflect the integrated outcome of localized amplifications of variability across the climate system since the Industrial Revolution.

Based on observed changes in temperature variability, our results emphasize the importance of focusing mitigation efforts on regions previously identified as highly vulnerable, particularly in low-latitude areas that play a critical role in regulating the global climate system. For example, local studies in Amazonia have documented high vulnerability and underscored the need for targeted interventions37, 38. In this context, efforts to restore resilience in southeastern Amazonia may offer insights relevant to preventing similar degradation in other tropical forests69. Restoring resilience and reducing local temperature variability could help disrupt reinforcing feedback between extreme weather events and ecosystem degradation. Beyond the near-term benefit of shifting southeastern Amazonia away from its role as a net carbon source, such interventions could contribute to broader carbon removal objectives and help reduce the risk of abrupt and unsafe transitions in the global climate system.

Despite global pledges, substantial uncertainties remain in how mitigation will be implemented, shaped by uneven national capacities and unequal exposure to climate risks75. Addressing these disparities requires not only international coordination but also transparent, accessible diagnostic approaches that are understandable to non-expert decision-makers and the public, who ultimately support and invest in climate solutions. Accordingly, our results suggest that stabilizing temperature variability, starting with targeted interventions in local hotspots and expanding to broader regions, can inform the prioritization of risk reduction efforts while comprehensive global mitigation proceeds more slowly. The observed divergence between variability amplification and national emissions further highlights the need to reassess prevailing notions of fairness in climate solutions.

Although further work is needed to clarify the mechanisms driving regional and seasonal variability, our empirical mapping of variability dynamics already provides a basis for sequencing climate actions by revealing spatial and temporal risk patterns, thereby helping to connect scientific urgency with more equitable policy design. While alternative temporal-scale perspectives may shift the mechanistic interpretation of the observed scaling behavior, the empirical month-specific patterns documented in the historical record remain unchanged. Future work could extend the present analysis by applying climacogram-based variance-scale diagnostics to higher-frequency temperature data, where such methods provide more stable estimation of scaling behavior, thereby complementing the Hemp-based month-specific comparison with a broader temporal-scale perspective76.
