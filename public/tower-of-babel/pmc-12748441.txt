Genetic evidence for causal association between platelet count and glioma risk through Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: XiaoYong Han, Mengyan Chen, Zhisheng Ji, Hui Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioma is the most common primary brain tumor in adults, with glioblastoma (GBM) being the most aggressive subtype. The causal relationship between platelet count and glioma risk remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate this relationship using Mendelian randomization (MR) methodology.MethodsWe performed MR analysis using 707 platelet count-associated SNPs as instrumental variables, validated by three statistical methods (IVW radial, Maximum likelihood, and MR IVW). Genome-wide association study (GWAS) identified glioma susceptibility loci. We also measured mRNA expression of 4 platelet-related genes (THPO, GP6, ITGA2B, MPL) in glioma cell lines (U87-MG and U251-MG) and normal astrocytes (NHA) using qRT-PCR.ResultsMR analysis demonstrated that genetically determined increased platelet count significantly reduces glioma risk (OR: 0.721–0.724, 95% CI: 0.554–0.938, P < 0.05), representing approximately 27–28% risk reduction per standard deviation increase. GWAS identified multiple susceptibility loci across chromosomes 1, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11, 16, and 20. This protective association was confirmed in GBM subtype analysis (OR: 0.65–0.95). All 4 platelet-related genes showed significant downregulation in glioma cells compared to normal astrocytes (P < 0.001), with expression ranging from 0.31 to 0.48-fold of controls.ConclusionsThis study establishes a causal relationship between platelet count and glioma risk through genetic evidence. Increased platelet count confers protection against glioma, including GBM. Suppressed expression of platelet-related genes in glioma cells supports this finding. These results provide new insights into glioma pathogenesis and suggest potential for platelet-based prevention strategies and biomarker development.Keywords: Glioma, Glioblastoma, Platelet count, Mendelian randomization, Genome-wide association study, Causal inference

Introduction

Glioma represents the most prevalent and devastating primary brain tumor in adults, accounting for approximately 80% of all malignant brain neoplasms [1, 2]. The annual incidence of glioma ranges from 4.7 to 5.7 per 100,000 individuals globally [1, 3], with glioblastoma (GBM) constituting the most aggressive subtype and carrying an extremely poor prognosis [4]. Despite significant advances in neurosurgical techniques, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy over the past decades, the median survival for GBM patients remains disappointingly short at approximately 12–15 months [5, 6], highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches and prevention strategies.

The etiology of glioma remains largely enigmatic, with only a few established risk factors identified, including exposure to ionizing radiation and rare hereditary syndromes such as neurofibromatosis and Li-Fraumeni syndrome [7, 8]. Recent epidemiological investigations have begun to explore the potential relationships between systemic inflammatory markers, hematological parameters, and glioma risk. These studies have suggested intriguing associations between various blood cell counts, inflammatory indices, and brain tumor development, though the causal nature of these relationships has remained uncertain due to inherent limitations of observational study designs.

Platelets, beyond their well-established role in hemostasis and thrombosis, have emerged as critical mediators in cancer biology through multiple mechanisms [9, 10]. These anucleate cell fragments possess the remarkable ability to interact with circulating tumor cells, facilitate metastatic dissemination, modulate immune responses, and influence angiogenesis [11]. The “tumor-educated platelets” concept has revolutionized our understanding of platelet-cancer interactions, demonstrating that platelets can sequester tumor-derived biomolecules and subsequently alter their RNA profiles, potentially serving as liquid biopsy markers for cancer detection and monitoring [12, 13].

In the context of brain tumors, the blood-brain barrier presents unique challenges and opportunities for understanding systemic-neurological interactions. While the brain was traditionally considered an immunologically privileged site, accumulating evidence suggests that peripheral immune and hematological factors can significantly influence central nervous system pathology. Several observational studies have reported associations between platelet counts, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratios, and glioma outcomes, though these investigations have been limited by potential confounding factors and reverse causation bias.

Mendelian randomization (MR) methodology offers a powerful approach to overcome the limitations of conventional observational studies by leveraging genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causal relationships [14]. This technique exploits the random allocation of genetic variants at conception, mimicking the design of randomized controlled trials and reducing the impact of confounding factors and reverse causation. The application of MR to investigate relationships between hematological parameters and cancer risk has yielded valuable insights across various malignancies [15], yet comprehensive investigations in the context of glioma remain limited.

The present study aimed to address this knowledge gap by conducting a comprehensive MR analysis specifically focused on glioma, a cancer type with distinct features including the blood-brain barrier that may fundamentally alter platelet-tumor biology compared to peripheral malignancies. The present study aimed to address this knowledge gap by conducting a comprehensive MR analysis to investigate the causal relationship between platelet count and glioma risk. By utilizing large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) data and employing multiple statistical approaches, we sought to provide robust evidence regarding the potential protective or predisposing effects of platelet count on glioma development, with particular attention to the most malignant subtype, glioblastoma.

Methods

Mendelian randomization study framework

This investigation leveraged Mendelian randomization techniques to assess causal associations between platelet levels and glioma susceptibility. The analysis incorporated 707 single nucleotide polymorphisms serving as instrumental variables, each demonstrating strong correlation with platelet counts while meeting fundamental MR assumptions. To verify the reliability of findings, three distinct statistical approaches were implemented for confirmatory testing: conventional inverse variance weighted (MR IVW), IVW radial, and Maximum likelihood methods. Consistency across analyses was maintained through the use of identical instrumental variable sets.

Genome-wide association study

A systematic genome-wide association study was performed to detect genetic loci conferring susceptibility to glioma. The investigation examined genetic variations spanning all 23 human chromosomes, applying stringent genome-wide significance criteria (P < 5 × 10^-8) to pinpoint genetic regions demonstrating significant association with glioma susceptibility. Association signal distribution patterns across the genome were visualized using Manhattan plots, revealing notable signal peaks on multiple chromosomes including 1, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11, 16, and 20. Association magnitude for loci meeting significance thresholds was quantified using -log10(P) values.

Hematological marker evaluation

A systematic investigation examined relationships between multiple blood-based parameters and glioma susceptibility, encompassing platelet count (BCX2_PLT), white blood cell count (BCX2_WBC), monocyte count (BCX2_MON), neutrophil count (BCX2_NEU), mean platelet volume (BCX2_MPV), and additional relevant markers. Mendelian randomization-based causal inference was applied to each hematological indicator. Through comparative evaluation of statistical significance (p-values) among parameters, the study determined which blood markers exhibited the strongest associations with glioma susceptibility. Circular visualization plots enabled straightforward comparison of relative association strengths.

Sensitivity testing and quality assurance

Rigorous quality assurance protocols and sensitivity assessments were executed to verify the validity of MR findings. Initially, concordance among effect estimates derived from multiple MR approaches (IVW, weighted median, MR-Egger regression) was examined to confirm robustness. Subsequently, funnel plot evaluation was utilized to identify potential horizontal pleiotropy, examining whether instrumental variables affected glioma risk via alternative pathways independent of platelet count. Additionally, leave-one-out sensitivity testing was performed through iterative removal of individual instrumental variables and recalculation of effect sizes to assess the impact of single SNPs on overall conclusions. Lastly, instrumental variable validity was confirmed by ensuring selected SNPs demonstrated appropriate effect distributions for both exposure and outcome measures.

Genetic fine-mapping and gene prioritization

Detailed fine-mapping was conducted within key glioma susceptibility loci to refine candidate gene regions and pinpoint potentially pathogenic variant positions. Linkage disequilibrium patterns among SNPs in candidate regions were characterized, utilizing color-based visualization to denote LD strength with lead variants: strong linkage (red), moderate linkage (yellow), and weak or absent linkage (green). Within 1 Mb genomic windows, SNP association patterns and LD structures were examined in conjunction with functional annotations to localize glioma susceptibility genes precisely. This fine-mapping approach enhances understanding of the molecular pathogenesis of glioma and identifies targets for future functional validation experiments.

Glioblastoma subtype analysis

Given the importance of glioblastoma (GBM) as the most malignant glioma subtype, separate Mendelian randomization analysis was conducted specifically for this tumor type. The same three statistical methods used in overall glioma analysis (Maximum likelihood, IVW radial, and Inverse variance weighted) were employed to evaluate the causal relationship between platelet count and GBM-specific risk. By comparing GBM subtype analysis results with overall glioma analysis results, we explored similarities and differences in platelet count effects on risk across different glioma subtypes, providing scientific evidence for developing personalized prevention strategies.

Cultivation of cell lines

U87-MG and U251-MG, two human glioma cell lines, were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences located in Shanghai, China. Culture conditions consisted of high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) along with antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin). A humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂ at 37 °C was maintained throughout cell cultivation. As a control population, Normal Human Astrocytes (NHA) obtained from ScienCell (USA) were grown following the supplier’s recommended astrocyte culture protocols. Mycoplasma contamination testing yielded negative results for all cell lines, and cellular identity was verified through short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. Through functional annotation screening and comprehensive literature mining of the 707 platelet count-linked SNPs discovered via Mendelian randomization, we prioritized 4 target genes with established roles in platelet genesis, activation, and physiological function:1. THPO (Thrombopoietin): Functions as a principal regulatory cytokine governing megakaryocyte maturation and platelet biogenesis; 2. GP6 (Glycoprotein VI): Serves as a critical platelet surface receptor that facilitates platelet adhesion and activation processes; 3. ITGA2B (Integrin Subunit Alpha 2b): Comprises the αIIb component of integrin that partners with ITGB3 to constitute the GPIIb/IIIa complex involved in platelet aggregation; 4. MPL (Myeloproliferative Leukemia Protein): Acts as the receptor for thrombopoietin, transducing THPO signaling cascades. Geographic proximity of these 4 genes to significant genetic susceptibility regions identified through MR analysis, combined with their well-characterized platelet biology functions, supported their selection as candidates.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis (qRT-PCR)

TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) was employed for total RNA isolation from cultured cells. A NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) measured RNA quantity and quality, with acceptable A260/A280 ratios ranging from 1.8 to 2.0. The PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan) converted 1 µg total RNA into first-strand cDNA. A QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) executed qRT-PCR amplification using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Japan) as the fluorescent indicator. Each 20 µL reaction contained the following components: TB Green Premix (10 µL), forward and reverse primers at 10 µM concentration (0.8 µL each), cDNA template (2 µL), and nuclease-free water (6.4 µL ddH₂O). Thermal cycling parameters included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 30 s, succeeded by 40 cycles alternating between 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 34 s. Expression normalization utilized GAPDH as the housekeeping reference, with the 2⁻ΔΔCt algorithm computing relative expression values. Three independent experimental replicates were conducted, with each sample analyzed in triplicate.Oligonucleotide Primer Sequences: The following primer pairs were utilized in amplification reactions: THPO gene - forward sequence 5′-AGCTGCTGAACCTGGAGATG-3′, reverse sequence 5′-TCCAGGTAGTCGTTGGTGAC-3′; GP6 gene - forward sequence 5′-CTACCTGGTGACCCTCAACA-3′, reverse sequence 5′-GGAGGCAGATGTAGGTCTGG-3′; ITGA2B gene - forward sequence 5′-CAGCAAGCCAGATGACAAGA-3′, reverse sequence 5′-TGGTGAAGGTGATGAGGTTG-3′; MPL gene - forward sequence 5′-GCCTACAACCTGTCCCTCAT-3′, reverse sequence 5′-CTCCAGGATGTTGACCTTGG-3′; GAPDH gene - forward sequence 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′, reverse sequence 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [16, 17].

Results

Mendelian randomization analysis results of platelet count and glioma risk

A Mendelian randomization analysis based on 707 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) demonstrated a significant causal relationship between platelet count and glioma risk. Three different statistical methods were employed for validation, including IVW radial, Maximum likelihood, and conventional MR IVW methods, showing high consistency across all approaches. All three methods yielded similar odds ratios (OR) ranging from 0.721 to 0.724, with 95% confidence intervals of 0.554–0.938, and P-values less than 0.05 (0.010–0.015), indicating statistical significance. These findings suggest a causal association between genetically determined increased platelet count and reduced glioma risk, with each standard deviation increase in platelet count corresponding to approximately 27–28% reduction in glioma risk (Fig. 1).

Comprehensive Mendelian randomization analysis of hematological parameters and glioma risk association

This study systematically evaluated the causal relationships between various hematological parameters and glioma risk using Mendelian randomization approaches. The analysis encompassed multiple blood biomarkers including platelet count (BCX2_PLT), monocyte count (BCX2_MON), mean platelet volume (BCX2_MPV), white blood cell count (BCX2_WBC), neutrophil count (BCX2_NEU), and others, with validation using different Mendelian randomization methods. The results revealed significant heterogeneity in the association strength between different hematological parameters and glioma risk. Platelet-related parameters (BCX2_PLT) demonstrated the strongest statistical significance with p-values approaching 0, indicating a robust causal association between platelet count and glioma risk. Monocyte count (BCX2_MON) and mean platelet volume (BCX2_MPV) also showed moderate levels of association. In contrast, other hematological parameters such as certain white blood cell subset counts exhibited weaker or non-significant associations with glioma risk. These findings provide important genetic evidence for potential connections between the hematological system and brain tumor pathogenesis mechanisms (Fig. 3).

Mendelian randomization analysis of platelet count and glioblastoma risk

For glioblastoma (GBM), the most malignant subtype of glioma, Mendelian randomization analysis further validated the causal relationship between platelet count and tumor risk. Three different statistical methods were employed for analysis, including Maximum likelihood, IVW radial, and Inverse variance weighted approaches, demonstrating high consistency and robustness in results. The odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals from all three methods were positioned below 1.0, ranging approximately between 0.65 and 0.95, indicating that genetically determined increased platelet count is causally associated with significantly reduced glioblastoma risk. This finding is particularly important because glioblastoma is the most common and poorest-prognosis primary brain tumor in adults, with a median survival typically not exceeding 15 months. Platelet count, as a simple and readily available hematological marker, provides new insights into tumor pathogenesis mechanisms and potential prevention strategies through its protective association with GBM risk (Fig. 6).

mRNA expression levels of platelet-related genes in glioma cell lines

To validate whether the platelet-related genetic variants identified through Mendelian randomization analysis affect the biological behavior of glioma cells, we first examined the mRNA expression levels of 4 key platelet-related genes (THPO, GP6, ITGA2B, MPL) in two human glioma cell lines (U87-MG and U251-MG) and normal human astrocytes (NHA).qRT-PCR results showed that compared to normal astrocytes, all 4 platelet-related genes exhibited significant downregulation in glioma cell lines (Fig. 7A–D). Specifically: THPO gene** expression in U87-MG and U251-MG cells was 0.42 ± 0.06 and 0.38 ± 0.05-fold relative to NHA cells, respectively (both P < 0.001); GP6 gene expression in U87-MG and U251-MG cells was 0.35 ± 0.07 and 0.31 ± 0.06-fold, respectively (both P < 0.001); ITGA2B gene expression in U87-MG and U251-MG cells was 0.48 ± 0.08 and 0.44 ± 0.07-fold, respectively (both P < 0.001); MPL gene** expression in U87-MG and U251-MG cells was 0.39 ± 0.05 and 0.36 ± 0.06-fold, respectively (both P < 0.001). Notably, there were no significant differences in gene expression levels between the two glioma cell lines (P > 0.05), indicating that this downregulation pattern is consistent across different glioma cell types. These results suggest that the suppressed expression of platelet-related genes in glioma cells may be associated with tumor development and progression, providing cellular-level support for the negative genetic correlation between platelet count and glioma risk observed in our MR analysis.

Discussion

The observed protective association between higher platelet count and reduced glioma risk challenges conventional assumptions about platelet-cancer relationships and suggests complex, context-dependent mechanisms in brain tumor biology. Unlike many peripheral malignancies where elevated platelet counts are often associated with increased cancer risk and poor prognosis, our findings indicate that higher constitutional platelet levels may confer protection against glioma development. This paradoxical finding is particularly noteworthy given that the brain’s unique microenvironment characterized by the blood-brain barrier, absence of conventional lymphatic drainage, and immunologically specialized status may fundamentally alter platelet-tumor interactions compared to systemic cancers.

Several biological mechanisms may explain this seemingly paradoxical relationship. First, platelets serve as crucial mediators of immune surveillance within the central nervous system [18]. Despite the blood-brain barrier’s selective permeability, platelets can interact with brain endothelial cells and potentially influence neuroinflammatory processes that may be relevant to tumor initiation and progression [19]. Higher platelet counts may enhance the capacity for immune surveillance and early detection of malignant transformation within the brain parenchyma.

Second, the unique microenvironment of the central nervous system may fundamentally alter platelet-tumor interactions compared to peripheral organs. The brain’s limited regenerative capacity and distinctive vascular architecture may create conditions where platelets primarily function as protective rather than tumor-promoting factors. The absence of lymphatic drainage in the brain and the specialized nature of the blood-brain barrier may modify how platelets interact with potential tumor cells, potentially favoring anti-tumorigenic rather than pro-tumorigenic effects [20].

Third, constitutional differences in platelet count may reflect underlying genetic variations that independently influence both hematopoiesis and brain tumor susceptibility [21]. Our Mendelian randomization approach, utilizing genetic variants associated with platelet count, may be capturing pleiotropic effects of genes that simultaneously regulate platelet production and neural cell biology. Several transcription factors and signaling pathways involved in megakaryopoiesis also play roles in neural development and glial cell regulation, suggesting potential shared molecular mechanisms [22]. However, our comprehensive sensitivity analyses, including MR-Egger regression and funnel plot assessment, indicate that the observed association operates primarily through platelet-related pathways rather than substantial horizontal pleiotropy, strengthening the validity of our causal inference.

An important consideration in interpreting our findings relates to the molecular heterogeneity of gliomas, particularly the distinction between IDH-mutant and IDH-wild-type tumors. The 2016 and subsequent 2021 WHO classification updates have established IDH mutation status as a fundamental molecular classifier with profound implications for prognosis, treatment response, and underlying biology. Emerging evidence suggests that IDH mutations may alter the hemostatic milieu, with some studies reporting reduced thrombotic risk and altered platelet-endothelial interactions in IDH-mutant glioma patients.

Unfortunately, the GWAS datasets utilized in our analysis do not include stratification by IDH mutation status, representing a significant limitation. Given that IDH-mutant gliomas tend to occur in younger patients, progress more slowly, and exhibit fundamentally different metabolic profiles (including altered 2-hydroxyglutarate accumulation and hypoxia signaling) compared to IDH-wild-type tumors, it is biologically plausible that platelet-mediated protective mechanisms might operate differently across these molecularly distinct entities. The protective association we observed in overall glioma and GBM may reflect heterogeneous effects that vary by IDH status, tumor grade, or other molecular features.

Future GWAS efforts incorporating comprehensive molecular annotations, including IDH mutation status, MGMT promoter methylation, 1p/19q codeletion, and other key alterations, would enable more refined Mendelian randomization analyses. Such molecularly stratified investigations represent a high-priority research direction that could substantially advance our understanding of which glioma subtypes are most influenced by constitutional platelet count and potentially identify patient subgroups who might benefit most from platelet-count-based risk stratification.

The magnitude of the observed association—with each standard deviation increase in platelet count corresponding to approximately 27–28% reduction in glioma risk suggests clinically meaningful effects that could inform individual risk counseling and potentially guide screening recommendations. Individuals with constitutionally low platelet counts within the normal range might benefit from enhanced surveillance or participation in prevention trials, while those with higher counts might receive reassurance about their relatively lower risk profile.

However, several important caveats must be considered before clinical implementation. First, our findings reflect the causal effects of genetically determined platelet counts rather than acquired changes in platelet levels due to medications, medical conditions, or lifestyle factors. The clinical relevance of acquired platelet count variations for glioma risk remains to be determined and may differ substantially from the genetic effects we observed.

Our results may also have implications for therapeutic development and drug repurposing efforts in glioma treatment. If higher platelet counts are indeed protective against glioma development, this raises questions about whether pharmacological interventions that increase platelet production might have therapeutic or preventive potential. Thrombopoietin receptor agonists, currently used to treat thrombocytopenia in various clinical contexts [23], could theoretically be investigated for their potential neuroprotective effects, though such applications would require extensive preclinical and clinical validation.

Conversely, our findings raise important safety considerations for antiplatelet therapies in high-risk populations [24]. While aspirin and other antiplatelet agents are widely used for cardiovascular protection, their potential effects on brain tumor risk have received limited attention [25]. Our results suggest that the benefits and risks of antiplatelet therapy might need to be carefully balanced, particularly in individuals with additional glioma risk factors or family histories of brain tumors.

The specific analysis of glioblastoma, the most aggressive glioma subtype, revealed similar protective associations with higher platelet counts. This finding is particularly significant given GBM’s devastating prognosis and limited therapeutic options. The consistency of effects across different glioma subtypes suggests that platelet-mediated protective mechanisms may be relevant to fundamental processes of glial tumorigenesis rather than specific histological variants.

For GBM patients and their families, these findings may inform genetic counseling and risk assessment discussions. Understanding that constitutional platelet levels may influence brain tumor susceptibility could contribute to more personalized approaches to surveillance and prevention, particularly for individuals with familial cancer syndromes or other predisposing factors.

Limitation

While Mendelian randomization provides robust evidence for causal relationships at the population level, our study has several important limitations. First, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying the observed associations remain to be elucidated through functional studies. Our analysis establishes genetic epidemiological evidence for causality but does not directly demonstrate the biological pathways through which platelet count influences glioma risk. Experimental validation through multiple complementary approaches would strengthen mechanistic understanding.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our comprehensive Mendelian randomization analysis provides compelling evidence for a causal protective association between higher platelet count and reduced glioma risk. These findings challenge existing paradigms about platelet-cancer relationships in the context of brain tumors and offer new insights into the complex biology of glioma development.
