Octahedral and tetrahedral site substitution in spinel Mn3O4 regulates oxygen evolution in acid
Authors: Haoqiang Song, Mingjun Nie, Jingkun Yu, Guoli Zhou, Zhiyong Tang, Siyu Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesigning cost-effective and acid-stable electrocatalysts for the oxygen evolution reaction is essential for advancing proton exchange membrane water electrolyzers. Here we report a dual-site substituted spinel Mn3O4 catalyst in which Ru partially occupies octahedral Mn sites to enhance Ru–O hybridization and intrinsic catalytic activity, while Co partially replaces tetrahedral Mn sites to stabilize the lattice and improve electronic conductivity. The catalyst requires an overpotential of 175 mV to reach 10 mA cmgeo−2 and maintains operation for more than 650 hours at 100 mA cmgeo−2 with a voltage degradation rate of 0.23 mV h−1 in 0.5 M H2SO4. When used as the anode in a proton exchange membrane water electrolyzer, it delivers 1 A cm−2 at a cell voltage of 1.69 V, demonstrating its potential for practical acidic water electrolysis. In situ characterization confirms that Ru and Co substitution alters the oxygen evolution pathway. These findings establish a site-specific cation substitution strategy for developing active, durable, and cost-effective Ru-based electrocatalysts under acidic conditions.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Synthesis and processing, Hydrogen energy

Introduction

Proton exchange membrane water electrolysis (PEMWE) has emerged as a promising technology for sustainable hydrogen production, owing to its high current density capability, rapid dynamic response, and the generation of high-purity hydrogen1–3. However, the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) at the anode suffers from sluggish kinetics under acidic conditions, and the lack of electrocatalysts that are both highly active and durable remains a critical barrier to its widespread commercialization4–6. Currently, iridium-based (Ir-based) catalysts dominate the commercial market due to their activity and stability, but their exorbitant cost and limited availability present significant challenges for large-scale deployment7–9. In contrast, ruthenium-based (Ru-based) catalysts are gaining increasing attention as promising alternatives, offering comparable OER activity to Ir while benefiting from lower cost and greater abundance10–12.

Although Ru-based catalysts exhibit high intrinsic activity toward the OER under acidic conditions, their long-term durability remains a major challenge13–16. This instability primarily originates from the oxidation of ruthenium into highly soluble high-valence species, such as RuO4 when subjected to anodic potentials, which leads to the progressive loss of active sites and eventual catalyst deactivation17,18. In addition, at high operating voltages, lattice oxygen participation in the OER may be triggered, which leads to structural disintegration and irreversible catalyst collapse19–21.

To enhance the durability of Ru-based catalysts under acidic OER conditions, extensive efforts have been devoted to stabilizing the local Ru environment and suppressing Ru dissolution22–24. Among these approaches, heteroatom doping of RuO2 has emerged as an effective strategy to regulate Ru–O bonding, modulate electronic structure, and mitigate the formation of soluble high-valence Ru species under anodic potentials25,26. In parallel, reducing Ru loading while maximizing atomic utilization has attracted increasing attention, particularly through dispersing Ru at the atomic scale on acid-stable oxide matrices27–29. Such strategies not only improve Ru utilization efficiency and mass activity, but also provide interfacial electronic stabilization that retards Ru oxidation and dissolution, while simultaneously lowering overall material cost30,31.

Among various oxide hosts explored for Ru stabilization, spinel-type Mn3O4 represents a particularly attractive platform due to its intrinsic coexistence of crystallographically and electronically distinct octahedral and tetrahedral metal sites, together with favorable electronic conductivity and ionic transport properties32,33. Unlike structurally homogeneous oxide supports, Mn3O4 inherently enables site-specific regulation of metal–oxygen interactions within a single lattice framework.

From a structural and electronic perspective, Mn3O4 features Mn3+ ions located at octahedral sites coordinated by edge-sharing MnO6 units, which typically exhibit stronger metal–oxygen covalency and more flexible redox chemistry, favoring the adsorption and activation of oxygenated intermediates during OER28,34–36. In contrast, Mn2+ ions at tetrahedral sites are coordinated by isolated MnO4 units with comparatively weaker metal–oxygen interactions, playing a crucial role in stabilizing the lattice framework and regulating oxygen vacancy formation22,34. Despite these intrinsically distinct coordination environments, a clear understanding of how site-selective substitution at octahedral versus tetrahedral positions governs OER activity, reaction pathways, and catalyst stability under acidic conditions has yet to be established.

In this work, we address this gap by designing a dual-site substituted spinel Mn3O4 catalyst, denoted as DSC, where Ru partially occupies octahedral Mn (Mnoct) sites, and Co preferentially substitutes tetrahedral Mn (Mntet) sites (Fig. 1), allowing us to disentangle the distinct roles of octahedral and tetrahedral substitution in regulating OER activity, reaction pathways, and catalyst stability under acidic conditions. The DSC delivered the most favorable acidic OER performance while requiring an Ru loading of just 0.06 mgRu cmgeo−2. In a standard three-electrode configuration, DSC displayed an low overpotential of 175 mV at 10 mA cmgeo–2 and notable operational durability, sustaining continuous OER at 200 mA cmgeo–2 for over 300 h with an degradation rate of 0.58 mV h–1. When employed as the anode in a PEMWE, DSC achieved current densities of 1 A cm−2 at cell voltages of 1.69 V. Comprehensive in situ spectroscopic analyses, including attenuated total reflection surface-enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS), differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS), and Raman measurements, revealed a mechanistic transition from the lattice oxygen mechanism to a dual-oxygen mechanism upon Ru and Co substitution. These results indicate that introducing Ru into octahedral sites and Co into tetrahedral sites modulates the surface reaction pathway while enhancing the structural robustness of the catalyst under acidic OER conditions. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further demonstrated that Ru3+ incorporation into octahedral sites strengthens Ru–O hybridization, thereby enhancing intrinsic OER activity, whereas Co2+ substitution at tetrahedral sites stabilizes the lattice oxygen and improves the electronic conductivity of the host oxide. Collectively, these findings establish a site-specific substitution strategy for designing cost-effective and acid-stable Ru-based catalysts toward high-performance PEMWE.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of DSC

In this study, we employed a facile electrodeposition method to synthesize Mn3O4 as the precursor material, which enables the formation of a uniform spinel framework with controllable composition and crystallinity. To introduce cobalt into the spinel lattice, a small amount of Co2+ was added to the electrolyte during electrodeposition. Owing to the comparable ionic radius and valence state between Co2+ and Mn2+, it is anticipated that Co2+ preferentially substitutes Mn2+ at the tetrahedral sites, resulting in the formation of a single-site substituted spinel Mn3O4 catalyst (denoted as SSC) intermediate. Subsequently, a conventional cation exchange strategy was applied by immersing the as-prepared SSC in an aqueous RuCl3 solution, thereby enabling partial replacement of Mn3+ at the octahedral sites by Ru3+. This site-selective substitution pathway is further supported by the structural characterization results discussed later. Upon calcination, Ru was stably anchored into the lattice, yielding the final DSC (Fig. 2a).

To identify the optimal balance between OER activity and stability, we systematically investigated the effect of the cation doping ratio, defined as v = nRu/nCo, on the catalytic performance. The elemental compositions of Mn, Co, and Ru in the synthesized catalysts were quantified using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) (Table S1). In a standard three-electrode configuration, electrochemical measurements showed that the optimal OER activity within the DSC series was achieved at a doping ratio of v = 1.03 (Figs. S1 and S2). When the v was lower than the optimal value, the reduced Ru content in the catalyst likely led to a deficiency of active sites, thereby compromising the catalytic performance. Conversely, when v value exceeded this optimal ratio, the introduction of excess Ru resulted in a significant decline in OER activity. This performance degradation can be attributed to structural changes induced by over-doping, which may result in a reduction in the number of active sites, as evidenced by electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) analyses (Figs. S3–10). These findings underscore the importance of precisely tuning the cation exchange ratio to balance active site density and structural integrity.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal that Mn3O4, SSC, and the DSC series across different v values display similarly uniform nanoparticle coverage on the titanium fiber substrate (Figs. 2b and S11–17). The consistent morphological features across the series indicate that elemental doping does not significantly alter the overall morphology of the catalysts. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of Mn3O4 (Fig. S18) reveal a lattice spacing of 0.277 nm, corresponding to the (103) crystal plane, which is further confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. S19). After calcination in air at 350 °C, no additional Co-containing phases were detected in the SSC sample, excluding the presence of amorphous cobalt species and suggesting that Co is atomically dispersed within the Mn3O4 host lattice. The lattice spacing of SSC is measured to be 0.276 nm, indicating that the incorporation of Co does not significantly distort the crystal structure (Figs. S20 and S21).

After Ru incorporation, lattice spacings of 0.250 and 0.310 nm, assigned to the (100) and (112) planes, were observed across the DSC series, suggesting a Ru-induced lattice expansion (Figs. S22–27 and 2c). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image and the corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental maps confirm the uniform distribution of Mn, Co, and Ru throughout the DSC particles (Fig. 2d), ruling out the formation of metal clusters or segregated oxide domains. Particle size analysis shows that the DSC nanoparticles possess an average diameter of approximately 2.7 nm, which is favorable for OER catalysis (Fig. 2e). Aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-STEM) images further verify the atomic dispersion of Ru and Co within the Mn3O4 lattice. As shown in Fig. 2f, DSC exhibits a well-defined atomic array along the (211) orientation. Brighter atomic columns highlighted by yellow circles are assigned to Ru atoms, while slightly dimmer ones marked by orange circles are attributed to Co atoms. Line intensity profiles across regions 1 and 2 in the AC-STEM images (Fig. 2g) provide additional evidence for the identity and location of these single-atom dopants.

Identification of Ru and Co site occupation and elucidation of Ru–Co–Mn electronic coupling

To elucidate the site-selective occupation of Ru and Co atoms within the Mn3O4 spinel lattice (tetrahedral vs. octahedral sites), we employed the highly sensitive Raman spectroscopy technique. As shown in Fig. 3a, the Raman spectrum of Mn3O4 exhibits characteristic vibrational modes in the regions of 100–200 cm–1 and 600–700 cm–1, which can be assigned to the F2g and A1g modes, corresponding to tetrahedral (MnO4) and octahedral (MnO6) coordination, respectively27,37. Compared with Mn3O4, SSC displays the same A1g feature, but the F2g band evolves into a distinct doublet, indicating the preferential occupation of Co at the Mntet sites. In contrast, DSC not only retains the F2g doublet but also shows the splitting of the A1g band into two peaks, accompanied by a decreased A1g/F2g intensity ratio. This spectral evolution strongly suggests that Ru preferentially substitutes Mn at Mnoct sites, whereas Co occupies Mntet sites.

Fourier-transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) analysis further corroborates these assignments. In the Mn K-edge spectra (Fig. 3b), the characteristic peaks at ~1.5, 2.3, and 3.2 Å correspond to Mn–O, Mnoct–O–Ru/Co/Mn, and Mntet–O–Ru/Co/Mn coordination paths, respectively, confirming that the fundamental spinel framework of Mn3O4 is largely preserved after Ru and Co substitution35,36. For Ru (Fig. 3c), besides the first-shell Ru–O peak at ~1.5 Å in DSC, a distinct second-shell feature at ~2.5 Å is assigned to Ru–O–Ru/Co/Mn coordination, which is clearly different from the Ru–O–Ru coordination in RuO2. This excludes the presence of RuO2 and indicates that Ru atoms successfully replace Mnoct sites. For Co (Fig. 3d), the coordination peak at ~1.5 Å corresponds to Co–O, while the feature at ~3.0 Å (Co–O–Ru/Co/Mn) confirms that Co substitutes Mntet sites. Coordination number analysis provides further quantitative evidence supporting these conclusions (Figs. S28, S29, and Tables S2, S3). Additionally, wavelet transform (WT) analysis of the Mn, Ru, and Co K-edges (Figs. S30–38) consistently verifies that Ru substitutes Mnoct sites, while Co occupies Mntet sites, fully consistent with the Raman results.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was then performed to probe the electronic redistribution associated with selective substitution. As shown in Fig. 3e, the Mn 2p3/2 spectra of Mn3O4, SSC, and DSC can be deconvoluted into Mn2+ and Mn3+ components, corresponding to Mntet and Mnoct sites, respectively. Compared with pristine Mn3O4, SSC exhibits a decreased Mn2+/Mn3+ ratio, consistent with the preferential replacement of Mntet sites by Co (Tables S4–6). Conversely, DSC shows an increased Mn2+/Mn3+ ratio, indicating that Ru selectively substitutes Mnoct sites. The Mn 2p3/2 binding energies of Mn3O4 (641.20 eV), SSC (641.95 eV), and DSC (641.36 eV) further suggest partial electron depletion of Mn upon Ru and Co incorporation. Similar conclusions are supported by the Co 2p and Ru 3d spectra (Figs. S39–51 and Tables S7–19).

To further determine the oxidation states, X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra were collected. As shown in Fig. 3f, the Mn white-line intensity in DSC is slightly higher than that in Mn3O4, indicating electron depletion of Mn, consistent with the XPS results. For Ru (Fig. 3g), the edge position of DSC lies below that of RuO2, suggesting a valence state lower than +4 due to electron accumulation at Ru sites. For Co (Fig. 3h), the edge position of DSC is slightly lower than that of Co3O4, implying that Co mainly exists as Co2+, consistent with its preferential substitution at Mntet sites.

Taken together, Raman, EXAFS, XPS, and XANES analyses consistently establish that Ru preferentially substitutes Mnoct sites, whereas Co occupies Mntet sites within the Mn3O4 spinel framework. Moreover, the dual incorporation of Ru and Co induces pronounced electronic redistribution, giving rise to strong Ru–Co–Mn coupling. This structural and electronic modulation not only stabilizes the spinel framework but also underpins the OER activity and durability observed in DSCs.

Enhanced OER performance in acid

To evaluate the effectiveness of our site-specific doping strategy in constructing high-performance OER catalysts, we systematically investigated the electrochemical behavior of each sample using a three-electrode configuration in 0.5 M H2SO4. As shown in Fig. 4a, the commercial RuO2 catalyst (Com-RuO2) exhibited a polarization curve (normalized to geometric area) comparable to previously reported values (300 mV@10 mA cmgeo−2). Notably, the DSC required only 175 mV of overpotential to reach 10 mA cmgeo−2, outperforming Mn3O4, and SSC counterparts. Moreover, at higher current densities of 20 and 100 mA cmgeo−2, DSC consistently required lower operating voltages than Com-RuO2 (Fig. 4b). Given the characteristics of Ru-doped Mn3O4, it has been studied as a separate system32.

A combination of microwave-assisted digestion and alkaline dissolution was employed to completely dissolve the titanium fiber substrate and the deposited catalyst, thereby enabling precise quantification of the Ru loading. To minimize experimental uncertainty and ensure reproducibility, the entire procedure was repeated multiple times. Based on these measurements, the Ru loading was determined to be 0.06 ± 0.003 mg cm−2. When normalized to the mass of Ru, DSC displayed a mass activity of 272.83 A gRu−1 at 1.4 V versus RHE, higher than that of Com-RuO2 (6.39 A gRu−1) (Fig. S52). A similar trend was observed in the polarization curves normalized by the ECSA (Figs. S53 and S54), further indicating the high intrinsic activity of the active sites in DSC. Under constant current electrolysis, the Faradaic efficiency of DSC was calculated to be approximately 99.5%, based on the amount of evolved oxygen, indicating that nearly all the current was utilized for the desired OER process (Fig. S55). To further probe the catalytic kinetics, Tafel slopes were extracted from the polarization data (Fig. 4c). DSC exhibited a Tafel slope of 59.8 mV dec−1, which is substantially lower than that of Mn3O4 (299.2 mV dec−1), SSC (150.0 mV dec−1), and Com-RuO2 (73.1 mV dec−1), highlighting the enhanced reaction kinetics enabled by the selective incorporation of Ru and Co at targeted lattice sites. Additionally, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements conducted at 1.3 V (vs. RHE) revealed that DSC exhibited a smaller charge transfer resistance than the reference catalysts, consistent with improved interfacial charge transport and OER kinetics (Fig. S55).

The long-term stability of electrocatalysts under acidic OER conditions is a critical parameter for practical industrial applications. To assess the durability of our materials, we conducted chronopotentiometry (CP) measurements on both Com-RuO2 and DSC at various current densities (Fig. 4d, e). Notably, at a current density of 50 mA cmgeo−2, DSC maintained stable operation for more than 2100 h with an average potential decay rate of 42.5 μV h−1, demonstrating its durability under acidic conditions. In stark contrast, Com-RuO2 operated for less than 80 h at a milder current density of 10 mA cmgeo−2, highlighting its limited durability in acidic environments. Under higher current density conditions, DSC maintained stable performance for more than 650 h at 100 mA cmgeo−2 with an average voltage decay of 0.23 mV h−1. Conversely, Com-RuO2 exhibited a rapid voltage increase under the same conditions, losing activity within 10 h, underscoring its poor acid stability at high current operation. DSC sustained continuous OER operation at current densities of 200 and 500 mA cmgeo−2 for over 300 and 100 h, respectively, demonstrating its potential for operation at high current densities. These results demonstrate competitive performance among recently reported Ru-based OER electrocatalysts (Table S20), indicating the potential of DSC for durable acidic water electrolysis. Post-stability characterization of the DSC was conducted using SEM and TEM (Figs. S57–64). The analyses revealed that the catalyst retained its original atomic structure after long-term operation, indicating structural robustness. The observed decline in catalytic activity can be primarily attributed to the partial loss of catalyst loading on the titanium felt substrate.

To gain further insight into catalyst stability, we monitored metal dissolution during OER using ICP-AES. As shown in Fig. 4f, DSC exhibits a slow dissolution rate of Ru and Co. The calculated S-number of DSC, based on 200 h of ICP-AES data, indicates competitive durability compared with recently reported Ir-based and Ru-based systems (Fig. S65 and Table S21).

Mechanistic elucidation of OER pathways

To elucidate the influence of Co and Ru substitution at the tetrahedral and octahedral sites of Mn3O4 on the OER pathway, in situ ATR-SEIRAS was employed to identify the reaction intermediates. As shown in Fig. S66, compared with the spectra collected under open-circuit potential, Mn3O4 exhibited two characteristic peaks at 1050 and 3395 cm–1 upon increasing potential, corresponding to *OO/*OO* and *OH intermediates, respectively. Similarly, SSC and DSC displayed the gradual emergence of *OO/*OO* (1052 and 1068 cm–1) and *OH (3406 and 3368 cm–1) signals with increasing potential, accompanied by an enhancement in vibrational intensity (Fig. 5a, b)12. This observation suggests that in situ ATR-SEIRAS alone cannot distinguish significant changes in the adsorbed oxygenated intermediates upon partial substitution of Mn3O4 with Co at tetrahedral sites and Ru at octahedral sites.

To further probe the role of heteroatom substitution in modulating the OER pathway, 18O isotopic labeling combined with in situ DEMS was conducted to determine whether lattice oxygen participates in the reaction. As illustrated in Fig. 5c, O2 generated from water electrolysis in the upper microcell diffuses through a nanoporous membrane under a pressure gradient and is subsequently analyzed by mass spectrometry. Specifically, using 0.5 M H2SO4 prepared with H218O as the electrolyte, cyclic voltammetry was applied in the potential window of 1.1–1.4 V vs. RHE to label the catalyst, while simultaneously monitoring 32O2, 34O2, and 36O2 signals (Figs. S67–69a). The electrode was then thoroughly rinsed with H216O to remove surface H218O, followed by repeated CV measurements in 0.5 M H2SO4 prepared with H216O. Quantitative analysis of the MS signals (Figs. S69b and 5d–f) revealed that for Mn3O4 and SSC, the proportions of 34O2 and 36O2 in the total oxygen products were comparable, suggesting significant lattice oxygen participation in the OER process. In contrast, DSC showed a marked decrease in the fraction of 34O2 and 36O2, indicating that Ru substitution at octahedral sites suppresses lattice oxygen involvement, thereby implying a distinct OER pathway and improved structural stability38.

To further validate this mechanistic insight, in situ Raman spectroscopy was carried out. The experimental setup is shown in Figs. 5g and S70, where the electrolyte flows laterally through the cell to enable continuous circulation. In SSC, oxygen radical species (*O/*OO) emerged and intensified in the 1000–1100 cm–1 region as the potential increased (Fig. 5h), consistent with the ATR-SEIRAS results. In contrast, DSC exhibited *O/*OO signals at significantly lower potentials (Fig. 5i), suggesting a reduced kinetic barrier for OER and accounting for its high catalytic activity37.

The adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM, Fig. 6a), lattice oxygen mechanism (LOM, Fig. 6b), and dual oxygen mechanism (DOM, Fig. 6c) represent three typical OER pathways. By integrating in situ ATR-SEIRAS, in situ DEMS, and in situ Raman spectroscopy, we elucidated the reaction mechanisms followed by different catalysts. The results reveal that Mn3O4 operates via the LOM pathway; after partial substitution of Mntet by Co, the resulting SSC catalyst still follows the LOM route. When the Mnoct sites are further substituted by Ru, the resulting DSC predominantly follows the DOM pathway. Understanding the structural-activity relationship of DSC and its intrinsic preference for the DOM mechanism provides important guidance for the rational design of future high-performance OER catalysts.

To uncover the origin of the enhanced activity and stability of DSC compared with SSC and Mn3O4, DFT calculations were performed. Based on the XRD and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) characterizations and previous reports, the (211) plane of spinel Mn3O4 was selected as the reference model32,39. SSC was simulated by replacing one Mntet atom with Co, while DSC was modeled by further substituting an adjacent Mnoct atom with Ru. The optimized structural models of Mn3O4, SSC, and DSC used in the DFT calculations are provided in Supplementary Data 1, which contains the corresponding input structure files. As shown in Fig. 6d, compared to Mn3O4 (−0.613 eV), the metal d-orbital centers (εd) of both SSC (−0.640 eV) and DSC (−0.652 eV) shift to lower energies. This indicates that substitution at the tetrahedral and octahedral sites modifies the electronic structure of the catalyst. Such a shift suggests a more moderate interaction strength between the catalyst and oxygenated intermediates, which is beneficial for OER activity. In addition, the p orbital center (εp) further supports this trend, suggesting a synergistic electronic modulation induced by Co and Ru co-substitution.

The energy gap between εd and εp (ΔEd–p) reflects the degree of d–p orbital hybridization. As shown in Figs. 6e and S71, the results indicate that the co-substitution of Co and Ru weakens the metal–oxygen covalency and optimizes the adsorption and conversion of OER intermediates40. Compared with pristine Mn3O4, Co and Ru doping leads to a clear redistribution of charge density in the system. For a more quantitative analysis, the average Bader charges of Mn in Mn3O4, SSC, and DSC were calculated. The values are 1.59, 1.61, and 1.64, respectively. This trend indicates that the introduction of Co and Ru results in a decrease in the electron density around Mn. This observation suggests electron transfer from Mn to the more electronegative Co and Ru species, which is consistent with the XANES results (Fig. S72). Regarding stability, the calculated dissolution energies (Fig. 6f) indicate that both SSC and DSC possess much higher thermodynamic dissolution barriers than Mn3O4, signifying enhanced structural robustness after site substitution.

Furthermore, the Gibbs free energy profiles for Mn3O4, SSC, and DSC along both LOM and DOM pathways were examined (Figs. S73–78 and 6g–i). For Mn3O4, the potential-determining step (PDS) along the LOM pathway corresponds to O–O bond formation (2.24 eV), which is slightly lower than that along the DOM route (2.27 eV), suggesting that the LOM mechanism is energetically preferred. Similarly, SSC also favors the LOM pathway, consistent with the in situ characterization results. In contrast, DSC exhibits a lower Gibbs free energy barrier for the PDS along the DOM pathway (2.03 eV) than along the LOM pathway (2.17 eV), indicating that Ru incorporation favors the DOM pathway under the modeled conditions. Notably, DSC achieves high OER activity via the DOM pathway, whereas Mn3O4 and SSC follow the LOM pathway, highlighting the thermodynamic advantage of the DOM mechanism. In summary, the cooperative substitution at Mntet and Mnoct sites in DSC weakens the metal–oxygen covalency, modulates the adsorption energy of reaction intermediates, and increases the thermodynamic dissolution barrier, which may contribute to the experimentally observed activity and stability of DSC.

PEM water electrolysis

Given the OER activity and stability exhibited by DSC in the three-electrode configuration, we further integrated the catalyst into a PEMWE for full-cell performance evaluation. The anode was fabricated using the synthesized DSC (with a Ru loading of 0.06 mg cmgeo−2), while the cathode consisted of commercial Pt/C. A Nafion 115 membrane was used as the separator, and the electrolyzer was operated at 80 °C with anode-only pure water feeding (Fig. 7a). For comparison, a benchmark cell employing Com-RuO2 was assembled under identical conditions.

PEMWE performance.

a Schematic illustration of proton exchange membrane water electrolyzer, including gas diffusion layer, anode, cathode, membrane, and catalyst. b PEM-based polarization curves for DSC and Com-RuO2 as anode materials, 40 wt% Pt/C as cathode material, and Nafion 115 as the membrane. c Long-term durability of DSC and Com-RuO2 during PEM electrolysis at 80 °C and a current density of 500 mA cmgeo−2.

As shown in Fig. 7b, the DSC achieved high current densities of 1, 2, and 3 A cmgeo−2 at PEMWE cell voltages of 1.69, 1.85, and 2.03 V, respectively. Compared to PEMWE on Com-RuO2, Mn3O4, and SSC, the DSC device consistently exhibited lower operating voltages, confirming that the site-specific co-doping of Ru and Co significantly enhances the intrinsic activity of the catalyst. We further evaluated the operational durability of DSC in the PEMWE configuration. Under a current density of 500 mA cmgeo−2, the electrolyzer maintained stable performance for over 150 h with an low voltage degradation rate of only 0.08 mV h−1 (Fig. 7c). Moreover, PEMWE devices based on the DSC series with varied v values showed performance trends consistent with the three-electrode results (Fig. S79), demonstrating the consistent performance trend of the catalyst series. Collectively, these findings confirm that the rational, site-specific co-substitution of Ru and Co enables a robust catalyst architecture that bridges intrinsic activity and device-level durability in PEM water electrolysis.

In conclusion, this work presents a site-engineered catalyst featuring dual-cation substitution, where Ru atoms partially occupy Mnoct sites and Co atoms partially replace Mntet sites within the Mn3O4 lattice. This dual-site substitution strategy precisely tailors the electronic structure and local coordination environment, leading to a balance between high activity and long-term durability under acidic oxygen evolution conditions. The optimized DSC exhibits an overpotential of 175 mV at 10 mA cmgeo−2 and sustains continuous operation at 200 mA cmgeo−2 for over 300 h with a degradation rate of 0.58 mV h−1. In a single-cell PEM electrolyzer, DSC demonstrates efficient anodic performance, delivering a current density of 1 A cm−2 at a cell voltage as low as 1.69 V, underscoring its suitability for acidic water electrolysis under practical operating conditions. In situ spectroscopic analyses and DFT calculations collectively reveal that Ru incorporation at octahedral sites optimizes Ru–O hybridization and accelerates the OER kinetics, whereas Co substitution at tetrahedral sites enhances lattice stability and electronic conductivity. These findings highlight a rational site-specific engineering strategy for modulating octahedral–tetrahedral chemistry in spinel oxides, paving the way toward the design of cost-efficient and acid-stable Ru-based catalysts for next-generation PEMWE.

Methods

Materials

All chemicals were purchased directly from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. Ruthenium(III) chloride hydrate (RuCl3·xH2O, 99%, Ru content: 37.5–41.0 wt%, premium grade), oxygen-18-enriched water (H218O, 98 atom% 18O), and commercial RuO2 (Com-RuO2, 99.95%) were purchased from Adamas Reagent (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate [Co(CH3COO)2·4H2O, ≥99.5%], manganese(II) acetate tetrahydrate [Mn(CH3COO)2·4H2O, ≥99%], sodium acetate trihydrate (CH3COONa·3H2O, ≥99%), and Nafion® ionomer solution (5 wt%) were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Commercial Pt/C catalyst (Pt/C, 40 wt% Pt), titanium felt, and carbon paper were supplied by Suzhou Sinero Technology Co., Ltd. Nafion 115 membranes were purchased from DuPont.

Synthesis of Mn3O4-Ti

Titanium felt (1 × 1.5 cm2) was cleaned in 20 wt% oxalic acid at 90 °C for 1 h, rinsed with deionized water, and sonicated for 30 min to remove surface oxides and residues. The pretreated Ti substrate was used as the working electrode in a three-electrode electrochemical setup with a Pt foil counter and an Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) reference electrode.

The electrolyte contained 0.01 M Mn(CH3COO)2·4H2O, 0.02 M CH3COONa·3H2O, and 5 mL DMSO in 50 mL deionized water. Electrodeposition was performed at 70 °C under a constant current density of −0.002 A cmgeo−2 for 1 h. The obtained MnOx/Ti electrode was annealed at 250 °C for 3 h under Ar to yield Mn3O4-Ti.

Preparation of SSC-Ti

The SSC-Ti electrode was prepared following the same procedure as Mn3O4-Ti, except that 0.001 M Co(CH3COO)2·4H2O was added to the electrolyte solution. Specifically, the electrolyte contained 0.01 M Mn(CH3COO)2·4H2O, 0.02 M CH3COONa·3H2O, 0.001 M Co(CH3COO)2·4H2O, and 5 mL DMSO in 50 mL deionized water. Electrodeposition was conducted at 70 °C under a constant current density of −0.002 A cmgeo–2 for 1 h. The resulting SSC-Ti electrode was annealed at 250 °C for 3 h under an Ar atmosphere.

Preparation of DSC-Ti series

The DSC-Ti series electrodes were fabricated through a post-ion-exchange modification of the pre-synthesized SSC-Ti samples. Briefly, the SSC-Ti electrodes were immersed in 20 mL aqueous RuCl3·xH2O solutions with varied precursor concentrations and maintained at 60 °C for 6 h to facilitate cation exchange between Ru3+ and surface metal sites. After ion exchange, the samples were thoroughly washed with deionized water and ultrasonically cleaned to remove residual surface species. The obtained electrodes were subsequently calcined in air at 250 °C for 3 h, yielding a series of DSC-Ti electrodes with different cation doping ratio (defined as v = nRu/nCo), denoted as DSCz/Ti, where z is related to v. The optimized sample, DSC-Ti, exhibited the best OER activity among the series.

Preparation of commercial RuO2-loaded Ti electrode (Com-RuO2-Ti)

To prepare the reference RuO2 electrode, 7 mg of commercial RuO2 powder was suspended in a solvent mixture containing 450 μL of ethanol and 50 μL of a 5 wt% Nafion binder solution. The suspension was ultrasonically treated for 1 h to obtain a uniform black ink. Subsequently, the catalyst ink was drop-coated onto a titanium felt substrate (1 × 1.5 cm2), achieving a total catalyst loading of approximately 3.8 mg cm–2geo. The coated electrode was dried under ambient conditions and denoted as Com-RuO2-Ti, which served as the benchmark catalyst for electrochemical comparison.

Electrochemical OER measurements

All electrochemical measurements were conducted in a nitrogen-purged 0.5 M H2SO4 solution using a conventional three-electrode configuration connected to a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation (CH Instruments, Shanghai). Catalyst-coated titanium felt (1 × 1 cm2), such as DSC-Ti, was directly employed as the working electrode. A graphite rod served as the counter electrode, while a saturated calomel electrode (SCE, saturated KCl) was used as the reference.

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed in the potential window of 0.86–1.86 V at a scan rate of 2 mV s−1. The current density was normalized both to the geometric surface area (geo). A 0.5 M H2SO4 electrolyte was freshly prepared before each electrochemical experiment by diluting concentrated sulfuric acid (98 wt%) with deionized water. The acid was slowly added to water during the dilution process. The prepared electrolyte was stored in a sealed polypropylene bottle and used on the same day. Prior to measurements, the reference electrode was calibrated in H2-saturated 0.5 M H2SO4. The electrolyte pH was verified to be 0.30 ± 0.01 using a pH meter (Sanxin SX-620). In situ ATR-SEIRAS measurements

In situ ATR-SEIRAS measurements were carried out on an INVENIO FTIR spectrometer, with instrument control and data processing performed using OPUS software. A liquid nitrogen-cooled HgCdTe (MCT Mid) detector was employed. The grating was set to 1.5 nm, and all spectra were collected at a resolution of 4 cm−1 with 32 co-added scans. The in situ measurements were conducted in a custom-designed three-electrode single-compartment electrochemical cell, using an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode and a Pt wire as the counter electrode. The catalyst loaded on titanium felt, electrically connected to the potentiostat via a glassy carbon electrode and an alligator clip, served as the working electrode. The electrolyte was 0.5 M H2SO4, and the potential was controlled by a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation. Prior to the in situ measurements, the ZnSe crystal was thoroughly cleaned to ensure a contamination-free surface. The background spectrum for ATR-SEIRAS was collected under open-circuit potential (OCP) conditions.

In situ DEMS with isotope labeling

In situ DEMS measurements were carried out using a QAS 100 system coupled to a custom-built three-electrode electrochemical cell (Figs. S67a and 5c). A hydrophobic microporous polytetrafluoroethylene membrane (20 nm pore size, 34–46 μm thickness, ~50% porosity) was positioned beneath the working electrode to separate the electrolyte from the vacuum chamber while allowing gaseous products to permeate into the mass spectrometer. Prior to measurements, Ar was purged through the electrolyte to remove dissolved oxygen. In situ DEMS was performed in 0.5 M H2SO4 prepared with either H218O or H216O, using cyclic voltammetry at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1. The ion currents of 32O2, 34O2, and 36O2 were continuously monitored without further signal correction or deconvolution.

Proton exchange membrane water electrolyzers (PEMWE) tests

PEMWEs were assembled using either DSC or commercial RuO2 as the anode catalyst. The cathode employed commercial Pt/C (40 wt% Pt) with a Pt loading of ~0.5 mg cm−2. Catalyst ink for the cathode was prepared by dispersing the powder in a mixed solution of Nafion (5 wt%), isopropanol, and deionized water, followed by spray-coating onto a piece of carbon paper, which simultaneously functioned as the gas diffusion layer. Nafion 115 was used as the proton-conductive membrane. Prior to assembly, the membrane was pretreated by immersion in 3 wt% H2O2 and 0.5 M H2SO4 at 80 °C for 1 h to ensure proton conductivity and remove organic residues. The activated DSC-Ti electrode was directly applied as the anode without further modification. The electrolyzer employed titanium bipolar plates featuring serpentine flow fields with an active area of 1 × 1 cm2. Distilled water was delivered to the anode chamber at a flow rate of 100 mL min−1 using a peristaltic pump, and all tests were conducted under a constant temperature of 80 °C.

Materials characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded using a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å). Surface morphologies were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) on a Thermo Scientific Helios G4 CX system. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-resolution TEM (HRTEM), and elemental mapping analyses were performed on a FEI Talos F200S instrument. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images were captured using a JEM-ARM200F microscope with aberration correction. The post-OER electrolyte solutions were analyzed for metal ion content using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES, Agilent 7700). Prior to ICP analysis, electrolyte samples were diluted fivefold with ultrapure water. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted on a Kratos AXIS Supra spectrometer using monochromatic Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV). All binding energies were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV from adventitious carbon. XAS measurements were carried out at the BL14W1 beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). Data were acquired in fluorescence mode using foil for calibration. Normalization of XANES spectra, energy alignment, and Fourier transforms of EXAFS data into R-space were performed using the Athena software package. Wavelet transform (WT) analyses were conducted using a Fortran-based program.

Computational details

All simulations were conducted within the framework of DFT using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)41,42. The exchange-correlation energy was treated using the revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (RPBE) functional, an improved formulation of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA), as originally proposed by Perdew et al.43. To accurately capture the dispersion interactions, Grimme’s DFT-D3 scheme was employed to account for van der Waals corrections. The interaction between core and valence electrons was described using the projector augmented wave (PAW) method. Plane-wave basis sets were employed with an energy cutoff of 450 eV. The convergence thresholds for self-consistent energy and ionic forces were set at 1.0 × 10−5 eV and 0.03 eV Å−1, respectively. The Brillouin zone was sampled using a 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh. A 1 × 2 × 1 supercell was constructed based on the conventional cell. The (211) surface was cleaved, and a four-layer slab model was employed, with the bottom two layers fixed during structural optimization.
