Gromov–Hausdorff Geometry of Metric Trees
A. O. Ivanov, I. N. Mikhailov, A. A. Tuzhilin

Abstract
Аннотация In this paper, we study metric trees, without any finiteness restrictions. For subsets of such trees, a condition that guarantees that the Hausdorff and Gromov–Hausdorff distances from the subset to the entire metric tree are the same is obtained. This result allows to construct a new class of shortest geodesics (in the proper class of all metric spaces) connecting such subset of a metric tree with the tree itself. In particular, the technique elaborated is demonstrated on subsets of the real line. Keywords : metric space, ultrametric spaces, ultrametrization, Hausdorff distance, Gromov–Hausdorff distance Gromov–Hausdorff class, clouds



1
Introduction

This paper is devoted to geometry of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance in the case of a metric tree cloud, i.e., a proper class of metric spaces at a finite Gromov–Hausdorff distance from a fixed metric tree (that need not be finite). As a particular case the cloud of the the real line is considered.

The
Gromov–Hausdorff distance
measures a degree of dissimilarity of metric spaces and is defined as the half of the infimum of distortions of all possible correspondences (i.e., multivalued surjective mappings) between metric spaces. The distortion of a correspondence is the supremum of the absolute differences of distances between point pairs of one space and a pair of their images in the other space. Evidently, the Gromov–Hausdorff distance is zero between isometric spaces, although it can be equal to zero between non-isometric ones too. Moreover, the Gromov-Hausdorff distance can also be equal to infinity, for example, between a bounded and an unbounded spaces. The details concerning the Gromov–Hausdorff distance can be found in
[
2
]
.

The family of all metric spaces forms a proper class in the sense of the von Neumann–Bernays–Gödel set theory. Metric spaces considering up to isometries, form a proper class too, which we denote by
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
and call the
Gromov–Hausdorff class
. However, even after such factorization, there remain pairs of different elements from
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
, the distance between which is zero. Even if one identifies the metric spaces at zero distance, the result is a proper class as well, and we denote it by
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}_{0}
. On this class the Gromov–Hausdorff distance is positively defined. Finally, we partition
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}_{0}
into maximal subclasses consisting of spaces on finite distance. We call these subclasses
clouds
, and thus, the Gromov–Hausdorff distance is a metric on each such cloud. If
X
is a metric space, then we denote by
[X]
the unique cloud containing
X
. In this paper, we will be especially interested in properties of the cloud
[\mathbb{R}]
of the real line
\mathbb{R}
and, more generally, of the ones containing metric trees.

When studying clouds, the mapping
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}_{0}
, which takes a metric space
X
and a positive real number
\lambda
to the metric space
\lambda X
obtained from
X
by multiplying all distances in
X
by
\lambda
. It turns out that this mapping, being a contraction on the cloud of bounded metric spaces, in general, possesses several unexpected properties. S. Bogaty and A. Tuzhilin
[
3
]
has found that the distance between spaces
X
and
\lambda X
can be infinite, i.e., multiplication by
\lambda
could take a metric space
X
to another cloud. But in the case of the cloud
[\mathbb{R}]
each such multiplication maps the cloud into itself. However, as I. Mikhailov shown
[
8
]
, multiplication by
\lambda
is not continuous in general: if we multiply integers
{\mathbb{Z}}\in[\mathbb{R}]
by any
\lambda>1
, then the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between
{\mathbb{Z}}
and the resulting space
\lambda{\mathbb{Z}}
is at least
1/2
. Thus, even in this case the multiplication by
\lambda
is not a contraction.

However, as it turns out, there is another way to ‘‘transform’’ some elements of this cloud to
\mathbb{R}
. Namely, here we show that for each
\varepsilon
-networks
S
in
\mathbb{R}
, the canonical Hausdorff geodesic connecting
S
and
\mathbb{R}
(see below for definitions) is, in fact, a geodesic in
[\mathbb{R}]
. This construction gives a hope for constructing a contraction that differs from the multiplication by
\lambda
. In proving this fact, we obtained another remarkable result: the distances between the real line and its subset, calculated in Hausdorff and Gromov-Hausdorff metrics, coincide with each other. Notice that the specific values of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance, in contrast to the Hausdorff distances, is a great rarity. Also notice that the latter result is proved using an interesting lower bound for the Gromov-Hausdorff distance, found in
[
4
]
by means of the so-called ultrametrization, see below.

Just before the publication of this paper, the paper
[
1
]
has appeared. It is shown in
[
1
]
that for an arbitrary subset
X
of a finite metric tree
T
, if the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}(X,\,T)
is greater than the oriented Hausdorff distance from the boundary of the tree
T
to
X
, then the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between
X
and
T
equals
d_{H}(X,\,T)
. We have observed that the technique of the proof that is based on Lemma 2.3 from
[
1
]
is also closely related to the ultrametrization mapping, although this mapping is not used explicitly. In this paper, we, in particular, generalize this result from
[
1
]
to equality of Hausdorff and Gromov–Hausdorff distances for infinite metric trees subsets.

2
Preliminaries

Let us recall the necessary concepts and results concerning Hausdorff and Gromov–Hausdorff distances. More detailed information can be found in
[
2
]
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
x
and
y
be its points, then
|xy|
will denote the distance between these points, and if
A
and
B
are non-empty subsets of
X
, then we put
|AB|=|BA|=\inf\bigl\{|ab|:a\in A,\,b\in B\bigr\}
. If
A=\{a\}
, then instead of
\bigl|\{a\}B\bigr|=\bigl|B\{a\}\bigr|
we will write
|aB|=|Ba|
. For non-negative real
r
we define
closed
r
-neighborhood of the set
A
as follows:

B_{r}(A)=\{x\in X:|xA|\leq r\}.

Let
X
be a metric space and
A
,
B
be non-empty subsets of
X
. The value

d_{H}(A,B)=\max\bigl\{\sup_{a\in A}|aB|,\,\sup_{b\in B}|Ab|\bigr\}

is called the
Hausdorff distance between
A
and
B
. Equivalently,

d_{H}(A,B)=\inf\bigl\{r:A\subset B_{r}(B)\ \ \text{and}\ \ B_{r}(A)\supset B\bigr\}.

The oriented Hausdorff distance
from
A
to
B
is called the value

\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(A,\,B)=\sup_{a\in A}|aB|.

We extend
\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(A,\,B)
for empty
A
as
\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(\emptyset,\,B)=0
.

It is well known that
d_{H}
is a generalized metric on the set
{\cal C}(X)
of all non-empty closed subsets of a metric space
X
. Here the word ‘‘generalized’’ means that
d_{H}
can be equal to infinity on some pairs of subsets (for example, on a bounded and unbounded ones).

Next, let
X
and
Y
be non-empty metric spaces. A multivalued surjective mapping
R
from
X
to
Y
is called
correspondence between
X
and
Y
. The set of all correspondences between
X
and
Y
are denoted by
{\cal R}(X,Y)
. For any correspondence
R\in{\cal R}(X,Y)
, we define its
distortion
as

\operatorname{dis}R=\sup\Bigl\{\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|:(x,y),\,(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R\Bigr\}.

The Gromov–Hausdorff distance
between
X
and
Y
is

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in{\cal R}(X,Y)\bigr\}.

Note
[
2
]
that this value is also equal to the infimum of the Hausdorff distances between the images of the spaces
X
and
Y
while their isometric embeddings into all possible metric spaces.

For a metric space
X
, we denote
\operatorname{diam}X
by its
diameter
:

\operatorname{diam}X=\sup\bigl\{|xy|:x,y\in X\bigr\}.

For a single-point metric space we reserve the notation
\Delta_{1}
. It is well known
[
2
]
that
2d_{GH}(\Delta_{1},X)=\operatorname{diam}X
.

3
Subsets of Euclidean space at a finite Gromov–Hausdorff distance

In the paper
[
9
]
a natural question was posed: to describe all subsets of a given finite-dimensional normed space at a finite Gromov–Hausdorff distance from it. We will need the main result of this work.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
9
]
)
.

A subset
X
of a finite-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is at a finite Gromov–Hausdorff distance from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if
X
is an
\varepsilon
-network for some finite
\varepsilon
. The latter is equivalent to the fact that the Hausdorff distance between
X
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is finite.

In fact, the statement of Theorem
3.1
can be reformulate as follows: for an arbitrary
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the conditions
d_{GH}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\,A)=\infty
and
d_{H}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\,A)=\infty
are equivalent. Thus, the question arises whether Hausdorff and Gromov–Hausdorff distances from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to its arbitrary subsets of
A
are the same in the case where both of these quantities are finite. Below we will prove this statement in the case
n=1
.

4
Ultrametrization

Let
X
be an arbitrary metric space. The finite the sequence
L=\{x=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=y\}
of points from
X
will be called
a dotted line connecting
x
and
y
. We put
|L|_{u}:=\max_{i=1}^{n}\bigl\{|x_{i-1}x_{i}|\bigr\}
and we call this value
the ultrametric length of
L
. The set of all dotted lines in
X
, connecting
x
and
y
, we denote by
{\cal L}_{x,y}
.

Following
[
4
]
, for each pair
x,y\in X
we put

|xy|_{u}=\inf\bigl\{|L|_{u}:L\in{\cal L}_{x,y}\bigr\}.

As shown in
[
4
]
, the function
|\cdot\cdot|_{u}
on
X\times X
is a pseudometric. Let
U(X)
denote the quotient space obtained from
X
by factorization by zeros of the pseudometric
|\cdot\cdot|_{u}
. The metric space
U(X)
, as well as the natural projection
\pi\colon X\to U(X)
are called
ultrametrizations
.

The following inequality was first formulated in the work
[
4
]
for finite metric spaces and subsequently generalized to the case of bounded
[
6
]
and arbitrary
[
7
]
metric spaces.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
4
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
)
.

For any non-empty metric spaces
X
and
Y
, we have the following inequality
:

d_{GH}(X,Y)\geq d_{GH}\bigl(U(X),U(Y)\bigr).

Corollary 4.2
.

If metric spaces
X
and
Y
are such that
d_{GH}(X,Y)=0
, then
d_{GH}\bigl(U(X),U(Y)\bigr)=0
. In particular, this is true for non-empty subsets
X
and
Y
of a metric space
Z
, for which
d_{H}(X,Y)=0
, where the latter is equivalent to the coincidence of the closures
{\bar{X}}
and
{\bar{Y}}
.

Let’s apply the triangle inequality and the corollary
4.2
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
X
,
Y
, and
Z
be nonempty metric spaces and
d_{GH}(X,Y)=0
, or
X
and
Y
are non-empty subsets of the metric spaces
W
such that
d_{H}(X,Y)=0

(
in other words, the closures of
X
and
Y
coincide
)
. Then
d_{GH}(Z,X)=d_{GH}(Z,Y)
and
d_{GH}\bigl(Z,U(X)\bigr)=d_{GH}\bigl(Z,U(Y)\bigr)
. In particular, this holds for
Z=\Delta_{1}
, so
\operatorname{diam}X=\operatorname{diam}Y
and
\operatorname{diam}U(X)=\operatorname{diam}U(Y)
.

A metric space
X
is called
dotted connected
if
U(X)=\Delta_{1}
. The latter means that each pair of points from
X
can be connected by a dotted line
L
with an arbitrarily small ultrametric length.

Note 4.4

(
[
7
]
)
.

Any path-connected metric space
X
is dotted connected.

Note that not every dotted connected space is path-connected, for example, the set of rational points of the segment
[0,\,1]
.

5
Kuratowski’s Embedding

Let
X
be an arbitrary metric space. By
C_{b}(X)
we denote the Banach space of all bounded continuous real-valued functions on
X
with
\sup
-norm.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

For an arbitrary metric space
X
and a fixed point
x_{0}\in X
, the mapping

\Phi\colon X\to C_{b}(X),\;\Phi(x)(y)=d_{X}(x,\,y)-d_{X}(x_{0},\,y)\quad\forall\,x,\,y\in X

is isometric.

We will need the following construction from
[
7
]
. Consider a metric space
X
and an arbitrary real
t>0
. Let
\Phi\colon X\to C_{b}(X)
be the Kuratowski embedding. Add to
\Phi(X)
all segments in
C_{b}(X)
with endpoints at
\Phi(x)
,
\Phi(y)
, for which
d_{X}(x,\,y)\leq t
. By
D_{t}(X)
we denote the resulting subset of
C_{b}(X)
with the induced metric. We also define
D_{0}(X)
, setting it equal to
\Phi(X)
.

Lemma 5.2

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space. Then

(1)

if
\operatorname{diam}U(X)<t<\infty
, then the space
D_{t}(X)
is path-connected
;

(2)

if
\operatorname{diam}U(X)=0
, then the space
D_{0}(X)
is dotted connected
;

(3)

for an arbitrary
t\geq 0
, it holds
d_{GH}\bigl(X,\,D_{t}(X)\bigr)\leq t/2
.

Corollary 5.3
.

The closure of the class of all path-connected metric spaces in the Gromov–Hausdorff class coincides with the class of all dotted connected metric spaces.

Доказательство.

First, let
X
be a dotted connected metric space. Then by Lemma
5.2
the space
D_{t}(X)
is path-connected for each
t>0
, and
d_{GH}\bigl(X,\,D_{t}(X)\bigr)\leq t/2
. Thus,
X
is contained in the closure of the family of all path-connected metric spaces.

Now suppose that the sequence
X_{n}
of path-connected metric spaces converges in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense to a metric space
X
. Then by Theorem
4.1
we have

d_{GH}(X_{n},\,X)\geq d_{GH}\bigl(U(X_{n}),\,U(X)\bigr)=d_{GH}\bigl(\Delta_{1},\,U(X)\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{diam}U(X).

Since
d_{GH}(X_{n},\,X)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we obtain that
\operatorname{diam}U(X)=0
, that is,
X
is dotted connected.
∎

6
Metric trees

Let
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in I}
be an arbitrary family of segments of the real line. Let us set
{\cal E}=\sqcup_{\alpha\in I}e_{\alpha}
and on the obtained topological space we introduce an arbitrary equivalence relation
\sim
, identifying some ends of the segments
e_{\alpha}
. The quotient space
{\cal G}={\cal E}/\!\sim
is called a
topological graph
. If
\pi\colon{\cal E}\to{\cal E}/\!\sim
is the canonical projection, then the
\pi
-images the endpoints of the segments
e_{\alpha}
are called
the vertices of
{\cal G}
, and the
\pi
-images of the segments
e_{\alpha}

the edges
{\cal G}
. The terminology of the graph theory is directly transferred to topological graphs, in particular, for they there are defined the degrees of vertices, the paths, the cycles, the concept of connectivity, of connected component, etc. A topological graph
{\cal G}
is called a
tree
, if the graph is connected and does not contain cycles. Note that for a connected graph
{\cal G}
, the standard distance functions on segments
e_{\alpha}
is naturally extended to the whole
{\cal G}
and generates an intrinsic pseudometric, see for details
[
2
]
. A topological tree with such distance function is be called a
metric tree
.

A metric tree is called
finite
if it contains a finite number of edges.
The boundary
\partial T
of a finite metric tree
T
is the subset of
T
consisting of all vertices of
T
of degree
1
. The next result was obtained in
[
1
]
for finite metric trees.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
T
be a finite metric tree. Then for any non-empty subsets
X\subset T
, the inequality
d_{H}(T,\,X)>\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(\partial T,\,X)
implies
d_{GH}(T,\,X)=d_{H}(T,\,X)
.

The proof of this theorem was based mainly on the following lemma.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
X
be a subset of a metric space
G
with intrinsic metric,
R\in{\cal R}(G,\,X)
,
\operatorname{dis}R<r
. Then for any connected subset
S\subset G
, the set
B_{r}\bigl(R(S)\bigr)
is connected.

Below we will show that the technique by which the Theorem
6.1
was proved is closely related to the ultrametrization, if instead of connectivity we consider the linear connectivity. We will also show that the statement of Theorem
6.1
can be transferred to the case of infinite metric trees without serious changes.

7
Canonical Hausdorff geodesics

Let
X
be an arbitrary proper geodesic metric space, and suppose that
A
and
B
are non-empty closed subsets of
X
at a finite Hausdorff distance from each other:
d=d_{H}(A,B)<\infty
. For each
t\in[0,d]\subset\mathbb{R}
, we put
C_{t}=B_{t}(A)\cap B_{dt}(B)
. Then each
C_{t}
is closed in
X
, i.e.
C_{t}\in{\cal C}(X)
for all
t
.

Theorem 7.1
.

In the notation introduced above, the sets
C_{t}
are non-empty, and the curve
t\mapsto C_{t}
is a shortest geodesic in
{\cal C}(X)
such that its length equals to the distance between its ends.

Доказательство.

Let us show that
C_{t}
are non-empty. We choose an arbitrary point
a\in A
and positive
\varepsilon
, then the set
D:=B_{d+\varepsilon}(a)\cap B
is non-empty and is compact, so there exists
b\in D
at which the distance from
a
to
D
is achieved. Since
\bigl|a\,(B\setminus D)\bigr|\geq d+\varepsilon
, and
|aD|\leq d+\varepsilon
, then

|aB|=\min\Bigl\{|aD|,\bigl|a\,(B\setminus D)\bigr|\Bigr\}=|aD|.

By definition of the Hausdorff distance,
|aB|<d+\delta
for all
\delta>0
, therefore
r:=|ab|=|aD|\leq d
.

Let us connect
a
and
b
by a shortest naturally parameterized geodesic
\gamma\colon[0,r]\to X
, then
r\leq d
, so there exists
t_{0}\in[0,r]
, for which
\bigl|a\gamma(t_{0})\bigr|\leq t
and
\bigl|\gamma(t_{0})b\bigr|\leq dt
. But then
\gamma(t_{0})\in C_{t}
, which proves that
C_{t}
is non-empty.

Let us now show that for
0\leq t\leq s\leq d
it holds
d_{H}(C_{t},C_{s})\leq st
. To do this, it is enough to check that for each
x\in C_{t}
we have
|xC_{s}|\leq st
and for each
y\in C_{s}
it holds
|yC_{t}|\leq s-t
. Since both statements are proven in exactly the same way, let’s check the first of them only.

By construction, there exist
a\in A
and
b\in B
for which
|xa|\leq t
and
|xb|\leq dt
. Let us connect
x
and
b
by a shortest geodesic and choose on this geodesic a point
z
, for which
|zb|=\min\bigl\{d-s,|xb|\bigr\}
. Then
|xz|\leq st
, whence, by virtue of the triangle inequality,
|az|\leq s
. Therefore,
z\in C_{s}
and
|xz|\leq st
, that was required. Now the assertion of the theorem follows from the triangle inequality for the Hausdorff distance.
∎

The curve from Theorem
7.1
is called
the canonical Hausdorff geodesic
.

8
Infinite metric trees

Let
X
be an arbitrary metric space. We put

\displaystyle t(X)

\displaystyle=\inf\bigl\{d_{GH}(X,\,Y)\colon Y\,\text{is path-connected}\bigr\},

\displaystyle\tilde{t}(X)

\displaystyle=\inf\bigl\{d_{GH}(X,\,Y)\colon Y\,\text{dotted connected}\bigr\}.

Theorem 8.1
.

The equalities
2t(X)=2\tilde{t}(X)=\operatorname{diam}U(X)
are satisfied.

Доказательство.

The first equality follows from Corollary
5.3
. Let us justify the second equality.

To start with, suppose that
\operatorname{diam}U(X)=\infty
. Assume that
\tilde{t}(X)<\infty
, i.e., there is a dotted connected space
Y
at a finite Gromov–Hausdorff distance from
X
. By Theorem
4.1
we have
d_{GH}(X,\,Y)\geq d_{GH}\bigl(U(X),\,U(Y)\bigr)
. However,
U(Y)=\Delta_{1}
by definition, therefore
d_{GH}\bigl(U(X),\,U(Y)\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{diam}U(X)=\infty
, whence
d_{GH}(X,\,Y)=\infty
, a contradiction.

Now let
c:=\operatorname{diam}U(X)<\infty
. For each
t>c
we construct the space
D_{t}(X)
described before Lemma
5.2
. By the same lemma, the space
D_{t}(X)
is path-connected and
d_{GH}\bigl(X,D_{t}(X)\bigr)\leq t/2
, whence
\tilde{t}(X)=t(X)\leq c/2
in due to the arbitrariness of
t
. On the other hand, Theorem
4.1
implies that for any path-connected metric space
Y
the following inequality holds:

d_{GH}(X,\,Y)\geq d_{GH}\bigl(U(X),\,U(Y)\bigr)=d_{GH}\bigl(U(X),\,\Delta_{1}\bigr)=c/2.

Passing to the infimum, we obtain
\tilde{t}(X)\geq c/2
, which completes the proof.
∎

Let
X
be a non-empty subset of a metric tree
T
. A point
a\in T\setminus X
is called
internal with respect to
X
if in the tree
T
there exists a path (topological embedding of a segment) with ends in
X
, passing through
a
. The set of all such points
a
is denoted by
\operatorname{Int}_{X}T
, and the remaining points from
T\setminus X
we call
end points with respect to
X
and their set we denote by
\partial_{X}T
. If
T
is a finite tree, then points from
(\partial T)\setminus X
are contained in
\partial_{X}T
and are the most farthest from
X
among all points in
\partial_{X}T
; points in
(\partial T)\cap X
are at zero distance from
X
, so for finite trees we have
\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(\partial T,\,X)=\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(\partial_{X}T,\,X)
(recall that the oriented Hausdorff distance from the empty set we defined to be equal to zero). For infinite trees, the set
\partial T
can be empty, but
\partial_{X}T
may not be empty. For example, if we glue the origins of the three rays together, and divide the rays into segments, thus obtaining an infinite tree
T
, and we take as
X
the union of all the segments of two rays, then the open third ray will be non-empty
\partial_{X}T
, however, in this case
\partial T=\emptyset
.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
T
be an arbitrary metric tree,
X
be non-empty subset of
T
such that either
\partial_{X}T=\emptyset
, or
\partial_{X}T\neq\emptyset
and
d_{H}(X,\,T)>\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(\partial_{X}T,\,X)
. Then

d_{GH}(X,\,T)=d_{H}(X,\,T).

Доказательство.

It is known that
d_{GH}(X,\,T)\leq d_{H}(X,\,T)
, so it suffices to prove the opposite inequality. If
d_{H}(X,\,T)=0
, then the reverse inequality is obvious. Now let
d_{H}(X,\,T)\neq 0
.

Let
c=\operatorname{diam}U(X)
. It follows from Theorem
4.1
that

d_{GH}(X,\,T)\geq d_{GH}\bigl(U(X),\,U(T)\bigr)=d_{GH}\bigl(U(X),\,\Delta_{1}\bigr)=c/2,

therefore, it suffices to justify the equality
c=2d_{H}(X,\,T)
.

To start with, let
d_{H}(X,\,T)=\infty
. We must show that
\operatorname{diam}U(X)=\infty
. Let’s consider two cases.

(1) Suppose that
\partial_{X}T=\emptyset
, then each point from
T\setminus X
is internal w.r.t.
X
. On the other hand, due to
d_{H}(X,\,T)=\infty
, for any
d>0
there exists
a\in T\setminus X
such that
|aX|>d
. Since
\partial_{X}T=\emptyset
, then in each of the connected components of the space
T\setminus\{a\}
there is a point from
X
. Let’s choose any two of these components, and in them arbitrary points
x
and
x^{\prime}
, respectively. Consider any dotted line
L=\{x_{0}=x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=x^{\prime}\}
, where
x_{i}\in X
for all
i
. Then there exist
x_{i-1}
and
x_{i}
lying in different components. Since
|aX|>d
, then
|x_{i-1}x_{i}|>2d
, whence
|L|_{u}\geq 2d
. By virtue of arbitrariness of the dotted line
L
, we have
|x_{i-1}x_{i}|\geq 2d
, whence
\operatorname{diam}U(X)\geq 2d
and, therefore,
\operatorname{diam}U(X)=\infty
, because
d
is arbitrary.

(2) Now suppose that
\partial_{X}T\neq\emptyset
, then
d_{H}(X,\,T)>\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(\partial_{X}T,\,X)
, from which the value
h=\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(\partial_{X}T,\,X)
is finite. Since
d_{H}(X,\,T)=\infty
, then there exists
a\in T\setminus X
for which
|aX|>h
and, therefore, the point
a
is internal w.r.t.
X
. It remains to repeat the reasoning from item
(1)
.

Now let
0<d_{H}(X,\,T)<\infty
. We already know that
d_{H}(X,\,T)\geq d_{GH}(X,\,T)\geq c/2
. We will show that
d_{H}(X,\,T)\leq c/2
. Note that

d_{H}(X,\,T)=\sup_{a\in T\setminus X}|aX|=\max\biggl\{\sup_{a\in\operatorname{Int}_{X}T}|aX|,\sup_{a\in\partial_{X}T}|aX|\biggr\}.

If
\partial_{X}T=\emptyset
, then
\operatorname{Int}_{X}T\neq\emptyset
, because otherwise
d_{H}(X,T)=0
. Therefore

d_{H}(X,\,T)=\sup_{a\in\operatorname{Int}_{X}T}|aX|=\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(\operatorname{Int}_{X}T,\,X).

If
\partial_{X}T\neq\emptyset
, then
d_{H}(X,\,T)>\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(\partial_{X}T,\,X)
, so
\operatorname{Int}_{X}T\neq\emptyset
and again

d_{H}(X,\,T)=\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(\operatorname{Int}_{X}T,\,X).

Since
d_{H}(X,\,T)=\sup_{a\in T\setminus X}|aX|<\infty
, then there exists
a\in\operatorname{Int}_{X}T
, for which
\bigl||aX|-d_{H}(X,\,T)\bigr|\leq\varepsilon
. Since
a\in\operatorname{Int}_{X}T
, then there is a path
\gamma\colon[0,\,1]\to T
such that
\gamma(0)\in X
,
\gamma(1)\in X
, and
\gamma(t)=a
for some
t\in(0,\,1)
. By definition of ultrametrization, for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a dotted line
x_{0}=\gamma(0),\,x_{1},\,\ldots,\,x_{n}=\gamma(1)
with vertices in
X
such that
\max_{0\leq i\leq n-1}|x_{i}x_{i+1}|\leq c+\varepsilon
. Since
\gamma
is an embedding,
a
is an interior point of the curve
\gamma
, and
T
is a tree, then the points
x
and
x^{\prime}
lie in different path-connected components of the space
T\setminus\{a\}
. This means that there exists an index
i
such that
a
lies on the only path in
T
connecting points
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
. Then
|aX|\leq|x_{i}x_{i+1}|/2\leq(c+\varepsilon)/2
. Since
\bigl||aX|-d_{H}(X,\,T)\bigr|\leq\varepsilon
, we obtain that
d_{H}(X,\,T)-\varepsilon\leq|aX|\leq(c+\varepsilon)/2
. The desired inequality follows from the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon>0
.
∎

9
New geodesics in Gromov–Hausdorff class

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
X
be a proper geodesic metric space and
A
a closed subset of
X
. Suppose that
d_{H}\bigl(A,\,X\bigr)=d_{GH}\bigl(A,\,X\bigr)<\infty
. Then the canonical Hausdorff geodesic
t\mapsto C_{t}
connecting
A
and
X
is the shortest curve in the cloud
[X]
.

Доказательство.

Let
t
run through the segment
[0,g]
,
C_{0}=A
,
C_{g}=X
. We choose arbitrary
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\ldots<t_{n}\leq g
. By Theorem
7.1
we have
\displaystyle d_{H}(A,X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{H}(A_{t_{i}},\,A_{t_{i+1}})
, therefore

d_{GH}(A,X)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{GH}(A_{t_{i}},\,A_{t_{i+1}})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{H}(A_{t_{i}},\,A_{t_{i+1}})=d_{H}(A,\,X)=d_{GH}(A,X),

hence
\displaystyle d_{GH}(A,\,X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{GH}(A_{t_{i}},\,A_{t_{i+1}})
. It follows that the length of the curve

t\mapsto C_{t}
in the cloud
[X]
is equal to the distance between its ends, i.e., this curve is a shortest geodesic in the cloud.
∎

Theorem 9.2
.

Let
X
be a subset of the real line
\mathbb{R}
, then
d_{GH}(X,\mathbb{R})=d_{H}(X,\mathbb{R})
.

Доказательство.

By Theorem
3.1
the set
X
is at a finite Gromov–Hausdorff distance from
\mathbb{R}
if and only if
d_{H}(X,\mathbb{R})<\infty
. Thus, if one of the quantities
d_{GH}(X,\mathbb{R})
or
d_{H}(X,\mathbb{R})
is infinite, then the second one is too. In other words, in the case for infinite distances the statement of the theorem is true.

Now let both of these quantities be finite. Note that
\mathbb{R}
can be considered as an infinite metric tree
T
, for which the set of vertices coincides with
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Since
d_{H}(X,\,\mathbb{R})<\infty
, then each point
a\in T\setminus X
is internal w.r.t.
X
, i.e.,
\partial_{X}T=\emptyset
. Then the equality
d_{GH}(X,\,\mathbb{R})=d_{H}(X,\,\mathbb{R})
follows from Theorem
8.2
.
∎

Corollary 9.3
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}
be a non-empty closed subset such that
d_{GH}(X,\mathbb{R})<\infty
. Then the canonical Hausdorff geodesic connecting
X
and
\mathbb{R}
is a shortest curve in the cloud
[\mathbb{R}]
.

Доказательство.

It follows from Theorem
9.2
and Theorem
9.1
.
∎

Corollary 9.4
.

Let
T
be a metric tree whose vertex degrees are finite,
X
be a non-empty closed subset of
T
such that either
\partial_{X}T=\emptyset
, or
\partial_{X}T\neq\emptyset
and
d_{H}(X,\,T)>\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(\partial_{X}T,\,X)
. Then the canonical Hausdorff geodesic connecting
X
to
T
is a shortest curve in the cloud
[T]
.

Доказательство.

Since the degree of each vertex
T
is finite, then
T
is proper. Since
T
is also geodesic, the statement follows from Theorem
8.2
and Theorem
9.1
.
∎

Denote by
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty}
the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
endowed with
\ell_{\infty}
-norm. Let
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\infty}
be the integer lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty}
.

Theorem 9.5
.

It holds
d_{H}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty},\,{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\infty})=d_{GH}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty},\,{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\infty})
.

Доказательство.

Since
d_{GH}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty},\,{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\infty})\leq d_{H}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty},\,{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\infty})=\frac{1}{2}
, it suffices to prove the opposite inequality.

By Theorem
4.1
we have
d_{GH}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty},\,{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\infty})\geq d_{GH}\bigl(U(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty}),\,U({\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\infty})\bigr)
. Note that
U(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty})=\Delta_{1}
, and
U({\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\infty})
is a countable simplex of diameter
1
. Hence,
d_{GH}\bigl(U(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty}),\,U({\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\infty})\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}
, which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 9.6
.

Canonical Hausdorff geodesic connecting
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\infty}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty}
is a shortest path in the cloud
[\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\infty}]
.

Доказательство.

It follows from Theorem
9.5
and Theorem
9.1
.
∎
