Introduction

Kommerell’s diverticulum is a developmental anomaly characterized by the persistence of the fourth dorsal aortic arch. It is named after Dr. Kommerell, a radiologist who first diagnosed this condition in a living patient [1]. This condition manifests as segmental aneurysmal dilation of the aorta and is often associated with aberrant subclavian arteries (ASAs), occurring on either the left or right side [2]. Currently, the definition also encompasses an aneurysmatic origin of an aberrant left subclavian artery arising from a right-sided aortic arch; it may also be observed in patients with double aortic arches [3]. Surgical and radiological studies estimate that the prevalence of left-sided aortic arches with aberrant right subclavian arteries ranges from 0.7% to 2.0%, while for right-sided arches with aberrant left subclavian arteries, it ranges from 0.04% to 0.4% [2, 4].

Various surgical techniques have been described for the repair of Kommerell’s diverticulum. Traditionally, open repair has been considered the primary treatment approach, typically performed under conditions of deep hypothermic arrest or partial left heart bypass involving resection of the aneurysmal segment and replacement with an interposition graft [5]. Over the past decade, surgical options have expanded to include endovascular and hybrid approaches as viable strategies for managing complex aortic diseases. This paper reviews a case of endovascular aortic repair to discuss indications for Kommerell’s diverticulum and explore the application of endovascular techniques in its treatment.

Case presentation

A 73-year-old female presented with shortness of breath for 2 month. The patient was diagnosed with Aortic arch aneurysm, Kommerll diverticulum. Although the aneurysmal neck diameter due to Kommerll diverticulum was only 2.42 cm, since the patient had developed dyspnea symptoms, the surgical team decided to use endovascular aortic repair surgery to treat the aortic aneurysm after discussion.

The operation was performed under general anesthesia. A 7 F arterial sheath was inserted into the right femoral artery, followed by the placement of a super-smooth loach guide wire. Aortography was conducted using a gold-labeled pigtail catheter. The imaging confirmed the presence of a right aortic arch, with the aortic arch aneurysm (Kommerell’s diverticulum) located approximately 2 cm from the right subclavian artery.

A super-rigid guide wire was subsequently inserted through the right femoral artery sheath for the exchange. A Medtronic straight covered vascular stent, model VAMF3030C200TE, was selected after precise measurements were taken. Using the super-rigid guide wire, the stent was advanced to the target site. After careful positioning, the proximal end of the stent was deployed horizontally at the opening of the right subclavian artery, positioned approximately 0.5 cm distal to it.

Post-deployment aortography demonstrated that the stent was well-placed with no evidence of significant internal leakage (Fig. 2). A postoperative computed tomography angiography (CTA) review confirmed that the aneurysm was effectively sealed, there was no internal leakage, and the angiographic assessment of each branch was deemed satisfactory (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Due to its incidental nature and presumed underreporting in the literature, the natural history and precise clinical consequences of Kommerell’s diverticulum remain largely unknown. Consequently, there are currently no established guidelines specifically addressing the management of patients with this condition.

Surgical indications

Symptomatic group

Severe compressive symptoms affecting the esophagus or trachea serve as definitive indications for surgical intervention in patients diagnosed with Kommerell diverticulum.

Asymptomatic group

Given the rarity of this condition, surgical indications for asymptomatic Kommerell diverticulum have not been well defined. Some experts advocate for a surgical approach to all identified cases of diverticulum due to a notably high rupture rate reported by Austin and Wolfe [6–8].

Certain investigators have proposed criteria for surgical intervention based on aneurysmal size; however, specific methodologies regarding how and where to measure the size of the diverticulum are inconsistently reported across studies. Cina et al. [9] suggested that a diameter of 3 cm at the level of the orifice of the diverticulum serves as an appropriate threshold for surgery in low-risk patients. Ota et al. measured maximum diameter from the wall adjacent to the trachea to that opposite descending aortic wall (Fig. 4a, b). Idrees et al. [10] and Tsukui et al. [11] recommended two measurement approaches: (1) assessing cross-sectional diameter from one side of the aortic wall to the tip of Kommerell’s diverticulum (Fig. 4c, d); and (2) measuring at the origin level of aberrant subclavian artery from its arch (Fig. 4c, d).

The choice between open surgery or hybrid/endovascular techniques should be guided by each surgeon’s experience and expertise.

Treatment options

Open surgery

The primary objective of all proposed open surgical treatments for both pediatric and adult patients with Kommerell’s diverticulum is to resect the diverticulum while reconstructing or relocating the aberrant subclavian artery.

Endovascular repair

In the past decade, successful endovascular repairs utilizing stent grafts for Kommerell’s diverticulum have been documented. However, complications such as reinterventions due to endoleak, arterial-esophageal fistula, and arm claudication have also been reported [10, 13], and long-term outcomes remain uncertain. There are eight reports detailing total endovascular repair of Kommerell’s diverticulum via either surgical femoral artery exposure or a percutaneous femoral approach [14–19]. Total thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) with a proximal landing zone in Zone 2 is more straightforward in cases involving right aortic arch configurations due to anatomical advantages that provide a more proximal landing zone [16]. In early reports, the aberrant subclavian artery was coiled or occluded without subsequent revascularization. In 2013, Silveira introduced a custom-designed endograft featuring fenestration and an internal branch [18], successfully preserving the aberrant left subclavian artery. With advancements in precise imaging techniques for assessing aortic configuration, Gafoor et al. [17] reported the first total percutaneous endovascular repair using a custom-designed fenestrated endograft to reconstruct both the left carotid artery and aberrant subclavian artery.

However, in patients with steep aortic arch angles and limited proximal landing zones, total endovascular repair may be challenging. In such cases, a hybrid approach involving partial aorta graft interposition combined with a debranching procedure can offer better access and create an adequate landing site for a stent graft. We also employed branched stent-grafts combined with fenestration techniques to treat patients with Kommerell’s diverticulum associated with aortic dissection at our center. A representative image from a recently treated case (Fig. 5a, b) is presented to illustrate this approach. Patients exhibited favorable recovery outcomes; all branches of the aortic blood flow were intact without any leakage.

Summary

In conclusion, Kommerell’s diverticulum, a remnant of the dorsal aortic arch, can be identified in both left - and right - sided arch configurations. Despite being a rare condition, the collective knowledge regarding this entity, including the existence of cystic medial necrosis within the diverticulum wall and abnormal physiological forces in the right aortic arch, may shed light on the nature of the diverticulum and account for its high rates of rupture and dissection. Nevertheless, the surgical indication for this condition remains a matter of debate.

Considering the relatively high incidence of aortic events and the degenerative changes in the diverticulum wall, it may be rational to contemplate early surgical intervention with a lower size threshold for Kommerell’s diverticulum as a surgical indication compared to that for standard thoracic aortic aneurysms. Additionally, recent progress in imaging techniques, surgical/endovascular approaches, and peri - operative management enables interventions for this entity at an earlier stage of the disease, leading to improved outcomes. The selection of a treatment strategy for Kommerell’s diverticulum should be founded on the patient’s anatomy, comorbidities, and the available surgical expertise.
