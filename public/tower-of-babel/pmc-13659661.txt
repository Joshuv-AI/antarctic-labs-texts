Arterial wall fragment embolism following balloon-assisted coiling: A case report
Authors: Ryosuke Kaneko, Hiroyuki Ikeda, Mai Tanimura, Minami Uezato, Masanori Kinosada, Yoshitaka Kurosaki, Masaki Chin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Arterial wall fragment embolism is a rare complication of balloon-assisted coiling, and its underlying mechanism remains poorly understood. We report a histopathologically confirmed case of arterial wall fragment embolism during balloon-assisted coiling for a ruptured aneurysm. A 74-year-old woman presented with World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grade V subarachnoid hemorrhage caused by a ruptured aneurysm at the junction of the right vertebral artery and the posterior inferior cerebellar artery. During balloon-assisted coiling, a contrast-filling defect at the aneurysm neck and irregularity of the parent artery wall were observed, followed by embolization to the basilar artery, resulting in acute occlusion. Histopathological examination of the retrieved embolus demonstrated arterial wall components, including the intima, internal elastic lamina, and media with smooth muscle cells, indicating that the embolus originated from the injured parent artery rather than representing thrombus. This case highlights that arterial wall fragment embolism should be considered in the differential diagnosis, in addition to thrombosis, when a contrast-filling defect and arterial wall irregularity develop during balloon-assisted coiling.

Introduction

Balloon-assisted coiling is a widely used treatment for wide-necked aneurysms and for cases in which preservation of branch vessels is required [1]. Although the risk of thromboembolic complications is well recognized [2], the mechanisms of nonthrombotic embolism remain poorly understood.

It has been reported that vascular wall injury associated with balloon inflation can occur in relation to vascular morphology [3]. In addition, vascular wall components have been identified in specimens retrieved during mechanical thrombectomy, suggesting that vascular wall injury and release of arterial wall fragments can occur during endovascular treatment [4,5]. However, the mechanisms by which arterial wall fragments migrate distally as embolic material during endovascular treatment have not been fully elucidated.

Here, we report a case of arterial wall fragment embolism during balloon-assisted coiling for a ruptured aneurysm at the junction of the right vertebral artery and the posterior inferior cerebellar artery and discuss its mechanism and clinical implications, highlighting a potential nonthrombotic mechanism of embolism.

Case report

History and examination

A 74-year-old woman with no remarkable medical history was brought to our hospital by ambulance. On arrival, her Glasgow Coma Scale score was E1V1M1. Plain computed tomography (CT) of the head demonstrated diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage and ventricular enlargement (Fig. 1A). CT angiography revealed a ruptured aneurysm at the junction of the right vertebral artery and the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (Fig. 1B). Based on these findings, she was diagnosed with subarachnoid hemorrhage due to a ruptured aneurysm classified as World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grade V. Endovascular surgery

Under general anesthesia, a 6-Fr Fubuki dilator kit (Asahi Intecc, Seto, Japan) was placed in the V2 segment of the right vertebral artery via the right femoral artery. Angiography demonstrated a ruptured aneurysm at the junction of the right vertebral artery and the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (neck, 6.3 mm; dome, 5.8 mm × 9.0 mm; height, 5.5 mm) and tortuosity of the vertebral artery adjacent to the aneurysm (Fig. 2A and B). A 6-Fr Navien 115 cm (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) was then advanced into the V4 segment of the right vertebral artery. Through this catheter, a Scepter XC 4 × 11 mm (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was navigated to the aneurysm neck, and a Headway 17 (Terumo) was advanced into the aneurysm sac (Fig. 2C). To preserve the origin of the posterior inferior cerebellar artery, framing was performed using an Axium Prime Frame 5 mm × 20 cm (Medtronic) with the Scepter XC herniated into the aneurysm (Fig. 2D). However, angiography showed insufficient coil distribution on the proximal side of the aneurysm. Subsequent angiography demonstrated a contrast-filling defect at the aneurysm neck and irregularity of the arterial wall along the lesser curvature of the vascular bend, but these findings were not recognized as abnormal at that time (Fig. 2G). A total of 12 additional coils were placed under balloon assistance. Angiography showed enlargement of the contrast-filling defect and improvement of arterial wall irregularity (Fig. 2H). The contrast-filling defect was interpreted as thrombosis, and 80 mg of ozagrel was administered intravenously; additional heparin was also given to prolong the activated clotting time to more than 250 seconds. Next, a Headway 17 was navigated into a proximal bleb of the aneurysm, and additional coiling was performed. Angiography showed further enlargement of the contrast-filling defect and worsening of arterial wall irregularity (Fig. 2I). Angiography 10 minutes after bleb coiling showed no change to the contrast-filling defect and arterial wall irregularity. To improve blood flow in the vertebral artery, the Headway17 and Scepter XC were withdrawn while aspiration was applied through the Navien. Angiography demonstrated occlusion at the tip of the basilar artery, resolution of the contrast-filling defect, and improvement of arterial wall irregularity (Fig. 2J).

The occlusion at the tip of the basilar artery was deemed to be caused by embolization of a thrombus originating from the vertebral artery at the aneurysm neck. Mechanical thrombectomy was performed using an aspiration catheter, resulting in complete recanalization of the basilar artery (Fig. 2K). Final angiography, performed 10 minutes after mechanical thrombectomy, showed no change to the embolized aneurysm, resolution of the contrast-filling defect, and no worsening of arterial wall irregularity (Fig. 2L). In this case, because of the wide-necked aneurysm, balloon assistance was used throughout the placement of all 13 coils. The mean balloon inflation duration was 37.64 seconds, with a maximum duration of 199 seconds.

Measurements obtained from 2-dimensional anteroposterior angiographic images showed that the parent artery measured 2.9 mm at the proximal segment, 2.1 mm at the proximal neck, 2.5 mm at the distal neck, and 3.2 mm at the distal segment. The maximum balloon inflation diameter was 3.0 mm.

Postoperative course

Plain CT and CT angiography of the head on postoperative day 4 showed no vascular narrowing suggestive of cerebral vasospasm, but scattered low-density areas in both cerebellar hemispheres (Fig. 3A and B). Pulmonary edema worsened on postoperative day 5, and the respiratory condition remained unstable. Tracheostomy was performed on postoperative day 6, and mechanical ventilation was continued thereafter. Histopathological findings

The retrieved embolus measured approximately 20 mm in length and appeared as a very thin reddish fibrous structure on gross examination (Fig. 4A). Discussion

The presence of vascular wall components in specimens retrieved during mechanical thrombectomy has previously been reported [4,5]. However, to our knowledge, there has been no pathological confirmation that emboli generated during balloon-assisted coiling contain arterial wall components.

In the present case, we initially considered thrombus to be the cause of the basilar artery occlusion, and the intraoperative management was performed under the assumption of thrombus formation. However, unlike previous reports [4,5] in which retrieved thrombi contained only focal vascular wall components, the retrieved specimen in our case consisted almost entirely of arterial wall tissue, as shown in Fig. 4. Furthermore, histopathological examination revealed little to no thrombotic component within the embolus. Taken together, these pathological findings strongly support our interpretation that the embolus was composed predominantly of arterial wall tissue rather than thrombus.

Therefore, this case represents a rare example of arterial wall fragment embolism occurring during balloon-assisted coiling and highlights an important diagnostic pitfall: a nonthrombotic embolus that can easily be mistaken for thrombus.

The most plausible explanation for this mechanism is that repeated balloon inflation caused focal stress concentration at the sharply curved lesser curvature of the parent artery, resulting in arterial wall dissection.

As the parent artery was markedly angulated along the lesser curvature, the balloon inflation process likely concentrated compressive force at the apex of the vascular bend, repeatedly imposing mechanical stress involving both stretching and shortening on the arterial wall [3,[6], [7], [8]].

Furthermore, progressive enlargement of the coil mass during embolization may have narrowed the space between the coil mass and the parent artery. Continued balloon inflation in this situation could have increased local pressure and contributed to progression of arterial wall injury. Transient arterial wall irregularity was indeed observed along the lesser curvature of the vascular bend, and these findings were deemed to be more indicative of vascular wall injury than thrombosis. While the contribution of catheter manipulation or contact with the coil mass cannot be entirely ruled out, repeated balloon inflation against a sharply curved parent artery was considered a major contributing factor to arterial wall dissection and the subsequent release of arterial wall fragments [[3], [4], [5]].

In this case, the aneurysm had a terminal-type configuration, with both the posterior inferior cerebellar artery and the main trunk of the vertebral artery arising as branches. With this configuration, a highly flexible balloon may provide better conformity to the arterial wall. In this case, the super-compliant Scepter XC was used. This balloon has been reported to provide better conformity to the vascular wall than the Scepter C, facilitating branch preservation and neck remodeling [[6], [7], [8]]. Although the choice of balloon was considered appropriate in terms of flexibility, balloon inflation inevitably subjects the arterial wall to simultaneous shear, stretch, and compressive forces. When the balloon is pressed against the lesser curvature of a vascular bend, as in the present case, greater local mechanical stress may be generated. Therefore, it is important to adhere to the basic principles of balloon-assisted coiling by limiting both the number of balloon inflations and the inflation diameter to the minimum necessary.

When the system was withdrawn to improve blood flow, aspiration was applied through the Navien catheter. This was intended to prevent the distal migration of any thrombus that might have already been present around the lesion. However, given that the retrieved embolus was ultimately identified as arterial wall tissue rather than thrombus, the possibility that aspiration exerted additional mechanical stress on the injured arterial wall should also be considered.

When a contrast-filling defect is observed during endovascular treatment, thrombosis should first be suspected. However, when a contrast-filling defect or arterial wall irregularity develops during balloon-assisted coiling, arterial wall injury and arterial wall fragment embolism should also be considered in the differential diagnosis as possible nonthrombotic causes.

There is no consensus on the indications for the additional treatment of vascular wall dissection. However, stent placement may be an effective treatment option for lesions that obstruct blood flow or affect hemodynamics [9,10]. In this case, no additional intervention was performed after mechanical thrombectomy because no obvious vascular insufficiency or extravasation of contrast media was observed. However, given that the characteristics of the retrieved embolus suggested release of arterial wall fragments, earlier consideration of additional treatment, including stent placement, may have been reasonable from the standpoint of preventing re-embolization and maintaining blood flow.

This report is limited by its single-case design and by the retrospective inference of the mechanism from angiographic and pathological findings. Nonetheless, the case shows that arterial wall fragment embolism should be considered when a contrast-filling defect and vascular wall irregularity appear during balloon-assisted coiling.

Conclusion

When a contrast-filling defect or arterial wall irregularity develops during balloon-assisted coiling, arterial wall injury and arterial wall fragment embolism should also be considered in the differential diagnosis as possible nonthrombotic causes.
