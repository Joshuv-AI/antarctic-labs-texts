Double‐Bundle Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction Provides Superior Short‐to‐Midterm Rotational Stability But Not Clinically Important Functional Gains Over Single‐Bundle: A Systematic Review and Meta‐analysis
Authors: Manuel Alberto Gutierrez‐Moreno, Luis Eduardo Arámbula‐Torres, Ramon Alonso Prieto‐Baeza, Carlos Leal‐Contreras, Cristina Alejandra Fuentes‐Flores, Edmundo Berumen‐Nafarrate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare the biomechanical and functional outcomes between single‐bundle and double‐bundle (DB) anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction techniques using the most current evidence, stratified by follow‐up duration.MethodsPubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases were searched from 2013 to September 2025 for randomized controlled trials comparing single‐bundle versus DB anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction with a minimum follow‐up of 24 months. Primary outcomes included anterior tibial translation, rotational stability (pivot shift), and patient‐reported outcomes (subjective International Knee Documentation Committee, Lysholm score). Secondary outcomes included return‐to‐sport and graft failure rate. Continuous outcomes were pooled using inverse‐variance random‐effects models (DerSimonian‐Laird) and dichotomous outcomes using Mantel‐Haenszel fixed‐effect models, with stratification by follow‐up duration (24‐48 months vs >48 months). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic.ResultsThirteen randomized controlled trials (1041 patients; 536 single‐bundle, 505 DB; mean age 30.2 years; 68% men; follow‐up 24‐122 months) were included. At 24 to 48 months, DB reconstruction significantly reduced the risk of a positive pivot shift (risk ratio 0.60, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.43‐0.84, P = .003; number needed to treat = 10) and showed statistically higher subjective International Knee Documentation Committee (mean difference 5.88 points, 95% CI 0.44‐11.33) and Lysholm (mean difference 7.47 points, 95% CI 0.06‐14.88) scores; however, neither reached established minimal clinically important difference thresholds (11.5 and 8.9 points, respectively). Anterior tibial translation did not differ significantly between techniques (mean difference −0.34 mm, 95% CI −1.04 to 0.35, P = .327). No study reported sex‐disaggregated outcomes or individual‐level minimal clinically important difference attainment. Beyond 48 months, no significant differences were observed for any outcome.ConclusionsDB reconstruction shows statistically significant improvements in rotational stability and patient‐reported functional scores at 24 to 48 months, but functional differences do not reach established minimal clinically important difference thresholds. No significant differences were observed beyond 48 months, a finding likely influenced by substantial study heterogeneity and graft selection. The current evidence does not support a universal clinical advantage for the DB technique; shared decision‐making based on patient activity demands and surgeon experience remains essential.Level of EvidenceLevel I, systematic review and meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials.

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture remains one of the most common sports‐related injuries, with an estimated incidence of 68.6 per 100,000 person‐years in the general population and substantially higher rates among athletes participating in pivoting sports. 1 Although ACL reconstruction has become the standard of care for active individuals seeking to return to sports, the optimal surgical technique to restore knee stability and function remains a matter of debate.

The native ACL consists of 2 functional bundles—the anteromedial (AM) and posterolateral (PL)—which work synergistically throughout the range of motion to provide both anteroposterior and rotational stability of the knee. 2 The AM bundle primarily resists anterior tibial translation (ATT) when the knee is flexed, whereas the PL bundle tightens in extension and contributes to rotational restraint. Traditional single‐bundle (SB) reconstruction typically reconstructs only the AM bundle, potentially leaving residual rotational instability, which may contribute to the 10% to 30% of patients who report subjective instability despite technically successful surgery. 3

Double‐bundle (DB) reconstruction aims to more closely replicate the native ACL anatomy by reconstructing both functional bundles. 4 Biomechanical studies have consistently shown superior restoration of knee kinematics with DB reconstruction, particularly regarding rotational stability. 5 However, translating these biomechanical advantages into clear clinical superiority has been inconsistent, with randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reporting conflicting outcomes. In parallel, the “all‐inside” ACL reconstruction technique has emerged as a relevant surgical alternative, offering potential advantages in graft harvest morbidity, tunnel length optimization, and soft‐tissue preservation, and has increasingly been compared with both conventional SB and DB approaches in the recent literature.

Previous meta‐analyses have yielded contradictory conclusions, with some reporting the superiority of DB reconstruction and others finding no significant differences. 6 , 7 , 8 Importantly, most prior analyses did not stratify outcomes based on follow‐up duration, potentially masking temporal changes in treatment effects. Moreover, recent high‐quality RCTs have not been incorporated into existing meta‐analyses. The purpose of this systematic review and meta‐analysis was to compare the biomechanical and functional outcomes between SB and DB ACL reconstruction techniques using the most current evidence, stratified by follow‐up duration. It was hypothesized that DB reconstruction would show statistically significant improvements in rotational stability compared with SB reconstruction at short‐to‐medium‐term follow‐up while acknowledging that any observed differences in patient‐reported functional outcomes might not reach established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds.

METHODS

Study Protocol and Registration

This systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidelines. 9 The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration number: CRD420251056585).

Literature Search Strategy

A precise and reproducible search strategy was developed for each database. The Boolean search string for PubMed included the following: (“anterior cruciate ligament”[MeSH] OR “ACL”) AND (“double bundle” OR “double‐bundle” OR “2‐bundle”) AND (“single bundle” OR “single‐bundle” OR “1‐bundle”) AND (“randomized controlled trial”[pt] OR “RCT”). Similar tailored strategies were used for Embase and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials.

Inclusion Criteria

Studies were included if they were RCTs comparing primary anatomic SB versus DB ACL reconstruction, featured a minimum follow‐up duration of 24 months, and reported at least 1 primary biomechanical or functional outcome. Conversely, studies with a follow‐up of less than 24 months were excluded to ensure the clinical stability of the reported outcomes.

Exclusion Criteria

The exclusion criteria were revision ACL reconstructions, severe osteoarthritis, patients aged less than 16 years, or concomitant ligament reconstructions, including the posterior cruciate ligament, medial collateral ligament, lateral collateral ligament, or posterolateral corner. Furthermore, case reports, case series, reviews, nonrandomized studies, editorials, laboratory biomechanical studies, and studies with duplicate patient populations were excluded. Finally, trials were excluded if they presented insufficient data for meta‐analysis even after attempts to contact the authors.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Two independent reviewers (A.E., P.R.) screened the titles and abstracts, followed by full‐text review for potentially eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (F.C.). Data extraction was performed independently by 2 reviewers using a standardized form that included study characteristics, participant demographics, surgical details, and clinical outcomes at all reported follow‐up time points.

Outcomes Assessed

Primary Outcomes

The primary outcomes evaluated included ATT, measured using a KT‐1000/2000 arthrometer, and rotational stability, assessed via the pivot shift test (with a positive result defined as grade ≥1). Functional recovery was documented using the subjective International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) score and the Lysholm knee score. Additionally, secondary outcomes encompassed the rate of return to sports at the same preinjury level and the graft failure rate (defined as revision ACL reconstruction or confirmed graft rupture).

Quality Assessment

Study quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool. Studies were classified as having a low, moderate, or high risk of bias.

Statistical Analysis

Meta‐analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4. For continuous outcomes, mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For dichotomous outcomes, risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs were used. Continuous outcomes were pooled using the inverse‐variance random‐effects model (DerSimonian‐Laird), and dichotomous outcomes were pooled using the Mantel‐Haenszel fixed‐effect model. Analyses were stratified a priori by follow‐up duration: short‐to‐midterm (24‐48 months) and long‐term (>48 months). The same pooling models (inverse‐variance random‐effects for continuous outcomes and Mantel‐Haenszel fixed‐effect for dichotomous outcomes) were applied to both subgroups. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic. The number needed to treat (NNT) was calculated for statistically significant dichotomous outcomes.

The certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation approach. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Publication bias could not be formally assessed because fewer than 10 studies contributed to each outcome. Subgroup analyses by sex were not performed because none of the included studies reported outcomes disaggregated by sex.

RESULTS

Study Selection

The initial database search yielded 847 potentially relevant citations. After removing duplicates and performing title/abstract screening, 73 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 60 studies were excluded for the following reasons: nonrandomized study design (n = 18), mixed populations without clear separation between SB and DB techniques (n = 12), follow‐up less than 24 months (n = 8), insufficient data for meta‐analysis (n = 6), nonanatomic techniques or use of synthetic grafts (n = 4), duplicate studies or secondary analyses (n = 2), and prospective observational study design (n = 1). Ultimately, 13 RCTs met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta‐analysis, comprising a total of 1041 patients: 536 in the SB group and 505 in the DB group. Study Characteristics

The 13 included studies were RCTs, comprising a total of 1041 patients (536 in the SB group and 505 in the DB group), with a mean age of 30.2 years and a 68% male predominance. The studies were conducted across 11 countries, with a mean follow‐up ranging from 24 to 48 months.

The most common graft type was hamstring autograft (used in 9 studies), followed by allograft (2 studies). The SB technique employed transtibial drilling in 3 studies and AM portal drilling in 10 studies. All DB reconstructions used anatomic tunnel placement (Table 2).

Primary Outcomes

Anterior Tibial Translation (ATT)

Seven studies (622 patients) reported ATT measurements at short‐to‐midterm follow‐up (the remaining 6 studies were excluded from this subanalysis because they did not use arthrometer or navigation‐based laxity measurements). The difference between DB and SB reconstruction did not reach statistical significance (MD −0.34 mm; 95% CI −1.04 to 0.35; P = .327; I 2 = 89%). Consequently, no conclusions regarding superior directionality can be drawn from these data. At long‐term follow‐up, 3 studies (204 patients) showed no significant difference between techniques (MD −0.14 mm; 95% CI −0.96 to 0.68; P = .740; I 2 = 35%) (Figure 2).

Rotational Stability

Six studies (582 patients) assessed rotational stability using the pivot shift test in the short‐to‐midterm follow‐up (7 studies were excluded because of the lack of categorical reporting of pivot shift results). DB reconstruction significantly reduced the risk of a positive pivot shift (RR 0.60; 95% CI 0.43‐0.84; P = .003; I 2 = 61%), with 43 events in the DB group versus 75 events in the SB group and an NNT of 10. In the long‐term follow‐up, 5 studies (382 patients) showed no significant benefit (RR 1.12; 95% CI 0.73‐1.70; P = .600; I 2 = 0%) (Figure 3).

Forest plot of pivot shift test results for 24‐ to 48‐month follow‐up shows that DB reconstruction significantly reduced the risk of a positive pivot shift (RR 0.60, P = .003). (CI, confidence interval; DB, double‐bundle; M‐H, Mantel‐Haenszel; RR, risk ratio; SB, single‐bundle.)

Subjective IKDC Score

Five studies (435 patients) reported subjective IKDC scores at short‐to‐midterm follow‐up (8 studies were excluded because they reported only the objective IKDC form or used different functional scales). DB reconstruction resulted in significantly higher scores (MD 5.88 points; 95% CI 0.44‐11.33; P = .034; I 2 = 94%). However, this difference did not reach the established MCID of 11.5 points. 10 Notably, no study reported the percentage of subjects who met or exceeded the MCID threshold; results were limited to group‐level comparisons. Two studies (84 patients) reported subjective IKDC scores at long‐term follow‐up, with no significant difference between techniques (MD −1.06; 95% CI −4.20 to 2.08; P = .510; I 2 = 0%) (Figure 4).

Lysholm Score

Three studies (268 patients) reported Lysholm scores at short‐to‐midterm follow‐up (10 studies were excluded because of nonreporting of this specific scale). DB reconstruction showed significantly higher scores (MD 7.47 points; 95% CI 0.06‐14.88; P = .048; I 2 = 95%), approaching but not reaching the MCID of 8.9 points. 11

As with the IKDC, the proportion of patients reaching the MCID threshold was not reported in any of the primary studies. At long‐term follow‐up, 3 studies (188 patients) showed no significant difference (MD 1.02 points; 95% CI −1.85 to 3.89; P = .490; I 2 = 12%) (Figure 5).

Forest plot of Lysholm scores at 24‐ to 48‐month follow‐up shows significant differences favoring DB reconstruction (MD 7.47 points, P = .048). (CI, confidence interval; DB, double‐bundle; IV, inverse variance; MD, mean difference; SB, single‐bundle; SD, standard deviation.)

Secondary Outcomes

Return to Sport

Five studies (384 patients) reported return to the same preinjury level of sports, with no significant difference between techniques (RR 1.08, 95% CI 0.89‐1.32, P = .430, I 2 = 10%). Return‐to‐same‐level rates were 44.9% for DB and 44.7% for SB, representing a clinically negligible difference. Kim 19 was not included in this analysis because it reported only return to any level of activity (100% in both arms) rather than return to the same competitive level (Figure 6).

Graft Failure

Eight studies (762 patients) reported graft failure rates, with no significant difference between techniques (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.45‐1.38, P = .401, I 2 = 24%). The overall failure rate was 5.5% (20/364) for DB and 7.5% (30/398) for SB; the absolute risk reduction was approximately 2%, which did not reach statistical significance (Figure 7).

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings. A minimum threshold of 10 participants per treatment arm was established to maintain a baseline level of statistical precision while maximizing study inclusion. To assess the impact of smaller studies, a “leave‐one‐out” sensitivity analysis was conducted; the exclusion of the smallest study (Beyaz, 20 n = 15 per arm) resulted in a minimal change in the overall effect size (RR change < 0.03) and did not alter the direction of findings for rotational stability.

Excluding the Berumen 12 study reduced the effect sizes but did not change the direction of findings for rotational stability. However, for patient‐reported functional scores, a dedicated sensitivity analysis excluding the allograft study contributing to these outcomes 12 resulted in a loss of statistical significance (IKDC: MD 3.57, 95% CI −2.04 to 9.19, P = .211; Lysholm: MD 4.39, 95% CI −1.01 to 9.79, P = .112). This suggests that the inclusion of allograft‐based reconstructions, which may exhibit different biological ligamentization rates, contributed to the observed early functional differences. Analysis by graft type further confirmed greater effect sizes in studies using allograft compared with hamstring autograft for both ATT and pivot shift outcomes.

The superior rotational stability achieved with DB reconstruction addresses a key limitation of traditional SB techniques. Residual rotational instability after SB reconstruction has been associated with reduced return‐to‐sport rates, persistent subjective instability, and potentially an increased risk of osteoarthritis development. However, the clinical relevance of the observed differences in patient‐reported outcomes requires careful consideration. Although statistically significant, the improvements in IKDC (5.88 points) and Lysholm (7.47 points) scores with DB reconstruction fall short of established MCID thresholds. Furthermore, because none of the included studies reported the proportion of individual subjects meeting MCID, patient acceptable symptom state, or substantial clinical benefit thresholds, these group‐level differences must be interpreted with caution.

Publication Bias

Funnel plot asymmetry was not formally assessed because fewer than 10 studies contributed to each outcome; funnel plot interpretation is unreliable below this threshold.

DISCUSSION

The main findings of this systematic review and meta‐analysis of 13 RCTs comparing SB and DB ACL reconstruction 6 , 8 were as follows: DB reconstruction was associated with a statistically significant improvement in rotational stability at 24 to 48 months of follow‐up, showing a 40% reduction in the risk of a positive pivot shift (RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.43‐0.84, P = .003; NNT = 10). However, neither IKDC nor Lysholm score differences reached established MCID thresholds (IKDC: MD 5.88 vs MCID 11.5 points; Lysholm: MD 7.47 vs MCID 8.9 points). 10 , 11 Furthermore, no significant differences in any clinical or functional outcome were observed beyond 48 months, a finding that closely aligns with several long‐term randomized trials in the literature. 13 , 14 , 15

To understand this apparent temporal decline in the superiority of DB reconstruction, the high heterogeneity observed across the included studies (I 2 > 75%) must be evaluated. The most parsimonious explanation for the divergence between short‐to‐midterm and long‐term results lies in the substantial methodological variability between trials. 8 , 16 Key sources of this heterogeneity include differences in SB femoral tunnel drilling techniques (transtibial vs AM portal), graft selections (hamstring autograft vs allograft), 12 , 17 rehabilitation protocols, and examiner blinding. Regarding the drilling technique specifically, the SB femoral tunnel was placed using transtibial drilling in 3 studies and the AM portal in 10 studies. Our post hoc descriptive comparison revealed that 2 of the 3 transtibial studies reported nonsignificant functional differences between groups. This is highly consistent with prior biomechanical evidence, showing that nonanatomic SB placement often underestimates the true performance of modern anatomic SB reconstruction relative to DB techniques. 5 This methodological evolution is further highlighted by the increasing adoption of concomitant lateral extra‐articular tenodesis or anterolateral ligament reconstruction as an adjunct to SB reconstruction in more recent studies. These lateral procedures are particularly relevant to our timeline analysis because they independently address the residual rotational laxity that DB reconstruction aims to correct through purely intra‐articular means. 18 By providing robust secondary restraint, the addition of lateral extra‐articular tenodesis or anterolateral ligament procedures potentially equalizes rotational stability between the SB and DB groups over time. Given this confounding methodological shift across study eras, the apparent temporal attenuation of DB reconstruction benefits should be interpreted with caution and regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than as definitive evidence of true biological convergence.

Beyond these strictly methodological considerations, secondary biological explanations may also contribute to the observed long‐term equilibration, although they remain partly speculative. Neuromuscular adaptation and dynamic joint stabilization mechanisms are well known to compensate partially for residual mechanical laxity over time, potentially masking subtle differences during long‐term clinical follow‐up. 3 Additionally, graft remodeling (ligamentization) and vascularization after ACL reconstruction are prolonged, multiyear processes. 19 During the early phases, the dual‐bundle architecture may confer temporary biomechanical advantages 2 , 5 before these differences are obscured by long‐term joint remodeling, tunnel widening, 20 or progressive osteoarthritic changes. 21

Importantly, the clinical interpretation of these results is restricted by how outcomes were documented. None of the included primary studies reported grade‐specific pivot shift data (i.e., the exact proportion of patients suffering from high‐grade 2 or grade 3 pivot shifts). 18 Such granular data would be essential to determine whether DB reconstruction provides a clinically meaningful reduction in severe rotational instability for specific high‐demand patients. 22 , 23 In the absence of individual patient‐level data and standardized pivot shift grading, no firm conclusions regarding the absolute clinical superiority of either technique can be definitively drawn.

Despite these limitations, the present meta‐analysis, conducted in accordance with rigorous reporting guidelines, 9 offers distinct advantages over previous reviews by providing increased statistical power through the inclusion of 1041 patients. 6 , 8 Our specific approach of temporal stratification successfully uncovers critical clinical patterns not identified in prior aggregate analyses, whereas the use of comprehensive sensitivity analyses further confirms the mathematical robustness of our primary findings.

Based on these primary findings, DB reconstruction should be considered for patients who participate in pivoting sports. However, the added technical complexity, increased operative time, and potential for complications must be weighed against the modest clinical benefits. For older patients, recreational athletes, or those with lower stability demands, SB reconstruction remains a valid option with similar long‐term outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Substantial heterogeneity in functional outcomes (I 2 > 75%) suggests significant variability in surgical techniques, rehabilitation protocols, or patient populations across the included studies. A significant limitation is that none of the 13 included RCTs reported the proportion of individual patients meeting or exceeding MCID, patient acceptable symptom state, or substantial clinical benefit thresholds; all studies reported group‐level means and standard deviations only. This prevents a true responder analysis, which would better reflect the clinical impact on individual patients.

Variations in surgical technique, particularly for DB reconstruction and the choice of transtibial versus AM portal drilling for SB tunnels, may influence outcomes in ways not captured by the present analysis. Furthermore, the analysis did not include the “all‐inside” ACL reconstruction technique as a comparator. Specifically, the use of transtibial drilling in a subset of studies represents a potential confounding factor because nonanatomic SB placement may underestimate its performance relative to DB. The included studies also showed considerable heterogeneity regarding graft choice. Although 8 RCTs used hamstring autografts exclusively, others incorporated quadriceps tendon, 14 a mix of patellar and hamstring autografts, 22 or even hybrid approaches using hamstring autograft for the AM bundle and allograft for the PL bundle. 19 Notably, 2 studies relied on allografts for 1 or both bundles. 12 , 21 A dedicated sensitivity analysis excluding the allograft study contributing to these outcomes 12 showed that statistical significance for functional outcomes was no longer maintained (IKDC: MD 3.57, P = .211; Lysholm: MD 4.39, P = .112), suggesting that the variability in graft donor source and biological ligamentization may have influenced early functional differences.

Beyond graft type, several important confounding variables were not systematically extractable or controlled for, including the performance of concomitant lateral extra‐articular tenodesis or anterolateral ligament reconstruction. The increasing clinical adoption of these lateral augmentation procedures addresses the same rotational instability that DB reconstruction aims to correct; thus, their presence in more recent cohorts may act as a confounder that narrows the perceived benefit of DB over SB techniques. Similarly, although the exclusion criteria focused on multiligament injuries (posterior cruciate ligament, medial collateral ligament, lateral collateral ligament, and posterolateral corner), the included trials did not systematically report or exclude concomitant meniscal or articular cartilage procedures. The lack of granular data on these associated injuries and their subsequent treatments limits our ability to assess their impact on long‐term functional recovery and joint stability.

Additionally, there was a notable lack of systematic reporting regarding postoperative complications (e.g., infection rates, arthrofibrosis, or secondary meniscal tears) across the included trials, preventing a formal pooled analysis of the safety profiles for each technique.

Furthermore, a significant clinical limitation is the lack of standardized protocols for performing the pivot shift test across the included studies, with inherent variability in patient positioning, examiner experience, and the use of anesthesia. Most trials reported rotational laxity as a binary outcome (positive vs negative) rather than providing data stratified by severity (e.g., grade 1 vs grades 2/3). Because only 2 studies provided grade‐specific outcomes, a formal pooled analysis for persistent high‐grade pivot shift was precluded, which may limit the clinical interpretation of residual instability.

Methodologically, the analysis was limited by the absence of time‐to‐event data. Because none of the included studies reported survival data or time‐to‐failure curves (e.g., Kaplan‐Meier estimates), a formal survival analysis was precluded. This is an important gap because the 48‐month threshold used for temporal stratification was defined a priori and may not optimally capture the inflection point where treatment effects or graft failure rates between SB and DB techniques may diverge.

Finally, none of the 13 included RCTs reported outcomes disaggregated by sex, precluding any sex‐stratified analysis; all studies provided only the aggregate proportion of male participants. This represents an important gap because sex‐based differences in ACL biomechanics, graft remodeling, and recovery are well recognized. Reporting of sex‐disaggregated outcomes in future RCTs is strongly encouraged, consistent with the sex and gender equity in research guidelines for sex and gender equity in research. 24 It is also important to note that many of the included primary studies had relatively small sample sizes and were likely underpowered to detect statistically significant differences in key outcomes, particularly for secondary endpoints such as graft failure or return to sports. It is recommended that future RCTs systematically report sex‐disaggregated outcomes in accordance with sex and gender equity in research guidelines, provide detailed complication rates, and report individual‐level attainment of clinical thresholds (MCID, patient acceptable symptom state, and substantial clinical benefit) to improve the clinical utility of future meta‐analytical findings.

CONCLUSIONS

DB reconstruction shows statistically significant improvements in rotational stability and patient‐reported functional scores at 24 to 48 months, but functional differences do not reach established MCID thresholds. No significant differences were observed beyond 48 months, a finding likely influenced by substantial study heterogeneity and graft selection. The current evidence does not support a universal clinical advantage for the DB technique; shared decision‐making based on patient activity demands and surgeon experience remains essential.
