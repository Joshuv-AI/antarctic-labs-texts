Sulfated Polysaccharides in Wound Healing: From Bioactive Materials Design to Regenerative Therapeutics
Authors: Guangyun Hu, Ling Li, Ronglan Ouyang, Xiaosheng Ke, Shurun Huang, Peng Yang, Can Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSulfated polysaccharides (SPs) are emerging as a versatile class of bioactive macromolecules for wound healing, owing to their intrinsic roles in cell signaling, immunomodulation, tissue repair, and host defense. As wound healing is a dynamic and tightly coordinated process, particularly in chronic and refractory wounds, a central question is how SP-based biomaterials can be engineered to modulate the wound microenvironment with spatiotemporal precision. Natural and engineered SPs offer distinct advantages, including structural diversity, inherent bioactivity, and tunable physicochemical properties. Mechanistically, SPs regulate key aspects of repair through macrophage polarization, sequestration and presentation of heparin-binding proteins and growth factors, antimicrobial activity, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. Recent advances have enabled SP-based platforms in the form of hydrogels, microneedles, microspheres, and nanofibrous scaffolds, which provide biochemical and mechanical cues to orchestrate inflammation resolution, angiogenesis, infection control, and tissue regeneration. This Review outlines the sources, structural features, and engineering strategies of SPs, highlights the transition from natural to engineered systems, and discusses recent progress and translational challenges in SP-based wound therapeutics.

Introduction

Polysaccharides of marine and animal origin have long been recognized for their structural diversity and broad bioactivity. This review stands as a notable advancement in our understanding of the biological functions of SPs and their multi-targeting mechanisms in regulating wound healing. This recognition, however, also underscores the inherent limitations of non-sulfated counterparts - biocompatible and biodegradable yet constrained by poor physicochemical properties, mechanical trade-offs, instability, and crosslinker-associated risks that preclude their standalone efficacy in complex wound environments.1–3 In comparison with previous studies, the present review employs a comprehensive approach, offering a comprehensive and nuanced examination of this subject.

Furthermore, the review places particular emphasis on the potential of these polysaccharides to function as a dynamic therapeutic platform. This theoretical framework provides a multifaceted approach to the treatment of challenging wounds, offering both scientific profundity and broad applicability. This review also initiates a comprehensive investigation into the domain of SPs, encompassing both natural and engineered forms. A thorough examination of these SPs discloses a multifaceted relationship between their structure and functionality. Moreover, this study methodically categorizes a diverse array of biomaterial platforms that are pertinent to the domain of wound healing.

The keywords, “sulfated polysaccharide”, “wound”, “glycosaminoglycans”, “hydrogels”, “microneedles”, “microspheres”, and “nanofibers” were searched in “PubMed” and “Google Scholar”. Through this comprehensive and logic-driven framework, this review aims not only to consolidate existing knowledge but also to offer practical insights for biomaterial engineers and clinicians working at the interface of materials science and regenerative medicine.

Wound Healing and Sulfated Polysaccharides

Wound Healing

The skin, the largest organ of the body, plays a vital role in a variety of essential physiological processes. It covers the entire body surface, forming a natural barrier that safeguards all organs and tissues, and it activates immune defenses, enabling the body to tolerate external stimuli imposed by mechanics, chemicals, and pathogenic microorganisms.4,5 Concurrently, the integumentary system participates in a multitude of neuroimmunoendocrine functions, including thermoregulation, secretion and activation of hormones and neuropeptides, and perception of external stimuli.6–8 Nevertheless, the skin provides only minimal intrinsic protection, rendering it vulnerable to trauma, burns, ulcers, surgical procedures, among other potential hazards. The process of wound formation frequently entails the loss of skin structural elements and their attendant functions.

The process of wound healing is a complex physiological process that occurs in four successive yet overlapping phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling.9–11 Following the infliction of a wound, the organism initiates a sophisticated healing process. Physiologic events occurring during the hemostasis stage encompass reactive constriction of the periwound microvessels, formation of platelet thrombi, and initiation of both endogenous and exogenous coagulation cascades.12,13 The inflammatory activity has been observed to commence in the hemostasis phase and can be broadly divided into an initial period of neutrophil recruitment and a subsequent chapter of monocyte migration and transformation. Furthermore, the process involves the activation of other immune cells, including dendritic cells and lymphocytes.14–16 Consequently, the proliferative phase ensues as the inflammatory response abates. The primary objective of this procedure is the epidermis reconstruction, granulation tissue formation, and vascular network restoration. In the context of wound tissue reconstruction, the dominant cell types implicated are endothelial cells, keratinocytes, and fibroblasts. In the course of remodeling, alterations in the extracellular matrix (ECM) components are evident, such as the reshaping of collagen fibers through protease degradation and the cessation of granulation tissue proliferation through apoptosis, a process that may span several months.17–20

The cascaded healing process entails the involvement of multiple cells, wherein gene silencing and activation may occur concurrently. And intracellular and intercellular information crosstalk must be integrated in a coordinated and orderly manner to ultimately restore tissue integrity and homeostasis.21–25 However, when this sequence of complex biological activities is disturbed by adverse factors, such as hyperglycemia, infections, and inflammatory storms, the wound will fail to undergo the anticipated histological repair and recovery of the corresponding anatomical structure and physiological function, resulting in an unfavorable healing outcome.26–29 Among chronic wounds, those resulting from diabetes mellitus are the most prevalent. Apart from hyperglycemia, abnormal vascularization, persistent and amplified inflammation, and elevated oxidative stress levels that collectively contribute to the resistance of diabetic wounds to healing, microbial infections may further deteriorate the wound microenvironment, destroy the deeper tissues, and provoke multifarious complications, which consequently raise the amputation rate.30–33 Burns are distinct from other wounds in that they are open wounds, the majority of which are caused by thermal exposure. The management of burn wounds is inherently complex, necessitating specialized expertise and equipment to ensure optimal outcomes. Third-degree burn wounds typically mandate surgical intervention, while multiple treatment modalities exist for deep second-degree burn wounds. The optimal approach between surgery and conservative treatment remains a challenging clinical dilemma.34–36 The inflammatory storm that occurs during the initial phase of deep second-degree burn wounds has been demonstrated to readily exacerbate tissue damage. Concurrently, excessive inflammation infiltration has been shown to disturb subsequent normal healing events, including neovascularization, granulation tissue generation, and orderly collagen arrangement. These effects can result in surgical interventions that impose a substantial burden on the patient.37–40

Sulfated Polysaccharides in Wound Healing

A principal objective in the advancement of biomimetic wound dressings with optimal cellular responses is the elucidation of the ECM physicochemical characteristics.41 The ECM furnishes a dynamic microenvironment for cells, presenting specific stimuli within the signaling cascade process. Additionally, the ECM offers mechanical support for resident cells and functions as a reservoir for recognition and adhesion of ligands and growth factors.42–46 The utilization of ECM-mimicking materials has the potential to mitigate the provisional deficiency of the ECM. Their interaction with cells minimizes the disturbance of the external environment to the wound, thus allowing for a more straightforward tissue regeneration and remodeling.47 As is widely acknowledged, a major component of the ECM is proteoglycans, which are comprised of core proteins modified with sulfated glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), such as heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate.48,49 SPs, as an integral component of the ECM, are emerging as the dominant factor in biomimetic materials and have garnered considerable attention for their exceptional performance in wound healing. This prominence is underpinned by their ability to regulate a diverse array of signaling pathways—including Wnt/β-catenin, NF-κB, MAPK, PI3K/Akt, and TGF-β/Smad—in a structure-dependent manner.50,51 Some SPs exhibit superior antioxidant properties, such as fucoidan, which eliminate excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) in wounds, diminish lipid peroxidation, protein modification, and DNA damage, and inhibit cell apoptosis and senescence, minimizing the detrimental effects of high oxidative stress levels on wound healing.52–55 Moreover, certain SPs (eg, heparin and sulfated alginate) share a particular affinity for heparin-binding proteins (HBPs), such as interleukin-8 (IL-8) and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), which can influence their distribution and function within the wound site. SP-based biomaterials have the potential to function as an effective “molecular sink” to sequester copious chemokines in hyperinflammatory wounds, thereby impeding further recruitment of immune cells and ultimately interrupting the inflammatory process.56–58 A particularly salient phenomenon pertains to diabetic and severe burn wounds, wherein M1 macrophages, subject to deleterious factors such as hyperglycemia, elevated levels of pro-inflammatory mediators, and bacterial infections, struggle to shift to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, leaving the wound stuck in the inflammatory phase.59,60 As a result, the wound fails to progress to the repair stage. Specific SPs, including sulfated chitosan and sulfated alginate, have been demonstrated to reshape the M1 macrophage phenotype, thereby ameliorating the dysfunctional inflammatory microenvironment in wounds.61,62 Mechanistically, chondroitin sulfate has been shown to antagonize Wnt/β-catenin signaling via PAPSS2-mediated sulfation and the bile acid-FXR-TLE3 axis, while its biosynthesis is regulated by p38 MAPK and SMAD3 phosphorylation.63,64 Heparan sulfate, acting as an OX40 ligand, directly activates AKT, MAPK, and NF-κB pathways, and promotes BMP2 signaling in the extracellular space through specific trisulfated motifs (IdoA(2S)-GlcNS(6S)).65,66 Fucoidan attenuates inflammatory responses by inhibiting NF-κB activation and participates in TGF-β/Smad and PI3K/Akt pathways during wound healing and tissue repair.67 Notably, the signaling outcomes elicited by different SPs are highly dependent on their sulfation patterns, molecular structure, and local microenvironment, reflecting a precise structure-function relationship that provides a molecular basis for the rational design of SP-based wound therapeutics.68

Furthermore, electrostatic complexes resulting from the interactions between SPs and HBPs render SPs-associated biomaterials poised to serve as reservoirs for critical growth factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF). This shields them from degradation by proteases and ensures their programmed signal transduction proceeds without a hitch.69–72 The advent of SPs in tissue engineering has the potential to yield a plethora of applications of SP formulations in the domain of wound healing (Figure 1). Natural Sulfated Polysaccharides

Natural sulfated polysaccharides (NSPs) are a ubiquitous class of compounds found across a diverse range of biological organisms, with a wide spectrum of functions.73,74 In animals, SPs are known as GAGs, including dermatan sulfate (DS), heparan sulfate (HS), chondroitin sulfate (CS), keratan sulfate (KS), and heparin (Hep). GAGs are present in almost all tissues, where they participate in cell growth modulation, immune response regulation, and coagulation reactions.75 Among these, HS and Hep have been the subject of the most extensive investigation. Their most notable characteristics are their specific binding to abundant receptor proteins, including cytokines, adhesion molecules, chemokines, growth factors, enzymes, and pathogen proteins, among others. This combination exerts a profound impact on the biological functions of receptor proteins.76 The molecular interaction between Hep and HBPs is primarily driven by strong electrostatic attraction between the negative charge of heparin chains and the positive charge of Hep -binding domains on the surface of HBPs. The binding of this compound is specific and contingent upon positively charged clusters formed by specific arginine/lysine-rich sequence patterns on HBPs, as well as the length and specific sulfation patterns of Hep chains. The binding process is frequently accompanied by conformational changes in HBPs, which serve as the core mechanism by which Hep performs essential biological functions. For example, the pentose structure of Hep permits their binding to antithrombin III, which initiates a conformational change in the protein and subsequently augments its anticoagulant activity.77–79 A comprehensive understanding of this mechanism is imperative for elucidating the role of Hep in anticoagulation, inflammation, and cancer metastasis, as well as for the development of heparin-based drugs and biomaterials.80–87 The interaction between SPs and HBPs offers a potential avenue for customizing specialized wound dressings.

Along with this, SPs have been identified in marine macroalgae, including brown, red, and green algae, which exhibit anticancer, anticoagulant, and immunomodulatory properties. Fucoidan, a known source of SPs, is found in the cell wall of brown algae. Other SPs, including carrageenan and ulvan, are present in red and green algae. These SPs exhibit significant structural diversity and may play a crucial role in protecting cells against desiccation and other forms of environmental stress.88–90 With respect to GAGs in animals, plant-derived SPs exhibit reduced constraints regarding abundance and sustainability, in addition to a diminished propensity for immunogenicity. More importantly, some SPs in marine algae have been demonstrated to share Hep-like nature, such as anticoagulant and HBP conjugation attributes,91–94 which will underpin their translational applications in the broader field of tissue engineering.

The anionic nature of SPs allows for the formation of electrostatic complexes that dynamically sequester bioactive compounds and the assembly of biomaterial structures, including hydrogels, microneedles, microspheres, nanofibers, and so forth, which can be utilized as drug and cell delivery vehicles. In certain instances, NSPs may impose constraints in relation to production costs, batch standardization, degradability, and other parameters. Notably, engineered sulfated polysaccharides (ESPs) may surmount some of these challenges and expand the scope of biomedical applications of SPs. Engineered Sulfated Polysaccharides

ESPs are naturally non-existent structural derivatives that frequently possess novel or enhanced properties. Typically, ESPS are generated by non-SP functionalization. However, they can also undergo tailoring based on the structure of NSPs. Such tailoring is possible via regionally selective sulfation, desulfation, or backbone modification to strengthen or modulate intrinsic properties.95 Recently, non-SPs have been subjected to chemical modification to achieve SPs with specific and controllable functional profiles, such as hyaluronic acid, chitosan, alginate, and cellulose.96 The sulfation modification can further promote the polysaccharide exploitation and utilization.

Owing to the immense variation in the origin and structure of natural polysaccharides, the polysaccharide sulfation does not follow a universal standardized protocol, but mainly occurs by chemically substituting hydroxyl groups on the polysaccharide chain for sulfate groups. The commonly adopted sulfation procedures encompass the concentrated sulfuric acid (CSA) method, the chlorosulfonic acid-pyridine (CSPA) method, the sulfamic acid-formamide (SAM) method, the chlorosulfonic acid-formamide (CSFA) method, and the sulfur trioxide-dimethylformamide (STDMA) method, among others.97–104 Of these, the former three methods are employed with greater frequency. Among the aforementioned schemes, the varying sulfation degrees of polysaccharides and the minimization of side reactions can be achieved by managing the chemical reaction conditions. To illustrate, we synthesized low and high sulfated alginate by varying the chlorosulfonic acid concentration in the reaction system.29 Chemical sulfation reactions are inherently non-regioselective. Nevertheless, minor discrepancies in the configuration and chemical composition of polysaccharides can also be leveraged to introduce targeted modification patterns, such as the location of glycosidic bonds and the presence of additional functional groups.95 Variations in the sulfation pattern of polysaccharides will result in a distinct orientation and accessibility of the negative charge during interactions with proteins. Consequently, regioselective sulfation facilitates the investigation of structure-function relationships, and the potential design of protein-binding epitopes with high specificity, thereby enabling customization of dressing for specific wounds, such as diabetic and burn wounds.105,106 This, in turn, will broaden the biomedical applications of ESPs. Additionally, enzymatic sulfation has been employed for the synthesis of Hep and CS derivatives and may be an equally valuable prospective technology to yield highly specific SPs. However, their application for the large-scale ESPs synthesis is still not feasible in light of the low commercial accessibility of the enzyme and suitable substrates.107–109

Notably, NSPs have also been observed to undergo desulfation, resulting in alterations to their physicochemical and biological properties.110–114 Chemical solvent desulfation is also generally non-specific and complete, but can manifest regioselectivity comparable to sulfation by tuning the substitution site reactivity and reaction conditions.115 For instance, various hydrogel dressings have been prepared for improving the inflamed wound microenvironment by removing the sulfate groups from Hep.116 In addition, chemical sulfation of NSPs can obtain oversulfated derivatives. A widely investigated case is CS, wherein disparate sulfation patterns of CS elicit disparate neuroplasticity and regeneration effects.117,118 Moreover, sulfation with SO3 in DMF led to a 30-fold increase in the anticoagulant activity of κ-carrageenan oligosaccharides derived from red algae.119,120 The desulfation and oversulfation of NSPs both inevitably contribute to their altered physicochemical properties, which, in turn, exert diverse biological effects, providing a broad platform for the biomimetic materials development.

In comparison to NSPs, ESPs exhibit distinctive pharmacokinetic characteristics, such as degradability, which may open them up to novel applications, including drug delivery carriers in wound healing. On top of that, ESPs, which can be produced on a large scale at low cost from renewable raw materials, represent a more environmentally and economically sustainable alternative to NSPs, which may gain a wider application in the foreseeable future. Specifically, ESPs provide precise manipulation of the sulfation pattern, which permits fine-tuning of the biological characteristics of wound dressings in a customizable manner, such as growth factor delivery, chemokine sequestration, and directed cellular differentiation induction. Innovative crosslinking strategies and composite designs will deliver complex biomaterials with tunable structure behavior, such as hydrogels, microneedles, nanofibers, microspheres, etc., which may address numerous challenges in clinically refractory wounds. In this context, the spatial presentation of sulfate groups critically dictates biological outcomes: chitosan-anchored sulfonates promote M2 macrophage polarization, while free sulfonates enhance GAG biosynthesis, collectively improving collagen deposition and angiogenesis.121 Sulfate position also determines target selectivity—sulfated chitosan activates VEGF/VEGFR2 signaling to accelerate angiogenesis, whereas unsulfated chitosan lacks this activity.122 Furthermore, DS and sulfation pattern modulate key pathways, including NF-κB, MAPK, and Wnt/β-catenin, with sulfate spatial arrangement dictating whether TLR4/NF-κB elicits pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory responses.50 However, inherent structural heterogeneity of natural SPs—variations in repeating units, sulfate position, and DS—hinders clear structure-activity relationships and formulation optimization.96 Achieving precise wound healing outcomes thus requires systematic elucidation of the cascade linking sulfate distribution, pathway activation, and tissue repair, alongside efforts toward structural standardization and function-directed design.123 For the convenience of subsequent exposition, structural and functional information of partial ESPs is summarized in Table 2, and corresponding supporting materials are presented in Supplementary Figure S2. Sulfated Polysaccharides in Biomedical applications

Hydrogel

Hydrogels are hydrophilic three-dimensional networks formed in polymeric materials by chemical or physical crosslinking.135–137 The highly hydrated nature, ECM-mimetic structure, tunable biomechanical performance, and excellent bioactive substance delivery capacity endow hydrogels with inherent advantages as potential wound dressings. Hydrogel dressings have been shown to effectively maintain optimal wound moisturization and continuously absorb wound exudate, outperforming traditional dressings such as gauze in these functions.138–140 In addition, certain hydrogels derived from natural polymers can undergo degradation over time, thus preventing secondary damage during dressing replacement, such as natural polysaccharides and peptides.141–144 In particular, hydrogel dressings with multiple beneficial characteristics, such as environment responsiveness (temperature-sensitive, pH-responsive, photosensitive, etc)., can be structurally designed and functionally integrated, thus taking on important contributions to all stages of wound healing.145–148

Initially, hydrogel dressings served as a basic physical barrier and created a moist milieu. However, in the pursuit of enhanced wound healing outcomes, hydrogel dressings are evolving into multifaceted biological devices. For instance, hydrogels with hemostatic effects can adhere tenaciously to the wound site, forming a seal over the vessels and promoting coagulation.149,150 Antimicrobial hydrogels equipped with nano-scale antimicrobial materials or antibiotics can fulfill high antimicrobial efficacy.151–153 Hydrogel for tissue regeneration can promote vascularization, granulation tissue regeneration, and epithelialization through the controlled release of small molecule drugs, growth factors, and cytokines.154–156 Advanced functional dressings, such as anti-freeze and heat-resistant hydrogels and electronic skin, can expedite healing while monitoring the wound microenvironment under extreme conditions.157–164 SPs are widely participated in the preparation of the above hydrogels for their superior degradability, ease of modification, and favorable cytocompatibility.

The negative charge in SPs serves to mimic the chemical signals present in the natural environment, thereby facilitating the induction and maintenance of embedded cell phenotypes.41 Secondly, the negative charge in SPs hydrogels affords the possibility of sustained release of positively charged therapeutic agents, such as growth factors. These agents stimulate cell growth, differentiation, and viability, and regulate tissue repair. However, their clinical efficacy is severely curtailed by their low stability, rapid internalization, and subsequent degradation.154 Furthermore, the sulfate moiety in NSPs or ESPs has the potential to intervene in the inflammatory process. SPs may coordinate immune events during the healing process.29 This is of particular importance for some refractory wounds, such as diabetic and severe burn wounds. These wounds are typically distinguished by the infiltration of excessive inflammatory factors and ROS, the persistence and overactivation of inflammatory cells, and the presence of elevated concentrations of proteolytic degradation enzymes. Collectively, these elements contribute to a markedly prolonged inflammatory phase, which subsequently causes delayed healing.13 NSPs- or ESP-based hydrogels may provide novel therapeutic solutions for these poorly healing wounds.

Hep is one of the most intensively studied NSPs, and is well documented for its biological functions, including anticoagulation and anti-inflammatory effects.165–168 Lohmann et al prepared selectively desulfated Hep which acquired different affinities for HBPs. Desulfated Hep-based hydrogels effectively sequestered chemokines, such as MCP-1 and IL-8, and dampened the migration activity of monocytes and neutrophils, which thus enhanced the granulation tissue formation and neovascularization.116 Hep has been widely applied in tissue engineering, but still faces demerits such as rapid degradation and impurities,41 which have driven the advancement of sulfate analogues. Chitosan is a non-sulfated linear polysaccharide derived from chitin in insects, crustaceans, and fungi.169,170 Shen et al prepared sulfated chitosan, which was then doped into collagen I to fabricate hybrid hydrogels. The hybrid hydrogel accelerated the regression of excessive inflammation by stimulating the M1 macrophage polarization in chronic diabetic wounds.171 Apart from sulfated GAGs from animals and ESPs, marine macroalgae are also rich in SPs. Fucoidan is a SP extracted from brown algal cell walls, which contains abundant L-fucose and sulfate groups.172–174 Egle et al prepared fucoidan/chitosan self-assembled hydrogels loaded with platelet-rich fibrin (PRF). VEGF, EGF, and TGF-β1 in the composite hydrogel were sustainably released for 7 days, which may be correlated with the interaction of fucoidan with HBPs. Moreover, the composite hydrogel exhibited the effects on promoting wound healing and tissue regeneration, which would be a significant step in the quest towards the improvement of growth factor release.175 As illustrated in Figure 2, a range of specific SPs-based hydrogels are employed in wound healing applications. Sulfated moieties in the ECM are crucial in regulating growth signaling and shaping the immune microenvironment within tissues. Despite numerous studies on SP-based hydrogels, direct comparisons between different SP types within the same wound model remain lacking, and the optimal sulfation degree for specific applications has not been systematically established. Furthermore, contradictory findings regarding the immunomodulatory effects of certain SPs-where some report anti-inflammatory activity while others note pro-inflammatory responses-remain largely unexplored. Future head-to-head studies with standardized evaluation criteria are needed to guide rational hydrogel design. Growth factor delivery vehicles, chemokine-sequestering scaffolds, and inflammation-regulating systems in hydrogels based on SPs are highly beneficial for wound healing. Aimed at key targets in intractable wounds, biomaterials based on the interaction between SPs and HBPs will present powerful functions that can be tailored on the basis of their own identity.

Microneedle

The skin serves as an indispensable organ for the absorption and metabolism of drugs. Upon entering the bloodstream via the skin, the drug may evade the degradation of digestive enzymes and the first-pass effect of the liver, thereby directly exerting its pharmacological effect on the intended region. However, the stratum corneum of the skin presents certain impediments with regard to drug transfer. Consequently, a negligible amount of the pharmaceutical agent reaches the dermal capillaries, thereby undergoing absorption and utilization.176,177 Microneedles may bridge this gap, as they possess a micron-sized and needle-shaped structure and integrate the double drug release advantages of transdermal patches and subcutaneous injections. The tips of these micron-sized needles can penetrate the stratum corneum and form pores, which provide a channel for macromolecular substances into the skin, allowing for smooth absorption of the drug by the capillaries in the dermis. Such a way of drug delivery is characterized by stability, rapidity, and painlessness, and while simultaneously reducing the delivery inaccuracy and markedly raising the drug bioavailability, etc.178–180 Microneedle systems for transdermal drug delivery have been optimized for detection and management of a myriad of diseases, including skin tumors, diabetes, obesity, and eye-related disorders.181–183 With the progress of micro-nanofabrication technology, solid microneedles, coated microneedles, dissolving microneedles, hollow microneedles, hydrogel microneedles, and so on have gradually emerged in tissue engineering.184–186

Solid microneedles bear no medication, and their punctured epidermis creates numerous microscopic pore channels through which pharmaceutical preparations (ointments, gels, and lotions) can be transported into the dermis and thus distributed throughout the body via systemic circulation. However, the application procedures of solid microneedles are relatively complicated and subject to certain constraints. When solid microneedles are applied on uneven skin surfaces, there is a low penetration efficiency and suboptimal treatment outcomes.187–189 As a drug transporter, coated microneedles significantly streamline drug delivery processes over solid microneedles. Once the coated microneedle has been successfully positioned on the skin, the coating will diffuse into the subcutaneous tissue and then be conveyed to other tissues through capillaries for systemic or topical disorders.190–192 The drug loading capacity of coated microneedles is primarily dependent on the coating thickness and tip geometry (eg, size, density, and shape). Nevertheless, the tip coating is finite in thickness (excessive coatings are detrimental for skin piercing), and thus, coated microneedles are incapable of delivering high doses of drugs.

The above microneedles will be removed after application, and the discarded microneedles are considered as biohazardous medical waste. The primary advantage of dissolving microneedles is their assimilation into the dermal interstitial fluid, which eliminates the need for subsequent replacement. After evenly distributed throughout the microneedle polymer matrix, the drug will progressively diffuse from the matrix into the skin as the matrix softens and dissolves in body fluids and thereby performs the healing function.193 Ulvan is a bioactive NSP with superior antibacterial, antioxidant, antiviral, and hypolipidemic properties.194–197 Don et al extracted ulvan from stalagmites for the first time to prepare dissolving microneedles (UMNS). The in vivo implantation experiments demonstrated that UMNs rapidly disintegrated and completely penetrated the porcine skin dermis, significantly increasing the cumulative drug release.198 Additionally, Younas et al formulated nanoparticles (MOXNPs) from chitosan, fucoidan, and moxifloxacin, and then MOXNPs, lidocaine, and thrombin were incorporated into a dissolving smart microneedle patch based on pullulan polysaccharide. This microneedle patch allowed for controlled drug release as required for wound healing with the restoration of collagen deposition, promotion of cell proliferation, and reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, fulfilling the combined effects of rapid hemostasis, analgesia, and sustained bactericidal activity.199 Smart combination therapy may be a potential strategy for high-quality wound healing. Despite the multitude of applications currently, there remain some issues associated with dissolving microneedles. Firstly, dissolving microneedles with water-absorbent properties are subject to strict requirements on storage environment, transportation conditions, and shelf-life. Secondly, dissolving microneedles, with their suboptimal mechanical behavior, may face incomplete insertion into highly elastic skin, causing drug dissipation. Furthermore, dissolving microneedles typically demonstrate inadequate drug loading, and indiscriminate elevation of the drug concentration is futile since the surface roughness of dissolving microneedles may increase in conjunction with the drug proportion, rendering them more challenging to puncture the skin. Finally, the dissolution of microneedles is a time-dependent process, and therefore there are issues with delayed administration.200 These challenges may be addressed with the progression of bioengineering technology.

The advent of hydrogel microneedles within the domain of biomedical materials can be situated within a relatively recent temporal context. These microneedles, once inserted into the skin, absorb the tissue fluid to form continuous, unobstructed microchannels, allowing the drug to enter the body through the interstices of the mesh structure, with no residual material from the needle remaining.201–203 Highly biodegradable SPs can interact with a range of biomolecules, and their therapeutic effects can be enhanced in combination with microneedle structures. Liu et al prepared a biomimetic hydrogel microneedle patch based on Hep, methacryloylated hyaluronic acid, and methacryloylated polycaprolactone. The microneedle patch combined with 3D printing technique can simultaneously accomplish exudate absorption and drug delivery, realizing the diagnostic and therapeutic function integration.204 The merging of detection and treatment wound healing will be a promising direction for biomaterials development. Current research on SP-based microneedles remains largely at the proof-of-concept stage, with limited translational validation. The specific contribution of the sulfated moiety to microneedle performance—beyond the carrier matrix effects—has not been clearly delineated, and comparative studies across different microneedle formats are notably absent. Future work should prioritize scalability, stability, and clinical feasibility assessments.96 Presently, the integration of SPs with microneedles is relatively uncommon. However, with the accelerated progress of biomaterials science, they will inevitably bestow microneedles with broader application prospects in the near future, and even appear in an unexpected form.

Microsphere

The objective of the drug delivery system is to facilitate the transportation of the biologically active substance to a designated target tissue at a predetermined rate, thereby exerting a desired effect on the organism.205–208 The controlled release process, an interdisciplinary field that intersects with chemistry, physics, pharmacology, and biomaterials, among other subjects, has firmly established itself as a practice within the domain of wound healing.209–212 In contemporary times, microspheres have garnered significant attention for their inherent stability, targeting, and controlled drug release properties, in addition to the established preparation process and straightforward operational methodology. The microspheres can be formulated with specific polymers to maximize the selective distribution of drugs, thereby increasing the local drug concentration, enhancing the drug efficacy and longevity, and reducing the overall drug intake. This effectively minimizes the incidence of side effects.213–215 Moreover, the targeted transportation of drugs can be realized via manipulation of microsphere particle size, which is beneficial for administration in the eyes, nasal cavity, wounds, and other locations.

Presently, the materials for synthesizing microspheres are primarily classified into the following three categories. The initial category comprises natural polymers, including gelatin, alginate, and Hep, which are biodegradable and deemed safe for human consumption.216–220 The next group is semi-synthetic polymers, exemplified by carboxymethyl cellulose and methyl cellulose. These are low toxicity, high viscosity, and augmented solubility.217–223 However, they are not compatible with high-temperature processing due to their susceptibility to hydrolysis. Consequently, they must be prepared on-site. The third category is synthetic polymers (polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), polylactic acid (PLA), etc)., which are non-toxic and stable, with favorable film-forming and sphericity properties.224–226 The physicochemical nature of SPs determines their capacity to interact with biological agents, and with the structure of the microspheres, they can optimize the biological effects of both themselves and the drugs. The most commonly employed methodologies for the preparation of drug-loaded microspheres encompass emulsification-solvent evaporation, phase separation, and electrospray, among others.

The most prevalent methodology for the fabrication of microspheres is emulsification. The fundamental principle underlying this process is the dissolution of the raw and auxiliary materials in two immiscible solvents, which are then emulsified through mechanical or ultrasonic oscillation. In this method, the liquid dispersed into droplets represents the inner dispersed phase, whereas the liquid scattered droplets constitute the outer continuous phase. Following the elimination of the solvent from the inner dispersed phase via volatilization, the microsphere-forming material is precipitated and cures into microspheres.227–229 This method is distinguished by an elevated encapsulation rate without the necessity for sophisticated apparatus. Nevertheless, it is susceptible to being influenced by the physicochemical characteristics of the encapsulated substances and additional variables.

The next approach is phase separation, which is also referred to as flocculation. Following the introduction of a non-solvent substance (flocculant) to the mixture comprising the drug and polymer carrier (either an emulsion or suspension), the solubility of the polymer will undergo a sudden decline. This process facilitates the precipitation of the polymer from the mixed solution, which subsequently envelopes the drug surface, forming a protective layer. The protective layer can be solidified, yielding microspheres with a high encapsulation rate.230–233 Coron et al produced sulfated alginate microspheres by phase separation with Ca2+/Ba2+ as crosslinking agents, which were then used for in vivo studies of surface fibrillation of microspheres.234 Additionally, Sezer et al developed polysaccharide microspheres for burn wound treatment by blending positively charged chitosan and negatively charged fucoidan utilizing the phase separation method. In vitro and in vivo studies have revealed that the microsphere system effectively stimulated re-epithelialization and shortened the burn wound treatment cycle.235

In recent years, electrospray has become a prevalent technique in the pharmaceutical and biochemical domains. The microspheres generated through this method can be transformed into formulations suitable for oral and intravenous administration, as well as targeted and controlled drug delivery systems. The drug and carrier polymer dissolved in an organic solvent are sprayed by an aerosol dispenser into an inert gas stream to form countless small droplets, and then the organic solvent evaporates rapidly by adjusting the temperature, resulting in the droplets shrinking into microspheres.236–238 Xue et al integrated Prussian blue nanoenzymes and VEGF into CS microspheres. The integration of nano-enzymes and growth factors has been demonstrated to produce a synergistic effect, which is critical for wound healing.239 By adjusting the voltage, nozzle size, and gas pressure within the electrospray system, microspheres can be generated in a high-throughput, rapid manner. The microspheres obtained by this approach are characterized by uniform particle size. However, this method is not suitable for the production of protein and peptide microspheres, which are vulnerable to high temperatures.

In addition to the aforementioned techniques, a plethora of novel methodologies, including microfluidics, have emerged for microsphere development.240–242 Pruett et al constructed Hep-containing microspheres through a high throughput microfluidic approach, which can protect endogenous local growth factors through microscale heterogeneous segregation, which consequently governed the migration competence of downstream cells and entailed an improvement in wound healing.243 While microfluidic chips offer high precision and tunability, their production is costly and time-consuming. Key challenges remain for SP-based microspheres, including the impact of crosslinking methods on SP bioactivity and the lack of systematic studies correlating microsphere physicochemical properties with wound healing outcomes. Moreover, the long-term safety and degradation profiles of SP-containing microspheres require more rigorous characterization. Presently, the application of microspheres based on SP in wound care is relatively rare due to constraints imposed by crosslinking methodology, stability, and production cost. Nevertheless, with the advent of sophisticated bioengineering techniques, the structural superiority of microspheres based on SP will become increasingly apparent.

Nanofiber

Nanofibers with three-dimensional structure have been introduced in a multitude of fields, wherein their functionality in regulating cellular processes and facilitating the transportation of biomolecules has been leveraged.245–247 Nanofiber scaffolds characterized by high surface area and porosity have a structural resemblance to the ECM, supplying an optimal microenvironment for cell adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, and communication.248–250 To ensure the proper transmission of molecular signals from growth factors and mechanical signals from the biomaterials, precise manipulation of the cellular growth milieu is essential. In consequence, biomimetic nanofibrous materials have come to the fore in the field of tissue engineering.

In the present era, the primary methodologies employed for the synthesis of nanofibers encompass self-assembly of minute fragments, phase separation, and electrostatic spinning.251–253 The process of self-assembly involves molecules self-aligning to form nanostructures via hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and electrostatic interactions.254–258 While the fabrication of nanofiber foams mainly rests on liquid-phase separation, whereby the morphology of the polymer structure is predominantly dominated by gel temperature and polymer concentration, among other factors.259–261

Electrostatic spinning represents a distinct departure from conventional spinning techniques, whereby ultrafine fibers are formed under the influence of high voltage electric fields. The production of polymer microfibers with diameters ranging from tens to hundreds of nanometers can be conveniently achieved through the implementation of this method.262,263 The nanofiber size and structure can be influenced by various preparation parameters such as polymer concentration, voltage magnitude, solution conductivity, capillary tube diameter, collection plate location, and Taylor pore size. In addition, the selection of appropriate materials and the optimization of machining parameters can yield nanofiber scaffolds with regulated degradation rates, surface modifications, and enhanced biocompatibility.264–267 Furthermore, the integration of synthetic macromolecular and bioactive molecules (such as inorganic ions, growth factors, cellular regulators, and living cells) allows the fabrication of powerful nanofibrous three-dimensional bioscaffolds with high surface area and porosity.268–270 These mimic the structure of the ECM and thus create a microenvironment that closely approximates the conditions necessary for cell growth.

In regard to cell adhesion, viability, proliferation, and differentiation, the natural ECM serves as an optimal scaffolding agent. However, natural ECM nanofibers lack mechanical stability. Synthetic polymers can be incorporated into the ECM components, such as Hep, to create composite nanofibers that are biodegradable into the normal metabolic products of the organism. At present, the polymers that have been approved by the FDA include polyglycolic acid (PGA), PLA, polycaprolactone (PCL), and other commonly utilized polymers such as PVA, poly (lactic acid)-glycolic acid (PLGA), and poly(hydroxybutyrate-valerate) copolyester (PHBV).271–275

Phulmogare et al developed a fucoidan/PVA/dextran nanofiber by electrostatic spinning. The nanofiber diameter, encapsulation rate, and water-absorption capacity remarkably augmented with increasing fucoidan concentration. In vivo studies indicated that fucoidan/PVA/dextran nanofibers dramatically improved the wound closure rate.276 Shiravandi et al designed electrospun sulfated chitosan/PVA nanofibers which were equipped with malachite green (MG) as a cationic substance. The sulfated chitosan/PVA nanofibers could be successfully loaded with substantial MG and released it consistently.277 Interestingly, Lu et al modified chitosan electrospun nanofiber membranes with fucoidan and CuS nanoparticles, and the composites showed better antimicrobial activity.278 Moreover, Kurpinski et al modified PLLA nanofibers with Hep. Functional modification of the nanofiber scaffolds promoted vascular endothelial cell infiltration in the three-dimensional scaffolds.279

Critical analysis of SP-based nanofibers reveals several limitations: the incorporation of SPs often compromises mechanical properties; the retention and bioactivity of SPs during electrospinning fabrication have not been adequately characterized; and the distinct contributions of SP bioactivity versus scaffold architecture to wound healing remain unclear. Future studies should systematically address these structure-function-processing relationships.100,280 Given its straightforward nature and relatively low cost, electrostatic spinning has become a highly competitive vehicle in tissue engineering, with applications including wound healing, the delivery of bioactive molecules, and biofabrication. Perspective

The evolution of SPs in the context of biological applications has been a protracted endeavor, marked by a gradual progression through various developmental stages. In the 1940s, Hep was introduced as the first anticoagulant, thereby solidifying the role of SPs in the treatment of hematologic diseases and propelling the advancement and utilization of other GAGs. The subsequent isolation and characterization of fucoidan, along with its observed anticoagulant properties, initiated a new era of research focused on marine SPs in the 1950s. In the 1970s, the advent of chemical sulfation techniques (eg, the chlorosulfonic acid-formamide method) marked a pivotal shift in the field of polysaccharide sulfation modification, propelling the rapid development of ESPs. The 1980s witnessed the discovery of the mechanism of electrostatic action of SPs, thereby establishing a robust foundation for biomedical applications. The biological application of SPs has undergone an expansive evolution. Initially utilized for anticoagulation and antiviral therapy, SPs have been repurposed for a range of applications including antitumor therapy, wound healing, neuroprotection, and the development of smart materials. This expansion has followed a logical and methodical progression, encompassing fundamental research, structure optimization, mechanism analysis, and ultimately, clinical transformation. Among them, SPs have gained a notable presence in the field of wound healing.281–287

Currently, the utilization of SPs in the context of wound healing has been predominantly confined to superficial investigations, with the underlying mechanisms remaining largely elusive. Furthermore, the majority of studies have been conducted within the framework of animal models. While these studies have yielded valuable insights, they have yet to be applied in a clinical setting. An enhanced comprehension of the fundamental mechanisms underlying the interaction between SP-based biomaterials and wound tissues will propel the advancement of translational research. This, in turn, will facilitate the integration of scientific research and clinical applications. Nevertheless, the existing techniques for sulfation synthesis exhibit inconsistencies in their results. Despite the employment of the same synthesis method, the resulting products demonstrate variation depending on the reagent ratio, reaction time, and temperature. These variations pose significant challenges to researchers in the field. Further research is necessary to refine the sulfation synthesis methodology and to determine the optimal sulfation conditions, as well as to ascertain the structural features and the stability of products, which are prerequisites for the biological applications of ESPs. A more exhaustive examination of SPs is imperative to achieve a more profound understanding of the structure-function relationship, elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism, and refine the toxicological assessment. This task is further complicated by the complex and variable sulfation patterns of natural SPs, which hinder the establishment of clear structure-activity relationships, making it difficult to predict biological outcomes and optimize formulations for specific wound types.96,288 In particular, while SPs are generally regarded as biocompatible, oversulfated polysaccharides can sometimes trigger immune responses. Therefore, for medical-grade SPs, it is essential that reagent residues are validated through ISO 10993 compliance, that non-animal-derived backbones are prioritized to ensure material safety, and that the distribution of sulfate groups and charge density are experimentally optimized to achieve an adequate balance between pro-healing activity and low irritancy.100,289,290 This will pave the way for the development of novel drugs.

In terms of biomaterial platforms, SPs can be combined with alternative natural or synthetic materials to form hydrogels, microneedles, microspheres, and nanofibers for wound healing applications. However, clinical translation remains encumbered by numerous challenges. Batch differences in certain marine-derived SPs (eg, fucoidan) have the potential to influence immunogenicity, thereby necessitating the standardization of the purification process. More broadly, the inherent structural heterogeneity of marine-derived SPs—influenced by species, harvest season, and geographic origin—poses significant challenges for batch-to-batch reproducibility and quality standardization, which are prerequisites for regulatory approval.291 The extraction cost of NSPs is considerable, and the synthesis efficiency of ESPs must be optimized. In large-scale manufacturing processes, the amplification effects of manufacturing techniques (eg, electrospinning) and quality control during production present considerable challenges.292 Moreover, despite the established biocompatibility of numerous SPs, there is an ongoing need for systematic and standardized preclinical toxicological studies for specific novel marine SPs.293,294 In addition, the majority of existing studies remain at the preclinical stage, with insufficient large-scale, randomized controlled trials to validate efficacy and safety in human patients. Addressing these translational gaps will require not only cost-reduction strategies but also concerted efforts in standardization, rigorous clinical evaluation, and structure-function elucidation.295,296

Chronic wounds have become a significant challenge for global health systems due to their prolonged duration, intricate treatment processes, and high rate of recurrence. Common chronic wounds include pressure ulcers, diabetic foot ulcers, venous ulcers, and surgical incisions with infection or delayed healing. In contemporary clinical practice, the following types of dressings are frequently utilized: hydrogel dressings, silver-containing dressings, and foam dressings. In comparison with the above dressings, SP-based dressings possess the functions of microenvironment regulation. However, they are associated with a higher cost. The following strategies are proposed for the reduction of costs. The first strategy is the optimization of the extraction process, which can be accomplished through the utilization of ultrasonic-assisted extraction technology, thereby enhancing the extraction rate of polysaccharides. The second strategy involves the promotion of large-scale production and the expansion of raw materials, with the objective of enhancing the efficiency of polysaccharide synthesis through the implementation of gene editing of improved algal strains. While this is a long-term solution, a more practical short-term approach is to address solvent recovery and waste treatment in chemical sulfation (eg SO3-DMF systems), which can be effectively managed through established recycling technologies and green process integration to balance cost, efficiency, and environmental responsibility.297–300 The third strategy is the synergistic application of composites, which involves the use of composite polysaccharides with low-cost materials (eg, chitosan) to minimize the utilization of high-cost components. It is essential to enhance collaboration among industry, academia, and research to promote large-scale production and clinical implementation of such dressings, thereby reducing the burden on healthcare systems.

Beyond cost, the limited clinical translation of SP-based materials can be attributed to several additional factors. First, the inherent structural heterogeneity of marine-derived SPs-influenced by species, harvest season, and geographic origin-poses significant challenges for batch-to-batch reproducibility and quality standardization, which are prerequisites for regulatory approval. Second, the majority of existing studies remain at the preclinical stage, with insufficient large-scale, randomized controlled trials to validate efficacy and safety in human patients. Third, the complex and variable sulfation patterns of natural SPs complicate the establishment of clear structure-activity relationships, making it difficult to predict biological outcomes and optimize formulations for specific wound types. Addressing these translational gaps will require not only cost-reduction strategies but also concerted efforts in standardization, rigorous clinical evaluation, and structure-function elucidation.

Looking forward, SP-based biomaterials may evolve toward precision modulation by combining wearable sensors and imaging devices for real-time wound microenvironment monitoring, assisting in timely adjustment of clinical treatment strategies. Integration with data science, artificial intelligence, and 3D bioprinting may achieve comprehensive monitoring-treatment integration.301 In SP synthesis, AI-driven structural design could facilitate targeted synthesis, potentially leading to engineered microorganisms that produce highly active SPs with reduced costs and enhanced safety. Advancing precision wound care will markedly enhance healing rates, mitigate pain, and reduce treatment burdens, though this necessitates multidisciplinary collaboration and collective efforts from diverse researchers and clinicians.302 In parallel with these SP-based strategies, recent advances in peptide-based biomaterials—including self-assembling peptides, peptide hydrogels, and growth factor-mimetic peptides—have also demonstrated considerable promise in diabetic wound healing, representing a complementary approach to the field.244,303–307

Conclusion

In summary, sulfated polysaccharides have evolved from early anticoagulant agents to versatile bioactive materials with significant promise in wound healing. Their therapeutic potential stems from multi-targeting mechanisms that modulate inflammation, promote angiogenesis, and regulate tissue remodeling through interactions with key cellular signaling pathways. The structural tunability of SPs—whether derived from natural sources or obtained through chemical sulfation—enables the design of diverse biomaterial platforms, including hydrogels, microneedles, microspheres, and nanofibers, each offering distinct advantages for wound microenvironments. However, translating these promising preclinical findings into clinical practice faces substantial hurdles, including batch-to-batch variability, high production costs, manufacturing scale-up difficulties, and insufficient standardized safety evaluations. Future progress will depend on interdisciplinary efforts integrating advanced manufacturing, artificial intelligence-assisted design, and rigorous regulatory compliance to overcome these barriers. With continued research and collaboration, SP-based biomaterials hold the potential to become a transformative therapeutic platform for managing chronic and complex wounds.
