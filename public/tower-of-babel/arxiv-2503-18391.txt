Finite-Time Bounds for Two-Time-Scale Stochastic Approximation with Arbitrary Norm Contractions and Markovian Noise
Siddharth Chandak, Shaan Ul Haque, Nicholas Bambos

Abstract
Two-time-scale Stochastic Approximation (SA) is an iterative algorithm with applications in reinforcement learning and optimization. Prior finite-time analyses of such algorithms have focused on fixed point iterations with mappings contractive under the Euclidean norm. Motivated by applications in reinforcement learning, we give the first mean square bound for non-linear two-time-scale SA with arbitrary norm contractive mappings and Markovian noise. We show that the mean square error decays at a rate of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / n 2 / 3 ) \mathcal{O}(1/n^{2/3}) in the general case, and at a rate of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / n ) \mathcal{O}(1/n) in a special case where the slower timescale is noiseless. Our analysis uses the generalized Moreau envelope to handle the arbitrary norm contractions and solutions of Poisson equation to deal with the Markovian noise. By analyzing the SSP Q-Learning algorithm, we give the first 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / n ) \mathcal{O}(1/n) bound for an algorithm for asynchronous control of MDPs under the average reward criterion. We also obtain a rate of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / n ) \mathcal{O}(1/n) for Q-Learning with Polyak-averaging as a special case of our result and provide an algorithm for learning Generalized Nash Equilibrium (GNE) for strongly monotone games which converges at a rate of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / n 2 / 3 ) \mathcal{O}(1/n^{2/3}) .



I
Introduction

Stochastic Approximation (SA) is a popular class of iterative algorithms for finding the fixed point of an operator given its noisy realizations. Many Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms
[
1
]
involve solving fixed point equations under martingale and Markovian noise, and hence can be modeled using SA. Beyond RL, SA algorithms have applications in optimization, communications and control
[
2
]
.

In many scenarios, including RL and optimization, problem structure requires updating iterates at different rates. Some of the well-known examples include GTD and TDC methods for policy evaluation with offline data
[
1
]
, SSP Q-Learning for control problem in average-reward RL
[
4
]
, SA with Polyak-Ruppert Averaging
[
28
]
or solving Generalized Nash Equilibrium Problems
[
29
]
. Broadly, these algorithms are instances of two-time-scale SA which we study in this work. Specifically, we study the following iterations:

\displaystyle x_{n+1}=x_{n}+\alpha_{n}(f(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-x_{n}+M_{n+1})

\displaystyle y_{n+1}=y_{n}+\beta_{n}(g(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-y_{n}+M^{\prime}_{n+1}).

Here
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
are the iterates updated on the faster and slower time-scale, respectively. These time-scales are dictated by the stepsizes
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
, respectively, where
\beta_{n}
decays faster than
\alpha_{n}
.
\{Z_{n}\}
is the underlying Markov chain which leads to the Markovian noise and
M_{n+1}
and
M^{\prime}_{n+1}
denote additive martingale noise. The objective of the algorithm is to find solutions to
\overline{f}(x^{*},y^{*})=x^{*}
and
\overline{g}(x^{*},y^{*})=y^{*}
, where
\overline{f}(x,y)
and
\overline{g}(x,y)
are averages of
f(x,y,i)
and
g(x,y,i)
, respectively, under the stationary distribution
\pi(i)
for the Markov chain
\{Z_{n}\}
.

While asymptotic convergence of SA has been widely studied in SA literature, applications in RL have led to recent interest in obtaining finite-time guarantees on SA algorithms. More specifically, works obtaining expectation bounds on SA algorithms predominantly assume contractions (or some negative drift) under the Euclidean norm. However, many RL algorithms exhibit the contractive property under different norms such as the
\ell_{\infty}
norm. A recent work
[
3
]
used generalized Moreau envelopes to obtain expectation bound for single-time-scale RL algorithms which involve a contraction mapping under arbitrary norms. We extend their result to two-time-scale SA, obtaining the first such finite time bound for RL algorithms such as SSP Q-learning for average cost Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)
[
4
]
.

Finite-time analysis for two-time-scale algorithms often relies on the assumption that that mappings
\overline{f}(x,y)
and
\overline{g}(x^{*}(y),y)
are contractive with respect to
x
and
y
, respectively. This is sufficient to ensure uniqueness of
(x^{*},y^{*})
and asymptotic convergence of the algorithm for appropriate choice of stepsizes. Here
x^{*}(y)
denotes the fixed point of
\overline{f}(\cdot,y)
. While prior works have assumed these contractions under Euclidean norm
[
5
]
, we assume arbitrary norm contractions. To deal with arbitrary norms, we construct smooth Lyapunov functions using the generalized Moreau envelope. In addition, we use solutions of Poisson equation to deal with the Markovian noise. Our mean square bounds require novel analysis to incorporate the above two techniques in the analysis for two-time-scale iterations.

Our main contributions are as follows.

1.

We show that
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{n}-x^{*}(y_{n})\|^{2}+\|y_{n}-y^{*}\|^{2}]
decays as
\mathcal{O}(1/n^{2/3})
. This is the first such bound for two-time-scale SA with arbitrary norm contractions. We also show almost sure convergence of iterates to
(x^{*},y^{*})
.

2.

We also show that the above mean square error decays at
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
when the slower time-scale is noiseless, i.e., lacks both martingale and Markovian noise.

3.

We show that SSP Q-learning for average cost MDPs satisfies the second framework above, and give the first finite time bound of
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
for the algorithm. This is the first
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
bound for an asynchronous algorithm for average cost MDPs. We also present the first a.s. convergence result for this algorithm which does not require projection of iterates in the slower time-scale.

4.

We adapt Q-learning with Polyak averaging into our framework and obtain a rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
for stepsizes independent of the system parameters.

5.

We also present a two-time-scale algorithm for learning GNE in strongly monotone games with linear coupled constraints, achieving a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/n^{2/3})
.

I-A

Related Work

There is a vast literature on SA which focuses on asymptotic analysis of the algorithms (see
[
6
,
2
]
for textbook references). Due to applicability in RL and optimization, there has been a recent interest in bounding the finite-time performance of these algorithms. These works can be further divided into two categories: obtaining high probability bounds on these algorithms (e.g., see
[
7
,
8
]
and references therein) and obtaining mean square bounds
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
. In particular,
[
3
,
12
]
give mean square bounds for RL algorithms with contractions under arbitrary norms in the martingale and Markov noise cases, respectively.

Among two-time-scale SA, there has been special focus on obtaining bounds for linear two-time-scale SA, where the maps
f(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
are linear. This is due to linear two-time-scale RL algorithms such as TDC and GTD2
[
14
]
. In
[
15
]

\mathcal{O}(1/n)
is obtained, while in
[
16
,
17
]
this has been extended to finite-sample guarantees. In
[
18
]
high probability guarantees have been given for such iterations. Note that our results can also be applied on linear two-time-scale SA, but we obtain weaker bounds as we do not exploit the additional structure provided by linearity.

Bounds for non-linear contractive two-time-scale SA have also been recently studied in
[
5
]
, where they obtained a bound of
\mathcal{O}(1/n^{2/3})
for the Euclidean norm contractions case. We extend their result to arbitrary norms and we further include Markov noise and martingale noise which scales linearly in norm with the iterates. These assumptions allow us to capture a broader range of algorithms in our framework.

In recent years, two-time-scale SA has also been studied in cases where the contractive assumption is not satisfied. While the mapping in the faster time-scale is often assumed to be contractive, mappings in the slower time-scale are assumed to be non-expansive in
[
20
]
and the slower time-scale is a gradient descent iteration for a smooth operator in the limit of
x\rightarrow x^{*}(y)
in
[
21
]
. In
[
22
]
, no contractive assumption is made and the rate of convergence of iterates to the solutions of the corresponding ODE is studied.

II
Problem Formulation and Main Result

In this section, we present the main result of this paper after setting up the necessary notation and assumptions. Recall the following coupled iterations.

\displaystyle x_{n+1}=x_{n}+\alpha_{n}(f(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-x_{n}+M_{n+1})

(1)

\displaystyle y_{n+1}=y_{n}+\beta_{n}(g(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-y_{n}+M^{\prime}_{n+1}).

We impose the following assumptions on these iterations, beginning with the required conditions on the Markov noise.

Assumption 1
.

The random process
\{Z_{n}\}
is a Markov chain taking values in a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
and satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{n+1}=j\mid Z_{m},m\leq n)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(Z_{n+1}=j\mid Z_{n})

\displaystyle=p(j\mid Z_{n}),

for
j\in\mathcal{S}
.
p(\cdot|\cdot)
is the transition probability of an irreducible Markov chain with unique stationary distribution
\pi(\cdot)
.

We next define functions
\overline{f}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
\overline{g}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
as the expectation of functions
f(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathcal{S}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
g(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathcal{S}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
with respect to the distribution
\pi
over the state space
\mathcal{S}
.

\overline{f}(x,y)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi(i)f(x,y,i)\;\textrm{and}\;\overline{g}(x,y)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi(i)g(x,y,i).

Note that
f(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-\overline{f}(x_{n},y_{n})
and
g(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-\overline{g}(x_{n},y_{n})
act as the Markovian noise in our framework.

The algorithm’s objective is to find fixed points for
\overline{f}(\cdot)
and
\overline{g}(\cdot)
, i.e., find
(x,y)
such that
\overline{f}(x,y)=x
and
\overline{g}(x,y)=y
. We next assume that
\overline{f}(x,y)
is contractive in
x
.

Assumption 2
.

Function
\overline{f}(x,y)
is
\lambda
-contractive in
x
, i.e., there exists a
\lambda\in[0,1)
such that

\|\overline{f}(x_{1},y)-\overline{f}(x_{2},y)\|\leq\lambda\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|,

(2)

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.

By the Banach contraction mapping theorem, the above assumption implies that
\overline{f}(\cdot,y)
has a unique fixed point for each
y
. That is, for each
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, there exists
x^{*}(y)
such that
\overline{f}(x^{*}(y),y)=x^{*}(y)
. Our next assumption is that the function
\overline{g}(x^{*}(y),y)
is contractive in
y
.

Assumption 3
.

Function
\overline{g}(x^{*}(\cdot),\cdot)
is
\mu
-contractive, i.e., there exists a
\mu\in[0,1)
such that for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.

\|\overline{g}(x^{*}(y_{1}),y_{1})-\overline{g}(x^{*}(y_{2}),y_{2})\|\leq\mu\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|.

(3)

The above assumption implies the unique existence of
y^{*}
such that
g(x^{*}(y^{*}),y^{*})=y^{*}
. For simplicity, we further define
x^{*}(y^{*})=x^{*}
. We next present two assumptions, standard in analysis of SA schemes
[
2
,
7
]
. The first assumption is about the martingale difference sequences.

Assumption 4
.

Define the family of
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma(x_{0},y_{0},Z_{k},M_{k},M^{\prime}_{k},k\leq n)
. Then
\{M_{n+1}\}
and
\{M^{\prime}_{n+1}\}
are martingale difference sequences with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[M^{\prime}_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=0.

Moreover, for
K>0
,

\|M_{n+1}\|+\|M^{\prime}_{n+1}\|\leq K(1+\|x_{n}\|+\|y_{n}\|),\;\forall n\geq 0.

We next assume that that the mappings are Lipschitz.

Assumption 5
.

Functions
f(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
are
L
-Lipschitz, i.e.,

\displaystyle\|f(x_{1},y_{1},i)-f(x_{2},y_{2},i)\|\leq L(\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|+\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|)

\displaystyle\|g(x_{1},y_{1},i)-g(x_{2},y_{2},i)\|\leq L(\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|+\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|),

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
and
i\in\mathcal{S}
. Moreover,

\|f(x,y,i)\|+\|g(x,y,i)\|\leq K(1+\|x\|+\|y\|),

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}},i\in\mathcal{S}
,

We finally make an assumption about the step-size sequences
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
.

Assumption 6
.

The step size sequences are of the form

\alpha_{n}=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{(n+1)^{\mathfrak{a}}}\;\text{and}\;\beta_{n}=\frac{\beta_{0}}{n+1},

where
0<\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}<1
and
0.5<\mathfrak{a}<1
.

This assumption implies that the two sequences are non-increasing, and non-summable but square-summable. We also define the sequence
\gamma_{n}=\beta_{n}/\alpha_{n}
. This further implies that
\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\gamma_{n}=0
.
The above assumption also implies that for constants
\mathfrak{c}_{1}=2
and
\mathfrak{c}_{2}=2/\min(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
we have the following bounds for all
n\geq 0
,

\alpha_{n}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{1}\alpha_{n+1}\;\text{and}\;\beta_{n}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{1}\beta_{n+1},

\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{n+1}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}\alpha_{n+1}^{2}\;\text{and}\;\beta_{n}-\beta_{n+1}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}\beta_{n+1}^{2}.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
-
6
hold. Then the iterates
(x_{n},y_{n})
almost surely converge to the fixed points
(x^{*},y^{*})
. There exists constant
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0
and time
n_{0}\geq 0
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
and
\beta_{0}\geq C_{3}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{n}-x^{*}\|^{2}\right]\leq C_{1}\left(\alpha_{n}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|y_{n}-y^{*}\|^{2}\right]\leq C_{2}\left(\alpha_{n}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right).

For explicit values of
C_{1},C_{2}
and
C_{3}
, refer to the proof in Appendix I.

II-A

Optimal Rate and Step Size Choice

The best possible rate based on our analysis is obtained when
\alpha_{n}=\Theta(\beta_{n}^{2}/\alpha_{n}^{2})
or
\beta_{n}^{2}=\Theta(\alpha_{n}^{3})
. This is achieved at
\mathfrak{a}=2/3
, i.e.,
\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{0}/(n+1)^{2/3}
and
\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}/(n+1)
. This matches the rate obtained when the functions
\overline{f}(\cdot)
and
\overline{g}(\cdot)
are contractive mappings under the Euclidean norm
[
5
]
, and hence we generalize the bound in
[
5
]
to arbitrary norms.

Note that the time
n_{0}
after which our bound holds is a fixed constant depending on the step sizes, contraction factors, and the norm
\|\cdot\|
. By taking assumptions similar to Assumption A2 from
[
17
]
, our result can be shown to hold for all time
n\geq 0
.

III
An Important Special Case

The formulation in Section
II
allows for martingale and Markov noises in both time-scales. While this allows us to cover a broader set of algorithms, the presence of the noise terms in the slower time-scale restricts the range of step size sequences our analysis can allow. As we discuss later in Section
V
, both SSP Q-Learning and Q-Learning with Polyak averaging are noiseless in the slower time-scale. Hence, in this section, we consider this special case and give a bound of
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
for such algorithms. Consider the following coupled iterations.

\begin{gathered}x_{n+1}=x_{n}+\alpha_{n}(f(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-x_{n}+M_{n+1})\\
y_{n+1}=y_{n}+\beta_{n}(\overline{g}(x_{n},y_{n})-y_{n}).\end{gathered}

(4)

Note that this is a special case of
(
1
)
as we can define
M^{\prime}_{n+1}=0
for all
n
and
g(x,y,i)=\overline{g}(x,y)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
and
i\in\mathcal{S}
. The assumptions of contractivity (Assumption
2
and
3
) are still satisfied, i.e.,
\overline{f}(x,y)
and
\overline{g}(x^{*}(y),y)
are
\lambda
-contractive in
x
and
\mu
-contractive, respectively. Similarly Assumptions
4
and
5
are also satisfied. Then, we have the following result.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
5
hold, and let
\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{0}/(n+1)
and
\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}/(n+1)
. For iterates generated by (
4
), there exist constants
C_{4},C_{5},C_{6}
and time
n_{0}
such that if
\beta_{0}/\alpha_{0}\leq C_{4}<1
and
\beta_{0}\geq C_{5}
, then for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}[\|x_{n}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\|y_{n}-y^{*}\|^{2}]\leq\frac{C_{6}}{n+1}.

The proof for this theorem differs only slightly from the proof for Theorem
1
. The constants have been computed explicitly in the proof presented in Appendix II.

Note that we can drop the assumptions
\beta_{0}/\alpha_{0}\leq C_{4}
if we choose
\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{0}/(n+1)^{1-\epsilon}
for
\epsilon\in(0,0.5)
. In that case it can be shown that
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{n}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\|y_{n}-y^{*}\|^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(1/(n+1)^{1-\epsilon})
. Here
\epsilon>0
can be arbitrarily small, giving us a bound which can be made arbitrarily close to
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
.

IV
Proof Outline for Theorem
1

In this section, we present a proof sketch for Theorem
1
through a series of lemmas. The complete proof has been presented in Appendix I. The proof takes inspiration from the analysis of non-linear two-time-scale iterations which are contractive under Euclidean norm
[
5
]
. But the presence of arbitrary norm contractions and Markov noise require additional tools which we discuss first.

IV-A

Moreau Envelopes

The convergence rate analysis of SA by constructing smooth Lyapunov functions of the iterates. Consider a single-time-scale iteration
x_{n+1}=x_{n}+\alpha_{n}(h(x_{n})-M_{n+1})
. For such an iteration,
\|x_{n}-x^{*}\|^{2}
acts as the Lyapunov function where
\|\cdot\|
is the norm with respect to which
h(x)
is contractive. Additionally, when this Lyapunov function is smooth with respect to some norm, we can obtain a finite-time bound for the algorithm. But for arbitrary norms (such as
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
),
\|x_{n}-x^{*}\|^{2}
is not guaranteed to be smooth. In such cases, we need to define a smooth Lyapunov function.

We first define the functions needed for our two-time-scale case and then verify that they satisfy the required properties.

Definition 1
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
and
q>0
define functions
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{B}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(x)

\displaystyle=\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|v\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2q}\|x-v\|_{2}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(y)

\displaystyle=\inf_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2q}\|y-w\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

Here
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the
\ell_{2}
norm.

Note that the dependence on
q
has been rendered implicit in the definitions of
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{B}(\cdot)
. We treat
q
as a constant and we show in the proof for Lemma
3
that choosing a small enough
q
is sufficient for our proof. The choice of
q
only affects the constants in Theorems
1
and
2
.

Due to the norm equivalence in finite-dimensional real spaces, there exists
\ell_{d_{1}}\in(0,1]
and
u_{d_{1}}\in[1,\infty)
such that
\ell_{d_{1}}\|x\|\leq\|x\|_{2}\leq u_{d_{1}}\|x\|
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
. We next reproduce a result from
[
3
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Function
\mathcal{A}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
satisfies the following.

(a)

\mathcal{A}
is convex and
1/q
smooth w.r.t. the
\ell_{2}
norm, i.e.,

\mathcal{A}(x_{2})\leq\mathcal{A}(x_{1})+\langle\nabla\mathcal{A}(x_{1}),x_{2}-x_{1}\rangle_{2}+\frac{1}{2q}\|x_{2}-x_{1}\|^{2}_{2},

holds true for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
.

(b)

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
,

\left(1+\frac{q}{u_{d_{1}}^{2}}\right)\mathcal{A}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}\leq\left(1+\frac{q}{\ell_{d_{1}}^{2}}\right)\mathcal{A}(x).

(c)

There exists a norm, denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A}}
, such that
\mathcal{A}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
.

For sufficiently small
q
, the first two parts of the lemma imply that
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
is a smooth approximation for the function
(1/2)\|\cdot\|^{2}
. The final part implies that
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
is in fact a scaled squared norm. Similar lemma for
\mathcal{B}(\cdot)
and other required results have been presented in Appendix I-A.

IV-B

Solutions of Poisson Equation

Recall that the fixed point and the contractive properties are defined with respect to the functions
\overline{f}(\cdot)
and
\overline{g}(\cdot)
. But the corresponding iterations contain
f(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
, respectively. Hence the terms
f(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-\overline{f}(x_{n},y_{n})
and
g(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-\overline{g}(x_{n},y_{n})
act as the Markovian noise in our framework. To handle this error term, we define the following solutions of Poisson equation.

Definition 2
.

For a fixed
i_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
,
\tau\coloneqq\min\{n>0:\!Z_{n}=i_{0}\}
and
E_{i}[\cdot]=E[\cdot|Z_{0}=i]
, define
V(x,y,i)
and
W(x,y,i)
as

\displaystyle V(x,y,i)

\displaystyle=E_{i}\left[\sum_{m=0}^{\tau-1}\Big(f(x,y,Z_{m})-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\pi(j)f(x,y,j)\Big)\right],

\displaystyle W(x,y,i)

\displaystyle=E_{i}\left[\sum_{m=0}^{\tau-1}\Big(g(x,y,Z_{m})-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\pi(j)g(x,y,j)\Big)\right],

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
and
i\in\mathcal{S}
.

For each
x,y
,
V(x,y,i)
is a solution to the following Poisson equation
[
23
]
, i.e.,
V(x,y,i)
satisfies

\displaystyle V(x,y,i)

\displaystyle=f(x,y,i)-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\pi(j)f(x,y,j)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}p(j|i)V(x,y,j).

Then the ‘error’
f(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-\overline{f}(x_{n},y_{n})
can be written as

\displaystyle f(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-\overline{f}(x_{n},y_{n})

\displaystyle=V(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}p(j|Z_{n})V(x_{n},y_{n},j)

\displaystyle=V(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n+1})-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}p(j|Z_{n})V(x_{n},y_{n},j)

\displaystyle\;\;\;+V(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})-V(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n+1}).

For the first term, we define
{\tilde{V}_{n+1}}=V(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n+1})-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}p(j|Z_{n})V(x_{n},y_{n},j)
. Note that
\{\tilde{V}_{n+1}\}
is a martingale difference sequence with respect to filtration
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, and is combined with
M_{n+1}
. The second term is appropriately combined with other terms to create a telescopic series. Hence the above definition allows us to express the Markov noise in terms of a martingale difference sequence.

We present the analogous Poisson equation for
W(\cdot)
along with the Lipschitz and linear growth properties for
V(\cdot)
and
W(\cdot)
in Appendix I-B.

IV-C

Proof Sketch

We next present a series of lemmas which together complete the proof for Theorem
1
. The first step in the proof is to show that
x^{*}(y)
is Lipschitz in
y
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
2
and
5
hold. Then the map
y\mapsto x^{*}(y)
is Lipschitz with parameter
L_{1}\coloneqq L/(1-\lambda)
, i.e.,

\|x^{*}(y_{1})-x^{*}(y_{2})\|\leq L_{1}\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|,

for
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.

The above lemma ensures that the target points for the faster time-scale iteration,
x^{*}(y_{n})
, move slowly as the iterates
y_{n}
move slowly. The next step is to derive intermediate recursive bounds on
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(x_{n}-x^{*}(y_{n}))]
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}(y_{n}-y^{*})]
.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose the setting of Theorem
1
holds. Then, for constants
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3}>0
, the following hold for all
n\geq 0
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(x_{n+1}-x^{*}(y_{n+1}))]\leq(1-\alpha_{n}\lambda^{\prime})\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(x_{n}-x^{*}(y_{n}))]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha_{n}(d_{n}-d_{n+1})\right]+\Gamma_{1}\left(\alpha_{n}^{2}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}}\right)\times

\displaystyle\left(1+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(x_{n}-x^{*}(y_{n}))+\mathcal{B}(y_{n}-y^{*})]\right).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}(y_{n+1}-y^{*})]\leq(1-\beta_{n}\mu^{\prime})\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}(y_{n}-y^{*})]

\displaystyle+\beta_{n}\mathbb{E}[(e_{n}-e_{n+1})]+\beta_{n}\Gamma_{3}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(x_{n}-x^{*}(y_{n}))]

\displaystyle+\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}\Gamma_{2}(1+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(x_{n}-x^{*}(y_{n}))+\mathcal{B}(y_{n}-y^{*})]).

Here
d_{n}=\langle\mathcal{A}(x_{n}-x^{*}(y_{n})),V(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})\rangle_{2}
, and
e_{n}=\langle\mathcal{B}(y_{n}-y^{*}),W(x_{n},y_{n},Z_{n})\rangle_{2}
.

Here the terms
\alpha_{n}(d_{n}-d_{n+1})
and
\beta_{n}(e_{n}-e_{n+1})
correspond to the telescoping series arising due to the Poisson equation solution approach to Markov noise. We add the two intermediate bounds above, and show that the iterates are bounded in expectation.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose the setting of Theorem
1
holds. Then the iterates
x_{k}
and
y_{k}
are bounded in expectation. Specifically, there exists constant
\Gamma_{4}>0
such that for all
k\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(x_{n}-x^{*}(y_{n}))+\mathcal{B}(y_{n}-y^{*})]\leq\Gamma_{4}.

The above lemmas are sufficient to show that the iterates
x_{n},y_{n}
almost surely converge to the fixed point using the ODE approach to analysis of SA schemes. A requirement for the ODE approach is that the iterates should be almost surely finite. This is guaranteed by the above lemma as bounded expectation implies almost sure finiteness of iterates. We substitute the above bound on iterates into the intermediate bounds in Lemma
3
to obtain the bounds on
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(x_{n}-x^{*}(y_{n}))]
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}(y_{n}-y^{*})]
.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose the setting of Theorem
1
holds. Then,

(a)

The iterates
(x_{n},y_{n})
almost surely converge to the fixed points
(x^{*},y^{*})
.

(b)

There exists
n_{1}\geq 0
and constant
\Gamma_{5}>0
such that for all
n\geq n_{1}
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(x_{n}-x^{*}(y_{n}))]\leq\Gamma_{5}\left(\alpha_{n}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right).

(c)

Assume that
\beta_{0}\geq C_{3}
as in Theorem
1
. Then, there exists
n_{2}\geq 0
and constant
\Gamma_{6}>0
such that for all
n\geq n_{2}
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}(y_{n}-y^{*})]\leq\Gamma_{6}\left(\alpha_{n}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right).

Using properties of Moreau envelopes and combining the above two bounds completes the proof for Theorem
1
.

V-A

SSP Q-Learning for Average Cost MDPs

In this subsection, we apply our theorems to asynchronous SSP Q-Learning, a two time-scale stochastic approximation algorithm applied to the average cost problem
[
4
,
24
,
25
]
. Consider a controlled Markov chain
\{X_{n}\}
on a finite state space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
,
|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|=\mathfrak{s}
, controlled by a control process
\{U_{n}\}
in a finite action space
\mathcal{U}
,
|\mathcal{U}|=\mathfrak{u}
. The controlled transition probability function
(i,j,u)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime 2}\times\mathcal{U}\mapsto p(j|i,u)\in[0,1]
satisfies
\sum_{j}p(j|i,u)=1\ \forall\;i,u.

Thus

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=j|X_{m},U_{m},m\leq n)=p(j|X_{n},U_{n}),\ a.s.

We assume that the graph of the Markov chain remains irreducible under all control choices. Let
k:S\times U\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a prescribed ‘running cost’ function. The objective is to choose actions
U_{n}
so as to minimize the average cost

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[k(X_{m},U_{m})].

(5)

Let
i_{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
be some fixed state, referred to as the reference state. Then the SSP Q-learning algorithm is:

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(i,u)=Q_{n}(i,u)+\alpha_{n}I\{X_{n}=i,U_{n}=u\}\Big(k(i,u)

\displaystyle+I\{X_{n+1}\neq i_{0}\}\min_{v}Q_{n}(X_{n+1},v)-Q_{n}(i,u)-\rho_{n}\Big),

(6a)

\displaystyle\rho_{n+1}=\rho_{n}+\beta^{\prime}_{n}(\min_{v}Q_{n}(i_{0},v)).

(6b)

Here
Q_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}},\rho_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
with any arbitrary
Q_{0}(\cdot,\cdot)\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}
.
I\{\cdot\}
denotes the indicator function, and
I\{X_{n}=i,Z_{n}=u\}
stems from the asynchronous update, i.e., at any time
n
, Q-values are updated only for the current state, action pair
\{X_{n},U_{n}\}
. Note that unlike
[
24
,
4
]
we do not use the projection operator on the iteration for
\rho_{n}
.

We study the offline SSP Q-Learning algorithm, where the
U_{n}
are decided by a randomized sampling policy
\Phi_{s}(u\mid i)
independent of
Q_{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(U_{n}=u\mid X_{m},Q_{m},U_{k},m\leq n,k<n)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;=\Phi_{s}(u|X_{n}),u\in\mathcal{U},n\geq 0.

We further assume that
\Phi_{s}(u|i)>0
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
and
i\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. For the purpose of applying our Theorem
1
, we define
Z_{n}=(X_{n},U_{n})
, i.e., the Markov chain in our formulation will be given by the controlled Markov chain
X_{n}
and the control
U_{n}
together and the state space is
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{U}
. The transition probabilities are then given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{n+1}=(i,u)\mid Z_{n})=p(i|X_{n},U_{n})\Phi_{s}(u|i).

The stationary distribution for
\{Z_{n}\}
is given by
\pi(i,u)=\pi_{\Phi}(i)\Phi(u|i)
. Here
\pi_{\Phi}(\cdot)
denotes the stationary distribution of the controlled Markov chain
\{X_{n}\}
under control
\Phi_{s}(\cdot|\cdot)
.

We first rewrite the iterations (
6
) in the form of (
4
).

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(i,u)

\displaystyle=Q_{n}(i,u)+\alpha_{n}(f^{i,u}(Q_{n},\rho_{n},Z_{n})

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ -Q_{n}(i,u)+M_{n+1}(i,u))

\displaystyle\rho_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\rho_{n}+\beta_{n}(\overline{g}(Q_{n},\rho_{n})-\rho_{n}).

Here,

\displaystyle f^{i,u}(Q,\rho,Z)=I\{Z=(i,u)\}\Big(k(i,u)-Q(i,u)-\rho

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ +\sum_{j\neq i_{0}}p(j|i,u)\min_{v}Q(j,v)\Big)+Q(i,u),

\displaystyle\overline{g}(Q,\rho)=\beta^{\prime}\min_{v}Q(i_{0},v)+\rho,

\displaystyle M_{n+1}^{i,u}=I\{Z_{n}=(i,u)\}\Big(I\{X_{n+1}\neq i_{0}\}\times

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \min_{v}Q_{n}(X_{n+1},v)-\sum_{j\neq i_{0}}p(j|i,u)\min_{v}Q(j,v)\Big).

(7)

Note that
\beta_{n}=\beta^{\prime}_{n}/\beta^{\prime}
. Here
\beta^{\prime}>0
is a sufficiently small constant, used in Proposition
1
. Recall that
\overline{f}(Q,\rho)=\sum_{(i,u)}\pi(i,u)f(Q,\rho,(i,u))
. Note that this algorithm is noiseless in the slower time-scale and hence, the formulation from Section
III
can be applied here.

Our following proposition details the contractivity and fixed point properties of functions
\overline{f}(\cdot)
and
\overline{g}(\cdot)
. For this, define the norm
\|\cdot\|_{w}
as the weighted max-norm. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\|x\|_{w}=\max_{j}|w_{j}x_{j}|
for positive
w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}
.

Proposition 1
.

Functions
f(\cdot)
and
\overline{g}(\cdot)
defined in (
7
) satisfy the following.

(a)

The function
\overline{f}(Q,\rho)
is contractive in
Q
under norm
\|\cdot\|_{w}
for some
w
, i.e., there exists some
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}}
such that
\|\overline{f}(Q_{1},\rho)-\overline{f}(Q_{2},\rho)\|_{w}\leq\lambda\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}\|_{w}
for all
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

Let
Q^{*}(\rho)
denote the fixed point for
\overline{f}(\cdot,\rho)
. Then, for sufficiently small
\beta^{\prime}
, there exists
\mu\in(0,1)
such that
|\overline{g}(Q^{*}(\rho_{2}),\rho_{2})-\overline{g}(Q^{*}(\rho_{1}),\rho_{1})|\leq\mu|\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}|
.

(c)

The fixed point
\rho^{*}
for
\overline{g}(Q^{*}(\cdot),\cdot)
is the minimum of the average cost defined in (
5
) and
Q^{*}=Q^{*}(\rho^{*})
is the vector of Q-values associated with the optimal policy.

We finally present the main result for SSP Q-Learning.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{0}/(n+1)
and
\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}/(n+1)
. Then there exist constants
C_{4}^{\prime},C_{5}^{\prime},n_{0}^{\prime}
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}}
such that if
\beta_{0}/\alpha_{0}\leq C_{4}^{\prime}
and
\beta_{0}\geq C_{5}^{\prime}
then for all
n\geq n_{0}^{\prime}

\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{n}-Q^{*}\|^{2}_{w}]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\right)

and

\mathbb{E}[|\rho_{n}-\rho^{*}|^{2}]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\right).

The proofs for the above proposition and corollary have been presented in Appendix III-A. By application of Theorem
1
, we can also show almost sure convergence of iterates
(Q_{n},\rho_{n})
to fixed points
(Q^{*},\rho^{*})
when
\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{0}/(n+1)^{1-\epsilon}
where
\epsilon\in(0,0.5)
.

V-B

Discounted-Reward Q-Learning with Polyak-Averaging

Define the controlled Markov chain
\{X_{n}\}
, the control
\{U_{n}\}
, the transition function
p(\cdot|\cdot,\cdot)
and the cost
k(\cdot,\cdot)
as in SSP Q-Learning. In the discounted reward setting, the objective is to minimize the discounted cost

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\gamma^{m}k(X_{m},U_{m})],

where
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor. One of the popularly used algorithms to find the optimal policy is Q-Learning, proposed by
[
26
]
. In order to improve the statistical efficiency and the rate of convergence, an additional step of iterate averaging is used, called Polyak-Ruppert averaging
[
27
,
28
]
. The combined iteration is given as

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(i,u)

\displaystyle=Q_{n}(i,u)+\alpha_{n}I\{X_{n}=i,U_{n}=u\}\Big(k(i,u)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ +\gamma\min_{v}Q_{n}(X_{k+1},v)-Q_{n}(i,u)\Big)

(8a)

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{n+1}(i,u)

\displaystyle=\bar{Q}_{n}(i,u)+\frac{\beta_{0}}{n+1}\left(Q_{n}(i,u)-\bar{Q}_{n}(i,u)\right)

(8b)

where
\{(X_{n},U_{n})\}_{n\geq 0}
is a sample trajectory collected using a sampling policy
\Phi_{s}(u|i)
. Note that the above iterations can be seen as a special case of two-time-scale SA where the slower time-scale iterate,
\bar{Q}_{n}
, does not affect the faster time-scale iterate,
Q_{n}
. Assuming
\Phi_{s}(u|i)>0
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
and
i\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, we again construct the Markov chain
Z_{n}=(X_{n},U_{n})
whose transition probability is given by
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n+1}=(i,u)\mid Z_{n})=p(i|X_{n},Z_{n})\Phi_{s}(u|i)
. Let
\pi(i,u)
denote the stationary distribution for the Markov chain
\{Z_{n}\}
as in SSP Q-Learning. Rearranging the iterations in (
8
), we get

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(i,u)

\displaystyle=Q_{n}(i,u)+\alpha_{n}(f^{i,u}(Q_{n},Z_{n})

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ -Q_{n}(i,u)+M_{n+1}^{i,u}

(9a)

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\bar{Q}_{n}+\beta_{n}(\overline{g}(Q_{n},\bar{Q}_{n})-\bar{Q}_{n}(i,u)).

(9b)

Here,

\displaystyle f^{i,u}(Q,\bar{Q},Z)=I\{Z=(i,u)\}\Big(k(i,u)-Q(i,u)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ +\gamma\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}p(j|i,u)\min_{v}Q(j,v)\Big)+Q(i,u),

\displaystyle\overline{g}(Q,\bar{Q})=Q,

\displaystyle M_{n+1}^{i,u}=\gamma I\{Z_{n}=(i,u)\}\Big(Q_{n}(X_{n+1},v)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ -\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}p(j|i,u)\min_{v}Q(j,v)\Big),

and
\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}/(n+1)
. As the slower time-scale is noiseless, we apply the formulation from Section
III
here. Since
\bar{Q}
does not affect the faster time-scale, the fixed point for
\overline{f}(\cdot,\bar{Q})
,
Q^{*}(\bar{Q})
, is independent of
\bar{Q}
. Let
Q^{*}
denote this fixed point, which is the vector of Q-values corresponding to the optimal policy, and satisfies the following for all
i\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime},u\in\mathcal{U}
.

Q^{*}(i,u)=k(i,u)+\gamma\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}p(j|i,u)\min_{v\in\mathcal{U}}Q^{*}(j,v).

(10)

Note that
\overline{g}(Q,\bar{Q})=Q
, and hence
\overline{g}(Q^{*}(\bar{Q}),\bar{Q})=Q^{*}(\bar{Q})=Q^{*}
. Using Section 5.1 from
[
7
]
, we have that
\overline{f}(Q,\bar{Q})
is contractive in
Q
under the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
with contraction factor
\lambda=1-\pi_{min}(1-\gamma)
where
\pi_{min}=\min_{i,u}\pi(i,u)
. On the other hand, the contraction factor for
\overline{g}(Q^{*}(\cdot),\cdot)
is simply zero.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{0}/(n+1)
and
\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}/(n+1)
. Then there exist constants
C_{4}^{\prime\prime}
and
n_{0}^{\prime\prime}
such that if
\beta_{0}/\alpha_{0}\leq C_{4}^{\prime\prime}
and
\beta_{0}\geq 8
then, for all
n\geq n_{0}^{\prime\prime}

\mathbb{E}[\|\bar{Q}_{n}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}^{2}]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\right).

The proof for the above corollary has been presented in Appendix III-B. Similar to SSP Q-Learning, almost sure convergence of
Q_{n}
and
\bar{Q}_{n}
to
Q^{*}
can be shown when
\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{0}/(n+1)^{1-\epsilon}
where
\epsilon\in(0,0.5)
.

Remark 1
.

In the original Polyak-Ruppert averaging,
\beta_{0}
corresponds to
1
which is equivalent to the time averaging of iterates. One of the main objectives of this averaging step is to achieve
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
rate of convergence without requiring knowledge of system parameters to tune the step-size (see Corollary 2.1.2 in
[
12
]
). While the only requirement on
\beta_{0}
is that
\beta_{0}\geq 8
which is independent of the system parameters,
\alpha_{0}
needs to be chosen according to
\alpha_{0}\geq\beta_{0}/C_{4}^{\prime\prime}
, which requires knowledge of the properties of the Markov chain.
At the expense of a larger
n_{0}^{\prime\prime}
, one can modify our proof to allow for
\beta_{0}
arbitrarily close to 1. An interesting future direction is showing the optimal rate of convergence for Polyak-Ruppert averaging of nonlinear stochastic approximation with Markovian noise.

V-C

Learning GNE in Strongly Monotone Games

Consider a set of
K
players
\{1,\ldots,K\}
. Each player
k
takes action
x^{(k)}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at each time
n
. We use
\mathbf{x}_{n}=(x^{(1)}_{n},\ldots,x^{(K)}_{n})
to denote the
Kd
dimensional concatenation of all players’ actions. Each player
k
receives utility
u_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{n})
which is a function of all players’ actions. The players want to converge to the Nash equilibrium under
c
linear constraints
A\mathbf{x}=b
where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times Kd}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{c}
. Note that these constraints are coupled across players’ actions. This is known as the Generalized Nash Equilibrium Problem (GNEP)
[
29
]
. Efficient computation of GNEs has been studied in the noiseless setting (e.g.,
[
30
]
,
[
31
]
). Learning of GNEs for stochastic games has been studied in settings where the players share information about their actions or rewards with each other
[
32
,
33
]
. The problem of learning GNE has also been formulated in the setting of game control
[
34
]
.

We study learning of GNE in the stochastic and distributed setting. At each timestep, player
k
observes a noisy gradient of their utility, i.e.,
\nabla_{x^{(k)}}u_{k}(\mathbf{x})+M^{(k)}_{n+1}
and the linear constraint violation
A\mathbf{x}-b+M^{\prime}_{n+1}
, which is common for all players. Note that there is no Markovian noise in this setting. Before making assumptions on the structure of the game, we define the gradient operator

F(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq(\nabla_{x^{(1)}}u_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,\nabla_{x^{(K)}}u_{K}(\mathbf{x})).

We make the following assumption on the game:

Assumption 7
.

The game is strongly monotone, i.e., there exists
\lambda_{0}
such that for all
\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd}

\langle\mathbf{x}_{2}-\mathbf{x}_{1},F(\mathbf{x}_{2})-F(\mathbf{x}_{1})\rangle\leq-\lambda_{0}\|\mathbf{x}_{2}-\mathbf{x}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}.

Additionally, we assume that
F(\cdot)
is
\ell-
Lipschitz.

Being a strongly monotone game, the players can converge to the Nash equilibrium by just gradient play, i.e., by performing gradient ascent on their utilities. To satisfy the additional coupled linear constraints, each player
k
performs the following two-time-scale iteration.

\displaystyle x^{(k)}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=x^{(k)}_{n}+\alpha^{\prime}_{n}(\nabla_{x^{(k)}}u_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{n})+M^{(k)}_{n+1}-B^{(k)}y_{n})

(11)

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=y_{n}+\beta^{\prime}_{n}(A\mathbf{x}_{n}-b+M^{\prime}_{n+1}).

Here
B=A^{T}
and
B^{(n)}
denotes the row relevant to player
k
in the matrix
A^{T}
and
y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{c}
. Note that the above two-time-scale iteration can be thought of as Lagrangian optimization under the constraint
A\mathbf{x}=b
where
y_{n}
acts as the Lagrange multiplier. Combining iteration (
11
) for all players, we can write the above algorithm as the following set of iterations.

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{n}+\alpha^{\prime}_{n}(F(\mathbf{x}_{n})-By_{n}+M_{n+1})

(12)

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=y_{n}+\beta^{\prime}_{n}(A\mathbf{x}_{n}-b+M^{\prime}_{n+1}).

Note that the algorithm is still distributed and (
12
) is just a concise method of representing the iterations. Similar to the other applications, we rearrange these coupled iterations.

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{n}+\alpha_{n}(f(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n})-\mathbf{x}_{n}+M^{\prime\prime}_{n+1})

(13)

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=y_{n}+\beta_{n}(g(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n})-y_{n}+M^{\prime\prime\prime}_{n+1}).

Here,
\alpha_{n}=\alpha^{\prime}_{n}/\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta_{n}=\beta^{\prime}_{n}/\beta^{\prime}
are scaled stepsize sequences and
M^{\prime\prime}_{n+1}=\alpha^{\prime}M_{n+1}
and
M^{\prime\prime\prime}_{n+1}=\beta^{\prime}M^{\prime}_{n+1}
are scaled martingale noise sequences. Note that
f(\mathbf{x},y)=\mathbf{x}+\alpha^{\prime}F(\mathbf{x})-\alpha^{\prime}By
and
g(\mathbf{x},y)=y+\beta^{\prime}A\mathbf{x}-\beta^{\prime}b
.

Let
\mathbf{x}^{*}(y)
denote the unique solution to
F(\mathbf{x})=By
(i.e.,
F(\mathbf{x}^{*}(y))=By
). This is the Nash equilibrium corresponding to parameter
y
. The existence and uniqueness of this Nash equilibrium is guaranteed by Proposition
2
below. Our following proposition states the required contraction and fixed point properties of above mappings.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that matrix
A
has full row rank and that Assumption
7
holds. Then,

a)

For sufficiently small
\alpha^{\prime}
, there exists
0<\lambda<1
such that
f(\mathbf{x},y)
is
\lambda
-contractive in
\mathbf{x}
under the
\ell_{2}
norm.

b)

For sufficiently small
\beta^{\prime}
, there exists
0<\mu<1
such that
g(\mathbf{x}^{*}(y),y)
is
\mu
-contractive under the
\ell_{2}
norm.

c)

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we obtained finite-time bounds for two-time-scale SA where each time scale is a fixed point iteration, and the fixed point mapping is contractive with respect to arbitrary norms. We additionally incorporate Markovian noise into our framework to allow for analysis of RL algorithms. We show that the mean square error decays at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/n^{2/3})
. Our analysis uses Moreau envelopes and solutions of Poisson equations to deal with arbitrary norm contractions and Markovian noise, respectively. We further note that two-time-scale algorithms such as SSP Q-learning for average cost MDPs and Q-learning with Polyak averaging lack noise in the slower time-scale allowing for better convergence rates of
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
.

Future directions include obtaining
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
bounds in the general case where both time-scales have noise. A possible approach is to extend the noise-averaging technique of
[
36
]
to arbitrary norm contractions and Markov noise. An open problem in non-linear two-time-scale SA is whether a finite-time mean-square bound of
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
can be established when the time-scales are truly separated, i.e., when
\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}\beta_{k}/\alpha_{k}=0
. Another promising direction is to establish concentration bounds for two-time-scale iterations, which could yield further insight into their behavior.

Lemma 10
.

Let assumption
6
hold for the step sizes
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
. Then, we have the following bounds

1.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}[d_{i}-d_{i+1}]\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})\leq\alpha_{0}\mathbb{E}[|d_{0}|]\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})+\mathfrak{c}_{1}\alpha_{n}\mathbb{E}[|d_{n}|]+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[|d_{i}|](\mathfrak{c}_{1}+\mathfrak{c}_{2})\alpha_{i}^{2}\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime}).

2.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\beta_{i}\mathbb{E}[e_{i}-e_{i+1}]\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\beta_{j}\mu^{\prime})\leq\beta_{0}\mathbb{E}[|e_{0}|]\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(1-\beta_{j}\mu^{\prime})+\mathfrak{c}_{1}\beta_{n}\mathbb{E}[|e_{n}|]+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[|e_{i}|](\mathfrak{c}_{1}+\mathfrak{c}_{2}^{\prime})\beta_{i}^{2}\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\beta_{j}\mu^{\prime}).

3.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\beta_{i}\mathbb{E}[d_{i}-d_{i+1}]\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{j}\mu^{\prime}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{0}\mathbb{E}[|d_{0}|]\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{j}\mu^{\prime}}{4}\right)+\mathfrak{c}_{1}\beta_{n}\mathbb{E}[|d_{n}|]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[|d_{i}|](\mathfrak{c}_{1}+\mathfrak{c}_{2}^{\prime})\beta_{i}^{2}\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{j}\mu^{\prime}}{4}\right).

Proof.

Since the proof for all the claims are identical, we only present the proof for the first bound. For this, note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}[d_{i}-d_{i+1}]\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}\mathbb{E}[d_{0}]\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})-\alpha_{n-1}\mathbb{E}[d_{n}]

\displaystyle\;\;\;+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(-\alpha_{i-1}\mathbb{E}[d_{i}]\prod_{j=i}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})+\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}[d_{i}]\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})\right).

For the last term here, we note that

\displaystyle-\alpha_{i-1}\mathbb{E}[d_{i}]\prod_{j=i}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})+\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}[d_{i}]\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}[d_{i}]-\alpha_{i-1}\mathbb{E}[d_{i}](1-\alpha_{i}\lambda^{\prime}))\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[d_{i}]\Big((\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i-1})+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{i-1}\lambda^{\prime}\Big)\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[|d_{i}|](\mathfrak{c}_{1}+\mathfrak{c}_{2})\alpha_{i}^{2}\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime}).

Combining the above relation and using
\mathbb{E}[X]\leq\mathbb{E}[|X|]
for any scalar random variable, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}[d_{i}-d_{i+1}]\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{0}\mathbb{E}[|d_{0}|]\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})+\mathfrak{c}_{1}\alpha_{n}\mathbb{E}[|d_{n}|]+\mathbb{E}[|d_{i}|](\mathfrak{c}_{1}+\mathfrak{c}_{2})\alpha_{i}^{2}\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime}).

Lemma 11
.

Let assumption
6
hold for the step sizes
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
. Then, we have the following bounds.

1.

Define
n_{I}=\max(2[\mathfrak{a}/(\lambda^{\prime}\alpha_{0})]^{1/(1-\mathfrak{a})},2[(2-2\mathfrak{a})/(\lambda^{\prime}\beta_{0}^{2})]^{1/(2\mathfrak{a}-1)})
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(\alpha_{i}^{2}+\frac{\beta_{i}^{2}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})\leq\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{n}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right)\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall n\geq n_{I}.

2.

Assume that
\beta_{0}\geq\max(2\mathfrak{a}/\mu^{\prime},4(1-\mathfrak{a})/\mu^{\prime})
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\Big(\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}+\frac{\beta_{i}^{3}}{\alpha_{i}^{2}}\Big)\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\beta_{j}\mu^{\prime})\leq\frac{2}{\mu^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{n}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right)\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall n\geq 0.

3.

Assume that
\beta_{0}\geq 8/\mu^{\prime}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\beta_{i}^{2}\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{j}\mu^{\prime}}{4}\right)\leq\frac{8\beta_{n}}{\mu}.

4.

For any constant
\kappa>0

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{i}\kappa)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[-\frac{\kappa\alpha_{0}}{1-\mathfrak{a}}\left((n+1)^{1-\mathfrak{a}}-1\right)\right]\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall n\geq 0.

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(1-\beta_{i}\kappa)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\right)^{\kappa\beta_{0}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall n\geq 0.

Proof.

1.

We will use a similar proof technique as in
[
3
]
. Define the sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
as follows

\displaystyle u_{0}=0;\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ u_{n+1}=(1-\alpha_{n}\lambda^{\prime})u_{n}+\left(\alpha_{n}^{2}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}}\right).

Then, it is easy to verify that
u_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(\alpha_{i}^{2}+\frac{\beta_{i}^{2}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)\prod_{j=i+1}^{n-1}(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\prime})
. We will use induction to show that for
n\geq n_{I}
,
u_{n}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{n}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right)
. Since
u_{0}=0
, the base case is easily verified. Let us assume that the bound holds for some
n
. Then for
n+1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{n+1}+\frac{\beta_{n+1}^{2}}{\alpha_{n+1}^{2}}\right)-u_{n+1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{n+1}+\frac{\beta_{n+1}^{2}}{\alpha_{n+1}^{2}}\right)-(1-\alpha_{n}\lambda^{\prime})\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{n}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right)-\left(\alpha_{n}^{2}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}\alpha_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{\alpha_{n+1}-\alpha_{n}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}+\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}}\left(\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\beta_{n}^{2}}\left(\frac{\beta_{n+1}^{2}}{\alpha_{n+1}^{2}}-\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right)+\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}\right)

Note that for any
\xi\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n+2)^{\xi}}-\frac{1}{(n+1)^{\xi}}=\frac{1}{(n+1)^{\xi}}\left(\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{n+1}\right)^{n+1}\right]^{-\frac{\xi}{n+1}}-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{\xi}}\left(\exp{{}^{-\frac{\xi}{n+1}}}-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{(n+1)^{\xi}}\frac{\xi}{n+1}

where we used
\left(1+\frac{1}{x}\right)^{x}<e
and
e^{x}\geq 1+x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Using this relation for
\xi=\mathfrak{a}
and
\xi=2-2\mathfrak{a}
for the first and second term, respectively, we get

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{n+1}+\frac{\beta_{n+1}^{2}}{\alpha_{n+1}^{2}}\right)-u_{n+1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}\alpha_{n}^{2}\left(-\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{\alpha_{0}(n+1)^{1-\mathfrak{a}}}+\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}}\left(-\frac{(2-2\mathfrak{a})\alpha_{0}}{\beta_{0}^{2}(n+1)^{2\mathfrak{a}-1}}+\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0

where the last inequality follows from
n\geq n_{I}=\max(2[\mathfrak{a}/(\lambda^{\prime}\alpha_{0})]^{1/(1-\mathfrak{a})},2[(2-2\mathfrak{a})/(\lambda^{\prime}\beta_{0}^{2})]^{1/(2\mathfrak{a}-1)})
. Thus, by induction
u_{n}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{n}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right)
.

2.

Using the same set of arguments as in the previous part, we define the sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
as follows

\displaystyle u_{0}=0;\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ u_{n+1}=(1-\beta_{n}\mu^{\prime})u_{n}+\left(\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{3}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right).

For this part, we want to show that
u_{n}\leq\frac{2}{\mu^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{n}+\frac{\beta_{n}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\right)
. Then following the steps from previous part, we have

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\mu^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{n+1}+\frac{\beta_{n+1}^{2}}{\alpha_{n+1}^{2}}\right)-u_{n+1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{\mu^{\prime}}\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}\left(-\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{\beta_{0}}+\frac{\mu^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\frac{2}{\mu^{\prime}}\frac{\beta_{n}^{3}}{\alpha_{n}^{2}}\left(-\frac{(2-2\mathfrak{a})}{\beta_{0}}+\frac{\mu^{\prime}}{2}\right).

Since
\beta_{0}\geq\max(2\mathfrak{a}/\mu^{\prime},4(1-\mathfrak{a})/\mu^{\prime})
, the first and second term are greater than zero, we have

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\mu^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{n+1}+\frac{\beta_{n+1}^{2}}{\alpha_{n+1}^{2}}\right)-u_{n+1}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

This completes the induction.

3.

Repeating the arguments from part 1, we define the sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
as follows

\displaystyle u_{0}=0;\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ u_{n+1}=\left(1-\frac{\beta_{n}\mu^{\prime}}{4}\right)u_{n}+\beta_{n}^{2}.

For this part, we want to show that
u_{n}\leq\frac{8}{\mu^{\prime}}\beta_{n}
. Then, following the steps from part 1, we have

\displaystyle\frac{8}{\mu^{\prime}}\beta_{n+1}-u_{n+1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{8}{\mu^{\prime}}\beta_{n}^{2}\left(-\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}+\frac{\mu^{\prime}}{8}\right).

Since
\beta_{0}\geq 8/\mu^{\prime}
, the r.h.s. is greater than zero, we have

\displaystyle\frac{8}{\mu^{\prime}}\beta_{n+1}-u_{n+1}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

This completes the induction.
