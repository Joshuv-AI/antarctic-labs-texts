Cross-sectional study assessing health outcome priorities of older adults with multimorbidity at a primary care setting in Singapore
Authors: Xin Rong Ng, Yu Xuan Sheryl Tey, Kaiwei Jeremy Lew, Poay Sian Sabrina Lee, Eng Sing Lee, Sai Zhen Sim
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesManaging older adults with multimorbidity may be challenging due to the conflicting benefits and harms of multiple treatments. Thus, it is important to identify patients’ health outcome priorities to align treatment goals with their health preferences. This study aimed to use the Outcome Prioritisation Tool (OPT) to describe the health outcome priorities of older adults with multimorbidity and determine the factors associated with these priorities. Additionally, it aimed to assess the ease of completing the OPT in Singapore’s primary care population.DesignCross-sectional study conducted from January to March 2022.SettingA public primary care centre in Singapore.Participants65 years and older with multimorbidity.Outcome measuresPrimary outcome measure was the most important health outcome priorities on the OPT. Secondary outcome measures were factors affecting these priorities and ease of completing the OPT.ResultsWe enrolled 180 participants (mean age: 73.2±6.1 years). Slightly more than half (54.4%) prioritised ‘staying alive’, while the remainder (45.6%) prioritised ‘maintaining independence’ (25.6%), ‘relieving pain’ (10.6%) and ‘relieving other symptoms’ (9.4%). Participants with six or more chronic conditions were three times (OR 3.03 (95% CI1.09 to 8.42)) more likely to prioritise ‘staying alive’ compared with participants with three conditions. Most participants (69.4%) agreed that the OPT was easy to complete, and the mean time taken to complete the OPT was 3.8±1.6 minutes.Conclusion‘Staying alive’ was the most important health outcome priority, especially for older adults with six or more chronic conditions. The OPT was easily completed among older adults with multimorbidity in primary care. Further qualitative studies can be conducted to understand the factors influencing patients’ priorities and explore the relevance of the OPT in guiding treatment decisions.Keywords: primary health care, decision making, aged

Background

With an ageing population, multimorbidity is very prevalent in primary care1 and the management of older adults with multimorbidity presents significant challenges. Clinicians apply multiple treatments targeting different chronic diseases to improve disease-specific outcomes such as mortality and target organ damage in older adults with multimorbidity.2 However, the accumulation of different treatments increases the risk of adverse events experienced by these older adults who consider such events as important health outcomes.3 Conflict between the benefit and harm of multiple treatments can be mitigated by setting treatment goals acceptable to both older adults and clinicians,4 as part of patient-centred care and shared decision-making.5 This includes the identification of their health outcome priorities and consideration of acceptable trade-offs among competing outcomes.6

Tools eliciting health outcome priorities have been developed to facilitate discussion of treatment options between patients and physicians.7 These tools consider trade-offs, either between current and future health, or among different health outcome domains that may be based on quantity or quality of life.8 The Outcome Prioritisation Tool (OPT) is one such tool that considers the latter type of trade-off. It was developed based on focus group discussions in older adults with multimorbidity on what they think of potentially competing treatment outcomes.9 When considering trade-offs among competing outcomes, these older adults were more likely to discuss global, cross-disease health outcomes, that is, ‘staying alive’, ‘maintaining independence’, ‘relieving pain’ and ‘relieving other symptoms’ rather than disease-specific outcomes, and the OPT prioritises these four outcomes on a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS).10 Unlike the OPT, The Time and Outcome Preference Scale (Attitude Scale) considers trade-offs between both quality and quantity of life in addition to trade-offs between current and future health, while the Present verus Future Health Prioritisation Tool focuses on the relative importance of quality of life at the current time compared with the future 1 year and 5 years later.8 Studies exploring older adults’ preferences for using these tools found that each tool took minutes to complete11 and although older adults found the tools equally easy to complete,8 the OPT was still the preferred tool to help them weigh their health outcome priorities.8

Since its conception, various studies have explored the clinical utility of the OPT and its health outcome prioritisation.10–19 The OPT has been used to facilitate deprescribing in older adults,12 13 and end-of-life care discussions in patients with non-curable cancer.14 It has also been used to show the poor concordance in health outcome priorities between patients and their healthcare professionals in tertiary care, highlighting the need for better treatment alignment to these priorities.15 Across various countries and healthcare settings, the OPT priority of ‘maintaining independence’ was consistently prioritised as the most important health outcome, suggesting that many patients prioritised quality of life, specifically functional capability, as most important.7

Only a few studies have investigated the factors associated with prioritisation of health outcome and these studies have found that marital status,16 education level,16 depression,16 recent hospitalisation,20 social support7 and health-related quality of life (HrQoL)21 were associated with health outcome priorities. Additionally, most of the studies were done in the USA and in European countries. There has yet to be a study assessing the health outcome priorities or the use of the OPT in an Asian primary care population.7 There are intrinsic differences in sociocultural values22 and the financial primary care models23 24 between different countries and regions, which may potentially impact health outcome priorities. By studying the health outcome priorities of older adults with multimorbidity in Singapore’s primary care, family physicians will be better able to understand the varying health outcome preferences of this group of patients and the importance of identifying these preferences for shared decision-making. This can help to improve patients’ satisfaction25 and adherence to treatment26 as well as build therapeutic relationships between physicians and patients.27 This is also in line with a new Singapore Healthier SG national initiative, where Singaporeans are encouraged to choose their personal family physicians to discuss personalised health plans and improve continuity of care.28 Having the OPT to assist older adults to deliberate more about their health outcome priorities may help to facilitate the discussion of individualised health plans29 and strengthen the doctor–patient relationships.27

Hence, the primary objective of the study was to assess the most important health outcome priority of older adults with multimorbidity among a primary care population. The secondary objectives were to explore the factors affecting these priorities as well as to assess the ease of completing the OPT among older adults with multimorbidity in Singapore’s primary care setting.

Methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted from January 2022 to March 2022 at Toa Payoh Polyclinic. The polyclinic serves the Toa Payoh township which has approximately 100 000 residents, with 1 in 5 residents aged 65 years and above. This town has one of the highest proportions of elderly residents in Singapore.30

We obtained the daily lists of older patients 65 years and above with physician appointments from the electronic medical record system. Potential eligible participants were selected at fixed hourly intervals based on the type of appointment (regular review or as walk-in). They were then approached and screened for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were (1) aged 65 years and above; (2) self-identified three or more chronic conditions out of a predetermined list of 23 chronic conditions; (3) spoke and understood English or Chinese and (4) provided written informed consent.

Recruitment, consent-taking and administration of the questionnaires and OPT were conducted by a trained research assistant in a quiet area of the polyclinic. The questionnaires and OPT were available in English and Chinese. The questionnaires collected information on multimorbidity, sociodemographic variables, social support, HrQoL, frailty, depressive symptoms, recent hospitalisation within a year, health outcome priorities and ease of completing the OPT.

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Measurement of multimorbidity

Multimorbidity was defined as the presence of three or more self-reported chronic conditions from a list of 23 chronic conditions.31 The list of chronic conditions included: hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, diabetes, arthritis or rheumatoid arthritis, obesity, cardiovascular disease, asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or chronic bronchitis, chronic hepatitis, thyroid disorder, stroke or transient ischaemic attack, heart failure (including valve problem or replacement), kidney disease or failure, depression or anxiety, chronic urinary problem, functional limitation, cognitive limitation, any cancer in the last 5 years, osteoporosis, dementia, chronic pain, gout, other mental health conditions and lastly, neurological disorders.31 The number of chronic conditions and the list of 23 chronic conditions to define multimorbidity in this study were derived from a Delphi study that obtained consensus among family physicians on the definition of multimorbidity in the ambulatory primary care setting of Singapore.31

Sociodemographic variables

The sociodemographic variables collected included age, sex, ethnicity, educational level, marital status and types of residence. In Singapore, the types of residence can serve as proxy to the household income.32

Outcome Prioritisation Tool

The OPT is a VAS to prioritise four health outcomes from most important to least important. The four outcomes include: ‘maintaining independence’, ‘staying alive’, ‘relieving pain’ and ‘reliving other symptoms’.

The instrument was administered by a trained research assistant. Participants were asked to place these outcomes, each presented in a moveable box, onto a VAS from 0 to 100 on a magnetic board, with higher score indicating that the outcome was more important. This exercise was preceded by an introductory script adapted from Fried et al to explain the ‘trade-off’ principle.10

Prior to the start of the study, a pilot study involving 10 English and 10 Chinese speaking older adults with multimorbidity was conducted to assess face validity and test–retest reliability of the English and Chinese-translated versions of the OPTs respectively. The test–retest reliability was similar to the original study10 with 80%–100% agreement for the English OPT, and 90%–100% agreement for the Chinese OPT most important and least important outcomes. The intraclass correlation coefficient of the English OPT was 0.71–1.00, while the intraclass correlation coefficient of the Chinese OPT was 0.78–1.00. Based on a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree), all the participants agreed or strongly agreed that the OPT was easy to understand. Content validity of the items on the OPT was assessed by a panel of experienced family physicians.

Ease of completing the OPT

The ease of completing the OPT was based on time taken to complete the OPT, and a 5-point Likert-scale (ranging from totally disagree to totally agree) on the extent to which the participants felt that the OPT was easy to complete. The time taken to complete the OPT spanned from the moment the interviewer started reading the script till the time the participant finished prioritising his or her health outcome priorities.

Social support

The Duke Social Support Scale chosen to measure social support,33 demonstrated good construct validity in the Singapore Chinese Health Study.34 It measured the level of family and non-family support from 10 different sources. The final overall score ranged from 0 to 100, with 0 representing no support and 100 representing most support.

Health-related quality of life

HrQoL35 was measured by the EuroQol-5 Dimensions-5 Levels (EQ-5D-5L) questionnaire, which has been validated locally.36 The EQ-5D-5L has two components. The first component is the health-state Utility Index which measures five domains (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression) on a five-point severity scale. The Singapore time trade-off values were used to convert the information into Utility Index scores,37 with −0.769 being the worst health state and 1.000 being the best health state. The second component is the VAS, with participants asked to rate their global health for that day on a scale of 0–100, with 0 representing the worst imaginable health state and 100 the best imaginable health state.

Frailty

Frailty was measured using the locally validated FRAIL (fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illness and loss of weight) scale.38 39 The five items of the FRAIL scale are self-reported, and they include fatigue, resistance, ambulation, loss of weight of 5% over the last 12 months and 5 or more specific illnesses from a list of 11 conditions.38 Participants were classified as ‘frail’ if they scored positive for three or more domains, ‘prefrail’ if they scored positive for one to two domains, and ‘robust’ if they had no positive answers.

Patient Health Questionnaire-9

Depressive symptoms were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) that has been validated locally.40 It measured the frequency of each of the nine symptoms under the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-V criteria for major depression over 2 weeks. Scores of 5, 10, 15 and 20 corresponded to mild, moderate, moderately severe and severe depression, respectively.40

Recent hospitalisation

Participants were asked if they had any recent hospitalisation within the last 1 year for at least one night. This was adapted from a local study measuring utilisation of healthcare services among older adults.41

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

With an expected proportion of 37%13 choosing maintaining independence as the most important health outcome priority, width of 0.15, and 95% CI, the minimum sample size was 158. Assuming 10% incomplete questionnaires, the sample size was further increased to 180 participants.

IBM SPSS V.23 was used for statistical calculations. Data collected were analysed descriptively; using mean with SD for continuous variables of parametric distribution; mean with SD and median with IQR for continuous variables of non-parametric distribution; frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The χ2 tests were used for investigating the association between categorical variables, and logistic regression was used for continuous independent variables to assess the association with health outcome preferences. All independent variables were included in the binomial logistic regression to investigate the association between independent variables and health outcome preferences. A p< 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Of 257 patients who were approached and screened, 22 patients were not eligible, and 55 patients declined to participate. One hundred and eighty participants were recruited, giving an overall response rate of 70.0%. All 180 participants provided written informed consent and completed the questionnaires and OPT.

Table 1 illustrates the characteristics of the participants in this study. Participants were aged between 65 and 90 years (mean age: 73.2±6.1 years). Genders were equally distributed. Majority of the participants were Chinese (81.7%), married (66.1%) and had either primary school education or no formal education (53.9%). Mean number of chronic conditions was 4.6±1.4. The most common conditions were hyperlipidaemia (90.6%), hypertension (86.1%) and diabetes mellitus (68.9%). None of the participants had self-reported cognitive limitation, dementia or other mental health conditions excluding depression or anxiety.

OPT rankings

For the most important health outcome, 54.4% prioritised ‘staying alive’, 25.6% prioritised ‘maintaining independence’, 10.6% prioritised ‘relieving pain’ and 9.4% prioritised ‘relieving other symptoms’.

For the least important health outcome, 38.9% chose ‘relieving other symptoms’, 23.3% chose ‘relieving pain’, 21.7% chose ‘staying alive’ and 16.1% chose ‘maintaining independence’.

Ease of completing the OPT

Based on a 5-point Likert scale, 69.4% of participants agreed or strongly agreed that the OPT was easy to complete. Mean time taken to complete the OPT was 3.8±1.6 minutes.

Frailty, social support, depressive symptoms, HrQoL

Based on the FRAIL scale, 50.6% were robust, 44.4% prefrail and 5.0% frail. Mean score for the Duke Social Support Scale was 37.0±16.4. Median score for the PHQ-9 was 1.0 (0.0–4.0). From the EQ-5D-5L, 26.7% reported difficulties in walking, 8.9% reported difficulties with usual activities and 5.6% reported difficulties in washing or dressing. The utility index scores ranged from −0.39 (lowest) to 1.00 (highest), with nearly half of the participants (41.7%) scoring the maximum. Mean UI score was 0.83±0.24.

Factors associated with health outcomes

The binomial logistic regression model in table 2 showed that participants with six or more chronic conditions, as compared with participants with three chronic conditions, had higher adjusted odds (OR 3.03 (95% CI 1.09 to 8.42), p=0.03) of prioritising ‘staying alive’ over not prioritising ‘staying alive’.

Discussion

Our study, the first to use the OPT in older adults with multimorbidity in an Asian primary care setting, found that slightly more than half of the participants prioritised ‘staying alive’ (54.4%), while slightly less than half prioritised other health outcomes (45.6%). Participants with six or more chronic conditions were more likely to prioritise ‘staying alive’ when compared with participants with three chronic conditions. The study also showed that the OPT took only a few minutes to complete with majority of participants finding it easy to complete (69.4%).

It was surprising that the majority of the older adults in our study prioritised ‘staying alive’. This differed from previous OPT studies among older adults with multimorbidity in senior care10 16 and primary care centres12 13 in America10 16 and Europe12 13 which showed ‘maintaining independence’ as the most important health outcome priority.

One possible contributor to our findings is the impact of COVID-19 pandemic on the health outcome priorities of patients. This study was conducted from January 2022 to March 2022, which coincided with the peak of the COVID-19 Omicron wave in Singapore. Higher risk of COVID-19 mortality among older adults with multimorbidity might have raised their fear of death.42 This could have influenced the participants’ health outcome priorities and contributed to their decision to prioritise ‘staying alive’. This could also be the reason for participants with six or more chronic conditions being more likely to prioritise ‘staying alive’ when compared with those with three conditions. Another possible contributor to our findings is the Asian cultural beliefs that may be influenced by stoicism43 and Confucian principles of needing to fulfil a family’s obligation to live as long as possible in Singapore’s predominant ethnic Chinese society.22 Our postulations can be further explored in qualitative studies to understand how older adults with multimorbidity prioritise their health outcomes and how the COVID-19 pandemic affected their priorities.

Our study also found that the time taken to complete the OPT assessment was only a few minutes (mean time taken=3.8±1.6 min) and most participants (69.4%) agreed that it was easy to complete. This was similar to the studies by van Summeren et al,12 13 and Case et al,11 and indicated that the OPT is an easy to complete tool for assessing health outcome priorities in older adults. The cross-disease nature of the global health outcome preferences on the OPT is also applicable to a range of specific treatment decisions.9 16 It can assist clinicians to provide treatment recommendations for multiple medical conditions based on patients’ universal health outcome preferences. Furthermore, universal health outcomes on the OPT are simpler for older adults to understand than disease-specific outcomes for each therapeutic option.9 These make the OPT a useful decision aid for older adults to deliberate about their values to facilitate more in-depth discussion about their therapeutic options with their doctors.11

Clinical implications

While ‘staying alive’ was the most important health outcome priority among Singaporean older adults with multimorbidity in primary care, there was still a significant proportion of older adults who chose other health outcome priorities. Given the poor concordance between patients’ priorities and physicians’ perceptions of their priorities in several health outcome studies,15 44 our study highlighted the need for healthcare providers to actively identify and understand older adults’ health outcome priorities, and the potential of the OPT in helping them to do so.

The association of number of chronic conditions with health outcome priorities informs clinicians about the importance of reassessing these priorities when health status of our patients changes to ensure alignment of treatment goals. The fact that only number of chronic conditions was associated with health outcomes also underlines the poor understanding we have of how patients prioritise their health outcomes, and the need for further studies to explore this. Gaining better understanding of the prioritisation of health outcomes can facilitate the provision of personalised care.

The ease of completion of the OPT in assessing health outcome priorities among older adults with multimorbidity in our study may encourage further studies on the use of the OPT to guide treatment decisions in primary care. Although the OPT was conceived back in 2011, only two studies have assessed the role of the OPT in reducing polypharmacy.12 13 The OPT was found to be useful in deprescribing medications for older adults with multimorbidity and polypharmacy in primary care.13 More studies are needed to assess how healthcare providers and patients can translate the generic health outcomes of the OPT to various treatment decisions, and their opinions on the feasibility and utility of the OPT.

Identifying health outcome priorities may become even more important as Singapore rolls out the national Healthier SG initiative. Under the national Healthier SG enrolment programme, Singaporeans are encouraged to choose their personal family physicians to discuss personalised health plans including preventative health, lifestyle and disease targets.28 The easy administration of the OPT for assessing health outcome priorities among older adults with multimorbidity in our study may encourage the use of this tool during discussion of personalised health plans to align treatment goals and build therapeutic relationships between physicians and patients.27

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths. First, this is the first study to use the OPT in an older adult Asian primary care population. Second, the administration of the questionnaires in English and Chinese languages would maximise the sample representativeness since majority of the primary care population in Toa Payoh could converse in these two languages. Additionally, the study used a systematic sampling method to select the study population that was representative of the primary care population in Toa Payoh Polyclinic.

However, the study also had a few limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of this study meant that causal relationships cannot be established. Second, as this was a single-centre study in public primary care, caution should be advised with regard to the generalisability of our findings to older adults with multimorbidity in private non-subsidised primary care centres. These patients are likely to be younger45 and have higher socioeconomic status.46 In addition, the medical conditions managed in private primary care may differ from those managed in public primary care.45 Third, the data collected were self-reported and there might be under or over-reporting of chronic medical conditions.47

Conclusion

‘Staying alive’ was the most important health outcome priority, especially for older adults with six or more chronic conditions and the OPT was easy to complete in primary care to elicit patients’ health outcome preferences. Further studies are required to understand the factors affecting patients’ priorities and to explore the feasibility and utility of the OPT in guiding treatment decisions in the primary care setting.
