Palmitic acid and eicosapentaenoic acid supplementation in 3T3 adipocytes: impact on lipid storage and oxidative stress
Authors: Edina Bakondi, Tobias Jung, Susanna Marg, Vanessa Schnell, Daniela Weber, Tim J. Schulz, Tilman Grune, Annika Höhn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesObesity is a worldwide public health problem, predisposing individuals to serious cardiovascular and metabolic complications such as type 2 diabetes mellitus. White adipose tissue serves as an important regulator of energy balance, and its expansion in obesity can trigger inflammatory reactions and oxidative stress, which can also lead to insulin resistance. Adipocytes, with a key role in regulating metabolic homeostasis, respond to increased calorie intake and altered fatty acid composition with hypertrophy or hyperplasia. Of particular interest are saturated fatty acids such as palmitic acid and omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids such as eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), which have differential effects on adipocyte function and inflammation.MethodsUsing 3T3-L1 cells as a model for adipocytes, we evaluated the effects of PA and EPA on lipid accumulation, droplet size, and oxidative stress markers.ResultsWe were able to show that EPA supplementation in 3T3 adipocytes does not lead to excessive lipid accumulation, but rather reduces the size of lipid droplets and also induces redox changes due to the unsaturated nature of EPA.DiscussionThese results emphasize the contrasting roles of PA and EPA and the importance of fatty acid composition in the regulation of adipocyte function.

Introduction

Obesity, one of the major present global public health problems, is a risk factor for the development of serious cardiovascular and metabolic complications such as type 2 diabetes mellitus [1–4]. Obesity, as a consequence of increased caloric intake and/or reduced energy expenditure over a prolonged period, leads to a temporary low-grade inflammation [5,6] associated with an elevated amount of pro-inflammatory molecules, constantly present in the tissue environment. This leads to the development of local inflammation in the long term and may further result in insulin resistance and also contributes to pancreatic dysfunction [7–10].

During obesity-induced inflammation and related disorders, the white adipose tissue (WAT) is assumed to be the most important organ regarding the regulation of immunological changes [11,12]. The WAT plays a key regulatory role in whole-body homeostasis. It stores excess energy in form of triglycerides or converts them into free fatty acids and glycerol upon demand. However, the WAT also functions as an endocrine organ and regulates food intake, insulin sensitivity and immune responses by secreting various adipokines [13–15]. The expansion of adipose tissue in obesity provides a plethora of intrinsic signals leading to pro-inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial stress resulting in WAT dysfunction and insulin resistance as a consequence [16–18]. Mitochondria are considered as the major source of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [16,19]. Some of these species have a short half-life being highly reactive towards proteins, lipids and DNA, leading to oxidative damage and dysfunction. As a consequence, the intracellular redox environment shifts to a more oxidized state which causes protein damage and also affects proteins involved in cellular glucose uptake and the insulin signaling pathway [20].

The expansion of the adipose tissue can occur via either hyperplasia (formation of new adipocytes from precursors) or hypertrophy (increase in adipocyte size) [21]. While expansion by hypertrophy leads to mechanical stress and hypoxia and thus to defects in organelle function, adipocyte inflammation and increased insulin resistance [22], small adipocytes can counterbalance the negative metabolic effects of obesity [23].

A surplus of dietary fat intake has been considered worldwide as the main factor in the development of obesity [24–26]. However, it is also important to underline that fatty acids are not equal. Depending on their chain length and number, configuration and exact position of the unsaturated bonds they can modulate the energy balance differently [27]. Both pre-clinical and clinical studies have demonstrated the beneficial, anti-inflammatory effects of ω-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) via changing phospholipid composition and activity of signaling pathways [28].

In this study, we aimed to investigate whether the different types of fatty acids (FA): the saturated fatty acid (SFA) palmitic acid (PA) and the ω-3 PUFA eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) can be stored in adipocytes to the same extent and induce hypertrophy equally. In addition to that, we addressed the question whether both fatty acids induce oxidative stress in adipocytes. For this, we used the 3T3-L1 cell line, representing the most frequently used pre-adipocyte model and also sharing many properties with normal adipocytes [29,30].

Material and methods

3T3-L1 cell culture and differentiation

Cells were grown in DMEM (4.5 g/L glucose, 2 mM glutamine; Cytiva) supplemented with 10% FCS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (Merck) (Normal Medium, NM) to post-confluency. Adipogenesis was induced with a standard procedure by incubation with differentiation medium (DM), consisting of NM with the addition of 0.5 mM 3-Isobutyl-1-Methylxantine (Sigma Aldrich), 1 µM dexamethasone (Sigma Aldrich) and 10 µg/ml insulin (Sigma Aldrich) (day 0). On day 3, medium was replaced by maintaining medium (MM, NM containing10 µg/ml insulin). Sample collection and measurements were carried out on day 6. Fatty acid treatments at the indicated concentrations were performed during DM and MM exposure as shown in the scheme of fatty acid treatment (Figure 1). Stock solutions of the fatty acids in ethanol were prepared (PA) or purchased (EPA) and then transferred to pre-warmed cell culture medium. EPA was transferred to the medium under nitrogen atmosphere. Figure 1.Scheme of the treatment of 3T3-L1 adipocytes with different fatty acids. 3T3-L1 adipocytes were treated with palmitate (PA) or eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) in differentiation medium (DM) and maintaining medium (MM), either with the same fatty acid for 6 days (A) or with swapping the type of fatty acids at the third day (B). Control cells (Con. in all further figures) were treated according to the same time schedule (3 days of differentiation medium followed by 3 days of maintaining medium) but without fatty acid treatment.

Scheme of the treatment of 3T3-L1 adipocytes with different fatty acids. 3T3-L1 adipocytes were treated with palmitate (PA) or eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) in differentiation medium (DM) and maintaining medium (MM), either with the same fatty acid for 6 days (A) or with swapping the type of fatty acids at the third day (B). Control cells (Con. in all further figures) were treated according to the same time schedule (3 days of differentiation medium followed by 3 days of maintaining medium) but without fatty acid treatment.

Calcein-AM assay for cell viability

Cells were cultured in 96 well plates. At D6 media were aspirated and cells were washed with medium then incubated at 37°C with NM containing 1 µM Calcein-AM (Sigma Aldrich). After 20 min, fluorescence was measured at 485/535 nm using a Tecan plate reader.

Oil red O staining

For intracellular lipid content determination, cells were washed with PBS and then fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 20 min. After washing, cells were incubated with Oil Red O dye (Sigma Aldrich) in 3:2 isopropanol/H2O. Unspecific dye bonding was removed by washing the cells with H2O. Afterwards the samples were air dried. For visualization, pictures were taken with the OLYMPUS IX53 microscope. For quantitative analysis, lipids were dissolved in DMSO and absorbance was measured at 519 nm using a Tecan plate reader.

Lipid droplet size determination

Cells grown on coverslips were stained with Oil Red O as described above and mounted with a mounting medium containing DAPI (Roth). Pictures were taken with a confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, LSM780). Droplet size was determined using the software FIJI (v. 1.52n).

DCF and MitoSOX™ assays

Cells were cultured in 96 well plates. At D6, media were aspirated and cells were washed with NM and then incubated at 37°C with NM containing 2.5 μM CM-H2DCFDA (cellular ROS production) (Invitrogen) or 5 μM MitoSOX™ Red (for mitochondrial superoxide production) (Invitrogen). Cells were washed with PBS and fluorescence intensity was measured in DMEM without FCS and phenol red at 494/538 nm (DCF) or 510/598 nm (MitoSOX™) using a Tecan plate reader.

HPLC measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA)

MDA was measured by HPLC after derivatization with thiobarbituric acid. Cell lysate supernatants were treated like plasma samples and processed as described by Weber et al. [31]

Immunoblotting

Cells were washed once in PBS and collected by scraping into 100 μl of ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF (Sigma Aldrich) and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Merck). The extracts were further lysed with sonication and the supernatants were collected after centrifugation. Protein concentrations were determined with the PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After addition of 4x Laemmli sample buffer (0.25 mM Tris (pH 6.8), 40% Glycerol, 20% 2-Mercaptoethanol, 8% SDS, 0.03% Bromophenol Blue) samples were incubated at 95°C for 10 min. Proteins (20 μg/lane) were separated in 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred via a semidry blotting system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA) to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 2.5% BSA in PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 for 90 min. Primary antibodies against Plin1 (Cell Signaling, #3470, dilution 1:1000), and β-actin (Cell Signaling, #8457, dilution 1:1000) were applied overnight at 4°C. After three washing steps in PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20, the fluorescent labeled secondary antibody conjugated to IRDye® 800CW (LI-COR; #925-32211) diluted in PBS (1:20.000) containing 0.05% Tween 20 and 2,5% BSA was applied for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were washed in PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 three times and once in PBS. Immunodetection and quantification was performed by Odyssey® (Li-Cor Biosciences). Membranes were scanned using the Odyssey® CLx Imaging System (LI-COR) and analyzed with the software Image Studio™ (LI-COR; v. 4.0.21).

Fatty acid composition analysis

Cells grown in six well cell culture plates were washed once in PBS then scraped in 200 µl PBS/well and stored in 16 × 100 mm Teflon coated screw capped vials at −80°C.

Lipids were extracted according to the protocol of Matyash et al. [32]. Lipid extracts were evaporated to dryness under a stream of nitrogen at 40°C and stored at −20°C. Lipids were separated on 3 mL glass SPE columns containing 500 mg aminopropyl-modified silica (Chromabond, Macherey Nagel GmbH & Co.) with a modified protocol of Kaluzny et al. [33]. Lipid extracts were redissolved in 300 µl chloroform and loaded on n-hexane-washed SPE columns, residual chloroform was pulled through. Neutral lipids and free fatty acids were eluted with 1X 2 ml chloroform/2-propanol (2/1, v/v) and 2X 2 ml chloroform/methanol/acetic acid (100/2/2, v/v/v). After changing vials, phospholipids were eluted with 3X 2 ml methanol. Solvents were evaporated under a stream of nitrogen at 40°C.

For hydrolysis and methylation of FA, the dried lipids were redissolved in 150 µl toluene, 12.5 µl trimethyl sulfonium hydroxide solution (TMSH, 0.2 mol/l in methanol, Sigma Aldrich) was added and the mixture was vortexed for 30 min at 750 rpm and 40°C on a thermomixer. Solutions containing this FAME were transferred to GC vials equipped with inserts and analyzed by GC-FID.

Analysis of FAMEs was performed using Agilent GC system 7890A equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID). Samples were injected using a CIS4 PTV-type GC-inlet (Gerstel) in solvent venting mode at 40°C, 40–260°C, ramp 12°C/s, hold min, 260–320°C, ramp 12°C/s. The FAME were separated on a GC capillary column (HP-88, 100 m × 0.25 mm I.D., 0.2 µm film thickness, Agilent) using a constant He carrier gas flow of 1 ml/min. A GC oven temperature program was employed as follows: Start at 70°C, held 1 min, 70–170°C, ramp 10°C/min, held 5 min, 170–175°C, ramp 5°C/min, 175–190°C, ramp 2°C/min, 190–205°C, ramp 5°C/min, held 17 min, 205–235 °C, ramp 5°C/min, held 8 min, total run time 58.5 min.

For identification and column check out, FAME standards (supelco 37-component FAME Mix (Supelco) and GLC 455 (Nu-Check-Prep Inc., Elysian, USA)) were used.

FA composition of fatty acids was expressed as area percentage of each FA relative to total area of all detected FA.

Statistical analysis

All values are expressed as mean ± SEM and experiments were repeated at least three to four times. A one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used to determine significances. All values of p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

To test the fat storage ability of 3T3-L1 cells while differentiating into adipocytes, we added palmitic acid (PA) to the differentiation medium (DM) for 3 days followed by a 3-day administration of PA in maintenance medium (MM) (Figure 1A). As shown in Figure 2(A,C), adipocytes accumulate lipids depending on the concentration of PA they are exposed to. We exposed the adipocytes to concentrations of up to 1000 µM without affecting the viability of cells (Figure 2B). Next, we tested the viability of adipocytes towards the highly unsaturated ω-3 fatty acid eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA). However, in concentrations higher than 250 µM EPA became toxic to the cells (data not shown). Therefore, for further experiments we used the non-toxic concentration of 250 µM (Figure 3A), as well as 500 µM PA to achieve a high lipid accumulation and at the same time viable conditions for the cells. While PA leads to a significant accumulation of lipids in the adipocytes after a 6-day incubation period, EPA did not have such an effect (Figure 3B). Interestingly, comparing the size of lipid droplets present in differentiated adipocytes, we see a clear increase in the droplet size after treatment with PA, while the size is reduced after EPA treatment compared to control cells (Figure 3C,D).

Since we observed a different effect of PA and EPA, this raises the question whether one of the fatty acids has a dominant effect. So, we exposed 3T3-L1 for 3 days with PA or EPA in DM and then swapped the fatty acid in the following 3 days of treatment with MM (Figure 1B). No effect on viability was observed in either treatment (Figure 4A). Interestingly, the treatment regimen of first PA followed by EPA (PA-EPA) results in increased lipid accumulation compared to treatment with first EPA followed by PA (EPA-PA). However, treatment with EPA after previous PA incubation (PA-EPA) shows a reduction in droplet size, regardless of the high fat accumulation. On the other hand, EPA treatment during differentiation, even when followed by PA treatment (EPA-PA) seems to decrease the total lipid accumulation, while the droplets tend to be larger (Figure 4A–D). High lipid accumulation should be achieved by a high amount of the droplet-coating and -stabilizing protein PLIN1, while low fat accumulation should require less PLIN1. Therefore, we investigated the protein levels of PLIN1 (Figure 4E) and the results confirmed the predicted patterns and support our findings on the influence of droplet size by different fatty acid treatments. While PA treatment alone or PA treatment followed by EPA induces the formation of high levels of PLIN1 expression, EPA alone or EPA treatment followed by PA leads to the formation of only low amounts of PLIN1.

Inadvertently, this leads to the question of the accumulated fatty acids. Therefore, we performed a fatty acid analysis and separated the neutral lipids (mainly lipid droplets) and the phospholipid (membrane) fraction. As can be seen in Figures 5(A) and (B), PA predominantly accumulates in the lipid droplets regardless of the treatment, while EPA is only present in relatively low amounts (Figure 5A), even if the exposure to EPA is the last in MM (PA-EPA) (Figure 5C). The presence of EPA in each treatment form in the phospholipid fraction gives a different picture (Figures 5B,D). Whenever EPA is present, whether EPA alone or EPA-PA/PA-EPA in the swapping model, it is one of the major fatty acids in the membrane (always compared to the control medium, without any fatty acid added). Given, the function of ω-3 PUFA (or in fact all PUFA) in maintenance of the fluidity of the membrane, this is important for cellular viability. On the other hand, the storage of a highly oxidizable PUFA as an energy store might be dangerous due to promotion of lipid peroxidation. To test the pro-oxidative effect of PA or EPA treatment, we used the common DCF-assay and the MitoSOX™ mitochondrial superoxide indicator dye. As can be seen in Figures 6(A) and (B), the treatment of adipocytes with EPA leads to a significant pro-oxidative shift. Treatment with PA followed by EPA leads to a pro-oxidative shift, while treatment with EPA followed by PA did not change the oxidative status compared to the control (Figures 6D and E). These data are supported by the measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA), an end product of lipid peroxidation and also a well-known biomarker for measuring oxidative stress. Both EPA alone and PA followed by EPA result in increased MDA concentrations, whereas EPA followed by PA shows no change in MDA levels (Figures 6Cand F). Therefore, one can conclude that the significant pro-oxidative influence of EPA mainly depends on the accumulation of this fatty acid in membranes, while the EPA accumulation in lipid droplets is low.

Discussion

Adipocytes are important for storing excess energy. This energy storage occurs most effectively in form of triglycerides containing long chain fatty acids and accumulate in lipid droplets. However, since long chain fatty acids may have a different degree of saturation, the question arises whether adipocytes can store PUFA and in particular n-3 PUFA, which are claimed to have a positive health effect. Furthermore, it seems important whether n–3 PUFA can induce adipocyte hypertrophy to the same extent as SFA and whether the lipid droplets do have the same sizes. It seems to be accepted that the size of the lipid droplet, directly affects adipose cell size and that less hypertrophic adipocytes, with small lipid droplets do have health benefits compared to the large lipid droplet containing hypertrophic adipocytes [34]. Adipocyte hypertrophy is closely linked to metabolic and cardiovascular disease with hypertrophy – not obesity per se – being a predictor of type 2 diabetes [34]. Here we showed that the addition of EPA to 3T3 adipocytes (i) does not lead to excessive lipid accumulation, but more importantly (ii) reduces the size of lipid droplets, (iii) leads to n–3 PUFA accumulation preferentially in phospholipids, (iv) rather reduces PLIN1 expression consequently leading to a reduction in lipid droplets and (v) induce some degree of redox changes due to unsaturation status of the PUFA.

One of the key factors in these different effects of PA and EPA seems to be the expression of PLIN1. It is known that in the PLIN1 promoter contains binding sequences for several transcription factors, including C/EBPβ, and SMAD3 [35]. It has already been described that SFA, in particular stearic acid promote lipogenic gene expression, whereas PUFA have an opposite effect [36]. Also, others demonstrated the strong effect of SFA on adipogenesis [37].

Li et al. demonstrated in 3T3 adipocytes that the plant-derived n-3 PUFA, stearidonic acid, suppresses differentiation and lipid accumulation [38]. The author concluded that, therefore, such n–3 PUFA can be used to treat obesity. However, as we demonstrated here, the effect of n–3 PUFA on lipid storage in 3T3 adipocytes after SFA accumulation is limited. Therefore, the role of palmitate as major regulator of cell adaptation to obesity [39] and inductor of insulin resistance [40] seems not to be blunted by n–3 PUFA, at least not by removing or replacing PA once stored in adipocytes. It is well known that adipocytes are not only an energy-storing cell type, but actively modulate metabolism by releasing adipokines. The release of adipokines by adipocytes depends on storage status and conditions, therefore it is important to further investigate whether adipokine release also depends on the triglycerides stored.
