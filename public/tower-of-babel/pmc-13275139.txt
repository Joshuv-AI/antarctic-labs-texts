Outpatient surgical referrals from primary care providers for people experiencing homelessness: a retrospective review
Authors: Madeline McDonald, Peter Huan, Caroline Hircock, Christina Pizzola, Timothy O’Shea, Carolyn Lévis, Margherita Cadeddu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:People experiencing homelessness suffer from a high burden of surgical conditions and face many barriers to accessing care. Our aim was to describe the unmeasured and unmet need of surgical care through outpatient surgical referrals.Methods:This is a retrospective review of electronic patient charts from the Shelter Health Network, a health care and social service organization serving people experiencing homelessness in Hamilton, Ontario, Canada. The review spanned a 2-year period from 2017 to 2018 and included referrals to all outpatient surgical services (except ophthalmology) and endoscopy.Results:In total, 167 surgical referrals were sent for 129 patients over the 2-year period. The average age of patients was 46 years, and 95% had provincial health insurance. Among referrals, 93% resulted in a scheduled appointment, and 58% resulted in the patient seeing a surgical provider. Overall, 61 surgical procedures were proposed and 64% of these were completed. Major procedures were completed at a higher rate than minor procedures. Patient, provider, and system factors contributed to patients not receiving care.Conclusion:To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe outpatient surgical referrals by primary care providers for people experiencing homelessness. There were significant gaps in both the accessing of surgical consultation and in the completion of proposed surgical procedures. These represent an ongoing unmet need for surgical care. We divided barriers into systemic, provider, and patient barriers, and those that have direct applications to policy and practice.

People experiencing homelessness (PEH) have significantly higher rates of medical, surgical, and psychiatric morbidity and mortality than housed individuals.1–5 People experiencing homelessness also face many barriers to accessing health care, even in countries with publicly funded health insurance such as Canada.1,6 Within the realm of surgical care, previous studies have described surgical need in the settings of hospital admissions and emergency departments, where PEH present with high rates of traumatic injuries, advanced disease, and more often require unplanned surgery than housed populations do.5,7,8 A small number of studies that have undertaken active screening outreach for surgical disease among PEH also found significantly higher rates of pathology than in the general population.9–11 Yet, among the general population, the most common pathway to surgical care is through outpatient referrals by primary care providers. To our knowledge, there are no studies describing the surgical need of PEH through outpatient referrals by primary care providers to surgical providers.

Local clinical context

As in other Canadian cities, Hamilton in Ontario has a significant population of people experiencing both episodic and chronic homelessness. Based on the nationally accepted definition described by the Canadian Observatory on Homelessness, homelessness includes individuals who are unsheltered, emergency sheltered, provisionally accommodated, and at risk of homelessness.12 In 2018, City of Hamilton data demonstrated that 2901 individuals accessed shelters,13 which represents only a portion of all those experiencing homelessness. Other reports using Canadian data have estimated that the true numbers of PEH are 3 times larger than the individuals who can be counted by traditional strategies, which include shelter data and point-in-time counts.14

Hamilton has a well-established Shelter Health Network (SHN), a collaboration of health care providers and social service organizations serving a high-risk population with complex health needs and without stable housing. 15 Shelter Health Network health providers include family physicians, nurse practitioners, internists, and psychiatrists who provide primary care at several locations (e.g., shelters, transitional housing programs, drop-in centres). Primary care providers within the SHN network estimate that approximately 2000 unique patients are seen each year. The number referred for surgical services is unknown to date. Our objectives for this study were to describe the volume, patterns, and outcomes of outpatient surgical referrals from primary care providers for PEH. We hypothesized that there is significant need for surgical care among people experiencing homelessness, which is not captured through the existing model of care delivery.

Methods

This study is a retrospective chart review spanning a 2-year period from Jan. 1, 2017, to Dec. 31, 2018, chosen to pre-date the COVID-19 pandemic and the resulting disruption in multiple health services. It included patient charts from the SHN, which provides primary care services for PEH. All patients receiving care at SHN meet the definition of homeless as per the accepted definition described by the Canadian Observatory on Homelessness. 12 Care is provided at 16 locations within the city of Hamilton and most operate on a drop-in basis. Anyone without an existing family physician can access services, regardless of provincial health insurance status.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included outpatient referrals by an SHN provider for surgical consultation to any of general surgery, otolaryngology, orthopedic surgery, plastic surgery, cardiac surgery, thoracic surgery, vascular surgery, gynecology, and urology services. Referrals for endoscopy were also included, given that both general surgeons and gastroenterologists complete endoscopy in Hamilton. Referrals for gastroenterology consultations were not included. Referrals were non-acute by definition, as outpatient referrals often take weeks to months before an appointment is confirmed.

Data collection

Patient demographic information, clinical notes, and records were accessed through SHN’s electronic medical record (EMR). The SHN EMR is not electronically linked with any other medical record system (i.e., not linked with local acute care hospitals). Notes from visits with SHN providers and referrals sent by fax are found within the EMR. Consultation notes, operative notes, admission, and discharge notes faxed back to the SHN provider as the primary care provider and referring physician have been scanned into the EMR. No other EMRs were accessed directly (neither hospital EMRs nor records of care provided outside of the SHN).

The EMR records all referrals sent for SHN patients. We screened all referrals sent during the period of January 2017 to December 2018 and selected those sent for surgical consultation and endoscopy as per the inclusion criteria. The associated patient charts were then reviewed in full by a member of the research team for relevant details, including demographic information, the reason for consultation, appointment information, visit summaries, operative notes, and relevant SHN provider notes. Multiple consults per patient were also recorded. Missing data were flagged, and the associated charts were reviewed again by a second team member for completeness. The initial 4 charts were extracted by all team members as a pilot exercise to ensure the extraction was analogous.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to characterize patient demographics and number and type of surgical referrals. Continuous variables are described as means with standard deviations (SDs), whereas categorical variables are reported as numbers and percentages. Where demographic data were missing, these were excluded within each analysis and the number of missing data points was recorded. We still included patient charts even when some data points were missing. When no documentation had been faxed back to the SHN provider after a consultation, we reviewed the SHN provider notes for details about what the patient reported. In cases where we found no documentation nor other details, the data were marked as missing (i.e., we could not confirm whether the patient attended the consultation appointment, what diagnosis was given, or the treatment proposed). We kept text data extracted from the charts as close to the original terminology as possible. We categorized procedures documented in the chart into major (involved a visceral body cavity or would typically be associated with an overnight hospital admission) and minor (not involving a visceral body cavity, or would typically be associated with same-day discharge). We performed data analysis using R version 4.3.1.

We extracted descriptive reasons that patients were not seen by surgical providers and reasons procedures were not completed, where documented. We grouped these by themes, and larger categories emerged inductively.

We presented the preliminary results to a panel of expert consultants that included 3 physicians (2 surgeons and 1 shelter health expert physician), as well as our lived experience consultant. All panel members work in and are familiar with the local Hamilton context. Expert panel members provided feedback on the presentation and explanation of data, and we incorporated suggestions. We presented the final results to the physician lead of the SHN for help in contextualizing the findings.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board at McMaster University (HIREB #14500-C) and by the Ethics Representative within the SHN. Individual ethics approval by patients was not required owing to the retrospective nature and minimal risk to patients. The SHN EMR is compliant with McMaster University’s requirements for ethical stewardship of patient data. We recognize as researchers that our experiences and worldviews influence our research, and we have elaborated on this within a reflexivity statement, available in Appendix 1A (www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.004524/tab-related-content).

Results

In total, 167 surgical referrals were sent for 129 patients over the 2-year period. The average age of patients was 46 years (SD 12.8, range 16 to 75 years), 47.3% were female, and 95.3% had provincial health insurance. The most prevalent comorbidities were substance use disorder (65.9%), other psychiatric disorders (60.2%), and cigarette smoking (44.7%). No comorbidity data were available for 6 patients (4.7%). Referrals by surgical specialty

Overall, 61 surgical procedures were proposed, of which 50 (82.0%) were categorized as minor and 11 (18.0%) were categorized as major, as per our defined criteria. Of the proposed 61 procedures, 39 (63.9%) were completed, 15 (24.6%) were not completed, and 7 (11.5%) data points were missing. In the cases where patients did see the surgeon, follow-up plans for 71 of the 97 patients were documented within the SHN chart. Of the 71 patients, 22 (31.0%) completed the entire follow-up course, 17 (23.9%) did not complete the follow-up course, and 32 (45.1%) did not require follow-up. There were missing data on the total length of time patients were followed and are thus not reported here.

Descriptive analysis of barriers to care

Themes of the reasons documented for patients not seeing surgical providers were similar to the themes for reasons that procedures were not completed. Upon exploring these themes, 3 categories emerged inductively: systemic factors, provider factors, and patient factors.

Systemic factors included complex referral pathways, which often required SHN providers to send referrals to multiple surgical providers before securing an appointment. Referrals were often rejected because surgical subspecialists did not accept consults for the pathology referred, wait times were too long, or there was no response. Preoperative pathways were complex, in many cases requiring multiple separate appointments for initial consultation, investigations, and preoperative assessment. There were also specific hospital protocols that created barriers to care, such as the policy that endoscopy patients require a family member or friend to drive them home (i.e., patients are not allowed to take public transport, taxi, or walk, etc.). Patients presenting to the endoscopy unit were denied the procedure if they did not have a ride home.

Provider factors that created barriers to care included that in many cases, patients were not rebooked after having missed 1 or multiple appointments or preoperative steps. In these cases, the referral pathway started all over again. Some providers also advertised fees for missing appointments (e.g., Can$100). Regardless of whether this was enforced, it may be a perceived barrier to patients returning to clinic after a missed appointment for fear of being charged a fee.

Patient factors included that many patients were difficult to contact for notification about upcoming appointments, as they often lacked a mailing address or a personal cell phone. Patients also faced challenges in attending scheduled appointments. Documented reasons for this included that patients were admitted to another facility at the time of the procedure (hospital, psychiatric facility, jail), patients declined the appointment or procedure or later chose to cancel, patients were ill at the time of the appointment or procedure, patients missed preoperative investigations, or that other significant psychosocial stressors created barriers. Discussion

This chart review describes the patterns and outcomes of outpatient surgical referrals for PEH in Hamilton, Canada. We believe this is the first study to examine surgical referrals for PEH from an outpatient primary care setting. As hypothesized, these findings reflect an unmet gap in surgical care in 2 aspects: first, the access to surgical consultation; second, the completion of surgical procedures. These gaps are considered in turn, with reference to existing evidence.

Access to surgical consultation

Access to surgical consultation refers to the health care pathway and processes that result in a patient being assessed by a surgical provider. A small number of Canadian studies have explored access to surgical consultation for PEH in different settings. Zuccaro and colleagues described surgical referrals of PEH after presenting to an emergency department.7 Two other Canadian studies sought to prospectively screen for specific types of surgical diseases (otolaryngological and ophthalmological needs).9,11 Another study described presentations to a vascular surgery outreach clinic aimed at PEH.16 Our findings contribute to the conclusions of these studies that surgical need in the PEH population is important and underrepresented by the number of PEH who manage to access surgical care within our existing systems.

In our study and in that of Zuccaro and colleagues, orthopedic surgery received the highest number of referrals. Demographic data in our study align with previous studies of PEH in mean age7,9 and a high prevalence of substance use and other psychiatric conditions.1,2 Zuccaro and colleagues noted that 49% of their patients attended at least 1 outpatient surgical appointment (this group included referrals and follow-up appointments).7 Our study showed a similar rate of 58.1% attendance at outpatient appointments. This is less than the average attendance rate to medical appointments: 77% within the general population (findings are from a systematic review, where attendance rates varied substantially across settings).17 Follow-up rates found in this study also align with previous evidence that PEH tend to have lower rates of follow-up than housed populations.4,7,18

Completion of surgical procedures

In our study, only 63.9% of proposed procedures were completed after the surgical consultation. Previous studies of surgical need described above did not report rates of PEH undergoing procedures,7,9,11,16 and we did not find comparable evidence from housed populations on what proportion of patients do undergo proposed procedures after surgical consultation. This study was not designed to collect information on which procedures were recommended by the surgical provider versus those that were considered more optional. However, there were only 2 cases where the patient’s choice not to proceed was the reason surgery was not completed. Rather, the relatively low completion rate represents a significant gap in surgical care and breakdown of the care pathways.

The higher completion rate of major procedures (72.7%) as compared with minor procedures (62.0%) could indicate that the system and providers were better able to mobilize resources in cases where the surgical need was greater. Our findings include a number of cases in which PEH successfully underwent major operations. This study was not designed to gather sufficient details to examine these examples of positive deviance, but deeper investigation of this phenomenon could be useful in discovering ways to increase the success of other cases.

Barriers to care

The barriers to care we identified at the levels of systems, providers, and patients also mirror those published elsewhere. 19,20 Building on a socioecological model of health,21 Jeleff and colleagues examined barriers to cancer screening for PEH and mapped identified factors onto a socioecological model of health at the policy, system, interpersonal and individual levels.22 Within our study, we recognize that the barriers identified are recorded by the health care providers and may not reflect patients’ perspectives. Qualitative interview studies give voice to PEH and better capture their perspectives with respect to health care in general.20,23 Yet, to our knowledge, no qualitative studies exist on the perspective of PEH with respect to surgical care specifically. Research is needed in this area to better understand barriers and facilitators to surgical care from the perspective of PEH. In particular, PEH may identify distinct barriers between accessing surgical consultation and in completing surgical procedures, a difference that this study did not elucidate.

Implications for policy and practice

Our findings have direct applications to local institutional policy and clinical practice. Institutional policies such as requiring patients to have another adult to drive them home after endoscopy, and not allowing alternative transportation (which would still be safe for patients postsedation), creates a barrier to care. Policies such as charging a missed appointment fee as a deterrent, although permitted by the governing body, the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario, can be a barrier when equal supports are not provided to help patients attend appointments. Equally, discharging a patient from practice or not rebooking patients after missed appointments creates a barrier to patients accessing care. Our goal is not to point fingers at providers and institutions, but to prompt discussion as to whether these policies can be redesigned in ways that promote rather than impede access to care.

Unfortunately, identifying solutions to these barriers was not within the scope of this study, but qualitative studies with PEH report facilitators to care including addressing multiple purposes in the same visit (e.g., investigations and consultations), flexible and forgiving appointment policies, and positive relationships with health care providers.20,24 Aside from PEH, many other groups of patients face similar barriers to care and would also benefit from improved accessibility along the lines of those policies suggested here.

Limitations

This study was limited by the fact that the province of Ontario does not have an interconnected EMR system through which patients’ health information and visits can be shared among different institutions. It is possible that patients included in this study were simultaneously seeking care through hospitals, other outpatient clinics, or in other locations. For example, we did not capture surgical disease that worsened and required emergency operative management within a hospital setting if this was not documented within the SHN EMR. Similarly, the current system accounts for a substantial amount of missing data, as specialist reports do not always make it back to the referring provider.

Another limitation is that a 2-year study period may not have been long enough to capture the outcome of all surgical referrals, given the length of outpatient wait-lists for surgeons in Ontario. A report from 2018 shows a provincial average wait time from referral to treatment of 18.2 weeks across the surgical specialties included here.25

Conclusion

The findings of this chart review describe the patterns and outcomes of outpatient surgical referrals for PEH in Hamilton, Ontario, Canada. Significant gaps in accessing surgical consultation and in completing surgical procedures were identified. Barriers to care were divided into systemic barriers, provider barriers, and patient barriers and have direct applications to policy and practice.
