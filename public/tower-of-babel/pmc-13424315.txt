Improved Pied Kingfisher Optimization Algorithm for optimal scheduling of microgrids with hybrid energy storage
Authors: Qisheng Liu, Changxi Chen, Honglin Ouyang, Muxuan Xiao, Liang Wang, Weixiang Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the increasing penetration of renewable energy sources, microgrids have become an effective platform for integrating distributed generation and energy storage. However, renewable intermittency and the integration of hybrid energy storage systems lead to a highly nonlinear and nonconvex scheduling problem, for which conventional deterministic solvers can be computationally demanding and may struggle with nonconvexity and coupled constraints. To address this challenge, this paper proposes an Improved Pied Kingfisher Optimization algorithm (IPKO) for microgrid optimal scheduling. The proposed IPKO integrates four complementary enhancement strategies, including Chebyshev-chaotic initialization, multi-source guidance with adaptive perturbation, differential–spiral development, and periodic dynamic adjustment, to improve population diversity, search adaptability, and convergence stability. A grid-connected microgrid scheduling model with a hybrid energy storage system comprising battery storage and hydrogen storage is further developed, with demand response modeled as time-of-use price-driven load shifting. Simulation results demonstrate that the IPKO-based scheduling framework consistently reduces both economic and environmental costs. Compared with representative metaheuristic algorithms over multiple independent runs, IPKO achieves about 4–10% lower mean daily operating cost with significantly reduced solution dispersion, confirming its superior solution quality and robustness for complex microgrid scheduling problems.

Introduction

In recent years, microgrids (MGs) have attracted growing attention as an effective paradigm for integrating distributed energy resources, such as solar and wind power, together with energy storage systems, in the context of the global energy transition1–3. By coordinating diverse energy sources at the local level, microgrids provide a promising approach to increasing renewable energy utilization and reducing reliance on conventional fossil-based generation. However, the inherent intermittency and uncertainty of renewable energy sources pose significant challenges to the secure, economical, and stable operation of microgrid systems4,5. In particular, mismatches between renewable generation and load demand may lead to inefficient resource utilization and increased operating costs if not properly managed6–8. To address these challenges, microgrid scheduling and optimization play a critical role in coordinating power generation, storage, and consumption across different time intervals, thereby enabling efficient resource allocation and ensuring system reliability9,10.

With the increasing penetration of renewable energy sources (RES), particularly wind and solar power, energy storage technologies have become essential for mitigating the mismatch between generation and demand11,12. Conventional battery energy storage systems (BESS) are widely deployed due to their fast response and high efficiency. Li et al.13 presented a battery management system design for grid energy storage with real-time validation, while Mancarella et al.14 provided an editorial overview of BESS for net-zero power systems and markets, underscoring its growing role in system flexibility. However, limited energy density and self-discharge characteristics constrain their suitability for long-term energy storage, especially in microgrids with high renewable penetration15–17.

To overcome these limitations, hydrogen energy storage (HES) has emerged as a promising complementary solution18–20. By converting surplus electrical energy into hydrogen through electrolysis and enabling long-term storage with high energy density, hydrogen-based systems provide additional operational flexibility for renewable-rich microgrids21–23. In recent years, increasing attention has been devoted to hydrogen-integrated microgrids, where HES is often combined with BESS to exploit their complementary characteristics across different time scales. Qi et al.24 investigated hybrid hydrogen–battery storage configurations and highlighted their time-scale complementarities, while Abdelghany et al.25 demonstrated that coordinated scheduling of battery and hydrogen storage improves economic performance and power balance in grid-connected wind–solar microgrids. Abomazid et al.26 further showed that integrating battery storage with photovoltaic-based hydrogen systems helps reduce hydrogen production fluctuations and operational costs.

Despite these advances, most existing studies primarily focus on component-level coordination or local energy management strategies27,28, whereas system-level optimal scheduling of hydrogen-integrated microgrids under multiple practical operational constraints has not been sufficiently investigated.

In microgrid scheduling studies, relying solely on supply-side regulation is often insufficient to cope with renewable intermittency and demand variability. As a result, demand response (DR) is commonly introduced as an auxiliary mechanism to enhance operational flexibility29–32. In particular, price-based DR driven by time-of-use (TOU) tariffs enables partial load shifting over time, thereby alleviating peak demand and reducing reliance on grid dependence33,34. For instance, Nikzad and Samimi35 incorporated price-based DR into a stochastic bi-level scheduling framework, while Hwang Goh et al.36 and Bolurian et al.37 demonstrated that moderate load shifting contributes to improved economic performance in grid-connected microgrids.

Microgrid scheduling coordinates heterogeneous resources under practical constraints and is inherently nonlinear and nonconvex due to coupled device dynamics such as ramping behavior and storage state evolution, time-coupled constraints, and mixed decision variables38–40. These intrinsic characteristics significantly complicate the optimization landscape and limit the effectiveness of conventional gradient-based optimization methods41,42. Consequently, increasing attention has been directed toward metaheuristic optimization approaches, which are better suited for exploring complex and highly constrained scheduling spaces43,44.

Beyond classical baselines such as genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization, and differential evolution that have been widely used for microgrid optimal scheduling45,46, recent studies have increasingly explored newer metaheuristics to improve robustness under nonlinear and time-coupled constraints. Abdelsalam et al.47 developed a Harris hawks optimization (HHO)–based scheduling strategy and demonstrated its effectiveness in handling nonlinear operating constraints in microgrids. Yadav et al.48 investigated grey wolf optimization for isolated microgrid scheduling and reported improved techno-economic performance under practical operational limits. Datta et al.49 compared multiple metaheuristic approaches for managing multi-microgrid systems under adverse operating conditions. In addition, Teo et al.50 applied NSGA-II to the optimal scheduling of a grid-connected microgrid, demonstrating the applicability of metaheuristic optimization methods to microgrid energy management problems. Fathy et al.51 introduced the honey badger algorithm to microgrid scheduling and verified its capability to balance exploration and exploitation for complex energy management tasks. Karthik et al.52 further formulated a multi-objective microgrid scheduling problem and applied a sine–cosine-based optimizer to obtain a set of trade-off solutions across conflicting objectives. Barua et al.53 proposed a Lévy-flight-enhanced arithmetic optimization approach, highlighting continued interest in improving convergence behavior and solution quality for microgrid operation.

Despite the progress achieved by metaheuristic-based microgrid scheduling studies, performance is still frequently constrained by search efficiency, convergence stability, and robustness, especially in microgrids with hybrid energy storage and time-coupled operating constraints. To address these issues, this paper proposes an improved Pied Kingfisher Optimization algorithm (IPKO) and applies it to the optimal scheduling of a microgrid equipped with a hybrid energy storage system under TOU price-based demand response.

Despite the progress achieved by metaheuristic-based microgrid scheduling studies, existing algorithms may still face challenges in solving scheduling problems involving hybrid energy storage systems and time-coupled operational constraints. In such problems, the coordinated operation of BESS and HES, renewable generation, controllable units, grid exchange, and demand response leads to a highly nonlinear and nonconvex search space. As a result, conventional metaheuristic algorithms may suffer from insufficient population diversity, premature convergence, or unstable convergence behavior. Therefore, improving the exploration–exploitation balance and convergence robustness remains necessary for hybrid-energy-storage microgrid scheduling.

Microgrid system description and modeling

Microgrid system configuration

The configuration of the studied microgrid is shown in Fig. 1. The system is interconnected with the main grid through an AC–DC interface converter, enabling bidirectional power exchange. The microgrid comprises renewable sources, including photovoltaic (PV) generation and wind turbines (WT), as well as dispatchable units such as a diesel engine (DE) and a micro gas turbine (MT). In addition, a hydrogen energy system (HES)—including an electrolyzer (EL), a hydrogen storage tank, and a fuel cell (FC)—together with a battery energy storage system (BESS), is integrated. All components are connected to the common bus through appropriate power electronic converters to support coordinated microgrid operation.

Battery energy storage system model

To enhance the operational flexibility of the microgrid, a BESS is incorporated to balance power fluctuations and provide short-term energy buffering. For modeling simplicity, the terminal voltage of the BESS is assumed to remain constant during charging and discharging processes, and the energy loss inside the battery is characterized by a self-discharge factor and charge–discharge efficiency.

Let EB t and EB t + 1 denote the stored energy of the BESS at time intervals t and t + 1, respectively. The charging and discharging processes of the BESS are modeled by the following discrete-time equations:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{{t+1}}^{{\mathrm{B}}}=\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}{l}} {(1 - \sigma )E_{t}^{{\mathrm{B}}}{\mathrm{+}}{\eta _{\mathrm{c}}}P_{t}^{{{\mathrm{B,c}}}}\Delta t,}&{{\mathrm{charging}},} \\ {(1 - \sigma )E_{t}^{{\mathrm{B}}} - \frac{{P_{t}^{{{\mathrm{B,d}}}}{\mkern 1mu} \Delta t}}{{{\eta _{\mathrm{d}}}}},}&{{\mathrm{discharging}}.} \end{array}} \right.$$\end{document}

where PB, c t and PB, d t represent the charging and discharging power of the BESS, respectively. σ denotes the self-discharge rate, ηc and ηd represent the charging and discharging efficiencies, and Δt denotes the scheduling time step.

The state of charge (SOC) of the BESS is updated according to7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{SOC}}_{{t+1}}^{{}}=\frac{{E_{{t+1}}^{{\mathrm{B}}}}}{{E_{{\hbox{max} }}^{{\mathrm{B}}}}}.$$\end{document}

where EB max denotes the rated energy capacity of the BESS. Accordingly, SOCt+1 represents the state of charge at time interval t + 1, which reflects the dynamic evolution of the energy storage state during charging and discharging operations.

Economic operating cost

In the microgrid scheduling problem, the total operating cost consists of multiple cost components arising from the operation of controllable generation units, power exchange with the utility grid, unit start-up and shutdown actions, and hydrogen energy system operation. These cost components jointly reflect the economic performance of the microgrid under different operating conditions.

Accordingly, the total operating cost over the scheduling horizon can be expressed as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{{}}^{{{\mathrm{op}}}}=\sum\limits_{{t=1}}^{{24}} {(C_{t}^{{{\mathrm{gen}}}}+C_{t}^{{{\mathrm{RES}}}}+C_{t}^{{{\mathrm{grid}}}}+C_{t}^{{{{\mathrm{H}}_2}}})} .$$\end{document}

Generation operating cost

The operating cost of controllable generation units mainly consists of fuel consumption cost and operation and maintenance cost. Since the fuel cell generates electricity by consuming stored hydrogen, its operating cost is primarily associated with operation and maintenance expenses.

Renewable generation operating cost and curtailment penalty

Although RES do not incur fuel consumption, their operation involves maintenance-related costs that are commonly modeled as being proportional to their actual power outputs.

Let Ppv t and Pwt t denote the actual output power of the PV and WT units at time interval t, respectively. Electricity trading cost

Environmental and pollution mitigation cost

Micro-source output and ramping constraints

To ensure the secure and feasible operation of the microgrid, the active power outputs of all micro-sources are subject to technical operating constraints. These constraints include output capacity limits and ramping rate limits, which reflect both physical limitations and operational flexibility.

where Pi, t denotes the active power of unit i at time interval t, and Pmin i and Pmax i represent its minimum and maximum operating limits, respectively.

Ramping power constraints

To capture the dynamic operational characteristics of controllable generation and conversion units, ramping rate constraints are imposed on the diesel engine, micro gas turbine, fuel cell, and the electrolyzer. These constraints limit the rate of change in output power between two consecutive time intervals:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}{l}} {\frac{{{P_{k,t}} - {P_{k,t - 1}}}}{{\Delta t}} \leqslant R_{k}^{{\mathrm{R}}},} \\ {\frac{{{P_{k,t - 1}} - {P_{k,t}}}}{{\Delta t}} \leqslant R_{k}^{{\mathrm{F}}},} \end{array}} \right.\quad k \in \{ {\mathrm{DE}},{\mathrm{MT}},{\mathrm{FC,EL}}\} .$$\end{document}

where RR k and RF k denote the ramp-up and ramp-down limits of unit k, respectively, and Δt is the scheduling time interval.

Microgrid–utility grid power exchange constraint

To maintain power balance and ensure secure operation, the microgrid is allowed to exchange power with the utility grid through the point of common coupling. However, the power exchanged with the main grid is constrained by the capacity limits of the interconnection equipment and distribution lines.

Let Pgrid t denote the power exchanged between the microgrid and the utility grid at time interval t, where a positive value indicates power import from the grid and a negative value represents power export. The grid power exchange is restricted within predefined bounds as follows:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{{\mathrm{g}}}^{{\hbox{min} }} \leqslant P_{t}^{{{\mathrm{grid}}}} \leqslant P_{{\mathrm{g}}}^{{\hbox{max} }}.$$\end{document}

Here, Pmin g and Pmax g represent the minimum and maximum allowable grid exchange power, respectively, which are determined by the rated capacity of the grid connection and operational requirements.

Power balance constraint

To ensure secure and stable operation of the microgrid, the total electricity supply must satisfy the system demand at each scheduling interval. The power balance constraint is formulated by explicitly considering the contributions of all generation units, the bidirectional BESS, the electrolyzer, and the power exchanged with the utility grid.

Specifically, the power balance at time interval t is expressed as:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{t}^{{\mathrm{L}}}+P_{t}^{{{\mathrm{EL}}}}+P_{t}^{{{\mathrm{B,c}}}}=\sum\limits_{{n \in F}} {{P_{n,t}}} +P_{t}^{{{\mathrm{grid}}}}+P_{t}^{{{\mathrm{B,d}}}}.$$\end{document}

where PL t denotes the total electrical load demand, PEL t represents the power consumption of the electrolyzer, and PB, c t and PB, d t denote the charging and discharging power of the BESS, respectively. The set F includes all electricity generation units within the microgrid, namely PV, WT, DE, MT, and FC. Pgrid t denotes the power exchanged with the utility grid, where a positive value indicates power purchase and a negative value indicates power export.

To ensure reliable operation of the microgrid, the constraints of energy storage systems are considered. Both the BESS and the HES are included, while different state and power variables are adopted to reflect their distinct physical characteristics.

For the BESS, the stored electrical energy is constrained by:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{t}^{{{\mathrm{B}},{\mathrm{min}}}} \leqslant E_{t}^{{\mathrm{B}}} \leqslant E_{t}^{{{\mathrm{B}},{\mathrm{max}}}}.$$\end{document}

and its charging and discharging operations satisfy:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}{l}} {\mu _{t}^{{{\mathrm{B}},{\mathrm{ch}}}}+\mu _{t}^{{{\mathrm{B}},{\mathrm{dis}}}} \leqslant 1,} \\ {0 \leqslant P_{t}^{{{\mathrm{B}},{\mathrm{c}}}} \leqslant \mu _{t}^{{{\mathrm{B}},{\mathrm{ch}}}}{P^{{\mathrm{B}},{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{max}}}},} \\ {0 \leqslant P_{t}^{{{\mathrm{B}},{\mathrm{d}}}} \leqslant \mu _{t}^{{{\mathrm{B}},{\mathrm{dis}}}}{P^{{\mathrm{B}},{\mathrm{d}},{\mathrm{max}}}}.} \end{array}} \right.$$\end{document}

Similarly, the hydrogen energy stored in the tank is limited by:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{t}^{{\hbox{min} }} \leqslant {H_t} \leqslant H_{t}^{{\hbox{max} }}.$$\end{document}

Here, PEL t and PFC t denote the hydrogen-related conversion power of the electrolyzer and the fuel cell, respectively. To enable cyclic operation of hybrid storage systems over the scheduling horizon, the terminal storage states at the end of the last scheduling interval are constrained to return to their predefined reserve levels:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{T}^{{\mathrm{B}}}=E_{0}^{{\mathrm{B}}},H_{T}^{{}}=H_{0}^{{}}.$$\end{document}

where EB 0 and H0 are preset reserve levels that serve as the initial states for the next day.

Demand response model

To enhance the operational flexibility of the microgrid and mitigate peak demand, demand response driven by time-of-use (TOU) electricity pricing is incorporated into the scheduling framework. This mechanism reshapes the original load profile by shifting a portion of flexible load among different time periods in response to TOU electricity prices.

Let Dbase tdenote the baseline load at hour t. The actual load after applying demand response is expressed as:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D_t}=D_{t}^{{{\mathrm{base}}}}+\Delta {D_t}.$$\end{document}

where ΔDt represents the net load adjustment induced by price-based demand shifting.

The proportion of load that can be shifted is assumed to depend on the electricity price difference between time periods and is modeled using a logistic function:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho (\Delta \tau )=\frac{\alpha }{{1+{e^{( - \frac{{\Delta \tau - \beta }}{\gamma })}}}}+\delta .$$\end{document}

where Δτ denotes the TOU electricity price difference and α, β, δ, and γ are empirical parameters characterizing the price sensitivity of load shifting. In this logistic formulation, α controls the adjustable range of the transferable load ratio, β determines the price-difference threshold for significant load shifting, γ reflects the response sensitivity to the TOU price difference, and δ denotes the basic response offset. This formulation captures the saturation effect of price-driven load shifting, where the transferable load ratio increases with the price difference but remains bounded.

Based on the transferable load ratio, the load adjustment at each hour is determined by the corresponding price segment and is formulated as:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {D_t}=\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}{l}} { - {\rho _{f \leftarrow p}}{\mkern 1mu} {{\bar {D}}_p}{\mkern 1mu} {\omega _{p,1}} - {\rho _{g \leftarrow p}}{\mkern 1mu} {{\bar {D}}_p}{\mkern 1mu} {\omega _{p,2}},}&{t \in {T_p},} \\ {\;{\rho _{f \leftarrow p}}{\mkern 1mu} {{\bar {D}}_p}{\mkern 1mu} {\omega _{f,1}} - {\rho _{g \leftarrow f}}{\mkern 1mu} {{\bar {D}}_f}{\mkern 1mu} {\omega _{f,2}},}&{t \in {T_f},} \\ {\;{\rho _{g \leftarrow p}}{\mkern 1mu} {{\bar {D}}_g}{\mkern 1mu} {\omega _{g,1}}+{\rho _{g \leftarrow f}}{\mkern 1mu} {{\bar {D}}_f}{\mkern 1mu} {\omega _{g,2}},}&{t \in {T_g}.} \end{array}} \right.$$\end{document}

where‾Dg,‾Df and‾DP denote the average baseline loads of the valley, flat, and peak price segments, respectively; Τg, Tf, and Tp are the corresponding sets of hours for these segments, and ω(.) ∈ [0,1] are weighting coefficients reflecting the practical acceptance level of load shifting in each segment.

Through the above formulation, part of the load is shifted from high-price periods to low-price periods while maintaining the overall energy consumption approximately unchanged. The resulting demand response–adjusted load profile is subsequently incorporated into the microgrid power balance constraints and optimization process, thereby introducing additional demand-side flexibility for the proposed IPKO-based optimal scheduling strategy.

Improved Pied Kingfisher Optimization Algorithm

Mechanism and limitations of the original PKO algorithm

The Pied Kingfisher Optimization (PKO) algorithm is a nature-inspired metaheuristic motivated by the hovering, perching, underwater-diving, and cooperative hunting behaviors of pied kingfishers. Its search process follows a biologically inspired sequence: individuals first perform wide-range global exploration through perching–hovering movements, then conduct targeted local exploitation by diving into promising regions, and finally engage in a symbiotic cultivation process that facilitates information exchange and enables escape from local optima. Through this behavioral flow, PKO establishes a functional balance among exploration, exploitation, and local-escape capability.

Despite these advantages, the original PKO algorithm exhibits several structural limitations when applied to complex optimization problems. Random population initialization may result in insufficient diversity, weakening early-stage exploration. Moreover, the search dynamics rely on a limited set of individual interactions, which can constrain global information exchange and lead to the population toward inefficient search trajectories. In addition, local exploitation can become overly aggressive as the search space narrows, increasing the risk of premature convergence. Furthermore, the evolutionary process follows a relatively fixed rhythm, which limits its ability to adapt to different optimization stages or dynamically changing landscapes. As a result, PKO may exhibit unstable search behavior and reduced robustness, particularly in complex and high-dimensional optimization problems. To address these limitations, the improvement strategies incorporated in the proposed IPKO are presented in Sect. 4.2.

The proposed IPKO algorithm

Chebyshev chaotic mapping-based initialization strategy

In most population-based optimization algorithms, the quality of the initial population strongly affects global search capability and subsequent convergence behavior. Conventional random initialization methods typically sample individuals uniformly at random, which may result in uneven coverage of the solution space and insufficient population diversity, thereby degrading the efficiency of subsequent exploration and exploitation. To alleviate this issue, the proposed IPKO incorporates a Chebyshev chaotic mapping mechanism to enhance the ergodicity of the initial population distribution.

The Chebyshev chaotic sequence is generated as:32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x_{n+1,j}}=\cos (a \cdot \arccos ({x_{n,j}})).$$\end{document}

where a is the control parameter. When a ≥ 2, the mapping exhibits chaotic behavior; in this study, a = 4 is adopted. For each dimension j, the initial value x1,j is independently drawn from a uniform distribution U (0,1). Here, n denotes the sequence index of the Chebyshev map. For each dimension j, the chaotic values {xn,j}N n = 1 are generated by iterating Eq. (32) with n = 1, 2, …, N-1, where N is the population size.

The chaotic sequence is then mapped onto the feasible search space [lbj, ubj] of the j-th dimension as:33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X_{i,j}}=|{x_{i,j}}|(u{b_j} - l{b_j})+l{b_j}.$$\end{document}

where Xi, j denotes the initial position of the i-th individual in the j-th dimension, and lbj, ubj are the corresponding lower and upper bounds. The absolute operation maps the chaotic values from [− 1,1] to [0,1], after which they are linearly scaled to [lbj, ubj]. For each dimension j, the first N chaotic values {x1,j, …, xN,j} are used to initialize the j-th components of the population. Specifically, the value allocated to the i-th individual is denoted by xi,j (i.e., xi,j= xn,j with n = i), and it is mapped to the feasible interval [lbj, ubj] via Eq. (33) to obtain Xi,j.

By leveraging the ergodicity of the Chebyshev map, the initial population becomes more broadly distributed across the solution space, effectively avoiding local clustering caused by purely random initialization. This strategy increases population diversity and provides richer starting points for the subsequent exploration and exploitation phases, thereby accelerating convergence and improving overall search accuracy. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the Chebyshev chaotic sequence and its histogram demonstrate broad coverage of the numerical space and a high-entropy distribution.

Dual-random individual guidance and Lévy mixed perturbation strategy

In the original PKO, the search update relies on the interaction between an individual and only one randomly selected companion. Although this single-source difference information provides basic exploratory capability, it leads to a directional bias and restricts the ability to cover a wider search region. Specifically, two limitations arise. First, relying solely on the difference with one random individual constrains the search trajectory to the local distribution of surrounding individuals, making the algorithm prone to blind spots in high-dimensional spaces where the global landscape is more complex. Second, the lack of effective integration of information from the current global best solution causes the update direction to lose global guidance, resulting in blind or inefficient exploration; as iterations proceed, population diversity decays and individuals easily fall into local optima.

Furthermore, the disturbance-generation mechanism in the original PKO does not account for the dynamic balance between exploration and exploitation. Without an adaptive perturbation mechanism that adjusts its behavior over iterations, the algorithm struggles to shift appropriately between long-range global exploration and fine-grained local refinement, limiting its performance in complex search environments.

To address these issues, the proposed IPKO incorporates a dual-random individual guidance mechanism together with a Lévy mixed perturbation strategy. By integrating information from two independently and randomly selected individuals and combining it with Lévy-based long-tailed perturbations, the algorithm enhances multi-source guidance, increases search diversity, and improves the adaptability and robustness of the exploration process.

The updated position is expressed as:34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{i}^{{{\mathrm{new}}}}={X_i}+Levy(D) \odot \left[ {0.5({X_i} - {X_{r1}})+0.5({X^*} - {X_i}+{X_{r2}} - {X_{r1}})} \right]+s\xi .$$\end{document}

where Xnew idenotes the updated position of the i-th individual; X* denotes the current global best position. The mapped values Xi, j form the initial population matrix X, where the i-th individual is denoted by the position vector Xi∈ℝD in the subsequent update equations. Xrk denotes an individual randomly selected from the current population. The indices rk are independently re-sampled at each iteration and for each update step, and thus do not correspond to fixed or shared individuals across different update equations. The binary switching variable s ∈ {0,1}is activated with a small probability (about 0.05) to introduce occasional extra disturbance, and ξ ∼ N (0,1) is implemented as a scalar Gaussian perturbation shared across dimensions. ⊙ is defined as the element-wise multiplication operator between vectors.

The Lévy step vector in D dimensions is generated by the Mantegna method as:35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Levy(D)=\frac{{\sigma u}}{{|v{|^{1/\beta }}}},\quad u,v\sim N{(0,1)^D},\quad \sigma ={\left[ {\frac{{\Gamma (1+\beta )\sin (\pi \beta /2)}}{{\Gamma \left( {(1+\beta )/2} \right)\beta \cdot {\mkern 1mu} {2^{(\beta - 1)/2}}}}} \right]^{1/\beta }}.$$\end{document}

where β = 1.5 denotes the characteristic parameter of Lévy flights, σ is the scale parameter computed accordingly. The absolute value, power, and division are applied element-wise.

By combining multi-source guidance and Lévy-based perturbations, this strategy improves the adaptability of the search process, expands the feasible search region, and enhances convergence stability. It effectively avoids premature clustering and strengthens the algorithm’s ability to balance global exploration and local refinement.

Differential–spiral fusion development strategy

In the original PKO, the development-stage update mechanism is primarily guided by a single information source and constructs its update direction solely based on the current best solution. This single-source guidance restricts the diversity of directional information and confines the search trajectory to a narrow region around the best position. Moreover, the linear difference terms used in traditional strategies may not sufficiently match the nonlinear distribution of promising regions during the development phase, leading to incomplete coverage of potential optimal areas.

To address these limitations, the proposed IPKO introduces a differential–spiral fusion development strategy that enhances multi-source information utilization and improves adaptability to nonlinear search landscapes. By adding a pairwise difference term between two randomly selected individuals (Xr3 − Xr4) alongside the core term (X* − Xi), the strategy embeds richer multi-source spatial information into the update direction and constructs a multi-source local information interaction component, compensating for the deficiency of relying on a single information source in the original development mechanism and enhancing the directional diversity of local search. This fusion expands the variety of potential search directions, prevents premature convergence near the best solution, and strengthens the robustness of the development process.

To further enhance nonlinear search capability, a spiral perturbation mechanism is integrated into the update equation. The spiral term generates a dimension-wise helical perturbation around the best position using a randomly angular vector and radius-scaling vector k. This mechanism provides structured and fine-grained local refinement while maintaining stochastic adaptability, enabling both precision and flexibility during the development phase.

The update equation of this strategy is expressed as:36\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{i}^{{{\mathrm{new}}}}={X_i}+F({X^*} - {X_i})+F({X_{{r_3}}} - {X_{{r_4}}})+0.05spiral.$$\end{document}

where the spiral perturbation is defined as:37\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$spiral=k \odot \cos (\theta ) \odot ({X^*} - {X_i})+k \odot \sin (\theta ) \odot ({X_{{r_5}}} - {X_i}),\qquad k={e^{0.1\theta }}.$$\end{document}

where the parameter F denotes a stochastic contraction–expansion factor drawn from F∼U (0,1), which regulates the relative influence of the differential and spiral components. The variable θ denotes a random angular vector whose elements are independently sampled from U (0,2π). The factor k from acts as a dimension-wise radius-scaling term that controls the amplitude of the spiral perturbation, thereby enabling both near-field and relatively larger-range refinement. The term spiral represents the nonlinear helical perturbation used to achieve fine-grained local exploitation.

Overall, the differential–spiral fusion strategy combines multi-source differential information with nonlinear helical perturbations to enhance directional diversity and improve the precision and stability of the developmental search stage. This design strengthens local refinement capability while maintaining adaptability, ultimately improving convergence accuracy and robustness during later iterations.

Periodic dynamic adjustment and hybrid perturbation symbiosis strategy

In the original PKO, the symbiotic stage provides limited and occasionally triggered positional updates, which may lead to search stagnation and inefficiency, especially in later iterations when population diversity decreases and individuals tend to cluster prematurely. Moreover, this fixed update pattern cannot accommodate the dynamic requirement of alternating “exploration–exploitation” intensities at different evolutionary stages. Even when updates are triggered, the strategy still relies on the absolute difference between two random individuals, resulting in a single-source information exchange pattern that is insufficient to stimulate effective cooperative search within the population.

To address these issues, the proposed IPKO introduces a periodic dynamic adjustment mechanism combined with a hybrid perturbation symbiosis strategy. This approach extends the idea of multi-source fusion by incorporating periodic modulation factors to dynamically regulate perturbation intensity and step size throughout the evolutionary process. By exploiting the sinusoidal periodicity, the search step size oscillates rhythmically between strong and weak phases, thereby broadening exploration in early iterations and gradually reducing perturbation magnitude in later iterations to promote convergence.

Furthermore, a contraction–expansion differential term together with a Lévy-flight-based perturbation is adopted. This design avoids over-reliance on a single absolute difference and enhances robustness against search stagnation. By additionally constructing a pairwise-difference term from two randomly selected individuals, the update rule injects cooperative information from multiple sources, preventing isolated search trajectories and improving the overall adaptability of the symbiotic stage.

Specifically, when the proposed symbiosis update is activated, two randomly controlled update forms are defined as:38\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{i}^{{{\mathrm{new}}}}=\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}{l}} {{X_i}+\varepsilon ({X_{{r_6}}} - {X_{{r_7}}})\left( {(1+c \cdot \sin \left( {2\pi \frac{t}{T}} \right)} \right),}&{{\mathrm{rand}}<0.5,} \\ {{X_i}+G \cdot \left( {Levy(D) \odot ({X_{{r_6}}} - {X_{{r_7}}})} \right)\left( {1+c \cdot \sin \left( {2\pi \frac{t}{T}} \right)} \right),}&{{\mathrm{rand}} \geqslant 0.5.} \end{array}} \right..$$\end{document}

where the variable ε denotes a stochastic expansion–contraction factor drawn from ε∼ U (0,1), which controls the magnitude of the differential perturbation. The parameter G denotes a fixed contraction factor, regulating the strength of Lévy-flight-based perturbation. The variable t denotes the current iteration index, and T denotes the maximum number of iterations. The term sin(2πt/T) denotes the periodic modulation factor that dynamically adjusts the search intensity within the range [− 1,1], while the coefficient c denotes the amplitude-scaling factor controlling the influence of the periodic modulation on step length.

Overall, this strategy integrates periodic dynamic modulation with hybrid perturbation and multi-source differential symbiosis, enhancing intra-population cooperative search and improving the stability and convergence performance of IPKO in complex optimization scenarios.

Overall procedure of the proposed IPKO algorithm

The overall procedure of the proposed IPKO algorithm is illustrated in Fig. 3. As shown in Fig. 3, the algorithm begins with population initialization based on Chebyshev chaotic mapping, followed by fitness evaluation and determination of the initial global best solution. At each iteration, the decay factor and predatory efficiency are updated to regulate the search dynamics. The population then proceeds to the exploration–exploitation module, after which candidate solutions are evaluated with boundary handling and fitness computation, and both individual solutions and the global best are updated using a greedy selection rule. Subsequently, a commensal symbiosis stage is executed, with a PE-controlled periodic-modulated hybrid-perturbation update is applied to strengthen population interaction and alleviate stagnation. The iteration continues until the stopping criterion is met.

In addition, the evolution of exploration and exploitation proportions is examined to illustrate how IPKO adaptively regulates its search behavior over iterations.

As shown in Fig. 6, the parameter-space distributions and contour plots indicate that IPKO effectively drives the population toward the global optimum while mitigating excessive trapping in local optima. The population search histories further show a progressive concentration of individuals around the optimal region as iterations proceed, indicating a stable convergence process. Meanwhile, the first-dimension trajectory exhibits pronounced fluctuations in the early stage, followed by reduced oscillations in later iterations, suggesting a clear transition from global exploration to local exploitation.

Convergence and robustness analysis on the CEC2017 benchmark

Convergence performance analysis

To evaluate the convergence behavior of the proposed IPKO algorithm, experiments were conducted on six representative benchmark functions from the CEC2017 test suite, including unimodal F1, multimodal F10, hybrid F12 and F14, and composition functions F21 and F26. The convergence performance of IPKO was compared with the following algorithms: PKO, IVY, PSO, SSA, GOOSE, WOA, and DBO.

As shown in Fig. 8, IPKO achieves faster convergence and lower final fitness values across most test functions. On the unimodal function F1, IPKO converges rapidly and exhibits a stable descent. On the multimodal and hybrid functions, IPKO maintains sustained improvement across iterations, which helps mitigate premature convergence. For the more challenging composition functions, several comparison algorithms stagnate at relatively high fitness levels, whereas IPKO continues to refine solutions and reaches lower fitness values. Overall, these results indicate that IPKO provides stable convergence behavior, strong search capability, and improved resistance to stagnation and premature convergence across diverse optimization scenarios.

During 0–8 h, wind power and grid import are preferentially utilized to supply the load, while surplus energy is stored in the BESS or converted into hydrogen. In 8–12 h, as wind power decreases and PV generation increases, grid import is reduced and excess renewable energy is allocated to hydrogen production to mitigate renewable curtailment. In these periods, the BESS mainly acts as a short-term buffer to absorb rapid renewable power fluctuations, while the HES converts surplus renewable electricity into hydrogen for longer-term energy storage. During 12–17 h, the fuel cell and BESS are dispatched to reduce the output of high-cost conventional generation units, such as gas turbines and diesel generators. In the 17–21 h peak period, the load demand is met by a coordinated combination of fuel cell generation, BESS discharging, and grid import. Finally, during 21–24 h, the energy storage systems are recharged to restore their predefined reserve levels, completing the daily energy cycle.

The energy variations of the BESS and hydrogen storage shown in Fig. 12 further confirm the effectiveness of the proposed IPKO-based scheduling strategy in achieving efficient energy management and stable multi-energy operation. The dashed line indicates the predefined reserve level imposed by the daily cycling constraint. By leveraging the complementary buffering capability of the BESS and the hydrogen energy chain, the strategy absorbs surplus renewable generation and mitigates curtailment, thereby improving renewable energy utilization. Specifically, the BESS responds quickly to intra-day power fluctuations and peak-load changes, while the HES provides a slower but larger-capacity energy-shifting pathway through the electrolyzer, hydrogen tank, and fuel cell. This complementary operation allows short-term power imbalance and longer-term renewable surplus to be handled simultaneously. Notably, the coordinated dynamics of the BESS and HES ensure that both energy states return to their predefined reserve levels by the end of the day, which serve as the initial states for the next day, satisfying the daily cycling requirement.

Conclusion

This work proposes the IPKO algorithm to enhance population diversity, search adaptability, and convergence stability for highly nonlinear and nonconvex optimization problems, with a particular focus on microgrid scheduling applications. By integrating four complementary enhancement strategies, including chaotic initialization, multi-source guidance with adaptive perturbation, differential and spiral development, and periodic dynamic adjustment, the proposed IPKO achieves an improved exploration–exploitation balance and more robust performance, as verified on benchmark test suites.

The proposed IPKO is further applied to the optimal scheduling of a grid-connected microgrid with hybrid energy storage, comprising a hydrogen energy storage system and a battery energy storage system, where price-based demand response is modeled using time-of-use pricing. Simulation results show that the IPKO-based framework consistently reduces economic and environmental costs. Compared with representative metaheuristic algorithms, it achieves about 4–10% lower average daily operating cost with reduced variability, confirming its superior solution quality and robustness. Overall, the proposed framework provides an effective metaheuristic solution for nonconvex scheduling of microgrids with hybrid energy storage.

Although the proposed framework demonstrates strong performance for the studied scheduling problem, some aspects still deserve further investigation. The current case study is mainly based on deterministic renewable generation and load profiles, and future work will incorporate uncertainty representations for PV, wind, and load demand to enhance the practical applicability and robustness of the scheduling framework. In addition, since the present study focuses on a typical grid-connected microgrid with hybrid energy storage, extending the proposed IPKO-based approach to larger-scale microgrids with more diverse resources will be considered to further evaluate its scalability and generalization capability. Battery degradation modeling can also be incorporated as a lifecycle-related cost term in future long-term scheduling studies.
