Measurement bias for age, sex, and years of education in selected RDoC tasks
Authors: Emily T. Sturm, Anastasia G. Sares, Raana M. Manavi, Maiele E. Minzie, Jazmin M. Diaz, Andrea Mendez-Colmenares, John R. Duffy, Jason R. Tregellas, Don C. Rojas, Jared W. Young, Michael L. Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Measurement bias – systematic errors that lead to inaccuracies in assessing latent constructs – threatens to invalidate analysis and interpretation of ourcome scores collected in the context of clinical trials, longtiudinal research, and other types of neurocognitive studies; however, there has been little effort to examine this issue in tasks recommended by the National Institute of Health’s Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) for neuropsychological research.Method:We aimed to evaluate measurement bias for sex, age, years of education, educational discouragement, underrepresented racial/ethnic identity, and household income in seven RDoC-recommended tasks. We recruited a sample of 320 individuals balanced for race, age, sex, and income. All participants completed a demographic survey and battery of neurocognitive tasks. We used Multiple Indicators Multiple Causes models to assess bias related to these factors.Results:Evidence of negative bias associated with older age was found for the Continuous Performance Task (CPT), and evidence of positive bias with older age was found for the Flanker task. Evidence of positive bias associated with sex, favoring women, was found for the Self-Ordered Pointing Task (SOPT). Additionally, evidence of negative bias associated with years of education was found for the CPT. No evidence of bias was found for the Sternberg task, Probabilistic Learning Task, or Effort-Expenditure for Rewards Task (EEfRT). Overall, we did not find evidence of bias related to income, race, or educational discouragement.Conclusions:Certain tasks in this battery may be biased due to age, sex, and education, suggesting that researchers should be cautious when using these particular tasks as part of a battery in neuropsychological research with diverse populations.

The Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework proposed by the National Institute of Mental Health aims to conceptualize psychopathology with an integrative approach of methods and tools from neuroscience and psychology (Cuthbert, 2022). As part of this initiative, RDoC aims to improve objective, quantitative measures of cognition. In this context, it is essential to have valid measures of individual differences in cognitive and positive valence performance across diverse demographic populations.

Bias is a major concern that has not been properly examined for RDoC measures. In particular, measurement bias – systematic errors that lead to inaccuracies in assessing latent constructs – can compromise the validity and fairness of cognitive assessments (Millsap, 2011). Before discussing possible causes of bias, it is helpful to define the psychometric concept of bias. Measurement bias does not refer to true differences in ability between individuals and groups. Rather, measurement bias exists when scores from individuals in different groups depend on something other than the trait or ability being assessed. Formally, bias exists when the conditional probability of a score depends on group membership after accounting for any true differences in ability (Millsap, 2011). Unrecognized bias could lead to misinterpretation of results, inaccurate and unfair comparisons between individuals and populations, and limited generalizability of findings. Given that factors such as ethnicity, gender, and years of education introduce bias in cognitive testing across numerous RDoC relevant domains (Crane et al., 2004), it is critical that researchers examine demographic bias before using tests in clinical trials, longitudinal research, and other types of neurocognitive studies. Fortunately, examination of bias could help mitigate the risk of scores being influenced by factors that are not associated with genuine differences in ability (Thomas & Duffy, 2023). Researchers can either choose to omit problematic measures or make statistical adjustments when examining outcomes.

In this study, we focused on six key demographic variables that may be a source of bias: age, sex, underrepresented racial/ethnic status, educational discouragement, years of education, and household income. These variables were selected based on their established relevance to cognitive performance in previous literature and their potential for introducing measurement bias. Our primary aim was to determine the individual contributions of these demographic factors to measurement bias in seven RDoC cognitive tasks: the 4-Choice Continuous Performance Test (CPT), Effort-Expenditure for Rewards task (EEfRT), Flanker, N-back, Probabilistic Learning Task (PLT), Self-Ordered Pointing task (SOPT), and Sternberg task.

Potential sources of bias

Age

Understanding age-related bias is crucial to avoiding misinterpretation of scores due to disease-related and/or age-related neurological changes (Crane et al., 2008). In general, older adults experience a decline in some cognitive functions with each decade of life (Deary et al., 2009). Thus, we can expect true differences in some cognitive abilities between younger and older adults, on average, due to aging. However, not all abilities decline at the same rate. For example, reaction time and visuospatial processing may undergo faster decline than working memory (Hartshorne & Germine, 2015). Knowing this, researchers should be aware that the types of tests making up a test battery might, therefore, influence the overall cognitive scores of different age groups. With age also comes certain experiences and habits that may introduce bias. For example, older adults may have biased cognitive scores due to potential test anxiety related to computerized testing (Laguna & Babcock, 1997), or negative expectations based on previous testing struggles (Cavuoto et al., 2020; Zygouris & Tsolaki, 2015). In addition, scoring approaches (Crane et al., 2004) and differences in test strategy (Fox et al., 2014) may also be sources of bias related to age.

Sex

Performance differences between males and females have been observed on various neuropsychological tests (Stoet, 2010), but it is not fully understood whether these performance differences are due to true differences in ability or measurement bias (Jones & Gallo, 2002; Roznowski, 1987). For example, one recent study found that the information and arithmetic subtests of the widely used Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale were negatively biased measures of general intelligence with women performing worse than men (Pezzuti et al., 2020). Authors suggested that the measurement bias could be related to greater encouragement for men in these areas or test topics that are more commonly discussed among men (Pezzuti et al., 2020). Although our cognitive tests did not include content areas like the WAIS-R, there could be an aspect of the content that is still related to enculturated gender norms. Another possible explanation for sex bias in testing is that women are subject to stereotype threat. Specifically, women perform worse on tests due to societal expectations about gender and certain types of ability/achievement proficiencies (Johns et al., 2005; Spencer et al., 1999).

Income

Although socio-economic status (SES) is known to affect cognitive performance, measurement bias is not typically considered as a potential reason for people of lower SES performing worse on cognitive tests (Dean et al., 2019; Farah, 2017; Korous et al., 2022). Notably, older adults with low-income have been shown to score lower on tests of cognitive impairment despite similar rates of dementia diagnosis in clinical interviews, suggesting that household income has a negative biasing effect on cognitive scores (O’Connor et al., 1991). Possible explanations for this effect include motivational barriers (Rodriguez et al., 2023), reward valuation differences (Westbrook et al., 2013), and lack of resources and related stress (Duncan & Magnuson, 2012).

Underrepresented racial/ethnic identity

Race and ethnicity are socially constructed variables; however, people who identify with, and are perceived as, members of different racial and ethnic groups have unique lived experiences. These include familiarity with different cultural phenomena, different values (Ardila, 2005), systemic and individual racism and other forms of hostility, education quality, opportunity, and privilege. Ethnoracial membership should therefore be viewed as a proxy variable; that is, individuals who identify as part of the same racial or ethnic group may have different experiences with cultural, socioeconomic, educational, and racial factors that may impact their cognitive performance and lead to bias (Manly, 2006). In fact, when looking at measurement bias in the context of race, group differences can be reduced when accounting for measurement bias (Jones, 2003). In other cases, measurement bias can be explained by factors such as neighborhood SES and culture (Espino et al., 2001), education quality (Fyffe et al., 2011; Shadlen et al., 2001), and health status (Crane et al., 2008).

Educational discouragement

Experiencing discrimination, such as educational discouragement, can also affect test performance, educational or employment opportunities, and mental health (Berry et al., 2011; Black et al., 2004). Systematic discouragement – such as that faced by engineering students who identify as women of color – is known to impact self-efficacy, which in turn affects test scores (Cavuoto et al., 2020; Zimmerman, 1995).

Years of education

Previous findings relating years of education, or educational attainment, to test bias have shown conflicting results. Some studies suggest little to no evidence for bias (Jones & Gallo, 2001; Jorm et al., 1988), others suggest the presence of bias (Black et al., 1999), and yet others found strong evidence of bias (Ainslie & Murden, 1993; Crane et al., 2004; Teresi et al., 1995). Many of the variations found in these results depend on the test and context. Educational attainment is often viewed in relation to cognitive testing as a component of, or proxy for, SES (Korous et al., 2022). The variable is often treated as a true cause of lower scores, rather than a proxy of differences in cultural and life experiences that could be a source for measurement bias (Mungas et al., 2021).

Methods

Participants

We collected data from 320 participants via the online participant recruitment platform, Prolific.com (Prolific | Online Participant Recruitment for Surveys and Market Research, 2022). Prolific enables researchers to obtain representative samples by selecting participants based on demographic characteristics. The sample was enriched with respect to recruitment of historically marginalized groups, with each historically marginalized group being represented across income, age, and sex assigned at birth. There were no significant differences in income, age, or sex between White and Underrepresented racial/ethnic categories due to intentional recruitment procedures.

Participants were included if they agreed to the consent which required that they were 18–80 years old, fluent in English, had visual acuity of 20/20 (or corrected to 20/20). Further, the consent required that participants had not been diagnosed with severe neurological or psychiatric illness or have met diagnostic criteria for a neurodevelopmental or neurocognitive disorder (apart from attention deficit hyperactivity disorder). Participants were also asked to self-exclude if they were currently intoxicated or currently meet diagnostic criteria for a substance-related disorder (other than tobacco) or currently in treatment for alcohol/drug abuse. Additional information characterizing the health status of the sample is available in the Supplementary Material.

Validity checks were performed using reaction time and accuracy thresholds, similar to other work using cognitive tasks (Moore et al., 2019). We removed all participants from the original data collection who failed two or more validity checks, resulting in 320 participants. This is consistent with recommendations for performance validity exclusions (Lippa, 2018). This study uses demographic and performance data from a previous study (Duffy et al., 2025), which examined the psychometric properties of the seven RDoC tasks but did not investigate the potential presence of measurement bias. See Table 1 and Supplementary Tables S1 and S2 for detailed participant demographics.

Based on the ethnicity information provided by participants when they create an account on Prolific, along with the self-report information in the Qualtrics survey, the final sample consisted of 109 White identifying, 55 Black identifying, 44 Asian identifying, 60 Hispanic identifying, and 52 Other identifying participants. The “Other” category was comprised of self-identification in the following groups: “Mixed” (N = 41), “Middle Eastern” (N = 4), “Native American or Alaskan Native” (N = 7), and “Other” (N = 5). We recruited comparable numbers in each division of the following Prolific categories: reported income level in USD per year (<40k, 40k-80k, >80k), age in years (18–29, 30–49, 50–80), and sex assigned at birth. The U.S. population proportions for income, age, sex, and racial/ethnic identity were not used, rather, the aim was to control for these variables by making sure they were well-represented and equally distributed.

Via Prolific, we recruited participants, collected demographic data for screening, and routed participants to an online survey platform, Qualtrics, (Qualtrics, Provo, UT). Using Qualtrics, we collected written informed consent and participants completed a survey, which asked questions to confirm demographic information, assess educational discouragement, and measure other experiences and traits of interest. Finally, participants clicked a link to the online experiment platform Pavlovia (Open Science Tools, Nottingham, UK), where the tasks were run, and task data were collected. This study was approved by the Internal Review Board at Colorado State University (#2083).

Tasks

The seven tasks chosen for this battery have been described in detail in previous work using this dataset (Duffy et al., 2025). Tasks were administered on laptop or desktop computers as required by the Prolific study sign up page and consent. In brief, three working memory tasks (Sternberg, SOPT, and N-Back), two cognitive control tasks (CPT and Flanker) and two positive valance tasks (PLT and EEfRT) were selected from the RDoC recommendations of tasks for these domains (Figure 1).

The CPT is within the RDoC cognitive systems domain under the construct of working memory. In this paradigm, participants pressed one of four buttons to match the location of a circle that flashed over one of four lines. If circles flashed over all four lines, participants were instructed to withhold the button press. Answers were counted as correct if the correct button was pressed before the next stimulus or if the button press was correctly withheld. The score used for analysis was discriminability (d’).

The EEfRT is a part of the RDoC positive valence systems domain. Subjects were asked to choose between hard and easy tasks for various reward amounts with various probabilities (12, 55, or 88% chance) of receiving the reward. For our study, the hard task consisted of pressing the spacebar as fast as possible with the pinky finger of a non-dominant hand and the easy task involved pressing the same button fewer times with the dominant-hand index finger. The score used for this task was the mean expected value chosen over all trials. The value for a trial was calculated by multiplying the reward chosen by the probability of receiving that reward.

The Flanker task is within the cognitive systems domain under the subconstructs of response selection, inhibition/suppression, and performance monitoring. In each trial, an array of arrows was presented in a straight, horizontal line. The arrow in the center of the array pointed either to the left or right. Participants were instructed to press the left or right arrow key with their dominant hand to indicate their perceived orientation of the stimuli after each block. A trial was counted as correct if the participant pressed the corresponding button before the next inter-trial interval began. The score used for this task was mean accuracy.

The N-back task is within the cognitive systems domain under the working memory construct. Participants were presented with a pseudoword made of three letters in each trial. Participants were instructed to indicate whether the current stimulus matched the one presented n-items before by pressing the spacebar. Difficulty levels ranged from one- to four-back. A response was counted as correct if the participant correctly pressed the spacebar upon seeing a pseudoword that was repeated n-back, or if they correctly withheld from pressing the spacebar when the word was not repeated n-back. The score used for this task was discriminability (d’).

The PLT is within the cognitive systems domain under the reward learning construct. The PLT showed two shapes side by side and instructed subjects to choose the shape that they thought was correct most often. Participants were told that both shapes would sometimes be correct and incorrect, but one shape was correct more often than the other. Participants received trial-by-trial feedback on the screen, which indicated whether they were either correct, incorrect or did not respond in time. Difficulty levels varied based on the likelihood of each shape receiving correct feedback (probability levels of each shape receiving correct feedback = 60/40, 70/30, 80/20, 90/10). The score used for this task was the log of the win-stay win-shift ratio.

The SOPT is within the cognitive systems domain under the working memory construct. The SOPT included abstract images in a set of 6, 9, or 12 images. Participants were presented with a set and instructed to choose an image for each trial without repeating any of their selections. Repeated selections were considered incorrect. After each selection, the locations of the images in the array were randomized. This continued for a number of trials equal to the set size before moving on to the next set. The score used for this task was mean accuracy.

The Sternberg Task is within the cognitive systems domain under the working memory construct. First, participants were shown a grid containing 2–12 letters (the grid spaces without letters were filled with dots), then a variable maintenance period occurred while participants looked at a fixation cross. Finally, participants were presented with a single letter probe and were instructed to identify whether the letter was included in the previous grid. The score used for this task was discriminability (d’).

The seven tasks used for this study are posted exactly as they were administered in the study and are freely available to download from the Pavlovia website. Data is available on the Open Science Framework platform OSF (osf.io; see “data availability” section).

Data analysis

Modeling analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.2, R Core Team 2021) and the lavaan R package (Rosseel, 2012) with full information maximum likelihood estimation using all available data. First, a confirmatory model was run without any demographic variables included, to determine whether the baseline one-factor model had an acceptable fit. A previous factor analysis, utilizing multiple measures from each task, found that all tasks loaded onto a single latent factor (Duffy et al., 2025) labeled “general ability,” referred to as “common cognitive ability” in the current paper. Next, a series of structural equation models called Multiple Indicators Multiple Causes (MIMIC) models (Jak et al., 2014; Woods, 2009) were created, adding the effect of a single demographic variable on common cognitive ability (base model) and then allowing the same demographic variable to directly influence a single test in the battery (MIMIC model). Due to the preferred use of continuous predictors of bias (where appropriate), we opted for the MIMIC model approach to assess bias. Continuous predictors are expected to be more sensitive to bias. The alternative, measurement invariance (MI), approach would require artificial dichotomization of truly continuous variables (e.g., age and education). In each iteration of the MIMIC models, one task and one demographic variable was tested, assuming all other items were invariant to determine whether demographic group membership predicts differential item functioning (DIF). In this approach, DIF is assessed by comparing a baseline model, which does not allow for a direct effect of the demographic variable on the task of interest, with a MIMIC model that does account for the direct effect of the demographic variable on the task. If the MIMIC model shows improved fit with a significant coefficient for the direct effect on the task, bias is indicated. An example of the MIMIC model for the demographic variable age on the Sternberg task is shown in Figure 2.

Model fit is reported using several indices: comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), Akaike information criterion (AIC), and the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) statistics. CFI and TLI values of approximately 0.97 or greater and RMSEA values of approximately 0.05 and lower are typically considered excellent; CFI and TLI values above 0.95 and RMSEA values below 0.08 are considered acceptable (T. A. Brown, 2015; Hu & Bentler, 1999; Schermelleh-Engel et al., 2003). Guidance about model fit was selected according to suggestions in the literature (Schermelleh-Engel et al., 2003). Comparatively lower AIC and BIC values both indicate improved fit among competing models. To determine whether bias existed, we compared the base and MIMIC models using the same demographic variable and task of interest. If AIC or BIC were lower for the MIMIC, and the CFI, TLI, and RMSEA were within the acceptable to good range, the coefficient was then examined for significance. If the MIMIC model had improved fit over the base model as well as a significant direct effect of the demographic variable on the task score, we concluded that bias existed for that task.

The MIMIC approach is limited to analyzing uniform bias (i.e., differences in cognitive test score intercepts) and cannot assess non-uniform bias (i.e., differences in cognitive test score loadings onto the general factor). Non-uniform bias is especially important to examine in the context of different demographic sub-groups. To ensure that non-uniform bias was not present, supplementary analyses were conducted using multiple-group models. These were not included in the main analyses due to concerns about sample size. In these models, the demographic predictors age, years of education, and household income, were discretized to create groups. We tested whether the models constraining loadings to be equal across groups provided more parsimonious fit.

Age was entered into the model as a continuous variable. Sex was entered into the model as a discrete variable (0 = male; 1 = female), years of education was entered as a continuous variable (no degree = 8, high school = 12, associate’s = 14, bachelor’s = 16, master’s = 18, and doctorate = 20), and race and ethnicity was entered as a discrete variable (0 = self-identified Non-Hispanic White; 1 = self-identified Asian, Black, Hispanic, or other). Educational discouragement was measured as the answer to the question “Have you ever been unfairly discouraged by a teacher or advisor from continuing your education?” (“yes” = 1, “no” = 0). Household income was based on the question “What is your current annual household income?” with ten categorical options. The midpoint of the range was used (e.g., $10,001–$30,000 = $20,000) and was scaled before being entered into the model.

Results

Model without demographic variables

The confirmatory single factor model that did not include any demographic variables had good (RMSEA = 0.04) to acceptable fit (CFI = 0.95), with the only one metric, TLI (0.93) suggesting nearly acceptable fit.

Age

The model fit statistics for base and MIMIC models for age are presented in Table 2. In general, the models including age showed relatively poor fit. The exception was the CPT MIMIC (RMSEA = 0.07) and Flanker MIMIC (RMSEA = 0.07) models, which had acceptable fit. Compared to the base model, the MIMIC model for the CPT had improved fit as indicated by AIC (base = 6271.25; MIMIC = 6159.24) and BIC (base = 6271.25; MIMIC = 6245.92). While the RMSEA value for the CPT MIMIC model met the acceptable fit threshold, the CFI and TLI values did not: CFI (base = 0.77; MIMIC = 0.89), TLI (base = 0. 0.68; MIMIC = 0.83), RMSEA (base = 0.10; MIMIC = 0.07). For the EEfRT, the MIMIC model improved fit over base as indicated by AIC (base = 6188.35; MIMIC = 6186.39), but none of these models had acceptable fit. No other measures had improved fit for the MIMIC model over the base model. The MIMIC model of the Flanker had improved fit as indicated by AIC (base = 6188.35; MIMIC = 6161.23) and BIC (base = 6271.25; MIMIC = 6247.91) but only met threshold for acceptable model fit as indicated by RMSEA (base = 0.10; MIMIC = 0.07). The MIMIC model of the PLT only showed improved fit as indicated by AIC (base = 6188.35; MIMIC = 6188.15) but did not meet threshold for good fit by any other measure. The MIMIC model of the Sternberg had improved fit as indicated by AIC (base = 6188.35; MIMIC = 6181.12) and BIC (base = 6271.25; MIMIC = 6267.79), although no models met threshold for acceptable or good fit. The MIMIC model of the N-back and SOPT tasks did not improve fit over any base models and did not meet threshold for acceptable or good fit.

The fully standardized regression coefficients (βstd) for age are presented in Supplementary Table S3. The direct effect of age on cognitive task score was significant for the CPT (βstd = −0.30, p < .001), EEfRT (βstd = −0.12, p < .05), Flanker (βstd = 0.33, p < .001), and Sternberg (βstd = 0.18, p < .01). Most of the coefficients of the indirect effect of age on cognitive task scores were negative with varying effect sizes (βstd = −0.08 – −0.21), and significance (p = .002 – < .001), except for the CPT MIMIC model, which did not have a significant indirect effect of age (βstd = −0.07, p = .10). The total effect of age on cognitive task score was significant for CPT (βstd = −0.37, p < .001), EEfRT (βstd = −0.20, p < .001), Flanker (βstd = 0.11, p = .04), and N-Back (βstd = −0.15, p < .001). It is important to note that the coefficient for the Flanker was positive while all other coefficients were negative.

Sex

The model fit statistics for base and MIMIC models for sex are presented in Table 3. Unlike the models with age, the models including sex showed acceptable or nearly acceptable fit (CFI & TLI = 0.91 – 0.95) to good fit (RMSEA = 0.04 for all base and MIMIC models). The MIMIC model of the SOPT improved fit as indicated by the AIC (base = 6202.11; MIMIC = 6199.98) but only met threshold for acceptable model fit as indicated by CFI (base = 0.94; MIMIC = 0.96).

The fully standardized regression coefficients (βstd) for sex are presented in Supplementary Table S4. In this sample, the coefficients for the effect of sex on common cognitive ability were all negative (with men performing better). They were also significant for the base models (βstd = − 0.14, p = .05), and the MIMIC models for Flanker (βstd = − 0.16, p = .025), SOPT (βstd = − 0.17, p = .02), and Sternberg (βstd = − 0.14, p = .05). However, the direct effect of sex on cognitive task score was positive and significant in the MIMIC model for the SOPT (βstd = 0.11, p = .04), meaning that scores were biased with women performing better for this task. The indirect effect of sex on cognitive task score was significant and negative in the base model for the CPT (βstd = −0.8, p = .05) and N-Back (βstd = −0.9, p = .05), and in the MIMIC model for the Flanker (βstd = −0.07, p = .03) and SOPT (βstd = −0.08, p = .02). The total effect of sex on cognitive task score was only significant for the MIMIC model of the CPT (βstd = −0.13; p = .02), favoring men again.

Household income

The model fit statistics for base and MIMIC models for household income are presented in Table 4. In general, the models including household income for all tasks showed acceptable or nearly acceptable fit (CFI & TLI = 0.94–0.96) to good fit (RMSEA = 0.03 – 0.04). No MIMIC models including household income showed improvement over the base models as indicated by AIC or BIC.

The fully standardized regression coefficients (βstd) for household income are presented in Supplementary Table S5. In this sample, the coefficients for the effect of household income on common cognitive ability were all positive but not significant (βstd = 0.01 – 0.04, p = .57– .88). The direct effect of household income on cognitive task score was not significant in any of the MIMIC models (βstd = −0.05 – 0.07, p = .17– .94). The indirect effect of household income on cognitive task score was not significant (βstd = 0.00 – 0.02, p = .58 −.88). The total effect of household income on cognitive task score was also not significant (βstd = −0.02 – 0.08, p = .17– .94).

Underrepresented racial/ethnic group

The model fit statistics for base and MIMIC models for underrepresented racial and ethnic groups are presented in Table 5. The base and MIMIC models of the CPT, Flanker, N-back, PLT, SOPT, and Sternberg measured by CFI and RMSEA met threshold for acceptable or good fit (CFI base = 0.95, CFI MIMIC = 0.95 – 0.96; RMSEA base = 0.04, RMSEA MIMIC = 0.04). The MIMIC model of the EEfRT met all these criteria (CFI base = 0.95, MIMIC = 0.97; RMSEA base = 0.04, MIMIC = 0.04) and had good fit for TLI in the MIMIC model (base = 0.93, MIMIC = 0.95), which showed improved fit as indicated by AIC (base = 6205.79, MIMIC = 6204.17).

The fully standardized regression coefficients (βstd) are presented in Supplementary Table S6. In this sample, the standardized coefficients for the effect of underrepresented on common cognitive ability were negative and not significant (βstd = −0.06 – −0.02, p = .44 – .80). The direct effect of underrepresented racial and ethnic group on cognitive task score was trending (an underrepresented advantage) for the MIMIC model of the EEfRT (βstd = 0.10, p = 0.06), but this effect was otherwise not significant in any of the MIMIC models (βstd = −0.06 – 0.2, p = .23 – .79). The indirect effects of underrepresented racial and ethnic group membership on cognitive task score were also not significant (βstd = −0.04 – −0.01, p = .44 – .80). The total effect of underrepresented racial and ethnic group membership on cognitive task score was not significant (βstd = −0.07 – 0.09, p = .12 – .93).

Education discouraged

The model fit statistics for base and MIMIC models for education discouraged are presented in Table 6. In general, the models including education discouraged showed poor fit for all tasks, except when measured by RMSEA, which showed good fit for both base and MIMIC models (RMSEA base = 0.05, RMSEA MIMIC = 0.04 – 0.05). The MIMIC models for the EEfRT and PLT also improved fit over base as indicated by the AIC (EEfRT base = 5860.50, EEfRT MIMIC = 5858.96; PLT base = 5860.50, PLT MIMIC = 5859.42).

The fully standardized regression coefficients (βstd) are presented in Supplementary Table S7. In this sample, the standardized coefficients for the effect of education discouraged on common cognitive ability were all negative (favoring non-discouraged) but not significant (βstd = −0.1 – −0.04, p = .16 – .55). The direct effect of education discouraged on cognitive task score in the MIMIC model for the EEfRT task was trending toward significance (βstd = 0.10, p = .06), but was otherwise not significant for any task (βstd = −0.10 – 0.07, p = .08 – .51). The indirect effect of education discouraged on cognitive task score was not significant for any task (βstd = −0.06 – −0.02, p = .17 – .55). The total effect of education discouraged on cognitive task score was negative (favoring non-discouraged) and only significant in the MIMIC model for the PLT task (βstd = −0.11, p = .04).

Years of education

The model fit statistics for base and MIMIC model for years of education are presented in Table 7. In general, the models including years of education showed either acceptable or good fit for both base and MIMIC models across all tasks as indicated by RMSEA (base = 0.06, MIMIC = 0.04 – 0.06), but less than acceptable fit measured by the CFI and TLI. The MIMIC model of the CPT uniquely met the threshold for acceptable fit measured by CFI (base = 0.89, MIMIC = 0.96) and nearly acceptable fit for TLI in the MIMIC model (0.94). The CPT MIMIC model also had improved fit as indicated by AIC (base = 6145.96, MIMIC = 6132.49), and BIC (base = 6228.66, MIMIC = 6218.95). The MIMIC model of the Flanker improved over base as indicated by AIC (base = 6145.96, MIMIC = 6142.03) and BIC (base = 6228.66, MIMIC = 6228.48). The MIMIC model improved fit as indicated by AIC for the Sternberg (base = 6145.96, MIMIC = 6145.26).

The fully standardized regression coefficients (βstd) are presented in Supplementary Table S8. In this sample, the standardized coefficients for the effect of years of education on common cognitive ability were nearly all negative and nonsignificant (βstd = −0.05 – −0.01, p = .46 – .87), except for the MIMIC model of the CPT which was positive and nonsignificant (βstd = 0.09, p = .24). The direct effect of years of education on CPT score was negative and significant (βstd = −0.21, p < 0.001), while it was positive and significant (βstd = 0.13, p = 0.01) for the Flanker. The remaining task MIMIC models were nonsignificant (βstd = 0.01 – 0.09, p = .1 – 1). The indirect effect of years of education on cognitive task score was not significant for any task (βstd = −0.02 – 0.05, p = .24 – .87). The total effect of years of education on cognitive task score was negative and significant for the CPT (βstd = −0.16, p = .005) but was positive and just above the non-significant threshold for the Flanker (βstd = 0.11, p = .052).

Non-uniform bias

To ensure that non-uniform bias was not present, supplementary analyses were conducted using multiple-group models. In these models, the demographic predictors were discretized to create groups. Using the groups, we tested whether the models constraining loadings to be equal across groups provided more parsimonious fit. In all cases, except years of education (which had lower BIC but not AIC), models that constrained loadings to be equal between groups demonstrated better fit statistics (lower AIC and BIC) compared to unconstrained models. Based on these results, we proceeded with the analyses under the assumption that non-uniform bias was absent (at least based on a course discretization of groups). These results are reported in Supplementary Table S9.

Discussion

In this study, we used MIMIC modeling to determine whether RDoC-recommended measures produce biased estimates of common cognitive ability. We determined the individual contributions of certain demographic factors to possible measurement bias in seven RDoC cognitive tasks. Overall, results do not provide evidence of bias for income, race, or educational discouragement. Some evidence of bias related to age, sex, and years of education was observed, however. We discuss each of these in detail below.

Measurement bias associated with age

Due to the generally poor fit of the models including age, interpretation must be made cautiously. Nevertheless, this poor fit is almost certainly due to bias. The CPT, in particular, was directly affected by age even after accounting for common cognitive ability. For the CPT, the MIMIC model showed substantially improved model fit by both relevant measures, acceptable fit indicated by RMSEA, and the direct effect of age on task score was significant and of moderate size. Based on the values, this test is a potentially biased measure of overall cognitive ability when participants of different ages were compared, such that older adults perform worse on the task.

Interestingly, the Sternberg and Flanker also showed improved model fit for the MIMIC model and significant direct effects of age on task score. The direct effect of age on task scores, however, was in the opposite direction, (i.e., older age improved cognitive scores). It is possible that the inclusion of the CPT in the measurement model biases the common cognitive ability measurement to the point where including the direct effect of age on most other tests serves to correct for this bias. Regardless, it is clear that age has highly variable effects on the cognitive tasks examined, and thus treating age as a direct predictor of general ability alone is ill-advised. Although several studies have found natural declines in cognitive functioning with age (Deary et al., 2009; Lenehan et al., 2015), few studies have investigated the potential for measurement bias when assessing cognition in different age groups, beyond cohort effects.

One possible explanation is that the CPT strongly relies on cognitive and motor abilities that are more affected by aging in comparison to other tasks. The version of the CPT used in the current study uses backward masking to increase the strain on vigilance (Bhakta & Young, 2017), a cognitive domain particularly affected by aging (Thomson & Hasher, 2017; Vallesi et al., 2021). The CPT also requires the use of all four fingers of one hand while the Flanker, Sternberg, N-Back, and PLT can be completed with one finger or one finger from each hand. Additionally, the SOPT uses a mouse or trackpad to click, and the EEfRT requires quick, repeated movements of dominant and non-dominant hands. It is possible that motor and coordination difficulties were related to worse scores for older adults in the CPT. Compared to other tasks, the CPT also had faster response windows. Given that reaction time slows over the course of adult life (Chen et al., 2017; Fozard et al., 1994; Hardwick et al., 2022), this could affect overall performance due to missed trials.

Older adults may also shift their strategy on slower tasks, trading time for accuracy, therefore scoring higher on slower tasks (speed-accuracy trade-off). However, since we did not probe participants regarding their strategy, we cannot be certain this is the cause of the results. Other, more general explanations are also possible. For example, technological barriers may impact performance, especially in computerized testing, as older adults tend to have higher anxiety related to computerized testing (Dorenkamp et al., 2023; Laguna & Babcock, 1997). However, without strategy and test anxiety measures, the true cause of the negative bias that is specifically observed on the CPT remains unclear and requires further assessment.

Measurement bias associated with sex

In terms of model fit, all base and MIMIC models met the threshold for good fit in terms of RMSEA, although none of the MIMIC models had improved fit over the base models in terms of AIC or BIC. The MIMIC model for the SOPT had acceptable fit indicated by CFI and RMSEA. It also had a significant positive direct effect of sex on task score such that females performed better than males, which may be indicative of sex bias for this task. In scientific research, it has become common practice to define sex as a dichotomous variable that can be categorized as male or female and determined by biological sex assigned at birth. However, it should be noted that gender identity is rapidly evolving from these previous constraints. Additionally, women have shown a preference for verbalizing visual stimuli in previous research so strategy may also play a role in apparent differences in cognitive ability measured by online tasks (Gaillard et al., 2021; Kimura, 2000). However, our study does not contain the measurements necessary to make this determination.

In this study, the demographic survey was conducted before the cognitive tests. There is a possibility that completing the demographic survey brought to mind gender identities that could be linked to stereotype threat. No message addressing stereotype threat was included, which could have reduced the possible impact of the survey (Johns et al., 2005). While it is known that implicit and explicit stereotype threat can lead to reduced test scores for women, research on men has only investigated explicit stereotype threat (Chaffee et al., 2020; Pennington et al., 2016). It is possible that the images used in this version of the SOPT may have affected current results. Specifically, the stimuli presented could be described as geometric representations of flower-like patterns, which females may be enculturated to have more numerous ways to describe and remember, or men may have implicit assumptions about their aptitude with this type of content. While most previous research shows a male advantage for shapes (Voyer et al., 2017), some research indicates a female advantage for familiar shapes but not abstract symbols (Chuderski & Jastrzębski, 2019). More research is needed to determine whether the content of the demographic survey or the content of the test itself may have triggered implicit stereotype threat that could explain the bias found in the SOPT.

Measurement bias associated with household income

All base models and most MIMIC models for household income met the threshold for acceptable or good fit, indicated by CFI and TLI. However, none of the MIMIC models had improved fit over the base models. None of the tasks showed significant direct effects of household income on task score. As such, results suggest a lack of bias in this cognitive battery related to household income. The question that we used to determine household income asked only about current household income and did not consider the importance of perceived income sufficiency, number of household members, differences in cost of living or lifetime income (Chakrabartty, 2024; Muhammad et al., 2022; Shavers, 2007). Additionally, the study was conducted during the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic when temporary loss of income was common, especially in marginalized groups (Andrea et al., 2022). It is therefore possible that the question about current income did not accurately capture SES, or the intersection of SES with other socio-cultural factors like racial/ethnic identity. This may be why our study did not detect bias for this complex demographic variable.

Measurement bias associated with underrepresented racial/ethnic group

Two of three fit statistics (CFI and RMSEA) indicated acceptable or good fit for all base and MIMIC models that included underrepresented racial/ethnic group, and the TLI indicated acceptable fit for the EEfRT. None of the MIMIC models had improved fit over the base models, although the EEfRT came the closest. There were also no significant direct effects of underrepresented demographic on cognitive task. As such, this battery of cognitive tests appears fairly robust against possible bias of White versus non-White identifying examinees. However, it should be noted that this analysis grouped all underrepresented demographics together, which may hide bias that may otherwise exist within more specific racial/ethnic groups. It is possible, if not likely, that the root causes of biased measurement are specific not just to non-White racial/ethnic groups, but unique combinations of these factors (i.e., intersectionality). Our sample was not large enough to consider bias at this more granular level, but care was taken to balance among subgroups during recruitment. Future studies looking at these same issues should attempt to further boost enrollment along these lines.

Measurement bias associated with education discouraged

All base and MIMIC models for education discouraged were near the acceptable threshold for CFI and TLI and met the threshold for good fit indicated by RMSEA. The MIMIC models for EEfRT and PLT had improved fit over the base model but neither had significant direct effects of educational discouraged on task scores. There was a trending positive direct effect of education discouraged on EEfRT. This means that there was a trend suggesting that those who were discouraged from higher education did better on this task than would be expected given a common cognitive ability model. Given the trend level effect, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Measurement bias related to systemic discouragement from education has not, to our knowledge, been previously investigated. Our goal was to measure perceived experience with discouragement in a simple and straightforward manner. Understandably, educational discrimination is a nuanced topic with numerous aspects, such as the frequency and intensity of discouragement, or source of discouragement (i.e., peers, parents, teachers). We acknowledge that using only one question cannot capture the complexity. Thus, this approach should be viewed as a preliminary step to uncovering potential bias due to educational discouragement. Future research will be needed to determine if these more nuanced aspects of educational discouragement play a role in performance and, if so, to what degree.

Measurement bias associated with years of education

The RMSEA indicated acceptable or good fit for all base and MIMIC models. Only the CPT had acceptable fit by CFI, and no models had acceptable fit indicated by TLI. The MIMIC model of the CPT and Flanker showed improvement from the base model for two measures and had significant direct effects of years of education on task scores. Interestingly, the CPT had a small negative effect while the Flanker had a very small positive effect. These effects would suggest that people with more education did worse on the CPT while those with more education did better on the Flanker. This observation is surprising given that the two tasks are both considered measures of cognitive control. Previous research has suggested that education is associated with gains in specific skills rather than general cognitive ability (Ritchie et al., 2015). It is possible, albeit seemingly unlikely, that education increases skills that are relevant to the Flanker but not the CPT.

The MIMIC model of the Sternberg task also improved fit over the base model but did not have a significant direct effect of years of education on task score. It could be that increasing sample size would reduce such effects, given that the effects are small. In this particular sample, underrepresented females and older adults tended to have a higher number of years of education than other groups which could reduce generalizability of these results.

Limitations

Results of this study should be interpreted considering certain limitations. First, all data acquisition was conducted using a combination of Pavlovia and Prolific and was thus limited to participants who have access to these online platforms. Participants may have performed differently if the tasks were administered in person. Second, despite our attempt to represent a wide range of socio-economic backgrounds via diverse income levels, it is possible that those reporting low income were only temporarily in this situation. Third, our sample was not large enough to accommodate sub-group analyses among underrepresented racial/ethnic identities and disentangle the complex dynamics related to these identities. In this study, our aim was to examine bias for five major ethnoracial groups in the U.S. population (White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, and Other) combined as a “underrepresented.” However, there are no perfect classifications of ethnoracial identity. Our study was of sufficient power to address these overarching groups, but not to examine variation within them (for example, the “Other” category included Native American or Alaska Native, Middle Eastern, and Mixed identities, which are highly distinct). Given the potential variability within these subgroups, combining subgroups may obscure effects in any individual subgroup. It is also important to capture the unique cultural experiences that come from geographical locale. For example, a White experience will be different in a predominantly White area than a non-White environment. Although we have attempted to address issues that appear alongside racial/ethnic identity by including variables such as household income and education discouragement, more needs to be done to unearth the root causes of bias in measurement. Future studies should focus on within-group variation in targeted populations and multicultural identities to further evaluate bias. Until we do so, we cannot be completely certain that our tasks produce unbiased measurements.

Similarly, education and SES can intersect with age, sex or ethnoracial identity in non-negligible ways. For example, individuals that are not part of a majority or socially privileged demographic group (white, male, etc.), may be discouraged from pursuing further education, which may affect educational attainment as well as self-confidence around cognitive testing. We acknowledge that significant differences in measurement bias between marginalized groups alone do not account for biological or cultural differences in cognitive performance.

The demographic variables considered here, although common, aggregate diverse people based on simplistic categorizations, and are far from comprehensive. For example, due to the limited information available from the Prolific accounts and Qualtrics survey, we do not have information on specific medications prescribed to participants which could introduce confounds, especially for tasks with quick reaction times that may be slowed by certain medications.

Finally, the tasks are available for customization online; therefore, the psychometric properties and potential biases of these tasks may change when the tasks are modified. Validity of online administration of cognitive tasks has been supported by previous research with other cognitive test batteries (Chaytor et al., 2021; Morrison et al., 2015; Watt et al., 2021), even in older adults (Wild et al., 2008) but potential lag time may be particularly relevant for tasks that require quick reaction times like the CPT.

Conclusion

Overall, the examined battery of cognitive tasks appears robust to measurement bias for household income, ethnoracial group, and education discouraged. Regarding age, however, the CPT showed measurement bias equating to worse scores for older adults while the Flanker and Sternberg tasks showed better scores for older adults. Further research is needed to determine whether motor coordination, familiarity with technology, or speed/accuracy trade-off could be related to this age-related measurement bias. The effect of bias was particularly stark for the CPT, indicating that including this task in a cognitive battery will specifically disadvantage older adults. Further, the CPT and Flanker showed the same pattern of effects related to years of education, with the CPT related to worse scores and the Flanker related to better scores with higher education. Since years of education and age were correlated, with more years of education associated with older age, this bias may be due to age rather than education per se, highlighting the need to control for age on this task in particular. Finally, sex bias was found for the SOPT, with women performing better than men. Possible explanations for this sex bias include strategy (e.g., greater verbalization by women), stereotype threat, or content that may be more familiar to women than men; however, this finding deserves further replication and exploration to uncover its sources.
