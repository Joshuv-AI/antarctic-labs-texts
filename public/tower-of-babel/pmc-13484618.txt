Literature Review: Recent Therapeutic Advances in The Management of Prurigo Nodularis: From Conventional Therapy to Biologics and Targeted Small-Molecule Agents
Authors: Erda Avriyanti, Ranisa Larasati, Sithra Rengasamy, Risa Miliawati Nurul Hidayah, Laila Tsaqilah, Miranti Pangastuti, Retno Hesty Maharani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrurigo nodularis (PN) is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by hyperkeratotic nodules on extensor surfaces, perpetuating a vicious itch-scratch cycle. Its epidemiologic burden is substantial, and diagnosis remains primarily clinical, supported by a structured diagnostic algorithm and evaluation for associated comorbidities. PN pathogenesis involves interactions among keratinocytes, immune cells, sensory nerve fibers and neuropeptides (substance P and calcitonin gene-related peptide) that elicit intense pruritus. Therapy aims to break the itch-scratch cycle and accelerate lesion resolution by reducing pruritus. Standard management with topical agents (corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors), systemic therapies (antihistamines, methotrexate, cyclosporine) and phototherapy often yields incomplete responses and requires prolonged treatment. Consequently, targeted therapies are critical to optimizing clinical efficacy. Dupilumab is currently the first Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved biologic for PN, followed by nemolizumab whereas other emerging agents—including additional monoclonal antibodies, Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, neurokinin-1 (NK1) receptor antagonists, opioid antagonists, and neuromodulators—remain at varying stages of clinical investigation (Phase II–III), acting on cytokine–neuroinflammatory pathways to relieve pruritus and improve patients’ quality of life.

Introduction

Prurigo nodularis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease characterized by the presence of hyperkeratotic nodules, ranging in color from skin-toned to pink, predominantly affecting the extensor surfaces of the extremities.1,2 These lesions are intensely pruritic and can occur across various age groups.1 The condition is often associated with a persistent itch–scratch cycle that exacerbates the lesions and significantly impairs patients’ quality of life.3,4 The prevalence of PN is relatively high, with an estimated 87,634 cases reported annually in the United States and between 3.27 to 8.8 cases per 10,000 population in the United Kingdom.5,6 PN most frequently affects individuals over the age of 50 and is commonly associated with multiple comorbidities, increasing the need for specialized dermatologic management.3,7 The exact pathophysiology of PN remains incompletely understood; however, previous studies suggest that its underlying mechanisms likely involve neuroimmune and inflammatory pathways.6,8 The pathological immune response is mediated by inflammatory cells such as eosinophils, mast cells, and T lymphocytes, along with the release of inflammatory mediators including interleukin (IL)-31, histamine, and prostaglandins.4 In addition, increased neural activity in the dermis occurs in conjunction with neuropeptide dysregulation involving substance P, calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), and the vanilloid receptor subtype 1 (VR-1).8 PN has also been reported to be associated with atopic comorbidities, such as AD as well as systemic conditions including type 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease.1,9

The standard management of PN includes topical therapies such as corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors, systemic treatments with immunosuppressive agents such as methotrexate and cyclosporine, and adjuvant modalities such as phototherapy.10,11 However, the effectiveness of these standard therapies remains limited, with potential adverse effects that warrant caution.3 A more comprehensive understanding of PN pathophysiology, particularly regarding cytokine dysregulation and neurocutaneous dysfunction, has driven the development of more targeted treatments, including biologic agents and small molecule inhibitors.3,4 The primary therapeutic goal is to achieve significant clinical improvement in pruritus and lesion count while interrupting the itch–scratch cycle that plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of this disease.4,12

This paper aims to provide an overview of the recent therapeutic advancements in the management of PN. To this end, a structured literature search was conducted using the PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases for relevant articles published [up to December 2025]. The search combined the terms “prurigo nodularis” and “chronic nodular prurigo” with keywords including “pathogenesis”, “diagnosis”, “treatment”, “management”, “biologics”, “monoclonal antibodies”, “Janus kinase inhibitors”, “dupilumab”, and “nemolizumab”. Peer-reviewed original articles, clinical trials, and reviews published in English that addressed the pathophysiology, diagnosis, or management of PN were considered, and the reference lists of relevant articles were screened manually to identify additional sources. Case reports of limited relevance and publications without full-text availability were excluded. As this is a narrative review, the search was structured but not intended to be systematic or exhaustive, and sources were selected based on their relevance to the scope of this review.

Epidemiology

The epidemiology of PN remains incompletely defined, including its incidence rate.1 In the United States, PN is estimated to affect approximately 170,000 adults, while in the United Kingdom, its prevalence has been reported at 3.27 per 10,000 individuals.3,6 In Asia, the incidence of PN in Korea has been reported at 0.036% in the general population and 4.82 cases per 1,000 dermatology outpatient visits across national referral centers,13 whereas a multicenter cross-sectional study in Japan recorded 1,229 PN patients (1.82%) out of 67,448 dermatology outpatient visits.14 PN can occur across all age groups and ethnicities but is most commonly diagnosed in individuals over 50 years old, with peak incidence between 51 and 65 years of age.1,15 Several studies have shown that PN occurs more frequently in females.1 Additionally, PN onset in individuals with atopic predisposition tends to occur at a younger age, approximately around 19 years.1,16

Etiology

The etiology of PN remains incompletely understood, although the chronic itch–scratch cycle is recognized as the primary factor contributing to lesion development.1 PN commonly arises as a response to repeated scratching in patients with persistent pruritus secondary to various dermatoses, such as AD and xerosis cutis; systemic disorders including chronic renal failure, diabetes mellitus, and thyroid disease; infections such as hepatitis C, Helicobacter pylori, Strongyloides stercoralis, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV); as well as neuropsychiatric conditions such as depression and anxiety.17,18 Pruritus in PN results from cutaneous neurogenic inflammation mediated by multiple neuropeptides, including substance P, CGRP, and VR-1, which lower the neural activation threshold and perpetuate the itch–scratch cycle.1,8

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of PN involves dysfunction of the immune system and the neuroimmune network.17,19 Histopathological features of PN lesions, including hyperkeratosis, epidermal hyperproliferation, hypergranulosis, and acanthosis, indicate the involvement of keratinocytes in its pathophysiological mechanisms.17 The pathogenesis of PN entails a complex interaction among keratinocytes, immune cells, and sensory nerve fibers.10

Initial activation occurs in keratinocytes, which subsequently release various inflammatory and pruritogenic mediators such as interleukin (IL)-4, IL-13, IL-31, neurotrophins including nerve growth factor (NGF), and neuropeptides such as substance P and CGRP.1,19 These mediators recruit and activate multiple immune cells, including T helper (Th) 2 lymphocytes, mast cells, eosinophils, and basophils within the dermis. These cells amplify the immune response by releasing histamine, eosinophil cationic protein (ECP), and eosinophil-derived neurotoxin (EDN), thereby creating a proinflammatory microenvironment surrounding sensory nerve endings.9,19

Unmyelinated C fibers and myelinated Aδ fibers play crucial roles in the transmission of itch sensations.10 Mediators such as IL-31 bind to IL-31 receptor α (IL-31Rα) and oncostatin M receptor β (OSMRβ), which are expressed on sensory nerve fibers and keratinocytes.10,11 The neuropeptide substance P interacts with neurokinin-1 receptor (NK1R), while NGF binds to its specific receptor, the nerve growth factor receptor (NGFR), also expressed on dermal nerve fibers. Activation of these pathways reduces the neuronal activation threshold, allowing minor stimuli to trigger intense pruritus. The neural impulses are transmitted through the dorsal root ganglia and spinal cord to the spinothalamic and spinoparabrachial tracts, then to the thalamus and somatosensory cortex, leading to the perception of itch and evoking a motor scratching response.10

Repetitive scratching behavior causes epidermal damage, which in turn reactivates keratinocytes and enhances the production of inflammatory and pruritogenic mediators, thereby perpetuating the chronic itch–scratch cycle.9,19 In addition, fibroblasts activated by IL-4 and IL-13 produce periostin, an extracellular matrix protein that not only amplifies IL-31 signaling and promotes keratinocyte proliferation but also directly induces pruritus by binding to integrin αvβ3 receptors on sensory nerve fibers.10 Furthermore, fibroblast activation contributes to increased angiogenesis and the accumulation of extracellular matrix components, such as collagen and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, resulting in the characteristic nodular fibrosis observed in PN.1,17

The Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway plays a key role in amplifying pruritic and inflammatory signaling in PN.9,10 IL-4 and IL-13 activate IL-4Rα receptors, which subsequently stimulate JAK1, JAK3, and TYK2, leading to the activation of STAT3 and STAT6 transcription factors. IL-31 also activates JAK1 and JAK2, which further stimulate STAT3 and STAT5.10,20 Activation of these pathways promotes epidermal proliferation, skin barrier dysfunction, and the maintenance of a chronic inflammatory milieu. This sequence of events establishes a self-perpetuating pathophysiological loop that sustains and exacerbates PN symptoms and lesions, making these pathways key therapeutic targets for the development of biologic agents and small molecule inhibitors in PN.10

Clinical Manifestation

The diagnosis of PN is established clinically through a thorough history and physical examination.19 PN should be suspected in patients presenting with pruritic nodules distributed over at least two body sites and persisting for six weeks or longer.4,19 The pruritus in PN may be paroxysmal, persistent, or sporadic, and can be localized to specific regions or diffusely distributed.2,19 Itching may be triggered by various factors, including sweating, heat, friction, extreme humidity, and irritation from personal care products or certain types of clothing.2

The nodules in PN are dome-shaped and vary in size from approximately 0.5 cm to over 3 cm.1,2 Their color ranges from normal skin tone to red, pink, dark brown, or black.1,15 Patients with PN can be categorized into three severity grades: mild (≤20 lesions), moderate (20–100 lesions), and severe (>100 lesions).21 The surface of the lesions often appears hyperkeratotic and may show excoriation marks resulting from chronic scratching.2 Lesions typically develop from normal or xerotic skin through repeated scratching.1 The distribution of lesions is generally symmetrical on the extensor surfaces of the arms and legs, but they may also appear on the occipital scalp, upper back, abdomen, and sacral region.1,2

Facial and palmar involvement is uncommon, and lesions usually occur in areas easily accessible to scratching.2 The midline upper back, which is difficult for patients to reach, often remains lesion-free, forming a characteristic pattern known as the “butterfly sign”.1,22 PN nodules may persist for months to years and typically resolve with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation or hypopigmentation, and occasionally scarring.2

Diagnosis

The diagnosis of PN is established based on a comprehensive clinical evaluation that includes history taking, physical examination, histopathological analysis, laboratory testing, and radiologic assessment. These components are essential to confirm the diagnosis, determine disease severity, identify underlying conditions, and formulate an appropriate treatment plan.18,23

Histopathological findings of PN typically include orthokeratosis, epidermal hyperplasia, the “hairy palm sign” characterized by folliculosebaceous units within a compact stratum corneum, hypergranulosis, and proliferation of fibroblasts and capillaries. However, these features may overlap with those of lichen simplex chronicus and other pruritic dermatoses secondary to scratching; therefore, clinical correlation remains essential.15,18

According to the diagnostic algorithm, patients with PN should undergo baseline laboratory screening to exclude systemic causes, including anemia, hepatorenal dysfunction, thyroid disorders, and chronic infections such as HIV and hepatitis.18,19 Additional investigations—such as serum protein electrophoresis, iron studies, or radiologic imaging—may be warranted based on clinical history and examination findings to rule out potential neoplastic, immunologic, or metabolic disorders.18,19 Laboratory and histopathological results must be interpreted in conjunction with clinical findings and lesion morphology to confirm the diagnosis of PN.18,19

Disease Burden and Quality of Life

Beyond its clinical features, PN imposes a substantial burden on patients’ quality of life and carries considerable socioeconomic costs.24 Health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL) impairment in PN is severe and comparable to that of other chronic systemic diseases, as reflected by markedly elevated DLQI and ItchyQoL scores.24,25 Persistent pruritus frequently disrupts sleep and is strongly associated with psychiatric comorbidities, including anxiety and depression, further compounding disease burden.26 The economic impact is likewise considerable: PN is associated with significant quality-adjusted life-year loss, high healthcare resource utilization, and both direct treatment costs and indirect costs from reduced work productivity.24,27 Most pivotal clinical-trial evidence for PN has been generated in Western populations, and treatment guidelines differ across regions. Real-world evidence from Asia highlights this gap. Surveys of Japanese patients and dermatologists show that treatment satisfaction remains suboptimal despite adherence to the 2020 Japanese Dermatological Association prurigo guidelines, with roughly two-thirds of dermatologists reporting dissatisfaction with the management of severe disease.28,29 This unmet need across different regions underscores the global relevance of emerging targeted therapies.

Management

Standard Therapy

The primary principle of PN management is to interrupt the chronic itch–scratch cycle and promote lesion healing by reducing pruritus intensity.9,17 Identification and treatment of underlying systemic diseases associated with PN are also essential.10 Patient education on avoiding scratching behaviors plays a critical role in treatment success. Additionally, psychological conditions contributing to scratching or skin-manipulating behaviors should be identified and appropriately managed as part of a comprehensive therapeutic approach.1 Regular use of emollients remains the cornerstone of PN management and should be implemented regardless of disease severity.2

First-line therapy generally consists of topical treatments, particularly corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors.9,22 High-potency topical corticosteroids, such as betamethasone valerate 0.1%, have been shown to effectively reduce pruritus and flatten nodules in PN patients.19,22 Intralesional corticosteroids, such as triamcinolone acetonide at concentrations of 10–20 mg/mL, are also beneficial in reducing lesion size and pruritus intensity.1,15 Corticosteroids act as immunomodulators by binding to glucocorticoid receptors, inhibiting inflammatory signaling pathways, and modulating neuropeptide regulation such as substance P and CGRP.8,20 Topical tacrolimus has also been successfully used as a steroid-sparing agent, although occlusive dressing may be required in some cases to enhance transcutaneous absorption.2 Tacrolimus, a calcineurin inhibitor, acts by inhibiting early T-cell activation and transcription of IL-2 and other cytokines. It may also exert direct effects on epidermal nerve endings through desensitization of transient receptor potential vanilloid type 1 (TRPV1), thereby reducing itch perception.8,30

Other topical options such as calcipotriol and capsaicin can be considered if corticosteroids fail to provide optimal results.17 Calcipotriol, a vitamin D3 analog, reduces pruritus by inhibiting tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α expression and modulating the distribution of Langerhans cells in the skin.8 The antipruritic effect of capsaicin results from activation of TRPV1 on C nerve fibers, leading to reduced neuropeptide release, including substance P.2,17 However, due to its short duration of action and the frequent occurrence of intense burning sensations, its widespread use is limited.19

Systemic therapy for PN typically begins with antihistamines as the first-line agents for relieving pruritus.17 However, because pruritus in PN is predominantly neuroimmune and non-histaminergic, antihistamines frequently provide only limited benefit and are mainly considered as adjunctive therapy in selected patients.31 In cases unresponsive to topical therapy, systemic immunosuppressants such as methotrexate and cyclosporine may be considered as second-line treatments.8,20 Methotrexate, a folic acid antagonist, inhibits dihydrofolate reductase and disrupts adenosine metabolism, leading to decreased production of proinflammatory cytokines including IL-6, IL-12, TNF-α, and interferon (IFN)-γ.20,32 Spring et al,32 reported that administration of methotrexate 7.5–20 mg/week for six months resulted in ≥75% reduction in lesion area and pruritus in 10 of 13 PN patients resistant to conventional therapy. Cyclosporine, another calcineurin inhibitor, can alleviate pruritus through IL-2 pathway modulation. The recommended initial dose is 3 mg/kg/day for 2–4 weeks, followed by incremental increases of 0.5–1 mg/kg/day every 2–4 weeks according to patient tolerance.8,20 Besides systemic therapy, phototherapy is an effective alternative, particularly in cases with widespread lesions.33 Phototherapeutic modalities include broadband or narrowband ultraviolet (UV) B radiation and psoralen–UVA (PUVA) therapy using oral or topical psoralen.2,22 The antipruritic effect of UVB is primarily attributed to the induction of regulatory T cells and inhibition of mast cell degranulation, while UVA therapy offers the advantage of deeper skin penetration. PUVA has been shown to decrease CGRP expression, contributing to reduced pruritic signaling.8

Recent Advances in Therapy

The persistent itch–scratch cycle in PN represents a major therapeutic challenge that remains difficult to manage with currently available treatment options.9 Most patients exhibit resistance to standard therapy and require a longer duration of treatment compared with those with other chronic pruritic disorders.8,9 Treatment failure is likely attributed to the heterogeneous etiology of chronic pruritus, which may not only arise from dermatological disorders but also involve systemic diseases, neurological, or psychiatric factors.8 An optimal clinical response is generally more difficult to achieve in patients with numerous inflammatory or excoriated nodules, as well as in individuals with severe impairment in quality of life and mental health.9,34 These findings highlight the urgent need for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies with mechanisms of action that address the multifactorial pathophysiology of PN, thereby improving clinical efficacy.9,22

Current treatment modalities for PN include antidepressants, antiepileptic agents, thalidomide, monoclonal antibodies, Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, neurokinin-1 (NK-1) receptor antagonists, and opioid receptor antagonists.9,19

Antidepressants

Antidepressants have been recommended in several studies as adjuvant therapies that can alleviate pruritus and promote lesion healing in PN cases refractory to standard treatments.11 Pharmacologically, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) act by inhibiting the serotonin transporter (SERT) located on the presynaptic terminal membrane, which is dependent on Na⁺/Cl− ions. This inhibition prevents the reuptake of serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) into presynaptic neurons, thereby increasing the concentration of serotonin within the synaptic cleft.5,11 The elevated serotonin levels subsequently activate 5-HT receptors on postsynaptic neurons, inhibiting the transmission of pruritic signals within the central nervous system.8,35 Additionally, activation of 5-HT receptors reduces the release of neuropeptides such as substance P and CGRP leading to a decrease in itch sensation.35 The use of antidepressants is particularly beneficial for PN patients with comorbid psychiatric disorders or those experiencing burning or stinging sensations in affected areas.11,36 Antidepressants such as paroxetine, fluvoxamine, duloxetine, and amitriptyline have been reported to provide mild to moderate improvement in pruritus severity.19 Paroxetine, administered at an initial dose of 10 mg per day for 3 days followed by a maintenance dose of 20–60 mg per day, and fluvoxamine, initiated at 25 mg per day with a maintenance dose of 50–150 mg per day, have shown efficacy in reducing pruritus in PN patients.11 Ständer et al,37 reported that paroxetine and fluvoxamine use in chronic pruritus patients resulted in adverse effects in 70.8% of cases, including vertigo, fatigue, nausea, vomiting, constipation, hyperhidrosis, and xerostomia.

Hashimoto et al,38 reported successful use of duloxetine hydrochloride in two PN patients unresponsive to other therapies. Administration of duloxetine at a dose of 20 mg/day resulted in improvement of pruritus within one week and gradual lesion healing over 5–6 weeks. Zalaudek et al,36 reported that oral amitriptyline therapy, starting at 60 mg/day for three weeks, reduced to 30 mg/day for two weeks and then 10 mg/day for one week, led to symptomatic improvement in 82.4% of PN patients unresponsive to standard therapy. Amitriptyline, a tricyclic antidepressant, exerts antipruritic and sedative effects through inhibition of serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake in serotonergic and adrenergic neurons, along with its affinity for histamine H1 receptors.36,39 Reported adverse effects include dry mouth and reduced daytime concentration.36

Antiepileptic Agents

Gabapentin and pregabalin were initially developed as antiepileptic agents; however, they are now also utilized for the management of neuropathic pain and chronic pruritus.11,40 Both agents are structural analogs of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) but do not directly interact with GABA receptors.8,40 Although the precise mechanisms of action of gabapentin and pregabalin are not fully elucidated, both act by inhibiting the α2δ subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels, particularly within the dorsal root ganglion and the dorsal horn of the spinal cord.40,41 This inhibition reduces calcium influx into neurons, thereby decreasing the release of excitatory neurotransmitters such as glutamate, norepinephrine, and substance P.41,42 Consequently, neuronal excitability thresholds to pruritogenic stimuli are elevated, resulting in decreased transmission of pruritic signals and an overall antipruritic effect.40 Gencoglan et al,43 reported successful gabapentin use in four PN patients who were refractory to standard therapies. Gabapentin initiated at 300 mg/day and titrated up to 900 mg/day resulted in significant improvement of pruritic symptoms within two months. In elderly patients or those with renal impairment, gabapentin dosing should be carefully adjusted to prevent adverse effects such as sedation, dizziness, ataxia, and cognitive impairment.21 Mazza et al,44 investigated pregabalin administration at 75 mg/day for three months in 30 PN patients, demonstrating optimal improvement defined as complete resolution of pruritus and lesion healing in 76% of subjects. Adverse effects were mild (sedation and dizziness) and occurred in six patients, indicating that pregabalin is an effective and well-tolerated therapeutic option for PN.

Thalidomide

Thalidomide is a neuroactive agent considered for patients with PN who are refractory to standard therapies, typically administered at doses ranging from 50 to 150 mg per day.19 This therapy exerts anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and neuromodulatory effects through multiple mechanisms. Thalidomide inhibits the production of TNF-α and the NF-κB signaling pathway while modulating the expression of IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, cyclooxygenase (COX)-2, and IFN-γ.45 As a neuromodulator, thalidomide also affects unmyelinated C fibers within pruritic neural pathways by inducing dysregulation of Wallerian degeneration.45,46 Due to the high risk of peripheral neuropathy and teratogenicity, thalidomide must be administered with extreme caution and only to patients unresponsive to conventional treatment.11,19,22

A study by Andersen et al,47 evaluated thalidomide therapy in 42 PN patients who were refractory to standard treatments. Thalidomide was administered at an average dose of 100 mg per day for a mean duration of 105 weeks. Thirty-two patients demonstrated clinical improvement, including one patient achieving complete remission. The most common adverse effect was peripheral neuropathy, which led to treatment discontinuation in several cases, highlighting the need for close monitoring during therapy.

Monoclonal Antibodies

Several novel therapeutic molecules for PN are currently being evaluated in randomized controlled trials conducted in the United States and Europe.9 As various molecular targets involved in the disease mechanism have been identified, more specific treatment approaches have been developed, including the use of monoclonal antibodies.19 It is important to distinguish established off-label options—such as antidepressants, antiepileptic agents/gabapentinoids, and thalidomide—from truly emerging targeted therapies, namely biologic agents and small-molecule inhibitors, which are the focus of the following discussion. As of January 2025, dupilumab has been approved by the US FDA as the first agent indicated for the treatment of PN.22 Numerous clinical trials remain ongoing to assess the efficacy and safety of other novel biologic agents for PN management.11,22 Monoclonal antibodies currently under investigation for PN include tralokinumab, lebrikizumab, nemolizumab, vixarelimab, benralizumab, and barzolvolimab, each targeting specific immunologic pathways implicated in the pathogenesis of chronic inflammation and pruritus associated with PN.19

Dupilumab

Dupilumab is a recombinant human IgG4 monoclonal antibody that targets the α-subunit of the interleukin-4 receptor (IL-4Rα), a heterodimeric receptor shared by IL-4 and IL-13 signaling pathways.9,22 By blocking IL-4 and IL-13 activity, two key cytokines involved in the Th2 immune response—dupilumab inhibits Th2 cell differentiation, macrophage activation, and B-cell–mediated immunoglobulin E (IgE) production, thereby reducing chronic inflammation and pruritus in PN.9 Yosipovitch et al,48 demonstrated the efficacy of dupilumab in patients with severe PN refractory to multiple standard therapeutic modalities. Dupilumab was administered as a 600 mg subcutaneous loading dose, followed by 300 mg every two weeks as maintenance. The treatment led to a significant reduction in pruritus intensity and substantial improvement in quality of life. The most frequently reported adverse events were conjunctivitis and upper respiratory tract infections, which were mild to moderate in severity and did not necessitate treatment discontinuation.

Tralokinumab

Tralokinumab is an IgG4 monoclonal antibody that specifically targets interleukin-13 (IL-13) and inhibits its interaction with the IL-13Rα receptor.9,10 Together with IL-4 and TGF-β, IL-13 plays a key role in inducing the expression of periostin, an extracellular matrix protein secreted by epithelial, endothelial, and fibroblast cells, which amplifies inflammatory responses and chronic pruritus.49,50 By inhibiting IL-13, tralokinumab is thought to reduce periostin expression and potentially provide therapeutic benefits in diseases with immunologic and neurogenic components, such as PN. Although clinical data on the use of tralokinumab in PN are still unavailable due to limited accessibility, this therapeutic approach is considered promising given its potential to not only alleviate pruritus but also modulate fibrotic processes and epidermal hyperplasia.9 Pezzolo et al,50 reported that tralokinumab significantly improved clinical manifestations in patients with AD exhibiting PN-like phenotypes refractory to dupilumab, with marked reductions in Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI), pruritus intensity, sleep disturbance, and DLQI after 16 weeks of therapy, without severe adverse effects. A Phase II clinical trial in AD demonstrated that tralokinumab rapidly reduced pruritus, with mild adverse events including conjunctivitis and upper respiratory tract infections.9 These findings suggest that tralokinumab may have therapeutic potential for PN by modulating key immunologic pathways; however, its efficacy in PN requires further validation through clinical studies.51

Lebrikizumab

Lebrikizumab is a monoclonal antibody that specifically targets IL-13 by preventing the formation of the IL-13Rα/IL-4Rα heterodimeric signaling complex.9,52 Similar to tralokinumab, lebrikizumab has shown rapid antipruritic effects and a favorable safety profile in adults with moderate-to-severe AD.9 Guttman et al,52 demonstrated that lebrikizumab significantly reduced pruritus and skin lesion severity in moderate-to-severe AD patients, with a good safety profile. In a phase IIb randomized clinical trial, symptom improvement was observed as early as week 2 of treatment, accompanied by significant reductions in EASI pruritus scores and sleep disturbance, without serious adverse effects. Reported mild side effects included mild conjunctivitis, injection-site reactions, localized urticaria, and mild stomatitis. To date, no clinical reports have described the use of lebrikizumab in PN due to its limited availability as an investigational agent; however, its mechanistic overlap with PN pathophysiology supports its potential as a future therapeutic option.9

Nemolizumab

Nemolizumab is a monoclonal antibody targeting the interleukin-31 receptor alpha (IL-31Rα), a component of the dimeric IL-31 receptor complex that pairs with the OSMRβ.10,22 By blocking IL-31 signaling, nemolizumab inhibits the activation of inflammatory cells and sensory neurons responsible for pruritus.53 In August 2024, nemolizumab received US FDA approval for the treatment of adults with PN, becoming the second approved biologic for PN after dupilumab and the first agent specifically targeting IL-31 signaling.54 Kwatra et al,55 demonstrated the efficacy of nemolizumab in a Phase III randomized clinical trial involving patients with moderate-to-severe PN. Approximately 58.4% of patients achieved a ≥4-point reduction in the Peak Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (PP-NRS), while 26.3% showed significant clinical improvement according to the Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) score. The most frequently reported adverse events included diarrhea, peripheral edema, and elevated hepatic enzyme levels.

Vixarelimab

Vixarelimab is a fully human monoclonal antibody that targets OSMRβ, thereby inhibiting both IL-31 and oncostatin M signaling pathways.9,22 In a phase IIa clinical trial, Sofen et al,56 evaluated the efficacy of subcutaneous vixarelimab administered for 8 weeks in 50 patients with PN, resulting in a rapid and significant reduction in pruritus as well as improvement in skin lesions. A total of 52.2% of patients achieved a ≥4-point reduction in the Worst Itch Numerical Rating Scale (WI-NRS), compared with 30.8% in the placebo group, and 30.4% attained an IGA score of 0 or 1 by week 8. Symptomatic improvement was observed as early as week 3, with some patients experiencing ≥8-point reductions in WI-NRS. The most common adverse events included upper respiratory tract infections, nasopharyngitis, and headache.10,56

Benralizumab

Benralizumab is a monoclonal antibody that specifically targets the alpha subunit of the interleukin-5 receptor (IL-5Rα), leading to selective eosinophil depletion.19 IL-5 plays a pivotal role in eosinophil biology, regulating differentiation, proliferation, activation, adhesion, and cell survival.57 Moreover, IL-5 contributes to mast cell and basophil migration and accumulation in cutaneous tissues.19,57 Currently, benralizumab is approved for the treatment of severe eosinophilic asthma and is being evaluated in a phase II clinical trial for its potential use in PN management.58

Barzolvolimab

Barzolvolimab is a human monoclonal antibody that targets the KIT receptor tyrosine kinase, a receptor expressed on mast cell membranes.9,19 Clinical studies in humans have demonstrated that barzolvolimab induces mast cell depletion in the dermal layer, which correlates with improvement in pruritic symptoms.59 Currently, barzolvolimab is under investigation in a phase II randomized controlled clinical trial for the treatment of PN.9

Janus Kinase (JAK) Inhibitors

The JAK/STAT signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the pathophysiology of pruritus, particularly in Th2-mediated diseases such as PN.10 The JAK family consists of four members, JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2 that associate with cytokine receptors and mediate intracellular signal transduction. The pathophysiology of PN involves several Th2 cytokines, including IL-4, IL-13, and IL-31, which contribute to pruritus and inflammation through activation of the JAK/STAT pathway, primarily involving JAK1, STAT3, and STAT6.9,10 IL-4 signals through two receptor complexes: the type I receptor, composed of IL-4Rα and the common gamma chain (γc), and the type II receptor, composed of IL-4Rα and IL-13Rα1, the latter also mediating IL-13 signaling.60 Engagement of these receptors activates JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2, leading to downstream activation of STAT3 and STAT6.10

To date, the use of JAK inhibitors has not yet received formal regulatory approval for the treatment of PN, although these agents have been approved for managing AD.9,22 JAK inhibitors are small-molecule drugs that inhibit multiple intracellular signaling pathways. They exhibit a faster onset of action compared to biologic agents, with improvement in key symptoms such as pruritus observed within several days, whereas the maximal efficacy of biologics typically occurs after ≥16 weeks of therapy.19

Ruxolitinib

Ruxolitinib is a selective inhibitor of JAK1 and JAK2 that acts by blocking JAK and STAT signaling pathways. This mechanism attenuates inflammatory processes through modulation of cytokine activity, reduction of eosinophil activation, and regulation of T regulatory cell function.61 Although topical ruxolitinib has been approved for the treatment of nonsegmental vitiligo, its therapeutic potential in PN remains under evaluation through several ongoing clinical trials.22 Kwatra et al,62 reported findings from a phase III clinical trial evaluating the efficacy of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream applied twice daily for 12 weeks in patients with PN, demonstrating significant improvement in pruritus and skin lesions. Reported adverse events were mild, including nasopharyngitis, with no application-site reactions or serious drug-related adverse effects observed.

Abrocitinib

Abrocitinib is an oral JAK1 inhibitor that has demonstrated efficacy in AD and is currently being investigated for the treatment of PN. Kwatra et al,63 reported in a phase II clinical trial that abrocitinib administered at 200 mg/day for 12 weeks showed significant efficacy in PN patients, with a 78.3% reduction in the Peak Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (PP-NRS) score. Additionally, 80% of patients achieved a ≥4-point reduction, accompanied by significant improvement in the DLQI scores. The therapy was generally well tolerated, with acneiform eruptions being the most frequently reported adverse event.22,63

Povorcitinib

Povorcitinib is an oral selective JAK1 inhibitor showing therapeutic potential for PN.22,34 A phase II double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial involving 146 adult PN patients demonstrated its efficacy. After 16 weeks of treatment, the proportion of patients achieving ≥4-point reduction in PP-NRS scores increased in a dose-dependent manner: 36.1% for 15 mg, 44.4% for 45 mg, and 54.1% for 75 mg, compared to only 8.1% in the placebo group. The treatment was generally well tolerated, with headache being the most commonly reported adverse effect.64

Upadacitinib

Upadacitinib is an oral JAK1 inhibitor approved for several inflammatory conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, AD, and Crohn’s disease.22,65 This therapy has proven effective in various AD phenotypes, including those resembling PN, suggesting potential benefit for patients with severe PN.22 A Phase IV clinical trial is currently evaluating the efficacy of upadacitinib in 25 patients with moderate-to-severe PN over a 24-week period. The primary endpoint is the proportion of patients achieving ≥4-point reduction in the Worst Itch Numeric Rating Scale (WI-NRS) score at week 12 after receiving 15 mg daily, with the option to increase to 30 mg at week 8 if needed. The study results are not yet available.66 Lee et al,67 reported that treatment with upadacitinib 15 mg daily for 24 weeks in refractory PN patients resulted in significant improvement in itch NRS, Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA), and DLQI scores. The therapy was well tolerated in all patients, with only one (10%) developing dyslipidemia at week 24.

Tofacitinib

Tofacitinib is an oral pan-JAK inhibitor currently being investigated as a potential therapy for PN due to its immunomodulatory properties.22 An ongoing prospective observational study aims to evaluate the safety and efficacy of tofacitinib in refractory PN patients, although it has not yet entered formal clinical trial phases.68 Peng et al,69 reported a successful case of tofacitinib treatment in a 65-year-old PN patient unresponsive to standard therapies. The patient received oral tofacitinib at a dose of 5 mg twice daily for two weeks, followed by 5 mg once daily for the next two weeks, and finally 5 mg every other day for two additional weeks. Significant improvement was observed as early as the first week, evidenced by reductions in Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) and DLQI scores, as well as a decrease in both the number and size of nodules. No adverse effects or recurrence were reported during five months of follow-up.

Neurokinin-1 Receptor (NK1R) Antagonists

Cutaneous sensory nerves express various receptors involved in itch perception and interact with multiple inflammatory cell types.19 This interaction triggers the release of neuropeptides such as substance P, which subsequently activates two main receptors: NK1R and Mas-related G-protein coupled receptor X2 (MRGPRX2).17,19 Agents such as aprepitant and serlopitant act by inhibiting NK1R activation.19

Aprepitant

Aprepitant is an NK1R antagonist that inhibits the activation of the substance P pathway, including inflammatory processes, vasodilation, eosinophil migration, and mast cell degranulation.34 This mechanism is considered promising for PN therapy, as PN patients exhibit elevated serum levels of substance P and NK1R expression, as well as a higher density of substance P–positive nerve fibers compared with healthy individuals.70 Tsianakas et al,71,72 reported that in a randomized, double-blind phase II clinical trial, oral administration of aprepitant 80 mg daily for 7 days resulted in a significant reduction in itch intensity, with a 4.8-point decrease in the visual analog scale (VAS) score within one week. The most commonly reported adverse effects of aprepitant included constipation, hypotension, fatigue, diarrhea, and elevated liver enzyme levels.72

Serlopitant

Serlopitant is an NK1R antagonist that inhibits the biological effects of substance P and has been developed for the treatment of chronic pruritus.10,34 Ständer et al,37 reported that oral administration of serlopitant 5 mg once daily for 8 weeks significantly reduced itch intensity compared with placebo in a phase II clinical trial involving 128 PN patients. However, these findings were not confirmed in two subsequent phase III trials, which failed to meet the primary endpoint of pruritus reduction. Reported adverse effects included nasopharyngitis, diarrhea, and fatigue.10

Opioid Receptor Antagonists

Differences in the activity of μ-opioid receptors (MOR), which promote pruritus, and κ-opioid receptors (KOR), which suppress pruritus, are thought to contribute to the mechanism of non-histaminergic itch.17 Activation of MOR can exacerbate itch sensation, whereas activation of KOR exerts antipruritic effects. Several pharmacological agents, such as nalbuphine, which acts as a KOR agonist and MOR antagonist, and naltrexone, a MOR antagonist have shown potential roles in modulating pruritus.19

Naltrexone

Naltrexone is an oral opioid receptor antagonist that has not been specifically approved by the FDA for primary dermatologic diseases but has been recommended in cases of persistent pruritus, such as PN and cholestatic pruritus.73,74 Naltrexone exerts its effect by blocking µ-, κ-, and δ-opioid receptors, thereby interfering with signaling pathways involved in itch modulation. Low-dose administration (1.5–4.5 mg per day) can induce compensatory upregulation of endogenous opioids such as β-endorphin and methionine-enkephalin, leading to a reduction in pruritus intensity.73 In addition, naltrexone inhibits Toll-like receptor 4 pathways in macrophages and microglia and suppresses histamine release from mast cells and basophils, thereby reducing proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α.74,75 Timoney et al,75 reported significant improvement in PN patients treated with oral naltrexone 3 mg nightly, with antipruritic effects achieved within several weeks. Commonly reported adverse effects include gastrointestinal disturbances (abdominal cramping, pain, nausea), musculoskeletal symptoms (arthralgia, myalgia), neurological complaints (insomnia, headache), and psychiatric effects (anxiety, restlessness).76

Nalbuphine

Nalbuphine is a partial MOR antagonist and KOR agonist that has been approved for pain management.34 This therapy has also been applied in the treatment of pruritus, as MOR inhibition and KOR activation have been shown to effectively suppress itch mediated by endogenous opioids.34,77 Weisshar et al,76 conducted a phase II clinical trial involving 62 patients with moderate-to-severe PN who received oral nalbuphine at doses of 81 mg, 162 mg, or placebo twice daily for 10 weeks. By week 10, a ≥30% reduction in pruritus according to the WI-NRS was achieved in 44.4% of patients receiving 162 mg nalbuphine, 27.3% of those receiving 81 mg, and 36.4% in the placebo group. These results indicate that oral nalbuphine 162 mg twice daily is effective in alleviating pruritus in PN patients. The most common adverse effects reported were nausea, dizziness, and headache, primarily occurring during the titration phase and generally mild to moderate in severity.

Future Directions and Research Gaps

Despite substantial progress in understanding the neuroimmune basis of PN and the emergence of targeted therapies, several important research gaps remain. PN is increasingly recognized as a heterogenous disease comprising distinct endotypes ranging from strongly type-2/atopic profiles to more neuropathic or Th1/Th17-skewed phenotypes-yet validated biomarkers to guide therapy selection are still lacking, and further studies using biomarkers are needed to support more personalized treatment approaches.34,78 Most pivotal clinical trials have compared a single agent with placebo; consequently, direct (head-to-head) comparisons between therapies, evidence on the optimal treatment duration, maintenance strategy, and treatment sequence, and long-term safety monitoring of targeted agents remain limited, with emerging real-world registries only beginning to characterize their extended safety and durability of response.51,79 Given that most clinical trial evidence is derived from Western populations, further studies involving more diverse and geographically representative cohorts, including Southeast Asian populations, are needed to confirm the global applicability of emerging therapies. Integrating validated biomarkers into future research may support safer, more effective, and more individualized treatment for patients with refractory PN.

Summary

Prurigo nodularis is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder characterized by intensely pruritic nodular lesions, commonly localized on the extremities. This condition is closely associated with a persistent itch–scratch cycle, which contributes to lesion progression and significantly impairs patients’ quality of life. The pathogenesis of PN involves dysfunction of the immune and neurocutaneous systems, including activation of the Th2 pathway, increased cytokines such as IL-4, IL-13, and IL-31, and the release of neuropeptides such as substance P and CGRP. Diagnosis of PN is established through comprehensive clinical evaluation, including history taking, physical examination, histopathologic assessment, laboratory testing, and radiologic evaluation to determine disease severity, identify underlying causes, and guide therapeutic decisions. Standard management includes topical therapy (corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors), systemic agents (antihistamines and immunosuppressants), phototherapy, as well as patient education and psychological support. However, standard treatment often yields suboptimal improvement, particularly in severe and refractory cases, necessitating more targeted therapeutic strategies for PN management.

Advances in understanding the molecular mechanisms of PN have led to the development of innovative, targeted therapies that modulate specific immune and neuroinflammatory pathways implicated in disease pathogenesis. The use of antidepressants (SSRIs, TCAs) and antiepileptic agents (gabapentin, pregabalin) has demonstrated efficacy in reducing pruritus in patients unresponsive to standard therapy. Biologic agents such as dupilumab, tralokinumab, lebrikizumab, nemolizumab, vixarelimab, benralizumab, and barzolvolimab have been developed to inhibit specific cytokine activities contributing to PN pathogenesis. Furthermore, JAK inhibitors (ruxolitinib, abrocitinib, upadacitinib, tofacitinib), neurokinin-1 receptor antagonists (aprepitant, serlopitant), and opioid receptor antagonists (naltrexone, nalbuphine) have shown significant clinical efficacy in multiple clinical trials. The advancement of these therapeutic modalities aims to achieve optimal pruritus control and enhance the quality of life for patients with PN.
