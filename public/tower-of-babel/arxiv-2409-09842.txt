The search for alternating surgeries
Kenneth L. Baker, Marc Kegel, Duncan McCoy

Abstract
. Surgery on a knot in S 3 S^{3} is said to be an alternating surgery if it yields the double branched cover of an alternating link. The main theoretical contribution is to show that the set of alternating surgery slopes is algorithmically computable and to establish several structural results. Furthermore, we calculate the set of alternating surgery slopes for many examples of knots, including all hyperbolic knots in the SnapPy census. These examples exhibit several interesting phenomena including strongly invertible knots with a unique alternating surgery and asymmetric knots with two alternating surgery slopes. We also establish upper bounds on the set of alternating surgeries, showing that an alternating surgery slope on a hyperbolic knot satisfies | p / q | ≤ 3 ​ g ​ ( K ) + 4 |p/q|\leq 3g(K)+4 . Notably, this bound applies to lens space surgeries, thereby strengthening the known genus bounds from the conjecture of Goda and Teragaito.

1.
Introduction

Given a knot
K
in
S^{3}
, we say that
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}
is an
alternating surgery slope
for
K
if
p/q
-surgery on
K
yields the double branched cover of an alternating knot or link. Since the double branched cover of a non-split alternating link is a Heegaard Floer homology L-space, knots with alternating surgeries provide examples of L-space knots. L-space knots and their properties have generated significant interest for a number of years, see for example
[
46
,
45
,
23
,
24
,
22
]
. Since alternating surgeries frequently arise on some of the simplest L-space knots, such as the torus knots, and more generally the Berge knots, it is natural to wonder to what extent alternating surgeries arise on generic L-space knots.

The set of all alternating surgery slopes on
K
will be denoted
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
. This paper is devoted to understanding
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
both from a theoretical and a concrete computational perspective. We show that there exists an algorithm to calculate
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
and develop methods to calculate
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
in practice. In particular, we calculate
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
for all hyperbolic knots in the SnapPy census.

Theorem 1.1

There exists an algorithm that, given a knot
K
in
S^{3}
, returns
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
.

There is a natural family of knots
\mathcal{D}
, associated to unknotting crossings in alternating diagrams of knots (see §
9.1
), which plays a crucial role in the classification of alternating slopes. In particular,
\mathcal{D}
contains all knots for which
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
is infinite. The theoretical computability of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
rests on the following structure theorem for
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
. If
K
is a non-trivial knot admitting alternating surgeries, then there is an associated non-empty tuple of integers,
\underline{\rho}(K)=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{\ell})
ordered so that
\rho_{1}\leq\dots\leq\rho_{\ell}
which we call the stable coefficients of
K
(see §
3
).

Theorem 1.2

Let
K
be a non-trivial knot admitting positive alternating surgeries with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}(K)=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{\ell})
. If
N
is the integer

N=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\rho_{i}^{2}+\max_{1\leq k\leq\ell}\left(\rho_{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\rho_{i}\right),

then precisely one of the following holds.

(1)

If
K\in\mathcal{D}
is a cable knot or a torus knot with reducible surgery slope
N
, then

\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=[N-1,N+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}.

(2)

If
K\in\mathcal{D}
is not a cable knot or a torus knot with reducible surgery slope
N
, then

\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=[N-1,N]\cap\mathbb{Q}.

(3)

If
K\notin\mathcal{D}
and
\Delta_{K}(x)\neq\Delta_{K^{\prime}}(x)
for every
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
, then

\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)\subseteq\{N-1,N\}.

(4)

If
K\notin\mathcal{D}
and there exists a knot
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
with
\Delta_{K}(x)=\Delta_{K^{\prime}}(x)
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
is a finite subset of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K^{\prime})
and there exists a computable constant
Q(K)
such that any
p/q\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
satisfies
|q|\leq Q(K)
.

It is worth noting that in addition to calculating the set
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
, one can also determine an alternating branching set for any slope
r\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
. If
K\not\in\mathcal{D}
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
is finite and this data is simply a finite set of diagrams. If
K\in\mathcal{D}
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
is infinite and the branching sets of the alternating surgeries are all obtained by an easily describable tangle replacement on a single alternating diagram.

1.1.
Calculating
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
for census knots

A full implementation of the theoretical algorithm lying behind Theorem

1.3.
Asymmetric L-space knots without alternating surgeries

There are exactly nine asymmetric L-space knots amongst the SnapPy census:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\{t12533,t12681,o9\_38928,o9\_39162,o9\_40363,o9\_40487,

\displaystyle o9\_40504,o9\_40582,o9\_42675\}.

Theorem
1.3
implies that none of the asymmetric L-space knots in the census admit an alternating surgery, thereby answering
[
2
, Question 10]
.

Corollary 1.5

None of the asymmetric L-space knots of the census have an alternating surgery.

This provides the first known examples of asymmetric L-space knots with no alternating surgeries. The existence of such knots is of interest, since the first asymmetric L-space knots, the Baker-Luecke knots, all possess alternating surgeries
[
5
]
. In fact, the Baker-Luecke knots were shown to be L-space knots by exhibiting an alternating surgery on each one.

1.4.
L-space knots with exactly two alternating surgeries

We also applied our methods to calculate
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
for several Baker-Luecke knots. Surprisingly, all of these knots that we checked turned out to have a second alternating surgery in addition to the one originally constructed by Baker and Luecke. In fact these knots turned out to provide the first examples of knots for which
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
takes the form
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=\{N-1,N\}
.

Theorem 1.6

For at least
14
of the asymmetric hyperbolic L-space knots constructed in
[
5
]
, the set of alternating surgeries is of the form
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=\{N-1,N\}
.

Although we were able to find second alternating surgeries in these small examples, we have yet to find a theoretical explanation for the existence of these second alternating surgeries. It would be interesting to know if the phenomenon persists.

Question 1.7

Do all of the asymmetric L-space knots of Baker-Luecke have two alternating surgeries?

1.5.
Conjectural structure of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)

Note that Theorem
1.2
gives a complete description of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
for knots in
\mathcal{D}
, so it remains only to understand
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
for knots which are not in
\mathcal{D}
. Our results point towards the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.8

Let
K\notin\mathcal{D}
be a knot admitting positive alternating surgeries, then

\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)\subseteq\{N-1,N\}.

In particular the only knots with non-integral alternating surgeries are in
\mathcal{D}
.

Theorem
1.2
shows that this conjecture holds for knots whose Alexander polynomials obstruct them from lying in
\mathcal{D}
. Thus it remains to verify the conjecture for knots
K\notin\mathcal{D}
such that
\Delta_{K}=\Delta_{K^{\prime}}
for some
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
. However, there are currently no known examples of such knots which admit alternating surgeries.

Conjecture 1.9

Let
K\notin\mathcal{D}
be a knot such that
\Delta_{K}(x)=\Delta_{K^{\prime}}(x)
for some
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
. Then
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=\emptyset
.

Note that Conjecture
1.9
implies Conjecture
1.8
.

Remark 1.10

There are examples of L-space knots which have the Alexander polynomial of a knot in
\mathcal{D}
but do not admit any alternating surgeries. The simplest example is the
(3,2)
-cable of
T_{3,2}
which has the same Alexander polynomial as
T_{3,4}
.

It also seems natural to wonder which subsets of slopes allowed by Conjecture
1.8
can actually occur. The knots in Theorem
1.3
which admitted a single alternating surgery provide example of knots for which
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=\{N-1\}
. There are Baker-Luecke knots providing examples where
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=\{N-1,N\}
. Thus there is only one remaining possibility that has not been realized.

Question 1.11

Does there exist a knot
K
such that
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=\{N\}
?

Computations suggest that for many Baker-Luecke knots the slope
N
is an alternating surgery slope. Thus, if any of these Baker-Luecke knots admits only a single alternating surgery, we would have an affirmative answer to Question
1.11
.

1.6.
Genus bounds on alternating surgeries

We obtain further restrictions on alternating surgery slopes. In
[
37
]
it was shown if
p/q
is an alternating surgery slope for a non-trivial knot
K
, then
|p/q|\leq 4g(K)+3

[
37
, Theorem 1.1]
. This bound is sharp with equality being attained by torus knots of the form
T_{2,2g+1}
. It turns out that these are the only such examples and that for all other knots a stronger bound holds.

Theorem 1.12

Let
K
be a non-trivial knot which is not a two-stranded torus knot. Then for any alternating slope
p/q
we have
|p/q|\leq 3g(K)+4
.

This allows us to refine the upper bound on lens space surgeries established by Rasmussen
[
47
]
.

Corollary 1.13

If
K
is a non-trivial knot which is not a two-stranded torus knot such that
S_{p}^{3}(K)
is a lens space, then
p\leq 3g(K)+4.

The bound in Theorem
1.12
is sharp with equality being realised by the
T_{3,n}
torus knots and a family of hyperbolic knots in
\mathcal{D}
which begins with the
(-2,3,7)
-pretzel knot (see §
11
).

Using these ideas we also generalize a result of Ni
[
44
, Corollary 1.7]
.

Theorem 1.14

Let
K
be a knot in
S^{3}
which admits a positive alternating surgery. If the symmetrized Alexander polynomial of
K
takes the form

\Delta_{K}(x)=x^{n}-x^{n-1}+x^{n-2}+\text{lower order terms},

then
K=T_{2,2n+1}
.

1.7.
Knots with common surgeries

It is a folklore theorem that for any two knots,
K
and
K^{\prime}
in
S^{3}
, the set of slopes for which
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)
and
S_{p/q}^{3}(K^{\prime})
are homeomorphic is finite. In order to prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
we need a version of this folklore result with effective bounds. Since the techniques required for such a result are peripheral to the rest of this paper we reserve its proof to the appendix. We note that the fact that one can obtain computable bounds depends on the quantitative bounds on hyperbolic Dehn fillings obtained by Futer-Purcell-Schleimer
[
14
]
.

Theorem
A.16
.

Let
K
and
K^{\prime}
be a pair of distinct knots in
S^{3}
. Then there is a computable integer
Q(K,K^{\prime})
such that if
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)\cong S_{p/q}^{3}(K^{\prime})
for some
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
|q|\leq Q(K,K^{\prime})
.

The constant
Q(K,K^{\prime})
appearing in this theorem is described in more detail in Definition
A.2
. We remark also that Theorem
A.16
is implied by the results of Sorya and Wakelin
[
50
]
, which appeared shortly after the first version of this paper. Their work establishes the stronger result that for every knot
K
, there is a computable constant
Q(K)
such that every slope
p/q
with
|q|\geq Q(K)
is a characterizing slope for

.

Part I
Changemaker lattices and obtuse superbases

A key ingredient in the study of alternating surgeries is the theory of changemaker lattices. This part of the paper is dedicated to the lattice theory necessary for our work. We remind the reader that an integral lattice is a pair
(L,\cdot)
, where
L
is a finitely generated free abelian group equipped with a symmetric, positive definite, bilinear pairing

\displaystyle L\times L

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto x\cdot y.

We will frequently omit the bilinear pairing from our notation and simply denote an integral lattice
(L,\cdot)
by
L
. Given
x\in L
we will use the notation
\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}=x\cdot x
.

Throughout the paper we will refer to
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
equipped with a symmetric bilinear pairing that admits an orthonormal basis as a diagonal lattice (of rank
r
) and we will denote the diagonal lattice by
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
.

Notational convention

Given a fixed orthonormal basis
\{\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{r}\}
for
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, we will write vectors in the form

\bm{v}=v_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+v_{r}\bm{e}_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}.

That is, a bold script symbol indicates an element of
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and the corresponding non-bold symbol with a subscript a coefficient with respect to the fixed basis.

2.
Obtuse superbases and graph lattices

The notion of an obtuse superbase will play a key role in understanding alternating surgeries.

Definition 2.1

(Obtuse superbase)

Let
L
be an integral lattice of rank
r
. We say that a set
B=\{v_{0},\dots,v_{r}\}\subseteq L
is an
obtuse superbase
for
L
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

the
v_{i}
span
L
;

(b)

for
0\leq i<j\leq r
we have
v_{i}\cdot v_{j}\leq 0
; and

(c)

v_{0}+\dots+v_{r}=0
.

The graph associated to
B
, denoted
G_{B}
, has vertex set
B=\{v_{0},\dots,v_{r}\}
and
|v_{i}\cdot v_{j}|
edges between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
for
i\neq j
. The obtuse superbase
B
is
planar
if
G_{B}
is a planar graph.

Obtuse superbases are closely related to the notion of graph lattices.

Definition 2.2

(Graph lattice)

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected, finite graph with no self-loops. Define
\Lambda(G)
to be the abelian group defined by

\Lambda(G)=\frac{\mathbb{Z}\langle v_{0},\dots,v_{r}\rangle}{\mathbb{Z}\langle v_{0}+\dots+v_{r}\rangle},

where
V=\{v_{0},\dots,v_{r}\}
are the vertices of
G
.
We denote by
d(v_{i})
the degree of
v_{i}
and by
e(v_{i},v_{j})
the number of edges between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
.
The group
\Lambda(G)
is a free abelian group of rank
r
and the bilinear pairing defined on the vertices by

(2.1)

v_{i}\cdot v_{j}=\begin{cases}d(v_{i})&\text{if $i=j$}\\
-e(v_{i},v_{j})&\text{if $i\neq j$},\end{cases}

descends to a bilinear pairing on
\Lambda(G)
, which can be further shown to be positive definite.
2
2

2

The hypothesis that
G
be connected is required to ensure that the pairing is positive definite and not just positive semi-definite.
The group
\Lambda(G)
endowed with this pairing is the
graph lattice
of
G
.

Remark 2.3

Let
H
be a subset of the vertices of
G
and
z\in\Lambda(G)
an element of the form
z=\sum_{v\in H}v
. Then (
2.1
) implies that the self-pairing
\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}
is equal to the number of edges between
H
and
G\setminus H
. In particular, this is why
G
must be connected to ensure that
\Lambda(G)
is positive definite.

By construction (the images of) the vertices of
G
form an obtuse superbase in
\Lambda(G)
and the associated graph for this obtuse superbase is a copy of
G
.

Thus we see that the relationship between obtuse superbases and graph lattices can be summarized as follows.

Proposition 2.4

An integral lattice
L
admits an obtuse superbase
B
with associated graph
G_{B}
if and only if
L
is isomorphic to the graph lattice
\Lambda(G_{B})
.∎

In general, a lattice
L
can admit multiple obtuse superbases. Recall that two connected graphs
G
and
G^{\prime}
are
2-isomorphic
if there is a cycle-preserving bijection between their edges sets.

Lemma 2.5

Let
L
be a lattice with an obtuse superbase
B
and let
G^{\prime}
be a graph. Then there is an obtuse superbase
B^{\prime}
of
L
with
G_{B^{\prime}}
isomorphic to
G^{\prime}
if and only if
G^{\prime}
is 2-isomorphic to
G_{B}
.

Proof.

If
L
possesses an obtuse superbase
B
, then
L
is isomorphic to the graph lattice of
G_{B}
by Proposition
2.4
. However Greene has shown that two graphs have isomorphic graph lattices if and only if they are 2-isomorphic
[
21
, Theorem 3.8]
.
∎

Remark 2.6

Since planarity of a graph is preserved by 2-isomorphism, Lemma
2.5
implies that there exists a planar obtuse superbase for a lattice
L
if and only if every obtuse superbase is planar. That is, planarity of an obtuse superbase is intrinsic to the lattice
L
rather than the particular choice of obtuse superbase.

A non-zero vector
v\in L
is
irreducible
if for any non-zero
x,y\in L
with
x+y=v
we have that
x\cdot y\leq-1
. In the presence of an obtuse superbase
B
, the irreducible elements of
L
have a nice interpretation in terms of the graph
G_{B}
: they correspond to connected subgraphs of
G_{B}
with connected complements.

Lemma 2.7

(

Lemma 2.10

Let

Proof.

Let

is 2-edge-connected. Fix a vertex

3.
Changemaker lattices

Let
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
be a diagonal lattice. We say that a non-zero vector
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
is a
changemaker vector
if there exists an orthonormal basis
\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{r}
for
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
such that
\bm{\sigma}
takes the form

\bm{\sigma}=\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}

where the coefficients satisfy
\sigma_{1}=1
and

\sigma_{i-1}\leq\sigma_{i}\leq 1+\sigma_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{i-1}

for
i=2,\dots,r
. Let
m\leq r
be the minimal index such that
\sigma_{m}\geq 2
. We refer to the tuple
(\sigma_{m},\dots,\sigma_{r})
as the
stable coefficients
of
\bm{\sigma}
.
3
3

3

In Part 2 it will be convenient to use some alternative conventions to express the stable coefficients. In that section, the stable coefficients will be written in the form
(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{\ell})
where the coefficients
\rho_{i}=\sigma_{r+1-i}
are a non-increasing sequence and
\ell=r+1-m
.
If no such index exists, that is,
\sigma_{r}=1
, then the tuple of stable coefficients is defined to be empty.

If there is an index
k\geq 2
such that
\sigma_{k}=1+\sigma_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{k-1}
, then the coefficient
\sigma_{k}
is
tight
and correspondingly the vector
\bm{\sigma}
is
tight
.

The key combinatorial property of changemaker vectors is contained in the following proposition, whose proof is left as a simple exercise.

Proposition 3.1

Let
\bm{\sigma}=\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}
be a changemaker vector.
Then for any
k\leq r
and any integer
T\leq\sigma_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{k}
, there is a subset
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}
such that

T=\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}.\qed

We also make the following observation about changemaker vectors with a given set of stable coefficients.

Proposition 3.2

Let
\underline{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{\ell})
be a tuple of non-decreasing integers with
\rho_{\ell}\geq 2
and let
n
be an integer.
Then there exists a changemaker vector
\bm{\sigma}
with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}
and
\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert^{2}=n
if and only if
n\geq N-1
where

N=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\rho_{i}^{2}+\max_{1\leq k\leq\ell}\left(\rho_{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\rho_{i}\right).

Proof.

A changemaker vector with these stable coefficients takes the form

\bm{\sigma}=\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\bm{e}_{m-1}+\rho_{1}\bm{e}_{m}+\dots+\rho_{\ell}\bm{e}_{m+\ell-1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m+\ell-1}.

If we write
\bm{\sigma}
in the form

\bm{\sigma}=\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{m+\ell-1}\bm{e}_{m+\ell-1},

then the coefficients satisfy

\sigma_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $1\leq i<m$}\\
\rho_{i-m+1}&\text{if $m\leq i\leq m+\ell-1$.}\end{cases}

Since these coefficients are non-decreasing, we see that
\bm{\sigma}
is changemaker vector if and only if the inequality

\sigma_{j}\leq 1+\sigma_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{j-1}

holds for all
j
in the range
m\leq j\leq m+\ell-1
. If we write such a
j
in the form
j=m-1+k
for
k
in the range
1\leq k\leq\ell
this condition becomes

\displaystyle\rho_{k}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sigma_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{j-1}

\displaystyle=m+\rho_{1}+\dots+\rho_{k-1}.

Thus
\bm{\sigma}
is a changemaker vector if and only if

m\geq\max_{1\leq k\leq\ell}\left(\rho_{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\rho_{i}\right).

However,
\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert^{2}=m-1+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\rho_{i}^{2}
, so we see that
\bm{\sigma}
is a changemaker vector if and only if

\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert^{2}=n\geq N-1
, where
N
is as in the statement of the proposition.
∎

For any rational number
p/q>0
, we can define the notion of a
p/q
-changemaker lattice. For technical reasons it is convenient to give the definition of integer and non-integer changemaker lattices separately. We mention also that the vast majority of our work will be with integer changemaker lattices, so the following definition is the most important of the two.

Definition 3.3

(Integer changemaker lattice)

For an integer
n\geq 1
, an
n
-changemaker lattice

L
is an orthogonal complement:

L=\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r},

where
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
is a changemaker vector satisfying
\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert^{2}=n
. We will refer to the stable coefficients of
\bm{\sigma}
as the stable coefficients of
L
. We say also that
L
is tight if the changemaker
\bm{\sigma}
is tight.

The corresponding definition for non-integer changemaker lattices is the following.

Definition 3.4

(Non-integer changemaker lattice)

Let
p/q>0
be a rational number which is not an integer. This admits a unique continued fraction of the form

p/q=[a_{0},\dots,a_{l}]^{-}=a_{0}-\cfrac{1}{a_{1}-\cfrac{1}{\ddots-\cfrac{1}{a_{l}}}},

where
a_{0}\geq 1
and
a_{i}\geq 2
for

. Let
\bm{w}_{0},\dots,\bm{w}_{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+s+1}
be vectors satisfying,

\bm{w}_{i}\cdot\bm{w}_{j}=\begin{cases}a_{i}&\text{if $i=j$,}\\
-1&\text{if $|i-j|=1$,}\\
0&\text{if $|i-j|>1$,}\end{cases}

such that for some orthonormal basis
\{\bm{e}_{-s},\dots,\bm{e}_{r}\}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{r+s+1}
following hold:

(I)

\bm{w}_{0}
takes the form
\bm{w}_{0}=\bm{e}_{0}+\bm{\sigma}
where

\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}=\langle\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{r}\rangle

is a changemaker vector.

(II)

|\bm{w}_{k}\cdot\bm{e}_{i}|\leq 1
for all
1\leq k\leq l
and all
\bm{e}_{i}
;

(III)

for any
0\leq i<j\leq l
,

\bm{w}_{i}\cdot\bm{w}_{j}=-|I_{j}\cap I_{i}|=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if $j=i+1$}\\
0&\text{if $j>i+1$};\end{cases}

where
I_{k}
is the set
I_{k}=\{\bm{e}_{j}\,|\,\bm{w}_{k}\cdot\bm{e}_{j}\neq 0\}
; and

(IV)

for any
\bm{e}_{i}
there is
\bm{w}_{k}
such that
\bm{e}_{i}\cdot\bm{w}_{k}\neq 0
.

Then the orthogonal complement

L=\langle\bm{w}_{0},\dots,\bm{w}_{l}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r+s+1}

is a
p/q
-changemaker lattice
. We call the stable coefficients of
\bm{\sigma}
the
stable coefficients
of
L
.

The only examples of non-integer changemaker lattices that we will ever need to consider in detail will be half-integer changemaker lattices, which will make an appearance in §

and §

Example 3.5

Definition

implies that an

The following summarizes the majority of what we need to know about arbitrary changemaker lattices.

Remark 3.6

(1)

A changemaker lattice
L
never contains any elements of length one. That is, for all
\bm{v}\in L\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
we have
\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert^{2}\geq 2
. In the non-integer case this is consequence of Condition IV.

(2)

A

-changemaker lattice is determined up to isomorphism by its stable coefficients. In both the integer and non-integer cases, all remaining non-zero coefficients of

are equal to one and so the number of such coefficients is determined by the requirement that

. In the non-integer case, the remaining

are determined up to automorphism of the ambient diagonal lattice by Conditions II and III.

(3)

Moreover given a non-decreasing tuple of integers

, there is a
p/q
-changemaker lattice
L
with these stable coefficients whenever

is sufficiently large. Explicitly there is a
p/q
-changemaker changemaker lattice with these stable coefficients if and only if

, where

is the quantity defined in Proposition

.

(4)

The discriminant of a

-changemaker lattice

is

. See
[
22
, Lemma 3.10]
for the integer case and
[
35
, Remark 2.19]
for the general case.

We now turn our attention to understanding when an integer changemaker lattice can admit an obtuse superbase. Throughout this section we will take

\bm{\sigma}=\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}

Remark 4.1

A changemaker lattice with empty stable coefficients takes the form

This always has an obtuse superbase. For example, one can take

Using Proposition

. The standard basis for

is tight and

\bm{v}^{(k)}=-\bm{e}_{k}+\sum_{i\in A_{k}}\bm{e}_{i}

where

is a subset such that

\sigma_{k}=\sum_{i\in A_{k}}\sigma_{i}

and that all elements of a standard basis are irreducible

Example 4.2

If

The following lemma is useful for finding irreducible vectors in a changemaker lattice. For example, it implies, among other things, that the standard basis vector

Lemma 4.3

(
[

Let

\bm{v}=\sum_{i\in A}\bm{e}_{i}-\sum_{j\in B}\bm{e}_{j}

is reducible if and only if there are proper non-empty subsets

\sum_{i\in A^{\prime}}\sigma_{i}=\sum_{j\in B^{\prime}}\sigma_{j}.\qed

Next we recall restrictions on decomposable changemaker lattices.

Lemma 4.4

(
[
22

Suppose that the changemaker lattice

is decomposable. Then

has two indecomposable summands. Moreover, if

. ∎

This allows us to show that an obtuse superbase for

contains a basis consisting of irreducible vectors.

denote a pair of disjoint graphs with distinguished vertices

Lemma 4.5

Any obtuse superbase for an integer changemaker lattice

contains at most one reducible vector. In particular, any obtuse superbase for

Proof.

Let
B
be an obtuse superbase for

We also obtain bounds on the coefficients of irreducible vectors.

Lemma 4.6

Let
\bm{z}\in L

E=2V-(b-a+1).

When combined with the earlier observations that
V\geq b-a
and
E\leq V-1
, this quickly yields the fact that
V=b-a
and
E=V-1
. Thus
H
is a path of length
b-a
.

Let
\bm{v}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{v}^{(b-a)}
be the vertices of the path
H
ordered so that
\bm{v}^{(j)}
and
\bm{v}^{(j+1)}
are adjacent for all
j
. For each pair
\bm{v}^{(j)}
and
\bm{v}^{(j+1)}
, there is a unique index
a\leq i\leq b
such that
\bm{v}^{(j)}_{i}=-\bm{v}^{(j+1)}_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
and
\bm{v}^{(k)}_{i}=0
for all other
k
. Given this, it is not hard see that by permuting the basis vectors
\bm{e}_{a},\dots,\bm{e}_{b}
and potentially reversing the order of labelling on the path
\bm{v}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{v}^{(b-a)}
we may assume that these vertices take the form

\bm{v}^{(1)}=-\bm{e}_{a+1}+\bm{e}_{a},\dots,\bm{v}^{(b-a)}=-\bm{e}_{b}+\bm{e}_{b-1},

as required.
∎

The following is useful for ruling out certain sets of stable coefficients from possessing obtuse superbases.

Proposition 4.8

Let
L
be a changemaker lattice with non-trivial stable coefficients that admits an obtuse superbase. Assume the changemaker coefficients satisfy

\sigma_{k}=\dots=\sigma_{k+3}

for some
k
. If
\bm{z}\in L
is an irreducible vector with
z_{k}=z_{k+1}
and
z_{k+2}=z_{k+3}
, then
z_{k+2}=z_{k+1}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.7
we can assume that
L
has an obtuse superbase
B
containing vectors
\bm{w}_{0}=\bm{e}_{k}-\bm{e}_{k+1},\bm{w}_{1}=\bm{e}_{k+1}-\bm{e}_{k+2},\bm{w}_{2}=\bm{e}_{k+2}-\bm{e}_{k+3}
. If
z_{k}=z_{k+1}
and
z_{k+2}=z_{k+3}
, then we have
\bm{z}\cdot\bm{w}_{0}=\bm{z}\cdot\bm{w}_{2}=0
. Since
\bm{z}
is irreducible, Lemma
2.7
gives a connected subgraph
R
of
G_{B}
such that
\bm{z}=\sum_{\bm{v}\in R}\bm{v}
.

Up to sign, the pairing
\bm{z}\cdot\bm{w}_{0}
counts the number of edges incident to
\bm{w}_{0}
with precisely one end point in
R
. Therefore, if precisely one of
\bm{w}_{0}
and
\bm{w}_{1}
were contained in
R
, then we would have
|\bm{z}\cdot\bm{w}_{0}|=1
. Since
\bm{z}\cdot\bm{w}_{0}=0
, it follows that either both
\bm{w}_{0}
and
\bm{w}_{1}
are contained in
R
or both are contained in the complement of
R
. Since
\bm{z}\cdot\bm{w}_{2}=0
, a similar argument applies to show that we cannot have precisely one of
\bm{w}_{1}
and
\bm{w}_{2}
contained in
R
.
Thus all three of
\bm{w}_{0},\bm{w}_{1},\bm{w}_{2}
are contained in
R
or all three are contained in the complement of
R
. In either case, neither of the edges incident to
\bm{w}_{1}
has precisely one end point in
R
. This implies that
\bm{w}_{1}\cdot\bm{z}=0
or, equivalently, that
z_{k+2}=z_{k+1}
.
∎

When applied to the changemaker coefficients satisfying
\sigma_{k}=1
, Proposition
4.8
yields the following restriction. This formed a key step in the proof of
[
37
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Proposition 4.9

Let
L
be an integral changemaker lattice of the form

L=\langle\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r}

with non-trivial stable coefficients. Let
m
be minimal such that
\sigma_{m}\geq 2
. If
L
admits an obtuse superbase, then

\sigma_{m}\geq m-2

and

\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert^{2}\leq 1+\sigma_{m}+\sum_{i=m}^{r}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

Proof.

Suppose that
m\geq 3+\sigma_{m}
. We will show that
L
does not admit an obtuse superbase. Consider the vector
\bm{v}=-\bm{e}_{m}+\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\bm{e}_{\sigma_{m}}\in L
. By Lemma
4.3
,
\bm{v}
is irreducible. However we have

\sigma_{\sigma_{m}-1}=\sigma_{\sigma_{m}}=\sigma_{\sigma_{m}+1}=\sigma_{\sigma_{m}+2}=1

and

1=v_{\sigma_{m}-1}=v_{\sigma_{m}}\neq v_{\sigma_{m}+1}=v_{\sigma_{m}+2}=0

so Proposition
4.8
implies that
L
does not admit an obtuse superbase. The bound on
\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert^{2}
follows from the fact that

\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert^{2}=m-1+\sum_{i=m}^{r}\sigma_{i}^{2}.\qed

5.
Very slack changemaker lattices

The aim of this section is to understand when a very slack changemaker lattice can admit an obtuse superbase. The conclusion to this work is Theorem
5.11
, which is the only result in this section that is used elsewhere in the paper. The bulk of the section is devoted to proving a technical lemma, namely Lemma
5.5
.

Let
\bm{\sigma}=\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}
be a changemaker vector with
\sigma_{1}=1
and
\sigma_{r}>1
. We take
m>1
to be minimal such that
\sigma_{m}>1
. The vector
\bm{\sigma}
is
very slack
if it satisfies

\sigma_{k}\leq\sigma_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{k-1}-1

for all
\sigma_{k}>1
. Unsurprisingly, we say that a changemaker lattice

L=\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r}

is very slack if
\bm{\sigma}
is a very slack changemaker vector.

Remark 5.1

An argument similar to that of Proposition
3.2
shows that if
L
is a
n
-changemaker lattice with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{\ell})
(ordered as an non-decreasing tuple) and

N=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\rho_{i}^{2}+\max_{1\leq k\leq\ell}\left(\rho_{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\rho_{i}\right),

then
L
is very slack if and only if
n\geq N+1
.

The very slack condition leads to the following refinement of Proposition
3.1
.

Lemma 5.2

Let
\bm{\sigma}=\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}
be a very slack changemaker vector and let
m>1
be minimal such that
\sigma_{m}>1
. Then for every
\sigma_{k}>1
, there is
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,k-1\}
such that

\sigma_{k}=\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}
and
1\leq|A\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}|\leq m-2
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
k\geq m
. Firstly the statement is true for
\sigma_{m}
since the condition that
\bm{\sigma}
is very slack implies that
\sigma_{m}\leq m-2
and hence that

\sigma_{m}=\sigma_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{\sigma_{m}}.

Now suppose that
k>m
. By Proposition
3.1
, there exists
B\subseteq\{1,\dots,k-1\}
such that
\sigma_{k}=\sum_{i\in B}\sigma_{i}
. There are three possibilities for the size of
|B\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}|
to consider.

•

If
1\leq|B\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}|\leq m-2
, then we may simply take
A=B
.

•

If
|B\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}|=0
, then take
\ell=\min B\geq m
. By induction there exists
A^{\prime}\subseteq\{1,\dots,\ell-1\}
such that
\sigma_{\ell}=\sum_{i\in A^{\prime}}\sigma_{i}
and
1\leq|A^{\prime}\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}|\leq m-2
. We may take
A=(B\setminus\{\ell\})\cup A^{\prime}
in this case.

•

If
|B\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}|=m-1
, then we take
\ell\geq m
to be the minimal index not contained in
B
. Since
\sigma_{k}<\sigma_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{k-1}
, we have
\ell<k
. By induction, there exists
A^{\prime}\subseteq\{1,\dots,\ell-1\}
such that
\sigma_{\ell}=\sum_{i\in A^{\prime}}\sigma_{i}
and
1\leq|A^{\prime}\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}|\leq m-2
. We may take
A=(B\setminus A^{\prime})\cup\{\ell\}
in this case.∎

We will also using the following two general results on obtuse superbases.

Lemma 5.3

(
[
36
, Lemma 2.1]
)

Let
L
be a lattice with an obtuse superbase
B
. If
\bm{z}=\sum_{\bm{v}\in R}\bm{v}
for
R\subset B
, then for any
\bm{x}\in L
, we have

(\bm{z}-\bm{x})\cdot\bm{x}\leq 0.\qed

Lemma 5.4

(
[
37
, Lemma 3.5]
)

Let
L
be an indecomposable lattice with an obtuse superbase
B
. Suppose that
\bm{v}\in B
can be decomposed as
\bm{v}=\bm{x}+\bm{y}
with
\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}=-1
. Then there exists a unique pair
\bm{u}^{(1)},\bm{u}^{(2)}\in B\setminus\{\bm{v}\}
such that
\bm{u}^{(1)}\cdot\bm{x}=1
,
\bm{u}^{(2)}\cdot\bm{y}=1
and
\bm{u}^{(1)}\cdot\bm{u}^{(2)}=-1
.
Moreover,

B^{\prime}=B\setminus\{\bm{v},\bm{u}^{(1)},\bm{u}^{(2)}\}\cup\{\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{u}^{(1)}+\bm{u}^{(2)}\}

is also an obtuse superbase for
L
.∎

5.1.
Proving Lemma
5.5

This entire subsection is devoted to the proof of the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5

Let
L
be a very slack changemaker lattice. If
L
admits an obtuse superbase, then
L
an admits an obtuse superbase
B
, such that
\bm{v}^{(2)}=-\bm{e}_{2}+\bm{e}_{1}
is in

and there is a vertex
\bm{w}
with
w_{1}=w_{2}=-1
. Moreover for any other vertex
\bm{v}\in B
distinct from
\bm{v}^{(2)}
and

.

Working Assumption:
Throughout this section we take

L=\langle\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r},

to be a very slack changemaker lattice admitting an obtuse superbase
B
. We take
m>2
to be minimal such that
\sigma_{m}\geq 2
. The very slack condition implies that
\sigma_{m}\leq m-2
and hence that

.

This has a number of consequences:

•

Proposition
4.9
implies that
\sigma_{m}=m-2
.

•

Lemma
4.4
shows that

is indecomposable.

•

Lemma
2.8
implies that every element of

is therefore irreducible and the associated graph

is 2-connected.

Given the existence of the obtuse superbase, we may further refine Lemma
5.2

Lemma 5.6

If
L
is very slack and admits an obtuse superbase, then for all
\sigma_{k}>1
, there exists a subset
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,k-1\}
such that

\sigma_{k}=\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}

and

A\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}=\{1\}
or
A\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}=\{2,\dots,m-1\}
.

Proof.

Lemma
5.2
shows that there exists a set
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,k-1\}
such that

\sigma_{k}=\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}

and

1\leq|A\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}|\leq m-2.

To conclude the proof it suffices to show that we have
A
with

\text{$|A\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}|=1$ or $m-2$}.

Thus suppose that

|A\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}|=\ell

for
2\leq\ell\leq m-3
.
Up to reordering the indices, we may assume that

A\cap\{1,\dots,m-1\}=\{1,\dots,\ell\}.

The vector

\bm{v}=-\bm{e}_{k}+\sum_{i\in A}\bm{e}_{i}\in L

is irreducible by Lemma
4.3
. But by construction
\bm{v}
satisfies

1=v_{\ell-1}=v_{\ell}\neq v_{\ell+1}=v_{\ell+2}=0,

shows to be incompatible with the existence of an obtuse superbase for

∎

Now we turn our attention to understanding the possibilities for the obtuse superbase
B
. By Lemma
4.7
we can assume that the the standard basis vectors

\bm{v}^{(2)}=\bm{e}_{1}-\bm{e}_{2},\dots,\bm{v}^{(m-1)}=\bm{e}_{m-2}-\bm{e}_{m-1}

are all in
B
. There are vertices
\bm{u}^{(1)},\bm{u}^{(2)}\not\in\{\bm{v}^{(2)},\dots,\bm{v}^{(m-1)}\}
such that
\bm{u}^{(1)}
pairs non-trivially with
\bm{v}^{(2)}
and
\bm{u}^{(2)}
pairs non-trivially with
\bm{v}^{(m-1)}
. Since
G_{B}
is 2-connected, we see that
\bm{u}^{(1)}
and
\bm{u}^{(2)}
must be distinct. Now we study the possibilities for
\bm{u}^{(1)}
and
\bm{u}^{(2)}
.

Lemma 5.7

The vertex
\bm{u}^{(1)}
must take one of the two forms:

(A1)

u^{(1)}_{1}=-1
and
u^{(1)}_{2}=\dots=u^{(1)}_{m-1}=0

(A2)

u^{(1)}_{1}=0
and
u^{(1)}_{2}=\dots=u^{(1)}_{m-1}=1

The vertex
\bm{u}^{(2)}
must take one of the following two forms:

(B1)

u^{(2)}_{m-1}=1
and
u^{(2)}_{1}=\dots=u^{(2)}_{m-2}=0

(B2)

u^{(2)}_{m-1}=0
and
u^{(2)}_{1}=\dots=u^{(2)}_{m-2}=-1

Any other vertex
\bm{v}\not\in\{\bm{v}^{(2)},\dots,\bm{v}^{(m-1)},\bm{u}^{(1)},\bm{u}^{(2)}\}
must satisfy one of the following conditions:

(C1)

v_{1}=\dots=v_{m-1}=0

(C2)

v_{1}=\dots=v_{m-1}=1

(C3)

v_{1}=\dots=v_{m-1}=-1

Proof.

Since
L
is not tight, Lemma
4.6
, shows that for any
\bm{v}\in B
, we have
|v_{i}|\leq 1
for
i=1,\dots,m-1
. The remaining restrictions on coefficients come from considering the pairings with
\bm{v}^{(2)},\dots,\bm{v}^{(m-1)}
. In particular,
\bm{u}^{(1)}\cdot\bm{v}^{(k)}=0
for
k>2
and
\bm{u}^{(2)}\cdot\bm{v}^{(k)}=0
for
k<m-1
.
∎

The following lemma allows us to restrict the number of
\bm{v}\in B
with
v_{1}\neq 0
.

Lemma 5.8

Let
\bm{z}=\sum_{\bm{v}\in R}\bm{v}
for
R\subseteq B
. Then

|z_{k}|\leq 2

for any
k
with
\sigma_{k}=1
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
|z_{1}|\leq 2
. Without loss of generality, we can consider the case that
z_{1}>0
. Let
g>1
be minimal such that
z_{g}\leq 0
. By Proposition
3.1
, there is a subset
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,g-1\}
such that
\sigma_{g}-1=\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}
. This allows us to define
\bm{x}\in L
by

\bm{x}=-\bm{e}_{g}+\bm{e}_{1}+\sum_{i\in A}\bm{e}_{i}.

By construction we have
x_{1}\in\{1,2\}
.
By Lemma
5.3
, we have that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\bm{x}\cdot(\bm{z}-\bm{x})=-(z_{g}+1)+(x_{1})(z_{1}-x_{1})+\sum_{i\in A\setminus\{1\}}(z_{i}-1)

\displaystyle\geq-1+x_{1}(z_{1}-x_{1}).

Since
x_{1}\in\{1,2\}
, this implies that
z_{1}\leq\frac{5}{2}
. Since

is an integer, this implies

, as required.
∎

This implies several possible combinations of vertices in

.

Lemma 5.9

We can assume one of the following situation holds:

(I)

\bm{u}^{(1)}
is of type
A1
,
\bm{u}^{(2)}
is of type
B1
, there is one vertex of type
C2
, one vertex of type

, and all other vertices are of type

(II)

\bm{u}^{(1)}
is of type
A2
,
\bm{u}^{(2)}
is of type
B1
, and there is one vertex of type
C3
and all other vertices are of type
C1
.

(III)

\bm{u}^{(1)}
is of type
A2
,
\bm{u}^{(2)}
is of type
B2
, and all other vertices are of type
C1

Proof.

Lemma

\bm{u}^{(1)}

\bm{u}^{(2)}

Suppose that
\bm{u}^{(1)}

\bm{u}^{(2)}

Suppose that
\bm{u}^{(1)}

\bm{u}^{(2)}

\bm{u}^{(1)}

Lemma 5.10

Let
\bm{v}
be a vertex of type

5.2.
The main result

Armed with Lemma

, we are ready to proof the key technical result of this section.

Theorem 5.11

Let
L_{n}
be a very slack
n
-changemaker lattice which admits an obtuse superbase
B
. Let
L_{n-\frac{1}{2}}
and
L_{n-1}
be the
(n-\frac{1}{2})
- and
(n-1)
-changemaker lattices with the same stable coefficients as

Proof.

6.
Changemaker foundations for genus bounds on alternating surgeries

In this section we develop the necessary changemaker material to prove the genus bounds on alternating surgeries comprising Theorem
1.12
. The key technical result on changemaker lattices will be the following.

Theorem 6.1

Let
L
be the
n
-changemaker lattice of the form

L=\langle\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r}

where
\sigma_{r}\geq 3
. If
L
admits an obtuse superbase, then

(6.1)

n\leq 4+\frac{3}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}(\sigma_{i}-1).

As an application of Proposition
4.8
we get the following restriction on changemaker lattices admitting an obtuse superbase.

Proposition 6.2

Suppose that
L
is an integral changemaker lattice of the form

L=\langle\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r}.

Suppose that for some
\ell\geq 4
and some
b>1
there the changemaker coefficients satisfy

\sigma_{b-1}<\sigma_{b}=\dots=\sigma_{b+\ell-1}.

If
L
admits an obtuse superbase, then for any
\sigma_{k}>\sigma_{b}
, we have
\sigma_{k}\geq(\ell-1)\sigma_{b}
.

Proof.

We will show that if there is
\sigma_{k}
satisfying
\sigma_{b}<\sigma_{k}<(\ell-1)\sigma_{b}
, then
L
does not admit an obtuse superbase. First suppose that
\sigma_{b}<\sigma_{k}\leq 2\sigma_{b}
. This implies that
\sigma_{b}>2\sigma_{b}-\sigma_{k}\geq 0
. By Proposition
3.1
, we can find a subset
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,b-1\}
such that

\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}=2\sigma_{b}-\sigma_{k}.

This allows us to take
\bm{z}=\bm{e}_{k}-\bm{e}_{b}-\bm{e}_{b+1}+\sum_{i\in A}\bm{e}_{i}\in L
. By Lemma
4.3
,
\bm{z}
is irreducible. However
\bm{z}
satisfies
z_{b}=z_{b+1}=-1
,
z_{b+2}=z_{b+3}=0
so
L
cannot admit an obtuse superbase by Proposition
4.8
.

Now suppose that
2\sigma_{b}\leq\sigma_{k}<(\ell-1)\sigma_{b}
. In this case we can write
\sigma_{k}=s\sigma_{b}+r
, where
0\leq r<\sigma_{b}
and
2\leq s\leq\ell-2
. By Proposition
3.1
, there is
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,b-1\}
such that

\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}=r.

This allows us to take
\bm{z}=-\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{b}+\dots+\bm{e}_{b+s-1}+\sum_{i\in A}\bm{e}_{i}\in L
, which is irreducible by Lemma
4.3
. By Proposition
4.8
, there can be no obtuse superbase since
z_{b+s-2}=z_{b-s-1}=1
and
z_{b+s}=z_{b+s+1}=0
(by hypothesis
b+s+1\leq b+\ell-1
, so we have
\sigma_{b+s-2}=\sigma_{b-s-1}=\sigma_{b+s}=\sigma_{b+s+1}
).
∎

The proof of Theorem
6.1
is broken down into several cases based on the stable coefficients of the lattice
L
. We will make frequent use of the following identity

(6.2)

n=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{3}{2}\sigma_{i}(\sigma_{i}-1)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{i}(3-\sigma_{i}).

Firstly, we treat the case where all the stable coefficients are at least three.

Lemma 6.3

Let
L
be an
n
-changemaker lattice of the form

L=\langle\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r}

where
\sigma_{r}\geq 3
and
\sigma_{i}\neq 2
for all
i
. If
L
admits an obtuse superbase, then (
6.1
) holds.

Proof.

Let
m
be minimal such that
\sigma_{m}\geq 2
. By hypothesis, we have in fact that
\sigma_{k}\geq 3
for all
k\geq m
. We thus have the bound

\displaystyle n-\frac{3}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}(\sigma_{i}-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}(3-\sigma_{i})

\displaystyle=m-1+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=m}^{r}\sigma_{i}(3-\sigma_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{m}(3-\sigma_{m})+\sigma_{m}+1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{m}(5-\sigma_{m})+1

\displaystyle\leq 4,

where we used that:
\sigma_{i}(3-\sigma_{i})\leq 0
for all
\sigma_{i}\geq 3
; that
m\leq\sigma_{m}+2
by Proposition
4.9
; and that
\sigma_{m}(5-\sigma_{m})\leq 6
since
\sigma_{m}\geq 2
is an integer.
∎

Next we address the case that the largest stable coefficient is at least four.

Lemma 6.4

Let
L
be an
n
-changemaker lattice of the form

L=\langle\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r}

where
\sigma_{r}\geq 4
. If
L
admits an obtuse superbase, then (
6.1
) holds.

Proof.

Let
m
be minimal such that
\sigma_{m}\geq 2
. If
\sigma_{m}>2
, then Lemma
6.3
yields the desired conclusion. Thus we can assume that
\sigma_{m}=2
. By Proposition
4.9
, this implies that
m\leq 4
. Let
\ell\geq 1
be the number of
\sigma_{i}
which equal 2, that is, we have that
\sigma_{m}=\dots=\sigma_{m+\ell-1}=2
and
\sigma_{m+\ell}\geq 3
.
By Proposition
6.2
, we have either
\ell\leq 3
or
\sigma_{m+\ell}\geq 2(\ell-1)
. In either case we have that

\ell\leq\max\left\{3,1+\frac{\sigma_{m+\ell}}{2}\right\}.

Since
\sigma_{r}\geq 4
and
\sigma_{r}\geq\sigma_{m+\ell}
, this yields

\ell\leq 1+\frac{\sigma_{r}}{2}.

Thus we obtain the bound

\displaystyle n-\frac{3}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}(\sigma_{i}-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}(3-\sigma_{i})

\displaystyle=\ell+m-1+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=m+\ell}^{r}\sigma_{i}(3-\sigma_{i})

\displaystyle\leq 4+\frac{\sigma_{r}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{r}(3-\sigma_{r})

\displaystyle\leq 4,

where the final inequality comes from the observation that

\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{r}(3-\sigma_{r})+\frac{\sigma_{r}}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{r}(4-\sigma_{r})\leq 0,

since
\sigma_{r}\geq 4
.
∎

By far the most delicate case is the one where the stable coefficients consist solely of twos and threes.

Lemma 6.5

Let
L
be an
n
-changemaker lattice of the form

L=\langle\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\bm{e}_{m-1}+2\bm{e}_{m}+\dots+2\bm{e}_{m+\ell-1}+3\bm{e}_{m+\ell}+\dots+3\bm{e}_{r}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r}

and
\ell\geq 1

(2,2,2,3)
,
(2,2,2,3,3)
or
(2,2,2,3,3,3)

Proof.

For such a changemaker lattice, we have

n-\frac{3}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}(\sigma_{i}-1)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}(3-\sigma_{i})=m+\ell-1.

Thus, we are required to show that if
L
admits an obtuse superbase, then
m+\ell\leq 5
.

If
\ell\geq 4
, then Proposition
6.2
shows that
L
does not admit an obtuse superbase. Thus we consider the possibilities
\ell=1,2,3
.

If
L
admits an obtuse superbase, then Proposition
4.9
shows that
m\leq 4
. This automatically yields the required bound when
\ell=1
.

Next suppose that
\ell=2
. If
m=4
, then the lattice
L
is very slack. Thus if
L
admitted an obtuse superbase, then Theorem
5.11
would imply that the changemaker lattice

L^{\prime\prime}=\langle\bm{e}_{1}+\bm{e}_{2}+2\bm{e}_{3}+2\bm{e}_{4}+3\bm{e}_{5}+\dots+3\bm{e}_{r-1}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r-1}

is decomposable. However, one can easily show that such a lattice is indecomposable using the techniques of
[
22

.
5
5

5

If one considers the standard basis for

, then the corresponding pairing graph is connected. Since the standard basis consists of irreducible vectors, this implies that
L^{\prime\prime}
is indecomposable.
Therefore, if
\ell=2
and
L
admits an obtuse superbase, then
m\leq 3
and the desired bound holds.

Finally, suppose that
\ell=3
, in this case
L
takes the form

L=\langle\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\bm{e}_{m-1}+2\bm{e}_{m}+2\bm{e}_{m+1}+2\bm{e}_{m+2}+3\bm{e}_{m+3}+\dots+3\bm{e}_{r}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r}.

The stable coefficients of this lattice take the form

(2,2,2,\underbrace{3,\dots,3}_{r-m-2})

Suppose that there are at least four changemaker coefficients equal to three. This allows us to apply Proposition
4.8
to the vector

\bm{z}=\bm{e}_{m+3}+\bm{e}_{m+4}-\bm{e}_{m+2}-\bm{e}_{m+1}-\bm{e}_{m}\in L,

which is irreducible by Lemma

does not admit an obtuse superbase if there are at least four changemaker coefficients equal to three (

).

Thus we have established (

) except when the stable coefficients of

take the form
(2,2,2,3)
,
(2,2,2,3,3)
or
(2,2,2,3,3,3)
.
∎

It remains to consider the cases where

has stable coefficients of the form
(2,2,2,3)
,
(2,2,2,3,3)
or
(2,2,2,3,3,3)

Lemma 6.6

Let

be an integer changemaker lattice with stable coefficients of the form
(2,2,2,3)
,
(2,2,2,3,3)
or

Proof.

Let
L

Putting all these results together we get the main theorem of this section.

Proof of Theorem

Let
(\sigma_{m},\dots,\sigma_{r})

7.
Algorithmically searching for obtuse superbases.

Although Lemma
2.10
is sufficient on its own to provide a naive algorithm that will find an obtuse superbase for a given changemaker lattice
L
whenever it exists (list the finitely many vectors
\bm{v}\in L
with
\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\operatorname{disc}(L)
and then check each subset of appropriate size in turn to see if it forms an obtuse superbase), this algorithm is far too slow in reality to be practical. This section collects various results that allow us to develop a reasonably efficient algorithm for finding an obtuse superbase or, more importantly, proving that no obtuse superbase exists. The results of this section will be used purely for computational purposes and will not be used in any of the theoretical results in this paper. We concentrate on the case of integer and half-integer changemaker lattices.

7.1.
Integer lattices

Throughout this subsection we will take
L
to be an
n
-changemaker lattice in the form

L=\langle\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r},

where
\sigma_{r}>1
and
m>1
is minimal such that
\sigma_{m}>1
.
We can exploit Lemma
2.10
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to get a coordinate-wise bound for obtuse superbase elements in a changemaker lattice.

Lemma 7.1

Let
L
be an
n
-changemaker lattice which admits an obtuse superbase
B
. Then for any element
\bm{z}\in B
and any index
1\leq k\leq r
we have

|z_{k}|\leq\sqrt{n-\sigma_{k}^{2}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.10
and the fact that
n
is the discriminant of
L

[
22
, Lemma 3.10]
, we have that
\left\lVert\bm{z}\right\rVert^{2}\leq n
.
Now since
\bm{z}\in L
we have that
\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{z}=0
, thus for any
k
we have

z_{k}\sigma_{k}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{r}\sigma_{i}z_{i}.

However, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have that

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{r}\sigma_{i}z_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{r}z_{i}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{r}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=(\left\lVert\bm{z}\right\rVert^{2}-z_{k}^{2})(n-\sigma_{k}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq(n-z_{k}^{2})(n-\sigma_{k}^{2}).

Thus we have that

|z_{k}\sigma_{k}|^{2}\leq(n-z_{k}^{2})(n-\sigma_{k}^{2}).

Rearranging and taking square roots gives the desired bound.
∎

Lemma 7.2

Let
\bm{z}\in L
be an irreducible vector. Then the coordinates
z_{k}
satisfy the following bounds.

(i)

If
\sigma_{k}=1
, then

|z_{k}|\leq\begin{cases}2&\text{if $L$ is tight}\\
1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(ii)

If
\sigma_{k}>1
is not tight and
\sigma_{k}=\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}
for
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,k-1\}
, then

|z_{k}|\leq\sum_{i\in A}(|z_{i}|+1).

(iii)

If
\sigma_{k}>1
is tight, then

|z_{k}|\leq 5+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(|z_{i}|+1).

Proof.

The bound
(i)
is a restatement of Lemma
4.6
.

Suppose that
\sigma_{k}>1
is not tight and
\sigma_{k}=\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}
for
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,k-1\}
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
z_{k}\leq 0
. Set
\bm{x}=-\bm{e}_{k}+\sum_{i\in A}\bm{e}_{i}\in L
. Since
\bm{x}
satisfies the bound in
(ii)
, we can assume that
\bm{x}\neq\bm{z}
. Using irreducibility gives

\displaystyle-1

\displaystyle\geq\bm{x}\cdot(\bm{z}-\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-(z_{k}+1)+\sum_{i\in A}(z_{i}-1).

Rearranging gives

z_{k}\geq\sum_{i\in A}(z_{i}-1)\geq-\sum_{i\in A}(|z_{i}|+1),

which is the bound
(ii)
.

Finally, suppose that
\sigma_{k}>1
is tight. Without loss of generality assume that
z_{k}\leq 0
In this case take
\bm{x}
to be the standard basis element
\bm{x}=-\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{k-1}+\dots+\bm{e}_{2}+2\bm{e}_{1}
. Again we may assume that
\bm{x}\neq\bm{z}
. So by irreducibility, we have

\displaystyle-1

\displaystyle\geq\bm{x}\cdot(\bm{z}-\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-(z_{k}+1)+\sum_{i=2}^{k-1}(z_{i}-1)+2(z_{1}-2).

Rearranging this inequality gives

z_{k}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(z_{i}-1)+z_{1}-3\geq-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(|z_{i}|+1)-5,

where we used the bound
z_{1}\geq-2
, derived from
(i)
in the last inequality. This establishes the bound
(iii)
.
∎

We will also make use of a more elaborate bound on irreducible elements.

Lemma 7.3

If
\bm{z}\in L
is irreducible, then
|z_{m}|\leq\sigma_{m}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality let us suppose that
z_{m}>0
. Consider also the sets

A_{+}=\{i\,|\,z_{i}\geq 1,1\leq i\leq m-1\}

and

A_{-}=\{i\,|\,z_{i}\leq-1,1\leq i\leq m-1\}.

First we observe that at least one of
A_{+}
or
A_{-}
must be empty. If we had
a\in A_{+}
and
b\in A_{-}
, then
\bm{x}=\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b}\in L
would be a vector
\bm{x}\neq\bm{z}
satisfying

\bm{x}\cdot(\bm{z}-\bm{x})=z_{a}-z_{b}-2\geq 0.

Since this contradicts the irreducibility of
\bm{z}
, we see that at least one of
A_{+}
or
A_{-}
must be empty.

Note that if
\sigma_{m}
is tight, then
\sigma_{m}=m
and if
\sigma_{m}
is not tight, then
\sigma_{m}\leq m-1
. We prove the lemma by considering three separate cases:

(1)

A_{-}
is non-empty and
\sigma_{m}
is tight.

(2)

\sigma_{m}
is not tight and
|A_{+}|+\sigma_{m}\leq m-1

(3)

A_{-}
is empty and
|A_{+}|+\sigma_{m}\geq m
.

We note that these three cases encompass all possibilities. Together (
1
) and (
2
) cover the case that
A_{-}
is non-empty. The case that
A_{-}
is empty is covered by (
2
) and (
3
). In particular, note that if
\sigma_{m}
is tight, then the condition
|A_{+}|+\sigma_{m}\geq m
automatically holds.

First, suppose that (
1
) holds: we have
A_{-}
is non-empty and
\sigma_{m}=m
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
1\in A_{-}
, i.e we have
z_{1}\leq-1
and
z_{i}\leq 0
for
i=2,\dots,m-1
. Consider
\bm{x}=\bm{e}_{m}-\bm{e}_{m-1}-\dots-\bm{e}_{2}-2\bm{e}_{1}\in L
. Note that if
\bm{z}=\bm{x}
, then
z_{m}=1
. Thus we can assume that
\bm{x}\neq\bm{z}
.
The irreducibility of
\bm{z}
gives

\displaystyle-1

\displaystyle\geq\bm{x}\cdot(\bm{z}-\bm{x})

\displaystyle=z_{m}-1-\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}(1+z_{i})-2(z_{1}+2)

\displaystyle\geq z_{m}-1-(m-2)-2

\displaystyle=z_{m}-1-m.

Rearranging gives
z_{m}\leq m=\sigma_{m}
, which is the desired bound if
A_{-}
is nonempty and
\sigma_{m}
is tight.

Now suppose that (
2
) holds: we have
\sigma_{m}\leq m-1
, and that
|A_{+}|+\sigma_{m}\leq m-1
. Note that this encompasses the case that
A_{+}
is empty. In this case we consider
\bm{x}=\bm{e}_{m}-\bm{e}_{1}-\dots-\bm{e}_{\sigma_{m}}\in L
. The hypothesis that
|A_{+}|+\sigma_{m}\leq m-1
implies that after relabelling the
\bm{e}_{i}
we can assume that
A_{+}\cap\{1,\dots,\sigma_{m}\}=\emptyset
, that is that
z_{i}\leq 0
for
i=1,\dots,\sigma_{m}
. Again we can assume that
\bm{z}\neq\bm{x}
since otherwise we would have
z_{m}=1
. Thus we find that

\displaystyle-1

\displaystyle\geq\bm{x}\cdot(\bm{z}-\bm{x})

\displaystyle=z_{m}-1-\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{m}}(z_{i}+1)

\displaystyle\geq z_{m}-1-\sigma_{m}.

Rearranging this gives
z_{m}\leq\sigma_{m}
. Thus we have established the required bound when
|A_{+}|+\sigma_{m}\leq m-1
.

Finally suppose that (
3
) holds: we have that
A_{-}
is empty and
|A_{+}|+\sigma_{m}\geq m
. Under these conditions we have that
z_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1,\dots,m-1
and, by assumption, that
z_{m}>0
. Since every non-zero vector in a changemaker lattice must have non-zero coordinates of both signs, there is some
i
with
z_{i}<0
. Moreover we see that such an
i
satisfies
i>m
. We take
g>m
to be minimal such that
z_{g}\leq 0
.

By Proposition
3.1
, there is a subset

B\subset\{1,\dots,g-1\}
such that

\sigma_{g}-\sigma_{m}=\sum_{i\in B}\sigma_{i}.

Thus we can define an element
\bm{x}\in L
of the form

\bm{x}=-\bm{e}_{g}+\varepsilon\bm{e}_{m}+\sum_{i\in C}\bm{e}_{i}+\sum_{j\in D}\bm{e}_{j},

where
C=\{1,\dots,m-1\}\cap B
,
D=\{m+1,\dots,g-1\}\cap B
and

\varepsilon=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $m\not\in B$}\\
2&\text{if $m\in B$.}\end{cases}

Furthermore, we assume that we have chosen
B
so that
A_{+}\cap C
is as large as possible. I.e so that
|C\setminus A_{+}|=\max\{0,|C|-|A_{+}|\}
. If
\bm{z}=\bm{x}
, then
z_{m}=\varepsilon\leq 2
, which is the desired bound. Thus we assume that
\bm{z}\neq\bm{x}
.
Since
\bm{z}
is assumed to be irreducible, we have

\displaystyle-1

\displaystyle\geq\bm{x}\cdot(\bm{z}-\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-z_{g}-1+\varepsilon(z_{m}-\varepsilon)+\sum_{i\in C}(z_{i}-1)+\sum_{j\in D}(z_{j}-1)

\displaystyle\geq-1+\varepsilon(z_{m}-\varepsilon)+\sum_{i\in C}(z_{i}-1)

\displaystyle\geq-1+\varepsilon(z_{m}-\varepsilon)-|C\setminus A_{+}|.

Thus rearranging we get the bound

z_{m}\leq\frac{|C\setminus A_{+}|}{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon.

Firstly, note that if
|C\setminus A_{+}|=0
, then we get the bound
z_{m}\leq\varepsilon\leq 2
. Thus we can assume that

|C\setminus A_{+}|>0
. Since
|C|\leq m-1
and
|A_{+}|+\sigma_{m}\geq m
, we have

|C\setminus A_{+}|=|C|-|A_{+}|\leq m-1-(m-\sigma_{m})\leq\sigma_{m}-1.

Since
\varepsilon\in\{1,2\}
, this gives the bound

z_{m}\leq\max\left\{\sigma_{m},\frac{\sigma_{m}+3}{2}\right\}.

Since
\sigma_{m}\geq 2
and
z_{m}
is an integer, this is sufficient to show
z_{m}\leq\sigma_{m}
. This concludes the final case of the lemma.
∎

Remark 7.4

We note that the bound in Lemma
7.3
is sharp. For example, one finds that

\bm{v}=-\bm{e}_{m+1}+n\bm{e}_{m}
is irreducible in a changemaker lattice of the form

L=\langle\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\bm{e}_{m-1}+n\bm{e}_{m}+n^{2}\bm{e}_{m+1}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{m+1},

where

is assumed sufficiently large to ensure this a changemaker lattice.

Putting these bounds together we describe a practical algorithm to decide whether or not an integer changemaker lattice admits an obtuse superbase.

Algorithm 7.5

Given an integral changemaker lattice

L=\langle\sigma_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{r}\bm{e}_{r}\rangle^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r},

with discriminant
n=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}^{2}
, the following procedure either finds an obtuse superbase for
L
or certifies that none exists.

(1)

Produce a list

of non-zero elements of
L
whose coordinates satisfy the bounds of Lemmas
7.1
,
7.2
, and
7.3
.

(2)

Let
V_{2}
be the set of all vectors in

of the form
\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{i+1}
.

(3)

Take
V_{\rm irred}
to be the subset of
V_{\rm bound}
consisting of
\bm{v}
such that

(7.1)

(\bm{v}-\bm{w})\cdot\bm{w}<0
for all
\bm{w}\in V_{\rm bound}\setminus\{\bm{v}\}
.

and

(7.2)

\bm{v}\in V_{2}
or
\bm{v}\cdot\bm{w}\in\{0,-1\}
for all
\bm{w}\in V_{2}
.

(4)

For each subset
\{\bm{v}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{v}^{(r-1)}\}\subseteq V_{\rm irred}
containing

set

\bm{v}^{(r)}=-(\bm{v}^{(1)}+\dots+\bm{v}^{(r-1)})

and verify whether the following conditions are satisfied:

(a)

\bm{v}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{v}^{(r-1)}
span
L

(b)

\bm{v}^{(i)}\cdot\bm{v}^{(j)}\leq 0
for all
1\leq i<j\leq r
.

If so, then we have found an obtuse superbase for
L
.

(5)

If no obtuse superbase is found in the previous step, then
L
does not admit an obtuse superbase.

Proof.

It is clear that a collection of vectors satisfying the two conditions of Step
4
form an obtuse superbase for
L
, so it suffices to justify why the set will find an obtuse superbase whenever one exists.

Suppose that
L
admits an obtuse superbase

. Lemma
4.5
shows that

contains at most one reducible vector. Furthermore, Lemma
4.7
allows us to assume that
B
contains
V_{2}
. Since

contains no elements of norm one all the elements of

, having length two, are necessarily irreducible. Thus we can assume that

takes the form

B=\{\bm{v}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{v}^{(r-1)},-(\bm{v}^{(1)}+\dots+\bm{v}^{(r-1)})\},

where
\{\bm{v}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{v}^{(r-1)}\}
is a set of irreducible vectors containing

. Thus each of the
\bm{v}^{(i)}
satisfies the coordinate bounds of Lemmas

,
7.2
, and
7.3
and hence is contained in

. Since each
\bm{v}^{(i)}
is irreducible, it satisfies

for all non-zero

such that
\bm{w}\neq\bm{v}^{(i)}
. Consequently each
\bm{v}^{(i)}
satisfies (
7.1
). Consider a vector
\bm{w}\in V_{2}
. Since
V_{2}
is contained in

we have for all

either that
\bm{w}=\bm{v}^{(i)}
or that
\bm{w}\cdot\bm{v}^{(i)}\leq 0
. On the other hand each
\bm{v}^{(i)}
is irreducible, so if
\bm{v}^{(i)}\neq\bm{w}
for some

-1\geq-(\bm{v}^{(i)}+\bm{w})\cdot\bm{w}=-2-\bm{v}^{(i)}\cdot\bm{w}.

From this we rearrange to see that
\bm{v}^{(i)}\cdot\bm{w}\geq-1

\bm{v}^{(i)}\neq\bm{w}
. Altogether we see that
\bm{v}^{(i)}
also satisfies (

\bm{v}^{(i)}
is also contained in

∎

Remark 7.6

8.4.

Algorithm 8.10

(Computation of Stable Coefficients)

Let

\Delta_{K}(x)=a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{g}a_{i}(x^{i}+x^{-i})

be the symmetrized Alexander polynomial of a knot of a non-trivial L-space knot
K
normalized so that
\Delta_{K}(1)=1
. Then the following procedure terminates in finite time and returns compatible stable coefficients if they exist.

(1)

Compute the torsion coefficients
t_{0},\dots,t_{g}
and the torsion coefficient counts
T_{0},T_{1},\dots,T_{t_{0}}
, where
T_{0}=0
.

(2)

Let
k=1
,
N=0
and let
\underline{s}^{(0)}=()
be the empty tuple.

While
k\leq t_{0}
and
T_{k}-T_{k-1}>2
perform the following loop:

•

Calculate

M=\begin{cases}\max_{\bm{\alpha}\in S_{k,N}}\bm{\alpha}\cdot\underline{s}^{(N)}.&\text{if $N>0$}\\
0&\text{if $N=0$}\end{cases}

•

If
M>T_{k}
, then terminate as no stable coefficients exist.

•

If
M<T_{k}
, then define
s_{N+1}:=T_{k}-T_{k-1}
, set

\underline{s}^{(N+1)}:=(s_{1},\dots,s_{N},s_{N+1})

and increment
N:=N+1
.

•

Increment
k:=k+1
and loop.

(3)

Calculate
d:=g-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}(s_{i}-1)
.

•

If
d<0
, then terminate as no stable coefficients exist.

•

If
d\geq 0
, then return

\underline{\rho}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{N},\underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{d}).

as the stable coefficients.

Proof.

Let

\bm{\sigma}=\rho_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\dots+\rho_{r}\bm{e}_{r}

be a changemaker vector compatible with
\Delta_{K}(x)
ordered so that the coefficients are non-increasing:

\rho_{1}\geq\dots\geq\rho_{r}=1.

Let
m\geq 0
be maximal such that
\rho_{m}\geq 3
. The first task is to verify that the loop in Step
2
terminates with
N=m
and

\underline{s}^{(m)}=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m}).

If
t_{0}=1
, then
[
37
, Lemma 2.12]
shows that
T_{1}=g\leq 3
and that the stable coefficients of
\bm{\sigma}
take the form

\underline{\rho}=\begin{cases}(2)&\text{if $g=1$}\\
(2,2)&\text{if $g=2$}\\
(3)&\text{if $g=3$}.\end{cases}

Since
T_{1}-T_{0}=g
, we see that if
g\leq 2
, then the loop in Step
2
does not perform any steps and so terminates with
N=0
and
\underline{s}^{(0)}=()
as required. If
g=3
, then the loop performs one step and terminates with
N=1
and
\underline{s}^{(1)}=(3)
, as required.

If
t_{0}>1
, then we may define the quantity

\mu:=\min\{T_{k}-T_{k-1}\,|\,1\leq k<t_{0}\}.

If
\mu\leq 2
, then the proof of
[
37
, Lemma 2.16]
shows that the loop in Step
2
will terminate with

\underline{s}^{(m)}=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m}),

as required.

Thus it remains to consider the case that
\mu\geq 3
. In this case
[
37
, Lemma 2.14 and Remark 2.15]
show that the stable coefficients of
\bm{\sigma}
take the form

\underline{\rho}=\begin{cases}(3,\dots,3)&\text{if $g\equiv 0\bmod 3$}\\
(3,\dots,3,2)&\text{if $g\equiv 1\bmod 3$}\\
(3,\dots,3,2,2)&\text{if $g\equiv 2\bmod 3$},\end{cases}

where there are
\lfloor g/3\rfloor
threes in each case. Let
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
be a changemaker vector with stable coefficients of the form
\underline{\rho}
given above. One can calculate that

\max_{\bm{\alpha}\in S_{k,r}}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{\alpha}=3k

for
1\leq k\leq\lfloor g/3\rfloor
. The key step is to observe that the maximizing
\bm{\alpha}\in S_{k,r}
must have all coefficients equal to zero or one. It follows from Lemma
8.7
that that
T_{k}=3k
for
0\leq k\leq\lfloor g/3\rfloor
. If
g\equiv 1,2\bmod 3
, then a similar calculation shows that

\max_{\bm{\alpha}\in S_{k,r}}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{\alpha}=3k+2\geq g

for
k=\lfloor g/3\rfloor+1
. By Lemma
8.7
this implies that
t_{0}=\lceil g/3\rceil
.

Running the loop in Step
2
for such a sequence of
T_{k}
s one finds that we increment
N
at each step and for each
0\leq N\leq\lfloor g/3\rfloor
, we have

\underline{s}^{(N)}=(\underbrace{3,\dots,3}_{N}).

Furthermore, the loop in Step
2
terminates with
N=\lfloor g/3\rfloor
and
k=N+1
. When
g\equiv 0\bmod 3
this terminates because
k>t_{0}=\lfloor g/3\rfloor
. When
g\equiv 1,2\bmod 3
this terminates because
T_{k}-T_{k-1}\leq 2
. In either case, we see that Step
2
has calculated the required tuple.

Given that Step
2
has terminated having calculated the stable coefficients
\rho_{i}\geq 3
, all remaining unknown stable coefficients must have value
\rho_{i}=2
. The number of these coefficients are calculated in Step
3
using the relation from Lemma
8.7
that

g=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\rho_{i}(\rho_{i}-1).

Thus if a compatible changemaker vector exists, this algorithm computes the stable coefficients.
∎

Remark 8.11

It is possible in theory that Algorithm
8.10
might return a potential set of coefficients, even when none exist. Thus, one should also verify that the putative stable coefficients do actually give rise to changemaker vectors that are compatible with the original Alexander polynomial.

9.
The structure of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
. However, first it is necessary to define and study
\mathcal{D}
, a class of knots admitting alternating surgeries constructed using the Montesinos trick.

9.1.
Knots in
\mathcal{D}

Let
(D,c)
be a reduced alternating diagram
D
of an alternating unknotting number one knot with a choice of an unknotting crossing
c
. Given such a pair we can construct a knot
K_{(D,c)}
with alternating surgeries via the Montesinos trick
[
40
]
. Let
T
be the tangle in
B^{3}
obtained by taking the complement of a ball containing the crossing
c
. More specifically, the crossing arc associated to
(D,c)
is the arc in the preimage of the double point of the crossing
c
of the diagram
D
that joins two points of the knot. The tangle
T
is the complement of a ball neighborhood of the crossing arc that meets the knot in only two properly embedded arcs. Taking the double cover of
B^{3}
branched along the tangle
T
yields a 3-manifold
X_{(D,c)}
with torus boundary. Since
c
was an unknotting crossing, this manifold can be filled with a solid torus to yield
S^{3}
. Thus we can view
X_{(D,c)}
as the complement of a knot
K_{(D,c)}
in
S^{3}
. This knot admits a surgery to the double branched cover
\Sigma_{2}(D)
and in fact, any replacement of
c
by a rational tangle that yields an alternating diagram corresponds to an alternating surgery on
K_{(D,c)}
. If one calculates the slopes of these alternating surgeries carefully, one finds that these tangle replacements give an interval’s worth of alternating surgeries
[
34
, Proposition 5.3]

(9.1)

\left[\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon\det D}{2}\right\rfloor,\left\lceil\frac{\varepsilon\det D}{2}\right\rceil\right]\cap\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K_{(D,c)}),

where the sign
\varepsilon=\pm 1
depends on the sign of the unknotting crossing
c
and the signature of the knot represented by
D
via the following formula

\varepsilon=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\sigma(D)/2}&\text{if $c$ is positive}\\
-(-1)^{\sigma(D)/2}&\text{if $c$ is negative}.\end{cases}

Definition 9.1

We define
\mathcal{D}
to be the set of L-space knots of the form
K_{(D,c)}
for some alternating diagram
D
and unknotting crossing
c
contained therein.

The class
\mathcal{D}
plays a distinguished role on account of the following theorem which is a blend of
[
34
, Theorem 1.3]
and
[
35
, Theorem 7.12]
. Given a non-integer changemaker lattice with a planar obtuse superbase, one can always construct a corresponding knot in
\mathcal{D}
.

Theorem 9.2

Let
L
be a
p/q
-changemaker lattice for some positive
p/q\notin\mathbb{Z}
with a tuple of stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}
. If
L
admits a planar obtuse superbase, then there exists a knot
K\in\mathcal{D}
with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}(K)=\underline{\rho}
and
[n,n+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
, where
n=\lfloor p/q\rfloor
.

Proof.

Given a planar obtuse superbase
B
for
L
, we may take an embedding of the graph
G_{B}
in the plane. Using this embedding, we construct a reduced alternating diagram
D
whose Goeritz lattice is isomorphic to the graph lattice
\Lambda(G_{B})
and hence to
L
. Since
p/q
is not an integer, the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) of
[
34
, Theorem 1.3]
shows that
D
is isotopic to another alternating diagram
D^{\prime}
, which can be obtained by replacing an unknotting crossing
c
in an alternating diagram
\widetilde{D}
by a rational tangle. Moreover this rational tangle replacement is precisely the one showing that
K_{(\widetilde{D},c)}
is a knot in
\mathcal{D}
satisfying
S_{p/q}^{3}(K_{(\widetilde{D},c)})\cong\Sigma_{2}(D)
. The fact that the stable coefficients satisfy
\underline{\rho}(K)=\underline{\rho}
follows from the argument in
[
35
, Proof of Theorem 7.12]
.
∎

In particular, this immediately implies the following embellishment of
[
35
, Theorem 7.12]
.

Theorem 9.3

Let
K
be a non-trivial L-space knot in
S^{3}
such that
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)
is an alternating surgery for some
p/q\not\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then there exists a knot
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
such that

(1)

S_{p/q}^{3}(K)\cong S_{p/q}^{3}(K^{\prime})
,

(2)

\Delta_{K}(x)=\Delta_{K^{\prime}}(x)
and

(3)

[n,n+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K^{\prime})
, where
n=\lfloor p/q\rfloor
.

Proof.

Theorem
8.5
implies that the stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}(K)
exist and the
p/q
-changemaker lattice with these stable coefficients admits a planar obtuse superbase. Since
p/q\not\in\mathbb{Z}
, Theorem
9.2
implies the existence of a knot
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}(K)=\underline{\rho}(K^{\prime})
and
[n,n+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K^{\prime})
. We have
\Delta_{K}(x)=\Delta_{K^{\prime}}(x)
, since the torsion coefficients, and hence the Alexander polynomial, are determined by the stable coefficients as in (
8.1
).
∎

This implies that the only knots with infinitely many alternating surgeries in a bounded interval are those in
\mathcal{D}
. Corollary
9.7
extends this to show that the knots with infinitely many alternating surgeries are those in
\mathcal{D}
.

Lemma 9.4

Let
K
be an L-space knot admitting alternating surgeries. If there is an integer
M
such that
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)\cap[M,M+1]
is infinite, then

K\in\mathcal{D}
and
[M,M+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
9.3
, there exists a knot
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
with the same Alexander polynomial as
K
and
[M,M+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K^{\prime})
. By Corollary
8.6
, we have
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)\cong S_{p/q}^{3}(K^{\prime})
for every
p/q\in[M,M+1]\cap\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
. In particular, we can find
p/q
with
|q|
arbitrarily large such that
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)\cong S_{p/q}^{3}(K^{\prime})
. This implies that
K
and
K^{\prime}
are isotopic (see Theorem
A.16
).
∎

One can also prove the following proposition, which is a variant of
[
37
, Proposition 4.2]
, directly using the theory of changemaker lattices.

Proposition 9.5

Let
L
be a
p/q
-changemaker lattice for some positive
p/q\notin\mathbb{Z}
which admits a planar obtuse superbase. Then for
n=\lfloor p/q\rfloor
the
n
- and
(n+1)
-changemaker lattices with the same stable coefficients as
L
also admit planar obtuse superbases.

Proof.

By Theorem
9.2
there exists a knot
K\in\mathcal{D}
with stable coefficients equal to the stable coefficients of
L
such that
n
and
n+1
are alternating surgery slopes for
K
. By Theorem
8.5
, this implies that the corresponding changemaker lattices admit planar obtuse superbases.
∎

9.2.
Analysis of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)

In this section we will take
K
to be a non-trivial L-space knot admitting alternating surgeries. We will take
\underline{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{\ell})
to be the stable coefficients of
K
and we define
N
to be the following integer:

(9.2)

N=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\rho_{i}^{2}+\max_{1\leq k\leq\ell}\left(\rho_{k}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{\ell}\rho_{i}\right).

First we check that
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
is necessarily a subset of
[N-1,N+1]
.

Lemma 9.6

Any alternating surgery slope
p/q
of
K
satisfies

N-1\leq p/q\leq N+1.

Proof.

By Theorem
8.5
, there is a
p/q
-changemaker lattice
L
with stable coefficients given by
\underline{\rho}
and
L
admits a planar obtuse superbase. The lower bound comes from Proposition
3.2
, which, after adjusting for differences in notation, shows that the changemaker lattice
L
exists if and only if
p/q\geq\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert^{2}\geq N-1
.

If
p/q\not\in\mathbb{Z}
, then Proposition
9.5
shows that the
\lceil p/q\rceil
-changemaker lattice with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}
also admits an obtuse superbase. If
p/q\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
p/q=\lceil p/q\rceil
and the
\lceil p/q\rceil
-changemaker lattice with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}
is already known to admit an obtuse superbase. From Proposition
4.9
it follows that

\lceil p/q\rceil\leq 1+\rho_{\ell}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\rho_{i}^{2},

since
\rho_{\ell}
corresponds to
\sigma_{m}
in the notation of that section. Since

\rho_{\ell}\leq\max_{1\leq k\leq\ell}\left(\rho_{k}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{\ell}\rho_{i}\right),

this implies that
p/q\leq N+1
, as required.
∎

Together Lemma
9.4
and Lemma
9.6
show that the only knots with infinitely many alternating surgeries belong to
\mathcal{D}
.

Corollary 9.7

If
K
is an L-space knot admitting infinitely many alternating surgeries, then
K\in\mathcal{D}
. ∎

Using the results of §
5
we analyze the knots admitting alternating surgeries in the range
(N,N+1]
.

Lemma 9.8

Let
K
be a non-trivial L-space knot admitting an alternating surgery. If
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)\cap(N,N+1]
is non-empty, then there exists a knot
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
\Delta_{K}(x)=\Delta_{K^{\prime}}(x)
and
S_{N}^{3}(K^{\prime})
is a reducible surgery.

Proof.

By Remark
3.6
(
3
), for any rational
r\geq N-1
there exists an
r
-changemaker lattice with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}=\underline{\rho}(K)
. We use
L_{r}
to denote this changemaker lattice.
If there exists a slope
p/q
in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)\cap(N,N+1]
, then
L_{N+1}
admits a planar obtuse superbase by Proposition
9.5
. However, as noted in Remark
5.1
, the lattice
L_{N+1}
is very slack and so Theorem
5.11
shows that
L_{N+\frac{1}{2}}
admits an obtuse superbase and that
L_{N}
is decomposable lattice. Given the planar obtuse superbase for
L_{N+\frac{1}{2}}
, Theorem
9.2
implies the existence of a knot
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}(K^{\prime})=\underline{\rho}
and
[N,N+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K^{\prime})
. Since the stable coefficients determine the Alexander polynomial, we have
\Delta_{K}(x)=\Delta_{K^{\prime}}(x)
.

Now consider the alternating surgery
S_{N}^{3}(K^{\prime})
. This is homeomorphic to the double branched cover of a diagram
D
, whose Goeritz lattice is isomorphic to
L_{N}
. Since
L_{N}
is decomposable, Lemma
2.8
implies that the white graph of
D
contains a cut-vertex. This implies that
D
is a non-prime diagram and hence that its double branched cover is a reducible manifold. Thus
N
is a reducible surgery slope for
K^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 9.9

Let
K
be a non-trivial knot in
\mathcal{D}
. If
N
is a reducible surgery slope for
K
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=[N-1,N+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
and
K
can be written as a cable of the form
C_{r,s}\circ K^{\prime}
, where
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
,
rs=N
and
r/s\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Using the formula for the genus found in Lemma
8.7
, we see that
N>2g(K)-1
. Thus, the results of Matignon-Sahari
[
32
]
imply that
K
must be a cable knot or a torus knot.

Therefore we may write
K
as a cable
K=C_{r,s}\circ K^{\prime}
, where
N=rs
is the reducible surgery slope on
K
. Here
s\geq 2
is the winding number of the pattern and we allow the possibility that
K^{\prime}
is unknotted. By
[
20
, Corollary 7.3]
, we have a homeomorphism

(9.3)

S_{rs\pm\frac{1}{q}}^{3}(K)\cong S_{\frac{rsq\pm 1}{s^{2}q}}^{3}(K^{\prime})

for all
q\geq 1
. Let
M
be the integer
M=\lfloor\frac{r}{s}\rfloor
. Since
s\geq 2
, we have

(9.4)

M<\frac{r}{s}-\frac{1}{s^{2}}\leq\frac{rsq\pm 1}{s^{2}q}\leq\frac{r}{s}+\frac{1}{s^{2}}<M+1,

for all
q\geq 1
. As shown by (
9.1
),
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
contains a unit interval with integer endpoints. Combined with Lemma
9.6
, this implies that at least one of the intervals
[N-1,N]\cap\mathbb{Q}
or
[N,N+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
is contained in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
. By (
9.3
) and (
9.4
), this implies that
K^{\prime}
has infinitely many alternating surgery slopes in the interval
[M,M+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
. By Lemma
9.4
, this means that
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
and
\left[M,M+1\right]\cap\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K^{\prime})
. On the other hand, (
9.3
), (
9.4
) and the fact that
\left[M,M+1\right]\cap\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K^{\prime})
implies that
rs\pm\frac{1}{q}
is an alternating surgery slope for
K
for all
q
. Thus
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
contains infinitely many alternating surgeries in both the intervals
[rs-1,rs]\cap\mathbb{Q}
and
[rs,rs+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
. By Lemma
9.4
and Lemma
9.6
this implies that

[rs-1,rs+1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K),

as required.
∎

We now have all the technical ingredients to prove our main result on the structure of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
. Note that in contrast to the rest of this section, the statement of Theorem
1.2
use the convention that the stable coefficients are non-decreasing.

Theorem
1.2
.

Let
K
be a non-trivial knot admitting positive alternating surgeries with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}(K)=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{\ell})

Proof.

Lemma 10.1

The slope

Proof.

Firstly, since

for a knot

[

]
. Thus

contains an

summand and Gabai’s proof of property R implies that

is the unknot
[

.
∎

Next, we remind the reader that by the resolution of the Tait conjecture
[
26
,
42
,

]
any reduced alternating diagram realizes the crossing number.
Since for any non-split alternating link we have
\operatorname{cr}(J)\leq\det(J)

Lemma 10.2

Given a knot

and a fixed slope

, there is an algorithm to decide whether

is an alternating surgery.

Proof.

According to Lemma

, deciding if

is equivalent to checking whether or not

is trivial.
For fixed

with determinant

Lemma 10.3

Genus bounds on

In this section we prove the genus bound on alternating surgeries provided by Theorem

Lemma 11.1

Let
K
be a knot in
S^{3}
which admits a positive alternating surgery. If the symmetrized Alexander polynomial of
K
takes the form

\Delta_{K}(x)=x^{g}-x^{g-1}+x^{g-2}+\text{lower order terms},

then the stable coefficients of
K
take the form
\underline{\rho}(K)=(\underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{g})
.

Proof.

This follows from the calculation of
\rho_{1}
in Remark
8.8
and the genus

(11.1)

g(K)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\rho_{i}(\rho_{i}-1)

from Lemma
8.7
.
If
g=1
or
g=2
, then according to (
11.1
), the only possibilities are
\underline{\rho}(K)=(2)
or
\underline{\rho}(K)=(2,2)
, respectively. If
g\geq 3
, then we calculate that the torsion coefficients satisfy
t_{g}=0
,
t_{g-1}=t_{g-2}=1
and
t_{g-3}=2
. By definition, this means
T_{1}=2
. Since
t_{0}\geq t_{g-3}>1
, we can apply Remark
8.8
to see that
\rho_{1}=2
. Since the stable coefficients are non-increasing it follows that
\underline{\rho}(K)=(\underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{g})
, as required.
∎

Notably the symmetrized Alexander polynomial of the knot
T_{2,2g+1}
takes the form

\Delta_{T_{2,2g+1}}(x)=x^{g}-x^{g-1}+x^{g-2}+\dots+x^{-g},

so the stable coefficients of
T_{2,2g+1}
are of the form provided by Lemma

.

Lemma 11.2

Let
K
be a knot which admits positive alternating surgeries. If the stable coefficients of
K
satisfy
\underline{\rho}(K)=(\underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{g})
, then
K
is the torus knot
T_{2,2g+1}
.

Proof.

Since the stable coefficients of a knot determine its Alexander polynomial, we see that
K
and
T_{2,2g+1}
must have the same Alexander polynomials. From Theorem
1.2
and by Corollary
8.6
it follows that

\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)\subseteq[4g+1,4g+3]\cap\mathbb{Q}=\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(T_{2,2g+1})

and for any
p/q\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
we have
S_{p/q}^{3}(T_{2,2g+1})\cong S_{p/q}^{3}(K)
. Greene has shown that the reducible surgery slope
p/q=4g+2
is a characterizing slope for
T_{2,2g+1}

[
24
]
. Rasmussen has shown that
p/q=4g+3
is a characterizing slope for
T_{2,2g+1}

[
48
]
. Ni has shown that if
S_{4g+1}^{3}(T_{2,2g+1})\cong S_{4g+1}^{3}(K)
, then
K=T_{2,2g+1}
or
\Delta_{K}=\Delta_{T_{5,4}}

[
44
]
. In any case, these results imply that if
K
has an integer alternating surgery, then
K=T_{2,2g+1}
.

Thus let us suppose that
p/q
is a non-integer alternating surgery slope for
K
. For such a slope
S_{p/q}^{3}(T_{2,2g+1})\cong S_{p/q}^{3}(K)
is an atoroidal Seifert fibered space. By Thurston’s trichotomy for knots in
S^{3}
,

must be either a hyperbolic knot, a satellite knot or a torus knot. Since
p/q>4g(K)
, the work of Ni immediately rules out the possibility that
K
is a hyperbolic knot
[
44
, Theorem 1.1]
. If
K
is a satellite knot, then

must be a cable knot
[
39
, Corollary 1.1]
. Suppose that
K
is the
(a,b)
-cable of a non-trivial knot
K^{\prime}
. Since
K
is an L-space knot we must have

. Thus we have that
g(K)=\frac{(a-1)(b-1)}{2}+ag(K^{\prime})
. Moreover, since the resulting space is atoroidal, the slope
p/q
must take the form
p/q=ab\pm 1/q
. Since
K^{\prime}
is non-trivial we have
g(K^{\prime})\geq 1
. Thus we have

p/q\geq 4g(K)+1\geq 2(a-1)(b-1)+4a+1=ab+(b+2)(a-2)+7>ab+7.

However this is a contradiction, since we can have an atoroidal surgery on
K
only if
p/q
takes the form
p/q=ab\pm 1/q\leq ab+1
.
Thus the only remaining case is that
K
is a torus knot. However since
\Delta_{K}(X)=\Delta_{T_{2,2g+1}}(X)
, the only possibility is that
K=T_{2,2g+1}
, as required.
∎

13.
Alternating surgeries on census knots

In this section, we describe our procedures for classifying the alternating surgeries on the knots in the SnapPy census (i.e Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
). As well as calculating
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
for all such knots in the census, we also explicitly determine branching sets for all their integer and half-integer alternating surgeries. If a knot admits a half-integer alternating surgery, we also create a tangle exterior which allows us to
determine the branching set for all other alternating surgeries by performing appropriate tangle fillings. This data and the code can be found at
[
3
]
.

Step 0: Restricting to L-space knots

Since any knot which admits an alternating surgery is an L-space knot it suffices to consider L-space knots. From Dunfield’s data
[
12
]
, we can extract a list of all L-space knot exteriors in the SnapPy census, cf.
[
2
]
. This gives a list of 632 census L-space knots which we denote by
L_space_knots
. We include also two knots,
o9\_30150
and
o9\_31440
whose L-space status was not resolved by Dunfield in
L_space_knots
, since these are now both known to be L-space knot exteriors
[
4
]
.

Step 1: Triage via stable coefficients and obtuse superbases

For each knot
K
in
L_space_knots
, we first applied Algorithm
8.10
in order to calculate stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}(K)
whenever they exist. This finds stable coefficients for 508 of the knots in
L_space_knots
and shows that the remaining 124 knots do not admit any compatible stable coefficients and hence have no alternating surgeries.

For each knot
K
in
L_space_knots
with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}=\underline{\rho}(K)
, let
N
be the integer as in Theorem
1.2
and let
L_{N-1}
,
L_{N}
and
L_{N+1}
be the
(N-1)
-,
N
- and
(N+1)
-changemaker lattices with stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}
respectively. Using Algorithm
7.5
, we check which of the three lattices
L_{N-1}
,
L_{N}
and
L_{N+1}
admit an obtuse superbase.

For the 508 knots under consideration, the lattice
L_{N+1}
never admitted an obtuse superbase. For the lattices
L_{N-1}
and
L_{N}
there were three possible outcomes.

(1)

For 115 knots, neither
L_{N-1}
nor
L_{N}
admitted an obtuse superbase. For these knots
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=\emptyset
.

(2)

For 12 knots,
L_{N-1}
is the only lattice which admits an obtuse superbase. For such knots,
K\not\in\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
contains at most one slope, namely
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)\subseteq\{N-1\}
.

(3)

For 381 knots,
L_{N-1}
and
L_{N}
both admit an obtuse superbase. For such knots, there are two possibilities, either
K\in\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=[N-1,N]\cap\mathbb{Q}
or
K\not\in\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
is a finite subset of
[N-1,N]\cap\mathbb{Q}
.

For each of the unobstructed alternating integer surgeries, the obtuse superbases found in this search determine a Goeritz matrix and hence the crossing number for a potential alternating branching set. Using this data, we see that if
K
is a knot in the SnapPy census with an integer alternating surgery, then that integer surgery is the branched double cover of an alternating knot or link with at most
13
crossings.

Step 2: Classification of integer and half-integer alternating surgeries

The analysis of the preceding step implies that there are at most
774
integer alternating surgery amongst the knots in the SnapPy census (1 for each of the 12 knots in (
2
) and two for each of the 381 knots in (
3
)). Similarly, there are at most
381
half-integer alternating slopes on the knots in the SnapPy census.

It turns out that all of these integer and half-integer slopes are actually alternating surgery slopes. This was shown by finding an explicit alternating branching set for each potential alternating surgery. Note that in showing all of these slopes are alternating surgery slopes, we have shown that
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)=\{N-1\}
for all 12 knots in (
2
). The procedure for finding these branching sets was as follows.

First, we recompute these slopes in question with respect to the SnapPy basis (this will in general be different since for some knots in the SnapPy census the
(1,0)
-curve does not necessarily correspond to the meridian).

From Dunfield’s data of exceptional fillings of the census knots
[
11
]
we can read-off that
59
of the integer slopes are hyperbolic and the other
715
integer slopes are exceptional. Amongst the half-integer slopes
298
are hyperbolic and
83
are exceptional. To handle the integer hyperbolic slopes, we iterated through all alternating links in the HT link table with crossing number at most
13
. For each of these alternating links we took the double branched cover and checked whether the resulting manifold was homeomorphic to one of the putative alternating surgeries via SnapPy. This quickly identified an explicit alternating branching set
J
such that
S_{p}^{3}(K)\cong\Sigma_{2}(J)
for all of the
59
potential hyperbolic integer alternating surgery slopes.

A similar search, but iterating over all alternating knots with crossing number at most
14
yields an alternating branching set for all of the
298
potential hyperbolic half-integer alternating surgery slopes.

For the exceptional slopes, we begin by searching Dunfield’s data of exceptional surgeries
[
11
]
for surgeries to small Seifert fibered spaces (including lens spaces) and check via SnapPy if the corresponding Montesinos link (whose double branched cover is the Seifert fibered space at hand) is alternating. We find that 178 of the integer surgeries are lens space surgeries and that 353 of the integer surgeries are other Seifert fibered spaces. In total, this identifies that
531
integer slopes admit an alternating Seifert fibered surgery. We note that none of the half-integer exceptional slopes are lens space or Seifert fibered surgeries. This is in keeping with the cyclic surgery theorem and the conjecture that non-integer surgery on a hyperbolic knot is never a Seifert fibered manifold.

We are left with
184
integer exceptional slopes and
83
half-integer slopes which might be alternating. In each of these cases the resulting manifold is toroidal. For the integer slopes we run again through all links in the HT link table with crossing number at most
13
, take the double branched covers and check if Dunfield’s recognition code
[
11
]
(using the combined power of SnapPy and regina) recognizes the double branched cover as the same manifold as a potential integer alternating surgery and if so search for a combinatorial equivalence through the Pachner graph. This identifies for any remaining possible alternating integer slope an explicit alternating branching set and thus verifies that all
774
integer slopes of the census knots that admit obtuse superbases are actually alternating slopes. We find branching sets for the remaining potential half-integer surgery slopes by a similar procedure, but iterating through the alternating knots with at most 14 crossings.

Step 3: Verifying that knots are in
\mathcal{D}
.

To finish the proof of Theorem
1.3
it remains to show that the 381 census knots with half-integer alternating surgeries are actually contained in the class
\mathcal{D}
. This is done by exhibiting for each knot
K
an alternating diagram
D
and an unknotting crossing
c
such that the complement of
K_{(D,c)}
is homeomorphic to the complement of
K
.

In practice, the pair
(D,c)
was found as follows. From the preceding step we have a reduced alternating diagram
D
such that
S_{N-\frac{1}{2}}^{3}(K)\cong\Sigma_{2}(D)
. The crossing
c
is then taken to be the unknotting crossing such that its two resolutions are branching sets for the
(N-1)
- and
N
-surgeries on
K
.

In practice, to verify that
K
and
K_{(D,c)}
have homeomorphic complements as follows. Let
D^{\prime}
be the almost-alternating diagram of the unknot obtained by changing

and let
c^{*}
be the dealternating crossing in
D^{\prime}
. We convert

into a knot in

by deleting a small unknotted loop
l
around the crossing
c^{*}
. In the double branched cover of
D^{\prime}
, a Conway sphere containing

and
c^{*}
lifts to a torus

which separates
\Sigma_{2}(D^{\prime})
into a copy of the complement of

and a pair of pants times

. For each of the census knots, we are able to use SnapPy to construct this double branched cover and identify the splitting torus

as a normal surface. Cutting along

we obtain two connected components, one of which has a single boundary component. In all cases, SnapPy verifies that this piece is isometric to the original knot complement, thereby verifying that
K
is in
\mathcal{D}
. This completes the calculation of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
for knots in the SnapPy census as presented in Theorem

.

Step 4: Finding the bandings in Theorem
1.4

Let

be one of the 12 census knots such that

we found an alternating diagram

For each

Finally we examine alternating surgeries on some small Baker-Luecke knots. These are natural candidates to study since they have

Theorem 14.1

Let

be one of the 14 Baker-Luecke knots listed in Table

(1)

K
is an asymmetric L-space knot

(2)

\Delta_{K}(x)\neq\Delta_{K^{\prime}}(x)
for all

(3)

Proof.

Let
K
be one of the 14 Baker-Luecke knots in question. One loads the surgery description of

given in
[

]
into SnapPy. The verified functions in SnapPy show that

is a hyperbolic knot with trivial symmetry group. That

is an L-space knot follows directly from

where it is shown to have at least one alternating surgery.

Next, we calculate the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{K}(x)
and apply Algorithm
8.10
to compute its stable coefficients
\underline{\rho}(K)
and the associated integer

. If there were a knot

with the same Alexander polynomial as

would have the same stable coefficients as

and, according to Theorem

would have

shows that this

-changemaker lattice does not admit an obtuse superbase.

Thus, Theorem

shows that

is a subset of

and calculation of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm alt}(K)
is completed by showing that both of these are alternating surgery slopes. This is achieved by finding explicit alternating branching sets for each of these slopes and using SnapPy to verify the necessary homeomorphisms. These branching sets were found through a variety of methods. Firstly, we iterated through the tables of alternating knots of at most
15

14

8
of the potential alternating surgeries. The remaining

A.1.
The hyperbolic case

We now summarize the quantitative results of Futer-Purcell-Schleimer on hyperbolic Dehn filling that we need in order to prove Theorem
A.16
. Given a cusped hyperbolic manifold

Note that

is invariant under rescaling

Lemma A.8

Let
X_{0}
be a hyperbolic outermost piece occurring in the JSJ decomposition of a non-trivial knot

on

Proof.

By
[
15

, we may consider
C
a horocusp neighbourhood of
\partial X_{K}
such that
\operatorname{Area}\partial C=\sqrt{12}
. Let
\mu
denote the meridian of
K
on
\partial X_{K}
. Since the meridian is an exceptional slope in
X_{0}
, the 6-theorem implies that
\len(\mu)\leq 6

[
1
,
29
]
. A geometric argument in the universal cover shows that for any slope

on

we have

\sqrt{12}\Delta(\mu,p/q)\leq\len(\mu)\len(p/q).

Since

as

L(p/q)=\frac{\len(p/q)}{\sqrt[4]{12}}\geq\frac{\sqrt[4]{12}|q|}{\len(\mu)}\geq\frac{\sqrt[4]{12}|q|}{6}=\frac{|q|}{\sqrt[4]{108}},

∎

]
shows that after Dehn filling, the class
p\mu-qw^{2}\lambda
is null-homologous in
X_{K}(p/q)\setminus\operatorname{int}X
. Thus
p/g\mu-qw^{2}/g\lambda
is the rational longitude. This gives a fiber-longitude number of
\frac{1}{g}|p-rsqw^{2}|
.
∎

Now we apply these ideas.

Lemma A.15

Let
K
and
K^{\prime}
be two cabled knots in
S^{3}
, say
K
is an
(r,s)
-cable and
K^{\prime}
is an
(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})
-cable. Let
p/q,p/q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}
be surgery slopes such that
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)\cong S_{p/q^{\prime}}^{3}(K^{\prime})
and
|p-rsq|>1
,
|p-r^{\prime}s^{\prime}q^{\prime}|>1
. If
q,q^{\prime}>Q(K,K^{\prime})
, then
K
and
K^{\prime}
are isotopic and
q=q^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
A.6
, the conditions
|p-rsq|>1
and
|p-r^{\prime}s^{\prime}q^{\prime}|>1
imply that the JSJ decompositions of
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)
and
S_{p/q^{\prime}}^{3}(K^{\prime})
are of the form

S_{p/q}^{3}(K)=X_{0}(p/q)\cup X_{1}\cup\dots\cup X_{n}

and

S_{p/q^{\prime}}^{3}(K^{\prime})=X^{\prime}_{0}(p/q^{\prime})\cup X^{\prime}_{1}\cup\dots\cup X^{\prime}_{n},

respectively (note that they necessarily have the same number of pieces since
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)
and
S_{p/q^{\prime}}^{3}(K^{\prime})
are homeomorphic). The piece
X_{0}(p/q)
is a Seifert fibered space over the disk with two exceptional fibers of orders
|s|
and
|p-rsq|
respectively and
X^{\prime}_{0}(p/q^{\prime})
is a Seifert fibered space over the disk with two exceptional fibers of orders
|s^{\prime}|
and
|p-r^{\prime}s^{\prime}q^{\prime}|
.
Let
f\colon S_{p/q}^{3}(K)\rightarrow S_{p/q^{\prime}}^{3}(K^{\prime})
be an orientation-preserving homeomorphism, which, after a suitable isotopy, can be assumed to carry JSJ pieces of
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)
to JSJ pieces of
S_{p/q^{\prime}}^{3}(K^{\prime})
. The first task to is to show that
f
carries
X_{0}(p/q)
homeomorphically onto
X^{\prime}_{0}(p/q)
.

Claim

The homeomorphism
f
carries
X_{0}(p/q)
onto
X^{\prime}_{0}(p/q)
.

Proof of claim.

If
f
does not carry
X_{0}(p/q)
homeomorphically onto
X^{\prime}_{0}(p/q)
, then it must provide a homeomorphism between
X_{0}(p/q)
and some other piece in the JSJ decomposition of
X_{K^{\prime}}
, which we assume to be
X^{\prime}_{n}
. Since
X_{0}(p/q)
is a Seifert fibered space over this disk, this implies that
X^{\prime}_{n}
must be the complement of a torus knot
T_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
. By comparing the orders of exceptional fibers, we may assume that this knot is of the form
a^{\prime}=s\geq 2
and
|b^{\prime}|=|p-rsq|
. Thus we may write
K^{\prime}
as a satellite knot of the form
K^{\prime}=P^{\prime}(T_{s,b^{\prime}})
with a pattern
P^{\prime}
such that its complement in
S^{1}\times D^{2}
is homeomorphic to
X^{\prime}_{0}\cup\dots\cup X^{\prime}_{n-1}
. Let
w^{\prime}
denote the winding number of
K^{\prime}
in
P^{\prime}
.

Since
K^{\prime}
is an
(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})
-cable knot, the winding number
w^{\prime}
of
K^{\prime}
in
P^{\prime}
is divisible by
s^{\prime}
. Next we verify that
\gcd(p,w^{\prime})=1
. Observe that
f
restricts to give a homeomorphism between
X^{\prime}_{0}(p/q^{\prime})\cup X^{\prime}_{1}\cup\dots\cup X^{\prime}_{n-1}
and
X_{1}\cup\dots\cup X_{n}
. Since the latter space is a knot complement, it is a homology solid torus. However
[
20
, Lemma 3.3(i)]
shows that the homology of
X^{\prime}_{0}(p/q^{\prime})\cup X^{\prime}_{1}\cup\dots\cup X^{\prime}_{n-1}
contains torsion of order
\gcd(p,w)
. Hence the conclusion
\gcd(p,w^{\prime})=1
.

By Lemma
A.13
, the fiber-longitude number of
X_{0}(p/q)
in
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)
is
|r|
. On the other hand, Lemma
A.14
calculates the fiber-longitude number of the complement of
T_{s,b^{\prime}}
to be
|p-q^{\prime}sb^{\prime}(w^{\prime})^{2}|
(using that
\gcd(p,w^{\prime})=1
). Thus we have that

|r|=|p-q^{\prime}sb^{\prime}(w^{\prime})^{2}|.

Likewise,
X^{\prime}_{0}(p/q^{\prime})
must be homeomorphic to some piece in the JSJ decomposition of
X_{K}
. This allows us to write
K
as a satellite in the form
K=P(T_{s^{\prime},b})
, where
|b|=|p-r^{\prime}s^{\prime}q^{\prime}|
. Since
r^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime}
are coprime, the product
r^{\prime}s^{\prime}
is not divisible by
(s^{\prime})^{2}
. However, since
w^{\prime}
is divisible by
s^{\prime}
, we see that
(s^{\prime})^{2}
divides
sb^{\prime}(w^{\prime})^{2}
. Thus the integers
sb^{\prime}(w^{\prime})^{2}
and
r^{\prime}s^{\prime}
are distinct. Consequently we see that the conditions

|r|=|p-q^{\prime}sb^{\prime}(w^{\prime})^{2}|
and
|b|=|p-r^{\prime}s^{\prime}q^{\prime}|

can only be satisfied if
q^{\prime}\leq r+b\leq Q(K)
.
∎

Thus
f
restricts to a homeomorphism between
X_{0}(p/q)
and
X^{\prime}_{0}(p/q^{\prime})
and between
X_{1}\cup\dots\cup X_{n}
and
X^{\prime}_{1}\cup\dots\cup X^{\prime}_{n}
. Thus if we write
K
as the
(r,s)
-cable of
J
and
K^{\prime}
as the
(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})
-cable of
J^{\prime}
, we see that
f
restricts to a homeomorphism between the complements of
J
and
J^{\prime}
. Thus by the knot complement theorem, we have that
J
and
J^{\prime}
are isotopic
[
19
]
. Furthermore, since the homeomorphism must carry the meridian and longitude of
J
to the meridian and longitude of
J^{\prime}
, respectively, we see that
K
and
K^{\prime}
must both be the
(r,s)
-cable of
J
.

Finally we verify that
q^{\prime}=q
. The homeomorphism between
X_{0}(p/q)
and
X^{\prime}_{0}(p/q^{\prime})
must preserve the rational longitudes of these manifolds. However, this rational longitude, when expressed as a slope on
\partial X_{J}
takes the form
p/(qs^{2})
and
p/(q^{\prime}s^{2})
. This implies that
q^{\prime}=q
as required.
∎

A.4.
Proof of Theorem
A.16

Theorem A.16

Let
K
and
K^{\prime}
be a pair of distinct knots in
S^{3}
. Then there is a computable integer
Q(K,K^{\prime})
such that if
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)\cong S_{p/q}^{3}(K^{\prime})
for some
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
|q|\leq Q(K,K^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We will show that if
K
and
K^{\prime}
are two knots and
p/q
is a slope such that
S_{p/q}^{3}(K)\cong S_{p/q}^{3}(K^{\prime})
for some
q>Q(K,K^{\prime})
, then
K
and
K^{\prime}
must be isotopic. We can assume that both
K
and
K^{\prime}
are non-trivial, since every slope is a characterizing slope for the unknot
[
27
]
. Furthermore, by Proposition
A.5
we can assume that neither
K
nor
K^{\prime}
is a torus knot. In accordance with Theorem
A.1
, we can consider each of the remaining possibilities for the outermost pieces in the JSJ decompositions of
X_{K}
and
X_{K^{\prime}}
in turn.

Case 1

At least one of
K
or
K^{\prime}
is a composite or has a hyperbolic outermost piece in its JSJ decomposition.

Proof.

Without loss of generality suppose that
K
is composite or has hyperbolic outermost piece in its JSJ decomposition. Lemma
A.10
and Lemma
A.11
imply that
K^{\prime}
must be a cable of
K
with winding number
s
, where
p/q=p/(qs^{2})
. This is clearly impossible since for
K^{\prime}
to be a non-trivial cable of
K
we must have
|s|\geq 2
.
∎

Thus it remains to consider the case that both
K
and
K^{\prime}
are cables. For the remainder of the proof, we assume that
K
is the
(r,s)
-cable of
J
and
K^{\prime}
is the
(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})
-cable of
J^{\prime}
, where
s,s^{\prime}>1
and
J
and
J^{\prime}
are non-trivial knots in
S^{3}
. Furthermore, Lemma
A.15
implies the theorem if
|p-rsq|>1
and
|p-r^{\prime}s^{\prime}q|>1
. Thus, it remains to consider the case that we have
|p-rsq|=1
or
|p-r^{\prime}s^{\prime}q|=1
. So without loss of generality we will further assume that
|p-rsq|=1
.

Under these assumptions, we have

(A.3)

S^{3}_{p/q}(K)\cong S^{3}_{p/(qs^{2})}(J)\cong S^{3}_{p/q}(K^{\prime}).

Using Theorem
A.1
we perform a case-by-case analysis based on the outermost piece of
X_{J}
. Note that that if
J
is a torus knot then,
K
is a cable of a torus knot and the theorem follows from Proposition
A.5
.

Case 2

J
is a composite knot or has a hyperbolic outermost piece.

Proof.

If
J
has hyperbolic outermost piece or is a composite knot, then Lemma
A.10
and Lemma
A.11
applied to the homeomorphism in (
A.3
) imply that
J
and
J^{\prime}
are isotopic,
|p-r^{\prime}s^{\prime}q|=1
and that
qs^{2}=qs^{\prime 2}
. Since
q\geq 3
, the conditions
|p-rsq|=1
and
|p-r^{\prime}s^{\prime}q|=1
imply that
r^{\prime}s^{\prime}=rs
. Since
s^{2}=s^{\prime 2}
, this implies that
r/s=r^{\prime}/s^{\prime}
and hence we
K
and
K^{\prime}
are both isotopic to the
(r,s)
-cable of
J
.
∎

Thus the only remaining possibility is that
J
is itself a cable knot.

Case 3

J
is a cable.

Proof.

In this case, suppose that
J
is the
(a,b)
-cable of a knot
L
. By Remark
A.7
, we have
|p-abs^{2}q|>1
. If
|p-r^{\prime}s^{\prime}q|>1
, then Lemma
A.15
would imply that
s^{2}q=q
, which is absurd. Thus we have that
|p-r^{\prime}s^{\prime}q|=1
and hence that
S_{p/(qs^{2})}^{3}(J)\cong S_{p/(qs^{\prime 2})}^{3}(J^{\prime})
. Now we may assume that
J^{\prime}
is itself a cable. If it were not, then we could reverse the roles of
K
and
K^{\prime}
and apply the analysis of the preceding case to conclude that
K
and
K^{\prime}
and are isotopic. Thus we may assume that
J^{\prime}
is the
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
-cable of a non-trivial knot. Furthermore, we have that
|p-a^{\prime}b^{\prime}s^{\prime 2}q|>1
. In this case, we may apply Lemma
A.15
to see that
J
and
J^{\prime}
are isotopic and that
qs^{2}=qs^{\prime 2}
. Furthermore, if
|q|\geq 3
, then we have
rs=r^{\prime}s^{\prime}
. Altogether, this implies that
K
and
K^{\prime}
are isotopic, both being the
(r,s)
-cable of
J
.
∎

This completes the final case and the proof of Theorem
A.16
.
∎

Here we present a concrete example of non-isotopic torus knots that share infinitely many surgeries with
different
slopes. This shows that Lemma
A.10
and Lemma
A.11
cannot hold for torus knots.

Example A.17

We consider the non-isotopic torus knots
T_{3,5}
and
T_{3,8}
. Then it follows from Moser’s classification of surgeries on torus knots
[
41
]
that for any integer
n
both
S_{(120n+53)/(8n+3)}^{3}(T_{3,5})
and
S_{(120n+53)/(5n+2)}^{3}(T_{3,8})
are homeomorphic Seifert fibered spaces.
