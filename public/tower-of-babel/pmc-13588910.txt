Mapping the lingual artery: a scoping review and proposed imaging-informed risk classification for surgical and radiological practice
Authors: Ingrid C Landfald, George Triantafyllou, Lyubomir Gaydarski, Łukasz Olewnik, Bento João Abreu, Judney Cley Cavalcante, Paloma Aragonés, Marta Pośnik, Yuriy Vasil’ev, Maria Piagkou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe lingual artery (LA) shows significant morphological and topographical variation that can affect the safety and results of head and neck surgeries, carotid interventions, and free flap reconstructions. Traditional descriptions such as the thyrolingual trunk (TLT), linguofacial trunk (LFT), and thyrolinguofacial trunk (TLFT) do not consider surgical risk assessment or the importance of modern imaging.ObjectiveTo comprehensively review LA origin and course variants, determine their prevalence in cadaveric and imaging datasets, and propose an imaging-informed, anatomy-based risk classification for surgical and radiological use.MethodsA PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR)-aligned scoping review of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (January 2007–May 2025) identified studies reporting LA variants through cadaveric dissection, computed tomography angiography (CTA), magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), or digital subtraction angiography. Only original studies in English containing primary variant data were included. Prevalence ranges were extracted, and a proposed anatomy-based classification (Classes I–III) was constructed according to branching pattern, shared trunk involvement, proximity to the carotid bifurcation or common carotid artery, and anticipated implications for vascular control. Because standardized clinical outcome data were inconsistently reported across the included studies, the framework should not be interpreted as outcome-validated.ResultsTen eligible studies (n = 1550 ECAs) from Europe, Asia, North America, and Africa were included from cadaveric and imaging datasets. Typical anatomy with independent STA, LA, and FA origins was found in 50.7–90% of cases. LFT occurred in 7.5–24.3%, TLT in 0.6–10.5%, TLFT in 0–2.85%, and aberrant origins from the CCA or carotid bifurcation (ALA) in 0.7–6.6%. In the original course-variant dataset (n = 111 body sides), superficial, submental, and dual-origin variants were each uncommon. CTA provided the most detailed three-dimensional depiction of moderate- and high-complexity variants in the included imaging studies. The proposed classification organizes origin and course variants according to expected procedural complexity and imaging needs.ConclusionsA substantial proportion of patients present with LA variants that may influence surgical planning and intraoperative decision-making. The proposed classification may serve as an imaging-informed, clinically oriented framework for preoperative evaluation and multidisciplinary communication. However, it remains anatomy-based and requires prospective clinical validation before outcome-based application.Keywords: Lingual artery, External carotid artery, Vascular anatomy, Variation, CTA, MRA, Surgical risk assessment, Head and neck surgery, Vascular mapping, Variant

Introduction

The lingual artery (LA) is the second anterior branch of the external carotid artery (ECA). It is the main blood supply to the tongue, sublingual gland, floor of the mouth, and nearby oropharyngeal tissues [11]. Typically, it originates anteromedially from the ECA between the superior thyroid artery (STA) and the facial artery (FA). The standard branching pattern and common origin variants relevant to radiological evaluation and surgical planning are illustrated in Fig. 1. Connections with neighboring branches, including the STA and FA, emphasize the clinical importance of accurate vascular mapping [11].

Advances in vascular mapping, including computed tomography angiography (CTA), magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), three-dimensional reconstruction, ultrasonography, and intraoperative navigation, have enabled high-resolution characterization of LA anatomy [1, 3, 12, 15]. These techniques are used to measure LA depth and trajectory for transoral surgery [7, 16], while CTA-based morphometric analyses have further detailed LA size, branching angles, and relationships to the hyoglossus muscle [1, 15], enhancing preoperative safety in oncologic and reconstructive procedures [4].

Although the typical configuration predominates, with separate origins for the STA, LA, and FA, less common variants such as the TLFT and aberrant LA origins may complicate vascular control and may increase bleeding risk during head and neck surgery, carotid exposure, or reconstructive procedures [2, 13]. These patterns are embryologically linked to altered persistence or fusion of second- and third-arch arterial precursors [17, 24].

Existing classification schemes [14, 17, 22, 24] define four main origin types but are primarily descriptive, lacking stratification by surgical risk or imaging detectability. Additionally, less common but clinically relevant variants are often underrepresented despite their significance for neurovascular, oncologic, and oral-maxillofacial procedures [13].

To address these gaps, this scoping review synthesizes anatomical, radiological, and surgical evidence on LA variants, summarizes reported prevalence ranges, and proposes an imaging-informed, clinically oriented risk classification. The framework integrates branching pattern, anatomical complexity, and anticipated implications for vascular control in order to support preoperative planning, interdisciplinary communication, and future prospective validation.

Study Design This scoping review adhered to the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) reporting framework. Data sources and search strategy: A comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science was conducted for studies published from January 2007 through May 2025. Search terms included a combination of MeSH and free-text keywords related to lingual artery, external carotid artery variations, vascular anatomy, and imaging modalities. The strategy was designed for maximum sensitivity and was supplemented by manual review of reference lists from eligible articles.

Eligibility criteria: Inclusion criteria were original peer-reviewed studies that reported LA origin variants based on cadaveric dissection, CTA, MRA, or digital subtraction angiography. Only English-language studies were included. Exclusion criteria were animal or pediatric-only studies; narrative reviews, editorials, or letters without primary anatomical data; conference abstracts without full text; single-patient case reports; and duplicated datasets without additional unique data.

Study selection: The search retrieved 606 records in total: 224 from PubMed, 197 from Scopus, and 185 from Web of Science (Table 1). After the removal of 174 duplicates, 432 records remained for screening. Title and abstract screening excluded 368 records that did not meet the inclusion criteria, leaving 64 full-text articles for detailed assessment. Of these, 54 were excluded for one or more of the following reasons: lack of primary LA variant data, restriction to pediatric or animal populations, overlap with previously published datasets, or incomplete variant reporting. The final dataset consisted of 10 eligible studies published between 2007 and 2024, collectively reporting 1550 ECAs. These included five cadaveric dissection studies, four CTA-based imaging studies, and one MRA study.

Data extraction and variant classification: For each study, the following data were extracted: author(s), publication year, modality, sample size, geographic origin, continent, and prevalence of LA origin variants. Typical origin was characterized by the independent origin of STA, LA, and FA from the ECA, while the variants were categorized as follows: TLT: common origin of STA and LA; LFT: common origin of LA and FA; TLFT: common origin of STA, LA, and FA; and ALA from the CCA or carotid bifurcation: aberrant LA arising from the common carotid artery or carotid bifurcation. In addition, course variants described according to the Seki et al. [20] framework were recorded when available. The prevalence values reported for the course variants were derived exclusively from the original Seki et al. dataset (n = 111 body sides) and were not calculated by pooling the other included studies. The proposed risk classes were assigned using an anatomy-based approach that considered branching pattern, shared trunk involvement, proximity to the carotid bifurcation or common carotid artery, and anticipated difficulty of vascular control if the variant was unrecognized. Dual-origin patterns were retained within the highest-complexity group only when reported in the smaller course-variant datasets and were not treated as part of the full 1550-ECA origin-variant denominator. Because standardized clinical outcome variables such as hemorrhage volume, operative time, and variant-specific vascular injury rates were not consistently reported across the included studies, the proposed classification should be interpreted as a conceptual, imaging-informed framework rather than a formally outcome-validated system.

Results

The ten studies included in this scoping review were published between 2007 and 2024 and originated from Europe, Asia, North America, and Africa. Together, they reported on 1550 ECAs derived from cadaveric and imaging datasets, including CTA and MRA cohorts. Several imaging-based studies incorporated intraoperative confirmation or correlation with surgical anatomy.

Across studies, the most common configuration was typical anatomy (independent origins of the STA, LA, and FA), with a prevalence range of 50.7–90%. The LFT was the second most frequent variant, occurring in 7.5–24.29% of ECAs. The TLT was rare, reported in 0.6–10.5% of ECAs, while the TLFT was extremely uncommon, with a prevalence of 0–2.85%. ALAs from the CCA or carotid bifurcation were observed in 0.7–6.6% of ECAs.

When stratified by modality, cadaveric studies tended to report slightly higher frequencies of LFT variants than CTA-based studies, whereas CTA was particularly useful for depicting low-frequency variants such as ALA and TLFT and for clarifying three-dimensional branching relationships.

Concerning imaging modality performance, CTA provided the most detailed three-dimensional depiction of LA branching patterns for preoperative planning in the included imaging studies. MRA was used in patients with contraindications to iodinated contrast media but may be less informative for subtle branching anomalies. Digital subtraction angiography was generally reserved for interventional planning or combined diagnostic–therapeutic purposes, whereas ultrasonography had limited utility for deep-neck vascular mapping because of inadequate visualization of the proximal LA.

When applying the proposed anatomy-based classification, the standard branching pattern was assigned to Class I, reflecting relatively predictable vascular control. Class II included the TLT and LFT variants, both of which share common origins and may affect more than one perfusion territory if unrecognized during surgery. Class III comprised the TLFT and ALA origins from the CCA or carotid bifurcation, configurations characterized by more complex proximal branching relationships and potentially greater procedural difficulty. Dual-origin patterns, when reported in the smaller course-variant datasets, were also retained within Class III because of increased anatomical unpredictability. For Class I variants, standard anatomical verification was generally sufficient, although preoperative imaging may be helpful in patients with previous neck surgery, radiotherapy, or suspected anatomical distortion. For Class II variants, CTA or MRA may support operative planning and branch preservation. For Class III variants, detailed vascular mapping and preparedness for technically demanding vascular control should be considered.

Discussion

Accurate identification of LA variants is more than just an anatomical curiosity it is crucial for intraoperative safety and successful reconstruction. While traditional teaching assumes a predictable branching pattern, contemporary cadaveric and imaging studies show that up to 30% of individuals deviate from the standard configuration [5, 21]. Although typically asymptomatic, these morphological and topographical variants can cause serious intraoperative complications, including uncontrolled bleeding and regional ischemia, during oncologic resection, floor-of-mouth surgery, selective neck dissection, reconstructive flap harvesting, and interventional procedures [2].

By analyzing data from 10 studies involving 1550 ECAs across four continents, this review shows that the typical anatomy - where the STA, LA, and FA have independent origins - predominates, being present in 50.7–90% of cases. Variants with an LFT are seen in 7.5–24.3%, the TLT in 0.6–10.5%, and the TLFT in 0–2.85%. Rarely, the LA originates from the CCA or carotid bifurcation, reported in 0.7–6.6%. In the original Seki et al. [20] dataset (n = 111 body sides), the classic deep course was present in 104/111 cases (93.7%), while the remaining course variants were individually uncommon.

From descriptive anatomy to an imaging-informed procedural risk framework

Historical classifications [14, 17, 24] primarily cataloged origin variants such as the LFT, TLT, and TLFT, without directly linking them to imaging strategies or operative planning. The present review extends this descriptive approach by proposing an anatomy-based, imaging-informed framework for procedural risk stratification. As summarized in Table 4, the proposed classification groups LA origin variants into three classes according to branching pattern, shared trunk anatomy, proximity to the carotid bifurcation or common carotid artery, and anticipated difficulty of vascular control, and it explicitly states the basis for each class assignment. Class I anatomy represents the standard branching pattern and generally predictable vascular access. Class II variants, including the TLT and LFT, involve common trunks that may influence more than one perfusion territory if ligated inadvertently. Class III variants, such as the TLFT and aberrant origins from the CCA or carotid bifurcation, are anatomically more complex and may create greater procedural difficulty. Dual origins were retained in this highest-complexity group only when reported in smaller course-variant datasets and should not be interpreted as part of the full origin-variant denominator. Because the included studies did not consistently report standardized clinical outcomes such as hemorrhage volume, operative time, or variant-specific injury rates, this framework should be regarded as conceptual and hypothesis-generating rather than outcome-validated.

Proposed anatomy-based clinical framework for lingual artery (LA) variants and procedural planning considerations

Risk classes and suggested planning considerations represent an anatomy-based interpretive framework derived from reported branching patterns and procedural considerations, rather than validated complication rates.

Imaging considerations CTA offers the most detailed three-dimensional depiction of LA branching anatomy for preoperative planning [9, 21]. MRA is a viable alternative for patients with contrast allergy or radiation concerns, although subtle anomalies may be less conspicuous. Digital subtraction angiography remains primarily useful in combined diagnostic–therapeutic settings, whereas ultrasonography has limited value for deep-neck vascular mapping. Emerging tools such as cone-beam CTA, fusion navigation overlays, and AI-assisted vessel recognition may further improve preoperative assessment.

Cross-specialty implications: The surgical significance of LA mapping goes beyond head and neck oncology. In transoral robotic surgery, three-dimensional vascular “heat maps” have improved navigation through high-risk areas [16], and surface landmark-based localization has been proposed for safer dissection [7]. Similar mapping approaches have been used for other craniofacial vascular regions, such as three-dimensional localization of the greater palatine artery to enhance maxillofacial and palatal surgery [18]. The same principles of preoperative vascular mapping and spatial awareness are vital in parapharyngeal space surgery, where exposure of the carotid artery and lower cranial nerves increases with surgical difficulty [10]. Changes in the LA pathway caused by tongue movement [23], and bleeding risks from anterior mandibular vascular variations [6] further emphasize the interdisciplinary importance of precise vascular assessment and the need for customized surgical approaches across different anatomical regions.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions. Strengths of this review include strict adherence to PRISMA-ScR, the integration of cadaveric and advanced imaging datasets, and the inclusion of both origin and course variants within a single clinically oriented framework. However, several limitations should be emphasized. The included studies showed inconsistent terminology, incomplete distal branching data, and geographic sampling bias. In addition, standardized clinical outcome variables—such as hemorrhage volume, operative time, flap compromise, or variant-specific vascular injury rates—were not consistently available, precluding formal outcome-based validation of the proposed classification. Accordingly, the framework should be interpreted as anatomy-based and hypothesis-generating. Future research should focus on prospective multicenter validation, incorporation of patient-specific anatomical and procedural variables, and the development of practical imaging-based decision tools. Simulation-based training and interactive atlases linking CTA datasets with cadaveric findings may further improve surgical preparedness.

By framing LA variation as part of structured preoperative risk assessment, the proposed classification aims to convert descriptive anatomical knowledge into a practical aid for surgical planning and interdisciplinary communication. Its principal value lies in helping clinicians anticipate branching complexity, select appropriate imaging, and recognize situations in which vascular control may be more demanding. Nevertheless, its clinical role should be regarded as preliminary until prospective outcome-based validation becomes available.
