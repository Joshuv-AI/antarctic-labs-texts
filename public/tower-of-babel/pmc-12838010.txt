Human herpesvirus 7 epithelial keratitis with geographic–dendritic ulceration

Background

Human herpesvirus 7 (HHV-7) is a β-herpesvirus that establishes lifelong latency in CD4⁺ T lymphocytes following primary infection, which typically occurs in early childhood and is often subclinical or manifests as exanthem subitum. Although HHV-7 has been associated with various neurologic and systemic conditions, its role as an ocular pathogen remains poorly defined [1].

Herpesviruses are well-known causative agents of ocular disease, particularly herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cytomegalovirus (CMV), with established mechanisms leading to keratitis, uveitis, and endotheliitis [2]. In contrast, HHV-7 has rarely been identified in ocular tissues; existing case reports have predominantly described its detection in aqueous or vitreous samples without clear clinical correlation. Notably, Inoue et al. documented the first confirmed case of HHV-7 corneal endotheliitis, with real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detection in aqueous humor and clinical response to ganciclovir [3]. Other reports include HHV-7 detection in vitreous fluid in the context of ocular toxoplasmosis and anterior uveitis through multiplex PCR in aqueous humor [4–6].

We present here a unique case of corneal HHV-7 infection, confirmed by both DNA and mRNA quantitative PCR in tear samples, presenting as a geographic-dendritic epithelial ulcer with subsequent Thygeson-like epithelial elevations in a healthy young adult following deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK). This case extends existing reports by demonstrating active viral replication on the corneal surface and highlights HHV-7 as a potential emerging pathogen in herpetic keratitis when more common herpesviruses are excluded.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old man with a typical keratoconus of the left eye underwent an uneven treatment of DALK using a cryopreserved donor graft button. The graft underwent a smooth process of epithelialization, which was fully epithelialized 5 days after the surgery. On day 12 after DALK, the patient complained of irritation, foreign body sensation, and blurry vision. The patient sought care at his local hospital, where he was managed by an ophthalmologist. Despite 7 days of treatment with a soft bandage contact lens, 0.1% fluorometholone, and antibiotic eye drops, the patient’s symptoms worsened. Slit-lamp examination revealed a corneal graft with an epithelial defect exhibiting irregular margins, accompanied by moderate conjunctival injection (Figure: Upper A1). Fluorescein staining revealed a large geographic epithelial defect covering more than half of the graft surface, with the ulcer margins extending into multiple dendritic patterns and terminal bulb-like formations (Figure: Upper A2). The intraocular pressure was normal. The patient was administered oral valacyclovir 1000 mg three times daily, 1% Ganciclovir eyedrops every 30 min, and antibiotic ointment twice daily. Two weeks after undergoing antiviral treatment, the epithelial defect had healed, leaving Thygeson-like epithelial elevations consisting of multiple small, grayish-white raised infiltrations on the corneal surface (Figure: Upper B1) that did not stain with fluorescein (Figure: Upper B2). Thirty-five days after antiviral treatment, the conjunctival injection had resolved, with a smooth and intact epithelium and a transparent graft that remained stable during the 6-month follow-up period (Figure: Upper C1 & C2). At each visit, tear samples were collected from the left eye of the patient using Schirmer test papers (Tear Production Measuring Strips; Meizilin Drug Co, Liaoning, China) without topical anesthesia and mydriasis for qPCR analysis for detecting DNAs of HHVs: HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), CMV, HHV-6, HHV-7, and HHV-8. qPCR test targeting the U57 gene (126 base pairs) of HHV-7, with a specific set of primer and probe, revealed positive detection of HHV-7 (DNA copies, 1.1 × 104/sample), as well as positive detection of mRNA targeting the U79 gene (147 base pairs) of HHV-7 with corresponding cDNA copies 1.3 × 102 at the first visit at which epithelial ulceration was initially identified (Figure: Lower: D & E). The detections for other HHVs except HHV-7 were all negative (data not shown; detailed methods for the routine simultaneous detection of eight human herpesviruses in tears will be reported elsewhere). Two weeks after treatment, qPCR for HHV-7 was positive with DNA copies 6.2 × 102, while the detection of HHV-7 mRNA was negative (Figure: Lower: D & E). Thirty-five days after treatment, neither HHV-7 DNA nor mRNA was detectable. During the following 8-month follow-up period, there was no recurrence of epithelial lesions and ocular inflammation. The visual acuity of the left eye at the final visit was 0.25 without correction and 0.6 with the best correction (Fig. 1).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case providing strong molecular and clinical evidence that HHV-7 can act as a primary corneal epithelial pathogen, inducing dendritic and geographic epithelial ulcers with terminal bulb edges, as the phenotype classically attributed to HSV-1. The diagnosis was supported by a combination of clinical features, specific detection of HHV-7 DNA [7] and mRNA in tear samples during active ulceration, and resolution following antiviral treatment of oral valaciclovir and topical ganciclovir targeting herpesviruses.

PCR of both HHV-7 DNA (1.1 × 10⁴ copies/sample) and U79 mRNA (1.3 × 10² copies) during active ulceration suggests true viral replication rather than latent presence. Tear viral loads above 10⁴–10⁶ copies are consistent with active HSV keratitis [8], and low DNA levels have been associated with latent or asymptomatic shedding in feline herpesvirus models [9, 10]. Moreover, mRNA detection via RT-PCR reliably differentiates active replication from latency in human herpesviruses [9, 10]. The absence of other herpesviruses (HSV-1/2, VZV, CMV, EBV, HHV-6/8) strengthens the likelihood of HHV-7 being the sole etiologic agent.

Dendritic ulcers with terminal bulbs are considered pathognomonic for HSV epithelial keratitis, where viral replication leads to cytopathic changes and epithelial lysis along corneal nerves [11]. The striking similarity in morphology in our case raises intriguing parallels between HHV-7 and HSV-1 pathogenic mechanisms, despite differences in viral tropism and latency. While HSV-1 infects corneal epithelial cells via nectin-1 and HVEM receptors [12–15], HHV-7 has been shown to enter CD4⁺ T cells using CD4 and potentially co-receptors such as CCR5 or CXCR4 [16, 17]. Studies have shown that CD46 is expressed in corneal epithelial infection [18, 19]. Whether corneal epithelial cells express low levels of these receptors or HHV-7 exploits alternative entry pathways warrants further investigation.

Interestingly, the patient had undergone DALK 12 days prior, with a regenerating epithelium over the graft. It is plausible that the immature epithelial barrier, lacking full tight junction integrity, served as a vulnerable substrate for viral entry and replication. This hypothesis aligns with prior observations that corneal trauma, surgery, or contact lens wear can predispose to herpesvirus reactivation or primary infection [20–22].

Another notable observation in our case was the appearance of multiple, grayish-white lesions reminiscent of Thygeson’s superficial punctate keratitis (TSPK) during the healing phase. These lesions were non-staining with fluorescein, suggesting epithelial cell involvement without overt breakdown. Our findings raise the possibility that HHV-7 may contribute to Thygeson-like keratitis, either as a direct cytopathic effect or via immune-mediated sequelae. However, this association remains speculative and warrants further research using controlled virological studies in TSPK patients.

Therapeutically, combined treatment with oral valacyclovir (1000 mg three times daily) and topical 1% ganciclovir resulted in complete corneal re-epithelialization within two weeks, accompanied by a marked decline and undetectable in both HHV-7 DNA and mRNA. While HHV-7 is relatively resistant to acyclovir, it is susceptible to ganciclovir and foscarnet in vitro [23]. Our clinical response suggests that standard anti-herpetic agents may still be effective against ocular HHV-7 infection when dosed adequately and early. The success of this treatment also strengthens the causal link between HHV-7 and the observed corneal disease.

Conclusions

This case expands the spectrum of human herpesviruses implicated in corneal epithelial disease. HHV-7 should be considered in the differential diagnosis of dendritic or geographic keratitis, particularly when routine PCR tests for HSV-1, VZV, and CMV are negative. The use of tear film or aqueous humor for quantitative PCR and mRNA detection may facilitate early diagnosis and appropriate antiviral treatment.
