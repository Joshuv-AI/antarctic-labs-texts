Oncogenic signaling pathways in ameloblastoma and their implications for precision based therapy
Authors: S. Boukssim, H. Soualem, L. Benjelloun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ameloblastoma is an odontogenic epithelial tumor characterized by a paradoxical clinical behavior. Although histologically benign and typically non-metastatic, it demonstrates aggressive local growth, extensive bone destruction, and a high propensity for recurrence. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality but is frequently associated with significant functional and aesthetic morbidity, highlighting the need for alternative, biology-driven therapeutic approaches. Advances in molecular profiling have revealed that ameloblastoma is driven by recurrent, highly specific oncogenic alterations rather than generalized genomic instability. Constitutive activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway—most commonly through BRAF V600E mutations and particularly prominent in mandibular ameloblastomas—represents the principal oncogenic mechanism in a major molecular subset, with additional involvement of FGFR2 and Hedgehog signaling in distinct subgroups. These pathways, which are key regulators of odontogenic development, confer strong oncogenic pathway dependency while maintaining a developmentally constrained tumor phenotype. Accumulating clinical evidence indicates that pathway-directed therapies, particularly combined BRAF and MEK inhibition, can induce rapid and durable responses in patients with locally advanced, recurrent, or metastatic ameloblastoma. Targeted therapy has also demonstrated potential roles in neoadjuvant tumor downstaging and long-term disease control, enabling surgical de-escalation in selected cases. Emerging strategies targeting FGFR, Hedgehog, epigenetic regulators, and PI3K signaling remain investigational but highlight additional therapeutic vulnerabilities. The objective of this review is to summarize current molecular and clinical evidence supporting pathway-directed targeted therapy in ameloblastoma and to explore its implications for precision-based treatment strategies beyond traditional surgical management.

Introduction

Ameloblastoma is a benign odontogenic tumor of epithelial origin. Although histologically benign and typically non-metastatic, it is characterized by locally aggressive growth, extensive bone destruction, and a high risk of recurrence, often requiring radical surgical resection with substantial functional and aesthetic morbidity [1, 2].Current management remains largely surgical, underscoring the need for alternative or adjunctive therapeutic strategies that reduce treatment-related burden while maintaining disease control [3]. Recent advances in molecular profiling have fundamentally reshaped the understanding of ameloblastoma biology, revealing recurrent oncogenic alterations that provide actionable therapeutic opportunities.

Clinical experience with pathway-directed therapies has expanded over the past decade. Recurrent alterations affecting the MAPK pathway, particularly BRAF V600E, have provided the strongest rationale for targeted treatment, and BRAF inhibition, often combined with MEK inhibition, has shown the most consistent clinical activity in reported cases and small series [4–7].

These responses have been documented not only in recurrent and metastatic disease, but also in locally advanced and neoadjuvant settings, where molecular therapy may facilitate less extensive surgery and reduce treatment-related morbidity. In parallel, emerging evidence suggests that additional molecular subsets, including tumors harboring SMO, FGFR2, or PIK3CA alterations, may also offer opportunities for pathway-directed intervention, although these approaches remain investigational and supported by limited data. Together, these developments have positioned ameloblastoma as a clinically relevant model of precision-based treatment in a low–mutational burden tumor.

In this review, we summarize current evidence supporting targeted therapies for ameloblastoma, with emphasis on clinical outcomes, treatment limitations, and future directions. By focusing on oncogenic pathway dependency rather than histological classification alone, we aim to highlight evolving treatment paradigms that may improve disease control while reducing morbidity in this rare but clinically significant tumor.

Literature search strategy

This narrative review was based on a literature search conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE and Web of science from 2015 to 2025. The main search terms included combinations of “ameloblastoma,” “BRAF,” “MAPK,” “MEK,” “Hedgehog,” “SMO,” “FGFR2,” “EGFR,” “PIK3CA,” “targeted therapy,” “precision therapy,” “dabrafenib,” “trametinib,” “vemurafenib,” and “neoadjuvant therapy.” Additional relevant articles were identified through manual review of the reference lists of selected publications.

Developmental oncogenic signaling as the biological basis of ameloblastoma

Ameloblastoma presents one of the most intriguing paradoxes in head and neck oncology. It is no longer viewed as a purely histologic odontogenic tumor, but rather as a molecularly defined neoplasm characterized by recurrent oncogenic driver alterations [1].

Among these, constitutive activation of the MAPK pathway represents the central pathogenic event for mandibular ameloblastomas, most commonly through BRAF V600E, resulting in persistent MEK–ERK signaling independent of external growth stimuli [8–11]. Upstream receptor tyrosine kinases, including FGFR2 and EGFR, may converge on RAS activation and downstream RAF–MEK–ERK signaling [10]. This sustained signaling promotes epithelial proliferation and survival and appears to play a central role in tumor initiation and long-term maintenance [3, 10]. Unlike in high-grade carcinomas, however, MAPK activation in ameloblastoma does not usually induce marked cytological atypia or overt metastatic competence [12]. Instead, it sustains slow but continuous tumor growth with progressive local invasion, suggesting that pathway activation may be sufficient for tumor persistence yet insufficient, on its own, to induce full malignant transformation [12–14].

MAPK activation does not account for all ameloblastomas. A distinct molecular subset is characterized by Hedgehog pathway alterations, most notably SMO mutations, particularly in tumors lacking MAPK/BRAF activation [3, 10]. These abnormalities further support the concept that ameloblastoma is driven by aberrant reactivation of developmental signaling programs. In this context, Hedgehog pathway activation may sustain tumor growth and local aggressiveness while preserving a generally low-grade histologic appearance [3].

The recurrent involvement of MAPK and Hedgehog signaling strongly supports the concept that ameloblastoma arises through aberrant reactivation of developmental programs rather than through the progressive genomic instability that typifies conventional carcinogenesis [9, 15]. This framework helps explain the paradoxical coexistence of bland histology, slow growth, and persistent local invasiveness, and provides a more coherent biological basis for understanding both its clinical behavior and its therapeutic vulnerabilities (Fig. 1).

While tumor-intrinsic signaling establishes the biological foundation of ameloblastoma, it alone does not fully account for the tumor’s marked local invasiveness. Increasing evidence highlights the critical contribution of the tumor microenvironment, particularly the bone compartment, in amplifying disease aggressiveness [16]. Reciprocal interactions between neoplastic epithelial cells, stromal fibroblasts, and bone tissue promote osteoclastogenesis through mediators such as RANKL, inflammatory cytokines, and matrix metalloproteinases, resulting in progressive bone resorption that frequently extends beyond radiologically apparent margins [15, 16]. From this perspective, the local aggressiveness of ameloblastoma may reflect not only epithelial oncogenic signaling but also a process of pathological bone remodeling, which likely contributes to infiltrative growth and recurrence after conservative surgical treatment [15]. The tendency of ameloblastoma to recur many years after initial treatment further suggests the persistence of biologically resilient tumor cell populations.

Reports describing stem cell–associated markers raise the possibility that stem-like cells may survive treatment and remain dormant for prolonged periods, thereby contributing to late recurrence [17]. Although this hypothesis remains incompletely established, it complements purely surgical explanations and underscores the importance of tumor-intrinsic biology in long-term disease control [3].

Genetic alterations alone do not fully explain the marked heterogeneity in clinical behavior observed among ameloblastomas sharing similar molecular profiles. Epigenetic regulation may therefore act as a molecular rheostat, modulating oncogenic pathway output and influencing growth, invasiveness, recurrence risk, and therapeutic sensitivity [18]. Emerging data implicate epigenetic regulators, including EZH2, in maintaining proliferative and stem-like tumor cell states, suggesting that epigenetic dependency may contribute to biological and clinical variability [3, 18]. Although current evidence remains limited, epigenetic dysregulation represents a plausible and biologically coherent contributor to disease heterogeneity and constitutes an important avenue for future investigation.

Altogether, these observations support a model in which ameloblastoma is a developmentally constrained, signaling-dependent neoplasm. Its clinically aggressive local behavior appears to result not only from persistent oncogenic pathway activation, but also from reciprocal interactions with the bone microenvironment and from regulatory mechanisms, including epigenetic modulation, that may influence recurrence and therapeutic responsiveness [3, 10, 18]. This biological framework is clinically relevant because it provides the rationale for pathway-directed therapy in a tumor that appears strongly dependent on a limited number of developmental signaling axes [3].

Targeted therapy in ameloblastoma: exploiting oncogenic pathway dependency

Activation of the MAPK signaling cascade represents a central oncogenic mechanism in ameloblastoma. Upstream receptors, including FGFR2 and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), converge on the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK pathway, driving epithelial proliferation, migration, invasion, and differentiation [8, 9, 12]. Constitutive activation of this cascade, most frequently through BRAF V600E mutations, provides a strong biological rationale for pathway-directed therapy.

Clinical experience accumulated over the past decade suggests that MAPK pathway inhibition can produce meaningful responses in selected patients with ameloblastoma, particularly in recurrent, metastatic, and locally advanced BRAF V600E–mutant disease. Early reports established proof of principle by documenting major radiologic responses and prolonged clinical benefit with BRAF-directed therapy in individual cases [7, 19, 20]. This broader pattern was further supported by a 2023 systematic review [21] of published BRAF inhibitor-treated cases across neoadjuvant, recurrent, and metastatic settings, while also emphasizing the limited follow-up and case-based nature of the available evidence. In parallel, Hirschhorn et al. [22] showed in 2021 that upfront BRAF inhibitor therapy in three consecutive pediatric patients with marginally resectable BRAF V600E–mutant ameloblastoma resulted in substantial tumor regression, conservative complete surgery, and disease-free survival at a median follow-up of 31 months. More recently, the 2024 series by Grynberg et al. [23] extended this neoadjuvant experience by reporting radiologic response in all 11 treated patients, with 10 of 11 subsequently undergoing mandible-preservation surgery. Interest in BRAF-directed therapy is also reflected in registered clinical studies. A pilot study evaluating dabrafenib plus trametinib in patients with BRAF-mutated ameloblastoma has also been registred [24].Taken together, these reports suggest that the clinical value of MAPK inhibition in ameloblastoma may lie not only in tumor regression, but also in its capacity to modify the extent of surgery required in selected patients. At the same time, the evidence base remains limited to case reports, small retrospective series, and short to intermediate follow-up, so these findings should be interpreted as promising but still preliminary.

Metastatic ameloblastoma remains rare, most often presenting as indolent pulmonary metastases, yet it represents the clinical scenario in which systemic therapy is most clearly justified [7]. Although the available evidence is limited to isolated reports and small series, molecularly matched therapy, particularly BRAF inhibition, often combined with MEK inhibition in BRAF V600E–mutant tumors, has been associated with meaningful radiologic responses and prolonged disease control in selected patients, including those with lung metastases [5, 6, 25].

Importantly, combined BRAF and MEK inhibition appears preferable to BRAF inhibitor monotherapy [4, 6]. Dual inhibition not only enhances antitumor efficacy but also delays the emergence of resistance and reduces paradoxical MAPK reactivation, a phenomenon well described in other MAPK-driven tumors [26]. Moreover, combination therapy has been associated with a more favorable toxicity profile compared with BRAF inhibitor monotherapy, reinforcing its role as the preferred strategy in BRAF-mutant ameloblastoma [3].

Despite these encouraging results, several limitations restrict the widespread application of MAPK-targeted therapy. Toxicity remains a key consideration, particularly with MEK inhibitors, which are associated with dermatologic, gastrointestinal, ocular, cardiovascular, and muscular adverse effects [27, 28]. Although most toxicities are manageable with dose modification and monitoring, rare severe complications underscore the need for careful patient selection and long-term surveillance [27, 28].

Beyond BRAF-driven disease, FGFR2 mutations represent an emerging therapeutic target that intersects with MAPK signaling. Preliminary clinical observations suggest that multikinase inhibitors such as lenvatinib can induce tumor regression in FGFR2-mutated ameloblastoma [29]. However, clinical experience remains limited, and the mechanisms underlying FGFR2-driven drug sensitivity are not fully understood. Adverse effects, although generally manageable, remain common, emphasizing the need for further investigation [29].

Aberrant activation of the HH signaling pathway represents a distinct oncogenic mechanism in a subset of ameloblastomas, particularly those harboring SMO mutations. Clinically, SMO-mutant ameloblastomas are predominantly located in the maxilla, a subgroup associated with higher recurrence rates [30], frequent soft-tissue invasion, and increased risk of orbital and intracranial extension [31, 32], making complete surgical resection challenging and highlighting the need for alternative therapeutic strategies. Despite this biological rationale, clinical translation of HH pathway inhibition in ameloblastoma remains limited. Currently approved HH-targeted agents include SMO inhibitors such as vismodegib and sonidegib, and the GLI inhibitor arsenic trioxide (ATO) [33]. However, preclinical evidence indicates that many SMO mutations identified in ameloblastoma—particularly conformational variants such as L412F and W535L—confer intrinsic resistance to classical SMO inhibitors [34]. Toxicity further complicates HH-targeted therapy. SMO inhibitors are associated with a high burden of adverse effects, including muscle cramps, dysgeusia, alopecia, weight loss, fatigue, and, in rare cases, severe or fatal complications [35, 36].

At present, no clinical studies have reported the use of HH pathway inhibitors in ameloblastoma patients. Consequently, HH-targeted therapy cannot yet be recommended as standard treatment. Nevertheless, given the prevalence of SMO mutations in anatomically complex, high-risk maxillary tumors, HH pathway inhibition—particularly through downstream GLI targeting—remains an attractive investigational approach.

Beyond canonical MAPK and HH signaling, epigenetic dysregulation also represents a potential therapeutic vulnerability. EZH2 overexpression has been identified as a candidate oncogenic mechanism in ameloblastoma [37]. Given the availability of EZH2 inhibitors such as tazemetostat [38], this pathway offers translational potential, particularly in tumors exhibiting epigenetic dependency.

Additional actionable alterations include FGFR2 and PI3K pathway mutations. FGFR2 mutations with distinct functional consequences suggest that mutation-specific FGFR inhibition may be effective in selected cases [3]. Similarly, rare PIK3CA mutations raise the possibility of PI3K-targeted therapy using agents already approved in other malignancies [39].

Across reported cases and small series (Table 2), MAPK pathway inhibition has repeatedly been associated with rapid radiologic and, in some reports, metabolic responses, often within weeks of treatment initiation [21, 25]. Durable disease control extending over months to several years has also been described, particularly with combined BRAF and MEK inhibition, supporting the hypothesis that at least a subset of ameloblastomas remain strongly pathway-dependent over time [21, 23]. However, the most important implication of recent reports may not be response alone, but a shift in therapeutic logic. In neoadjuvant settings, targeted therapy may permit surgical de-escalation, preservation of mandibular continuity, and reduction of reconstructive morbidity in carefully selected BRAF-mutant tumors [23]. This reframes precision therapy in ameloblastoma less as a conventional systemic treatment for advanced disease and more as a tool for treatment modulation and organ preservation [3]. Because the available evidence remains case-based and potentially affected by publication bias, these benefits should be regarded as indication-specific rather than universally established.

Redefining therapeutic goals: neoadjuvant and long-term targeted strategies

In contrast to malignant epithelial tumors, where targeted therapy is primarily used to control biologically aggressive disease or prolong survival, the objectives of molecular therapy in ameloblastoma appear to be different [3]. Because ameloblastoma typically exhibits indolent growth, low metastatic potential, and marked dependency on a limited number of oncogenic signaling pathways, targeted therapy may be relevant not only as a cytoreductive strategy but also as a means of modifying therapeutic planning and reducing treatment-related morbidity [3].

In advanced, recurrent, unresectable, or metastatic disease, targeted therapy may provide prolonged disease control and help avoid repeated mutilating operations in selected patients [5, 40].

Although the available evidence remains limited, reported responses suggest that some tumors may remain dependent on MAPK signaling over extended periods, raising the possibility that systemic therapy can function as a form of long-term disease modulation rather than merely short-term tumor shrinkage [3].

More recent neoadjuvant reports have further shifted the clinical significance of targeted therapy in ameloblastoma. In large but potentially resectable tumors, particularly those associated with major functional or aesthetic morbidity, preoperative molecular inhibition may downstage disease and facilitate less extensive surgery [22, 41]. In this setting, the benefit of targeted therapy may lie not only in radiologic response, but also in its capacity to preserve mandibular continuity, limit reconstructive burden, and reduce long-term functional sequelae [3, 10]. Recent experience therefore suggests that targeted therapy may alter surgical intent in selected patients rather than simply serve as salvage treatment.

Collectively, these observations suggest that the relevance of targeted therapy in ameloblastoma extends beyond tumor shrinkage alone. Rather than replacing surgery as a universal standard, precision-based treatment may be best understood as an individualized strategy for disease modulation, surgical de-escalation, and organ preservation in carefully selected clinical settings [21, 40, 41]. This perspective helps explain why ameloblastoma has emerged as a distinctive model for biology-guided therapy in a low–mutational burden tumor [3].

Limitations, resistance mechanisms, and future directions

Despite encouraging reported responses, several factors still limit the broader adoption of targeted therapy in ameloblastoma. The current evidence is based mainly on case reports, small retrospective series, and highly selected patients, which reflects both the rarity of the disease and the lack of prospective studies. Follow-up is often limited, making it difficult to assess the durability of response, the risk of late relapse, and the long-term safety of extended pathway inhibition. In addition, treatment duration, sequencing with surgery, and follow-up strategies have not been standardized. These limitations make it difficult to draw firm conclusions about the role of targeted therapy in routine practice. Publication bias is also likely, since favorable outcomes are more likely to be reported than treatment failure, short-lived responses, or significant toxicity. At present, targeted therapy is better considered a promising approach in selected settings rather than an established alternative to surgery.

Possible resistance mechanisms should also be taken into account. These may include reactivation of MAPK signaling, activation of alternative pathways, or adaptive changes in the tumor microenvironment that reduce treatment efficacy [26, 33, 34]. Although these mechanisms have not been systematically studied in ameloblastoma, observations from other MAPK-driven tumors suggest that resistance may emerge during prolonged pathway inhibition, which supports interest in combination strategies. In tumors that are not MAPK-driven, mutation-specific resistance further underlines the importance of careful molecular interpretation when considering targeted treatment [34].

Beyond its therapeutic relevance, ameloblastoma may also represent a useful model for precision oncology, as it shows how a tumor with a relatively low mutational burden can still depend on a limited oncogenic signaling network. Future studies should move beyond isolated reports of response and focus on clinically relevant questions such as patient selection for neoadjuvant therapy, timing of surgery after response, duration of treatment, mechanisms of resistance, and long-term recurrence outcomes. Prospective multi-institutional data will be important to better define the place of targeted therapy in ameloblastoma management.
