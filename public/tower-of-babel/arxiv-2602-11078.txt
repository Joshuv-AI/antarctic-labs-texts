First-order phase transition for Gibbs point processes with saturated interactions
D. Dereudre, C. Renaud-Chan

Abstract
We study first-order phase transitions in continuum Gibbs point processes with saturated interactions. These interactions form a broad class of Hamiltonians in which the local energy in regions of high particle density depends only on the number of points. Building on ideas of Pirogov–Sinaĭ-Zahradnik theory and its adaptations to the continuum, we develop a general method for establishing the existence of two distinct infinite-volume Gibbs measures with different intensities in this setting, demonstrating a first-order phase transition. Our approach extends previous results obtained for the Quermass model and applies in particular to a new class of diluted pairwise interactions introduced in this work.



1
Introduction

A grand canonical Gibbs point process on a finite volume
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a probability measure with unnormalized density given by the Boltzmann factor
e^{-\beta H}
with respect to the Poisson point process on
\Delta
with activity
z>0
. Here,
\beta>0
is the inverse temperature and
H
is the Hamiltonian. Infinite-volume Gibbs point processes are defined as solutions to the Dobrushin–Lanford–Ruelle equations, which characterize equilibrium states of the system. The question of uniqueness or non-uniqueness of such probability measures on the configuration space is both difficult and has a long history since
21
.

It is known that for pairwise interactions in regimes where both
z
and
\beta
are small, the Gibbs measure is unique. This has been established through various approaches: in
20
using the Kirkwood–Salsburg equations; in
17
via cluster expansion techniques to prove analyticity of the pressure; in
7
and
13
using Dobrushin’s uniqueness criterion; and in
1
through a coupling with the random connection model.

A first-order phase transition occurs when the pressure of the system is non-differentiable. This phenomenon is related to the non-uniqueness of Gibbs measures, and one can show the existence of two Gibbs measures with different intensities. In the literature, there are only a few results on phase transitions for continuum Gibbs measures without spins.

The most well-known results concern the Area-Interaction model, which was introduced by
23
and the phase transition was established using different methods in
21
,
2
, and
11
. Its phase diagram was completed in
3
, showing that non-uniqueness occurs only when
z=\beta
; however, the question remains open in a small neighborhood of the critical point. In one dimension, a phase transition for a Lennard–Jones interaction was proved in
14
. We also mention the classical result of
16
for Kac-type interactions, whose proof adapts Pirogov-Sinaï theory to the continuum. More recently, a similar phase transition result was obtained for a modified version of this Kac-type interaction in
12
.

In this paper, we develop a general method to prove first-order phase transitions for saturated interactions. This work generalizes previous results on the Quermass model
5
to a broader class of interactions. Our approach is inspired by the ideas and techniques of Pirogov, Sinaï, and Zahradník
18
;
19
;
24
, adapted to the continuum. This class of saturated interactions corresponds to Hamiltonians for which the energy in regions of high local particle density depends only on the number of points. We introduce a new class of interaction that we call the diluted pairwise interaction. It is characterized by a pair potential and a parameter
R>0
that we call the dilution scale. The diluted pairwise interaction is an approximation of the pairwise interaction under a proper renormalisation and as
R\to 0
we recover the pairwise interaction. Using our framework, we establish a new phase transition result for the diluted pairwise interactions. Moreover, for pair potential that is strongly repulsive at short range, such as the Lennard-Jones potential, we show that for any scale of dilution there is always a way to truncate the pair potential at the origin and have a phase transition for this truncated pair potential. This corollary opens a new path to study phase transition for pairwise interactions for which no phase transition results are known.

Our paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the notation, the classes of saturated and diluted pairwise interactions, and provide the definition of Gibbs point processes and the notion of contours. In Section
3
, we present the main results of this paper. In Section
4
, we prove the general first-order phase transition result for saturated interactions. Finally, in Section
5
, we establish that under certain assumptions, the diluted pairwise interaction satisfies a Peierls condition and thus exhibits a first-order phase transition.

2
Definitions and models

2.1
Setting and notations

We denote by
\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of bounded Borel sets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with positive Lebesgue measure. For any sets
A
and
B
in
\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
A\oplus B
stands for the Minkowski sum of these sets, i.e.
A\oplus B=\{a+b,\forall a\in A,\forall b\in B\}
. Let
M
ne a Polish space describing the mark or the spin state of particles. We define
\mathcal{S}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times M
the state space of a single marked point. For any
(x,m)\in\mathcal{S}
, the first coordinate
x
is for the location of the point and the second coordinate
m
is the mark of a particle. For any subset
C\subset\mathcal{S}
and any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
C+u:=\{(x+u,m),(x,m)\in C\}
is the translation of
C
by a vector
u
. For any set
\Delta\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\mathcal{S}_{\Delta}
is the local state space
\Delta\times M
. A configuration of marked points
\omega
is a locally finite set in
\mathcal{S}
; i.e.
N_{\Delta}(\omega):=\#(\omega\cap E_{\Delta})
is finite for any
\Delta\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We denote by
\Omega
the set of all marked point configurations and by
\Omega_{f}
its restriction to finite configurations. For any
\omega\in\Omega
, its projection in
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by
\omega_{\Delta}:=\omega\cap\mathcal{S}_{\Delta}
. We equip the state space
\Omega
with the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the counting functions on
\mathcal{S}
,
\omega\mapsto\#(\omega\cap(\Delta\times A))
for
\Delta\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
A\in\mathcal{B}(M)
. We consider a reference measure
\lambda\otimes\mathbb{P}_{M}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times M
where
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{P}_{M}
a probability measure on the mark space,
M
, of the particle.
A point process
\mathbb{X}
is a random variable on the set of configurations,
\Omega
, and it is said to be stationary in space if for any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbbm{X}+u\overset{d}{=}\mathbbm{X}
. Let
z>0
be the activity, we denote by
\Pi^{z}
the Poisson point process with intensity measure
z\lambda\otimes\mathbb{P}_{M}
. For any
\Delta\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we denote by
\Pi_{\Delta}^{z}
the restriction of the Poisson point process to the configurations inside
\Delta
.

2.2
Interaction

Let us introduce the general assumptions on the interaction between particles of our system. The Hamiltonian, also called the energy functional, is a measurable function from
\Omega_{f}
to
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. For any
\Lambda\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and any configuration
\omega\in\Omega
we define the local energy in
\Lambda
of the configuration
\omega
as

H_{\Lambda}(\omega):=\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}\left(H(\omega_{\Lambda\oplus B(0,r)})-H(\omega_{\Lambda\oplus B(0,r)\setminus\Lambda})\right)

(2.1)

if the limit exists. Similarly we define the local energy of a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in a configuration
\omega\in\mathcal{C}
as

h(x,\omega):=\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}\left(H(\{x\}\cup\omega_{B(x,r)})-H(\omega_{B(x,r)})\right)

(2.2)

if the limit exists. We assume that the Hamiltonian satisfies the following set of assumptions:

1.

Stability
. There exists
C_{s}\geq 0
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
,
H(\omega)\geq-C_{s}N(\omega)
.

2.

Non-degeneracy
.
H(\emptyset)<+\infty
.

3.

Heredity
. If for
\omega\in\Omega_{f}

H(\omega)=\infty
then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
H(\omega\cup\{x\})=+\infty
.

4.

Invariance by translation
. For all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
, we have
H(\omega)=H(\omega+u)
.

5.

Finite range
. There is
R>0
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega
and
\Delta\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
H_{\Delta}(\omega)=H_{\Delta}(\omega_{\Delta\oplus B(0,R)})
.

These are classical assumptions on the Hamiltonian that are verified by pairwise interactions with Strauss potential or truncated Lennard-Jones potential and also by multibody interaction such as the Area interaction or the Quermass interaction. In this paper, however we will be interested in a subclass of interaction that satisfy a saturation property.

2.3
Coarse graining and saturation

Let us consider a paving of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with tiles of length
\delta>0
. For any integer
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we denote the
i
-th tile with
T_{i}:=\left[-\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{d}+i\delta
. For any subset
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we denote by
\hat{\Lambda}=\bigcup_{i\in\Lambda}T_{i}
. Before going further, we define two sets of locally homogeneous configurations around a tile. Let
L>0
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define the homogeneous dense (resp. empty) configurations around
T_{i}
,

\displaystyle\Omega_{i,L}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(1)}}}}}:=\{\omega\in\Omega,\omega_{T_{i}}\neq\emptyset,\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\delta\left\lVert i-j\right\rVert\leq L\},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Omega_{i,L}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(0)}}}}}:=\{\omega\in\Omega,\omega_{T_{i}}=\emptyset,\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\delta\left\lVert i-j\right\rVert\leq L\},

(2.4)

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
denotes the Euclidean norm. We assume that there is a measurable function
E_{0}:\Omega_{f}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
such that for any
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
,

H(\omega)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}E_{i}(\omega),

(2.5)

where
E_{i}(\omega)=E_{0}(\omega-\delta i)
.
E_{i}
is the energy assigned to the tile
T_{i}
. In order to simplify the notations, for any
\Lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the total energy assigned on
\hat{\Lambda}
is denoted by

E_{\Lambda}=\sum_{i\in\Lambda}E_{i}.

(2.6)

Note that such a measurable function
E_{0}
might not be unique. We assume that the energy assigned to
T_{0}
,
E_{0}
, satisfies the following set of assumptions :

1.

Non-degenerate
.
E_{0}(\emptyset)=0
.

2.

Local function
. There exists
r>0
such that for any configuration
\omega\in\Omega
,

E_{0}(\omega)=E_{0}(\omega_{T_{0}\oplus B(0,r)}).

3.

Stable
. There exists
c_{s}\geq 0
such that for any configuration
\omega\in\Omega
,

E_{0}(\omega)\geq-c_{s}(1+N_{T_{0}\oplus B(0,r)}(\omega)).

4.

Tame function
. There exists
c_{t}\geq 0
such that for any configuration
\omega\in\Omega
,

E_{0}(\omega)\leq c_{t}(1+N_{T_{0}\oplus B(0,r)}^{2}(\omega)).

5.

Saturation
. There exists
L>0
,
\delta>0
,
\overline{e}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\overline{e}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for any homogeneous configurations around
T_{0}
,

\forall\omega\in\Omega_{i,L}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(0)}}}}}\cup\Omega_{i,L}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(1)}}}}},\quad E_{0}(\omega)=\overline{E}_{0}(\omega):=\overline{e}N_{T_{0}}(\omega)+\overline{e}_{0}\mathds{1}_{\omega_{T_{0}}\neq\emptyset}.

The non-degeneracy assumption
1
, is required to ensure a non-degenerate Hamiltonian
2
. If we assume
2
then the Hamiltonian is finite range,
5
, with a range of
r+2\sqrt{d}\delta
. Furthermore, if we assume
3
then the Hamiltonian is stable,
1
. The assumption
4
is a technical assumption needed in order to prove Lemma
4.3
. The saturation property,
5
, is one of the main ingredients needed to prove the first-order phase transition. Here we assume that in the setting where the configuration is homogeneous the energy depends only on the number of points inside the tiles. One can show that if
\overline{E}_{0}
depends only on the number of points in
T_{0}
, then it must take the form described in
5
, other expression is not possible. Now let us provide examples of Hamiltonian that satisfy these assumptions and especially the saturation property.

1.

K-nearest neighbour Strauss interaction
. It is an example of pairwise interaction with nearest neighbours
10
. Let
i\in\mathbb{N}
, given a configuration
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
, a point
y\in\omega
is the
i
-th neighbour of
x\in\omega
if
\#B(x,|x-y|)\cap\omega=i
and we denote by
y=v_{i}(x,\omega)
. If there are several points at the same distance to
x
we can list these points using the lexicographical order on the cartesian coordinate. We consider
K\geq 1
,
R>0
and
A\geq 0
, for any configuration
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
the Hamiltonian is given by,

H(\omega)=\begin{cases}\sum_{x\in\omega}\sum_{i=1}^{\min(K,N(\omega)-1)}A\mathds{1}_{|x-v_{i}(x,\omega)|\leq R}&\text{if }N(\omega)\geq 2,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.7)

For this interaction, a natural way to assign energy to each tile is, for any configuration
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
,

E_{0}(\omega)=\begin{cases}\sum_{x\in\omega_{T_{0}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\min(K,N(\omega)-1)}A\mathds{1}_{|x-v_{i}(x,\omega)|\leq R}&\text{if }N(\omega)\geq 2,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.8)

By definition,
E_{0}
satisfies
1
and
2
. Furthermore, for any configuration
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
we have

0\leq E_{0}(\omega)\leq AKN(\omega_{T_{0}}).

(2.9)

Therefore,
E_{0}
satisfies
3
and
4
. We can fix
L=R
and
\delta
small enough such that for any
x\in T_{0},\#\{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},T_{i}\subset B(x,R)\}\geq K+1
. As a consequence, for any homogeneous configurations around
T_{0}
,
\omega\in\Omega_{i,L}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(0)}}}}}\cup\Omega_{i,L}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(1)}}}}}
,

E_{0}(\omega)=AKN(\omega_{T_{0}})=:\overline{E}_{0}(\omega),

(2.10)

and thus
E_{0}
satisfies the saturation property,
5
.

2.

Quermass interaction with random bounded radii
. It is a classical morphologic interaction
15
. We consider the mark space to be
M=[R_{1},R_{2}]
and let
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i\in[0,d]
. For any configuration,
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
, the Hamiltonian is given by,

H(\omega)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\theta_{i}M_{i}^{d}(L(\omega)),

(2.11)

where
M_{i}^{d}
is the
i
-th Minkowski functional and
L(\omega)
is the halo of the configuration, i.e.

L(\omega)=\bigcup_{(x,r)\in\omega}B(x,r).

(2.12)

In particular,
M_{d}^{d}
is the Lebesgue measure,
M_{d-1}^{d}
is the
d_{1}
Hausdorff measure of the surface and
M_{0}^{d}
is the Euler-Poincaré characteristic. When
\theta_{i}=0
for
i=0,\dots,d-1
and
\theta_{d}>0
, the Quermass interaction corresponds to the one component Widom-Rowlinson model,
23
. In dimension
d\leq 2
, the Quermass interaction satisfies our assumption on the Hamiltonian, especially the stability assumption
1
,
15
. In higher dimension
(d\geq 3)
, the Quermass interaction might not be stable, see
15
for further details.

We call a facet
F
any non-empty intersection of closed tiles
\left(\overline{T}_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}
the set of all facets and
\mathcal{F}_{0}:=\{F\in\mathcal{F},F\cap T_{0}\neq\emptyset\}
. Therefore, by additivity property of the Minkowski functionals the natural way to assign energy to each tile is, for any configuration
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
,

E_{0}(\omega)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\theta_{i}M_{i,0}^{d}(L(\omega)),

(2.13)

where for any subset
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

M_{i,0}^{d}(A)=\sum_{k=i}^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}_{0}\\
\dim(F)=k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{d-k}M_{i}^{d}(A\cap F).

By definition
E_{0}
satisfies
1
and since the radii of each particle is bounded it also satisfies
2
. In order to verify
3
and
4
we will consider that
\theta_{i}=0
for
i=0,\dots,d-2
when
d\geq 3
and for
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i=0,\dots,d
when
d\leq 2
. Indeed, at any dimension we have that for any facet
F\in\mathcal{F}_{0}
and any configuration
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
we have,

\displaystyle 0\leq M_{d,0}^{d}(L(\omega)\cap F)\leq N_{T_{0}\oplus B(0,R_{2})}M_{d}^{d}(T_{0}),

(2.14)

\displaystyle 0\leq M_{d-1,0}^{d}(L(\omega)\cap F)\leq N_{T_{0}\oplus B(0,R_{2})}(\omega)M_{d-1}^{d}(B(0,R_{2})).

(2.15)

Now we consider that we are in dimension 2 and look at the contribution of the Euler-Poincaré characteristic to the energy of a tile. We have for facets of dimension 0,
M_{0}^{2}(L(\omega)\cap F)\in\{0,1\}
. Then for facets of dimension 1, we have that
L(\omega)\cap F
has no holes and therefore
M_{0}^{2}(L(\omega)\cap F)=N_{cc}(L(\omega)\cap F)\leq N_{T_{0}\oplus B(0,R_{2})}(\omega)
, where
N_{cc}
counts the number of connected components. For the only facet of dimension 2,
\overline{T}_{0}
, we know that the number of holes is at most linear,
15
. Therefore, there is a constant
C_{0}>0
such that

|M_{0,0}^{2}(\omega)|\leq C_{0}(1+N_{T_{0}\oplus B(0,R_{2})}(\omega)).

(2.16)

The same bound can be obtained for
M_{0,0}^{1}
in dimension 1. As a consequence, under these restrictions the Quermass interaction satisfies
3
and
4
. Finally, if we fix
\delta\leq\frac{R_{1}}{\sqrt{d}}
and
L>R_{2}
, we have that for any homogeneous configuration,
\omega\in\Omega_{i,L}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(0)}}}}}\cup\Omega_{i,L}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(1)}}}}}
,

E_{0}(\omega)=\delta^{d}\mathds{1}_{\omega_{T_{0}}\neq\emptyset}=:\overline{E}_{0}(\omega),

(2.17)

and therefore the Quermass interaction satisfies the saturation property
5
.

3.

Diluted pairwise interaction
. This example is new and introduced in this paper to provide an saturated approximation of the pairwise interaction which is not saturated. Let
R>0
and
\phi
be a radial pair potential such that
r^{d-1}\phi\in L^{1}
and has a compact support. We define
R_{1}=\sup\{r\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\phi(r)>0\}
and
R_{2}>0
such that for
r>R_{2}
,
\phi(r)=0
. For any configuration,
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
, the Hamiltonian is given by,

H(\omega)=\iint_{L_{R}(\omega)^{2}}\phi(|x-y|)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

(2.18)

where

L_{R}(\omega)=\bigcup_{x\in\omega}B(x,R).

This Hamiltonian is an approximation of the usual pairwise interaction. Indeed, when rescaled properly and having scale of dilution,
R
, going to
0
we obtain the pairwise interaction. The way we assign energy to each tile is, for any configuration
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
,

E_{0}(\omega)=\int_{L_{R}(\omega)\cap T_{0}}\int_{L_{R}(\omega)}\phi(|x-y|)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

(2.19)

By definition
E_{0}
satisfies
1
. Since
\phi
has a compact support therefore
E_{0}
satisfies
2
. We have for any configuration
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
,

|E_{0}(\omega)|\leq\delta^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\phi(|x|)\right|\mathrm{d}x,

(2.20)

and therefore
E_{0}
satisfies
3
and
4
. Finally, if we fix
\delta\leq\frac{R}{\sqrt{d}}
and
L>R_{2}+2\sqrt{d}\delta
, we have that for any homogeneous configuration,
\omega\in\Omega_{i,L}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(0)}}}}}\cup\Omega_{i,L}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(1)}}}}}
,

E_{0}(\omega)=\delta^{d}C_{\phi}\mathds{1}_{\omega_{T_{0}}\neq\emptyset}=:\overline{E}_{0}(\omega)\quad\text{where }C_{\phi}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(|x|)\mathrm{d}x,

(2.21)

and therefore diluted pairwise interaction satisfies the saturation property
5
.

2.4
Gibbs marked point process

Now we introduce the concept of (infinite-volume) Gibbs measures.

Definition 2.1
.

A probability measure
P
on
\Omega
is a Gibbs measure for the Hamiltonian
H
, the activity
z>0
and the inverse temperature
\beta\geq 0
if
P
is stationary in space and if for any
\Delta\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and any bounded positive measurable function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\int f(\omega)P(\mathrm{d}\omega)=\int\int\frac{1}{Z_{\Delta}^{z,\beta}(\omega_{\Delta^{c}})}f(\omega_{\Delta}^{\prime}\cup\omega_{\Delta^{c}})e^{-\beta H_{\Delta}(\omega_{\Delta}^{\prime}\cup\omega_{\Delta^{c}})}\Pi_{\Delta}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}_{\Delta})P(\mathrm{d}\omega)

(2.22)

where
Z_{\Delta}^{z,\beta}(\omega_{\Delta^{c}})
is the partition function given the outer configuration
\omega_{\Delta^{c}}

Z_{\Delta}^{z,\beta}(\omega_{\Delta^{c}})=\int e^{-\beta H_{\Delta}(\omega_{\Delta}^{\prime}\cup\omega_{\Delta^{c}})}\Pi_{\Delta}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}).

We denote by
\mathcal{G}(H,z,\beta)
the set of all Gibbs measures.

The equations
2.22
are to referred as the DLR equations (for Dobrushin, Lanford and Ruelle) and describe the system at equilibrium. The existence and uniqueness of Gibbs measures are challenging and natural questions. Under the assumptions
2
-
5
the existence of such Gibbs measures are ensured by
(
4
, Theorem 1)
or by
22
. Non-uniqueness of Gibbs measures at some parameter
(z,\beta)
exhibits the coexistence of several state of matter. Our strategy to prove non-uniqueness is, for some set of parameters
(z,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
, to exhibit two Gibbs measures
\mathbb{P}^{+},\mathbb{P}^{-}\in\mathcal{G}(H,z,\beta)
such that

\rho(\mathbb{P}^{+}):=E_{\mathbb{P}^{+}}(N_{[0,1]^{d}})>\rho(\mathbb{P}^{-}).

(2.23)

We call this phenomenon a first-order phase transition. The measure of a jump in density between two pure phases is effective in detecting a liquid-gas phase transition. However it is not enough as one needs to prove that crystallisation and symmetry breaking does not occur. In relation with the Gibbs measures, the pressure, denoted by
\psi
, is a quantity of interest and it is defined as

\psi(z,\beta):=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{|\Delta_{n}|}\ln(Z_{\Delta_{n}}^{z,\beta}),

(2.24)

where
(\Delta_{n})
is the sequence of the following boxes
[-n,n]^{d}
and
Z_{\Delta_{n}}^{z,\beta}
is the partition function with free boundary condition (i.e.
Z_{\Delta_{n}}^{z,\beta}(\emptyset)
). Under the assumption of finite range interaction, this limit always exists as a consequence of sub-additivity of the sequence
(\ln Z_{\Delta_{n}}^{z,\beta})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, see
(
6
, Lemma 1)
for more detail. The regularity of the pressure is related to the phase transition phenomenon. In particular the first order phase transition is related to its non-differentiability for some parameters. As a result of the Pirogov-Sinaï-Zahradník method, we will prove that the pressure is non-differentiable for a critical activity and
\beta
large enough.

2.5
Contours

In order to state our main results, , it is necessary to introduce the notion of contours, as it plays a crucial role in the statement of our results. We define the following application

\displaystyle\sigma:

\displaystyle\Omega_{f}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rightarrow\{0,1\}

\displaystyle(\omega,i)\mapsto\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\omega_{T_{i}}=\emptyset\\
1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

In the following we use the notation
\sharp
for either
0
or
1
. Let
L>0
and
\omega\in\Omega
, a site
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is said to be
\sharp
-correct if for all sites
j
such that
\delta\left\lVert i-j\right\rVert\leq 2L
, we have
\sigma(\omega,j)=\sharp
. A site
i
is non-correct when it fails to be
\sharp
-correct for any
\sharp\in\{0,1\}
. The set of all non-correct sites is denoted by
\overline{\Gamma}(\omega)
. We can partition
\overline{\Gamma}(\omega)
into its maximum connected components that we denote by
\overline{\gamma}(\omega)
and we call it the support of a contour. We define a contour as the pair
\gamma(\omega):=(\overline{\gamma}(\omega),(\sigma(\omega,i))_{i\in\overline{\gamma}(\omega)})
and we denote by
\Gamma(\omega)
the set of all contours that appear with the configuration
\omega
.

3
Results

We now present our results on phase transition for saturated interactions. We begin with a general result on first-order phase transitions for saturated interactions for which the Hamiltonian satisfies a Peierls condition. The proof of this result will be given in the following section. Afterwards, we explore methods to verify a Peierls condition and some examples of phase transitions that can be obtained using this approach.

Theorem 1
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian that satisfies
1
-
5
such that
E_{0}
exists and satisfies
1
-
5
. Furthermore, we suppose that the interaction satisfies a Peierls-like condition, i.e. there is
\overline{e}_{+}>0
such that for any contour
\gamma
and any configuration
\omega
that achieves this contour, we have

\displaystyle E_{\overline{\gamma}}-\overline{E}_{\overline{\gamma}}(\omega)\geq\overline{e}_{+}|\overline{\gamma}|.

(3.1)

For any
\beta>0
, we fix
z_{\beta}^{-}
and
z_{\beta}^{+}
as

\displaystyle z_{\beta}^{-}:=\frac{e^{\beta\overline{e}}}{\delta^{d}}\ln\left(1+e^{\beta\overline{e}_{0}-2}\right),\quad z_{\beta}^{+}:=\frac{e^{\beta\overline{e}}}{\delta^{d}}\ln\left(1+e^{\beta\overline{e}_{0}+2}\right)

(3.2)

and the open interval
O_{\beta}:=(z_{\beta}^{-},z_{\beta}^{+})
. Then there is
\beta_{c}>0
such that for
\beta\geq\beta_{c}
there exists
z_{\beta}^{c}\in O_{\beta}
for which a first-order phase transition occurs. More specifically, we have

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z^{+}}(\beta,z_{\beta}^{c})>\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z^{-}}(\beta,z_{\beta}^{c}),

(3.3)

and we can find two Gibbs measures
\mathbb{P}^{+},\mathbb{P}^{-}\in\mathcal{G}(H,z_{\beta}^{c},\beta)
such that

\rho(\mathbb{P}^{+})=z+z\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z^{+}}(z_{\beta}^{c},\beta)\quad\text{and }\quad\rho(\mathbb{P}^{-})=z+z\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z^{-}}(z_{\beta}^{c},\beta).

(3.4)

The proof of this result is given in Section
4
and is inspired by the techniques developed by Pirogov, Sinaï and Zahradník for lattice systems. In addition to the saturation property, the Peierls condition is one of the main ingredients for proving a phase transition. However, this condition is often difficult to establish. We provide an easier way to verify it using what we call dominoes, which, given a contour, are couples of adjacent tiles with different occupation status. For any contour
\gamma
, we define the set of dominoes as

D(\gamma):=\left\{(i,j)\in\overline{\gamma}^{2},\left\lVert i-j\right\rVert_{\infty}=1,\sharp_{i}=1,\sharp_{j}=0\right\}.

(3.5)

We know by

5
, Lemma 5)
that there is

such that for any contour
\gamma

|D(\gamma)|\geq r_{D}|\overline{\gamma}|.

(3.6)

Therefore, if we assume that for any configuration
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
we have
E_{0}(\omega)\geq\overline{E}_{0}(\omega)
and that for any contour
\gamma(\omega)
and any domino
(i,j)\in D(\gamma(\omega))
, we have
E_{j}(\omega)\geq\overline{e}_{\emptyset}>0
, we can derive the following inequality,

E_{\overline{\gamma}}-\overline{E}_{\overline{\gamma}}(\omega)\geq\overline{e}_{\emptyset}r_{D}|\overline{\gamma}|

(3.7)

and therefore, we obtain the following corollary where the Peierls condition is established through a tile-by-tile analysis of the energy. We call this phenomenon the energy from the vacuum.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian which satisfies
1
-
5
such that it has
E_{0}
that satisfies
1
-
5
. Furthermore, we suppose that for any configuration
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
we have
E_{0}(\omega)\geq\overline{E}_{0}(\omega)
and that there exists
\overline{e}_{\emptyset}>0

\gamma(\omega)

The saturation property alone is not sufficient to prove a phase transition. We have not been able to prove that the K-nearest neighbour Strauss interaction satisfies a Peierls condition and simulations seem to suggest that no such phenomenon occurs. Building on these general results on first-order phase transitions, we can derive several consequences for specific models, such as the Quermass interaction and the diluted pairwise interaction. For instance,

Theorem 2

\left|z_{\beta}^{c}-\beta C_{\phi}\right|=O(e^{-c\beta}).

and
P_{n-2p}
and with it independence between the configurations in the bulk

and in the boundary

. As a consequence, we obtain the following

\displaystyle Z_{\widehat{\Lambda}_{n}}^{z,\beta}

\displaystyle\geq\int e^{-\beta(E_{\Lambda_{n+p}\setminus\Lambda_{n-p}}+\overline{E}_{B_{n}})}e^{-\beta(\overline{E}_{P_{n-2p}}+E_{\Lambda_{n-3p}})}\mathbbm{1}_{F_{n}}\Pi_{\widehat{\Lambda}_{n}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega)

\displaystyle\geq Z_{\Lambda_{n-2p}}^{(1)}\int e^{-\beta(E_{\Lambda_{n+p}\setminus\Lambda_{n-p}}+\overline{E}_{B_{n}})}\mathbbm{1}_{F_{n}}\Pi_{\widehat{B_{n}\cup P_{n}}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega).

Since
E_{0}
satisfies
4
we have

\displaystyle E_{\Lambda_{n+p}\setminus\Lambda_{n-p}}\leq c_{t}\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{n+p}\setminus\Lambda_{n-p}}1+N_{T_{i}\oplus B(0,r)}^{2}(\omega).

For
\omega\in F_{n}
and

we have

\displaystyle N_{T_{i}\oplus B(0,R)}(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq\#\{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},T_{i}\cap(T_{j}\oplus B(0,r))\neq\emptyset\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|B(0,r+\sqrt{d}\delta)|}{\delta^{d}}.

Thus for any
\omega\in F_{n}
,

\displaystyle E_{\Lambda_{n+p}\setminus\Lambda_{n-p}}(\omega)\leq c_{t}\left(1+\frac{|B(0,r+\sqrt{d}\delta)|^{2}}{\delta^{2d}}\right)|\Lambda_{n+p}\setminus\Lambda_{n-p}|.

Furthermore, we have for
\omega\in F_{n}

\displaystyle\overline{E}_{B_{n}}(\omega)=\overline{e}_{0}|B_{n}|.

Therefore, there is a constant
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\int e^{-\beta(E_{\Lambda_{n+p}\setminus\Lambda_{n-p}}+\overline{E}_{B_{n}})}\mathbbm{1}_{F_{n}}\Pi_{\widehat{B_{n}\cup P_{n}}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega)\geq(z\delta^{d}e^{-(z\delta^{d}+\beta c)})^{|\Lambda_{n+p}\setminus\Lambda_{n-2p}|}.

As a consequence, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\ln Z_{\widehat{\Lambda}_{n}}^{z,\beta}}{\delta^{d}|\Lambda_{n}|}\geq\frac{|\Lambda_{n+p}\setminus\Lambda_{n-2p}|}{|\Lambda_{n}|}\frac{\ln(z\delta^{d}e^{-(z\delta^{d}+\beta c)})}{\delta^{d}}+\frac{|\Lambda_{n-2p}|}{|\Lambda_{n}|}\frac{\ln Z_{\Lambda_{n-2p}}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(1)}}}}}}{\delta^{d}|\Lambda_{n-2p}|}.

By taking the limit we get
\psi\geq\psi^{(1)}
. Now let us consider the event

\omega\in E_{n}

\displaystyle Z_{\Lambda_{n}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\geq\int e^{-\beta(\overline{E}_{P_{n}}+E_{\Lambda_{n-p}\setminus\Lambda_{n-3p}})}e^{-\beta(\overline{E}_{P_{n-3p}}+E_{\Lambda_{n-4p}})}\mathbbm{1}_{E_{n}}\Pi_{\widehat{\Lambda}_{n}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega)

\displaystyle\int e^{-\beta(\overline{E}_{P_{n}}+E_{\Lambda_{n-p}\setminus\Lambda_{n-3p}})}\mathbbm{1}_{E_{n}}\Pi_{\Lambda_{n}\setminus\Lambda_{n-3p}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega)\geq(z\delta^{d}e^{-(z\delta^{d}+\beta c)})^{|\Lambda_{n}\setminus\Lambda_{n-3p}|}.

(0)_{\Lambda_{n}}=\bigcap_{i\in P_{n}\cup B_{n}}\{N_{T_{i}}(\omega)=0\}

\displaystyle Z_{\Lambda_{n}}^{(0)}

4.2
Polymer development

We have proved that a Gibbs measure can be obtained as limits of empirical fields for each boundary condition. We want to prove that these measures are different. In order to do that, we will compare the partition function of the model with boundary conditions,
Z_{\Lambda}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}
, with the partition function of the model that saturates each tile,
\overline{Z}_{\sharp}^{|\Lambda|}
, where

\overline{Z}_{\sharp}=\int e^{-\beta\overline{E}_{0}(\omega)}\mathds{1}_{\{\sigma(\omega,0)=\sharp\}}\Pi_{T_{0}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega)=\begin{cases}e^{-z\delta^{d}}&\text{if }\sharp=0\\
e^{-\beta\overline{e}_{0}}(1-e^{-z\delta^{d}})&\text{if }\sharp=1.\end{cases}

(4.13)

For this purpose, we analyse the ratio of partition functions for any subset
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
given by

\Phi_{\Lambda}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}:=\frac{Z_{\Lambda}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}}{\overline{Z}_{\sharp}^{|\Lambda|}}.

(4.14)

We define a new boundary for any
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}

\partial^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{-}}}}}\Lambda:=\{i\in\Lambda,\delta d_{2}(i,\Lambda^{c})\leq L\}.

For any configuration
\omega\in\Omega
and any contour
\gamma(\omega)
, the tiles in
\partial^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{-}}}}}\overline{\gamma}(\omega)\cup\partial\overline{\gamma}(\omega)
are saturated. In the following proposition, we prove that this ratio can be written as a polymer development.

Proposition 4.4
.

For any
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
finite and any
\sharp\in\{0,1\}
, we have

\Phi_{\Lambda}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}(\Lambda)}\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma}w_{\gamma}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}},

where
w_{\gamma}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}=\overline{Z}_{\sharp}^{-|\overline{\gamma}|}I_{\gamma}\frac{Z_{\Int_{\sharp^{*}}\gamma}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp^{*})}}}}}}{Z_{\Int_{\sharp^{*}}\gamma}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}}
,
\sharp^{*}:=1-\sharp
and

I_{\gamma}:=\int e^{-\beta\left(E_{\overline{\gamma}\setminus\partial^{-}\overline{\gamma}}+\overline{E}_{\partial^{-}\overline{\gamma}}\right)(\omega)}\mathds{1}_{\{\forall i\in\overline{\gamma},\sigma(\omega,i)=\sigma_{i}\}}\Pi_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega).

The quantity,
w_{\gamma}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}
, is called the weight of the contour
\gamma
.

The proof of this proposition follows the same outline as in
(
5
, Proposition 2)
. Therefore, the analysis of
\Phi_{\Lambda}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}
can be done via cluster expansion methods. For that, we need to show that for
\tau>0
large enough the weights satisfy

\displaystyle w_{\gamma}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}\leq e^{-\tau|\overline{\gamma}|},

(4.15)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial w_{\gamma}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}}{\partial z}\right|\leq|\overline{\gamma}|^{\frac{d}{d-1}}e^{-\tau|\overline{\gamma}|}`.

(4.16)

A weight is said to be
\tau
-stable if it satisfies (
4.15
). In the following lemma, we obtain nice bounds on

if the interaction satisfies a Peierls condition.

Lemma 4.5
.

We assume that for any contours
\gamma

where

For the proof of (

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\beta\overline{e}_{+}|\overline{\gamma}|}\int e^{-\beta\overline{E}_{\overline{\gamma}}(\omega)}\mathds{1}_{\{\forall i\in\overline{\gamma},\sigma(\omega,i)=\sigma_{i}\}}\Pi_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega)

For the second inequality (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial I_{\gamma}}{\partial z}=-\delta^{d}|\overline{\gamma}|I_{\gamma}+\frac{1}{z}\int N_{\widehat{\overline{\gamma}}}(\omega)e^{-\beta\left(E_{\overline{\gamma}\setminus\partial^{-}\overline{\gamma}}+\overline{E}_{\partial^{-}\overline{\gamma}}\right)(\omega)}\mathds{1}_{\{\forall i\in\overline{\gamma},\sigma(\omega,i)=\sigma_{i}\}}\Pi_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega).

By injecting (

) into the second term we have

\displaystyle\int N_{\widehat{\overline{\gamma}}}(\omega)e^{-\beta\left(E_{\overline{\gamma}\setminus\partial^{-}\overline{\gamma}}+\overline{E}_{\partial^{-}\overline{\gamma}}\right)(\omega)}

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\{\forall i\in\overline{\gamma},\sigma(\omega,i)=\sigma_{i}\}}\Pi_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\beta\overline{e}_{+}|\overline{\gamma}|}\int N_{\widehat{\overline{\gamma}}}(\omega)e^{-\beta\overline{E}_{\overline{\gamma}}(\omega)}\mathds{1}_{\{\forall i\in\overline{\gamma},\sigma(\omega,i)=\sigma_{i}\}}\Pi_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\beta\overline{e}_{+}|\overline{\gamma}|}\sum_{i\in\overline{\gamma}_{1}}\int N_{T_{i}}(\omega)e^{-\beta\overline{E}_{\overline{\gamma}}(\omega)}\mathds{1}_{\{\forall i\in\overline{\gamma},\sigma(\omega,i)=\sigma_{i}\}}\Pi_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\beta\overline{e}_{+}|\overline{\gamma}|}\overline{Z}_{0}^{|\overline{\gamma}_{0}|}\overline{Z}_{1}^{|\overline{\gamma}_{1}|}\sum_{i\in\overline{\gamma}_{1}}\frac{1}{\overline{Z}_{1}}\int N_{T_{i}}(\omega)e^{-\beta\overline{e}_{0}}\mathds{1}_{\{\forall i\in\overline{\gamma},\sigma(\omega,i)=1\}}\Pi_{T_{i}}^{z}(\mathrm{d}\omega)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{z\delta^{d}}{1-e^{-z\delta^{d}}}|\overline{\gamma}_{1}|\overline{Z}_{0}^{|\overline{\gamma}_{0}|}\overline{Z}_{1}^{|\overline{\gamma}_{1}|}e^{-\beta\overline{e}_{+}|\overline{\gamma}|}.

Using this previous upper bound and (
4.18
), we obtain (
4.19
).
∎

Under the assumption of a Peierls bound and according to Lemma

(4.21)

4.3
Truncated pressures and weights

In general, the ratio of partition functions for different boundary conditions might grow too fast with the volume of
\Lambda
and therefore the weights of some contours might not satisfy (
4.15
) and (
4.16

\displaystyle\kappa(s)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }s\leq\frac{\beta\overline{e}_{+}}{8},\\

\widehat{Z}_{\Lambda}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}:=\overline{Z}_{\sharp}^{|\Lambda|}\widehat{\Phi}_{\Lambda}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}},\quad\text{ where }\quad\widehat{\Phi}_{\Lambda}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}}(\Lambda)}\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\widehat{w}_{\gamma}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\sharp)}}}}},

(4.33)

Equivalently, the truncated pressures can also be obtained as the limit of the truncated pressures of rank
