TRPM4 expression predicts prognosis and modulates the immune microenvironment in pancreatic adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yuanxue Qin, Haopeng Du, Zixiang Wu, Guosheng Wang, Xiaobing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTRPM4, a nonselective cation channel, has been implicated in a variety of tumorigenic processes and is closely linked to insulin secretion. However, its functions and underlying mechanisms in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PAAD) progress remain elusive, and the potential as PAAD biomarker and therapeutic target remains unexplored. This study was meticulously crafted to experimentally validate the expression of TRPM4 in PAAD and to undertake a thorough examination of its effects on expression patterns, prognostic significance, mutational landscapes, methylation statuses, protein-protein interaction networks, as well as the correlation between TRPM4 expression and immune infiltration in PAAD. Our analytical approach included KEGG pathway analysis and GO enrichment analysis to identify signaling pathways associated with TRPM4. Our findings reveal a significant overexpression of TRPM4 in PAAD tissues, with heightened expression levels tightly correlated with unfavorable clinical outcomes. A comprehensive analysis utilizing the cBioPortal database unveiled a high mutation rate of TRPM4 in pancreatic cancer, predominantly attributed to gene amplification events. Examination of PPI networks and co-expression gene profiles highlighted notable interactions between TRPM4 and crucial ion channel proteins, including voltage-gated sodium channels and calcium release-activated calcium channels, shedding light on its potential role in PAAD progression. Functional enrichment analysis implicated TRPM4 in a range of biological processes, encompassing immune responses, neural signaling, and cellular electrophysiology, with particular emphasis on endocrine system regulation, especially pancreatic hormone secretion. Furthermore, TRPM4 expression exhibited striking associations with infiltration patterns of diverse immune cell populations in PAAD, particularly demonstrating strong correlations with Th17 cells and iTregs, hinting at its potential regulatory role in the tumor immune microenvironment. TRPM4 demonstrates heightened expression in pancreatic cancer tissues relative to normal tissues. It is also linked to the prognosis of pancreatic cancer patients, the intratumoral immune microenvironment, and the dissemination of the disease. These findings imply a potential oncogenic role for TRPM4. Introduction

Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) stands as one of the most prevalent forms of cancer, with a mortality rate that nearly mirrors its incidence rate1. In China, both the incidence and mortality rates of PAAD exceed those in the United States, ranking sixth among all cancer types domestically2. This highlights the substantial global and domestic burden imposed by PAAD. As China’s population ages, socioeconomic progress advances, and Westernized lifestyles become more prevalent, the incidence of PAAD is forecasted to rise in the coming years, potentially emerging as the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths by 20303. Previous research has pinpointed numerous factors that influence PAAD risk, including smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, chronic pancreatitis, obesity, unhealthy dietary patterns, and inherited genetic mutations. These factors may act independently or synergistically to increase an individual’s susceptibility to PAAD4. Treatment for PAAD is personalized and encompasses surgical resection, neoadjuvant therapy, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, interventional therapy, supportive care, and integrative medicine5. Despite advancements in PAAD treatment, challenges persist in early diagnosis, chemotherapy resistance, metastasis, and prognosis. Given the severity of PAAD, there is an urgent need to deepen our understanding of its molecular biological mechanisms and explore innovative therapeutic strategies to enhance patient outcomes and prognosis.

The Transient Receptor Potential (TRP) family comprises a class of non-selective cation channels that play pivotal roles in a wide array of physiological processes. A growing body of research has increasingly demonstrated that TRP channels can disrupt key cancer signaling pathways through dysfunctional Ca2+ signaling, ultimately affecting cancer cell proliferation, apoptosis, gene transcription, and angiogenesis6. Transient Receptor Potential Melastatin 4 (TRPM4) functions as a calcium-activated, nonspecific cation channel that is triggered by elevations in intracellular Ca2+ and ATP depletion. Although TRPM4 does not directly conduct Ca2+ ions, it indirectly modulates intracellular Ca2+ levels by regulating the membrane potential. Upon activation, TRPM4 facilitates Na+ influx, which depolarizes the membrane potential and reduces the driving force for Ca2+ influx7. As such, TRPM4 exhibits a diverse range of functions and participates in numerous vital physiological activities. These include the regulation of pancreatic β-cells in response to glucose uptake, thereby promoting insulin secretion8, and involvement in Ca2+-dependent immune responses in T-lymphocytes and dendritic cells9. Studies have indicated that variations in TRPM4 gene expression are closely linked to the pathophysiological mechanisms of various cancers, including prostate cancer10, breast cancer11, colorectal cancer12, and endometrial cancer13, among others. Currently, while research on TRPM4 and PAAD is relatively limited, several studies suggest a strong connection between TRPM4 and pancreatic secretion14–16.

Given the pivotal role of TRPM4 in diverse cellular physiological processes and its implication in the progression of multiple oncogenic conditions, TRPM4 is deemed to possess substantial research potential within the realm of PAAD. The aim of this study is to provide a theoretical basis for the early detection and improved prognostic assessment of PAAD by combining bioinformatic analysis with cellular experimentation.

Materials and methods

Pan‑oncogene expression

TIMER2.0 (http://timer.cistrome.org/) represents a comprehensive resource dedicated to the systematic analysis of immune infiltrates across diverse cancer types. We employed the TIMER2.0 web interface, specifying “TRPM4” as our query, to explore the differential expression of TRPM4 between tumor tissues and their adjacent normal counterparts across 32 distinct cancer types, utilizing data sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA).

GEPIA2 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index) is a web-based online tool tailored for the analysis of RNA sequencing expression data, encompassing 9,736 tumor samples and 8,587 normal samples. This tool offers a range of functionalities, including differential expression analysis, correlation analysis, and patient survival analysis. Data acquisition was initially carried out from the TCGA and GTEx databases using GEPIA2. Subsequent analysis of TCGA and GTEx data enabled the investigation of TRPM4 expression across a pan-cancer spectrum and specifically within digestive tumors.

Differential expression in PAAD

UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu) is a comprehensive, interactive, web-based platform dedicated to the analysis of cancer-omics data, which includes clinical data for 31 different cancer types. In this study, TRPM4 expression levels were evaluated in both PAAD and normal samples, as well as in tumor models stratified by tumor stages. To complement this analysis, HPA (The Human Protein Atlas, https://www.proteinatlas.org/) was utilized to compare TRPM4 protein expression between normal pancreatic tissue and PAAD tissue (Antibody ID: HPA041169). Furthermore, GEPIA2 was employed again to assess the differential expression of TRPM4 mRNA across various stages of PAAD.

Cell culture

The human PAAD cell line PANC1 and the human normal pancreatic cell line HPDEM were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). PANC1 cells were cultivated in DMEM complete medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% double-antibiotics. Likewise, HPDEM cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% double-antibiotics. Subsequently, both PANC1 and HPDEM cells were incubated at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Western blot (WB)

PANC1 and HPDEM cells were collected and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer for total protein extraction. The concentration of the extracted total protein was determined using a BCA kit. Equal amounts of protein (80 µg per well) were loaded onto 7.5%–10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels (SDS-PAGE), separated by electrophoresis, and subsequently transferred onto nitrocellulose (NC) membranes. The pre-cut membranes were blocked with a rapid blocking solution (GenScript, USA) for 15 min at room temperature. Thereafter, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes with PBST (phosphate-buffered saline containing Tween-20), the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Lastly, the membranes were scanned and analyzed using an ODYSSEY imaging system (LI-COR, USA). The obtained data was also normalized. The relative expression levels of TRPM4 were calculated using the standard approach of dividing the TRPM4 band intensity by the corresponding GAPDH band intensity for each sample. This ratio was then normalized to the control group (set as 1) and the results are presented as fold changes relative to the control group. Subsequently, GraphPad Prism 9.0 was used to analyze the data and produce statistical graphs.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA extractions from cultured cells were conducted utilizing TRIzolTM Reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Subsequent reverse transcription of the RNA samples followed the manufacturer’s instruction (TOYOBO, ReverTraAce® qPCR RT Kit, Shanghai, China). QRT-PCR assays were executed with SYBR Green Master on the ABI 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR platform (Applied Biosystems, USA). Relative expression levels were determined using the 2-ΔΔCT approach. QRT-PCR primer sequences are as follows: GAPDH-Forward: 5-CAT GAG AAG TAT GAC AAC AGC CT-3; Reverse: 5-AGT CCT TCC ACG ATA CCA AAGT-3. TRPM4-Forward: 5-GCA CGA CGT TCA TAG TTG ACT-3; Reverse: 5-CTT CTC CGT GGT GTG TGC AT-3.

Survival prognosis analysis

The Kaplan-Meier Plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) is a tool capable of assessing the association between mRNA expression and survival across a large number of PAAD samples. It utilizes databases such as GEO, EGA, and TCGA. A significant correlation has been identified between TRPM4 mRNA expression and survival in PAAD patients.

The GEPIA2 tool was exclusively employed for conducting patient survival analyses utilizing the TCGA database. The TRPM4 gene was entered into the “survival analysis” module, with specific cancer types selected for the examination of overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). The cohorts were divided into high- and low-expression groups based on a 50% expression threshold.

Genetic alteration analysis

The cBioPortal website (https://www.cbioportal.org/) was utilized to analyze genetic alterations. This comprehensive database was employed to perform a pan-cancer mutation frequency analysis specifically focused on the TRPM4 gene.

The GSCA platform (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/GSCA/#/) comprises data on 33 different cancer types, sourced from the TCGA and GDSC databases. Additionally, the “mutations” module within GSCA provided information on the methylation status and copy number variations of the TRPM4 gene in PAAD. This module also assessed survival differences between cancer patients carrying TRPM4 mutant versus wild-type signatures, as well as variations in overall survival between groups with higher versus lower methylation levels.

The PPI network of TRPM4 and co-expressed genes in PAAD

In STRING (https://www.string-db.org/), both direct and indirect protein interactions are integrated. We utilized STRING to identify proteins that interact with TRPM4. These interactions were subsequently analyzed using Cytoscape software (Version 3.9.1: https://cytoscape.org) and visualized in the form of a circle graph, where the degree of each node is represented by the intensity of green coloration. Furthermore, TIMER2.0 was employed to investigate the correlation between TRPM4 and 20 indicators of TRPM4-binding across various cancer types. For this purpose, the TRPM4 gene and a list of genes corresponding to these 20 indicators were input into the “Gene-Corr” module.

The LinkedOmics database (https://linkedomics.org/login.php) represents a comprehensive repository of global proteomics data across multiple omics platforms, often utilized for integrating and comparing intra-tumor and inter-tumor multi-omics data. Leveraging this resource, we conducted a query to identify genes co-expressed with TRPM4 within the RNA-seq data of PAAD patients archived in the TCGA database. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated, and volcano plots illustrating the co-expressed genes were generated through the LinkedOmics platform. Furthermore, heat maps were plotted for the top 50 genes exhibiting significant positive and negative correlations with TRPM4 in PAAD.

The results were visualized by analyzing the unique and common components between the two datasets, with a Wayne diagram generated using the “ggplot2” and “VennDiagram” R packages. Overlapping genes between the TRPM4 co-expressed genes and interacting proteins were identified. Simultaneously, RNAseq data processed by the STAR algorithm from the TCGA-PAAD project were obtained from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). TPM format data from normal samples were extracted, filtered, and subsequently analyzed using the “ggplot2” R package to illustrate the correlation between TRPM4 and the overlapping genes. This correlation was further confirmed using the TIMER2.0 database (http://timer.cistrome.org/).

Gene enrichment analysis

The RNA-seq data, processed using STAR from the PAAD project available in the TCGA database, were extracted in TPM format, with normal samples filtered out. Initially, DESeq2 was utilized to determine Differential Expression Genes (DEGs) between the TRPM4 high and low expression groups in PAAD, applying a significance threshold of P-adj < 0.05 and a log |Fold Change (FC)| > 1. Subsequently, the results of the differential analysis were visualized as volcano plots using the “ggplot2” package, adhering to the same thresholds. The identified DEGs were further annotated and characterized through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses using the Metascape databases (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1).

Immune infiltration analysis

We employed the TIMER2.0 platform (http://timer.cistrome.org/) to estimate the abundance of immune infiltrates using multiple immune deconvolution approaches. To investigate the correlation between TRPM4 expression and immune infiltrates across various cancer types, we integrated the TRPM4 gene into the “Genes” module within the “Immune” section. Immune cell infiltration was assessed using a standardized scale, including CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and others.

We utilized the TISIDB dataset (http://147.8.185.80/TISIDB/) to investigate the correlations between tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) abundance and NUDCD1 expression through the “lymphocyte” module. Furthermore, the relationship between immune cell infiltration in PAAD and the Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA, a non-parametric, unsupervised bioinformatics algorithm that is primarily used to convert gene-level expression data into gene-set-level enrichment scores, revealing differences in sample activity for specific biological functions) enrichment score of TRPM4 was assessed using the “Immune” module of the GSCA database (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/GSCA/#/).

Statistical analysis

Biological triplicate experimental data were depicted with error bars representing mean ± SD. A two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was employed for intergroup comparisons. Statistical significance was ascertained using p-values < 0.05. Significance levels were annotated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

mRNA expression of TRPM4 in human pan‑cancer

Changes in TRPM4 gene expression are closely related to the pathophysiological mechanisms of many cancers, so we assessed TRPM4 mRNA expression using data extracted from the TCGA and GTEx databases to compare tumour tissue with normal tissue. To investigate the discrepancies in TRPM4 expression between tumors and adjacent normal tissues, we conducted an analysis of TRPM4 mRNA levels across the entire spectrum of TCGA tumors using the TIMER2.0 database (Fig. 1A). Our findings reveal distinct expression patterns of TRPM4 mRNA across BLCA, BRCA, CHOL, COAD, ESCA, HNSC, KICH, LIHC, PRAD, READ, SKCM, STAD, THCA, and UCEC. Additionally, the GEPIA2 database was leveraged to examine TRPM4 expression in a pan-cancer analysis, integrating the GTEx dataset with normal samples to enhance the sample size of the normal tissue cohort. Compared with normal tissue, elevated TRPM4 expression levels were observed in DLBL, PAAD, PRAD and THYM (Fig. 1B). To corroborate these findings, a supplementary analysis of TRPM4 expression in digestive tumors was performed using the GEPIA2 database. The results underscored a significant upregulation of TRPM4 mRNA expression in PAAD (Fig. 1C).

Proteins represent the fundamental molecular components directly associated with diseases, and alterations in their expression levels are closely tied to pathogenesis, pharmacological responses, and toxicity. By harnessing the UALCAN database, we performed an analysis of TRPM4 protein expression in PAAD versus normal tissues (Fig. 2A), which unveiled a pronounced upregulation of TRPM4 in PAAD compared to healthy pancreatic tissues. Utilizing datasets from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA), immunohistochemical evaluations were conducted to further explore the differential expression of TRPM4 between tumorigenic and non-tumorigenic tissues, confirming a substantial increase in TRPM4 expression within PAAD tissues (Fig. 2B). Western blot analysis was applied to assess TRPM4 protein expression (Fig. 2C), demonstrating a significant elevation in the PAAD cell line PANC-1 relative to normal cells (Original blots are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, qRT-PCR was employed to quantify mRNA expression levels of TRPM4 in PANC-1 cells and normal pancreatic cells (Fig. 2D), with the results indicating markedly higher mRNA levels of TRPM4 in the PAAD cell line.

The expression of TRPM4 was examined in relation to the pathological stage of PAAD in patients (Fig. 2E). Our findings revealed a statistically significant difference in TRPM4 mRNA expression across various stages, suggesting a potential involvement of TRPM4 in the progression of cancer.

High levels of TRPM4 predict poor clinical outcomes in PAAD

Based on the mRNA expression levels of TRPM4, patients were divided into high and low expression groups to investigate the association between TRPM4 expression and prognosis in PAAD. Our findings indicated that high TRPM4 expression was associated with reduced overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) in PAAD, as evidenced by the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database (Fig. 3A-B). To further validate these findings, we conducted additional survival analysis using the GEPIA2 database, which relies on the TCGA dataset (Fig. 3C-D). These results reaffirmed that high mRNA expression of TRPM4 in PAAD predicts a poor disease-free survival (DFS) outcome. Collectively, our data imply that TRPM4 expression is strongly correlated with a poor prognosis in PAAD and may represent a potential prognostic marker.

Mutation feature of TRPM4 in PAAD

To illuminate the mutational landscape and biological role of TRPM4 in tumor progression, we conducted a pan-cancer analysis of TRPM4 genetic alterations using the cBioPortal database. Our results revealed that the mutation frequency of the TRPM4 gene in PAAD was the fourth highest (reaching 10.51%), with “Amplification” being the predominant alteration (Fig. 4A). Utilizing the GSCA database, we observed a mild correlation between TRPM4 mRNA expression and copy number variation in PAAD, yet no significant survival disparity was noted between copy number variant mutants and wild-type cases (Fig. 4B-C). Aberrant DNA methylation mechanisms are implicated in various diseases, and cancer cells are characterized by altered DNA methylation patterns, including genomic hypomethylation and site-specific hypermethylation. Furthermore, we leveraged the GSCA database to explore DNA methylation of TRPM4 in PAAD. Our findings unveiled a negative correlation between TRPM4 DNA methylation and its mRNA expression in PAAD, with no notable difference in overall survival between higher and lower methylation groups (Fig. 4D-E).

To delve deeper into the molecular mechanism of TRPM4, we conducted a protein-protein interaction network analysis to screen for TRPM4-binding proteins. Following this, we analyzed the expression correlation between TRPM4 and these 20 PPI members (Fig. 5B). Our findings revealed that Calcium Release-Activated Calcium Channel Protein 3 (ORAI3) and Epithelial Sodium Channel Delta Subunit (SCNN1D) exhibited a notable positive correlation with TRPM4 in PAAD, whereas Sodium Voltage-Gated Channel Beta Subunit 3 (SCN3B), Calcium Release-Activated Calcium Channel Protein 2 (ORAI2), and Epithelial Sodium Channel Beta Subunit (SCNN1B) displayed significant negative correlations with TRPM4.

Screening of TRPM4 differential gene in PAAD and its enrichment analysis

In PAAD patients, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were compared between the TRPM4 high- and low-expression groups using the “ggplot2” R package (Fig. 6A). This comparison yielded a total of 942 DEGs, comprising 475 up-regulated and 467 down-regulated genes. To gain insights into the biological functions of these DEGs, we conducted GO/KEGG analysis via the Metascape database. Enrichment analysis revealed that the GO terms were enriched in molecular functions such as antigen binding, gated channel activity, and hormonal activity (Fig. 6B)17,18. The primary biological processes identified included adaptive immune response, regulation of membrane potential, and regulation of chemical synaptic transmission (Fig. 6C), while the cellular components were associated with T-cell receptor complexes, immunoglobulin complexes, and synaptic membranes (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, KEGG analysis indicated that the DEGs were significantly enriched in signaling pathways, namely neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, pancreatic secretion, and fat digestion and absorption (Fig. 6E).

To explore this, we investigated the association between TRPM4 expression in tumors and immune cell infiltration. Initially, we examined the correlation between TRPM4 mRNA expression in PAAD and the infiltration of six immune cell types using the TIMER2.0 database (Fig. 7A). Our results revealed a significant negative correlation between TRPM4 mRNA expression levels and the infiltration levels of macrophages and dendritic cells.

Discussion

PAAD is notorious for its high malignancy, rapid progression, challenging early diagnosis, and exceedingly poor prognosis. Despite the advent of novel therapeutic strategies, such as targeted therapies, the survival rate of PAAD has seen relatively little improvement. One of the primary challenges lies in the advanced stage at which patients are often diagnosed19. The TRP channel family shares a molecular structure similar to that of voltage-gated K+ channels, exhibiting cation selectivity and distinct activation mechanisms. It is now well-established that TRPM220, TRPM721, TRPM822, and TRPV623 are upregulated in PAAD tissues or cell lines, and they mediate signaling by regulating intracellular concentrations of Ca2+, Mg2+, and other ions, thereby acting as pro-oncogenes in PAAD. Although TRPM4 does not directly conduct Ca2+, it indirectly regulates intracellular Ca2+ levels by modulating Na+. For instance, in colorectal cancer, TRPM4 activates key molecules downstream of intracellular calcium signaling via calpain and inhibits the FAK/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, exerting an inhibitory effect on colorectal tumor cells12. In addition, multiple existing reports have demonstrated that TRPM4 channels exhibit tissue-specific expression in various types of tumors. It is particularly noteworthy that, despite the limited number of studies on the role and mechanism of TRPM4 in PAAD, this channel plays a crucial role in the pancreas, especially in regulating insulin secretion24 and intracellular Ca2+ signaling25. Furthermore, TRPM4 may also be implicated in glucagon secretion26.

In this study, we initially performed a bioinformatics analysis of the TCGA database and observed marked differences in TRPM4 gene mRNA expression levels between cancerous and paracancerous tissues across various tumor types within the TCGA samples. Further differential expression analysis utilizing the GEPIA2 database revealed distinct TRPM4 mRNA expression patterns in multiple tumors compared to normal tissues. Specifically, we examined TRPM4 expression in digestive tumors, including PAAD, and found that TRPM4 mRNA expression was upregulated in PAAD. To further substantiate this finding, we utilized the UALCAN database to confirm that TRPM4 protein expression was significantly elevated in PAAD compared to normal pancreatic tissues. And this high expression can lead to a worse clinical prognosis of patients. Subsequently, we employed the GEPIA2 database to analyze the correlation between TRPM4 expression levels and clinicopathological factors in PAAD. The results indicated notable differences in TRPM4 mRNA expression levels across different stages, suggesting a potentially pivotal role for TRPM4 in tumor progression, and the development of the tumor needs further investigation.

To assess the prognostic implications of TRPM4 in PAAD, we performed a survival analysis using the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database. Our findings indicated that PAAD patients with lower TRPM4 expression experienced better OS and RFS compared to those in the high expression group. Additionally, survival analysis conducted through the GEPIA2 database confirmed that increased TRPM4 mRNA expression was associated with poorer DFS. In conclusion, we hypothesize that high TRPM4 expression is associated with an elevated risk of mortality and reduced survival in PAAD patients, thereby serving as an unfavorable prognostic marker.

Considering that mutations often act as crucial drivers of carcinogenesis within epigenetic pathways, we employed the cBioPortal database to investigate genetic alterations in TRPM4 across various cancer types. Our findings reveal that TRPM4 exhibits the fourth highest mutation rate in PAAD, reaching 10.51%, predominantly in the form of gene “amplification”. TRPM4 is a Ca²⁺-activated monovalent cation-selective channel. Its amplification can lead to the overexpression of proteins and may promote the progression of PAAD by disrupting calcium signalling, opening up a promising avenue for future research and therapeutic strategies.

To delve deeper into the underlying mechanism by which TRPM4 influences PAAD progression, we conducted an analysis of PPI networks and co-expressed gene sets. Our findings supported the interaction of TRPM4 with voltage-gated sodium channel proteins and calcium store-operated calcium channel proteins in PAAD. This suggests that TRPM4 may be involved in a complex signalling network that promotes cell proliferation and survival and activates multiple pro-carcinogenic signalling pathways, such as the JNK-MAPK pathway and the calcium signalling pathway. These pathways promote tumour cell proliferation, inhibit apoptosis and enhance invasive and metastatic abilities through ion channel interactions. Although aberrant TRPM4 expression may contribute to the initiation and progression of PAAD, the exact mechanism remains to be elucidated. The results of DEG enrichment analysis for TRPM4 in PAAD suggest a potential involvement in immune responses, neural signaling, and cellular electrophysiological properties, possibly modulating the endocrine system, particularly hormone secretion related to the pancreas.

Tumor immune infiltration is a fundamental characteristic of most malignant tumors, closely linked to tumorigenesis, diagnosis, progression, and metastasis. TRPM4 has been shown to play a role in Ca2+-dependent immune responses in T lymphocytes and dendritic cells9, with previous enrichment analyses suggesting its potential involvement in the immune response to PAAD27. Therefore, our study focused on exploring the correlation between TRPM4 expression in tumors and immune cell infiltration, utilizing multiple public databases. Notably, TRPM4 demonstrated significant correlations with the infiltration of various immune cell types in PAAD, particularly showing a positive association with Th17 cells and a negative association with iTreg cells. These findings imply that TRPM4 plays a critical role in the immune microenvironment of PAAD, potentially influencing tumor progression and patient prognosis. While our study does not directly test therapeutic interventions, the identification of TRPM4 as a key oncogenic driver and prognostic marker in PAAD nominates it as a potential therapeutic target, warranting future investigation into TRPM4 inhibitors in combination with existing regimens.

In the present study, we undertook a comprehensive analysis of multiple aspects of TRPM4 in PAAD for the first time, including its expression pattern, survival prognosis, epigenetic modifications, interaction network, enrichment analysis, and immunoassay, among other facets. This endeavor is expected to unveil a novel prognostic biomarker for PAAD and provide a theoretical framework and preliminary evidence for the development of anti-tumor therapeutic strategies targeting TRPM4. Nevertheless, the current study acknowledges certain limitations. On the one hand, while our study demonstrates a significant upregulation of TRPM4 in PAAD and its correlation with aggressive clinicopathological features, it is important to consider that this overexpression could be a part of broader transcriptional dysregulation in cancer cells. Future studies employing functional gain-of-function and loss-of-function experiments are necessary to definitively establish TRPM4’s direct oncogenic role in pancreatic carcinogenesis. On the other hand, although we verified the expression level of TRPM4 using qRT-PCR and Western Blot, systematic experiments to elucidate the oncogenic mechanism of TRPM4 in PAAD have yet to be conducted. Therefore, future research must delve further into the specific role of TRPM4 in PAAD progression through both in vitro and in vivo experiments, thereby furnishing a more solid scientific basis for clinical treatment.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that TRPM4 expression is significantly upregulated in tumors and exhibits a strong correlation with clinical prognosis, immune microenvironment, and the pathogenesis of PAAD. Moreover, TRPM4 may interact with other ion channels, including voltage-gated sodium channels and calcium release-activated calcium channels, collectively modulating the physiological characteristics of PAAD cells. These findings provide a fundamental basis and robust support for the potential utilization of TRPM4 as a therapeutic and prognostic biomarker in cancers.
