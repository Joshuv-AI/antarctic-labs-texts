Oncofertility outcomes after fertility-sparing surgery for borderline ovarian tumors: results of a large retrospective study
Authors: Rongmin Wang, Yinping Xiao, Taniya Fernando, Yanli Chen, Xiaoyong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFertility-sparing surgery (FSS) is a key therapeutic strategy for borderline ovarian tumors (BOTs) in young women. However, the optimal surgical approaches and the prognostic significance of clinicopathological features remain a subject of debate. This study aimed to evaluate the oncological safety and reproductive outcomes following FSS in a large cohort of young BOT patients.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 533 patients (aged 20–40 years) diagnosed with BOTs who underwent FSS at a single academic medical center between 2010 and 2021. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. To identify independent prognostic factors for recurrence, variable selection was performed using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression, followed by multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis with backward elimination.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 61.90 months (range: 1.03-176.53months), 85 (15.95%) patients experienced recurrence, comprising 77 (14.45%) borderline recurrences and 8 (1.50%) invasive transformation. The 5-year and 10-year disease-free survival rates were 82.0% and 75.9%, respectively. Multivariable analysis identified bilateral ovarian lesions (HR 2.07, p = 0.017), exophytic growth (HR 3.66, p < 0.001), intraepithelial carcinoma (HR 4.23, p = 0.004), and FIGO stage III (HR 2.23, p = 0.014) as independent prognostic risk factors for recurrence. Notably, peritoneal biopsy was associated with a significantly lower recurrence risk (HR 0.42, p = 0.004). Histological stratification revealed that for serous BOTs, exophytic growth (HR 4.05, p < 0.001), cystectomy (HR 3.03, p = 0.008), FIGO stage III (HR 2.46, p = 0.011) were risk factors, while peritoneal biopsy (HR 0.44, p = 0.018) remained protective. For MBOT, intraoperative chromotubation was identified as a risk factor (HR 8.62, p < 0.001). Among 176 patients attempting conception, the live birth rate was 70.45%. In those utilizing assisted reproductive technology (n = 31), the pregnancy and live birth rates were 77.42% and 48.39%, respectively.ConclusionsFSS is oncologically safe and offers favorable reproductive outcomes for young women with BOTs. Introduction

Borderline ovarian tumors (BOTs) are a type of low malignant potential tumor exhibiting epithelial proliferation and cytologic atypia without destructive stromal invasion. This unique pathology underpins their clinically indolent course and good prognosis. BOTs are not an obligate precancerous lesion. BOTs are commonly diagnosed at a younger age, with approximately one-third of cases diagnosed in women under 40 years old [1]. Consequently, preserving the childbearing potential of young patients is increasingly prioritized in the management of BOTs, making fertility-sparing surgery (FSS) a key therapeutic option for these patients [2].

While FSS is widely adopted, it is associated with a higher risk of recurrence compared to radical surgery, though most of these recurrences are in the borderline form [2, 3]. Due to the lack of high-level evidence, the optimal techniques for FSS are hampered by significant controversies centering on two critical aspects: the extent of ovarian tissue conservation and the necessity for comprehensive surgical staging. Regarding the ovarian surgical regimen, some studies suggest that cystectomy, as opposed to oophorectomy, increases the risk of recurrence [4–6], but also increase the subsequent fertility rate [7]. In contrast, other reports indicate that the type of ovarian surgery does not influence either recurrence or fertility outcomes [8]. A parallel controversy exists concerning the necessity for comprehensive surgical staging surgery, although it is recommended for BOTs [9]. Data from a nationwide survey shows that only 12%-41% of patients undergo complete staging procedures [10]. Omission of stage procedures may bring in the underestimate of stage and extraovarian lesion. Surgical staging procedures are reported to contribute to the prognosis of serous BOT and the recurrence risk escalation with each omitted staging step [11]. Conversely, other studies have found that staging procedures do not confer a significant survival benefit for women with BOTs [12].

These unresolved controversies in surgical management can lead to either undertreatment or overtreatment in young patients. To better tailor individualized strategies, this study was designed to identify the surgical and clinicopathological factors associated with recurrence, and to evaluate reproductive outcomes in a substantial, single-institution cohort of young patients exclusively managed for BOTs.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective cohort study conducted at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University included young women who underwent fertility-sparing surgical treatment from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2021, with pathologically confirmed primary borderline ovarian tumor. Patients were identified through a review of hospital’s medical records. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed BOT; (2) availability of complete medical records; (3) age between 20 and 40 years; and (4) receipt of initial FSS. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) concomitant hysterectomy or bilateral oophorectomy; (2) prior history of other malignancies; (3) absence of ascertainable outcome data from medical records or telephone interviews; (4) serous BOT (SBOT) with invasive implants; and (5) mucinous BOT (MBOT) with peritoneal pseudomyxoma or malignant mural nodules (including anaplastic carcinoma, sarcoma).

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the hospital (2023 − 153). For the follow-up telephone interviews conducted to update outcomes, informed consent was obtained from the participants. And the study adhered to the ethical standards of the institution. The patient selection is summarized in Fig. 1.

Surgical procedures and definitions

FSS was defined as preservation of the uterus and at least a portion of one ovary and included unilateral ovarian cystectomy (UOC), unilateral adnexectomy (UA), unilateral adnexectomy plus contralateral cystectomy (UAC), and bilateral ovarian cystectomy (BOC). Radical surgery was defined as bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy with or without hysterectomy.

Surgical staging and intraoperative management

Disease stage was determined according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) 2014 system based on the operative descriptions and pathology reports, with specific attention to the presence of exophytic growth and occurrences of intraoperative tumor rupture. Complete surgical staging required pelvic washings, omental biopsy, peritoneal biopsies, or cytoreductive surgery to achieve a complete resection of macroscopic disease. Omission of any procedures in complete surgical staging was considered as incomplete surgical staging. Lymph node dissection was not mandatory and was not regarded as a necessary part of staging. However, it was performed at the discretion of the gynecologic surgeon in specific scenarios, such as the presence of micropapillary growth patterns or macroscopically suspicious lymph nodes. A biopsy of the contralateral ovary was occasionally performed and documented accordingly. If the appendix was macroscopically normal upon intraoperative inspection and evaluation, it could be preserved. For patients who underwent restaging surgery, the type of ovarian surgery and the quality of staging was documented after both the initial and restage procedures.

Intraoperative chromotubation was selectively performed in patients with a history of infertility to assess tubal patency. This procedure was typically conducted under the presumptive diagnosis of benign adnexal pathology, prior to the suspicion of BOTs. Briefly, a diluted methylene blue solution was injected transcervically, and tubal spillage was monitored under direct visualization.

Postoperative management

Following surgery, adjuvant chemotherapy was not administered routinely. In accordance with the ESGO-ESMO (European Society of Gynecological Oncology – European Society for Medical Oncology) consensus [13] and NCCN guidelines [9], adjuvant chemotherapy is not recommended for BOTs, irrespective of FIGO stage, primarily due to the inherent chemo-resistance associated with the low proliferative activity of these tumors. However, as the study spans over a decade, a small subset of patients received postoperative chemotherapy. These decisions were made by attending gynecological oncologists based on specific high-risk features identified at the time.

Histology of borderline tumor

The histological subtype of BOTs included SBOT, MBOT, endometrioid BOT (EBOT), seromucinous BOT (SMBOT), clear cell BOT and mixed BOT (one case was identified as SMBOT combined with EBOT). Histological features comprising of microinvasion, micropapillary growth patterns, intraepithelial carcinoma (IEC), and implants were recorded. Implants, resembling extraovarian lesions, were subclassified into invasive and non-invasive implants. In accordance with the 2014 classification, invasive implants, currently categorized as low-grade serous carcinomas were excluded from this study, representing a distinct clinical entity with different management protocols. Exophytic growth was defined based on the macroscopic evaluation of surgical specimens. Tumors were categorized as exophytic if they exhibited prominent external projections or nodules originating from the ovarian surface. All findings were validated and documented in official pathology reports.

Data collection

Demographic features, disease characteristics and follow-up records were derived from medical records and were comprehensively reviewed. Data collection was performed in collaboration by two trained researchers using a standardized, pre-piloted data extraction form. Any discrepancies were resolved by a third senior investigator. Patient follow-up visit included a clinical examination, tumor markers (according to the subtype), and an ultrasound scan, every 3 months during the first year following the procedure, then every 6 months for 2 years and then yearly thereafter. Disease recurrence defined as either borderline or invasive was confirmed histologically following re-operation. For patients with incomplete clinical follow-up, oncological and fertility outcomes were ascertained via structured telephone interview to minimize loss to follow-up. The pregnancy rate was defined as the ratio of patients who achieved pregnancy to those desiring fertility. Similarly, the live birth rate was defined as the ratio of patients who achieved a live birth to those desiring fertility.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software (version 23.0; IBM SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R (version 4.5.2). Descriptive statistics were used to analyze patient characteristics. Normally distributed data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). Values are presented as the median and range for data that were not normally distributed for continuous variables and number (%) for categorical variables. The normality of the continuous data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The Mann–Whitney U test or t-test was used to compare continuous variables between two groups. The chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables between the groups.

Candidate variables for selection included patient demographics (age, nulliparity), tumor characteristics (histological subtype, laterality, FIGO stage, and pathological features), surgical modalities, and in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET) status. For analysis, ovarian surgical procedures were dichotomized into two groups: the cystectomy group (at least one ovarian cystectomy performed, including unilateral/bilateral cystectomy or UAC) and the unilateral adnexectomy group. Missing data were addressed using the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method via the mice package in R.

Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the period from the date of initial surgery to that of first recurrence or the last follow-up investigation. The relationship between age (as a continuous variable) and recurrence was first evaluated via univariate Cox regression. The proportional hazards assumption was tested via the cox.zph function, and the linearity of the age variable was confirmed through Martingale residual plots. Subsequently, LASSO-Cox regression was employed for variable selection to identify independent prognostic factors for DFS. Following LASSO screening, multivariate Cox regression analysis with backward elimination was performed to estimate Hazard Ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Subgroup analyses were further conducted, with patients stratified by histological subtype and tumor laterality (unilateral vs. bilateral). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Oncological outcomes

During the median follow-up period of 61.90 months (range: 1.03-176.53months), 85 (15.95%) patients experienced recurrence (borderline and invasive recurrence). Eight cases (1.50%) exhibited malignant transformation at the time of first recurrence, one of whom died of the disease (Table 3). No patients died without a prior documented recurrence. Nearly two-thirds of the recurrences were SBOTs. Three patients were diagnosed with recurrent BOT in the perinatal period (one during Cesarean section and two post-termination). Among the invasive recurrences, seven originated from Stage 1 MBOTs, and one from an advanced-stage SBOT. A unique case of EBOT was noted: 21 months postoperatively, a uterine lesion was detected and resected via hysteroscopy. Initial pathology identified Grade 1 endometrioid adenocarcinoma (clinical stage IA). The patient underwent total hysterectomy, adnexectomy, and sentinel lymph node dissection. The pathological assessment of the hysterectomy specimen revealed no evidence of residual malignancy. No adjuvant therapy was administered, and the patient remains in stable condition under regular surveillance.

Stratified analyses were performed to identify sub-group-specific risk factors. Stratified by laterality, FIGO stage III(HR 3.70, 95%CI: 1.78–7.71, p<0.001) was the primary risk factor for patients with bilateral involvement. And peritoneal biopsy (HR 0.31, 95%CI: 0.14–0.71, p = 0.005) remained protective. For those with unilateral lesions, exophytic growth (HR 4.91, 95%CI: 2.47–9.75, p<0.001), IEC (HR 3.84, 95%CI: 1.47–10.04, p = 0.006)) and intraoperative chromotubation (HR 2.32, 95%CI: 1.02–5.29, p = 0.044) were significantly associated with worse prognosis.

Stratified by histology, exophytic growth (HR 4.05, 95%CI: 2.13–7.70, p<0.001), cystectomy (HR 3.03, 95%CI: 1.34–6.86, p = 0.008), FIGO stage III (HR 2.46, 95%CI: 1.23–4.89, p = 0.011) were risk factors in SBOT based on multivariable analysis. Peritoneal biopsy (HR 0.44, 95%CI: 0.23–0.87, p = 0.018) was beneficial for prognosis. For MBOT, intraoperative chromotubation was the sole independent risk factor (HR 8.62, 95%CI: 2.81–26.49, p<0.001) for recurrence.

Fertility outcomes

A total of 176 (33.02%) patients attempted conception after the FSS. The surgical procedures for these patients were as follows: UA with or without contralateral ovary biopsy (n = 61, 34.66%), UOC with or without contralateral ovary biopsy (n = 80, 45.45%), BOC (n = 32, 18.18%), and UAC (n = 3, 1.70%). Most patients (n = 156) were FIGO stage I, 9 stage II and 11 stage III.

Of these 176 patients, 132 attempted natural conception and 31 underwent IVF-ET; the method of conception was not recorded for 13 patients. The overall pregnancy rate was 82.39% (145/176), with a live birth rate of 70.45% (124/176). Nineteen women delivered twice and six were pregnant during the last follow-up. Adverse pregnancy outcomes occurred in 15 women and included ectopic pregnancy (n = 5), early abortion (n = 7), second-trimester miscarriage (n = 2), and biochemical pregnancy (n = 1). Notably, among the 20 patients with advanced-stage disease (FIGO II-III), 14 conceived and 12 achieved a live birth.

Among the thirty-one women who underwent IVF-ET, the pregnancy and live birth rates were 77.42% and 48.39%, respectively. Specifically, 7 failed to conceive, 8 experienced adverse pregnancy outcomes, 1 had an ongoing pregnancy and 15 achieved live births. Regarding oncological safety, recurrence was observed in 8 of the 31 patients (25.81%) who underwent IVF-ET. Specific to the subset of 9 IVF patients managed at our institution, 17 controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) cycles were performed, primarily utilizing mild stimulation (n = 8) or antagonist protocols (n = 4) (Table S1). Among these 9 patients, 3 experienced recurrence following COS; detailed clinical courses and hormonal parameters for these cases were provided in Table S1 and Table S2. For the remaining 22 patients treated at external centers, detailed COS parameters and immediate follow-up data were not fully ascertainable, which was addressed in the limitations section.

Discussion

Oncological outcomes

Although restricted by its retrospective nature, this study offers several meaningful findings and unanswered questions that merit further exploration. Due to the favorable prognosis and limited mortality events, DFS was utilized as the primary oncological outcome. After a median follow-up of 61.90 months, the overall recurrence rate of borderline tumors for young women who underwent FSS was 15.95%, with a rate of malignant transformation of 1.50%. The recurrence rate was similar to the recently reported data in the management of BOT in young patients [3], although higher rates (up to 38%) have been noted in smaller cohorts [14]. Critically, invasive recurrence has an impact on overall survival. In our cohort, 7 of the 8 invasive recurrences were MBOTs. MBOT was speculated to carry a worse prognosis compared with other histological types of BOTs. The results align with a series of studies suggesting that the tumor histological subtypes particularly in MBOT have an impact on a higher risk of recurrent invasive disease [15]. Unlike the dual-track development of SBOT, mucinous ovarian tumors develop on a continuum from benign epithelium to borderline lesions and, ultimately, to invasive adenocarcinoma [16]. The progression is driven by early Kirsten rat sarcoma virus oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutations, which are present in a vast majority of MBOTs and mucinous carcinomas [17, 18]. The subsequent accumulation of genetic alterations, such as TP53 mutations and copy number alterations, trigger the transition to an invasive phenotype [17]. The clinical challenge is further compounded by the macroscopic nature of MBOTs, which are typically large and multi-loculated [19]. Performing a cystectomy in such cases carries a high risk of leaving behind biologically ‘primed’ residual epithelium. Furthermore, the diagnostic sensitivity of intraoperative frozen sections for MBOT is notably low (approximately 40.4%) [20]. Despite recommendations for extensive sampling (e.g.2 sections per cm for tumors > 10 cm), occult invasive foci can remain undetected even within macroscopically normal ovarian tissue [19, 21]. Consequently, for MBOT, which are mostly unilateral, unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy over cystectomy is recommended to mitigate the inherent risk of invasive recurrence [13, 15]. For patients with inadequate initial sampling or suspected residual disease, surgical restaging should be considered to ensure a thorough pathological reassessment and to rule out occult malignancy. Given the limited sensitivity of intraoperative frozen sections for MBOT, accurate preoperative grading is essential for optimal clinical decision-making. Integrating MRI findings with tumor marker profiles were reported to improve diagnostic precision [22, 23]. The presence of lesions positive for T1-hyper/T2-hypo and negative for CA199 can help differentiate MBOT from mucinous carcinoma. In contrast, lesions negative for T1-hyper/T2-hypo and positive for CEA, or positive for both T1-hyper/T2-hypo and CA199 or solid components suggest malignancy [22]. Incorporating such multi-modal preoperative assessments may refine the surgical strategy, helping clinicians balance the need for radicality with the preservation of fertility.

Exophytic growth on prognosis

Exophytic growth emerged as a significant independent risk factor for recurrence in both the overall cohort and the SBOT subgroup. Unlike MBOTs, which are typically large and encapsulated, SBOTs are more prone to developing papillary projections or nodules on the external ovarian surface. Intuitively, tumor growth above the ovarian surface is expected to promote the spillage of the neoplastic cells into the abdominal cavity. The exophytic pattern was associated with invasive and non-invasive peritoneal implants [24]. Consequently, neoplastic cells could rest on the peritoneal surfaces leading to neoplastic cells within the peritoneal washing, and subsequent peritoneal neoplastic infiltration. All these pathogenetic events would explain the significant association of exophytic BOT with negative prognostic factors.

Surgical procedures of fertility-sparing strategies on prognosis

The optimal FSS for BOTs–cystectomy versus adnexectomy–remains a subject of ongoing debate. Our subgroup analysis revealed that cystectomy was a significant independent risk factor for recurrence specifically in SBOT (HR 3.03, 95% CI: 1.34–6.86, p = 0.008). This elevated risk may be attributed to the potential for microscopic residual disease at the cyst wall or the multifocal nature often observed in serous histology. Although adnexectomy provides more definitive local control, it does not prevent contralateral relapse [8], which is particularly detrimental to the reproductive potential of patients who have already undergone a previous oophorectomy. Importantly, although cystectomy increases the risk of recurrence of SBOT, most relapses maintain a borderline histology and can be successfully managed through secondary surgery. For bilateral lesions, BOC does not significantly increase the recurrence rate compared with UAC. Given that BOC maximizes the preservation of functional ovarian tissue, it remains a highly beneficial approach for optimizing reproductive outcomes in young women with bilateral involvement [25].

In our MBOT subgroup analysis, the surgical procedure (cystectomy vs. adnexectomy) was not a significant predictor of recurrence. Instead, intraoperative chromotubation emerged as the sole independent risk factor. While IEC was identified as a risk factor for the overall BOT cohort, it lost statistical significance within the MBOT subgroup. This discrepancy arises because IEC was almost exclusively concentrated within the MBOT population (21/22 cases), serving as a surrogate marker for high-risk mucinous histology in the general model. However, our findings suggest that the mechanical risk of iatrogenic seeding–driven by hydrostatic pressure during chromotubation–may overshadow the intrinsic biological aggressiveness of IEC. Among 47 patients with fertility desire who underwent this procedure based on benign preoperative ultrasound and tumor marker profiles, nine were postoperatively diagnosed with MBOT. Notably, four of these nine patients experienced recurrence, including one who progressed to carcinoma. This suggests that the hydrostatic pressure during chromotubation may facilitate the iatrogenic seeding of mucinous cells, which possess high peritoneal implantation potential. Therefore, for patients with preoperative findings suggestive of mucinous neoplasms, even if malignancy is not confirmed, we recommend deferring chromotubation until the tumor is completely excised and its benign nature is verified, thereby minimizing the risk of accidental tumor dissemination.

Despite comprehensive staging surgery being recommend for all patients with BOTs [9], it remains challenging to implement in routine clinical practice. In our cohort, 80.52% of the patients received inadequate staging surgery. This high rate may be attributed not only to the limitations of preoperative diagnostic tools and intraoperative frozen sections, but also to variations in surgical discretion and temporal discrepancy between intraoperative frozen section diagnosis and the completion of the surgical procedure. In clinical practice, a definitive frozen section result is often rendered at the conclusion of the initial surgery. In such cases, immediate restaging procedure is frequently deferred to minimize surgical trauma in young patients, particularly when macroscopic evaluation appears benign, shifting the management focus to postoperative surveillance. Besides, surgeons may omit peritoneal biopsies or omentectomy if the surfaces appear macroscopically normal. Although some studies suggest that omentectomy and multiple biopsies of peritoneum may upstage patients and may affect prognosis [10, 11, 26]. Others have failed to prove the prognostic benefit in macroscopically stage I BOTs [12, 27–29]. Our analysis revealed that peritoneal biopsy specifically was associated with a more favorable prognosis, particularly in patients with SBOT and those with bilateral involvement. This may be attributed to a ‘detection-and-clearance’ effect, where thorough inspection and biopsy of the peritoneum may lead to the incidental removal of microscopic implants—a common feature in 15–40% of SBOTs [4]. Surgical restaging is suggested for SBOT cases with suspected residual disease after the first procedure and/or on imaging [9].

Reproductive outcomes

In our cohort, the pregnancy and live birth rates were 82.39% and 70.45%, respectively, exceeding the previously reported data of 63% [14] and aligning with the 84.6% reported by Delle Marchette M et al. [8]. These favorable outcomes are likely attributable to the young age of our patients and rigorous preoperative fertility screening.

Regarding assisted reproduction technology (ART), the pregnancy rate in our IVF-ET subgroup was 77.42%, while the live birth rate (48.39%) was lower than the overall cohort. This discrepancy is primarily attributed to intrinsic infertility factors within this subgroup rather than the history of BOT itself. This is consistent with findings by Li et al., who demonstrated comparable IVF success between post-FSS BOT patients and those without ovarian tumors [30].

The oncological safety of ART remains a critical area of investigation. While some studies suggest that postoperative ART might increase recurrence rates [31], other evidence, including a systematic review, has found no significant association between ART parameters such as peak estradiol levels and BOT recurrence [30, 32]. In the present study, while 8 recurrences were observed among the 31 IVF patients, it is important to note that 5 of these occurred in patients treated at external centers where granular timing between COS and relapse could not be definitively retrieved. However, in our institutional sub-cohort (n = 9) where precise monitoring was available, the recurrences (n = 3) occurred at a substantial remove from the hormonal stimulation (21–30 months) and were associated with lower peak estradiol levels than those seen in non-recurrent cases. This suggests that disease biology, rather than ART intervention, may be the primary driver of recurrence.

Consequently, the optimization of ovulation stimulation regimen remains a crucial area of focus. Mild ovarian stimulation protocols are often preferred for BOT patients following FSS to theoretically minimize estrogen exposure [33, 34], although they may result in lower oocyte yield [35]. Our previous multicenter study of 35 patients (79 cycles) demonstrated that mild stimulation (41.8%) and natural cycle protocols (25.3%) were the most frequently employed strategies [36]. Notably, when comparing patients with and without recurrence, no significant differences were identified in the distribution of COS regimens, the interval between surgery and COS initiation, total gonadotropin doses, or peak estradiol levels on the trigger day [36]. While our data and previous findings [36] support the safety of various COS regimens, the limited sample size of patients with granular stimulation data warrants cautious interpretation. Large-scale multicenter studies are essential to definitively validate the safety of specific protocols and provide individualized reproductive guidance for BOT survivors.

Limitations and conclusion

Despite being one of the largest single-center series on FSS for BOTs, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduced inevitable documentation and selection biases. Specifically, the missing data on intraoperative rupture—often omitted from surgical narratives when uneventful—likely led to an overrepresentation of rupture cases in the documented subgroup. Second, the long study duration spans evolving clinical guidelines, leading to temporal inconsistencies in management (e.g., lymphadenectomy and adjuvant chemotherapy). Third, the attrition rate and potential loss of out-of-system follow-up might lead to an underestimation of the true recurrence rate. Furthermore, regarding reproductive outcomes, our study is constrained by the absence of granular neonatal and obstetric data. Additionally, the lack of baseline ovarian reserve data and detailed stimulation protocols for all IVF patients limits a more granular analysis of fertility potential. Moreover, a notable limitation is the absence of a centralized second pathological review. Given the inherent diagnostic challenges of BOTs, the potential for diagnostic re-categorization cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, as a single-center study without genetic counseling data, our findings require validation through large-scale, prospective multicenter trials.

In summary, our findings confirm the oncological safety and favorable reproductive outcomes of FSS for BOT patients. Postoperative chemotherapy showed no independent prognostic benefit, reinforcing its limited role in chemo-resistant BOTs. For surgical optimization, management should be highly individualized: BOC is preferred for bilateral lesions to maximize functional preservation, whereas USO is recommended over cystectomy for MBOTs due to their significantly higher risk of invasive recurrence. Furthermore, peritoneal biopsy is associated with a favorable prognosis, particularly in SBOT. Conversely, intraoperative chromotubation should be strictly avoided in suspected MBOT to prevent iatrogenic dissemination under hydrostatic pressure. Ultimately, patients with high-risk features, such as exophytic lesions or mucinous histology, require intensive surveillance and comprehensive oncofertility counseling.
