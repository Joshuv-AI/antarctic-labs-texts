Quantifying crowding-ventilation effect on respiratory infections
Authors: Wei Jia, Pan Cheng, Jian Hang, Hua Qian, Yuguo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Crowded and poorly ventilated indoor spaces drive outbreaks of respiratory infections such as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), often leading to superspreading events. While overcrowding heightens risk, its interplay with space size and ventilation remains underexplored. We introduce a framework to assess this risk, using effective clean flow rate, which combines clean airflow (outdoor air, filtration, ultraviolet germicidal irradiation) with volume-time air clearance (VTAC), i.e., dilution from indoor air volume per person over time, and intake fraction time, which quantifies aerosol exposure risk independent of infectious quanta generation. Analysis of real outbreak data from jails, earthquake shelters, and dormitories shows modest spaciousness (16-32 m3/person) increases effective clean flow by 8.7 to 17.6 L/s per person in low-ventilation settings (0.5 L/s per person, 1-hour exposure), reducing exposure. Current standards, such as American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE) 62.1-2022, fall short in crowded spaces, heightening infection risks. Our model predicts infection patterns, showing VTAC is a critical yet overlooked factor in limiting long-range airborne transmission. These insights call for updated ventilation and spaciousness standards to strengthen public health strategies.

Introduction

Crowded spaces have long driven outbreaks of respiratory infections, from tuberculosis in 19th-century tenements to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) in modern jails, nursing homes, and shelters1,2. Overcrowding persists across diverse settings, including hospitals3, public transit4, and homes in both developed and developing regions5,6. Epidemiological studies show elevated rates of acute respiratory infections in densely packed environments, such as earthquake shelters, compared to less crowded settings7. However, the mechanisms linking crowding to heightened infection risk, particularly how indoor space size and ventilation interact, remain underexplored, limiting effective public health interventions.

Crowding is typically measured by occupant density (persons/m2), floor area per person (m2/person) or persons per room8. We propose spaciousness, defined as indoor air volume per person (m3/person), as a critical metric for diluting infectious aerosols. While close contact within 1─2 meters can drive short-range inhalation transmission, infection risks do not drop to zero beyond this range9–11. This observation supports that long-range airborne transmission also plays a significant role in disease spread, particularly for pathogens with high quanta generation rates. During the COVID-19 pandemic, outdoor spaces and large indoor venues (e.g., airport terminals) exhibited low transmission12,13, while superspreading events thrived in crowded, poorly ventilated settings like the Skagit Valley Choir outbreak, with an 87% attack rate in a 252 m2 hall14. These patterns underscore the importance of spaciousness and ventilation on airborne transmission for respiratory viruses, including influenza, rhinovirus, and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2)15,16.

Despite these insights, most studies on crowding overlook ventilation’s role. Current ventilation standards, e.g., the American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE) 62.1-2022, prescribe outdoor air rates primarily to dilute non-infectious pollutants (volatile organic compounds, particulate matter, and carbon dioxide) and perceived odor, typically using per-person rates scaled to expected occupancy and floor area. Because airborne pathogen emission is largely independent of floor area, these standards systematically under-ventilated crowded spaces (often ≤5 L/s per person), potentially elevating infection risk during respiratory outbreaks. This disconnect highlights a critical gap in understanding how space size and airflow together mitigate transmission.

To address this, we introduce two metrics: the effective clean flow rate, which integrates clean air equivalents (e.g., outdoor air, filtration, ultraviolet germicidal irradiation) with volume-time air clearance (VTAC), i.e., the dilution effect of spaciousness over exposure time, and intake fraction time, which quantifies aerosol exposure risk independent of infectious quanta. Analyzing outbreak data from jails, earthquake shelters, and dormitories, we demonstrate how these metrics predict infection patterns, revealing that modest spaciousness significantly reduces risk in poorly ventilated settings. Our findings challenge existing standards and advocate for new guidelines that balance spaciousness and ventilation to curb respiratory infections, offering an approach to creating safer indoor environments for public health.

Results

Spaciousness enhances dilution in poorly ventilated settings

To unravel how crowding fuels respiratory infections, we developed a framework centered on the effective clean flow rate, a metric that blends clean air equivalents (outdoor air, filtration, particle settling, and natural and artificial deactivation) with VTAC. Using a quasi-steady adaptation of the Wells-Riley equation17,18 (see “Methods”), we standardized this metric to compare aerosol dilution across diverse spaces, building on ASHRAE 241’s equivalent clean airflow concept19.

Removal of respiratory infectious aerosols by clean air equivalents in a room is in analogue with that of carbon dioxide (CO2) from exhaled breath by ventilation air. To demonstrate VTAC, we modeled CO2 buildup from exhaled breath in two idealized settings: a crowded space with 1.8 m3/person and a spacious one with 72 m3/person, both ventilated at 10 L/s per person over a 4-h co-exposure period (Fig. 1a). Despite identical ventilation, the crowded space, with a high air change rate per hour (20 ACH), reaches CO2 equilibrium quickly, while the spacious space (0.5 ACH) shows slower buildup, reducing early aerosol exposure (Fig. 1b). The effective clean flow rate quantifies this effect (Fig. 1c). In the crowded space, it matches clean air equivalents (~10 L/s per person). In the spacious space (64 m3/person), it surges to 16.6 L/s per person, with 6.6 L/s per person attributed to VTAC. At an extreme spaciousness of 1000 m3/person, the effective clean flow rate reaches 145.6 L/s per person, almost entirely due to VTAC. Crucially, modest spaciousness (increase from 2 to 32 m3/person) has the greatest impact in poorly ventilated settings (0.5 L/s per person, 4-h exposure), boosting effective clean flow rate from 0.7 to 4.8 L/s per person, a nearly seven-fold increase (Fig. 1d). For 1-h exposure, a spaciousness of 32 m3/person provides a VTAC of 17.8 L/s per person. In contrast, in a 4-h exposure setting with high ventilation rates (100 L/s per person), typical spaciousness levels contribute minimally (e.g., ~26% increase even at 300 m3/person), as physical airflow dominates dilution (Fig. 1c).

We also examined exposure after an infected individual leaves the space (Fig. 1e). Using the quasi-steady approach, the effective clean flow rate decreases as post-exposure time extends (Fig. 1f), reflecting the persistence of aerosols in larger volumes with low ventilation. This underscores VTAC’s role in mitigating lingering risks in poorly ventilated environments.

We validated this model using six datasets of respiratory infection outbreaks, i.e., influenza in military camps20,21, acute respiratory infections in earthquake shelters7, pneumococcal disease in jails22, common colds in dormitories23, and COVID-19 in prisons24. Because quanta generation rates differ significantly across pathogens and are heterogeneous even for the same pathogen, we normalized the observed incidence rates in each dataset to its most-crowded condition, eliminating the need for pathogen-specific generation rates (see Supplementary Note 2). Normalizing incidence rates to align with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{iF}}_{t}$$\end{document}iFt, we found strong agreement with our predicted crowding factor at a typical poor ventilation rate of 0.5 L/s per person for 4- and 12-h exposures (Fig. 2c, d). For example, shelters with 35–40 m3/person or more showed significantly lower infection rates, a pattern unexplained by short-range airborne transmission alone (distances <2 meters), supporting the role of long-range airborne transmission in crowded settings.

We applied ASHRAE 62.125 and ASHRAE 24119 ventilation standards, assuming a 3-meter ceiling to estimate spaciousness across various occupancy categories, such as offices, lecture halls, and prisons. ASHRAE 62.1, designed for general indoor air quality, assigns lower ventilation rates to crowded spaces based on smaller surface areas (Fig. 4c), resulting in a wide intake fraction time range (0.00002–0.2 h, Fig. 4a). This variability is inadequate for infection control, as crowded settings require more, not less, ventilation to offset low spaciousness. ASHRAE 241, tailored for infectious disease control, reduces this range to 0.003-0.1 h (Fig. 4b), a notable improvement, but still fails to fully address crowding’s impact. The effect of age-, sex-, and weight-related variability in inhalation rate on intake fraction time is investigated in Supplementary Fig. 11. Lower outdoor air flow rates are assigned by ASHRAE 62.1 in crowded settings, although the corresponding air change rates are greater. To address this, we propose revised ventilation rates targeting an intake fraction time of 0.02 h (for quanta generation rate of 50 quanta/h), which requires higher ventilation in crowded settings (Supplementary Tables 4, 5, Supplementary Fig. 9 in Supplementary Note 6). For example, a space with 2 m3/person and 4-h exposure needs ~20 L/s per person (at 1 MET) to meet this threshold, compared to ASHRAE 62.1’s typical 2.5 L/s per person. This highlights a critical flaw in current standards, where crowded spaces like jails or shelters are under-ventilated, exacerbating infection risk.

Discussion

For centuries, crowded spaces have been hotspots for respiratory infections, from tuberculosis in 19th-century slums to COVID-19 in modern jails, nursing homes, and shelters1,2. Our study offers a framework to explain this link, showing how spaciousness—indoor air volume per person (m3/person)—works alongside ventilation to dilute infectious aerosols, especially in poorly ventilated environments. We introduced two metrics: the effective clean flow rate and intake fraction time. These metrics illuminate why cramped, stuffy settings exacerbate outbreaks while larger, well-ventilated spaces curb transmission, providing a mechanistic basis for a long-standing public health challenge.

Our results demonstrate that modest spaciousness (16–32 m3/person) can boost the effective clean flow rate by 8.7 to 17.6 L/s per person in low-ventilation settings (0.5 L/s per person and 1-h exposure), reducing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{iF}}_{t}$$\end{document}iFt from 0.07 to below 0.02 h—a critical threshold for preventing secondary infections at a quanta generation rate of 50 quanta/h (Fig. 2a, b). This effect is evident in outbreaks like the Skagit Valley Choir event, where a spacious 252 m2 hall (4.1 m3/person, <1 ACH) saw an 87% attack rate due to poor ventilation and prolonged singing14. Similarly, a Guangzhou restaurant outbreak (145 m2, ~1.6 m3/person, ~0.5 ACH) reported 9 cases, driven by stagnant air despite ample space26. In contrast, airplane cabins with high ventilation (15-20 ACH) and low spaciousness (~1.5 m3/person) had low transmission rates27, as ventilation dominated12. These examples highlight VTAC’s pivotal role when ventilation is weak, reducing airborne inhalation exposure beyond the close range.

Crowding is traditionally measured by occupant density (persons/m2), floor area per person, or persons per room8. Our focus on spaciousness captures the air volume available for dilution, distinct from occupancy’s effect of increasing susceptible individuals. Earthquake shelter data showed lower infection rates at 35-40 m3/person7, a pattern not fully explained by short-range airborne transmission (distances <2 meters), supporting long-range airborne transmission16. Prison outbreaks, with <1 m3/person and 4–6 ACH, had infection rates three times the general population, underscoring the combined risk of crowding and poor ventilation24. The close alignment of our model with these real-world datasets validates the predictive power of VTAC and offers new mechanistic insight for infection control.

Current ventilation standards, such as ASHRAE 62.1, paradoxically assign lower per-person ventilation rates to crowded spaces with smaller surface areas, yielding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{iF}}_{t}$$\end{document}iFt spanning four orders of magnitude (0.00002–0.2 h, Fig. 4a). Even the infection-control-oriented ASHRAE 241 narrows this range only modestly (0.003-0.1 h, Fig. 4b) and still under-ventilates high-density settings like jails and shelters. For 1-h exposure setting, we propose either a minimum spaciousness of 35–40 m3/person (~12–13 m2/person at 3 m ceiling height for baseline ventilation of 0.5 L/s per person) or equivalently higher ventilation rates (e.g., ≥20 L/s per person at 2 m3/person) is required to achieve a target \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{iF}}_{t}$$\end{document}iFt of 0.02 h and minimize secondary infections for pathogens like COVID-19 or influenza. This threshold aligns with observed low transmission in spacious emergency facilities ( ~ 413 m3/patient)13 and elevated community transmission in Hong Kong’s densely subdivided flats (average 5.3 m2/person)28. These findings suggest standards must prioritize both spaciousness and ventilation, especially where airflow is limited.

Designing substantially larger spaces isn’t always feasible—greater air volumes increase construction costs, energy use, and land demands29. Nevertheless, our framework shows that targeted interventions can achieve comparable protection: in existing crowded buildings, portable High-Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filtration or upgraded Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning (HVAC) systems can compensate for low VTAC, whereas new high-occupancy designs (hospitals, schools, transit hubs, emergency shelters) can prioritise generous air volume to reduce long-term reliance on mechanical ventilation and energy use. Policymakers and building-code authorities should therefore integrate both spaciousness and ventilation requirements into standards, particularly for vulnerable settings where crowding is unavoidable. Air change rate alone is also misleading. For instance, 12 ACH recommendation by most existing guidelines translates to 80 L/s in a 4 × 2 × 3 m3 room but 320 L/s in a 6 × 4 × 4 m3 room, yielding four-fold lower exposure in the larger space30–32.

Our study has limitations. We assumed uniform air mixing, which may not hold in settings with strong air stratification or directional flows, as seen in some outbreaks26,33. However, the effect of non-uniformity on clean air equivalent can be adjusted by mean air distribution effectiveness, whereas VTAC itself remains unaffected (Supplementary Note 1). Quanta generation rates are heterogeneous, varying with the immunity of infectors and susceptible individuals, as well as respiratory activities34. This impacts infection risk estimates for a given \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{iF}}_{t}$$\end{document}iFt. Crowding also affects the interpersonal distance and close contact duration in terms of short-range airborne transmission35. Future work should validate effective clean flow rate and intake fraction time across diverse real-world environments (offices, transit, or informal settlements) and incorporate variable quanta rates, non-uniform mixing, and multi-route transmission through field studies and advanced modeling (e.g., computational fluid dynamics). Integrating these factors could refine our understanding of aerosol dynamics.

In conclusion, spaciousness, through VTAC, and ventilation are deeply intertwined, with even modest air volume per person serving as a critical buffer against respiratory infection when ventilation is limited. The effective clean flow rate and intake fraction time metrics introduced here provide a unified, mechanistic framework to predict exposure and guide evidence-based ventilation standards. By explicitly accounting for both spaciousness and airflow, we can move beyond air-change-rate-centric guidelines and design resilient indoor environments that substantially reduce transmission risk for COVID-19, influenza, and future aerosol-borne pathogens36,37.

Methods

To investigate how crowding drives respiratory infection risk, we developed a framework to quantify the dilution of infectious aerosols in indoor spaces, focusing on the interplay between spaciousness (indoor air volume per person) and ventilation. Our approach introduces two metrics: the effective clean flow rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${q}_{e}$$\end{document}qe, which integrates clean air equivalents with VTAC, and intake fraction time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{iF}}_{t}$$\end{document}iFt, which measures airborne exposure risk independent of infectious quanta. Below, we outline the mathematical modeling, outbreak data analysis, ventilation standards application, and key assumptions used to develop and validate these metrics.

No ethics approval was required for this research, as the study involved only mathematical modeling and secondary analysis of publicly available, aggregated epidemiological data from the literature, with no new human participants or identifiable individual-level data. Written informed consent was therefore not applicable.

Model assumptions and limitations

The model assumes uniform air mixing, constant quanta generation rates across infectors, and constant inhalation rates (e.g., 0.1 L/s for a resting adult at 1 MET) in the main text. Real-world settings may exhibit non-uniform mixing, as seen in outbreaks like a restaurant26,33, or variable quanta generation rates due to activities like singing14. Non-uniform concentration distributions and individual exposure variations were partly explored using a generalized Wells-Riley equation to account for spatial and temporal heterogeneity in Jia et al.18. We also extend this work by investigating the effects of non-uniform air mixing (Supplementary Notes 1, 2), heterogeneous quanta generation (Supplementary Note 4), and individual-specific inhalation rates (Supplementary Note 7).

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were conducted using ANSYS Fluent (2024 R2, ANSYS Inc.), and final figure assembly was carried out using Microsoft PowerPoint (365, Microsoft).
