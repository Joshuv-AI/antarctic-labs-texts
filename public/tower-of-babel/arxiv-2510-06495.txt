Qudit low-density parity-check codes
Daniel J. Spencer, Andrew Tanggara, Tobias Haug, Derek Khu, Kishor Bharti

Abstract
Qudits offer significant advantages over qubit-based architectures, including more efficient gate compilation, reduced resource requirements, improved error-correction primitives, and enhanced capabilities for quantum communication and cryptography. Yet, one of the most promising family of quantum error correction codes, namely quantum low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes, have been so far mostly restricted to qubits. Here, we generalize recent advancements in LDPC codes from qubits to qudits. We introduce a general framework for finding qudit LDPC codes and apply our formalism to several promising types of LDPC codes. We generalize bivariate bicycle codes, including their coprime variant; hypergraph product codes, including the recently proposed La-cross codes; subsystem hypergraph product (SHYPS) codes; high-dimensional expander codes, which make use of Ramanujan complexes; and fiber bundle codes. Using the qudit generalization formalism, we then numerically search for and decode several novel qudit codes compatible with near-term hardware. Our results highlight the potential of qudit LDPC codes as a versatile and hardware-compatible pathway toward scalable quantum error correction.

2.1
Fields and rings

We begin with finite field theory (also known as Galois theory) and ring theory

]

Definition 1

(Finite field)
.

A
finite
or
Galois field

\mathbb{F}_{q}
of order
q=p^{s}
for some prime
p
, which we call the characteristic of the field, and some
s\in\mathbb{N}
, is a field containing the
0
element and
q-1
other nonzero elements.

In essence, moving from qubits to qudits means expanding the
“alphabet”
that we use for our codes. For qubits, we work over the binary field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
(with elements 0 and 1, where
1+1=0
). For qudits, we use a larger field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This richer mathematical structure allows for more complex error models and potentially more efficient codes, but it also introduces subtleties in how we ensure that different types of parity checks commute. If
q
is prime (i.e.,
s=1
), then
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is isomorphic to the integers modulo
q
:
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cong\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
. For example, for qubits, the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
contains the elements
\{0,1\}
and is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. When
s\geq 2
, this is not true and
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is instead called a finite field extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, where we have
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/\text{irr}(x)
for an irreducible polynomial in
x
of degree
s
. It is important to note that when
s\geq 2
, the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is not simply isomorphic to the integers modulo
q
,
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
. Instead, it is constructed using polynomials. This construction is necessary because
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
does not form a field when
q
is not prime (e.g., in
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
, 2 has no multiplicative inverse). The field extension ensures that every nonzero element remains invertible. If we denote the nonzero elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
by
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, then a primitive element
\omega\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
is an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that each element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
can be written as
\omega^{i}
for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
. If
n
is a positive integer coprime to

and

is the smallest positive integer such that
q^{m}=1\pmod{n}
, the field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
is the smallest extension field of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
containing a primitive
n\textsuperscript{th}
root of unity. Fixing a primitive element
\omega
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
, a primitive
n\textsuperscript{th}
root of unity in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
is given as

\displaystyle\beta=\omega^{\frac{q^{m}-1}{n}}\,.

(1)

A
ring

2.2
Homological algebra and chain complexes

We now give a basic introduction to homological algebra, a concept from algebraic topology; for a more detailed exposition, we refer the reader to
[
45
,
74
]
. Homological algebra provides a powerful, abstract language for describing quantum error-correcting codes, especially Calderbank–Shor–Steane (CSS) codes. A chain complex organizes the components of the system (the physical qudits and the stabilizer checks) into a sequence of vector spaces connected by “boundary operators”
\partial_{j}
. We start with a
chain complex
:

\displaystyle\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_{j+1}}\mathcal{A}_{j}\xrightarrow{\partial_{j}}\mathcal{A}_{j-1}\xrightarrow{\partial_{j-1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}\mathcal{A}_{0}\xrightarrow{\partial_{0}=0}0

(2)

for vector spaces
\mathcal{A}_{j}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and linear maps
\partial_{j}\colon\mathcal{A}_{j}\to\mathcal{A}_{j-1}
, called
boundary operators
, with the property
\partial_{j}\partial_{j+1}=0
(or equivalently,
\operatorname{im}\partial_{j+1}\subseteq\ker{\partial_{j}}
). The crucial property
\partial_{j}\partial_{j+1}=0
captures a fundamental topological idea: the boundary of a boundary is empty. For example, the boundary of a solid disk is a circle, but that circle itself has no boundary. In the context of quantum error correction, this condition is directly related to the requirement that
X
and
Z
stabilizers must commute. A
k
-complex
is a chain complex with
\mathcal{A}_{j}=0
for all
j>k
and
\mathcal{A}_{k}\neq 0
. The basis elements of
\mathcal{A}_{j}
are called
j
-cells
and vectors in
\mathcal{A}_{j}
are called
j
-chains
. The Hamming weight of a chain is the number of cells in the chain with nonzero coefficients. The
homology
of a chain complex
\mathcal{A}
is a sequence of vector spaces

\displaystyle H_{j}(\mathcal{A})=\ker{\partial_{j}}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{j+1}\,.

(3)

The
j\textsuperscript{th}

Betti number
is defined as
b_{j}(\mathcal{A})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}{H_{j}(\mathcal{A})}
. An element of
\ker{\partial_{j}}
is called a
j
-cycle
. The homology groups
H_{j}(\mathcal{A})
measure the presence of “cycles” that are not boundaries. In quantum codes, these groups correspond to the logical operators, and the dimension of the homology group (the Betti number
b_{j}
) typically tells us the number of encoded logical qudits
k
. The
cohomology

H^{j}(\mathcal{A})
is the homology of the dual chain:

\displaystyle\left(\cdots\xleftarrow{\partial^{*}_{j+1}}\mathcal{A}_{j}^{*}\xleftarrow{\partial_{j}^{*}}\mathcal{A}_{j-1}^{*}\xleftarrow{\partial_{j-1}^{*}}\cdots\xleftarrow{\partial_{1}^{*}}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}\xleftarrow{0}0\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\left(\cdots\xleftarrow{\partial_{j+1}^{\intercal}}\mathcal{A}_{j}\xleftarrow{\partial_{j}^{\intercal}}\mathcal{A}_{j-1}\xleftarrow{\partial_{j-1}^{\intercal}}\cdots\xleftarrow{\partial_{1}^{\intercal}}\mathcal{A}_{0}\xleftarrow{0}0\right)\,,

(5)

where
\mathcal{A}_{j}^{*}
is the vector space of linear functionals on
\mathcal{A}_{j}
.

2.2.1
Tensor product of chain complexes

A standard construction in algebraic topology, which is also central to the construction of many quantum error-correcting codes (such as hypergraph product codes), is the tensor product of chain complexes. This allows us to build larger, more complex structures from simpler components. Suppose we have two chain complexes,
(A,\partial^{A})
and
(B,\partial^{B})
. We define their tensor product
C=A\otimes B
. The vector spaces of the resulting complex
C
are formed by combining the spaces of
A
and
B
such that their dimensions add up. Specifically, the space of
k
-chains,
C_{k}
, is defined as the direct sum of the tensor products of the constituent spaces:

\displaystyle C_{k}=\bigoplus_{i+j=k}A_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}B_{j}\,,

(6)

where the tensor product is taken over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
underlying the vector spaces. (We will omit the subscript
\mathbb{F}_{q}
in subsequent notation when the underlying field is obvious.) An element in
C_{k}
is a linear combination of elements of the form
a\otimes b
, where
a\in A_{i}
and
b\in B_{j}
with
i+j=k
. The crucial step is defining the boundary operator
\partial^{C}_{k}:C_{k}\to C_{k-1}
for the product complex. We want this operator to utilize the existing boundary operators
\partial^{A}
and
\partial^{B}
, and it must satisfy the fundamental property of a chain complex:
(\partial^{C})^{2}=0
. One might intuitively try to define the boundary operator analogously to the product rule in calculus:

\partial_{\text{naive}}(a\otimes b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}(\partial^{A}a)\otimes b+a\otimes(\partial^{B}b).

(7)

Let us check if this definition satisfies the required property. Applying the boundary operator twice:

\displaystyle\partial_{\text{naive}}^{2}(a\otimes b)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\text{naive}}\left((\partial^{A}a)\otimes b+a\otimes(\partial^{B}b)\right)

(8)

\displaystyle=\left((\partial^{A})^{2}a\right)\otimes b+(\partial^{A}a)\otimes(\partial^{B}b)+(\partial^{A}a)\otimes(\partial^{B}b)+a\otimes\left((\partial^{B})^{2}b\right)\,.

(9)

Since
A
and
B
are chain complexes,
(\partial^{A})^{2}=0
and
(\partial^{B})^{2}=0
. The expression simplifies to:

\partial_{\text{naive}}^{2}(a\otimes b)=2\left((\partial^{A}a)\otimes(\partial^{B}b)\right)\,.

(10)

This expression is not necessarily zero unless the underlying field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has characteristic 2 (i.e., where
1+1=0
). For general fields, this naive definition fails to produce a valid chain complex. To ensure that the cross-terms cancel and
(\partial^{C})^{2}=0
holds regardless of the field characteristic, we must introduce a sign adjustment known as the
Koszul sign rule
. The rule introduces a sign based on the dimension of the element that the second operator effectively “jumps over.” The correct boundary operator
\partial^{C}
acting on an element
a\otimes b
, where
a\in A_{i}
, is thus defined as:

\partial^{C}(a\otimes b)=(\partial^{A}a)\otimes b+(-1)^{i}a\otimes(\partial^{B}b)\,.

(11)

We can now verify that this definition ensures
(\partial^{C})^{2}=0
. Note that
\partial^{A}a\in A_{i-1}
.

\displaystyle(\partial^{C})^{2}(a\otimes b)

\displaystyle=\partial^{C}\left((\partial^{A}a)\otimes b+(-1)^{i}a\otimes(\partial^{B}b)\right)

(12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=(\partial^{A})^{2}a\otimes b+(-1)^{i-1}(\partial^{A}a)\otimes(\partial^{B}b)\\
&\quad+(-1)^{i}(\partial^{A}a)\otimes(\partial^{B}b)+(-1)^{i}a\otimes(\partial^{B})^{2}b\end{split}

(13)

\displaystyle=0+\left((-1)^{i-1}+(-1)^{i}\right)(\partial^{A}a)\otimes(\partial^{B}b)+0

(14)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(15)

as desired.

2.2.2
Tensor product of co-chain complexes

The construction is entirely analogous for the tensor product of
co-chain complexes
. Recall that a co-chain complex utilizes
co-boundary operators
, which we denote by
\delta
. Unlike boundary operators, these maps increase the dimension. That is,
\delta_{j}:A^{*}_{j}\to A^{*}_{j+1}
, but they must still satisfy the fundamental property
\delta^{2}=0
. When working over a field,
\delta
corresponds to the transpose of the boundary operator,
\partial^{\intercal}
. Suppose we have two co-chain complexes
(A^{*},\delta^{A})
and
(B^{*},\delta^{B})
. We define their tensor product
C^{*}=A^{*}\otimes B^{*}
. The underlying vector spaces are defined similarly to the chain complex case in
Equation

6
:

\displaystyle C^{*}_{k}=\bigoplus_{i+j=k}A^{*}_{i}\otimes B^{*}_{j}\,.

(16)

We now seek a co-boundary operator
\delta^{C}_{k}:C^{*}_{k}\to C^{*}_{k+1}
. Just as before, we might try a naive definition analogous to the product rule:

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{naive}}(a\otimes b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}(\delta^{A}a)\otimes b+a\otimes(\delta^{B}b).

(17)

However, applying this operator twice yields the same problem encountered previously:

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{naive}}^{2}(a\otimes b)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\text{naive}}\left((\delta^{A}a)\otimes b+a\otimes(\delta^{B}b)\right)

(18)

\displaystyle=\left((\delta^{A})^{2}a\right)\otimes b+(\delta^{A}a)\otimes(\delta^{B}b)+(\delta^{A}a)\otimes(\delta^{B}b)+a\otimes\left((\delta^{B})^{2}b\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=2\left((\delta^{A}a)\otimes(\delta^{B}b)\right)\,.

(20)

This is not generally zero unless the field has characteristic 2. The
Koszul sign rule
is therefore also essential for the tensor product of co-chain complexes to ensure the resulting structure is a valid complex. For
a\in A^{*}_{i}
and
b\in B^{*}_{j}
, the co-boundary operator
\delta^{C}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\delta^{C}(a\otimes b)=(\delta^{A}a)\otimes b+(-1)^{i}a\otimes(\delta^{B}b)\,.

(21)

We can verify that this definition ensures that
(\delta^{C})^{2}=0
. It is crucial to note how the degree changes: if
a\in A^{*}_{i}
, then
\delta^{A}a\in A^{*}_{i+1}
. When we apply
\delta^{C}
a second time, the Koszul sign rule must account for this increased degree

\displaystyle(\delta^{C})^{2}(a\otimes b)

\displaystyle=\delta^{C}\left((\delta^{A}a)\otimes b+(-1)^{i}a\otimes(\delta^{B}b)\right)

(22)

\displaystyle=\delta^{C}\left((\delta^{A}a)\otimes b\right)+(-1)^{i}\delta^{C}\left(a\otimes(\delta^{B}b)\right)

(23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\left((\delta^{A})^{2}a\otimes b+(-1)^{i+1}(\delta^{A}a)\otimes(\delta^{B}b)\right)\\
&\quad+(-1)^{i}\left((\delta^{A}a)\otimes(\delta^{B}b)+(-1)^{i}a\otimes(\delta^{B})^{2}b\right)\end{split}

(24)

\displaystyle=0+\left((-1)^{i+1}+(-1)^{i}\right)(\delta^{A}a)\otimes(\delta^{B}b)+0

(25)

\displaystyle=0\,.

(26)

The introduction of the sign
(-1)^{i}
, adjusted appropriately when applying the operator sequentially (becoming
(-1)^{i+1}
because the degree is increased), ensures the cancellation of the cross-terms, guaranteeing that the tensor product of two co-chain complexes is itself a valid co-chain complex.

2.3
Coding theory

Finally, we give a brief overview of coding theory. We start by defining a
linear code
:

Definition 3

(Linear code)
.

A
linear code

C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is a linear subspace of the
n
-dimensional vector space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. The elements
c
of the code
C
are called the
codewords
.

The cardinality of the code
C
is given as
\absolutevalue{C}=q^{k}
, where
k
is called the code
dimension
. To generate the set of codewords, we need the
generator matrix

G^{k\times n}
, where
uG
for all
u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
gives us the codewords. Its
parity-check matrix

H
annihilates all codewords, namely
GH^{\intercal}=0
. Given two codewords
c,c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, the
Hamming distance
is given by

\displaystyle d(c,c^{\prime})=\absolutevalue{\{1 \leq i \leq n | c_i \neq c'_i\}}\,.

(27)

The
Hamming weight

w_{H}(c)
of an element
c\in C
is the number of nonzero elements of
c
. The minimum distance
d
of
C
is then the minimum Hamming distance between distinct codewords, which for linear codes is given by

\displaystyle d=\min\{w_{H}(c)|c\in C\backslash\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\}\,,

(28)

that is, the minimum Hamming weight of any nonzero codeword in the code
C
. The code
C
can then correct
d-1
erasures and
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor
errors.

The above formalism applies to both classical and quantum codes. We now focus our attention on just quantum codes. Given two classical codes
C_{Z}^{\perp}
and
C_{X}
such that
C_{Z}^{\perp}\subseteq C_{X}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we can use the Calderbank–Shor–Steane (CSS) construction
[
17
,
93
]
to define the corresponding CSS code as

\displaystyle\text{CSS}(C_{X},C_{Z}^{\perp})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{X}\oplus\mathcal{Q}_{Z}

(29)

\displaystyle=C_{Z}/C_{X}^{\perp}\oplus C_{X}/C_{Z}^{\perp}\,.

(30)

We denote this code using the familiar
\llbracket n,k,d\rrbracket_{q}
notation, where
n
is the number of physical qudits (the code length),
k
is the number of logical qudits (the code dimension), and
d
is the code distance. We denote the local qudit dimension with the subscript
q
. When
q=2
, we have a qubit code and we usually omit the subscript. The code dimension
k
can be found as

\displaystyle k=n-\rank_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}{H_{X}}-\rank_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}{H_{Z}}

(31)

for
X
- and
Z
-parity check matrices
H_{X}
and
H_{Z}
, respectively, where we explicitly denote that all operations are done over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The minimum distance is given as

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in C_{Z}/C_{X}^{\perp}\\
\text{ or }c\in C_{X}/C_{Z}^{\perp}\end{subarray}}w_{H}(c)

(32)

\displaystyle=\min\{d_{X},d_{Z}\}\,.

(33)

A CSS code is considered a
low-density parity-check
(LDPC) code if the row and column weights of its parity check matrices are bounded by constants
\Delta_{\text{col}}
and
\Delta_{\text{row}}
. In this work, we mostly consider LDPC codes of weights 4, 5 and 6 as these are amenable to near-term hardware.

Finally, to reference the mathematical background given in the previous section, we can describe an
n
-qudit CSS code
\text{CSS}(C_{X},C_{Z}^{\perp})
by a length-3 chain complex
C
over the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
vector space with boundary map
\partial
, which we write as

\displaystyle C=0\xrightarrow{}C_{2}\xrightarrow{\partial_{2}}C_{1}\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}C_{0}\xrightarrow{}0

(34)

where
C_{2}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m_{Z}}
,
C_{1}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and
C_{0}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m_{X}}
, with boundary maps given by
\partial_{2}=H_{Z}^{\intercal}
and
\partial_{1}=H_{X}
which satisfy
\partial_{1}\circ\partial_{2}=0
. This chain complex representation elegantly captures the structure of a CSS code. The physical qudits reside in the middle space
C_{1}
. The
X
-checks are defined by
\partial_{1}=H_{X}
, and the
Z
-checks are defined by
\partial_{2}=H_{Z}^{\intercal}
. Crucially, the homological requirement
\partial_{1}\circ\partial_{2}=0
is precisely the CSS compatibility condition
H_{X}H_{Z}^{T}=0
. This will be useful when we generalize some of the codes that are derived from chain complexes, such as high-dimensional expander codes.

2.4
Quditization

Finally, before presenting our first qudit LDPC code, we specify what we mean by generalizing the error correction codes in this paper from qubits to qudits, a process we call
quditization
. Intuitively (or physically), this is simple: instead of considering a two-level system, a qubit, we now consider a multilevel system, a qudit. Mathematically, this introduces some subtleties, which we deal with throughout the rest of the paper.

The starting point for quditization is the promotion of the field that we work over from
\mathbb{F}_{2}
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q
is a prime power. This expands the space over which the mathematical objects that we work with are defined (i.e., vector spaces, polynomials, coefficients, chain complexes, etc), and it is this expanded space that we take advantage of to find new error correcting codes. One implication of working with
\mathbb{F}_{q}
instead of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is a modified CSS condition, where, recall, the CSS condition is that, given parity-check matrices
H_{X}
and
H_{Z}
, the following must hold:

\displaystyle H_{X}H_{Z}^{\intercal}=0\,.

(35)

Since we mostly consider CSS codes in this work, this condition must still be true for the qudit codes we present, but the way in which this is achieved for some codes is slightly different. The easiest way to ensure that
Equation

35
holds is to introduce coefficients at appropriate places, such as at the point of defining the parity-check matrices, as we will see in several codes later on.

Once the subtleties that arise when promoting codes from
\mathbb{F}_{2}
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
are handled appropriately, the primary thing left to do is to find the code parameters, that is, find
\llbracket n,k,d\rrbracket_{q}
. After this is done, in this work, we consider the code
“quditized,”
though of course for a fully-fledged qudit LDPC code, we must also provide a decoding algorithm, describe the logical operators and gates, etc. We leave these questions to future work.

3
Qudit bivariate bicycle codes

The first LDPC code that we quditize is the
bivariate bicycle code
, which was introduced in the work by Bravyi
et al.

[
14
]
. We define a qudit bivariate bicycle code of local qudit dimension
q=p^{s}
for prime
p
and
s\geq 1
by the polynomials

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}A_{1}+\alpha_{2}A_{2}+\alpha_{3}A_{3}\,,

(36)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}B_{1}+\beta_{2}B_{2}+\beta_{3}B_{3}\,,

where
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
A_{i},B_{i}
are powers of the commuting matrices
x=S_{\ell}\otimes I_{m}
and
y=I_{\ell}\otimes S_{m}
. Here,
\ell,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
I_{m}
is the
m\times m
identity matrix and
S_{\ell}
is the
cyclic shift matrix
of size
\ell\times\ell
, which is defined such that the
i\textsuperscript{th}
row has a single nonzero element equal to 1 in column
(i+1)\mod{\ell}
. For example, the first two cyclic shift matrices are

\displaystyle S_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }S_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(37)

A
and
B
are defined such that each
A_{i}
is unique and each
B_{i}
is unique; that is,
A_{i}\neq A_{j}
and
B_{i}\neq B_{j}
for
i\neq j
. The
t
-variate generalization introduced in
[
97
]
is defined similarly, except each
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
is a power of one of
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}\}
; for example, for
t=3
(the trivariate case), we have
x\equiv x_{1}=S_{\ell}\otimes I_{m}
,
x_{2}\equiv y=I_{\ell}\otimes S_{m}
, and
x_{3}\equiv z=S_{\ell}\otimes S_{m}
and each
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
is a power of
x
,
y
, or
z
. Like the toric code, the physical qudits of a bivariate or multivariate bicycle code can be arranged on a two-dimensional grid with periodic boundary conditions, where the check operators can be obtained from a single pair of
X
- and
Z
-checks by applying horizontal and vertical shifts on the grid. Unlike the toric code, though, these check operators are not geometrically local.

We define
A
and
B
as we do in
Equation

36
over the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x,y]
. This is effectively generalizing the cyclic shift matrices used to define the qubit bivariate bicycle codes to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Alternatively, we could have quditized the qubit bivariate bicycle codes using the
\ell\times\ell

circulant matrix
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, which is defined as

\displaystyle C_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}&c_{1}&c_{2}&\dots&c_{\ell-1}\\
c_{\ell-1}&c_{0}&c_{1}&\dots&c_{\ell-2}\\
c_{\ell-2}&c_{\ell-1}&c_{0}&\dots&c_{\ell-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&c_{3}&\dots&c_{0}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(38)

where
c_{0},\dots,c_{\ell-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Defining the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}_{q}[S_{\ell}]
, where
S_{\ell}
is the
\ell\times\ell
cyclic shift matrix,
C_{\ell}
is then just an element of this ring:

\displaystyle C_{\ell}=c_{0}S_{\ell}^{0}+c_{1}S_{\ell}^{1}+\dots+c_{\ell-1}S_{\ell}^{\ell-1}\,.

(39)

Circulant matrices have the nice property that any two circulant matrices commute, that is,
[C_{\ell},C^{\prime}_{\ell}]=0
. These circulant matrices then define the variables
x
and
y
used to construct the polynomials
A
and
B
. We note that the construction via the cyclic shift matrices and the construction via circulant matrices are equivalent, but for the rest of this analysis, we adopt the former.

We note a few properties of the objects that we have introduced. First,
x
and
y
must commute:

\displaystyle xy

\displaystyle=(S_{\ell}\otimes I_{m})(I_{\ell}\otimes S_{m})=S_{\ell}\otimes S_{m}

(40)

\displaystyle yx

\displaystyle=(I_{\ell}\otimes S_{m})(S_{\ell}\otimes I_{m})=S_{\ell}\otimes S_{m}

(41)

\displaystyle\implies xy

\displaystyle=yx\,.

(42)

We also have
x^{\ell}=y^{m}=I_{\ell m}
:

\displaystyle x^{\ell}

\displaystyle=(S_{\ell}\otimes I_{m})^{\ell}

(43)

\displaystyle=S_{\ell}^{\ell}\otimes I_{m}^{\ell}

(44)

\displaystyle=I_{\ell}\otimes I_{m}

(45)

\displaystyle\equiv I_{\ell m}\,,

(46)

and likewise for
y^{m}
. Using the commutation relation of
x
and
y
, we can easily check that
A
and
B
, which comprise sums of powers of
x
and
y
, also commute. Note that
A,B\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell m}
, so
A
and
B
define a qudit bivariate bicycle code with length
n=2\ell m
. With all of this in hand, we define the parity-check matrices:

\displaystyle H_{X}=[\gamma_{1}A|\gamma_{2}B]\text{ and }H_{Z}=[\delta_{1}B^{\intercal}|\delta_{2}A^{\intercal}]\,,

(47)

where, as discussed in
Section

2.3
, we have introduced a set of nonzero
block coefficients

\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
; for qubits, these coefficients are simply all 1. The CSS condition,

\displaystyle H_{X}H_{Z}^{\intercal}=0\,,

(48)

must hold, which gives us a constraint on
\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}
:

\displaystyle H_{X}H_{Z}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=[\gamma_{1}A|\gamma_{2}B]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\delta_{1}B\\
\hline\cr\delta_{2}A\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}AB+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}BA

(51)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}AB+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}AB

(52)

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2})AB\,,

(53)

where we used the fact that
A
and
B
commute. For this to be equal to 0 (if
AB\neq 0
), we must have
\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}=0
, where arithmetic is done over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For any
q
, this is true if one of the coefficients is equal to
-1
and the others are equal to 1 (or three are equal to
-1
and one is equal to 1), such as
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=\delta_{1}=1
and
\delta_{2}=-1
, giving the following parity-check matrices for qutrit bivariate bicycle codes:

\displaystyle H_{X}=[A|B]\text{ and }H_{Z}=[B^{\intercal}|-A^{\intercal}]\,.

(54)

In general, any set of
\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}
that satisfy
\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}=0
is a valid set of block coefficients. However, when searching for codes for a fixed
\{A_{i},B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
and varying
\{\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
and
\{\gamma_{i},\delta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2}
, we may without loss of generality restrict to the case above with
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=\delta_{1}=1
and
\delta_{2}=-1
. To see this, observe that scaling the parity check matrices does not change the code spaces, so we may replace
H_{X}=[\gamma_{1}A|\gamma_{2}B]
and
H_{Z}=[\delta_{1}B^{\intercal}|\delta_{2}A^{\intercal}]
with
H_{X}=[\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}A|B]
and
H_{Z}=[B^{\intercal}|\delta_{2}\delta_{1}^{-1}A^{\intercal}]
. The condition
\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}=0
then implies that
\delta_{2}\delta_{1}^{-1}A^{\intercal}=-\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}A^{\intercal}
. Now, replacing each
\alpha_{i}
by
\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}\alpha_{i}
instead, we see that
A
is replaced by
\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}A
, which in turn means we have
H_{X}=[A|B]
and
H_{Z}=[B^{\intercal}|-A^{\intercal}]
, as desired. In fact, swapping
A
and
B
only permutes the elements of the codewords and yields an equivalent code, so we may without loss of generality let
A_{1}
correspond to the lowest-degree monomial (e.g., in lexicographic order) among the
A_{i},B_{j}
with nonzero coefficient. Since scaling
H_{X}
and
H_{Z}
does not change the code space, it is also possible to fix
\alpha_{1}=1
, leaving just the five variables
\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}
and the choice of monomials
\{A_{i},B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
with
A_{1}
corresponding to the lowest-degree monomial.

We now formally present the code parameters of our qudit bivariate bicycle codes in the following theorem, which is the main result of this section. Here, we denote by
\ker(\cdot)
the kernel and by
\operatorname{rs}\left(\cdot\right)
the row space:

Proposition 1

(Qudit multivariate bicycle code parameters)
.

The matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell m\times\ell m}
, defined above, define a qudit bivariate bicycle code with parameters
\llbracket n,k,d\rrbracket_{q}
, where

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=2\ell m\,,

(55)

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=2\cdot\dim\left(\ker(A)\cap\ker(B)\right)\,,

(56)

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\min\{d_{X},d_{Z}\}\,,

(57)

where
d_{X}
and
d_{Z}
are the code distances for
X
-type and
Z
-type errors defined as

\displaystyle d_{X}

\displaystyle=\min\{\absolutevalue{v}:v\in\ker(H_{Z})\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{X}\right)\}\,,

(58)

\displaystyle d_{Z}

\displaystyle=\min\{\absolutevalue{v}:v\in\ker(H_{X})\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{Z}\right)\}\,,

(59)

where
\operatorname{rs}\left(\cdot\right)
denotes row space and
\absolutevalue{v}
is the Hamming weight of a vector
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, that is, the number of nonzero elements in
v
. Furthermore,
d_{X}=d_{Z}
, so the distance is simply
d=d_{X}=d_{Z}
.

Proof.

We can see that
n=2\ell m
by looking at the number of columns in
H_{X}
and
H_{Z}
(which both have the same dimension). Since
H_{X}=\left[\gamma_{1}A|\gamma_{2}B\right]
and
A
and
B
are
\ell m\times\ell m
matrices,
H_{X}
has dimension
(\ell m)\times(2\ell m)
, so
n=2\ell m
. Thus,
H_{X}
and
H_{Z}
are of dimension
\frac{n}{2}\times n
.

To find
k
, we follow the proof for qubits in
[
14
, Lemma 3]
. We start by using a known result for CSS codes, which we presented in
Section

2.3
:

\displaystyle k=n-\rank{H_X}-\rank{H_Z}\,,

(60)

We start by finding
\rank{H_Z}
:

\displaystyle\rank{H_Z}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}-\dim{\ker{H_{Z}^{\intercal}}}

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}-\dim{\ker{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\delta_{1}B\\
\hline\cr\delta_{2}A\end{array}\right]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}-\dim(\ker(A)\cap\ker(B))\,,

(64)

where the first line follows from a combination of the relation
\rank{H}=\rank{H^\intercal}
and the rank-nullity theorem. It turns out that
\rank{H_X}=\rank{H_Z}
, which we can see by the following transformation. Take
\Pi_{\ell}
to be a self-inverse permutation matrix such that the
i\textsuperscript{th}
column of
\Pi_{\ell}
has one nonzero entry equal to 1 (the multiplicative identity of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
) at row
j=-i\bmod{\ell}
. Define
\Pi_{m}
in the same way and take
\Pi=\Pi_{\ell}\otimes\Pi_{m}
. Using these matrices, we have
\Pi_{\ell}S_{\ell}\Pi_{\ell}=S_{\ell}^{\intercal}
and
\Pi_{m}S_{m}\Pi_{m}=S_{m}^{\intercal}
, which yields

\displaystyle A^{\intercal}=\Pi A\Pi,\quad B^{\intercal}=\Pi B\Pi\,.

(65)

Thus, we can write

\displaystyle H_{Z}

\displaystyle=[\delta_{1}B^{\intercal}|\delta_{2}A^{\intercal}]

(66)

\displaystyle=\Pi[\delta_{2}(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{1}^{-1})A|\delta_{1}(\gamma_{2}\gamma_{2}^{-1})B]\begin{bmatrix}0&\Pi\\
\Pi&0\end{bmatrix}

(67)

\displaystyle=\Pi H_{X}\begin{bmatrix}0&\delta_{2}\gamma_{1}^{-1}\Pi\\
\delta_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}\Pi&0\end{bmatrix}\,.

(68)

The matrix on the left hand side of
H_{X}
has an inverse since the permutation matrix
\Pi
is invertible, that is,
\Pi\Pi^{-1}=I_{\ell m}
, and both
\delta_{2}\gamma_{1}^{-1},\delta_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
have a multiplicative inverse in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
2
2

2

This is because
\gamma_{1},\delta_{1},\gamma_{2},\delta_{2}
are nonzero by assumption, hence
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
have multiplicative inverses
\gamma_{1}^{-1},\gamma_{2}^{-1}
which are nonzero. Thus
\delta_{2}\gamma_{1}^{-1},\delta_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}
are also nonzero and have nonzero multiplicative inverses.
.
Denoting the multiplicative inverses of
\delta_{2}\gamma_{1}^{-1}
and
\delta_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}
as
\xi_{2}
and
\xi_{1}
, respectively, we have

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0&\delta_{2}\gamma_{1}^{-1}\Pi\\
\delta_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}\Pi&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&\xi_{1}\Pi^{-1}\\
\xi_{2}\Pi^{-1}&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{2}\delta_{2}\gamma_{1}^{-1}I_{\ell m}&0\\
0&\xi_{1}\delta_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}I_{\ell m}\end{bmatrix}

(69)

\displaystyle=I_{2\ell m}\,.

(70)

Since we can obtain
H_{X}
by multiplying
H_{Z}
on the left and right by an invertible matrix, we conclude that
\rank{H_X}=\rank{H_Z}
. Using this, we have

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=n-\rank{H_X}-\rank{H_Z}

(71)

\displaystyle=n-2\left(\frac{n}{2}-\dim\left(\ker(A)\cap\ker(B)\right)\right)

(72)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\dim\left(\ker(A)\cap\ker(B)\right)\,,

(73)

as stated in the theorem.

We now prove the statements for the distance
d
. Recall, the Hamming weight
w_{H}
of
v
is the number of nonzero elements in
v
:
w_{H}(v)=\absolutevalue{\{i : v_i \neq 0\ \forall i \in[n]\}}
. Thus, to find
d
, we find the set of vectors
v
that are in the space
\ker{H_{Z}}\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{X}\right)
and
\ker{H_{X}}\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{Z}\right)
, take the vector of smallest Hamming weight for
d_{X}
and
d_{Z}
, and take the minimum value of
d_{X}
and
d_{Z}
.

Finally, we prove that finding both
d_{X}
and
d_{Z}
and taking the minimum of the two is unnecessary as
d_{X}=d_{Z}
. Let
f\in\ker{H_{Z}}\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{X}\right)
such that
|f|=d_{X}
so that
X(f)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}^{f_{j}}
is a logical operator with minimum weight
d_{X}
. Then, there exists
Z(g)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}Z_{j}^{g_{j}}
such that
f^{\intercal}g=a\neq 0
and
g\in\ker{H_{X}}\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{Z}\right)
.
Here,
f
and
g
are length
n
vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
which we take to be
f=(f_{1},f_{2})
and
g=(g_{1},g_{2})
, where
f_{i},g_{i}
are length
n/2
vectors for
i=1,2
. Then, to have
f\in\ker{H_{Z}}\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{X}\right)
and
g\in\ker{H_{X}}\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{Z}\right)
, we must have
H_{Z}f=0
and
H_{X}g=0
, which is true if and only if the following holds:

\begin{gathered}H_{Z}f=0\iff\delta_{1}B^{\intercal}f_{1}=-\delta_{2}A^{\intercal}f_{2}\\
H_{X}g=0\iff\gamma_{1}Ag_{1}=-\gamma_{2}Bg_{2}\,.\end{gathered}

(74)

Now, for the
\Pi
matrices defined above, we define

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}\Pi f_{2}\\
\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\Pi f_{1}\end{bmatrix}

(75)

and

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Pi g_{2}\\
\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}\Pi g_{1}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(76)

We now show that
H_{X}h=0
:

\displaystyle H_{X}h

\displaystyle=[\gamma_{1}A|\gamma_{2}B]\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}\Pi f_{2}\\
\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\Pi f_{1}\end{bmatrix}

(77)

\displaystyle=\Pi(\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}\gamma_{1}A^{\intercal}f_{2}+\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{2}B^{\intercal}f_{1})

(78)

\displaystyle=\Pi(\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}\gamma_{1}A^{\intercal}f_{2}-\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}\delta_{1}^{-1}A^{\intercal}f_{2})

(79)

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}\gamma_{1}-\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}\delta_{1}^{-1})\Pi A^{\intercal}f_{2}

(80)

\displaystyle=0\,.

(81)

Likewise,
H_{Z}e=0
:

\displaystyle H_{Z}e

\displaystyle=[\delta_{1}B^{\intercal}|\delta_{2}A^{\intercal}]\begin{bmatrix}\Pi g_{2}\\
\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}\Pi g_{1}\end{bmatrix}

(82)

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}B^{\intercal}\Pi g_{2}+\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}\delta_{2}A^{\intercal}\Pi g_{1}

(83)

\displaystyle=\Pi\left(\delta_{1}Bg_{2}+\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}\delta_{2}Ag_{1}\right)\,.

(84)

Using
\gamma_{1}Ag_{1}=-\gamma_{2}Bg_{2}\implies Ag_{1}=-\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}Bg_{2}
from

, we have

\displaystyle H_{Z}e

\displaystyle=\Pi\left(\delta_{1}Bg_{2}-\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}\delta_{2}\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}Bg_{2}\right)

(85)

\displaystyle=\left(\delta_{1}-\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}\delta_{2}\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}\right)\Pi Bg_{2}

(86)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(87)

Thus, we conclude that
h\in\ker{H_{X}}
and
e\in\ker{H_{Z}}
. Furthermore, since
f^{\intercal}g=a
for some
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
a\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle h^{\intercal}e

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1})f_{2}^{\intercal}\Pi\Pi g_{2}+(\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\delta_{1}\delta_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1})f_{1}^{\intercal}\Pi\Pi g_{1}

(88)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}(f_{2}^{\intercal}g_{2}+f_{1}^{\intercal}g_{1})

(89)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}(f^{\intercal}g)

(90)

\displaystyle=a\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}\,,

(91)

which implies that
h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{Z}\right)
and
e\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{X}\right)
because

is nonzero. Then,
X(e)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}^{e_{j}}
and
Z(h)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}Z_{j}^{h_{j}}
correspond to logical operators with weights
|e|
and
|h|
, respectively, since
h\in\ker{H_{X}}\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{Z}\right)
and
e\in\ker{H_{Z}}\backslash\operatorname{rs}\left(H_{X}\right)
. Hence, by using the relation

, we conclude that

\displaystyle d_{Z}

\displaystyle\leq\absolutevalue{h}

(92)

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{\gamma_1^{-1}\gamma_2 \Pi f_2}+\absolutevalue{\delta_1\delta_2^{-1} \Pi f_1}

(93)

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{f_2}+\absolutevalue{f_1}

(94)

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{f}

(95)

\displaystyle=d_{X}\,,

(96)

Starting with
Z(g)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}Z_{j}^{g_{j}}
such that
|g|=d_{Z}
, we can follow a similar argument to obtain a logical operator
X(e)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}^{e_{j}}
such that
|e|=|g|=d_{Z}
, which implies that
d_{X}\leq d_{Z}
. Thus we have
d_{Z}=d_{X}
, which we simply call
d
, concluding the proof.
∎

We conclude our discussion of qudit bivariate bicycle codes with a result that is helpful in looking for new codes in higher local qudit dimensions:

Lemma 1

(Qudit bivariate bicycle codes via extension of scalars)
.

Fix a prime power
q
and an integer
t\geq 2
. Given an
\llbracket n,k,d\rrbracket_{q}
qudit bivariate bicycle code over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
H_{X}=[\gamma_{1}A|\gamma_{2}B]
and
H_{Z}=[\delta_{1}B^{\intercal}|\delta_{2}A^{\intercal}]
for some integers
\ell,m\geq 1
, matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell m\times\ell m}
and
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, the qudit bivariate bicycle code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}
defined by extension of scalars along
\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}
, i.e., with the same
H_{X}
and the same
H_{Z}
, has parameters
\llbracket n,k,d\rrbracket_{q^{t}}
.

Proof.

First, it is clear that the same
H_{X}
and
H_{Z}
gives rise to a valid bivariate bicycle code because the condition
\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}=0
continues to hold. Let the parameters of the new code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}
be
\llbracket n^{\prime},k^{\prime},d^{\prime}\rrbracket_{q^{t}}
. From
Proposition

1
, the number of physical qubits does not change since
n^{\prime}=2\ell m=n
. Similarly,
k=2\dim(\ker(A)\cap\ker(B))
where
\ker(A)\cap\ker(B)
is the kernel of the matrix
\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A\\
\hline\cr B\end{array}\right]
when viewed as a matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, while
k^{\prime}
is defined similarly except the matrix is defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}
. These quantities are equal since the dimension of the kernel of a matrix does not change under field extensions. One way to see this to note this is true for ranks, for example, by row reduction, and then apply the rank-nullity theorem

3

3

More abstractly, this invariance is a consequence of the fact that tensoring with a field extension is an exact functor, as field extensions are flat; thus, the kernel of the linear map represented by the matrix is preserved under base change.
.

It remains to check that

does not change when we consider

and
H_{X}
as matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}
(and their kernels and row spaces as subspaces of

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
). To see this, choose

and

achieving the minimum weights

and
d^{\prime}
respectively. We can fix a basis
\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{b}\}
for
\ker_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}(H_{Z})
such that
\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{c}\}
is a basis for
\operatorname{rs}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}(H_{X})
(for
c<b
). Note that these are also bases for
\ker_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}^{n}}(H_{Z})
and
\operatorname{rs}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}^{n}}(H_{X})
respectively

4

4

This is because
\ker_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}^{n}}(H_{Z})=\ker_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}(H_{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}
and
\operatorname{rs}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}^{n}}(H_{X})=\operatorname{rs}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}(H_{X})\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}
, which follow from the exactness of tensoring with a field extension.
. Since
v\in\ker_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}(H_{Z})\backslash\operatorname{rs}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}(H_{X})
,
v
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear combination of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{b}
, with a nonzero coefficient for some
v_{i}
with
i>c
. Then,

as well, so its weight

must be greater than or equal to
d^{\prime}
, by definition of
d^{\prime}
. Similarly,
v^{\prime}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}
-linear combination of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{b}
, with a nonzero coefficient for some
v_{j}
with
j>c
. We may assume without loss of generality (by rescaling

by a nonzero constant, which does not change

) that the coefficient of
v_{j}
has a nonzero field trace
5
5

5

The field trace of
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}
is the trace of the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear transformation from
\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}
to
\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}
defined by
x\mapsto xy
. It is a fact that this is not the zero map, since
\mathbb{F}_{q^{t}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a separable field extension.
. Now, we can consider the vector

formed by applying the field trace map to each coefficient of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{b}
in the expansion of

. Since its coefficient of

is at least

is also at most that of

.

There are a few useful implications of this result. First, it limits the search space for qudit bivariate bicycle codes in higher dimensions. That is, given a good code found over
\mathbb{F}_{q}

. Another implication is that, for the same set of

3.1
Coprime qudit bivariate bicycle codes

Bivariate bicycle codes were further developed in
[
100
,
83
]
, where their algebraic properties were explored in more detail, resulting in
coprime
bivariate bicycle codes. We extend this formalism to
qudit coprime bivariate bicycle
codes and give analytic results for calculating the code dimension, avoiding some of the costly numerical search required for the more general qudit bivariate bicycle codes presented above.

Instead of performing the numerical search for
k
as is done for general bivariate bicycle codes by calculating the rank of the parity-check matrices, we can get some analytic insight on
k
by using the algebraic properties of these codes. Let
\ell
,
m
, and
q
be mutually coprime and define
z=xy
, where
x
and
y
are defined as they were above. Then, the bivariate polynomials

and

can be expressed as univariate polynomials
A(z)
and

:

Fact 1

(Univariate generator
[
83
, Thm. 2.2]
)
.

Let
q=p^{s}
for prime
p
and
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\ell
,
m
, and
q
all be mutually coprime. Then, bivariate bicycle codes allow for a univariate representation. Set
z=xy
. Since
\langle x\rangle
and
\langle y\rangle
are cyclic groups of order
\ell
and
m
, respectively,
\langle xy\rangle
is a cyclic group of order
\ell m
. More concretely, there exists a bijective ring homomorphism
\psi
such that

\displaystyle\psi:\frac{\mathbb{F}_{q}[x,y]}{(x^{\ell}-1,y^{m}-1)}\to\frac{\mathbb{F}_{q}[z]}{(z^{\ell m}-1)}\,.

(97)

This mapping is carried out by

\displaystyle\psi:x\mapsto z^{m^{-1_{\ell}}m},y\mapsto z^{\ell^{-1_{m}}\ell}\,,

(98)

where
m^{-1_{\ell}}
is the multiplicative inverse of
m
in
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
and
\ell^{-1_{m}}
is defined similarly.

For proof of this statement, we refer the reader to
[
83
, Theorem 2.2]
. Then, we have the following result:

Proposition 2

(Qudit coprime bivariate bicycle code dimension)
.

Given
q=p^{s}
for prime
p
and
s\geq 1
, coprime integers
\ell
and
m
that are also coprime to
q
, and
z=xy
for
x
and
y
defined above, let
h(z)=\gcd(A(z),B(z),z^{\ell m}-1)
. Then, the bivariate bicycle code defined by
A(z)
and
B(z)
of length
n=2\ell m
has dimension

\displaystyle k=2\deg(h(z))\,.

(99)

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as the proof for the equivalent statement in
[
83
]
. Let
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell m\times\ell m}
, and note that
\langle x\rangle
and
\langle y\rangle
are cyclic groups of orders
\ell
and
m
, respectively. Define
z=xy
. Then,
\langle z\rangle
is a cyclic group of order
\ell m
, so any monomial in the set
\{x^{i}y^{j}|0\leq i\leq\ell,0\leq j\leq m\}
can be expressed as a power of
z
. Then, any polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x,y]/(x^{\ell}-1,y^{m}-1)
can be expressed as one in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[z]/(z^{\ell m}-1)
under the isomorphism
\psi
in
1
. The column space of the parity-check operator
H_{X}=[\gamma_{1}A|\gamma_{2}B]
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{cs}\left(H_{X}\right)

\displaystyle=\{H_{X}x|x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2\ell m}\}

(100)

\displaystyle=\{\gamma_{1}Au+\gamma_{2}Bv|u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell m}\}\,,

(101)

which differs from the qubit case only by the introduction of the block coefficients
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, which we can drop by absorbing these into
u,v
respectively. This can be written as

\displaystyle\operatorname{cs}\left(H_{X}\right)=\{A(z)u(z)+B(z)v(z)|u(z),v(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[z]/(z^{\ell m}-1)\}\,.

(102)

Since
\mathbb{F}_{q}[z]/(z^{\ell m}-1)
is the quotient of a univariate polynomial ring, all its ideals are principal (see
Definition

2
). Thus,
\operatorname{cs}\left(H_{X}\right)
, which is the ideal generated by
A(z)
and
B(z)
, is a principal ideal and is generated by
h(z)=\gcd(A(z),B(z),z^{\ell m}-1)
and
\rank{H_X}=\dim(\operatorname{cs}\left(H_{X}\right))=\ell m-\deg(h(z))
. The code dimension is then

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=2\ell m-2\rank{H_X}

(103)

\displaystyle=2\ell m-2(\ell m-\deg(h(z)))

(104)

\displaystyle=2\deg(h(z))\,.

(105)

∎

4
Qudit hypergraph product codes

We now move to
qudit hypergraph product
(HGP) codes
[
95
,
82
]
. The HGP construction provides a powerful method to generate large quantum codes from two smaller, classical codes. Conceptually, it generalizes the product structure of the toric code. By taking the
“product”
of the parity-check matrices of the classical codes, we can obtain quantum LDPC codes with significantly better parameters (i.e., the encoding rate and the distance) than the surface code. There are two frameworks with which we can work: an algebraic approach similar to what we presented above for bivariate bicycle codes or a homological approach. For completion, we quditize HGP codes in both frameworks. We start with the algebraic framework, then quditize the HGP codes in the homological framework, and finally we conclude by studying in detail a particular type of HGP codes, the La-cross codes.

4.1
Algebraic framework

We start with two classical linear LDPC codes
C_{A}
and
C_{B}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where, as usual,
q=p^{s}
for prime
p
and
s\in\mathbb{N}
. The codes have parameters
[n_{i},k_{i},d_{i}]_{q}
for
i=A,B
, where
n_{i}
,
k_{i}
, and
d_{i}
are the code size, dimension, and distance, respectively. These codes are represented by their parity-check matrix
H_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}^{\intercal}\times n_{i}}
, where
n_{i}^{\intercal}:=n_{i}-k_{i}
is the number of checks. In terms of the parity-check matrix, the code is simply given as
C_{i}=\ker{H_{i}}
. For each of these classical codes, we have a corresponding
transposed code
, denoted
C_{i}^{\intercal}
, with parameters
[n_{i}^{\intercal},k_{i}^{\intercal},d_{i}^{\intercal}]_{q}
with parity-check matrix
H_{i}^{\intercal}
. Thus, the transposed code is
C_{i}^{\intercal}=\ker{H_{i}^{\intercal}}
. The HGP construction then combines the classical linear codes along with their transposes to give an
\llbracket n,k,d\rrbracket_{q}
quantum stabilizer code with quantum parity-check matrices

\displaystyle H_{X}

\displaystyle=[\gamma_{1}H_{A}\otimes I_{n_{B}}|\gamma_{2}I_{n_{A}^{\intercal}}\otimes H_{B}^{\intercal}]\,,

(107)

\displaystyle H_{Z}

\displaystyle=[\delta_{1}I_{n_{A}}\otimes H_{B}|\delta_{2}H_{A}^{\intercal}\otimes I_{n_{B}^{\intercal}}]\,,

(108)

where we again introduce the block coefficients
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
to satisfy the CSS condition:

\displaystyle H_{X}H_{Z}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=[\gamma_{1}H_{A}\otimes I_{n_{B}}|\gamma_{2}I_{n_{A}^{\intercal}}\otimes H_{B}^{\intercal}]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\delta_{1}I_{n_{A}}\otimes H_{B}^{\intercal}\\
\hline\cr\delta_{2}H_{A}\otimes I_{n_{B}^{\intercal}}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}H_{A}\otimes H_{B}^{\intercal}+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}H_{A}\otimes H_{B}^{\intercal}

(111)

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2})H_{A}\otimes H_{B}^{\intercal}\,,

(112)

which must be equal to 0. This is trivially true in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
for
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}=1
, but does not hold over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
for odd
q
. Thus, to satisfy the CSS condition, we require

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}=0\,,

(113)

where arithmetic is done over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The easiest choice (which we made for the qudit bivariate bicycle codes) is to take
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=\delta_{1}=1
and
\delta_{2}=-1
, but any choice of block coefficients that satisfy
\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}=0
suffices
6
6

6

By a similar reasoning as the one presented in the paragraph after
Equation

54
, it is in fact sufficient (for the purpose of searching for new codes) to restrict the search space to the special case
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\delta_{1}=1
and
\delta_{2}=-1
.
. The number of stabilizers is
n_{1}n_{2}^{\intercal}+n_{2}n_{1}^{\intercal}
, which is the number of rows in
H
. The quantum code parameters are the same as they are for the qubit case

, which are given as
n=n_{A}n_{B}^{\intercal}+n_{A}^{\intercal}n_{B}
,

,

, all of which we get from our choice of the classical, linear LDPC seed codes.

4.2
Homology framework

Alternatively, since an HGP code is a CSS code, it can be described using the language of chain complexes and homology

C_{A}

\llbracket n_{A},k_{A},d_{A}\rrbracket_{q}

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:0\xrightarrow{\partial_{2}^{A}}A_{1}\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}^{A}}A_{0}\xrightarrow{\partial_{0}^{A}}0

and

\partial_{1}^{A}

\displaystyle C_{A}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\ker(\partial_{1}^{A})^{\intercal}=\{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{A}^{\intercal}}:(\partial_{1}^{A})^{\intercal}c=0\}\,,

(116)

\displaystyle C_{B}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\ker(\partial_{1}^{B})^{\intercal}=\{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{B}^{\intercal}}:(\partial_{1}^{B})^{\intercal}c=0\}\,,

(117)

with parameters
\llbracket n_{A}^{\intercal},k_{A}^{\intercal},d_{A}^{\intercal}\rrbracket_{q}
and
\llbracket n_{B}^{\intercal},k_{B}^{\intercal},d_{B}^{\intercal}\rrbracket_{q}
, respectively. The tensor product between chain complexes

\displaystyle(A\otimes B)_{k}=\bigoplus_{i,j:i+j=k}A_{i}\otimes B_{j}\,.

(118)

For

\displaystyle\partial_{i+j}^{A\otimes B}(u\otimes v)

\displaystyle=(\partial_{i}^{A}\otimes I_{j}^{B})(u\otimes v)+(-1)^{i}(I_{i}^{A}\otimes\partial_{j}^{B})(u\otimes v)

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}^{A}(u)\otimes v+(-1)^{i}u\otimes\partial_{j}^{B}(v)\,,

(120)

where
I_{i}^{A}
and

are identity operators over
A_{i}
and
B_{j}
, respectively. Taking the chain complexes
A
and
B
in
Equation

114
, we obtain the tensor product chain complex

given by

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\partial_{3}^{A\otimes B}}A_{1}\otimes B_{1}

(121)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\partial_{2}^{A\otimes B}}(A_{0}\otimes B_{1})\oplus(A_{1}\otimes B_{0})

(122)

(123)

(124)

which gives us a hypergraph product code with

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}^{A\otimes B}=\left[I_{0}^{A}\otimes\partial_{1}^{B}|\partial_{1}^{A}\otimes I_{0}^{B}\right]

\displaystyle H_{Z}

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{2}^{A\otimes B}\right)^{\intercal}=\left[\left(\partial_{1}^{A}\right)^{\intercal}\otimes I_{1}^{B}|-I_{1}^{A}\otimes\left(\partial_{1}^{B}\right)^{\intercal}\right]\,,

satisfying the CSS code condition

\displaystyle H_{X}H_{Z}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=(I_{0}^{A}\otimes\partial_{1}^{B})(\partial_{1}^{A}\otimes I_{1}^{B})-(\partial_{1}^{A}\otimes I_{0}^{B})(I_{1}^{A}\otimes\partial_{1}^{B})

(129)

The number of logical qudits

encoded is given by the dimension of the first homology group of

, that is,
H_{1}(A\otimes B)=\ker{\partial_{1}^{A\otimes B}}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{A\otimes B}
.
Note that this construction is the HGP construction in Section
4.1
with block coefficients given by
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=\delta_{1}=1
and
\delta_{2}=-1
.

Proposition 3

(Qudit hypergraph product code parameters)
.

The
\llbracket n,k,d\rrbracket_{q}
parameters of a qudit hypergraph product code defined by parity check matrices
H_{X},H_{Z}
in
Equation

124
such that
\partial_{1}^{A},\partial_{1}^{B},(\partial_{1}^{A})^{\intercal},(\partial_{1}^{B})^{\intercal}
are not full-rank is given by

,

, and
d=\min\{d_{A},d_{A}^{\intercal},d_{B},d_{B}^{\intercal}\}
.

Proof.

The number of data qudits of a CSS code is the dimension of the first vector space of the chain complex that defines it.
For the qudit HGP code the first vector space is
(A_{0}\otimes B_{1})\oplus(A_{1}\otimes B_{0})
, which is a vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of dimension
n_{A}n_{B}^{\intercal}+n_{A}^{\intercal}n_{B}
.

Now we derive the number of logical qudits. By the Künneth formula
[
55
]
, the
k\textsuperscript{th}
homology group of
A\otimes B
is given by

\displaystyle H_{k}(A\otimes B)=\bigoplus_{i,j:i+j=k}H_{i}(A)\otimes H_{j}(B)\,.

(130)

The number of logical qudits
k
in the HGP code defined by
H_{X},H_{Z}
in
Equation

124
is given by the first homology group of
A\otimes B
in
Equation

124
:

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\dim{H_{1}\left(A\otimes B\right)}

(131)

\displaystyle=\dim{H_{1}(A)}\dim{H_{0}(B)}+\dim{H_{0}(A)}\dim{H_{1}(B)}

(132)

\displaystyle=\dim{\ker{\partial_{1}^{A}}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{A}}\dim{\ker{\partial_{0}^{B}}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{1}^{B}}+\dim{\ker{\partial_{0}^{A}}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{1}^{A}}\dim{\ker{\partial_{1}^{B}}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{B}}\,,

(133)

where the second equality comes from the Künneth formula and the third equality comes from the definition of the first and zeroth homology groups of
A
and
B
. Note that
\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{A}=\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{A}=\{0\}
, since the second vector space of
A
and
B
are both trivial. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\dim{\ker{\partial_{1}^{A}}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{A}}

\displaystyle=\dim{\ker{\partial_{1}^{A}}}

(134)

\displaystyle=\dim{C_{A}}

(135)

\displaystyle=k_{A}

(136)

and

\displaystyle\dim{\ker{\partial_{1}^{B}}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{B}}

\displaystyle=\dim{\ker{\partial_{1}^{B}}}

(137)

\displaystyle=\dim{C_{B}}

(138)

\displaystyle=k_{B}\,.

(139)

Also, note that
\ker{\partial_{0}^{A}}=A_{0}
and
\ker{\partial_{0}^{B}}=B_{0}
, since the
-1\textsuperscript{st}
vector spaces of
A
and
B
are trivial. On the other hand,
\operatorname{im}\partial_{1}^{A}=\operatorname{rs}\left(\partial_{1}^{A}\right)^{\intercal}
and
\operatorname{im}\partial_{1}^{B}=\operatorname{rs}\left(\partial_{1}^{B}\right)^{\intercal}
. At the same time, the transposed codes
C_{A}^{\intercal}
and
C_{B}^{\intercal}
are given by
\ker{\partial_{1}^{A}}^{\intercal}
and
\ker{\partial_{1}^{B}}^{\intercal}
, respectively. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\dim{\ker{\partial_{0}^{A}}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{1}^{A}}

\displaystyle=\dim{A_{0}/\operatorname{rs}\left(\partial_{1}^{A}\right)^{\intercal}}

(140)

\displaystyle=\dim A_{0}-\dim\operatorname{rs}\left(\partial_{1}^{A}\right)^{\intercal}

(141)

\displaystyle=\dim A_{0}-\dim\operatorname{cs}\left(\partial_{1}^{A}\right)^{\intercal}

(142)

\displaystyle=\dim\ker{\partial_{1}^{A}}^{\intercal}

(143)

\displaystyle=\dim{C_{A}^{\intercal}}

(144)

\displaystyle=k_{A}^{\intercal}

(145)

and

\displaystyle\dim{\ker{\partial_{0}^{B}}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{1}^{B}}

\displaystyle=\dim{B_{0}/\operatorname{rs}\left(\partial_{1}^{B}\right)^{\intercal}}

(146)

\displaystyle=\dim{C_{B}^{\intercal}}

(147)

\displaystyle=k_{B}^{\intercal}\,.

(148)

Substituting
Equations

136
,
139
,
145
and
148
into the expression for
k
in
Equation

131
gives
k=k_{A}k_{B}^{\intercal}+k_{A}^{\intercal}k_{B}
.

Finally, we derive the distance. Recall that the distance of a CSS code with parity check matrices
H_{X}
and
H_{Z}
with corresponding classical codes
C_{X}
and
C_{Z}
with parameters
[n_{X},k_{X},d_{X}]
and
[n_{Z},k_{Z},d_{Z}]
, respectively, is given by
d=\min\{d_{X},d_{Z}\}
where

\displaystyle d_{X}

\displaystyle=\min\{\absolutevalue{v}:v\in\ker\partial_{1}^{A\otimes B}\backslash\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{A\otimes B}\}

(149)

\displaystyle d_{Z}

\displaystyle=\min\{\absolutevalue{v}:v\in\ker(\partial_{2}^{A\otimes B})^{\intercal}\backslash\operatorname{im}(\partial_{1}^{A\otimes B})^{\intercal}\}\,,

where
\partial_{1}^{A\otimes B}=H_{X}
and
(\partial_{2}^{A\otimes B})^{\intercal}=H_{Z}
.
The distances of the seed codes
C_{A}
and
C_{B}
and their transpose codes
C_{A}^{\intercal}
and
C_{B}^{\intercal}
are given by

\displaystyle d_{A}

\displaystyle=\min\{\absolutevalue{v}:v\in\ker\partial_{1}^{A}\setminus\{0\}\}

(150)

\displaystyle d_{B}

\displaystyle=\min\{\absolutevalue{v}:v\in\ker\partial_{1}^{B}\setminus\{0\}\}

(151)

\displaystyle d_{A}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\min\{|v|:v\in\ker(\partial_{1}^{A})^{\intercal}\setminus\{0\}\}

(152)

\displaystyle d_{B}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\min\{|v|:v\in\ker(\partial_{1}^{B})^{\intercal}\setminus\{0\}\}\,,

(153)

respectively.

Let
{\mathrm{bas}}(X)
be the presumed basis of vector space
X
(for vector spaces
A_{j},B_{j}
in the chain complex
A,B
, their bases
{\mathrm{bas}}(A_{j}),{\mathrm{bas}}(B_{j})
are determined by the parity check matrices
\partial_{1}^{A},\partial_{1}^{B}
) and
{\mathrm{supp}}(v)
be the support of vector
v
.
Then consider

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{A}_{1}(p)=\{a^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{bas}}(A_{1})\mid\exists b\in{\mathrm{bas}}(B_{0})\text{ such that }a^{\prime}\otimes b\in{\mathrm{supp}}(p)\}\,,\\
\mathcal{B}_{1}(q)=\{b^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{bas}}(B_{1})\mid\exists a\in{\mathrm{bas}}(A_{0})\text{ such that }a\otimes b^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{supp}}(q)\}\end{gathered}

(154)

for
p\in A_{1}\otimes B_{0}
and
q\in A_{0}\otimes B_{1}
.
Now let
\partial_{1}^{A_{1}(p)}
being the restriction of
\partial_{1}^{A}
to
A_{1}(p):={\mathrm{span}}(\mathcal{A}_{1}(p))
and
\partial_{1}^{B(q)}
the restriction of
\partial_{1}^{B}
to
B_{1}(q):={\mathrm{span}}(\mathcal{B}(q))
so that for all
u\in A_{1}(p)
and
v\in B_{1}(q)
it holds that

\partial_{1}^{A}(u)=\partial_{1}^{A(p)}(u)\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{1}^{B}(v)=\partial_{1}^{B(q)}(v)\,,

(155)

which are the boundary maps of chain complexes

A(p):A_{1}(p)\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}^{A(p)}}A_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad B(q):B_{1}(q)\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}^{B(q)}}B_{0}\,,

(156)

respectively. The product chain complex
C(p,q):=A(p)\otimes B(q)
therefore is given by

\begin{gathered}C(p,q):A_{1}(p)\otimes B_{1}(q)\rightarrow A_{0}\otimes B_{1}(q)\oplus A_{1}(p)\otimes B_{0}\rightarrow A_{0}\otimes B_{0}\,,\end{gathered}

(157)

so
Equation

155
implies that

\partial_{1}^{A}\otimes I_{0}^{B}(p)=\partial_{1}^{A(p)}\otimes I_{0}^{B}(p)\quad\text{and}\quad I_{0}^{A}\otimes\partial_{1}^{B}(q)=I_{0}^{A}\otimes\partial_{1}^{B(q)}(q)\,.

(158)

Now consider a
w\in(A\otimes B)_{1}=(A_{0}\otimes B_{1})\oplus(A_{1}\otimes B_{0})
and its restriction to
A_{0}\otimes B_{1}
(resp.
A_{1}\otimes B_{0}
), denoted by
p
(resp.
q
) so that
w=p\oplus q
.
We show that if
w\in\ker\partial_{1}^{A\otimes B}
and
|w|<\min\{d_{A},d_{B}\}
, then
w\in\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{A\otimes B}
.
Note that all
u\in A_{1}(p)
has weight
|u|<d_{A}
since
\dim A_{1}(p)=|\mathcal{A}_{1}(p)|\leq|w|<d_{A}
.
Thus, because
|u^{\prime}|\geq d_{A}
for all
u^{\prime}\in\ker\partial_{1}^{A}\setminus\{0\}
it must hold that

u\in A_{1}(p)\Rightarrow u\notin(\ker\partial_{1}^{A}\setminus\{0\})\,.

(159)

By a similar argument, all
v\in B_{1}(q)
has weight
|v|<d_{B}
since
\dim B_{1}(q)=|\mathcal{B}_{1}(q)|\leq|w|<d_{B}
, so that

v\in B_{1}(q)\Rightarrow v\notin(\ker\partial_{1}^{B}\setminus\{0\})\,.

(160)

Therefore the first homology group of
A(p)
and
B(q)
are trivial, i.e.

H_{1}(A(p))=\{0\}\quad\text{and}\quad H_{1}(B(q))=\{0\}\,,

(161)

which implies that the first homology group of
C(p,q)
is also trivial:

\displaystyle H_{1}(C(p,q))=H_{0}(A(p))\otimes H_{1}(B(q))\oplus H_{1}(A(p))\otimes H_{0}(B(q))=\{0\}\,,

(162)

where the first equality is by the Künneth formula.

Now, by the assumption that
w=p\oplus q\in\ker\partial_{1}^{A\otimes B}
we have
w\in\ker\partial_{1}^{C(p,q)}
because
w=p\oplus q\in C(p,q)_{1}=A_{0}\otimes B_{1}(q)\oplus A_{1}(p)\otimes B_{0}\subseteq(A\otimes B)_{1}
.
But because
H_{1}(C(p,q))=\{0\}
and because
\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{C(p,q)}\subseteq\ker\partial_{1}^{C(p,q)}
(due to
\partial_{1}^{C(p,q)}\partial_{2}^{C(p,q)}=0
) we have
\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{C(p,q)}=\ker\partial_{1}^{C(p,q)}
.
So there exists some
w^{\prime}\in C(p,q)_{2}=A_{1}(p)\otimes B_{1}(Q)
such that
\partial_{2}^{C(p,q)}(w^{\prime})=w
.
Since
A_{1}(p)\otimes B_{1}(Q)\subseteq A_{1}\otimes B_{1}=(A\otimes B)_{2}
therefore
w^{\prime}\in(A\otimes B)_{2}
such that
\partial_{2}^{A\otimes B}(w^{\prime})=\partial_{2}^{C(p,q)}(w^{\prime})=w
.
Thus
w\in\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}^{A\otimes B}
.

By the same line of argument, we can also show that if
w\in\ker(\partial_{2}^{A\otimes B})^{\intercal}
and
|w|<\min\{d_{A}^{\intercal},d_{B}^{\intercal}\}
, then
w\in\operatorname{im}(\partial_{1}^{A\otimes B})^{\intercal}
. Thus, combining both bounds we have

d=\min\{d_{X},d_{Z}\}\geq\min\{d_{A},d_{A}^{\intercal},d_{B},b_{B}^{\intercal}\}\,,

(163)

where
d_{X},d_{Z}
are given in
Equation

149
.

Now we show that
d\leq\min\{d_{A},d_{A}^{\intercal},d_{B},b_{B}^{\intercal}\}
.
We adopt the proof of Eq. (19) in
[
106
]
.
For
j\in\{0,1\}
, let
\mathcal{A}_{j}
and
\mathcal{B}_{j}
be sets of basis for
H_{j}(A)
and
H_{j}(B)
, respectively.
Then by the Künneth formula

\displaystyle H_{1}(A\otimes B)

\displaystyle=H_{0}(A)\otimes H_{1}(B)\oplus H_{1}(A)\otimes H_{0}(B)

(164)

\displaystyle=(A_{0}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{1}^{A})\otimes(\ker\partial_{1}^{B}/\{0\})\oplus(\ker\partial_{1}^{A}/\{0\})\otimes(B_{0}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{1}^{B})\,.

(165)

\mathcal{A}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{B}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{B}_{0}
is a basis of
H_{1}(A\otimes B)
. Suppose that
\mathcal{A}_{1}
(resp.
\mathcal{B}_{1}
) contains a class
[u]\in H_{1}(A)
where
u\in\ker\partial_{1}^{A}
is a minimum weight codeword i.e.
|u|=d_{A}
(resp. contains class
[v]\in H_{1}(B)
such that
v\in\ker\partial_{1}^{B}
and
|v|=d_{B}
). Note also that for a basis element
e\in A_{0}\setminus\operatorname{im}\partial_{1}^{A}
(which always exist since
\partial_{1}^{A}
is not full-rank), the zeroth homology group
H_{0}(A)=A_{0}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{1}^{A}
contains a class
[e]=\{e+f:f\in\operatorname{im}\partial_{1}^{A}\}
. Since
[e]
contains
e
, which is a basis element (of weight 1), therefore
\tilde{d}_{A}:=\min\{|t|:[t]\in H_{0}(A)\}=1
. By a similar argument, we also have
\tilde{d}_{B}:=\min\{|t|:[t]\in H_{0}(B)\}=1
. Combining these facts with
\mathcal{A}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{B}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{B}_{0}
being a basis of
H_{1}(A\otimes B)
, we have

\displaystyle d_{X}\leq\min\{\tilde{d}_{A}d_{B},d_{A}\tilde{d}_{B}\}=\min\{d_{B},d_{A}\}\,.

(166)

By considering cohomology group

\displaystyle H^{1}(A\otimes B)

\displaystyle=H^{0}(A)\otimes H^{1}(B)\oplus H^{1}(A)\otimes H^{0}(B)

(167)

\displaystyle=(\ker(\partial_{1}^{A})^{\intercal}/\{0\})\otimes(B_{1}/\operatorname{im}(\partial_{1}^{B})^{\intercal})\oplus(A_{1}/\operatorname{im}(\partial_{1}^{A})^{\intercal})\otimes(\ker(\partial_{1}^{B})^{\intercal}/\{0\})\,,

(168)

the same line of argument give us

\displaystyle d_{Z}\leq\min\{\tilde{d}_{A}d_{B}^{\intercal},d_{A}^{\intercal}\tilde{d}_{B}\}=\min\{d_{B},d_{A}\}\,.

(169)

Therefore, the distance of the HGP code is upper bounded as

\displaystyle d=\min\{d_{X},d_{Z}\}\leq\min\{d_{A},d_{B},d_{A}^{\intercal},d_{B}^{\intercal}\}\,,

(170)

which completes the proof.
∎

4.3
Qudit La-cross codes

We now specify the details of a specific type of qudit HGP code, namely the quditized
La-cross codes

[
82
]
. The La-cross code is obtained by taking both seed codes of the HGP code to be a length-
n_{c}
cyclic code described by parity-check matrix
H
, which gives a CSS code with check matrices

\displaystyle H_{X}

\displaystyle=[I_{n}\otimes H|H^{\intercal}\otimes I_{n^{\intercal}}]

(171)

and

\displaystyle H_{Z}

\displaystyle=[H\otimes I_{n}|-I_{n^{\intercal}}\otimes H^{\intercal}]\,.

(172)

Again, we take the block coefficients to be
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=\delta_{1}=1
and
\delta_{2}=-1
. Before moving on with the qudit La-cross code construction, we formally define cyclic codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and discuss their properties relevant to La-cross codes:

Definition 4

(
\mathbb{F}_{q}
cyclic codes)
.

A length-
n
linear code
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is a
cyclic code
if, for any
c=(c_{0},\ldots,c_{n-1})\in C
, it holds that
(c_{n-1},c_{0},\ldots,c_{n-2})\in C
.

A cyclic code
C
can be described in terms of polynomials in a quotient polynomial ring
\mathcal{R}:=\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]/(x^{n}-1)
, where a codeword
c\in C
corresponds to a polynomial
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}x^{i}}
(for a more extensive discussion, see, for example,
[
48
, Chapter 4]
). In its polynomial representation,
C
can be described as an
ideal
of
\mathcal{R}
. Recall the definition in
Definition

2
, an ideal is a subset
I
of
\mathcal{R}
such that:

1.

0\in I
,

2.

\forall f,g\in I,f+g\in I
, and

3.

\forall f\in I
and
\forall g\in\mathcal{R}
, it holds that
fg\in I
.

Note that by considering the set of polynomials
I(C)=\{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}x^{i}}:c\in C\}
it holds that:

1.

The zero polynomial
0
is in
I(C)
since
0^{n}\in C
.

2.

For polynomials
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}x^{i}}
and
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}^{\prime}x^{i}}
in
I(C)
, it holds that
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{(c_{i}+c_{i}^{\prime})x^{i}}
, since for
c,c^{\prime}\in C
we have
c+c^{\prime}\in C
.

3.

For any
f^{\prime}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{f_{i}^{\prime}x^{i}}\in\mathcal{R}
and
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}x^{i}}\in I(C)
, it holds that the polynomial
f=f^{\prime}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}x^{i}}
is in
I(C)
since

\displaystyle f=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}{f_{j}^{\prime}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}x^{i+j}}}

(173)

is nothing but an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear combination of
\{\text{shift}_{j}(c)\}_{j}
, where
\text{shift}_{j}(c)
is a shift of elements of
c
to the right
j
times, that is,
c\mapsto(c_{n-1-j},\ldots,c_{n-1},c_{0},\ldots,c_{n-j-2})
. Namely,
\sum_{j}{f_{j}^{\prime}\,\text{shift}_{j}(c)}\in C
.

Thus, we can always describe
C
as an ideal
I(C)\subseteq\mathcal{R}
. On the other hand, since
\mathcal{R}
is a
principal ideal ring
, there always exists a
generator polynomial

g\in\mathcal{R}
such that
C
is described by an ideal

\displaystyle I={\langle}g{\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}}=\{fg:f\in\mathcal{R}\}\,.

(174)

We may always replace
g
by the greatest common divisor of
g
and
x^{n}-1
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
, so we can assume
g
divides
x^{n}-1
when viewed as polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
. For a polynomial
f=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{f_{i}x^{i}}\in\mathcal{R}
, the elements of
I
are of the form

\displaystyle fg=\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1}{f_{i}g_{j}x^{i+j}}\,,

(175)

where the polynomial
f=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{f_{i}x^{i}}\in I
corresponds to codeword
(f_{0},\ldots,f_{n-1})
. For any given generator polynomial
g
, it can be checked that for an ideal
I={\langle}g{\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}}
, the set
C(I)=\{(f_{0},\ldots,f_{n-1}):f\in{\langle}g{\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is a cyclic code:

1.

0^{n}\in C(I)
because
0\in I
.

2.

If
(c_{0},\cdots,c_{n-1}),(c_{0}^{\prime},\cdots,c_{n-1}^{\prime})\in C(I)
, then
(c_{0}-c_{0}^{\prime},\cdots,c_{n-1}-c_{n-1}^{\prime})\in C(I)
because for
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}x^{i}}
and
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}^{\prime}x^{i}}
in
I
, it must be that
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{(c_{i}-c_{i}^{\prime})x^{i}}
is also in
I
.

3.

If
(c_{0},\cdots,c_{n-1})\in C(I)
, then
(c_{n-1},c_{0},\cdots,c_{n-2})\in C(I)
because
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}x^{i}}
in
I
implies that
x\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}x^{i}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i}x^{i+1}}=c_{n-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{c_{i-1}x^{i}}
is in
I
(using that
x^{n}=1
in
\mathcal{R}
).

Thus, we can see that for any
c\in C(I)
, it holds that
(c_{n-1},c_{0},\ldots,c_{n-2})\in C(I)
, which implies that
C(I)
is a cyclic code.

A parity-check matrix
H
of a code
C
(i.e., a matrix
H
such that
Hc=0
for all
c\in C
) can also be described by a polynomial
h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
, called the
check polynomial
, which we encountered for the coprime qudit bivariate bicycle codes in
Section

3.1
. For a generator polynomial
g
(dividing
x^{n}-1
) of the ideal
I
, the check polynomial
h
is given by

\displaystyle h=\frac{x^{n}-1}{g}

(176)

so that for a polynomial
c=fg=\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1}{f_{i}g_{j}x^{i+j}}\in I={\langle}g{\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}}
for some
f\in\mathcal{R}
, it holds that

\displaystyle hc=hfg=(x^{n}-1)f=0\,,

(177)

since
x^{n}-1=0
in
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]/(x^{n}-1)
.

The dimension
k
of an
[n,k]
cyclic code
C
with generator polynomial
g
and check polynomial
h
can be determined from either the degree of
g
or the degree of
h
. In particular, the dimension
k
of
C
is given by

\displaystyle k=n-\text{deg}(g)\,,

(178)

as can be seen in, for example,
[
48
, Theorem 4.2.1]
). Since
hg=x^{n}-1
by the definition of the check polynomial of
h
in

5
Qudit subsystem hypergraph product simplex codes

We now quditize subsystem hypergraph product (SHP) codes
[
58
]
, specifically the subsystem hypergraph product simplex (SHYPS) codes
[
70
]
. SHYPS codes are a combination of SHP codes and classical simplex codes and allow for efficient implementation of Clifford operations. As their name suggests, SHYPS codes are a type of of
quantum subsystem code
, which can be thought of as a generalization of stabilizer codes
[
61
]
. We first give an overview of classical simplex codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, then an overview of SHP codes for qubits, and finally combine these into qudit SHYPS codes.

5.1
Classical simplex codes

We start by reviewing classical binary simplex codes and then generalize to
q
-ary simplex codes. We take
r
to be an integer greater than or equal to 3,
r\geq 3
, and define
n_{r}:=2^{r}-1
and
d_{r}:=2^{r-1}
. Then, the classical simplex codes
C(r)
are a family of
[n_{r},r,d_{r}]
-linear codes that are dual to the Hamming codes. For
3\leq r<500
, it is known that there is a polynomial
h(x)=1+x^{a}+x^{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/(x^{n_{r}}-1)
such that
\gcd(h(x),x^{n_{r}}-1)
is a primitive polynomial of degree
r
. The
n_{r}\times n_{r}
matrix

\displaystyle H=\begin{pmatrix}h(x)\\
xh(x)\\
\vdots\\
x^{n_{r}-1}h(x)\end{pmatrix}

(182)

then defines a parity-check matrix for
C(r)
, we use the polynomial notation for cyclic matrices (similar to that used for the bivariate bicycle codes in
Section

3
):

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n_{r}-1}{a_{i}x^{i}\pmod{x^{n_{r}}-1}}\mapsto(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n_{r}-1})\,,

(183)

where
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n_{r}-1})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n_{r}}
. These codes have parameters
[2^{r}-1,r,2^{r-1}]
.

Over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we have the
q
-ary simplex codes, denoted
S(q,r)
, with code parameters
[n_{r},r,q^{r-1}]_{q}
, where, as usual,
q
is a prime power and
n_{r}=(q^{r}-1)/(q-1)
. We denote by
G
the generator matrix of this code, whose columns are in one-to-one correspondence with the elements of the projective space
PG(r-1,q)
, where each column is a chosen representative of the corresponding element. Then, if we write
G
in standard form as
G=[I_{k}|P]
, we can use the constraint that
GH^{\intercal}=0
, where
H
is the parity-check matrix, to find that
H=[-P^{\intercal}|I_{n-k}]
. Alternatively, given
G
, we can find
H
by using the relation
GH^{\intercal}=0
, as mentioned in
Section

2.3
.

5.2
Subsystem codes

Quantum subsystem codes are a generalization of stabilizer codes in that they are defined with respect to a subgroup
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}
called the
gauge group
, where, recall,
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the
n
-qubit Pauli group. The effectively adds a new set of qubits to the code that we call
“gauge qubits.”
We do not need to protect the information stored in these gauge qubits. Instead of measuring high-weight stabilizers directly, the error correction procedure involves measuring low-weight
“gauge operators”
(the generators of
\mathcal{G}
). The actual stabilizers are then products of these gauge operators.

We can define subsystem codes formally according to the discussion in Ref.
[
70
, Sec. VIII.B]
. Given
\mathcal{G}
, the corresponding stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}
is the center of
\mathcal{G}
modulo phases:

\displaystyle\langle S,iI\rangle=Z(\mathcal{G}):=C_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}(\mathcal{G})\cap\mathcal{G}\,.

(184)

The fixed point space is nontrivial and decomposes into
C_{\mathcal{L}}\otimes C_{\mathcal{G}}
, where elements of
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{S}
fix
C_{\mathcal{L}}
, where
C_{\mathcal{L}}
denotes the set of logical qubits and
C_{\mathcal{G}}
denotes the set of gauge qubits. These codes have two types of logical operators: the operators given by
C_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}(\mathcal{G})\backslash\mathcal{G}
that act nontrivially on
C_{\mathcal{L}}
, which we call
bare logicals
, and the operators given by
C_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}(\mathcal{S})\backslash\mathcal{G}
that act on
C_{\mathcal{L}}
and
C_{\mathcal{G}}
, which we call
dressed logicals
. The minimum distance is given by

\displaystyle d=\min\{\absolutevalue{P}:P\in C_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}(\mathcal{S})\backslash\mathcal{G}\}\,,

(185)

that is, the minimum weight Pauli that acts nontrivially on the logical qubits (i.e., the minimum weight of the dressed logicals).

5.3
Qudit subsystem hypergraph product simplex codes

We now put everything together to give our qudit subsystem hypergraph product simplex (SHYPS) codes. We start by defining the SHYPS codes for qubits
[
70
]
:

Definition 6

(Subsystem hypergraph product simplex code
[
70
, Def. VIII.8]
)
.

Let
r\geq 3
,
n_{r}=2^{r}-1
,
d_{r}=2^{r-1}
, and
H_{r}
be the parity-check matrix for the
[n_{r},r,d_{r}]
-classical simplex code. Then, the subsystem hypergraph product of two copies of
H_{r}
, which we denote by SHYPS(
r
), is the subsystem CSS code with gauge generators

\displaystyle G_{X}=(H_{r}\otimes I_{n_{r}})\,,\quad G_{Z}=(I_{n_{r}}\otimes H_{r})\,.

(186)

SHYPS(
r
) is then called a
subsystem hypergraph product simplex code
.

SHYPS(
r
) codes are a type of quantum LDPC code with gauge generators of weight 3. The code parameters are given in the following theorem:

Fact 2

(SHYPS code parameters
[
70
, Thm. VIII.9]
)
.

Let
r\geq 3
and
H
be the parity-check matrix for the
(2^{r}-1,r,2^{r-1})
-classical simplex code. The subsystem hypergraph product simplex code SHYPS(
r
) is an
\llbracket n,k,d\rrbracket
-quantum subsystem code with gauge group generated by 3-qubit operators and

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=(2^{r}-1)^{2}\,,

(187)

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=r^{2}\,,

(188)

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=2^{r-1}\,.

(189)

Proof.

We reproduce the proof from
[
70
]
. We start by making an
n_{r}\times n_{r}
lattice such that the number of physical qubits needed is
n=n_{r}^{2}
. To find the number of logical qubits
k
, we note that the Pauli operators that commute with all gauge generators are generated by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X}=(I_{n_{r}}\otimes G)\,,\quad\mathcal{L}_{Z}=(G\otimes I_{n_{r}})\,,

(190)

where
G
is a rank-
r
generator matrix for the classical simplex code such that
HG^{\intercal}=0
. The stabilizer generators are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{X}=(H\otimes G)\,,\quad\mathcal{S}_{Z}=(G\otimes H)\,.

(191)

The number of logical qubits
k
is then the difference between the ranks:

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\rank{\mathcal{L}_X}-\rank{\mathcal{S}_X}

(192)

\displaystyle=n_{r}\cdot r-(n_{r}-r)\cdot r

(193)

\displaystyle=r^{2}\,.

(194)

Finally, the distance is the same as for the classical simplex code:
d=d_{r}=2^{r-1}
.
∎

While for all of the other codes that we have analyzed in this paper, it has been sufficient to replace
\mathbb{F}_{2}
by
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, in this case it is not so simple; we cannot simply promote the polynomials
h(x)
to be defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]/(x^{n_{r}}-1)
instead of
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/(x^{n_{r}}-1)
. The reason for this is that, while three-term polynomials
h(x)
that meet the restrictive criteria above exist over the quotient ring
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/(x^{n_{r}}-1)
, they do not exist over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]/(x^{n_{r}}-1)
for
q>2
(even if we drop the three-term criteria). Formally, we have

Proposition 6

(Existence of higher order polynomials)
.

For integers
q>2
,
r\geq 3
, and
n_{r}=(q^{r}-1)/(q-1)
, there does not exist a polynomial
h(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]/(x^{n_{r}}-1)
such that
\gcd(h(x),x^{n_{r}}-1)
is a primitive polynomial of degree
r
.

Proof.

We define
p(x)=\gcd(h(x),x^{n_{r}}-1)
, where
n_{r}=(q^{r}-1)/(q-1)
for a
q
-ary simplex code
[
3
]
. If
p(x)
is a primitive polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]/(x^{n_{r}}-1)
, the smallest positive integer
k
such that
p(x)
divides
x^{k}-1
is
k=q^{r}-1
. But
p(x)=\gcd(h(x),x^{n_{r}}-1)
implies that
p(x)
divides
x^{n_{r}}-1
, so
k\leq n_{r}
by the minimality of
k
. Since
n_{r}=(q^{r}-1)/(q-1)=k/(q-1)
, we must have
q-1\leq 1
, and thus
q=2
is the only value for
q
that works.
∎

Instead, we take as the classical simplex seed code the
q
-ary simplex codes defined in
Section

5.1
. Recall from above, a
q
-ary simplex code
S(q,r)
has parameters
[n_{r},r,q^{r-1}]_{q}
, where
n_{r}=(q^{r}-1)/(q-1)
. Here, the
q
-ary simplex code
S(q,r)
is a projective code whose generator matrix is constructed by taking the columns as chosen representatives of each element in the projective space
PG(r-1,q)
. Then, the qudit SHYPS code parameters are given by the following:

Proposition 7

(Qudit SHYPS code parameters)
.

Let
r\geq 3
and
H
be the parity-check matrix for the
(n,r,q^{r-1})_{q}
-classical
q
-ary simplex code, where
n=\frac{q^{r}-1}{q-1}
. The qudit subsystem hypergraph product simplex code
\text{SHYPS}(q,r)
is an
[n,k,d]_{q}
-quantum subsystem code, where

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=n_{r}^{2}=\left(\frac{q^{r}-1}{q-1}\right)^{2}\,,

(195)

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=r^{2}\,,

(196)

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=q^{r-1}\,.

(197)

Proof.

The proof is just an extension of the proof for qubits in
2
. The number of physical qudits is derived from the
n_{r}\times n_{r}
lattice, so we have
n=n_{r}^{2}
, where
n_{r}=(q^{r}-1)/(q-1)
is inherited from the classical seed code. For
k
, we construct the gauge generators in the same way:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X}=(I_{n_{r}}\otimes G)\,,\quad\mathcal{L}_{Z}=(G\otimes I_{n_{r}})\,,

(198)

where the only difference is in how we construct
G
, though
\rank{G}=r
still holds and we still have
GH^{\intercal}=0
, where
H
is the parity-check matrix of the classical
q
-ary simplex code. Likewise, the stabilizer generators are still defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{X}=(H\otimes G)\,,\quad\mathcal{S}_{Z}=(G\otimes H)\,,

(199)

where
\rank{H}=n_{r}-r
holds. Thus, the number of logical qudits is

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\rank{\mathcal{L}_X}-\rank{\mathcal{S}_X}

(200)

\displaystyle=r^{2}\,,

(201)

the same as it was for qubits. Finally, the distance is inherited from the distance of the classical
q
-ary simplex code, which yields
d=d_{r}=q^{r-1}
.
∎

Since we do not construct the classical gauge generator matrix
G
out of a three-term polynomial and instead from elements of the subspaces of the projective group
PG(r-1,q)
, we lose the constant weight of 3 for all values of
r
that the qubit SHYPS codes benefit from. Instead, as can be seen in
Figure

3
, while the minimum number of nonzero elements in each row of the parity-check matrix for different values of
r
remains constant at 3, the maximum number scales as
r+1
. Thus, while qudit SHYPS codes benefit from a favorable distance scaling as
q^{r-1}
, it comes at the cost of an increasing weight.

Qudit high-dimensional expander codes

In this section, we quditize the
high-dimensional expander
(HDX) codes
[
32
]
. HDX codes are a class of CSS codes defined by a quantum code and a classical linear code. The HDX codes take advantage of concepts from graph theory to construct codes with excellent distance properties. Expander graphs are simultaneously sparse (maintaining the LDPC property) yet highly-connected. This ensures that errors spread out quickly and are efficiently detected by the sparse checks. HDX codes utilize high-dimensional generalizations of these structures to achieve superior distance scaling.

We begin by considering the chain complexes (see
Section

2.2
for a primer on homological algebra)

(204)

\displaystyle:0\xrightarrow{\delta_{2}^{Y}}B\xrightarrow{\delta_{1}^{Y}}A\xrightarrow{\delta_{0}^{Y}}0\,.

whereas the co-complex of

(207)

(216)

7
Qudit fiber bundle codes

The first quantum LDPC codes to achieve a distance greater than
n^{1/2}{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)
was introduced a few years ago and is based on fiber bundles
[
44
]
. For qubits, this code achieves a distance of
\Omega\left(n^{3/5}/{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)\right)
and a code dimension of
\tilde{\Theta}\left(n^{3/5}\right)
for
n
physical qubits. Now, the final LDPC codes that we quditize are the
fiber bundle codes
to give
qudit fiber bundle codes
. In the first part of this section, we produce the background and code properties from the original work by Hastings, Haah, and O’Donnell
[
44
]
; we refer the reader to this reference for all of the details, where here we only reproduce what we need to find the code parameters of our qudit fiber bundle codes.

We start with a bit of mathematical background needed to understand this class of codes. Familiarity with the concepts introduced in
Section

2.2
is helpful for this section. We first define the fundamental object these codes are based on, the
fiber bundle
:

Definition 8

(Fiber bundle)
.

A
fiber bundle
is a structure that consists of:

•

A base space
B
,

•

A total space
E
,

•

A fiber
F
, and

•

A continuous surjection
\pi:E\to B
that satisfies a local triviality condition.

The fiber bundle
(E,B,\pi,F)
is often denoted as

\displaystyle F\to E\xrightarrow{\pi}B\,.

(249)

A fiber bundle generalizes a product space. Stated differently, a fiber bundle can be thought of as a space that is locally a product space but globally may have a non-product-like topological structure. While a simple product (like a cylinder, which is a product of a circle and an interval) has the same structure everywhere, a fiber bundle allows for a global
“twist.”
A common example of a fiber bundle is a Möbius strip, where locally it looks like the product of a circle with an interval, but globally there is a twist, where going around once reverses the interval. Fiber bundle codes exploit this by starting with a homological product (like the HGP codes discussed in
Section

4
) and introducing intentional twists. This modification to the boundary operators significantly enhances the code’s distance, which led to the first LDPC codes that surpass the
\sqrt{n}
distance barrier
[
44
]
. In the construction of fiber bundle codes, we associate the fiber with a cycle graph and a random base with a random classical LDPC code. The main result that Hastings, Haah, and O’Donnell derive for qubit fiber bundle codes is given as follows:

Fact 3

(Qubit fiber bundle codes
[
44
, Thm. 1.1]
)
.

There exists a family of quantum codes on
n
qubits with
d_{X}=\Omega\left(n^{1/2}{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)\right)
and
d_{Z}=\Omega\left(n^{3/4}{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)\right)
, where all stabilizer generators have weight at most
{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)
and all qubits participate in at most
{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)
stabilizer generators. The code has
\Theta\left(n^{1/2}\right)
logical qubits.

Note that this code is
not
LDPC, but it can be weight-reduced to LDPC at polylogarithmic cost in the distance and number of physical qubits. For qubit LDPC fiber bundle codes, we have the following corollary:

Corollary 1

(Qubit LDPC fiber bundle codes
[
44
, Cor. 1.2]
)
.

There exists a family of quantum LDPC codes on
n
qubits having distance
d=\Omega\left(n^{3/5}/{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)\right)
and with
\Omega\left(n^{3/5}/{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)\right)
logical qubits.

The fiber bundle code construction relies on homological algebra and chain complexes, which we introduced in
Section

2.2
. Here, a chain complex defines a quantum code by picking an integer
\nu>0
and associating
\nu
-cells of the complex with qubits and
(\nu-1)
- and
(\nu+1)
-cells with
X
- and
Z
-stabilizer generators, respectively.
Z

\nu\textsuperscript{th}
homology class and
X
logical operators are the
\nu\textsuperscript{th}
cohomology class.
d_{X}
is the lowest possible Hamming weight of a vector that represents a nontrivial
\nu\textsuperscript{th}
cohomology and
d_{Z}
is likewise defined for the homology.

Section

2.2

7.1
Extension to qudits

We now extend the above formalism to qudits and recover the number of physical qudits, number of logical qudits, and the distance. We have already done a lot of the heavy-lifting by developing the theory of fiber bundles and the codes we can derive from them. We start with a chain complex over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
for some prime power

for prime

\displaystyle\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_{j+1}}\mathcal{A}_{j}\xrightarrow{\partial_{j}}\mathcal{A}_{j-1}\xrightarrow{\partial_{j-1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}\mathcal{A}_{0}\xrightarrow{\partial_{0}=0}0\,.

Here, each vector space is over
\mathbb{F}_{q}

\displaystyle H_{j}(\mathcal{A})=\ker_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}{\partial_{j}}/\text{im}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}{\partial_{j+1}}\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho+1}^{E}(b^{\mu}\otimes f^{\nu})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mu}b^{\mu}\otimes\partial_{\nu}^{F}f^{\nu}+\partial_{\mu}^{B}b^{\mu}\otimes f^{\nu}\,.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{\rho}^{E}\partial_{\rho+1}^{E}(b^{\mu}\otimes f^{\nu})=&(-1)^{\mu}b^{\mu}\otimes\partial_{\nu}^{F}f^{\nu}\\

\displaystyle(-1)^{\mu}\left[(-1)^{\mu}b^{\mu}\otimes\underbrace{\partial_{\nu_{1}}^{F}(\partial_{\nu}^{F}f^{\nu})}_{0}+\partial_{\mu}^{B}b^{\mu}\otimes(\partial_{\nu}^{F}f^{\nu})\right]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mu-1}(\partial_{\mu}^{B}b^{\mu})\otimes(\partial_{\nu}^{F}f^{\nu})+(-1)^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}^{B}b^{\mu}\otimes(\partial_{q}\nu Ff^{\nu})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mu}\left(\partial_{\mu}^{B}b^{\mu}\otimes\partial_{\nu}^{F}f^{\nu}-\partial_{\mu}^{B}b^{\mu}\otimes\partial_{\nu}^{F}f^{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(275)

which holds for all
\rho

.
∎

We now discuss the choice of circle bundle over classical codes. The fiber can remain a cycle graph (i.e., a circle), which is a 1-complex with
m_{F}
0-cells and
n_{F}
1-cells with
m_{F}=n_{F}>1
. The only difference between the case for qubits and that of qudits is that the qudit cells and their connections are defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For qubits, the circle admits an automorphism group that is the dihedral group of order
2n_{F}
and
[
44
]
uses only the rotational symmetry. Over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the automorphism group generalizes to a linear transformation on the fiber vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
while preserving the cycle structure, but possibly changing the coefficients. This circle construction still obeys the conditions of
Proposition

9
, as long as the changes mentioned in the proof are taken into account (e.g., the sum of the coefficients of
\partial f^{1}
equals 0). We keep with the convention in
[
44
]
and choose
n_{F}=m_{F}=\ell^{2}
for some integer
\ell
.

For the base complex, we now choose a random classical LDPC code over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, rather than a binary code, which can still be represented by a Tanner graph. The Tanner graph is a bipartite graph with
m_{B}
left-vertices and
n_{B}
right-vertices, where we choose
m_{B}=\frac{3}{4}n_{B}
and a vertex degree of
\Delta=\Theta\left(\log^{2}{n_{B}}\right)
as in
[
44
]
. With high probability, a random classical non-binary LDPC code will have its parity-checks be linearly independent, which is equivalent to
H_{0}(B)=0
. With all of this formalism established, we can give the qudit fiber bundle code parameters:

Lemma 2

(Qudit fiber bundle code parameters)
.

For a prime power
q=p^{s}
for prime
p
and
s\geq 1
, a
qudit fiber bundle code
is a quantum LDPC code with parameters
\llbracket n,k,d\rrbracket_{q}
, where

•

n=n_{B}\cdot m_{F}+n_{F}\cdot m_{B}

•

k=\Theta\left(n_{B}\right)

•

d=\min\{d_{X},d_{Z}\}
,

where
d_{X}=\Omega\left(m_{F}/{\mathsf{polylog}}(n_{B})\right)
and
d_{Z}=\Omega\left(n_{B}\cdot m_{F}^{1/2}/{\mathsf{polylog}}(n_{B})\right)
and where
m_{B}
,
m_{F}
,
n_{B}
, and
n_{F}
are defined above. Choosing
n_{B}\sim m_{B}
gives
n=\Theta\left(n_{B}^{2}\right)
, which simplifies the code dimension to
k=\Theta\left(n^{1/2}\right)
and the distances to
d_{X}=\Omega\left(n^{1/2}/{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)\right)
and
d_{Z}=\Omega\left(n^{3/4}/{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)\right)
.

Proof.

The number of physical qudits
n
and the number of logical qudits
k
follow directly from the analysis in
[
44
]
. The scaling of the distance is the same as for qubits (extending to the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
does not affect the scaling), but the specific constants will be different.
∎

Since we only care about the scaling of the distance with the number of physical qudits, we do not do a detailed analysis of the constants in this work, which would require a significant reworking of the proofs in
[
44
]
and would add several pages to this work. An easy alternative is to calculate the distance numerically, as was done for several of the other code constructions we presented above.

8
Discussion

In this paper, we introduced a framework for generalizing quantum LDPC codes from qubits to qudits. We gave the formalism for several promising LDPC codes, namely bivariate bicycle codes, hypergraph product codes, subsystem hypergraph product simplex codes, high-dimensional expander codes, and fiber bundle codes. Furthermore, our numerical code searches and decoding simulations introduce several novel qudit error correcting codes. For the decoding of our qudit codes, we relied on mixed-integer programming, which, while accurate, is relatively slow. For future work, one could find faster decoders tailored to qudits, such as one based on belief propagation
[
87
,
79
]
, neural networks
[
96
,
34
]
, or
MaxSAT

[
7
,
78
]
. We also leave to future work the details of logical gates and operators in the qudit regime, which can be done, for example, using code automorphisms
[
90
]
.

We hope this work serves as a reference for researchers working on qudit error correction. The set of codes considered in this work is by no means exhaustive and there are many quantum error correcting codes that remain to be generalized to qudits. We also leave to future work more numerical analysis and searching for codes of the types that we introduced here, especially the high-dimensional expander and fiber bundle codes, for which we only gave theoretical results.
