Discovery of the Spin-down glitch in PSR J1835 − - 1106 Facilities: Nanshan 26-m radio telescope, Parkes 64-m radio telescope TEMPO2 ( Edwards et al. 2006 ; Hobbs et al. 2006 ) , PSRCHIVE ( Hotan et al. 2004 ; van Straten et al. 2012 ) ,
Mingyang Wang, Peng Liu, Jianping Yuan, Ang Li

Abstract
We report the detection of a spin-down glitch with a fractional frequency change of Δ ν / ν = − 3.46 ( 6 ) × 10 − 9 \Delta\nu/\nu=-3.46(6)\times 10^{-9} in the rotation-powered pulsar PSR J1835 − - 1106 at MJD 55813, based on timing observations collected with the Nanshan 26-m and Parkes 64-m radio telescopes from January 2000 to July 2022. A comparison of the average pulse profiles within ± 300 \pm 300 d of the event reveals no significant morphological changes. We also estimate the angular velocity lag between the normal and superfluid components at the time of the glitch, demonstrating that one of the spin-down glitch models proposed by Kantor & Gusakov 2014 is not applicable to PSR J1835 − - 1106.



I
Introduction

Pulsars are rapidly rotating, highly magnetized neutron stars known for their exceptional rotational stability. However, some exhibit irregular spin behavior, most notably in the form of glitches
(
Antonopoulou et al. 2022
)
. A glitch is characterized by a sudden, discrete increase in spin frequency occurring over a very short timescale. These events typically appear as an abrupt acceleration in the pulsar’s rotation, often followed by a gradual recovery phase
(
Howitt et al. 2016
)
. In contrast, a few rare events show a sudden decrease in spin frequency, known as spin-down glitches (also named anti-glitches)
(
Archibald et al. 2013
;
Younes et al. 2020
;
Şaşmaz Muş et al. 2014
;
Ray et al. 2019
)
.
Previous studies have shown that spin-down glitches predominantly occur in objects with very strong surface magnetic fields—magnetars—and are often accompanied by radiative outbursts and changes in pulse profile, indicating a close association with magnetospheric activity
(
Dib & Kaspi 2014
;
Hu & Ng 2019
)
.

Recently,
Tuo et al. 2024
reported the radiatively quiet spin-down glitch in the rotation-powered pulsar (RPP) PSR B0540
-
69, noting that its pulse profile remained essentially unchanged before and after the event. Subsequently,
Zhou et al. 2024
identified a series of spin-down glitch episodes in the RPP PSR J1522
-
5735; similarly, these events showed no detectable alterations in pulse shape. These observations suggest that spin-down glitches in RPPs may originate from physical mechanisms fundamentally similar to those of normal glitches—likely involving internal processes such as the transfer of angular momentum from the neutron star’s superfluid interior to its charged component.

In the present work, we report a new spin-down glitch in the RPP PSR J1835
-
1106, based on timing observations from the Nanshan 26-m and Parkes 64-m radio telescopes.
The paper is organized as follows: Section
II
provides a brief overview of the timing observations conducted with these telescopes. In Section
III
, we present our analysis of the spin-down glitch and explore potential triggering mechanisms. Finally, Section
IV
summarizes our main findings.

II
Observations and Data analysis

The Nanshan 26-m radio telescope is a fully steerable Cassegrain-focus parabolic antenna with a primary dish diameter of 26 m. It is equipped with four cryogenic receivers covering five wavelength bands: 1.3 cm, 3.6 cm, 6 cm, 13 cm, and 18 cm. Initially, timing observations were performed with a 128
-
channel analog filter bank (AFB), each channel having a bandwidth of 2.5 MHz. In January 2010, the system was upgraded to a 1024
-
channel digital filter bank (DFB) centered at 1556 MHz, providing a total bandwidth of 512 MHz and an effective bandwidth of 320 MHz. Pulsar timing uses the 18 cm receiver at a center frequency of 1540 MHz with 320 MHz of usable bandwidth, covering the range 1381.25–1701.25 MHz
(
Wang et al. 2001
)
. Observations are carried out at least three times per month, with each integration lasting 4 minutes prior to October 2017 and 8 minutes thereafter. It should be noted that no timing data were acquired between 2014 and 2016 owing to the receiver and backend upgrades.

The Parkes radio telescope is a fully steerable, 64 m diameter dish located in New South Wales, Australia, and is among the most prolific instruments for pulsar discovery. It hosts multiple receivers and digital backend systems; in this work, we utilize timing data obtained with the 13
-
beam (20 cm) receiver and the Parkes Digital Filterbanks PDFB1, PDFB2, PDFB3 and PDFB4. These observations were conducted at a center frequency of 1369 MHz with a total bandwidth of 256 MHz, spanning 1241–1497 MHz
(
Staveley-Smith et al. 1996
;
Hobbs et al. 2020
)
. The Parkes timing programme typically observes each target pulsar every 2–4 weeks, with total integration times ranging from 2 to 15 minutes per epoch.

Upon data acquisition, we processed the raw observations with PSRCHIVE to perform coherent dedispersion and fold subintegrated profiles into average pulse templates
(
Hotan et al. 2004
;
van Straten et al. 2012
)
. To derive high
-
precision pulse times-of-arrival (ToAs), we employed PSRADD to sum all folded profiles into a high–signal
-
to
-
noise standard template, then cross
-
correlated each individual profile against this template to extract TOAs referenced to the observatory. Finally, these site
-
TOAs were converted to the Solar System barycenter using TEMPO2
(
Hobbs et al. 2006
;
Edwards et al. 2006
)
together with the JPL DE440
(
Park et al. 2021
)
ephemeris and the Barycentric Coordinate Time (TCB) scale, thereby removing delays introduced by the Earth’s motion.

We use TEMPO2 to fit the ToAs data and obtain an accurate rotation phase model. The phase is expanded using a Taylor series
(
Edwards et al. 2006
)
:

\phi(t)=\phi_{0}+\nu_{0}\,\bigl(t-t_{0}\bigr)+\frac{1}{2!}\,\dot{\nu}_{0}\,\bigl(t-t_{0}\bigr)^{2}+\frac{1}{3!}\,\ddot{\nu}_{0}\,\bigl(t-t_{0}\bigr)^{3}\ .

(1)

Here,
t
denotes the time of observation, and
\phi_{0}
,
\nu_{0}
,
\dot{\nu}_{0}
,
\ddot{\nu}_{0}
are, at the reference epoch
t_{0}
, the pulse phase, spin frequency, and the first and second time derivatives of the spin frequency, respectively. The observed glitch is modeled by the following equation
(
Edwards et al. 2006
)
:

\phi_{\rm g}(t)=\Delta\phi+\Delta\nu_{\rm p}(t-t_{\rm g})+\frac{1}{2!}\Delta\dot{\nu}_{\rm p}(t-t_{\rm g})^{2}\ .

(2)

Here,
t_{\rm g}
denotes the epoch at which the spin
-
down glitch occurs,
\Delta\nu_{\rm p}
and
\Delta\dot{\nu}_{\rm p}
are the permanent increments in the spin frequency and its first derivative, respectively, and
\Delta\phi
is the phase offset immediately following the glitch.

The newly detected spin-down glitch (glitch 2) occurred at MJD 55813(9), with a fractional frequency change of
\Delta\nu/\nu=-3.46(6)\times 10^{-9}
(see Fig.
1
). We also examined the average pulse profiles within a
\pm
300-day window around this event (Fig.
2
) and found no significant changes in pulse morphology before or after the glitch.
As noted earlier, many spin-down glitches in magnetars are accompanied by magnetospheric activity. However, the spin-down glitch reported here occurred in an RPP, with no detectable changes in pulse profile, suggesting that its origin is likely internal rather than magnetospheric.

Previously, the spin-down glitch in PSR J1522
-
5735 was interpreted as inward motion of vortex lines driven by external forces, leading to angular momentum transfer from the crust to the superfluid
(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
. In contrast, the spin-down glitch in PSR B0540
-
69 was explained via the
\Delta V
effect proposed by
Kantor & Gusakov 2014
, where partial conversion of superfluid to normal matter increases the moment of inertia of the normal component while reducing that of the superfluid component.

According to the modified vortex creep model
(
Kantor & Gusakov 2014
)
, the spin frequency change during a spin-down glitch is expressed as:

\Delta\Omega_{\rm{c}}=-\frac{I_{\rm{s0}}-I^{\prime}_{\rm{c}}\Delta\Omega_{0}}{I_{\rm{c0}}+I^{\prime}_{\rm{s}}\Delta\Omega_{0}}\Delta\Omega_{\rm{s}}\ ,

(3)

where
I_{\rm{s0}}
and
I_{\rm{c0}}
are the moments of inertia of the superfluid and normal components before the glitch,
\Delta\Omega_{0}
is the angular velocity lag between the components prior to the event, and
I^{\prime}_{\rm{c}}=-dI_{\rm{s}}/d(\Delta\Omega)
. A spin-down glitch is expected to occur when
I_{\rm{s0}}<I^{\prime}_{\rm{c}}\Delta\Omega_{0}
. However, this mechanism requires stringent conditions: the superfluid density must exhibit significant sensitivity to
\Delta\Omega
, necessitating high internal temperatures (
T\gtrsim 10^{7}\,\mathrm{K}
) and a substantial angular velocity lag (
\Delta\Omega\gtrsim 1\ \mathrm{rad\ s^{-1}}
), which are likely met only in a few young pulsars.

Notably, PSR J1835
-
1106 exhibited a small glitch preceding its spin-down glitch. If this precursor originated from vortex unpinning, it could allow estimation of whether the angular velocity lag
\Delta\Omega
at the spin-down glitch epoch (MJD 55813(9)) satisfies
\Delta\Omega\gtrsim 1\ \mathrm{rad\ s^{-1}}
.According to
Tuo et al. 2024
, a normal glitch also preceded the spin-down glitch in PSR B0540
-
69. Assuming the superfluid angular velocity remained unchanged after the normal glitch while the crust was braked, the inferred lag for PSR B0540
-
69 at the spin-down glitch was
0.88\ \mathrm{rad\ s^{-1}}
, close to the critical threshold. However, applying the same method to PSR J1835
-
1106 yields
1.4\times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad\ s^{-1}}
, far below the required value. For PSR J1522
-
5735, the short interval between events (
1000\ \mathrm{d}
) also precludes sufficient time to accumulate a significant lag. Thus, if the precursor glitch in PSR J1835
-
1106 involved vortex unpinning, the subsequent spin-down glitch cannot be explained by the
\Delta V
effect.

Achieving
\Delta\Omega\gtrsim 1\ \mathrm{rad\ s^{-1}}
likely requires a high spin-down rate and prolonged intervals without glitches. PSR B0540
-
69, with its substantial spin-down rate (
\sim 2.5\times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{s^{-2}}
) attributed to a pulsar wind nebula
(
Petre et al. 2007
)
and a
~7000\ \mathrm{d}
glitch-free interval, could accumulate sufficient lag. In contrast, PSR J1835
-
1106 would need
~6800\ \mathrm{yr}
to build up such a lag under crustal motion assumptions. This strongly disfavors the
\Delta V
effect as the origin of its spin-down glitch.

IV
Conclusions

In this study, we analyzed timing observations of PSR J1835
-
1106 from the Nanshan 26-m and Parkes 64-m radio telescopes and identified a spin-down glitch at MJD 55813(9) with fractional size
\Delta\nu/\nu=-3.46(6)\times 10^{-9}
, representing the third such event detected in a RPP. We compared the average pulse profiles within
\pm 300
d of the spin-down glitch and found no significant morphological changes, implying that the origin of this event is unlikely to be magnetospheric. Furthermore, we estimated the angular
-
velocity lag between the normal and superfluid components at the glitch epoch. Owing to PSR J1835
-
1106’s relatively small spin
-
down rate, this lag is far below
1\ \mathrm{rad\ s^{-1}}
, indicating that the spin-down glitch model proposed by
Kantor & Gusakov 2014
does not apply to PSR J1835
-
1106.

For future work, detailed investigations into the angular momentum exchange mechanisms between the superfluid interior and the normal component hold promise for developing a self-consistent physical model of these anti-glitch phenomena. Furthermore, simultaneous analyses incorporating polarization measurements and other diagnostics to explore the relationship between anti-glitches and pulsar electromagnetic emission will help clarify whether their origin lies in the magnetosphere or the internal superfluid. Upcoming high-cadence, high signal-to-noise, multi-frequency pulsar observations—such as those enabled by eXTP
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
—may significantly expand the sample of anti-glitches in RPPs, potentially uncovering the universal phenomenology underlying these events.
