Air embolism resulting from contrast agent injection during coronary computed tomography: a case report
Authors: Yucheng Fan, Hang Han, Zhi Chai, Yuxia Zhang, Yongjun Du, Li Jing, Feng Jing, Fangjing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAir embolism is a rare but potentially lethal iatrogenic complication. Clinicians must recognize its warning signs and implement timely standardized interventions, as early detection is crucial to reduce morbidity and mortality. This report presents a case of venous air embolism following coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA).Case presentationA 60-year-old female developed air embolism due to contrast agent administration during a coronary CT study, presenting with new-onset dyspnea and dizziness approximately 1 min after the end of contrast injection, immediately after completing the CT scan. Following treatment including high-flow oxygen therapy (10 L/min), fluid resuscitation, and left lateral decubitus position with head down, her vital signs stabilized and arterial blood gas parameters returned to normal. A follow-up CT scan 7 days later showed complete absorption of the air emboli, and the patient was discharged uneventfully.DiscussionThe patient was diagnosed with grade 1 venous air embolism (VAE) according to the Tubingen VAE grading scale, with an estimated air volume of 5-10mL. The mild clinical manifestations were consistent with the small air volume and slow entry rate, and the patient achieved a favorable prognosis with timely standardized interventions. This case highlights the importance of vigilance for VAE during contrast-enhanced CT, even in patients with mild and non-specific symptoms.ConclusionWhile rare, air embolism can cause mild symptoms with small volumes, but larger volumes may be life-threatening. Air embolism should be suspected in patients with sudden neurological, respiratory or cardiovascular symptoms, especially in the presence of iatrogenic risk factors. Prompt cessation of the embolism source, appropriate patient positioning, high-flow oxygen therapy, and hyperbaric oxygen treatment when indicated should be initiated immediately to minimize air entry into the right ventricular outflow tract. Introduction

Air embolism is a pathological condition characterized by vascular flow obstruction and tissue hypoxia caused by air entry into the vascular system. This clinical syndrome can lead to severe circulatory dysfunction, with rapid progression and potentially critical outcomes. The success rate of resuscitation for massive air embolism is low, with a high overall mortality rate [1, 2]. In clinical practice, air embolism can occur during radiological procedures including angiography, needle aspiration biopsy, and pulmonary imaging, as well as in other medical settings such as central venous catheter insertion and maintenance, surgery, and gynecological procedures [3, 4]. In computed tomography (CT) examinations, contrast agents are administered to improve image definition. However, the rapid injection of contrast medium via a high-pressure syringe may inadvertently introduce air into the venous circulation, thereby inducing air embolism [2].

Although typical presentations of air embolism have been documented in the literature, there is still a lack of standardized diagnostic criteria, resulting in low clinical incidence and diagnostic rates. Due to its rarity and potential lethality, air embolism poses challenges for early clinical diagnosis, and may be misdiagnosed or overlooked in patients with atypical manifestations, leading to delayed treatment and disease progression. In this report, we present a case of pulmonary artery air embolism in a 60-year-old female after contrast injection during cardiac CT. The patient presented with dyspnea and dizziness, and was successfully treated and discharged. Timely and effective identification, diagnosis and management of air embolism are critical to improve patient prognosis and reduce mortality.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old female patient was admitted to our hospital due to complaints of chest tightness and fatigue. The patient had a 15-year history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and bronchial asthma, with a long-term regimen of home oxygen therapy and nebulized medication. No history of alcohol or drug use was reported. Her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) was 15, vital signs included a blood pressure (BP) of 102/73 mmHg, a heart rate (HR) of 74 beats per minute, a respiratory rate (RR) of 18 breaths per minute, oxygen saturation (SaO2) of 95% on room air, and a body temperature of 36.3 °C. Pre-procedure physical examination revealed coarse breath sounds bilaterally on lung auscultation, consistent with her underlying respiratory disease, with no significant abnormalities in cardiac or neurological assessments. Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, liver and kidney function tests, electrolyte levels, thyroid function tests, brain natriuretic peptide, cardiac enzymes, coagulation profile, and D-dimer assays, showed no remarkable abnormalities. No pre-procedure arterial blood gas analysis was performed as the patient was in a stable clinical state with no acute respiratory exacerbation. An electrocardiogram (ECG) indicated sinus rhythm and ST-T changes with ST segment depression in leads II, III, and aVF. A preliminary diagnosis of coronary artery disease was made, and a contrast-enhanced coronary CT scan was planned.

The patient was placed in a supine position with upper limb abduction during the procedure. Continuous pulse oximetry monitoring was performed throughout the examination. An 18G intravenous indwelling needle was placed in the right antecubital vein, connected to an automatic high-pressure injector. Contrast agent (iohexol 37.5 g/100 ml) 70 ml was injected at a flow rate of 4.5 ml/s, followed by 30 ml normal saline at the same flow rate. Approximately 1 min after the end of contrast injection, immediately after completing the CT scan and leaving the examination table, the patient developed new-onset mild dyspnea and dizziness. The dyspnea was defined as worsening within 10 m of walking, which was significantly different from her baseline state of no dyspnea during daily activities before the examination. No chest pain, disturbance of consciousness, nausea or vomiting was reported.

The CT findings revealed multiple air emboli in the right atrium, right ventricle, main pulmonary artery, and right pulmonary artery (Fig. 1). On assessment, the patient was conscious with the above-mentioned mild dizziness and dyspnea. Post-procedure auscultation of the lungs revealed coarse breath sounds bilaterally, with no new adventitious sounds compared with pre-procedure baseline. Heart sounds were normal with no significant murmurs detected. Vital signs at this time included a heart rate of 59 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, blood pressure of 117/70 mmHg, and SaO2 of 100% with 3 L/min supplemental oxygen therapy. Arterial blood gas analysis was performed immediately after symptom onset, which indicated a pH of 7.37, a carbon dioxide partial pressure of 33.5 mmHg↓, an oxygen partial pressure of 92 mmHg, with an alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient of 15.85 mmHg↑ (calculated based on the blood gas results). No preoperative baseline measurement of this gradient was available for this patient. Further laboratory tests, including complete blood count, liver and kidney function, electrolyte levels, cardiac biomarkers, troponin tests, and D-dimer levels, showed no significant changes.

Axial contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography images demonstrating air bubbles within (A) the main pulmonary artery and (B) the right atrium and right ventricle (red arrows)

Given the high risk of acute pulmonary air embolism, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for continuous cardiac monitoring for 24 h. Bedside transthoracic echocardiography was performed immediately after diagnosis, which showed a small amount of microbubbles in the right atrium and right ventricle, normal right heart size, good ventricular wall motion, no obvious right heart insufficiency, and no intracardiac shunt. She received high-flow supplemental oxygen therapy (10 L/min), was placed in left lateral decubitus position with head down, alongside intravenous fluid resuscitation and frequent blood gas monitoring. Treatment for her underlying conditions was continued. One week later, the patient was able to ambulate independently without supplemental oxygen, with complete resolution of dizziness and dyspnea. A follow-up pulmonary artery contrast-enhanced CT scan showed no residual air emboli or other abnormalities (Fig. 2). One month after discharge, telephone follow-up revealed no discomfort or activity limitation in the patient.

Discussion

Venous air embolism (VAE) is an iatrogenic complication caused by gas entry into the venous system, which subsequently enters the right heart and pulmonary circulation. It most commonly occurs due to improper operation during central venous catheterization, venous cannulation, surgery, or contrast-enhanced radiological procedures. Some literature indicates that VAE can occur proactively when intravenous contrast agents are injected under pressure, with approximately 23% of patients showing small amounts of air during such procedures [5, 6].However, there is currently no consensus regarding the correlation between the incidence of VAE and factors such as injection speed, injection site, volume or type of contrast agent, as well as the positioning, size, and nature of the intravenous catheter used [7]. It has been shown in the literature that this may be related to the presence of microbubbles in syringes, pressure tubes or contrast media [8].

The pathophysiological impact of VAE depends on the volume and rate of air entry. The pulmonary circulation has a limited capacity to filter venous microbubbles; once this capacity is exceeded, air may enter the arterial circulation, causing vital organ ischemia. Air bubbles can also trigger coagulation disorders, microcirculatory disturbance, and inflammatory responses, further exacerbating tissue injury. Crucially, the volume and number of bubbles, the rate of entry, and the positioning of the patient can all significantly influence the pathophysiological responses to gas embolism [9, 10, 11]. The lethal air volume for adults is reported to be 3–5 mL/kg, or a total volume of 200–300 mL [12]. Clinical manifestations of VAE are non-specific, including dyspnea, chest pain, dizziness, and even cardiac arrest in severe cases [12, 13]. In non-conscious patients, clinical symptoms may be easily overlooked, underscoring the necessity for vigilant monitoring of vital signs [14, 15]. The unique and characteristic sign of VAE is the presence of a “water-hammer” murmur, which is produced by the turbulence generated by gas and air within the cardiac chambers [16]. During surgical procedures or invasive examinations, if patients suddenly experience clinical symptoms such as chest pain and dyspnea, along with a decrease in blood pressure and oxygen saturation, as well as abrupt changes in heart rate (either increased or decreased) and arrhythmias, clinicians should maintain a heightened awareness of the potential occurrence of venous air embolism.

In this case, the patient was diagnosed with VAE immediately after CCTA, with typical imaging findings and consistent clinical symptoms. According to the Tubingen VAE grading scale, the patient was classified as grade 1 VAE (only imaging evidence of air embolism, no hemodynamic disturbance, with mild clinical symptoms). The estimated volume of embolized air was 5–10 mL based on CT images, which explained the mild manifestations and favorable prognosis. The core management principles for VAE include stopping the air entry source, optimizing patient positioning to prevent air from entering the right ventricular outflow tract, high-flow oxygen therapy to accelerate bubble dissolution, and hyperbaric oxygen therapy for severe cases [17, 18, 19]. In this case, the patient’s symptoms resolved rapidly with the above-mentioned conservative treatment, and complete air absorption was confirmed on follow-up CT. This case highlights the importance of pre-procedure air elimination from syringes and tubing, as well as intra-procedure monitoring and post-procedure vigilance for VAE, even in patients with mild non-specific symptoms.

Conclusion

Although iatrogenic air embolism is rare, it is characterized by an insidious onset and rapid progression. For patients with risk factors who present with sudden respiratory, circulatory or neurological symptoms, immediate differential diagnosis for this condition is essential. Prompt elimination of the embolus source, adoption of appropriate body positioning, high-flow oxygen therapy, and selective hyperbaric oxygen treatment constitute the core strategies to halt disease deterioration and improve prognosis.

Limitations

This study is a single-center, retrospective single case report, which limits the external generalizability of the findings. No pre-procedure arterial blood gas analysis was performed, so there was no baseline alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient for comparison. The exact volume of the embolized air could not be accurately quantified by CT imaging. In addition, dynamic echocardiographic assessment was not performed during the treatment process to evaluate the real-time changes of intracardiac air bubbles and right heart function.
