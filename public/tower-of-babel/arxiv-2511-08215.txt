Evaluating Gemini LLM in Food Image-Based Recipe and Nutrition Description with EfficientNet-B4 Visual Backbone
Rizal Khoirul Anam

Abstract
The proliferation of digital food applications necessitates robust methods for automated nutritional analysis and culinary guidance. This paper presents a comprehensive comparative evaluation of a decoupled, multimodal pipeline for food recognition. We evaluate a system integrating a specialized visual backbone (EfficientNet-B4) with a powerful generative large language model (Google’s Gemini LLM). The core objective is to evaluate the trade-offs between visual classification accuracy, model efficiency, and the quality of generative output (nutritional data and recipes). We benchmark this pipeline against alternative vision backbones (VGG-16, ResNet-50, YOLOv8) and a lightweight LLM (Gemma). We introduce a formalization for ”Semantic Error Propagation” (SEP) to analyze how classification inaccuracies from the visual module cascade into the generative output. Our analysis is grounded in a new Custom Chinese Food Dataset (CCFD) developed to address cultural bias in public datasets. Experimental results demonstrate that while EfficientNet-B4 (89.0% Top-1 Acc.) provides the best balance of accuracy and efficiency, and Gemini (9.2/10 Factual Accuracy) provides superior generative quality, the system’s overall utility is fundamentally bottlenecked by the visual front-end’s perceptive accuracy. We conduct a detailed per-class analysis, identifying high semantic similarity as the most critical failure mode.



I
Introduction

In the digital era, there is a growing fascination with food, driven by a desire to understand its ingredients, explore diverse cuisines, and improve personal health
[
13
]
. The rapid advancement of internet technologies and the proliferation of smartphones have created a demand for more intuitive and automated tools to simplify the process of gathering food-related information
[
5
,
14
]
.

Digital food recognition has emerged as a premier solution to these challenges
[
8
]
. Unlike traditional manual lookup, which is cumbersome and slow, AI-driven systems can analyze a food image and provide detailed information. These recognition techniques are broadly categorized into two main approaches:

•

Traditional Computer Vision
methods, such as those using Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) or color histograms, attempt to manually engineer features
[
6
,
9
]
. These methods are lauded for their simplicity but are extremely fragile, failing to handle the high intra-class variance of food.

•

Deep Learning (CNN)
methods, such as VGG
[
20
]
, ResNet
[
19
]
, and EfficientNet
[
17
]
, automate the feature extraction process
[
7
,
8
]
. These models learn a rich hierarchy of visual features, offering far greater robustness and accuracy in classification tasks.

The central challenge in modern food analysis lies in a ”context gap”
[
16
]
. Most systems excel at one of two tasks:

1.

Visual Classification:
CNNs can accurately answer ”What is this food?” but provide no further context
[
7
]
.

2.

Generative Knowledge:
Large Language Models (LLMs) can answer ”What is in this food?” and ”How do I make it?” but only if the user provides a *manual text description*
[
3
,
12
,
15
]
.

This creates a trade-off between perceptual accuracy and contextual depth. To bridge this gap,
decoupled ”hybrid” pipelines
have gained attention
[
29
]
. These systems combine a visual backbone (like EfficientNet) with a generative model (like Gemini). The vision model acts as a ”visual tokenizer,” converting the image into a semantic token (the food name), which is then ”expanded” by the LLM into rich, useful information.

This paper provides a rigorous comparative
evaluation
of this decoupled pipeline, focusing on its performance, efficiency, and failure modes. Our contributions are:

1.

We introduce a **Custom Chinese Food Dataset (CCFD)** to specifically address and mitigate the well-documented cultural bias in existing public food datasets like Food-101
[
2
,
28
]
.

2.

We provide a comprehensive benchmark of vision models (VGG-16, ResNet-50,
EfficientNet-B4
, YOLOv8) evaluating both accuracy and efficiency (parameters, inference time).

3.

We benchmark generative models (Gemma vs.
Gemini
) on both quantitative (BLEU/ROUGE) and qualitative (factual accuracy) metrics.

4.

We formalize and analyze **Semantic Error Propagation (SEP)**, providing a deep-dive evaluation into *how* and *why* the system fails, distinguishing between semantic-mismatch and semantic-similarity errors.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section II reviews related literature. Section III details the methodology and mathematical formalism for all evaluated models. Section IV describes the experimental setup. Section V presents and discusses the detailed experimental results and evaluation. Finally, Section VI provides conclusions.

II
Related Work

Digital food analysis has been an active research field. This section reviews key developments in CNN-based recognition, object detection, multimodal models, and the recent impact of LLMs.

II-A

Classical and CNN-based Food Recognition

Spatial domain techniques, or classical CV, were the first approach. Methods using SIFT, LBP, and SVMs were common
[
9
]
but lacked robustness
[
6
]
. The field shifted definitively to deep learning with the success of CNNs
[
8
]
. Foundational work used architectures like AlexNet and VGG
[
20
]
. More advanced systems employed ensembles, such as Fakhrou et al.
[
7
]
, who used an ensemble of DenseNet201 and InceptionV3 for a smartphone-based system. This confirmed that deeper, more complex CNNs were the most effective approach for classification.

II-B

Object Detection in Food Analysis

As systems grew more sophisticated, classifying an entire image as one dish was insufficient for real-world meals containing multiple items. This led to the adoption of object detection models
[
18
]
. Models from the YOLO family
[
10
]
or R-CNN variants
[
9
,
19
]
can draw bounding boxes around each food item. Wang et al.
[
1
]
used Tiny-YOLOv2 to detect food items, and other works like
[
11
]
used segmentation for quality inspection. This allows for a more granular, multi-item analysis.

II-C

Multimodal and Vision-Language Models (VLMs)

VLMs represent a tighter integration of vision and language. Architectures like CLIP
[
3
]
learn a shared semantic space, enabling zero-shot reasoning. Ma et al.
[
3
]
demonstrated this by using VLMs for high-throughput nutrition screening from package images. Other approaches, like ”inverse cooking”
[
4
]
, attempt to predict ingredients (text) directly from an image, which are then fed into a recipe recommendation system.

II-D

LLMs in Nutritional Analysis

While VLMs are powerful, their application is often distinct from pure generative LLMs. Text-based LLMs have shown immense capability in nutritional science. Wu et al.
[
15
]
used LLMs for automated text classification of food initiatives. Hua et al.
[
12
]
introduced NutriBench, a dataset specifically for evaluating an LLM’s ability to estimate nutrition from *text descriptions*. This highlights the LLM’s strength as a ”knowledge engine,” which our pipeline leverages.

II-E

Research Gap

Despite these advancements, a critical gap, identified by Li et al.
[
2
]
, persists: most systems are trained on culturally biased datasets (e.g., Food-101
[
28
]
) and fail significantly when applied to complex, non-Western cuisines like Chinese food. Furthermore, while systems exist for classification
[
7
]
or text-based generation
[
12
]
, there is a lack of a comprehensive, end-to-end evaluation of a *decoupled* pipeline (Classifier-to-LLM) on such a culturally-specific dataset. Our work aims to fill this gap.

III
Methodology and Model Formalism

This section details the algorithmic implementation and mathematical formalism of the two main modules evaluated: the Visual Backbone (V) and the Generative Knowledge Model (L).

III-A

Visual Backbone (V): Classifiers

The classifier function
f_{V}:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
maps an image
I
to a logit vector
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
for
N
classes. The final probability vector
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
is computed using the Softmax function:

\hat{y}_{i}=\text{Softmax}(z_{i})=\frac{e^{z_{i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{z_{j}}}

(1)

All classifiers are trained by minimizing the categorical cross-entropy loss
\mathcal{L}_{CE}
:

\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{y},\hat{\mathbf{y}})=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\log(\hat{y}_{i})

(2)

where
\mathbf{y}
is the one-hot ground-truth vector.

III-A
1
VGG-16 (Baseline 1)

VGG-16
[
8
,
20
]
is a sequential CNN composed of blocks
B_{VGG}
of
k
3x3 convolutions followed by max-pooling:

B_{VGG}(\mathbf{x})=\text{Pool}(\text{ReLU}(\text{Conv}(\dots\text{ReLU}(\text{Conv}(\mathbf{x})))))

(3)

Its depth provides strong feature extraction but with high computational cost.

III-A
2
ResNet-50 (Baseline 2)

ResNet-50
[
8
,
19
]
introduces the residual (or ”shortcut”) connection:

\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x},\{W_{i}\})+\mathbf{x}

(4)

where
\mathcal{F}
is the residual function. This allows the network to learn an identity mapping, mitigating the vanishing gradient problem.

III-A
3
EfficientNet-B4 (Primary Model)

EfficientNet
[
7
,
17
]
uses **compound scaling** to balance network depth
d
, width
w
, and resolution
r
using a coefficient
\phi
:

d=\alpha^{\phi},\quad w=\beta^{\phi},\quad r=\gamma^{\phi}

(5)

subject to
\alpha\cdot\beta^{2}\cdot\gamma^{2}\approx 2
. This principled scaling, combined with Mobile Inverted Bottleneck (MBConv) blocks with Squeeze-and-Excitation
[
25
]
, provides the optimal trade-off between accuracy and efficiency.

III-B

Visual Backbone (V): Detector

III-B
1
YOLOv8 (Detector Baseline)

YOLOv8
[
10
]
is a single-stage object detector. Its function is
f_{V}:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\{B_{i},c_{i},p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
. Its multi-part loss function is:

\mathcal{L}_{YOLO}=\lambda_{\text{box}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{box}}+\lambda_{\text{cls}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}+\lambda_{\text{obj}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{obj}}

(6)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{box}}
is the bounding box regression loss, often an advanced metric like Complete-IoU (CIoU) Loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}}=1-\text{IoU}+\frac{\rho^{2}(b,b_{gt})}{c^{2}}+\alpha v

(7)

where
\rho^{2}
is the Euclidean distance between box centers,
c
is the diagonal of the enclosing box, and
\alpha v
is a term penalizing aspect ratio discrepancies.

III-C

Generative Knowledge Model (L)

The generative module
f_{L}:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
takes a class
c
and a prompt
p
to generate a structured text output
T^{\prime}
.

III-C
1
Transformer and Self-Attention

Both Gemini and Gemma are based on the Transformer architecture
[
21
]
. The core mechanism is scaled dot-product self-attention:

\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{T}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V

(8)

where
Q
(Query),
K
(Key), and
V
(Value) are projections of the token embeddings. This allows the model to weigh the importance of different tokens when generating a response.

III-C
2
Gemini 1.5 Pro and Gemma

Gemini 1.5 Pro is a large, proprietary model
[
22
]
. Gemma (7B) is a lightweight, open-source model from the same family
[
10
]
. Both are autoregressive, modeling the probability of the next token
t_{i}
:

P(T|p(c);\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}P(t_{i}|t_{1},\dots,t_{i-1},p(c);\theta)

(9)

The key difference is the size of the parameter set
\theta
and the vastness of the training data, which impacts the model’s factual accuracy
[
12
,
27
]
.

Evaluation Metrics

We used a comprehensive set of metrics for each component.

IV-C
1
Vision Model (V) Metrics

For a class
c
,
TP
is True Positives,
FP
is False Positives, and
FN
is False Negatives.

\displaystyle\text{Precision}_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{TP_{c}}{TP_{c}+FP_{c}}

(10)

\displaystyle\text{Recall}_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{TP_{c}}{TP_{c}+FN_{c}}

(11)

\displaystyle\text{F1-Score}_{c}

\displaystyle=2\cdot\frac{\text{Precision}_{c}\cdot\text{Recall}_{c}}{\text{Precision}_{c}+\text{Recall}_{c}}

(12)

For mAP,
P(k)
is the precision at
k
items, and
\text{rel}(k)
is the relevance indicator:

\text{mAP}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{c=1}^{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{k=1}^{n}P(k)\times\text{rel}(k)

(13)

IV-C
2
Generative Model (L) Metrics

BLEU
(Bilingual Evaluation Understudy)
[
23
]
:

\text{BLEU}=BP\cdot\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\log p_{n}\right)

(14)

where
BP
is the Brevity Penalty and
p_{n}
is the n-gram precision.

ROUGE-L
(Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation)
[
26
]
is based on the Longest Common Subsequence (LCS).

R_{lcs}=\frac{LCS(X,Y)}{m},\quad P_{lcs}=\frac{LCS(X,Y)}{n},\quad F_{lcs}=\frac{(1+\beta^{2})R_{lcs}P_{lcs}}{R_{lcs}+\beta^{2}P_{lcs}}

(15)

3
End-to-End System Metric (SEP)

We propose the **Semantic Error Propagation (SEP)** score, calculated over the set of misclassified images
\mathcal{D}_{\text{err}}
:

SEP=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{err}}|}\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{err}}}d_{\text{sem}}(f_{L}(p(c_{\text{pred}})),f_{L}(p(c_{\text{true}})))

(16)

where
d_{\text{sem}}
is
1-\text{cosine similarity}
of SBERT embeddings
[
24
]
.

VI-A

Discussion: The Bottleneck of Perception

Our evaluation confirms that the end-to-end utility of this system is **fundamentally bottlenecked by its perceptive accuracy**. The LLM, in this decoupled architecture, acts as a ”garbage-in, garbage-out” amplifier. As seen in Case 2, Gemini’s coherence and factual accuracy become a *disadvantage* when the visual premise (token
c

VI-D

Conclusion

This paper presented an implementation and in-depth comparative evaluation of a decoupled, multimodal pipeline for food analysis, integrating an EfficientNet-B4 visual backbone with a Gemini LLM. Our findings, grounded in a new Custom Chinese Food Dataset (CCFD), are conclusive: 1) **EfficientNet-B4** (89.0% Acc.) and **Gemini 1.5 Pro** (Factual Accuracy 9.2/10) are the superior components for this pipeline, outperforming VGG, ResNet, and Gemma baselines. 2) The system’s primary failure point is the **visual backbone**. Our ”Semantic Error Propagation” (SEP) analysis showed that visual errors cascade, with semantic similarity errors (SEP score 0.15) being the most insidious as they are difficult to detect. 3) The use of a **culturally-specific dataset (CCFD)** was critical to achieving high accuracy
[
2
]
.

This research validates that this decoupled pipeline is highly effective, but its utility is ultimately limited by the quality of its ”vision.”
