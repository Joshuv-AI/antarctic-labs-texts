Rotational susceptibility of a hot and dense hadronic matter: A possible probe of QCD phase transition
Bhagyarathi Sahoo 1, Kshitish Kumar Pradhan 1, Dushmanta Sahu 2, Raghunath Sahoo 1

Abstract
We study the effect of rotation and the consequent angular momentum fluctuations in a hadron resonance gas produced in ultra-relativistic heavy ion collisions. The rotational susceptibilities ( χ ω \chi_{\rm\omega} , χ ω 2 \chi^{2}_{\rm\omega} , etc.), which quantify how much the system responds to a small angular velocity, are estimated for the first time, considering that these can be valuable indicators of the QCD phase transition. The higher-order rotational susceptibilities and their ratios are estimated in the presence and absence of baryon chemical potential ( μ B \mu_{\rm B} ) in the system. The effect of particle spin ( s s ) and rotational chemical potential ( ω \omega ) on the fluctuation of the angular momentum is studied. To consider a more realistic scenario, the effect of interactions between hadrons is taken into account by considering van der Waals-like interactions, which include both attractive and repulsive interactions. A phase transition, absent in an ideal hadron gas model, can be observed in an interacting hadron gas model.



I
Introduction

One of the interesting areas of research in high-energy physics is understanding the quantum chromodynamics (QCD) phase diagram. Both temperature (
T
) and baryon density (
\mu_{\rm B}
) play crucial roles in characterizing the phase transition from hadrons to a strongly interacting thermalized partonic matter, known as quark-gluon plasma (QGP). At vanishing or small baryochemical potential and very high temperature, the phase transition is of crossover type
Aoki:2006we
;
Ding:2015ona
;
Ratti:2018ksb
;
Bazavov:2019lgz
. However, at very high baryochemical potential and smaller temperatures, we expect a first-order phase transition to happen
Fukushima:2013rx
;
Fukushima:2010bq
;
Fischer:2018sdj
;
Fu:2022gou
. Both kinds of these phase transition lines are expected to meet at a point called the critical endpoint (CEP)
Stephanov:1998dy
;
Stephanov:2004wx
;
Asakawa:2015ybt
. Although the existence of CEP is yet to be confirmed by experiments, it has been a very hot topic of interest in the community. At the CEP, the static thermodynamic quantities show significant fluctuations due to large correlation lengths
Stephanov:1998dy
;
Stephanov:1999zu
. The conserved charges associated with the susceptibilities have a very different value in the confined and deconfined medium, thus allowing us to distinguish between the two phases. The measurements of the moments of conserved charges, such as baryon number (
B
), strangeness (
S
), and charge (
Q
), which are linked to their corresponding susceptibilities, can help us understand the critical point and subsequently the full QCD phase diagram
Bazavov:2020bjn
;
Li:2023mpv
. Thus, a theoretical study of such conserved quantities to understand the systems formed in ultra-relativistic collisions becomes necessary. Many studies have been conducted in this area by taking the hadron resonance gas (HRG) model
Garg:2013ata
and its modifications such as the excluded volume hadron resonance gas (EVHRG) model
Bhattacharyya:2013oya
;
Fu:2012zzc
, mean-field hadron resonance gas (MFHRG) model
Pal:2023zzp
, and van der Waals hadron resonance gas (VDWHRG) approach in the hadronic phase
Samanta:2017yhh
;
Vovchenko:2016rkn
. Although the HRG model agrees well with the lattice QCD (lQCD) results at a low-temperature regime for estimating thermodynamic quantities, it fails to match with lQCD while estimating the higher-order susceptibilities
Vovchenko:2016rkn
. An excluded volume HRG or the mean-field HRG model does a better job compared to an ideal HRG model. However, with both attractive and repulsive interactions, the van der Waals HRG model can give us better estimations of these susceptibilities while matching well with the lQCD data up to
T\sim 180
MeV
Samanta:2017yhh
;
Vovchenko:2016rkn
.

Recently, a new window has opened up in the field of high-energy collisions related to the initial orbital angular momentum created during the collisions and the subsequent vorticity formation and evolution. It is expected that a huge amount of vorticity is produced in peripheral heavy-ion collisions, along with a substantial magnetic field being produced due to the charged spectators. In a thermalized medium, this vorticity causes spin polarization of the particles in the system. Thus, the initial angular momentum of the system can be connected to the observation of spin polarization of various vector mesons and hyperons
STAR:2017ckg
;
Sahoo:2024yud
;
Sahoo:2024egx
;
Sahoo:2023oid
;
Sahoo:2025bkx
. This vorticity or rotation in the medium brings new insights into the area of heavy-ion collisions and, more importantly, to the study of phase transitions. Like temperature and baryon density, rotation (
\omega
) can also help in the deconfinement of partons
Mameda:2023sst
. Thus, the 2D QCD phase diagram changes into a 3D one, with rotational chemical potential in the 3rd axis. Exploring this new avenue for a better understanding of the medium formed in heavy-ion collisions is fascinating and timely.

Apart from this, there are various other ways in which vorticity can be generated in the system. For example, the jet-like fluctuations in the expanding fireball can induce a smoke-loop-type vortex around fast-moving particles
Betz:2007kg
. In addition, the inhomogeneous expansion of the fireball can also generate vorticity
Xia:2018tes
;
Jiang:2016woz
;
Wei:2018zfb
;
Becattini:2017gcx
. Moreover, vorticity can also be generated due to the Einstein-de Haas effect, which states that the magnetization in the QCD matter will induce a finite rotation in the system
Einstein:1915
. The finite viscosity present in the system can also generate vorticity and vice-versa
Sahoo:2023xnu
. Thus, even if the system is not produced from a non-central collision, there are still various ways in which rotation can be introduced into the medium. The strength of the vorticity field produced in the heavy-ion collisions can be estimated from the average global hyperon polarization measurement of
\Lambda
and
\bar{\Lambda}
hyperons using a non-relativistic statistical thermal model
Becattini:2016gvu
. Recently, the slope of the directed flow of hadrons has been used to get an estimate of the strength of the vorticity field
Jiang:2023vxp
. Moreover, this vorticity field induces the flow of axial current along the axis of rotation known as the chiral vortical effect (CVE), and collective excitations are called chiral vortical wave (CVW)
Gao:2012ix
. Thus, it becomes essential to understand and study the effect of the rotation of the medium formed in high-energy collisions.

In the presence of rotation, the general Euler’s thermodynamic relation changes, with the rotation chemical potential playing an important role
Becattini:2010
;
Florkowski:2017ruc
. The conserved quantum number related to rotation in the medium is the angular momentum, which is globally conserved. Near the QCD phase transition, the medium undergoes a significant change in its degrees of freedom, and consequently in its response to rotation. This manifests as a non-trivial behavior of the rotational susceptibility, reflecting a redistribution of angular momentum between the partonic and hadronic phases. Although total angular momentum is conserved globally, the medium’s capacity to store and transport angular momentum via spin and vorticity changes. This potentially offers a novel probe of the QCD critical region. This can be quantified by the rotational susceptibility, as an analogue of magnetic susceptibility, which is defined as the derivatives of the free energy of the medium with respect to the rotational chemical potential. The rotational (or vortical) susceptibility estimates the spin polarization of quarks and antiquarks in a finite-density QCD matter. The first and second-order quark spin polarization (first and second-order rotational susceptibility) has been estimated using the three-flavor rotating Nambu–Jona–Lasinio (NJL) model
Sun:2021hxo
;
Chen:2024utf
. The vortical susceptibility of finite-density QCD matter in the chirally broken phase was calculated in Ref.
Aristova:2016wxe
. Moreover, the study of rotational susceptibility has implications in low-energy solid-state physics
Gadagkar:2012
;
Fur:2012
. Although the rotational susceptibility calculation on the lattice simulation has not been explored yet, the magnetic susceptibility is estimated in Refs.
Bali:2012jv
.

To study this in the hadronic phase, one can use the hadron resonance gas (HRG) model, where hadrons are treated as point-like particles without any interactions among them. This simplistic model can explain the lQCD data of the thermodynamic behavior of the matter produced in high-energy collisions at low temperatures. However, after
T\simeq 150
MeV, the hadrons start to melt, and the HRG model estimations deviate from the lQCD data
Karsch:2003vd
. The HRG model is still a very robust model, albeit with its various assumptions, it is widely used in the literature. However, a more realistic case is incorporated by including both attractive and repulsive interactions among the hadrons in the so-called van der Waals hadron resonance gas (VDWHRG) model
Vovchenko:2016rkn
. In our previous work
Pradhan:2023rvf
, we have studied the effect of rotation on the thermodynamic properties of an interacting hadron resonance gas, where we showed how rotation affects the liquid-gas phase transition. In this work, we explore the angular momentum fluctuation through the rotational susceptibility in both the ideal and van der Waals hadron resonance gas models. We examine the effect of both attractive and repulsive interactions in a hadron gas under rotation. The higher-order rotational susceptibilities and their ratios are estimated at zero and finite baryon chemical potential. We find that these ratios show peculiar characteristics near the phase transition temperature and baryon chemical potential. Further, these ratios are found to be sensitive to rotational chemical potential and follow a particular ordering among themselves.

The structure of this article is as follows. In section
II
, we establish the methodology that we use for the calculation of rotational susceptibilities within the scope of a rotating VDWHRG model. In the next section
III
, we discuss the findings, and finally, in section
IV
, we summarise our results and highlight the possibility of future study along these directions.

II
Formulation

II.1
Rotating HRG model with van der Waals interaction

The single-particle phase-space distribution function
f({\bf x},{\bf p},\boldsymbol{\omega})
for an ideal relativistic system of massive fermions (bosons) with half-integral (integral) spin (
s
) rotating with angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is obtained in our previous study
Pradhan:2023rvf
. The phase-space distribution function
f({\bf x},{\bf p},\boldsymbol{\omega})
is given by

\begin{split}f({\bf x},{\bf p},\boldsymbol{\omega})&=\frac{\exp{[({\bf p}\cdot{\bf v})/T]}}{\exp{[(E_{i}-\mu_{i})/T]}\pm 1}\frac{\sinh(s+\frac{1}{2})\frac{\omega}{T}}{\sinh(\frac{\omega}{2T})},\end{split}

(1)

where
E_{i}=\sqrt{p^{2}+m_{i}^{2}}
is the energy and
m_{i}
being the mass of the
i^{th}
hadron. Here,
\bf v
is the velocity of the particle. The
\pm
sign corresponds to fermions and bosons, respectively. Here,
\mu_{i}
is the total chemical potential of the system, which can be further expanded into

\mu_{i}=B_{i}\mu_{B}+S_{i}\mu_{S}+Q_{i}\mu_{Q}

(2)

where
\mu_{\rm B}
,
\mu_{\rm S}
, and
\mu_{\rm Q}
are the baryon chemical potential, strangeness chemical potential, and electric charge chemical potential, respectively. With
B_{i}
,
S_{i}
, and
Q_{i}
denoting the baryon number, strangeness, and electric charge quantum number of the
i^{th}
hadron, respectively.

To derive Eq. (
1
), we assume a system of relativistic gas, rotating with a constant angular velocity vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}
. To have a constant angular velocity vector,
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, we can define the rigid velocity,
\mathbb{v}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbb{x}
for a system of size
x
. A similar phase space distribution function is obtained for classical rotating gas in Ref.
Becattini:2009wh
. The non-relativistic thermodynamic equilibrium in a rotating system can be defined when the rotation is assumed to be rigid
Becattini:2007nd
;
Becattini:2009wh
;
landau
. In the relativistic system, another constraint of
|\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbb{x}|\ll 1
(in natural units) is added to respect the causality condition. Therefore, the ratio of
\omega
to
T
is very small for a proper macroscopic system (and in fact, for the majority of practical reasons), i.e.,
\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_{B}T}\ll 1
. In this study, we assume a rotation to be a rigid rotation; for this, we take the system size,
x=R
, to be 5 fm throughout the work, which is a safe assumption for the hadronic system produced in high-energy collisions. But, it is important to note that the value of the chosen
R
will have a significant impact on the results. The values of
\omega
are chosen such that they respect the causality condition (
\omega R<1
). Also, for the considered values of
\omega
, the Lorentz factor is found to be close to unity and will have a negligible impact on the results. Hence, it is neglected in the current formalism.

The ideal thermodynamic pressure variable for the
i^{th}
particle (
P_{i}^{id}
) for a rotating system can be obtained from the phase-space distribution function
f({\bf x},{\bf p},\boldsymbol{\omega})
in Eq. (
1
) and is given by;

P_{i}^{id}=\frac{g_{i}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}p^{2}dp\ \frac{p^{2}}{3E_{i}}f({\bf x},{\bf p},\boldsymbol{\omega}).

(3)

Here,
g_{i}
is the degeneracy of
i^{th}
hadron. By summing the pressure over all hadron species, the total pressure of the hadron resonance gas model can be obtained as follows

P^{id}=\sum_{i}P^{id}_{i}.

(4)

It is worth mentioning that the ideal HRG model does not incorporate any interactions among the hadrons. Therefore, the ideal HRG model is unable to explain different thermodynamic quantities, and conserved charge susceptibilities results estimated from the first principle based on lQCD calculations at high temperatures and baryon densities. With time, several statistical models have been developed to include the interactions in the medium. Now, to start with, we use the van der Waals equation of state in the canonical ensemble representation, which can be written as
Vovchenko:2016rkn

\Bigg(P+\bigg(\frac{N}{V}\bigg)^{2}a\Bigg)\big(V-Nb\big)=NT

(5)

where
a
and
b
are the VDW parameters, and describe the attractive and repulsive interactions among the hadrons, respectively. The
P
,
V
,
T
, and
N
represent the pressure, volume, temperature, and the number of particles in the system, respectively.

Writing the above Eq. (
5
) in terms of the number density,
n\equiv N/V,
we have

P(T,n)=\frac{nT}{1-bn}-an^{2},

(6)

The two correction terms in the above Eq. (
6
) compared to the ideal case (
P(T,n)=nT
) are due to the repulsive and attractive interaction. The repulsive interaction (first term) is incorporated via the excluded volume correction method by changing the total volume V to an effective volume that is accessible to particles. It is taken care of by using the appropriate volume parameter
b=16\pi r^{3}/3
, where
r
is the particle’s hardcore radius. We use
r_{M}=0.2
fm for mesons, and
r_{B,(\bar{B})}=0.62
fm for (anti)baryons. In contrast, the attractive interaction (second term) is set to
a=0.926
GeV fm
3
. There are various ways to extract the VDW parameters. These can be estimated by reproducing the ground state of the nuclear matter
Vovchenko:2015vxa
, or by fitting lQCD results for different thermodynamic quantities like energy density and pressure, etc.
Samanta:2017yhh
;
Sarkar:2018mbk
. The Eq. (
6
) reduces to the EVHRG equation of state for
a=0
, where only repulsive interactions are included. And it reduces to the ideal HRG case for both
a=0
,
\&

b=0
.

For the Grand Canonical Ensemble (GCE) similar procedure can be followed, and the VDW equation of state can be expressed as follows
Vovchenko:2015vxa
;
Vovchenko:2015pya

P(T,\mu)=P^{id}(T,\mu^{*})-an^{2}(T,\mu),

(7)

where,
P(T,\mu)
, and
P^{id}(T,\mu)
is the VDW, and ideal pressure, respectively. When there is no interaction in the system, the VDW pressure reduces to the ideal HRG pressure. The particle number density of the VDWHRG model,
n(T,\mu
) is given by

n(T,\mu)=\frac{\sum_{i}n_{i}^{id}(T,\mu^{*})}{1+b\sum_{i}n_{i}^{id}(T,\mu^{*})}.

(8)

Here,
i
runs over all hadrons and resonances in the interacting system, and
\mu^{*}
is the modified chemical potential due to the interaction present in the system. This
\mu^{*}
can be parametrized as follows

\mu^{*}=\mu-bP(T,\mu)-abn^{2}(T,\mu)+2an(T,\mu).

(9)

Using Eqs. (
7
) and (
6
),
\mu^{*}
can also be written as

\mu^{*}=\mu-\frac{bn(T,\mu)T}{1-bn(T,\mu)}+2an(T,\mu).

(10)

The VDWHRG model, as originally proposed, incorporates interactions limited to all baryon or anti-baryon pairs
Samanta:2017yhh
;
Vovchenko:2015vxa
;
Vovchenko:2015pya
;
Vovchenko:2016rkn
. The interaction between baryon-antibaryon couples was neglected by assuming the short-range interactions are dominated by annihilation processes
Andronic:2012ut
;
Vovchenko:2016rkn
. All meson-related interactions, including meson-meson and meson-(anti)baryon interactions, were previously disregarded because the inclusion of these interactions suppresses the thermodynamic quantities in comparison with lQCD data at the crossover region
Vovchenko:2016rkn
. Nevertheless, a more realistic formalism that considers meson-meson interactions was developed by selecting the VDW parameters that best suit the lQCD data by assuming a hard-core radius
r_{M}
for mesons
Sarkar:2018mbk
. Consequently, the total pressure of the VDWHRG model is denoted as;

P(T,\mu)=P_{M}(T,\mu)+P_{B}(T,\mu)+P_{\bar{B}}(T,\mu),

(11)

where,
P_{M}(T,\mu),P_{B(\bar{B})}(T,\mu)
denotes the pressure contributions due to mesons
M
and (anti)baryons
B(\bar{B})
, respectively. The pressure in the VDWHRG model is related to the ideal pressure via the relation

P_{M}(T,\mu)=\sum_{i\in M}P_{i}^{id}(T,\mu^{*M}),

(12)

P_{B}(T,\mu)=\sum_{i\in B}P_{i}^{id}(T,\mu^{*B})-an^{2}_{B}(T,\mu),

(13)

P_{\bar{B}}(T,\mu)=\sum_{i\in\bar{B}}P_{i}^{id}(T,\mu^{*\bar{B}})-an^{2}_{\bar{B}}(T,\mu).

(14)

Because of interaction, the
\mu^{*M}
,
\mu^{*B}
and
\mu^{*\bar{B}}
represent the modified chemical potentials for mesons, baryons and anti-baryons, respectively, and can be determined from Eq. (
2
) and Eq. (
9
) as;

\mu^{*M}=-bP_{M}(T,\mu),

(15)

\mu^{*B(\bar{B})}=\mu_{B(\bar{B})}-bP_{B(\bar{B})}(T,\mu)-abn^{2}_{B(\bar{B})}+2an_{B(\bar{B})},

(16)

where
n_{M}
,
n_{B}
, and
n_{\bar{B}}
denote the modified number densities for mesons, baryons, and anti-baryons, respectively, which are given by

n_{M}(T,\mu)=\frac{\sum_{i\in M}n_{i}^{id}(T,\mu^{*M})}{1+b\sum_{i\in M}n_{i}^{id}(T,\mu^{*M})},

(17)

n_{B(\bar{B})}(T,\mu)=\frac{\sum_{i\in B(\bar{B})}n_{i}^{id}(T,\mu^{*B(\bar{B})})}{1+b\sum_{i\in B(\bar{B})}n_{i}^{id}(T,\mu^{*B(\bar{B})})}.

(18)

It is important to note that the van der Waals parameters
a
and
b
used in this study are obtained in the absence of rotation in the medium. In principle, these parameters should vary as a function of rotation. However, as it is non-trivial to have
a
and
b
as functions of
\omega
, we neglect the dependency in the current study.

II.2
Fluctuation and conserved charge

The nth-order susceptibility is defined as

\chi_{i}^{n}=\frac{\partial^{n}(P(T,\mu_{i})/T^{4})}{\partial(\frac{\mu_{i}}{T})^{n}}.

(19)

Here,
\mu_{i}
is the chemical potential for the corresponding conserved charges. We take
i
to be baryon number (
B
),
electric charge (
Q
) and strangeness (
S
). The
n=1,2,3,4
susceptibilities are related to the mean, variance, skewness, and kurtosis of the distribution of the conserved charges.

In the presence of rotation, the standard Euler’s thermodynamic relation is modified into,

\varepsilon+P=Ts+\mu n+\omega\rm w

(20)

where,
\omega
is the rotation and
\rm w
is the rotation (or angular momentum) density. Equation (
20
) signifies that in a rotating system, rotation adds a new kind of chemical potential into the system called rotational chemical potential for a conserved charge. The correlations and fluctuations of any conserved charges are intrinsically linked to the corresponding susceptibility. Thus, we define a susceptibility for the rotational chemical potential, which can be written as,

\chi_{\rm\omega}^{n}=\frac{\partial^{n}[P(T,\mu_{i},\omega)/T^{4}]}{\partial(\frac{\omega}{T})^{n}}.

(21)

The first order derivative of the pressure with rotation is called the angular momentum and defined as follows;

J=\frac{\partial P(T,\mu_{i},\omega)}{\partial\omega}.

(22)

While the angular momentum is not strictly conserved, the rotational susceptibility
\chi_{\omega}
can still be meaningfully defined via the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
Kubo:1957mj
. In particular, it is connected to the thermal fluctuation of angular momentum through the relation
\chi_{\omega}=\frac{\langle(\Delta J_{z})^{2}\rangle}{k_{B}TV}
, where
\Delta J_{z}
denotes the fluctuation of the total angular momentum along the rotation axis, and
V
is the system volume. Unlike conserved charge susceptibilities (e.g., for baryon number, electric charge, or strangeness),
\chi_{\omega}
does not originate from a fundamental symmetry. Instead, it is analogous to the magnetic susceptibility
\chi_{M}
, which is also defined as a statistical response function tied to fluctuations of a non-conserved quantity, known as the magnetization. Both
\chi_{M}
and
\chi_{\omega}
reflect the system’s intrinsic tendency to respond to weak external fields (magnetic or rotational) through thermodynamic fluctuations. Thus, studying the susceptibility under the influence of rotation can provide insights about the correlations and fluctuations of angular momentum.

By using the formalism mentioned above, we estimate the rotational susceptibilities and their ratios for a rotating hadron gas with van der Waals interaction.

The first-order derivative of pressure (or free energy) with respect to rotation is called the rotation density or spin density, which is similar to the number density defined as the first-order derivative of pressure with respect to chemical potential. In general, the spin density contains crucial information about the system, such as the spin polarization of hadrons induced due to rotation (called rotational polarization). The angular momentum and the moment of inertia can also be calculated from the first-order derivative of free energy with respect to rotation. It is noteworthy to mention that in the presence of diffusion and smearing effects, rotational susceptibilities may offer a more robust probe of the QCD phase transition compared to traditional conserved charge susceptibilities such as baryon number susceptibility. Baryon number fluctuations are sensitive to local diffusion and freeze-out dynamics, making higher-order cumulants susceptible to thermal noise and experimental resolutions. On the other hand, rotational susceptibilities are tied to the system’s global angular momentum and collective vorticity. These rotational properties are less prone to being washed out by local diffusion processes, as they reflect coherent motion across the medium rather than individual particle number variations. Furthermore, rotational susceptibility, defined via the thermodynamic response of pressure to angular velocity, is less sensitive to local smearing and non-equilibrium effects because it characterizes a global thermodynamic response to rotation rather than local density fluctuations. This makes it a potentially cleaner observable. Thus, rotational susceptibilities provide a promising and complementary avenue for probing the QCD phase structure in high-energy heavy-ion collisions.

IV
Summary

In this study, we investigate angular momentum fluctuations in a rotating hadron resonance gas produced in ultra-relativistic heavy-ion collisions. For the first time, rotational susceptibilities, quantifying the system’s response to rotation, are evaluated in both ideal and interacting HRG models, with the latter incorporating van der Waals interactions to simulate realistic hadronic interactions. The analysis shows that higher-order rotational susceptibilities are particularly sensitive to rotation and display distinctive behaviors near the QCD phase transition region. The influence of hadron spin and baryon chemical potential on these susceptibilities is also explored. Notably, non-monotonic behavior in susceptibility ratios is observed in the case of VDWHRG, suggesting possible signatures of a phase transition. These findings highlight rotational susceptibility as a promising observable for probing QCD thermodynamics in heavy-ion collisions. In addition, such studies involving rotation exploring the phase diagram are essential and timely. Polarization obtained from experiments is proportional to spin-vorticity coupling (
<S^{\mu}>~\sim~\chi_{\rm\omega}~.~\omega^{\mu}
)
Li:2020eon
, thus, by comparing polarization data to hydrodynamic models with vorticity, one can constrain the rotational susceptibility.
