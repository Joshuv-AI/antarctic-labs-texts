Comparative analysis and practical applications of cubic transmutations for the Pareto distribution
Edoh Katchekpele, Issa Cherif Geraldo, Tchilabalo Abozou Kpanzou

Abstract
Transmutation is a technique for extending classical probability distributions in order to give them more flexibility. In this paper, we are interested in cubic transmutations of the Pareto distribution. We establish a general formula that unifies existing cubic transmutations of the Pareto distribution and facilitates the derivation of new cubic transmutations that have not yet been explored in the literature. We also derive general formulas for the related mathematical properties. Finally, we perform a comparative analysis of the six transmutations existing in the literature using real-world data. The results obtained confirm the flexibility and effectiveness of cubic transmutations in modeling various types of data. Keywords Cubic transmutation; Pareto distribution; Parameter estimation; Real-world data analysis. AMS Subject Classification (MSC2020) 60E05, 62E15, 62F10, 62F99, 62P99.



1
Introduction

Cubic transmutations of probability distributions are widely recognized for their ability to increase the flexibility of classical distributions, enabling them to better model complex data. By introducing additional parameters, these transformations enhance the capacity to fit empirical data across various applications, including those involving heavy-tailed distributions. In particular, such methods are used to extend traditional distributions, like the Pareto distribution, to accommodate real-world datasets more effectively. This paper focuses on cubic transmutations applied to the Pareto distribution.

Several studies have explored different transformations of the Pareto distribution.
Rahman et al., 2020a ()
investigated the cubic transmuted Pareto distribution, analyzing its statistical properties, parameter estimation techniques, and performance through real data applications. Similarly,
Eledum, (2020)
introduced two generalized cubic transmuted versions of the Exponentiated Pareto-I distribution, demonstrating that one formulation (CTEP-I-G) outperforms the other (CTEP-I-R) in empirical studies. In contrast,
El-Monsef et al., (2024)
presented a three-parameter G-Transmuted Pareto distribution, providing an in-depth analysis of its mathematical properties and reliability characteristics. While their work highlights the effectiveness of transmutation in failure time modeling, it does not specifically address cubic transmutations.

In a more recent study,
Geraldo et al., (2025)
compared various cubic transmutation approaches, particularly emphasizing the extension of classical probability distributions to better model complex data. The study explored relationships between different cubic transmutation methods, proposed modified versions by extending parameter ranges, and applied these modifications to the Pareto distribution as a baseline. However, this paper did not specifically address the six distinct cubic transmutations of the Pareto distribution, which is the focus of the current study.

The main objective of this paper is to derive a general formula that encompasses all six cubic transmutations of the Pareto distribution, unifying the existing ones and introducing the three that have not yet been explored in the literature. We also perform a comparative analysis of these six transmutations, assessing their performance using real-world data to evaluate their practical applicability in various contexts.

To achieve these objectives, the paper is structured as follows. Section
2
provides a brief review of the key definitions and recent improvements in cubic transmutation formulas. Section
3
introduces a generalized formulation for cubic transmuted Pareto distributions. In Section
4
, we discuss the statistical properties of these distributions, including moment calculations and other relevant results. The methodology for parameter estimation is presented in Section
5
. Section
6
then applies the six cubic transmutations to real-world datasets, comparing their goodness-of-fit and practical relevance. Finally, Section
7
summarizes the key findings and suggests directions for future research.

2
A brief review on definitions and improvements of cubic transmutation formulas

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
G(x)
be a cumulative distribution function (cdf). In general,
G(x)
may depend on a real parameter
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
or a parameter vector
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d\geqslant 2
. However, for simplicity and without loss of generality, we will omit
\xi
in the notation.

In what follows, we first recall the key definitions related to cubic transmutation formulas. Then, we discuss some recent improvements and refinements that enhance their applicability to probability distributions, particularly in the case of heavy-tailed models such as the Pareto distribution.

2.1
Existing formulas

In this subsection, we present six existing cubic transmutation formulas that have been proposed in the literature. These formulas define transformed cumulative distribution functions (cdfs) based on a given baseline cdf
G(x)
. Each transmutation is characterized by specific parameter constraints that ensure the resulting function remains a valid cdf.

The cubic transmutation due to
Granzotto et al., (2017)
(denoted
CT_{G}
) corresponds to the cdf

F_{G}(x)=\lambda_{1}G(x)+(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})G^{2}(x)+(1-\lambda_{2})G^{3}(x),

(1)

where the parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
satisfy

\mathscr{S}_{G}=\Big\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):0\leqslant\lambda_{1}\leqslant 1\;\;\text{and}\;\;-1\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant 1\Big\}.

(2)

Similarly, the cubic transmutation of
AL-Kadim and Mohammed, (2017)
(denoted
CT_{A}
) is given by the cdf

F_{A}(x)=(1+\lambda)G(x)-2\lambda G^{2}(x)+\lambda G^{3}(x),

(3)

where
-1\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 1
.

Rahman et al., 2018a ()
proposed another cubic transmutation formula, whose cdf is

F_{R18a}(x)=(1+\lambda_{1})G(x)+(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})G^{2}(x)-\lambda_{2}G^{3}(x),

(4)

with parameters constrained by

\mathscr{S}_{R18a}=\Big\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):-1\leqslant\lambda_{1}\leqslant 1,\;\;-1\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant 1\;\;\text{and}\;\;-2\leqslant\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\leqslant 1\Big\}.

(5)

Building on this approach,
Rahman et al., 2018b ()
introduced a new transformation of the form

F_{R18b}(x)=(1+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})G(x)-(\lambda_{1}+2\lambda_{2})G^{2}(x)+\lambda_{2}G^{3}(x),

(6)

where the parameters are constrained by

\mathscr{S}_{R18b}=\Big\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):-1\leqslant\lambda_{1}\leqslant 1\;\text{and}\;0\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant 1\Big\}.

(7)

Later,
Rahman et al., (2019)
proposed another cubic transmutation given by the cdf

F_{R19}(x)=(1-\lambda)G(x)+3\lambda G^{2}(x)-2\lambda G^{3}(x),

(8)

where
-1\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 1
.

More recently,
Rahman et al., (2023)
developed a modified cubic transmuted family of distributions, whose cdf is given by

F_{R23}(x)=\left[1-\lambda(\eta-1)\right]G(x)+\lambda(2\eta-1)G^{2}(x)-\lambda\eta G^{3}(x),

(9)

where

(\lambda,\eta)\in\mathscr{S}_{R23}=\Big\{(\lambda,\eta):-1\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 1\;\;\text{and}\;\;0\leqslant\eta\leqslant 2\Big\}.

(10)

While these cubic transmutation formulas provide flexible extensions of the baseline distribution, there is still room for improvement in terms of generalization and practical applicability. In the next section, we explore possible enhancements to these transformations.

2.2
Improvements of cubic transmuted distributions

Let
G(x)
be the cumulative distribution function (cdf) of a given random variable.
Geraldo et al., (2025)
have made theoretical comparisons of different cubic transmutation formulas with baseline cdf
G(x)
and proposed modified versions based on new parameter ranges. They also demonstrated that some of these distribution families are subfamilies of others.

Their main findings are summarized hereafter.

\bullet

The Granzotto Cubic Transmuted distribution with cdf given by Equation (
1
) is not always a well-defined distribution when parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
satisfy Equation (
2
).

The revised set of conditions

\mathscr{S}_{MG}=\Big\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):0\leqslant\lambda_{1}\leqslant 3,\;0\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant 3,\;\text{and}\;0\leqslant\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\leqslant 3\Big\}.

(11)

ensures the validity of
CT_{G}
leading to the modified Granzotto Cubic Transmutation
denoted
CT_{MG}
.

\bullet

The cubic transmuted family
CT_{A}
associated with
G(x)
, is well defined under the extended condition
-1\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 3
and this revised version is referred to as the modified Al-Kadim Cubic Transmutation and denoted
CT_{MA}
.

\bullet

The cubic transmuted distribution denoted
CT_{R18a}
, proposed by
Rahman et al., 2018a ()
and originally defined under conditions (
5
) is well-defined. However, the extended parameter range

\mathscr{S}_{MR18a}=\Big\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):-1\leqslant\lambda_{1}\leqslant 2,\;-1\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant 2,\;\text{and}\;-2\leqslant\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\leqslant 1\Big\}

(12)

ensures a broader applicability and leads to the modified version denoted
CT_{MR18a}
.

\bullet

Similarly, the cubic transmuted distribution introduced by
Rahman et al., 2018b ()
, denoted
CT_{R18b}
and originally constrained by conditions (
7
) is more flexible under the more general parameter range given by

\mathscr{S}_{MR18b}=\Big\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):-2\leqslant\lambda_{1}\leqslant 1,\;-2\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant 1,\;\text{and}\;-1\leqslant\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\leqslant 2\Big\},

(13)

thus leading to the modified version denoted
CT_{MR18b}
.

\bullet

The modified families
CT_{MG}
,
CT_{MR18a}
, and
CT_{MR18b}
have been found to be equivalent.

\bullet

The cubic transmuted distribution introduced by
Rahman et al., (2019)
, originally denoted
CT_{R19}
, remains well defined under the extended range
-2\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 1
and this leads to the modified cubic transmutation, denoted
CT_{MR19}
.

The modifications introduced by
Geraldo et al., (2025)
and presented above, extend
the applicability of cubic transmuted distributions by redefining their parameter ranges while preserving their flexibility. Building on these refined formulations, we now introduce a general definition of cubic transmutation that unifies these different approaches.

2.3
A proposed definition of cubic transmutation

To the best of our knowledge, and as reported in the literature
(
Rahman et al., 2020b,
;
Ali and Athar,, 2021
;
Imliyangba et al.,, 2021
, see, for example,)
, cubic transmutations of a cumulative distribution function (cdf)
G(x)
follow the general form:

F(x)=\delta_{1}G(x)+\delta_{2}G^{2}(x)+\delta_{3}G^{3}(x),

(14)

where
\delta=(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a parameter vector, with each
\delta_{i}
(
i=1,2,3
) potentially depending on one or more real parameters. The function
F(x)
must also be a valid cdf, meaning that it satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}F(x)=0,

(15a)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}F(x)=1,

(15b)

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\quad F^{\prime}(x)\geq 0.

(15c)

Since
G(x)
is itself a cdf, condition (
15a
) is always satisfied. Additionally, condition (
15b
) leads to the constraint:

\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}+\delta_{3}=1.

(16)

To examine condition (
15c
), let
g(x)=\frac{dG(x)}{dx}
be the probability density function (pdf) associated with
G(x)
, and define the pdf of
F(x)
as:

f(x)=\frac{dF(x)}{dx}=g(x)\left[\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2}G(x)+3\delta_{3}G^{2}(x)\right].

Thus, condition (
15c
) is equivalent to requiring that the function
r(t)=\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2}t+3\delta_{3}t^{2}
, defined for all
t=G(x)\in[0,1]
, remains non-negative. This observation leads to the following formal definition of cubic transmutation.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
G(x)
be a cumulative density function (cdf). A cdf is a cubic transmutation of
G(x)
if it has the form

F(x)=\delta_{1}G(x)+\delta_{2}G^{2}(x)+(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})G^{3}(x),

(17)

where
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is such that

\forall t\in[0,1],\quad r(t)=\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2}t+3(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})t^{2}\geqslant 0

(18)

or

\inf_{t\in[0,1]}\Big(\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2}t+3(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})t^{2}\Big)\geqslant 0.

(19)

Remark 2.1
.

Note that, in Definition
2.1
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}r(t)\,dt=\left[\delta_{1}t+\delta_{2}t^{2}+\delta_{3}t^{3}\right]_{0}^{1}=1.

Thus, the assumption that
r(t)
is positive for all
t\in[0,1]
is equivalent to stating that
r(t)
is a valid probability density function (pdf) on
[0,1]
. Moreover, if
R(t)=\delta_{1}t+\delta_{2}t^{2}+\delta_{3}t^{3}
is the cdf corresponding to the pdf
r(t)
, then

F(x)=R(G(x))=\int_{0}^{G(x)}r(t)\,dt.

(20)

This corresponds to a special case of a general formula proposed by
Alzaatreh et al., (2013)
.

3
A generalized formula for the Cubic Transmutation of Pareto distributions

The Pareto distribution is defined by its cdf as

G(x)=1-\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha},\quad x\geqslant x_{0},

(21)

and its pdf as

g(x)=\frac{\alpha x_{0}^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha+1}},\quad x\geqslant x_{0},

(22)

where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
.

Based on the quadratic transmutation proposed by
Shaw and Buckley, (2009)
,
Merovci and Puka, (2014)
developed the transmuted Pareto (TP) distribution. Several authors have since investigated cubic transmuted Pareto (CTP) distributions.

Building on the cubic transmutation (CT) formula of
AL-Kadim and Mohammed, (2017)
,
Ansari and Eledum, (2018)
introduced a CTP distribution, which we denote as
CTP_{A}
.
Rahman et al., 2020a ()
proposed another CTP distribution, denoted as
CTP_{R18a}
, based on the formula of
Rahman et al., 2018a ()
. Similarly,
Rahman et al., (2021)
introduced yet another CTP distribution, denoted as
CTP_{R18b}
, using the formula of
Rahman et al., 2018b ()
.

To the best of our knowledge, other CT formulas have not yet been applied to the Pareto distribution. We denote the CTP distributions derived from the formulas of
Granzotto et al., (2017)
,
Rahman et al., (2019)
, and
Rahman et al., (2023)
as
CTP_{G}
,
CTP_{R19}
, and
CTP_{R23}
, respectively.

Furthermore, the modified versions of
CTP_{A}
,
CTP_{R18a}
,
CTP_{R18b}
,
CTP_{G}
, and
CTP_{R19}
will be referred to as
CTP_{MA}
,
CTP_{MR18a}
,
CTP_{MR18b}
,
CTP_{MG}
, and
CTP_{MR19}
, respectively.

The following proposition provides a generalized formula for the cumulative distribution function (cdf) and probability density function (pdf) of the CTP distribution.

Proposition 3.1
.

The cumulative distribution function (cdf) and the probability density function (pdf) of the cubic transmutation of the Pareto distribution are respectively given by

F(x)=1+(2\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-3)\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+(3-3\delta_{1}-2\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}+(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-1)\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{3\alpha}

(23)

and

f(x)=\frac{\alpha x_{0}^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha+1}}\left[(3-2\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})+(6\delta_{1}+4\delta_{2}-6)\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+(3-3\delta_{1}-3\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}\right],

(24)

where
x_{0}>0
,
x\geqslant x_{0}
,
\alpha>0
and
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
satisfy the conditions (
18
) or (
19
) so that
f
is indeed a true pdf.

Proof
.

Let
F
be the cdf and
f
the pdf of any cubic transmutation of Pareto distribution. We have, using Equation (
17
),

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}\left(1-\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}\right)+\delta_{2}\left(1-\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}\right)^{2}+(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\left(1-\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}\right)^{3}

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}\left(1-\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}\right)+\delta_{2}\left(1-2\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\left(1-3\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+3\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}-\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{3\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}-\delta_{1}\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+\delta_{2}-2\delta_{2}\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+\delta_{2}\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}

\displaystyle\quad+(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})-3(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+3(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}-(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{3\alpha}

\displaystyle=(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}+1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})+(-\delta_{1}-2\delta_{2}-3+3\delta_{1}+3\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(\delta_{2}+3-3\delta_{1}-3\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}-(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{3\alpha}

\displaystyle=1+(2\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-3)\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+(3-3\delta_{1}-2\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}+(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-1)\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{3\alpha}.

Since
f(x)=F^{\prime}(x)
, we also have

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=(3-2\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\frac{\alpha x_{0}}{x^{2}}\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha-1}+(6\delta_{1}+4\delta_{2}-6)\frac{\alpha x_{0}}{x^{2}}\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(3-3\delta_{1}-3\delta_{2})\frac{\alpha x_{0}}{x^{2}}\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{3\alpha-1}

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha x_{0}^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha+1}}\left[(3-2\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})+(6\delta_{1}+4\delta_{2}-6)\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+(3-3\delta_{1}-3\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}\right].

∎

4
Moments and other results

4.1
Moments

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
be a random variable following the CTP distribution. For all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the
k^{\text{th}}
moment of
X
is given by

E(X^{k})=\alpha x_{0}^{k}\left[\frac{-\delta_{1}k^{2}+(5\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2})k\alpha-6\alpha^{2}}{(k-\alpha)(k-2\alpha)(k-3\alpha)}\right],\quad\alpha>k.

(37)

The mean and variance are respectively given by

E(X)=\alpha x_{0}\left[\frac{-\delta_{1}+(5\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2})\alpha-6\alpha^{2}}{(1-\alpha)(1-2\alpha)(1-3\alpha)}\right],\quad\alpha>1

(38)

and

V(X)=\alpha x_{0}^{2}\Bigg[\frac{-4\delta_{1}+(10\delta_{1}+4\delta_{2})\alpha-6\alpha^{2}}{(2-\alpha)(2-2\alpha)(2-3\alpha)}\\
-\alpha\left(\frac{-\delta_{1}+(5\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2})\alpha-6\alpha^{2}}{(1-\alpha)(1-2\alpha)(1-3\alpha)}\right)^{2}\Bigg],\qquad\alpha>2

(39)

Proof
.

By definition, the moment of order
k
is defined as

E(X^{k})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{k}f(x)\dd{}\mathrm{d}x.

(40)

By applying this formula to the pdf
f(x)
of any CTP distribution defined by Equation (
24
), we have:

\displaystyle E(X^{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}x^{k}\frac{\alpha x_{0}^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha+1}}\left[(3-2\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})+(6\delta_{1}+4\delta_{2}-6)\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+(3-3\delta_{1}-3\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}\right]\dd{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(3-2\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha x_{0}^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha+1-k}}\dd{}\mathrm{d}x+(6\delta_{1}+4\delta_{2}-6)\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha x_{0}^{2\alpha}}{x^{2\alpha+1-k}}\dd{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+(3-3\delta_{1}-3\delta_{2})\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha x_{0}^{3\alpha}}{x^{3\alpha+1-k}}\dd{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\alpha x_{0}^{\alpha}(3-2\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\left[-\frac{1}{(\alpha-k)x^{\alpha-k}}\right]_{x_{0}}^{\infty}+\alpha x_{0}^{2\alpha}(6\delta_{1}+4\delta_{2}-6)\left[-\frac{1}{(2\alpha-k)x^{2\alpha-k}}\right]_{x_{0}}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha x_{0}^{3\alpha}(3-3\delta_{1}-3\delta_{2})\left[-\frac{1}{(3\alpha-k)x^{3\alpha-k}}\right]_{x_{0}}^{\infty}.

The limits in
\infty
involved are finite (and zero) if we simultaneously have
\alpha>k
,
2\alpha>k
and
3\alpha>k
. Since
\alpha>0
, this is equivalent to
\alpha>k
. So, if
\alpha>k
,

\displaystyle E(X^{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha x_{0}^{\alpha}(3-2\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})}{(\alpha-k)x_{0}^{\alpha-k}}+\frac{\alpha x_{0}^{2\alpha}(6\delta_{1}+4\delta_{2}-6)}{(2\alpha-k)x_{0}^{2\alpha-k}}+\frac{\alpha x_{0}^{3\alpha}(3-3\delta_{1}-3\delta_{2})}{(3\alpha-k)x_{0}^{3\alpha-k}}

\displaystyle=\alpha x_{0}^{k}\left[\frac{2\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-3}{k-\alpha}+\frac{6-6\delta_{1}-4\delta_{2}}{k-2\alpha}+\frac{3\delta_{1}+3\delta_{2}-3}{k-3\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha x_{0}^{k}\left[\frac{(2\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-3)(k-2\alpha)(k-3\alpha)+(6-6\delta_{1}-4\delta_{2})(k-\alpha)(k-3\alpha)}{(k-\alpha)(k-2\alpha)(k-3\alpha)}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{(3\delta_{1}+3\delta_{2}-3)(k-\alpha)(k-2\alpha)}{(k-\alpha)(k-2\alpha)(k-3\alpha)}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha x_{0}^{k}\left[\frac{(2\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-3)(k^{2}-5k\alpha+6\alpha^{2})+(6-6\delta_{1}-4\delta_{2})(k^{2}-4k\alpha+3\alpha^{2})}{(k-\alpha)(k-2\alpha)(k-3\alpha)}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{(3\delta_{1}+3\delta_{2}-3)(k^{2}-3k\alpha+2\alpha^{2})}{(k-\alpha)(k-2\alpha)(k-3\alpha)}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha x_{0}^{k}\left[\frac{(2\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-3)k^{2}+(-10\delta_{1}-5\delta_{2}+15)k\alpha+(12\delta_{1}+6\delta_{2}-18)\alpha^{2}}{(k-\alpha)(k-2\alpha)(k-3\alpha)}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{(6-6\delta_{1}-4\delta_{2})k^{2}+(-24+24\delta_{1}+16\delta_{2})k\alpha+(18-18\delta_{1}-12\delta_{2})\alpha^{2}}{(k-\alpha)(k-2\alpha)(k-3\alpha)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{(3\delta_{1}+3\delta_{2}-3)k^{2}+(-9\delta_{1}-9\delta_{2}+9)k\alpha+(6\delta_{1}+6\delta_{2}-6)\alpha^{2}}{(k-\alpha)(k-2\alpha)(k-3\alpha)}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha x_{0}^{k}\left[\frac{-\delta_{1}k^{2}+(5\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2})k\alpha-6\alpha^{2}}{(k-\alpha)(k-2\alpha)(k-3\alpha)}\right].

The mathematical expectation
E(X)
and the variance
V(X)
are easily deduced by applying Equation (
37
) for
k=1
,
k=2
and by using the relation
V(X)=E(X^{2})-\left[E(X)\right]^{2}
.
∎

4.2
Moment generating function

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
X
be a random variable following the CTP distribution. The moment generating function is defined for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
by

M_{X}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha t^{k}x_{0}^{k}\left[-\delta_{1}k^{2}+(5\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2})k\alpha-6\alpha^{2}\right]}{k!\,(k-\alpha)(k-2\alpha)(k-3\alpha)},

(47)

where
\alpha>k
.

Proof
.

The moment generating function of a v.a
X
is given by

M_{X}(t)=E\left(\e^{tX}\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\e^{tx}f(x)\dd{}\mathrm{d}x.

From the power series expansion of
\e^{tx}
, we have

\displaystyle M_{X}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(tx)^{k}}{k!}\right)f(x)\dd{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(f(x)+txf(x)+\frac{t^{2}x^{2}}{2!}f(x)+\cdots+\frac{t^{n}x^{n}}{n!}f(x)+\cdots\right)\dd{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{k}E(X^{k})}{k!}.

∎

in Equation (

, we obtain Equation (

).

4.4
Reliability and hazard functions

Proposition 4.2
.

The reliability and hazard functions of CTP distribution are respectively given for all
t>0
by

R(t)=(3-2\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+(3\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2}-3)\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}+(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{3\alpha}

(49)

and

h(t)=\frac{\alpha}{x}\left[\dfrac{(3-2\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})+(6\delta_{1}+4\delta_{2}-6)\left(\dfrac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+(3-3\delta_{1}-3\delta_{2})\left(\dfrac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}}{(3-2\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})+(3\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2}-3)\left(\dfrac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}+(1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\left(\dfrac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{2\alpha}}\right].

(50)

Proof
.

The proof is simple recalling that for all
t>0
, the reliability function is given by
R(t)=1-F(t)
and the hazard function is given by

h(t)=\frac{f(t)}{1-F(t)}=\frac{f(t)}{R(t)}.

∎

5
Parameter estimation

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
be a random sample of size
n
from the Cubic Transmuted Pareto defined by pdf (
24
). The general form of the log-likelihood of a CTP is

\ell=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(x_{i})

(51)

which can be rewritten as

\ell=n\log(\alpha)+n\alpha\log(x_{0})-(\alpha+1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[(3-2\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})+(6\delta_{1}+4\delta_{2}-6)\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}+(3-3\delta_{1}-3\delta_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{2\alpha}\right].

(52)

Since for all
i=1,\ldots,n
,
x_{0}\leqslant x_{i}
, the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) of
x_{0}
is the minimum of the sample values i.e.
\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}x_{i}
. The MLEs of the other parameters are obtained by maximizing the respective log-likelihoods of the different models defined below.

\bullet

The log-likelihood corresponding to both the
CTP_{G}
and the
CTP_{MG}
is

\ell_{G}(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=n\log(\alpha)+n\alpha\log(x_{0})-(\alpha+1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[(3-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})+(2\lambda_{1}+4\lambda_{2}-6)\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}+(3-3\lambda_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{2\alpha}\right].

(53)

The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE)
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda}_{1},\hat{\lambda}_{2})
of
(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is the solution to the constrained optimization problem

(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda}_{1},\hat{\lambda}_{2})=\operatorname*{arg\;max}_{(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathscr{S}}\ell_{G}(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),

(54)

where
\mathscr{S}=\mathscr{S}_{G}
if we consider the
CTP_{G}
and
\mathscr{S}=\mathscr{S}_{MG}
if we consider the
CTP_{MG}
.

\bullet

The log-likelihood corresponding to both the
CTP_{A}
of
Ansari and Eledum, (2018)
and the
CTP_{MA}
is

\ell_{A}(\alpha,\lambda)=n\log(\alpha)+n\alpha\log(x_{0})-(\alpha+1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[1-2\lambda\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}+3\lambda\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{2\alpha}\right].

(55)

The MLE
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda})
of
(\alpha,\lambda)
is the solution to the constrained optimization problem

(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda})=\operatorname*{arg\;max}_{(\alpha,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathscr{S}}\ell_{A}(\alpha,\lambda),

(56)

where
\mathscr{S}=[-1,1]
if we consider the
CTP_{A}
and
\mathscr{S}=[-1,3]
if we consider the
CTP_{MA}
.

\bullet

The log-likelihood corresponding to both
CTP_{R18a}

(
Rahman et al., 2020a,
)
and
CTP_{MR18a}
is

\ell_{R18a}(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=n\log(\alpha)+n\alpha\log(x_{0})-(\alpha+1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[(1-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})+2(\lambda_{1}+2\lambda_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}-3\lambda_{2}\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{2\alpha}\right].

(57)

The MLE
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda}_{1},\hat{\lambda}_{2})
of
(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is the solution to the constrained optimization problem

(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda}_{1},\hat{\lambda}_{2})=\operatorname*{arg\;max}_{(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathscr{S}}\ell_{R18a}(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),

(58)

where
\mathscr{S}=\mathscr{S}_{R18a}
if we consider the
CTP_{R18a}
and
\mathscr{S}=\mathscr{S}_{MR18a}
if we consider the
CTP_{MR18a}
.

\bullet

The log-likelihood corresponding to both
CTP_{R18b}
of
Rahman et al., (2021)
and
CTP_{MR18b}
is

\ell_{R18b}(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=n\log(\alpha)+n\alpha\log(x_{0})-(\alpha+1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[(1-\lambda_{1})+2(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}+3\lambda_{2}\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{2\alpha}\right].

(59)

The MLE
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda}_{1},\hat{\lambda}_{2})
of
(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is the solution to the constrained optimization problem

(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda}_{1},\hat{\lambda}_{2})=\operatorname*{arg\;max}_{(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathscr{S}}\ell_{R18b}(\alpha,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),

(60)

where
\mathscr{S}=\mathscr{S}_{R18b}
if we consider the
CTP_{R18b}
and
\mathscr{S}=\mathscr{S}_{MR18b}
if we consider the
CTP_{MR18b}
.

\bullet

The log-likelihood corresponding to both
CTP_{R19}
and
CTP_{MR19}
is

\ell_{R19}(\alpha,\lambda)=n\log(\alpha)+n\alpha\log(x_{0})-(\alpha+1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[(1-\lambda)+6\lambda\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}-6\lambda\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{2\alpha}\right].

(61)

The MLE
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda})
of
(\alpha,\lambda)
is the solution to the constrained optimization problem

(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda})=\operatorname*{arg\;max}_{(\alpha,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathscr{S}}\ell_{R19}(\alpha,\lambda),

(62)

where
\mathscr{S}=[-1,1]
if we consider the
CTP_{R19}
and
\mathscr{S}=[-2,1]
if we consider the
CTP_{MR19}
.

\bullet

The log-likelihood corresponding to
CTP_{R23}
is

\ell_{R23}(\alpha,\lambda,\eta)=n\log(\alpha)+n\alpha\log(x_{0})-(\alpha+1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[(1-\lambda)+2\lambda(1+\eta)\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}-3\lambda\eta\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\right)^{2\alpha}\right].

(63)

The MLE
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda},\hat{\eta})
of
(\alpha,\lambda,\eta)
is the solution to the constrained optimization problem

(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\lambda},\hat{\eta})=\operatorname*{arg\;max}_{(\alpha,\lambda,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times[-1,1]\times[0,2]}\ell_{R23}(\alpha,\lambda,\eta).

(64)

All these constrained optimization problems are difficult to solve analytically and therefore require the use of a numerical optimization algorithm. One key requirement for such algorithms is their ability to handle inequality constraints. In the remainder of this paper, we will use the R function
constrOptim
, which includes optimization algorithms that incorporate inequality constraints.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we conducted a comprehensive study of six cubic transmutations of the Pareto distribution, introducing a general formulation that unifies and extends both previously studied and novel cubic transmutations. This formulation provides a systematic framework for constructing new transmuted versions while ensuring key probabilistic properties. By analyzing these cubic transmutations theoretically and empirically, we demonstrated their ability to enhance the flexibility of the Pareto distribution, leading to improved fits across diverse real-world datasets, particularly in reliability analysis, economics, and engineering.

Our comparative analysis of the six cubic transmuted Pareto distributions highlighted their respective strengths, with some transmutations offering better fits depending on the dataset. The empirical study, based on the Wheaton River dataset
(
Choulakian and Stephens,, 2001
)
and Norwegian fire insurance claims
(
Reynkens and Verbelen,, 2024
)
, showcased the practical relevance of these models. Notably, the refinements proposed by
Geraldo et al., (2025)
improved the behavior of some of the transmutations, ensuring they remain well-defined probability distributions and significantly enhancing their performance in capturing extreme values.

While this work strengthens the theoretical foundation of cubic transmutations, future research could explore further extensions and applications across broader domains. Additionally, investigating the mathematical conditions that guarantee well-defined transmutations, along with a deeper analysis of the trade-off between model complexity and interpretability, would contribute to optimizing their practical use in statistical modeling.
