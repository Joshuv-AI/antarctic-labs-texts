The quantum communication power of indefinite causal order
Xuanqiang Zhao, Benchi Zhao, Giulio Chiribella

Abstract
Quantum theory is in principle compatible with scenarios where physical processes take place in an indefinite causal order, a possibility that was shown to yield advantages in several information processing tasks. However, advantages in communication, the most basic form of information processing, have so far remained controversial and hard to prove. Here we develop a framework that can be used to rigorously assess the role of causal order in a scenario where communication links are built by assembling multiple quantum devices. In this setting, we establish a clear-cut advantage of indefinite order in the one-shot transmission of classical messages. On the other hand, we also show that the advantage is not generic to all communication tasks. Notably, we find that indefinite order does not offer any advantage over shared entanglement in the asymptotic scenario where a large number of uses of the same communication device is employed. Overall, our results unveil non-trivial relations between communication, causal order, entanglement, and no-signaling resources in quantum mechanics.



I
Introduction

Most physical theories, with the notable exception of general relativity and its quantum extensions, describe the evolution of physical systems through time.
In this picture, the evolution of a physical system typically consists of a sequence of physical processes taking place in a well-defined order.
It has been observed, however, that the structure of quantum theory is in principle compatible with scenarios where the order becomes indefinite
Chiribella et al. 2009a
;
Oreshkov et al. 2012
;
Chiribella et al. 2013
.
Part of the motivation to study these scenarios comes from the long-standing quest to quantize gravity, which naturally leads to the hypothesis that spacetime and causal structure could be subject to quantum indeterminacy
Hardy 2007
;
Hardy 2009
.
Further motivation comes from the attempt to characterize quantum theory in terms of information processing
Fuchs 2003
;
Brassard 2005
;
Chiribella and Spekkens 2016
.
In this context, the in-principle possibility of indefinite order is a striking consequence of the quantum framework, and the study of its information-theoretic implications offers new insights into the nature of the framework itself.

Over the past two decades, advantages of indefinite order have been discovered in a variety of information processing tasks, including quantum processes discrimination
Chiribella 2012
;
Bavaresco et al. 2021
;
Bavaresco et al. 2022
, query complexity
Abbott et al. 2024
;
Abbott et al. 2025
, communication complexity
Guérin et al. 2016
;
Wei et al. 2019
, quantum metrology
Zhao et al. 2020
;
Yin et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
, and inversion of unknown dynamics
Quintino et al. 2019a
;
Quintino et al. 2019b
;
Quintino and Ebler 2022
.
In all these tasks, quantum operations with indefinite order were proven to outperform all possible quantum operations compatible with the existence of a definite order.
Quite strikingly, however, no such separation was found so far in the most basic information-processing task, upon which the whole of information theory was built, namely the communication of messages from a sender to a receiver
Shannon 1948
;
Shannon 1956
.
In the context of communication, the role of indefinite order has remained controversial so far.
A body of papers
Ebler et al. 2018
;
Salek et al. 2018
;
Goswami et al. 2020
;
Chiribella et al. 2021a
;
Chiribella et al. 2021b
showed that combining noisy channels in an indefinite order leads to enhancements with respect to the standard quantum communication scenario, in which channels are combined in parallel or in sequence, without introducing additional systems that control their configuration.
However, other works observed that the above enhancements only represent an advantage of indefinite causal order over a restricted set of scenarios with definite order, and therefore do not establish a separation between the performance of definite and indefinite order
Abbott et al. 2020
;
Guérin et al. 2019
.
Ref.
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
pointed out that establishing a separation requires a resource-theoretic framework where the basic resources are communication devices, assembled into communication networks by a restricted set of allowed operations.
However, the specific choice of allowed operations remained open since then, and no communication advantage of indefinite causal order was proven so far.

Here, we provide the first rigorous demonstration of an advantage of indefinite order in quantum communication.
To this purpose, we develop a resource theory of communication that treats definite and indefinite order in a unified way.
In this resource theory, devices that can transmit signals are regarded as a resource, and operations that do not generate signaling from signaling-incapable devices are regarded as allowed.
Among the allowed operations, we show that operations with indefinite order offer advantages in one-shot communication tasks.
Specifically, we show that indefinite order enables a noiseless, single-shot transmission of a bit through two amplitude damping channels, whereas all the operations with definite order yield a noisy communication.
This result establishes a clear-cut separation between the communication power of definite and indefinite causal order.

We also show, however, that the communication advantage of indefinite causal order is not generic to all quantum channels and to all communication tasks.
In particular, we show that indefinite order does not offer any advantage for the class of Pauli channels, whose maximum classical capacity can already be reached by placing the channels in parallel and exploiting no-signaling resources.
Furthermore, we show that every advantage of indefinite causal order in classical communication must necessarily disappear in the asymptotic limit of a large number of channel uses.
In this asymptotic scenario, we prove that shared entanglement between the sender and receiver is sufficient to achieve the highest possible value of the classical channel capacity.
Our results reveal that the landscape of relations between communication, causal order, entanglement, and no-signaling resources is more complex, and more profound than previously envisaged.

II
Results

The resource theory of signaling.

Here we develop a resource theory where the key resource is the ability to send signals.
In this resource theory, the basic objects are communication devices, and the basic operations assemble communication devices into links connecting a sender to a receiver.
Mathematically, the basic resources are represented by lists of quantum channels (completely positive, trace preserving maps transforming the density matrix of an input system into the density matrix of an output system.)
For a list of
N
channels, we will use the vector notation
\vec{{\cal C}}=({\cal C}_{1},\dots,{\cal C}_{N})
, and we will denote by
X_{i}
(
Y_{i}
) the input (output) system of the
i
-th channel in the list.

The resource theory is constructed by first specifying a set of devices which are regarded as non-resourceful, or free, and then by considering the largest set of operations that do not create resources
Chitambar and Gour 2019
.
Here, we focus on the resource of signaling: we regard as free all the devices that block any signal, by outputting a fixed state independent of their input.
Mathematically, these devices are described by replacement channels, that is, quantum channels
{\cal C}
satisfying the condition
{\cal C}(\rho)=\rho_{0}\,\forall\rho
, where
\rho
is an arbitrary density matrix of the input system, and
\rho_{0}
is a fixed density matrix of the output system.
In general, free resources are described by lists of replacement channels.

Let us now specify the allowed operations that can be used to assemble quantum channels into communication links.
Mathematically, operations on quantum channels are described by quantum supermaps
Chiribella et al. 2008a
;
Chiribella et al. 2008b
;
Chiribella et al. 2013
;
Bisio and Perinotti 2019
, that is, linear maps transforming (lists of) quantum channels into quantum channels.
In general, a supermap can transform a list of
N
channels into another list of
M
channels.
In the following, we will focus on the case of
M=1
, corresponding to the case in which the
N
input channels in the initial list are assembled to build a communication channel connecting a sender to a receiver.
The input and output systems of the channel produced by the supermap will be denoted by
A
and
B
, respectively.

The largest set of operations that do not generate resources is the set of signaling-non-generating supermaps, that is, quantum supermaps that transform lists of replacement channels into replacement channels.
In our resource theory, signaling-non-generating supermaps will be regarded as free operations.
In the special case where each list consists of a single channel, the set of signaling-non-generating supermaps has been known as “no-signaling resources” and has been fruitfully adopted in quantum Shannon theory
Duan and Winter 2016
;
Wang and Duan 2016
;
Wang and Duan 2018
.
No-signaling resources have been recently cast in a resource-theoretic framework
Fang et al. 2020
;
Takagi et al. 2020
, and our resource theory can be regarded as the extension of this resource-theoretic framework to the case where multiple input channels can be assembled in a non-trivial order.

A complete characterization of the set of free operations is provided in Methods, where we show that signaling-non-generating supermaps are in one-to-one correspondence with a subset of multipartite channels satisfying a no-signaling condition, which we call the “No Forward Signaling Condition.”
Explicitly, a signaling-non-generating supermap
{S}
transforming a list of
N
channels with input/output pairs
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
into a channel with input/output pair
(A,B)
corresponds to a quantum channel with input system
AY
and output system
BX
(with the notation
X\coloneqq X_{1}\cdots X_{N}
and
Y\coloneqq Y_{1}\cdots Y_{N}
), satisfying the condition that no signal can be sent from
A
to
B
.
Informally, the No Forward Signaling Condition states that the supermap does not allow signals to travel directly from a sender to a receiver, bypassing the devices that are used to build the communication link.
This condition guarantees that our theory satisfies a requirement known as no-side channel generation, previously put forward as a minimal requirement that any resource theory of communication should satisfy
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
.

The resource theory where the free resources are replacement channels and the free operations are the signaling-non-generating supermaps will be called the “resource theory of signaling.”
With the set of free operations in place, we are now in position to rigorously analyze the role of indefinite causal order.
To this purpose, we define three subsets of the set of free operations
, ordered by mutual inclusion.
The smallest set is the set of free supermaps that can be achieved by placing the
N
input channels in parallel inside a quantum circuit
Chiribella et al. 2008b
.
The second set is the set of free supermaps that can be achieved by placing the
N
input channel inside a quantum circuit in a fixed, pre-assigned order.
These supermaps are known as quantum combs
Chiribella et al. 2008b
;
Chiribella et al. 2009b
.
The third set is the set of free supermaps that can be achieved by placing the
N
input channels inside a quantum circuit, in an order that is definite but possibly dynamically determined at subsequent steps of the circuit.
These supermaps correspond to quantum circuits with classical control
Oreshkov and Giarmatzi 2016
;
Wechs et al. 2021
, and represent the most general class of operations with definite causal order.
In the following, the sets of free supermaps with parallel placement, fixed-order placement, and definite (dynamical) order placement will be denoted by
{\bf FreePar}
,
{\bf FreeFix}
, and
{\bf FreeDef}
, respectively.
One has the (generally strict) inclusions
{\bf FreePar}\subseteq{\bf FreeFix}\subseteq{\bf FreeDef}\subseteq{\bf Free}
, where
{\bf Free}
denotes the set of general supermaps that are free in our resource theory.
Free supermaps that are in
{\bf Free}
but not in
{\bf FreeDef}
are free operations with indefinite causal order.
In the following, we will show that using general free operations with indefinite causal order offers an advantage in one of the most fundamental communication tasks: the transmission of bits from a sender to a receiver.

Communication advantages of indefinite causal order.

Suppose that Alice wants to transmit classical messages to Bob, using a communication link built from
N
communication devices.
We now show that assembling the devices in an indefinite order can increase the number of bits Alice can reliably send to Bob.

Let us start from the ideal scenario in which the communication is free from errors.
The task of zero-error classical communication can be formulated as the task of converting
N
communication devices into a noiseless classical channel by means of free operations.
Mathematically, the conversion is implemented by a supermap
{S}
that transforms a list of
N
quantum channels
\vec{{\cal C}}
into a noiseless classical channel
\Delta^{m}
, of the form
\Delta^{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\rho\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bra{j}\rho\ket{j}\,|j\rangle\!\langle j|
, where
m
is the number of classical messages the channel can send and
\left\{\ket{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{m}
is a fixed orthonormal basis for the vector space
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
.

Now, the key question is: what is the maximum number of messages Alice can communicate to Bob in an error-free way?
When the
N
communication devices are assembled using operations in the set
{\bf X}
(where
{\bf X}
can be
{\bf FreePar}
, or
{\bf FreeFix}
, or
{\bf FreeDef}
, or
{\bf Free}
), the maximum number of messages is
m_{0}^{\bf X}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)\coloneqq\max\{m\,|\,\exists{S}\in{\bf X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,{S}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)=\Delta^{m}\}
.
Due to the inclusion
{\bf FreeDef}\subset{\bf Free}
, the inequality
m_{0}^{\bf FreeDef}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)\leq m_{0}^{\bf Free}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)
holds for every list of channels
\vec{{\cal C}}
.
We now show that the inequality is generally strict: assembling the devices in an indefinite causal order generally increases the number of messages Alice can communicate to Bob in an error-free way.
In fact, we show that there exists scenarios in which error-free communication is impossible when the channels are combined in definite order, and yet a non-zero number of bits can be perfectly transmitted when the channels are combined in an indefinite order.

Let us introduce the notion of one-shot zero-error capacity of the channels in the list
\vec{{\cal C}}
assisted by signaling-non-generating operations in the set
\bf X
, defined as
C^{\bf X}_{0}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)\coloneqq\log_{2}m_{0}^{\bf X}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)
.
Then, consider the case of
N=2
amplitude damping channels, that is, channels of the form
{\cal A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\rho\mapsto{\cal A}(\rho)=A_{0}\rho A_{0}^{\dagger}+A_{1}\rho A_{1}^{\dagger}
, where
\rho
is an arbitrary density matrix of a single-qubit, and
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
are the 2-by-2 matrices given by
A_{0}\coloneqq\sqrt{\eta}|0\rangle\!\langle 1|
and
A_{1}\coloneqq\sqrt{I-A_{0}^{\dagger}A_{0}}
, where
\eta\in[0,1]
is the damping parameter.
We then evaluate the maximum number of messages that Alice can transmit to Bob, setting
N=2
and
{\cal C}_{1}={\cal C}_{2}={\cal A}
.
In Methods, we show that the evaluation can be reduced to a semidefinite program.

Using the semidefinite programs for
C_{0}^{\bf FreeDef}
and
C_{0}^{\bf Free}
, we can now pin down the advantage of indefinite causal order both analytically and numerically.
On the analytical side, we prove that for damping parameter
\eta=0.1
, the zero-error capacity assisted by definite causal order is

\displaystyle C_{0}^{\bf FreeDef}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)=0\,,

(1)

meaning that no information can be communicated without error whenever the two channels are combined in a definite order.
In start contrast, we find that the zero-error capacity assisted by indefinite causal order is

\displaystyle C_{0}^{\bf Free}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)=1\,,

(2)

meaning that one bit of information can be communicated without any error if the two channels are combined in an indefinite order.
Numerically, we determine the values of the zero-error capacity for values of damping parameter
\eta
between
0
and
0.5
, showing that the advantage of indefinite causal order is present for all values of
\eta
in the interval between
\eta=0
(excluded) and
\eta\approx 0.2
, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
Together, these results provide the first rigorous evidence of an advantage of indefinite causal order in quantum communication.

A similar approach can be used to derive advantages of indefinite causal order in classical communication beyond the zero-error scenario.
The maximum number of messages that can be transmitted with error upper bounded by
\epsilon
, using the channels
\vec{{\cal C}}
assembled by operations in
{\bf X}
, is given by
m_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)\coloneqq\max\{m\,|\,\exists{S}\in{\bf X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,\|{S}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)-\Delta^{m}\|_{\diamond}/2\leq\epsilon\}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\diamond}
denotes the diamond norm
Kitaev 1997
.
We then introduce the one-shot capacity
C_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)\coloneqq\log_{2}m_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)
, and show that, in general, the maximum one-shot capacity achieved by signaling-non-generating operations with definite causal order is strictly smaller than the one-shot capacity achievable by signaling-non-generating operations with indefinite causal order.
For
N=2
amplitude damping channels, an illustration of this fact is provided in Fig.
1
.

Fig 1:
Advantages of indefinite causal order in zero-error and bounded-error communications.
We compare the one-shot classical capacities of a pair of amplitude damping channels
({\cal A},{\cal A})
assisted by
{\bf Free}
and
{\bf FreeDef}
in both the zero-error case and the case with an error tolerance of
0.02
.

No advantage for Pauli channels.

We now show that the advantage of indefinite causal order shown in the previous section is far from generic: no advantage is present for a large class of channels, known as Pauli channels
Wilde 2013
.

A Pauli channel acting on
q
qubits is a channel of the form
{\cal P}(\rho)=\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q}}\,p_{i_{1}\dots i_{q}}\,(\sigma_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{i_{q}})\,\rho\,(\sigma_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{i_{q}})
, where
\sigma_{0}
is the identity matrix,
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
are the three Pauli matrices, and
(p_{i_{1}\dots i_{q}})
is a probability distribution, providing the probability that the
q
qubits undergo the product gate
\sigma_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{i_{q}}
.
Here we consider the situation where the initial communication devices are
N
, possibly different, Pauli channels
\vec{{\cal P}}\coloneqq({\cal P}_{1},\dots,{\cal P}_{N})
, possibly acting on different numbers of qubits.
In this case, we prove that the ability to control the order of the channels does not enhance the classical capacity, even in the presence of an error tolerance.
In fact, we prove an even stronger result: placing the
N
channels in parallel with the assistance of no-signaling resources is already sufficient to achieve the largest possible value of the capacity; in formula,
C_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}(\,\vec{{\cal P}}\,)=C_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}(\,\vec{{\cal P}}\,)
for every
\epsilon\geq 0
, where
\vec{{\cal P}}=({\cal P}_{1},\dots,{\cal P}_{N})
are the
N
initial channels (See Supplementary Note
E
for the proof).
In other words, neither definite nor indefinite causal order can enhance the capacity beyond the maximum value achievable by placing the channels in parallel with the assistance of no-signaling resources.

No advantage for channel simulation.

We now provide another no-go result highlighting a limitation of the power of indefinite causal order.
Explicitly, we show that no advantage arises in the task of simulating a noisy channel through classical communication assisted by no-signaling resources
Bennett et al. 2002
;
Fang et al. 2020
.
This task is the inverse of the classical communication task: while in communication one uses a noisy channel to simulate a noiseless channel for the transmission of bits, in simulation one uses the noiseless transmission of bits to simulate a noisy channel.

In our resource-theoretic framework, the task of channel simulation can be phrased as the problem of converting a set of ideal classical channels into a target channel
{\cal C}
by means of signaling-non-generating operations.
Let us denote the list of ideal classical channels as
\vec{\Delta}^{m}=(\Delta^{m_{1}},\Delta^{m_{2}},\dots)
, where
m_{i}
is the number of messages transmitted by the
i
-th channel, and
m\coloneqq\sum_{j}m_{j}
is the total number of messages transmitted.
With this notation, the classical simulation cost for one realization of channel
{\cal C}
assisted by signaling-non-generating operations in the set
{\bf X}
is

S_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}({\cal C})\coloneqq\min\{\log_{2}m\,|\,\exists{S}\in{\bf X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,\|{S}(\vec{\Delta}^{m})-{\cal C}\|_{\diamond}/2\leq\epsilon\}
, which measures the minimum number of classical bits consumed by the simulation.
This type of one-shot cost is operationally widely used in quantum resource theories
Liu et al. 2019
;
Takagi et al. 2022
, and has played an important role in the study of entanglement
Buscemi and Datta 2011
;
Wang and Wilde 2020
, coherence
Zhao et al. 2018
;
D\́mathbf{i}az et al. 2018
, and communication
Duan and Winter 2016
;
Fang et al. 2020
.

Since enlarging the set of allowed operations can in principle reduce the simulation cost, one has the inequalities
S_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}({\cal C})\geq S_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreeFix}({\cal C})\geq S_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreeDef}({\cal C})\geq S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})
for every error tolerance
\epsilon\geq 0
.
Surprisingly, however, we found out that this chain of inequalities collapses to a single equality:
S_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}({\cal C})=S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})
for every
\epsilon\geq 0
(see Supplementary Note
F
for the derivation).
In other words, indefinite causal order does not offer any advantage over parallel operations in reducing the simulation cost: for the task of simulating a noisy channel with no-signaling resources, all the classical communication can be sent in a single round.

No classical communication advantage in the asymptotic scenario.

We now consider the scenario in which Alice and Bob communicate classical messages through an asymptotically large number of uses of the same quantum channel
{\cal C}
.
In our resource-theoretic framework, the problem can be formalized as follows: Alice and Bob communicate through a link established by combining
N
communication devices implementing the same quantum channel, namely
\vec{{\cal C}}_{N}=({\cal C}_{1},\dots,{\cal C}_{N})
with
{\cal C}_{j}={\cal C}
for every
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
Then, the classical capacity of the channel
{\cal C}
assisted by operations in the set
{\bf X}
is defined as the maximum number of bits communicated reliably per channel use, when the channels are assembled by signaling-non-generating operations in
{\bf X}
.
In formula,
C^{\bf X}\coloneqq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}C_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}}_{N})/N
.

We now show that the order of the channels, either definite or indefinite, does not affect the capacity in the asymptotic limit.
The proof is based on the quantum reverse Shannon theorem
Bennett et al. 2014
;
Berta et al. 2011
, which states the equality
C^{\bf Ent}({\cal C})=S^{\bf Ent}({\cal C})
, where
C^{\bf Ent}({\cal C})
and
S^{\bf Ent}({\cal C})
denote the entanglement-assisted classical capacity and the entanglement-assisted classical simulation cost of the channel
{\cal C}
, respectively.

To prove that indefinite causal order does not offer any classical capacity advantage in the asymptotic scenario, we consider the asymptotic classical simulation cost assisted by supermaps in the set
{\bf X}
, defined as
S^{\bf X}({\cal C})\coloneqq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}S_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}}_{N})
.
Since shared entanglement is a special case of a no-signaling resource, we have the inequalities
C^{\bf Ent}({\cal C})\leq C^{\bf FreeDef}({\cal C})\leq C^{\bf Free}({\cal C})
and
S^{\bf Free}({\cal C})\leq S^{\bf FreeDef}({\cal C})\leq S^{\bf Ent}({\cal C})
.
Moreover, the classical capacity of a channel cannot be larger than its simulation cost, whence we have the inequality
C^{\bf Free}({\cal C})\leq S^{\bf Free}({\cal C})
.
Combining the above inequality with the quantum reverse Shannon theorem, we then obtain the chain of equalities
C^{\bf Ent}({\cal C})=C^{\bf Free}({\cal C})=S^{\bf Free}({\cal C})=S^{\bf Ent}({\cal C})
.
This chain of equalities has two implications.
The first implication is that channel transformations under signaling-non-generating supermaps are reversible even if indefinite causal order is allowed.
The second implication is that neither definite nor indefinite causal order provide any advantage in the asymptotic regime: the assistance of entanglement is already sufficient to achieve the maximum possible classical capacity.

Note that the above argument also holds for the communication of quantum information.
By teleportation
Bennett et al. 1993
and superdense coding
Bennett and Wiesner 1992
, both the entanglement-assisted quantum capacity
Q^{\bf Ent}
and the entanglement-assisted quantum simulation cost
S_{Q}^{\bf Ent}
are half of their classical counter part:
Q^{\bf Ent}=\frac{1}{2}C^{\bf Ent}=\frac{1}{2}S^{\bf Ent}=S_{Q}^{\bf Ent}
.
Applying the same logic, this implies that neither definite nor indefinite causal order can enhance quantum communication in the asymptotic regime.

III
Discussion

In this work, we introduced a rigorous framework for investigating the communication advantages of indefinite causal order.
Our framework consists of resource theory of signaling, where the free resources are the replacement channels, and the set of free operations consists of the signaling-non-generating supermaps.
In this framework, one can examine the performance of different causal structures within the set of free supermaps, and, when a gap arises, one can rigorously attribute it to the different causal structures.

Despite active research in quantum resource theories for various types of dynamical resources
Ben Dana et al. 2017
;
Rosset et al. 2018
;
Theurer et al. 2019
;
Saxena et al. 2020
;
Gour and Scandolo 2020
;
Takagi et al. 2020
;
Kim et al. 2021
;
Saxena and Gour 2022
;
Stratton et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Zhu et al. 2025

and general resource-independent approaches
Liu and Winter 2019
;
Gour and Winter 2019
;
Liu and Yuan 2020
;
Yuan et al. 2020
;
Regula and Takagi 2021a
;
Regula and Takagi 2021b
;
Ji and Chitambar 2023
, supermaps with general causal structures has remain relatively unexplored in the resource-theoretic setting, with a few exception like
Milz et al. 2022
.
In this respect, our framework provide a first study of the role of different causal structures within the set of free operations.

Although the focus of the present work was on communication, the approach developed here can also be applied to other quantum resources.
For example, quantum SWITCH has been shown to enhance cooling a cold reservoir
Felce and Vedral 2020
and preserving nonstabilizerness
Mo et al. 2024
.
However, whether such enhancements can be attributed to indefinite causal order requires further investigation.
An interesting direction of future research is to employ our paradigm to explore the advantages of indefinite causal order in the context of other resource theories, such as thermodynamics and magic.

IV
Methods

Characterization of supermaps.

It is known that quantum supermaps are in one-to-one correspondence with quantum channels.
Specifically, a supermap mapping
N
channels with input/output pairs
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
into a quantum channel with input
A
and output
B
corresponds to a multipartite quantum channel with input
YA
and output
XB
, where
X\coloneqq X_{1}\cdots X_{N}
and
Y\coloneqq Y_{1}\cdots Y_{N}
.
According to the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism
Jamiołkowski 1972
;
Choi 1975
, every channel can be represented by a unique linear operator, known as its Choi operator, which can also serve as a representation of the supermap corresponding to this channel.

The requirement of different classes of causal orders can be expressed as linear constraints imposed on Choi operators.
In particular, the characterization of causally fixed supermaps (i.e., quantum combs) in terms of their Choi operators was introduced in Ref.
Chiribella et al. 2008b
, and the characterization of causally definite supermaps was studied in Ref.
Wechs et al. 2021
.
Such characterizations facilitate the formulation of various quantities studied in our work as optimization programs over linear operators.
See Supplementary Note
B
for details of these characterizations.

The characterization of supermaps with general, possibly indefinite, causal structures in terms of their Choi operators was first studied in Ref.
Oreshkov et al. 2012
in the language of “process matrices.”
A set of linear constraints describing the Choi operators of general supermaps was later given in Ref.
Araújo et al. 2015
.
In Supplementary Note
B
, we show that the aforementioned characterization is equivalent to a simpler one that has a clear physical interpretation.
In particular, we prove that a linear operator
J_{XYAB}
is the Choi operator of some general supermap if and only if

\displaystyle J_{XYAB}\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J_{XYA}\right)=\frac{I_{XYA}}{d_{X}},

(3)

where
J_{XYA}\coloneqq\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[J_{XYAB}]
, and
{\cal L}^{\rm NS}
is the orthogonal projection onto the subspace of Hermitian operators spanned by the Choi operators of no-signaling multipartite quantum channels.

Note that supermaps in
{\bf FreePar}
,
{\bf FreeFix}
,
{\bf FreeDef}
, and
{\bf Free}
should not only satisfy the causal order constraints for their respective classes, but also be signaling-non-generating.
This latter requirement enables us to fairly compare how well supermaps in different classes make use of the given communication channel.
In Supplementary Note
A
, we prove that a supermap is signaling-non-generating if and only if it satisfies the No Forward Signaling Condition, namely, its corresponding channel is no-signaling from
A
to
B
.
This No Forward Signaling Condition can be conveniently expressed as a constraint on the supermap’s (also the corresponding channel’s) Choi operator
J_{XYAB}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[J_{XYAB}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}_{XA}\left[J_{XYAB}\right]\otimes\frac{I_{A}}{d_{A}},

(4)

where
d_{A}
denotes the dimension of system
A
.
This constraint says that the output at system
B
(tracing out the other output system
X
) does not depend on the input at system
A
.

One-shot classical capacities as semidefinite programs.

Given a list of quantum channels
\vec{{\cal C}}
, classical communication of
m
messages is to find an admissible code, represented by a supermap
{S}
, such that
{S}
transforms the channels
\vec{{\cal C}}
into a channel as close as possible to
\Delta^{m}
, the
m
-dimensional noiseless classical channel.
Specifically, the desired supermap
{S}
should achieve the minimum error
\omega^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},\Delta^{m})
defined as
\omega^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},{\cal M})\coloneqq\min\{\|{S}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)-{\cal M}\|_{\diamond}/2\,|\,{S}\in{\bf X}\}
, where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\diamond}
denotes the diamond norm
Kitaev 1997
, measuring the closeness of two quantum channels,
{\bf X}
is the set of allowed supermaps, and
{\cal M}
refers to a target quantum channel.
Based on this minimum error, we define the one-shot classical capacity of a given list of quantum channels
\vec{{\cal C}}
assisted by supermaps in
{\bf X}
as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)\coloneqq\max\{\log_{2}m\,|\,\omega^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},\Delta^{m})\leq\epsilon\},\end{aligned}

(5)

where
m
is maximized over all positive integers, and
\epsilon\geq 0
is a given error tolerance.
This one-shot classical capacity quantifies the maximum number of classical bits that can be reliably transmitted through a single use of
\vec{{\cal C}}
when assisted by supermaps in
{\bf X}
.

It is not straightforward to see if
C_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}
can be formulated as a semidefinite program.
One major obstacle is the involvement of the diamond norm.
Instead of diamond distance, average probability of error has been commonly used in classical communication.
Given a quantum channel
{\cal M}
, the average probability of error of transmitting
m
equiprobable classical messages through
{\cal M}
is
p({\cal M},m)\coloneqq 1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}\left[{\cal M}\left(|j\rangle\!\langle j|\right)|j\rangle\!\langle j|\right]
.
Then, we define the minimum average probability of error of a list of noisy channel
\vec{{\cal C}}
assisted by supermaps in
{\bf X}
as
p^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},m)\coloneqq\min\{p({S}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,),m)\,|\,{S}\in{\bf X}\}
.
Similar to Eq. (
5
), one can define the one-shot classical capacity using
p^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},m)
, which can be formulated as a semidefinite program for
{\bf X}={\bf FreePar}

Leung and Matthews 2015
;
Wang et al. 2018
.

In general, given a noisy channel
{\cal M}
, its diamond distance
\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\cal M}-\Delta^{m}\right\|_{\diamond}
differs from its average probability of error
1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\operatorname{Tr}\left[{\cal M}\left(|j\rangle\!\langle j|\right)|j\rangle\!\langle j|\right]
.
However, in Supplementary Note
C
, we show that these two quantities are actually equivalent after minimized over a set of supermaps if the set is convex and closed under simultaneous permutations on
A
and
B
.
These generic conditions are satisfied by the sets of free supermaps considered in this work, including the no-signaling-assisted codes in the standard communication scenario.
This equivalence, together with the characterizations of supermaps in terms of their Choi operators and the symmetry of the noiseless classical channel
\Delta^{m}
, allows us to formulate
C_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}
as semidefinite programs for
{\bf X}
considered in this work.
This also enables us to use a unified measure of error, the diamond distance, to address both communication and noisy channel simulation.

In particular, the one-shot classical capacity assisted by supermaps in
{\bf Free}
can be formulated as the following semidefinite program:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)=\log_{2}\max&\;\left\lfloor m\right\rfloor\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\geq m(1-\epsilon),\\
&\;E_{Y}=I_{Y},\,0\leq E_{XY}\leq F_{XY},\\
&\;{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(F_{XY}\right)=\frac{m}{d_{X}}I_{XY},\end{aligned}

(6)

where
m
is maximized over real numbers, and Hermitian matrices
E_{XY}
and
F_{XY}
are optimization variables.
The
\star
operation, known as link product, acting on two linear operators
P_{AB}
and
Q_{BC}
is defined as
P_{AB}\star Q_{BC}\coloneqq\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\left[\left(P_{AB}\otimes I_{C}\right)\left(I_{A}\otimes Q_{BC}^{{\rm T}_{B}}\right)\right]
, where
{\rm T}_{B}
is the partial transpose over system
B
.
It gives the Choi operator of the quantum process (channel or supermap) resulted from composing two quantum processes with Choi operators
P_{AB}
and
Q_{BC}

Chiribella et al. 2008b
;
Chiribella et al. 2009b
.
Similarly, the one-shot classical capacity assisted by supermaps in
{\bf FreeDef}
can also be formulated as a semidefinite program.
The derivation of both semidefinite programs is presented in Supplementary Note
C
.
These semidefinite program formulations allow us to rigorously prove the advantage of indefinite causal order (see Supplementary Note
D
for the proof) as well as to numerically compute the capacities for different
\vec{{\cal C}}
, as we did in Fig.
1

Proposition 1

A general supermap transforming
N
channels with input/output pairs
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
into a channel with input
A
and output
B
is signaling-non-generating if and only if it satisfies the No Forward Signaling Condition: its corresponding quantum channel, with input
YA
and output
XB
, is no-signaling from
A
to
B
.

Proof.

Given a general supermap
{S}
, suppose it satisfies the No Forward Signaling Condition, i.e., its corresponding quantum channel
{\cal S}
being no-signaling from
A
to
B
.
Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists a list of replacement channels
\vec{{\cal C}}
such that
{\cal M}\coloneqq{S}(\vec{{\cal C}})
is not a replacement channel.
That is, there exists a pair of quantum states
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
such that
{\cal M}(\rho)\neq{\cal M}(\rho^{\prime})
.
Because
\vec{{\cal C}}
is a list of replacement channel, when inserting
\vec{{\cal C}}
into the supermap
{S}
, the state sent into the system
Y
is always fixed, and we denote this fixed state by
\sigma_{Y}
.
Hence,
{\cal M}(\omega_{A})=\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[{\cal S}\left(\omega_{A}\otimes\sigma_{Y}\right)\right]
for every state
\omega_{A}
.
Then,
{\cal M}(\rho)\neq{\cal M}(\rho^{\prime})
implies that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[{\cal S}\left(\rho_{A}\otimes\sigma_{Y}\right)\right]\neq\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[{\cal S}\left(\rho^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{Y}\right)\right],

(7)

which contradicts with our assumption that
{\cal S}
is no-signaling from
A
to
B
.
Therefore,
{S}
must map any list of
N
replacement channels to a replacement channel.
That is, every supermap satisfying the No Forward Signaling Condition must be signaling-non-generating.

For the other direction, let us assume that
{S}
does not satisfy the No Forward Signaling Condition.
As a result, there exist states
\rho_{A}
,
\rho^{\prime}_{A}
, and
\sigma_{Y}
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[{\cal S}(\rho_{A}\otimes\sigma_{Y})\right]\neq\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[{\cal S}(\rho^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{Y})\right].

(8)

Define
{\cal M}\coloneqq{S}(\vec{{\cal C}})
, where
\vec{{\cal C}}
is a list of
N
replacement channels that replaces every state on system
X
with the state
\sigma_{Y}
.
Then, Eq. (
8
) implies that
{\cal M}(\rho)\neq{\cal M}(\rho^{\prime})
, and thus
{S}
is not signaling-non-generating.
Therefore, every signaling-non-generating supermap must satisfy the No Forward Signaling Condition.
Together with what we proved in the previous paragraph, we conclude that a supermap is signaling-non-generating if and only if it satisfies the No Forward Signaling Condition.

Supplementary Note B
Characterizations of supermaps with different causal structures

In Methods, we mentioned that each supermap mapping
N
channels with input/output pairs
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
into a quantum channel with input
A
and output
B
can be represented by a unique linear operator
J_{XYAB}
, known as its Choi operator.
In this note, we specify the characterizations of supermaps with different causal structures in terms of linear constraints on their Choi operators.
These linear constraints, together with the the constraint

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[J_{XYAB}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}_{XA}\left[J_{XYAB}\right]\otimes\frac{I_{A}}{d_{A}},

(9)

which imposes the No Forward Signaling Condition, offer convenient characterizations of the sets of free supermaps considered in this work (
{\bf FreePar}
,
{\bf FreeFix}
,
{\bf FreeDef}
, and
{\bf Free}
).

B.1
Causally Fixed Supermaps

Causally fixed supermaps, also known as quantum combs, are transformations between quantum channels that can be implemented by inserting input quantum channels into quantum circuits with open slots in a fixed order.
These supermaps consists of both parallel and sequential strategies, where sequential strategies are also known as adaptive strategies sometimes.
Such supermaps are introduced in Refs.
Chiribella et al. 2008a
;
Chiribella et al. 2008b
;
Chiribella et al. 2009b
with the following characterization.

Proposition 2

(Characterization of causally fixed supermaps
Chiribella et al. 2008b
)

A linear operator
J_{XYAB}
is the Choi operator of some causally fixed supermap mapping
N
channels with input/output pairs
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
into a channel with input
A
and output
B
if and only if

\displaystyle J_{XYAB}\geq 0,\quad{}_{\left[1-Y_{n}\right]}J_{X_{1}Y_{1}\dots X_{n}Y_{n}A}=0\quad\forall\;n=1,\dots,N,\quad J_{A}=d_{Y}I_{A}.

(10)

Parallel strategies correspond to the simplest type of quantum combs, the single-slot ones:

\displaystyle J_{XYAB}\geq 0,\quad{}_{\left[1-Y\right]}J_{XYA}=0,\quad J_{A}=d_{Y}I_{A}.

(11)

B.2
Causally Definite Supermaps

Causally fixed supermaps are not the most general type of supermaps allowed by definite causal order. By dynamically control the order of input channels in a classical manner, one can realize supermaps that are causally non-fixed but still causally definite

;
Wechs et al. 2021

Proposition 3

(Characterization of causally definite supermaps

A linear operator
J_{XYAB}
is the Choi operator of some causally definite supermap mapping
N
channels with input/output pairs
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
into a channel with input
A
and output
B
if and only if

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}J_{XYAB}=\sum_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})}J^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})}_{XYAB},\quad\sum_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})}J^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})}_{A}=d_{Y}I_{A},\\
J^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})}_{XYAB}\geq 0\quad\forall\;(k_{1},\dots,k_{N}),\\
{}_{\left[1-Y_{k_{n}}\right]}\left(\sum_{(k_{n+1},\dots,k_{N})}J^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},k_{n+1},\dots,k_{N})}_{X_{k_{1}}Y_{k_{1}}\dots X_{k_{n}}Y_{k_{n}}A}\right)=0\quad\forall\;n=1,\dots,N-1\quad\text{and}\quad\forall\;(k_{1},\dots,k_{n}),\\

B.3

The most general causal structure allows indefinite causal order

Oreshkov et al. 2012

The formalism of process matrices was first proposed in Ref.

In this work, a general supermap mapping

The first

(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}

The

According to Ref.

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}J_{XYAB}\geq 0,\quad\operatorname{Tr}\left[J_{XYAB}\right]=d_{YA},\quad{\cal L}\left(J_{XYAB}\right)=J_{XYAB},\end{gathered}

{}_{\left[\sum_{j}s_{j}X_{j}\right]}J\coloneqq\sum_{j}s_{j}\cdot{}_{X_{j}}J

Proposition 4

(Characterization of general supermaps)

A linear operator
J_{XYAB}
is the Choi operator of some general supermap mapping
N
channels with input/output pairs
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
into a channel with input
A
and output
B
if and only if

\displaystyle J_{XYAB}\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J_{XYA}\right)=\frac{I_{XYA}}{d_{X}}.

(17)

Consider that

\displaystyle{\cal L}\left(J_{XYAB}\right)

\displaystyle=J_{XYAB}-{}_{\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1-Y_{j}+X_{j}Y_{j}\right)\right]}J_{XYA}\otimes\frac{I_{B}}{d_{B}}+\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[J_{XYAB}\right]I_{XYAB}}{d_{XYAB}}

\displaystyle=J_{XYAB}-\left({}_{\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1-Y_{j}+X_{j}Y_{j}\right)\right]}J_{XYA}-\frac{I_{XYA}}{d_{X}}\right)\otimes\frac{I_{B}}{d_{B}},

(19)

where the second equality follows from
\operatorname{Tr}\left[J_{XYAB}\right]=d_{YA}
as given in Eq. (

Then, to require

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J_{XYA}\right)\coloneqq{}_{\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1-Y_{j}+X_{j}Y_{j}\right)\right]}J_{XYA}=\frac{I_{XYA}}{d_{X}}.

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[J_{XYAB}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J_{XYA}\right)\right]=d_{YA},

This characterization also applies to general processes involving
N

A
and

Note that we use the superscript

in

to emphasize the fact that

is actually the orthogonal projection onto the subspace of Hermitian operators spanned by the Choi operators of no-signaling multipartite quantum channels, as we show below.

Proposition 5

(Projection onto no-signaling subspace)

{\cal L}^{\rm NS}
is the orthogonal projection of Hermitian operators onto the subspace spanned by the Choi operators of no-signaling channels.

Proof.

We call an
N
-partite channel no-signaling if the input state at any one local input system does not affect the output state at the rest
N-1
local output systems.
In terms of its Choi operator
J_{XY}
, it is equivalent to require

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{Y_{j}}\left[J_{XY}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}_{X_{j}Y_{j}}\left[J_{XY}\right]\otimes\frac{I_{X_{j}}}{d_{X_{j}}}\quad\forall\;j=1,\dots,N.

(22)

These no-signaling constraints can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle{}_{\left[Y_{j}-X_{j}Y_{j}\right]}J_{XY}=0\quad\forall\;j=1,\dots,N.

(23)

Note that the no-signaling constraints implies
{}_{Y}J_{XY}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[J_{XY}\right]I_{XY}/d_{XY}
, which says that
J_{XY}
is proportional to the Choi operator of some trace-preserving map.
Due to the linearity of this set of constraints, the subspace spanned by Choi operators of no-signaling channels consists of all Hermitian operators satisfying the same set of constraints.
For each
j
, the constraint
{}_{\left[Y_{j}-X_{j}Y_{j}\right]}J_{XY}=0
is satisfied if and only if

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{{\rm NS}}_{X_{j}Y_{j}}\left(J_{XY}\right)\coloneqq{}_{\left[1-Y_{j}+X_{j}Y_{j}\right]}J_{XY}=J_{XY}.

(24)

Indeed, by checking that

and it is self-adjoint, one can verify that
{\cal L}^{{\rm NS}}_{X_{j}Y_{j}}
is the orthogonal projection onto the subspace of Hermitian operators satisfying
{}_{\left[Y_{j}-X_{j}Y_{j}\right]}J_{X_{j}Y_{j}}=0
. The intersection of subspaces associated with all
{\cal L}^{{\rm NS}}_{X_{j}Y_{j}}
is exactly the no-signaling subspace. Since all the projections
{\cal L}^{{\rm NS}}_{X_{j}Y_{j}}
commute as they all act on different systems, the orthogonal projection onto the no-signaling subspace is given by

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{X_{1}Y_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{X_{N}Y_{N}}={\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY},

(25)

which completes the proof.

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,j^{\prime},k^{\prime}=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{AB}\left[J^{S}_{XYAB}\left(I_{XY}\otimes|k\rangle\!\langle j|_{A}\otimes|k^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle j^{\prime}|_{B}\right)\right]\otimes|\tau(j)\rangle\!\langle\tau(k)|_{A^{\prime}}\otimes|\tau(j^{\prime})\rangle\!\langle\tau(k^{\prime})|_{B^{\prime}}

(35)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,j^{\prime},k^{\prime}=1}^{m}\left(I_{XY}\otimes\bra{j}_{A}\otimes\bra{j^{\prime}}_{B}\right)J^{S}_{XYAB}\left(I_{XY}\otimes\ket{k}_{A}\otimes\ket{k^{\prime}}_{B}\right)\otimes|\tau(j)\rangle\!\langle\tau(k)|_{A^{\prime}}\otimes|\tau(j^{\prime})\rangle\!\langle\tau(k^{\prime})|_{B^{\prime}}

(36)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,j^{\prime},k^{\prime}=1}^{m}\left(I_{A}\otimes\ket{\tau(j)}_{A^{\prime}}\bra{j}_{A}\otimes\ket{\tau(j^{\prime})}_{B^{\prime}}\bra{j^{\prime}}_{B}\right)J^{S}_{XYAB}\left(I_{A}\otimes\ket{k}_{A}\bra{\tau(k)}_{A^{\prime}}\otimes\ket{k^{\prime}}_{B}\bra{\tau(k^{\prime})}_{B^{\prime}}\right)

(37)

\displaystyle=\left(I_{XY}\otimes\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\ket{\tau(j)}_{A^{\prime}}\bra{j}_{A}\otimes\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{m-1}\ket{\tau(j^{\prime})}_{B^{\prime}}\bra{j^{\prime}}_{B}\right)J^{S}_{XYAB}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left(I_{XY}\otimes\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\ket{k}_{A}\bra{\tau(k)}_{A^{\prime}}\otimes\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{m-1}\ket{k^{\prime}}_{B}\bra{\tau(k^{\prime})}_{B^{\prime}}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle=\left(I_{XY}\otimes\tau_{A}\otimes\tau_{B}\right)J^{S}_{XYAB}\left(I_{XY}\otimes\tau_{A}\otimes\tau_{B}\right)^{\dagger}.

(39)

More importantly,
J^{{S}^{\tau}}_{XYAB}
achieves the same average probability of error as
J^{S}_{XYAB}
since

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(J^{{S}^{\tau}}_{XYAB}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right)J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\left(I_{XY}\otimes\tau_{A}\otimes\tau_{B}\right)J^{S}_{XYAB}\left(I_{XY}\otimes\tau_{A}\otimes\tau_{B}\right)^{\dagger}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right)J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right]

(40)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(J^{S}_{XYAB}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right)\left(\tau_{A}\otimes\tau_{B}\right)^{\dagger}J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\left(\tau_{A}\otimes\tau_{B}\right)\right]

(41)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(J^{S}_{XYAB}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right)J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right],

(42)

where the last equality holds due to
\left(\tau_{A}\otimes\tau_{B}\right)^{\dagger}J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\left(\tau_{A}\otimes\tau_{B}\right)=J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}
.
Thus, the optimality of
J^{S}_{XYAB}
implies the optimality of
J^{{S}^{\tau}}_{XYAB}
.
Moreover, any convex combination of optimal Choi operators is still optimal due to the convexity of
{\bf X}
and the linearity of the average error probability, implying that

\displaystyle J^{{S}^{\prime}}_{XYAB}\coloneqq\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\tau\in S_{m}}J^{{S}^{\tau}}_{XYAB}

(43)

is also optimal.
Then, by Schur’s Lemma, we can restrict the optimization range of the operator
J^{S}_{XYAB}
to operators of the form
E_{XY}\otimes J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+F_{XY}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)
, where
E_{XY}
and
F_{XY}
are arbitrary Hermitian operators.
For that

\displaystyle\left(E_{XY}\otimes J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+F_{XY}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)\right)\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}=\left(E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right)J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+\left(F_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right)\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right),

(44)

the optimization program can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} p^{\bf X}\left(\vec{{\cal C}},m\right)=\min&\;1-E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;E_{XY}\otimes J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+F_{XY}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)\in\widetilde{\bf X}.\end{aligned}

(45)

For the minimum error
\omega^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},\Delta^{m})
, we can use the semidefinite program (SDP)
Watrous 2009
for the diamond distance between quantum channels to write it as the following optimization program:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \omega^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},\Delta^{m})=\min&\;\epsilon\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;Y_{AB}\geq J^{S}_{XYAB}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB},\,Y_{AB}\geq 0,\,Y_{A}\leq\epsilon I_{A},\,J^{S}_{XYAB}\in\widetilde{\bf X}.\end{aligned}

(46)

Similarly, we can restrict the optimization range of the operator
J^{S}_{XYAB}
to operators of the form
E_{XY}\otimes J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+F_{XY}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)
so that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \omega^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},\Delta^{m})=\min&\;\epsilon\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;Y_{AB}\geq\left(E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}-1\right)J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+\left(F_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right)\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right),\,Y_{AB}\geq 0,\,Y_{A}\leq\epsilon I_{A},\\
&\;E_{XY}\otimes J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+F_{XY}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)\in\widetilde{\bf X}\end{aligned}

(47)

\displaystyle\phantom{\omega^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},\Delta^{m})}\begin{aligned} \mathllap{}=\min&\;\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\cal M}-\Delta^{m}\right\|_{\diamond}\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;E_{XY}\otimes J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+F_{XY}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)\in\widetilde{\bf X},\end{aligned}

(48)

where
{\cal M}
denotes the quantum channel whose Choi operator is

\displaystyle J^{\cal M}_{AB}\coloneqq\left(E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right)J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+\left(F_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right)\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right).

(49)

One constraint that holds for the Choi operator
J^{{S}}_{XYAB}
of every general supermap
{S}
is
{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J^{S}_{XYA}\right)=\frac{I_{XYA}}{d_{X}}
(see Proposition
4
).
In terms of operators
E_{XY}
and
F_{XY}
, this constraint can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(E_{XY}+(m-1)F_{XY}\right)=\frac{I_{XY}}{d_{X}},

(50)

which implies that

\displaystyle F_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}

\displaystyle=F_{XY}\star{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right)

(51)

\displaystyle={\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(F_{XY}\right)\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m-1}\left(\frac{I_{XY}}{d_{X}}-{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(E_{XY}\right)\right)\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}

(53)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m-1}\left(1-E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right),

(54)

where the first equality holds because
\vec{{\cal C}}
is no-signaling and the second equality is due to that
{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}
is self-dual.
It then follows that

\displaystyle J^{\cal M}_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\left(E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}-1\right)J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+\frac{1}{m-1}\left(1-E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\right)\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}}{m-1}\left(I_{AB}-mJ^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right).

(56)

According to the definition of the diamond norm, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal M}-\Delta^{m}\right\|_{\diamond}=\max_{\rho_{RA}}\left\|{\cal M}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)-\Delta^{m}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)\right\|_{1},

(57)

where
R
is an m-dimensional quantum system.
Denote
1-E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}
by
\alpha
.
For any quantum state
\rho_{RA}
, consider that

\displaystyle{\cal M}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)-\Delta^{m}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\left(I_{AB}\star\rho_{RA}-mJ^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\star\rho_{RA}\right)

(58)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\left(\rho_{R}\otimes I_{B}-m\operatorname{Tr}_{A}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}I_{R}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{A}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{B}\right)\left(\rho_{RA}\otimes I_{B}\right)\right]\right)

(59)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\rho_{R}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{B}-m\sum_{j=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}\left[\left(I_{R}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{A}\right)\rho_{RA}\right]\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{B}\right)

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\rho_{R}-m\operatorname{Tr}_{A}\left[\left(I_{R}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{A}\right)\rho_{RA}\right]\right)\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{B}

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\rho_{R}-m\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}\right)\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{B},

(62)

where we define
\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}\coloneqq\operatorname{Tr}_{A}\left[\left(I_{R}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{A}\right)\rho_{RA}\right]
, which is positive semidefinite for every
j
.
Since
\rho_{R}=\operatorname{Tr}_{A}\left[\left(I_{R}\otimes I_{A}\right)\rho_{RA}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}\left[\left(I_{R}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{A}\right)\rho_{RA}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}
, we have

\displaystyle{\cal M}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)-\Delta^{m}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\rho_{R}-\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}-(m-1)\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}\right)\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{B},

(63)

where
\rho_{R}-\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}=\sum_{k=1,\,k\neq j}^{m}\widetilde{\rho}^{k}_{R}
is positive semidefinite.
Then,

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal M}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)-\Delta^{m}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\rho_{R}-\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}-(m-1)\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}\right)\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{B}\right\|_{1}

(64)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\|\rho_{R}-\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}-(m-1)\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}\right\|_{1}

(65)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\left\|\rho_{R}-\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}\right\|_{1}+(m-1)\left\|\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}\right\|_{1}\right)

(66)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(1+(m-2)\operatorname{Tr}\left[\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}\right]\right)

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\left(m+(m-2)\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{R}\right]\right)

(68)

\displaystyle=2\alpha.

(69)

Since this holds for any state
\rho_{RA}
, we obtain an upper bound on the diamond distance:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\cal M}-\Delta^{m}\right\|_{\diamond}=\frac{1}{2}\max_{\rho_{RA}}\left\|{\cal M}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)-\Delta^{m}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)\right\|_{1}\leq\alpha.

(70)

On the other hand,
\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\cal M}-\Delta^{m}\right\|_{\diamond}
is lower bounded by
\alpha
because, for
\rho_{RA}=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{R}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{A}
,
\rho_{R}=\widetilde{\rho}^{0}_{R}=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{R}
and
\widetilde{\rho}^{j}_{R}=0
for all
j\neq 0
.
As a result, Eq. (
65
) implies that

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal M}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)-\Delta^{m}\left(\rho_{RA}\right)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\left(\left\|-(m-1)\widetilde{\rho}^{0}_{R}\right\|_{1}+(m-1)\left\|\rho_{R}\right\|_{1}\right)

(71)

\displaystyle=2\alpha.

(72)

Since
\alpha
is both an upper bound and a lower bound on
\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\cal M}-\Delta^{m}\right\|_{\diamond}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\cal M}-\Delta^{m}\right\|_{\diamond}=\alpha=1-E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}.

(73)

Therefore, we can write the optimization program of the minimum error as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \omega^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},\Delta^{m})=\min&\;1-E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;E_{XY}\otimes J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+F_{XY}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)\in\widetilde{\bf X}\end{aligned}

(74)

\displaystyle\phantom{\omega^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},\Delta^{m})}=p^{\bf X}\left(\vec{{\cal C}},m\right),

(75)

as we set out to prove.

C.2
SDP for one-shot classical capacities assisted by free general supermaps

Proposition 7

Given a list of
N
quantum channels
\vec{{\cal C}}
, its one-shot classical capacity assisted by free general supermaps with error tolerance
\epsilon\geq 0
can be formulated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)=\log_{2}\max&\;\left\lfloor m\right\rfloor\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\geq m(1-\epsilon),\,E_{Y}=I_{Y},\\
&\;0\leq E_{XY}\leq F_{XY},\,{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(F_{XY}\right)=\frac{m}{d_{X}}I_{XY},\end{aligned}

(76)

where
m
is maximized over real numbers, and
E_{XY}
and
F_{XY}
are optimization variables.

Proof.

Recall that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)\coloneqq\log_{2}\max&\;m\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;\omega^{\bf X}(\vec{{\cal C}},\Delta^{m})\leq\epsilon,\end{aligned}

(77)

where
m
is maximized over all positive integers.
According to Eq. (
74
), we can write
C_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)
as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{\epsilon}^{\bf X}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)=\log_{2}\max&\;m\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;1-E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\leq\epsilon,\\
&\;E_{XY}\otimes J_{AB}^{\Delta^{m}}+F_{XY}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J_{AB}^{\Delta^{m}}\right)\in\widetilde{\bf X}.\end{aligned}

(78)

Choi operators of signaling-non-generating supermaps should satisfy constraint (
9
), which can be compactly written as

\displaystyle{}_{[1-A]}J_{YAB}=0

(79)

for the Choi operator
J_{XYAB}
of a supermap.
Applying this constraint to
E_{XY}\otimes J_{AB}^{\Delta^{m}}+F_{XY}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J_{AB}^{\Delta^{m}}\right)
results in

\displaystyle{}_{[1-A]}\left(E_{Y}\otimes J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+F_{Y}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=E_{Y}\otimes{}_{[1-A]}J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+F_{Y}\otimes{}_{[1-A]}\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)

(80)

\displaystyle=\left(E_{Y}-F_{Y}\right)\otimes\left(J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}-\frac{1}{m}I_{AB}\right)

(81)

\displaystyle=0,

(82)

which is true if and only if
E_{Y}=F_{Y}
.

The case where
{\bf X}={\bf Free}
corresponds to the constraints given in Proposition
4
, in addition to the forward no-signaling constraint (
79
).
The first constraint in Proposition
4
is requiring both
E_{XY}\geq 0
and
F_{XY}\geq 0
.
The second constraint gives us

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(E_{XY}+(m-1)F_{XY}\right)=\frac{I_{XY}}{d_{X}},

(83)

which, as we show for Eq. (
157
), implies

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(E_{XY}+(m-1)F_{XY}\right)\right]=E_{Y}+(m-1)F_{Y}=I_{Y}.

(84)

Under the above equality, the forward no-signaling constraint
E_{Y}=F_{Y}
is equivalent to
mE_{Y}=I_{Y}
.
Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)=\log_{2}\max&\;m\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;1-E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\leq\epsilon,\,mE_{Y}=I_{Y},\\
&\;E_{XY}\geq 0,\,F_{XY}\geq 0,\,{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(E_{XY}+(m-1)F_{XY}\right)=\frac{I_{XY}}{d_{X}}.\end{aligned}

(85)

Relabeling
mE_{XY}
as
E_{XY}
and
mE_{XY}+m(m-1)F_{XY}
as
F_{XY}
leads to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)=\log_{2}\max&\;m\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;E_{XY}\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\geq m(1-\epsilon),\,E_{Y}=I_{Y},\\
&\;0\leq E_{XY}\leq F_{XY},\,{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(F_{XY}\right)=\frac{m}{d_{X}}I_{XY}.\end{aligned}

(86)

While
m
is maximized over integers, it can actually be maximized over real numbers and then take the largest integer smaller than it.
To see this, suppose
E^{*}_{XY}
,
F^{*}_{XY}
,
m^{*}
are optimal when
m
is maximized over real numbers, and
m^{*}
is not an integer.
Then, denoting
p\coloneqq\frac{\left\lfloor m^{*}\right\rfloor-1}{m^{*}-1}
, one can verify that
pE^{*}_{XY}+\frac{1-p}{d_{X}}I_{XY}
,
pF^{*}_{XY}+\frac{1-p}{d_{X}}I_{XY}
, and
\left\lfloor m^{*}\right\rfloor
are feasible.
This allows us to formulate
C_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)
as an SDP.

C.3
SDP for one-shot classical capacities assisted by free causally definite supermaps

The one-shot classical capacity assisted by free causally definite supermaps can be formulated as an SDP following a similar procedure.
Here, we consider the bipartite case for demonstration.

Proposition 8

Given a list of two quantum channels
\vec{C}
, its one-shot classical capacity assisted by free causally definite supermaps with error tolerance
\epsilon\geq 0
can be formulated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreeDef}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)=\log_{2}\max&\;\left\lfloor m\right\rfloor\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;\left(E_{XY}+G_{XY}\right)\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\geq m(1-\epsilon),\,E_{Y}+G_{Y}=I_{Y},\\
&\;0\leq E_{XY}\leq F_{XY},\,0\leq G_{XY}\leq H_{XY},\,\operatorname{Tr}\left[F_{XY}+H_{XY}\right]=md_{Y},\\
&\;{}_{\left[1-Y_{2}\right]}F_{XY}=0,\,{}_{\left[1-Y_{1}\right]}F_{X_{1}Y_{1}}=0,\,{}_{\left[1-Y_{1}\right]}H_{XY}=0,\,{}_{\left[1-Y_{2}\right]}H_{X_{2}Y_{2}}=0,\end{aligned}

(87)

where
m
is maximized over real numbers, and
E_{XY}
,
F_{XY}
,
G_{XY}
, and
H_{XY}
are optimization variables.

Proof.

In the bipartite case, the constraints given in Proposition
3
on the Choi operator
J_{XYAB}
of a causally definite supermap reduce to

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}J_{XYAB}=J^{(1,2)}_{XYAB}+J^{(2,1)}_{XYAB},\quad J^{(1,2)}_{A}+J^{(2,1)}_{A}=d_{Y}I_{A},\\
J^{(1,2)}_{XYAB}\geq 0,\quad J^{(2,1)}_{XYAB}\geq 0,\\
{}_{\left[1-Y_{1}\right]}J^{(1,2)}_{X_{1}Y_{1}A}=0,\quad{}_{\left[1-Y_{2}\right]}J^{(2,1)}_{X_{2}Y_{2}A}=0,\\
{}_{\left[1-Y_{2}\right]}J^{(1,2)}_{XYA}=0,\quad{}_{\left[1-Y_{1}\right]}J^{(2,1)}_{XYA}=0.\end{gathered}

Since simultaneous permutation at
A
and
B
on
J_{XYAB}
does not change the optimal value when solving the optimization program of the minimum error, we can restrict
J^{(1,2)}_{XYAB}
and
J^{(2,1)}_{XYAB}
to operators of the form

\displaystyle J^{(1,2)}_{XYAB}=E_{XY}\otimes J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+F_{XY}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad J^{(2,1)}_{XYAB}=G_{XY}\otimes J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+H_{XY}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right),

(92)

where
E_{XY}
,
F_{XY}
,
G_{XY}
, and
H_{XY}
are arbitrary Hermitian operators.
Then, we can rewrite constraints (
C.3
) in terms of
E_{XY}
,
F_{XY}
,
G_{XY}
, and
H_{XY}
.

The constraint
J^{(1,2)}_{A}+J^{(2,1)}_{A}=d_{Y}I_{A}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[E_{XY}+(m-1)F_{XY}+G_{XY}+(m-1)H_{XY}\right]=d_{Y}.

(93)

The constraints
J^{(1,2)}_{XYAB}\geq 0
and
J^{(2,1)}_{XYAB}\geq 0
correspond to

\displaystyle E_{XY}\geq 0,\quad F_{XY}\geq 0,\quad G_{XY}\geq 0,\quad\text{and}\quad H_{XY}\geq 0.

(94)

Since
J^{(1,2)}_{XYA}=\left(E_{XY}+(m-1)F_{XY}\right)\otimes I_{A}
, the constraints
{}_{\left[1-Y_{2}\right]}J^{(1,2)}_{XYA}=0
and
{}_{\left[1-Y_{1}\right]}J^{(1,2)}_{X_{1}Y_{1}A}=0
correspond to

\displaystyle{}_{\left[1-Y_{2}\right]}\left(E_{XY}+(m-1)F_{XY}\right)=0\quad\text{and}\quad{}_{\left[1-Y_{1}\right]}\left(E_{X_{1}Y_{1}}+(m-1)F_{X_{1}Y_{1}}\right)=0.

(95)

Similarly,
{}_{\left[1-Y_{1}\right]}J^{(2,1)}_{XYA}=0
and
{}_{\left[1-Y_{2}\right]}J^{(2,1)}_{X_{2}Y_{2}A}=0
correspond to

\displaystyle{}_{\left[1-Y_{1}\right]}\left(G_{XY}+(m-1)H_{XY}\right)=0\quad\text{and}\quad{}_{\left[1-Y_{2}\right]}\left(G_{X_{2}Y_{2}}+(m-1)H_{X_{2}Y_{2}}\right)=0.

(96)

Finally, comparing
J_{XYAB}=\left(E_{XY}+G_{XY}\right)\otimes J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}+{F_{XY}+H_{XY}}\otimes\left(I_{AB}-J^{\Delta^{m}}_{AB}\right)
with Eq. (
78
), we note that
E_{XY}
and
F_{XY}
in Eq. (
78
) correspond to
E_{XY}+G_{XY}
and
F_{XY}+H_{XY}
, respectively, in this case.
Hence, we arrive at

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreeDef}(\,\vec{{\cal C}}\,)=\log_{2}\max&\;m\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;\left(E_{XY}+G_{XY}\right)\star J^{\vec{{\cal C}}}_{XY}\geq 1-\epsilon,\,E_{Y}+G_{Y}=F_{Y}+H_{Y},\\
&\;\operatorname{Tr}\left[E_{XY}+(m-1)F_{XY}+G_{XY}+(m-1)H_{XY}\right]=d_{Y},\\
&\;E_{XY}\geq 0,\,F_{XY}\geq 0,\,G_{XY}\geq 0,\,H_{XY}\geq 0,\\
&\;{}_{\left[1-Y_{2}\right]}\left(E_{XY}+(m-1)F_{XY}\right)=0,\,{}_{\left[1-Y_{1}\right]}\left(E_{X_{1}Y_{1}}+(m-1)F_{X_{1}Y_{1}}\right)=0,\\
&\;{}_{\left[1-Y_{1}\right]}\left(G_{XY}+(m-1)H_{XY}\right)=0,\,{}_{\left[1-Y_{2}\right]}\left(G_{X_{2}Y_{2}}+(m-1)H_{X_{2}Y_{2}}\right)=0.\end{aligned}

(97)

Since
J_{XYAB}
also satisfy the causality constraint
{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J_{XYA}\right)=\frac{I_{XYA}}{d_{X}}
for a general supermap, we can equivalently write the forward no-signaling constraint
E_{Y}+G_{Y}=F_{Y}+H_{Y}
as

\displaystyle m\left(E_{Y}+G_{Y}\right)=I_{Y},

(98)

as we did in the case with general supermaps.
Relabeling
mE_{XY}
as

also form an optimal solution, as we shall show now.

For the error tolerance constraint, observe that

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}_{XY}\star\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}p_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}p_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\widetilde{E}_{XY}\star\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}

(126)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\sum_{j_{1}\dots j_{q_{\rm tot}}}\alpha_{j_{1}\dots j_{q_{\rm tot}}}p_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\Gamma^{j_{1}\dots j_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\star\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}

(127)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\alpha_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}p_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\cdot 4^{q_{\rm tot}}

(128)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}p_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[E^{*}_{XY}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\right]

(129)

\displaystyle=E^{*}_{XY}\star\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}p_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}

(130)

\displaystyle\geq m^{*}(1-\epsilon),

(131)

where in the third equality, we use the identity
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\Gamma^{j_{1}\dots j_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\right]=4^{q_{\rm tot}}\delta_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}},j_{1}\dots j_{q_{\rm tot}}}
.
Since
E^{*}_{XY}\geq 0
, we know
\operatorname{Tr}\left[E^{*}_{XY}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\right]
and thus
\alpha_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}
are nonnegative for every
i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}
, implying that
\widetilde{E}_{XY}\geq 0
.
Similarly, because
E^{*}_{XY}\leq F^{*}_{XY}
, we have
\operatorname{Tr}\left[E^{*}_{XY}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\right]\leq\operatorname{Tr}\left[F^{*}_{XY}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\right]
, and thus
\alpha_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\leq\beta_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}
for every
i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}
, implying that
\widetilde{E}_{XY}\leq\widetilde{F}_{XY}
.
For the causality constraint, first note that, for every
i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[F^{*}_{XY}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[F^{*}_{XY}{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\right)\right]

(132)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(F^{*}_{XY}\right)\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\right]

(133)

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{*}}{2^{q_{\rm tot}}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[I_{XY}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\right]

(134)

\displaystyle=m^{*},

(135)

where the first equality is due to that
\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}
is the Choi operator of a no-signaling
N
-partite channel, and the second equality holds because the projection
{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}
is self-dual.
Then,
\beta_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}=\frac{m^{*}}{4^{q_{\rm tot}}}
for every
i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}
, resulting in

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{XY}=\frac{m^{*}}{4^{q_{\rm tot}}}\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}=\frac{m^{*}I_{XY}}{2^{q_{\rm tot}}},

(136)

where the second equality is due to the identity
\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}=2^{q_{\rm tot}}I_{XY}
.
By Eq. (
136
), it is straightforward to see that the causality constraint
{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(\widetilde{F}_{XY}\right)=\frac{m^{*}I_{XY}}{2^{q_{\rm tot}}}
holds.
Finally, consider that

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\alpha_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\right]

(137)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{q_{\rm tot}}}\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[E^{*}_{XY}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\right]I_{Y}

(138)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{q_{\rm tot}}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[E^{*}_{XY}\left(2^{q_{\rm tot}}I_{XY}\right)\right]I_{Y}

(139)

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[E^{*}_{XY}\right]}{2^{q_{\rm tot}}}I_{Y}

(140)

\displaystyle=I_{Y},

(141)

where the last equality is due to
\operatorname{Tr}\left[E^{*}_{XY}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[E^{*}_{Y}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[I_{Y}\right]=2^{q_{\rm tot}}
.

So far, we have shown that
m^{*}
,
\widetilde{E}_{XY}
, and
\widetilde{F}_{XY}
satisfy all the constraints in SDP (
125
).
Since
m^{*}
is optimal, they form an optimal solution.
Now, by Eq. (
100
),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}(\,\vec{{\cal P}}\,)=\log_{2}\max&\;\lfloor m\rfloor\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;E_{XY}\star\sum_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}p_{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}\Gamma^{i_{1}\dots i_{q_{\rm tot}}}_{XY}\geq m(1-\epsilon),\,E_{Y}=I_{Y},\\
&\;0\leq E_{XY}\leq F_{X}\otimes I_{Y},\,\operatorname{Tr}\left[F_{X}\right]=m.\end{aligned}

(142)

Clearly,
m^{*}
,
\widetilde{E}_{XY}
, and
\frac{1}{2^{q_{\rm tot}}}\widetilde{F}_{X}
form a feasible solution as
\widetilde{F}_{XY}=\frac{m^{*}}{2^{q_{\rm tot}}}I_{X}\otimes I_{Y}=\frac{1}{2^{q_{\rm tot}}}\widetilde{F}_{X}\otimes I_{Y}
.
Thus, we know
C_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}(\,\vec{{\cal P}}\,)\geq\log_{2}\lfloor m^{*}\rfloor
.
Since
C_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}(\,\vec{{\cal P}}\,)\leq C_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}(\,\vec{{\cal P}}\,)
and
C_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}(\,\vec{{\cal P}}\,)=\log_{2}\lfloor m^{*}\rfloor
, we conclude that
C_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}(\,\vec{{\cal P}}\,)=C_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}(\,\vec{{\cal P}}\,)
.

Supplementary Note F
No advantage for channel simulation

Theorem 3

For any quantum channel
{\cal C}
and error tolerance
\epsilon\geq 0
, the one-shot classical simulation cost assisted by free general supermaps is equal to that assisted by free parallel supermaps, i.e.,

\displaystyle S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})=S_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}({\cal C}).

(143)

Proof.

By definition, it is straightforward to see that
S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})\leq S_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}({\cal C})
since
{\bf FreePar}\subseteq{\bf Free}
.
Now, we show that
S_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}({\cal C})
is also a lower bound to
S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})
.

The first step is to express
S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})
as an optimization program in terms of the Choi operator of channel
{\cal C}
with input
A
and output
B
.
The diamond distance between two quantum channels
{\cal C}_{1}
and
{\cal C}_{2}
can be written as an SDP
Watrous 2009
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2}\left\|{\cal C}_{1}-{\cal C}_{2}\right\|_{\diamond}=\min&\;\mu\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;Y_{AB}\geq 0,\,Y_{AB}\geq J^{{\cal C}_{1}}_{AB}-J^{{\cal C}_{2}}_{AB},\,\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\left[Y_{AB}\right]\leq\mu I_{A},\end{aligned}

(144)

where
J^{{\cal C}_{1}}_{AB}
and
J^{{\cal C}_{2}}_{AB}
are the Choi operators of the corresponding channels.
It then follows that the simulation cost
S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})
can be formulated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})=\log_{2}\min&\;m\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;Y_{AB}\geq 0,\,Y_{AB}\geq J^{\cal M}_{AB}-J^{\cal C}_{AB},\,Y_{A}\leq\epsilon I_{A},\,J^{\cal M}_{AB}=J^{S}_{XYAB}\star J^{\vec{\Delta}^{m}}_{XY},\\
&\;J^{S}_{XYAB}\geq 0,\,{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J^{S}_{XYA}\right)=\frac{I_{XYA}}{d_{X}},\,{}_{[1-A]}J^{S}_{YAB}=0,\end{aligned}

(145)

where
m
is minimized over positive integers.
The constraints in the last line is requiring
{S}
to be a supermap in
{\bf Free}
.
We obtain a lower bound to
S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})
by relaxing the constraint
{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J^{S}_{XYA}\right)=\frac{I_{XYA}}{d_{X}}
into constraints
J^{\cal M}_{A}=I_{A}
and
J^{S}_{YA}=I_{YA}
.
To show that this is indeed a relaxation, we now prove that these two relaxed constraints are implications of
{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J^{S}_{XYA}\right)=\frac{I_{XYA}}{d_{X}}
.
For the first constraint, consider that

\displaystyle J^{\cal M}_{A}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\left[J^{S}_{XYAB}\star J^{\vec{\Delta}^{m}}_{XY}\right]

(146)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{XYB}\left[J^{S}_{XYAB}\left({\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J^{\vec{\Delta}^{m}}_{XY}\right)\otimes I_{AB}\right)\right]

(147)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{XY}\left[{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J^{S}_{XYA}\right)\left(J^{\vec{\Delta}^{m}}_{XY}\otimes I_{A}\right)\right]

(148)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{XY}\left[\frac{I_{XYA}}{d_{X}}\left(J^{\vec{\Delta}^{m}}_{XY}\otimes I_{A}\right)\right]

(149)

\displaystyle=I_{A},

(150)

which is what we set out to show.
For the other constraint, note that, in one direction, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J^{S}_{XYA}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[\frac{I_{XYA}}{d_{X}}\right]

(151)

\displaystyle=I_{YA}.

(152)

In another direction, consider that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J^{S}_{XYA}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\operatorname{Tr}_{X_{j}}\circ{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{X_{j}Y_{j}}\right)\left(J^{S}_{XYA}\right),

(153)

where
N
is the number of ideal classical channels in the list
\vec{\Delta}^{m}
.
Observe that for any linear operator
P_{X_{j}Y_{j}R}
with an arbitrary system
R
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{X_{j}}\circ{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{X_{j}Y_{j}}\left(P_{X_{j}Y_{j}R}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{X_{j}}\left[{}_{\left[1-Y_{j}+X_{j}Y_{j}\right]}\left(P_{X_{j}Y_{j}R}\right)\right]

(154)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{X_{j}}\left[P_{X_{j}Y_{j}R}-P_{X_{j}R}\otimes\frac{I_{Y_{j}}}{d_{Y_{j}}}+\frac{I_{X_{j}Y_{j}}}{d_{X_{j}Y_{j}}}\otimes P_{R}\right]

(155)

\displaystyle=P_{Y_{j}R}.

(156)

As a result, Eq. (
153
) gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{X}\left[{\cal L}^{\rm NS}_{XY}\left(J^{S}_{XYA}\right)\right]=J^{S}_{YA},

(157)

which, together with Eq. (
152
), implies

\displaystyle J^{S}_{YA}=I_{YA}.

(158)

Hence, we arrive at

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})\geq\log_{2}\min&\;m\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;Y_{AB}\geq 0,\,Y_{AB}\geq J^{\cal M}_{AB}-J^{\cal C}_{AB},\,Y_{A}\leq\epsilon I_{A},\,J^{\cal M}_{AB}=J^{S}_{XYAB}\star J^{\vec{\Delta}^{m}}_{XY},\\
&\;J^{S}_{XYAB}\geq 0,\,J^{\cal M}_{A}=I_{A},\,J^{S}_{YA}=I_{YA},\,{}_{[1-A]}J^{S}_{YAB}=0.\end{aligned}

(159)

Similar to what we did in deriving the SDPs for the capacities, here we use the symmetry of
J^{\vec{\Delta}^{m}}_{XY}
to simplify the above optimization program, arriving at

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})\geq\log_{2}\min&\;\lceil\operatorname{Tr}\left[F_{B}\right]\rceil\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&\;Y_{AB}\geq 0,\,Y_{AB}\geq E_{AB}-J^{\cal C}_{AB},\,Y_{A}\leq\epsilon I_{A},\\
&\;0\leq E_{AB}\leq I_{A}\otimes F_{B},\,E_{A}=I_{A}.\end{aligned}

(160)

Observe that the right hand side is exactly the SDP for one-shot classical simulation cost assisted by parallel supermaps as given in Ref.
Fang et al. 2020
.
Hence,
S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})\geq S_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}({\cal C})
.
Therefore, we conclude that
S_{\epsilon}^{\bf Free}({\cal C})=S_{\epsilon}^{\bf FreePar}({\cal C})
.
