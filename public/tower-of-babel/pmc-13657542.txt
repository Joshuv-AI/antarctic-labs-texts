Short- and long-term outcomes of Deflux injection versus ureteral reimplantation in pediatric vesicoureteral reflux: a narrative review
Authors: Izhar Ali, Farman Ullah, Javed Miandad, Waqar Hameed Ghazi, Naveed Ahmad Khan, Ijaz Khan, Maryam Rehman, Muhammad Tanveer Sajid, Ramin Khan, Sardar Noman Qayyum, Adnan Ayub, Samim Noori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) is a common urologic disorder associated with recurrent febrile urinary tract infections (UTIs), pyelonephritis, and renal scarring. For children who require intervention, endoscopic injections of dextranomer/hyaluronic acid copolymer (Deflux) and ureteral implantation are the two options. This narrative review employed a structured search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Embase to identify guideline documents, systematic reviews, and original studies with long-term follow-up, comparing these two approaches. The outcomes include radiographic reflux resolution, durability, febrile UTI recurrence, renal outcomes, complications, retreatment, recovery, cost, and quality of life. Evidence states that ureteral reimplantation provides the highest one-procedure radiographic success, and the most durable anatomical correction, especially in high-grade reflux and complex anatomy. Deflux is though less invasive, outpatient alternative with shorter recovery, and low perioperative morbidity, and lower initial treatment burden, but repeat injections, and late radiographic recurrence are more frequent. Both interventions reduce febrile UTI with favorable renal outcomes in most children, if reflux is controlled. Bladder and bowel dysfunction, reflux grade, renal scarring, anatomic complexity, previous treatment, and family preferences should guide the treatment. Deflux is favored over ureteral reimplantation for lower-risk children and families prioritizing minimal invasiveness, whereas ureteral reimplantation remains the more definitive treatment option for high-grade reflux, or where endoscopic therapy failed. Therefore, comparative studies with standardized long-term reporting are needed to refine patient selection.

Introduction

Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) is one of the most common pediatric urologic disorders, affecting approximately 1–3% of children. It is associated with febrile urinary tract infections (UTIs), recurrent pyelonephritis, and renal scarring. The principal objective of management is to reduce recurrent infection and preserve long-term renal health, either through spontaneous resolution, prevention of clinical sequelae, or definitive correction of reflux. Ureteral reimplantation has long been regarded as a standard for surgical correction of VUR because of its consistently high success and durable radiographic resolution. In most modern series, success rates approach 95–98% or higher, and the procedure is often favored in children with high-grade reflux, recurrent febrile UTIs, failed conservative or endoscopic management, or complex anatomy. However, despite its excellent efficacy, ureteral reimplantation is more invasive than endoscopic treatment, typically requiring general anesthesia, hospitalization, temporary catheter drainage, and a longer postoperative recovery period. Although minimally invasive and robotic approaches have expanded surgical options, ureteral reimplantation still carries a greater perioperative burden than endoscopic therapy[4,6–9]. By contrast, endoscopic injection with dextranomer/hyaluronic acid (Dx/HA) offers a minimally invasive and usually an outpatient alternative with low perioperative morbidity, shorter recovery, and less postoperative discomfort. Early experience with Deflux showed moderate single-procedure success, but refinement of technique, particularly the hydrodistention implantation technique (HIT) and Double-HIT approach, has improved outcomes substantially. Studies report good short-term radiographic resolution, especially in children with low- to moderate-grade reflux, and repeat injection can further increase overall treatment success. These advantages, combined with lower treatment burden and high parental acceptance, have made Deflux an important option in current pediatric VUR practice[8,10–18].

Current American Urology Association and European Association of Urology/European Society of Pediatric Urology guidance recognize both approaches as appropriate, with treatment choice individualized according to reflux grade, age, febrile UTI, renal scarring, bladder and bowel dysfunction (BBD), anatomic complexity, previous treatment, family preference, and local expertise[19]. This review compares the short- and long-term outcomes of Deflux endoscopic injection and ureteral reimplantation and proposes a practical, risk-based approach to patient selection for the type of intervention.

Methods

In this narrative review, we included published evidence comparing endoscopic injections and ureteral reimplantation for pediatric primary VUR. A literature search was conducted on PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Embase through March 2026 using the following search terms: vesicoureteral reflux, VUR, child, children, pediatric, paediatric, Deflux, dextranomer/hyaluronic acid, Dx/HA, endoscopic injection, subureteral injection, STING, HIT, Double-HIT, ureteral reimplantation, anti-reflux surgery, reflux resolution, febrile UTI, renal scarring, dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA), recurrence, retreatment, complications, cost, and parental preference. Eligible studies were English-language pediatric studies and guideline documents that reported outcomes after Deflux, ureteral reimplantation, or both. Current guidelines, systematic reviews, randomized or prospective comparative studies, and large retrospective cohorts with long-term follow-up were prioritized and included. The included studies reported radiographic reflux resolution, febrile UTI recurrence, renal outcomes, complications, retreatment, recovery burden, cost, or family-centered outcomes. Evidence was synthesized descriptively, as the included studies differed in design, reflux grade distribution, injection technique, follow-up duration, and outcome definitions. The manuscript specifies whether outcomes were reported per ureter, per renal unit, or per patient, and distinguishes radiographic, clinical, and renal endpoints.

Outcome definitions used in this review

Radiographic success was defined as the resolution or clinically meaningful downgrading of reflux on the postoperative voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG). Clinical success referred primarily to a reduction in or absence of recurrent febrile UTIs or pyelonephritis after intervention. Renal outcomes included new renal scarring, changes in differential renal function, progression of reflux nephropathy, or other long-term renal preservation indicators. Short-term outcomes

Reflux resolution (initial success)

Deflux injection

Success rates after the first Deflux injection vary according to VUR grade, injection technique, and patient-related factors. Reported radiographic cure rates after one or two injections generally range from 65% to 90% in children with low- to moderate-grade reflux (grades I–III)[8,10]. Meta-analyses and large series have shown that treatment success declines with increasing reflux grade but improves with repeat injection. For example, Chung et al reported ureter-level radiographic success of 85.9% after two injections, while Kalisvaart et al reported 93% clinical and radiographic success after Double-HIT treatment in a selected cohort[10,13,20,21]. In higher-grade reflux, primary cure rates are lower; Seibold et al reported lower success in higher-grade reflux, supporting the general finding that endoscopic success declines as VUR grade increases[10]. In addition, the presence of anatomical anomalies or BBD has been associated with lower endoscopic success rates[9]. Although multiple injections improve cumulative resolution, they may increase cost and the need for repeated procedures.

Ureteral reimplantation

Open ureteral reimplantation achieves consistently high initial success rates across all reflux grades and remains the benchmark surgical procedure for definitive correction of VUR. Reported radiographic cure rates generally range from 95% to 100%, usually at the ureteral or renal-unit level, and guideline-based reviews cite overall success rates of approximately 98%[9,22]. In the randomized trial by Salih et al, involving children with grade III–IV reflux, 93.75% of ureters were successfully corrected after a single reimplantation procedure[23]. Minimally invasive approaches, including laparoscopic and robotic ureteral reimplantation, have also shown high success rates approaching those of open surgery after technical maturation, generally in the range of 90–98% in experienced centers[24,25]. Although outcomes may be slightly lower in bilateral or very high-grade cases, success remains above 95% in most contemporary series.

Comparative success of Deflux and ureteral reimplantation

Comparative studies suggest that ureteral reimplantation provides higher primary radiographic cure rates than Deflux injection, although both are effective treatment options. In the randomized trial by García-Aparicio et al, ureter-level radiographic resolution was achieved in 91% of Deflux-treated ureters (32/35) compared with 100% of ureters treated with Cohen ureteral reimplantation (32/32)[22]. At 5-year follow-up, persistent ureter-level radiographic resolution was reported in 94% of Deflux-treated ureters (30/32) versus 100% in the reimplantation group[22]. Similarly, Salih et al reported ureter-level radiographic resolution of 80% after a single Dx/HA injection compared with 93.75% after open Lich–Gregoir reimplantation[23]. These findings indicate that while endoscopic treatment is less invasive, ureteral reimplantation generally offers superior one-procedure radiographic success and durability.

Short-term UTI outcomes

Successful correction of reflux reduces the risk of recurrent febrile UTI after both endoscopic and surgical treatment. In a long-term follow-up study of Deflux treatment, Lightfoot et al surveyed a long-term cohort; postoperative febrile UTI occurred in 10.8% of children who had preoperative febrile UTI, indicating a favorable patient-level clinical outcome[26]. Ureteral reimplantation is also associated with high clinical success and low rates of postoperative pyelonephritis. In a long-term cohort study, Nelson et al reported patient-level clinical success of 96.5% after reimplantation; any postoperative UTI was still reported in some children, while pyelonephritis occurred in 6.5%[27]. Thus, both modalities are effective in reducing infectious morbidity when reflux is successfully corrected.

Recovery, pain, and hospital stay

Deflux injection is typically performed as a day-case procedure and is associated with shorter hospitalization and lower postoperative analgesic requirements than ureteral reimplantation. In a multicenter international retrospective study of 400 children (232 endoscopic injections, 168 ureteral reimplantations), Nascimben et al reported a substantially shorter hospital stay after endoscopic injection (approximately 1 day vs 7 days), shorter analgesic requirement (1.8 vs 4.7 days), and no postoperative catheter or drain after injection, whereas ureteral reimplantation required bladder catheterization in all cases for a mean of 5.35 days and drainage in 98.2% of cases for a mean of 2.34 days. These findings support the short-term recovery advantage of endoscopic treatment, although the paper’s exact paracetamol-only percentages for the reimplantation group are internally inconsistent and should be interpreted cautiously[17].

Complications

Endoscopic injection

Deflux injection has a favorable safety profile and low perioperative morbidity[28]. Reviews of the literature describe complications after endoscopic treatment as uncommon and mostly related to ureteral obstruction rather than major perioperative injury[28]. Reported post-injection obstruction rates are generally low, around 0.5–1.0% in published series[1–3]. In the long-term series by Lightfoot et al, 1 of 99 patients (1.0%) underwent ureteral reimplantation for symptomatic obstruction 2.8 years after the initial injection; across the surveyed and comparison cohorts combined, 2 of 220 patients (0.9%) developed symptomatic obstruction[26]. In a mixed endoscopic-agent cohort that included both polytetrafluoroethylene and Dx/HA, Chertin et al reported febrile UTI in 11 children (2.2%) after successful endoscopic correction; only 3 cases occurred in the Dx/HA group[29].

Ureteral reimplantation

Open ureteral reimplantation remains very safe in experienced hands, but it carries the expected morbidity of an open antireflux operation[9,30]. Review-level sources note postoperative hematuria, bladder spasm, urinary frequency or dysuria, and the need for temporary catheter drainage as common short-term burdens[9,30]. Open repair complications include postoperative obstruction in about 2% and contralateral reflux in about 9% per review data[30]. Intravesical Politano–Leadbetter repair carries a recognized risk of ureteral kinking or obstruction, whereas extravesical repairs can be associated with transient urinary retention[31]. In the recent 12-year follow-up study by Gerwinn et al, postoperative ureterovesical junction obstruction occurred in 7.5% of renal units in that selected long-term cohort, exclusively after Politano–Leadbetter repair and within 8 months[4]. However, the authors also stated that their overall institutional obstruction rate was <1%, which is consistent with the broader literature[4]. Long-term hypertension attributable to ureteral reimplantation itself has not been demonstrated in that cohort; no hypertension was observed, and late renal impairment was limited to a small number of children with pre-existing reflux nephropathy[4].

Long-term outcomes

Durability (recurrence of VUR)

After Deflux

Long-term follow-up after Deflux shows that although most initially successful cases remain clinically well, a proportion will have recurrent reflux on delayed imaging. In the long-term series by Lightfoot et al, median follow-up was 8.4 years, and among children with preoperative febrile UTIs, 10.8% had postoperative febrile UTI, indicating favorable patient-level clinical outcomes in the surveyed cohort. Lightfoot et al also noted that prior studies reported 13–26% recurrence on follow-up VCUG 1–5 years after endoscopic injection among patients or ureters that were initially reflux-free[26]. Lee et al specifically demonstrated that late radiographic failure may be substantial: among ureters that were reflux-free on the initial postoperative VCUG, 74% remained radiographically cured at 1 year, corresponding to a 26% recurrence rate among initially successful units and an overall 1-year success of 46.1% when early failures were included. This paper supports the need for continued follow-up after an apparently successful initial injection[32].

Most recurrent reflux after injection is lower-grade and may be asymptomatic, but febrile UTI after apparently successful endoscopic treatment should prompt reassessment. In Chertin et al’s mixed endoscopic-agent cohort, 11 of 507 children (2.2%) developed febrile UTI after successful endoscopic correction, and recurrent reflux was identified in 8 of those 11 children (72.7%) on reevaluation[29]. The randomized trial by García-Aparicio et al[22] supports reasonably good medium-term durability of Deflux in selected grades II–IV reflux: VUR was still resolved in 30 of 32 ureters (94%) at 5 years after endoscopic treatment, compared with 32 of 32 (100%) after ureteral reimplantation. Overall, Deflux durability is good in many children, but secondary procedures are not uncommon. In Lightfoot et al[26], 13/99 (13.1%) underwent secondary surgery overall, although only 3/99 (3.0%) ultimately required open or laparoscopic surgery rather than repeat injection.

After ureteral reimplantation

Ureteral reimplantation provides excellent long-term durability, and true late reflux failure after a technically successful repair is uncommon. In the recent 12-year follow-up study by Gerwinn et al[4], children who had an uneventful recovery and no postoperative ureterovesical junction obstruction in the first year had a low risk of later surgical complications. In that series, obstruction occurred only after Politano–Leadbetter repair and was diagnosed within 8 months, with no late pUVJO beyond the first year. Long-term renal outcomes after successful reimplantation are generally favorable. Webster et al[33] prospectively followed children with DMSA imaging and found new renal scars in only 3 children (2%) after reimplantation. For postoperative infectious outcomes, Nelson et al[27] reported patient-level clinical success of 96.5% after open ureteral reimplantation. Although 21.8% had at least one postoperative UTI, postoperative pyelonephritis occurred in only 6.5%, supporting strong long-term protection against febrile upper-tract infection. In the Nascimben long-term comparative cohort[17], mean follow-up was 177.8 months and cohort-level global success was 76.5% overall, with 66.81% in the endoscopic group and 89.88% in the ureteral reimplantation group.

Therefore, ureteral reimplantation may still be presented as the more definitive long-term option, with late failures being uncommon and usually related to early technical issues, obstructive complications, or underlying congenital urinary tract pathology rather than routine recurrence of reflux.

Febrile UTI recurrence

Successful correction of reflux reduces the risk of febrile UTI after both endoscopic injection and ureteral reimplantation. After Dx/HA injection, Lightfoot et al reported recurrent febrile UTI in 10.8% of previously symptomatic children at a median follow-up of 8.4 years[26]. Kamdem et al likewise found that recurrent febrile UTI after Dx/HA treatment was uncommon overall; 18.9% had one or more febrile UTIs over a mean follow-up of 51.6 months, and no recurrences were observed after 4 years of follow-up[34]. Chertin et al reported febrile UTI after successful endoscopic correction in 11 of 507 children (2.2%), with recurrent reflux identified in 8 of those 11 (72.7%) on reevaluation[29]. Hunziker et al also found a low rate of febrile UTI after successful endoscopic correction (5.7%) and showed that female sex and pre- or postoperative BBD were the strongest predictors of recurrence[35]. After ureteral reimplantation, febrile upper-tract infection is also uncommon, although postoperative UTI is not absent; Nelson et al reported postoperative pyelonephritis in 6.5% and any postoperative UTI in 21.8% after a median follow-up of 2.9 years[27]. Overall, both approaches substantially reduce febrile UTI risk when reflux is corrected, but persistent BBD and recurrent infection remain important clinical warning signs.

Renal scarring and functional outcomes

The ultimate goal of intervention is preservation of renal parenchyma and differential renal function. Available postoperative series suggest that new renal scarring is uncommon after successful correction with either ureteral reimplantation or endoscopic treatment, although direct comparative evidence remains limited[29,33]. In a prospective ureteral reimplantation series, Webster et al found new renal scars in only 3 of 143 children (2%) at a median follow-up of 3.4 years; the mean change in differential renal function was 2.5%, and 7 children (5%) had a decrease of more than 6% without new scarring[33]. After successful endoscopic treatment, Chertin et al reported a similarly low burden of adverse renal outcomes: patients with downgraded reflux developed no new renal scars, and among 446 refluxing renal units without severe baseline damage, 27 (6.1%) showed a decrease greater than 5% in relative function without new scarring[29]. However, in renal units with severe pre-existing scarring and less than 20% uptake on baseline DMSA, deterioration still occurred in 11 of 26 units, indicating that pre-existing renal injury remains a key determinant of long-term outcome[29]. Overall, both Deflux and ureteral reimplantation preserve renal outcomes in most children when reflux and infection are controlled, and new scarring appears to be driven more by baseline renal damage and recurrent infection than by the choice of intervention itself.

Need for re-treatment

Deflux often requires repeat treatment, particularly in children with higher-grade reflux. In a pooled meta-analysis, if the first endoscopic injection failed, a second injection achieved radiographic success in 68% and a third in 34%, with aggregate ureter-level success of 85%. In a long-term recurrence series, Nascimben et al reported that 23.3% of children recurred after the first injection; among those with first-injection recurrence, 39/49 (79.6%) underwent a second injection, and recurrence increased with higher VUR grade. Lightfoot et al similarly found that 13.1% underwent secondary surgery after Dx/HA, most commonly repeat injection, while only 3.0% ultimately required open or laparoscopic surgery[21,26,36]. By contrast, ureteral reimplantation is rarely repeated. In the 2024 multicenter comparative study by Nascimben et al, redo surgery was required in 27.15% of endoscopic patients versus 2.97% after ureteral reimplantation, which is about a ninefold difference. In the randomized trial by Salih et al, open Lich–Gregoir reimplantation achieved 93.75% ureteral resolution after a single procedure for grade III-IV VUR. In the same Nascimben comparative cohort, overall long-term success was 89.88% for the ureteral reimplantation group, with very low redo-surgery rates[17,23]. Importantly, failed Deflux does not appear to compromise the success of subsequent open repair. Chertin et al reported 100% resolution after open ureteral reimplantation for persistent VUR following failed endoscopic treatment[29].

Complications (early and late)

Deflux injection

Deflux injection has an excellent safety profile, and significant perioperative complications are uncommon[26,28]. The most important late complication is ureteral obstruction, but this remains rare. Long-term and review data describe post-injection obstruction rates generally around 0.5–1.0%, although individual series and case reports document both acute and delayed obstruction requiring stenting or, less commonly, ureteral reimplantation[26,28,37,38]. Mild transient symptoms such as dysuria, hematuria, or urinary frequency may occur, but serious adverse events are infrequent in modern series.

Ureteral reimplantation

Ureteral reimplantation is also highly safe in experienced hands, but it carries the expected morbidity of an open reconstructive procedure[9,30]. Short-term postoperative issues include hematuria, bladder spasms, dysuria, temporary catheter-related morbidity, and, particularly after bilateral extravesical repair, transient urinary retention[9,30,31]. Late ureterovesical obstruction is uncommon. In a recent 12-year follow-up study by Gerwinn et al, postoperative obstruction occurred only after Politano–Leadbetter repair and was detected within the first 8 months; children without early obstruction had a low risk of late complications[4]. De novo contralateral reflux can occur after either approach; in the multicenter Nascimben study, it was seen in 4.74% after the first endoscopic injection and in 1.19% after ureteral reimplantation, without a significant difference[17]. Overall complication rates remain low when repair is performed by experienced pediatric teams.

Cost-effectiveness and resource use

Endoscopic treatment generally has lower up-front direct cost and lower short-term resource utilization than ureteral reimplantation because it is usually a day-case procedure with shorter operative time and minimal hospitalization[17]. In the multicenter international study by Nascimben et al, the median direct cost of a single endoscopic injection was €659, compared with €5000 for open reimplantation, €5200 for laparoscopic reimplantation, and €5700 for robotic reimplantation; even total pathway costs remained lower for endoscopic treatment in that cohort[17]. Older economic modeling by Kobelt et al also found that replacing open surgery with endoscopic injection reduced average overall costs while preserving similar overall cure rates[39]. However, the cost literature is not uniform: Saperston et al reported that, in their reimbursement system, unilateral outpatient extravesical reimplantation was actually less expensive than subureteral Deflux injection[40]. Therefore, Deflux often appears less costly initially, but the final economic advantage depends heavily on retreatment rates, local hospitalization costs, reimbursement systems, and the surgical pathway used for reimplantation. Parental preference

Parental satisfaction after successful Deflux treatment is high. In the long-term study by Lightfoot et al, 94.1% of surveyed families reported good satisfaction with the outcome after a median follow-up of 8.4 years[26]. Seibold et al similarly reported very good postoperative quality-of-life questionnaire results and noted that parents of successfully treated children would choose the same treatment again[10].

More broadly, the available parental-preference literature suggests that decision-making is shaped by a balance between treatment effectiveness, complication risk, invasiveness, and family circumstances rather than by a single universally preferred option[41–43]. In the Swedish Reflux Trial follow-up, parental satisfaction was high regardless of treatment group[42]. Other preference studies show that parents often value high effectiveness and low complication risk most strongly, while some families still prefer endoscopic treatment because it is less invasive[41,43]. Family preference is also explicitly recognized in guideline-based decision-making when options have a similar risk-benefit balance[1,44].

Factors influencing treatment choice

Treatment choice should be individualized and risk-based[1,44,45]. Age matters mainly because it affects the probability of spontaneous resolution; younger children and children with lower-grade reflux are more likely to resolve without surgery, so many infants are managed conservatively at first[44,45]. Low-grade reflux is often observed or managed with less invasive strategies, whereas persistent high-grade reflux, recurrent febrile UTI, renal scarring, or failure of prior management more often lead to surgical correction.

BBD is one of the most important modifiers of outcome. It increases the risk of breakthrough UTI, reduces the success of endoscopic injection, and increases the risk of postoperative UTI; accordingly, current reviews and guidelines recommend treating BBD before intervention whenever possible[19,46].

Anatomic factors also matter: duplicated collecting systems and other urinary tract abnormalities reduce the predictability of endoscopic success and may favor ureteral reimplantation[46]. Finally, parental preferences and willingness to accept repeat procedures versus a more invasive one-time operation are central to shared decision-making. Discussion and practical recommendations

Deflux injection and ureteral reimplantation are both effective interventions for pediatric VUR, but the choice between them varies across different clinical situations. Deflux is less invasive and usually offers faster recovery, while ureteral reimplantation provides higher radiographic success and greater long-term durability. In practice, Deflux injection is most appropriate for selected children with low- to moderate-grade reflux, uncomplicated anatomy, and for families who prioritize minimal invasiveness. Ureteral reimplantation should be considered for high-grade reflux, recurrent febrile UTIs, renal scarring, complex anatomy, or failed endoscopic treatment. BBD should be treated before intervention, whenever possible. Follow-up should be personalized; repeat VCUG is most relevant after either treatment.

Evidence limitations

The available evidence has several limitations: the strongest randomized prospective comparisons are small; many available studies are retrospective; studies differ in reflux grade distribution, injection technique, definition of radiographic cure, and use and timing of postoperative VCUG, as well as follow-up duration; and there is inconsistent reporting of clinical versus radiographic endpoints. Renal outcomes are especially difficult to compare because baseline renal scarring, congenital dysplasia, infection history, and follow-up imaging protocols vary substantially. As this is a narrative review, study selection and synthesis may also be affected by selection bias despite a structured search approach.

Conclusions

Deflux injection and ureteral reimplantation each have a role in pediatric VUR management. Deflux offers a favorable safety and recovery profile with good, but generally lower, radiographic cure rates. Ureteral reimplantation provides the highest success and durability, at the cost of more invasive surgery. Long-term clinical and renal outcomes are generally favorable with either modality when reflux and infection are controlled. Current evidence suggests using Deflux for younger children with low-to-moderate reflux and reserving ureteral reimplantation for higher grades or failed injections. Both approaches are associated with generally favorable renal outcomes, although direct comparative evidence on renal outcomes remains limited. High-quality comparative studies and standardized outcome reporting are needed to refine these strategies.
