Food Safety Practices and Associated Factors Among Street Food Vendors in Urban Areas of Eastern Zone of Sidama Region, Ethiopia: A Community Based Cross Sectional Study
Authors: Amelo Bolka, Firew Hussien, Amenu Asfaw Gujo, Aregahegn Dona, Bereket Sisay Gebremichael
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionFood safety research in Ethiopia has largely focused on major cities, leaving zonal and district urban areas underexplored. This evidence gap limits the development of context-specific interventions to reduce foodborne disease risks. This study assessed food safety practices and associated factors among street food vendors in urban areas of the Eastern Zone of the Sidama Region, Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from 25 January to 20 February 2025 among 546 street food vendors selected using multistage sampling. Data were collected using a pretested, structured interviewer-administered questionnaire through the Kobo Toolbox system. Multivariable logistic regression identified factors associated with food safety practices. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05, and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.ResultsOverall, 52.2% (95% CI: 47.9%–56.4%) of street food vendors demonstrated good food safety practices. Good food safety practices were significantly associated with being female (AOR = 2.02, 95% CI: 1.35–3.01), earning a monthly income of ≥5,000 Ethiopian Birr (AOR = 2.86, 95% CI: 1.93–4.26), having ≥5 years of work experience (AOR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.47–3.24), receiving food safety training (AOR = 2.91, 95% CI: 1.92–4.39), being licensed (AOR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.09–2.40), and possessing good food safety knowledge (AOR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.42–3.18).ConclusionSlightly more than half of the vendors demonstrated good food safety practices. Expanding food safety training, strengthening health education, and enforcing licensing requirements are recommended to improve food safety practices among street food vendors.

Introduction

Food safety is globally acknowledged as a critical public health issue, affecting populations in both developed and developing countries. 1 It refers to the range of conditions and practices implemented to ensure that food is suitable for consumption and free from contaminants that may pose health risks. Street-vended foods are typically described as foods and beverages that are prepared and sold by vendors in public spaces, such as streets, for immediate consumption or for later consumption without additional processing. 2 In urban environments, these foods serve an essential socio-economic function by offering affordable and convenient meal options, while simultaneously creating employment opportunities, particularly for economically disadvantaged groups. 3 However, the largely informal nature of street food vending is frequently associated with notable concerns related to hygiene standards and food safety practices. 4

On a global scale, contamination of food by microbiological pathogens remains a major contributor to morbidity and mortality. 5 Foodborne diseases arise from the consumption of food contaminated with bacteria, viruses, parasites, or chemical agents, leading to a broad spectrum of health conditions. 6 These infections continue to represent a substantial public health challenge in both developed and developing regions. 1 Nevertheless, the impact is markedly greater in low- and middle-income countries, where food safety regulatory frameworks are often inadequate and food handling practices are insufficiently controlled. 7 In these contexts, street-vended foods are commonly prepared and distributed under conditions that may promote contamination and heighten the risk of disease transmission. 1

Unsafe food constitutes a significant global economic burden. 8 An estimated 600 million people—almost one in ten globally—develop foodborne illnesses each year, resulting in about 420,000 deaths. The economic burden is considerable, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where productivity losses related to unsafe food are estimated at approximately USD 110 billion annually. 9 These economic consequences stem from increased healthcare costs, reduced labor productivity, and diminished workforce capacity. 10 Despite the scale of this burden, foodborne diseases frequently receive limited attention within public health agendas, largely due to underreporting and the prioritization of more conspicuous health challenges.4,8 As a result, the actual extent of foodborne illness is likely underestimated, particularly in developing regions where surveillance and reporting systems remain inadequate. 11

Food safety practices among street food vendors are influenced by multiple factors, including socio-demographic characteristics, environmental conditions, and the availability of institutional support.12,13 Evidence from studies conducted in Ethiopia and other developing countries highlights key determinants of safe food handling, such as educational attainment, access to clean water, participation in food safety training, routine medical check-ups, and the presence of regulatory inspections. 14 Vendors who have undergone training and have access to adequate sanitation facilities are more likely to adhere to recommended hygiene practices during food preparation and service.15,16 Accordingly, interventions that emphasize capacity building, improvements in infrastructure, and strengthened regulatory oversight are likely to enhance food safety practices. 17

Despite a growing body of research on food safety practices among street food vendors, important contextual gaps persist.18,19 In Ethiopia, most studies have been concentrated in a limited number of urban centers, with findings demonstrating variability in hygiene practices across locations.20-22 Such variations are likely influenced by differences in infrastructure, regulatory enforcement, and vendor awareness. 23 To our knowledge, no published study has assessed food safety practices among street food vendors in the Eastern Zone of the Sidama Region. This absence of localized data constrains the ability to design targeted and context-specific public health interventions to mitigate foodborne disease risks. Therefore, this study aimed to assess food safety practices and their associated factors among street food vendors in urban areas of the Eastern Zone, Sidama Regio, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study Setting, Design and Period

This community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from January 20 to February 20, 2025, in the Eastern Zone of the Sidama Region, Ethiopia. The zone has a population of 678,913 and comprises eight districts (Aroresa, Bona Zuria, Bensa, Bura, Chabe Gambeltu, Chire, Hoko, and Daela) and the Daye town administration, each with its own administrative town. The zone is characterized by a mix of rural and rapidly urbanizing settings, with eight nine towns and several smaller urban centers, reflecting an increasing trend of rural-to-urban migration and expansion of informal food vending. According to the zonal health department’s 2025 report, there are 1,658 registered street food vendors, yielding a density of about 24 vendors per 10,000 population. District health offices, in coordination with the zonal health department, conduct quarterly inspections of each vendor and enforce corrective actions for non-compliance, though inspection consistency varies by district due to resource constraints.

Study Population and Eligibility Criteria

The study population consisted of all street food vendors present in selected urban areas of the Eastern Zone during the data collection period. Vendors who had resided in the area for less than six months or who did not provide informed consent were excluded from the study.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula, assuming a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error. The proportion of good food safety practices among street food vendors (31.5%) was taken from a previous study. 24 After accounting for a design effect of 1.5 and a 10% non-response rate, the final calculated sample size was 546.

Sampling Procedure

A multi-stage sampling technique was employed. In the first stage, four districts (Chire, Bensa, Aroresa, and Daye Town) were randomly selected from the eight districts and one town administration in the zone. Lists of registered street food vendors were obtained from the health offices of the selected districts and town administration, yielding a total of 965 vendors. The sample size was proportionally allocated to each district and town based on the number of registered vendors. Data Collection Procedure

Data were collected using a pretested, structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted from previously published literature.15,25-31 The questionnaire captured information on socio-demographic factors, food safety knowledge, practices, and environmental conditions. It was developed in English, translated into Sidamu Afoo by language experts, and back-translated into English by an independent translator to ensure accuracy.

Data were collected using the Kobo Toolbox mobile application by four environmental health graduates (Bachelor of Science degrees), supervised by two Master’s-level public health professionals. Prior to data collection, all team members received three days of training on the study objectives, data collection procedures, and ethical considerations. The questionnaire was pre-tested on 5% of the sample size (n = 27) two weeks prior to the main data collection using vendors from outside the selected study areas. Pretesting the questionnaire allowed us to identify key problems—such as confusing questions, ambiguous wording, and incomplete response options—before the main data collection. Data collection was closely supervised, and completed questionnaires were randomly rechecked daily for completeness and consistency. At the end of each day, the principal investigator reviewed all questionnaires to identify and address any errors or omissions.

Study Variables

Food safety practice was the dependent variable for this study. It was measured using twenty-two questions. A correct response to a practice question was scored as ‘1’, and an incorrect response as ‘0’. Vendors who scored at or above the mean for practice questions were classified as having good food safety practices; all others were classified as having poor practices.25,31 Food safety knowledge was assessed using fifteen standard questions. Vendors who scored at or above the mean score on these questions were categorized as having good knowledge, while those scoring below this threshold were categorized as having poor knowledge.25,31 Reliability testing yielded Cronbach’s alpha scores of 0.728 for knowledge and 0.724 for practice, confirming good internal consistency (>0.7).

Independent variables considered as potential confounders in this study included vendors’ sex, monthly income level, work experience, working time, hand washing facility availability, receiving inspection from health professionals, medical checkup, food safety training, food safety knowledge, and license status.

Data Processing and Analysis

Data collected via the Kobo Toolbox mobile application were exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the socio-demographic characteristics, food safety knowledge, practices, and environmental conditions of participants. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with food safety practices. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable model to control for potential confounders. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF ≤1.154). The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated adequate model fit (p = 0.583). The model showed excellent discriminatory ability (ROC-AUC = 0.89). Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

More than half (57.5%) of the vendors were married. About one-third (35.3%) of the vendors had completed elementary school. Food Safety Knowledge of Street Food Vendors

The majority of vendors (74.0%) were aware that handwashing before food handling reduces contamination risk. Similarly, 72.5% recognized that washing utensils with soap and water lowers contamination risk, and 65.4% reported using personal protective equipment for this purpose. Over half of the vendors (59.3%) dressed neatly, and 74.4% kept their fingernails clean while cooking. However, only 59.0% wore caps during food preparation or vending. More than two-thirds (68.7%) acknowledged that utensil cleaning and good sanitation reduce contamination, and 61.5% were aware that reheating cooked food decreases contamination risk. Food Safety Practices of Street Food Vendors

About 63% of vendors reported washing their hands with soap and water before food preparation, and 72.5% after handling waste; however, only 34.8% reported drying their hands with a towel. The majority reported wearing gloves when handling ready-to-eat foods (70.7%) and clean uniforms during food handling (71.6%), yet only 36.6% had short-trimmed fingernails, and 39.6% wore jewelry while working. Observable hygiene concerns included skin rashes or boils (8.2%) and visible discharges, cuts, or wounds (9.9%). Regarding food storage and sanitation practices, 68.7% reported separating raw from ready-to-eat foods, 64.3% stored perishable items separately from non-perishable foods, and 77.1% regularly checked refrigerator temperatures. Discussion

This study aimed to assess food safety practices and associated factors among street food vendors in urban areas of the Eastern Zone of Sidama Region, Ethiopia. The findings revealed that 52.2% of street food vendors demonstrated good food safety practices. Factors significantly associated with good food safety practices among vendors in the study area included being female, earning a monthly income of ≥ 5,000 Ethiopian Birr (39 $US), having ≥ 5 years of work experience, receiving training in safe food handling, being licensed, and possessing good knowledge of food safety.

In the present study, 52.2% of street food vendors demonstrated good food safety practices. Comparable proportions were reported in studies conducted in Debre Markos, Ethiopia (54%), 31 North Shewa Zone of Ethiopia (50.5%), 27 Northwest Ethiopia (52.4%), 30 and West Gojjam, Ethiopia (51.4%), 29 possibly reflecting similarities in study design and urban study settings. This could be due the same study design and both conducted in urban settings. However, lower prevalence estimates were documented in Bako Town, Ethiopia (30.4%), 28 Gondar, Ethiopia (37.6%), 32 Southern Ethiopia (40), 33 and Somaliland (47.7%), 34 whereas higher proportions were observed in Bishoftu Town, Ethiopia (64.8%), 15 Central Ethiopia (64.7%), 25 and Eastern Ethiopia (57.1%). 26 These variations may be attributed to differences in study period, sample size, and socioeconomic characteristics of vendors, as well as disparities in infrastructure availability, personal hygiene practices, and enforcement of regional food safety regulations.

The present study revealed that gender was significantly associated with food safety practices among street food vendors. Female vendors had two times higher odds of demonstrating good food safety practices compared to their male counterparts. Our finding aligns with studies conducted in Bishoftu Town, Ethiopia, 15 Eastern Ethiopia, 26 Gondar, Ethiopia, 32 and Northwest Ethiopia, 30 all of which consistently reported that being female was associated with increased odds of good food safety practices among street food vendors. This association may be explained by behavioral perspectives, which suggest that women often acquire greater practical experience in food preparation and hygiene through socially assigned caregiving and domestic responsibilities, leading to stronger food safety behaviors. Likewise, female street food vendors may demonstrate better food safety practices due to socially reinforced gender roles that emphasize domestic food preparation, and caregiving, which can enhance practical experience in safe food handling. Moreover, women may exhibit greater compliance with hygiene norms, contributing to improved adherence to recommended food safety standards. 35

Vendors’ income level was significantly associated with food safety practices in the present study. Those earning ≥ 5,000 Ethiopian Birr per month demonstrated better food safety practices compared with lower-income counterparts. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Central Ethiopia, 25 Northwest Ethiopia, 30 Southern Ethiopia, 33 and West Gojjam, Ethiopia, 29 which similarly reported that higher income levels were significantly associated with an increased likelihood of good food handling practices. Higher income may facilitate investment in essential food safety resources, including reliable refrigeration, access to potable water, improved storage facilities, quality raw ingredients, and hygiene supplies. In addition, greater financial stability may reduce economic pressures that compromise safe practices and may enhance access to training opportunities and regulatory compliance. 36

Vending experience was a significant predictor of food safety practices among street food vendors. Vendors with ≥ 5 years of vending experience were about twice as likely to demonstrate good food handling practices compared with those who had less than five years of experience This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Bishoftu Town, Ethiopia, 15 Debre Markos, Ethiopia, 31 Eastern Ethiopia, 26 North Shewa Zone of Ethiopia, 27 West Gojjam, Ethiopia, 29 and Somaliland. 34 These studies consistently reported that longer vending experience was significantly associated with increased likelihood of good food safety practices. Prolonged vending experience contributes to good food safety practices, supporting the need for targeted support and mentorship for less-experienced vendors to improve safe food handling.

License status was a factor independently associated with the food safety practices of street food vendors. Vendors who were licensed had higher odds of demonstrating good food safety practices compared to those who were not licensed. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Gondar City, Northwest Ethiopia. 37 A possible explanation is that being licensed may improve food safety practices by requiring vendors to meet minimum regulatory standards, including compliance with hygiene guidelines, facility requirements, and periodic inspections. 38 Furthermore, licensing processes often involve training and ongoing monitoring, which can reinforce accountability and promote adherence to recommended food handling practices. 39

Receipt of training in safe food handling was significantly associated with improved food safety practices among street food vendors. Vendors who had undergone food safety training were three times more likely to demonstrate good food safety practices compared with those who had not received such training. Our results are consistent with previous studies conducted in Bako Town, Ethiopia, 28 Debre Markos, Ethiopia, 31 Northwest Ethiopia, 30 West Gojjam, Ethiopia, 29 and Somaliland, 34 which similarly reported that vendors who received food safety training were significantly more likely to adhere to recommended food handling standards. The consistency of these findings across different geographical settings highlights the importance of expanding and institutionalizing regular, competency-based food safety training to enhance food safety practices.

In this study, good knowledge of food safety was significantly associated with improved food safety practices among street food vendors. Vendors who demonstrated good knowledge were about twice as likely to report appropriate food handling practices compared with those with poor knowledge. Consistent with our results, studies conducted in Bako Town, Ethiopia, 28 Bishoftu Town, Ethiopia, 15 Central Ethiopia, 25 North Shewa Zone of Ethiopia, 27 Debre Markos, Ethiopia, 31 West Gojjam, Ethiopia, 29 as well as in Somaliland, 34 similarly reported that vendors with good knowledge of food handling were significantly more likely to implement recommended food safety practices. The convergence of evidence across these diverse geographical contexts highlights the importance of strengthening food safety education and training interventions as a strategic approach to improving hygienic practices and reducing foodborne disease risks associated with street-vended foods.40,41

While this study identified several factors associated with food safety practices, food safety inspection, availability of handwashing facilities, medical checkups, and working hours were not significantly associated with food safety practices in the multivariable analysis. However, this finding contradicts previous studies that reported significant associations between food safety practices and food safety inspection,34,42 availability of handwashing facilities,26,33 medical checkups, 27 and working hours. 43 This could indicate variation in standards and frequency of inspection, where routine inspections may focus on compliance but less on longer term behavior change. 44 Likewise, simply providing hand-washing facilities may not automatically lead to their actual use without a supply of water or soap or other added motivation for hygiene behaviors.45,46 Failure to link with periodic medical checkups implies that this periodical health screening without intermediate help may affect those behaviors linked with food supply only marginally. 47 Similarly, working hours do not directly impact ultimate food safety performance as compliance to safe food handling will more likely be formulated by knowledge and attitudes than work duration. 48

The findings have important implications for policy and practice. Municipal authorities and licensing agencies need to tighten up on licensing systems, incorporating mandatory food safety training and regular refresher courses for street food vendors. 49 Public health inspectors should continue routine inspections while supporting high-impact supervision and training in behavior change strategies to increase compliance with food safety standards. 50 Moreover, vendor associations should facilitate peer learning, training access, and compliance with recommended food safety practices particularly among newer and lower-income vendors.

The following limitations need to be considered while interpreting the findings of the study. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the reliance on self-reported food safety practices without observational verification may have introduced social desirability bias. Second, the study also did not assess the microbial safety of street foods, despite microbial contamination being a major contributor to foodborne diseases and a significant public health concern. Finally, the use of a mean-based classification to categorize food safety practices may have resulted in participant misclassification.

Conclusions

This study revealed that slightly more than half (52.2%) of the vendors in the study area demonstrated good food safety practices. Food safety practices were significantly associated with gender, monthly income, vending experience, receipt of training in safe food handling, license status, and knowledge of food safety.

Recommendations

Regional Health Bureau

Conduct quarterly food safety training sessions specifically targeting male vendors, vendors with lower income, and those with less than five years of vending experience, with a focus on practical hygiene practices and safe food handling techniques.

Municipal Authorities

Strengthen licensing enforcement by conducting regular inspections and requiring proof of food safety training as a prerequisite for license renewal. Consider offering reduced or waived licensing fees for vendors who complete certified training programs.

Vendor Associations

Ndertake annual refresher training on food safety practices and actively seek information on proper food handling, storage, and hygiene through available health extension programs and peer learning groups.

Health Extension Workers

Integrate food safety education into routine health outreach activities, targeting informal food vendors with tailored messages that address their specific knowledge gaps and practical challenges.
