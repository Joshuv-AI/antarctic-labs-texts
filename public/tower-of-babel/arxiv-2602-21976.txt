A consistent phase-averaged model of the interactions between surface gravity waves and currents
Jacques Vanneste 1, William R. Young 2

Abstract
We formulate a model of the two-way interactions between surface gravity waves and ocean currents. The model couples the transport of wave action in the four-dimensional (horizontal) position–wavevector phase space with the Craik–Leibovich system for the currents. Coupling is via the Doppler shift in the dispersion relation governing action transport, and wave pseudomomentum in the Craik–Leibovich system. The velocity in the Doppler shift is a vertical integral of the Lagrangian mean velocity of the currents, with a weight that is consistent with the vertical structure of the pseudomomentum. This consistency ensures conservation of momentum and energy in the coupled wave–current system. The conservation properties of the wave–current model stem from an underlying variational structure. We derive this structure from that of the rotating Euler equations for an incompressible fluid with free surface by introducing a Lagrangian wave–mean decomposition, making simplifying approximations, and Whitham averaging. We apply the wave–current model to the problem of generation of inertial oscillations by surface waves originally considered by Hasselmann.

1
Introduction

Two-way interactions between ocean currents and high-frequency surface gravity waves dominate sea-surface dynamics. The slow, phase-averaged evolution of the wave field can be modelled with the wave-action transport equation, (
1
) below. The action transport equation captures the effect of currents on waves, namely advection and refraction, through a Doppler-shift term in the dispersion relation. On the other hand, the effect of waves on currents via the Stokes–Coriolis force
(
Hasselmann 1970
;
Huang 1979
)
and the vortex force is modelled by the Craik–Leibovich equation
(
Craik & Leibovich 1976
;
Leibovich 1980
)
and its extensions
(
Ardhuin et al. 2008
;
McWilliams et al. 2004
;
McWilliams 2022
)
. Both forces depend on wave action through the Stokes velocity
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{S}}}
. Doppler-shift and wave-averaged forces are the non-dissipative wave–mean-flow interactions by which energy and momentum are exchanged between waves and currents.

Although this exchange can proceed in both directions, a popular simplification considers the wave field for prescribed currents. This simplification underpins recent the work on the action transport equation
(
Villas Bôas et al. 2020
;
Villas Bôas & Young 2020
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
and the vast literature based on ray tracing
(
Gallet & Young 2014
, e.g.)
.
Likewise many studies solve the Craik–Leibovich equation (
8
) with prescribed Stokes velocity
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{S}}}(z)

(
Skyllingstad & Denbo 1995
;
McWilliams et al. 1997
;
Seshasayanan & Gallet 2019
;
Wagner et al. 2021
;
Wagner et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. Prescription of either waves or currents severs the wave–mean-flow interaction problem into non-communicating halves.

Coupled models have emerged in the last decade. Driven by the demands of operational oceanography, established wave models based on the action transport equation are paired with ocean circulation models
(
Breivik et al. 2015
;
Clementi et al. 2017
;
Couvelard et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2023
;
Porcile et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. This a posteriori coupling does not treat the wave action and Craik–Leibovich equations in a consistent manner. As a result, the conservation laws of energy and momentum are damaged.

Here we formulate a consistent model describing the joint evolution of waves and currents (§
2
). Main elements of this framework are familiar: currents enter the action transport equation via the Doppler shift
\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{U}}
in the dispersion relation. But it is not obvious how the Doppler velocity
\bm{\mathcal{U}}
relates to the current in the Craik–Leibovich system.
We show that taking
\bm{\mathcal{U}}
to be a suitable (wavenumber-dependent) weighted vertical average of the Lagrangian mean velocity
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
leads to a coupled model conserving energy and momentum (§
3
).

The coupled model is consistent and enjoys conservation properties because of an underlying variational formulation. We obtain this formulation from a variational form of the rotating Euler equation with free surface by making a series of approximations: wave–mean decomposition, linear-wave ansatz and Whitham averaging (§
4
).
This applies to surface gravity waves the approach pioneered by
Dewar 1970
and
Bretherton 1971
for hydromagnetic and acoustic waves, and developed by
Grimshaw 1984
,
Gjaja & Holm 1996
and
Salmon 2016
for internal waves
(
Xie & Vanneste 2015
;
Pizzo & Salmon 2021
;
Salmon 2020
;
Holm et al. 2023
, see also)
.

The exact conservation of energy and momentum in the absence of forcing and dissipation for the coupled model of §
2
is key to understanding wave–current interactions. Without a conserved energy it is impossible to decide on the ‘best’ definition of mean kinetic energy, whether the Lagrangian mean kinetic energy
\frac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}|^{2}
or its Eulerian counterpart
\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{E}}}|^{2}
, neither of which are conserved according to the Craik–Leibovich equation alone. Without a consistently coupled wave action equation, the non-conservative terms in the energy equation of the currents can always be attributed to energy exchanges with the waves.
The consistent wave–current model in §
2
answers questions that are inherently ambiguous when examined with either prescribed Stokes velocity in the Craik–Leibovich equation or prescribed currents in the action equation. For example, with a complete understanding of energy conservation, we shown in §
3
and §
5
that the conserved energy involves the Lagrangian kinetic energy
|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}|^{2}/2
rather than its Eulerian counterpart
|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{E}}}|^{2}/2
.

As a simple application, we revisit the generation of inertial oscillations by surface waves considered by
Hasselmann 1970
(§
5
).
We replace
Hasselmann 1970
’s prescribed Stokes velocity
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{S}}}
by a prescribed forcing of the action transport equation. Prescribed forcing of the action represents the effect of wind more directly than prescription of
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{S}}}
in the Craik–Leibovich equation. The current dynamics obtained with the wave–current model is identical to that of
Hasselmann 1970
but the energetics of the process is now clear: the energy of the inertial oscillations is not drawn from the wave energy but instead requires additional wind work.

2
A consistent wave–current model

The coupled model summarised in this section – referred to as ‘consistent wave–current model’, CWCM for short – follows from application of Hamilton’s principle to an approximate Lagrangian, given in (

The phase-averaged wave field is characterized by the wave action density

3
Conservation laws

The CWCM in §
2
conserves action, mass, energy, momentum, circulation and potential vorticity. According to (
1

\mathcal{N}(\bm{x},\bm{k})
is transported by the non-divergent phase-space velocity
(\bnabla_{\!\bm{k}}\,\Omega,-\bnabla_{\!\bm{x}}\,\Omega)

\iint F(\mathcal{N}(\bm{x},\bm{k}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}

(20)

\mathcal{N}^{\circ}=0

, however, an alternative viewpoint regards

\int\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}-\bm{\mathsf{p}}\,\mathrm{d}z

(32)

as the Lagrangian mean momentum,
\int\bm{\mathsf{p}}\,\mathrm{d}z
as the wave momentum and their sum (
30
) as the total momentum. This viewpoint is consistent with a geometric interpretation of the Lagrangian mean momentum
(
Gilbert & Vanneste 2018
;
Gilbert & Vanneste 2025
)
.

In this section, for simplicity, we have focused on the depth-integrated momentum. By forming
\iint\eqref{waveAct}Q\bm{k}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}
and combining with the Craik–Leibovich equation (
18
), it is possible to derive an equation for the total momentum
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
at each
z
. This equation reduces to (
29
) when integrated with respect to
z
. It provides an alternative to (
18
) in which wave forcing appears through radiation stresses with vertical as well as horizontal components.

3.3
Energy conservation

Following
Bretherton & Garrett 1968
, we expect that the wave-energy density is

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{x},\bm{k})=\sigma(\bm{x},\kappa)\,\mathcal{N}(\bm{x},\bm{k})\,,

(33)

where
\sigma(\bm{x},\kappa)
is the intrinsic frequency in (
3
).
We also expect that the total energy density,

E(\bm{x})=\int\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z+\iint\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{x},\bm{k})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\,,

(34)

satisfies a physical space conservation law

\partial_{t}E+\bnabla_{\!\bm{x}}\!\bcdot\!\bm{J}=\,{E}^{\circ}\,.

(35)

The horizontal vector
\bm{J}(\bm{x})
above is the energy flux.

To determine
\bm{J}
and
{E}^{\circ}
in (
35
), start with the depth integral of
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\eqref{CL2}
and use the vertical boundary conditions (
12
) and (
13
) to find

\p_{t}\int\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z+\bnabla_{\!\bm{x}}\bcdot\int\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\pi\,\mathrm{d}z=\int\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bm{\mathsf{p}}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}z+\int\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bm{u}^{\circ}\mathrm{d}z\,.

(36)

Equation (
36
) is the energy equation for the currents.

Turning to the wave energy in (
33
),
\iint\eqref{waveAct}\Omega\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}
produces

\p_{t}\iint\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{w}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}+\iint(\bm{\mathcal{U}}\bcdot\bm{k})\mathcal{N}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}+\bnabla_{\!\bm{x}}\bcdot\iint\bm{\mathcal{C}}\Omega\mathcal{N}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}=\iint\Omega\mathcal{N}^{\circ}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\,,

(37)

where
\bm{\mathcal{C}}
is the group velocity in (
14
). The key identity

\iint(\bm{\mathcal{U}}\bcdot\bm{k})\mathcal{N}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}=\int\!\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\!\bcdot\!\iint Q\bm{k}\mathcal{N}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\,\mathrm{d}z=\int\!\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\!\bcdot\bm{\mathsf{p}}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}z

(38)

puts (
37
) in the form

\p_{t}\iint\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{w}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}+\int\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bm{\mathsf{p}}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}z+\bnabla_{\!\bm{x}}\bcdot\iint\bm{\mathcal{C}}\Omega\mathcal{N}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}=\iint\Omega\mathcal{N}^{\circ}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\,.

(39)

Summing (
36
) and (
39
) eliminates the energy-exchange term

\int\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bm{\mathsf{p}}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}z

(40)

and produces energy conservation in (
35
) with

\bm{J}=\int\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\pi\,\mathrm{d}z+\iint\bm{\mathcal{C}}\Omega\mathcal{N}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\,,

(41)

and

{E}^{\circ}=\iint\Omega\mathcal{N}^{\circ}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}+\int\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bm{u}^{\circ}\,\mathrm{d}z\,.

(42)

The right of (
39
),

\iint\Omega\mathcal{N}^{\circ}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}=\iint\sigma\mathcal{N}^{\circ}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}+\iint\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{U}}\,\mathcal{N}^{\circ}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\,,

(43)

involves a non-obvious contribution of the Doppler shift to
{E}^{\circ}
. We return to this in §
5
.

Using the dispersion relation (
2
), the final term in (
41
) can be split into physically distinct contributions

\iint\bm{\mathcal{C}}\Omega\mathcal{N}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}=\iint\bm{\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{w}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}+\iint\mathcal{G}\bm{k}\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{N}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}+\iint\bm{\mathcal{U}}\bm{\mathcal{U}}\bcdot\bm{k}\mathcal{N}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\,.

(44)

The first term on the right of (
44
) is group-velocity transport of the wave energy
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{w}}
. The second term is the wave radiation stress (
31
) acting on
\bm{\mathcal{U}}
. The third term is the momentum flux associated with
\bm{\mathcal{U}}
acting on the wave pseudomomentum.

The mean kinetic energy in the conserved energy (
34
) is the Lagrangian kinetic energy
\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}|^{2}
rather than the Eulerian kinetic energy
\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{E}}}|^{2}
. For the CWCM, this is natural:
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
is the primary dynamical variable and
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{E}}}
does not appear explicitly. Moreover, the form of the conserved energy is linked to the variational formulation of the CWCM in §
4
via Noether’s theorem.

Wagner et al. 2021
and
Czeschel & Eden 2023
contrast the simplicity of the Lagrangian kinetic energy budget for the (uncoupled) Craik–Leibovich system with the complexity of the Eulerian kinetic energy budget. The CWCM explains this simplicity by the privileged role of the Lagrangian kinetic energy as one of the two terms in the conserved total energy (
34
).
In §
5
we return to the primacy of
\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}|^{2}
over
\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{E}}}|^{2}
in a discussion of wave-forced inertial oscillations In this basic example the CWCM is forced by prescribing
\mathcal{N}^{\circ}
in the action transport equation (
1
) and all terms in the energy balance can be calculated explicitly.

3.4
Circulation and potential vorticity

Material conservation of circulation and potential vorticity is a property of the Craik–Leibovich equation (
8
), i.e. these laws are independent of the wave action equation (
1
). We state the results and defer to
Andrews & McIntyre 1978
or
Holm 1996
for proof.

Introduce the ‘absolute Lagrangian mean momentum’

\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}=\bm{h}+\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}-\bm{\mathsf{p}}\,,

(45)

where

\bm{h}(\bm{x}_{3})=\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{f}\times\bm{x}_{3}

(46)

with
\bm{x}_{3}=(\bm{x},z)
. A key property of
\bm{h}
is that
\bnabla\!\times\!\bm{h}=\bm{f}
. The corresponding absolute vorticity is

\bm{\zeta}^{\mathrm{a}}=\bnabla\!\times\!\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}=\bm{f}+\bnabla\times(\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}-\bm{\mathsf{p}})\,.

(47)

Then conservation of circulation is

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}\bcdot\mathrm{d}\bm{\ell}=0\,,

(48)

where
\mathcal{C}
is a material circuit and we assume
\bm{u}^{\circ}=0
.

As noted by
Andrews & McIntyre 1978
, for
\bm{f}=0
, conservation of circulation implies that
\bnabla\times(\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}-\bm{\mathsf{p}})=0
for all times if this holds initially. In this irrotational flow regime,
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
is diagnosed from
\bm{\mathsf{p}}
as
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}=\bm{\mathsf{p}}-\bnabla\phi
for a potential
\phi
that is uniquely determined by the Poisson equation
\nabla^{2}\phi=\bnabla\bcdot\bm{\mathsf{p}}
and the boundary conditions satisfied by
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
. The CWCM thus reduces to the action equation (
1
), with
\bm{\mathcal{U}}(\bm{x},\kappa)
related linearly to
\mathcal{N}(\bm{x},\bm{k})
via solution of the Poisson equation.

For potential vorticity, suppose that we have a passive scalar
b(\bm{x},z,t)
satisfying wave-averaged material conservation,

b_{t}+\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bnabla b={b}^{\circ}\,.

(49)

Potential vorticity is

\displaystyle\Pi=\bm{\zeta}^{\mathrm{a}}\bcdot\bnabla b\,,

(50)

and satisfies

\p_{t}\Pi+\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bnabla\Pi=\bnabla\bcdot\left(\bm{u}^{\circ}\times\bnabla b+\bm{\zeta}^{\mathrm{a}}{b}^{\circ}\right)\,.

(51)

4
Variational derivation

The conservation properties of the CWCM can be traced to its variational formulation. We obtain this variational formulation from that of the rotating incompressible Euler equations.

4.1
Rotating Euler equations

Variational formulations of fluid models typically involve the flow map,
\bm{\varphi}
, which sends labels
\bm{a}
identifying fluid particles to the time-
t
positions of the particles,
\bm{x}=\bm{\varphi}(\bm{a},t)

(
Salmon 1988
, e.g.)
. Incompressibility implies that the flow map preserves volume, that is,
|\partial\bm{\varphi}/\partial\bm{a}|=\mathrm{const}
. We can take the constant to be
1
without loss of generality.
Flow map and velocity field are related via

\partial_{t}\bm{\varphi}(\bm{a},t)=\bm{u}(\bm{\varphi}(\bm{a},t),t).

(52)

Following
Dewar 1970
,
Bretherton 1971
and
Gjaja & Holm 1996

(
Holm et al. 2023
, see also)
, we make minimal use of labels and flow map by adopting an Eulerian variational formulation. In this formulation – an instance of the Euler–Poincaré framework
(
Holm et al. 1998
)
– the flow map only appears implicitly through a constrained form of the variations of
\bm{u}

first obtained by
Newcomb 1962
and
Bretherton 1970
.

In Appendix
B
, we show that the rotating, incompressible Euler equations, with a free surface at
z=s(\bm{x},t)
, follow from Hamilton’s principle
\delta\int\mathcal{L}\,\mathrm{d}t=0
applied to the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}[\bm{u},s]=\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\left(\int_{-d}^{s}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}|^{2}+\bm{h}\bcdot\bm{u}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z-\tfrac{1}{2}gs^{2}\right).

(53)

The Eulerian variations
\delta\bm{u}
and
\delta s
are induced by variations
\delta\bm{\varphi}
of the flow map. Introducing an Eulerian vector field
\bm{q}(\bm{x},t)=(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})
by

\delta\bm{\varphi}(\bm{a},t)=\bm{q}(\bm{\varphi}(\bm{a},t),t),

(54)

leads to

\displaystyle\delta\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\bm{q}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{q}-\bm{q}\cdot\nabla\bm{u},

(55a)

\displaystyle\delta s

\displaystyle=q_{3}-q_{1}s_{x}-q_{2}s_{y},

(55b)

where
\bm{q}
satisfies
\bnabla\bcdot\bm{q}=0
and is otherwise arbitrary (see appendix
B
). Incompressibility is enforced by considering only volume preserving flow maps and hence divergence-free
\bm{u}
and
\bm{q}
. No explicit constraint enforcing
\bnabla\bcdot\bm{u}=0
is therefore required in (
53
).

4.2
Lagrangian mean framework

Following the GLM framework, we introduce a wave–mean flow decomposition at the level of the flow map by writing

\bm{\varphi}(\bm{a},t)=\bar{\bm{\varphi}}(\bm{a},t)+\bm{\xi}(\bar{\bm{\varphi}}(\bm{a},t),t),

(56)

where
\bar{\bm{\varphi}}
and
\bm{\xi}
denote the mean flow and perturbation maps. Below we introduce a linear-wave ansatz for
\bm{\xi}
that captures the wavy displacements resulting from surface gravity waves;
\bar{\bm{\varphi}}(\bm{a},t)
captures the mean currents.

Differentiating (
56
) with respect to
t
and using the mean position
{\bm{x}_{3}}=(\bm{x},z)=\bar{\bm{\varphi}}(\bm{a},t)
as independent variable gives

\bm{u}^{\bm{\xi}}=\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}+D_{t}\bm{\xi}.

(57)

Here,
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}(\bm{x},t)
defined by

\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}(\bar{\bm{\varphi}}(\bm{a}),t)=\partial_{t}\bar{\bm{\varphi}}(\bm{a},t)

(58)

is the Lagrangian mean velocity,

D_{t}=\partial_{t}+\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bnabla\,,

(59)

and

\bm{u}^{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}_{3},t)=\bm{u}(\bm{x}_{3}+\bm{\xi}(\bm{x}_{3},t),t).

(60)

The decomposition (
56
) involves a degree of arbitrariness. We take advantage of this to impose that the mean flow map preserves volume and leaves the free surface flat, that is,

\left|\partial\bar{\bm{\varphi}}/\partial\bm{a}\right|=1\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bar{\varphi}_{3}(a,b,c=0)=0.

(61)

In contrast with standard GLM, the assumptions in (
61
) imply a non-zero average for the perturbation map,
\overline{\bm{\xi}}\not=0

(
Vanneste & Young 2022
, see)
. Below in (
63
), however, we use a linear approximation for
\bm{\xi}
so that
\overline{\bm{\xi}}
vanishes up to neglected terms.

Volume preservation implies that
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
is divergence free, i.e. (
9
). With this, we can rewrite the Lagrangian (
53
) using the mean position
(\bm{x},z)=\bar{\bm{\varphi}}(\bm{a})
as an independent variable. This results in

\mathcal{L}[\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}},\bm{\xi}]=\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\left(\int_{-d}^{0}\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}+D_{t}\bm{\xi}|^{2}+\bm{h}^{\bm{\xi}}\bcdot(\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}+D_{t}\bm{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}z-\tfrac{1}{2}g\zeta^{2}\right),

(62)

where
\zeta=\xi_{3}(\bm{x},z=0)
and
\bm{h}^{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}_{3})=\bm{h}(\bm{x}_{3}+\bm{\xi}(\bm{x}_{3}))=\bm{h}(\bm{x}_{3})+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{f}\times\bm{\xi}(\bm{x}_{3})
.
(Without the assumption of volume preservation (
61
), (
62
) would contain a Jacobian factor
\tilde{\rho}
, as in the GLM formulation of
Andrews & McIntyre 1978
.)

4.3
Averaged Lagrangian

Up to (
62
) no approximations have been made. We now make a WKB ansatz for
\bm{\xi}
, assuming a scale separation between rapid phase variations and slow envelope modulations, and Whitham average. Following the prescription of
Whitham 1965
;
Whitham 1974
, the displacement field
\bm{\xi}
is approximated by that of a linear wavetrain,

\bm{\xi}\approx\mathrm{Re}\,a(\bm{x},t)\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x},z,\bm{k})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(\bm{x},t)}\,,

(63)

where
a(\bm{x},t)
is a slowly varying amplitude,
\theta(\bm{x},t)
the phase, and
\bm{k}=(k,l)=\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\theta
. The vertical structure is dictated by

\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x},z,\bm{k})=\frac{1}{\kappa\sinh(\kappa d)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{i}k\cosh(\kappa(z+d))\\
\mathrm{i}l\cosh(\kappa(z+d))\\
\kappa\sinh(\kappa(z+d))\end{array}\right).

(64)

This structure is that obtained for surface waves propagating in a uniform current, with uniform depth and neglecting background rotation. The dependence of
\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x},z,\bm{k})
on
\bm{x}
stems from that of
d(\bm{x})
.

With
\hat{\bm{\xi}}
in (
64
), the displacement
\bm{\xi}(\bm{x},t)
in (
63
) is approximately incompressible and irrotational:

\bnabla\bcdot\bm{\xi}\approx 0\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bnabla\!\times\!\bm{\xi}\approx 0\,.

(65)

This approximation is associated with Whitham’s variational derivation of the WKB approximation: applying
\bnabla=(\bnabla_{\!\bm{x}},\p_{z})
to (
63
) the derivatives act only on the phase
\theta(\bm{x},t)
and on the
z
-dependence of
\hat{\bm{\xi}}
. For instance,

D_{t}\bm{\xi}\approx\mathrm{Re}\,a(\bm{x},t)\left(\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x},z,\bm{k})(-\mathrm{i}\omega+\mathrm{i}\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bm{k})+w^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}(\bm{x},z)\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{z}(\bm{x},z,\bm{k})\right)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(\bm{x},t)}\,.

(66)

We further neglect the term involving
w^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{z}
in (
66
) on the grounds that
w^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\ll u^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}},v^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
.

Introducing (
63
) into the Lagrangian (
62
) and averaging over the phase
\theta
leads to the averaged Lagrangian

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{L}}[\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}},\theta,a]

\displaystyle=\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\left(\int_{-d}^{0}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}|^{2}+\bm{h}(\bm{x})\bcdot\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z\right.

(67)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left.\tfrac{1}{4}\left(\int^{0}_{-d}\frac{\cosh(2\kappa(z+d))\left(\omega-\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\right)^{2}}{\sinh^{2}(\kappa d)}\,\mathrm{d}z-g\right)|a|^{2}\right),

where
\omega=-\partial_{t}\theta
and we neglect the wave contributions to the Coriolis term.

Using the definition of
Q(\bm{x},z,\kappa)
in (
5
), we rewrite the second
z
-integral in (
67
) as

\int^{0}_{-d}\frac{\cosh(2\kappa(z+d))\left(\omega-\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\right)^{2}}{\sinh^{2}(\kappa d)}\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{\kappa\tanh(\kappa d)}\int^{0}_{-d}\left(\omega-\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\right)^{2}Q(\bm{x},z,\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}z\,.

(68)

Justified by the smallness of the Doppler shift against the wave frequency, we make the approximation

\displaystyle\int_{-d}^{0}(\omega-\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}})^{2}Q\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}-2\omega\bm{k}\bcdot\int^{0}_{-d}\!\!\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}Q\,\mathrm{d}z+\int^{0}_{-d}\!\!(\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}})^{2}Q\,\mathrm{d}z\,,

(69a)

\displaystyle\approx(\omega-\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{U}})^{2}\,,

(69b)

where the Doppler velocity is

\bm{\mathcal{U}}=\int^{0}_{-d}\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}Q\,\mathrm{d}z\,,

(70)

as in (
4
).
This maneuveur simplifies (
67
) to

\overline{\mathcal{L}}[\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}},\theta,a]=\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\left(\int_{-d}^{0}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}|^{2}+\bm{h}(\bm{x})\bcdot\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z+\tfrac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\left(\omega-\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{U}}\right)^{2}}{\kappa\tanh(\kappa d)}-g\right)|a|^{2}\right).

(71)

The approximation in (
69
) is not necessary: one can proceed from the Lagrangian in (
67
). The resulting model has a more complicated dispersion relation than (
2
), and a more complicated expression for the pseudomomentum than (
10
). Alternatively, one obtains a superficially simpler model by boldly dropping the final term in (
69a
). But this is one step too many: we prefer the dispersion relation (
2
) with a physically intuitive Doppler shift that preserves Galilean invariance.

The Lagrangian (
71
) is the surface-wave analogue of the Lagrangian obtained by
Bretherton 1971
,
Gjaja & Holm 1996
and
Salmon 2016
for acoustic and gravity waves. Hamilton’s principle can be applied using variations
\delta\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
similar to (
55a
),
\delta a
and
\delta\theta
. The leads to the Craik–Leibovich system and to equations governing the phase
\theta(\bm{x},t)
and amplitude
a(\bm{x},t)
of a single wavetrain. We do not detail these equations here. Instead, we follow
Dewar 1970
to relax the assumption of a single wavetrain and consider a spectrum of linear waves.

4.4
Variational form of the CWCM

A spectrum of waves can be regarded as a superposition of independent wavetrains indexed by a two-dimensional (continuous) variable,
\bm{\alpha}
say (see
Dewar 1970
’s (
Dewar 1970
) discussion of the ‘weak turbulence’ assumption). Phase
\theta(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},t)
and amplitude
a(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},t)
then depend on
\bm{\alpha}
, and the Lagrangian (
71
) acquires an additional integration over
\bm{\alpha}
to become

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{L}}[\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}},\theta,a]

\displaystyle=\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\left(\int_{-d}^{0}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}|^{2}+\bm{h}(\bm{x})\bcdot\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left.\tfrac{1}{4}\iint\left(\frac{\left(\omega-\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{U}}\right)^{2}}{\kappa\tanh(\kappa d)}-g\right)|a|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}\right)\,.

(72)

The representation using
(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha})
as independent variables and
(\theta,a)
as dependent variables is inconvenient. It is preferable to use instead
(\bm{x},\bm{k})
as independent variables and
(\omega,\mathcal{N})
as dependent variables, where

\mathcal{N}=\frac{\sigma|a|^{2}}{2\kappa\tanh(\kappa d)}\left|\frac{\partial\bm{k}}{\partial\bm{\alpha}}\right|^{-1}

(73)

is the wave action density in
(\bm{x},\bm{k})
-space. Making this change of variables in (
71
) puts the averaged Lagrangian in its final form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{cwc}}[\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}},\mathcal{N},\omega]

\displaystyle=\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\left(\int_{-d}^{0}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}|^{2}+\bm{h}(\bm{x})\bcdot\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\,\tfrac{1}{2}\iint\left(\sigma^{-1}\left(\omega-\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{\mathcal{U}}\right)^{2}-\sigma\right)\mathcal{N}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\right).

(74)

The Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{cwc}}
is the Lagrangian of the CWCM.
The term on the second line is a standard Whitham-averaged Lagrangian governing wave dynamics. Because of its dependence on the current variable
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
via
\bm{\mathcal{U}}
, this term contributes to the dynamics of both waves and currents. This is key to consistency of the CWCM.

Hamilton’s principle can now be applied to (
74
). The variations are

\delta\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}=\p_{t}\bm{q}+\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bnabla\bm{q}-\bm{q}\bcdot\bnabla\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}},

(75)

with
\bm{q}
satisfying
\bnabla\bcdot\bm{q}=0
but otherwise arbitrary (in analogy with (
55a
)),
\delta\mathcal{N}
, and

\delta\omega=\partial_{t}r+\bnabla_{\bm{x}}r\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\omega-\bnabla_{\bm{k}}r\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\omega

(76)

with
r
arbitrary. This leads to the Craik–Leibovich equation in the form (
16
), dispersion relation (
2
) and action transport equation (
1
). Details are given in Appendix
C
. The form (
76
) of the variation of
\omega
, reminiscent of (
75
), emerges when changing variables from the mixed Eulerian-Lagrangian
\theta(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha})
to the Eulerian
\omega(\bm{x},\bm{k})
(see
Dewar 1970
and Appendix
D
).

5
Hasselmann’s problem

Hasselmann 1970
showed that the rectified effect of surface waves is to drive inertial oscillations through ‘a vertical shear stress induced by the rotation of the earth’; see also
Ursell & Deacon 1950
. To exhibit this nonlinear interaction between surface waves and inertial oscillations, we consider a flat-bottom (
\bnabla_{\!\bm{x}}d=0
), initially motionless ocean with no waves and no currents:

\mathcal{N}(\bm{x},\bm{k})=\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}(\bm{x},z)=0\qquad\text{at}\ \ t=0\,.

(77)

Currents and waves are jointly forced by suddenly switching on horizontally uniform wind forcing. This
t>0
forcing is modelled with

6
Discussion

CWCM, the model of two-way coupling between surface waves and ocean currents introduced in §
2
, is perhaps the simplest that conserves momentum and energy. This simplicity is achieved in part by adopting a wave–mean separation that differs from standard GLM to ensure (i) that the mean current is exactly divergence free, and (ii) that the mean free surface is flat. Property (ii) amounts to restricting mean configurations to those that minimise gravitational potential energy. Such a restriction is standard in the treatment of dynamical systems under a strong constraining potential
(
Takens 1980
, e.g.)
. It has the benefit of filtering surface waves from the mean equations. Property (ii) does not preclude capturing mean deformations in the free-surface elevation: in principle, these mean deformations are represented through non-zero mean perturbations,
\overline{\bm{\xi}}\not=0
. Our formulation, however, ignores these by following Whitham and using the linear approximation (
64
) for
\bm{\xi}
.

The simplicity of the CWCM also stems from the variational approach used for its derivation. Compared with formal asymptotic expansions carried out at the level of the dynamical equations, the variational approach has the advantage of preservation of symmetry-related conservation and ease of implementation. (Exploratory calculations indicate that systematic asymptotics to fourth order in wave steepness, as required to capture terms such as
|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{S}}}|^{2}/2
in the energy equation, is exceedingly complicated.) Against this, an inconvenience is the difficulty in ascertaining the asymptotic accuracy of the variational model. But for a problem as complex as the two-way interactions between surface gravity waves and currents, involving multiple small parameters and regimes of physical interest, we believe that the variational formulation has overwhelming advantages.

The CWCM in §
2
, with conservation laws on display in §
3
, follows from the application of Hamilton’s principle to the Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}_{\textsc{cwc}}
in (
74
). The primary approximation used in the construction
\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{cwc}}
is Whitham’s linear-wave approximation in (
63
) and (
64
). We then make a few secondary approximations, such as simplification of the Doppler shift as in (
69
). These secondary approximations can be avoided at the expense of a more complicated model. For example, retaining
w^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{z}
in (
66
) results in a three-dimensional pseudomomentum. Presumably this more complicated model is also a more accurate representation of the two-way interaction between currents and waves.

Controversial issues such as the divergence and vertical component of
\bm{\mathsf{p}}\approx\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{S}}}
or whether the current kinetic energy involves
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{E}}}
or
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
do not arise in our formulation. Only the Lagrangian mean velocity
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
and pseudomomentum
\bm{\mathsf{p}}
play a dynamical role. The pseudomomentum
\bm{\mathsf{p}}
appears as the variational derivative of the wave Lagrangian with respect to
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
, a vector directly related to wave action, rather than as an approximation to the general three-dimensional form of
Andrews & McIntyre 1978
. The primacy of
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{p}}
over
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{E}}}
and
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{S}}}
is an unavoidable feature of variational formulations of coupled wave–mean models. This aspect of the CWCM will remain if some of the approximations we have made are relaxed.

The CWCM provides a sound basis for revisiting problems that have previously been examined with prescribed
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{S}}}
or
\bm{\mathsf{p}}
.
The generation of inertial oscillations by a uniform field of surface waves considered in §
5
is the simplest such problem. Because of the assumption of horizontal homogeneity, the dynamics of action is not affected the mean current that is generated. The main benefit of the CWCM is then to provide a consistent picture of the energy exchanges between wind, waves and currents. It will be interesting to consider problems in which currents and wave action vary in the horizontal so that the two-way interactions come into play. These problems include instabilities such as the Craik–Leibovich instability responsible for the formation of Langmuir cells. Stratification effects can be included straightforwardly by treating buoyancy as a passive scalar, as in (
49
), and adding the buoyancy force in the momentum equation.

Applications of the Craik–Leibovich system to realistic ocean conditions often involve turbulence. The exchanges between waves and currents are then complicated by the separation of the currents into mean and turbulent components and the need to parameterise turbulent fluxes
(
Suzuki & Fox-Kemper 2016
;
Czeschel & Eden 2023
, e.g.)
. Work along these lines should use the CWCM or a similar model that recognises the importance of the Lagrangian kinetic energy
|\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}|^{2}/2
.

\delta\int\mathcal{L}\,\mathrm{d}t=\delta\int\mathrm{d}t\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\left(\int_{-d}^{0}\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}\bcdot\delta\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\,\mathrm{d}z+\iint\mathcal{N}\delta\omega\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\right)=0.

(116)

The variations
\delta\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
and
\delta\omega
are independent and can be treated separately.

For
\delta\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
, (
75
) gives

\displaystyle\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\int_{-d}^{0}\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}\bcdot\delta\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\int_{-d}^{0}\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}\bcdot(\partial_{t}\bm{q}+\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bnabla\bm{q}-\bm{q}\bcdot\bnabla\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}})\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\int_{-d}^{0}\partial_{t}(\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}\bcdot\bm{q})+\bnabla\bcdot(\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}\bcdot\bm{q}\,\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}})

(117)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-(\partial_{t}\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}+\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bnabla\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}+\bnabla\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}})\bcdot\bm{q}\,\mathrm{d}z.

The terms on the first line make no contributions to (
116
), bar boundary terms that vanish provided that the boundary conditions (
12
) and (
13
) hold. The terms on the second line vanish for all divergence-free
\bm{q}
provided that

\partial_{t}\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}+\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bnabla\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}+\bnabla\bm{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}\bcdot\bm{\upsilon}^{\mathrm{a}}=-\bnabla\hat{\pi}

(118)

for some scalar
\hat{\pi}
. We recognise (
118
) as version (
16
) of the Craik–Leibovich equation in vector form and with
\bm{u}^{\circ}=0
.

For
\delta\omega
, (
76
) gives

\displaystyle\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\iint\mathcal{N}\delta\omega\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}

\displaystyle=\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\iint\mathcal{N}(\partial_{t}r+\bnabla_{\bm{x}}r\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\omega-\bnabla_{\bm{k}}r\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}

\displaystyle=\iint\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\iint\Big(\partial_{t}(\mathcal{N}r)+\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\bcdot(\mathcal{N}r\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\omega)-\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\bcdot(\mathcal{N}r\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\omega)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-(\partial_{t}\mathcal{N}+\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\omega\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{N}-\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\omega\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{N})r\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}

(119)

Again, the terms in the first line are total derivatives and make no contribution to (
116
). The vanishing of the remaining terms for arbitrary
r
implies the wave action transport equation (
1
) with
\mathcal{N}^{\circ}=0
.

Appendix D
Constrained variation
\delta\omega

The variation
\delta\omega
in (
76
) arises from unconstrained the variation
\delta\theta(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},t)
via the change of dependent and independent variables

(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},\theta(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},t))\mapsto(\bm{x},\bm{k},\omega(\bm{x},\bm{k},t))

(120)

with

\bm{k}=\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},t)\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\theta(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},t)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\omega(\bm{x},\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},t),t)=-\partial_{t}\theta(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},t).

(121)

We define the function
r(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)
by

r(\bm{x},\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},t),t)=-\delta\theta(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},t).

(122)

Taking the variation at fixed
\bm{\alpha}
of the second equation in (
121
) and using (
122
) gives

\delta\omega=\partial_{t}r+\partial_{t}\bm{K}\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{k}}r-\delta\bm{K}\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\omega.

(123)

On the other hand, the variation and time derivative of the first of (
121
) give

\displaystyle\delta\bm{K}

\displaystyle=-\bnabla_{\bm{x}}r-\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{K}\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{k}}r,

(124a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{K}

\displaystyle=-\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\omega-\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{K}\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\omega,

(124b)

where the contractions are between
\bm{K}
and
\bnabla_{\bm{k}}
.
Introducing (
124
) into (
123
) and using that
\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{K}=\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\theta
is a symmetric tensor leads to expression (
76
) for
\delta\omega
.
