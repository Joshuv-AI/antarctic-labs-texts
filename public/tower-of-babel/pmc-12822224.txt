Exploring and explaining nurses’ resilience and developing strategies for its enhancement: an explanatory sequential mixed method study
Authors: Shiva Khaleghparast, Nahid Dehghan Nayeri, Fidan Shabani, Feridoun Noohi, Majid Maleki, Avisa Tabib, Mahmood Sheikh Fathollahi, Mehdi Panjehbashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe healthcare environment is considered a challenging and complex setting. Background

Nurses work in a complex and constantly changing work environment [1]. They face unpredictable work settings, heavy workloads, long shifts, traumatic events, crowding, lack of support, violence, and patients with acute trauma, illness, or injury [2]. Studies have shown that the work environment affects the psychosocial status and interpersonal relationships of nurses, as well as the quality of patient care [3]. The healthcare environment is considered a challenging and complex setting on a global scale. Excessive workload and lack of independence further complicate the issue [4]. Occupational stress can occur in all professions, but certain jobs are particularly vulnerable to work-related stress, and nursing is one of these professions [5]. If anxiety is not controlled, it can lead to long-term effects on the performance and job satisfaction of nurses. Implementing strategies to reduce nurses’ anxiety can help prevent its consequences [6].

Resilience is an essential skill that can assist clinical professionals in adapting to and recovering from physical and psychological trauma, as well as coping with work-related stress [7]. Resilience is the individual’s ability to maintain psychological balance in stressful conditions [8]. It is a multifaceted construct that regulates optimal individual performance [9] and is a dynamic process in which individuals demonstrate positive coping skills despite experiencing trauma or serious challenges [10]. Nurses with high resilience not only do not leave their jobs but also provide better care [11]. When nurses exhibit resilient behavior, they can become empowered to cope with work-related stress and prevent burnout [12]. The results of a study by Rivas and colleagues showed that nurses with higher average resilience scores coped better with emotional exhaustion [13]. Resilience is related to job satisfaction and individual satisfaction with their professional status. Resilience may be a combination of personal characteristics, organizational factors, and the environment in which the nurse works, as well as the interactions and reactions that facilitate coping with stressful events [14]. Creating and strengthening resilience in high-stress work environments is very important. Nurses are exposed to chronic systemic stressors in an environment that may not be able to provide long-term support for them [15].

Despite the significant attention given to the issue of resilience, knowledge about its contributing factors and mediators remains limited [16]. Most existing studies have concentrated on personal characteristics that may influence resilience, while far fewer investigations have examined external and workplace-related factors that shape nurses’ resilience [17]. Understanding the characteristics of nurses’ resilience can help them develop strategies to enhance their ability to cope with unbearable stress [18]. Therefore, the present study was conducted with the aim of exploring and explaining the resilience of nurses in order to provide strategies for strengthening their resilience. This study was carried out using a sequential explanatory mixed-methods approach, whereby quantitative data were first collected and analyzed to measure the level of resilience and identify related factors. Subsequently, to provide a deeper explanation of the quantitative findings and to understand the experiences and perspectives of nurses, a qualitative phase was conducted using semi-structured interviews. This approach allows for the quantitative results to be enriched with qualitative insights, leading to the extraction of practical solutions tailored to the work context of nurses.

Methods

The present study was a sequential explanatory mixed-methods study conducted since August to November 2023 at the Rajaie Cardiovascular Institute, one of the largest and most specialized cardiovascular centers in the Middle East. Cardiac care units are recognized as highly demanding and high-stress environment due to the critical condition of patients, need for continues monitoring and advanced interventions. These factors make cardiac nurses particularly vulnerable to emotional exhaustion and resilience challenges. Throughout the study, both quantitative and qualitative research approaches were utilized. The use of this method allows the researcher to pose new questions based on the initial quantitative results and to explain the specific outcomes obtained from the quantitative phase with the qualitative phase.

The researcher visited the wards of the institute. In the quantitative phase, the average resilience score of nurses was determined using the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale. This questionnaire consists of 25 items, and for each question, a five-point Likert scale ranging from not true at all to true nearly all of the time was used, scoring from zero (not true at all) to four (true nearly all of the time), resulting in a total score. The score range is from zero to 100, with higher scores reflecting greater resilience. The CD-RISC has been tested in the general population and clinical samples, and has good internal consistency and test–retest reliability [19]. In a study by Ghasempour et al. (2024), Cronbach’s alpha of the Persian version of this scale was calculated as 0.94 [20]. In the present study, the reliability of the scale was also calculated as Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87.

According to the study by LoGiudice and Bartos in 2021 in the United States [3], and considering the standard deviation of the resilience score of nurses based on the Brief Resiliency Coping Scale (BRCS) (σ = 2.3)and a population size of N = 860 (the total number of eligible nurses at the Rajaie Cardiovascular Institute), with a 95% confidence level (Z1−α/2=1.96) and a precision of d = 0.35, and using the following formula (Cochran’s sample size formula for estimating the mean in a finite population), the required sample size was calculated to be at least 140 nurses working at Rajaie Cardiovascular Institute.

Questionnaires were distributed among 149 nurses working at Rajaie Cardiovascular Institute based on the inclusion criteria and were collected after one day with prior coordination. The inclusion criteria included consent to participate in the study and being employed in one of the departments of Rajaie Cardiovascular Institute in the past year. Sampling at this stage was conducted using a stratified method, such that sampling in all departments was done randomly in proportion to the population of each department. After determining the sample size for each department, random sampling was performed in each department using a lottery method. The data were analyzed using SPSS software version 16. A significance level of 0.05 was considered in the tests. Quantitative results were used to identify nurses with the highest resilience scores, and in the qualitative method, these nurses were purposefully selected. The qualitative phase was conducted using conventional content analysis. The interviews began in an unstructured manner and then continued in a semi-structured format, such that nurses who had achieved the highest resilience scores were selected for interviews, and sampling continued until data saturation was reached, with interviews conducted with 10 nurses. Informed consent was obtained for participation in the research and audio recording during the interviews. A sample of the interview questions included: “Please share your experiences regarding your resilience?”, “When a problem or crisis arises, how do you usually solve it? (Please provide an example from your experience)”, “Who helps you with your problems?”, “How would you describe your lifestyle?”, “What recommendations do you have for improving nurses’ resilience?”, Can you explain your experiences in dealing with challenges?”, Please describe a real experience you have had when facing difficulties in your work environment.”

The entire interview was recorded with the coordination and permission of the participants. Duration of interviews was at least 30 min, and the maximum was 48 min. Immediately after each interview, the content was transcribed verbatim onto paper. For data analysis, qualitative content analysis with a conventional approach was used. Based on the participants’ responses, follow-up and probing questions were asked. The content analysis involved three stages: preliminary, organizing, and reporting the analysis process and results [21]. From the text of each interview, semantic units were extracted, followed by abstraction and coding. Then, similarities were considered.

To ensure the trustworthiness of the data, Guba and Lincoln’s criteria of credibility, confirmability, dependability, transferability and authenticity were used [22]. Credibility was supported by repeated engagement with data, dependability was ensured by detailed documentation of the research procedures, confirmability was achieved by maintaining an audit trail and minimizing researcher bias, transferability was ensured through detailed descriptions of the study context, and authenticity was achieved by representing diverse and genuine experiences of cardiac nurses.

Discrepancies and codes were placed under subcategories. Then, the subcategories were combined, and the main categories were extracted. The software MAXQDA version 10 was used for data analysis.

Results

Among the 149 participating nurses in the study, most nurses (83.9%) were female, and 94.6% had a bachelor’s degree, while 5.4% had a master’s degree. Out of 149 nurses,30.9% of the nurses were permanently employed. Most nurses (55.7%) were married, and 85.9% had no history of any illness. The largest number of studied nurses (57%) were from adult intensive care units and 77.9% of nurses had experience caring for a patient with COVID-19 (Table 1). The age range of the participating nurses was 23 to 50 years, and their work experience ranged from 1 to 25 years.

Positive Thinking: Participants emphasized the necessity of maintaining a positive outlook on issues, not wasting the present moment, and engaging in cognitive practices to enhance resilience. This category includes subcategories: Mindful living in the present moment, cognitive exercises to increase resilience, maintaining a positive outlook and directing the mind towards solutions.

Mindful living in the present moment

One participant stated, “I try to adapt myself to the existing conditions and move forward with the problem, albeit slowly. I tell myself that this problem will eventually end, and this moment belongs to me; if I lose it, it won’t come back. I try to maintain a positive view of issues. The essence of the problem is negative, and if we think negatively, its negative burden increases, so it’s better to think positively. The feeling I get from reaching an ideal and solving the problem is very enjoyable” (Participant 1).

Another participant said, “Thinking about the present moment gives me peace. For example, when I’m on shift, I only think about that moment, not about what will happen tomorrow or the night after” (Participant 5).

Cognitive exercises to increase resilience

In this regard, one participant mentioned, “To increase resilience, I believe we need to practice mentally. For example, we should be grateful to God that we are healthy. Health is very important, and we should always see the positive aspects. We need to push negative thoughts away from ourselves and look for the positive side in everything” (Participant 6).

Maintaining a positive outlook and directing the mind towards solutions

In this context, one participant stated, “To increase resilience, we should pay attention to the positive aspects of issues and always see the glass as half full. Whenever a problem arises, we should remind ourselves that the situation could have been worse” (Participant 7). Additionally, another participant said, “Sometimes, if you look at a simple negative issue, it can turn into a crisis. But if you look at it positively, the same issue can be resolved, and you can focus better on finding a solution” (Participant 4).

Healthy lifestyle

One common issue among all participants was having a healthy lifestyle. Most participants engaged in walking and had appropriate nutrition and recreation. Some participants had previously practiced professional sports.

This category includes subcategories: Participation in leisure and exercise activities, targeted nutrition and physical health, and allocating time for oneself.

Participation in leisure and exercise activities

One participant said, “I used to participate in professional sports and have been swimming, playing volleyball, and horseback riding since I was 12. Now, I try to walk every day and go to the gym once a week” (Participant 8).

Another participant stated, “I don’t do professional sports, but I walk for an hour and a half every day” (Participant 1).

Targeted nutrition and physical health

One participant mentioned, “I have a healthy diet and don’t eat fast food. When I’m tired, even if I have to work at night, I try to have a tea or coffee. These little things help maintain your spirit and energy” (Participant 2).

Allocating time for oneself

In this regard, another participant expressed, “Outside the hospital, to cope with difficulties, you need to have a good hobby and take time for yourself to be able to take you out of the hospital environment. It shouldn’t be that I only do daily tasks and hospital work. In every city, there are some cafes and some places to visit. Even if there’s nothing else, walking is an option. These things really help a person endure many things” (Participant 10).

One of the participants stated, “I shouldn’t pour my inner feelings out in the ward, but outside the hospital, activities like nature walks, watching TV, coloring, and taking care of plants enhance my resilience“(Participant 3).

Individual and social-organizational support: Participants emphasized the importance of having support from the organization and supervisors, as well as the necessity of discussing problems with colleagues, friends, family, and loved ones, considering this aspect effective in increasing resilience. This category includes subcategories: emotional support from family and friends, synergy and support from colleagues, and support from organizational leaders and managers.

Emotional support from family and friends

One participant said, “When a problem arises, I definitely seek help because no one is perfect and no one has everything figured out. I discuss my problem with close ones or friends, and when I consult with them, I can find solutions better and have more strength to cope with my issue” (Participant 6).

Synergy and support from colleagues

A participant stated, “Talking with colleagues and friends and sharing problems has an impact on resilience. But we need to learn that when we go out with our friend after the hospital, we shouldn’t talk about the hospital anymore. A friend is good enough that I can say my piece once, and they listen, and that’s it” (Participant 9).

Support from organizational leaders and managers

Another participant expressed, “Support for nurses to increase resilience is very important, and this should start from the university. Nurses often lack support and do not have job or financial backing. The nursing system is reprimanded more than any other and lacks support. It would be better to create a change and give more importance to nursing and provide support for nurses and nursing” (Participant 4).

One participant said, “When management values us and our voices are heard, it gives a sense of encouragement, hope, and strength to continue” (Participant 10).

Strengthening Cognitive Problem-Solving Skills: One of the strategies that participants suggested for increasing nurses’ resilience was enhancing problem-solving skills. This category includes subcategories: Skill in realistically assessing conditions, focusing on practical solutions instead of dwelling on the problem, and seeking help from specialized consultants.

Skill in realistically assessing conditions

One participant said, “I have learned to first stay calm and then find a solution to the problem. I shouldn’t drown in stress” (Participant 2).

Another participant stated, “For me, the most important way to be resilient is patience and thinking about solutions. You can’t make immediate decisions in a crisis” (Participant 9).

Focusing on practical solutions instead of dwelling on problems

One participant in this area said, “Every person should have 5 solutions for every problem, so that if one doesn’t work, they can use another solution. I resist and endure as much as I can. I think about solutions, I try to think and not rebel. I have completed a 4-year psychology course and obtained a degree in dealing with issues. This course has been a miracle in my life, and now problems have become much less significant for me.” (Participant 3).

Seeking help from specialized consultants

A participant said, “I recommend that nurses consult a qualified psychologist and talk through their feelings, as this helps them build better relationships with the patients and their companions.” (Participant 5).

Discussion

The quantitative results of the present study showed that most nurses (63.75%) had moderate to high resilience, while 36.25% of nurses had low to moderate resilience. There was no significant correlation between nurses’ resilience and their age, work experience, or number of children. Additionally, there was no significant relationship between resilience and demographic characteristics such as gender, marital status, education level, type of employment, medical history, department of service, or history of caring for COVID-19 patients. While the study by Alameddine and colleagues (2021) indicated a relationship between education level and resilience, showing that nurses with a master’s degree had higher resilience than those with a bachelor’s degree, and that women’s resilience was lower than men’s [23], these results are inconsistent with the findings of the present study. Furthermore, the results of the study by Manomenidis and colleagues showed that education level, anxiety, and the use of mental preparedness strategies were the main predictors of nurses’ resilience. Resilient nurses had higher education levels and lower anxiety, and they used mental preparedness strategies more before starting their shifts [24].

In the present study, no significant relationship was found between resilience and gender, while in the study by Afshar and colleagues, it was reported that the resilience of female nurses during the COVID-19 crisis was significantly lower than that of male nurses, and overall, the resilience of nurses was low [25]. In the study by Aqtam and colleagues, the resilience of female nurses was also found to be lower than that of males [26], which is inconsistent with the current study. The lack of significant differences in resilience between female and male nurses in the present study may be due to the small number of male nurses (16.1%) among the studied nurses.

Among the departments examined, the highest resilience was related to pediatric critical care units, although this difference was not significant. Resilience is associated with job satisfaction. Training in resilience can have a positive impact on the quality of patient care [14]. If nurses are not sufficiently resilient over time, it can lead to depression, physical complications, and negative outcomes for patients [15]. In a study conducted by Salari and colleagues in 2014, nurses identified their interest in nursing and spirituality in their work as important factors for coping with job challenges, stating that despite not having high salaries, they expect spiritual rewards from God [27].

Qualitative data analysis showed that resilience is shaped within the complex interaction of individual, interpersonal, and organizational factors, and these interactions play a decisive role in high-stress clinical environments. Four main categories were extracted as key elements in enhancing resilience: positive thinking, healthy lifestyle, individual and social-organizational support, and strengthening cognitive problem-solving skills. The results of the study by Chang and colleagues indicated that participants in the study, who were nurses in nursing homes, demonstrated their resilience by striving for positive adaptation to difficult situations using internal resources and external support [28]. In the present study, one of the subcategories of positive thinking referred to having a positive outlook and directing the mind towards solutions. The findings of the study by Stacey and colleagues in 2019 also support the hypothesis that resilience is related to individuals’ ability to respond positively to adversities [4]. Another subcategory of positive thinking in the present study was cognitive practice aimed at increasing resilience. In this context, in the theory of positive appraisal style of resilience, cognitive flexibility is also considered a general mechanism.

Resilience is conceptualized as it is assumed to allow individuals to flexibly adjust their emotional responses to potential stressors, thereby protecting them from the harmful effects of stress [1]. Participants in this study emphasized the role of healthy nutrition, exercise, and dedicating time for enjoyable activities. Foster et al. (2019) also stated in their study that self-care is related to an individual’s resilience, and sufficient self-knowledge leads to activities that support resilience-related behaviors, which include diet, sleep, exercise, and having a strong social network [29]. In the present study, participants referred to enhancing cognitive problem-solving skills and believed in focusing on practical solutions rather than dwelling on the problem. They felt that they should be able to find appropriate solutions to issues by strengthening their problem-solving skills. In this regard, Foster et al. in their review study noted that several authors of the articles they examined considered resilience to be a personal self-concept; meaning that resilient nurses were self-aware professionals who possessed a wide range of personal characteristics, including tenacity and creativity [29].

Participants in the current study highlighted the importance of support from the organization and supervisors, as well as the necessity of discussing problems with colleagues, friends, family, and close ones, considering this to be effective in increasing resilience. McCann et al. (2013) also stated that studies have shown that nurses utilize several positive coping strategies, including problem-focused coping, spending time outdoors, and receiving support from colleagues [30]. On the other hand, one of the dimensions of the present study is the emphasis by nurses on effective organizational support. Many participants felt that the attention and understanding of managers could play a significant role in enhancing their sense of worth and resilience. This finding reinforces the need to review human resource management policies in hospitals. Building resilience in nurses through formal education and social support is an important focus for nursing leaders in creating a healthy work environment and retaining the nursing workforce [31]. The results of the meta-analysis by Castillo-Gonzalez et al. indicated that a significant portion of nurses suffer from or are at risk of burnout syndrome, likely due to their low levels of resilience [32]. Previous studies have shown that nursing students and educators regularly encounter challenges and workplace adversities, making resilience an essential factor in adapting to these challenges. These studies also highlight that resilience has been understood in different ways, either as a personal trait or a dynamic process. In the context of nursing education, resilience is further supported by various protective factors that can lessen the negative impacts of stress on both students and educators [33].

Learning and improvement are essential, and educational programs are recommended to enhance them. These educational programs contribute to resilience through the improvement of positive and supportive professional relationships, fostering optimism, emotional insight, life balance, and spirituality [34].

This study had some limitations. The self-reported data in the quantitative section may have been influenced by response bias. In the qualitative section, the findings reflect the experiences of a limited group of nurses and may not be fully generalizable.

Conclusions

In the present study, most nurses had moderate to high resilience, and no significant relationship was observed between resilience and the demographic characteristics of the nurses. The qualitative results indicated that nurses’ resilience is shaped by the interaction between positive thinking, a healthy lifestyle, individual and social-organizational support, and the enhancement of cognitive problem-solving skills. Based on the findings of this study, recommendations for resilience enhancement among nurses may be considered at three levels: Individual level: positive thinking, healthy life style, cognitive coping and self-care practices to strengthen personal resilience resources. Organizational level: supportive leadership and workload management. Policy level: institutional strategies for resilience enhancement.
