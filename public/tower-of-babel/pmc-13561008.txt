Visual outcome in 5-year-old children operated for non-syndromic craniosynostosis
Authors: Evangelia Ntoula, Daniel Nowinski, Gerd E Holmstrom, Eva Larsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate visual outcomes in children operated for non-syndromic craniosynostosis at preschool age, in order to elucidate long-term ophthalmological effects and guide treatment and screening strategies.MethodsA cohort of 89 children (63 boys) with various types of non-syndromic craniosynostosis operated at Uppsala Craniofacial Centre between 2012 and 2019 and an age-matched control group of 33 healthy children underwent ophthalmological evaluations at age 5. High-contrast visual acuity (HCVA), crowding ratio, low-contrast visual acuity (LCVA), colour vision and stereo acuity were examined using standardised protocols. Statistical analyses comparing outcomes across craniosynostosis subtypes and between the study group and control group were conducted.ResultsDistance HCVA and LCVA were lower in children who underwent operation for unilateral coronal craniosynostosis, mainly due to the higher prevalence of strabismus and refractive error on the side contralateral to the fused suture. Amblyopia and absence of measurable stereoacuity were also more frequent in this subtype. Crowding was common in the craniosynostosis group. Near HCVA was generally lower in all craniosynostosis subtypes compared with controls.ConclusionLong-term visual outcomes assessed at preschool age varied in children operated for non-syndromic craniosynostosis. Unicoronal craniosynostosis had the greatest risk of visual impairment. Tailored screening is needed in order to address the ophthalmological challenges associated with different craniosynostosis subtypes.Keywords: Vision, Epidemiology, Child health (paediatrics)

Introduction

Craniosynostosis refers to the premature fusion of cranial sutures leading to compensatory cranial deformations and is categorised as single-suture or multiple-suture and as syndromic or non-syndromic. Non-syndromic craniosynostosis typically involves a single suture, without associated genetic syndromes or additional malformations. The most common types are sagittal craniosynostosis, followed by metopic, unilateral coronal and unilateral lambdoid craniosynostosis.1

Previous research has focused on addressing the ophthalmological sequelae in non-syndromic craniosynostosis as well as evaluating the effects of craniofacial surgery and reported higher rates of strabismus, astigmatism and anisometropia in unilateral coronal craniosynostosis.2–9 Furthermore, the risk of iatrogenic strabismus associated with fronto-orbital advancement and remodelling, the most common surgical approach for unicoronal craniosynostosis, has been acknowledged.10–14 Only a limited number of studies have attempted to address the impact of the different subtypes of non-syndromic craniosynostosis on more functional aspects of vision, and the battery of tests used for visual function assessment varies in the literature.15–17 The European Reference Network for Rare Craniofacial Anomalies and ENT Disorders (ERN CRANIO) clinical practice guidelines also recommend ophthalmological evaluation and vision screening as part of the long-term management of children with craniosynostosis, while acknowledging the limited evidence to support subtype-specific recommendations.18

In previous studies, we systematically analysed ophthalmic abnormalities in relation to the subtypes of non-syndromic craniosynostosis preoperatively and after craniofacial surgery and followed up the children in order to evaluate the longitudinal effects on refraction and strabismus at preschool age.8 9 These studies demonstrated subtype-specific patterns, with children with unilateral coronal craniosynostosis exhibiting the highest risk of anisometropia and strabismus. The present study is part of this longitudinal follow-up programme and focuses specifically on functional visual outcomes at preschool age, including best corrected high-contrast visual acuity (HCVA), low-contrast visual acuity (LCVA), crowding and stereopsis—outcomes that were not addressed in the earlier reports. By evaluating these functional measures at 5 years of age, this study aims to complement previous findings and provide clinically relevant evidence for long-term visual screening and follow-up strategies.

Materials and methods

89 children (63 boys) operated for various types of non-syndromic craniosynostosis at Uppsala Craniofacial Centre, Uppsala University Hospital, Sweden, between September 2012 and April 2019 were included and constituted the study group. For the purpose of this study, non-syndromic craniosynostosis was defined as single-suture craniosynostosis without evidence of a syndromic diagnosis based on multidisciplinary clinical, radiological, surgical and genetic assessments at the time of inclusion. Diagnosis was established using a standardised multidisciplinary protocol incorporating clinical evaluation, radiological assessment and genetic testing when indicated. All children underwent preoperative low-dose CT with three-dimensional cranial reconstruction, reviewed by the craniofacial surgical team to confirm the affected suture and exclude multi-suture involvement or radiological features suggestive of syndromic craniosynostosis. All children were examined by a clinical geneticist preoperatively. Screening for genetic mutations associated with craniosynostosis was routinely performed in all patients with coronal suture involvement, atypical or multi-suture craniosynostosis or clinical features suggestive of a syndromic condition. Only children meeting combined clinical, radiological and genetic criteria for isolated, non-syndromic, single-suture craniosynostosis were included in the study.

Ophthalmological examinations were performed prospectively at 5 years of age by experienced orthoptists and paediatric ophthalmologists as part of the routine multidisciplinary craniofacial follow-up at our centre. For 10 children, unable to attend the follow-up appointments, medical records regarding visual outcome at age 5 were retrieved from their local hospitals. An age-matched control group of 33 healthy children was recruited through nursery schools or referrals to the ophthalmological department for other reasons. The control group was examined by the same team, following the same protocol.

Ophthalmological examinations

HCVA was assessed with linear Lea Hyvärinen charts at both distance and near.19 Distance HCVA was tested monocularly and binocularly at 3 m and near HCVA binocularly at 40 cm. Values were noted in logMAR (logarithm of minimal angle of resolution) units and converted to Snellen decimal equivalents for clinical interpretability. All visual acuity assessments were conducted under best corrected conditions following spectacle prescription based on cycloplegic refraction and a period of refractive adaptation as part of routine clinical care.

Subnormal HCVA was defined as >0.1 logMAR (<0.8 Snellen decimal) both at distance and near, in line with normative visual acuity values and commonly used clinical thresholds for preschool-aged children in Sweden.20

Unilateral (monocular) amblyopia was defined as a difference of at least two logMAR lines in best corrected HCVA between the normal and the amblyopic eye, whereas bilateral amblyopia was defined as best corrected visual acuity >0.3 logMAR (<0.5 Snellen decimal) in the better eye.21 Amblyogenic factors included strabismus; anisometropia of astigmatism or spherical component; and bilateral ametropia.

Monocular HCVA using single Lea Hyvärinen optotypes was evaluated at 3 m, and a crowding ratio was calculated by dividing the single-optotype distance HCVA by the linear distance HCVA for each eye. Values >1.2 indicated crowding.22

Low-contrast linear visual acuity (LCVA) was measured binocularly and monocularly at 3 m using Lea Hyvärinen 2.5% low-contrast charts.

Colour vision was assessed binocularly and monocularly with Hardy Rand and Rittler pseudoisochromatic plates.

Stereopsis was tested at near (40 cm) with Lang 1 stereotest, a screening tool commonly used in preschool-aged children.23 A positive result was defined as correct identification of all test figures, while inability to identify any figure was considered absence of measurable stereopsis.

Funduscopy was conducted through dilated pupils.

Additional assessments included manifest or intermittent strabismus, evaluated with cover–uncover and alternate cover tests at distance and near, eye motility and refraction in cycloplegia. These results were reported in a previous study.9 In the present study, orthoptic and refractive findings are referred to only when relevant for the interpretation of functional visual outcomes. Hypermetropia was considered significant if ≥2.00 D, myopia ≥0.50 D, astigmatism and anisometropia (spherical component or astigmatism) if ≥1.00 D, in accordance with the definitions used in our previous study.9 The same definitions were applied here to ensure methodological consistency.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics analysis was performed using SPSS V.28 (IBM) and further analyses using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute). Lambdoid craniosynostosis was excluded due to the small number of children. Proportional odds logistic regression models were fitted for ordinal outcomes and exact logistic regression models for binary outcomes, adjusting for age, gender, refractive error and strabismus, as appropriate, to assess the effect of the type of craniosynostosis. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Due to the explanatory nature of the study and lack of adjustment for multiplicity, the p values should be interpreted as exploratory only.

Subnormal distance HCVA (>0.1 logMAR in one or both eyes) was present in 19/86 children (22%) with craniosynostosis and occurred most frequently in unicoronal craniosynostosis (8/13, 61.5%), compared with sagittal (8/56, 14.3%) and metopic craniosynostosis (2/14, 14.3%). In unicoronal craniosynostosis, reduced distance HCVA predominantly affected the left eye, which corresponded to the eye contralateral to the fused suture and was consistently associated with refractive error, frequently accompanied by strabismus. Amblyopia was observed exclusively in unicoronal craniosynostosis, affecting the contralateral eye in 4/13 children (30.7%). In sagittal craniosynostosis, most cases of subnormal distance HCVA were not associated with refractive error or strabismus, or other ophthalmological abnormalities. In these cases, increased crowding was frequently observed. In metopic craniosynostosis, reduced distance HCVA was associated with hypermetropia. In the control group, subnormal distance HCVA was observed in one child with bilateral hypermetropia.

Near visual acuity

Mean/median binocular near HCVA was 0.84/0.10 logMAR in the craniosynostosis cohort and 0.16/0.00 logMAR in controls (table 2). Subnormal near visual acuity (>0.1 logMAR) was observed in 5/53 children (9.4%) with sagittal craniosynostosis and 3/11 children (27.2%) with unicoronal craniosynostosis. Near visual acuity was normal in all examined children with metopic craniosynostosis and in all controls. In sagittal craniosynostosis, most children with subnormal near visual acuity had no identifiable refractive or strabismic cause.

Crowding

Crowding was common across all craniosynostosis subtypes (table 2). A crowding ratio >1.2 was observed in 32/45 children (71.1%) with sagittal craniosynostosis, without consistent association with strabismus or refractive error, and in 9/11 children (81.8%) with metopic craniosynostosis, occasionally in association with hypermetropia and/or astigmatism. In unicoronal craniosynostosis, a crowding ratio >1.2 was observed in 4/5 children (80%) and was frequently associated with refractive errors and/or strabismus. In the control group, a crowding ratio >1.2 was observed in 9/29 children (31%), typically without associated ophthalmic pathology or refractive errors (only one child had hypermetropia+2.75 D).

Low-contrast visual acuity

Mean/median binocular LCVA was 0.31/0.40 logMAR in the craniosynostosis group and 0.36/0.40 logMAR in the control group (table 3). Subnormal binocular LCVA (>0.4 logMAR) was observed across all craniosynostosis subtypes and was most frequent in unicoronal craniosynostosis (table 3). Subnormal binocular LCVA was present in 2/5 children (40%) with unicoronal craniosynostosis, compared with 10/41 children (24.4%) with sagittal craniosynostosis and 4/10 children (40%) with metopic craniosynostosis. In contrast, subnormal binocular LCVA was observed in 3/29 control children (10.3%).

Stereopsis

Impaired stereopsis was most frequently observed in unicoronal craniosynostosis, where 7/14 children (50%) lacked measurable stereoacuity. In all cases, impaired stereopsis was associated with manifest strabismus. Absence of measurable stereoacuity was not observed in sagittal or metopic craniosynostosis, nor in controls.

Colour vision

Colour vision testing was normal in all examined children across craniosynostosis subtypes and controls with the exception of one child with sagittal craniosynostosis who demonstrated mild red-green deficiency.

Funduscopy

Funduscopic examination revealed no structural abnormalities in any child, and no signs of optic nerve pathology or papilloedema were observed. However, three children with sagittal synostosis had a history of elevated intracranial pressure. Papilloedema had been detected during routine ophthalmological follow-up at approximately 3 years of age, and raised intracranial pressure was subsequently confirmed, requiring secondary craniofacial surgery. Secondary coronal synostosis had been confirmed in two of these cases.

Statistical comparison of ophthalmological outcomes between the subtypes of craniosynostosis

When adjusted for gender, significant differences were observed in the left eye for distance HCVA and LCVA (p=0.01 and p=0.009), driven by lower values in the unicoronal group (tables 2 and 3). In this subgroup, the left eye was contralateral to the fused suture in most cases (12/14), indicating that the observed differences predominantly reflect contralateral eye involvement. These differences were no longer statistically significant after adjustment for strabismus and refractive error. Crowding and prevalence of crowding ratio >1.2 did not differ statistically between subtypes.

Statistical comparison between the craniosynostosis and the control groups

When adjusted for gender, eye-level analyses demonstrated statistically significant differences between the craniosynostosis and control groups for distance HCVA and LCVA in left-eye models (p=0.01 and p=0.003), driven by the lower values in unilateral craniosynostosis (tables 2 and 3). The prevalence of subnormal HCVA and subnormal LCVA was also higher in the left-eye analyses of the craniosynostosis group compared with controls (p=0.005 and p=0.014). However, these differences were no longer statistically significant after adjustment for strabismus and refractive error. Binocular near visual acuity differed significantly between the controls and all craniosynostosis subtypes (p<0.01, table 2). Crowding was more common in the craniosynostosis group (table 2). In eye-level analyses, statistically significant differences were observed in left-eye models between sagittal craniosynostosis and controls (p=0.03). The prevalence of crowding ratio >1.2 differed between the craniosynostosis and control groups in both right-eye and left-eye analyses (p=0.002 and p=0.0001, respectively). Although children in the control group were slightly older than those in the craniosynostosis group (p<0.001), analyses adjusting for age did not indicate age as a confounding factor for visual outcomes.

Discussion

In this cohort of children operated for non-syndromic craniosynostosis and examined at 5 years of age, functional visual outcomes varied according to craniosynostosis subtype. Children with unicoronal craniosynostosis demonstrated poorer outcomes, including lower distance HCVA and LCVA, higher rates of amblyopia and absence of measurable stereopsis. Crowding was frequently observed across all subtypes, while near HCVA was generally lower in children with craniosynostosis compared with controls, irrespective of subtype.

Visual acuity represents fine (central) vision and is a fundamental measure of visual function, analogous to the maturation of the visual and central nervous system. Visual system maturation depends on the anatomical integrity of the visual pathways, adequate visual stimulation, interaction with the environment and the child’s cognitive and neuropsychomotor development, with full maturation typically reached by approximately 10 years of age.24 Craniosynostosis may interfere with visual development, particularly in syndromic cases.25 In non-syndromic craniosynostosis, the presence and severity of visual impairment appear to correlate with craniosynostosis subtype.17 Previous research has focused on refractive errors and strabismus as risk factors for reduced vision, while few studies have investigated functional visual outcomes. Vasco et al15 and Ricci et al16 assessed ocular movements, fixation behaviour, visual fields and visual attention in children with various craniosynostosis subtypes, but follow-up was limited to the first postoperative year. Similarly, Chieffo et al26 investigated neuro-ophthalmological and neurovisual deficits up to 1 year after surgery.

The current study extends the literature by evaluating visual function at preschool age when the cooperation and testability improve and more reliable assessments across multiple visual domains become feasible. Assessments of distance and near HCVA, LCVA, colour vision and stereopsis provide novel insight into long-term functional visual outcomes in children operated for non-syndromic craniosynostosis.

In this study, the overall visual outcomes in the craniosynostosis group were good. However, distance HCVA was reduced in children with unicoronal craniosynostosis, particularly on the side contralateral to the fused suture. Statistical analysis confirmed the negative effect of refractive errors and strabismus on visual acuity indicating amblyogenic mechanisms as primary contributors. The prevalence of amblyopia in children with unicoronal craniosynostosis in this study was comparable to previous reports.3 7 In contrast to other studies,27 28 no cases of amblyopia were identified in the metopic craniosynostosis group despite higher rates of hypermetropia reported previously in this subtype.9 Differences in age at assessment and early spectacle correction in our cohort may partly explain these discrepancies.

Binocular near visual acuity was generally lower in children with craniosynostosis compared with controls. Interestingly, most of these children did not present with refractive errors or strabismus. The underlying mechanism for reduced near visual acuity therefore remains unclear. Reduced accommodation may be a contributing factor; however, this aspect was not evaluated in the present study.

Crowding, defined as difficulty in identifying closely spaced optotypes, is a recognised developmental phenomenon in normally sighted young children.29 A crowding ratio of 1.2 has been reported in healthy children 5–7 years of age.22 30 Crowding is also a characteristic feature in amblyopic eyes31 and has been associated with lesions in white matter and dysfunction in the dorsal visual stream.32 In the present study, a crowding ratio >1.2 was more frequent in the craniosynostosis group including cases without amblyopia. Current evidence suggests that single-suture craniosynostosis may negatively affect visuospatial processing at preschool age; however, data on subtype-specific differences and long-term persistence of these effects remain limited.33

LCVA assesses the ability to discern fine details at reduced contrast levels and may reveal subtle visual dysfunction in amblyopia and neuro-ophthalmological disorders.34 To our knowledge, LCVA has not been systematically evaluated in children with craniosynostosis previously. In this study, LCVA was generally good; however, children with unicoronal synostosis had lower values and higher prevalence of subnormal LCVA compared with the other subtypes of craniosynostosis and the controls. The association with refractive errors and strabismus suggests amblyogenic mechanisms rather than optic neuropathy or primary neuro-ophthalmological pathology as the main contributors.

Stereopsis, a critical component of binocular visual function, was largely preserved in our study group. Nonetheless, a significant number of children in the unicoronal synostosis group lacked measurable stereopsis consistently in association with strabismus. These findings highlight the importance of early detection and management of strabismus in order to optimise binocular visual development in this subgroup.

Colour vision was generally unaffected. One child with sagittal synostosis presented with red-green colour vision deficiency, consistent with prevalence rates reported in the general paediatric population.35

The strengths of this study include its prospective design, a well-defined cohort of children with various types of non-syndromic craniosynostosis managed at a single centre and standardised ophthalmological assessments conducted by experienced orthoptists and paediatric ophthalmologists. Additionally, the study extended beyond conventional visual acuity measures by incorporating functional aspects of vision that have not been reported in this population previously. The inclusion of an age-matched control group enabled meaningful comparisons, both between craniosynostosis subtypes but also between this population and normally developing peers.

Limitations include the relatively small number of participants in some craniosynostosis subgroups. This, however, reflects the natural distribution of craniosynostosis subtypes in the general population where sagittal synostosis accounts for almost half of all cases. Furthermore, only surgically treated children with single-suture craniosynostosis were included. Consequently, the findings may not be generalisable to children with milder forms of single-suture craniosynostosis managed conservatively. The control group was also relatively small, and some controls were enrolled through the ophthalmological department. To mitigate potential bias, our findings were interpreted in the context of previous population-based studies on visual outcomes in children of similar age.20 36

In conclusion, this study provides new insights into functional visual outcomes at preschool age in children operated for non-syndromic craniosynostosis. Visual function differed across non-syndromic craniosynostosis subtypes, with children with unicoronal craniosynostosis demonstrating the greater risk of visual impairment. Together with our previous findings, these results support continued ophthalmological follow-up in all children with craniosynostosis, with particular attention to those with unicoronal synostosis, and contribute to the evidence base needed to inform subtype-specific screening and follow-up strategies. In this context, our results may help address the current lack of subtype-specific ophthalmological recommendations highlighted in existing ERN CRANIO guidance.
