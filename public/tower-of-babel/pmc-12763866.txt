From multi-omics to deep learning: advances in cfDNA-based liquid biopsy for multi-cancer screening
Authors: Xinwei Luo, Sijia Xie, Feitong Hong, Xiaolong Li, Yijie Wei, Yuwei Zhou, Wei Su, Yuhe Yang, Lixia Tang, Fuying Dao, Peiling Cai, Hao Lin, Hongyan Lai, Hao Lyu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with early detection being critical for improving survival rates. Traditional diagnostic methods, such as tissue biopsies and imaging, face limitations in invasiveness, cost, and accessibility, making liquid biopsy a compelling non-invasive alternative. Among liquid biopsy approaches, circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) analysis has gained prominence for its ability to capture tumor-derived genetic and epigenetic alterations. This review summarizes key cfDNA biomarkers, including gene mutations, copy number variations (CNVs), DNA methylation, fragmentation patterns, and end motifs (EMs), and highlights their utility in cancer detection and monitoring. By integrating these multi-modal cfDNA biomarkers, feature fusion approaches have not only enhanced the performance of cancer classification models but also stabilized low-abundance signals, thus ensuring more reliable cancer detection and monitoring. Furthermore, the diagnostic power of cfDNA analysis has been further amplified by machine learning (ML), with both traditional ML and deep learning (DL) methods demonstrating strong predictive performance in routine clinical liquid biopsy applications. However, challenges remain, including tumor heterogeneity, standardization of data processing, model explainability, and cost constraints. Future advancements should focus on refining multi-modal feature integration, developing explainable AI (XAI) models, and optimizing cost-effective strategies to enhance clinical applicability. As computational methodologies advance, the integration of cfDNA biomarkers with ML frameworks holds great promise to reshape non-invasive cancer detection by enabling earlier diagnostics, more accurate prognostic evaluation and personalized treatment strategies.

Background

Cancer is one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, with delayed diagnosis and tumor heterogeneity being key contributing factors [1–3]. Unfortunately, there remains a substantial gap in scalable, early diagnosis of malignant tumors [4]. Common tissue biopsy-based diagnostic strategies are hindered by their inability to fully capture tumor heterogeneity, leading to challenges in monitoring tumor evolution [5]. Moreover, surgery or tissue biopsy can have a negative impact on patients’ quality of life and survival [6]. Therefore, the development of non-invasive early detection methods capable of extracting comprehensive tumor characteristic information is crucial for advancing cancer treatment and improving patient outcomes [7].

To this end, the early cancer detection based on blood and circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) offers hope in narrowing the gap in cancer screening among underserved populations [8]. cfDNA refers to short DNA fragments released into the bloodstream, primarily originating from apoptotic or necrotic cells. In healthy individuals, cfDNA mainly derives from normal cell turnover, such as white blood cells, endothelial cells, and hepatocytes. However, in cancer patients, a portion of cfDNA—known as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)—originates from tumor cells, carrying tumor-specific genetic and epigenetic alterations. This tumor-derived cfDNA provides a valuable molecular window into cancer biology [7, 9]. As early in 1948, cfDNA molecules were described, with most originating from deceased white blood cells, vascular endothelial cells, and hepatocytes [10, 11]. With the advancement of cfDNA characterization technologies, including deep sequencing techniques such as cancer personalized profiling by deep sequencing (CAPP-Seq) and tagged-amplicon deep sequencing (Tam-Seq), blood-based tests have proven feasible and clinically practical for guiding the management of cancer patients [12–15]. For instance, in the early 2010s, methods such as non-invasive prenatal diagnosis (NIPD) were leveraged to detect maternal cancers through cfDNA, highlighting the potential of cfDNA for detecting early asymptomatic cancers [16]. Moreover, recent studies on advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) have led to the development of a cfDNA-based diagnostic approach, which has been incorporated into clinical guidelines for lung cancer [17–19].

Despite the proven ability to detect cancer signals via blood tests in asymptomatic/pre-symptomatic individuals, significant challenges persist. How to enhance the detection sensitivity for precancerous lesions and early-stage cancers? Is it possible to perform these tests at an affordable price to enable broad access? Is the evidence of cancer derived from cfDNA beneficial for cancer patients? Recently, the rise of artificial intelligence has propelled cfDNA-based cancer detection with its superior performance, showing great potential in improving diagnostic outcomes [20–23]. Therefore, this review will integrate state-of-the-art artificial intelligence methods to systematically assess the current advantages and limitations of cfDNA features (genomic, epigenomic, and fragmentomic) in cancer detection. Biomarkers in cfDNA-based cancer liquid biopsies

Definitions and current landscape of key biomarkers

The detection of tumor specific gene mutations in cfDNA has emerged as a transformative, noninvasive strategy for cancer diagnosis, therapeutic monitoring, and prognostic assessment [24]. Tumor derived cfDNA enters the circulation through apoptosis, necrosis, and active secretion, and it carries somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions and deletions (indels), and structural rearrangements that mirror the tumor genome [25]. With next-generation sequencing (NGS), actionable mutations have been repeatedly identified across cancers, including EGFR, TP53, and KRAS in NSCLC when tissue is unavailable, KRAS and NRAS in colorectal cancer (CRC) to guide anti-EGFR therapy [26], PIK3CA and ESR1 in breast cancer associated with endocrine resistance [27], and BRAF in melanoma for targeted treatment [28]. Longitudinal changes in ctDNA levels during therapy correlate with tumor burden and treatment efficacy, enabling dynamic disease assessment [29, 30].

Copy number variations (CNVs) are arm-level or focal gains and losses that index chromosomal instability and tumor evolution. Early applications of CNV detection in cfDNA relied on low-pass whole genome sequencing (lpWGS), which offers a cost-effective approach for identifying CNVs in plasma cfDNA. Li et al. [31] examined cfDNA CNV profiles in CRC patients and identified recurrent amplifications in chromosomal regions such as 1q, 8q, and 5q, along with deletions in 1p, 4q, 8p, 17p, 18q, and 22q. These alterations were also observed in tumor tissues, reinforcing the feasibility of using cfDNA CNV signatures as a liquid biopsy proxy for tumor genomic content. Notably, several studies have highlighted that using arm-level CNV alterations, rather than focal events, can yield more reproducible and predictive results, especially under lpWGS conditions [32]. Moreover, recent developments in ultra-low coverage sequencing strategies, with depths below 0.1×, have been coupled with probabilistic modeling approaches to enable CNV detection in resource constrained settings. These emerging methods reduce sequencing costs substantially while maintaining acceptable analytical performance, thereby offering scalability for population wide screening programs [33].

DNA methylation in cfDNA refers to the covalent addition of a methyl group to cytosine at CpG dinucleotides, with cancer associated patterns that typically include promoter hypermethylation and genome-wide hypomethylation [34, 35]. In liquid biopsy workflows, methylation is assayed using whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) or targeted bisulfite and enzymatic panels, followed by methylation calling and identification of differentially methylated regions (DMRs) [36]. These assay formats have been adapted to plasma, including low-input protocols suitable for screening [37], highlighting the potential of cfDNA methylation as a broadly applicable early screening biomarker.

cfDNA fragmentation patterns are quantified at two complementary levels: fragment size distribution (FSD) and genome-wide coverage oscillations, which together capture signals from apoptotic cleavage, nuclease activity, and nucleosome organization [38]. In standard plasma workflows, shallow or lpWGS is performed, read pairs are aligned and de-duplicated (with UMI handling when available), insert sizes are used to compute size histograms and short-to-long ratios, and coverage is aggregated in fixed genomic bins and around regulatory landmarks to estimate nucleosome linked footprints [38]. These signals are recoverable from shallow data and remain informative at low tumor fractions, indicating clear potential for early detection and longitudinal monitoring.

Complementing size and coverage based fragmentomics, end motifs (EMs) are the nucleotide sequences immediately adjacent to the 5’ and 3’ termini of plasma cfDNA fragments and encode enzymatic cleavage preferences and local chromatin context [39]. In standard workflows, adapters are trimmed, paired end reads are aligned, and fragment coordinates are reconstructed; orientation specific k-mers are extracted at both ends, then normalized for GC content, background dinucleotide composition, and library depth to generate motif enrichment spectra or discordance scores versus hematopoietic reference profiles [39]. Motif features can be computed around nucleosome dyads and regulatory landmarks to assess concordance with nucleosome positioning, and quality control evaluates end repair bias, overhang handling, strand-aware duplication, and batch effects, all of which can influence terminal sequence composition [40]. cfDNA biomarker axes in cancer detection: diagnostic strengths and practical limitations

Gene mutation: mechanistic, time-resolved, and patient-specific insights into tumor biology

In cfDNA-based testing, cfDNA mutation profiles confer several distinct advantages for cancer detection and clinical management. First, mutations provide variant level, mechanistically explainable signals that map directly to oncogenic pathways and targeted therapies, offering clear clinical actionability and having been repeatedly validated across multiple tumor types and loci noted [22, 26–28]. Second, mutations enable time resolved disease assessment: quantitative changes in ctDNA variants during therapy correlate with tumor burden and therapeutic efficacy, and the appearance of specific resistance mutations allows early identification of molecular resistance, thereby supporting response-adaptive decisions before radiographic progression [29, 30, 65]. Third, mutation calls function as patient specific genomic fingerprints, permitting molecular confirmation of recurrence or progression when tissue is unavailable and supporting consistent longitudinal surveillance. Together, these features make mutation analysis a precise, decision ready readout that complements imaging and routine clinical indicators.

Gene mutations also have limitations, chiefly in two respects: very low ctDNA fractions in early-stage disease restrict the detection of rare variants, and clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) may introduce non-tumor variants that must be distinguished to preserve specificity [8, 66]. With advances in ultra deep sequencing, digital PCR, and ML assisted error suppression, sensitivity and specificity have improved, and artifacts related to CHIP and sequencing noise have been reduced [25, 41].

CNVs: depth-efficient chromosomal-instability and clonal-architecture signatures for cancer classification

As a genome dosage readout derived from plasma cfDNA, CNVs retain high information content at low sequencing depth, enabling population scale workflows. By tracking chromosomal instability and macro-clonal evolution (e.g., whole arm aneuploidies and large clonal shifts), CNVs capture tumor biological features that elude point mutation assays and are less vulnerable to locus dropout [12]. When mutational biomarkers are sparse or confounded, arm-level CNV burden and topology provide an independent readout of disease activity and, through longitudinal trajectories of plasma genomic abnormality (PGA) and tumor fraction, convey prognosis, therapeutic response, and progression dynamics [67]. For example, higher PGA is associated with shorter progression free survival in advanced CRC, and tumor fraction estimates track treatment response in breast and lung cancers [31]. What’s more, CNV analysis has increasingly been integrated into multi-cancer early detection (MCED) frameworks. For instance, Hallermayr et al. [68] developed a ML-based classifier that integrates genome-wide CNVs, fragmentation profiles, and chromatin signals, demonstrating high sensitivity in detecting CRC. Similarly, Qiang et al. [46] investigated CNV-based risk assessment in plasma cell myeloma and found that gains in chromosomes 1q, 9, and 15 were frequently detected in patients but absent in healthy controls. Taken together, CNVs have diagnostic utility and also serve as prognostic and pharmacodynamic indicators, establishing a stable, scalable cfDNA signal axis suited to constrained sequencing resources and real world deployment.

Key limitations include subclonal or subtle CNV events in early-stage cancers, which can attenuate sensitivity, and pipeline heterogeneity across segmentation methods that complicates cross-study comparability [69]. Under low coverage, arm-level summaries tend to be more reproducible than focal calls [12]. Ultra-low coverage approaches reduce sequencing cost but rely on appropriate probabilistic models and calibration to maintain analytical utility.

Dna methylation: early, tissue-informed regulatory signatures at low tumor fraction

Compared with genome-dosage readouts, cfDNA methylation provides a regulatory and cell identity signal that arises early in tumorigenesis and is strongly tissue informed. Because aberrant methylation occurs in regional CpG blocks and tracks cell type specific chromatin states, it yields a high density signal that remains detectable even when tumor fraction is low, thereby improving sensitivity and enabling tissue of origin (TOO) inference in screening and surveillance settings [70, 71]. These properties have translated into robust performance across indications and assay scales: CRC specific markers trained on tissue blood contrasts achieved an AUC of 0.96 [35], surpassing typical single marker panels and comparing favorably to mutation centric assays at comparable depth. A methylation classifier for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) reached 76.2% sensitivity and 94.1% specificity [34], exceeding prior blood-based reports in similar cohorts while maintaining high specificity, and the low-input THUNDER approach reported high performance across six cancers (colorectal, esophageal, liver, lung, ovarian, pancreatic) [37]. WGBS has also been applied in early hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) to nominate informative DMRs for plasma testing [36]. Methodologically, methylation features are amenable to windowed aggregation and regional DMR modeling, which stabilize estimates at shallow depth and facilitate platform agnostic pipelines suited to high throughput screening [72].

Balanced against these strengths, several sources of variability can constrain generalizability and deployment. Explanation can be affected by background blood cell composition and cohort differences, some markers show limited cross cancer generalizability or subtype specificity, and protocol variability introduces batch effects that require standardized preprocessing and reference panels. Models trained in restricted populations may under generalize without external validation [73, 74].

Fragmentation pattern: low-depth chromatin footprints for early detection and longitudinal monitoring

Fragmentation features are highly informative at shallow depth, making them well suited to screening and longitudinal surveillance when sequencing budgets are constrained. Size-based metrics, particularly short fragment enrichment and fragment size ratios (FSRs) relative to the 166 bp peak, separate malignant from benign states and have demonstrated utility in early disease (e.g., early breast cancer) [38, 61]. Coverage-based signals linked to nucleosome positioning—including promoter or enhancer footprints and genome-wide periodicity—reflect altered chromatin accessibility and reliably distinguish cancer from non-cancer across cohorts [75]. These indices often remain measurable when tumor fraction is low and can support time resolved assessments of disease activity. In practice, fragmentation driven models have achieved strong performance across indications, including early esophageal cancer using stacked frameworks that incorporate nucleosome positioning [62], early CRC and advanced adenomas with high accuracy in integrated fragmentomics models [63]. For gynecologic screening, Liu et al. [64] reported a fragmentomics-based classifier for uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) (AUC 0.994; sensitivity 97.8%), exceeding the other single feature baselines within the same study and comparing favorably with mutation centric liquid biopsy workflows in comparable settings.

However, reproducibility is sensitive to pre-analytical handling, library and platform differences, and biological variability in cfDNA release dynamics. Consequently, standardized protocols, calibrated computational pipelines, and external validation are required to ensure generalizability [76].

EMs: cut-site sequence signatures that enrich ctDNA and improve cancer classification

EMs provide a sequence resolved view of the cfDNA cut site, capturing nuclease preferences and local chromatin state at the moment of cleavage—biological information that fragment size or coverage alone does not encode [77]. Their terminal k-mer patterns track active chromatin remodeling and nucleosome phasing, and concordance with marks such as H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) supports mechanistic explainability and can inform TOO inference [78]. Analytically, leveraging the discordance between tumor derived termini and hematopoietic nucleosome positioning enables motif guided fragment selection to enrich ctDNA at low tumor fractions, delivering a depth efficient gain in signal to noise [39]. Evidence from representative studies indicates that incorporating EM features outperforms size only or coverage only baselines at matched specificity—for example, one multi-cancer assay achieved an AUC of 0.983 with 95.5% sensitivity and 95.0% specificity in the same study, outperforming other single feature baselines [79]. This finding was also validated in a separate stage-I lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) study using EM profiling [8]. Together, these observations position EMs as a biologically grounded, sequence level axis that strengthens early detection while illuminating tumor chromatin biology at nucleotide resolution—without requiring deeper sequencing.

Turning this promise into dependable practice requires hardening assays against technical and biological drift. End-motif readouts are sensitive to library preparation and end repair biases, platform dependent sequence artifacts, and biological variability in nucleosome occupancy and nuclease activity. Robust, strand aware normalization, batch correction, and standardized reporting are needed to ensure cross study comparability and generalizability [39].

Multi-modal cfDNA feature fusion: complementary biological axes for robust cancer classification

The integration of multiple cfDNA features has emerged as a transformative approach for improving the accuracy of cancer detection through liquid biopsy. Traditional single feature analyses, such as somatic mutations, DNA methylation, or fragmentation signatures alone, often struggle with sensitivity and specificity, particularly in the early detection of cancer. Recent advances in feature fusion have demonstrated significant improvements in diagnostic performance by leveraging multiple cfDNA biomarkers simultaneously [80, 81].

Integrating cfDNA features across genetic, dosage, and chromatin and regulatory layers yields a composite view of tumor biology that no single modality can provide. Mutations encode variant level mechanism, CNVs capture chromosomal instability and clonal architecture, methylation reports cell identity and regulatory state, while fragmentation patterns and EMs register nucleosome organization and cut-site biochemistry at nucleotide resolution [76]. Fusing these orthogonal signals improves detection at low tumor fractions, mitigates single modality blind spots (e.g., locus dropout for rare variants, CHIP related noise), and strengthens TOO inference by reconciling sequence, structure, and epigenetic context within the same plasma sample [81].

Empirically, multi-modal feature fusion consistently outperforms single feature baselines at matched specificity across diseases and assay scales. In a pan-cancer setting, combining EM, methylation, and CNV features delivered cancer detection sensitivity of 72.4% at 97.0% diagnostic specificity, exceeding single modality counterparts under identical conditions [32]. In esophageal cancer, augmenting 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) with EM patterns significantly improved cancer detection accuracy relative to either modality alone, reflecting the complementarity of regulatory and cut-site signals [82]. Beyond detection, integrating methylation, fragmentation, and CNV features enabled TOO classification with AUC 0.966 across seven tumor types, indicating that regulatory identity, chromatin organization, and genome dosage jointly constrain the classification space [46]. Within fragmentomics, models that jointly use fragment size, EMs, and nucleosome positioning achieved AUC 0.990 for early CRC and advanced adenomas, surpassing mutation centric assays evaluated in the same context and demonstrating convergent chromatin proximal readouts at shallow depth [63]. Consistent gains were also seen in ESCC, where an expanded multi-modal analysis (EMMA) increased cancer detection AUC from 0.90 to 0.99 while maintaining specificity above 95% [23].

Taken together, multi-modal fusion reframes plasma cfDNA from a set of isolated biomarkers into an integrated biological portrait that links oncogenic mechanism through mutations, genome architecture through CNVs, and regulatory and chromatin state through methylation, fragmentation, and EMs. This joint view delivers higher sensitivity in early disease, more reliable TOO assignment, and more stable longitudinal readouts than any single modality, paving the way for clinically robust and scalable cancer classification.

DL and traditional ML frameworks for cfDNA-based early cancer detection

Convolutional neural networks extract local cfDNA signatures for low-depth early cancer detection

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are deep models built from locally connected filters with weight sharing and nonlinear activations; by sliding learned kernels across structured inputs, they extract position tolerant local features and hierarchically aggregate them into discriminative representations. This inductive bias makes CNNs especially suitable for genomics and epigenomics, where signals are organized along genomic coordinates or short windows of sequence context, and where local patterns carry biological meaning [83].

In cfDNA analytics, coverage oscillations, fragment-size distributions, and terminal sequence contexts can be rendered as one-dimensional tracks or two-dimensional images, allowing CNNs to learn cancer related signatures directly from shallow sequencing without heavy manual feature engineering. Authoritative overviews of DL for cancer epigenetics emphasize CNNs’ strength in capturing local patterns from methylation and sequencing-based assays, supporting their use as core architectures for blood-based detection tasks [84, 85].

Applied to plasma cfDNA, CNNs have delivered consistent gains under early-detection constraints. In lung cancer, a CNN trained on a two-dimensional “methylation by fragment size” representation improved detection over single feature baselines reported in the same study and reached 70.0% sensitivity at 98% specificity overall, with 87.5% sensitivity for squamous cell carcinoma and a limit of detection down to a 0.1% tumor fraction—evidence that learning the joint methylation size structure can recover weak signals at low burden [84]. Beyond targeted panels, CNNs have been used directly on genome-wide fragmentomics: for copy number maps indexed in 1-Mb bins and for fragment size coverage sequences, convolutional blocks capture spatial hierarchies characteristic of CNVs and size-linked footprints, providing a principled way to mine multi-scale cfDNA structure from lpWGS [86]. At finer resolution, encoding per-CpG cleavage profiles as 2-D matrices and training a CNN to infer methylation status showed that adding cfDNA cleavage patterns markedly improved single-CpG classification compared with sequence context alone (AUC 0.93 vs 0.72), and CG containing EM features further enabled high discrimination of HCC from non-cancer (AUC 0.98), underscoring the ability of CNNs to capture cut-site coupled biology that traditional features miss [87]. These examples illustrate a common theme: by operating on structured cfDNA representations, CNNs learn local chromatin linked patterns and aggregate them hierarchically, yielding sensitivity at constrained depth while remaining compatible with standardized preprocessing and cross-cohort generalization.

CNNs turn heterogeneous cfDNA signals into diagnostic readouts that stay sensitive at shallow depth and generalize across cohorts. Reliable use rests on three essentials. First, consistent preprocessing that removes end repair artifacts and harmonizes inputs through strand aware trimming, UMI deduplication, and GC and coverage normalization with patient level binning. Second, architectures matched to biological scales, with kernels and pooling tuned to nucleosome periodicity, receptive fields extended by residual or dilated blocks, and training stabilized by batch normalization and dropout. Third, rigorous evaluation with patient wise splits, external validation, class imbalance handling, probability calibration, and straightforward explainability checks such as ablations and saliency over regulatory regions [84]. With these practices in place, CNN pipelines deliver reproducible screening and surveillance at low sequencing cost and remain strong defaults for fragmentomics, EM modeling, and low-pass CNV analysis.

Transformers capture long-range cfDNA patterns for early cancer detection

Transformers are attention-based deep networks that treat inputs as sequences of tokens and learn global dependencies through self-attention. Each token attends to every other token, producing context aware representations. Multi-head attention captures different relation types, positional encodings supply order information, and feed-forward blocks with residual connections and layer normalization stabilize deep stacks. Compared with CNNs or RNNs, transformers model long range structure without a fixed receptive field and train efficiently in parallel [85, 88].

In cfDNA analytics, tokens can be CpG sites, coverage bins, EM windows, or raw bisulfite fragments. Self-attention links distant CpG co-variation, relates coverage oscillations to nucleosome spacing, and ties terminal k-mers to local chromatin state. The architecture tolerates sparse, low-depth signals that are common in early detection and supports pretraining on large methylome or fragmentome corpora with later fine-tuning on modest plasma cohorts. When only sample-level labels are available, attention can drive multiple instance learning to highlight informative fragments and provide explainable cues [89].

Applied to cfDNA, transformers have advanced detection accuracy and simplified analysis pipelines. In epithelial ovarian cancer, a methylation transformer (MethylBERT) pretrained on more than one hundred thousand cancer methylomes outperformed a conventional LASSO-logistic model, improving sensitivity by about six percentage points and specificity by about five points in the same study, and then supported a low-cost ddPCR assay for early screening. This result illustrates how transfer learning expands limited plasma markers into a richer methylation representation for classification [90]. In bisulfite treated plasma reads, a transformer with multiple instance learning (DECIDIA) operated directly on raw fragments and exceeded methylation intensity baselines. It reported AUROC 0.98 for CRC in cross-validation and 0.91 on an external HCC test set without using HCC data in model development, while also revealing sequence signatures that differed between cancer and control and across cancer types. These findings show that transformers can dispense with hand-crafted features and still generalize across diseases and cohorts [89]. At the single molecule level, a hybrid CNN-transformer model on SMRT-seq kinetics improved 5mC detection AUC from 0.91 to 0.99 and enabled cfDNA-based HCC detection with AUC 0.97. The same framework detected 6 mA and used it to read jagged cfDNA ends and infer chromatin accessibility, linking transformer outputs to biologically explainable cfDNA properties [91].

In sum, transformers bring three advantages to cfDNA analytics. They capture long range structure across CpG blocks and fragments that local models miss. They learn efficiently through pretraining and weak supervision, which matters when cohorts are small and tumor fraction is low. They offer explainability, since attention maps can localize influential CpG regions and terminal patterns consistent with chromatin features. As preprocessing is standardized and external validation expands, transformers are likely to become a core approach for cfDNA-based screening and surveillance.

Other DL families for cfDNA-based cancer detection

Beyond CNN and transformer architectures, other DL families have seen more limited but growing use in cfDNA analytics (Fig. 2). Two representative lines are autoencoders and graph convolutional neural networks (GCNNs). Autoencoders compress high-dimensional inputs into low-dimensional latent codes and reconstruct the original signal, yielding compact representations that denoise sparse, noisy cfDNA methylation matrices [92]. GCNNs generalize convolution to graphs through message passing over an adjacency structure, so each node (e.g., a CpG or genomic region) updates its representation by aggregating features from biologically linked neighbors; this allows models to encode co-methylation networks, chromatin contacts, and other non-contiguous dependencies [93]. Applied to cfDNA methylation, autoencoders have delivered tangible gains in early detection and in tissue signal deconvolution. DeepMeth treats each targeted methylation region as a small image, learns region embeddings with an autoencoder, and then concatenates them to represent each sample; across a multicenter lung cancer cohort, this representation improved AUC by about 5–8% over predefined scalar metrics, and further boosted classification when combined with conventional features, illustrating the value of learned representations in low-signal regimes [94]. In parallel, explainable encoder-decoder frameworks such as Oncoder learn a reference methylation atlas directly from patient plasma and attach probabilistic uncertainty, which reduced tumor-signal prediction errors by at least 30% versus common deconvolution baselines and enabled fast batched inference for large cohorts. These designs support longitudinal monitoring because the decoder’s atlas offers biological explainability while the encoder tracks patient-level tumor fraction over time [95].

GCNNs leverage interaction structure to improve multi-cancer detection and TOO tasks. A self-attention GCNN first learns a methylation interaction graph and then selects informative sites via attention guided pooling before classification. External validations reported high AUC and strong precision-recall across tumor types using both tissue and cfDNA methylation sets, and the approach outperformed traditional ML comparators in the same study, indicating that graph priors help stabilize performance across platforms and panels [96]. In a multi-modal cfDNA assay (SPOT-MAS), GCNNs are used in the second stage to localize tissue of origin after a first-stage cancer vs healthy call, reflecting the suitability of graph models for topology-aware classification when features span targeted methylation, genome-wide methylation, fragment length, copy number change, and EMs [97].

In summary, autoencoders offer compact, denoised representations and explainable deconvolution of cfDNA mixtures, while GCNNs encode biological relationships among CpGs and regions to support robust multi-cancer classification and localization. Although their application scope is smaller than that of CNNs and transformers, current evidence shows practical value when cohorts are modest, tumor fractions are low, and biologically informed inductive biases are needed (Table 2). Traditional ML for cfDNA: pragmatic baselines for screening and surveillance

Although DL delivers high performance and is increasingly central in cfDNA-based early cancer detection, traditional machine learning remains indispensable. Real world constraints—modest and imbalanced cohorts, very low tumor fractions, label noise from CHIP and pre-analytical variability, and cross-platform batch effects—often favor lighter models. In these settings, tree ensembles, support vector machines (SVM), regularized linear models, and gradient boosting offer strong bias-variance control, fast training, and straightforward probability calibration at high specificity, which eases cross-cohort transfer and prospective validation. They fuse heterogeneous inputs from methylation, fragmentation, EMs, and CNVs without elaborate representation learning, and their coefficients or feature importance profiles support traceable explanation and regulatory review. Computational cost is low, enabling rapid iteration, uncertainty estimation by resampling, and deployment on routine hospital hardware. Against this backdrop, four workhorse models—random forests (RFs), SVMs, logistic regression (LR), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)—cover most real world cfDNA needs and complement one another when cohorts are modest, labels are noisy, and high specificity is required (Table 2). RFs absorb the high-dimensional, partly redundant inputs typical of methylation fragmentation panels and remain stable under noise. In a cancer localization pipeline that coupled tumor fraction estimation with competing classifiers, the RF arm outperformed SVM and CancerLocator in head to head testing, reaching 92.1% sensitivity with AUC 0.98, which illustrates its strength when integrating heterogeneous cfDNA features [58]. RFs have also transferred across heterogeneous cohorts, including EM-based multi-cancer settings, and have supported risk modeling beyond detection, for example venous thromboembolism (VTE) prediction from cfDNA features with a C-index of 0.73 [99, 106]. Compared with XGBoost, RFs are often more stable on very small cohorts and less sensitive to hyperparameters, although computational load grows with tree count and explainability is coarser.

SVMs are effective when sample sizes are limited but feature spaces are large. Liu et al. [107] developed an adaptive SVM that paired SVM with a shuffled-frog leaping optimizer and increased early-detection sensitivity by more than ten percentage points over a conventional SVM, achieving AUC 0.938 in their cfDNA study, which underscores SVMs value in small-data regimes. SVMs remain competitive for pan-cancer discrimination and TOO classification using mixed fragmentation methylation features when kernel choice and calibration are handled carefully [76, 107]. They train quickly and resist overfitting with few samples, though multi-class extensions and kernel tuning become cumbersome as scale and heterogeneity increase.

LR provides a transparent, well-calibrated baseline that is easy to deploy. In cfDNA methylation CRC screening, an LR classifier achieved AUROC 0.956 and surpassed serum markers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and CA19-9 within the same study, highlighting the clinical utility of a simple, explainable model [60]. With multi-omics integration, LR reached AUROC 0.981 with 92% sensitivity and 95% specificity for early CRC, and it has shown strong discrimination for lung cancer detection from cfDNA alterations, which is useful when regulatory transparency and probability calibration are priorities [22, 49]. Although linear decision boundaries limit nonlinearity, L1/L2 regularization and LASSO selection improve robustness and parsimony.

XGBoost delivers high accuracy and flexible handling of heterogeneous biomarkers. Using arm-level CNVs for lung cancer prediction, an XGBoost model achieved near perfect test performance in a small cohort and outperformed traditional statistical approaches in the same work, showing how boosted trees can exploit structured genome dosage signals [104]. When methylation was integrated with fragmentomic features for CRC screening, XGBoost reported AUC 0.986, and in a gastric cancer assay that combined fragment size, CNVs, and nucleosome footprints it achieved AUROC 0.962 on independent validation, indicating strong generalization under mixed feature sets [32, 108]. Relative to RF, XGBoost can capture higher-order interactions but requires careful regularization in small datasets; Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analyses help attribute predictions to specific cfDNA features and improve explainability [104, 105].

Method choice should be guided by data regime and operational goals. When cohorts are small or class imbalance is severe, begin with LR or SVM for calibrated, explainable baselines; add L1/L2 regularization and stability selection to curb overfitting, and use nested cross-validation with patient wise splits for honest estimates. When feature interactions are expected and dimensionality is high, RF and XGBoost often yield superior discrimination; mitigate overfitting with shallow trees, learning rate control, and early stopping, and quantify contributions with permutation importance or SHAP to preserve clinical explainability. Across all models, handle batch effects with GC and coverage normalization, strand aware processing for termini, and site-level calibration; address class imbalance with stratified sampling, cost-sensitive loss, or threshold adjustment to operate at high specificity. In practice, these traditional ML pipelines offer robust, low-cost baselines and can be deployed today; they also serve as anchors to judge when deep architectures provide clear, reproducible gains on external validation.

Challenges and prospects of cfDNA-based cancer detection leveraging ML

Managing tumor heterogeneity and low tumor fraction

The inherent heterogeneity of tumors poses a significant challenge for cfDNA-based cancer detection. Tumor-derived cfDNA exhibits considerable variability in terms of fragmentation patterns, methylation profiles, and CNVs, which may differ across cancer types and stages [39]. For instance, studies have demonstrated that different cancers, such as colorectal and lung cancer, release cfDNA with distinct fragmentation characteristics [38]. This biological diversity complicates the development of generalized ML models, which necessitating model designs capable of accommodating a wide spectrum of cfDNA profiles.Moreover, the low abundance of tumor-derived cfDNA, especially in early-stage cancers, limits detection sensitivity [108]. Addressing these limitations requires large, multi-center datasets to improve generalization [109]. Current efforts have demonstrated the advantages of integrating multi-omics features, such as methylation, fragmentation, and CNVs, to enhance detection accuracy [24]. Future studies should explore more refined feature selection methods, leveraging more different DL architectures to automatically identify optimal biomarkers [22], while multi-modal fusion with complementary signals such as cell-free RNA (cfRNA) and proteomics strengthens robustness for MCED [38].

Standardization and data robustness

A major hurdle in cfDNA-based cancer diagnostics is the lack of standardized protocols for sample collection, processing, and sequencing. Different pre-analytical factors, such as storage conditions and sequencing depth, can introduce confounding variability that affects downstream ML analyses [64]. Studies have demonstrated that coverage biases, PCR artifacts, and sequencing noise can significantly impact the reliability of cfDNA feature extraction [38]. Harmonized protocols and reporting standards are therefore essential, complemented by robust normalization (e.g., z-score standardization and GC-content correction) to mitigate technical artifacts [64]. In parallel, shared benchmarks and cross-site datasets will facilitate comparability, external validation, and scalable robustness [110].

Explainability and clinician trust

Despite the high accuracy of DL models in cancer type classification, their “black-box” nature limits clinical adoption. Physicians require transparency in model decision making to establish confidence and ensure informed decision making [103]. Explainable AI (XAI) approaches such as SHAP and local explainable model agnostic explanations (LIME) can expose feature attributions, but practical deployment must balance model complexity and explainability [109, 111]. Operationalizing these tools to link the cfDNA features driving predictions with known biological mechanisms can enable clinical verification [39]. Clinician facing interfaces that visualize cfDNA patterns and explanations may further promote adoption and human-in-the-loop review [109].

Cost reduction strategies

The financial burden associated with high-depth sequencing and complex computational modeling is a barrier to accessibility [107]. A coherent path to reduce cost while maintaining accuracy is to simplify the assay and statistically recover signal: either shrink the locus footprint or lower per-sample depth and then apply ML-based imputation to reconstruct informative features (e.g., targeted sequencing with imputation and lpWGS with imputation) [73, 112]. In parallel, fragmentomics draws information dense, largely orthogonal cues from shallow data—such as FSDs and nucleosome footprint patterns—thereby reducing dependence on depth while retaining discriminative power [107]. Used in a tiered workflow (screen with shallow/targeted assays and reflex to deeper sequencing for indeterminate or positive cases), these complementary strategies preserve sensitivity at scale and materially lower overall testing cost, easing the path to routine clinical deployment.

The future of cfDNA biomarkers and ML in cancer detection

Looking ahead, progress in cfDNA-ML will hinge on aligning three complementary enablers. First, at the signal level, multi-modal feature fusion can improve accuracy and generalizability beyond single feature models [108]. Second, on the assay side, cost-effective sequencing strategies will broaden access without materially compromising sensitivity, enabling population scale implementation. Third, for clinical uptake, XAI must deliver case-level rationales and auditable attribution to sustain clinician trust and support decision-making [111]. Converting these advances into practice requires standardized protocols, shared benchmarks, and multi-center evaluation for calibration, stage-specific performance, and external validation, underpinned by international and interdisciplinary collaboration [109]. With these elements in place, cfDNA-based ML is well positioned to transform early cancer detection into more precise, affordable, and deployable diagnostics.

Conclusions

As a minimally invasive and information-rich indicator, cfDNA biomarkers provide a non-invasive, dynamic, and comprehensive approach to capturing tumor-associated genetic and epigenetic alterations, and have been widely applied in the early detection, treatment monitoring, and prognostic evaluation of various cancers. In conclusion, integrating cfDNA biomarker analysis with DL and traditional ML presents significant potential for advancing cancer detection, monitoring, and therapeutic decision-making. Although challenges persist, including tumor heterogeneity, cost-related barriers, and computational complexity, recent technological and methodological innovations are progressively mitigating these limitations. The development of multi-modal, explainable, and cost-effective frameworks holds promise for enabling robust, scalable, and clinically applicable diagnostic solutions. By combining biological insights with computational advancements, cfDNA-based methods emerge as a transformative approach in oncology, paving the way for early detection and personalized cancer management that can ultimately improve survival rates and quality of life for patients worldwide.
