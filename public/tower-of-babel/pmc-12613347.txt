Glutamine metabolism reprogramming promotes bladder cancer progression via PYCR1: a multi-omics and functional validation study
Authors: Xinjia Ding, Enkui Zhang, Zhou Huang, Xiaohui Li, Zhigao Wang, Yanping Wu, Chao Liu, Shikai Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBladder cancer (BLCA) is a prevalent malignancy worldwide, with advanced stages linked to poor prognosis. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) show clinical promise in treating both operable and advanced BLCA, predicting patient responses remains a major challenge. Glutamine metabolism, a key aspect of metabolic reprogramming, has been implicated in tumor progression and immune modulation. However, the exact role of glutamine metabolism in BLCA remains poorly understood. This study aims to explore its association with clinical outcomes and immunotherapy response while functionally validating key regulatory genes.MethodsAn integrated approach combining targeted metabolomics, single-cell RNA sequencing, and bulk transcriptomic data was used to profile glutamine metabolism in BLCA comprehensively and identify potential metabolic biomarkers. A prognostic model, termed GMscore, based on glutamine metabolism, was constructed using principal component analysis (PCA). Key regulatory genes were identified through random forest analysis. Functional assays, including in vitro proliferation, migration, and metabolic assays, as well as in vivo xenograft models, were employed to validate the findings.ResultsTargeted metabolomics revealed increased glutamine metabolism in BLCA cell lines. The GMscore model, developed and validated across multiple cohorts, accurately predicted patient survival. In two immunotherapy cohorts (IMvigor210 and GSE91061), a lower GMscore correlated with improved therapeutic response, suggesting its potential as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy efficacy. PYCR1 was identified as a key regulatory gene, exhibiting high expression in epithelial cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). Functional assays demonstrated that PYCR1 knockdown inhibited cell proliferation and migration and suppressed tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, PYCR1 facilitated proline synthesis through P5CS and activated the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, which modulated glutamine utilization and metabolic reprogramming in BLCA.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive analysis of glutamine metabolism in BLCA and introduces a clinically relevant prognostic model. PYCR1 was identified as a central metabolic regulator, underscoring its critical role in tumor development and progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07386-2.Keywords: Bladder cancer, PYCR1, Glutamine metabolism, Prognostic model, Metabolic reprogramming, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BLCA) ranks among the most prevalent malignancies of the urinary system, presenting a major global health challenge [1]. It is generally categorized into non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), with the latter often progressing to advanced or metastatic stages [2]. Despite the adoption of cisplatin-based chemotherapy and the growing use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), many patients with MIBC face poor outcomes due to recurrence and resistance to treatment [3].

Recent advances in immunotherapy, particularly the introduction of ICIs, have expanded treatment options for BLCA [4]. Pembrolizumab has been approved as a first-line therapy for patients with advanced BLCA who are ineligible for platinum-based chemotherapy [5]. Additionally, neoadjuvant ICI-based regimens, either alone or combined with chemotherapy, have demonstrated promising pathological responses in clinical trials [6–8]. Emerging checkpoints, such as CD47–SIRPα, offer promising strategies to enhance immunotherapy efficacy [9].

Despite the transformative impact of ICIs, including PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, response rates remain suboptimal, and many patients eventually develop resistance or immune escape. This underscores the need to understand the mechanisms underlying immunotherapy failure. Moreover, molecular regulators such as circRNAs and metabolic pathways have been implicated in immune escape mechanisms [10, 11], emphasizing the need for further biomarker discovery in BLCA. A critical clinical challenge persists in identifying patients who will benefit from immunotherapy, with a pressing need for novel, reliable biomarkers to predict immunotherapy response.

Metabolic reprogramming, a hallmark of cancer, has garnered increasing attention for its role in promoting tumor growth, therapy resistance, and immune evasion [12, 13]. The efficacy of immunotherapy is closely linked to tumor metabolism. In cancer cells, even in the presence of sufficient oxygen, glutamine metabolism is essential for sustaining cellular energy production and biosynthesis. Glutamine is primarily converted to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), which enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to fuel cellular energy production [14]. This metabolic shift is a characteristic feature of cancer cells, ensuring a continuous supply of amino acids, nucleotides, and lipids necessary for rapid tumor growth and progression [15].

Among various metabolic pathways, glutamine metabolism has emerged as a critical pathway supporting tumor proliferation and modulating immune suppression. Dysregulated glutamine utilization not only meets the anabolic demands of cancer cells but also facilitates immune evasion by impairing T-cell function and reshaping the tumor microenvironment (TME) [16, 17]. Although the immunometabolic role of glutamine has been widely studied in other cancers, its specific association with immunotherapy response in BLCA remains largely unexplored.

This study investigated glutamine metabolic reprogramming in BLCA, aiming to clarify its role in clinical outcomes and immunotherapy response, while identifying potential metabolic targets to improve therapeutic efficacy.

Additionally, this study analyzed single-cell transcriptomic data and developed a glutamine metabolism-based signature (GMscore), which stratifies patients according to prognosis and predicts immunotherapy response in the IMvigor210 urothelial cancer cohort. This model identified pyrroline-5-carboxylate reductase-1 (PYCR1) as a core gene linked to glutamine metabolic reprogramming. Functional validation, both in vitro and in vivo, confirmed that PYCR1 promotes tumor proliferation and migration. These findings underscore the clinical relevance of glutamine metabolism in BLCA and position PYCR1 as a potential metabolic target and biomarker for the disease. Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

Human BLCA cell lines (T24, J82, UMUC3, SW780) and a normal urothelial cell line (SV-HUC-1) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. To generate stable gene knockdown cells, lentiviral vectors expressing specific short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting the desired gene, along with corresponding negative controls, were sourced from Genechem (Shanghai, China). The shRNA sequences are listed below. Lentivirus packaging was performed in 293T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in conjunction with the helper plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G. Lentiviral particles were harvested 72 h post-transfection and stored at -80 °C. Target cells were transduced as per the supplier’s instructions, incubated for 48 h, and selected with puromycin to establish stable transfectants for subsequent studies. T24 and J82 cells were transfected with either an empty vector or a PYCR1-overexpressing plasmid (oe-PYCR1), followed by treatment with or without the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (10 µM, HY-10108, MedChemExpress, USA) for 24 h.

Targeted metabolomics analysis

Targeted metabolomics profiling of BLCA cell lines (T24, J82, UMUC-3, SW780) and the normal urothelial cell line (SV-HUC-1) was conducted using UHPLC-MS/MS (ExionLC™ AD UHPLC-QTRAP® 6500+, AB SCIEX, USA). Amino acid standards and stable isotope-labeled internal standards were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, and solvents (methanol, acetonitrile, formic acid) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Samples were initially centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 10 min. After discarding the supernatant, 250 µL of an acetonitrile/methanol solution (1:1) containing internal standards was added, followed by thorough vortexing to achieve homogenization. The samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept on ice for 30 min. A second centrifugation was performed at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was collected and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis.

Bulk RNA sequencing data of BLCA and processing

This study integrated BLCA transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets (TCGA-BLCA, GSE13507 [18], GSE31684 [19], GSE32548 [20], and GSE169455 [21]), alongside clinical survival data. Additionally, datasets from immunotherapy trials (Imvigor210 [22] and GSE91061 [23]) were included to assess immunotherapy response. To minimize batch effects and ensure data consistency, expression data from these diverse sources were harmonized using the “Combat” function within the “SVA” package in R, resulting in a unified dataset for further analysis. The expression of PYCR1 in BLCA tissues (n = 404) compared to normal bladder tissues (n = 28) and its gene interaction networks were evaluated using the BLCA dataset from the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn) [24].

Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing and analysis

The GSE135337 single-cell RNA-seq dataset was selected for in-depth gene feature analysis. scRNA-seq data were processed and analyzed using Seurat (v5.2.1). Cells with fewer than 200 or more than 6,000 detected genes, or those with > 10% mitochondrial gene expression, were excluded. A total of 36,426 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analysis. The dataset was normalized, and highly variable features were identified separately before integration. Batch correction and dataset integration were performed using the FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions, with 30 dimensions. The integrated data were scaled, and dimensionality reduction was conducted using principal component analysis (PCA) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Clustering was performed using the first 30 principal components (PCs) with a resolution of 0.5. Clusters were annotated based on the expression of well-established marker genes for each cell type: urothelial cells (EPCAM, KRT8, KRT18), myeloid/macrophage (CD14, CSF1R, AIF1), T cells (CD2, CD3D, CD3E), fibroblasts (DCN, PDPN, TAGLN), and endothelial cells (PECAM1, VWF, CLDN5). To validate the findings, the study utilized the GSE267718 single-cell dataset, processed in a manner identical to GSE135337.

Scoring and differential gene expression analysis at the single-cell level

To assess glutamine metabolic activity at the single-cell level, a curated list of 105 glutamine metabolism-related genes (Supplementary Table 1) was utilized. The Seurat function AddModuleScore was employed to score each cell in the scRNA-seq dataset based on the expression of this gene set. Cells were classified into high and low glutamine metabolic activity groups based on module scores for subsequent gene feature analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the high and low activity groups were identified using the FindMarkers function from Seurat (v5). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to determine statistical significance. DEGs were defined based on the criteria of an absolute log2 fold change (log2FC) ≥ 0.5, ensuring biological relevance, and a P-value < 0.05 as the threshold for statistical significance.

Construction of the glutamine metabolism-based prognostic model (GMscore)

To explore the prognostic relevance of glutamine metabolism, PCA was performed based on DEGs associated with glutamine-related pathways. The first and second principal components (PC1 and PC2) were extracted to represent the core features of glutamine-related gene expression. This method prioritizes gene sets with strong internal correlation while minimizing the influence of unrelated genes. A composite Glutamine Metabolism Score was defined as follows: GMcore = ∑(PC1i + PC2i). Additionally, LASSO Cox regression analysis was performed on prognosis-related genes using the glmnet R package, constructing a risk score model to predict overall survival (OS) in BLCA samples. The Coef values represent the non-zero regression coefficients derived from the LASSO Cox analysis. All samples were subsequently divided into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the resulting risk scores.

Validation and performance assessment

Survival differences between the high- and low-GMscore subgroups, stratified by the median score, were assessed using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. The predictive accuracy of the GMscore for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, generated with the “timeROC” R package [25].

Prognostic nomogram construction

To develop a clinical prognostic tool, a nomogram was constructed by integrating the GMscore with key clinicopathological variables using the “rms” package in R. The nomogram’s performance and reliability were assessed using calibration curves and decision curve analysis.

Functional enrichment and immune infiltration analysis

Functional enrichment analysis was performed with the “clusterProfiler” R package to explore biological functions and signaling pathways associated with different glutamine metabolism profiles [26]. Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were evaluated. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) were used to compare pathway activation patterns across defined subgroups [27]. The TME landscape was assessed using the ESTIMATE algorithm, which calculates immune scores, stromal scores, and ESTIMATE scores and infers tumor purity based on gene expression data [28].

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer, quantified by BCA assay, and analyzed via SDS-PAGE. After transferring the proteins to PVDF membranes, blots were incubated with primary and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, visualized using ECL, and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software (version 1.46r). The primary antibodies used included PYCR1 (Proteintech, #13108-1-AP, 1:2000), β-actin (Proteintech, #66009-1-Ig, 1:50000), Vinculin (Abcam, #ab129002, 1:20000), SLC1A5/ASCT2 (Proteintech, #20350-1-AP, 1:5000), P5CS (Proteintech, #17719-1-AP, 1:3000), p-mTOR (Abcam, #ab109268, 1:2000), mTOR (Abcam, #ab32028, 1:2000), p-AKT (Abcam, #ab38449, 1:3000), AKT (Abcam, #ab8805, 1:2000), p-PI3K (Cell Signaling Technology, #4228, 1:2000), and PI3K (Proteintech, #82796-4-RR, 1:2000). Antibodies were diluted using QuickBlock™ Western primary antibody dilution buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Cat. No. P0256).

Flow cytometry

Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using the Beyotime C1052 Cell Cycle Detection Kit. Cells were fixed in 70% ethanol, stained with PI/RNase solution, and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences). Data processing was performed with FlowJo v10. Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-IF647/PI Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (G1514, Servicebio) and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences).

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Lablead, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, BLCA cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and incubated in complete growth medium for 24 h to allow cell attachment. After the designated treatment period, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a BioTek microplate reader (USA).

Colony formation and migration assays

BLCA cells (1,000 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted. For migration assays, 5 × 104 BLCA cells were seeded in the upper chamber of Transwell inserts with serum-free medium. The lower chamber was filled with medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h, migrated cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed, stained, imaged, and quantified in five random fields.

Proline content detection in BLCA cell lines

Proline content in BLCA cells was measured using the Beijing Solarbio Proline Detection Kit (#BC0295). Cells (5 × 106) were resuspended in 1 mL of extraction solution and sonicated, then heated in a water bath for 10 min. After centrifugation at 10,000 g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected. The assay was performed by adding 0.25 mL of the supernatant to a reaction mixture. After heating for 30 min, absorbance was measured at 520 nm.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by the inhibition of endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% hydrogen peroxide (China National Pharmaceutical Group Co., Ltd., #73113760) for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed, and the slides were washed with PBS. To reduce non-specific binding, 5% BSA (Sigma, Shanghai, China, #B2064) was incubated on the slides for 20 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C: PYCR1 (Proteintech, #13108-1-AP, 1:2000) and α-SMA (Abcam, #ab7817, 1:4000). On the following day, the slides were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (HRP), Abcam, #ab205718, 1:2000) at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing with PBS, TSA-488 reagent (Runnerbio, #Bry-try488) was added for signal amplification and incubated for 10–30 min at room temperature. Finally, slides were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, China, #P0131) for nuclear counterstaining, followed by immunofluorescence imaging for analysis. The human BLCA tissue microarray was obtained from AiFang Biological.

Xenograft model

Four-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions and handled according to protocols approved by the Animal Care Committee of Peking University First Hospital (Ethical approval number: J2025011). Wild-type or PYCR1 knockdown cells were trypsinized, washed with PBS, and resuspended at 2 × 106 cells per mouse for implantation.

The PYCR1 inhibitor was purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA; catalog no. HY-126271). For PYCR1 inhibitor treatment, T24 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were injected into the axillae of nude mice at a density of 2 × 106 cells per mouse. One week after tumor implantation, mice were randomly assigned to two groups, weighed, and treated every three days with either a PYCR1 inhibitor (50 mg/kg) or an equal volume of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for a total of five treatments. Tumor size was monitored weekly using digital calipers, with volumes calculated using the formula: volume = 1/2 (length × width2). After 28 days, the mice were euthanized, and tumor volumes were measured.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Paraffin-embedded mouse tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to H&E staining (hematoxylin for 5 min, eosin for 1 min), followed by dehydration and mounting. For IHC, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min. After blocking with 5% BSA (Sigma, #B2064) for 20 min at room temperature, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PYCR1 (Abcam, #ab279385, 1:500) and Ki67 (Servicebio, #GB121141-50, 1:500). After washing with PBS, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000) were applied for 30 min at 37 °C, and staining was visualized using DAB.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10) and R software (version 4.4.0). Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, while multiple group comparisons were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted, with survival differences assessed using the log-rank test. Pearson correlation was used to evaluate the relationship between variables. Statistical analysis of immunofluorescence staining results was performed using the Wilcoxon test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Comprehensive characterization of glutamine metabolism and its upregulation in bladder cancer

To investigate metabolic alterations in BLCA, the metabolic profiles of BLCA cell lines (T24, J82, UMUC3, SW780) and normal SV-HUC-1 urothelial cells were analyzed using LC-MS-based metabolomics (Fig. 2A). The PCA score plot (Figure S1A) showed a distinct separation between tumor and normal cells along PC1, indicating significant metabolic differences between the two groups. Pathways related to glutamine metabolism were notably enriched in the tumor cell lines compared to normal cells. This upregulation of glutamine, alongside other amino acids such as glycine and serine, highlights the increased reliance on glutamine metabolism in BLCA cells (Figure S1B). The box plot further illustrates the significant upregulation of several amino acids in cancer cells, including glutamine, proline, and asparagine (Fig. 2B). These patterns suggest a metabolic reprogramming of amino acids in BLCA, characterized by enhanced glutamine utilization to support tumor progression. To explore the metabolic interplay among amino acids in BLCA cells, a correlation analysis based on targeted metabolomic data was performed (Figure S1C). The heatmap revealed distinct co-regulation of amino acids, with glutamine showing strong positive correlations with asparagine, glutamate, and proline.

To investigate the impact of glutamine metabolism on BLCA, scRNA-seq data from the GSE135337 dataset, which includes seven BLCA tissue samples and one paracancerous tissue sample, were analyzed. After stringent quality control, a total of 36,518 high-quality cells were integrated using the Harmony algorithm for batch effect correction.

Dimensionality reduction and clustering via UMAP identified 14 distinct cell subpopulations (Fig. 2C). Based on the cell annotation strategy established by Lai et al. [29], these cells were classified into five major cell types: Urothelial_cells (35,303 cells), Endothelial_cells (57 cells), Fibroblasts (196 cells), Myeloid_macrophages (573 cells), and T_cells (297 cells) (Fig. 2D). Additionally, cell type-specific differential expression analysis was conducted to validate the reliability of these annotations (Fig. 2E). To further explore glutamine metabolism-related genes, 105 genes from the MSigDB database were integrated. Glutamine metabolic activity in the BLCA single-cell data was assessed using the AddModuleScore method based on these genes, and the results were visualized with UMAP (Fig. 2F). Differential gene expression analysis between high- and low-glutamine metabolism groups identified 423 DEGs (Supplementary Table 2). The top 50 DEGs (Fig. 2G-H) were subjected to GO enrichment analysis, revealing significant enrichment in immune-related biological processes such as leukocyte migration, chemotaxis, and response to oxidative stress, as well as metabolic processes related to glutamine family amino acid metabolism (Fig. 2I). These findings suggest a potential functional impact of glutamine metabolism on BLCA progression.

Establishment and validation of a glutamine metabolism-based prognostic signature

Given the elevated activity of glutamine metabolism in tumors, its potential as a prognostic biomarker was evaluated. Univariate Cox regression analysis of the DEGs identified from the single-cell data revealed 23 prognostic genes (Supplementary Table 3), which were visualized in a forest plot (Fig. 3A). A glutamine metabolism-based prognostic model (GMscore) was then developed using a PCA-based scoring algorithm applied to an integrated dataset of patients with BLCA from the TCGA and GSE13507 cohorts. Patients were divided into low and high GMscore subgroups based on an optimal cutoff value (Supplementary Table 4). The overall workflow for GMscore construction is outlined in Figure S2A. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed significant survival differences between the subgroups, with the low-GMscore group demonstrating notably better survival outcomes compared to the intermediate and high GMscore groups (Fig. 3B). To validate these results, the GMscore was applied to independent cohorts, including GSE31684 and GSE32548. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of these cohorts confirmed that lower GMscore values were associated with improved survival outcomes (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table 5). Additionally, the GMscore was integrated with key clinical features to develop a nomogram (Fig. 3D), demonstrating its predictive accuracy for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates. Calibration analysis affirmed the nomogram’s accuracy, showing strong concordance between predicted and actual survival probabilities (Fig. 3E). To further validate the robustness of the prognostic model, LASSO regression was applied for risk stratification, yielding similarly favorable results (Figure S2B-C). This further confirmed the negative correlation between glutamine metabolism scores and OS in patients with BLCA (Figure S2D, Supplementary Table 6).

Analysis of differential expression gene, tumor microenvironment, and immune profile based on GMscore

To investigate the molecular and immune landscape associated with glutamine metabolism, differential gene expression analysis was conducted between high and low GMscore groups using the TCGA and GSE13507 datasets. A significant number of genes were upregulated in the high GMscore group, with DEGs predominantly enriched in biological processes related to cell cycle progression and oncogenic signaling pathways (Fig. 4A), indicating a potential link between glutamine metabolism and tumor proliferation. GO enrichment analysis highlighted processes such as mitotic spindle organization, sister chromatid segregation, and regulation of the mitotic phase transition (Fig. 4B). KEGG pathway analysis further revealed enrichment in pathways associated with the cell cycle, p53 signaling, and DNA replication, suggesting that glutamine metabolism may contribute to tumor progression by influencing proliferation and genomic regulation (Fig. 4C).

Next, mutation patterns of the top 10 most mutated genes were examined in both high and low GMscore groups (Fig. 4D). The high GMscore group exhibited a higher frequency of mutations in key genes, such as TP53 and PIK3CA, which are involved in critical pathways influencing cancer progression in BLCA. Mutations in KMT2D and ARID1A, two chromatin-remodeling genes frequently altered in BLCA, were also more prevalent in the high GMscore group. Loss of these genes has been linked to impaired DNA repair and reduced antigen presentation, potentially contributing to an immunosuppressive microenvironment in BLCA [30]. In contrast, the low GMscore group showed fewer overall mutations, suggesting a lower tumor mutational burden (TMB) and greater genomic stability, which may explain the better prognosis observed in this cohort. These mutation patterns align with previous studies on BLCA and reinforce the association between high GMscore, genomic instability, and tumor aggressiveness [31, 32].

Given the critical role of the TME in shaping patient prognosis, this study also examined the correlation between GMscore and TME characteristics by conducting comprehensive immune profiling based on TME-related signatures. The high GMscore group exhibited significantly reduced StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATE Score, indicating a less active and potentially immunosuppressive TME (Fig. 4E). Further analysis of the TME using immune-related signatures revealed that the high GMscore group had lower scores in key immune-related pathways, including antigen processing machinery, CD8+ T effector function, and immune checkpoint activity (Fig. 4F). These findings suggest that elevated GMscore is associated with a less immunologically active microenvironment, potentially indicating reduced responsiveness to immunotherapy.

GMscore and its association with immunotherapy response and prognosis

To assess the relationship between GMscore and immunotherapy, the IMvigor210 cohort, a prominent Phase II clinical trial examining the response to anti-PD-L1 (atezolizumab) therapy in patients with advanced urothelial carcinoma, was analyzed. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that higher GMscore values were associated with poorer survival outcomes following immunotherapy (P = 0.016) (Fig. 4G, Supplementary Table 7). Further analysis of the IMvigor210 dataset showed that lower GMscore values were significantly correlated with better treatment responses (CR/PR) compared to patients with stable or progressive disease (SD/PD) (P = 0.00028) (Fig. 4H). These findings suggest that a low GMscore may be a favorable indicator for improved therapeutic response. To validate these results in other cancer types, the GSE91061 melanoma immunotherapy cohort was analyzed. Although statistical significance was not reached (P = 0.065), a trend was observed, suggesting that GMscore could serve as a prognostic marker for survival in patients with melanoma undergoing immunotherapy (Fig. 4I). Additionally, analysis of immune checkpoint gene expression between the high and low GMscore groups revealed significant differences in the expression of key immune checkpoint genes, such as PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4 (Fig. 4J), further supporting the role of GMscore in modulating the immune response to therapy.

Identification and characterization of PYCR1 as a key gene in glutamine metabolism

To investigate the role of PYCR1 in glutamine metabolism in BLCA, a random forest algorithm was employed to identify prognostically significant genes (Supplementary Table 8). Genes with relative importance scores greater than 0.01 were highlighted, including PYCR1, TALDO1, LIPT1, and GFPT2, with PYCR1 exhibiting the highest prognostic weight, underscoring its central role in glutamine metabolism (Fig. 5A). Survival analysis of the TCGA and GSE13507 datasets revealed that high PYCR1 expression was significantly associated with poorer OS (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B), highlighting its potential as a poor prognostic marker in BLCA. To explore PYCR1 expression at the single-cell level, scRNA-seq data from the GSE135337 dataset were analyzed. After quality control and dimensionality reduction using UMAP, 22 distinct clusters were identified (Fig. 5C). These clusters were annotated into five major cell types based on canonical marker genes: urothelial cells, T cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and myeloid/macrophages (Fig. 5D). Urothelial cells constituted the dominant population, followed by immune and stromal components, reflecting the complex TME in BLCA. UMAP visualizations (Fig. 5E, F) demonstrated significant upregulation of PYCR1 expression in both BLCA cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). Further analysis of the GSE267718 dataset confirmed that PYCR1 expression was elevated in both epithelial cells and CAFs (Figure S3A, B), a finding corroborated in the GSE135337 dataset. Additionally, PYCR1 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues in the TCGA database (Fig. 5G), reinforcing its role as a key regulator of metabolic processes in both cancer cells and the tumor stroma.

This study further validated the expression of PYCR1 at the protein level and assessed its clinical significance. Comparative analysis revealed that PYCR1 expression was significantly upregulated in BLCA tissues relative to paired normal tissues (Fig. 5H, I). To evaluate the clinical relevance of PYCR1 expression, patients were stratified into two groups based on the median fluorescence intensity of PYCR1 expression: high and low expression groups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that high PYCR1 expression was significantly associated with poorer prognosis (P = 0.0249) (Fig. 5J).

Additionally, multiplex IHC (mIHC) was performed on tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissue sections from 48 patients with BLCA. PYCR1 was co-expressed in both epithelial tumor cells and CAFs (Fig. 5K).

Knockdown or inhibition of PYCR1 significantly suppresses the growth and migration of BLCA cells

To further investigate the role of PYCR1 in tumor proliferation, the expression levels of PYCR1 were assessed in several BLCA cell lines (T24, UMUC3, SW780, J82) and the normal urothelial SV-HUC-1 cell line. Western blot analysis revealed significantly higher PYCR1 expression in the BLCA cell lines compared to the normal urothelial cells, with T24 and J82 showing the highest levels (Fig. 6A, Figure S4A). Based on these findings, T24 and J82 cell lines were selected for subsequent PYCR1 knockdown experiments. Lentiviral transfection with three different shRNA constructs targeting PYCR1 identified shRNA construct 3 as the most efficient in silencing PYCR1 expression (Fig. 6B, Figure S4B). To evaluate the effects of PYCR1 knockdown on BLCA cells, a series of phenotypic assays were performed. Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay, which demonstrated a significant decrease in the proliferation rate of PYCR1 knockdown cells (Fig. 6C). Similarly, colony formation assays revealed a marked reduction in colony-forming ability in PYCR1 knockdown cells (Fig. 6D). In Transwell migration assays, PYCR1 knockdown significantly impaired the migration capacity of BLCA cells (Fig. 6E).

Flow cytometry was further utilized to analyze the effects of PYCR1 knockdown on the cell cycle. The results showed a significant reduction in the percentage of cells in the G2 phase following PYCR1 knockdown (Fig. 6F). Additionally, flow cytometry analysis revealed that PYCR1 knockdown significantly increased the proportion of apoptotic cells in both T24 and J82 cell lines compared to the negative control groups (Fig. 6G). To assess the in vivo role of PYCR1 in BLCA, T24 cells were implanted subcutaneously into nude mice to establish xenografts. Seven days later, mice received either a PYCR1 inhibitor (50 mg/kg, every 3 days, for 5 cycles) or DMSO. After 4 weeks, tumors were collected for H&E and IHC (PYCR1, Ki67) analyses to evaluate growth and proliferation (Fig. 6H). Tumors in the PYCR1 inhibitor-treated group were significantly smaller than those in the DMSO-treated control group. IHC analysis revealed that PYCR1 inhibition led to a substantial reduction in Ki67-positive cells, indicating a decrease in cell proliferation (Fig. 6I).

To confirm these findings and exclude potential effects from other components of the drug, a parallel xenograft experiment was performed using T24-NC and T24-shPYCR1 cells. Consistent with the inhibitor treatment, PYCR1 knockdown markedly suppressed tumor growth in vivo (Fig. 6J). Quantitative IHC analysis showed that both PYCR1 and Ki-67 expression levels were significantly reduced after treatment with the PYCR1 inhibitor or PYCR1 knockdown (Fig. S4F-G).

PYCR1 drives glutamine–proline metabolic axis and activates PI3K/AKT signaling to promote BLCA progression

Following PYCR1 knockdown in T24 and J82 cells, a significant reduction in proline content was observed (Fig. 7A). Correlation analysis revealed that PYCR1 expression was associated with P5CS and SLC1A5 (Fig. 7B), two critical enzymes involved in proline synthesis and glutamine uptake. P5CS (Pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase) is essential for proline biosynthesis from glutamate [33], catalyzing the conversion of glutamate to proline through a two-step reaction. Western blot analysis confirmed that protein expression levels of P5CS and SLC1A5 were also reduced following PYCR1 knockdown (Fig. 7C, Figure S4C). The downregulation of P5CS after PYCR1 knockdown indicates a diminished capacity for proline synthesis.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which PYCR1 promotes malignant progression in BLCA, RNA-seq was performed on T24 and J82 cells with PYCR1 knockdown. The heatmap illustrated gene expression changes following PYCR1 silencing (Fig. 7D, Supplementary Table 9). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that downregulated genes were predominantly involved in the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, while upregulated genes were enriched in pathways such as Cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction and Neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction (Fig. 7E, F). To further validate these findings, Western blot analysis of proteins extracted from PYCR1-knockdown BLCA cells showed reduced expression of key proteins in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway (Fig. 7G, Figure S4D). Additionally, Western blot analysis revealed that overexpression of PYCR1 significantly increased the levels of p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR, indicating activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. However, this effect was notably suppressed upon treatment with the PI3K inhibitor LY294002, confirming that PYCR1-driven signaling is dependent on PI3K pathway activity. These results suggest that PYCR1 functions upstream of PI3K and promotes downstream oncogenic signaling (Fig. 7H, Figure S4E). These signaling pathway alterations help explain the observed phenotypic changes and highlight the role of PYCR1 in BLCA progression.

Discussion

In recent years, tumor metabolic reprogramming has emerged as a hallmark of cancer progression and therapeutic resistance [13]. Increasing evidence highlights the close association between glutamine metabolism and TME remodeling, as well as its role in cancer cell proliferation, metastasis, and drug resistance [34–36].

Targeting glutamine metabolism as an anti-tumor strategy has garnered significant attention in recent studies [16]. Beyond its central role in glutamine utilization and immune suppression, our findings must be considered within the broader context of interconnected amino acid and one-carbon metabolism. Glutamine replenishes the TCA cycle through α-KG and also provides carbon and nitrogen sources that fuel serine–glycine–one-carbon (SGOC) metabolism [37, 38]. Through this cross-talk, glutamine indirectly supports nucleotide synthesis, methylation reactions, and antioxidant defense, all crucial for sustaining rapid tumor proliferation. Serine and glycine, in turn, contribute to folate-dependent one-carbon flux and redox balance, linking glycolysis-derived intermediates with glutamine-driven anaplerosis [39]. This metabolic integration suggests that glutamine reprogramming in BLCA is not an isolated event but part of a coordinated network of metabolic adaptations that collectively sustain tumor growth, immune evasion, and therapy resistance. Notably, existing models have failed to demonstrate a significant correlation with survival outcomes following immunotherapy in the IMvigor210 cohort, nor have they identified core genes or explored their functions and underlying mechanisms through in vitro and in vivo experiments. This study comprehensively profiles glutamine metabolism in BLCA. Importantly, a high GMscore was associated with poor prognosis across multiple BLCA cohorts. Tumors in the high-GMscore group exhibited a higher frequency of genetic alterations, including mutations in TP53, PIK3CA, KMT2D, and ARID1A, linking glutamine metabolic reprogramming to molecular features of tumor aggressiveness. TP53 mutations, associated with impaired DNA damage repair (DDR) and aggressive phenotypes in BLCA [31, 40], have been linked to a poor prognosis. A prior study showed that TP53/PIK3CA/ATM mutations predict the response to ICIs in BLCA, with a higher mutation burden correlating with worse survival outcomes [41]. Similarly, mutations in KMT2D [42] and ARID1A [43], both involved in chromatin remodeling and transcriptional regulation, have been associated with poor prognosis and immune evasion. In contrast, the low GMscore group exhibited a lower mutation burden and fewer alterations in these key genes, suggesting a more favorable genetic profile that may contribute to better prognosis. These findings align with emerging evidence suggesting that tumors with elevated glutamine metabolism may present immunosuppressive microenvironments. Furthermore, patients with lower GMscore values displayed a more immune-activated TME and achieved better therapeutic outcomes in the IMvigor210 cohort, highlighting the potential of GMscore as a clinically relevant indicator for predicting immunotherapy responsiveness.

Among the genes involved in the glutamine metabolic network, PYCR1 was identified as a key driver gene, predominantly expressed in both epithelial tumor cells and CAFs across multiple single-cell datasets. PYCR1 plays a critical role in proline biosynthesis, essential for maintaining redox balance and supporting tumor growth [44, 45]. It is often overexpressed in various cancers, including liver [46], pancreatic [47], gastric [48], and lung cancers [49], where it contributes to tumor progression, metastasis, and therapy resistance. Our findings highlight the role of PYCR1 in regulating glutamine metabolism and proline synthesis, although its function extends beyond this single pathway. PYCR1 participates in the broader amino acid metabolic network, linking glutamine-derived carbon flux to proline and serine–glycine metabolism. This suggests that PYCR1 may act as a metabolic hub coordinating multiple amino acid pathways to support tumor growth and redox homeostasis. Moreover, CAFs exhibit elevated glutamine production and secretion compared to normal fibroblasts, leading to increased glutamine levels in the TME [50]. PYCR1 was co-expressed in epithelial tumor cells and CAFs, and its high expression correlated with poor OS, suggesting its role as a dual-compartment biomarker of tumor and stromal metabolism.

Beyond mechanistic insights, the GMscore, based on 23 glutamine metabolism-related genes, shows potential for clinical implementation. Patients with a low GMscore exhibited better responses to immunotherapy, indicating that this subgroup may derive greater clinical benefit from immune checkpoint blockade. These findings support the potential for patient stratification in clinical settings using multiplex quantitative PCR or targeted RNA sequencing panels. However, before clinical adoption, the model requires prospective validation in independent patient cohorts and standardization of detection methods to ensure reproducibility and clinical reliability.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective nature of multi-omics integration and the need for further mechanistic exploration of the PYCR1-proline-glutamine axis highlight the necessity of prospective validation in clinical cohorts and animal models. Additionally, the therapeutic potential of targeting PYCR1 and its metabolic partners in combination with ICIs remains a significant area of interest and warrants further investigation in preclinical settings. While our findings link PYCR1 to the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and its metabolic regulatory role, direct molecular interactions were not experimentally verified. Further research using techniques like co-immunoprecipitation or CRISPR-based genetic perturbation will be essential to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying PYCR1-mediated signaling and metabolic regulation in BLCA. Furthermore, given the current lack of selective PYCR1 inhibitors, our pharmacologic results serve as proof of concept rather than specific targeting. Continued efforts to develop potent, selective inhibitors and assess their in vivo efficacy are crucial for translating these findings into clinical applications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study comprehensively analyzed glutamine metabolism in BLCA, identifying PYCR1 as a critical biomarker and potential therapeutic target. The GMscore, constructed through the risk model, demonstrated improved predictive accuracy for prognosis and immunotherapy response in patients with BLCA. Our findings highlight the biological role of PYCR1 in tumor progression and TME remodeling, providing valuable insights into targeting metabolic vulnerabilities in BLCA and paving the way for novel therapies focused on glutamine metabolism.
