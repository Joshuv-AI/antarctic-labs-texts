Testing relevant difference in high-dimensional linear regression with applications to detect transferability
Xu Liu

Abstract
Most of researchers on testing a significance of coefficient 𝜷 \beta in high-dimensional linear regression models consider the classical hypothesis testing problem H 0 c : 𝜷 = 𝟎 versus ​ H 1 c : 𝜷 ≠ 𝟎 H_{0}^{c}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}\mbox{ versus }H_{1}^{c}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$0$} . We take a different perspective and study the testing problem with the null hypothesis of no relevant difference between 𝜷 \beta and 𝟎 0 , that is, H 0 : ‖ 𝜷 ‖ ≤ δ 0 ​ versus ​ H 1 : ‖ 𝜷 ‖ > δ 0 H_{0}:\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\|\leq\delta_{0}\mbox{ versus }H_{1}:\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\|>\delta_{0} , where δ 0 \delta_{0} is a prespecified small constant. This testing problem is motivated by the urgent requirement to detect the transferability of source data in the transfer learning framework. We propose a novel test procedure incorporating the estimation of the largest eigenvalue of a high-dimensional covariance matrix with the assistance of the random matrix theory. In the more challenging setting in the presence of high-dimensional nuisance parameters, we establish the asymptotic normality for the proposed test statistics under both the null and alternative hypotheses. By applying the proposed test approaches to detect the transferability of source data, the unified transfer learning models simultaneously achieve lower estimation and prediction errors with comparison to existing methods. We study the finite-sample properties of the new test by means of simulation studies and illustrate its performance by analyzing the GTEx data.



Keywords:

High-dimensional test,
Random matrix theory,
Relevant hypothesis,
Transfer learning.

1
Introduction

It is a fundamental problem in statistical learning of high-dimensional linear regression models to test the significance of a high-dimensional vector. Specifically, given control factors that are known to be correlated with the response, it is important to accurately and efficiently test the significance of a high-dimensional subvector of the model coefficients corresponding to the rest covariates. A lot of literature focus on the high-dimensional test to detect whether the entire coefficients are zero or not, in which the crucial idea comes originally from the two-sample test.
Chen and Qin, 2010
considered the two-sample mean test, and replaced the covariance matrix with the identity matrix to avoid issues in the case of “large
p
small
n
” as considered by
Bai and Saranadasa, 1996
. Other works, such as
Zhong and Chen, 2011
;
Cui et al., 2018
;
Zhang et al., 2018
, applied this technique in the linear regression
coefficient test.
Chen et al., 2024
studied the hypothesis test of high-dimensional variables on quantile regression with low-dimensional control factors, in which the authors proposed a novel test statistic by constructing a U-statistic based on score function. However, all these methods are not applicable in the presence of high-dimensional control factors.
Chen et al., 2023
proposed a U-test statistic for generalized linear models in the presence of high-dimensional control factors, which extends previous work by
Guo and Chen, 2016
in which they handle low-dimensional control factors.
Yang et al., 2023
proposed a score-based test for ultrahigh-dimensional linear regression model in the presence of high-dimensional control factors. More references can be found in
Ning and Liu, 2017
;
Li and Li, 2022
.
Liu, 2024
applied the testing method proposed by
Chen et al., 2023
to detect transferability of the source data in transfer learning frameworks.

This work is motivated by the following fact: borrowing information from useful source data improves the efficiency of fitting on target data in the transfer learning framework if the difference between parameters in the models fitting target and source data is quite small. According to
Tian and Feng, 2023
;
Li et al., 2022a
;
Li et al., 2022b
;
Li et al., 2023
;
Li et al., 2024
, when
\delta=\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\|_{2}
is small enough, such as
\delta\ll\sqrt{sn_{0}^{-1}\log p}
, the upper bounds of their method are better than the classical Lasso bound
\mathcal{O}_{p}(\sqrt{sn_{0}^{-1}\log p})
only on target data, where
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}
are
p
-dimensional coefficients of the target and the
k
th source models, respectively. In other words, it is helpful to fit the target data by borrowing the source data if
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}
is in the neighborhood of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
with a small radius
\delta
, instead of the strict requirement
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
.
This fact implies that it is necessary to relax the hypothesis testing problem

\displaystyle H_{0}^{c}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}\quad\mbox{versus}\quad H_{1}^{c}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$0$},

(1)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
.
Thus, instead of testing problem (
1
), we consider
testing the relevant difference versus no small relevant difference, that is,

\displaystyle H_{0}:\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\|\leq\delta_{0}\quad\mbox{versus}\quad H_{1}:\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\|>\delta_{0},

(2)

where
\delta_{0}
is a prespecified constant that represents the maximal difference of coefficients in regression models such that the source data is helpful for fitting target data.

Hypothesis test of the relevance in (
2
) is also called precise hypothesis (
Berger and Delampady, 1987
) or approximate hypothesis (
Hodges and Lehmann, 1954
;
Rosenblatt, 1962a
;
Rosenblatt, 1962b
;
Berger and Sellke, 1987
;
Berger and Hsu, 1996
) in the literature, which has nowadays been widely recognized in various fields of statistical inference including medical, pharmaceutical, chemistry and environmental statistics (see
Chow and Liu, 2008
,
McBride, 1999
,
Callegaro et al., 2019
, and
Lehmann and Romano, 2022
). A relevant change in the change point analysis of the time series was studied by
Dette and Wied, 2016
.
Dette et al., 2020
studied testing relevant hypotheses for functional time series.

Dette et al., 2020
;
Bucher et al., 2021
and
Dette and Tang, 2024
investigated the hypothesis test for relevant differences for functional data.
Bastian et al., 2024
developed a test procedure for testing the mutual independence of the components of a high-dimensional vector with the null hypothesis that all pairwise associations do not exceed a certain threshold in absolute value.
To our best knowledge, however, the problem of testing for relevant difference in high-dimensional regression models has not been discussed in the literature so far. As pointed out by
Berkson, 1938
, the hypothesis testing problem (
2
) avoids the consistency problem, that is, any consistent test will detect any arbitrary small change in the parameters if the sample size is sufficiently large.

The considered testing problem (
2
) leads to an immediate application to detect transferability of source data in the transfer learning. Transfer learning has been widely utilized in medical and biological studies, such as predictions of protein localization (
Mei et al., 2011
), biological imaging diagnosis (
Shin et al., 2016
), drug sensitivity prediction (
Turki et al., 2017
) and integrative analysis of “multi-omics” data (
Sun and Hu, 2016
,
Hu et al., 2019
, and
Wang et al., 2019
). More details can be found in the recent survey papers (
Pan and Yang, 2009
;
Zhuang et al., 2020
). Recently, many authors focused on development of methodological and theoretical works for transfer learning.
Tong et al., 2021
established the transferability measure on the discrete and continuous data by the
\chi^{2}
-distance between the source and target data.
Tripuraneni et al., 2020
provided a two-stage empirical risk minimization procedure for transfer learning and derived generalization bounds with general losses, tasks, and features.
Li et al., 2022b
focused on prediction, estimation and minimax optimality of transfer learning for high-dimensional linear regressions.
Cai and Wei, 2021
established the minimax rate of convergence and constructed a rate optimal two-sample weighted K-NN classifier by transfer learning.
Liu, 2024
studied the unified transfer learning models for high-dimensional linear regression. More literature on transfer learning in high-dimensional models can be referred to
Bastani, 2021
;
Tian and Feng, 2023
;
Li et al., 2022a
;
Li et al., 2023
;
Li et al., 2024
.
These papers either assume that the transferable source data is known or detect the transferability of source data by applying test procedure to hypothesis testing problem (
1
) instead of (
2
). This may be too strict to detect transferable source data in transfer learning according to their theoretical properties.

In this paper, we first investigate the test procedure for relevant difference (
2
) in high-dimensional linear regression models, where we use random matrix theory to estimate the largest eigenvalue of a high-dimensional covariance that is a crucial ingredient in our procedure. It is well-known that estimating the largest eigenvalue of high-dimensional covariance is a big challenge.
We provide two algorithms to solve the spectrum estimation based on the Marčenko-Pastur equation (
Karoui, 2008
and
Kong and Valiant, 2017
) with moment constraints.
In more challenging settings where the nuisance coefficients in high-dimensional linear regression models are also high-dimensional, we derive asymptotic normality of the proposed test statistics. The theoretical and numerical results demonstrate that the proposed test statistic is powerful to test the relevant difference of coefficients.
By applying the proposed test method to the transfer learning, the unified transfer learning models simultaneously achieve lower estimation and prediction errors, which demonstrates the proposed testing methods efficiently detect the transferable source data. Two efficient algorithms are designed to implement the proposed test methods and transfer learning.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we investigate the test procedure for testing relevant difference in high-dimensional linear regression models. We provide estimation procedures for the largest eigenvalue, and build the asymptotic normality of proposed test statistic under regular conditions. Section
3
provides the testing procedure for detecting the transferability of source data. In Section
4
, we conduct simulation studies to evaluate the finite-sample performance of the proposed test statistics and transfer learning procedures. In Section
5
, we apply the proposed method to the GTEx data. Finally, in Section
6
, we conclude with remarks and further extensions. Conditions, proofs and additional simulation studies are included in the Supplementary Material.

https://github.com/xliusufe/hdtrd
.

2
Test of relevant difference

Let
y
be the response variable,
x
be
p_{1}
-dimensional covariates, and
z
be
p_{2}
-dimensional control factors.
Given independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples
(y_{i},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i},\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, we consider the linear regression model

\displaystyle y_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}+\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}+\varepsilon_{i},

(3)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
is the parameter of interest,
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
is the nuisance parameter,
p=p_{1}+p_{2}
, and the error term
\varepsilon_{i}
is independent of
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{\rm T},\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}
satisfying

\E\varepsilon_{i}=0
and
\E\varepsilon_{i}^{2}=\sigma^{2}<\infty
. Herein, we allow that both
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are greater than sample size
n
and diverge as
n
goes to infinity.

Consider following hypothesis testing problem,

\displaystyle H_{0}:\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\|\leq\delta_{0}\quad\mbox{versus}\quad H_{1}:\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\|>\delta_{0},

(4)

where
\delta_{0}
is a specified constant.
According to
Tian and Feng, 2023
,
Li et al., 2023
and
Li et al., 2024
, when

\delta_{0}\ll\sqrt{sn_{0}^{-1}\log p}
,
the upper bounds of their method
\mathcal{A}_{\delta_{0}}
-Trans are better than the classical Lasso bound
\mathcal{O}_{p}(\sqrt{sn_{0}^{-1}\log p})
on target data only.
Without loss of generality, we can set
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
, that is, we can consider the testing problem (
2
). It is seen that when
\delta_{0}=0
, the testing problem (
2
) reduces to the classic testing problem (
1
),
which has been well studied in the literature, such as
Guo and Chen, 2016
,
Cui et al., 2018
,
Chen et al., 2023
and
Yang et al., 2023
.

2.1
Test statistic when
\delta_{0}=0

In this section, we review the classic testing problem (
1
).
When
z
’s dimension
p_{2}
diverges but is low,
Guo and Chen, 2016
proposed a test statistic

\displaystyle T_{n,p}^{0}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{j}(y_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})(y_{j}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{j}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}),

(5)

where
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
is a consistent estimator of
\gamma
under the null.

Chen et al., 2023
extended test statistic (
5
) to high-dimensional
z
, where
p_{2}>n
diverges. They replaced
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
by
minimizing the penalized loss function with Lasso penalty
(
Tibshirani, 1996
)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}=\argmin_{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}\in R^{p_{2}}}\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}\right)^{2}+\lambda\|\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}\|_{1}.

(6)

For the testing problem (
1
),
Chen et al., 2023
and
Yang et al., 2023
showed that the test statistic
T_{n,p}^{0}
is asymptotically normally distributed under some conditions. The following Theorem
1
needs some notation. Denote
\Sigma_{xz}=\E\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
and by
\Sigma_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times p_{2}}
the covariance of
z
. Define a family of coefficient
\beta

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}=\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=o(1),\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=o\left(\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{x}^{2})}{\varsigma_{n}^{2}}\right),\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta}\Sigma_{x}\Sigma_{\eta}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=o\left(\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{x}^{2})}{n}\right)\right\},

where
\Sigma_{\eta}=\Sigma_{x}-\Sigma_{xz}\Sigma_{z}^{-1}\Sigma_{xz}^{\rm T}
is the covariance of
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\Sigma_{xz}\Sigma_{z}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}
,
\varsigma_{n}=ns_{\phi}\log p_{2}
with
s_{\phi}=\|\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{\phi}\|_{0}
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{\phi}
is defined in Lemma A1 of the Supplementary Material.

Theorem 1

If Conditions
(
A.1
)-(
A.5
)
in the Supplementary Material hold, it follows that for any given
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\in\mathfrak{F}
,

\displaystyle\frac{n(T_{n,p}^{0}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})}{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{2\tr(\Sigma_{x}^{2})}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\cal L}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\cal L}}}{{\longrightarrow}}
stands for convergence in distribution.

Theorem
1
states that one can reject
H_{0}^{c}
at a significant level
\alpha
if
T_{n,p}^{0}>\frac{z_{\alpha}}{n}\sqrt{2\widehat{\sigma^{4}\tr(\Sigma_{x}^{2})}}
,
where
z_{\alpha}
is the upper
\alpha
-quantile of standard normal distribution, and
\widehat{\sigma^{4}\tr(\Sigma_{x}^{2})}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{j})^{2}(y_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})^{2}(y_{j}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{j}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})^{2}
proposed by
Guo and Chen, 2016
is a consistent estimator of
\sigma^{4}\tr(\Sigma_{x}^{2})
.
Here,
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
is a consistent estimator of
\gamma
given in (
6
).

From Lemma B1 in the Supplementary Material, the asymptotic normality in Theorem
1
requires the weak correlation between
z
and
x
, which is imposed in Condition (A.5)
\varrho_{n}\lambda_{\max}^{1/2}(\Sigma_{x})\{s_{\phi}a_{n}\log n\log p_{2}\}^{1/2}=o(\tr^{1/2}(\Sigma_{x}^{2}))
, where
\varrho_{n}=\max_{k\in\{1,\cdots,p_{2}\}}\|\E\mbox{\boldmath$x$}z_{k}\|
with
z_{k}
being the
k
th element of
z
.
\varrho_{n}
could be
O(p)
if
z_{k}
correlates highly with
x
, which results in
\lambda_{\max}^{1/2}(\Sigma_{x})\{s_{\phi}a_{n}\log n\log p_{2}\}^{1/2}>n^{1/2}p_{2}^{-1}\tr^{1/2}(\Sigma_{x}^{2})
and may break Condition (A.5).

It is necessary to reduce the correlation between
z
and
x
, which can be realized by the regularized projection score method
(
Cheng et al., 2022
)
. Specifically, we consider the projection score for
\gamma
as
S(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$})=-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}(y-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$})
.
The projection score is defined as the residual of the projection of the score function
S(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})=-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(y-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$})
onto the closure of the linear span of the score function
S(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$})
in the Hilbert space
L_{2}(P)
, where
P
is the distribution of
(y,\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$z$})
. Thus, we need to find a matrix
H\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
that minimizes
\E\|S(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})-HS(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$})\|^{2}=\sigma^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p_{1}}\E(x_{j}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{j})^{2}
,
where
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
is the
j
th column of
H^{\rm T}
. In particular,
H
satisfies the normal equation
\E(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-H\mbox{\boldmath$z$})\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\rm T}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
, which yields
H=\Sigma_{xz}\Sigma_{z}^{-1}
if
\Sigma_{z}
is invertible.

However, the sample version of
\Sigma_{z}
, which is estimated traditionally by
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}^{\rm T}
, is not invertible when
p_{2}>n
. Therefore, it is needed to regularize the projection calculation, in which we apply the standard Lasso
(
Cheng et al., 2022
)

\displaystyle\widehat{H}=\argmin_{H\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}}\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}-H\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}\|^{2}+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p_{1}}\|\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{j}\|_{1}.

(7)

Based on the residual of the projection score
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}-\widehat{H}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}
for
i=1,\cdots,n
, we construct a test statistic
T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}
as

\displaystyle T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{i}^{\rm T}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{j}(y_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})(y_{j}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{j}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}).

(8)

Define a family of coefficients as
\mathfrak{F}^{Proj}=\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\in\mathfrak{F}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=o\left(\varsigma_{n}^{-1}\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})\right)\right\}
.
We show that the test statistic
T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}
is asymptotically normally distributed in the following Theorem
2
.

Theorem 2

If Conditions
(
A.1
)-(
A.7
)
in the Supplementary Material hold,
it follows that for any given
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\in\mathfrak{F}^{Proj}
,

\displaystyle\frac{T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}{n^{-1}\sigma^{2}\sqrt{2\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\cal L}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Yang et al., 2023
has provided the same test statistic
T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}
as well as its asymptotic normality with the known projection
H
or
\widehat{H}
estimated based on another data independent of
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{n}=\{(y_{i},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i},\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. In this paper,
with the assistance of U-process
(
Chen and Kato, 2020
)
, we establish the asymptotic normality of
T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}
based on
\widehat{H}
estimated from
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{n}
.

To compare theoretically asymptotic power of test statistics between
T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}
and
T_{n,p}^{0}
,
we define asymptotic relative efficiency (ARE) as
\mbox{ARE}(T_{n,p}^{0,Proj},T_{n,p}^{0})=\frac{\tr^{1/2}(\Sigma_{x}^{2})}{\tr^{1/2}(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})}
.
Since
\Sigma_{xz}\Sigma_{z}^{-1}\Sigma_{xz}^{\rm T}
is positive-definite, by the definition
\Sigma_{\eta}=\Sigma_{x}-\Sigma_{xz}\Sigma_{z}^{-1}\Sigma_{xz}^{\rm T}
, it is seen that
\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})\leq\tr(\Sigma_{x}^{2})
, which results in
\mbox{ARE}(T_{n,p}^{0,Proj},T_{n,p}^{0})\geq 1
. Thus, projection test statistic
T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}
achieves a greater asymptotic power than the competitor
T_{n,p}^{0}
.

From Theorem
2
, one can reject
H_{0}^{c}
in testing problem (
1
) at a significant level
\alpha
if

\displaystyle T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}>\frac{z_{\alpha}}{n}\sqrt{2\widehat{\sigma^{4}\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})}},

(9)

where
\widehat{\sigma^{4}\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{i}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{j})^{2}(y_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})^{2}(y_{j}-\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{j}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})^{2}
.

On the other hand, Theorem
2
implies that the rejection region (
9
) can not control the type-I error for testing problem (
2
). The power function for testing problem (
1
) is

\Omega_{n}^{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})=\Phi\left(-z_{\alpha}+n\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(2\sigma^{4}\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2}))^{-1/2}\right)\{1+o(1)\}
, where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cumulative distribution function of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
The inflation comes from the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
d_{n}^{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})=n\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(2\sigma^{4}\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2}))^{-1/2}
.
To control the type-I error, it is natural to construct an efficient test statistic by subtracting
\sup_{\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\|\leq\delta_{0}}d_{n}^{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
from
T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}
. This observation motivates us to construct a new test statistic for testing problem (
2
), which is investigated in Section
2.2
.

2.2
Test statistic when
\delta_{0}>0

Now we are ready to consider the testing problem (
2
). Following arguments in previous section, we have
\sup_{\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\|\leq\delta_{0}}d_{n}^{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})=\delta_{0}^{2}\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Sigma_{\eta})\left\{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{2\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})}\right\}^{-1}
.
According to Theorem
2
, we can observe that
under
H_{0}
in testing problem (
2
)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\left(T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}-\delta_{0}^{2}\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Sigma_{\eta})>\sigma^{2}\sqrt{2\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})}z_{\alpha}/n\right)\leq\alpha,

and under

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}

\displaystyle P\left(T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}-\delta_{0}^{2}\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Sigma_{\eta})>\sigma^{2}\sqrt{2\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})}z_{\alpha}/n\right)=\begin{cases}\alpha,&\hbox{if }~n\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}-\delta_{0}^{2}\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Sigma_{\eta})}{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{2\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})}}=o(1),\\
1,&\hbox{if }~n\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}-\delta_{0}^{2}\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Sigma_{\eta})}{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{2\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})}}\rightarrow+\infty.\end{cases}

Finally, we reject
H_{0}
in (
2
) at a significant level
\alpha
if

T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}>\delta_{0}^{2}\widehat{\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Sigma_{\eta})}+\frac{z_{\alpha}}{n}\sqrt{2\widehat{\sigma^{4}\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})}}
,
where
\widehat{\sigma^{4}\tr(\Sigma_{\eta}^{2})}
is defined as in Section
2.1
, and
\widehat{\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Sigma_{\eta})}
is a consistent estimator of
\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Sigma_{\eta})
. This observation encourages
us to propose a new test statistic for testing problem (
2
)

\displaystyle T_{n,p}=T_{n,p}^{0,Proj}-\delta_{0}^{2}\widehat{\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Sigma_{\eta})}.

(10)

In Appendix F of the Supplementary Material, we provide an estimator
\widehat{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\eta})}=\alpha_{n}(1+\tau_{n})^{-2}\lambda_{\max}(S_{n})
for the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{\eta}
. It is convenient to implement, but is not consistent, and consequently brings bias in practice. In this section, we provide two consistent estimators for the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{\eta}
.

Karoui, 2008
proposed an algorithm to solve the spectrum estimation problem by the Marčenko-Pastur equation

\displaystyle z=-\frac{1}{\underline{m}_{G}(z)}+\tau^{2}\int\frac{t}{1+t\underline{m}_{G}(z)}dG(t),~\forall z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

(11)

where
\tau=\lim p/n
,
\mathbb{C}^{+}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mbox{IM}(z)>0\}
, and
\underline{m}_{G}(z)
is the Stieltjes transform for the spectral distribution
G(\cdot)
.
\underline{m}_{G}(z)
can be approximated by the Stieltjes transform for the empirical spectral distribution
G_{n}(\cdot)
, that is,

\displaystyle\underline{m}_{G}(z)\simeq\underline{m}_{G_{n}}(z)=-\frac{1-p_{1}/n}{z}+\frac{1}{n}\tr\left((S_{n}-z\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{p_{1}})^{-1}\right).

(12)

By applying discretization strategy, we approximate the integral in Marčenko-Pastur equation (
11
) by

\displaystyle\int\frac{t}{1+t\underline{m}_{G}(z)}dG(t)\simeq\sum_{j=1}^{J_{t}}\omega_{j}\frac{t_{j}}{1+t_{j}\underline{m}_{G}(z)},

(13)

where
J_{t}
is a positive integer, and
\{\omega_{j},j=1,\cdots,J_{t}\}
are weights with the constraints
\sum_{j=1}^{J_{t}}\omega_{j}=1
and
\omega_{j}\geq 0
.
Thus, following above arguments, Mar
\check{c}
enko-Pastur equation (
11
) turns into

\displaystyle z\simeq-\frac{1}{\underline{m}_{G_{n}}(z)}+\frac{p_{1}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{t}}\omega_{j}\frac{t_{j}}{1+t_{j}\underline{m}_{G_{n}}(z)},\forall z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

(14)

where
\{t_{j}\in[a,b],j=1,\cdots,J_{t}\}
with
t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{J_{t}}
is a set of discrete points, and
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
is a preset interval.
To approximate the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{\eta}
, it suffices to find
t_{j_{0}}\in\{t_{j},j=1,\cdots,J_{t}\}
with
j_{0}=\min\{j:\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\omega_{\ell}\geq p_{1}/(p_{1}+1),j=1,\cdots,J_{t}\}
. Thus, it is crucial to find weights
\{\omega_{j},j=1,\cdots,J_{t}\}
.

Recall
t_{j}\in[a,b]
with
t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{J_{t}}
. In practice, we take
a=\lambda_{\min}(S_{n})/\lambda_{\max}(S_{n})
and
b=\lambda_{\max}(S_{n})
. To solve
\{\omega_{j},j=1,\cdots,J_{t}\}
from equation (
14
), we set
z_{k}=u_{k}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}i
,
k=1,\cdots,J_{z}
, where
i=\sqrt{-1}
stands for the imaginary unit,
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
J_{z}>J_{t}
is a positive integer. In practice, we generate
u_{k}
randomly from standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Invoking that
\mbox{RE}(z)
and
\mbox{IM}(z)
denote the real and imaginary parts of
z\in\mathbb{C}
, respectively. Denote
y_{R,k}=\mbox{RE}(z_{k}+1/\underline{m}_{G_{n}}(z_{k}))
,
y_{I,k}=\mbox{IM}(z_{k}+1/\underline{m}_{G_{n}}(z_{k}))
, and

\displaystyle x_{R,k,j}=\mbox{RE}\left(\frac{p_{1}t_{j}/n}{1+t_{j}\underline{m}_{G_{n}}(z_{k})}\right),\mbox{ and }x_{I,k,j}=\mbox{IM}\left(\frac{p_{1}t_{j}/n}{1+t_{j}\underline{m}_{G_{n}}(z_{k})}\right).

To find weights
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=(\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{J_{t}})^{\rm T}
, we define a loss function of weights as follows,

\displaystyle L_{\mbox{eig}}(\mbox{\boldmath$w$})=\frac{1}{J_{t}}\sum_{k=1}^{J_{z}}\left|y_{R,k}-\sum_{j=1}^{J_{t}}x_{R,k,j}\omega_{j}\right|+\frac{1}{J_{z}}\sum_{k=1}^{J_{z}}\left|y_{I,k}-\sum_{j=1}^{J_{t}}x_{I,k,j}\omega_{j}\right|

(15)

with the constraints
\sum_{j=1}^{J_{t}}\omega_{j}=1
and
\omega_{j}\geq 0
,
j=1,\cdots,J_{t}
.

Tian et al., 2015
proposed the unbiased and consistent estimators of the first four moments of population spectrum
G(t)
. Let
\zeta_{k}=\int t^{k}dG(t)
and
\hat{\xi}_{k}=\int t^{k}dG_{n}(t)
be the
k
th moment of population spectral distribution
G(t)
and its empirical version
G_{n}(t)
, respectively. The unbiased and consistent estimators of the first four moments are constructed by
\hat{\zeta}_{1}=\hat{\xi}_{1}
,

\hat{\zeta}_{2}=c_{2n}(\hat{\xi}_{2}-\tau_{n}^{2}\hat{\xi}_{1})
,

\hat{\zeta}_{3}=c_{3n}(\hat{\xi}_{3}-3\tau_{n}^{2}\hat{\xi}_{2}\hat{\xi}_{1}+2\tau_{n}^{4}\hat{\xi}_{1}^{3})
,

\hat{\zeta}_{4}=c_{4n}(\hat{\xi}_{4}-4\tau_{n}^{2}\hat{\xi}_{3}\hat{\xi}_{1}-\tau_{n}^{2}\hat{\xi}_{2}^{2}\frac{2n^{2}+3n-6}{n^{2}+n+2}+\tau_{n}^{4}\hat{\xi}_{1}^{2}(2\hat{\xi}_{2}-\tau_{n}^{2}\hat{\xi}_{1}^{2})\frac{5n^{2}+6n}{n^{2}+n+2})
,
where
\tau_{n}=\sqrt{p_{1}/n}
,
c_{2n}=n^{2}[(n-1)(n+2)]^{-1}
,
c_{3n}=c_{2n}n^{2}[(n-2)(n+4)]^{-1}
, and
c_{4n}=c_{3n}n(n^{2}+n+2)[(n-3)(n+1)(n+6)]^{-1}
. Thus, it is natural to consider following four moment constraints to improve the estimation efficiency
\hat{\zeta}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{J_{t}}\omega_{j}t_{j}^{k}
,
k=1,2,3,4
.
The optimization problem (
15
) imposing above four moment constraints is convex, and is easily solved by classic algorithm in linear program. In fact, minimizing optimization problem (
15
) is equivalent to following linear programming problem (LP),

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\min_{\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2J_{z}}}\mbox{\boldmath$1$}^{\rm T}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}},\\
&\mbox{subject to: }A\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}},\\
&\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{z}&\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{2J_{z}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{z}&\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{2J_{z}}\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}\\
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\\
\end{array}\right)\geq\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{z}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{z}\\
\end{array}\right),\\
&~\mbox{and }\omega_{j}\geq 0,j=1,\cdots,J_{t},\end{split}

(16)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{z}=(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{R}^{\rm T},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{I}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}
with
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{R}=(x_{R,k,j})_{k,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{J_{z}\times J_{t}}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{I}=(x_{I,k,j})_{k,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{J_{z}\times J_{t}}
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{z}=(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{R}^{\rm T},\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{I}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}
with
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{R}=(y_{R,1},\cdots,y_{R,J_{z}})^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{J_{z}}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{I}=(y_{I,1},\cdots,y_{I,J_{z}})^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{J_{z}}
,
A=(\mbox{\boldmath$1$},\mbox{\boldmath$t$},\mbox{\boldmath$t$}^{2},\mbox{\boldmath$t$}^{3},\mbox{\boldmath$t$}^{4})^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{5\times J_{t}}
with
\mbox{\boldmath$t$}=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{J_{t}})^{\rm T}
, and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}}=(1,\hat{\zeta}_{1},\hat{\zeta}_{2},\hat{\zeta}_{3},\hat{\zeta}_{4})^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
.
We employ the optimization package
limSolve
within R
(
Van den Meersche et al., 2009
)
. The estimator of the largest eigenvalue is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\eta})}=\min\left\{t_{j}:\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\hat{\omega}_{\ell}\geq\frac{p_{1}}{p_{1}+1},j=1,\cdots,J_{t}\right\},

(17)

which is the
p_{1}
th
(p_{1}+1)
st-quantile of distribution corresponding to
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}
, where
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}=(\hat{\omega}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\omega}_{J_{t}})^{\rm T}
is the estimator of
w
by minimizing LP (
16
).

Kong and Valiant, 2017
introduced another way to estimate the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{\eta}
. They proposed a consistent estimator of the
k
th moment of population spectral distribution, and then the consistent estimator of eigenvalue was provided based on the estimated moments. Compared with the method by
Karoui, 2008
, a computationally cheaper algorithm was provided by
Kong and Valiant, 2017
to estimate the largest eigenvalue.
Kong and Valiant, 2017
proposed the
k
th moment of population distribution by

\tilde{\zeta}_{k}=n^{k}p_{1}^{-1}\binom{n}{k}^{-1}\tr(\tilde{G}^{k-1}\tilde{S}_{n})
,
where
\tilde{S}_{n}=n^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and
\tilde{G}
is the matrix
\tilde{S}_{n}
with the diagonal and lower triangular entries set to zero. Then, they estimated the weight
w
by solving following LP,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\min_{\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\mbox{\boldmath$1$}^{\rm T}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}},\\
&\mbox{subject to: }V\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}},\\
&\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-V&\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{M}\\
V&\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{M}\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}\\
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\\
\end{array}\right)\geq\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}}\\
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}}\\
\end{array}\right),\\
&~\mbox{and }\omega_{j}\geq 0,j=1,\cdots,J_{t},\end{split}

(18)

where
M
is the number of estimated moments,
V=(v_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times J_{t}}
with element
v_{ij}=t_{j}^{i}
, and
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}}=(\tilde{\zeta}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\zeta}_{M})^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Therefore, the largest eigenvalue is estimated by
\widehat{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\eta})}
in (
17
) with
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}=(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{J_{t}})^{\rm T}
minimizing LP (
18
).

2.3
Implementation

In Section
2.2
, we have proposed methods to estimate consistently the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{\eta}
based on
S_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{i}^{\rm T}
when
H
is known. However,
H
is unknown in practice, which results in that
S_{n}
is not available. It is natural to replace
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{i}
by its estimator
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}-\widehat{H}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}
, where
\widehat{H}
is a consistent estimator of
H=\Sigma_{xz}\Sigma_{z}^{-1}
. Thus, we can replace
S_{n}
by

\widehat{S_{n}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{i}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{i}^{\rm T}
, which is the sample covariance of
\{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{n}\}
.
In the high-dimensional setting, we can consistently estimate
H
by minimizing the penalized quadratic loss

\displaystyle\widehat{H}=\argmin_{H\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}}\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}-H\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{i}\|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p_{1}}\lambda_{w}\|\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{j}\|_{1},

(19)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{j}
is the
j
th column of
H^{\rm T}
.
We summarize the new test procedure in Algorithm
1
, named “HDTRD”. In Appendix G.1 of the Supplementary Material, we conduct a simulation study to investigate the performance of estimation procedures for
\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\eta})
, from which it is seen that the proposed methods estimate
\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\eta})
well.

3
Test of transferability

3.1
Unified transfer learning

Let
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{k}=(y_{k1},\cdots,y_{kn_{k}})^{\rm T}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{k}=(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{k1},\cdots,\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{kn_{k}})^{\rm T}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{k}=(\varepsilon_{k1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{kn_{k}})^{\rm T}
for
k=0,1,\cdots,K_{s}
, where
\{y_{ki},i=1,\cdots,n_{k}\}
and
\{\varepsilon_{ki},i=1,\cdots,n_{k}\}
are the responses and errors from the
k
th dataset
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{k}
, respectively. Here
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{0}=\{(y_{0i},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0i}),i=1,\cdots,n_{0}\}
denotes the target dataset, and
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{k}=\{(y_{ki},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ki}),i=1,\cdots,n_{k}\}
denotes
k
th source dataset for
k=1,\cdots,K_{s}
. We consider following linear regression model for the
k
th dataset,

\displaystyle y_{ki}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ki}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}+\varepsilon_{ki},~i=1,\cdots n_{k}.

(20)

Let
N=\sum_{k=0}^{K_{s}}n_{k}
.
Define
\mathbb{Y}=(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{0}^{\rm T},\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}^{\rm T},\cdots,\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{K_{s}}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\mathbb{E}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{0}^{\rm T},\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{1}^{\rm T},\cdots,\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{K_{s}}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}^{\rm T},(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0})^{\rm T},\cdots,(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{K_{s}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0})^{\rm T})^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{p(K_{s}+1)}
, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{0}&\mbox{\boldmath$0$}&\cdots&\mbox{\boldmath$0$}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{1}&\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{1}&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{K_{s}}&\mbox{\boldmath$0$}&\cdots&\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{K_{s}}\\
\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(pK_{s}+p)}.

Liu, 2024
proposed a unified procedure for transfer learning in high-dimensional linear regression model

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}=\mathbb{X}\mbox{\boldmath$B$}+\mathbb{E}.

(21)

A penalized quadratic loss function is proposed by
Liu, 2024

\displaystyle L(\mbox{\boldmath$B$})=\frac{1}{2N}\left\|\mathbb{Y}-\mathbb{X}\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\right\|^{2}+\lambda\|\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\|_{1},

(22)

where
\lambda
is a tuning parameter.
Then, the first
p
elements of
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}
yield the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
of interest, where
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}
is the minimizer of the loss function
L(\mbox{\boldmath$B$})
in (
22
).

3.2
Transferability testing

For
k=1,\cdots,K_{s}
, according to the difference of two linear regression models in (
20
) based on data
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{0}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{k}
, we construct a new linear regression model

\displaystyle y_{0ki}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ki}^{\rm T}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k})+\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0ki}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}+\varepsilon_{0ki},~i=1,\cdots,\check{n}_{k},

(23)

where
\check{n}_{k}=\min\{n_{0},n_{k}\}
,
y_{0ki}=y_{0i}-y_{ki}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0ki}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0i}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ki}
, and
\varepsilon_{0ki}=\varepsilon_{0i}-\varepsilon_{ki}
,
i=1,\cdots,\check{n}_{k}
.
To detect the transferability of the
k
th source dataset
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{k}
, it is of interest to test traditionally whether the contrast coefficient
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
is
0
or not.
From literature
(
Tian and Feng, 2023
;
Chen et al., 2023
)
, researchers turn the hypothesis test of transferability into the testing problem

\displaystyle H_{0}^{c,k}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}\quad\mbox{versus}\quad H_{1}^{c,k}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$0$}.

(24)

In model (
23
), the above testing problem is equivalent to the testing problem (
1
).

In practice, since
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
is unknown, we can consistently estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
by minimizing the penalized quadratic loss based on the pooled data from
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{0}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{k}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0,k}^{ini}=\argmin_{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\frac{1}{2\check{n}_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{\check{n}_{k}}(y_{0ki}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0ki}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})^{2}+\lambda\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\|_{1},

(25)

where
\lambda
is a tuning parameter.
Thus, based on
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0,k}^{ini}
, we propose a new test statistic for the classic testing problem (
24
)

\displaystyle T_{n,p}^{0,k}=\frac{1}{\check{n}_{k}(\check{n}_{k}-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ki}^{\rm T}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{kj}(y_{0ki}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0ki}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0,k}^{ini})(y_{0kj}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0kj}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0,k}^{ini}).

(26)

For each
k=1,\cdots,K_{s}
, we define a family of contrast coefficients for the
k
th model

\mathfrak{F}_{k}=\bigg\{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta,k}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=o(1),\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta,k}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=o\left(\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{x,k}^{2})}{\varsigma_{k,n}}\right),\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\rm T}\Sigma_{\eta,k}\Sigma_{x,k}\Sigma_{\eta,k}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=o\left(\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{x,k}^{2})}{\check{n}_{k}}\right)\bigg\},

where
\varsigma_{k,n}=\check{n}_{k}s_{k,\phi}\log p
with
s_{k,\phi}=\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k,\phi}\|_{0}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k,\phi}
defined in Lemma F1 in the Supplementary Material, and
\Sigma_{\eta,k}=\Sigma_{x,k}-\Sigma_{x,k}\left(\Sigma_{x,0}+\Sigma_{x,k}\right)^{-1}\Sigma_{x,k}
is the covariance of
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{ki}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ki}-H_{k}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0ki}
with

.
The asymptotic normality of

is established under the null and alternative hypotheses in following Theorem

Theorem 4

Genotype-tissue expression data

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a brain disorder, and it gets worse over time. AD that causes the brain to shrink and brain cells to eventually die is characterized by changes in the brain that lead to deposits of certain proteins. AD is the most common cause of dementia, which is a gradual decline in memory, thinking, behavior and social skills. These changes affect badly a patient’s ability to function. 6
Conclusion

This paper studies hypothesis testing for the relevant difference of coefficients in high-dimensional linear regression models. This testing problem with the null hypothesis of no relevant difference between
\beta
and
0
is
H_{0}:\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\|\leq\delta_{0}
, instead of the classical hypothesis testing problem
H_{0}^{c}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
. We propose a novel test procedure by overcoming the challenge of the estimation of the largest eigenvalue of a high-dimensional covariance matrix with assistance of the random matrix theory. It is natural to apply the test of relevant difference of coefficients to detect the transferability of source data in the transfer learning framework. We develop a testing algorithm, and furthermore, a transfer learning algorithm for estimation and prediction that is adaptive to the unknown informative set.

The classical hypothesis testing problem
H_{0}^{c}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}\mbox{ versus }H_{1}^{c}:\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
has been investigated in the high-dimensional generalized linear models (GLMs) and high-dimensional quantile regression models.
In the further work, using our similarity characterization, the test procedure of relevant difference of coefficients in other nonlinear regression models can be formulated.
Its application to detect the transferability of source data in the transfer learning beyond linear models remains an open problem, and is worthy of investigation under different characterizations for the similarity testing procedure.
