Penalized mixed models to adjust for batch effects and unobserved confounding in high dimensional regression
Yujing Lu, Patrick Breheny

Abstract
Confounding can lead to spurious associations. Typically, one must observe confounders in order to adjust for them, but in high-dimensional settings, recent research has shown that it becomes possible to adjust even for unobserved confounders. The methods for carrying out these adjustments, however, have not been thoroughly investigated. In this study, we derive a confounding framework in which the signal, bias, and variability can be cleanly partitioned and quantified, thereby enabling simulations in which one varies the bias-to-signal ratio while holding the signal-to-noise ratio fixed. Using this construction, we demonstrate the impact of the amount and complexity of unobserved confounding on the performance of competing methods, including the LASSO, principal components LASSO (PC-LASSO), and penalized linear mixed models (PLMMs). We identify scenarios in which each method outperforms the others and find that overall, PLMM is the most robust approach.



1
Introduction

Confounding leads to spurious associations and estimation bias if not properly adjusted for. Traditionally, adjusting for confounders relies on including them in a regression model. To do so, however, the confounders must be observed. Recently, research has shown that in high-dimensional settings, it is possible to adjust for confounders even when they are unobserved.
Wang and Blei 2019
demonstrated how high-dimensional data could be used to estimate confounding structures and proposed a three-step deconfounding method using factor analysis techniques. A similar phenomenon arises in the context of batch effects for high-throughput data
(
Leek et al. 2010
)
, where approaches have been developed to remove batch effects even when batches are unknown
(
Leek and Storey 2008
;
Gagnon-Bartsch and Speed 2012
)
.

In penalized regression, principal components analysis (PCA) is the most widely used approach to adjusting for confounding effects. This is accomplished by including the top principal components (PCs) as unpenalized variables in the model. One caveat of PCA is that it requires choosing the number of PCs, which is not straightforward and may lead to inefficient adjustment. More recently, preconditioning techniques have been proposed, providing a new paradigm for handling confounding effects. The Puffer transformation
(
Jia and Rohe 2015
)
and Lava estimator
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2017
)
were among the first preconditioning approaches proposed, although their focus was on variable selection as opposed to confounding correction.
Ćevid et al. 2020
showed that these approaches, along with the Trim transformation, belong to a family of spectral transformations and that all of these approaches could be used to deal with hidden confounding structures.

Mixed models, despite being widely used in genetics, have not received much attention outside that context. The potential of mixed models in dealing with confounding effects outside of genome-wide association studies has not been investigated thoroughly.

This paper develops a rigorous framework for investigating unobserved confounding in high-dimensional regression that allows us to decompose the unobserved confounding effect into two components, bias and noise. Using this framework enables us to carry out simulations in which we vary the bias-to-signal ratio while holding the signal-to-noise ratio fixed, or vary the structure of the confounding while fixing the bias-to-signal and signal-to-noise ratios. These simulations highlight scenarios in which PC-LASSO outperforms PLMM and vice versa, while also demonstrating the ability of both methods to outperform the LASSO when observed confounders are present.

In Section
2
, we describe the three methods under comparison. In Section
3
, we formulate the framework described above, which we refer to as the linear confounding model, and illustrate how it can be decomposed into bias and noise. In the remaining sections, we compare the aforementioned methods using both simulated and real data.

2
Methods

In this manuscript, we consider the following setting:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\textrm{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{e}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
and the outcome
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
depends on both an observable
n\times p
matrix of features
\mathbf{X}
and an unobservable
n\times q
matrix of confounders
\mathbf{Z}
, with effects
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, respectively. We refer to features with nonzero effects as signals. Throughout, we assume without loss of generality that
\mathbf{X}
has been standardized such that
\sum_{i}x_{ij}=0
and
\sum_{i}x_{ij}^{2}/n=1
for all
j\in 1,...,p
.

Since
\mathbf{Z}
is not observed, however, we cannot directly include it in the model. Therefore, we consider various models in which the mean structure is misspecified as

\mathbb{E}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}.

Below, we introduce a standard LASSO (which does not address unobserved confounding) as well as two methods, PC-LASSO and PLMM, that attempt to adjust for unobserved confounding.

2.1
LASSO

We begin by giving a brief review of the LASSO
(
Tibshirani 1996
, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator;)
. Unlike stepwise variable selection, which retains or discards covariates without shrinking coefficients, or ridge regression, which shrinks coefficients towards zero but does not perform variable selection, LASSO achieves both simultaneously.

LASSO obtains its coefficient estimates by minimizing the objective function

\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{\beta}\,|\,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}\rVert_{1},

where
\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}\rVert_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left\lvert\beta_{j}\right\rvert
is the
\ell_{1}
norm and
\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}
is the
\ell_{2}
norm. The tuning parameter
\lambda
controls the trade-off between goodness-of-fit and shrinkage and is typically selected using cross-validation.

LASSO’s ability to perform both variable selection and coefficient shrinkage allows it to retain both interpretability and prediction accuracy in high-dimensional settings where the number of covariates
p
may be larger than the number of observations
n
. LASSO can be extended to handle other types of outcomes, such as counts or binary data, through appropriate generalized linear models.

However, LASSO does not account for unobserved confounding. Confounding can affect the performance of LASSO in many ways, potentially leading to the selection of spurious features, bias in the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, and reduced prediction accuracy.

2.2
PC-LASSO

One method to adjust for an unobserved
\mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is to include principal components (PCs) derived from the observed features
\mathbf{X}
. The motivation is that unobserved confounding likely affects many features, and therefore the unobserved confounding may be captured by the leading PCs. Including them in the model will therefore adjust for the effect of confounding and allow for better estimation of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Let
\mathbf{C}
denote an
n\times k
matrix containing the first
k
PCs. Then by incorporating
\mathbf{C}
into the model, PC-LASSO estimates the coefficients by minimizing the objective function

\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\,|\,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}\rVert_{1},

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the coefficient vector corresponding to the
k
leading PCs. Note that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is not penalized.

2.3
PLMM

Like PC-LASSO, PLMM is motivated by the idea that unobserved confounding likely affects many features. However, whereas PC-LASSO adjusts for unobserved confounders through the mean structure, PLMM carries out its adjustment through the variance. Specifically, PLMM treats the unobserved confounding as a random effect with mean zero and
\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\sigma_{s}^{2}\mathbf{K}
.

Under PLMM, therefore, the outcome follows the distribution
\mathbf{y}\sim\textrm{N}(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma_{s}^{2}\mathbf{K}+\sigma_{e}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}
. Here,
\sigma_{s}^{2}
controls the magnitude of the structured variance due to the confounding. Similar to generalized least squares models, we can transform the model to obtain a pseudo-response with independent errors:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{y}\sim\textrm{N}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{I}_{n}).\end{split}

(1)

Letting
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{y}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{X}
denote the pseudo-response and rotated features, respectively, the objective function for PLMM is then equivalent to that of the LASSO, and existing algorithms can be used to solve for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
:

Q(\boldsymbol{\beta}\,|\,\widetilde{\mathbf{X}},\tilde{\mathbf{y}})=\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}\rVert_{1}.

Estimating
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
requires estimates of
\mathbf{K}
as well as the variance components,
\sigma_{s}^{2}
and
\sigma_{e}^{2}
. The matrix
\mathbf{K}
is estimated by

\hat{\mathbf{K}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top};

with standardized features, this estimates the
n\times n
correlation structure among the instances. The variance components are typically estimated using the null model
(
Lippert et al. 2011
)
. It is possible to estimate
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
in other ways, but this is the most common approach and what is implemented in the software package used in later sections.

The correlation between observations also affects prediction. Suppose
\mathbf{X}_{\text{old}}
and
\mathbf{y}_{\text{old}}
are used for fitting the model, and
\mathbf{X}_{\text{new}}
is the data used for predicting
\mathbf{y}_{\text{new}}
. Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
denote the estimated coefficients using
\mathbf{X}_{\text{old}}
. We can aggregate
\mathbf{X}_{\text{old}}
and
\mathbf{X}_{\text{new}}
to estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
, which consists of four blocks: the top-left block
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}
is the estimated covariance matrix of
\mathbf{X}_{\text{old}}
; the bottom-right block
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}
is the estimated covariance matrix of
\mathbf{X}_{\text{new}}
; off-diagonal blocks
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{12}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{21}
represent the estimated covariance between
\mathbf{X}_{\text{old}}
and
\mathbf{X}_{\text{new}}
. Based on the conditional distribution of the multivariate normal distribution, the best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) of
\mathbf{y}_{\text{new}}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{\text{old}}
is

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{\text{new}}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{\text{old}}=\mathbf{X}_{\text{new}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{21}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}_{\text{old}}-\mathbf{X}_{\text{old}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}).

In cross-validation (CV),
\mathbf{X}_{\text{new}}
corresponds to the data in the testing fold, and
\mathbf{X}_{\text{train}}
consists of all other folds used as training data.
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{21}
can be obtained by computing the covariance matrix between
\mathbf{X}_{\text{test}}
and
\mathbf{X}_{\text{train}}
. And
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}
is computed as the variance-covariance matrix of
\mathbf{X}_{\text{train}}
.

3
Decomposition of Unobserved Confounding

Our goal in this paper is to examine how unobserved confounding affects LASSO estimates, to determine whether PC-LASSO and PLMM can correct for this confounding, and to investigate whether certain types of confounding favor one method over the other. However, confounding can affect the model in many ways: it can introduce bias, it can increase noise, the bias can be away from zero or towards zero, and it can be mistaken for signal, which affects selection of the regularization parameter
\lambda
. Careful design is necessary to isolate effects such as increasing the amount of confounding, as misleading artifacts often arise when multiple aspects of the data are changing at the same time. In this section, we derive the mathematical model and decompositions that allow us to isolate aspects of unobserved confounding and derive useful quantities such as the bias to noise ratio that we utilize in the simulations that follow.

3.1
Implications of Model Misspecification

We begin by working out the implications of model misspecification on the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. We consider the features
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{p})
, the confounders
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},...,z_{q})
, and the outcome
y
as random variables. We further assume that (
\mathbf{x},y,\mathbf{z})
has a joint distribution such that each dataset can be seen as multivariate realizations from this distribution. Without assuming any model,
y
can be written as
y=\mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})+\epsilon
, where
\mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
denotes the mean structure of
y
and
\epsilon
is the zero-mean noise. As discussed in
Buja et al. 2019
, the best population linear approximation to
y
using only the features
\mathbf{x}
can be written, assuming
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})
is full rank, as

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{x}\mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\right).

If the true mean structure is linear such that
\mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, then the only source of misspecification comes from omitting
\mathbf{z}
from the linear model, thereby projecting
\mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
onto
\mathbf{x}
. In this case,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}&=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)\\
&=\boldsymbol{\beta}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})^{-1}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\\
&=\boldsymbol{\beta}+\hskip 38.41121pt\boldsymbol{\tau}.\end{split}

(2)

Equation (
2
) shows that in the linear regression setting, unobserved confounders introduce bias
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})^{-1}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\boldsymbol{\gamma}
: as more data becomes available, the least squares estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
converges to
\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\tau}
, not
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. The bias
\boldsymbol{\tau}
can also be interpreted as the extent to which the confounding effects
\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
can be projected onto the nearest distribution in the working model.

Using the definition above and under the linearity assumption, the response random variable
y
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\begin{split}y&=\hskip 19.91692pt\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hskip 7.11317pt+\left(\hskip 11.38109pt\mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\hskip 10.81204pt-\hskip 19.91692pt\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)\hskip 21.33955pt+\epsilon\\
&=\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\overbrace{(\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})}+\left(\overbrace{\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\overbrace{(\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\tau})}\right)\hskip 4.2679pt+\epsilon\\
&=\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}(\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\hskip 1.42262pt+\hskip 34.1433pt\underbrace{(\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\tau})}\hskip 45.5244pt+\hskip 2.84526pt\epsilon\\
&=\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}(\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\hskip 1.42262pt+\hskip 59.75078pt\psi\hskip 68.2866pt+\hskip 0.56905pt\epsilon.\end{split}

(3)

Equation (
3
) illustrates the full impact of unobserved confounding on the response random variable. When the confounders
\mathbf{z}
are omitted from the model, there are two consequences: in addition to the bias term
\boldsymbol{\tau}
discussed already, the part of
\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
that cannot be projected onto the distribution of
\mathbf{x}
adds additional noise
\psi
into the model, and this noise is not correlated with
\mathbf{x}
(i.e., it is exogenous).

As in
Buja et al. 2019
, equation (
2
) shows that when a model is misspecified, its coefficient estimates become dependent on the distribution of both the features
\mathbf{x}
and the confounders
\mathbf{z}
. This dependence is summarized by
\boldsymbol{\tau}
, which depends on both
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})
and
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})
. Note that if
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}
are uncorrelated, then
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\mathbf{0}
and all confounding effects
\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
contribute to noise
\psi
without introducing bias. In the next section, we introduce a linear structure to define the relationship between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}
.

3.2
Linear Confounding Model

In the previous section, we demonstrated that the bias
\tau
introduced by confounding depends on the joint distribution of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}
. In this section, we propose a linear relationship between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}
, which allows us to derive
\boldsymbol{\tau}
in closed form.

Let
\mathbf{d}
denote a random vector of length
p
, with
\mathbf{d}
independent of
\mathbf{z}
, and
\mathbf{A}
be a fixed
p\times q
matrix. Consider the following data generating mechanism, which we refer to as the
linear confounding model
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}y&=\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\epsilon,\\
\mathbf{x}&=\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z},\\
\mathbf{d},\mathbf{z}&\sim\textrm{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{p}),\quad\epsilon\sim\textrm{N}(0,\sigma_{e}^{2}),\end{split}

(4)

where
\mathbf{d}
,
\mathbf{z}
, and
\epsilon
are mutually independent. In this model
\mathbf{A}
determines the correlation between the confounders
\mathbf{z}
and features
\mathbf{x}
. Note that the confounders influence both the features and the response. Since
\mathbf{z}
are drawn from a standard normal distribution, we have
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})=\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{I}_{p}
. Combining equations (
2
) and (
4
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\tau}&=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})^{-1}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\boldsymbol{\gamma}\\
&=(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{A}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\boldsymbol{\gamma}\\
&=(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})^{-1}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\gamma}.\end{split}

(5)

Likewise, the additional noise added to the model by the confounders is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{V}(\psi\,|\,\boldsymbol{\tau})&=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{z})\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\tau}-2\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\tau}\\
&=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\boldsymbol{\tau}-2\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\tau}.\end{split}

(6)

The linear confounding assumption that
\mathbf{z}
follows a standard Gaussian distribution, and thus that the confounders are independent, is not as restrictive as it seems. Suppose that we are in the presence of batch effects and
\mathbf{z}
indicates which batch an instance came from; for example,
\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}=[0\quad 1\quad 0]
for an instance in the second of three batches. Clearly,
\mathbf{z}
is no longer Gaussian; furthermore,
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\neq\mathbf{I}_{p}
. However, there exists an equivalent linear confounding model with different values of
\mathbf{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. Specifically, if we rotate
\mathbf{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
by
\left\{\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\right\}^{1/2}
, we can compute
\boldsymbol{\tau}
from equation (
5
) as

\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},

where
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}\left\{\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\right\}^{1/2}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\left\{\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\right\}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. In other words, since the covariance structure of
\mathbf{z}
can be absorbed into
\mathbf{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, it suffices to consider the
\mathbf{z}\sim\textrm{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
case without loss of generality. The linear confounding model is easily modified to produce features that are standardized (mean zero, unit variance); see supplemental material.

3.3
Decomposition of Signal, Bias, and Noise

In simulation studies, the signal-to-noise ratio is a standard quantity that characterizes the difficulty of the problem. In our setting, the presence of unobserved confounding introduces the additional complication of bias. To account for this more complex situation, below we define two additional measures: the bias-to-noise ratio and the bias-to-signal ratio. Explicitly defining these quantities allows us to vary one while holding others fixed — for example, adjusting the bias-to-signal ratio while keeping the signal-to-noise ratio constant. We have found that the ability to isolate effects in this manner is critical to designing clear, interpretable simulations.

Section
3.1
illustrated how unobserved confounding can be decomposed into bias and noise. Given the quantities introduced in equations (
3
), (
5
) and (
6
), we define:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Signal-to-Noise Ratio }&=\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\mathbb{V}(\psi\,|\,\boldsymbol{\tau})+1},\\
\text{Bias-to-Signal Ratio }&=\frac{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\tau}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\beta}},\\
\text{Bias-to-Noise Ratio }&=\frac{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\tau}}{\mathbb{V}(\psi\,|\,\boldsymbol{\tau})+1}.\\
\end{split}

(7)

The Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) quantifies the magnitude of the signal relative to the total noise (from both error and confounding) in
\mathbf{y}
. The Bias-to-Signal Ratio (BSR) measures the magnitude of the bias relative to the magnitude of the signal. Lastly, the Bias-to-Noise Ratio (BNR) quantifies the relative magnitude of the bias compared to the noise. With unobserved confounding, both SNR and BNR contribute to the difficulty of the problem. A larger SNR will tend to make the problem easier, but a problem with a high SNR may be difficult if BNR is also high. Note that specifying any two of these three quantities automatically determines the third one. Together, they facilitate the construction of simulation studies and the interpretation of simulation results.

4
Simulation

In this section, we present simulation studies comparing the performance of LASSO, PC-LASSO, and PLMM under different confounding scenarios. Values of the tuning parameter
\lambda
are chosen based on the minimum cross-validation errors (CVE) for all three methods.

4.1
Simulation Design

Throughout, all simulation studies are based on the linear confounding model (
4
). However, because these simulations involve variable selection and penalized regression, some care is needed in the construction of
\mathbf{A}
to avoid misleading results. In particular, if the confounding were such that coefficients were always biased away from zero, this would, this bias would counteract the LASSO’s tendency to shrink estimates toward zero. The two biases would cancel, producing a simulation that is skewed in favor of LASSO.

To resolve this issue, both positive and negative biases need to be incorporated for a more balanced and realistic assessment of LASSO’s estimation performance. Consider the case where the true signals in
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are all positive, and let
\boldsymbol{\delta}
denote LASSO’s shrinkage toward zero. Figure
1
depicts two scenarios for a single signal
\beta
, depending on the direction of the bias
\tau
introduced by confounding. In the left panel, the confounding bias
\tau
is positive, pushing the estimate away from zero. The combined effect of confounding and LASSO yields an estimate of
\beta-\delta+\tau
, with total bias
\delta-\tau
. Here, the confounding bias partially offsets LASSO’s shrinkage, thereby making LASSO look better. In the right panel,
\tau
is in the same direction as LASSO’s shrinkage, pushing the estimate closer to zero. The resulting estimate becomes
\beta-\delta-\tau
, and the total bias is
\delta+\tau
. In this case, the two biases compound, leading to a worse estimate for LASSO. Neither scenario alone accurately captures LASSO’s performance in the presence of confounding. To ensure a fair comparison with other methods, the simulation must balance positive and negative bias across features so that the LASSO’s shrinkage does not create systematic advantages or disadvantages.

The following construction of
\mathbf{A}
avoids the above phenomenon. To illustrate, suppose there are four features, two confounders, and

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
-a&0\\
0&a\\
0&-a\\
\end{bmatrix}.

With this setup, the correlation structure of
\mathbf{x}
is

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&-\rho&0&0\\
-\rho&1&0&0\\
0&0&1&-\rho\\
0&0&-\rho&1\\
\end{bmatrix},

where
\rho=a^{2}/(1+a^{2})
.

In this configuration, the first two features are influenced by the first confounder, while the remaining two are influenced by the second. Features affected by the same confounder are correlated, with parameter
a
controlling the strength of these correlations. The presence of both positive and negative entries in
\mathbf{A}
induces a mixture of positive and negative bias in
\boldsymbol{\tau}
. For the
4\times 2
matrix
\mathbf{A}
in this example and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}=\begin{bmatrix}g&g\end{bmatrix}
, we can apply equation (
5
) to see that
\boldsymbol{\tau}
is evenly balanced between positive and negative biases:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}=\frac{ag}{1+2a^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&1&-1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

This pattern can be generalized to incorporate more features and confounders. For example, if there are 8 features and 2 confounders, then
\mathbf{A}
is the
8\times 2
matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}=\begin{bmatrix}a&a&-a&-a&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&a&a&-a&-a\\
\end{bmatrix},

indicating that the first 4 features are affected by the first confounder. The first 2 of these 4 features are positively correlated with the confounder, while the other 2 features are negatively correlated with the confounder. The same idea applies to the other 4 features, which are affected by the second confounder. The correlation structure of
\mathbf{x}
is therefore an
8\times 8
block-diagonal matrix, where the two off-diagonal matrices are all 0 and the two
4\times 4
matrices on the diagonal are

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho&-\rho&-\rho\\
\rho&1&-\rho&-\rho\\
-\rho&-\rho&1&\rho\\
-\rho&-\rho&\rho&1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

As before,
\boldsymbol{\tau}
is an equal mixture of positive and negative biases, although here, both positive and negative correlations exist among the features. For a general
p\times q
block-diagonal matrix
\mathbf{A}
, each column of
\mathbf{A}
has
m=p/q
nonzero entries, half of which equal
a
, the other half equal to
-a
. The remaining
p-m
entries are zero, indicating that those features are unaffected by the confounder.

With this design of
\mathbf{A}
, if all elements of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
are equal to
g
, then
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\mathbb{V}(\psi\,|\,\boldsymbol{\tau})
are functions of
a
and
g
. However, the magnitude of the bias is not a monotone function of
a
— multiple values of
a
and
g
correspond to the same
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\boldsymbol{\tau}
. To fix this, we reparameterize by specifying the ratio
g=ra
(this causes
\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}\rVert
to be a monotone function of
a
and
r
; see Appendix for details).

This parameterization above allows us to fix
\mathbb{V}(\psi\,|\,\boldsymbol{\tau})=1
throughout and specify the value of BNR, then solve for
a
and
r
. Given these, and assuming
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a vector of length
p
with a specified number of nonzero elements equal to
b
, the signal effect size
b
is then determined by the SNR.

In simulation studies, we kept
\mathbb{V}(\psi\,|\,\boldsymbol{\tau})=1,n=300,p=600
and specified 8 true signals that were randomly chosen among all 600 features. We utilized SNR, BNR, and BSR to investigate how the amount and complexity of unobserved confounding affects model performance. PC-LASSO models were fit using 10 PCs, unless specified otherwise.

4.3
Number of Features

In the previous section, we saw that PC-LASSO and PLMM were essentially unaffected by the presence of unmeasured confounders. This would seem to contradict classical statistical theory, which indicates that unobserved confounders should have a negative impact on estimation and variable selection. Here, we show that these methods are only able to adjust for unmeasured confounding in high-dimensional scenarios.

In Section
2
, we discussed how both PC-LASSO and PLMM adjust for unobserved confounding using information from the observed covariate matrix
\mathbf{X}
. PC-LASSO achieves this by including the first
k
PCs of
\mathbf{X}
as unpenalized covariates, while PLMM models the confounders as random effects and estimates their variance-covariance structure based on
\mathbf{X}
. In this section, we present a simple visualization to illustrate that, in order to adequately account for varying levels of unobserved confounding, both methods require a large number of features, which enables a more accurate approximation of the unobserved confounding.

While larger sample sizes contribute to improved approximation and better estimation, they cannot compensate for insufficient features. That is, if

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a linear confounding model that allows decomposition and explicit control over the amount and complexity of unobserved confounding effects. This enables systematic evaluation of model performance under various confounding scenarios, which provided novel insights into the scenarios in which various approach to deconfounding work best. While we used the linear confounding model here to compare PC-LASSO and PLMM, the framework also lays a foundation for future studies of other methods of adjusting for unobserved confounding.

We carried out extensive simulations using this model and showed that with high-dimensional data, PC-LASSO and PLMM are both effective ways to adjust for unobserved confounding. Both approaches selected fewer false positives and resulted in more accurate estimates than a standard LASSO analysis in situations where confounding is present. Furthermore, we identified scenarios in which method outperforms the other. Overall, PLMM is more robust to the complexity of the confounding structure, as it does not rely on a specific low-dimensional representation. This was particularly noticeable in the analyses involving real data. In addition, while PC-LASSO and PLMM both prevent the inclusion of spurious features in the regression model, PLMM consistently produces more parsimonious models.

Appendix A
Standardization in Linear Confounding Model

For the linear confounding model described in (
4
), we will have
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}
, but the variance of
\mathbf{x}
will not be
\mathbf{1}
. Here, we describe a modified version of the linear confounding model that produces a standardized matrix of features.

The variance-covariance matrix of
\mathbf{x}
is given by

\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{A}\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

Let
\mathbf{S}
be a
p\times p
diagonal matrix with elements
s_{ii}=(v_{ii})^{-1/2}
, where
v_{ii}
is the corresponding element of
\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{x})
; i.e., the variance of feature
i
. Pre-multiplying
\mathbf{x}
by
\mathbf{S}
so the variance-covariance matrix of
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}
is

\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{S}\left(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{A}\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\right)\mathbf{S},

where the diagonal elements are all 1. In other words,
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{X}
is the standardized version of
\mathbf{X}
(mean zero and unit variance).

With the standardized features, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{A}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\right]^{-1}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\boldsymbol{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\right]^{-1}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\gamma}

if
\mathbf{z}
follows a standard normal distribution and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\right]^{-1}\mathbf{S}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},

if
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\neq\mathbf{I}_{p}
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}\left\{\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\right\}^{1/2}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\left\{\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top})\right\}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.
