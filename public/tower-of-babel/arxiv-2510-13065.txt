Absolute indices for determining compactness, separability and number of clusters
Adil M. Bagirov, Ramiz M. Aliguliyev, Nargiz Sultanova, Sona Taheri

Abstract
Finding “true” clusters in a data set is a challenging problem. Clustering solutions obtained using different models and algorithms do not necessarily provide compact and well-separated clusters or the optimal number of clusters. Cluster validity indices are commonly applied to identify such clusters. Nevertheless, these indices are typically relative, and they are used to compare clustering algorithms or choose the parameters of a clustering algorithm. Moreover, the success of these indices depends on the underlying data structure. This paper introduces novel absolute cluster indices to determine both the compactness and separability of clusters. We define a compactness function for each cluster and a set of neighboring points for cluster pairs. This function is utilized to determine the compactness of each cluster and the whole cluster distribution. The set of neighboring points is used to define the margin between clusters and the overall distribution margin. The proposed compactness and separability indices are applied to identify the true number of clusters. Using a number of synthetic and real-world data sets, we demonstrate the performance of these new indices and compare them with other widely-used cluster validity indices.



Keywords:
Cluster analysis, Compactness function, Compactness index, Separability index, Absolute cluster validity index.

1
Introduction

Clustering is an important task in data mining with numerous real-world applications
[
bagkartah2024
,
kaufman1990
,
Mirkin1996
,
Wierzchon2018
]
. Finding an optimal or “true” number of clusters within a data set is a highly challenging problem. As noted in
[
Jain1988
]
, “refining clustering processes to obtain accurate, meaningful results is an art”. Nevertheless, there is no explicit criterion to determine the optimal number of clusters. Usually, this number is defined based on cluster distributions having compact and well-separated clusters. However, such distributions may not be unique for a given data set.

Additional tools, called cluster validity indices, are often utilized to determine the number of the clusters
[
bagkartah2024
,
kaufman1990
,
Rousseeuw1987
,
Franti2009
,
Zhao2014
]
. These indices reflect the compactness and/or separability of clusters. However, most of these indices are relative. They are commonly used to benchmark different clustering algorithms or fine-tune the parameters of a specific algorithm. It is quite common that, in data sets with complex clustering structures, different cluster validity indices perform differently and therefore, generate opposite setups
[
Zimek2020
]
. It was shown in
[
Zimek2020
]
that absolute cluster validity indices are better suited for estimating the compactness and separability of clusters produced by a single clustering algorithm.

In this paper, we propose a novel approach to develop an absolute cluster validity index. We introduce the concept of a compactness function for a cluster, which serves as the basis for assessing cluster compactness. The compactness of the cluster distribution is defined using the compactness of all clusters. For any pair of clusters, we identify a set of neighbouring points to determine the margins between them. These margins are used to measure the pairwise separability of clusters, which is then extended to compute the separability of the overall cluster distribution. Both compactness and separability criteria are utilized to determine the number of clusters. The proposed indices are evaluated using several synthetic and real-world data sets, and their performance is compared against a number of well-known cluster validity indices.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: Section

5
. The problem of finding the number of clusters is discussed in Section

. Finally, Section

2
Related work

Given a finite point set
A=\{{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{a}}_{m}\}
in the
n
-dimensional space
\mathds{R}^{n}
and an integer
k\geq 2
, hard clustering with a predefined similarity measure partitions the set
A
into
k
nonempty, disjoint subsets (clusters)
A_{j},~j=1,\ldots,k
such that their union is
A
.

Determining the optimal number of clusters has been a fundamental challenge in cluster analysis since its early days
[
Hennig2015
,
Milligan1985
]
. The optimal number corresponds to the configurations that provide clusters which are the “most” compact and well-separated. Achieving this requires proper definitions of “compactness” and “separability” of clusters. The distribution of points in most real-world data sets is uneven due to noise, outliers, or other factors. Therefore, the compactness and separability of clusters may vary across the data set, and no unique criterion exists for characterizing the optimal number of clusters.

Objective functions in most optimization based clustering models reflect intra-cluster similarity and inter-cluster dissimilarity. The optimal values of these functions can be considered as an internal criteria for assessing clustering quality, particularly in terms of cluster compactness. However, they may not always effectively measure cluster separability. Recent approaches have emerged to address this limitation. These include parametric modelling of the quantization error
[
Kolesnikov2015
]
, the use of internal cluster validity indices
[
Zhou2018
]
, and a curvature-based algorithm
[
Zhang2017
]
. Additionally,
[
Saha2021
]
proposed an algorithm for identifying the number of clusters during the clustering process. Efforts have also been made to incorporate cluster validity indices into the optimization model of clustering problems. For instance,
[
Batool2021
]
used the average silhouette width as an objective function to optimize separable clusters, and
[
BagAlSu2023
]
employed the silhouette index to develop a model and algorithm for finding compact and well-separated clusters. An optimisation model in
[
BagirovCAOR
]
was introduced to improve cluster separability.

Cluster validity indices are primarily applied to evaluate clustering solution quality, focusing on compactness and separability
[
Rousseeuw1987
,
Bouldin1979
,
Dunn1974
]
. Comparative assessments of these indices are available, for example, in
[
Franti2009
,
Perez2012
,
Gagolewski2021
]
. However, these indices are often utilized as post-clustering evaluation tools, rather than being part of the clustering process itself, and their success may depend on the underlying structure of the data set.

In
[
Zimek2020
]
, an absolute cluster validity index was introduced, based on the geometric measurement of the solution space. This index provides a coherent interpretation of data structure by incorporating inter- and intra-cluster distances, density, and multimodality within clusters.

3
Compactness function

Consider the set
A=\{{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{a}}_{m}\}
, where the points
{\mathbf{a}}_{i},~i=1,\ldots,m
are referred to as observations, and each point has
n
attributes. Let
{\mathbf{x}}
be the center of
A
. The radius
R_{A}
and the average radius
R_{av}
of
A
are defined as:

R_{A}=\max_{{\mathbf{a}}\in A}~d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})~~\mbox{and}~~R_{av}=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{{\mathbf{a}}\in A}d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}}),

(1)

where
d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})
is the Euclidean distance between the points
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{a}}
and
|\cdot|
is the cardinality of a set.
Define the set

S(t)=\Big\{{\mathbf{a}}\in A:~d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})\leq t\Big\},~t\in[0,\infty).

If
{\mathbf{x}}\in A
, then
S(0)=\{{\mathbf{x}}\}
; otherwise
S(0)=\emptyset
. Additionally, we have
S(R_{A})=A
and
S(t)=S(R_{A})
for all
t\in[R_{A},\infty).

Definition 1
.

The compactness function
f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
of the set
A
is defined as:

\displaystyle f(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\quad\mbox{if}~~S(t)=\emptyset,\\
\frac{1}{|S(t)|}\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{a}}\in S(t)}d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}}),&\quad\mbox{if}~~S(t)\neq\emptyset.\end{array}\right.

Proposition 1
.

The compactness function
f
is a non-decreasing step function.

Proof.

Take any
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,R_{A}],~t_{1}<t_{2}
. We show that
f(t_{1})\leq f(t_{2})
. It is clear that
S(t_{1})\subseteq S(t_{2})
. If
|S(t_{1})|=|S(t_{2})|
, then
f(t_{1})=f(t_{2})
. Assume that
S(t_{1})\subset S(t_{2}),~S(t_{1})\neq S(t_{2})
. Denote by
\hat{S}=S(t_{2})\setminus S(t_{1})
. Then
\hat{S}\neq\emptyset
. Let

d_{max}=\max_{{\mathbf{a}}\in S(t_{1})}~d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}}).

It is clear that
d_{max}<d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})
for
{\mathbf{a}}\in\hat{S}
. Then we have

\begin{array}[]{lrl}f(t_{1})&=&\frac{1}{|S(t_{1})|}\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{a}}\in S(t_{1})}d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})\\
&=&\frac{|S(t_{2})|}{|S(t_{1})|}\times\frac{1}{|S(t_{2})|}\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{a}}\in S(t_{1})}d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})\\
&=&\frac{1}{|S(t_{2})|}\Big[\frac{|\hat{S}|+|S(t_{1})|}{|S(t_{1})|}\times\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{a}}\in S(t_{1})}d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})\Big]\\
&=&\frac{1}{|S(t_{2})|}\Big[\frac{|\hat{S}|}{|S(t_{1})|}\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{a}}\in S(t_{1})}d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})+\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{a}}\in S(t_{1})}d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})\Big]\\
&\leq&\frac{1}{|S(t_{2})|}\Big[|\hat{S}|d_{max}+\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{a}}\in S(t_{1})}d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})\Big]\\
&=&\frac{1}{|S(t_{2})|}\Big[|\hat{S}|d_{max}+\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{a}}\in S(t_{2})}d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})-\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{a}}\in\hat{S}}d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})\Big]\\
&<&\frac{1}{|S(t_{2})|}\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{a}}\in S(t_{2})}d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})\\
&=&f(t_{2}).\end{array}

The fact that
f
is a step function follows from the finiteness of the point set
A
. This completes the proof.
∎

Compactness functions

In general, “compactness” refers to the quality of utilizing a small space. When applied to data sets, this concept can be characterized as “dense” and “uniformly distributed.” A data set with many empty spaces indicates low compactness. The compactness function can help identify these sparse regions. In the graphs of the compactness functions for the Liver Disorders and Ionosphere data sets, these regions correspond to constant subintervals. The shorter these constant subintervals are, the higher is the compactness of the data set.

Let
d_{i}=d({\mathbf{a}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}),~i=1,\ldots,m
for
{\mathbf{a}}_{i}\in A
and define the set

\displaystyle D=\Big\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{m}\Big\}.

Sort the distances in ascending order and set
\bar{d}_{0}=0
. Denote the modified set by

{\cal D}=\Big\{\bar{d}_{0},\bar{d}_{1},\bar{d}_{2},\ldots,\bar{d}_{p}\Big\},

where
\bar{d}_{i-1}<\bar{d}_{i},~i=1,\ldots,p,~p\leq m.
If
{\mathbf{x}}\in A
, then
\bar{d}_{1}=\bar{d}_{0}=0
; otherwise
\bar{d}_{1}>0
. Moreover,
\bar{d}_{p}=R_{A}
. Using the set
{\cal D}
, we can construct the following intervals:
(\bar{d}_{i-1},\bar{d}_{i}],~i=1,\ldots,p
. The compactness function
f
is constant on each open interval
(\bar{d}_{i-1},\bar{d}_{i}),~i=1,\ldots,p
. Some intervals are short, while others are much longer (as illustrated in Figure
1
with the graphs of this function for the Liver Disorders and Ionosphere data sets). For a given
\varepsilon>0
, there are intervals
(\bar{d}_{i-1},\bar{d}_{i}]
where
\bar{d}_{i}-\bar{d}_{i-1}\leq\varepsilon
, and others where
\bar{d}_{i}-\bar{d}_{i-1}>\varepsilon
. Based on this, the index set
\{1,\ldots,p\}
can be divided into two groups:

\displaystyle Q_{1}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\Big\{i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}:~\bar{d}_{i}-\bar{d}_{i-1}\leq\varepsilon\Big\},\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle Q_{2}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\Big\{i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}:~\bar{d}_{i}-\bar{d}_{i-1}>\varepsilon\Big\}.

It is obvious that as
\varepsilon
increases, the cardinality of the set
Q_{1}(\varepsilon)
increases, while the cardinality of
Q_{2}(\varepsilon)
decreases. Define the numbers

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}=\min_{i=1,\ldots,p}~\big(\bar{d}_{i}-\bar{d}_{i-1}\big),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\varepsilon_{2}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,p}~\big(\bar{d}_{i}-\bar{d}_{i-1}\big).

If
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
, then
Q_{1}(\varepsilon)=\emptyset
and
Q_{2}(\varepsilon)=\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Conversely, if
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{2}
, we have
Q_{1}(\varepsilon)=\{1,\ldots,p\}
and
Q_{2}(\varepsilon)=\emptyset
. In particular,
Q_{1}(0)=\emptyset
and
Q_{2}(0)=\{1,\ldots,p\}
.

For a given
\varepsilon>0
, define the set

\displaystyle U(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i\in Q_{1}(\varepsilon)}\Big(\bar{d}_{i-1},\bar{d}_{i}\Big].

Some of the intervals
(\bar{d}_{i-1},\bar{d}_{i}],~i\in Q_{1}(\varepsilon)
are connected. By merging such intervals into a single interval, we obtain a new set of
\bar{p}\leq|Q_{1}(\varepsilon)|
intervals. Denote the endpoints of the merged intervals by
r_{j-1}
and
r_{j},~j=1,\ldots,\bar{p}
. Then, the set
U(\varepsilon)
can be represented as:

\displaystyle U(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\bar{p}}\Big(r_{j-1},r_{j}\Big].

Consider the sets

\displaystyle V_{j}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\Big\{{\mathbf{a}}\in A:r_{j-1}<d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})\leq r_{j}\Big\},~j=1,\ldots,\bar{p}.

Note that for any
i\in Q_{2}(\varepsilon)
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{V}_{i}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\equiv\Big\{{\mathbf{a}}\in A:~\bar{d}_{i-1}<d({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{a}})<\bar{d}_{i}\Big\}=\emptyset.

The sets
V_{j}(\varepsilon),j=1,\ldots,\bar{p}
and
\bar{V}_{i}(\varepsilon),~i\in Q_{2}(\varepsilon)
are illustrated in Figure
2
. In this example,
\bar{p}=2
and
Q_{2}(\varepsilon)=\{1\}
. Specifically,
V_{1}(\varepsilon)
corresponds to the inner ball;
V_{2}(\varepsilon)
corresponds to the outer annulus, and
\bar{V}_{1}(\varepsilon)
corresponds to the middle annulus.

4
Compactness index

We define the compactness index using the parameter
\varepsilon
and the sets
V_{j}(\varepsilon),~j=1,\ldots,\bar{p}
and
\bar{V}_{i}(\varepsilon),~i\in Q_{2}(\varepsilon)
. It is important to assess how uniformly the data points are distributed within each non-empty set
V_{j}(\varepsilon),~j\in\{1,\ldots,\bar{p}\}.
However, this becomes challenging when the number of attributes
n
is large. To simplify this, we define a finite number of directions and determine the presence of data points along and around these directions.

Consider the positive spanning set
\mathds{E}=\big\{{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{u}}_{l}\big\},~l>n
in the
n
-dimensional space
\mathds{R}^{n}
where
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\|=1,~j=1,\ldots,l
. This means that any vector in
\mathds{R}^{n}
can be represented as a linear combination of these vectors with nonnegative coefficients. A commonly used positive spanning set is
\mathds{E}=\big\{{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{u}}_{2n}\big\},
where
{\mathbf{u}}_{i}={\mathbf{e}}_{i},~{\mathbf{u}}_{n+i}=-{\mathbf{e}}_{i},~i=1,\ldots,n
and
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
is the
i
-th standard unit vector in
\mathds{R}^{n}
.

Select a parameter
\eta\in(0,1]
, and using the set
V_{j}(\varepsilon)
, define the subset of
\mathds{E}
as:

\bar{E}_{j}=\Big\{i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}:\max_{{\mathbf{a}}\in V_{j}(\varepsilon),{\mathbf{a}}\neq{\mathbf{x}}}\frac{\langle{\mathbf{a}}-{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\rangle}{\|{\mathbf{a}}-{\mathbf{x}}\|\|{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\|}\geq\eta\Big\},~j=1,\ldots,\bar{p}.

Here,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product of vectors in
\mathds{R}^{n}
,
{\mathbf{x}}
represents the center of the set
A
, and
\|\cdot\|
is the norm of a vector. The ratio under the maximum determines the cosine of the angle between vectors
({\mathbf{a}}-{\mathbf{x}})
and
{\mathbf{u}}_{i}
. One can select
\eta\approx 0.7071,
which ensures that the angle between these vectors does not exceed
45^{0}
for each
{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\in\bar{E}_{j}
. If the cardinality of the set
|\bar{E}_{j}|=l
, the data points are nearly uniformly distributed within
V_{j}(\varepsilon)
. If
|\bar{E}_{j}|<l
, there are empty regions in the set
V_{j}(\varepsilon)
.

Definition 2
.

For a given
\varepsilon\geq 0,
the number
\alpha_{j\varepsilon}=|\bar{E}_{j}|/l
is called the
\varepsilon
-compactness coefficient of the set
V_{j}(\varepsilon),~j=1,\ldots,\bar{p}
.

Definition 3
.

For a given
\varepsilon\geq 0,
the number

\displaystyle c_{A}(\varepsilon)=1

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{R_{A}}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{p}}(1-\alpha_{j\varepsilon})\Big(r_{j}-r_{j-1}\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\in Q_{2}(\varepsilon)}\big(\bar{d}_{i}-\bar{d}_{i-1}-\varepsilon\big)\Big]

is called the
\varepsilon
-compactness index of the set
A
.

Using a synthetic data set containing two clusters, depicted in Figure
3
, we illustrate this definition. It is obvious that the blue cluster is more compact than the red cluster. While the points in the red cluster are less dense compared to those in the blue cluster, they are distributed almost uniformly. The compactness index for the blue cluster is 0.5525, whereas it is 0.4546 for the red cluster.

c_{A}(\varepsilon)
has the following properties:

•

If
\varepsilon=0
then
Q_{1}(0)=\emptyset,~\bar{p}=0,~Q_{2}(0)=\{1,\ldots,p\}
and

c_{A}(0)=1-\frac{1}{R_{A}}\sum_{i\in Q_{2}(0)}(\bar{d}_{i}-\bar{d}_{i-1})=0.

•

If
\varepsilon=R_{A},
then
\bar{p}=1,~Q_{1}(\varepsilon)=\{1\}
and
Q_{2}(\varepsilon)=\emptyset
. In this case
V_{1}(R_{A})=A,~r_{1}=R_{A},r_{0}=0
. Let
\alpha_{11}
be the compactness coefficient of the set
A
. Then

c_{A}(R_{A})=1-\frac{1}{R_{A}}(1-\alpha_{11})\Big(r_{1}-r_{0}\Big)=\alpha_{11}.

•

If for some number
b>0
, the set
A
is scaled to
\hat{A}=A/b
, then we get the following ordered distance values:

\hat{{\cal D}}=\Big\{\hat{d}_{0}/b,\ldots,\hat{d}_{p}/b\Big\},

and
c_{A}(\varepsilon)=c_{\hat{A}}(\varepsilon/b)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0

The presence of outliers can significantly deteriorate the compactness of a data set. Since outliers are typically few in number and generally distant from other data points, they can be removed to calculate compactness using the remaining data set. To achieve this, we can use two subsets of the set
\mathcal{D}
. The first subset is defined using the average radius
R_{av}
from (
1
) and the standard deviation
s_{d}
of the set
\mathcal{D}
:
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\big\{\bar{d}\in\mathcal{D}:\bar{d}\leq R_{av}+2s_{d}\big\}.
According to Chebyshev’s inequality
[
Saw1984
]
, at least
75\%
of different distances from the set
\mathcal{D}
fall into this set. The second subset is defined using the median of
\mathcal{D}
as
\mathcal{D}_{2}=\big\{\bar{d}\in\mathcal{D}:\bar{d}\leq\mbox{median}(\mathcal{D})\big\}.
The set
\mathcal{D}_{2}
contains nearly
50\%
of different distance values.

The compactness index depends on
\varepsilon,
making its choice critical. In particular, the following two values of
\varepsilon
can be considered:

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=R_{A}/p,\quad\mbox{and}

(5)

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\mbox{median}\Big\{\bar{d}_{i}-\bar{d}_{i-1},~i=1,\ldots,p\Big\}.

In this paper, we will use the first definition of
\varepsilon
. According to this definition, the compactness index of the Liver Disorders data set is
c_{A}(0.759)=0.2155

5
Adjacent sets and separability of clusters

Given any two finite point sets in the
n
-dimensional space
\mathds{R}^{n},
the boundary between these sets is defined by points that are adjacent between the two sets. The separability of these sets is determined by this set of boundary points. To identify these points, we introduce the concept of adjacent sets between two sets.

5.1
Adjacent sets

Consider two finite point sets
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
in the
n
-dimensional space
\mathds{R}^{n}
. Let
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
be the centers of these sets, respectively. Denote the distance between these centers by
d_{12}=d({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}).

Definition 5
.

The set

Z_{12}=\Big\{{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}:~d({\mathbf{x}}_{2},{\mathbf{a}})\leq d_{12}\Big\}

(6)

is called the subset of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
adjacent to the set
\mathcal{A}_{2}
and the set

Z_{21}=\Big\{{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}:~d({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{b}})\leq d_{12}\Big\}

(7)

is called the subset of
\mathcal{A}_{2}
adjacent to the set
\mathcal{A}_{1}
. The set
\bar{Z}_{12}=Z_{12}\cup Z_{21}
is called the adjacent set between the sets
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
.

It follows from the definition that:

1.

The set
Z_{12}
consists of all points in
\mathcal{A}_{1}
whose distance to the center of
\mathcal{A}_{2}
is less than or equal to the distance between the centers of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
;

2.

Similarly, the set
Z_{21}
includes all points in
\mathcal{A}_{2}
whose distance to the center of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
is less than or equal to the distance between the centers of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
.

To illustrate the adjacent sets, we consider the synthetic data set in Figure
7
(a). This set contains two clusters: red and blue. The adjacent set of these two sets is depicted in Figure
7
(b), where the green points represent the set
\bar{Z}_{12}
.

Next, we compute

\Delta_{12}=\max_{{\mathbf{a}}\in Z_{12}}d({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{a}}),~~\Delta_{21}=\max_{{\mathbf{b}}\in Z_{21}}d({\mathbf{x}}_{2},{\mathbf{b}}).

Definition 6
.

The number

\beta_{12}=d_{12}-\Delta_{12}-\Delta_{21}

is called the margin between sets
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
. These sets are called separable if
\beta_{12}\geq 0
, and non-separable otherwise.

In Figure
7
(b), the two sets are separable, with a margin of
\beta_{12}=0.024
.

Definition 7
.

The number
\bar{\beta}_{12}=\beta_{12}/d_{12}
is called the scaled margin between the sets
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any nonempty finite point sets and
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
be their centers, respectively. Assume that the following conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle d({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{a}})<d({\mathbf{x}}_{2},{\mathbf{a}}),~\forall{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathcal{A}_{1},~~\mbox{and}

\displaystyle d({\mathbf{x}}_{2},{\mathbf{b}})<d({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{b}}),~\forall{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}.

(8)

Then
\bar{\beta}_{12}\in[-1,1]
.

Proof.

It follows from the definition of sets
Z_{12}
and
Z_{21}
, along with the assumption
8
that
d({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{a}})<d({\mathbf{x}}_{2},{\mathbf{a}})\leq d_{12}
for all
{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
d({\mathbf{x}}_{2},{\mathbf{b}})<d({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{b}})\leq d_{12}
for all
{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
. Given that
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
are finite point sets, we have
\Delta_{12}\leq d_{12}
and
\Delta_{21}\leq d_{12}
. This means that

\beta_{12}=d_{12}-\Delta_{12}-\Delta_{21}\geq-d_{12},

which implies that
\bar{\beta}_{12}\geq-1
. Since
\Delta_{12}\geq 0
and
\Delta_{21}\geq 0
, it follows that
\bar{\beta}_{12}\leq 1
. The value
\bar{\beta}_{12}=1
occurs when both
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
are singletons. In this case
\Delta_{12}=\Delta_{21}=0
. Therefore,
\bar{\beta}_{12}\in[-1,1]
.
∎

Since the assumption
8
is satisfied for all clusters in any cluster distribution, Proposition
2
holds for any pair of clusters.

Consider the set
A
and its cluster distribution
\bar{A}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\}
. Let
\beta_{ij}
be the margin between clusters
A_{i}
and
A_{j},i\neq j,~i,j=1,\ldots,k
. The matrix

M=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&\beta_{12}&\cdots&\beta_{1k}\\
\beta_{21}&0&\cdots&\beta_{2k}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\beta_{k1}&\beta_{k2}&\cdots&0\\
\end{array}\right].

is called the margin matrix of the cluster distribution
\bar{A}
. This matrix is symmetric:
\beta_{ij}=\beta_{ji},~i,j=1,\ldots,k
.
