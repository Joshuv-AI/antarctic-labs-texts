Non-asymptotic Analysis of Diffusion Annealed Langevin Monte Carlo for Generative Modelling
Paula Cordero-Encinar, Ö. Deniz Akyildiz, Andrew B. Duncan

Abstract
We investigate the theoretical properties of general diffusion (interpolation) paths and their Langevin Monte Carlo implementation, referred to as diffusion annealed Langevin Monte Carlo ( DALMC ) , under weak conditions on the data distribution. Specifically, we analyse and provide non-asymptotic error bounds for the annealed Langevin dynamics where the path of distributions is defined as Gaussian convolutions of the data distribution as in diffusion models. We then extend our results to recently proposed heavy-tailed (Student’s t t ) diffusion paths, demonstrating their theoretical properties for heavy-tailed data distributions for the first time. Our analysis provides theoretical guarantees for a class of score-based generative models that interpolate between a simple distribution (Gaussian or Student’s t t ) and the data distribution in finite time . This approach offers a broader perspective compared to standard score-based diffusion approaches, which are typically based on a forward Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) noising process.

The goal of generative modelling is to learn the underlying probability distribution

Diffusion models, a particular class of

The learnt scores are then used to sample from the path by discretising the time-reversed diffusion process

2
Generative Modelling via Diffusion Paths

We present the background and setting for our analysis.

2.1
Diffusion Paths

In practice, implementing the reverse process in diffusion models consists in sampling along a path of probability distributions
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, which starts at a simple distribution
\mu_{0}
and ends at an arbitrarily complex data distribution
\mu_{T}=\pi_{\text{data}}
.
In particular, when the forward process is an
OU
process and evolves the data distribution for time
T
, the starting distribution of the reversed process takes the form
{e^{dT}}\pid(e^{T}x)*\ \mathcal{N}(0,(1-e^{-2T})I)
and the interpolated distributions
(\mu_{t})_{t}
are the marginals of the
OU
process.
Building on this, we can describe a more general version of the probability distribution paths that diffusion models attempt to sample from
[
Chehab and Korba 2024
]
, as

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(x)=\frac{\pi_{\text{data}}(x/\sqrt{\lambda_{t}})}{{\lambda_{t}}^{d/2}}*\frac{\nu\left({x}/{\sqrt{1-\lambda_{t}}}\right)}{(1-\lambda_{t})^{d/2}},

(2)

where
*
denotes the convolution operation,
\nu
describes the base or
noising
distribution, and
\lambda_{t}
is an increasing function called schedule, such that,
\lambda_{t}\in[0,1]
and
\lambda_{T}=1
. We refer to the probability path
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
in (
2
) as the
diffusion path
. In the setting of the
OU
(i.e. variance preserving) process,
\lambda_{t}
corresponds to
\lambda_{t}=\min\{1,e^{-2(T-t)}\}
.

The diffusion path with the
OU
schedule has demonstrated very good performance in the generative modelling literature and has recently started to be explored for sampling
[
Huang et al. 2024
;
Richter and Berner 2024
;
Vargas et al. 2024
]
. For instance,
Phillips et al. 2024
empirically observed that the diffusion path may have a more favourable geometry for the Langevin sampler than the geometric path, obtained by taking the geometric mean of the base and target distributions, as is typically done in annealing due to the tractability of the score (Figure
1
).

While successful, the use of the
OU
process presents some challenges in practice.
As mentioned earlier, the forward
OU
process cannot reach
\nu
in finite time, meaning that, in theory the reversed path starts from a non-Gaussian distribution
\mu_{0}
. However, in practice, the paths are initialised from Gaussians, introducing a bias that is present in error bounds
[
Chen et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Benton et al. 2024
]
. In our setting, by selecting an appropriate schedule for the diffusion path (
2
), which satisfies
\lambda_{0}=0
and
\lambda_{T}=1
, the path of probability distributions
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
can interpolate exactly between
\mu_{0}=\nu
and
\mu_{T}=\pid
in finite time, unlike the
OU
process. This formulation is equivalent to that of linear one-sided stochastic interpolants which can also be realised through
SDEs

[
Albergo et al. 2023
, Theorem 5.3]
.

We will next explore an alternative approach for generative modelling with general linear diffusion paths, namely, running annealed Langevin dynamics on paths
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
that are constructed to meet the correct marginals.

2.2
Annealed Langevin Dynamics for Diffusion Paths

For general diffusion paths, the “reverse process” cannot be described by a closed form
SDE
.
While
Albergo et al. 2023
, estimate the intractable drift term of the
SDE
using neural networks, their approach can experience numerical instabilities at
t=T
(see
Albergo et al. 2023
) due to singularities in the drift term.
Therefore, in this work, we focus on
annealed Langevin dynamics

[
Song and Ermon 2019
]
to explicitly implement a sampler along the diffusion path, avoiding the extra control terms introduced in
Albergo et al. 2023
. Note that the score at each time
t
can be learnt via score matching techniques, as in
Song and Ermon 2019
.

Our annealed Langevin dynamics consists of running a time-inhomogeneous Langevin
SDE
, where the drifts are given by the scores of reparametrised probability distributions from the diffusion path
(\hat{\mu}_{t}=\mu_{\kappa t})_{t\in[0,T/\kappa]}
, for some
0<\kappa<1
. That is, we will use the following
SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\quad t\in[0,T/\kappa],

(3)

where
X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}=\nu
and
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion.
We refer to (
3
) as
diffusion annealed Langevin dynamics
.
This strategy does provide a viable alternative to implement interpolation paths as the scores can be learnt.
In particular, we consider the diffusion annealed Langevin Monte Carlo (
DALMC
) algorithm given by a simple Euler-Maruyama discretisation of (
3
) and the use of a score approximation function
s_{\theta}(x,t)

[
Song and Ermon 2019
]
:

X_{l+1}=X_{l}+h_{l}s_{\theta}(X_{l},t_{l})+\sqrt{2h_{l}}\xi_{l},

(4)

where
h_{l}>0
is the step size,
\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
,
s_{\theta}(x,t)
approximates
\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(x)
,
l\in\{1,\dots,M\}
and
0=t_{0}<\dots<t_{M}=T/\kappa
is a discretisation of the interval
[0,T/\kappa]
.

It is important to note that, even if simulated exactly, diffusion annealed Langevin dynamics introduces a bias, as the marginal distributions of the solution of the
SDE
(
3
) do not exactly correspond to
(\hat{\mu}_{t})_{t}
, unlike in the stochastic interpolants formulation
[
Albergo et al. 2023
]
. One of the contributions of our work will be to quantify this bias non-asymptotically.
A key component in determining the effectiveness of the diffusion annealed Langevin dynamics will be the action of the curve of probability measures
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
interpolating between the base distribution and the data distribution, denoted by
\mathcal{A}(\mu)
. As noted by
Guo et al. 2025
, the action serves as a measure of the cost of transporting
\nu
to
\pid
along the given path. Formally, the action of an absolutely continuous curve of probability measures
[
Lisini 2007
]
with finite second-order moment is defined as follows

\mathcal{A}(\mu):=\int_{0}^{T}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{W_{2}(\mu_{t+\delta},\mu_{t})}{|\delta|}.

Based on Theorem 1 from
Guo et al. 2025
, we have that the
KL
divergence between the path measure of the diffusion annealed Langevin dynamics (
3
),
\mathbb{P}_{\text{DALD}}=(p_{t,\text{DALD}})_{t\in[0,T/\kappa]}
, and that of a reference
SDE
such that the marginals at each time have distribution
\hat{\mu}_{t}
,
\mathbb{P}=(\hat{\mu}_{t})_{t\in[0,T/\kappa]}
, can be bounded in terms of the action.
In particular, when
p_{0}=p_{0,\text{DALD}}
, it follows from Girsanov’s theorem that

\kl\left(\mathbb{P}\;||\mathbb{P}_{\text{DALD}}\right)\leq\kappa\mathcal{A}(\mu).

See Theorem
A.3
in Appendix
A
for the proof and further details. Note that by the data processing inequality, we have that
\kl\left(\pid\;||p_{T/\kappa,\text{DALD}}\right)\leq\kl\left(\mathbb{P}\;||\mathbb{P}_{\text{DALD}}\right)
, meaning that the
KL
divergence between the data distribution and the final marginal distribution of the diffusion annealed Langevin dynamics (
3
) is bounded, provided that the action is finite. In that case by choosing
\kappa=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}/\mathcal{A}(\mu))
, we ensure that
\kl\left(\pid\;||p_{T/\kappa,\text{DALD}}\right)\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
.

2.3
Initial Assumptions

In what follows, we will provide an in-depth analysis of the
DALMC

\nu
is Gaussian or multivariate Student’s

Our results in both cases are based on the following assumptions, with additional ones introduced later as necessary.

First, as is typical in the diffusion model literature we require an

;
Chen et al. 2023
]

A 1
.

The score approximation function

satisfies

\sum_{l=0}^{M-1}h_{l}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}_{t}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{l}(X_{t_{l}})-s_{\theta}(X_{t_{l}},t_{l})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon_{score}^{2}.

where
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{M}=T/\kappa
is a discretisation of the interval
[0,T/\kappa]
.

A 2
.

The data distribution

has a finite second-order moment, that is,
M_{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\pid}[\|X\|^{2}]<\infty
.

3
Gaussian Diffusion Paths

In this section, we focus on analysing algorithms to simulate the diffusion path
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
defined in (
2
) when the base distribution
\nu
is Gaussian,
\nu\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{\nu},\sigma^{2}I)
. For simplicity, we will assume that
\pid
has mean
0
and set
m_{\nu}=0
.
This diffusion path has the remarkable property, illustrated in Figure
1
, that when
\pid
has finite log-Sobolev and Poincaré constants, these constants remain uniformly bounded along the entire path, as summarised in the following result.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
\pid
has a finite log-Sobolev constant
C_{\text{LSI}}(\pid)
, respectively Poincaré constant
C_{\text{PI}}(\pid)
, the Gaussian diffusion path
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
defined in (
2
) with base distribution
\nu\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)
satisfies for all

\displaystyle C_{\text{LSI}}(\mu_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{t}C_{\text{LSI}}(\pid)+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{\text{LSI}}(\nu),

\displaystyle C_{\text{PI}}(\mu_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{t}C_{\text{PI}}(\pid)+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{\text{PI}}(\nu),

respectively, where
C_{\text{LSI}}(\nu)=C_{\text{PI}}(\nu)=\sigma^{2}
.

The proof follows immediately from
Chewi 2024
. This result is highly favourable, as, unlike geometric annealing
[
Chehab et al. 2025
]
, the log-Sobolev and Poincaré constants remain uniformly bounded along the entire path by the worst constant independently of the distance between
\pid
and
\nu
.
We can visually observe this in Figure
1
, when the data distribution is given by a mixture of a Gaussian and a smoothed uniform distribution,
\pid=(1-e^{-m^{2}/4)}\mathcal{N}(m,1)+e^{-m^{2}/4}u_{m}
,
where
u_{m}
is the smoothed uniform distribution on
I_{m}=[-m,2m]
for
m=10

[
Chehab et al. 2025
]
.

Motivated by this, we analyse the diffusion annealed Langevin dynamics (

) to simulate from (
2
).

3.1
Analysis of the Gaussian Diffusion Path

We start by analysing the properties of the
Gaussian diffusion path

(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

Smoothness of
(\mu_{t})_{t}
.

We consider the following assumption on the Lipschitz continuity of the scores
\nabla\log\mu_{t}
.

A 3
.

For all
t\in[0,T]
, the scores of the intermediate distributions
\nabla\log\mu_{t}(x)
are Lipschitz with finite constant
L_{t}
.

2
and
3
are sufficient for one of our non-asymptotic analyses of the Gaussian diffusion path (Theorem
3.4
).
However,
3
is generally difficult to verify.
Therefore, we introduce two
alternative
assumptions,
4
and
5
, which separately ensure that
(\nabla\log\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfies assumption
3
. In particular, we show that
4
is satisfied by a mixture of Gaussians with different covariances, given certain conditions on the covariances. While assumption
5
is shown to hold for heavy-tailed data distributions.

A 4
.

The data distribution
\pi_{\text{data}}
has density with respect to Lebesgue, which we write
\pi_{\text{data}}\propto e^{-V_{\pi}}
. The potential
V_{\pi}
has Lipschitz continuous gradients, with Lipschitz constant
L_{\pi}
. In addition,
V_{\pi}
is strongly convex outside of a ball of radius
r
with convexity parameter
M_{\pi}>0
, that is,

\inf_{\|x\|\geq r}\nabla^{2}V_{\pi}\succcurlyeq M_{\pi}I,\quad\inf_{\|x\|<r}\nabla^{2}V_{\pi}\succcurlyeq-L_{\pi}I.

In Lemma
B.1
of the Appendix, we demonstrate that assumption
4
extends the standard assumption on the data distribution that
\pid
is modelled as a convolution of a compactly supported distribution
\tilde{\pi}
and a Gaussian, see, e.g.,
Saremi et al. 2024
or
Grenioux et al. 2024
, under some conditions on the compact support of
\tilde{\pi}
.
Additionally, we prove that a mixture of Gaussians with different covariances satisfies assumption
4
under some mild conditions on the covariances (see Lemma
B.4
and Remark
B.5
for a further discussion).
However, Lemma
B.6
shows that, in general, a mixture of Gaussians cannot be expressed as a convolution of a compactly supported measure with a Gaussian.
We consider the results regarding the mixture of Gaussians to hold independent significance, as we could not find explicit results in the literature addressing the smoothness properties in this case.

Leveraging the existence of a smooth strongly convex approximation of
V_{\pi}

[
Ma et al. 2019
]
and the Holley-Stroock perturbation lemma
[
Holley and Stroock 1987
]
, we show that under
4
,
\pi_{\text{data}}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with a finite constant which itself implies a finite Poincaré constant (Lemma
B.7
) – which is sufficient for Proposition
3.1
to hold. As a consequence,
4
implies that the data distribution
\pid
has finite second order moment (i.e.
4

\Rightarrow

2
).

On the other hand, heavy-tailed data distributions, such as Student’s
t
-like distributions, do not satisfy assumption
4
, since their potential
V_{\pi}
is not strongly convex outside of a ball. Specifically, the Hessian of the potential tends to zero as
\|x\|
tends to infinity.
We provide the following alternative assumption for heavy-tailed data distributions.

A 5
.

The data distribution
\pid
has density with respect to Lebesgue, which we write
\pi\propto e^{-V_{\pi}}
. The potential
V_{\pi}
has Lipschitz continuous gradients, with Lipschitz constant
L_{\pi}
. In addition,
\nabla^{2}V_{\pi}(x)
decays to 0 with order
\mathcal{O}(\|x\|^{-2}I)
as
\|x\|
tends to
\infty
. That is, outside of a ball of radius
r

-\frac{I}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\|x\|^{2}}\preccurlyeq\nabla^{2}V_{\pi}(x)\preccurlyeq\frac{I}{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\|x\|^{2}}\;\;\|x\|>r,

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

In Appendix
B.2
we show that multivariate Student’s

\pid(x)\propto\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}(x-\mu)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right)^{-(\alpha+d)/2}

5
.

The following Lemma establishes that assumption
3
holds when the data distribution satisfies either
4
or
5
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under
4
or
5
, we have that for all
t\in[0,T]
the score
\nabla\log\mu_{t}(x)
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{t}
provided in the proof (i.e.

\Rightarrow

3
and
5

\Rightarrow

3

An important element in the proof, given in Appendix
B.3

.
Notably, these bounds improve those in

5
Related Work

Score-based generative models.

Our approach is similar to earlier generative modelling techniques based on annealed Langevin dynamics
[
Song and Ermon 2019
]
, which inspired the advancement of diffusion models.
The existing literature analysing these Langevin Monte Carlo algorithms is limited.

Block et al. 2022
derive an error bound in Wasserstein distance that scales exponentially with the data dimension, while
Lee et al. 2022
establish a non-asymptotic bound in total variation, which is weaker than our bound in
KL
, as implied by Pinsker’s inequality.

On the other hand, the convergence of diffusion models
[
Song et al. 2021
;
Ho et al. 2020
]
has been extensively studied.
Early results either established non quantitative bounds
[
Pidstrigach 2022
]
, relied on restrictive assumptions about the data distribution, such as functional inequalities
[
Lee et al. 2022
]
, or exhibited exponential dependence on the problem parameters
[
De Bortoli 2022
]
.
Recent works have established polynomial convergence bounds under more relaxed assumptions
[
Chen et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Benton et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
]
. In particular,
Chen et al. 2022
introduce two bounds on the
\kl
error: a linear bound in the data dimension under smoothness conditions along the entire diffusion path, and a second one which scales quadratically with
d
, achieved through early stopping and the assumption of a finite second-order moment on
\pid
. In contrast,
Benton et al. 2024
provide a bound that is linear in the data dimension, up to logarithmic factors, assuming only that the data distribution has a finite second-order moment. Their proof exploits the specific structure of the
OU
process to control the error arising from discretising the reverse
SDE
.

Stochastic interpolants.

Stochastic interpolants
[
Albergo et al. 2023
]
are generative models that unify flow-based and diffusion-based methods. These models make use of a broad class of continuous-time stochastic processes designed to bridge any two arbitrary probability density functions exactly in finite time, akin to our work.
Specifically, the formulation of linear one-sided stochastic interpolants
[
Albergo et al. 2023
;
Gao et al. 2024
]
, which interpolate between a Gaussian and the data distribution, is equivalent to the Gaussian diffusion path (
2
).
Unlike our approach, they incorporate intractable control terms into the drift of the
SDE
to ensure the marginals have the desired distributions. This may result in numerical instabilities caused by singularities in the drift at
t=T

[
Albergo et al. 2023
, Section 6]
. In contrast, we implement the diffusion path using Langevin dynamics. Furthermore, their theoretical analysis does not include explicit non-asymptotic convergence bounds.

Tempering.

Tempering
[
Swendsen and Wang 1986
;
Geyer 1992
;
Marinari and Parisi 1992
]
is a well-known technique in the sampling literature that involves sampling the system at multiple temperatures: starting with higher temperatures to facilitate transitions between modes, gradually cooling the system to focus on the local structure of the target distribution.
The sequence of tempered target distributions is typically defined using the geometric path, as it can be computed in closed form when the target density is known up to a normalising constant.
Recently, several works have established theoretical guarantees for the convergence of geometric annealed Langevin Monte Carlo for non-log-concave distributions. In particular,
Guo et al. 2025
provides a bound on the
\kl
similar to that of Theorem
3.4
. However, they are unable to obtain a closed-form expression for the action of the path.
Besides,
Chehab et al. 2025
derive upper and lower convergence bounds for the
\kl
of the marginals, based on functional inequalities assumptions.
In particular, they demonstrate that in some cases the log-Sobolev constant of the intermediate distributions along the path can deteriorate exponentially compared to those of the base and data distributions, unlike for the diffusion path.

6
Conclusions

In this work we provided a rigorous non-asymptotic analysis of Diffusion Annealed Langevin Monte Carlo (
DALMC
) for generative modelling, focusing on both Gaussian and heavy-tailed diffusion paths. By examining general diffusion paths that interpolate between complex data distributions and simpler base distributions, we have obtained theoretical insights into the convergence behaviour of
DALMC
under a range of assumptions.
For Gaussian diffusion paths, we derived explicit non-asymptotic path-wise error bounds in KL divergence, improving upon prior results by relaxing smoothness assumptions and addressing the bias introduced through discretisation. Extending the framework to heavy-tailed diffusion paths, such as those based on Student’s
t
-distributions, we presented the first theoretical guarantees for these models, demonstrating comparable complexity to Gaussian diffusion paths under mild conditions.

Our analysis highlighted how smoothness assumptions, such as Lipschitz continuity of the scores and properties of the data distribution (e.g., bounded second-order moment and convexity or heavy-tailed behaviour), naturally ensure bounded action and efficient convergence. This generalisation broadens the applicability of
DALMC
beyond the settings considered in prior work. While
DALMC
introduces some bias compared to reverse
SDE
implementations, it avoids numerical instabilities and provides a simpler approach, making it a compelling alternative for score-based generative modelling, in certain settings.

Looking ahead, further work could focus on developing more efficient numerical schemes, reducing dimensional dependencies in error bounds, and applying this framework to other generative models.

Appendix A
Background

We introduce some concepts from optimal transport and the Girsanov theorem which will be useful for the subsequent analysis.

Optimal transport.

Let
v=(v_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a vector field and
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{t\in[a,b]}
be a curve of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite second-order moments.
\mu
is generated by the vector field
v
if the continuity equation

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\mu_{t}v_{t})=0,

holds for all
t\in[a,b]
. The metric derivative of
\mu
at
t\in[a,b]
is then defined as

\left|\dot{\mu}\right|_{t}:=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{W_{2}(\mu_{t+\delta},\mu_{t})}{\left|\delta\right|}.

If
\left|\dot{\mu}\right|_{t}
exists and is finite for all
t\in[a,b]
, we say that
\mu
is an absolutely continuous curve of probability measures.

Ambrosio and Kirchheim 2000
establish weak conditions under which a curve of probability measures with finite second-order moments is absolutely continuous.

By
Ambrosio et al. 2008
we have that among all velocity fields
v_{t}
which produce the same flow
\mu
, there is a unique optimal one with smallest
L^{p}(\mu_{t};X)
-norm. This is summarised in the following lemma.

Lemma A.1

(Lemma 2 from
Guo et al. 2025
)
.

For an absolutely continuous curve of probability measures
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{t\in[a,b]}
, any vector field
(v_{t})_{t\in[a,b]}
that generates
\mu
satisfies
\left|\dot{\mu}\right|_{t}\leq\|v_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{t})}
for almost every
t\in[a,b]
. Moreover, there exists a unique vector field
v_{t}^{\star}
generating
\mu
such that
\left|\dot{\mu}\right|_{t}=\|v_{t}^{\star}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{t})}
almost everywhere.

We also introduce the action of the absolutely continuous curve
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[a,b]}
since it will play a key role in our convergence results. In particular, we define the action
\mathcal{A}(\mu)
as

\mathcal{A}(\mu):=\int_{a}^{b}\left|\dot{\mu}\right|_{t}^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

Girsanov’s theorem.

Consider the
SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}B_{t},

for
t\in[0,T]
, where
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Denote by
\mathbb{P}^{X}
the
path measure
of the solution

of the
SDE
, which characterises the distribution of
X
over the sample space
\Omega
.

The
\kl
divergence between two path measures can be characterised as a consequence of Girsanov’s theorem
[
Karatzas and Shreve 1991
]
. In particular, the following result will be central in our analysis.

Lemma A.2
.

Consider the following two
SDEs
defined on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=a_{t}(X)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad\quad\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=b_{t}(Y)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad\quad t\in[0,T]

with the same initial conditions
X_{0},Y_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
. Denote by
\mathbb{P}^{X}
and
\mathbb{P}^{Y}
the path measures of the processes
X
and

, respectively. It follows that

\kl(\mathbb{P}^{X}\|\mathbb{P}^{Y})=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathbb{P}^{X}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|a_{t}(X)-b_{t}(X)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right].

Proof.

Let

Lemma B.3

By Lemma

Recall that

Because

Intuitively, this means that for sufficiently large

Taking the limit as

The final step is to note that

To analyse each term in the previous sum, we first consider the case where one covariance matrix majorises the other. Specifically, without loss of generality, we assume that for the pair
\{i,j\}\in D_{1}
we have
\Sigma_{i}\succ\Sigma_{j}
, which implies
\Sigma_{j}^{-1}-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\succ\alpha I
for some
\alpha>0
. We observe that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}x^{\intercal}\left(\Sigma_{j}^{-1}-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\right)x+x^{\intercal}\left(\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\mu_{j}-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\alpha\|x\|^{2}+2\|x\|\left(\left\|\Sigma_{j}^{-1/2}\right\|_{2}^{2}\|\mu_{j}\|+\left\|\Sigma_{i}^{-1/2}\right\|_{2}^{2}\|\mu_{i}\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\alpha\|x\|^{2}+2\|x\|\left(\sigma_{j,\min}^{-1}\|\mu_{j}\|+\sigma_{i,\min}^{-1}\|\mu_{i}\|\right),

(9)

which gives

\displaystyle\frac{w_{i}w_{j}p_{i}p_{j}\|x\|^{2}}{(\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{w_{i}w_{j}p_{i}p_{j}\|x\|^{2}}{(w_{i}p_{i})^{2}}=\frac{w_{j}p_{j}\|x\|^{2}}{w_{i}p_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{j}\det(\Sigma_{i})^{1/2}}{w_{i}\det(\Sigma_{j})^{1/2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mu_{j}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\mu_{j}-\mu_{i}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}\right)}\|x\|^{2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\intercal}\left(\Sigma_{j}^{-1}-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\right)x+x^{\intercal}\left(\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\mu_{j}-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}\right)}\xrightarrow{\|x\|\to\infty}0.

(10)

On the other hand, when
\Sigma_{j}^{-1}-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}
is neither positive-definite nor negative-definite, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we can write
x=\|x\|u
, where
u
is a unit vector satisfying
u\in P_{+}^{(j,i)}
or
u\in P_{-}^{(j,i)}
because
P_{0}^{(j,i)}
is empty by definition for
\{i,j\}\in D_{1}
.
These two cases can be treated simultaneously since the indices
i,j
are interchangeable. Without loss of generality, we assume that
u\in P_{+}^{(j,i)}
. Following a similar approach to equations (
9
) and (
10
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{w_{i}w_{j}p_{i}p_{j}\|x\|^{2}}{(\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i})^{2}}\leq\frac{w_{i}w_{j}p_{i}p_{j}\|x\|^{2}}{(w_{i}p_{i})^{2}}=\frac{w_{j}p_{j}\|x\|^{2}}{w_{i}p_{i}}\xrightarrow{\|x\|\to\infty}0.

Therefore, for every unit vector
u\in S^{d-1}
, the limit of each term in the sum over
\{i,j\}\in D_{1}
along the line
\|x\|u
is zero as
\|x\|
tends to
\infty
. Since the sum contains finitely many terms, this implies

\lim_{\|x\|\to\infty}f_{D_{1}}(\|x\|u)=\lim_{\|x\|\to\infty}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in D_{1}}\frac{w_{i}w_{j}p_{i}(\|x\|u)p_{j}(\|x\|u)}{(\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i}(\|x\|u))^{2}}\left\|M_{\|x\|u}+M_{\|x\|u}^{\intercal}\right\|_{2}=0.

Since
f_{D_{1}}
is a continuous function and
S^{d-1}
is compact, the behaviour of
f_{D_{1}}
can be controlled uniformly across all directions. That is, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
R>0
such that
\|x\|>R
implies

\left|\sum_{\{i,j\}\in D_{1}}\frac{w_{i}w_{j}p_{i}p_{j}}{(\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i})^{2}}\|M_{x}+M_{x}^{\intercal}\|_{2}\right|<\varepsilon.

(2)

For
\{i,j\}\in D_{2}\cup D_{3}\cup D_{4}
, following the same reasoning as above, the limit of the spectral norm of each term when
\|x\|
tends to
\infty
along the directions
u\in P_{+}^{(j,i)}
or
u\in P_{-}^{(j,i)}
is
0
.
To analyse the limit along the directions
u\in P_{0}^{(j,i)}
, we consider the following cases.

•

\{i,j\}\in D_{2}
. For every
x
such that
x=\|x\|u
with
u\in P_{0}^{(j,i)}
, we have that
\left(\Sigma_{i}^{-1}-\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\right)u=0_{d}
. Consequently,
M_{x}=0_{d\times d}
, which demonstrates that the limit along these directions is
0
.

•

\{i,j\}\in D_{3}
. Take
u\in P_{0}^{(j,i)}
, by condition
(ii)
we have that either
u^{\intercal}\left(\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\mu_{j}-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}\right)<0
or
u^{\intercal}\left(\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\mu_{j}-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}\right)>0
. Without loss of generality, assume that
u^{\intercal}\left(\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\mu_{j}-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}\right)<0
. Then for every
x=\|x\|u
we have

\displaystyle\frac{w_{i}w_{j}p_{i}p_{j}}{(\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i})^{2}}\|M_{x}+M_{x}^{\intercal}\|_{2}\leq 10\left(\left\|\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\right)^{2}\frac{w_{j}p_{j}}{w_{i}p_{i}}\|x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 10\left(\left\|\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\right)^{2}\frac{w_{j}\det(\Sigma_{i})^{1/2}}{w_{i}\det(\Sigma_{j})^{1/2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mu_{j}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\mu_{j}-\mu_{i}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}\right)}\|x\|^{2}e^{\|x\|u^{\intercal}\left(\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\mu_{j}-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}\right)}\xrightarrow{\|x\|\to\infty}0.

•

\{i,j\}\in D_{4}
. Take
u\in P_{0}^{(j,i)}
, by condition
(iii)
, there exists
k
such that
u\in P_{+}^{(j,k)}
or
u\in P_{+}^{(i,k)}
. These two cases are symmetric and can be treated together. Without loss of generality, assume
u\in P_{+}^{(j,k)}
. In this case, following a similar argument to those in equations (
9
) and (
10
), we have that for every
x=\|x\|u

\frac{w_{i}w_{j}p_{i}p_{j}}{(\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i})^{2}}\|M_{x}+M_{x}^{\intercal}\|_{2}\leq 10\left(\left\|\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\right)^{2}\frac{w_{j}p_{j}}{w_{k}p_{k}}\|x\|^{2}\xrightarrow{\|x\|\to\infty}0.

Since the sum in equation (
8
) contains a finite number of terms, we have that for every
u\in S^{d-1}

\lim_{\|x\|\to\infty}\left\|C_{\|x\|u}\right\|_{2}=0.

Furthermore, because the function
\|C_{x}\|_{2}
is continuous and
S^{d-1}
is compact, the limit
\lim_{\|x\|\to\infty}\left\|C_{x}\right\|_{2}
exists and is equal to zero.
Consequently,
\|C_{x}\|_{2}
is bounded for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which concludes that
\nabla\log\pi
is Lipschitz.

To complete the proof, we need show that
-\nabla\log\pi
is strongly convex outside of a ball of radius
r
. Using the same technique as above, we analyse the spectral norm of
B_{x}
. Let us define the following sets

\displaystyle D_{5}

\displaystyle=\{\{i,j\}|1\leq i,j\leq M,\Sigma_{i}\mu_{i}\neq\Sigma_{j}\mu_{j}\;\text{and}\;\nexists\;u\;|\;\|u\|=1\;\text{and}\;u^{\intercal}\left(\Sigma_{i}^{-1}-\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\right)u=0\},

\displaystyle D_{6}

\displaystyle=\{\{i,j\}|1\leq i,j\leq M,\Sigma_{i}\mu_{i}\neq\Sigma_{j}\mu_{j}\;\text{and}\;\exists\;u\;|\;\|u\|=1\;\text{and}\;u^{\intercal}\left(\Sigma_{i}^{-1}-\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\right)u=0\;\text{and}\;(ii)\;\text{holds}\},

\displaystyle D_{7}

\displaystyle=\{\{i,j\}|1\leq i,j\leq M,\Sigma_{i}\mu_{i}\neq\Sigma_{j}\mu_{j}\;\text{and}\;\exists\;u\;|\;\|u\|=1\;\text{and}\;u^{\intercal}\left(\Sigma_{i}^{-1}-\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\right)u=0\;\text{and}\;(iii)\;\text{holds}\},

it follows that

\|B_{x}\|_{2}\leq\max_{\{i,j\}\in D_{5}\cup D_{6}\cup D_{7}}\left\|\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}-\Sigma_{j}^{-1}\mu_{j}\right\|^{2}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in D_{5}\cup D_{6}\cup D_{7}}\frac{w_{i}w_{j}p_{i}p_{j}}{(\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i})^{2}}.

By applying the same reasoning as above, we find that for each pair in the sum

\lim_{\|x\|\to\infty}\frac{w_{i}w_{j}p_{i}p_{j}}{(\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i})^{2}}=0.

Since there is a finite number of pairs
\{i,j\}
in
D_{5}\cup D_{6}\cup D_{7}
, we can conclude that

\lim_{\|x\|\to\infty}\|B_{x}\|_{2}=0.

Thus, as
\|x\|
tends to
\infty
, the only term whose spectral norm does not vanish is

A_{x}=\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}}{\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i}}\succcurlyeq\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i}\sigma_{i,\max}^{-1}}{\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i}}I\succcurlyeq\min_{i}\{\sigma_{i,\max}^{-1}\}I.

Therefore, we can conclude that
\pi
is strongly log-concave outside of a ball, and hence satisfies assumption
4
.
∎

Remark B.5
.

A concurrent work
[
Gentiloni-Silveri and
Ocello 2025
]
examines the smoothness of a mixture of Gaussians with covariances of the form
\Sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2}I
, a specific case that satisfies the assumption of Lemma
B.4
.
However, their result does not extend to the case of general covariance matrices.
In particular, the following example, which falls outside the assumptions of Lemma
B.4
, serves as a counterexample. Let
\pi=\frac{1}{2}(p_{1}+p_{2})
, where
p_{i}=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma_{i})
with
\mu=(1,0)
and

\Sigma_{1}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&2\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\Sigma_{2}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&3\\
\end{pmatrix}.

We can show that
\nabla^{2}\log\pi((x,0))
is unbounded when
\|x\|\to\infty
. To establish this, first note that

p_{1}((x,0))=(2\pi\sqrt{3})^{-1}e^{-(x-1)^{2}},\quad p_{2}((x,0))=(2\pi\sqrt{6})^{-1}e^{-(x-1)^{2}}.

Substituting this into the expression of the Hessian given above (
7
) we have

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}\log\pi((x,0))=\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}2\sqrt{2}+1&\sqrt{2}\\
\sqrt{2}&2\sqrt{2}+3\end{pmatrix}-(x-1)^{2}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3+\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

which is clearly unbounded as
\|x\|\to\infty
, meaning that
\nabla\log\pi
is not Lipschitz continuous.

Besides, in general a mixture of Gaussians with different covariances does not satisfy assumption
11
.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
\pi=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i})
be a mixture of Gaussians in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If either one of the two following assumptions holds

(i)

There exists at least one covariance matrix
\Sigma_{i}
that cannot be expressed as
\Sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i}I
.

(ii)

There exists at least one pair
\{i,j\}
such that
\Sigma_{i}\neq\Sigma_{j}
.

Then,
\pi
does not satisfy assumption
11
.

Proof.

We want to determine if
\pi(x)
can be written as

\pi(x)=(h*\gamma)(x),

where
h
is a compactly supported measure and
\gamma
is a Gaussian distribution
\gamma=\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2}I)
for some
\tau^{2}
.
Assume that
\pi(x)=(h*\gamma)(x)
, we will show that
h
cannot be compactly supported for any
\tau^{2}
.

Since the convolution in real space corresponds to multiplication in Fourier space, we have

\hat{\pi}(k)=\hat{h}(k)\hat{\gamma}(k)

where
\hat{\pi}(k),\hat{h}(k),\hat{\gamma}(k)
denote the respective Fourier transforms, which have the following expressions

\hat{\pi}(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}e^{-\frac{1}{2}k^{\intercal}\Sigma_{i}k-i\mu_{i}^{\intercal}k},\quad\hat{\gamma}(k)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\tau^{2}k^{\intercal}Ik}.

Then, the function
\hat{h}(k)
has to satisfy

\hat{h}(k)=\frac{\hat{\pi}(k)}{\hat{\gamma}(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}e^{-\frac{1}{2}k^{\intercal}(\Sigma_{i}-\tau^{2}I)k-i\mu_{i}^{\intercal}k}.

Note that
\tau^{2}
needs to satisfy
\tau^{2}I\preccurlyeq\Sigma_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,M
as otherwise the inverse Fourier transform of
\hat{h}
would not yield a real-valued function.
Under this condition, we have that

h(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}-\tau^{2}I)

and since, by assumption, there exist either an index
i
such that
\Sigma_{i}\neq\sigma_{i}I
, or a pair
\{i,j\}
such that
\Sigma_{i}\neq\Sigma_{j}
, then
h
cannot be compactly supported for any choice of
\tau^{2}
.

Note that when neither condition
(i)
nor
(ii)
holds, we can take
h=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\delta_{\mu_{i}}
, where
\delta_{\mu_{i}}
denotes a Dirac delta function centred at
\mu_{i}
.
∎

One final implication of assumption
4
is provided in the following proposition.

Proposition B.7

(Proposition 1
[
Ma et al. 2019
]
)
.

If
\pid
satisfies assumption
4
, then it has a finite log-Sobolev constant
C_{\text{LSI},\pid}\leq\frac{2}{M_{\pi}}e^{16L_{\pi}r^{2}}
.

Proof.

Recall that under
4
we have that
\pi_{\text{data}}\propto e^{-V_{\pi}}
satisfies

\inf_{\|x\|\geq r}\nabla^{2}V_{\pi}\succcurlyeq M_{\pi}I,\quad-L_{\pi}I\preccurlyeq\nabla^{2}V_{\pi}\preccurlyeq L_{\pi}I.

By
Ma et al. 2019
, there exists
\tilde{V}_{\pi}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\tilde{V}_{\pi}
is
M_{\pi}/2
strongly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and has a Hessian
\nabla^{2}\tilde{V}_{\pi}
that exists everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore, using the Bakry-Émery criterion
[
Bakry and Émery 1985
]
,
\tilde{\pi}\propto e^{-\tilde{V}_{\pi}}
satisfies log-Sobolev inequality with constant
C_{\text{LSI},\tilde{\pi}}\leq 2/M_{\pi}
.
Moreover, Lemma 1 in
Ma et al. 2019
also guarantees that

\sup\left(\tilde{V}_{\pi}(x)-V_{\pi}\right)-\inf\left(\tilde{V}_{\pi}(x)-V_{\pi}\right)\leq 16L_{\pi}r^{2}.

Applying the Holley-Stroock perturbation principle
[
Holley and Stroock 1987
]
, it follows that
\pid
has a finite log-Sobolev constant satisfying

C_{\text{LSI},\pid}\leq\frac{2}{M_{\pi}}e^{16L_{\pi}r^{2}}.

∎

B.2
Comments on Assumption
5

We show below that assumption
5
is satisfied by multivariate Student’s
t
distributions of the form

\pid(x)=C_{\pi}\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}(x-\mu)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right)^{-(\alpha+d)/2},\;\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where the covariance matrix
\Sigma
is a positive definite matrix satisfying
\sigma_{\min}I\preccurlyeq\Sigma\preccurlyeq\sigma_{\max}I
and
\alpha>0
denotes the degrees of freedom.
The Hessian of the potential has the following expression

-\nabla^{2}\log\pid(x)=\frac{\alpha+d}{\alpha}\frac{\Sigma^{-1}}{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}(x-\mu)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)}-\frac{2(\alpha+d)}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)(x-\mu)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}}{\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}(x-\mu)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right)^{2}}.

The matrix
(x-\mu)(x-\mu)^{\intercal}
is positive semidefinite and satisfies

0\preccurlyeq(x-\mu)(x-\mu)^{\intercal}\preccurlyeq\|x-\mu\|^{2}I.

Since the eigenvalues of the product of symmetric positive semidefinite matrices satisfy the following

\lambda_{\min}(ABC)\geq\lambda_{\min}(A)\lambda_{\min}(B)\lambda_{\min}(C),\quad\lambda_{\max}(ABC)\leq\lambda_{\max}(A)\lambda_{\max}(B)\lambda_{\max}(C),

we have that

0\preccurlyeq\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)(x-\mu)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}\preccurlyeq\sigma_{\min}^{-2}\|x-\mu\|^{2}I.

This leads to

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}\log\pid(x)

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\frac{\alpha+d}{\alpha}\frac{\Sigma^{-1}}{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}(x-\mu)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)}\preccurlyeq\frac{(\alpha+d)\sigma_{\min}^{-1}}{\alpha}\frac{I}{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\|S^{-1/2}U(x-\mu)\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\frac{(\alpha+d)\sigma_{\min}^{-1}}{\alpha+{\sigma_{\max}^{-1}}\|x-\mu\|^{2}}I,

where we have used that
\Sigma^{-1}=U^{\intercal}S^{-1}U
,
U
being an orthogonal matrix and
S
being diagonal.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}\log\pid(x)

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq\frac{(\alpha+d)\sigma_{\max}^{-1}}{\alpha+{\sigma_{\min}^{-1}}\|x-\mu\|^{2}}I-\frac{2(\alpha+d)\sigma_{\min}^{-2}}{\sigma_{\max}^{-1}}\frac{\sigma_{\max}^{-1}\|x-\mu\|^{2}}{\left({\alpha+{\sigma_{\max}^{-1}}\|x-\mu\|^{2}}\right)^{2}}I

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq\frac{(\alpha+d)\sigma_{\max}^{-1}}{\alpha+{\sigma_{\min}^{-1}}\|x-\mu\|^{2}}I-\frac{2(\alpha+d)\sigma_{\min}^{-2}}{\sigma_{\max}^{-1}}\frac{I}{{\alpha+{\sigma_{\max}^{-1}}\|x-\mu\|^{2}}}.

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq-\frac{2(\alpha+d)\sigma_{\min}^{-2}}{\sigma_{\max}^{-1}}\frac{I}{{\alpha+{\sigma_{\max}^{-1}}\|x-\mu\|^{2}}},

which concludes that
\pid
satisfies assumption
5
.

B.3
Proof of Lemma
3.2

Before jumping into the proof of Lemma
3.2
, we provide a generalisation of the Poincaré inequality that will be required later.

Lemma B.8
.

Let
\pi
be a probability distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite Poincaré constant
C_{\text{PI},\pi}
. Then, for all functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
, it holds that

\text{Cov}_{X\sim\pi}\left[f(X)\right]\preccurlyeq C_{\text{PI},\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\nabla f(X)\nabla f(X)^{\intercal}\right].

Proof.

Given the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
.
For every unit vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, consider the function
u^{\intercal}f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Since
\pi
satisfies a Poincaré inequality we have

u^{\intercal}\text{Cov}_{X\sim\pi}\left[f(X)\right]u=\text{Cov}_{X\sim\pi}\left[u^{\intercal}f(X)\right]\leq C_{\text{PI},\pi}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi}\left[\left\|\sum_{i}u_{i}\nabla f_{i}(X)\right\|^{2}\right]=C_{\text{PI},\pi}u^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\nabla f(X)\nabla f(X)^{\intercal}\right]u,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.2
under assumption
4
.

To simplify notation in the proof, we denote
\pi_{\text{data}}
as
\pi
.
Let
\mu_{t}\propto e^{-U_{t}}
, our aim is to show that
\nabla U_{t}
is Lipschitz, to do so we are going to show that the Hessian
\nabla^{2}U_{t}
is bounded for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Using that if
f
and
g
are differentiable functions then
\nabla(f*g)=(\nabla f)*g=f*(\nabla g)
, we have the following expressions for
\nabla U_{t}
and
\nabla^{2}U_{t}

\displaystyle\nabla U_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{t})}\left(x-\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,x}(y)}\left[Y\right]\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,x}(y)}\left[\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}\right)\right]

(11)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}U_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{t})}\left(I-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{t})}\text{Cov}_{\rho_{t,x}}\left[Y\right]\right)

(12)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}U_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{t}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,x}}\left[\nabla^{2}V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}\right)\right]-\text{Cov}_{\rho_{t,x}}\left[\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}\right)\right]\right)

(13)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}U_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{t})\lambda_{t}}\text{Cov}_{\rho_{t,x}}\left[Y,\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}\right)\right],

(14)

where
\rho_{t,x}(y)\propto e^{-V_{\pi}(y/\sqrt{\lambda_{t}})-\|x-y\|^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{t}))}
.
It is worth mentioning that as
t\to 0
,
\rho_{t,x}
tends to a Dirac delta centred at
0
, and as
t\to T
, it approaches a Dirac delta centred at
x
, both of which have zero variance.
Note that (
12
) and (
13
) admit the following upper bounds

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}U_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{t})}I

(15)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}U_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\frac{L_{\pi}}{\lambda_{t}}I,

(16)

where we have used that the covariance matrix is positive semidefinite.
To find a lower bound for
\nabla^{2}U_{t}
we need to upper bound
\text{Cov}_{\rho_{t,x}}\left[Y\right]
and
\text{Cov}_{\rho_{t,x}}\left[\nabla V_{\pi}(Y/\sqrt{\lambda_{t}})\right]

\rho_{t,x}
satisfies the Poincaré inequality with constant
C_{\text{PI},\rho_{t}}
independent of
x

B.8

\displaystyle\text{Cov}_{\rho_{t,x}}

\displaystyle\left[Y\right]\preccurlyeq C_{\text{PI},\rho_{t}}I

\displaystyle\left[\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}\right)\right]\preccurlyeq\frac{C_{\text{PI},\rho_{t}}}{\lambda_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,x}}\left[\nabla^{2}V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}\right)\nabla^{2}V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}\right)^{\intercal}\right]\preccurlyeq\frac{C_{\text{PI},\rho_{t}}L_{\pi}^{2}}{\lambda_{t}}I.

This implies that for each

we have that

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}U_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\min\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{t})},\frac{L_{\pi}}{\lambda_{t}}\right\}I=:a_{t}I

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}U_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{t})}\left(1-\frac{C_{\text{PI},\rho_{t}}}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{t})}\right),-\frac{L_{\pi}}{\lambda_{t}}\left(1+\frac{C_{\text{PI},\rho_{t}}L_{\pi}}{\lambda_{t}}\right)\right\}I=:b_{t}I.

(18)

Therefore, the Lipschitz constant
L_{t}
satisfies the following

(21)

independent of

\displaystyle\kl\left(\mathbb{P}\;||\mathbb{Q}\right)\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{M}\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}L_{\kappa t}^{2}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|X_{t}-X_{t_{-}}\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}\|v_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{t})}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=1}^{M}\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}\left(dh_{l}L_{\kappa t}^{2}+h_{l}^{2}L_{\kappa t}^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pid}\left[\left\|X\right\|^{2}\right]+d\right)+dh_{l}^{2}L_{\kappa t}^{3}\right)\mathrm{d}t.

To bound
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|X_{t}-X_{t_{-}}\right\|^{2}\right]
we note that under
\mathbb{P}
, for
t\in[t_{l-1},t_{l})
, we have

X_{t}-X_{t_{-}}=\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t}(\nabla\log{\hat{\mu}}_{\tau}+v_{\tau})(X_{\tau})\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2}(B_{t}-B_{t_{l-1}}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|X_{t}-X_{t_{-}}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t}(\nabla\log{\hat{\mu}}_{\tau}+v_{\tau})(X_{\tau})\mathrm{d}\tau\right\|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\sqrt{2}(B_{t}-B_{t_{l-1}})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim(t-t_{l-1})\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t}\left(\left\|\nabla\log{\hat{\mu}}_{\tau}\right\|_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{\tau})}^{2}+\left\|v_{\tau}\right\|_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{\tau})}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\tau+d(t-t_{l-1})

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{l}\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}\left(\left\|\nabla\log{\hat{\mu}}_{\tau}\right\|_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{\tau})}^{2}+\left\|v_{\tau}\right\|_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{\tau})}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\tau+dh_{l},

where the second inequality arises from the application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the last inequality is due to the definition
h_{l}=t_{l}-t_{l-1}
.
Taking the integral over
t\in[t_{l-1},t_{l}]
, it follows

\displaystyle\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}{L}_{\kappa t}^{2}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|X_{t}-X_{t_{-}}\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t\lesssim

\displaystyle\left(\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}{L}_{\kappa t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\right)\left(h_{l}\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}\left(\left\|\nabla\log{\hat{\mu}}_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{t})}^{2}+\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{t})}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t+dh_{l}\right).

Putting this together we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{M}\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}L_{\kappa t}^{2}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|X_{t}-X_{t_{-}}\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}\|v_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{t})}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{l=1}^{M}\left(\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}L_{\kappa t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\right)\left(h_{l}\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}\left(\left\|\nabla\log{\hat{\mu}}_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{t})}^{2}+\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{t})}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t+dh_{l}\right)+\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}\|v_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{t})}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{l=1}^{M}\left(\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}L_{\kappa t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\right)\left(h_{l}\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}\left(dL_{\kappa t}\ +\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{t})}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t+dh_{l}\right)+\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}\|v_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{t})}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{l=1}^{M}\left(1+h_{l}^{2}\max_{[t_{l-1},t_{l}]}L_{t}^{2}\right)\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}\left|\dot{\hat{\mu}}\right|_{t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t+\left(dh_{l}\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}L_{\kappa t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\right)\left(1+h_{l}\max_{[t_{l-1},t_{l}]}L_{t}\right).

(27)

This results in the following bound for the
KL
divergence between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
:

\displaystyle\kl\left(\mathbb{P}\;||\mathbb{Q}\right)\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{M}\left(1+h_{l}^{2}\max_{[t_{l-1},t_{l}]}L_{t}^{2}\right)\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}\left|\dot{\hat{\mu}}\right|_{t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t+\left(dh_{l}\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}L_{\kappa t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\right)\left(1+h_{l}\max_{[t_{l-1},t_{l}]}L_{t}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=1}^{M}\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}\left(dh_{l}L_{\kappa t}^{2}+h_{l}^{2}L_{\kappa t}^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pid}\left[\left\|X\right\|^{2}\right]+d\right)+dh_{l}^{2}L_{\kappa t}^{3}\right)\mathrm{d}t

Note that intuitively we want to take smaller steps
h_{l}=t_{l}-t_{l-1}
when
L_{t}
is larger.
Define
h=\max_{l\in\{1,\dots,M\}}h_{l}
, we can further simplify the previous expression to obtain

\displaystyle\kl\left(\mathbb{P}\;||\mathbb{Q}\right)\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{M}(1+h^{2}L_{\max}^{2})\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}\left|\dot{\hat{\mu}}\right|_{t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\ +dh_{l}(1+hL_{\max})\int_{t_{l-1}}^{t_{l}}L_{\kappa t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t+\frac{Th^{2}L_{\max}^{2}}{\kappa}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pid}\left[\left\|X\right\|^{2}\right]+d\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+h^{2}L_{\max}^{2})\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}\left|\dot{\hat{\mu}}\right|_{t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\ +dh(1+hL_{\max})\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}L_{\kappa t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t+\frac{Th^{2}L_{\max}^{2}}{\kappa}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pid}\left[\left\|X\right\|^{2}\right]+d\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+h^{2}L_{\max}^{2})\kappa\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}(\mu)+\frac{dh}{\kappa}(1+hL_{\max})\int_{0}^{T}L_{t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t+\frac{Th^{2}L_{\max}^{2}}{\kappa}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pid}\left[\left\|X\right\|^{2}\right]+d\right).

The step size
h
can be expressed in terms of the number of steps
M
and
\kappa
as
h\asymp\frac{1}{M\kappa}
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\kl\left(\mathbb{P}\;||\mathbb{Q}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1+\frac{L_{\max}^{2}}{M^{2}\kappa^{2}}\right)\kappa\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}(\mu)+\frac{d}{M\kappa^{2}}\left(1+\frac{L_{\max}}{M\kappa}\right)\int_{0}^{T}L_{t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t+\frac{L_{\max}^{2}}{M^{2}\kappa^{3}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pid}\left[\left\|X\right\|^{2}\right]+d\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1+\frac{L_{\max}^{2}}{M^{2}\kappa^{4}}\right)\kappa C_{\lambda}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pid}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]+d\right)+\frac{d}{M\kappa^{2}}\left(1+\frac{L_{\max}}{M\kappa}\right)\int_{0}^{T}L_{t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t,

where we have used the bound on the action derived in Lemma
3.3
and
T=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

To derive the previous bound, we have assumed that the score of the intermediate distributions
\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}
, can be computed exactly. In practice, however, we use an approximation, introducing an additional error term into the analysis. Let
s_{\theta}(\cdot,t)
denote our estimator for
\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}
and let
\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}
be the path measure of the continuous-time interpolation of the
DALMC
algorithm (
4
). We conclude that

\displaystyle\kl\left(\mathbb{P}\;||\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})-s_{\theta}(X_{t_{-}},t_{-})+v_{t}(X_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t_{-}}(X_{t_{-}})+v_{t}(X_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t_{-}}(X_{t_{-}})-s_{\theta}(X_{t_{-}},t_{-})\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{L_{\max}^{2}}{M^{2}\kappa^{4}}\right)\kappa C_{\lambda}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pid}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]+d\right)+\frac{d}{M\kappa^{2}}\left(1+\frac{L_{\max}}{M\kappa}\right)\int_{0}^{T}L_{t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=0}^{M-1}h_{l}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}_{t}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{l}(X_{t_{l}})-s_{\theta}(X_{t_{l}},t_{l})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\;\left(1+\frac{L_{\max}^{2}}{M^{2}\kappa^{4}}\right)\kappa C_{\lambda}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pid}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]+d\right)+\frac{d}{M\kappa^{2}}\left(1+\frac{L_{\max}}{M\kappa}\right)\int_{0}^{T}L_{t}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t+\varepsilon_{\text{score}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\;\left(1+\frac{L_{\max}^{2}}{M^{2}\kappa^{4}}\right)\kappa(M_{2}\vee d)+\frac{d}{M\kappa^{2}}\left(1+\frac{L_{\max}}{M\kappa}\right)L_{\max}^{2}+\varepsilon_{\text{score}}^{2},

where we have used the control of the score approximation given in assumption
1
.
∎

B.8
Proof of Theorem
3.6

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{Q}
denote the path measure associated with the continuous-time interpolation of the modified
DALMC
algorithm with exact scores (
25
), which corresponds to the SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t_{-}}(X_{t_{-}})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\;t\in\left[0,T/\kappa\right],

where given a discretisation of the interval
\left[0,T/\kappa\right]
,
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{M}=T/\kappa
, we define
t_{-}:=t_{l-1}
when
t\in[t_{l-1},t_{l})
for
l=1,\dots,M
. On the other hand, let
\mathbb{P}
be the path measure corresponding to the following reference SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=(\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}+v_{t})(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\;t\in\left[0,T/\kappa\right].

As in the proof of Theorem
3.4
, the vector field
v=(v_{t})_{t\in\left[0,T/\kappa\right]}
is designed such that
X_{t}\sim\hat{\mu}_{t}
for all
t\in\left[0,T/\kappa\right]
and
\|v_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\hat{\mu}_{t})}=\left|\dot{\hat{\mu}}\right|_{t}
.
Using Girsanov’s theorem we have

\displaystyle\kl\left(\mathbb{P}\;||\mathbb{Q}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t_{-}}(X_{t_{-}})+v_{t}(X_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t_{-}}(X_{t_{-}})\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T/\kappa}\|v_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\hat{\mu}}_{t})}\mathrm{d}t.

Note that the first term involves both a time and space discretisation error.
Inspired by
Chen et al. 2022
, we start analysing

E_{t,s}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{s}(X_{s})\right\|^{2}\right],

with
0\leq s<t\leq 1
. Recall that we can write

X_{s}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\kappa s}}{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}X_{t}+\sigma\left(\sqrt{1-\lambda_{\kappa s}}-\sqrt{\frac{(1-\lambda_{\kappa t})\lambda_{\kappa s}}{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right)Z=\alpha_{t,s}X_{t}+\tilde{\sigma}_{t,s}Z,

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
independent of
X_{t}
. So, we have

\hat{\mu}_{s}(x)\propto\int\alpha_{t,s}^{-d}\ \hat{\mu}_{t}\left(\frac{y}{\alpha_{t,s}}\right)e^{-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2\tilde{\sigma}_{t,s}}}\mathrm{d}y=\int\hat{\mu}_{t}(y)e^{-\frac{\|x-\alpha_{t,s}y\|^{2}}{2\tilde{\sigma}_{t,s}}}\mathrm{d}y.

Therefore, similarly to the proof of Lemma
3.2
, we can express the score of
\hat{\mu}_{s}
in terms of that of
\hat{\mu}_{t}
,

\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{s}(x)=\alpha_{t,s}^{-1}\ \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\varphi_{t|x,s}}\left[\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(Y)|x,s\right],

where
\varphi_{t|x,s}(y)\propto\hat{\mu}_{t}(y)e^{-\frac{\|x-\alpha_{t,s}y\|^{2}}{2\tilde{\sigma}_{t,s}^{2}}}
.
Substituting this we have

\displaystyle E_{t,s}\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{s}(X_{s})-\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(\alpha_{t,s}^{-1}X_{s})\right\|^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(\alpha_{t,s}^{-1}X_{s})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\varphi_{t|X_{s},s}}\left[(\alpha_{t,s}^{-1}-1)\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(Y)+\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(Y)-\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(\alpha_{t,s}^{-1}X_{s})\right]\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(\alpha_{t,s}^{-1}X_{s})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4(\alpha_{t,s}^{-1}-1)^{2}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\right]+6\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(\alpha_{t,s}^{-1}X_{s})\right\|^{2}\right].

(28)

Using the score expressions provided in Lemma
3.2
Eq. (
11
), we can bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\right]
as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}_{t}}\left[\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{\kappa t})}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,X_{t}}}\left[\left\|\frac{X_{t}-Y}{\sigma\sqrt{1-\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right\|^{2}\right]=\frac{d}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{\kappa t})},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}_{t}}\left[\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{\kappa t}}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,X_{t}}}\left[\left\|\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right)\right\|^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\kappa t}}\mathbb{E}_{\pid}\left[\left\|\nabla V_{\pi}\left(Y\right)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{L_{\pi}}{\lambda_{\kappa t}}d,

where we have used the Lipschitzness of
\pid
.
This provides,
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\right]\leq\min\left\{\frac{d}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{\kappa t})},\frac{L_{\pi}d}{\lambda_{\kappa t}}\right\}
.
Following a similar argument to
Chen et al. 2022
, we study the second term in (
28
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(\alpha_{t,s}^{-1}X_{s})\right\|^{2}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t}+aZ_{t,s})Z_{t,s}\mathrm{d}a\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t}+aZ_{t,s})Z_{t,s}\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}a,

where
Z_{t,s}=\alpha_{t,s}^{-1}X_{s}-X_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\left(\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{t,s}}{\alpha_{t,s}}\right)^{2}I\right)
is independent of
X_{t}
. For simplicity, we denote

\hat{\sigma}_{t,s}=\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{t,s}}{\alpha_{t,s}}=\sigma\left(\sqrt{\frac{(1-\lambda_{\kappa s})\lambda_{\kappa t}}{\lambda_{\kappa s}}}-\sqrt{1-\lambda_{\kappa t}}\right).

To bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t}+aZ_{t,s})Z_{t,s}\right\|^{2}\right]
we consider the following change of variable

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t}+aZ_{t,s})Z_{t,s}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})Z_{t,s}\right\|^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{X_{t}+aZ_{t,s},Z_{t,s}}(X_{t},Z_{t,s})}{\mathrm{d}P_{X_{t},Z_{t,s}}(X_{t},Z_{t,s})}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})Z_{t,s}\right\|^{4}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{X_{t}+aZ_{t,s},Z_{t,s}}(X_{t},Z_{t,s})}{\mathrm{d}P_{X_{t},Z_{t,s}}(X_{t},Z_{t,s})}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Let
M_{t}=\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})\left(\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})\right)^{\intercal}
and
N_{t,s}=Z_{t,s}Z_{t,s}^{\intercal}
, which by definition they are independent. We now need to bound the two previous factors. By the properties of the tensor product, we have

\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})Z_{t,s}\right\|^{4}=\mathbb{E}\left[\text{Tr}\left(M_{t}^{\intercal}Z_{t,s}\right)^{2}\right]=\left\langle\mathbb{E}M_{t}\otimes M_{t},\mathbb{E}Z_{t,s}\otimes Z_{t,s}\right\rangle.

Using the properties of the
\chi^{2}
distribution, we obtain

\mathbb{E}(Z_{t,s}\otimes Z_{t,s})_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}}=\begin{cases}3\hat{\sigma}_{t,s}^{4},\;i_{1}=i_{2}=i_{3}=i_{4},\\
\hat{\sigma}_{t,s}^{4}\;i_{1}\neq i_{2},(i_{1},i_{2})=(i_{3},i_{4})\;\text{or}\;(i_{1},i_{2})=(i_{4},i_{3}),\\
0,\;\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Substituting this we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})Z_{t,s}\right\|^{4}\lesssim\hat{\sigma}_{t,s}^{4}\left(\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2})=(i_{3},i_{4})}+\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2})=(i_{4},i_{3})}\right)\mathbb{E}(M_{t}\otimes M_{t})_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\hat{\sigma}_{t,s}^{4}\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2})=(i_{3},i_{4})}\mathbb{E}(M_{t}\otimes M_{t})_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}}\lesssim\hat{\sigma}_{t,s}^{4}\mathbb{E}\|M_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\lesssim\hat{\sigma}_{t,s}^{4}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})\|_{F}^{4}.

Applying Lemma 12 from
Chen et al. 2022
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}\|\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})\|_{F}^{4}\lesssim\left(\frac{d}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{\kappa t})}\right)^{4}.

This bound becomes arbitrarily large as
\lambda_{\kappa t}
tends to
1
, however, using the alternative expression for the Hessian provided in (
13
), we can write

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\kappa t}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,X_{t}}}\left[\nabla^{2}V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right)\right]-\text{Cov}_{\rho_{t,X_{t}}}\left[\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right)\right]\right),

where
\rho_{t,X_{t}}(y)\propto e^{-V_{\pi}(y/\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa t}})-\|x-y\|^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{\kappa t}))}
.
Due to the Lipschitzness of
\pid
, we have

-L_{\pi}I\preccurlyeq\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,X_{t}}}\left[\nabla^{2}V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right)\right]\preccurlyeq L_{\pi}I,

where the Frobenius norm of the identity matrix is
\|I\|_{F}=\sqrt{d}
.
For the covariance term we proceed as follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{\kappa t}^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}_{t}}\left\|\text{Cov}_{\rho_{t,X_{t}}}\left[\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right)\right]\right\|_{F}^{4}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\kappa t}^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,X_{t}}}\left\|\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right)\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right)^{\intercal}\right\|_{F}^{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\kappa t}^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{T_{\lambda_{\kappa t}^{-1/2}}\#\pid}\left\|\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right)\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right)^{\intercal}\right\|_{F}^{4}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\kappa t}^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{T_{\lambda_{\kappa t}^{-1/2}}\#\pid}\left\|\nabla V_{\pi}\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa t}}}\right)\right\|^{8}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\kappa t}^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{\pid}\left\|\nabla V_{\pi}\left(Y\right)\right\|^{8}\leq\frac{K_{\pi}^{2}}{\lambda_{\kappa t}^{4}}.

Therefore, we have

Next, we bound the term concerning the change of variable

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{X_{t}+aZ_{t,s},Z_{t,s}}(X_{t},Z_{t,s})}{\mathrm{d}P_{X_{t},Z_{t,s}}(X_{t},Z_{t,s})}\right)^{2}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{X_{t}+aZ_{t,s}|Z_{t,s},X_{T_{\kappa}}}(X_{t}|Z_{t,s},X_{T_{\kappa}})}{\mathrm{d}P_{X_{t}|X_{T_{\kappa}}}(X_{t}|X_{T_{\kappa}})}\right)^{2},

where we have used the data processing inequality and
X_{T_{\kappa}}\sim\pid
. Since

, we can explicitly compute the previous expression, as it corresponds to the
\chi^{2}
divergence between two Gaussians, that is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{X_{t}+aZ_{t,s},Z_{t,s}}(X_{t},Z_{t,s})}{\mathrm{d}P_{X_{t},Z_{t,s}}(X_{t},Z_{t,s})}\right)^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{a^{2}\|Z_{t,s}\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(1-\lambda_{\kappa t})}\right)\leq\left(1-2a^{2}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\kappa t}(1-\lambda_{\kappa s})}{\lambda_{\kappa s}(1-\lambda_{\kappa t})}}\right)^{2}\right)^{-d/2},

where we have used the expression of the moment generating function of a
\chi^{2}
distribution. Under the assumption on the schedule and for

, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\log\hat{\mu}_{t}(\alpha_{t,s}^{-1}X_{s})\right\|^{2}\right]\lesssim

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{t,s}^{2}\min\left\{\frac{d^{2}}{\sigma^{4}(1-\lambda_{\kappa t})^{2}},\frac{L_{\pi}^{2}d+K_{\pi}}{\lambda_{\kappa t}^{2}}\right\}

Lemma C.1
.

If
\tilde{\pi}
is supported in a closed Euclidean ball
B_{d}(0,R)
and
\gamma\sim t(0,\tau^{2}I,\alpha)
, then
\pi=\tilde{\pi}*\gamma
satisfies a weighted Poincaré inequality.

Proof.

Recall that

\pi=\tilde{\pi}*\gamma=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{x}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(x),

where
\gamma_{x}\sim t(x,\sigma^{2}I,\alpha)
.
Following
Bardet et al. 2018
, the variance of a function
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
can be decomposed as

\text{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}*\gamma}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\text{Var}_{\gamma_{x}}(f)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(x)+\text{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}}\left(x\mapsto\int f\mathrm{d}\gamma_{x}\right):=A+B.

Since
\gamma_{x}
satisfies a weighted Poincaré inequality with constant
C_{\text{PI},\gamma}
and weight function
\omega=1+\|x\|^{2}

[
Cattiaux et al. 2010a
, Proposition 2.17]
, the first term
A
is bounded by

A\leq C_{\text{PI},\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla f|^{2}\omega\mathrm{d}\gamma_{x}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(x)=C_{\text{PI},\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla f|^{2}\omega\mathrm{d}(\tilde{\pi}*\gamma).

For the second term
B
, consider
g:x\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\mathrm{d}\gamma_{x}
. Using this,
B
can be rewritten as

B=\frac{1}{2}\int\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(g(x)-g(y))^{2}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(x)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(y),

where using Cauchy-Schwartz inequality

(g(x)-g(y))^{2}\leq\text{Var}_{\gamma_{x}}(f)\text{Var}_{\gamma_{x}}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{y}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{x}}\right).

For the first factor, we reapply the weighted Poincaré inequality for the
t
distribution
\gamma_{x}
. The second factor is the
\chi^{2}
divergence between the
t
distributions
\gamma_{x}
and
\gamma_{y}
.

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\gamma_{x},\gamma_{y})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\gamma_{x}(z)}{\gamma_{y}(z)}-1\right)^{2}\gamma_{y}(z)\;\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\gamma_{x}(z)^{2}}{\gamma_{y}(z)}\;\mathrm{d}z-1=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}{\alpha+(z-y)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-y)}\right)^{-(\alpha+d)/2}\gamma_{x}(z)\mathrm{d}z-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1+\frac{(z-y)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-y)-(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}\right)^{(\alpha+d)/2}\gamma_{x}(z)\mathrm{d}z-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1+\frac{y^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}y-x^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}x-2z^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}\right)^{(\alpha+d)/2}\gamma_{x}(z)\mathrm{d}z-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{A=\{\|z-x\|^{2}>1\}}\left(1+\frac{y^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}y-x^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}x-2z^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}\right)^{(\alpha+d)/2}\gamma_{x}(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus A}\left(1+\frac{y^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}y-x^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}x-2z^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}\right)^{(\alpha+d)/2}\gamma_{x}(z)\mathrm{d}z-1.

For
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following holds

(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)=(z-x)^{\intercal}U^{\intercal}S^{-1/2}S^{-1/2}U(z-x)=\|S^{-1/2}U(z-x)\|^{2}\geq 0.

The second integral can be upper bounded as follows

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus A}\left(1+\frac{y^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}y-x^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}x-2z^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}\right)^{(\alpha+d)/2}\gamma_{x}(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus A}\left(\frac{\alpha+(z-y)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-y)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}\right)^{(\alpha+d)/2}\gamma_{x}(z)\mathrm{d}z\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus A}\left(\frac{\alpha+\sigma_{\min}^{-1}\|z-y\|^{2}}{\alpha}\right)^{(\alpha+d)/2}\gamma_{x}(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus A}\left(\frac{\alpha+\sigma_{\min}^{-1}2(\|x-y\|^{2}+\|z-x\|^{2})}{\alpha}\right)^{(\alpha+d)/2}\gamma_{x}(z)\mathrm{d}z\leq\left(\frac{\alpha+\sigma_{\min}^{-1}2(4R^{2}+1)}{\alpha}\right)^{(\alpha+d)/2}=\kappa_{R},

where we have used that
\|x-y\|
is bounded by
2R
.
On the other hand, for
z\in A
we have

\displaystyle 1+\frac{y^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}y-x^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}x-2z^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}=1+\frac{(y-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)-2(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{(y-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}+\frac{2\|(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)\|}{(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{(y-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}+\frac{2\|S^{-1/2}U(z-x)\|\|S^{-1/2}U(y-x)\|}{\|S^{-1/2}U(z-x)\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{(y-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}+\frac{2\|S^{-1/2}U(y-x)\|}{\|S^{-1/2}U(z-x)\|}\leq 1+\frac{4R^{2}\|S^{-1/2}\|^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{4R\|S^{-1/2}\|}{\sigma_{\max}^{-1/2}}\leq 1+\frac{4R^{2}}{\alpha\sigma_{\min}}+\frac{4R\sigma_{\max}^{1/2}}{\sigma_{\min}^{1/2}}.

Therefore, we obtain

\int_{A=\{\|z-x\|^{2}>1\}}\left(1+\frac{y^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}y-x^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}x-2z^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)}{\alpha+(z-x)^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(z-x)}\right)^{(\alpha+d)/2}\gamma_{x}(z)\mathrm{d}z\leq\left(1+\frac{4R^{2}}{\alpha\sigma_{\min}}+\frac{4R\sigma_{\max}^{1/2}}{\sigma_{\min}^{1/2}}\right)^{(\alpha+d)/2}=\beta_{R}

This shows that
\chi^{2}(\gamma_{x},\gamma_{y})
is upper bounded with dependence on
R
of the form
R^{(\alpha+d)}
. In particular,
B
satisfies the following bound

B\leq C_{\text{PI},\gamma}(\kappa_{R}+\beta_{R}-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla f|^{2}\omega\mathrm{d}(\tilde{\pi}*\gamma).

Therefore, the measure
\tilde{\pi}*\gamma
satisfies a weighted Poincaré inequality with constant

C_{\text{PI}}\leq C_{\text{PI},\gamma}(\kappa_{R}+\beta_{R}).

∎

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\tilde{\mu}_{\lambda},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{\lambda+\delta})\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|X_{\lambda}-X_{\lambda+\delta}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|(\sqrt{\lambda}-\sqrt{\lambda+\delta})X\right\|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\left(\sqrt{1-\lambda}-\sqrt{1-\lambda-\delta}\right)\sigma Z\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{\lambda}-\sqrt{\lambda+\delta})^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]+\left(\sqrt{1-\lambda}-\sqrt{1-\lambda-\delta}\right)^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}d\alpha}{\alpha-2}.

Using the definition of the metric derivative we have

\displaystyle\left|\dot{\tilde{\mu}}\right|_{\lambda}^{2}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{W_{2}^{2}(\tilde{\mu}_{\lambda},\tilde{\mu}_{\lambda+\delta})}{\delta^{2}}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]}{4\lambda}+\frac{1}{4(1-\lambda)}\frac{\sigma^{2}d\alpha}{\alpha-2}.

Since
\mu_{t}=\tilde{\mu}_{\lambda_{t}}
, we have that
|\dot{\mu}|_{t}=\left|\dot{\tilde{\mu}}\right|_{\lambda}\left|\partial_{t}{\lambda_{t}}\right|
. Using assumption
10
for the schedule, we have the following expression for the action

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}|\dot{\mu}|_{t}^{2}\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\left|\dot{\tilde{\mu}}\right|_{\lambda}^{2}\left|\partial_{t}{\lambda_{t}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]}{4\lambda_{t}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}\alpha}{4(1-\lambda_{t})(\alpha-2)}d\right)\left|\partial_{t}{\lambda_{t}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]\sqrt{1-\lambda_{t}}}{4\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}\alpha\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}{4\sqrt{1-\lambda_{t}}(\alpha-2)}d\right)\left|\frac{\partial_{t}{\lambda_{t}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{t}(1-\lambda_{t})}}\right|\left|\partial_{t}{\lambda_{t}}\right|\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C_{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]\sqrt{1-\lambda_{t}}}{4\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}\alpha\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}{4\sqrt{1-\lambda_{t}}(\alpha-2)}d\right)\left|\partial_{t}{\lambda_{t}}\right|\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C_{\lambda}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]\sqrt{1-\lambda}}{4\sqrt{\lambda}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}\alpha\sqrt{\lambda}}{4\sqrt{1-\lambda}(\alpha-2)}d\right)\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\lambda}\pi}{8}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]+\frac{\sigma^{2}d\alpha}{\alpha-2}\right).

∎

Lemma C.2
.

Suppose that
p(x)\propto e^{-V(x)}
is a probability density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\nabla V(x)
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L
and let
\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x)
be the density function of a Student’s t distribution
t(0,\sigma^{2}I,\alpha)
.
Then

\left\|\nabla\log\frac{p(x)}{p*\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x)}\right\|\leq L\ \mathbb{E}_{Y|x}\left[\left\|Y-x\right\|\right],

where the distribution of
Y|x
is of the form
\frac{p(y)\varphi_{\sigma}^{2}(x-y)}{p(x)*\varphi_{\sigma}^{2}(x)}
.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\nabla\log p*\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla V(y)e^{-V(y)}\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\|y-x\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha+d}{2}}\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-V(y)}\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\|y-x\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha+d}{2}}\mathrm{d}y}=-\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla V(y)p(y)\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(y)\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y}=-\mathbb{E}_{\gamma_{x,\sigma^{2}}}\left[\nabla V(Y)\right],

where
\gamma_{x,\sigma^{2}}
denotes the probability density

\gamma_{x,\sigma^{2}}(y)=\frac{p(y)\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x-y)}{p(x)*\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x)}.

Using the Lipschitzness of
\nabla V
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\log\frac{p(x)}{p*\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x)}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\gamma_{x,\sigma^{2}}}\left[\nabla V(Y)-\nabla V(x)\right]\right\|\leq L\ \mathbb{E}_{\gamma_{x,\sigma^{2}}}\left[\left\|Y-x\right\|\right].

∎

Lemma C.3
.

With the setting in Lemma
C.2
. Denote
p_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda^{d}p(\lambda x)
for
\lambda\geq 1
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\log\frac{p(x)}{p_{\lambda}*\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x)}\right\|\lesssim

\displaystyle\lambda(\lambda-1)\|x\|+(\lambda-1)\|\nabla V(x)\|+\lambda^{2}L\ \mathbb{E}_{Y|x}\left[\|Y-x\|\right],

where the law of
Y|x
is given by
\frac{p_{\lambda}(y)\varphi_{\sigma}^{2}(x-y)}{p_{\lambda}(x)*\varphi_{\sigma}^{2}(x)}
.

Proof.

Using the triangle inequality,

\left\|\nabla\log\frac{p(x)}{p_{\lambda}*\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x)}\right\|\leq\left\|\nabla\log\frac{p(x)}{p_{\lambda}(x)}\right\|+\left\|\nabla\log\frac{p_{\lambda}(x)}{p_{\lambda}*\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x)}\right\|.

The first term can be bounded as

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\log\frac{p(x)}{p_{\lambda}(x)}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\lambda\nabla V(\lambda x)-\nabla V(x)\right\|\leq\left\|\lambda\nabla V(\lambda x)-\lambda\nabla V(x)\right\|+\left\|\lambda\nabla V(x)-\nabla V(x)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda(\lambda-1)\|x\|+(\lambda-1)\|\nabla V(x)\|.

By the result in Lemma
C.2
, we have the following bound for the second term

\left\|\nabla\log\frac{p_{\lambda}(x)}{p_{\lambda}*\varphi_{\sigma^{2}}(x)}\right\|\lesssim\lambda^{2}L\ \mathbb{E}_{Y|x}\left[\|Y-x\|\right],

where we have used that
\lambda\nabla V(\lambda x)
is
\lambda^{2}L
-Lipschitz and
Y|x
has a distribution of the form

\frac{p_{\lambda}(y)\varphi_{\sigma}^{2}(x-y)}{p_{\lambda}(x)*\varphi_{\sigma}^{2}(x)}.

∎
