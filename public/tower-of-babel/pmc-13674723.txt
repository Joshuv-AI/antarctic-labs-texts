Loss of Acid Ceramidase in Myeloid Cells Protects From Chronic Colitis in IL10‐Deficient Mice
Authors: Keila S Espinoza, Brandon K Dahl, Ella M Ryan, Mary R Gordon, Chelsea L Doll, Marilyn T Marron, Cameron A Beard, Pujarini D Dey, David G Besselsen, Richard J Simpson, Justin M Snider, Justin E Wilson, Pawel R Kiela, Ashley J Snider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPatients with inflammatory bowel disease exhibit elevated expression of acid ceramidase (AC), an enzyme in sphingolipid metabolism. Recent studies have shown that myeloid cells contribute to the elevated expression of AC, such that the conditional loss in these cell types protects from acute colitis. We crossed conditional ASAH1 LyzM CRE knockout mice with IL10 knockout mice to determine the role of AC in a model of spontaneous, chronic colitis. Colon tissues were analyzed for lipids, mRNA, and protein. We performed flow cytometry to determine the role of myeloid AC in recruiting immune cells in disease. In this study, the conditional loss of AC in myeloid cells protected from chronic colitis, reduced markers of inflammation, increased ceramides in the colon, and reduced the inflammatory metabolite sphingosine‐1‐phosphate (S1P). Recruitment of immune cells into intestinal tissue was significantly impaired, specifically recruitment of neutrophils and effector Th1/Th17 T cells. The findings from this study indicate that AC may be a promising therapeutic target for patients with inflammatory bowel disease by modulating sphingolipid metabolism in immune cells.Keywords: acid ceramidase, colitis, inflammatory bowel disease, macrophage, sphingolipid

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is an umbrella term for chronic, non‐resolving intestinal inflammation consisting of two major forms: Crohn's disease (CD), which can affect any portion of the gastrointestinal tract, and ulcerative colitis (UC), which is restricted to the colon. Histologically, CD presents with more extensive inflammation that penetrates deeper intestinal layers, aggregates of immune cells, and discontinuous patchy lesions [1, 2]. Comparatively, UC is characterized by more superficial inflammation and distorted crypts infiltrated by neutrophils. Recent estimates indicate that IBD affects 1.3% of adults [3], approximately twice the prevalence estimated in the early 2000s, with an increasing proportion of diagnoses occurring in pediatric populations [4]. Patients with IBD are at a greater risk (4–10×) of developing colitis‐associated colorectal cancer (CAC) [5, 6], and demonstrate a higher risk of disease‐associated mortality (1.7×) [7]. Although the primary cause of IBD remains unknown, many factors contribute to the onset and severity of disease including diet, the gut microbiome, environmental factors, intestinal infection, and immune cell infiltration [8].

Infiltration and aberrant activation of immune cells are among the many factors that contribute to chronic IBD. Disruption of the tolerogenic intestinal environment is a key feature of disease and compromises the mechanisms that normally maintain immune homeostasis in the intestines. Macrophages, dendritic cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) contribute to this tolerogenic environment through the production of anti‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL10 and TGFβ, suppressing immune cell activation via multiple mechanisms. Disruptions in this tolerogenic environment are a key factor in IBD, where impairment of these immunosuppressive functions or acquisition of pro‐inflammatory phenotypes can promote intestinal inflammation and drive the recruitment and activation of both innate and adaptive immune cells. Among these, neutrophils, effector Th1 and Th17 cells are considered major drivers of intestinal inflammation, though additional immune cells also contribute to the pathogenesis of IBD.

Sphingolipids are bioactive lipids generated in all cells, with roles ranging from membrane structure to cell signaling, including significant roles in inflammation and immune cell trafficking [9, 10, 11]. Ceramide, a bioactive sphingolipid, is often referred to as the hub of sphingolipid metabolism. It can be generated through the action of serine palmitoyl transferase (SPT) and (dihydro)ceramide synthases (CerS), by hydrolysis of complex sphingolipids such as glucosylceramide (GluCer) or sphingomyelin (SM), or by reacylation of sphingosine by CerS. Ceramide can in turn be deacylated by one of five ceramidases to form sphingosine, which can be phosphorylated by sphingosine kinases (SKs) to generate sphingosine‐1‐phosphate (S1P). S1P binds to one of five G‐protein coupled receptors, S1P receptors (S1PRs), to activate signal transduction pathways involved in proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and immune cell trafficking [12]. Several of these sphingolipids and their metabolizing enzymes have been implicated in inflammation [13], and, more specifically, by our group and others, in intestinal inflammation and cancer [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. For example, global loss of SK1 was protective in a mouse model of colitis [15], whereas whole body deletion of either alkaline ceramidase 3 (ACER3) or neutral ceramidase (nCDase) exacerbated colitis [19, 20].

Although the role of S1PRs in regulating immune cell trafficking has been studied extensively [21, 22, 23, 24, 25], specific sphingolipids and their metabolizing enzymes in immune cells have only recently been investigated. In bone marrow transplant studies, deficiency of SK1 [17], but not sphingomyelin synthase 2 (SMS2) [26], in hematopoietic cells conferred partial protection from dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)‐induced colitis. In adoptive transfer models of IBD, T cells deficient in CerS4 [27] or CerS6 [28] reduced neutrophil recruitment and inflammation scores, whereas transfer of T cells overexpressing S1PR1 elicited severe weight loss and colon thickening [29]. Further, adoptive transfer of SPT‐deficient T cells protected from colitis by impairing Th17 cell differentiation [30], and GluCer‐laden nanoparticles rescued colitis in mice with T cell‐specific glucosylceramide synthase (GCS) deficiency [31]. Together, this limited body of literature, which has largely focused on T cells, highlights the complexity of sphingolipid metabolism in IBD and underscores a gap in our understanding of how sphingolipids in myeloid cells contribute to the pathogenesis of IBD.

Acid ceramidase (AC) is a lysosomal enzyme that hydrolyzes ceramides into sphingosine and fatty acids. AC is predominantly expressed in colonic mucosal macrophages in IBD patients and in Mac‐3+ macrophages in murine DSS colitis [32]. Conditional loss of AC in LyzM expressing myeloid cells (ACΔMYE) protected mice from DSS‐induced colitis and reduced tumor burden in azoxymethane (AOM)/DSS‐induced CAC. These studies were the first to provide insight into the role of sphingolipids in myeloid cells in IBD and CAC. However, DSS induces acute, chemically driven injury, and whether myeloid AC contributes to chronic, immune‐mediated colitis remains unknown. In the present study, we therefore examined the role of AC in a physiologically relevant model of spontaneous, chronic colitis, namely IL10‐deficient (IL10−/−) mice. Loss of AC in myeloid cells in IL10−/− mice resulted in reduced M1‐ and M2‐like macrophage populations and impaired the recruitment of neutrophils and effector T cells. These findings highlight the contribution of sphingolipid metabolism in innate immune cells to chronic intestinal inflammation and indicate that AC may serve as a promising therapeutic target for IBD.

Materials and Methods

Animals

We generated AC conditional knockout mice resulting in deletion of the AC gene in the myeloid cell lineage (ACΔMYE ) [32]. We crossed ACΔMYE mice with IL10 deficient mice B6.129P2‐Il10tm1Cgn /J (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME) to generate ACfl / flIL10 −/− and ACΔMYEIL10 −/− mice. Animals were maintained in a temperature‐controlled environment with a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. Male and female littermates were euthanized at 8 or 24 weeks of age. Mice were monitored for body weight, rectal bleeding, and prolapse. Upon euthanasia colon tissues were flash frozen or Swiss‐rolled for histology. Single knockout ACfl / flIL10 +/+ and ACΔMYEIL10 +/+ mice were maintained in a specific pathogen‐free room with room conditions as described. All animal procedures were approved by the University of Arizona Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (protocol #19‐604).

RNA Isolation and qRT‐PCR

RNA was isolated using the PureLink RNA isolation kit (Thermo‐Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to manufacturer instructions. Colon tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer containing β‐mercaptoethanol with glass beads using MP Biomedicals FastPrep‐24. Isolated RNA was quantified via QuickDrop prior to reverse transcription utilizing qScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Quantabio, Beverly, MA) at 1 μg/μL. cDNA was used for quantitative real‐time RT‐PCR performed in triplicate using QuantStudio 7 (Thermo‐Fisher Scientific). Expression of genes of interest was normalized to VIC probe β‐actin mRNA. Catalog numbers for primers and RNA isolation kit used are available in Table S1.

Protein Isolation and ELISAs

Colon tissues were homogenized with glass beads using MP Biomedicals FastPrep‐24 in 400 μL of lysis buffer containing cOmplete EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid)‐free protease inhibitor (Millipore Sigma, Carlsbad, CA). Supernatant and tissue were transferred into Eppendorf tubes and beads were rinsed with an additional 400 μL of buffer. Samples were sonicated and centrifuged at 15 000× g for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatant was collected and stored in aliquots to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles. Protein concentration was quantified by Pierce BCA assay. ELISA buffer and antibody kits for IL1β and CCL5 (Thermo‐Fisher Scientific) were used on tissue lysate according to manufacturer instructions. Catalog numbers for buffer and antibody kits used are available in Table S1.

Histology

Swiss‐rolled colons were incubated in buffered formalin overnight prior to transfer into 70% ethanol. Tissue embedding, slide mounting, and H&E staining were performed by the University of Arizona Cancer Center (UACC) Tissue Acquisition and Cellular/Molecular Analysis Shared Resource (TACMASR). H&E stained slides were analyzed by a veterinary pathologist blinded to the study design and groups. For DAB (3,3′‐diaminobenzidine) staining, slides were deparaffinized and serially rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed at 110°C in sodium citrate buffer for 10 min. Slides were incubated in horseradish peroxidase for 15 min, then in 10% goat serum to block nonspecific binding. Slides were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C. HRP linking was performed using the Rabbit HRP/DAB kit (Abcam, Waltham, MA) followed by DAB substrate development (Thermo‐Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturer instructions. Slides were briefly counterstained in Mayer's hematoxylin prior to serial dehydration and mounting. Slides were imaged at 20× and analyzed using Fiji software by a researcher blinded to the experimental groups. Catalog numbers for antibodies, immunohistochemistry kits, and respective dilutions used are available in Table S1.

FITC‐Dextran Permeability Assay

Four hours prior to euthanasia, mice were administered 600 mg/kg FITC‐dextran (Millipore Sigma) via oral gavage. Blood was collected, coagulated, then centrifuged at 2000× g for 12 min. Serum was collected and diluted 1:5 in PBS. 100 μL of sample was plated in an opaque 96‐well plate and read on a fluorescent plate reader (excitation 485 nm, emission 535 nm).

Lipid Mass Spectrometry

Lipids from colon tissues were extracted as previously described [33]. Tissues were homogenized with glass beads using MP Biomedicals FastPrep‐24 in 2 mL cell extraction solvent (2:3, 70% isopropanol/ethyl acetate). Samples and internal standards were dried under nitrogen in a warmed water bath and resuspended in mobile phase (1 μM ammonium formate, 2% formic acid in methanol). Analyses were conducted by the UACC Analytical Chemistry Shared Resource Core. Data were normalized to total lipid phosphate (Pi) present in the organic phase [34] of the Bligh and Dyer extraction [35] detected by phosphomolybdate assay. Sphingolipid levels are expressed as pmol/nmol of lipid Pi.

Flow Cytometry

Upon euthanasia, blood was collected into EDTA tubes to prevent coagulation. Blood samples were treated twice with RBC lysis buffer (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH). Spleens were excised and mashed on a Petri dish over a 70 μm filter. Splenocytes were treated once with RBC lysis buffer after centrifugation. Colon tissues were collected and processed as previously described [36]. Colons were trimmed of fat and cut longitudinally, then cut in 2 mm pieces. Tissue was then rinsed at least seven times in Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) containing 1% HEPES [4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid] and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to remove mucus. Pieces were then incubated in HBSS containing 1% HEPES, 1% FBS, and 1 μ DTE (dithioerythritol) for 2 × 20 min and strained over a 40 μm filter each time to isolate intraepithelial cells. Pieces were then incubated in ETDA buffer for 2 × 25 min to remove epithelial cells. Pieces were then incubated in Type‐1 collagenase buffer for 1 h, then strained over a 40 μm filter to isolate cells in the lamina propria. After isolation of intestinal immune cells, samples were pelleted and resuspended in 44% room temperature Percoll, then overlaid on 66% room temperature Percoll. Samples were then spun at 1600g for 20 min at room temperature with the brakes turned off. Immune cells were isolated at the 44%/66% interface. For surface staining (CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD19, CD11b, CD11c, MHC II, F480, Ly6G, CD25, Ly6C, CD206), samples were pelleted in a 96‐well round bottom plate and resuspended in FC block for 10 min. Antibody cocktail was incubated at 4° for 1 h. Samples were rinsed and fixed for 10 min in 1% formaldehyde at room temperature, then transferred to FACS tubes. For intracellular staining (FoxP3, IFNγ, IL17A, Granzyme B), samples were first incubated in complete RPMI media containing 4 μM of Monensin (Thermo‐Fisher Scientific) for 3 h at 37°C [37, 38, 39]. After surface staining, samples fixed for 30 min in 1% formaldehyde at room temperature, then rinsed in 1× permeabilization buffer (Thermo‐Fisher Scientific). Intracellular antibodies were added at 1:250 and incubated overnight at 4°C. Samples were rinsed in permeabilization buffer and transferred to FACS tubes. All samples were read on a BD LSR Fortessa provided by the University of Arizona Department of Pediatrics. Data were exported and analyzed using FlowJo v10.8 software (BD Life Sciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). Fluorescence minus one (FMO) was used in gating for FOXP3, Granzyme B, IFNγ, and IL17, and gates were carefully selected to best align with the negative space created by FMO.

Catalog numbers for antibodies, flow cytometry reagents, and respective dilutions used are available in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Analysis Software GraphPad Prism v10.6 (Boston, MA) was used for statistical analysis and graph generation. Data were analyzed using two‐way ANOVA followed by Fisher's LSD multiple comparison post hoc test when multiple groups were compared. An unpaired T‐test was used when only comparing two groups. Outliers were identified using Grubbs' outlier test with an alpha of 5%.

Schematics were generated via BioRender (San Francisco, CA).

Results

Loss of AC in Myeloid Cells Reduces Inflammation in an IL10 −/− Model of Chronic Colitis

To best understand the pathophysiologic relevance of myeloid derived AC as a key driver of chronic colitis, we utilized IL10−/− mice which spontaneously develop colitis, thereby modeling the chronic nature of human disease. Although change in body weight over time did not differ between ACfl/flIL10−/− and ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice (Figure 1A), ACfl/flIL10−/− mice exhibited significantly lower body weight at 24 weeks of age as compared to ACfl/flIL10+/+ mice (Figure 1B). Most notably, final body weights were not different between ACΔMYEIL10−/− and ACΔMYEIL10+/+ mice, suggesting protection from restricted weight gain in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice. Similarly, ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice were protected from splenomegaly and colon shortening (Figure 1C,D), though there were no differences in intestinal permeability (Figure S1). Proinflammatory cytokine (TNFα and IL1β) and chemokine (CXCL1, CXCL2, CCL2, and CCL5) expression significantly increased in colon tissues between 8 and 24 weeks in ACfl/flIL10−/− mice (Figure 1E,F); however, this was ablated in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice. In addition, inflammatory markers for macrophages (F480) and neutrophils (MMP8), as well as intestinal inflammation (COX2 and S100A9) were increased in ACfl/flIL10−/− mice at 24 weeks, but not in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice. Lastly, our study found sex‐based differences in disease where the loss of AC may be more protective in males than in females (Figure S2). Collectively, these data suggest that loss of AC in myeloid cells protects against chronic colitis.

pSTAT3 May Be Regulated by Myeloid Cell AC Expression

Chronic colitis typically results in significantly damaged intestinal architecture. Therefore, we performed histology to determine whether the conditional loss of AC visibly alleviated intestinal inflammation. There was no difference in the intestinal colitis severity score between ACfl/flIL10−/− and ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice at either 8 or 24 weeks (Figure 2A). Expression of COX2, a marker of inflammation, was also similar between genotypes (Figure 2B). Furthermore, the expression of Ki67 and cleaved caspase 3, markers of proliferation and apoptosis, respectively, was not impacted by the conditional loss of AC (Figure 2C,D). Our group and others have demonstrated the importance of sphingolipids, specifically S1P, in STAT3 phosphorylation. Indeed, intestinal pSTAT3 was elevated in ACfl/flIL10−/− mice, but not in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice (Figure 2E). These findings suggest that AC may play a role in mediating pSTAT3 expression, which may be reflected in the decreased inflammatory markers with loss of myeloid AC.

Loss of AC in Myeloid Cells Regulates Sphingolipid Levels in Colon Tissues

As AC degrades ceramide and can provide a substrate for the generation of S1P, we assessed sphingolipids in the colon. Total ceramides were elevated in colon tissues from ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice at 8 and 24 weeks of age (Figure 3A), though this did not reach significance at the 24‐week timepoint. Assessment of specific chain length ceramides revealed significant increases in C16 and C18 ceramides at 8 weeks, with C16 ceramide remaining elevated in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice at 24 weeks (Figure 3B). The sphingoid bases, dihydrosphingosine and sphingosine, were reduced in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice, as were dihydroS1P and S1P at either 8 or 24 weeks (Figure 3C,D). While S1P was reduced at 24 weeks of age by ~20%, this did not reach statistical significance. Analysis of additional sphingolipids including dihydroceramides, generated via de novo sphingolipid synthesis were not altered by loss of AC. Similarly, complex glycosphingolipids, generated by the addition of glucose or galactose to ceramide (hexosylceramides), were also largely unaffected by the loss of AC (Figure S3). In contrast, SMs, which are generated by the addition of phosphocholine to ceramide, exhibited an age dependent reduction in both genotypes. Of note, SMs were elevated in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice as compared to ACfl/flIL10−/− mice at 24 weeks (Figure 3E), specifically C18 and C20 SMs (Figure 3F). Interestingly, lyso‐SM, which can be generated by AC via the diacylation of SM, was also reduced in colon tissues from ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice with chronic disease (Figure 3G). These data emphasize the contribution of sphingolipid metabolism specifically in myeloid cells to colonic sphingolipid metabolism.

Myeloid AC Regulates Both Innate and Adaptive Immune Cell Recruitment to Colon Tissue

Our data indicate that loss of AC reduced the expression of proinflammatory markers, most notably the chemokine CCL5, which we have shown to be downstream of AC in breast cancer cells [40]. To determine the role of myeloid cell AC in immune cell recruitment during colitis, we performed flow cytometry on blood, spleen, and colon tissues in ACfl/flIL10−/− and ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice (gating strategy, Figure S4). Loss of AC in myeloid cells resulted in slight but significant changes in T cells in spleen and blood at 24 weeks and significantly reduced expression of MHC II on B cells in circulation and all tissues examined (Figure S5). Macrophages in the spleen (Figure 4A) and circulating monocytes (Figure 4B) were significantly reduced in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice; however, macrophages in the colon were not altered between genotypes (Figure 4C,D) (representative dot plots, Figure S6). Neutrophils were reduced in the spleen at 8 weeks but not at 24 (Figure 4E), while circulating neutrophils were significantly elevated in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice (Figure 5F). Comparatively, neutrophil infiltration in the colon lamina propria (LP) and intra‐epithelial cell compartments (IEC) were significantly reduced in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice at 24 weeks (Figure 4G,H). These data suggest that while neutrophils may be elevated in circulation, loss of AC in myeloid cells may restrict their recruitment into colonic mucosa, potentially due to reduced chemokine expression by AC‐deficient macrophages (Figure 1).

As innate immune cells, macrophages are the first line of defense against inflammation and pathogens, and can polarize into a spectrum of effector macrophages, simplified as M1‐ or M2‐like cells. Additionally, macrophages shape adaptive immune responses by promoting the activation and expansion of effector T cell subsets. To determine the role of AC in the ability of macrophages to polarize and recruit effector cells during colitis, we first assessed proinflammatory M1‐like and pro‐resolving M2‐like macrophages in circulation, spleen, and colon tissues of ACfl/flIL10−/− and ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice (gating strategy, Figure S7). M1‐like macrophages (Figure 5A–D) were reduced in spleen and colon tissues at 8 weeks in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice, while M2‐like macrophages (Figure 5E–H) were reduced in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice at 8 weeks in circulation and colon tissues (representative dot plots, Figure S8). Overall, the assessment of polarized macrophages demonstrated reduced numbers in colon tissues of ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice at 8 weeks, but not at 24.

Although the overall CD4+CD25+ effector T cell populations were unchanged by loss of AC in myeloid cells, further analysis revealed a reduction in CD8+GranzymeB+ cytotoxic T cells in the spleen at 8 weeks and in colon tissues at both 8 and 24 weeks in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice (Figure S9). Furthermore, while regulatory FOXP3+ Tregs remained unchanged between genotypes, IFNγ+Tregs were predominantly reduced in blood and spleen but not colons of ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice. Similarly, IL17A+Tregs were reduced in the colonic LP of ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice at 8 weeks, but not 24. Interestingly, Th1 T cells were reduced in circulation, spleen, and colon LP at 24 weeks (Figure 6A–D), though Th17 T cells were reduced in the spleen and colon IEC at 8 weeks and in circulation at 24 (Figure 6E–H; representative dot plots, Figure S10). These data suggest that myeloid cell AC may play a role in regulating T cell recruitment and/or differentiation toward Th1/Th17 phenotypes; however, it is challenging to discern whether this may be in a direct or indirect manner and requires further investigation.

Discussion

The role of sphingolipid metabolism in IBD and CAC has been extensively investigated using genetically engineered mouse models and pharmacological inhibitors (reviewed in [41]). However, the specific role of sphingolipid metabolism in immune cells in IBD remains poorly understood. In this study, we sought to define the role of AC in myeloid cells in regulating inflammation and chronic colitis in IL10−/− mice. Our data demonstrate that loss of AC in myeloid cells in vivo reduced inflammation, increased ceramide levels in colonic tissue, and reduced recruitment of immune cells to the colon. The findings from our in vivo model of chronic colitis show that loss of AC in macrophages may reshape the intestinal environment to reduce inflammation, as well as decrease neutrophil and T cell infiltration.

Our lab has now shown in two models of colitis that loss of AC in myeloid cells protected against colitis. We previously showed that loss of AC protected from acute, chemically induced‐DSS colitis [32], and in the current study we showed that loss of AC protected against spontaneous, chronic colitis in IL10 deficient mice. DSS‐induced colitis utilizes the chemical interference of intestinal gap junction proteins to disrupt the intestinal epithelial barrier and is often utilized as a highly reproducible model for acute colitis. In contrast, the IL10 deficient model is variable and represents chronic disease similar to human physiology in IBD with the loss of IL10 signaling. Despite their differences, both lead to a hyperactivated immune response. Where DSS‐induced colitis can be dominated by Th1/Th2 response, IL10 deficiency results in a Th1/Th17 response. In our studies the conditional loss of AC reduced cytokine and chemokine expression in both models. However, the IL10 deficient model decreased IL1β and CCL5, but not IL6 or TNFα. In contrast, CXCL1 and IL17a were reduced by loss of AC in DSS‐induced colitis [32]. Further, our previous studies did not examine subsets of effector immune cells. Here, we demonstrated differences in recruitment of M1 macrophages and effector T cells in the intestines of ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice. IFNγ expressing Th1 cells, key mediators of inflammation and immune cell recruitment in the IL10 deficient model, were significantly reduced in intestinal tissues in chronic colitis, which may in part be due to reduced CCL5 [40]. Notably, while Th17 cells were reduced in the intestinal IEC in mice lacking myeloid cell AC, this was absent at 24 weeks of age. This reduction in Th1 cells in the IL10 deficient model of colitis raises the possibility that the conditional loss of AC may have resulted in similar changes to subsets of T cells in the DSS‐induced colitis model; however, this was not assessed in our previous study. In summary, though cytokines and chemokines were generally reduced in both studies, this study expanded our knowledge as to the role of AC in the recruitment of specific effector cells in chronic colitis.

Previous studies investigating the role of sphingolipids in immune cells in colitis have focused on T cells, with a significant body of literature focusing on the role of S1P receptors (reviewed in [42, 43, 44, 45, 46]). Evidence supporting a role for sphingolipid metabolism in immune cells includes studies demonstrating that loss of CerS4 in T cells exacerbated weight loss in DSS‐induced colitis and increased tumorgenicity in an AOM/DSS model [27]. T cell specific loss of GCS increased disease activity and histological scores and facilitated colon shortening in DSS‐induced colitis [31]. Comparatively, adoptive transfer of T cells lacking either CerS6 [28] or SPT [30] protected against weight loss and histopathology. These data suggest that de novo generation of ceramides in T cells, predominantly C14 and C16 ceramides, can promote inflammation in colitis, whereas GluCer may be protective. We previously demonstrated that loss of AC in myeloid cells protected from weight loss in DSS colitis and reduced tumorgenicity in AOM/DSS induced CAC [32].

In the current study, loss of AC in myeloid cells protected from splenomegaly, colon shortening, and expression of pro‐inflammatory markers. However, the role of AC in inflammatory responses and inflammation‐induced signaling in macrophages remains poorly defined. In our previous study, bone marrow‐derived macrophages lacking AC exhibited reduced secretion of IL6 and TNFα following LPS stimulation [32]. The mechanisms underlying these effects remain to be elucidated and highlight the limited understanding of the relationship between AC and LPS‐mediated signaling through TLR4. There is some evidence for a link between LPS and AC in hepatic tissues from a mouse model of alcohol‐induced cancer [47]. CD14 and AC mRNA expression were both elevated in the hepatic tissues, though the relationship between them was not investigated. In additional in vivo studies, obese mice exhibiting increased cardiac inflammation and TLR4 expression exhibited reduced AC expression [48]. Similarly, in vitro studies using pancreatic islets treated with palmitate and LPS demonstrated increased cell death, reduced neutral ceramidase expression, and elevated TLR4 expression; however, the direct relationship between neutral ceramidase and TLR4 was not established [49]. These hints from the literature suggest interplay between LPS and ceramidases, though this is not currently well understood. Our future directions aim to further investigate the impact of AC in TLR4 signaling in myeloid cells.

Sphingolipids, specifically S1P, have been shown to regulate pSTAT3 expression in colitis. Mice deficient in either SK1 [17] or SMS2 [26] subjected to DSS‐induced colitis exhibited decreased STAT3 phosphorylation, whereas loss of SK2 [16] amplified phosphorylation. Our immunohistochemistry findings indicate that loss of AC may decrease inflammation in colitis in part by reducing phosphorylation of pSTAT3. Although S1P levels were not significantly altered at 24 weeks of age, the observed reduction in S1P at that timepoint may be sufficient to contribute to the decreased pSTAT3 expression. As a known downstream target of S1P signaling, several studies have illustrated that S1P increases phosphorylation of STAT3 [16, 50, 51, 52]. These studies also highlight the potential of therapeutically targeting the S1P‐cytokine‐pSTAT3 axis in IBD using S1P receptor modulators [53]. Furthermore, although Th1 cells have been shown to promote M1 macrophage polarization via pSTAT3 signaling [54], these cells were reduced at 8 weeks of age in ACΔMYE mice. It is also possible that pSTAT3 expression may be decreased due to the reduced recruitment of Th1 T cells and M1 macrophages. Although the present study did not demonstrate changes to histology scoring or COX2 expression, our data indicate that AC may play a role in modulating intestinal STAT3‐dependent pathways.

Our previous study identified that loss of AC in myeloid cells reduced the recruitment of neutrophils but not macrophages to the colons of mice treated with DSS [32]. This study recapitulated these findings and interestingly found that MHC II expression in B cells was significantly downregulated in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice. Macrophages demonstrate the ability to regulate B cell activation and proliferation [55, 56], primarily suppressing B cell activity via IL10 or TGFβ, though the impact of AC deficient macrophages on B cells was outside the scope of this project and remains to be elucidated. Our study was designed to better identify effector macrophages and found significant reductions in M1‐ and M2‐like macrophage populations in the colon. Previous studies assessing loss of sphingolipid enzymes in colitis have shown that CerS4 deficient T cells impaired neutrophil and macrophage recruitment into colon tissues in DSS‐induced colitis, whereas adoptive transfer of CerS4 deficient splenocytes increased macrophage populations [27]. These findings suggest that alterations in sphingolipid metabolism can differentially influence immune cell recruitment depending on cell type and model used. In contrast, CerS6 deficient T cells did not alter the recruitment of either Th1 or Th17 cells in vivo [28]. In our study, effector Th1 and Th17 cells were reduced in the colonic tissues of ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice. This reduction may reflect, at least in part, alterations to other immune cell populations that influence effector T‐cell responses. Supporting this possibility, in vitro studies have shown that neutrophils may play additional roles in driving Th1 and Th17 differentiation [57], and in our study the reduction in effector T cells may be due in part to reduced cytokine and chemokine expression in vivo, as well as reduced neutrophil recruitment. Moreover, because loss of AC in myeloid cells impaired inflammatory responses and reduced MHC II expression in B cells, multiple alterations in the immune environment may collectively contribute to the reductions in effector T cells in ACΔMYEIL10−/− mice.

Recent studies have shown that Tregs can acquire expression of IL17A, and that Th17 T cells may be able to trans‐differentiate toward this Treg phenotype as well [58, 59]. The role of IL17A+FoxP3+ Tregs may indicate retention of anti‐inflammatory programming; however, it is challenging to discern the role of these cells in our model given the loss of IL10 production. Conversely, Th17 T cells have also shown the ability to trans‐differentiate into Th1 T cells, contributing to the development of colitis [60]. Indeed, our findings suggest that myeloid cell AC may exhibit an indirect role in the recruitment or maturation of effector Th1 and Th17 T cells. The limited body of literature, together with our findings suggests that sphingolipid metabolism in immune cells may play a role in the recruitment of immune cells to the intestinal mucosa and highlights the need for further investigation.

In summary, this study indicates that loss of AC in myeloid cells partially protects from chronic colitis by reducing inflammation in colon tissues and impairing the recruitment of effector immune cells, suggesting AC as a potential novel therapeutic target.
