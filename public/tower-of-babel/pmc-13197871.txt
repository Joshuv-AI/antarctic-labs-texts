Use and Public Reporting of Predetermined Change Control Plans for AI-Enabled Medical Devices
Authors: Patryk A. Dabek, Florence T. Bourgeois, Ariel D. Stern, Yi Zhu, Alexander O. Everhart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study describes predetermined change control plan use among artificial intelligence (AI)–enabled medical devices and the comprehensiveness of public reporting documenting predetermined change control plans.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) can improve existing medical devices by dynamically modifying functionalities in response to real-world data.1 However, this iterative process exists in tension with the current approach by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to authorize devices, which generally requires manufacturers to resubmit devices for review when modifications affect safety or effectiveness.2

To reduce this tension, the FDA drafted guidance in April 2023 and finalized guidance in December 2024 clarifying their expectations regarding predetermined change control plans (PCCPs) specifically for AI-enabled devices.3 Within PCCPs, which were first statutorily permitted for devices generally in December 2022,3 manufacturers prespecify device modifications and methodologies for evaluating them. If authorized, manufacturers can then implement these modifications without additional FDA review, even when modifications affect safety or effectiveness. This cross-sectional study describes PCCP use among AI-enabled medical devices and the comprehensiveness of public reporting documenting PCCPs.

Methods

From public FDA databases,4,5 we identified AI-enabled medical devices authorized from 2023 through 2025 and extracted information on device characteristics, including authorization pathway, authorization date, clinical specialty, review time, count of prior AI-enabled devices from the manufacturer (manufacturer experience), and PCCP use. Using public manufacturer summary documents, we manually assessed disclosure of 4 key PCCP elements: (1) planned modifications (modification list), (2) how manufacturers test/validate modifications (modification protocol), (3) how manufacturers communicate modifications to users (notification plan), and (4) risks/benefits of modifications (impact assessment). Additionally, the FDA recommends that manufacturers publicly summarize modification lists, modification protocols, and notification plans (but does not direct manufacturers to summarize impact assessments)3; we assessed whether manufacturers disclosed all recommended elements. Finally, within notification plans, we identified whether manufacturers contacted users through updates to labeling/instructions for use, device interface notifications, direct correspondence, and/or release notes.

Descriptive statistics and 2-sided tests were calculated using R, version 4.4.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing), with statistical significance defined as P < .05. The eMethods in Supplement 1 provide additional details. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. This study used publicly available materials and did not involve humans; therefore, institutional review board review was not required.

Results

Among 794 AI-enabled medical devices, 43 (5.4%) had FDA-authorized PCCPs (Table). Clinical specialty varied by PCCP use, while there were no meaningful differences in authorization pathway, review time, or manufacturer experience. The first PCCP for an AI-enabled device was observed in the third quarter of 2023, with 2 devices (3.5%) with PCCPs that quarter (Figure). By the fourth quarter of 2025, 7 (9.7%) AI-enabled devices included PCCPs.

Reporting of PCCP elements varied: 39 devices (90.7%) reported modification lists, 36 (83.7%) reported modification protocols, 32 (74.4%) reported notification plans, 14 (32.6%) reported impact assessments, and 31 (72.1%) reported all FDA-recommended elements (Table). For devices with public notification plans, 30 (69.8%) notified users via labeling/instructions for use changes, 14 (32.6%) used interface notifications, 13 (30.2%) used direct correspondence, 8 (18.6%) issued release notes, and 24 (55.8%) used 2 or more methods.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, only 5% of AI-enabled medical devices had an FDA-authorized PCCP during the study period. However, in contrast to previous work,1,6 we observed a modest increase in PCCP use starting in 2025. Among devices with PCCPs, public reporting of PCCP elements was reasonably high: nearly three-quarters reported all FDA-recommended elements. Additionally, many manufacturers used multiple methods to communicate updates to users.

PCCPs allow manufacturers to quickly implement device modifications while prespecifying plans for mitigating risk.1,6 As such, low PCCP use may represent a missed opportunity for improving device performance. The PCCP authorization process was generally accessible to manufacturers with differing levels of experience; experience with AI-enabled devices was not associated with PCCP use, nor did manufacturers undergo meaningfully different review times when submitting a PCCP. Nonetheless, low overall adoption indicates that there remain unidentified barriers to PCCP use or that certain AI-enabled devices may simply be unamenable to prespecified design changes that preserve safety and effectiveness.

This study was limited by using public summaries, which are less detailed than full regulatory submissions received by the FDA, and the inability to assess the value of PCCPs. Nonetheless, these findings indicate that manufacturers have generally been comprehensive in documenting PCCPs when used. This work supports further efforts by the FDA to engage stakeholders to facilitate appropriate PCCP adoption.
