An innovative electrochemical approach for selective and sensitive urea sensing using NiNPs modified GCE
Authors: Weaam Hakami, Ekram Y. Danish, Amna N. Khan, Arwa Khudaysh, M. Aslam, M. Tahir Soomro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Urea contamination from waste and sewage sources necessitates a simple, selective, and sensitive method for its detection in food and environmental samples. In this work, an innovative electrochemical approach is presented for urea sensing using a NiNPs modified GCE in 0.1 M NaOH electrolyte. A highly stable colloidal solution of NiNPs was synthesized using tris base as a capping agent and ascorbic acid as a reducing agent, followed by drop-casting onto the GCE surface. The formation of NiNPs was confirmed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) observed in UV-Visible spectroscopy, while FTIR and Raman analysis validated effective capping by tris base. Structural and morphological characterization using XRD, SEM, and TEM revealed a face-centered cubic (fcc) crystalline structure and oval-shaped nanoparticles with smooth surfaces and an average size of 50–90 nm, dispersed heterogeneously across the GCE surface. The enhanced electrochemical charge transfer properties of NiNPs/GCE were demonstrated using [Fe(CN)6]−3/−4, NaOH, and urea in NaOH. The distinct catalytic activity of NiNPs for the electrochemical oxidation of urea, facilitated by the catalytically active NiOOH form, was clearly exhibited through cyclic voltammetry (CV), highlighting the enhanced performance of the NiNPs/GCE in urea sensing, with tris-capped NiNPs outperforming malic acid-capped counterparts. Square wave voltammetry (SWV) effectively decoupled urea oxidation from the reversible oxidation of Ni(OH)2 to NiOOH, enabling selective detection of urea. As a result, a linear detection range of 0.0625-1.25 mM for urea with a regression coefficient (R2) of 0.9972 and a detection limit of 28.8 µM (S/N = 3) was established. Aside from NH3, other interferences were well tolerated, with urea recovery exceeding 98.7% in municipal and 94% in agricultural wastewater.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23245-y.

Introduction

Due to its versatility, urea has multiple applications, primarily in the agricultural and industrial sectors. In agriculture, it is commonly used alongside other nitrogen fertilizers to enhance soil productivity, while in animal nutrition, urea supplementation helps maintain productivity and health. Its moisturizing and exfoliating properties make it a valuable ingredient in cosmetics and some medicines. Additionally, urea-formaldehyde resin is widely utilized in the plastic industry1–3. These applications account for over 80% of urea entering the environment as waste. Urea is also naturally produced in the liver as a byproduct of protein metabolism, excreted through urine, and ultimately reaches domestic waste4. Environmental urea accumulates in water reservoirs and watercourses, promoting algal growth, degrading water quality, harming aquatic life and biodiversity, and affecting human health and food security5. Its breakdown produces toxic ammonia, which can be fatal at high concentrations4. While urea use in other sectors is controllable, agricultural consumption is rising with growing food demand. To mitigate risks, urea levels should remain below 12 mM in wastewater and 0.16 mM in drinking water6. Therefore, a simple, selective, and sensitive detection method with high selectivity is essential for accurate urea monitoring.

Urea detection in body fluids is crucial for assessing kidney function and monitoring hemodialysis treatments. In clinical diagnostics, biosensors can detect urea at the picomolar level7,8. However, reliable and simple methods for monitoring urea in environmental and food samples at comparable sensitivity are still lacking due to the complexity of these matrices. Conventional techniques, including infrared (IR) spectroscopy, liquid and gas chromatography, fluorimetry, and chemiluminescence, have been applied for urea detection but often require extensive sample pretreatment, long analysis time, and are susceptible to interference from ions such as K+, Na+, NH4+, uric acid, and others9,10. Electrochemical sensors offer a cost-effective, easy-to-use, and highly selective alternative for detecting gases, ions, and biomolecules8. For urea detection, they exploit its redox reaction at the electrode surface, either via tailored electrode materials or by modifying traditional electrodes with advanced nanomaterials. Nonetheless, interference from other electroactive species can limit selectivity, and the detection limit typically remains in the micromolar range. Electrochemical sensing platforms are designed and fabricated using affordable materials with high electrocatalytic activity, favorable electrochemical properties, selectivity, and biocompatibility. Accordingly, working electrodes have been modified with a variety of nanomaterials, including metal nanoparticles, metal oxide nanoparticles, and nanocomposites for detecting a wide range of analytes8,11–13. Their simple fabrications, versatility, and reliability have promoted adoption in fields such as medical diagnostics, environmental monitoring, energy systems, food safety, and agriculture. Recently, thin-film-coated electrodes, where nanomaterials in colloidal form are applied directly to the electrode surface without solvent suspension, have gained significant attention14,15. In addition to their unique electrochemical, catalytic, and electrical properties, these thin-film coated electrodes require fewer materials, offer greater flexibility, reduce interference, and enhance reproducibility, contributing to more accurate and reliable signal detection.

Electrochemical urea detection can be achieved via enzymatic and non-enzymatic approaches. Enzymatic methods, however, face limitations such as enzyme immobilization on the working electrode surface, high cost, limited reproducibility, and restricted operating conditions, which hinder widespread adoption8,16. Non-enzymatic detection relies on direct urea oxidation using electrodes modified with suitable nanomaterials. NiO-based electrodes have exhibited excellent catalytic performance for electrochemical urea detection6,8,13,17. However, conversion of Ni(OH)2 to the catalytically active NiOOH form in an alkaline medium can lead to deactivation due to surface adsorption or poisoning by urea oxidation products, including CN−, carbonate, or CO28,17–19. This deactivation of active sites reduces current density and lowers sensitivity. To mitigate CO2 poisoning, nanomaterials with higher NiOOH active-site density, improved structural stability, and enhanced sensitivity are needed18. Consequently, Ni has been combined with other metal oxides and carbon-based materials to improve catalytic performance and long-term stability. Recent examples include nickel-sphere coated Ni-MN17, Nickel/cobalt oxide-decorated 3D graphene nanocomposite20, nickel oxide immobilized on the carbonized eggshell membrane13, and nickel sulfide/graphene oxide nanocomposite21. Apart from Ni-based nanomaterials, other nanomaterials such as graphene-polyaniline composites22, AgNP-deposited commercial test strip12, CuO/Co3O4@MWCNTs, Ag-N-SWCNTs nanocomposite23, and ZnO@rGO nanocomposites11 have been developed for sensitive urea detection. Despite these advances, designing a catalyst with higher NiOOH site density and surface defects remains a key focus for improved performance.

Metallic NiNPs provide high surface area and abundant active sites due to closely packed surface Ni atoms, enhancing electrocatalytic efficiency24,25. However, their application in electrochemical urea detection remains largely unexplored. In this study, metallic NiNPs were synthesized using tris base as a capping agent and ascorbic acid as a reducing agent. Tris base, a primary amine with three hydroxyl groups26, facilitates ligand exchange with Ni(NO3)2.6H2O, where its nitrogen atoms coordinate with Ni2+ ions, replacing water molecules. The stronger donor ability of amines stabilizes the resulting Ni(II)-amine complex. Upon addition of ascorbic acid, Ni2+ ions are reduced to metallic NiNPs, while the amine ligands remain on the surface, preventing aggregation and promoting dispersion. This surface confinement also inhibits oxidation of metallic Ni to Ni2+ or Ni3+ oxide/hydroxide layers under ambient conditions. The morphology of NiNPs is influenced by these ligands, affecting the density of active sites and electrocatalytic behavior. Moreover, amine and hydroxyl groups facilitate CO2 desorption and conversion into non-adsorbing species, minimizing electrode poisoning and preserving catalytic sites for urea oxidation. Thus, tris base-capped NiNPs offer a promising platform for electrochemical urea detection.

Non-enzymatic urea detection has primarily been performed using CV, with SWV employed less frequently17. SWV combines the benefits of CV and pulse techniques, offering superior analytical performance and valuable diagnostic insights into electrochemical processes, with detection limits in the nanomolar range. In SWV, short forward and backward potential pulses are applied, and the net current is recorded after each step27. This method allows decoupling of overlapping urea oxidation from the Ni(OH)2 to NiOOH oxidation, enabling selective urea detection without interference. It also reduces adsorption of intermediate species on the electrode surface, minimizing surface poisoning and improving detection accuracy. Thus, a highly stable colloidal solution of metallic NiNPs was synthesized and characterized using spectroscopic, surface analytical, and electrochemical techniques. The NiNPs were drop-casted onto a GCE to form a thin film, followed by optimization for selective and sensitive urea sensing. Minimal interferences were observed, and recovery from municipal wastewater was high.

Experimental

Chemicals and reagents

Ni(NO3)2.6H2O, ascorbic acid, tris base (NH2C(CH2OH)3), malic acid, urea, sodium hydroxide, and potassium ferricyanide were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. The solutions were prepared using milli-Q water.

Synthesis of colloidal NiNPs and fabrication of NiNPs/GCE

Colloidal metallic NiNPs were synthesized by stirring 1 mL of 0.4 M Ni(NO3)2.6H2O in 5 mL of Milli-Q water, followed by the addition of 1 mL of 0.5 M tris base to achieve a uniform mixture. The initial emerald-green solution gradually turned leafy-green and then brown upon the addition of 1 mL 0.5 M ascorbic acid. Stirring at room temperature continued until the color gradually changed to yellow-brown and eventually black-brown, indicating the complete reduction of Ni2+ to Ni0. To promote reduction and prevent the formation of nickel oxide/hydroxide precipitates, the pH was maintained at ~ 9-9.5 using 0.5 M NaOH. The reduction of Ni2+ to Ni0 at room temperature progresses gradually, typically completing within 24 h. The GC electrode was first polished to a mirror-like finish using a 0.05 μm alumina slurry, followed by rinsing with Milli-Q water and air drying. Then, ~ 10 µL of colloidal NiNPs was drop-casted onto the GC electrode, ensuring it covered the entire surface. The electrode was then oven-dried at 50 °C for 2 min. The modified electrode was subsequently labeled as NiNPs/GCE. For comparison, Ni2+ ions from the precursor were directly deposited onto a GC electrode (Ni2+/GCE), while malic acid-capped NiNPs were synthesized by substituting tris base with malic acid in the same procedure and subsequently deposited onto a GC electrode (NiNPs-MA/GCE).

Characterization

The surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of NiNPs was monitored using a Cary 60 spectrophotometer (Agilent Technology, USA), while the Fourier transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrum of NiNPs was recorded on a Cary 630 (Agilent Technology, USA). The Raman spectrum was obtained with a DXR Raman Microscope (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using a 532 nm diode-pumped solid-state (DPSS) laser at 8 mW power. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted using an automated multipurpose X-ray diffractometer (Ultima IV, Rigaku, Akishima, Japan), with Cu Kα radiation in the 2θ range of 10° to 80°, at a 40 kV accelerating voltage and a current of 40 mA. The surface morphology of the NiNPs/GCE and NiNPs-MA/GCE was examined by field emission electron microscopy (FESEM, JEOL, SM-IT700HR, Tokyo, Japan) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL, JEM-2100f), with samples prepared on silicon wafer and carbon tape substrates. The zeta potential of the NiNPs was determined using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, United Kingdom) to assess their surface charge and colloidal stability.

Electrochemical measurements

A standard three-electrode assembly was used, consisting of a Pt wire as the counter electrode, a saturated Ag/AgCl reference electrode, and a NiNPs/GC as the working electrode. A 0.1 M NaOH solution was used as the supporting electrolyte. All electrochemical measurements were performed using a VSP multi-channel potentiostat (Biologic Science Instrument, France). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of the NiNPs/GCE was conducted under three conditions: (i) in 0.1 M KCl containing 2 mM [Fe(CN)6]−3/−4 at 0.23 V (vs. Ag/AgCl), (ii) in 0.1 M NaOH at open circuit voltage (OCV), and (iii) in 0.1 M NaOH containing 10 µM urea at -0.52 V. In all cases, the frequency was scanned from 1 Hz to 100 kHz using a 10 mV AC amplitude, and the impedance data were fitted using the Randles equivalent circuit model. The EIS response of NiNPs-MA/GCE was recorded in 0.1 M NaOH containing 10 µM urea at -0.52 V, using a 10 mV AC signal over a frequency range of 1 Hz to 100 kHz. CV was conducted at a scan rate of 100 mV, starting from the OCV, sweeping first to a vertex potential of 0 V, then to a second vertex of + 1.0 V, and finally returning to the OCV. SWV was performed by cycling the potential from − 0.5 V to + 1.0 V and back to -0.5 V, using optimized parameters: a pulse height (PH) of 75 mV, pulse width (PW) of 50 ms, and s step height (SH) of 10 mV.

Real sample

Municipal and agricultural wastewater were collected from Jeddah and stored at room temperature. The pH and conductivity were 7.5 and 65.25 µS/cm for municipal wastewater, and 7.9 and 300 µS/cm for agricultural wastewater. Samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane and diluted with 0.1 M NaOH. SWV measurements were performed under optimized conditions, and urea recovery was evaluated using the standard addition method.

Results and discussion

UV-Visible, FTIR, and Raman spectroscopic analysis of NiNPs

SPR occurs when the frequency of incident electromagnetic radiation resonates with the collective oscillation of conduction band electrons. For NiNPs, the maximum extinction typically appears within the 390–540 nm wavelength range, corresponding to particle size between 50 and 120 nm, as reported in the literature25,28,29. Figure 1a shows the UV-Visible absorption spectrum of colloidal metallic NiNPs recorded over the 200–700 nm range. The synthesized NiNPs exhibited a well-defined single SPR band with maximum extinction at 352 nm, in good agreement with reported values. The position and shape of the SPR band are strongly influenced by the size and morphology of the NPs30. Smaller NPs typically shift the SPR peak toward shorter wavelengths, while larger ones result in a shift toward longer wavelengths. Spherical or smooth-surfaced NPs usually display a single SPR band, whereas anisotropic or rod-like structures often exhibit multiple SPR bands. Accordingly, the broad, single absorption band observed at 352 nm suggested that the synthesized NiNPs were predominantly spherical or smooth-shaped with moderate particle size.

The synthesis of NiNPs was performed at room temperature, with fully developed NiNPs forming after 24 h, as depicted in the inset of Fig. 1a. As the reaction progressed, the solution gradually darkened, reaching a deep brown color at completion, indicating successful NiNPs formation. While metallic NiNPs are typically prone to oxidation in aqueous solutions, the as-synthesized NiNPs remained stable for up to 7 days. The excellent colloidal stability of the NiNPs was attributed to the capping effects of tris base, which will be discussed in the synthesis mechanism.

The FTIR spectrum of NiNPs capped with tris base is shown in Fig. 1b, revealing that the colloidal solution of NiNPs was predominantly influenced by the tris base. The characteristic peaks at 3331 and 3261 cm− 1 corresponded to the stretching vibrations of hydroxyl (O-H) and amine (N-H) groups, respectively. The absorption bands at 2942 and 2895 cm− 1 were attributed to the C-H stretching vibration of alkyl chains. A prominent peak at 1597 cm− 1 was associated with N-H bending, while the shoulder at 1414 cm− 1 corresponded to the C-H bending vibrations. The stretching vibrations of C-N, C-C, and C-O were observed at 1340 cm− 1, 1125 cm− 1, and 1041 cm− 1, respectively. These findings are in good agreement with previously reported FTIR data for tris base31,32. During the formation of NiNPs, ascorbic acid was oxidized to dehydroascorbic acid. However, no distinct C = O stretching vibrations associated with residual ascorbic acid or dehydroascorbic acid were observed in the FTIR spectrum of NiNPs. This absence may be attributed to the complete reaction of ascorbic acid, additionally, to the overlap or merging of the C = O peak with N-H bending vibrations due to hydrogen bonding or vibrational coupling effects. Overall, FTIR analysis confirmed that the NiNPs were successfully capped with tris base.

The Raman spectrum shown in Fig. 1c further supports the FTIR findings. In the NiNPs spectrum, the ν(OH) stretching band appeared at 3315 cm− 1, while the ν(NH) stretching band was observed at 3249 cm− 1. The ν(CH) stretching bands were detected at 3039 and 2901 cm− 1, respectively. The band at 1529 cm− 1 was assigned to the δ(NH) bending mode of the tris amino group. The δ(CH) bending vibrations were observed at 1406 cm− 1, while ν(CN) and ν(CC), and ν(CO) stretching vibrations appeared at 1297, 1163, and 1054 cm− 1, respectively. Additionally, the bands at 592 and 183 cm-1 were attributed to δ(HCN) and δ(NCC) bending modes, respectively, in accordance with literature data and the sensitivity of Raman in the fingerprint region33–35. The appearance of the δ(NH) bending vibrations at a lower frequency indicated involvement in hydrogen bonding with the NiNPs surface, as Raman shifts are highly sensitive to such interactions. The FTIR and Raman analysis unambiguously demonstrated that the tris base caps the NiNPs, rather than participating in the reduction of Ni2+ ions. This capping mechanism enhances the stability of NiNPs in the colloidal solution over prolonged periods.

Synthesis mechanism and capping of NiNPs

The synthesis mechanism and capping process of NiNPs are illustrated in Fig. 2. Initially, the amine groups of the tris base coordinated with Ni2+ ions, replacing water molecules and forming a tris-Ni2+ complex. The solution pH was then adjusted to pH ~ 9-9.5 using NaOH, followed by the addition of ascorbic acid. At this alkaline pH, ascorbic acid becomes deprotonated, enhancing its reducing capability. The Ni2+ ions, stabilized through coordination with NH2 groups, were subsequently reduced to Ni0 by the ascorbate ions via a two-electron, two-proton transfer process. Meanwhile, the NH2 groups remained adsorbed or loosely bound to the Ni0 surface through weak coordination via nitrogen lone pairs, van der Waals forces, and electrostatic interactions.

Tris base, with a pKa of 8.126, helps maintain mildly basic conditions that are favorable for the stabilization of zero-valent metals. In this environment, the three hydroxyl groups of the tris base also interacted with the Ni0 surface through electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions. These interactions then contributed to the capping of NiNPs by forming a passivating layer that retarded oxidation and prevented agglomeration. Additionally, the hydroxyl groups imparted a surface charge to the NiNPs, creating electrostatic repulsion between particles, which enhanced their dispersion and excellent stability in the colloidal solution.

Structural and morphological properties

The XRD pattern of NiNPs, shown in Fig. 3 within the 2θ range of 20–80°, was found to correspond to the fcc structure of Ni, with peaks marked by an asterisk attributed to the crystalline phase of tris base. Three prominent peaks, observed at 2θ values of 44.5°, 52.1°, and 77.0ƒ, were matched with the lattice planes of (111), (200), and (200), respectively, as identified by JCPDS: 03-105125,36. The lattice parameters of the NiNPs calculated from the XRD pattern were 0.3520 ± 0.001 nm, which was slightly larger than that of bulk fcc Ni (0.3517 nm), likely due to the interactions with the capping agent. The average crystallite size, d = 65 nm, was determined using peak broadening in the Scherrer equation, as described in previous studies.

The XRD pattern of NiNPs.

SEM analysis was used to investigate the surface morphology of the NiNPs/GCE. As depicted in Fig. 4a, b, the modified electrode surface exhibited heterogeneously distributed, oval-shaped NiNPs. The images were captured at magnifications of 3,500x and 15,000x, with a scale bar of 1 μm. The elongated morphology of the NiNPs likely promoted increased surface interactions with urea molecules, enhancing their adsorption and thereby improving the electrode’s sensitivity for urea detection. A closer examination of the TEM image in Fig. 4c revealed that the NiNPs predominantly exhibit an oval morphology, with a few smaller spherical particles also present. The particle sizes ranged from approximately 10 to 130 nm. The corresponding size distribution, illustrated in the bar diagrams in Fig. 4d, indicated that most NiNPs were within the 50–90 nm range. Compared to spherical particles, the oval-shaped NiNPs exposed a greater active surface area, particularly at their ends or poles, which may enhance urea adsorption37. Furthermore, their elongated shape could lead to the formation of interparticle channels or pores that facilitate the diffusion of urea to active sites.

SEM images of NiNPs/GCE at magnification of (a) 3,500x and (b) 15,000x; (c) TEM image of NiNP/GCE at a scale of 200 nm; and (d) particle size distribution histogram derived from TEM analysis.

Both SEM and TEM images revealed a bimodal distribution of NiNPs on the GCE surface, with larger particles in the 50–90 nm range and smaller ones between 10 and 30 nm. This variation in particle size is advantageous for introducing porosity on the modified electrode surface. Additionally, the -OH and -NH2 groups of the tris base coating on the NiNPs facilitated interactions with urea, thereby enhancing its adsorption. Specifically, reversible non-covalent hydrogen bonds could form between the carbonyl group of urea and the -OH and -NH2 groups of the tris base. These interactions likely brought urea molecules into close proximity with the modified electrode, promoting their adsorption onto the active sites. Additionally, the presence of differently sized NiNPs disrupts the dense packing, thereby increasing surface porosity and improving accessibility of urea. Consequently, enhanced urea oxidation during electrochemical analysis is plausible. Zeta potential measurements revealed a strong negative charge of -32 mV on the NiNPs, corroborating the SEM observation of heterogeneously distributed oval-shaped NiNPs across the GCE surface. This substantial negative surface charge induces electrostatic repulsion between particles, which not only prevents aggregation but also helps to loosen particle packing. Furthermore, the -OH and -NH2 functional groups from the tris base capping provide steric hindrance and enhance surface stabilization. Together, these effects improve dispersion and stability of NiNPs on the electrode surface, increasing porosity and accessibility to active catalytic sites, key factors for efficient urea adsorption and oxidation.

Nyquist and bode plot analysis

The charge transfer properties of NiNPs/GCE were assessed using EIS. Figure 5(a) compares the Nyquist plot for bare GCE and NiNPs/GCE in the presence of [Fe(CN)6]−3/−4, where the bare GCE displayed a smaller semicircle, while the NiNPs/GCE exhibited a larger one. The estimated charge transfer resistance (Rct) values were 244 Ω for bare GCE and 719 Ω for NiNPs/GCE (Table 1). The surface of the bare GCE electrode was neutral, while the surface of NiNPs/GCE was electron-rich due to the presence of -OH and -NH2 groups from the tris base. As [Fe(CN)6]−3/−4 carries a negative charge, electrostatic repulsion between [Fe(CN)6]−3/−4 and NiNPs surface likely contributed to the increase Rct, reflected by the larger semicircle38. This suggests that in the presence of positively charged or neutral species, the transfer behavior at the NiNPs/GCE surface could be significantly different, potentially improving electron transfer kinetics.

The charge transfer behavior of bare GCE and NiNPs/GCE in 0.1 M NaOH at OCV is shown in Fig. 5b. A significant reduction in the Rct was observed for NiNPs/GCE compared to bare GCE, confirming the enhanced conductivity and superior electrochemical performance of the NiNPs/GCE in alkaline conditions, an environment well-suited for urea sensing. To further assess urea detection capability, the charge transfer behavior of NiNPs/GCE was evaluated in the presence and absence of 10 µM urea in 0.1 M NaOH at a fixed potential of 0.52 V. As shown in Fig. 5c and Table 1, the Rct value for bare GCE increased in the presence of urea, whereas for NiNPs/GCE, Rct further decreased, indicating enhanced electron transfer kinetics and high sensitivity toward urea. These EIS findings clearly demonstrate the effectiveness of NiNPs/GCE in facilitating urea detection in alkaline media, as evidenced by significantly reduced Rct. Figure 5d shows the Bode plots of NiNPs/GCE in the presence and absence of 10 µM urea in 0.1 M NaOH, depicting both the logarithm of frequency versus impedance and phase angle. In the absence of urea, NiNPs/GCE exhibited the highest overall impedance and the widest frequency range at the maximum phase angle (~ 63°). In contrast, the presence of urea led to lower impedance and a narrower phase angle (~ 37°), indicating an accelerated charge transfer process. These observations further confirm the enhanced electrochemical activity and sensitivity of the NiNPs/GCE toward urea under alkaline conditions.

Due to their oval shape, NiNPs likely expose a greater active surface area, enhancing urea adsorption, oxidation, and electrochemical response37. In contrast, spherical or hexagonal NiNPs may lack such surface efficiency. To assess morphology and capping agent effects, NiNPs were also synthesized using malic acid, following the same method as tris base-capped NiNPs. These were coated on a GC electrode and labelled NiNPs-MA/GCE. SEM images (Supplementary Figure S1(a), 2000x, 10 μm scale) show distorted spherical particles with rounded edges, typical of malic acid-mediated growth. These larger particles likely reduced surface area, limiting urea adsorption and charge transfer. This is supported by the Nyquist plot (Supplementary Figure S1(b)), where NiNPs-MA/GCE showed a higher Rct (11744 Ω) than NiNPs/GCE (Table 1) under identical conditions. Thus, the capping agent significantly affects electrochemical performance by altering morphology. Overall, oval-shaped, tris base-stabilized NiNPs provide greater surface area and superior charge transfer, making them more effective for urea detection.

Cyclic voltametric studies

Urea sensing with Ni-based electrodes depends on the availability of active NiOOH sites, CO2 tolerance, and long-term stability. EIS confirmed improved charge transfer and electrode kinetics for NiNPs/GCE. To assess whether the NiNPs can sustain the formation of active NiOOH following urea oxidation and resist CO2 poisoning, CV studies were performed6,8,18. Although various Ni2+-based materials like NiO, NiCo2O4, and NiO-MoO38,39,40 have been used for urea sensing, metallic NiNPs have not been reported. To isolate the catalytic role of NiNPs, a GCE modified with Ni2+ ions was prepared for comparison. As expected, bare GCE showed no urea response. In contrast, CV curves of Ni2+/GCE in 0.1 M NaOH with and without 50 µM urea (Fig. 6a) showed distinct redox peaks. Without urea, the oxidation peak at 0.54 V corresponds to Ni(OH)2 → to NiOOH, and the reduction peak at 0.41 V to NiOOH → Ni(OH)2, as described in Eq. (1)6,8,17,18.

Thus, scanning the potential positively forms catalytically active NiOOH, crucial for urea oxidation, while comparable peak currents indicate a reversible redox process.

In 0.1 M NaOH with 50 µM urea (Fig. 6a), a significant increase in the forward scan oxidation peak confirms NiOOH’s strong catalytic activity. The overlapping peak at 0.69 V corresponds to both Ni(OH)2 and urea oxidation. Initially, Ni(OH)2 is oxidized to NiOOH, which then catalyzes urea oxidation and is reduced back to Ni(OH)2. A second oxidation peak in the reverse scan indicates further urea oxidation, enabled by regenerated NiOOH sites previously blocked by adsorbed urea or intermediates. These adsorbed molecules were subsequently oxidized to final products under the still favorable applied potential. The overall process is summarized in Eq. (2)6,8,17,18.

Urea oxidation in the forward regenerated Ni(OH)2, which was re-oxidized to NiOOH under the still favorable potential, enabling further urea oxidation, evidenced by the second peak in the reverse scan. The subsequent NiOOH reduction at 0.41 V matched the peak seen without urea. These results indicate that urea enhances the regeneration of active NiOOH on Ni2+/GCE, alongside the effect of CV cycling.

Compared to Ni2+/GCE, NiNPs/GCE in 0.1 M NaOH showed two distinct oxidation peaks (Fig. 6b). The first, at -0.2 V in the forward scan, corresponds to Ni0 oxidation to Ni(OH)2, confirming the metallic nature of NiNPs. However, Ni(OH)2 oxidation appeared only in the reverse scan, indicating NiOOH formation occurs then, driven by the still favorable potential. The tris base capping layer, with a strong negative zeta potential (-32 mV), remains bound to NiNPs, limiting OH− access and slowing Ni(OH)2 oxidation in the forward scan. Upon reversing the potential, the accumulated surface charge or intermediate states facilitate oxidation, revealing the peak. Additionally, the applied potential may cause reorientation or detachment of the capping layer, exposing Ni(OH)2 sites for oxidation in the reverse scan. A reduction peak at 0.41 confirms NiOOH formation. The tris base layer also prevents electrode poisoning by blocking the adsorption of oxidized urea products. As a result, NiNPs/GCE showed an oxidation peak current three times higher than Ni2+/GCE, due to enhanced formation and stability of active sites. The limited reduction of NiOOH suggests a stable catalytic layer remains, beneficial for urea oxidation and relevant to oxygen evolution reactions (OER).

In Fig. 6b, urea enhanced the oxidation of Ni0 to Ni(OH)2 due to the inherent catalytic activity of Ni-based electrodes. A pronounced increase in urea oxidation current appeared in the reverse scan at 0.72 V. This behavior is linked to the tris base capping layer, which delays Ni(OH)2 oxidation in the forward scan by shielding active sites, hindering charge transfer, and undergoing restructuring, causing the oxidation peak to emerge only in the reverse scan. Since NiOOH was absent in the forward scan, urea oxidation did not occur. In the reverse scan, Ni(OH)2 oxidized to NiOOH, enabling urea oxidation (Eq. 2). The oxidation current was about twice that of Ni2+/GCE, confirming the higher sensitivity of NiNPs/GCE, attributed to more active sites and reduced surface poisoning. Thus, NiNPs/GCE provides a sensitive platform for urea detection with superior catalytic activity and stability. The absence of a NiOOH → Ni(OH)2 reduction peak indicates complete consumption of NiOOH by urea.

The protective role of tris base was confirmed by comparing NiNPs capped with different ligands for urea detection. Malic acid-capped NiNPs (NiNPs-MA/GCE) were tested against tris base-capped NiNPs (Supplementary Figure S1(c)). NiNPs-MA showed a delayed Ni(OH)2 oxidation peak due to surface -OH/-COOH shielding, but its acidic nature (pKa 3.4, 5.1)15 and less negative zeta potential (≈ 19 mV) promoted prolonged urea adsorption. While transient adsorption supports oxidation, prolonged adsorption causes fouling, blocking sites, and lowering currents. The weaker negative charge also failed to repel anionic byproducts (CO32−, CN−, and CO2), accelerating fouling, deactivating sites, and reducing sensitivity. Morphology further contributed: distorted spherical NiNPs-MA had a lower electroactive surface area than the uniform oval tris base-capped NiNPs, resulting in lower oxidation current.

The reduced fouling with tris base arises from -OH and -NH2 groups binding to NiNPs, blocking adsorption-prone sites and preventing strong attachment of urea oxidation intermediates such as CO2 and carbonate species. NiNPs capped with tris base showed a zeta potential of -32 mV, imparting strong electrostatic repulsion against anionic byproducts and protecting NiOOH active sites. Thus, the tris base layer suppresses fouling via charge repulsion, steric hindrance, and passivation, maintaining site availability, stability, and durability for sustained urea oxidation.

In all the CVs discussed above, the scans were initiated at the OCV, swept forward to the first vertex potential of 0 V, then to the second vertex potential of + 1.0 V, and finally returned to the end potential, which was also set at the OCV. Figure 6c examines the influence of vertex and end potentials on the urea oxidation behavior. When the second vertex potential was increased to + 1.5 V (red curve), the tris base capping layer became unstable at such a high anodic potential. The breakdown of the capping agent likely led to irreversible oxidation of NiNPs into NiO species and possible over-oxidation of the electrolyte, both of which damaged the electrode surface. As a result, a sluggish and broadened urea oxidation peak was observed. In contrast, when the second vertex potential was set at + 1.0 V (pink and green curves), a sharp and well-defined urea oxidation peak appeared. However, the green curve showed a reduced peak current compared to the pink curve. This was attributed to the end potential being set at -0.5 V, which may have affected the regeneration of catalytically active NiOOH form during the reverse scan. Therefore, to achieve a sharp and high urea oxidation peak, the optimal scan parameters were: starting and ending at the OCV, with vertex potentials set at 0 V and + 1.0 V.

To evaluate the protective role of the tris base capping layer, SEM images of NiNPs/GCE were captured after CV scanning and are presented in Fig. 7a–d, at magnifications of 7,000x (scale bar: 2 μm), 4,500 × (5 μm), 6,000 × (2 μm), and 2,000 × (10 μm). During the electrochemical process, NiNPs were oxidized to Ni(OH)2 and further converted to NiOOH. Following urea oxidation, most of the Ni(OH)2 was regenerated, indicating that Ni(OH)2 is the predominant form present on the GCE surface after the reaction. Although the internal structure of the NiNPs undergoes electrochemical transformation, the capping layer appears to moderate surface restructuring and help retain the external morphology. In other words, while the core material converts into Ni(OH)2 or NiOOH, the nanoparticles can still maintain their original shape if adequately protected by the capping layer. The tris base capping, bearing a strong negative surface charge (-32 mV), not only contributes to preserving the external morphology but also plays a stabilizing role by protecting the catalytically active sites from deactivation.

This protective effect is evident in the SEM images at magnifications of 7,000x (scale bar: 2 μm) and 4,500 × (5 μm), shown in Fig. 7a, b, where distorted oval-shaped NiNPs remain clearly visible. Their retained shape suggests that the tris base capping effectively maintains the nanoparticles’ morphology during electrochemical cycling. Whereas, at magnification of 6,000 × (2 μm), and 2,000x, presented in Fig. 7c, d, a rearrangement of the ova-shaped NiNPs into petal- or leaf-like assemblies can be observed. This transformation may be attributed to the strong electrostatic repulsion caused by the negative surface charge, as well as structural changes induced by immersion in the alkaline NaOH electrolyte. Importantly, the NiNPs remained well-dispersed, with no signs of agglomeration. Overall, these observations confirm that the tris base capping layer effectively preserves the catalytic activity of the NiNPs by maintaining their structural integrity and preventing morphological collapse, even after urea detection.

A scan rate study was conducted to determine whether urea oxidation at the NiNPs/GCE is governed primarily by diffusion or adsorption. Figure 8a presents CV curves for urea oxidation at various scan rates ranging from 20 to 140 mV/s. The oxidation current increased with scan rate up to 100 mV/s, but a sharp decrease was observed at 120 and 140 mV/s, Fig. 8b. Urea oxidation is a complex, multi-electron, multi-step process involving adsorption of urea, electron transfer, and the formation and decomposition of intermediates, as reported in the literature. These processes are relatively slow compared to the rate of potential change at higher scan rates. At elevated scan rates, the potential changes too rapidly for urea molecules to effectively diffuse to and adsorb onto the NiOOH surface. Consequently, incomplete oxidation of urea occurs, leading to a significant decrease in oxidation current at higher scan rates.

To further analyze the mechanism, plots of peak current (Ip) versus scan rate and Ip versus the square root of the scan rate are shown in Fig. 8c and d. Neither plot exhibited strong linearity, with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.917 and 0.815, indicating that urea oxidation follows a mixed control mechanism involving both diffusion and adsorption. Additionally, the log Ip versus log v plot (Supplementary Figure S1(d)) showed a value of 0.2863, deviating from the theoretical value of 0.5 expected for a diffusion-controlled process. This deviation suggests that the oxidation of urea is predominantly diffusion-controlled but influenced by slow electron transfer kinetics and coupled chemical reactions (EC mechanism) involving intermediate species. This behavior implies that urea molecules near the electrode surfaces first adsorb onto catalytically active NiOOH sites prior to oxidation. Subsequently, urea from the bulk solution replenishes the surface coverage. This mass transport step becomes more prominent at higher urea concentrations and lower scan rates, where the oxidation rate is partly limited by the diffusion of urea to the modified electrode surface. Therefore, under typical conditions, where the NiNPs/GCE remains immersed in solution and the potential is applied in the forward direction, some urea adsorbs onto the surface while the remaining diffuses from the bulk solution, confirming the mixed adsorption-diffusion controlled nature of the process.

The concentration of urea significantly influenced the electrochemical behavior of the NiNPs/GCE and further confirmed the mixed adsorption-diffusion controlled mechanism of urea oxidation. As the urea concentration increased, a greater number of urea molecules became available at the electrode surface, enhancing the oxidation current, particularly under conditions where mass transport was not yet limiting. However, beyond a certain concentration, the current no longer increased proportionally with urea concentration. This deviation indicates a transition to mass transport-limited behavior, where the diffusion of urea from the bulk solution to the electrode surface could not keep pace with its consumption. This behavior is evident in Fig. 9a, b, where urea concentrations were varied from 0 to 100 mM. A linear increase in oxidation current was observed up to 50 mM. Beyond this point, particularly at 100 mM, the increase in current was minimal, suggesting that above 50 mM the system was no longer limited by surface kinetics but by diffusion. According, these CV results indicate that the NiNPs/GCE is most effective for the detection of urea within the concentration range of 10 to 50 mM or even below, where the electrode response remains sensitive and reliable.

SWV-Based selective urea sensing

As observed in the CV studies, the electrochemical oxidation of urea overlaps with the oxidation of Ni(OH)2 to NiOOH, making it challenging to distinguish between the two processes. This overlap results in broad, merged, or asymmetric peaks, complicating the assignment of current solely to urea oxidation and hindering the generation of a reliable calibration curve. Additionally, in real sample analysis, where complex matrices such as biological or environmental components are present, this challenge is further amplified, making it even harder to confirm that the measured current originates exclusively from urea oxidation. Therefore, separating the NiOOH formation peak from the urea oxidation peak is essential to improve the clarity, selectivity, and sensitivity of the detection system. To address this, we employed SWV, which provides superior resolution of overlapping peaks compared to CV. SWV is more sensitive to kinetically distinct processes, enabling signal amplification and better separation of peaks that would otherwise overlap in CV41.

Additionally, the high amplitude used in SWV makes it particularly suitable for the sensitive detection of analytes, especially in diffusional processes or surface-sensitive, slow electron-transfer reactions. In SWV, amplitude refers to the height of the square pulses superimposed on a staircase potential. The potential is applied as a series of short-duration square pulses at each step of the staircase, with current measured at the end of both forward and reverse pulses. The net current, calculated as the difference between the forward and reverse currents, is then plotted. This differential measurement amplifies the faradic signal while minimizing the capacitive (non-faradaic) currents, thereby enhancing sensitivity and signal clarity41,42.

In Fig. 10a, the optimized SWV response of NiNPs/GCE in 0.1 M NaOH is presented. The potential was swept from − 0.5 to + 1.0 using a PH of 75 mV, a PW of 50 ms, and a SH of 10 mV. Two sequential scans were recorded, one in the forward direction (-0.5 V to + 1.0 V) and the other in the reverse direction (+ 1.0 V to -0.5 V), analogous to the forward and backward scans in CV. As observed, no distinct oxidation peak appeared during the first forward scan. This result is consistent with the CV findings, where the oxidation of Ni(OH)2 to NiOOH was predominantly observed in the reverse scan at NiNPs/GCE. The absence of a Ni0 to Ni2+ oxidation peak can be attributed to the nature of the Ni0 to Ni2+ redox system, which is surface-confined rather than diffusional. Since SWV is most effective for diffusional redox processes, surface-bound irreversible reactions often yield lower current responses and suppressed or absent peak signals. Furthermore, the inherent slow kinetics of Ni0 to Ni2+ redox couple means that the current measured at each step of the staircase potential, based on the difference between the forward and reverse pulses, results in a negligible net faradaic signal, thereby leading to a weak or missing peak.

During the reverse scan of the SWV, a broad reduction peak was observed at 0.481 V. We anticipated an oxidation peak, similar to that observed in CV, corresponding to the conversion of Ni(OH)2 to NiOOH. However, a reduction peak was observed instead. SWV data is typically recorded as delta Ip versus E, but when converted to Ip versus E using the inbuilt EC-Lab software, a clear oxidation peak was seen in the forward scan, and a distinct reduction peak appeared in the reverse scan, Fig. 10b. This suggests that the broad reduction peak observed during the reverse scan of SWV is likely due to the combined effects of slow oxidation of Ni(OH)2 to NiOOH and the reduction of NiOOH back to Ni(OH)2. Since surface-confined reversible oxidation and reduction processes may overlap or occur simultaneously, SWV may struggle to clearly separate them, leading to merged features. Therefore, the peak observed at 0.481 in SWV is attributed to the combined processes of Ni(OH)2 to NiOOH and NiOOH reduction back to Ni(OH)2.

To further verify, the oxidation current in the SWV data converted to CV (Fig. 10b) was measured as 200 µA, while the reduction current was around 80 µA. The total currents observed in the SWV for the reduction peak were approximately 280 µA. This supports the conclusion that the reversible oxidation of Ni(OH)2 to NiOOH results in a single peak in the SWV reflecting both oxidation and reduction processes. Unlike CV, which measures current continuously and can capture distinct oxidation peaks even during the reverse (decreasing potential) scan known as reverse-scan oxidation, SWV operates differently.

During the reverse scan of SWV at each staircase potential step, the forward pulses primarily capture the reduction of NiOOH, producing negative currents. The reverse pulses, in turn, capture oxidation of Ni(OH)2, generating positive currents. Thus, the net current becomes, net current = (-reduction current) – (+ oxidation current), which leads to a large and broad reduction peak. This possibly explains why the broad reduction peak at 0.481 V in the reverse SWV scan encompasses both reduction and oxidation processes. However, a clear observation from the SWV analysis in Fig. 10a and b is that the broad reduction peak seen during the reverse scan represents a combined signal from the reduction of NiOOH to Ni(OH)2 and the subsequent oxidation of Ni(OH)2 back to NiOOH. These two processes become distinctly visible when the SWV data is converted and plotted in a CV-like format. Thus, the appearance of a single peak associated with the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox pair may be advantageous for distinguishing the urea oxidation signal when urea is present.

(a) SWV responses of NiNPs/GCE in 0.1 M NaOH recorded in the absence and presence of 10 µM urea. (b) corresponding SWV data presented in a CV-like format for comparison.

Figure 10a presents the SWV response of NiNPs/GCE in the presence of 10 µM urea in 0.1 M NaOH. A new oxidation peak appeared at 0.691 V, consistent with the CV results, confirming the selective electrochemical oxidation of urea. Additionally, an increase in the reduction peak associated with the reversible Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox couple was observed, attributed to enhanced NiOOH formation. The presence of urea promotes redox cycling by facilitating the continuous reoxidation of Ni(OH)2 to NiOOH at more positive potentials, thereby increasing the number of catalytically active sites. Urea acts as a chemical electron donor, reducing NiOOH and creating conditions that favor its regeneration. To further interpret these results, the SWV data in Fig. 10a were converted into a CV-like format, as shown in Fig. 10b, where two distinct oxidation peaks and one reduction peak become evident. The enhanced oxidation peak at 0.61 V and the corresponding reduction peak at 0.41 V were attributed to the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox couple. The second oxidation peak, appearing at the same potential but with lower current, was assigned to the oxidation of urea. Thus, the peak observed at 0.691 V in the reverse scan of the SWV (Fig. 10a) is attributed to urea oxidation. In the reverse SWV scan, urea does not undergo reduction; hence, the forward pulses (which record reduction current) show no signal for urea, while the reverse pulses generate a positive current due to urea oxidation, in addition to the signal from the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox process. This analysis demonstrates that SWV enables the clear separation of the urea oxidation signal from that of Ni(OH)2 oxidation, allowing for effective decoupling. Consequently, SWV provides enhanced resolution and selectivity for urea sensing, enabling clear identification of the urea oxidation peak.

The SWV technique is defined by three key parameters: PH, PW, and SH43. These parameters significantly influence the current response, peak shape, and the ability to decouple urea oxidation from the NiOOH-related redox processes. Therefore, their effects on the SWV behavior of NiNPs/GCE in 10 µM urea in 0.1 M NaOH were systematically investigated. Supplementary Figure S2(a) illustrates the influence of PH on both the urea oxidation peak and the peak associated with the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox couple. As PH increased, both peaks initially intensified. However, beyond 75 mV, the urea oxidation peak began to decline, while the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH peak continued to increase in current. Based on this trend, a PH of 75 mV was selected as the optimal value for detecting urea. Supplementary Figure S2(b) displays the impact of varying PW on the peak responses. At a PW of 10 ms, the urea oxidation peak was absent, although a distinct and intensified Ni(OH)2/NiOOH peak was observed. As the PW increased, the urea oxidation peak gradually emerged and reached a maximum at 50 ms. Beyond this point, the peak became less defined, indicating a loss in resolution. Therefore, 50 ms was chosen as the optimal PW for effectively observing a decoupled urea oxidation signal. Additionally, at higher PW values, a notable decrease in the current of the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH peak was observed. The effect of SH on SWV behavior was evaluated and is shown in Supplementary Figure S2(c). Between 5 and 15, the changes in the urea oxidation peak current and position were minimal. However, SH values above 15 mV led to a significant decline in the urea oxidation current. Thus, 10 mV was selected as the optimal SH for detecting urea oxidation. A similar trend was observed for the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH peak, where increased SH resulted in a gradual decrease in peak current. Overall, careful optimization of SWV parameters, PH, PW, and SH, was essential to achieving enhanced resolution and selectivity in urea sensing.

The influence of dissolved oxygen on urea oxidation and the reversible Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox process was also examined. Supplementary Figure S2(d) shows the SWV responses of NiNPs/GCE in 10 µM urea in 0.1 M NaOH under varying durations of nitrogen purging. As shown, the oxidation of urea progressively increased with nitrogen purging and reached a maximum after 25 min, indicating that the removal of dissolved oxygen enhances the urea oxidation signal. Similarly, the current associated with the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH peak also increased with extended nitrogen purging. These observations confirm that dissolved oxygen interferes with both urea oxidation and Ni redox cycling. Therefore, effective nitrogen purging is essential for achieving sensitive, selective, and interference-free detection of urea.

The formation of Ni(OH)2 and its subsequent oxidation to NiOOH are strongly influenced by the concentration of NaOH. To investigate this effect, the SWV response of 10 µM urea was recorded in varying concentrations of NaOH: 0.01 M (pH = 12), 0.05 M (pH = 12.7), 0.1 M (pH = 13), 0.2 M (pH = 13.3), and 0.5 M (pH = 13.7), as shown in Fig. 11a. The NaOH concentration had a pronounced effect on both the urea oxidation peak and the reversible oxidation peak of the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox couple. At 0.01 M NaOH, the urea oxidation peak was barely distinguishable. However, at 0.05 M and 0.1 M, the urea oxidation peaks became well-defined and sharp. Further increases in NaOH led to broadening and eventual decrease in the urea oxidation peak. These findings suggest that at higher NaOH concentrations, the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox process becomes dominant, and the excess OH− ions may hinder urea oxidation by competing for active sites on the electrode surface. Additionally, elevated OH− concentrations may reduce the adsorption of urea and impede its diffusion from the bulk solution to the electrode surface.

The electrochemical sensing of urea can be influenced by the presence of interfering species; therefore, their effects were systematically investigated. Ammonia (NH3) and melamine, both chemically related to urea due to their amine groups, were examined as potential interferents. As shown in Fig. 11b, NH3 significantly suppressed the urea oxidation peak, making urea detection unreliable in its presence. In contrast, melamine exhibited a less pronounced effect, allowing urea to still be detected. In both cases, the concentration of the interfering species was equal to that of urea (10 µM), and both also slightly influenced the reversible Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox process. The impact of metal ions on urea detection was also evaluated. Figure 11c displays the SWV responses of NiNPs/GCE in 10 µM urea with the addition of equal concentrations of Cu2+ and Zn2+ ions, separately, in 0.1 M NaOH. Both metal ions reduced the urea oxidation peak, with Zn2+ exhibiting a more significant inhibitory effect than Cu2+. Notably, no new redox peaks emerged, and the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH peak remained unaffected. Furthermore, the influence of industrial pollutants such as 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) and 2-chlorophenol (2-CP) on urea oxidation was examined, Fig. 11d. Both compounds interfered with the urea oxidation signal but had no impact on the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH peak. Among them, 2-CP had a more pronounced effect than 4-NP, suggesting that chlorophenols pose a greater challenge for urea detection than nitrophenols. In summary, among the tested species, only NH3 completely suppressed the urea oxidation peak, rendering urea detection unfeasible in its presence. NH3 competes with urea for adsorption sites on the electrode surface, reducing urea oxidation efficiency, and can alter the local pH, affecting reaction kinetics. It may also interact with the tris base capping via hydrogen bonding, potentially impacting NiNPs/GCE stability. Interactions between NH3 and NiOOH have been reported, including oxidation of NiOOH to nitrogen oxides (NOx), which facilitates NH3 oxidation at potentials slightly higher than Ni(OH)2 → to NiOOH44. These effects indicate that NH3 can interfere with urea detection and compromise electrode performance. Therefore, the presence of NH3 must be considered when using NiNPs/GCE for urea sensing. While alternative electrodes may avoid NH3 oxidation, they may not maintain urea selectivity. Acid-impregnated membrane filters provide a practical solution: in aqueous solution, urea remains largely neutral, whereas NH3 reacts with acidic membrane sites to form NH4+ and is captured. This selective removal of NH3 enhances urea detection sensitivity. In contact, urea oxidation remained discernible in the presence of melamine, metal ions, and industrial pollutants, indicating that NiNPs/GCE can selectively detect urea under these conditions, except in the presence of NH3.

Calibration

The selective sensing of urea using SWV was further explored by evaluating the response of NiNPs/GCE to various concentrations of urea in 0.1 M NaOH. The resulting SWV curves are presented in Fig. 12a. Urea concentrations range from 0.0625 mM to 1.25 mM (i.e., 0.0625, 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, and 1.25 mM) were tested. At 0 mM, no increase in oxidation current was observed. However, starting from 0.0625 mM, the urea oxidation current increased progressively and linearly with concentration. This linearity is illustrated in Fig. 12b, which shows a direct correlation between peak current and urea concentration. The linear regression equation was Ip (µA) = 79.17 x C (mM) + 1.1698 with an excellent correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.9971. The detection limit was calculated based on three measurements of the blank signal using the formula LOD = 3.3 x (standard deviation of blank)/slope, assuming a signal-to-noise ratio of 3. The limit of detection (LOD) was estimated to be 28.8 µM, which falls within the acceptable range for non-enzymatic nickel-based electrodes and compares favorably with similar systems reported in Table 2. The stability of NiNPs/GCE was assessed over 7 consecutive days, with measurements taken daily (Supplementary Figure S3(a)). The electrode exhibited consistent performance, with a relative standard deviation (RSD) below 4.1%. Reproducibility was also examined across electrodes fabricated at different times (Supplementary Figure S3(b)), yielding an RSD of 1.8%, indicating good fabrication consistency. Furthermore, reproducibility across three different urea solutions (10 µM urea in 0.1 M NaOH, Supplementary Figure S3(c)) showed results withing acceptable variability (RSD = 2.9%), confirming the reliability of the sensor for urea detection. Additionally, metallic NiNPs offer advantages over complex Ni-based and other nanomaterials listed in Table 212,13,17,20,39]– [40,44,45. Their synthesis is straightforward, carried out at room temperature without the need for calcination or harsh reagents. Both ascorbic acid and tris base serve as green agents, ascorbic acid as a reductant, while tris base functions as a stabilizer. These compounds are non-toxic, biodegradable, and environmentally friendly. Fabrication is equally simple, with NiNPs/GCE prepared by drop-casting the colloidal solution at room temperature. Moreover, the basic nature of tris base enhances stability in alkaline media, making NiNPs more durable than many reported nanomaterials for efficient urea detection.

Real sample

Municipal and agricultural wastewater samples were collected from Jeddah, filtered through a 0.45 µM membrane filter, and diluted with 0.1 M NaOH. The samples were stored at 4 °C until measurement. Using the NiNPs/GCE, urea concentrations of 2.2 mM in municipal wastewater and 7.35 mM in agricultural wastewater were detected. These values are in line with those reported in the literature, although variations can occur depending on the wastewater source and treatment stage47,48. The presence of NH3 in municipal or agricultural wastewater depends on factors such as sample freshness, pH, and climate. In fresh samples, urea may not have fully hydrolyzed into NH3, and under slightly acidic or neutral pH conditions, this hydrolysis occurs more slowly. Additionally, NH3 volatilization is enhanced in warm climates such as that of Jeddah. Therefore, the detection of urea in both samples suggests that NH3 was either absent or present only at minimum levels, insufficient to significantly interfere with urea detection.

To further assess the practical applicability of the sensor, recovery tests were performed using the standard addition method. Known concentrations of urea (0.25-1.0 mM) were spiked into municipal and agricultural wastewater samples. The recovery results, summarized in Table 3, ranged from 98.7% to 101% for municipal wastewater and from 94% to 98.1% for agricultural wastewater. The slightly lower recovery in agricultural wastewater may result from enzymatic urea hydrolysis, reducing detectable levels. These results confirm the NiNPs/GCE sensor’s excellent accuracy and reliability in complex matrices.

Conclusion

Tris base-capped NiNPs were successfully synthesized using ascorbic acid as a reducing agent and immobilized onto a GCE via the drop-coating method. Comprehensive characterization confirmed the presence of tris base as an effective capping agent, stabilizing Ni0 through surface-bound -OH and -NH2 groups of tris base that not only sterically confined the NiNPs but also provided electrostatic repulsion against dissolved oxygen and OH− ions, ensuring long-term stability of the metallic Ni0 state. The NiNPs exhibited an oval morphology with smooth and uniform surface, and an average size of 50–90 nm, providing a favorable platform for the efficient adsorption of urea molecules. EIS demonstrated the excellent charge transfer capabilities of the NiNPs/GCE, while CV revealed that the -OH and-NH2 groups not only stabilize the nanoparticles but also reduce electrode fouling by minimizing adsorption of urea oxidation products. In contrast, malic acid-capped NiNPs displayed distorted spherical morphology, and both EIS and CV revealed inferior performance compared to the oval-shaped tris-base-capped NiNPs. SWV enabled the clear decoupling of urea oxidation from the reversible Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox couple, facilitating selective and sensitive urea sensing. A low detection limit of 28.8 µM was achieved in 0.1 M NaOH, which is comparable to or better than many previously reported Ni-based sensors. The NiNPs/GCE also demonstrated excellent stability and reproducibility over 7 days. Its practical applicability was demonstrated by the successful detection of urea in municipal and agricultural wastewater, with high recovery rates of 98.7–101% and 94.98.1%, respectively, using the standard addition method. Therefore, this study introduces a novel NiNPs-based electrode system coupled with SWV for the selective and sensitive electrochemical sensing of urea, a combination not previously reported.
