Sepsis mortality prediction using machine learning and deep learning - a systematic review
Authors: Mohannad N AbuHaweeleh, Adiba Tabassum Chowdhury, Mehrin Newaz, Purnata Saha, Khandaker Reajul Islam, Jaya Kumar, Muhammad E H Chowdhury, Shona Pedersen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis, a critical infection-induced inflammatory condition, poses substantial global health challenges, demanding timely detection for effective intervention. This article explores the application of machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) in predicting sepsis using electronic health record (EHR) to enhance patient outcomes. A comprehensive search across PubMed, IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar, and Scopus yielded 39 studies meeting stringent inclusion criteria. Predominantly retrospective (n = 34) and geographically diverse, these studies, spanning North America (n=19), Asia (n=13), Europe (n=6), and Australia (n=1), exhibited varied datasets, sepsis definitions, and prevalence rates, necessitating data augmentation strategies. Heterogeneous parameter usage, diverse model distribution, and inconsistent quality assessments were identified. Despite differences, longitudinal data showcased the potential of early sepsis prediction. The review outlines the challenges posed by disparate funding and article quality correlation, emphasizing the need for standardized evaluation metrics. In conclusion, this systematic review highlights the promising role of ML/DL methodologies in sepsis detection and early prediction through EHR, underscoring the imperative for standardized approaches and comprehensive quality assessments.Keywords: Machine learning, Deep learning, Sepsis mortality prediction, Predictive analytics, Clinical decision-making, Feature extraction, Model interpretability, Real-time monitoring

Introduction

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Organ dysfunction can be identified as an acute increase of ≥ 2 points in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score. Septic shock is a subset of sepsis in which profound circulatory, cellular, and metabolic abnormalities substantially increase mortality [1–3]. It continues to be a global health issue, with an estimated 48.9 million cases and 11 million deaths globally in 2017, representing almost 20% of all worldwide fatalities [4, 5]. In the United States alone, the annual incidence is reported to be about 750,000 cases [6]. Although care has improved, sepsis remains linked to high mortality rates that vary from 22% to 76%, depending on the region and severity of the case [7].

While bacterial infections are the primary cause, viral (such as influenza and COVID-19) and fungal infections also play a role. Patients frequently exhibit comorbid conditions, including chronic pulmonary disease or immune compromise, and nearly a third have had recent healthcare interactions prior to hospitalization [8]. Clinical symptoms like fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, confusion, oliguria, hypotension, and general malaise are often nonspecific. This complicates early recognition and timely intervention [9].

Many survivors, aside from facing acute mortality, encounter enduring health burdens. A cohort study of patients indicated that 7% developed post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) three months after sepsis, rising to 12% at the six-month mark. Additionally, nearly 25% exhibited clinically significant subsyndromal symptoms [10]. Furthermore, individuals who have survived severe sepsis demonstrate a 1.9 times greater risk of cardiovascular disease, akin to that observed in those with diabetes, smoking habits, or hyperlipidemia [11]. The urgency of timely detection, risk stratification, and accurate prognostic prediction is underscored by these long-term sequelae.

Numerous studies have investigated machine learning techniques like gradient boosting, support vector machines, logistic regression, random forests, and k-nearest neighbors to enhance the early detection and prognostic prediction of sepsis [12–14]. These models employ a variety of physiological markers—such as lactic acid, body temperature, blood pressure, hemoglobin, urine composition, and oxygen saturation—to improve the estimation of mortality risk [12, 13].

Numerous studies like Bao et al. [12], Li et al. [13] Miao Wu et al. [14] and Zhang et al. [15] have investigated machine learning techniques like gradient boosting, support vector machines, logistic regression, random forests, k-nearest neighbors and generative large language models to enhance the early detection and prognostic prediction of sepsis. Early goal-directed therapy remains the standard initial management strategy, and timely initiation of treatment is associated with improved outcomes. Although deep learning and machine learning approaches have shown significant promise in predicting sepsis outcomes, there is a distinct lack of thorough synthesis of these developments in the literature. There may not be enough information available or the study on sepsis mortality prediction using these technologies is currently dispersed. In order to close this gap, a thorough analysis of the literature has been conducted to determine the frequency, variety, and effectiveness of machine learning and deep learning techniques in forecasting sepsis mortality. The main goal is to bring disparate pieces of information together, spot new patterns, and offer a summarized picture that will help guide future studies and improve sepsis outcome prediction models. We hope to clarify the current situation, correct any biases in the study, and promote a better understanding of the state and future prospects of deep learning and machine learning in sepsis mortality prediction with this review.

The aim of this review is to investigate the field of machine learning applications for predicting sepsis mortality. By examining the current methodologies, algorithms, datasets, and evaluations of forecasting sepsis mortality, this study strives to offer a thorough comprehension of the advancements and limitations in this domain. Furthermore, it aims to highlight the potential implications and future directions for leveraging these technologies in clinical practice, with a focus on improving patient outcomes in sepsis management.

Materials and methods

Conventional machine learning models and assessment criteria

Machine learning models can be powerful tools for sepsis detection when trained on relevant clinical data. Below are some common machine learning models and evaluation metrics used in sepsis detection:

Conventional machine learning models

Logistic Regression: For binary classification tasks such as sepsis detection, logistic regression provides a straightforward linear model [17].

Random Forest: An ensemble learning technique that creates forecasts by combining several decision trees. Strong and capable of managing intricate relationships in the data are random forests [21].

Support vector machines (SVMs): SVMs work well for classification tasks that are both linear and nonlinear. They can capture complex decision limits and perform effectively with high-dimensional data [22].

Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM): GBM is an ensemble learning technique that constructs a series of decision trees one after the other, each of which fixes the mistakes of the preceding one. According to Kong et al. [23], it frequently offers good accuracy and is resistant to overfitting.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs): RNNs are deep learning models that are useful for time-series data such as patient vitals because they can capture temporal dependencies in sequential data. For these kinds of tasks, RNNs such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are frequently employed [2].

Evaluation metrics

When it comes to sepsis mortality detection, the evaluation metrics become even more critical, as the consequences of misclassifying a severe condition like sepsis can be dire. Below are some evaluation metrics that are particularly relevant for sepsis mortality detection:

Accuracy: The overall accuracy of the model’s predictions is measured by accuracy. However, accuracy alone might not be a sufficient metric for imbalanced datasets, especially if the mortality cases are rare compared to non-mortality cases [16].

Precision: Precision, sometimes referred to as positive predictive value, gauges how well the model predicts the positive outcomes. It indicates the proportion of patients identified as at risk of mortality who actually succumbed to sepsis. A high precision indicates that when the model predicts mortality, it is likely to be correct [17].

Recall (Sensitivity): Recall, sometimes referred to as sensitivity, gauges the model’s capacity to recognize every pertinent case. Recall, as it relates to sepsis mortality detection, is the percentage of actual mortality cases that the model correctly predicted [17].

F1-Result: The harmonic mean of recall and precision is known as the F1-score. When working with unbalanced datasets, such those in sepsis mortality prediction, it offers a compromise between precision and recall and is particularly helpful [17].

Area Under Curve (AUC)/Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC): The AUROC scale gauges how well the model can differentiate between patients who made a full recovery and those who passed away from sepsis. A higher AUC value indicates better discrimination ability [17].

Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve, or AUC-PR, is a metric that summarizes how well a model performs at different sensitivity levels. When working with unbalanced datasets, it is especially helpful and can offer a more insightful assessment than AUROC [17].

Confusion Matrix: The model’s predictions, including true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, are broken out in great detail in the confusion matrix. It is valuable for understanding the types of errors the model is making [24].

Calibration Curves: Calibration curves compare the predicted probabilities from the model against the actual outcomes. Well-calibrated models have predicted probabilities that align closely with the actual probabilities of outcomes, indicating reliability in predictions [25].

Quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA): It is a tool used to identify patients who are not in the intensive care unit (ICU) but are at risk of poorly managing sepsis. It facilitates the prompt assessment of a patient’s risk of organ failure associated with sepsis by healthcare personnel [20].

APACHE II: It is a rating system for critically unwell patients, especially those with sepsis, that is used to determine their severity and estimate their chance of dying. To determine a score, it assesses physiological characteristics as well as long-term medical issues. Age, chronic health conditions, and a variety of physiological factors are all taken into consideration by APACHE II. Higher scores correspond to a higher chance of death and a more severe disease [22].

Sequential Organ Failure Assessment, or SOFA, is a technique that is frequently used in the context of sepsis and critical illness to monitor a patient’s status with respect to organ dysfunction or failure over time. It evaluates the way in which various organ systems work. The respiratory, coagulation, liver, cardiovascular, central nervous system, and renal systems are the six organ systems that SOFA assesses. Points are given according to each system’s level of malfunction or failure. More severe organ dysfunction is indicated by a higher SOFA score [20].

Approach and procedure

Search approach

To find relevant studies from electronic resources like IEEE Xplore, PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar, a thorough search approach was implemented. Many terms related to “sepsis mortality,” “electronic health records,” “machine learning,” “early prediction,” and “deep learning” were included in the search. These terms were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR) and arranged inside parentheses to create efficient search queries. Examples of these are: “(sepsis mortality OR sepsis early prediction) AND (machine learning OR deep learning) AND (electronic health records OR AI in healthcare).” Only English-language articles published between January 2016 and September 2023 were included in the search. Each database had filters for language and publication date that were used to focus the results, and the search method was recorded for reporting needs.

Study selection

After retrieving search results from the designated databases and removing duplicates, a manual screening process was initiated. During this phase, the titles and abstracts of the articles were scrutinized to assess their relevance in alignment with our research question and predetermined inclusion/exclusion criteria. Articles that clearly lacked relevance were excluded at this stage. For the remaining studies that successfully passed the initial screening, we obtained and thoroughly reviewed their full-text versions. Each article underwent a meticulous examination against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, ensuring a comprehensive assessment. This rigorous evaluation guaranteed that the selected articles adhered to the specific requirements of our systematic review. Any articles failing to meet these criteria were systematically excluded. Throughout this process, detailed records were diligently maintained, documenting the reasons for the exclusion of each article.

In addition to extracting dataset characteristics, we systematically categorized validation strategies across studies into internal validation, temporal validation, external validation using independent cohorts, and geographic transportability. This enabled structured comparison of methodological robustness.

In addition, we systematically recorded whether each study reported clinical-utility and robustness assessments. Specifically, we extracted whether the study reported calibration metrics (calibration plots, calibration slope/intercept or Hosmer–Lemeshow statistics), decision-curve analysis (DCA) or net-benefit analysis, reporting of predictive values at chosen thresholds (PPV/NPV), and whether decision thresholds were pre-specified. We also recorded robustness checks relevant to overfitting and leakage: whether the study performed external validation, temporal validation, nested cross-validation or held-out test sets, and whether authors reported procedures to prevent information leakage (for example, strict separation of preprocessing and feature engineering between training and test partitions, time-aware feature construction, and separation of hyperparameter tuning from the final test set).

Bias assessment

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) paradigm for quasi-experimental research was used in our systematic review to evaluate the likelihood of bias in studies that use machine learning and deep learning to predict sepsis mortality. Every paper was rigorously assessed by three separate reviewers, guaranteeing a thorough examination of methodological quality. A systematic evaluation of research design, participant selection, and other pertinent aspects was made easier by the JBI recommendations. The manual assessment method prioritized a detailed and context-specific examination over automated techniques. Our review findings are more transparent and reliable because of this rigid technique.

Impact of funding source

The integrity of the systematic review’s design, execution, analysis, interpretation, and reporting must remain impartial and detached from the funding source. Preserving the autonomy and objectivity of the review process is crucial for ensuring the credibility of the results. In cases where funding is earmarked for a specific setting, demographic, or intervention, a meticulous evaluation is essential to discern the applicability of the findings to different contexts. This consideration influences the broadness of the results’ applicability. During the scrutiny of financial information associated with a publication, we conducted a thorough assessment of its quality and found no indications of a correlation between the examined metrics and the funding sources.

Results

Selection procedure

Following the establishment of inclusion and exclusion criteria based on publication date, machine learning/deep learning application, population characteristics, outcome, language, and study design, we developed an extensive search strategy to find pertinent studies from four electronic databases, as shown in Fig. 3. First, we evaluated the possible relevance of the retrieved studies by screening their titles and abstracts. After obtaining the full-text papers of the studies that made it beyond the first screening, we carefully examined them to determine which ones should be included. Out of the 1872 articles that were found in the first search, 39 satisfied all inclusion requirements. The majority (n = 1020 + 540 = 1560) were disqualified for a variety of reasons, including duplicate issue, not utilizing deep learning or machine learning, addressing subjects unrelated to mortality prediction, using a different study design from the one in the current publication and going without peer review.

Characteristics of the studies

Out of the 39 papers, 5 studies (12.8%) that made up this systematic review were prospective, and the other 34 (87.2%) were retrospective as shown in Fig. 4(a). Geographically, the 39 studies included North America (n = 19, mainly U.S.-based cohorts), Asia (n = 13, comprising Taiwan, Korea, China, and Turkey), Europe (n = 6, including Sweden and multi-center datasets), and Australia (n = 1). This breakdown underscores the prevalence of U.S. datasets, while also showcasing significant contributions from healthcare systems in Europe and Asia.

Distribution of (a) Study type, (b) Data sources and (c) Used dataset in this study

The included studies employed various research designs with distinct methodological characteristics. Retrospective cohort studies, comprising the majority of research, analyzed historical EHR data, offering large sample sizes but potential selection bias and incomplete data recording. Prospective cohort studies followed patients forward in time, allowing standardized data collection with reduced recall bias but requiring greater resources. Cross-sectional studies provided snapshots useful for model validation but limited in establishing temporal relationships. Experimental studies, including machine learning competitions, focused on novel predictive approaches with methodological innovation but potentially limited external validity. Understanding these design differences is crucial for interpreting evidence strength and clinical applicability. Upon analysis, studies were classified into two distinct prediction problems: sepsis mortality prediction (n = 33, 89.2%) focusing on death prediction in patients with established sepsis, and sepsis onset prediction (n = 4, 10.8%) focusing on sepsis development in at-risk populations.

Numerous research made use of information from several sources. It should be noted that while the included studies variably applied Sepsis-2 or Sepsis-3 definitions, our review consistently refers to Sepsis-3 as the standardized definition for sepsis and septic shock. This ensures alignment with current clinical practice guidelines. Classical machine learning and deep learning models

A large variety of deep learning and machine learning models were employed in numerous investigations. Out of the 39 studies that were reviewed, 17.9% employed alternative deep learning techniques, and 69.2% used traditional machine learning models. The two kinds of models were blended in about 7.7% of the articles and 5.1% discussed about transformers and generative large language models.

Neural Networks (NN), k-nearest neighbor (kNN), AdaBoost, Naïve Bayes (NB) classifiers, and other classical machine learning models were examples of regression models with survival analysis. These models included Treebag, Decision trees (DT), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGboost), Random Forests (RF), LightGBM, Logistic Regressions (LR), Gradient Boosting (GB), Support Vector Machines (SVM), SDG, Bootstrap Forests, Stochastic Gradient Boosting (SGB), and Selection Operators (LASSO), respectively. Deep learning models included temporal convolutional networks (TCN), GRU-RNN, RNN-LSTM, recurrent neural networks (RNN), CNN-GRU, SoftMax, gated recurrent units (GRU), CNN-LSTM, deep artificial intelligence (DeepAISE), deep SOFA-sepsis prediction algorithms (DSPA), convolutional neural networks (CNN), long short-term memory networks (LSTM), deep convolutional neural networks (GRU), and more.

However, the field has recently progressed beyond traditional architectures to transformer-based and generative large language models (LLMs), which are at the forefront of artificial intelligence today. Recent research has shown that transformer-based generative LLMs like GPT-4, GPT-4o, Qwen2.5-instruct, and DeepSeek-R1 can be integrated into applications related to sepsis. These models were integrated into Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) frameworks that merge pretrained language models with external knowledge sources to enhance biomedical reasoning. This strategy tackles essential difficulties like reducing hallucinations, ensuring responses are faithful, and maintaining clinical relevance. Custom benchmarks (RAGAs) were used to evaluate precision, recall, and reasoning quality in sepsis-related queries. In addition, these frameworks investigated explainability via ontology-guided enrichment and expert validation, which further bolstered their clinical utility.

Furthermore, research has been conducted on the use of transformer-based LLMs for personalized sepsis management, utilizing models like GPT-4, GPT-4o, and Qwen2.5-instruct in RAG pipelines to enhance decision support systems. These studies underscore the promise of LLMs in biomedical text mining and knowledge extraction, as well as in promoting personalized clinical decision-making for sepsis patients. When considered collectively, these nascent models driven by transformers serve to augment the current array of ML and DL methods, indicating a significant transition toward AI systems in sepsis research that are more context-aware, explainable, and scalable.

Our research shows that across ICU cohorts, tree-based ensemble approaches (Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM) consistently outperformed baseline logistic regression when aggregated by methodological families, especially in datasets with more than 5,000 patients. While their advantage over ensembles decreased in smaller datasets, deep learning models—particularly LSTMs and CNN-LSTM hybrids—performed better in temporal prediction tasks utilizing continuous physiological streams. Although few transformer-based frameworks and extensive language model integrations demonstrated promise for jobs involving unstructured text and multimodal data.

Validation techniques

Validation techniques differed greatly among the 39 included papers. With 26 studies using it, K-fold cross-validation was by far the most popular method. Five-fold CV was utilized in twelve of these studies, ten-fold CV in eight, three-fold CV in one, and stratified five-fold CV in one. K-fold CV was referenced in four studies, although the precise number of folds was not specified. Furthermore, six studies included external validation using independent datasets like PhysioNet, eICU, or cohorts from various hospitals or nations, while seven studies used random split validation or simple train-test. In order to replicate potential deployment, five studies reported temporal validation, saving data from subsequent years for final testing. Notably, reproducibility was limited by two studies that provided no description of their validation process. K-fold CV predominated throughout, particularly 5-fold and 10-fold, but temporal and external validation, which are essential for generalizability, were far less common.

3.5 Metrics of the Performance Most of the articles used the same benchmarks, despite the fact that they employed distinct performance metrics. These include the area under the curve (AUC) or area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (AUROC) in 84.2% of cases; sensitivity or recall (R) in 36.8% of cases; specificity (S) in 23.7% of cases; accuracy (A) in 31.5% of cases; precision (P) in 15.8% of cases; and F1 score/F2 score (F) in 21% of cases. Because datasets and experimental procedures differ throughout studies, it is inaccurate to compare performance just based on measures like AUROC or accuracy. Overview of sepsis onset prediction studies (n = 4)

While discrimination metrics (most commonly AUROC/AUC) were consistently reported (AUROC/AUC in 84.2% of included studies), reporting of clinical-utility and robustness measures was limited. Only a minority of studies reported calibration information (calibration plots or calibration slope/intercept) or conducted decision-curve analysis/net-benefit assessments. Similarly, although internal validation (cross-validation or random splits) was common, temporal validation and external/geographic validation were much less frequent. Nested cross-validation and explicit statements about measures taken to avoid information leakage were uncommon.

There was little reporting of clinical usefulness indicators other than AUROC/accuracy. Only three studies used DCA to estimate net benefit across clinical thresholds, whereas eleven studies used calibration assessments (such as calibration plots, calibration-in-the-large, Hosmer–Lemeshow test, or intercept/slope). Two studies visualized calibration using nomograms, while seven studies reported positive and negative predictive values (PPV/NPV) at selected cutoffs. More than 70% of studies lacked a calibration or net-benefit analysis, despite the significance of bedside applicability.

Regarding robustness checks, around nine articles dealt with handling class imbalances, while six studies explained methods for imputation of missing data. Leakage avoidance techniques like causal convolutions and time-aware feature building were only specifically covered in three papers. Sensitivity analysis (e.g., subgroup AUROC stability, permutation testing, year-based splits) were conducted in four investigations. Nevertheless, systematic leakage checks were not mentioned in the majority of research, raising questions about the validity of high AUROC values that were reported.

When applied to ICU-based cohorts, pooled AUROC scores for the majority of the 39 included studies ranged between 0.70 and 0.85; Random Forest, XGBoost, and LSTM models most frequently showed up in the top range ( > 0.80). With AUROCs varying from 0.65 to 0.88 based on sample size and prediction window, studies carried out in ED displayed more variability. Instead of reporting a statistical meta-analysis, we present a pooled descriptive synthesis due to the significant methodological heterogeneity.

Prediction of sepsis-mortality onset

Among the 39 included studies, 35 (89.7%) focused on sepsis mortality prediction in patients with established sepsis diagnosis, while 4 (10.3%) addressed sepsis onset prediction in at-risk populations. Sepsis mortality prediction studies used longitudinal data to predict mortality at various time horizons following sepsis diagnosis. The temporal prediction windows varied considerably, with one study achieving predictions as early as 2 hours post-presentation, while others provided insights into mortality prediction with lead times extending from 12 to 48 hours after sepsis recognition. The majority of studies (16 studies) demonstrated that mortality could be predicted within a 24-hour window following sepsis diagnosis, with some studies extending predictions to 48–72 hours, and one study achieving 96-hour mortality forecasting.

Sepsis onset prediction studies (n = 4) represented a smaller but distinct research area, focusing on identifying at-risk patients who would subsequently develop sepsis. These studies utilized diverse clinical settings including ED, general hospital wards, and mixed populations, with prediction horizons ranging from immediate risk assessment at hospital admission to 24–48 hours before sepsis criteria were met. The limited number of onset prediction studies highlights a significant research gap, particularly given the clinical importance of early sepsis recognition.

Analysis of 35 sepsis mortality prediction studies showed ICU studies (n = 24) predominantly utilized 24–48 hour prediction windows following sepsis diagnosis, while ED studies (n = 7) demonstrated greater temporal diversity including immediate mortality risk assessment and extended 72+ hour forecasting as illustrated in Fig. 6. Ward-based mortality prediction studies were absent from the literature, representing a significant research gap. The concentration of studies in the 24–48 hour window reflects standard clinical decision-making timelines for sepsis mortality assessment and intervention planning.

Quality evaluation of the included studies

Using 16-factor evaluation criteria, the quality measurement findings are shown in Table 3. The quality evaluation’s objective was to evaluate the extent to which certain prediction models were used and recorded. To assess the quality of the eligible publications, we used a thorough process that considered factors like repeatability, robustness, unmet needs, clinical significance, and generalizability. Sample quantity, data availability, code availability, web deployment, missing data handling, feature engineering, mortality symptoms prevalence, ML models, hyperparameters, performance metric reporting, external validation, clinical applicability, and explainability etc. were among the factors that made up our quality assessment criteria, which we adapted from Moor et al. [54]. A quality assessment table was utilized to systematically examine these 16 pertinent criteria, with each category being graded as either “yes” or “no.” This methodical technique made sure that the study’s quality assessment was transparent. While no study satisfied all 16 requirements, none also failed to give due credit to the machine learning and deep learning models.

A tool for evaluating the quality of the included studies

The articles under consideration have been systematically classified using our recently implemented assessment tool as shown in Table 3 into 4 distinct categories: low quality (LQ) with scores ranging from 0 to 40%, average quality (AQ) with scores spanning 40 to 60%, above-average quality (AAQ) ranging from 60 to 80%, and high quality (HQ) scoring between 80 and 100%. The distribution of the 39 articles under investigation in this study shows the following categorization based on the applicable evaluation criteria: There are four HQ, nineteen AAQ, thirteen AQ, and one LQ article.

The quality of the 37 studies that were examined varied. Four studies (10.8%) consistently reported various robustness and clinical-utility characteristics, earning them a high quality (HQ) rating. Nineteen studies (51.3%) met most but not all of the criteria for above-average quality (AAQ). Due to a lack of external validation, calibration reporting, or code/data availability, 13 studies (35.1%) were classified as average quality (AQ). Merely one study (2.7%) received a poor quality (LQ) rating, indicating a lack of adherence to reporting and methodological guidelines.

Notably, almost all studies indicated data availability, although web/mobile deployment and code availability were uncommon. Though trustworthy methods to reduce overfitting (such as nested cross-validation and leakage avoidance) were cited in less than half of the studies, missing data handling and feature engineering were universally accepted. Many published models’ generalizability was diminished by the uneven application of external validation. Despite growing consideration, explainability and clinical application were not consistently combined.

While a number of studies used longitudinal EHR data to forecast sepsis mortality within 6–96 hours of onset, only a handful assessed real-time or near real-time implementation. While prospective designs like those of Cheng et al. (2022) [21] and Kong et al. (2020) [23] showed that dynamic monitoring of vital signs is feasible, they did not evaluate latency, clinician workload, or alert fatigue. The existence of this gap emphasizes the necessity for future trials that assess both predictive accuracy and clinical usability.

The application of interpretability methods varied across the studies included. SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) was the most prevalent method, allowing for global and patient-level feature attribution (e.g., emphasizing blood pressure, lactate, and respiratory rate as significant predictors) [34, 35]. LIME was used less often and primarily for local explanation of logistic regression models [42]. Some deep learning architectures utilized attention weights to prioritize temporal features. It is worth mentioning that counterfactual and causal interpretability methods were not present, highlighting a chance for upcoming studies to improve clinician trust and decision support [13, 32].

As an additional measure of methodological quality, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was employed. The majority of studies received scores between 6 and 9 out of 9, which further supports the idea that although technical rigor is getting better, there are still systematic deficiencies in clinical translation, transparency, and repeatability.

Discussion

Key findings

After thorough screening, 39 articles out of the 1,872 retrieved articles for the systematic review met all inclusion criteria. Duplication, lack of ML/DL application, irrelevant subject matter, improper study designs, or lack of peer review were the main reasons for the exclusion of studies.

34 (87.2%) and 5 (12.8%) of the 39 included studies were retrospective and prospective, respectively, indicating the preponderance of past EHR-based research with some forward-looking data gathering. North America (n = 19, mostly the United States), Asia (n = 13, including China, Taiwan, Korea, and Turkey), Europe (n = 6), and Australia (n = 1) were the geographical sites of the studies. The importance of U.S. datasets is highlighted by this regional distribution, which also highlights the significant contributions of Asian and European health systems.

Sepsis mortality prediction (n = 35, 89.7%) and sepsis onset prediction (n = 4, 10.3%) were the two prediction tasks into which the studies were divided. The majority of mortality prediction studies (n = 24) were conducted in ICU, where prediction windows of 24 to 48 hours following diagnosis were most prevalent. In contrast, trials conducted in ED (n = 7) showed more temporal diversity, with some predicting mortality beyond 72 hours and others offering instant risk assessments. With prediction horizons ranging from admission to 48 hours prior to sepsis onset, at-risk patients in EDs, wards, and mixed settings were the focus of these research, which, despite their rarity, represented a significant direction

Methodologically, 69.2% of studies employed conventional machine learning models, 17.9% used deep learning architectures, 7.7% blended the two, and 5.1% investigated LLMs or transformer-based models. While deep learning studies used CNNs, RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs, and temporal convolutional models, traditional machine learning techniques used Random Forest, Logistic Regression, XGBoost, kNN, SVM, and Naïve Bayes. Transformer-based generative LLMs (such as GPT-4, Qwen2.5, and DeepSeek-R1) have been used in more recent research and have been evaluated in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) frameworks for decision support and biological reasoning. Taken together, ensemble methods are most reliable in structured tabular EHR data, while recurrent deep learning models are advantageous when handling sequential time-series data such as vital signs. Logistic regression and simpler models remain competitive in smaller cohorts due to reduced overfitting risk. Early evidence suggests transformers may augment both structured and unstructured inputs, but validation remains sparse.

A critical limitation observed across the reviewed studies is the insufficient attention to model interpretability and clinical usability. While the primary metric used for performance reporting was AUROC/AUC (84.2%), which was augmented by F1/F2 scores (21%), sensitivity (36.8%), specificity (23.7%), accuracy (31.5%), and precision (15.8%), fewer than one-quarter provided explainability features or discussed clinical decision support integration necessary for real-world implementation. Clinical-utility measures were infrequently provided; just 11 trials reported calibration, 3 reported decision-curve analysis, and 7 gave PPV/NPV, restricting direct bedside applicability. Deep learning approaches predominantly functioned as “black box” models without transparent reasoning pathways, creating significant barriers to clinician acceptance and regulatory approval. The disconnect between algorithmic complexity and clinical interpretability represents a fundamental challenge for translation into practice, where understanding prediction rationale is essential for patient safety and therapeutic decision-making. Most studies focused exclusively on predictive accuracy rather than addressing practical deployment considerations such as workflow integration, computational requirements, alert fatigue mitigation, or user interface design. This gap between model performance and clinical utility suggests that future research must prioritize interpretable-by-design approaches or robust post-hoc explanation methods specifically validated in clinical environments to achieve meaningful clinical adoption.

The methods used for validation were diverse. Seven research employed train-test or random split methods, and 26 studies (66.7%) used K-fold cross-validation (12 with 5-fold, 8 with 10-fold, and 1 with 3-fold or stratified 5-fold). Five articles used temporal validation by withholding data from subsequent years, and six studies conducted external validation on other cohorts. However, two studies failed to disclose their validation procedure, which raises questions about reproducibility.

Methodological diversity was further revealed by the quality evaluation (Table 3). One study (2.7%) was classified as low quality (LQ), 13 (35.1%) as average quality (AQ), 19 (51.3%) as above-average quality (AAQ), and 4 (10.8%) as high quality (HQ). Code exchange and deployment readiness were uncommon, but data availability was widespread. While addressing missing data and feature engineering were commonly mentioned, robustness checks like layered cross-validation and leakage avoidance were not always addressed. Explainability was not consistently incorporated, and generalizability was frequently constrained by a lack of external validation.

When combined, these results show that research on sepsis prediction is varied and dynamic, with significant methodological innovation but enduring deficiencies in clinical utility reporting, repeatability, and validation rigor.

Overview of Recent Systematic Reviews in Applicable Fields

This section delves into recent systematic reviews focusing on ML and DL applications in predicting sepsis mortality. Analyzing five systematic review papers, it synthesizes crucial insights into the landscape of research methodologies, strengths, and limitations within this domain. As a critical area of interest in healthcare, these reviews provide a comprehensive overview, emphasizing gaps, trends, and potential advancements in leveraging ML and DL for accurate sepsis mortality prediction. This synthesis aims to guide future research, advancing clinical practices to confront and manage the impact of sepsis on patient outcomes within a succinct analysis.

Bauer et al. [55] systematically reviewed 170 papers within this domain. The authors observed that the mean death rate over a period of 30 days for sepsis was 24.4% (95% CI 21.5–27.2%), and the 90-day death rate was 32.2% (95% CI 27.0–37.5%). Notably, death rates from Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) were less than those from follow-up studies. Geographical variations were evident, death rates over a 30 day period for septic shock were at 33.7% (95% CI 31.5–35.9) within North America. The rates were 32.5% (95% CI 31.7–33.3) for Europe and 26.4% (95% CI 18.1–34.6) for Australia. Regardless, the authors underscored the necessity for further research to examine the influence of disease burden trends on sepsis and septic shock mortality rates over a 30 and 90 day period.

Luhr et al. [56] suggests a decrease in the death rate over 28 days among medical cases with acute sepsis and septic shock between 1991 and 2013, as observed in data obtained from Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs). However, upon adjusting for the severity at the time of study enrollment using parameters like APACHE II, SAPS II, and SOFAscore, there seemed to be no notable change in death rates over the examined period. Out of the 44 trials analyzed, only 4 demonstrated effectiveness in reducing mortality, and none of the approaches utilized in these trials have been integrated into the current standard treatment procedures for sepsis.

Pregernig et al. [57] conducted an examination of 44 studies, with 28 of them being incorporated into a meta-analysis. The authors observed that clinical parameters such as Ang-1, Ang-2, and suPAR offer valuable predictive data concerning death rates within medical subjects with sepsis. Advancing this field, authors suggest the refinement of consistent assessments and evaluations when incorporated into biomarker panels.

Stevenson et al. [58] revealed that individuals with acute sepsis under standard care exhibited a death rate of 33.2% over a 28-day period. Notably, the recorded death rates depicted an annual decrease of 3.0% (95% CI 0.8%, 5.0%, p = 0.009), declining from 46.9% [SMR 0.94, 95% CI (0.86, 1.03)] between 1991 and 1995 to 29% [SMR 0.53, (95% CI (0.50, 0.57)] from 2006 to 2009, reflecting a 3.0% annual change. Moreover, the fatality patterns observed in medical trial volunteers resembled those recognized through institutional records, thereby supporting utilization of institutional records for monitoring fatality patterns among individuals with severe sepsis.

Hajj et al. [24] conclusion underscores that the impact of sepsis on the population is multifaceted and has been comprehensively assessed. The incidence of sepsis has shown a consistent rise over the past three decades, exhibiting significant variations across different age groups, notably burdening the elderly population. Furthermore, readmission rates, length of hospital stay, and mortality rates significantly surpass those of patients admitted with non-sepsis diagnosis. This discrepancy in results corresponds to a considerably elevated economic load linked to this medical condition.

These reviews emphasize the diversity in methodologies and the essential role of various clinical features and biomarkers in prognostication. Discrepancies in mortality rates, the impact of data standardization, and the efficacy of different predictive models underscore the necessity for further research. Advancements in standardized assays and the development of comprehensive biomarker panels remain crucial for improving prognostic accuracy. This collective review highlights the dynamic landscape and the potential for Artificial Intelligence in enhancing sepsis mortality prediction.

Limitations

A number of persistent issues plague research on machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) for the prediction of sepsis mortality. Reliability and generalizability are limited by incomplete or unbalanced datasets, high rates of imputation (up to 50% in MIMIC databases, for example), and variability in specific clinical variables. These issues continue to be key concerns about data quality and standardization. Additionally, interpretability is still an issue because complicated models frequently lack clear decision-making procedures, which raises ethical and medical questions. Furthermore, because the majority of research are retrospective and single-center, which limits their application to larger populations, transferability across healthcare systems is dubious. Challenges and future directions

As we delve into the promising realm of artificial intelligence-based applications in sepsis mortality prediction, it is imperative to acknowledge both the potential avenues for future exploration and the inherent limitations that guide our current understanding. This section aims to delineate the trajectory for future research, identifying areas for refinement and expansion, while also highlighting the boundaries that necessitate careful consideration. By addressing these aspects, we aim to contribute to the ongoing dialogue surrounding the optimization and ethical application of machine learning methodologies in the critical field of sepsis mortality prediction. Utilizing machine learning algorithms for early sepsis prediction through Electronic Health Records (EHRs) has significant potential to enhance patient outcomes [59].

The timely anticipation of sepsis facilitates prompt interventions, including suitable antibiotic therapy and fluid resuscitation, contributing to a substantial reduction in morbidity and mortality rates [60]. Nevertheless, the effective integration of predictive models for sepsis based on machine learning into medical practice demands the resolution of several challenges. This section outlines the main issues and suggests probable future directions in this domain.

Data Quality and Availability: Limited access to high-quality, standardized data can impede the development of accurate models. Incomplete or biased datasets may affect the model’s generalizability and performance. EHR information is often collected through diverse origins, resulting in heterogeneous formats, structures, and standards. It may contain missing or incomplete entries, leading to gaps in patient records. To address these challenges, further investigations should emphasize initiatives to improve information integrity and normalization practices, enhance interoperability between healthcare systems, and establish comprehensive strategies for handling missing or incomplete data [61].

Interpretability and Explainability: Deep Learning models, particularly, are sometimes regarded as “black box” models, rendering their decision-making procedures difficult to decipher. This ambiguity hinders the acceptance of these models in a clinical setting. For successful integration into clinical practice, models must provide results in a comprehensible manner that aligns with the existing medical knowledge and reasoning of healthcare professionals.

Clinical Heterogeneity: EHR data presents challenges in heterogeneity, incompleteness, and susceptibility to errors, thereby complicating the development of precise prediction models. Given the extensive array of variables within EHR data, not all may be pertinent to sepsis forecasting. Variable selection methodologies and sophisticated learning techniques, i.e. deep learning, offer avenues to discern discriminative features and derive significant depictions from patient information [62].

Imbalanced Datasets: Sepsis occurrences are comparatively infrequent when contrasted with non-sepsis cases, resulting in imbalanced datasets. The presence of class imbalance can exert an impact on the performance of models, necessitating the utilization of methods like oversampling, under-sampling and the implementation of sophisticated algorithms specifically developed to address imbalanced information [38]. Therefore, Models may exhibit biases and may not adequately capture the nuances of sepsis prediction.

Clinical Adoption and Integration: Resistance to change and concerns about model reliability may hinder widespread adoption by healthcare professionals. Establishing medical significance of Machine learning-driven models for sepsis forecasting is imperative. It necessitates future validation experiments conducted in medical environments to evaluate the impact of these models, their influence on medical subject results, and their economic efficiency in comparison to prevailing medical methods [63].

Ethical and Legal Considerations: In sepsis mortality prediction, ethical and legal considerations are paramount. These encompass safeguarding patient privacy through meticulous anonymization and consent procedures, addressing transparency concerns by embedding interpretability features, mitigating biases to ensure fairness across demographic groups, and rigorously assessing the clinical impact of these models on patient outcomes. Furthermore, compliance with legal frameworks governing data protection and healthcare regulations is imperative.

Limited Generalization: Machine learning models developed and trained within a specific healthcare system may exhibit challenges in generalization when applied to different healthcare systems or patient demographics. Subsequent experimentation efforts must prioritize outside corroboration and assess the applicability of sepsis forecasting models across varied medical environments [64]. This approach is essential to ascertain the real-world effectiveness and robustness of these models beyond their original training context.

AI offers significant potential to enhance clinical decision-making, but it cannot replace the foundations of evidence-based medicine (EBM). EBM, grounded in trials and systematic reviews, remains the gold standard for reliable practice, while AI excels at analyzing high-dimensional, real-world data. The two are best viewed as complementary: AI can generate insights that EBM alone may not capture, and EBM provides the validation framework to ensure safety and clinical relevance. Integration requires hybrid models of evaluation, where AI predictions are tested against established evidence and validated in prospective clinical studies. Human-in-the-loop systems allow clinicians to oversee AI outputs, ensuring alignment with medical reasoning and patient safety. For sepsis mortality prediction, this means embedding ML tools within guideline-driven protocols to assess predictive accuracy, clinical impact, and cost-effectiveness. Future work should prioritize frameworks that integrate AI outputs into evidence pipelines, including standardized reporting, prospective AI-guided trials, and alignment with clinical endpoints. Such approaches ensure that AI strengthens—rather than supplants—the principles of EBM [14].

The challenges and future directions in employing Machine Learning and Deep Learning for sepsis fatality forecasting underscore the intricate landscape of healthcare data. Addressing issues such as data quality, imbalances, and generalizability requires concerted efforts in research and application. Ethical considerations, including patient privacy and model transparency, necessitate ongoing attention. The evolving field’s success hinges on collaborative endeavors between data scientists, healthcare professionals, ethicists, and legal experts. Future research should persistently strive for comprehensive, standardized datasets, improved model interpretability, and adherence to ethical and legal standards, fostering advancements in accurate, generalizable, and ethically sound models for sepsis mortality prediction.

Conclusions

With the increasing use of deep learning and machine learning techniques in sepsis mortality prediction, precision medicine is entering a new age. These techniques have the potential to increase the promptness and precision of risk assessment by utilizing a variety of data sources and sophisticated algorithms. With a median of 0.85 with AUROCs ranging from 0.69 to 0.99, our pooled synthesis of reported performance demonstrates consistently strong discrimination across investigations. However, because these values are often generated from retrospective single-center cohorts with limited external or temporal validation, they probably represent optimistic upper boundaries.

Our evaluation of quality also shows that there are comparatively few high-quality designs, inconsistent calibration reporting, and diverse definitions of sepsis that limit the scope of existing research. These limitations highlight the fact that the area is still in its infancy and that there is not yet enough data to declare a revolution in clinical decision-making.

Resolving these issues through multi-center validations, transparent reporting, standardized study designs, and meticulous clinical impact assessment will be essential to future advancement. Crucially, the development process must incorporate ethical and legal considerations. In order to guarantee that predictive models develop into reliable, broadly applicable, and therapeutically significant instruments in the future, cooperation between physicians, data scientists, and legislators will be crucial. Only then will AI techniques be able to advance precision healthcare and reduce sepsis mortality in a responsible manner.
