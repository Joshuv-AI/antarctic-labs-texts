An analysis of facilitating and hindering factors in effective evidence-based medical education: a study within the context of medical education in Iran
Authors: Ali Reza Yusefi, Zahra Dehghani, Seyedeh Zahra Nahardani, Ghobad Ramezani, Mohammad Hasan Keshavarzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEvidence-Based Medical Education (EBME) is increasingly recognized as a key strategy for enhancing the quality and relevance of medical training. Despite its global endorsement, its implementation in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Iran, faces significant challenges. Highlighting the Iranian context is particularly important, as Iran represents a unique case of a rapidly expanding medical education system in a middle-income setting with distinct cultural and institutional dynamics. This study aimed to explore the factors that support or hinder the effective implementation of EBME in Iranian medical universities.MethodsThis qualitative study employed a conventional content analysis approach and was conducted between January and May 2025 across five major academic medical centers in Iran (Tehran, Shiraz, Isfahan, Mashhad, and Iran Universities of Medical Sciences). A purposive sample of 25 participants, including medical educators, curriculum developers, academic leaders, national experts, and senior medical students, was selected to ensure diversity in perspective and role. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews and analyzed using Graneheim and Lundman’s content analysis framework. MAXQDA 2022 software supported data management and coding. Trustworthiness was ensured through member checking, audit trails, peer debriefing, and external validation.ResultsThe analysis yielded eight main categories and 30 sub-categories representing barriers to EBME implementation, as well as eight main categories and 33 sub-categories as facilitators. Key barriers included insufficient faculty training, lack of institutional incentives, rigid curricula, cultural resistance, low student motivation, poor access to resources, absence of national policy guidelines, and misaligned assessment systems. For example, many faculty members lacked the necessary training to translate EBME principles into teaching practice, while students often demonstrated low motivation due to limited exposure to evidence-based approaches. Facilitators encompassed faculty competence and motivation, strong leadership support, innovative curriculum design, institutional and national-level professional development programs, active student engagement, improved access to educational technologies, supportive accreditation standards, and international collaboration. Illustratively, the presence of motivated faculty with prior EBME training and institutional support through professional development initiatives were repeatedly highlighted as drivers of successful adoption.ConclusionThe study highlights that effective EBME implementation in Iran requires a systemic approach addressing individual, institutional, and policy-level factors. Strategic investments in faculty development, curriculum reform, student engagement strategies, and the establishment of clear national policy frameworks, such as embedding EBME in accreditation standards and ensuring resource allocation, are essential for sustainable progress. The findings not only provide insights into the unique Iranian setting but also offer actionable strategies for practice and policy, particularly in strengthening EBME through faculty training, curriculum reform, and well-defined national guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08187-3.Keywords: Evidence-Based medical education, Medical curriculum, Faculty development, Student engagement, Higher education policy, Qualitative research, Iran

Introduction

Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM) has transformed medical education and clinical decision-making by promoting the integration of the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and patient values [1]. Evidence-Based Medical Education (EBME), an extension of this paradigm, refers to the use of high-quality evidence to inform educational decisions and teaching strategies in medical training programs [2]. It emphasizes the need to evaluate educational interventions through rigorous research, aiming to improve learning outcomes and ultimately, patient care quality [3]. Despite its global acceptance, the implementation of EBME remains highly variable across countries, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as Iran [4].

The core principles of EBME include the systematic application of educational research findings, critical appraisal of evidence, and adaptation of teaching methods based on outcome data [5]. Harden et al. define EBME as the “conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the education of medical students and health professionals” [6]. The goal is not only to transmit medical knowledge but also to enhance critical thinking, clinical reasoning, and life-long learning skills [7].

In Iran, despite several policy efforts and the integration of EBM principles into medical curricula, EBME implementation still faces substantial challenges [8]. These challenges stem from individual, institutional, and systemic factors such as limited access to educational evidence, insufficient faculty training, lack of institutional support, and resistance to change [9, 10]. While EBM has been gradually incorporated into clinical training, its educational counterpart, EBME, has not received the same level of attention in curriculum design or faculty development programs [10]. Moreover, Iran’s centrally governed medical education system, under the Ministry of Health and Medical Education, often creates tension between clinical service demands and educational innovation [11]. Cultural norms emphasizing hierarchy and deference to senior faculty further reinforce traditional, lecture-based methods [11, 12], slowing the adoption of evidence-informed teaching [12].

When compared to other LMICs, Iran shares many of the same barriers. These include limited infrastructure, insufficient investment in faculty development, and competing health system priorities [13]. However, unique structural constraints, particularly centralized governance and rapid medical school expansion, limit institutional autonomy [13]. For instance, while countries like India and Pakistan report faculty resistance and lack of institutional resources as key barriers [14, 15], Iran’s dual mandate of education and service provision adds another layer of complexity [16]. In contrast, some Latin American LMICs benefit from decentralized educational reforms that enable more local flexibility [17, 18].

The importance of EBME implementation in Iran is magnified by evolving healthcare needs, rising quality expectation, and a young, expanding medical education system [19]. Policymakers and educators are under pressure to align practices with international standards and evidence-based approaches [20, 21]. Yet, without a clear understanding of the barriers and enablers influencing EBME, achieving these goals remains challenging [20, 21].

Previous studies in high-income countries highlight institutional commitment, educational databases, collaboration, and faculty development as facilitators [21, 22], whereas hindrances include lack of time, resources, and organizational inertia [23]. Similar challenges are reported in LMICs due to weak infrastructure and policy frameworks [24]. In Iran’s context, these issues are compounded by centralized management, necessitating national-level rather than local reforms [25].

However, few empirical studies in Iran have systematically examined EBME barriers and facilitators. Most evidence remains anecdotal or limited to small-scale cases, restricting generalizability [26, 27]. Addressing this research gap is essential to guide reforms, optimize faculty development, and design evidence-based curricula suited to Iran’s context. Identifying locally relevant enablers also aids in adapting global best practices sustainably.

This study seeks to analyze factors that facilitate or hinder effective EBME implementation in Iranian medical schools. Using a qualitative approach and insights from educators, administrators, and policymakers, it explores contextual dynamics shaping EBME practices.

Nationally, the study can guide educational reforms, curriculum planning, and resource allocation tailored to Iran’s system. Internationally, it contributes to the global discourse on EBME by offering perspectives from a middle-income setting, relevant to countries with similar constraints.

In summary, effective EBME is crucial for improving medical education and patient care. This study addresses the need to understand how EBME is locally enacted within Iran, providing actionable insights for policymakers and educators.

Methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative study was conducted using a conventional content analysis approach, which is suitable for exploring under-researched areas where existing theoretical frameworks may be limited. The aim was to identify and explain the facilitating and hindering factors involved in effective evidence-based medical education (EBME) in Iran. The research was carried out between January and May 2025 in five major academic medical centers across Iran, including Tehran, Shiraz, Isfahan, Mashhad, and Iran Universities of Medical Sciences. These institutions were selected based on their leading role in medical education, geographic distribution, and their ongoing efforts to integrate evidence-based approaches in both clinical and educational contexts.

Participants

Participants were selected through purposive sampling with a maximum variation strategy. This ensured diversity in roles, experience levels, institutional affiliations, and perspectives on EBME. A total of 25 individuals took part in the study. These included medical educators actively involved in curriculum development and EBM teaching, educational administrators and deans of medical faculties, national experts in evidence-based medicine and medical education, and senior medical students who had direct experience with EBM content in their training. Medical students were selected from the final year of study using a combination of academic performance records and recommendations from faculty members to identify those most engaged with EBM courses. The selection of this varied group was intended to capture a broad spectrum of perspectives on both the implementation and reception of EBME, ranging from macro-level policy to classroom-level practices.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility for participation required a minimum of three years’ experience in the field of medical education for faculty and administrators, or being in the final year of medical school for student participants. All participants needed to have direct exposure to EBM-related programs or activities. Individuals who lacked familiarity with EBME, were unwilling to participate, or did not hold an active role in curriculum development or medical education management were excluded from the study.

Pilot testing of the interview guide

The interview guide was developed by the research team based on an extensive review of international and local literature on evidence-based medical education (see supplementary File 1). The initial version of the guide was reviewed and refined through a pilot test involving three individuals, one faculty member, one medical education administrator, and one final-year medical student. These participants met the inclusion criteria but were not included in the final sample. These pilot interviews were used to evaluate the clarity, relevance, and flow of the questions. Based on participants’ feedback, refinements included rewording technical terms for clarity (e.g., changing “curricular constraints” to “teaching schedule limitations”), reordering questions to follow a more logical sequence, and adding probing prompts such as “Can you give an example of how this challenge affected your teaching?” to capture richer details. These enhancements helped ensure that the final version was more context-sensitive and user-friendly. The final version included open-ended and probing questions such as: “Can you describe your experience with evidence-based teaching practices?”, “What factors do you think support or hinder the use of evidence in teaching medical students?”, and “In what ways could institutional policies be improved to facilitate EBME?”

Data collection

Data collection was conducted through in-depth, semi-structured interviews. These were conducted either face-to-face or via secure video conferencing platforms such as Zoom, depending on participant preference and logistical feasibility. Each interview lasted between 45 and 70 min. Interviews were conducted in Persian by experienced qualitative researchers who were trained in interview techniques and familiar with the context of Iranian medical education. Before each interview, participants received an information sheet explaining the study’s purpose, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and verbal consent was also recorded at the start of each interview. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. The interviews were supplemented with field notes and researcher memos that captured nonverbal cues, contextual observations, and emerging ideas throughout the data collection phase. Interviews continued until thematic saturation was achieved. Saturation was assessed both collectively and across participant groups. This means that additional interviews within each subgroup no longer generated new insights or concepts, and overall thematic patterns became stable across the full dataset.

Data analysis

At the outset, the research team engaged in reflexive consideration of their own backgrounds, professional experiences, and potential biases, and implemented strategies such as independent coding and regular team discussions to manage these influences and ensure the trustworthiness of the analysis. The research team consisted of five members with diverse professional expertise, including two assistant professors of medical education, one assistant professor of health services management with extensive experience in educational policy and curriculum evaluation, one assistant professor of restorative dentistry with a background in dental education, and one researcher from a clinical education research center. This multidisciplinary composition provided rich interpretive depth but also the potential for disciplinary bias. To address this, the team explicitly reflected on how their educational and professional orientations might shape the analysis. Reflexive meetings were held after each major coding phase to identify and challenge interpretive tendencies and ensure that final themes reflected participants’ perspectives rather than researchers’ assumptions.

The transcribed data were analyzed using conventional content analysis, following the steps outlined by Graneheim and Lundman [28]. First, all transcripts were read multiple times to gain an overall understanding of the data. Then, meaning units, phrases or sentences that conveyed a single idea relevant to the research question, were identified. These meaning units were condensed, labeled with initial codes, and systematically organized. Codes with similar meanings were grouped into sub-categories. These sub-categories were then further abstracted into main categories and overarching categories.

Two members of the research team independently conducted the initial coding and categorization. Their results were compared and discussed until consensus was reached. Disagreements were resolved through collaborative dialogue and, when necessary, consultation with a third team member. The software MAXQDA (version 2022) was used to manage data coding, retrieval, and visualization. Throughout the analysis, the researchers engaged in reflexive memoing to track their interpretations, assumptions, and evolving understanding of the data. Constant comparison techniques were used both within and across interviews to ensure consistency and depth in category development. Attention was paid to both manifest content, the visible, surface-level meanings, and latent content, which involved interpreting underlying assumptions and cultural nuances.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the rigor of the study, Lincoln and Guba’s four criteria for trustworthiness, credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability, were systematically applied [29]. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with participants, and member checking, where brief summaries of key interpretations were returned to participants for validation. Transferability was addressed by providing detailed descriptions of the research setting, participant demographics, and contextual factors influencing EBME in Iran. Dependability was achieved through maintaining a detailed audit trail, including interview guides, coding decisions, and analysis memos. Confirmability was ensured by documenting researcher reflexivity, explicitly acknowledging the multidisciplinary backgrounds of the research team and reflecting on how these experiences may have influenced data interpretation. In addition, two external qualitative research experts reviewed the coding framework and thematic structure to enhance neutrality and analytical rigor.

Results

A total of 25 individuals participated in this qualitative study, including 10 medical educators, 6 educational policymakers and curriculum planners, 4 clinical faculty members with active roles in EBM implementation, and 5 final-year medical students. Participants were purposefully selected from five major medical universities in Iran, Tehran, Shiraz, Isfahan, Mashhad, and Iran University of Medical Sciences, ensuring diversity in institutional background, academic position, and experience in evidence-based education. The participants included 14 males and 11 females, with ages ranging from 24 to 62 years. Their years of experience in the field of medical education ranged from 3 to 30 years, with a mean of 13.2 years. This diversity provided a multidimensional understanding of EBME implementation. Senior educators and policymakers tended to emphasize systemic and policy-related barriers such as lack of national standards and institutional support, while younger faculty and students highlighted challenges in teaching practice, motivation, and assessment methods. Female educators also frequently noted the limited access to mentorship opportunities, whereas male participants more often stressed institutional and leadership constraints. This variety of viewpoints enriched the data and ensured that findings reflected perspectives across different organizational levels and personal experiences (Table 1).

Participants identified a wide range of barriers that prevent the effective adoption and practice of evidence-based medical education (EBME) in Iran, which were categorized into 8 main categories and 30 sub-categories. The challenges emerged from multiple dimensions including faculty competencies, institutional environment, curriculum design, cultural attitudes, student preparedness, resource availability, policy framework, and assessment methods. Among these, insufficient faculty training, weak institutional support, and curriculum-related constraints were the most frequently emphasized barriers across participants. A major issue highlighted was insufficient faculty training, where educators often lacked both the theoretical knowledge and practical skills to implement EBME effectively. As one senior educator stated, “Many faculty members lack a clear understanding of EBME, which prevents its effective application in teaching” (P3). In contrast, a student expressed frustration from the learner’s side: “We rarely see teachers applying research findings in real teaching sessions; it feels like a gap between what is taught and what is practiced” (P21). Similarly, a policymaker emphasized systemic gaps, noting, “There is no unified national guideline to ensure EBME implementation across universities” (P16). These contrasting viewpoints underline the disconnect between policy intentions, faculty capacity, and classroom realities.

Moreover, institutional support was found to be weak with few incentives for faculty to innovate their teaching methods, leadership commitment that was often symbolic rather than practical, and limited dedicated funding for EBME activities. Curricular constraints were frequently mentioned, with participants emphasizing the overcrowded nature of existing curricula that left little room for new EBME content. The rigidity of teaching schedules also restricted the ability to adapt or introduce evidence-based teaching methods. Cultural resistance was a significant barrier, with many senior faculty members adhering to traditional lecture-based approaches and expressing apprehension about changing long-established practices. Student-related challenges included low motivation to engage with EBME and insufficient foundational knowledge in evidence-based medicine, which negatively affected learning outcomes. Access to up-to-date and relevant educational resources was reported as limited, including restricted access to important online databases and reliance on outdated teaching materials. From a policy perspective, the absence of clear national guidelines and standards for EBME implementation hindered systematic adoption. Furthermore, heavy clinical workloads and prioritization of patient care over teaching activities limited faculty availability for educational innovation. Finally, the existing assessment systems were criticized for being misaligned with EBME principles, focusing primarily on rote memorization instead of fostering critical thinking and evidence appraisal skills. Inhibiting factors in effective EBME implementation in Iran

Key Barriers to the Implementation of EBME in Iran

The participants described a wide range of factors that positively influence the implementation of evidence-based medical education (EBME) in Iran. These were categorized into 8 main categories and 33 sub-categories. Their insights revealed that the success of EBME depends not only on individual efforts but also on organizational, infrastructural, and cultural dimensions. Students and faculty shared complementary perspectives on facilitators; while faculty highlighted leadership support and institutional commitment (“Visible institutional support for EBME validates our efforts and makes it easier to plan long-term programs,” P4), students emphasized the motivating role of active learning and early exposure (“When EBME concepts are introduced from the first year, we naturally start questioning and searching for evidence,” P9). Policymakers, on the other hand, focused on the role of accreditation systems (“Including EBME in national accreditation standards pushes universities to take it seriously,” P18). Together, these viewpoints demonstrate that effective EBME implementation requires coordination across policy, institutional, and classroom levels.

Faculty development, institutional leadership, and curriculum-level strategies were identified as the most dominant facilitators. These were consistently highlighted across participant narratives. Faculty development emerged as a critical facilitator, particularly when educators possessed prior training in EBME, maintained high levels of motivation, and had access to professional peer networks. Institutional leadership also played a significant role by offering logistical and financial support, fostering an innovation-friendly climate, and ensuring transparency in communication. Curriculum-level strategies such as integrating EBME objectives into course syllabi, offering interdisciplinary modules, and encouraging modern teaching methods helped normalize evidence-based thinking among students. Meanwhile, learning environments that promoted student engagement, through active learning, early exposure to EBME, and accessible feedback mechanisms, proved effective in cultivating critical thinking. Infrastructure, including access to digital resources, international databases, and updated teaching materials, was another pillar. Equally important were national policies and accreditation standards that endorsed EBME as a core requirement, thereby aligning institutional behavior with global educational trends. Lastly, collaboration with international institutions and researchers enabled faculty and students to benchmark their practices and adopt innovative models. Discussion

The findings of this study revealed a complex interplay of barriers and facilitators influencing the implementation of evidence-based medical education (EBME) in Iran. These results are consistent with earlier international literature. Yet, they also reflect specific contextual dynamics within the Iranian medical education system. Notably, the analysis demonstrates that these factors do not exist in isolation but are deeply interconnected. For instance, the lack of faculty training is directly reinforced by weak institutional support and policy gaps, while student-related challenges are exacerbated by outdated curricula and assessment misalignments. Importantly, our findings indicate that policy-level deficiencies, such as the absence of national EBME standards, create downstream effects that weaken institutional accountability and reinforce faculty inertia. For example, in universities where EBME was not explicitly included in institutional evaluation frameworks, faculty reported feeling little motivation to modify teaching practices, whereas institutions that voluntarily aligned their internal goals with ministry recommendations showed relatively higher engagement among educators. This interrelationship underscores the systemic and cyclical nature of the barriers identified.

One of the most prominent barriers identified was the lack of faculty training. Participants noted that many educators lacked both conceptual understanding and practical teaching skills related to EBME. This echoes findings from studies in similar settings, such as those by Doja et al. (2022), where insufficient professional development limited faculty members’ confidence in adopting evidence-based strategies [23]. Resistance to change and the absence of mentoring systems for junior faculty seem to further complicate the issue. This reinforces a cycle of traditionalism that resists innovation. However, this barrier also underscores the essential role of continuous faculty development as both a challenge and a potential solution. Without adequate training and sustained professional development, even well-designed curricula may fail to translate into effective teaching. Global evidence supports that structured faculty development, encompassing training, mentorship, and peer learning, improves educators’ confidence, teaching efficacy, and EBME adoption [30–32]. Similarly, regional research from Pakistan and Saudi Arabia confirms that targeted workshops and mentoring significantly enhance faculty engagement with EBME, even in resource-limited contexts [33, 34]. In Iran, hierarchical traditions and limited institutional incentives magnify this gap; therefore, faculty development must be embedded within institutional and policy frameworks to ensure sustainability and overcome cultural resistance [35, 36].

Institutional support was another major limiting factor. Weak leadership engagement, symbolic rather than substantive support for educational innovation, and the absence of incentives for adopting EBME were repeatedly mentioned. Previous studies in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have similarly emphasized the importance of institutional culture and leadership in driving educational reform [37]. It appears that the lack of genuine institutional commitment creates an environment where educational innovation struggles to take root. When leadership involvement is superficial and incentives are lacking, faculty are less likely to adopt evidence-based approaches. This suggests that without cultural and structural shifts at the institutional level, meaningful reform in medical education remains unlikely. Moreover, the lack of dedicated funding and the organizational unwillingness to prioritize EBME over other institutional goals may further entrench existing educational models. The Iranian case illustrates how centralized governance structures and bureaucratic inertia slow down innovation; reforms require not only local leadership commitment but also alignment with ministerial policies and approval mechanisms [38]. This inter-level dependency, where institutional leaders must navigate bureaucratic policies to initiate reform, demonstrates how policy rigidity reinforces faculty passivity, resulting in a multi-layered stagnation.

The structural rigidity of medical curricula emerged as a critical barrier. Overcrowded content and inflexible scheduling left little space for integrating EBME components. This finding aligns with the work of Pawlina (2007), who argued that curriculum overload hinders the successful implementation of contemporary educational models [39]. Moreover, the lack of interdisciplinary collaboration was another issue emphasized by the participants. They noted that it limited the comprehensive delivery of EBME content and promoted fragmented learning rather than integrated thinking. In Iran, the traditional dominance of discipline-based departments and limited collaboration across faculties magnify these challenges. Findings from Egypt support this view, reporting that rigid departmental boundaries and dense syllabi constrain opportunities for evidence-based and problem-based learning [40]. Encouraging cross-disciplinary partnerships and flexible course delivery is therefore essential to promote integrated, evidence-based learning.

Cultural resistance also featured prominently in participant narratives. Many senior faculty members were deeply attached to didactic lecture-based methods. They viewed EBME as a challenge to their traditional authority. In this regard, AlFaris et al. (2006), in their study, emphasized the significant role of culture. They stated that evidence-based medical education (BEME) is a mindset that requires educators to reflect critically on their teaching, consult the best available evidence, and revise accordingly [41]. This finding suggests that deep-rooted cultural norms and hierarchical dynamics may act as significant barriers to educational innovation. Notably, several participants emphasized that the absence of national recognition for innovative teaching reinforces cultural conservatism at the faculty level. Without top-down incentives, faculty perceive little value in risking traditional prestige. A particularly Iranian dimension is the interplay of respect for senior authority and a hierarchical classroom culture that values teacher-centeredness [42]. Similar observations have been made in other Middle Eastern academic environments, where collectivist and authority-driven cultures impede the shift toward reflective and student-centered pedagogies [43]. Addressing this requires cultural change strategies that engage senior faculty as champions rather than adversaries and link recognition systems to evidence-based teaching performance.

Student-related challenges, such as low motivation, inadequate foundational knowledge in evidence-based medicine, and weak critical thinking skills, further hindered the effective delivery of EBME. Inadequate exposure to English-language literature and limited training in EBM methodology created additional layers of difficulty. This reinforces the importance of introducing EBM principles early in medical training to build a stronger academic base, as recommended in some studies [44]. Similar patterns were observed among medical students in India and Saudi Arabia, where early integration of EBM in preclinical courses was associated with greater self-efficacy and engagement [45, 46]. These findings indicate that without early and systematic exposure to EBM principles, students may struggle to engage meaningfully with EBME content. Gaps in language proficiency, critical thinking, and foundational knowledge not only limit comprehension but also reduce motivation. This highlights the need for curriculum reforms that embed EBM training from the early stages of medical education. Such reforms are essential to foster long-term competency and engagement.

Access to resources was reported as uneven and unreliable. Participants lamented the restricted access to digital databases and reliance on outdated materials. This is a known issue in resource-constrained environments, where technological infrastructure often lags behind educational aspirations [47]. Reliable access to updated digital resources, online platforms, and educational databases is therefore a foundational enabler for both faculty and students, ensuring consistency and equity in EBME delivery.

At the policy level, the lack of clear national guidelines and absence of EBME-related standards in institutional evaluations undermined consistency and accountability. These findings align with research from similar contexts that advocate for stronger top-down policy support to standardize EBME practices [48]. The absence of policy-driven accountability mechanisms causes educational reform to be deprioritized. Bureaucratic obstacles and heavy clinical workloads further drain faculty capacity. Hence, developing a coordinated national policy framework and embedding EBME standards in accreditation processes [49, 50] can align institutional behavior with ministry objectives and ensure sustained educational reform.

The assessment system was also criticized for prioritizing memorization over analytical skills. This misalignment between teaching methods and assessment tools represents a systemic disconnect that stifles pedagogical innovation. Prior literature has stressed that educational reform must be accompanied by changes in evaluation strategies [51]. As long as exams reward rote memorization, students and faculty alike have little incentive to adopt critical thinking–based approaches. Reforming assessment frameworks to include case-based, open-book, and reflective formats would reinforce EBME principles and motivate both faculty and students toward deeper learning.

Conversely, several facilitating factors were identified that could serve as leverage points for improving EBME implementation. Importantly, the effectiveness of these facilitators is influenced by context-specific factors unique to Iran. Faculty competence and continuous professional development, strong leadership, curricular innovation, access to digital resources, and policy alignment were the most significant facilitators.

Faculty competence and professional development, in particular, emerged as the most pivotal enabler. Studies show that educators who undergo EBME training and participate in continuous development demonstrate higher confidence and efficacy [30–32, 36]. Structured mentorship, workload adjustments, and recognition mechanisms can enhance these effects by addressing cultural resistance and limited incentives. Leadership support, especially when combined with transparent communication and budget al.location, also fosters an enabling environment. Steinert et al. (2016) found that institutions prioritizing academic freedom and curricular flexibility encouraged pedagogical innovation [52]. Within Iran’s centralized governance, active leadership advocacy and protected time for educational reform are therefore essential.

Curricular innovation was another key driver of pedagogical effectiveness. Integrating EBME principles into problem-based learning, journal clubs, and interdisciplinary teaching enables students to apply evidence in real clinical contexts and develop professional identity [53]. Access to digital resources, supported by institutional investment, strengthens both teaching quality and student engagement [54, 55].

Furthermore, policy alignment, student engagement, and international collaboration emerged as strategic facilitators. Aligning EBME within accreditation standards promotes accountability and consistency [49, 50]. Student-centered approaches that promote active learning and early exposure to EBM principles enhance motivation and cognitive engagement [56, 57]. Finally, international collaboration, through faculty exchanges and exposure to global models, acts as a catalyst for contextual innovation and adaptation [58]. These partnerships also provide external benchmarks and foster capacity building, particularly valuable in resource-limited settings.

It is worth noting that some facilitators recognized in global literature, such as student peer networks and informal learning communities, were mentioned less frequently by participants. This suggests that faculty development, leadership commitment, and curriculum reform remain the most salient and contextually relevant drivers in the Iranian setting.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the multidimensional factors that facilitate or hinder the effective implementation of evidence-based medical education (EBME) in Iran. The findings reveal that challenges are not confined to individual-level shortcomings; rather, they span across systemic, institutional, and cultural domains. Insufficient faculty training, limited institutional support, outdated assessment practices, and rigid curricula were among the most critical barriers. At the same time, the presence of motivated faculty, supportive leadership, access to modern resources, and alignment with national and international educational standards emerged as key enablers. Importantly, the study underscores that implementing EBME is not merely a technical adjustment but a complex organizational transformation that requires shifting mindsets, re-aligning incentives, and reconfiguring educational infrastructures. Effective EBME integration demands a collaborative effort involving educators, administrators, students, policymakers, and external partners. The study highlights several concrete levers for change, including targeted faculty development programs, stronger institutional leadership commitment, curriculum redesign to embed EBME principles, realignment of assessment methods toward critical thinking, and improved access to digital and international resources.

Furthermore, aligning Iran’s EBME initiatives with international frameworks such as the World Federation for Medical Education (WFME) standards and the Best Evidence Medical Education (BEME) collaboration can ensure quality assurance, comparability, and global relevance of medical training outcomes. These frameworks offer structured guidance for aligning national reforms with international best practices, promoting continuous improvement in educational quality and learner competency.

Overall, strengthening these levers can accelerate the adoption of EBME, enhance learner outcomes, and produce more competent, reflective, and evidence-oriented physicians. Linking EBME implementation to national health priorities, such as improving clinical decision-making, patient safety, and healthcare quality, positions EBME as a strategic driver for advancing Iran’s health system goals. In essence, the take-home message is that implementing EBME is not only an educational innovation but a vital national strategy to prepare physicians who can translate evidence into better health outcomes for society.

Policy implications and recommendations

Policymakers should develop and disseminate clear national guidelines for EBME. They should ensure their integration into accreditation standards and institutional evaluations. Among the recommendations, the top priority actions are: (a) developing and implementing national EBME guidelines that explicitly address hierarchical and cultural constraints in Iranian medical education, (b) increasing investment in faculty development programs, including mentorship and training workshops that are coupled with protected time and incentive mechanisms, and (c) revising curricula to create space for EBME content, embedding it across disciplines, and adopting student-centered assessment tools that align with critical thinking and analytical skills. Institutions must also adopt these measures alongside governmental and institutional policies. These policies should allocate dedicated funding for educational innovation and prioritize EBME within strategic planning. In addition, applying structured change management strategies, such as phased implementation, stakeholder engagement, and continuous monitoring, can help overcome resistance and ensure sustainability of EBME reforms. Strengthening partnerships with international organizations and aligning EBME development with WFME standards and BEME collaboration principles can enhance international credibility and benchmarking capacity. These collaborations can promote cross-cultural learning, faculty exchange, and joint capacity building, ensuring that Iranian medical education evolves in harmony with global educational trends and health priorities. Ultimately, the successful institutionalization of EBME will contribute not only to academic excellence but also to the broader national vision of achieving equitable, evidence-informed, and high-quality healthcare.

Limitation

While this study offers valuable insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the research was confined to academic institutions within Iran. This may limit the generalizability of the findings to other countries or educational systems with different policy frameworks, resource availabilities, and cultural contexts. Specifically, cultural norms, hierarchical structures in academia, and local educational values in Iran may influence participants’ perceptions and behaviors. In particular, gender roles and institutional hierarchies may have affected participants’ willingness to speak openly during interviews, potentially introducing bias in reported perceptions. This potentially limits applicability to other settings. However, the findings may still provide relevant insights for other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) with similar educational challenges and resource constraints. Second, the qualitative methodology, while appropriate for exploring in-depth perceptions, carries inherent limitations. These include subjectivity, potential researcher bias, and difficulties in replicating results. While rigorous coding and thematic analysis were applied, the interpretations remain influenced by the context and the participants’ openness during interviews. Additionally, the translation of interview data from Persian to English may have introduced subtle nuances or meaning shifts, which could affect interpretation and cross-cultural applicability. Third, logistical and time constraints prevented longitudinal tracking of institutional change over time. This would have provided deeper insights into the sustainability of EBME practices.

Suggestions for future research

Future research should adopt specific mixed-methods designs, such as Delphi consensus studies, sequential explanatory designs, or quantitative validation surveys. This would help quantify the prevalence and impact of specific barriers and facilitators across different institutional contexts. Longitudinal studies are needed to assess the sustainability of EBME interventions over time. Researchers should also examine the effectiveness of structured change management approaches in implementing EBME. This includes strategies for engaging faculty, overcoming cultural resistance, and integrating policy-level supports. Further exploration of student perspectives, as well as the role of interdisciplinary collaboration and digital innovation in supporting EBME, would enrich the existing knowledge base. Comparative studies across countries with similar educational systems could also provide valuable insights into transferable strategies for EBME implementation and inform best practices for educational reform in resource-constrained settings.
