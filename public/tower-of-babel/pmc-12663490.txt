Dynamic analysis of quality characteristics and flavor development in yogurt fermented with Lentinula edodes polysaccharides
Authors: Xiaocui Liu, Xiaoqin Sun, Tianhang Wang, Feiyang Sui, Xialin Ran, Kai Teng, Yage Xing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the dynamic changes in quality of yogurts containing Lentinula edode polysaccharide (LEP) and enzymatically hydrolyzed Lentinula edode polysaccharide (EH-LEP). The results demonstrated that fermentation of LEP-fortified enhanced nutrient formation (such as polyphenols and proteins) and increased in vitro antioxidant activity across all samples. Electronic nose and GC × MS–MS analyses revealed that fermentation significantly enriched the aroma profiles and induced distinct flavor differences among the yogurts. Analysis of 162 identified volatile compounds indicated that fermentation promoted the production or elevation of aromatic substances while reducing undesirable odors. Notably, the EH-LEP yogurt outperformed the blank and LEP yogurts, maintaining superior levels of total phenolics (increased by 30.04 % and 11.35 %), viable lactic acid bacteria (26.40 % and 8.97 %), and flavor compounds (9.62 % and 4.59 %) throughout fermentation. This study supports the application of shiitake components in functional foods and provides a theoretical and practical basis for innovative product development.Keywords: Lentinula edode polysaccharide, Enzymolysis, Yogurt fermentation, Quality attributes, Flavor profile

Introduction

Lentinula edodes polysaccharide (LEP), extracted from shiitake fruiting bodies, is one of its primary active components with notable anticancer, antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and immunomodulatory properties. In addition to these bioactivities, the complex monosaccharide composition of LEP supports essential energy for lactic acid bacteria (LAB) metabolism (Choi et al., 2021). LEP influences LAB in two key ways: Firstly, its excellent hydrophilicity, gelling properties, and surface activity enhance LAB adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells—critical for maintaining gut mucosal balance (Bukša et al., 2025). Secondly, it strengthens probiotic resistance to gastric acid and digestive enzymes, promoting effective modulation of gut microbiota (Ji et al., 2022). Xu et al. (2023) demonstrated that fermentation increases uronic acid content and decreases LEP molecular weight without altering its monosaccharide composition, improving water solubility by 65.07 % and antioxidant activity. Yang et al. (2025) reported that LEP is efficiently utilized by gut microbiota and metabolites via in vitro fermentation. The results indicated that LEP was used effectively by gut microbiota. Its fermentation increased the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria (e.g., Bifidobacterium, Parabacteroides) while reducing the abundance of harmful bacteria (e.g., Veillonella, Klebsiella). Furthermore, LEP catabolism also promoted amino acid catabolism, particularly involving the biosynthesis of alanine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and 5′-monophosphate adenosine. Collectively, these findings confirm that LEP has prebiotic potential and its growing application in food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic fields (Huang et al., 2024).

Functional yogurt, a widely accepted and economical food, offers an ideal vehicle for fortification with micronutrients and probiotics (Alizadeh Behbahani et al., 2023). Given LEP's strong bioactivity and consumer appeal, LEP-based health products hold promising economic and social benefits. Polysaccharides—linear or branched polymers composed of ketose or aldose monosaccharides—are vital food carbohydrates (Zhou et al., 2022). However, Lentinula edodes polysaccharides' large molecular weight, complex spatial structure, and ambiguous structure-activity relationships cause poor solubility, high viscosity, and low bioavailability, significantly limiting their efficient utilization and application (Li et al., 2023). Numerous studies have demonstrated that polysaccharide degradation can improve physicochemical properties and enhance application value (Sun et al., 2016). Furthermore, degradation can increase bioactivity by modifying molecular weight, monosaccharide composition, and spatial conformation (Ning et al., 2022; Paulert et al., 2021). This study optimizes enzymatic degradation of LEP using β-glucanase and incorporates both LEP and its hydrolysates into yogurt to produce functional products with enhanced quality and distinct flavor.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Fresh shiitake mushrooms (Lentinula edodes polysaccharide) were purchased from Yichang Nongjiacaiyuan (Hubei Province, China). Food-grade Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus freeze-dried starter cultures were purchased from Baiyibao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Zhengzhou, China). Pasteurized milk was supplied by Mengniu Dairy Co., Ltd. (Inner Mongolia, China). Food-grade erythritol was purchased from Yunbanshan Health Food Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). Glucose (CAS: 50-99-7), De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS, R42466) and 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS, R27125) obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. Bovine serum albumin (BSA; CAS: 9048-46-8; BR), concentrated sulfuric acid (CAS: 7664-93-9; HPLC, 95.0–98.0 %), phenol (CAS: 108-95-2; AR), sodium dihydrogen phosphate (CAS: 7558-80-7; AR; 99 %), and citric acid (CAS: 77-92-9; AR) were purchased from Chengdu Kelong Chemical Reagent Factory (Chengdu, China). Absolute ethanol (CAS: 64-17-5; AR), xylanase (CAS: 9025-57-4), β-glucanase (CAS: 9025-70-1), pectinase (CAS: 9032-75-1), papain (CAS: 9001-73-4), and cellulose (CAS: 9004-34-6) (food-grade) purchased from Hefei Bomei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hefei, China). A digital pH meter (PHS-320) and a precision constant-temperature incubator (BPX-82) were purchased from Chengdu Century Ark Technology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). A high-speed refrigerated centrifuge (TD-5 M) was sourced from Sichuan Shuke Instrument Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China), and a vacuum freeze dryer (FDU-1200) was purchased from Shanghai Ailang Instrument Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). An electronic nose (E-nose) and a gas Chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) instrument were used for volatile compound analysis.

Extraction of mushroom polysaccharides and preparation of enzymatically hydrolyzed polysaccharides

Fresh shiitake mushrooms (Lentinula edodes polysaccharide) were dried, ground into powder, and extracted following the method of Shu et al. (2019) with minor modifications. The powder was soaked in 20 volumes of 95 % ethanol overnight to remove small molecules, vacuum-filtered, and dried. 10 g of sample was weighed into a conical flask and mixed with distilled water at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1 g: 40 mL. Extraction was performed in a 90 °C water bath with continuous stirring for 3 h, repeated three times. The combined supernatant was collected by vacuum filtration, concentrated by rotary evaporation, precipitated with ethanol, stored at 4 °C overnight, and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting precipitate was collected, redissolved, concentrated by rotary evaporation, and freeze-dried under vacuum to obtain LEP.

For enzymatic hydrolysis, LEP solution was adjusted to pH 4.5 with disodium hydrogen phosphate-citrate buffer and treated with β-glucanase (70 U/mL) at 45 °C for 14 h, at a polysaccharide concentration of 4 mg/mL. The reaction was terminated by heating at 95 °C for 15 min. The hydrolysate was centrifuged for 10 min at high speed, dialyzed (8000 Da molecular weight cutoff, 72 h), concentrated, and freeze-dried to obtain enzymatically hydrolyzed Lentinula edodes polysaccharide polysaccharide (EH–LEP).

Preparation of fermented milk

Fermented milk was prepared according to Du et al. (2023) with slight modifications. Briefly, 100 mL of milk was prewarmed to 60 °C, dissolved with 8 % (w/v) erythritol, and supplemented with either LEP or EH–LEP. The mixture was pasteurized at 90 °C for 5 min, cooled to 40 °C, and inoculated with 0.1 % (w/v) composite LAB starter. Fermentation was conducted at 42 °C for 5 h, followed by refrigeration at 4 °C for 24 h to obtain LEP and EH–LEP yogurts.

Determination of viable lactobacillus count during yogurt fermentation process

Viable Lactobacillus colonies were enumerated using YPGL agar after incubation at 30 °C for 48 h. The yogurt cultures were plated on MRS agar under aerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48 h, following ISO 7889/IDF 117 (International Organization for Standardization, 2009).

Determination of total sugar, total phenols, protein content, and total soluble solids (TSS) during yogurt fermentation process

Sample preparation: 30 mL yogurt was mixed with 45 mL of acidified methanol containing 150 μL of concentrated hydrochloric acid, homogenized at 12,000 rpm for 1 min, then stored at −20 °C for 3 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was filtered (0.22 μm). The filtrate was used to determine total sugar and phenol content.

Protein and TSS were analyzed according to the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 2006). Protein (Method 920.87) was determined using nitrogen-to-protein conversion (factor 6.38). TSS was measured at ambient temperature using a digital refractometer (MyBrix; Mettler-Toledo GmbH; Switzerland). Total sugar content was measured following the method of Wei and Zhang (2023). 1 mL diluted sample was mixed with 2 mL of distilled water, then 1 mL of 5 % (w/v) phenol solution was added. After adding 5 mL concentrated H₂SO₄, the mixture was shaken, boiled for 15 min, cooled, and measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. The total phenolic content was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method (Kan et al., 2017). 1 mL of diluted sample was mixed with 1 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, followed 30 s later by 3 mL of 7.5 % Na₂CO₃. After 30 min in the dark at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 764 nm using a microplate reader.

Color determination during yogurt fermentation

Color measurements were performed using a chroma meter (Ci64-XR; X-Rite Incorporated; Shanghai, China). Results were expressed in terms of three color parameters: L* (lightness), a* (red-green axis), and b* (yellow-blue axis).

Determination of rheological properties during yogurt fermentation

Rheological measurements were performed at 25 °C using a rheometer (MCR 302; Anton Paar; Shanghai, China) equipped with parallel plates (PP25, 25 mm diameter, 1 mm gap). The temperature was maintained at 25 ± 0.1 °C. Viscosity was determined by performing shear rate sweeps from 0 to 100 s−1. Oscillatory strain tests were conducted at a frequency of 1 Hz with strain amplitudes ranging from 0.1 % to 100 % to determine the linear viscoelastic region.

Electronic nose (E-nose) analysis of different yogurt fermentation processes

Volatile profiles of the three yogurt types were analyzed using a PEN3 electronic nose (AIRSENSE Analytics; Germany) equipped with 10 metal oxide sensors (W1C, W1S, W1W, W2S, W2W, W3C, W3S, W5S, W5C, and W6S). For each sample, 5 g of yogurt was sealed in a 20 mL headspace vial. Measurement parameters were as follows: 1 s sampling interval, 200 s flush time, 5 s preparation time, 180 s detection time, and a carrier gas flow rate of 400 mL/min. Six replicates (n = 6) were analyzed at 20 °C in an odor-free environment.

GC × GC–MS analysis of different yogurt fermentation processes

Sample preparation

Yogurt samples were equilibrated in a constant-temperature water bath at 50 °C for 20 min, followed by volatile extraction for 30 min using a solid-phase microextraction (SPME) fiber. The fiber was immediately transferred into the GC–MS injection port for desorption at 250 °C for 3 min. Data acquisition was performed using comprehensive two-dimensional gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC × GC–MS).

GC × GC–MS conditions

The comprehensive GC × GC–MS system (Agilent J&W, USA) equipped with a DB-WAX column (36.40 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) in the first dimension and a DB-17MS column (1.30 m × 0.18 mm × 0.18 μm) in the second, with a solid-state thermal modulator. High-purity helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The oven temperature program was: initial 40 °C (2 min hold), ramped at 5 °C/min to 230 °C, held for 4 min. Injection was performed in splitless mode. The injection port temperature was 250 °C; desorption time 3 min, and modulation period 4 s (1 s hot pulse). Columns were interconnected via an HV modulator column (1.20 m × 0.25 mm). All analyses were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Qualitative and quantitative qnalysis

Data processing was conducted using EXCEL 2020. Principal component analysis (PCA) of GC × GC–MS data was performed with Origin 9.8 (Microcal Software Inc., Northampton, MA, USA). Orthogonal partialleast squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed with SIMCA 14.1 (Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden). Statistical significance (p < 0.05) was evaluated using Tukey's test in SPSS 25.0 (IBM, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Prior to ANOVA, the normality of the data was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was confirmed using Levene's test. A heatmap of differential metabolites among the three yogurt types was generated in RStudio 4.1 (RStudio Inc., Boston, MA, USA). All analyses were performed in triplicate (n = 3), except for E-nose measurements (n = 6). Volatile flavor compounds were identified by referencing standardized odor profiles from The Good Scents Company database (www.thegoodscentscompany.com), which provides validated sensory and chemical descriptions.

Results and analysis

Process optimization for EH-LEP, LEP yogurt and EH-LEP yogurt

Optimization of enzymatic hydrolysis for LEP (Table S1, Figs. S1–S2) and fermentation parameters for LEP and EH-LEP yogurts (Tables S2–S3, Figs. S3–S6) was conducted, alongside sensory evaluation development (Table S4).

Optimal enzymatic hydrolysis conditions were determined as follows: β-glucanase concentration of 85 U/mL; pH 4.70; temperature 51 °C; and 14 h duration, yielding a 17.02 % hydrolysis rate (Tables S5–S6). The solubility of enzymatically hydrolyzed polysaccharides exceeded that of water-extracted polysaccharides. For LEP yogurt, optimal fermentation parameters included 0.13 % polysaccharide addition, 8 % erythritol, 0.10 % starter culture, 42 °C fermentation temperature, and 6 h duration, achieving a sensory score of 89.30 (Tables S7–S8). For EH-LEP yogurt, optimal fermentation conditions were similar—0.10 % hydrolyzed polysaccharide, 8 % erythritol, 42 °C temperature, 0.10 % starter, and 6 h fermentation—yielding a higher sensory score of 91.10 (Tables S9–S10).

Changes in pH, TA and viable lactic acid bacteria count during different yogurt fermentation processes

Acidity serves as a key physicochemical parameter in yogurt production, as lower pH inhibits spoilage and pathogenic microorganisms. During fermentation, pH decreased while TA increased across all samples. After fermentation, TA increased by 71.60°T, 72.80°T, and 73.40°T in blank, LEP, and EH-LEP yogurts, respectively. After postripeness, acidity reached 83.20 ± 0.27°T, 84.30 ± 2.16°T, and 84.50 ± 2.08°T (Fig. 1B), while pH values decreased significantly to 4.62 ± 0.19, 4.51 ± 0.09, and 4.47 ± 0.07, respectively (Fig. 1A). The viable LAB count initially increased, then declined slightly before stabilizing postripeness, following the order: EH-LEP > LEP > blank yogurts (Fig. 1C). Yogurts supplemented with LEP exhibited higher LAB counts than the control group (blank yogurts), while EH-LEP further enhanced bacterial growth, likely due to oligosaccharides generated from β-glucanase-mediated mycelial degradation, serving as additional carbon sources for probiotic proliferation (Shaikh-Ibrahim et al., 2025). LEP addition consistently reduced pH and increased TA, consistent with previous reports for citrus pectin and truffle polysaccharides (Arioui et al., 2016; Miao et al., 2011), This likely resulted from LAB-LEP interactions, where LAB continued slow growth during postripeness, converting lactose, galactose, and glucose into lactic acid. As a prebiotic, LEP promoted LAB proliferation and metabolic activity, accelerating organic acid accumulation. Consequently, EH-LEP yogurt exhibited a greater pH decline than LEP yogurt, as enzymatic hydrolysis improved polysaccharide utilization and enhanced LAB growth kinetics.

Changes in total sugar, total phenolics, TSS, and protein content during yogurt fermentation

Dynamic changes in total sugar, total phenolic content, TSS, and protein content during fermentation are shown in Fig. 2. As shown in Fig. 2A, EH-LEP yogurt exhibited significantly higher total sugar accumulation rates during the initial fermentation stage compared with blank and LEP yogurts, which can be attributed to differences in initial nutrient content and sequential sugar utilization by LAB. This initial increase primarily resulted from the hydrolysis of lactose into its constituent monosaccharides (glucose and galactose) by microbial β-galactosidase during the early fermentation stage. The subsequent decrease was associated with the progressive utilization of these simple sugars to support microbial growth and acid production (Vieira et al., 2023). Total phenolic content initially increased, then declined, reaching a stable level after postripeness, During the fermentation process, the EH-LEP yogurt outperformed the blank and LEP yogurts, maintaining superior levels of viable LAB (26.40 % and 8.97 %). (Fig. 2B). Polysaccharide supplementation enhanced phenolic bioactivity, particularly in EY. Postripeness pH instability, Maillard reactions between amino acids, lactose, and phenolic compounds, as well as LAB metabolism, contributed to phenolic degradation (Sun-Waterhouse et al., 2013).

As shown in Fig. 2C, initial TSS contents were comparable among all samples (19.26–19.30 %). During fermentation, the control yogurt (blank yogurts) showed a continuous TSS decline (19.26 % ± 0.28 to 13.50 % ± 0.40), while LEP-supplemented yogurts (LEP and EH-LEP) displayed transient increases followed by significant decreases (19.30 % ± 0.20 to 13.70 % ± 0.34; 19.27 % ± 0.37 to 14.10 % ± 0.05, respectively). All samples met the requirements of the Chinese National Standard GB 19302-2010 for fermented dairy products (≥8.10 % non-fat solids). LEP addition stimulated bacterial proliferation, enhancing sugar utilization efficiency. During the lag phase, LAB adaptation and biomass accumulation preceded accelerated sugar consumption, leading to rapid TSS decline (Zhang et al., 2020). Throughout fermentation, protein content remained relatively stable across all three yogurt types. Subsequent to fermentation, LEP-supplemented samples exhibited slightly lower protein levels than the control (Fig. 2D). The trend aligns with previous studies indicating that edible mushroom polysaccharides enhance LAB proliferation and catalyze protein hydrolysis into small peptides and amino acids, thereby improving nutritional bioavailability, sensory properties, and human absorption (Ge et al., 2022).

Changes in buffering capacity and rheological properties during yogurt fermentation

Buffering capacity reflects yogurt's ability to resist pH fluctuations when exposed to acids or bases. As shown in Fig. 3A, buffering capacity decreased gradually with decreasing pH across all samples. Throughout fermentation, blank yogurt exhibited consistently lower buffering capacity than LEP yogurt and EH-LEP yogurt. The addition of LEP-supplementation markedly enhanced buffering capacity, with LEP yogurt showing superior buffering during early fermentation, while EH-LEP yogurt surpassed both LEP yogurt and blank yogurt from late fermentation through early postripeness. This improvement is closely associated with the yogurt's gel microstructure; the hydrophilic nature of polysaccharides enhances water retention, strengthening the protein gel network (Xu et al., 2019). These results demonstrate that appropriate polysaccharide supplementation can effectively improve fermented milk's buffering capacity.

Rheological profiles during pH reduction are shown in Fig. 3B. As the shear rate increased from 0 to 100 s−1, all yogurt samples exhibited typical shear-thinning behavior, evidenced by decreasing apparent viscosity. This behavior results from the disruption of non-covalent interactions between casein micelles during shear deformation (Xu et al., 2019). At equivalent shear rates, apparent viscosity followed the order EH-LEP > LEP > blank yogurt. Similar findings were reported by Ding et al. (2023), who demonstrated that the addition of polysaccharides increases ionic bonding strength within yogurt gel matrices, altering casein charge distribution and modifying electrostatic interactions. Consistent with the results of Sun et al. (2021), our data confirm that LEP supplements enhance yogurt viscosity by stabilizing the gel matrix. Consequently, LEP-supplementation strengthens intermolecular cohesion, reduces syneresis, and improves the overall structural integrity of yogurt.

Changes in in vitro antioxidant capacity during yogurt fermentation

The ·OH scavenging capacity (Fig. 4A), ABTS+ scavenging rate (Fig. 4B), and DPPH· scavenging capacity (Fig. 4C) exhibited similar trends, with all antioxidant indices increasing during the later stages of fermentation compared to the initial stages. This enhancement can be attributed to the enzymatic conversion of bound phenolics to free phenolics by LAB, which elevates total phenolic content and overall antioxidant activity (Li et al., 2021). Concurrently, protein hydrolysis during fermentation releases antioxidant peptides (Fardet & Rock, 2017). Overall, blank yogurt and EH-LEP demonstrated higher antioxidant activity than LEP yogurt. LEP acts as a stabilizer in milk systems; at appropriate concentrations, it stabilizes yogurt structure and modulates LAB metabolism. Notably, LAB-secreted exopolysaccharides possess intrinsic antioxidant capacity. The observed increase in activity may also originate from LEP, analogous to the documented antioxidant potential of Cordyceps militaris polysaccharides (Radzki et al., 2016). Incorporation of LEP thus synergistically enhances radical-scavenging rates in yogurt.

Antioxidant potential primarily arises from reducing-active non-sugar residues in molecular structures, particularly phenolic compounds and proteins (Jaehrig et al., 2008; Kozarski et al., 2012). Fernandes and Coimbra (2023) emphasized that alcohol precipitation during polysaccharide extraction can trap phenolics within phenolic-carbohydrate complexes, while microbial fermentation further amplifies the antioxidant capacity of crude polysaccharides. As reported by Sinaga et al. (2022), this effect can be attributed to the release of additional phenolic compounds from polysaccharides during fermentation, thereby increasing antioxidant potential. Studies have shown that Lactobacillus plantarum can ferment polysaccharides, thereby enhancing the antioxidant potential of polysaccharide molecules in yogurt. This enhancement is further supported by the degradation of LEP into oligosaccharides via β-glucan degradation, which boosts antioxidant activity (Fang et al., 2021). Interacting with antioxidant compounds in yogurt. Furthermore, yogurt containing EH-LEP exhibited stronger antioxidant activity than that containing non-hydrolyzed polysaccharides. This enhancement is likely attributed to the improved radical-scavenging capacity following molecular weight reduction (Zhao et al., 2011). The underlying mechanism may involve hydrogen donation from polysaccharides, which combines with radicals to form a stable radical and terminate the radical chain reaction. Alternatively, polysaccharides may bind to radical ions essential for propagation, thereby halting propagation (Wu et al., 2020). Consequently, EH-LEP yogurt demonstrates significantly higher overall antioxidant activity.

Correlation analysis

As shown in Fig. 4D, preliminary data analysis revealed significant correlations among multiple evaluation indices. Decreases in pH and increases in TA were negatively correlated, reflecting lactic acid production from LAB lactose metabolism. Higher viable LAB counts accelerated pH reduction but inversely correlated with TSS: increased LAB proliferation increased sugar utilization, lowering TSS content. Buffering capacity progressively declined (e.g., EH-LEP: from 1.61 to 0.11), due to lactic acid accumulation, pH decrease, and reduced TSS. Viscosity showed a positive correlation with TSS. Total sugar variations reflected LAB metabolic—sugar conversion to lactic acid—forming an inverse relationship with LAB viability. Enhanced LAB activity also accelerated phenolic accumulation. Total phenolics positively correlated with antioxidant capacity (DPPH·, ABTS+, and ·OH scavenging), as evidenced by EH-LEP's increases: DPPH· (47.19 %–61.46 %), ABTS+ (28.56 %–57.39 %), and ·OH (58.35 %–66.09 %). Protein content and color parameters showed minimal variation and weak correlations with other indices. These findings highlight the complex interactions among polysaccharides, LAB activity, and compositional factors (sugars and phenolics) that collectively influence yogurt's physicochemical and functional properties. This knowledge supports targeted fermentation optimization to enhance the accumulation of beneficial compounds.

Electronic nose analysis of different yogurt fermentation processes

Comparative analysis of electronic nose sensor response values

Dynamic changes in electronic nose response values for the three yogurts are presented in Fig. 5A. Each yogurt type produced distinct sensor responses, with significant differences among them. Pronounced signal changes appeared on W5S, W1W, and W2W sensors, while W1C and W1S also exhibited notable fluctuations, indicating their substantial contribution in detecting volatile compounds. Following fermentation, EH-LEP yogurt consistently yielded higher sensor response values than blank yogurt and LEP yogurt. These dynamics reflect compositional changes in aromatics, alkanes, nitrogen oxides, and other volatiles that shape the evolving flavor profile during fermentation.

Electronic nose radar plot (A) and PCA analysis (B) of the electronic nose of three kinds of yogurt before and after fermentation.

Principal component analysis (PCA) of electronic nose sensor response variations during yogurt fermentation

Electronic nose (e-nose) technology, which simulates human olfaction to enable rapid and non-destructive evaluation of complex volatile mixtures in food matrices, has been widely applied in the food quality sector. PCA was used to evaluate volatile compounds among different yogurt fermentations. The cumulative contribution rates of PC1 and PC2 exceeded 95 % for all samples, confirming that these two components effectively captured the majority of variance and distinctly differentiated flavor profiles across fermentation stages.

As shown in Fig. 5B, clear, non-overlapping separations were observed between samples collected before and after the completion of fermentation, confirming the electronic nose's strong discriminatory ability for both fermentation processes and yogurt types. Prior to fermentation, the samples predominantly clustered in the negative region of PC1, whereas progressive fermentation shifted their sample distribution toward positive PC1 coordinates, visually illustrating the dynamic evolution of volatile flavor compounds. To further elucidate these specific volatile transformations, comprehensive two-dimensional gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC × GC–MS) was employed for qualitative and quantitative identification of the volatile compounds.

GC × GC–MS analysis of different yogurt fermentation processes

Odor is a critical sensory attribute influencing consumer preference, and fermentation markedly alters volatile compound profiles in dairy and plant-based products. During lactic acid fermentation, a variety of bioactive and aroma compounds are produced, contributing to yogurt's distinctive flavor. Fig. 6 illustrates the variation in the composition and abundance of volatile compounds in the three yogurt types (EH-LEP, LEP and blank yogurt) at different fermentation stages (detailed results for volatile components at these stages for various types of yogurts are shown in Appendix Tables S1–S7). Across all systems, 162 volatile compounds were identified. Control yogurt (blank yogurt) contained 104 volatiles (19 alkenes, 19 ketones, 8 aldehydes, 26 alcohols, 11 esters, 11 acids, 5 miscellaneous, and 5 aromatics). LEP yogurt yielded 109 compounds (32 alcohols, 18 alkenes, 15 ketones, 7 aldehydes, 14 esters, 6 acids, 8 miscellaneous, and 9 aromatics). In contrast, EH-LEP yogurt exhibited 114 volatiles (25 alkenes, 11 aldehydes, 17 ketones, 30 alcohols, 11 esters, 7 acids, 8 miscellaneous, and 5 aromatics), indicating the highest volatile richness (EH-LEP > LEP > blank yogurt). And after fermentation, the flavor compounds of EH- LEP yogurt increased by 9.62 % and 4.59 % respectively compared to blank yogurt and LEP yogurt. Volatile richness followed the order EH-LEP > LEP > blank. The limited bioavailability of LEP in yogurt can be attributed to its high molecular weight and branched structure, which lead to the formation of low-molecular-weight polysaccharides or oligosaccharides, thereby enhancing solubility and providing a more diverse and readily utilizable carbon source for LAB. Enzymatic hydrolysis breaks down the macromolecular structure into low-molecular-weight polysaccharides or oligosaccharides, enhancing solubility and providing a more abundant and diverse carbon source for LAB. This process effectively compensates for LAB's intrinsic limitations in secreting enzymes capable of degrading complex polysaccharides. Consequently, LAB demonstrate accelerated growth and heightened metabolic activity, redirecting fermentation pathways toward the synthesis of secondary metabolites (Gänzle & Follador, 2012). Consequently, the accumulation of key flavor compounds—including organic acids (e.g., acetic acid), carbonyls (e.g., acetaldehyde, diacetyl), alcohols, and esters is markedly enhanced.

Distribution changes of compound quantities and contents in three types of yogurt at different fermentation stages.

Compound profile in blank yogurt (A), LEP yogurt (B), EH-LEP yogurt (C) and changes in compound concentrations in blank yogurt (D), LEP yogurt (E) and EH-LEP yogurt (F) during fermentation;

Fermentation significantly altered the aroma profiles of the three yogurts. Newly formed furfural (0.69 %) contributed sweet and nutty notes. LEP yogurt was characterized by octanal (citrus), benzaldehyde (cherry, nutty), and nonanal (rosy, orange), whereas EH- LEP yogurt uniquely contained tetradecanal (fruity, coconut), phenylacetaldehyde (floral, honey-like), and myristaldehyde (creamy, milky). Ketone content increased substantially after fermentation, reaching 47.54 % (blank yogurt) to 48.98 % (LEP yogurt), and 50.87 % (EH- LEP yogurt).

The key flavor compound, 2,3-butanedione (diacetyl), was present in all samples, imparting a buttery, sweet, creamy aroma. Its concentration rose from 7.13 % (blank yogurt) to 17.48 % (LEP yogurt) and 18.72 % (blank yogurt), indicating a synergistic effect of diacetyl formation by LEP and EH-LEP supplementation.

Acid content generally also increased throughout fermentation. LEP yogurt contained hexanoic and acetic acids, while blank yogurt and EH-LEP yogurt newly formed octanoic and hexanoic acids, contributing cheese-like and fruity notes, respectively. Although some acids impart pungent odors, their relatively low concentrations minimize adverse sensory effects.

Esters primarily derived from fatty acid oxidation and microbial metabolism were essential for the yogurt aroma due to their low odor thresholds. Ethyl isonicotinate, a predominant ester with sweet and apple-like notes, increased 8.74 % (blank yogurt), 14.14 % (LEP yogurt), and 18.41 % (EH-LEP yogurt) after fermentation. Alcohol content generally decreased in EH-LEP yogurt; however, EH-LEP yogurt uniquely produced 3-buten-1-ol, (R)-(−)-2-octanol, and trans-2-decen-1-ol, contributing fruity aromas. LEP yogurt contained only butanol, 1,2-butanediol, and D-aminopropanol, contributing balsamic sweetness, while blank yogurt uniquely exhibited 2,3-butanediol. Due to their high odor thresholds, alcohols, alkanes, alkenes, and aromatic compounds had limited direct flavor impact, though they may modulate flavor through synergistic interactions. Sulfur-, nitrogen-, and heterocyclic-containing compounds decreased after fermentation but may still contribute subtly to the overall aroma profile.

Multivariate statistical analysis of volatile flavor compounds during yogurt fermentation

The PCA score plot revealed clear separation among the three yogurt groups. As shown in Fig. 7A, samples collected before fermentation clustered in the first quadrant, demonstrating similar volatile profiles at the initial stage. In contrast, post-fermentation samples were distributed across distinct quadrants, confirming pronounced variation in flavor composition across fermentation stages and yogurt types. In the OPLS-DA model (Fig. 7B), the horizontal axis represents inter-group discrimination, whereas the vertical axis represents intra-group variation. Model evaluated yielded R2Y(cum) = 0.984, indicating strong explanatory power, and Q2(cum) = 0.943, demonstrating strong predictive ability (Liu et al., 2024). A 200-cycle permutation test further validated the model's robustness (Fig. 7C); the Q2 regression line intersected the y-axis below zero, and both original Q2 and R2 values exceeded those of the permutated models, confirming the absence of overfitting and validating the model's reliability and predictive performance.

Cluster heatmap analysis of key volatile flavor compounds in yogurt before and after fermentation

The cluster heatmap (Fig. 7D) presents 77 differential volatile compounds identified by multivariate statistical analysis across the three yogurt types at different fermentation stages. Samples formed distinct clusters, reflecting substantial compositional and sensory evolution during fermentation. Cluster 1 (post-fermentation EH-LEP/LEP yogurt) was enriched in ketones and alcohols, supplemented by esters and aldehydes, imparting dairy, floral, and fruity notes. All yogurts exhibited elevated levels of 2,3-butanedione and 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, compounds responsible for creamy and buttery aromas. LEP yogurt contained higher benzaldehyde (woody, sweet-buttery) and 2,3-pentanedione levels, while EH-LEP yogurt demonstrated the most pronounced acetaldehyde increase, suggesting intensified lactose-to‑carbonyl conversion. Cluster 2 (unfermented blank and EH-LEP yogurt) contained predominantly alkenes and minor ethers associated with gasoline- or ether-like off-odors, which diminished after fermentation. Cluster 3 (fermented EH-LEP yogurt) featured phenylacetaldehyde, δ-decalactone, and 3-octen-2-one, contributing floral-fruity and herbaceous notes, with elevated 2-butanone, likely related to microbial community shifts and secondary metabolite formation (Narzary et al., 2021). Cluster 4 primarily included volatiles in LEP yogurt before fermentation, including hexacosane and carvacrol, which contributed waxy and spicy notes that decreased after fermentation. In contrast, blank yogurt exhibited increased levels of 2-heptanone and decanal after fermentation, enhancing sweet and fruity notes.

Integrated analysis of the heatmap and final flavor profiles shows that blank yogurt retained pronounced dairy and creamy notes; LEP yogurt developed woody aromas that complemented its dairy base; and EH-LEP yogurt exhibited enhanced herbaceous and fruity-sweet characteristics along with intensified creamy notes. These findings indicate that both EH-LEP and LEP yogurts have strong market potential due to their distinctive sensory profiles. Critically, the heatmap analysis confirms that polysaccharide-fermented modulates volatile compound distribution, while LAB-fermented polysaccharides enhance aromatic constituents.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that yogurts supplemented with LEP and EH-LEP exhibited greater antioxidant activity and superior physicochemical quality than the control. Correlation analysis revealed complex interrelationships among polysaccharides, LAB viability, and compositional dynamics (sugars and phenolics), which collectively influence physicochemical properties and health functionalities. Electronic nose profiling and GC × GC–MS analysis identified 162 volatile flavor compounds, confirming that fermentation enhanced overall aroma characteristics and introduced distinctive inter-group variations. Fermentation promoted desirable aromatic compounds while reducing bitter, pungent, and paraffin-like notes. Among all samples, EH-LEP yogurt showed the highest diversity and abundance of volatiles, reflecting its enhanced sensory complexity. Overall, LEP fermentation significantly improved both olfactory profile and functional quality, providing a scientific basis for developing polysaccharide-enriched fermented dairy products with superior health and sensory appeal.
