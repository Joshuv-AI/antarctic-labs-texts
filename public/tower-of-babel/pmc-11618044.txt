Structural and Electronic Factors Controlling the Efficiency and Rate of Intersystem Crossing to the Triplet State in Thiophene Polycyclic Derivatives
Authors: Cameron Griffrith, Sarah E. Krul, Sean J. Hoehn, Tram Phan, Carlos E. Crespo‐Hernández
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThiophene polycyclic derivatives are widely used in organic light‐emitting diodes, photovoltaics, and medicinal chemistry applications. Understanding the electronic and structural factors controlling their intersystem crossing rates is paramount for these applications to be successful. This study investigates the photophysical, electronic structure, and excited state dynamics of 1,2‐benzodiphenylene sulfide, benzo[b]naphtho[1,2‐d]thiophene, and benzo[b]naphtho[2,3‐d]thiophene in polar aprotic and non‐polar solvents. Steady‐state absorption and emission spectroscopy, femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy, and DFT and TD‐DFT calculations are employed. Low fluorescence quantum yields of 1.2 to 2.7 % are measured in acetonitrile and cyclohexene, evidencing that the primary relaxation pathways in these thiophene derivatives are nonradiative. Linear interpolation of internal coordinates calculations predict that an S−C bond elongation reaction coordinate facilitates the efficient intersystem crossing to the T1 state. Excitation of 1,2‐benzodiphenylene sulfide and benzo[b]naphtho[1,2‐d]thiophene at 350 nm or benzo[b]naphtho[2,3‐d]thiophene at 365 nm, populates the lowest‐energy 1ππ* state, which relaxes to the 1ππ* minimum in tens of picoseconds or intersystem crosses to the triplet manifold in ca. 500 ps to 1.1 ns depending on the position at which the benzene rings are added. Excitation at 266 nm does not affect the intersystem crossing rates. Laser photodegradation experiments demonstrate that the thiophene polycyclic derivatives are highly photostable.

Introduction

The multifaceted photophysical properties of thiophene derivatives are known to play crucial roles in such applications as organic light‐emitting diodes (OLEDs),[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] photovoltaics,[ 8 , 9 ] and medicinal chemistry,[ 10 , 11 ] among others. One of the crucial driving forces behind the wide range of applications of the thiophene derivatives and oligomers resides in the efficient population of their triplet state. While some studies have reported the photophysical properties of several thiophene derivatives and oligomers from both computational[ 12 , 13 , 14 ] and experimental[ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] perspectives, few have put such studies together to address structure‐photophysical property relationships in their electronic relaxation mechanism.[ 13 , 18 , 20 ]

In 2017, Schnappinger et. al. used quantum chemical calculations and dynamic simulations to determine the origin of the weak phosphorescence signal present in thiophene. [13] They predicted that a small energy barrier of 0.06 eV needs to be overcome to reach a ring‐opening structure, which give rise to intersystem crossing to populate the T1(ππ*) state, not observed in previous computational studies. [9] More recently, analogous electronic relaxation mechanisms have been proposed to explain the efficient intersystem crossing observed in thianaphthene [20] and dibenzothiophene [18] derivatives. For the thianaphthene derivatives, [20] S−C bond elongation leads to a crossing seam between S1(ππ*) and S1(πσ*) states requiring surmounting a small energy barrier of 0.17 to 0.21 eV, from which intersystem crossing via the S1(πσ*) and T2(ππ*) states can occur. While the S−C bond elongation is also proposed to play an important role in the intersystem crossing mechanism of dibenzothiophene derivatives, [18] reaching the S1(ππ*) and S1(πσ*) crossing seam requires an excess of 0.4 eV, making it less energetically favorable to access it. Therefore, an intersystem crossing region that can be accessed prior to the S1(ππ*)/S1(πσ*) crossing seam and that has significant spin‐orbit coupling (SOC) between the S1 and T3 was proposed as the most favorable intersystem crossing pathway in the dibenzothiophene derivatives. [18]

This study investigates the photophysical properties and the intersystem crossing relaxation pathways of 1,2‐benzodiphenylene sulfide (12BS), benzo[b]naphtho[1,2‐d]thiophene (BN12TP), and benzo[b]naphtho[2,3‐d]thiophene (BN23TP), depicted in Scheme 1 (BN derivatives, hereafter). It highlights structure‐photophysical property relationships that are observed upon the addition of benzene rings to the thiophene chromophore and their correlation with the efficiency and rate of intersystem crossing to the triplet manifold in polar aprotic and non‐polar solvents. Specifically, the investigation uses steady‐state absorption and emission spectroscopy, femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy, and DFT and TD‐DFT computational chemistry calculations to investigate the photophysical properties and excited state dynamics of these BN derivatives in acetonitrile and cyclohexene. While Seixas et al. [19] reported the fluorescence, triplet, and internal conversion quantum yields for 12BS in ethanol to be 3.7, 70, and 26 %, respectively, its photophysical properties in acetonitrile and cyclohexene, as well as those of BN12TP and BN23TP in any solvent, have not been reported until now.

Optimized Ground and S1 State Geometries

The ground and S1min(ππ*) optimized geometries were found to be planar, as can be seen in Figure S1. The computed vacuum VEEs, Kohn‐Sham orbitals, and associated transitions are presented in Figuress S2–S4 and Tables S1–S3. The computed energy for the S1 and S2 states for 12BS, BN12TP, and BN23TP support the idea that excitation at 350 nm (or 365 nm for BN23TP) populates directly the S1 state for each BN derivative. The computed absorption spectra shown in Figure S5 for the BN derivatives are in good agreement with the experimental spectra shown in Figure 1a. The VEEs, S1min energies, oscillator strengths, and E0,0 energies in acetonitrile and cyclohexene are presented in Table S4. The VEEs for the BN derivatives in both solvents show the same spectral shift seen in the experimental absorption spectra in cyclohexane and acetonitrile.

Optimized S1(πσ*) State and Conical Intersections

Recent studies focusing on thiophene based aromatic derivatives have predicted that an S−C bond elongation/ring‐opening is the dominant pathway for intersystem crossing.[ 12 , 13 , 18 , 22 ] Hence, the ring‐open S1 optimization for the BN derivatives were also investigated in this study. As the BN derivatives are not symmetrical about the sulfur atom due to the substitution of benzyl and naphthenic moieties on either side of the thiophene chromophore, both S−C ring‐open S1 minima were optimized. The optimized structures are shown in Figures S6–S8. All minimum energy structures optimized for the ring‐opened S1 excited states were found to have πσ* character, as expected.

CIs along the S1 PES between the S1(ππ*) and S3(πσ*) states were also computed using as guess the geometries at the S1 energy barriers obtained through linear interpolation of internal coordinates (LIIC) shown in Figures 2, 3, 4 and described in the following section. While the CI calculations were unsuccessful for both S−C bond elongated structures of 12BS, CIs with similar structure and VEEs to those structures at the energy barriers were found for the other two BN derivatives, as is displayed in Figure S9 and Tables S5 and S8. The energy difference between the optimized CIs and the energy barriers found through LIIC is about 0.1 eV for both BN12TP and BN23TP.

Linear Interpolation of Internal Coordinates

LIIC were used to connect the S1(min)(ππ*) to the S1(min)(πσ*) in vacuum, via twenty‐four geometries for each BN derivative, to produce two‐dimensional potential energy surfaces (PES), as seen in Figures 2, 3, 4. Figures S10–S15 depict analogous LIICs calculated in the presence of acetonitrile and cyclohexane solvents. The solvent effect has minor variance in the calculations performed in vacuum, hence, only vacuum results are discussed herein. The LIIC results are used in this study to predict potential intersystem crossing pathways that involve small energy differences between the singlet and triplet manifold and SOCME that are above 2 cm−1 (see Tables S5–S10).

Stretching the S−C6b bond of 12BS gives rise to three avenues for intersystem crossing. Before reaching the S1(ππ*)/S1(πσ*) energy barrier (Figure 2), an energy crossing between [S1(ππ*)/T3(πσ*)]1 emerges with a SOCME of 25.8 cm−1. Once the energy barrier is surpassed, crossings between [S1(πσ*)/T3(ππ*)]2 and [S1(πσ*)/T2(ππ*)]1 are observed, with SOCME of 10.7 and 30.9 cm−1, respectively. Stretching the S−C7a bond of 12BS gives rise to six pathways for intersystem crossing. Preceding the S1(ππ*)/S1(πσ*) energy barrier, two energy crossings occur between [S1(ππ*)/T4(πσ*)]1 and [S1(ππ*)/T5(ππ*)]1, exhibiting SOCME of 27.6 and 4.5 cm−1, respectively. Subsequent to the barrier, four crossings are obtained involving the [S1(πσ*)/T5(ππ*)]2, [S1(πσ*)/T3(ππ*)]1, [S1(πσ*)/T4(ππ*)]2, and [S1(πσ*)/T2(ππ*)]1 states, with SOCME of 3.1, 4.5, 25.6, and 12.4 cm−1, respectively.

Four routes for intersystem crossing are observed for the S−C6a bond stretch of BN12TP (Figure 3). Before reaching the S1(ππ*)/S1(πσ*) energy barrier, two energy crossings between [S1(ππ*)/T3(πσ*)]1 and [S1(ππ*)/T4(πσ*)]1 are observed, exhibiting SOCME of 3.3 and 29.8 cm−1, respectively. After surmounting the energy barrier, two intersystem crossing pathways involve the [S1(πσ*)/T3(ππ*)]2 and [S1(πσ*)/T2(ππ*)]1 states, with SOCME values of 21.6 and 14.1 cm−1, respectively. Stretching the S−C7a bond of BN12TP, four intersystem crossing pathways are obtained. Prior to reaching the energy barrier, an energy crossing is observed between the [S1(ππ*)/T3(πσ*)]1 states, with a SOCME of 26.3 cm−1. After the energy barrier, two crossings are observed involving the [S1(πσ*)/T3(ππ*)]2 and [S1(πσ*)/T2(ππ*)]1 states, with SOCME values of 16.9 and 30.6 cm−1, respectively.

Stretching the S−C4a bond of BN23TP reveals three pathways for intersystem crossing (Figure 4). Prior to reaching the S1(ππ*)/S1(πσ*) energy barrier, an energy crossing is observed between the [S1(ππ*)/T3(πσ*)]1 states, displaying a SOCME of 7.0 cm−1. After surpassing the energy barrier, two crossings between the [S1(πσ*)/T3(ππ*)]2 and [S1(πσ*)/T2(ππ*)]1 states are observed, with SOCME of 3.6 and 9.6 cm−1, respectively. Stretching the S−C5a bond of BN23TP, five potential avenues for intersystem crossing are obtained. Before reaching the energy barrier, three energy crossings occur between [S1(ππ*)/T2(ππ*)]1, [S1(ππ*)/T3(ππ*)]1, and [S1(ππ*)/T2(πσ*)]2 states, presenting SOCME of 3.6, 6.5, and 7.9 cm−1, respectively. Post the energy barrier, two crossings involve the [S1(πσ*)/T3(ππ*)]2 and [S1(πσ*)/T2(ππ*)]3 states, exhibiting SOCME of 21.1 and 9.4 cm−1, respectively.

Excitation of 12BS at 350 nm gives rise to a spectrum with four dominant peaks at 374, 420, 520, and 642 nm in acetonitrile, while BN12TP initially gives rise to three bands at 383, 420, and 494 nm in acetonitrile. Excitation of BN23TP at 365 nm produces three bands with maxima at 382, 417, and 574 nm in acetonitrile. These spectra can be better visualized by EADS1 Figure 5a. Upon excitation of each respective compound in cyclohexane, the same bands are present but slightly red shifted (see EADS1, Figure 5b).

Interestingly, a small but reproducible increase in the amplitude of ΔA is observed for 12BS, BN12TP, BN23TP in going from EADS1 to EADS2 in both solvents (Figure 5a and b). This increase in intensity is also observed in the kinetic traces shown in Figure 6. The phenomenon of an increase in ΔA in going from EADS1 to EADS2 was also observed in the dibenzothiophene derivatives. [18] In that study, it was proposed to be associated with an increase in the absorption cross sections of the transient species as the nuclear coordinate traverse from the Franck‐Condon region of the S1 potential energy surface. [18]

In both solvents, 12BS, BN12TP, and BN23TP undergoes intersystem crossing through a state‐to‐state transition after 1170 ps, ca. 725 ps, and ca. 550 ps lifetime, respectively. This transition is evident from the drastic spectral changes and the presence of multiple isosbestic points. The EADS3 for 12BS displays bands at 406, 426, 514, and 550 nm in cyclohexane, while shows a single band at 502 nm with a shoulder at 471 nm for BN12TP (Figure 5). The EADS3 for BN23TP exhibits a plethora of bands at 381, 428, 451, 482, 513, 566, and 621 nm in cyclohexane. The spectral features of the EADS3 of these BN derivatives in acetonitrile are similar to those in cyclohexane but are slightly blue shifted (Figure 5).

Excitation at 266 nm results in similar EADSs and lifetimes compared to those upon excitation at 350 or 365 nm for the EADS2 and EADS3 and τ2 and τ3, respectively (Figures S16 and S17). However, τ1 has a 14 ps lifetime across all derivatives in both solvents. This is also accompanied with a significant change in the EADS1 for each derivative (Figure S16). The bands seen in EADS1266 show less vibrational resolution in their bands to those of the EADS1350/365, in addition, to being red shifted by ca. 5–10 nm.

Discussion

12BS and BN12TP exhibit similar absorption maximum for the lowest energy band and Franck‐Condon energies for both the S1 and S2 excited states, while the S1 of BN23TP is red shifted by ca. 20 nm. The computed VEEs for these BN derivatives align well with the experimental absorption spectra. The agreement between the theoretical and experimental absorption spectra shown in Figures 1 and S5 evidence that excitation of 12BS and BN12TP at 350 or BN23TP at 365 nm populates the S1 state in these BN derivatives. 12BS and BN12TP also exhibit similar emission spectra and fluorescence quantum yields, while the emission spectrum of BN23TP is significantly red shifted and its fluorescence quantum yield is ca. 0.5 % lower in both solvents (Figure 1b and Table 1). Importantly, the relatively low fluorescence quantum yield of these BN derivatives evidences that most of the excited state population that reaches the S1(ππ*) state decays to the ground state via one or more nonradiative decay pathways.

The femtosecond transient absorption experiments provide important spectroscopic information evidencing the population of long‐lived species with negligible radiative probabilities (Figures 5 and 6 and Table 2). The first lifetime reported in Table 2 is attributed to vibrational relaxation to the S1(min)(ππ*), given that the S1(ππ*) state is directly populated in these BN derivatives upon excitation of 12BS and BN12TP at 350 or BN23TP at 365 nm. In other words, excitation of 12BS and BN12TP at 350 or BN23TP at 365 nm initially populates the vibrationally excited S1(ππ*) state, which relaxes within the first lifetime to populate the S1(min)(ππ*). Thus, EADS1 is assigned to the vibrationally excited S1(ππ*) absorption spectrum, while EADS2 is assigned to the absorption spectrum of the S1(min)(ππ*) for each BN derivative. The second lifetime is assigned to intersystem crossing to populate the triplet manifold in both solvents. This is supported by the agreement of the computed T1(min)(ππ*) absorption spectra in Figure S18 with the EADS3 shown in Figure 5, the significant spectral changes observed between the EADS2 and EADS3 accompanied by the isosbestic points (Figures 5), and the previous investigation performed by Seixas et al. for 12BS in ethanol evidencing the population of the T1 state with a 70 % yield. [19] The third lifetime of >3 ns is associated with the decay of the T1 state, also in agreement with the results of Seixas et al. showing that the T1 state of 12BS decays in 43 μs in ethanol. [19]

While excitation of the BN derivatives at 266 nm populates one or more high energy Sn(ππ*) states, no significant changes are observed in the intersystem crossing lifetime or the EADS2 and EADS3 spectra (Figures S16 and S17). However, noticeable spectral changes are observed in the EADS1266 compared to those of EADS1350/365 (Figures S16 and 5) and the magnitude of the first lifetime decreases to 14 ps in all three BN derivatives. We propose that the spectral differences between EADS1266 and EADS1350/365 are due to ultrafast internal conversion (i. e., faster than our time resolution) from high energy Sn(ππ*) states to the S1(ππ*) state in these BN derivatives, which populates their S1(ππ*) states with significantly higher excess vibrational energy upon excitation at 266 nm. This higher excess vibrational energy in the S1(ππ*) state could be used to more efficiently internally convert a fraction of the S1(ππ*) state population to the ground state nonradiatively, which may explain the shorter, 14 ps lifetime in both solvents. As noted above, the intersystem crossing lifetime of these BN derivatives is not appreciably affect with excitation energy.

Intersystem Crossing to the Triplet Manifold

Most of the thianaphthene,[ 19 , 20 ] dibenzothiophene,[ 18 , 19 ] and BN [19] derivatives investigated thus far exhibit efficient intersystem crossing to the triplet state with yields approaching unity and low fluorescence and internal conversion quantum yields in solution. Hence, the focus of this section is in understanding the intersystem crossing pathways leading to the population of the long‐lived T1 state in the BN derivatives. According to the LIIC calculations (Figures 2, 3, 4 and S10–S15), each BN derivative has several presumed intersystem crossing relaxation pathways leading to the population of the T1 state. These intersystem crossing pathways are available both before and after surmounting the energy barriers to reach the S1(min)(ππ*) and S1(min)(πσ*) CIs (Figures 2, 3, 4 and S10–S15). Given that the energy barriers to access the S1(min)(ππ*)/S1(min)(πσ*) CIs are relatively large (>0.4 eV) for all three BN derivatives, it is proposed intersystem crossing pathways prior to reaching the S1(min)(ππ*)/S1(min)(πσ*) CIs are more likely involved in the intersystem crossing events to populate the T1 state (see Figures 2, 3, 4 and S19). These intersystem crossing pathways are also El‐Sayed allowed and exhibit small energy gaps and significant SOCME.

Two different C−S bond elongation pathways were considered in this study for each BN derivatives (Figures 2, 3, 4). For 12BS and BN12TP, it is observed that the energy increases by ca. 0.1 eV in going from [S1(ππ*)/T4(πσ*)]1 (S−C7a) to [S1(ππ*)/T3(πσ*)]1 (S−C6b) crossing region or from [S1(ππ*)/T3(πσ*)]1 (S−C7a) to [S1(ππ*)/T4(πσ*)]1 (S−C6a) crossing region, respectively. In addition, both [S1(ππ*)/T4(πσ*)]1 (S−C7a) and [S1(ππ*)/T3(πσ*)]1 (S−C6b) crossing regions have similar SOCME of 27.6 and 25.8 cm−1 in 12BS, and both [S1(ππ*)/T3(πσ*)]1 (S−C7a) and [S1(ππ*)/T4(πσ*)]1 (S−C6a) crossing regions also have similar SOCME of 26.3 and 29.8 cm−1 in BN12TP. Taken together, these results suggest that the bond elongation pathway is more favorable along the naphthalene side of the molecule compare to the benzene side. This is further supported by the electron density of the Kohn‐Sham orbitals at the Franck‐Condon region favoring the S−C6b and S−C6a bonds of 12BS and BN12TP, respectively (Figures S3 and S4). For BN23TP, a prediction on which of the two elongation pathways play a major role in intersystem crossing based on energetic and SOCME arguments is more challenging. This is so because the crossing regions for both S−C4a and S−C5a elongation pathways before reaching the S1(min)(ππ*)/S1(min)(πσ*) CI are practically isoenergetic and the SOCME are also very similar within the computational uncertainties. Furthermore, the electron density of their Kohn‐Sham orbitals shows no preference towards either bond elongation. Based on these observation, it is proposed that the [S1(ππ*)/T3(πσ*)]1 (S−C4a), [S1(ππ*)/T3(ππ*)]1 (S−C5a) and [S1(ππ*)/T2(πσ*)]2 (S−C5a) crossing regions should have similar probabilities to be the dominant intersystem crossing pathways for BN23TP. The proposal that multiple intersystem crossing regions could play a preponderant role in intersystem crossing to the T1 state in BN23TP is in line with the observation that the intersystem crossing lifetime in BN23TP is ca. 2.1 to 1.3‐fold shorter than those in 12BS and BN12TP in both solvents (Table 2). Similarly, the energy required to reach the [S1(ππ*)/T3(πσ*)]1 (S−C7a) intersystem crossing pathway for BN12TP is 0.1 eV lower than that required to reach the [S1(ππ*)/T4(πσ*)]1 (S−C7a) for 12BS, also in line with the shorter intersystem crossing lifetime observed for BN12TP compared to 12BS in Table 2. While these are compelling correlations, we note that high level dynamics simulations that include both singlet and triplet states [13] are required to unequivocally assign the primary intersystem crossing pathways populating the long‐lived T1 state in these BN derivatives.

Structure‐Photophysical Property Relationships

Recent results have shown that there is enough internal energy to reach the S1(min)(ππ*)/S1(min)(πσ*) CIs upon direct excitation of the S1(ππ*) state for thiophene and the thianaphthene derivatives.[ 13 , 20 ] Interestingly, the substitution of thiophene with benzene rings to form DBT [18] and BN derivatives systematically increases the energy required to access the S1(min)(ππ*)/S1(min)(πσ*) CIs in the DBT and BN derivatives from the Franck‐Condon region of the S1 potential energy surface. The intersystem crossing lifetime increases in going from thianaphthene (387 to 397 ps) [20] to dibenzothiophene (820 to 870 ps) [18] to 1,2‐benzodiphenylene sulfide (1170 ps in Table 2) in both polar aprotic and non‐polar solvents. In addition, the triplet quantum yield decreases in going from thianaphthene (98 %) to dibenzothiophene (97 %) to 1,2‐benzodiphenylene sulfide (70 %) in ethanol. [19] Collectively, these correlations show that the addition of benzene rings to both sides of the thiophene chromophore reduces the intersystem crossing rate and the efficiency of triplet state population in these derivatives. Similarly, the position at which a benzene ring is added to dibenzothiophene significantly affect its intersystem crossing lifetime: dibenzothiophene (820 to 870 ps), [18] 1,2‐benzodiphenylene sulfide (1170 ps in Table 2), benzo[b]naphtho[1,2‐d]thiophene (720 to 730 ps in Table 2), and benzo[b]naphtho[2,3‐d]thiophene (530 to 570 ps in Table 2).

Methylation of thianaphthene at the C2 or C3 position is shown to increase its intersystem crossing rate by ca. 1.4‐ and 2.3‐fold in acetonitrile and cyclohexene, respectively. [20] Interestingly, however, methylation of dibenzothiophene has no appreciable effect on its intersystem crossing rate in aprotic polar and non‐polar solvents, [18] suggesting that methylation of the BN derivatives should not significantly affect their intersystem crossing rates and efficiency to populate their triplet manifold.

Photodegradation experiments were also performed exciting directly the S1 state of the investigated TN, [20] DBT, [18] and BN derivatives, subjecting them to laser irradiation for two to three hours in both acetonitrile and cyclohexane. Surprisingly, no evidence of photodegradation were observed from the absorption spectra as a function of irradiation time, leading to the conclusion that these TN, DBT, and BN derivatives are highly photostable.

Conclusions

The experimental and computational results presented in this study provide compelling evidence that the S−C bond elongation reaction coordinate facilitates the efficient intersystem crossing to the T1 state in the BN derivatives investigated in this study in both aprotic polar and non‐polar solvents. Similar experimental and computational results supporting the S−C bond elongation as the primary intersystem crossing pathway have been recently presented for thiophene [13] and several thianaphthene [20] and dibenzothiophene [18] derivatives. Collectively, the spectroscopic and computational evidence gathered thus far provide strong support of S−C bond elongation controls the intersystem crossing pathways in the thiophene based derivatives. Importantly, the computational results presented in this study suggest that intersystem crossing to the triplet manifold can occur before reaching the S1(min)(ππ*)/S1(min)(πσ*) CIs in these BN derivatives. The calculations presented in this study also explain the variation in the intersystem crossing lifetimes experimentally observed for 12BS, BN12TP, and BN23TP in both polar aprotic and non‐polar solvents. Finally, the correlations disclosed above could be used to rationally add methyl or benzene groups to thiophene derivatives and oligomers in order to control the efficiency of T1 state population and the intersystem crossing rate for prospective applications in OLEDs, photovoltaics, and medicinal chemistry.

Experimental Methods

Chemical

Benzo[b]naphtho[2,3‐d]thiophene (BN23TP) and 1,2‐benzodiphenylene sulfide (12BS) were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich (99 % purity), while benzo[b]naphtho[1,2‐d]thiophene (BN12TP) was obtained from TCI (98 % purity). Cyclohexane (CHX, 99 % purity, Millipore Corporation) and acetonitrile (ACN, 99.8 % purity, Fisher Scientific) were used as received.

Steady State Spectroscopy

Steady‐state absorption and emission spectra were taken using Varian Cary 100 and Varian Cary Eclipse, respectively. A PMT voltage of 540 V with 5 nm slit widths and excitation at 335 or 350 nm were used to obtain the emission spectra of the BN derivatives. Fluorescence quantum yields were measured using quinine bisulfate as a standard. [23]

Transient Absorption Spectroscopy

The femtosecond broadband transient absorption setup (BTAS) used in this study is described in detail elsewhere.[ 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 ] Briefly, the excitation wavelength of 350 or 365 nm was generated through the use of an optical parametric amplifier. Excitation at 266 nm was generated using an optical rail kit (FKE series, EKSMA optics).[ 29 , 30 ] A fraction of the fundamental pulse was sent through a delay stage and focused on a translating CaF2 crystal to generate a white light continuum probe pulse in a spectral range of ~360 to 680 nm. A 2 mm fused silica cuvette with a stir bar was used to ensure homogeneity of the solutions during the pump‐probe experiments. Steady‐state absorbance measurements were taken before and after transient experiments and no photodegradation of the samples was observed.

The femtosecond transient absorption data were collected using SurfaceXplorer and analyzed using global and target analysis. [31] Time‐zero was defined as the time of maximum overlap of the pump and probe pulses, as seen using the stimulated Raman emission signal of acetonitrile. Experiments in cyclohexane and acetonitrile were run back‐to‐back in order to use the Raman signal in acetonitrile to define time zero for all samples in both solvents. A 3‐component sequential kinetic model was used to model the transient absorption data obtained for the BN derivatives in acetonitrile and cyclohexane using the Glotaran 1.5.1 graphical user interfaced to the R‐package TIMP software. [32] Transient absorption signals surviving for more than 3 ns were observed in the BN derivatives in both solvents, which are longer the 3 ns time widow used in this study. Hence, the third lifetime for each BN derivatives is reported as >3 ns because it cannot be accurately measured. All measurements were run in duplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Computational Methods

Ground and excited state optimizations, vertical excitation energies (VEE), and spin‐orbit coupling matrix elements (SOCME) calculations were performed using Orca 5.0.2. [33] The calculations made use of the B3LYPG‐D3BJ[ 34 , 35 ] functional with the def2‐TZVPD [36] basis for both DFT and TD‐DFT calculations, as this level of theory has been shown to accurately model organic compounds.[ 37 , 38 ] Frequency calculations were performed for all ground state optimizations to ensure that the ground‐state optimized geometries contain no imaginary frequencies and are local minima. A split‐resolution of identity‐j with chain‐of‐spheres exchange (RIJCOSX) [39] was also included to reduce the computational cost with negligible cost to accuracy. SOCME were computed using the TD‐DFT quasi‐degenerate perturbation theory at all geometries between the singlet and triplet states. [40] The conductor‐like polarizable continuum model (CPCM) [41] was used to model solvent effects. The computed absorption spectra for the steady‐state and transient triplet→triplet were visualized using Gabedit with a FWHM of 20 nm. [42]

The ring opening structures of the BN derivatives were optimized at same level of theory TD−B3LYPG‐D3BJ/CPCM/def2‐TZVPD||B3LYPG‐D3BJ/CPCM/def2‐TZVPD in vacuum, cyclohexane, and acetonitrile using an initial guess geometry involving the elongation of the S−C bond. The S1 geometry optimizations for 12BS and BN23TP were conducted at the TD−B3LYPG‐D3BJ/CPCM/def2‐TZVPD||B3LYPG‐D3BJ/CPCM/def2‐TZVPD level of theory and converged to nearly planar S1 structures in addition to the ring opening structures. However, BN12TP displayed a non‐planar S1 structure in addition to the ring opening structure. The linear interpolation of internal coordinates (LIIC) was done using 24 linear interpolation geometries from S1(min)(ππ*) structure to the S1(min)(πσ*) structure. [43] VEEs and SOCME for those linear interpolated geometries were then computed at the TD−B3LYPG‐D3BJ/CPCM/def2‐TZVPD||B3LYPG‐D3BJ/CPCM/def2‐TZVPD level of theory. To benchmark and verify LIIC calculations, conical intersection (CI) searches were performed followed by VEEs calculations at the TD−B3LYPG‐D3BJ/CPCM/def2‐TZVPD||B3LYPG‐D3BJ/CPCM/def2‐TZVPD level of theory using the geometry at the energy barrier maxima as the initial guesses.
