Evaluating hemoglobin versus hematocrit measurements in the diagnosis of anemia among pregnant women in Woliso town, Ethiopia: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Firaol Regea Gelassa, Gedefa Tafa, Tesfu Zewdu, Desalegn Tamiru, Melese Adugna, Abera Beyera, Elias Andesha Fana, Hailu Merga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnemia during pregnancy remains a public health concern in Ethiopia. While hemoglobin (Hb) concentration is the WHO-recommended standard for diagnosing anemia, hematocrit (Hct) continues to be used in many resource-limited areas. However, evidence comparing Hb- and Hct-based diagnostic classifications among pregnant women in Ethiopia remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the agreement between hemoglobin- and hematocrit-based diagnostic methods and to determine whether these methods yield comparable estimates of anemia among pregnant women attending antenatal care in Woliso town. A facility-based convergent mixed-methods cross-sectional study was conducted among 280 systematically selected pregnant women from February 1–28, 2023. Venous blood samples were analysed using the Mindray BC-5150 automated haematology analyser (reference standard) and the Mission Hb Hemoglobin Meter. Anemia was defined as Hb < 11 g/dL or Hct < 33%. Agreement between methods was assessed using intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC), Bland–Altman analysis, kappa statistics, and McNemar’s test. Logistic regression analysis was performed using hemoglobin-based anemia as the primary outcome. Thirteen purposively selected anemic pregnant women participated in in-depth interviews. Qualitative data were analysed using inductive thematic analysis and integrated with quantitative findings at the interpretation stage. Hemoglobin-based assessment identified anemia in 11.4% (95% CI 8–15%) of participants, whereas hematocrit-based assessment identified 18.6% (95% CI 14–23). The 7.2 percentage-point difference was statistically significant (McNemar’s p = 0.018). Agreement between Hb and Hct measurements was substantial (κ = 0.72) with excellent correlation (ICC = 0.89). However, Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated limits of agreement that may influence clinical classification near diagnostic thresholds. Factors associated with haemoglobin-based anemia included unplanned pregnancy, malaria infection, lack of nutritional counselling, and non-use of iron–folate supplementation, although some estimates were imprecise. Qualitative findings converged with quantitative results, highlighting prior illness, limited dietary diversity, and challenges with iron supplementation. Hematocrit-based assessment yielded significantly higher anemia prevalence than hemoglobin measurement, suggesting potential overestimation when Hct is used alone. While agreement between methods was substantial, they are not fully interchangeable in clinical practice. Routine use of standardized hemoglobin-based measurements is recommended to ensure accurate anemia classification among pregnant women.Keywords: Anemia, Haematocrit, Haemoglobin, Pregnant mothers, Woliso town, EthiopiaSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Background

Anemia during pregnancy remains a major public health challenge in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), including Ethiopia, where it contributes to increased risks of maternal morbidity, preterm birth, and low birth weight1–4. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), anemia in pregnancy is defined as a hemoglobin concentration below 11 g/dL5. Recent national and regional studies indicate that anemia affects a substantial proportion of pregnant women in Ethiopia, with prevalence estimates ranging from 20 to 40%6–9. Accurate diagnosis is therefore essential for appropriate management and program monitoring.

Hemoglobin concentration is globally recognized as the standard biomarker for diagnosing anemia5. Although hemoglobin measurement is recommended for anemia diagnosis, hematocrit (packed cell volume) has historically been used as a proxy indicator in some resource-constrained settings, particularly where automated hematology analyzers or point-of-care hemoglobin devices are limited. Its continued use is often attributed to its operational simplicity and lower cost10. Although hemoglobin and hematocrit are physiologically related, they are not analytically identical. Physiological plasma volume expansion during pregnancy may influence hematocrit differently from hemoglobin concentration, potentially leading to discrepant classification of anemia status. Such discrepancies may result in under- or overestimation of disease burden, with implications for clinical decision-making and public health reporting.

Previous Ethiopian studies have primarily focused on estimating anemia prevalence and identifying associated factors9,11–13. However, few investigations have directly compared hemoglobin- and hematocrit-based diagnostic classifications among pregnant women. Evidence validating point-of-care devices, particularly among pregnant populations, remain limited in Ethiopia14–16. Without clear evaluation of agreement between these diagnostic approaches, reliance on hematocrit measurements alone may compromise diagnostic precision.

Beyond diagnostic performance, understanding contextual and behavioral factors associated with anemia remains important. Determinants such as nutritional practices, infection history, and supplementation adherence have been documented in previous studies9,28–30. However, quantitative findings often lack contextual explanation regarding how women perceive and experience these factors during pregnancy.

Therefore, this study employed a convergent mixed-methods approach to (1) evaluate the agreement and diagnostic consistency between hemoglobin- and hematocrit-based measurements and (2) explore contextual factors associated with anemia among pregnant women attending antenatal care in Woliso town. The qualitative component was designed to complement quantitative findings by providing explanatory insight into women’s lived experiences related to anemia risk and care.

Methods and materials

Study setting and populations

A facility-based convergent mixed-methods cross-sectional study was conducted from February 1 to February 28, 2023, in Woliso town, Southwest Shewa Zone, Oromia Regional State, Ethiopia. The town has one government hospital, one private hospital, and two public health centres. According to the town health office report, 6,173 pregnant women were enrolled in antenatal care services during the previous reporting year.

Study population

All pregnant women attending ANC follow-up in public health facilities during the study period were eligible. Women, who were critically ill, defined operationally as those unable to communicate or provide informed consent due to severe medical instability or those with hearing or speech impairment, were excluded.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The quantitative sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula considering 21.3% of the proportion of the outcome from a previous study prevalence16, 95% confidence level, and 5% margin of error. After adding 10% non-response, the final sample size was 284. A total of 280 women participated (98.6% response rate).

Participants were selected using systematic random sampling with a sampling interval (K = 2) calculated based on average monthly ANC attendance. Every 2nd eligible woman was recruited after selecting the first participant by lottery method.

For the qualitative component, 13 anemic pregnant women were purposively selected based on hemoglobin test results obtained from the Mindray BC-5150 automated hematology analyzer. Selection considered variation in residence, parity, pregnancy planning status, and ANC history to ensure diverse perspectives. Sampling continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new themes emerged from successive interviews.

Data collection tools and procedures

Questionnaire development and validation

A structured questionnaire was used to collect data on socio-demographic characteristics, reproductive history, dietary practices, and maternal health service utilization. The questionnaire was developed after reviewing relevant literature and previously published studies conducted in similar settings17–21. Items measuring key variables were adapted from validated tools used in maternal nutrition research. The questionnaire was first prepared in English and then translated into the local language and back-translated to ensure consistency. Prior to the actual data collection, the tool was pretested on 5% of the sample size in a comparable health facility not included in the study area to assess clarity, relevance, and comprehensibility. Necessary modifications were made based on the pre-test findings. The internal consistency reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for multi-item scales, and the value was found to be within the acceptable range (α ≥ 0.7), indicating good reliability.

Dietary diversity assessment

Maternal dietary diversity was assessed using the Minimum Dietary Diversity for Women (MDD-W) indicator developed by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). Participants were asked to recall all foods consumed during the previous 24 h. The consumed foods were categorized into ten standard food groups according to the FAO MDD-W guideline: (1) grains, white roots and tubers, and plantains; (2) pulses; (3) nuts and seeds; (4) dairy products; (5) meat, poultry, and fish; (6) eggs; (7) dark green leafy vegetables; (8) other vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables; (9) other vegetables; and (10) other fruits. Women who consumed five or more of the ten food groups in the previous 24 h were classified as having adequate dietary diversity, whereas those consuming fewer than five food groups were classified as having low dietary diversity20,21.

Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) measurement

Maternal nutritional status was assessed using Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) measurement. MUAC was measured on the left arm at the midpoint between the acromion process of the shoulder and the olecranon process of the elbow using a non-stretchable MUAC measuring tape. The measurement was taken with the arm relaxed and hanging freely along the body. To ensure accuracy, the measurement was taken to the nearest 0.1 cm, and trained data collectors performed the procedure following standard anthropometric techniques. In this study, a MUAC cutoff value of < 23 cm was used to classify pregnant women as undernourished, while those with MUAC ≥ 23 cm were considered nutritionally normal, according to commonly used maternal nutrition assessment guidelines18.

Laboratory procedures

Venous blood samples were collected from all participants under aseptic conditions. Hemoglobin and hematocrit levels were measured using the Mindray BC-5150 automated hematology analyzer (reference standard) and Mission Hb Hemoglobin Meter (point-of-care device)13–15. Hematocrit values from both devices were directly measured, not calculated. Hemoglobin values were adjusted for altitude according to WHO recommendations5. Quality control procedures were conducted daily following manufacturer guidelines22.

Measurements

Anemia in this study was defined as both hemoglobin and hematocrit levels of less than 11 g/dL and 33%, respectively5. It was classified into three categories: mild (10–10.9 g/dL); moderate (7–9.9 g/dL); and severe (less than 7 g/dL)5. Test agreement of the Mindray BC-5150 CBC Hematology Analyzer and Mission HB Hemoglobin Meter was interpreted using ICCs: < 0 = poor; 0.01–0.20 = slight; 0.21–0.40 = fair; 0.41–0.60 = moderate; 0.61–0.80 = substantial; and 0.81–1.00 = almost perfect agreement. Dietary diversity was assessed and scored as follows: Low (DDS ≤ 3), medium (DDS of 4 or 5), or high (DDS ≥ 6)20.

Data analysis

Data were entered into EpiData version 3.1 and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Agreement between hemoglobin and hematocrit measurements was assessed using the Bland and Altman method and McNamara’s test for paired proportions, where the mean, standard deviation, and limit of agreement of paired results were calculated, and accuracy was estimated by calculating bias values along with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) and concordance correlation coefficients (CCCs)13–15. Performance evaluation was based on measurements of sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values.

For regression analysis, hemoglobin-based anemia (Hb < 11 g/dL) was used as the primary outcome variable. Variables with p < 0.25 in bi-variable analysis were entered into multivariable logistic regression. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test9,23,28–30.

Qualitative data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, translated into English, coded manually, and organized into themes. Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings was performed at the interpretation stage to assess convergence and complementarity17,19–21.

The majority (76.8%) resided in urban areas, and 98.2% were married. Regarding educational status, 34.6% had completed primary education. This proportion does not represent two-thirds of the sample and has been revised for accuracy (See Table 1).

Diagnostic comparison between hemoglobin and hematocrit-based classification

Using the Mindray BC-5150 automated hematology analyzer as the reference standard, hemoglobin-based assessment identified anemia in 11.4% (95% CI 8–15) of pregnant women. In contrast, hematocrit-based assessment classified 18.6% (95% CI 14–23) as anemic. The 7.2 percentage-point difference between methods was statistically significant (McNemar’s χ2 test, p = 0.018), indicating that hematocrit-based assessment yielded a higher anemia prevalence compared with hemoglobin-based classification.

Agreement analysis demonstrated substantial concordance between methods (κ = 0.72, interpreted using Landis & Koch classification). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.89 (95% CI 0.86–0.91), indicating excellent correlation between continuous measurements. However, correlation does not imply interchangeability.

Bland–Altman analysis

For hemoglobin, the mean bias between the Mission Hb device and the Mindray analyzer was small. The limits of agreement ranged from − 1.97 to + 1.61 g/dL. Although the average bias was minimal, the observed variation of nearly ± 2 g/dL may influence anemia classification among women whose hemoglobin values lie near the 11 g/dL diagnostic threshold.

For hematocrit, the mean bias was + 0.21%, with limits of agreement from − 5.02 to + 5.42%. This range suggests that hematocrit values could differ by approximately five percentage points between methods. Given the diagnostic cutoff of 33%, such variability may contribute to reclassification of anemia status in borderline cases. These findings indicate that while agreement between methods was substantial at the population level, hematocrit and hemoglobin measurements are not fully interchangeable for individual clinical decision-making.

Diagnostic performance of the mission Hb device

When compared with the Mindray BC-5150 analyzer as the reference standard:

Hemoglobin parameter Sensitivity: 84.4% (95% CI 71.8–96.9), Specificity: 97.2% (95% CI 95.1–99.2), Positive predictive value: 79.4%, Negative predictive value: 98.0%, Overall agreement: 95.7%. The high specificity suggests that the Mission Hb device is effective in correctly identifying non-anemic individuals. However, sensitivity below 90% indicates that some anemic cases may be missed.

Hematocrit parameter Sensitivity: 75.5% (95% CI 63.9–87.1), Specificity: 98.7% (95% CI 97.2–100) and Overall agreement: 94.4%. Sensitivity for hematocrit-based detection was notably lower than that of hemoglobin, suggesting greater potential for misclassification when hematocrit is used alone (See Table 3).

Factors associated with hemoglobin-based anemia

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted using hemoglobin-based anemia (Hb < 11 g/dL) as the dependent variable. Variables with p < 0.25 in bi-variable analysis were entered into the multivariable model. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated adequate model fit (p value > 0.05). The following factors were independently associated with anemia: Unplanned pregnancy (AOR = 3.45; 95% CI 1.21–9.85), History of malaria infection (AOR = 6.90; 95% CI 1.16–41.40), Lack of nutritional counseling (AOR = 4.77; 95% CI 1.21–18.79), Non-use of iron–folate supplementation (AOR = 3.99; 95% CI 1.39–40.70). The association with lack of formal education was modest (AOR = 1.20; 95% CI 1.04–4.23). Given the small effect size and wide confidence interval, this variable is interpreted as a demographic correlate rather than a strong predictor.

For malaria history and iron- folate supplementation, confidence intervals were wide, suggesting limited precision, possibly due to small subgroup numbers. These estimates should therefore be interpreted cautiously (See Table 4).

Qualitative findings

The qualitative component followed an inductive thematic approach. Thirteen purposively selected anemic pregnant women participated in in-depth interviews. Data saturation was achieved when no new themes emerged in the final interviews. Accordingly, three major themes were identified.

Theme 1: history of infectious and chronic illnesses

Participants frequently described previous or current malaria infection as contributing to their perception of anemia. These narratives reflected women’s understanding of illness recurrence during pregnancy. For example, one participant explained:

“When I became pregnant and started feeling dizziness, they tested my blood and told me I had malaria and anemia”. (WP2, 28 years).

These accounts represent participants’ interpretations rather than clinical verification. Several women also linked prior anemia episodes to their current vulnerability, suggesting that previous illness shaped their expectations of recurring anemia during pregnancy.

Chronic conditions such as kidney disease and hypertension were similarly described as factors that “reduce blood”, reflecting a perceived interaction between long-term illness and pregnancy-related weakness.

Analytically, these narratives suggest that women interpret anemia within broader experiences of illness, rather than as an isolated condition.

Theme 2: limited nutritional counseling and low dietary diversity

Participants described limited access to nutritional information during early ANC visits. Several reported that dietary advice was brief or absent. One participant stated: “They did not explain about different foods during my first visit. Later they said my blood was low”. (WP1, 25 years). Others described economic constraints affecting dietary diversity: “I eat what I can get from daily work. Mostly bread and injera. Meat is difficult”. (WP10, 18 years).

These narratives highlight structural barriers, including financial limitations and food insecurity that may constrain dietary practices. The qualitative data suggest that inadequate counseling combined with limited resources may influence dietary diversity.

Theme 3: Iron supplementation challenges

Women described nausea and discomfort after taking iron tablets, which influenced adherence. “When I swallowed the pill, I felt nausea and stopped taking it”. (WP7, 26 years). Some participants reported limited counseling regarding side-effect management. Others described medication stock interruptions at health facilities, suggesting that both individual and system-level factors may influence supplementation adherence. These findings indicate that non-use of iron–folate supplementation may reflect complex behavioral and service-delivery dynamics rather than simple refusal.

Joint display integrating quantitative results with qualitative themes, illustrating areas of convergence, complementarity, and divergence.

The joint display demonstrates overall convergence and complementarity between quantitative and qualitative findings. Key statistically significant factors—such as malaria infection, lack of nutritional counseling, and non-use of iron–folate supplementation—were consistently supported by participants’ narratives, reinforcing the credibility of these associations.

Qualitative findings further provided contextual explanations for these relationships. For example, women described experiencing malaria during pregnancy as a condition that “reduces blood”, aligning with the observed statistical association. Similarly, limited nutritional counseling during antenatal care visits was both quantitatively associated with anemia and qualitatively described as insufficient or absent, particularly during early visits.

The association between non-use of iron-folate supplementation and anemia was also strongly supported. Participants reported discontinuation due to side effects such as nausea and highlighted occasional supply interruptions, suggesting that both individual-level and health system factors influence adherence.

For unplanned pregnancy, partial convergence was observed. While quantitative analysis showed a significant association, qualitative narratives indirectly suggested mechanisms such as delayed care engagement, though this was not a dominant theme.

A minor divergence was noted regarding educational status. Although lack of formal education showed a statistically significant but modest association, it was not prominently reflected in participant narratives, suggesting it may function as a distal or background factor rather than a directly perceived determinant.

Overall, no major contradictions were identified. Instead, the qualitative data enriched the interpretation of quantitative findings by illustrating how biomedical risk factors are shaped by behavioral experiences and health system realities. This integrated analysis strengthens the validity and interpretability of the study findings.

Discussion

Diagnostic comparison between hemoglobin and hematocrit

The central finding of this study is the significant difference in anemia prevalence identified by hemoglobin- and hematocrit-based diagnostic approaches. Hematocrit classified 18.6% of women as anemic, whereas hemoglobin identified 11.4%, representing a 7.2 percentage-point higher prevalence when hematocrit was used. The McNemar test confirmed that this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.018). This suggests that hematocrit-based assessment may overestimate anemia compared with hemoglobin measurement in this population13,15 [32]. Although correlation between continuous values was strong (ICC = 0.89), Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated variability at the individual level. The limits of agreement for hemoglobin approached ± 2 g/dL, and hematocrit varied by approximately ± 5 percentage points. Such variation is clinically relevant near diagnostic cutoffs (Hb < 11 g/dL; Hct < 33%), where small measurement differences may alter classification5,13.

The high specificity of both parameters (97–99%) indicates strong ability to correctly identify non-anemic individuals. However, hematocrit demonstrated lower sensitivity (75.5%) compared with hemoglobin (84.4%), suggesting a greater likelihood of missed cases when relying solely on hematocrit13,14. In resource-limited settings where hematocrit is often used as a proxy for hemoglobin, these findings have practical implications. Overestimation may inflate reported prevalence, whereas reduced sensitivity may delay detection in borderline cases. Therefore, hemoglobin measurement using standardized devices should remain the preferred diagnostic approach when feasible5,13,14.

Prevalence comparison with other settings

The hemoglobin-based anemia prevalence (11.4%) observed in Woliso town is lower than many previously reported estimates in Ethiopia and other low- and middle-income countries6–12,23–26. Differences may reflect improved antenatal care utilization, increased iron–folate supplementation coverage, or urban predominance in the study population. However, comparisons must be interpreted cautiously due to differences in study year, gestational age distribution, diagnostic method, and sample size across studies6,12. Variations in altitude, nutritional patterns, and malaria transmission intensity may also influence prevalence estimates5,8,9.

The association between lack of formal education and anemia showed a modest effect size (AOR = 1.20) with a wide confidence interval. While statistically significant, the magnitude suggests that education may function as a background demographic correlate rather than a strong independent predictor9,23,27. Educational status may influence health literacy, dietary practices, and adherence to supplementation; however, the present study design does not allow causal inference. Future studies could explore mechanisms linking maternal education and anemia outcomes.

Women with unplanned pregnancies had higher odds of anemia (AOR = 3.45). This association may reflect delayed initiation of antenatal care, reduced preparedness, or nutritional vulnerability prior to pregnancy recognition9,23. The qualitative findings supported this observation, as several participants described limited early engagement with health services in unplanned pregnancies. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional design, temporal relationships cannot be established. From a public health perspective, this finding underscores the importance of family planning services and early ANC enrollment.

A history of malaria infection was associated with higher odds of anemia (AOR = 6.90). However, the confidence interval (1.16–41.40) was wide, indicating limited precision and potential instability due to small subgroup counts. Qualitative narratives reflected participants’ perceptions that malaria “reduces blood”9,28. While biologically plausible, the observed association should be interpreted cautiously and not as proof of causation. Malaria may coexist with other socioeconomic and nutritional vulnerabilities, which were not fully controlled in this analysis8,9.

Lack of nutritional counseling was associated with anemia (AOR = 4.77). Qualitative data complemented this finding by revealing limited dietary guidance during early ANC visits and economic constraints affecting food choices19,20,28. Rather than implying direct causation, the integration of findings suggests that limited counseling may contribute to suboptimal dietary practices within existing structural constraints. Food insecurity and daily labor occupations were described as barriers to dietary diversity20,28–30. This convergence strengthens the interpretation that counseling alone may be insufficient without addressing broader socioeconomic factors.

Non-use of iron–folate supplementation was associated with anemia (AOR = 3.99), though the confidence interval (1.39–40.70) was wide. This imprecision suggests cautious interpretation9,28,29. Qualitative findings indicated that nausea, discomfort, and occasional supply interruptions influenced adherence. Some women reported stopping supplementation due to side effects, while others described stock shortages. These findings suggest that both behavioral and health system factors may influence supplementation use9,28.

Mixed-methods integration

The mixed-methods design followed a convergent approach. Quantitative associations (malaria history, lack of counseling, supplementation non-use) were supported by participants’ narratives describing illness experiences, dietary challenges, and supplementation barriers. No major divergences were observed between datasets. Instead, qualitative findings provided contextual depth, illustrating how biomedical risk factors intersect with lived experiences. This integration enhances the interpretive validity of the findings beyond what either method could provide independently9,28–30.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish temporal or causal relationships between associated factors and anemia. Second, the study was conducted in public health facilities within a single town, which may limit generalizability to rural areas or private facilities. Third, some adjusted estimates had wide confidence intervals, suggesting limited precision due to small subgroup numbers. Fourth, although efforts were made to ensure qualitative rigor, the qualitative sample was relatively small and interviewer bias cannot be completely excluded28–30. Finally, physiological changes during pregnancy, such as plasma volume expansion, may differentially influence hemoglobin and hematocrit measurements, potentially affecting classification5. Despite these limitations, the study provides important comparative diagnostic evidence in a setting where hematocrit is frequently used.

Conclusions

Hematocrit-based assessment yielded a significantly higher anemia prevalence compared with hemoglobin measurement among pregnant women in Woliso town, suggesting potential overestimation when hematocrit is used alone.

Although agreement between methods was substantial at the population level, variability at the individual level indicates that the two measures are not fully interchangeable near diagnostic thresholds. Several contextual factors- including unplanned pregnancy, malaria history, limited nutritional counseling, and iron–folate supplementation challenges were associated with anemia. Qualitative findings complemented these associations by highlighting lived experiences and structural constraints. Hemoglobin measurement using standardized devices should be prioritized where feasible to ensure accurate anemia diagnosis during pregnancy.
