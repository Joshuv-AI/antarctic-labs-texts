Abstract
In this paper, an automated linear parameter-varying (LPV) model conversion approach is proposed for nonlinear dynamical systems. The proposed method achieves global embedding of the original nonlinear behavior of the system by leveraging the second fundamental theorem of calculus to factorize matrix function expressions without any approximation. The implementation of the proposed method in the LPVcore toolbox for Matlab is discussed, and its performance is showcased on a comprehensive example of automated LPV model conversion of an unbalanced disk system, which is then used to design an LPV controller that is deployed on the original nonlinear system. In addition, the conversion capabilities are further demonstrated by obtaining an LPV embedding of a three-degree-of-freedom control moment gyroscope. All software implementations are available at www.lpvcore.net .



keywords

Model conversion, LPV embedding, LPV systems, nonlinear systems.

1
Introduction

The general concept of
linear parameter-varying
(LPV) systems has been introduced to provide
an easily applicable and deployable analysis and control synthesis framework for
nonlinear
(NL) systems based on the extension of powerful approaches of the
linear time-invariant
(LTI) framework
(
Mohammadpour and Scherer 2011
;
Tóth 2010
;
Apkarian et al. 1995
)
. In LPV systems, the signal relations between inputs and outputs are considered linear but, at the same time, dependent on a so-called
scheduling variable

p
. In this way, the variation of
p
represents changing operating conditions, nonlinear effects, etc., described by an underlying
scheduling map
, and aims at complete embedding of the original NL behavior into the solution set of an LPV system representation
(
Tóth 2010
;
Rugh and Shamma 2000
)
. While the former objective is pursued by the so-called
global
LPV modeling approaches; alternatively, one can aim for only an approximation of the NL behavior by interpolation of various linearizations of the system around operating points or signal trajectories, often referred to as
local
modeling; see,
e.g.
,
(
Bachnas et al. 2014
;
Shamma and Athans 1990
)
.

Although many practical approaches have been introduced for local LPV modeling and have also been implemented in various software packages such as
Matlab
, the development of global LPV modeling methods has been limited, especially in terms of widely usable software implementations.
Existing approaches for
global
LPV modeling of NL dynamical systems can be classified into two main categories:
substitution based transformation
(SBT) methods
(
Shamma and Cloutier 1993
;
Papageorgiou et al. 2000
;
Gáspár et al. 2007
;
Tóth et al. 2011
;
Rugh and Shamma 2000
;
Leith and Leithhead 1998
;
Marcos and Balas 2004
)
and
automated conversion procedures

(
Donida et al. 2009
;
Kwiatkowski et al. 2006
;
Kwiatkowski and Werner 2008
;
Tóth 2010
;
Hoffmann and Werner 2015
;
Abbas et al. 2021
;
Sadeghzadeh et al. 2020
;
Rotondo et al. 2015
)
. Specifically,
Donida et al. 2009
proposed a promising symbolic conversion method based on
Modelica
, but the approach has never been released as an available software package. In
Abbas et al. 2021
, a multipath feedback linearization-based LPV model conversion method has been proposed that provides global embedding guarantees, but can generally result in dependence of
p
on various derivatives of the input and output of the system.
Summand decomposition and factorization of nonlinear terms have been proposed in various forms such as in
Kwiatkowski et al. 2006
;
Tóth 2010
;
Rotondo et al. 2015
, resulting in applicable conversion methods, which also allow choosing between complexity and conservativeness of possible LPV embeddings. However, these techniques need the exploration of all possible decomposition and division possibilities, leading to decision trees with a combinatorial explosion of possibilities and suffering from high computational load due to the involved symbolic manipulations and lack of reliable software implementation. In
Hoffmann and Werner 2015
, a
linear matrix inequalities
-based optimization approach has been proposed to efficiently solve the choice of optimal trade-off between the combination of various conversion possibilities. Even if this provided a powerful tool, it is limited to small or moderate state and scheduling dimensions and requires pre-proposed conversion terms. All these problems have been overcome by data-driven techniques
(
Kwiatkowski and Werner 2008
;
Sadeghzadeh et al. 2020
)
, representing an evolution of conversion methods based on
principal components analysis
(PCA), capable of automatic determination of the relevant scheduling dimension and the construction of a data-based scheduling map. These methods have also been implemented in toolboxes such as
LPVcore
. However, success of these modeling techniques depends largely on the available data sets and they are inherently approximative in their nature. Hence, in general there is a lack of reliable and efficiently computable analytic LPV model conversion methods that ensure global embedding of the original NL system and available in terms of an off-the-shelf software solution.

Recently, the idea of a novel LPV modeling procedure has been proposed in the PhD thesis
(
Koelewijn 2023
)
for general NL models using the
Second Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
(FTC) to factorize matrix function expressions without any approximation and alleviating the need for summand decompositions, decision trees, or other complex embedding processes. Then, the approach has been successfully applied to construct a solution to various problems, such as in
(
Hoekstra et al. 2023
)
, but has never been properly formulated and discussed as a global LPV embedding and model conversion tool and no software implementation has been developed for this purpose. In this paper, our main contributions are as follows:

•

Mathematically rigorous formulation of the global LPV embedding of a rather general class of NL systems using an FTC-based factorization of nonlinearites.

•

Analysis of the properties of the resulting systematic LPV modeling process, discussing its pros and cons.

•

Developing a publicly available software implementation of the proposed FTC-based conversion in the toolbox
LPVcore
and demonstrating the capabilities of the approach in practical examples.

The paper is structured as follows: In Section
2
, the problem of global embedding-based LPV model construction for nonlinear systems is introduced. For this problem, the proposed concept of FTC-based model conversion is proposed in Section
3
, also discussing its properties. Then, in Section
4
implementation of the approach in
Matlab
is discussed, followed in Section
5
by the demonstration of the conversion capabilities of the developed tooling on LPV model-based controller design for an unbalanced disk system and LPV modeling of a three-degree-of-freedom control moment gyroscope. Finally, in Section
6
, the main conclusions on the achieved results are drawn.

2
Problem definition

Consider a nonlinear dynamical system given by the
state-space
(SS) representation

\displaystyle\xi x(t)

\displaystyle=f(x(t),u(t)),

(1a)

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=h(x(t),u(t)),

(1b)

where
t\in\mathbb{T}
is time,
\xi
is
\xi x(t)=\frac{d}{dt}x(t)
in the continuous-time case with
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}
and
\xi x(t)=x(t+1)
in the discrete-time case with
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{Z}
,
x(t)\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{x}}}
,
u(t)\in\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{u}}}
and
y(t)\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{y}}}
with
n_{\mathrm{x}},n_{\mathrm{u}},n_{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathbb{N}
are the state, input, and output signals associated with the system, respectively. The functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{x}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{u}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{x}}}
and
h:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{x}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{y}}}
are continuously differentiable once, i.e.,
f,h\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, and the solutions of (
1
) have left compact support, they are forward complete and unique, i.e., for any initial condition
x(t_{0})
and any input trajectory
u(t)
, the solutions of (
1
) are uniquely determined for all
t\geq t_{0}
. Moreover,
(0,0)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}
. Finally we denote the behavior of (
1
), i.e., the set of all possible trajectories by

\mathcal{B}\coloneq\left\{(x,u,y)\in\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y}\right)^{\mathbb{T}}\mid(x,u,y)~\text{satisfy}~\eqref{eq:nl_dyn}\right\},

(2)

where
\mathcal{X}^{\mathbb{T}}
denotes the set of all signals
\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
with left compact support.

In this paper, our objective is to automatically convert the system description (
1
) into a
linear parameter-varying
(LPV) representation of the form

\displaystyle\xi x(t)

\displaystyle=A(p(t))x(t)+B(p(t))u(t);

(3a)

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=C(p(t))x(t)+D(p(t))u(t),

(3b)

where
p\in\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{p}}}
with
n_{\mathrm{p}}\in\mathbb{N}
is the scheduling variable,
A:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{x}}\times n_{\mathrm{x}}}
,
B:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{x}}\times n_{\mathrm{u}}}
,
C:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{y}}\times n_{\mathrm{x}}}
, and
D:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{y}}\times n_{\mathrm{u}}}
are smooth real-valued matrix functions, and the solutions of (
3
) with left compact support are forward complete and unique. For a given scheduling trajectory
p(t)\in\mathcal{P}
, the behavior of (
3
) is defined as

\mathcal{B}_{p}\coloneq\left\{(x,u,y)\in\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y}\right)^{\mathbb{T}}\mid(x,u,y,p)~\text{satisfy}~\eqref{eq:lpv_dyn}\right\},

(4)

and the behavior of (
3
) for all possible scheduling trajectories is defined as

\mathcal{B}_{\text{LPV}}\coloneq\bigcup_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{T}}}\mathcal{B}_{p}(p).

(5)

We consider the LPV representation (
3
) to be a so-called global LPV embedding of (
1
) if in addition, we can construct a so-called
scheduling map

\eta:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}
,

p(t)=\eta(x(t),u(t)),

(6)

such that

\displaystyle f(x,u)

\displaystyle=A(\eta(x,u))x+B(\eta(x,u))u,

(7a)

\displaystyle h(x,u)

\displaystyle=C(\eta(x,u))x+D(\eta(x,u))u,

(7b)

for all
(x,u)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}
. This gives that
\eta(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U})\subseteq\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{p}}}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is often chosen be a compact convex set, if the model is further utilized for analysis or control synthesis.
Consequently, the behavior of the nonlinear system is included (embedded) in the behavior of the LPV system, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\text{LPV}}
. In the next section, we will discuss the proposed method to achieve this objective.

3
Method

To achieve our objective of automatically converting the nonlinear system description (
1
) into a global LPV embedding given by (
3
) with
p(t)=\eta(x(t),u(t))
, we leverage the
Second Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
(FTC)
(
Apostol 1980
)
for function factorization as proposed in
(
Koelewijn 2023
, Appendix C.1)
.

Consider a function
g(z)=[g_{1}(z)\ \cdots\ g_{m}(z)]^{\top}
where
g_{i}(z):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
for
i=1,\dots,m
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For
z,\,z_{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{n}}
, we define the auxiliary function

\bar{g}_{i}(\lambda)=g_{i}(z_{\ast}+\lambda(z-z_{\ast})),

(8)

for
i=1,\dots,m
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Then by the FTC, we have that

\bar{g}_{i}(1)-\bar{g}_{i}(0)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d\bar{g}_{i}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)\,d\lambda,

(9)

and using the relations
\bar{g}_{i}(1)=g_{i}(z)
and
\bar{g}_{i}(0)=g_{i}(z_{\ast})
, obtained by evaluating (
8
) at
\lambda=\{0,1\}
, it follows that

g_{i}(z)-g_{i}(z_{\ast})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d\bar{g}_{i}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)\,d\lambda.

(10)

Now, by taking the chain rule of the integrand in (
10
):

\frac{d\bar{g}_{i}}{d\lambda}=\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial z}\frac{d\bar{g}_{i}}{d\lambda}=\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial z}(z_{\ast}+\lambda(z-z_{\ast}))(z-z_{\ast}),

(11)

where
\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial z}
is the transpose of the gradient of
g_{i}
, and by substituting (
11
) into (
10
), we end up with

g_{i}(z)-g_{i}(z_{\ast})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial z}(z_{\ast}+\lambda(z-z_{\ast}))\,d\lambda\,(z-z_{\ast}),

(12)

for
i=1,\dots,m
. Then, combining the elements,
g
is expressed as

\displaystyle g(z)-g(z_{\ast})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}(z)-g_{1}(z_{\ast})\\
\vdots\\
g_{m}(z)-g_{m}(z_{\ast})\end{bmatrix};

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial g_{1}}{\partial z}(z_{\ast}+\lambda(z-z_{\ast}))\,d\lambda\,(z-z_{\ast})\\
\vdots\\
\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial g_{m}}{\partial z}(z_{\ast}+\lambda(z-z_{\ast}))\,d\lambda\,(z-z_{\ast})\end{bmatrix};

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial g}{\partial z}(z_{\ast}+\lambda(z-z_{\ast}))\,d\lambda\,(z-z_{\ast}),

(13)

where
\frac{\partial g}{\partial z}(z_{\ast}+\lambda(z-z_{\ast}))\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the Jacobian of
g
evaluated at
z_{\ast}+\lambda(z-z_{\ast})
and the integral of the Jacobian is taken element-wise.

Now, we can apply (
3
) to factorize the functions
f
and
h
in (
1
) as follows:

\displaystyle f(x,u)-f(\bar{x},\bar{u})

\displaystyle=\bar{A}(x,u)(x-\bar{x})+\bar{B}(x,u)(u-\bar{u});

(14a)

\displaystyle h(x,u)-h(\bar{x},\bar{u})

\displaystyle=\bar{C}(x,u)(x-\bar{x})+\bar{D}(x,u)(u-\bar{u}),

(14b)

where

\displaystyle\bar{A}(x,u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\left(\bar{x}+\lambda(x-\bar{x}),\bar{u}+\lambda(u-\bar{u})\right)\,d\lambda;

\displaystyle\bar{B}(x,u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\left(\bar{x}+\lambda(x-\bar{x}),\bar{u}+\lambda(u-\bar{u})\right)\,d\lambda;

\displaystyle\bar{C}(x,u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\left(\bar{x}+\lambda(x-\bar{x}),\bar{u}+\lambda(u-\bar{u})\right)\,d\lambda;

\displaystyle\bar{D}(x,u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial h}{\partial u}\left(\bar{x}+\lambda(x-\bar{x}),\bar{u}+\lambda(u-\bar{u})\right)\,d\lambda.

Moreover, it is often the case that
f(0,0)=0
and
h(0,0)=0
, i.e., that the origin is an equilibrium point of the system. Otherwise, we can perform a coordinate transformation to ensure this property. In these cases, we can further simplify (
14
) by taking
\bar{x}=0
and
\bar{u}=0
, resulting in:

\displaystyle f(x,u)

\displaystyle=\bar{A}(x,u)x+\bar{B}(x,u)u,

(15a)

\displaystyle h(x,u)

\displaystyle=\bar{C}(x,u)x+\bar{D}(x,u)u,

(15b)

where

\displaystyle\bar{A}(x,u)\hskip-1.0pt

\displaystyle=\hskip-2.0pt\int_{0}^{1}\!\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(\lambda x,\lambda u)\,d\lambda,\ \bar{B}(x,u)=\hskip-2.0pt\int_{0}^{1}\!\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}(\lambda x,\lambda u)\,d\lambda,

\displaystyle\bar{C}(x,u)\hskip-1.0pt

\displaystyle=\hskip-2.0pt\int_{0}^{1}\!\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}(\lambda x,\lambda u)\,d\lambda,\ \bar{D}(x,u)=\hskip-2.0pt\int_{0}^{1}\!\frac{\partial h}{\partial u}(\lambda x,\lambda u)\,d\lambda.

To obtain an LPV form (
3
) for a given factorization of
f
and
h
in (
15
), a
scheduling map
\eta
in terms of (
6
) is constructed
such that the matrix functions
A,B,C,D
resulting from (
7
) via

\displaystyle A(\overbrace{\eta(x,u)}^{p})

\displaystyle=\bar{A}(x,u),

\displaystyle B(\overbrace{\eta(x,u)}^{p})

\displaystyle=\bar{B}(x,u),

(16)

\displaystyle D(\underbrace{\eta(x,u)}_{p})

\displaystyle=\bar{C}(x,u),

\displaystyle D(\underbrace{\eta(x,u)}_{p})

\displaystyle=\bar{D}(x,u).

have a
desired type of scheduling dependency, e.g., affine, polynomial, or rational. An LPV representation with an affine scheduling dependency can for example be obtained by choosing
\eta
to contain the nonlinear elements of the matrix functions
\bar{A},\,\bar{B},\,\bar{C}
, and
\bar{D}
.

Through this process, the nonlinear system represented by (
1
) is directly embedded into a global LPV model of the form (
3
) with (
6
) satisfying (
7
) via (
15
) and (
16
) and hence achieving
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\text{LPV}}
.
An important property of factorization (
15
), compared to other conversion methods, is that there is no approximation involved; hence, embedding of the complete nonlinear behavior
\mathcal{B}
is guaranteed on the target sets
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}
. Also, no decisions are required to be made that which functional terms are split or substituted in which order as in other conversion concepts (e.g.,
Kwiatkowski et al. 2006
). Furthermore, the factorization (
15
), i.e., the matrix functions
\bar{A},\ldots,\bar{D}
are well defined for
x=0
and
u=0
, because

\lim_{(x,u)\rightarrow(0,0)}\bar{A}(x,u)=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{\tau}\!\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(\lambda x,\lambda u)\,d\lambda=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(0,0),

which exists for all
\bar{A},\ldots,\bar{D}
as
f,h\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

In addition to the advantages of the embedding process, a clear price to pay for automatic conversion is that the required information for the scheduling map
\eta
cannot be controlled during the conversion. For real-world applications, the matrix functions
\bar{A},\ldots,\bar{D}
often only depend on certain elements of
x
and
u
, therefore only those elements are required for the scheduling map
\eta
. This will also be exemplified in Section
5
. On the other hand, mathematically it is possible that the entire
(x,u)
vector is required to compute the resulting
p
. Furthermore, the resulting dimension of
p
, that is,
n_{\mathrm{p}}
, depends on the chosen dependency class of
A,\ldots,B
. The more simplistic dependence, e.g., affine, is chosen for these matrix functions, the more complex and higher dimensional the resulting scheduling map
\eta
becomes. These disadvantages can be efficiently mitigated by recently introduced scheduling reduction techniques, such as
Koelewijn and Tóth 2020
;
Rizvi et al. 2016
;
Rizvi et al. 2018
, which can often provide dramatic reduction of
n_{\mathrm{p}}
without a significant loss of accuracy of the representation; see
Olucha et al. 2024
for a comparative study.

5
Examples

In this section, the capabilities of the proposed LPV embedding method are demonstrated through two examples. The first example involves a simple nonlinear model of an unbalanced disk. This example is used to analyze the LPV model obtained with the proposed approach, evaluate the accuracy of the LPV embedding via time-domain simulations, and to design an LPV controller using the obtained model. The second example considers a more complex system to assess the scalability of the proposed method. Specifically, the Quanser 3DOF
control moment gyroscope
(CMG) is considered, and the performance of the method is demonstrated through time-domain simulations.

3DOF control moment gyroscope

The 3DOF CMG consists of three actuated gimbals and a flydisk mounted on the inner gimbal, as illustrated in Fig.
7
.

Figure 7:
Schematics of the Quanser 3DOF control moment gyroscope, where the angular positions are indicated by
q_{i}
and the motor inputs by
i_{i}

The nonlinear dynamics that describe the CMG are presented in
(
Bloemers and Tóth 2019
)
, resulting in the differential equation

M(q(t))\ddot{q}(t)+\left(C\left(q(t),\dot{q}(t)\right)+F_{\mathrm{v}}\right)\dot{q}(t)=K_{\mathrm{m}}u(t),

(23)

where
t\in\mathbb{R}
is time,
q(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
are the angular positions of the gimbals,
i(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
are the input currents to the motors,
M
is the inertia matrix,
C
is the Coriolis matrix,
F_{\mathrm{v}}
is the viscous friction matrix, and
K_{\mathrm{m}}
is the motor gain matrix. For this example, we consider that the third gimbal is locked, i.e.
q_{3},\ \dot{q}_{3}\equiv 0
and only the first two gimbals are actuated, i.e.
i_{3},\ i_{4}\equiv 0
. As outputs of the system, we consider the angular position of the unlocked gimbals,
y=\operatorname{col}\left(q_{1},q_{2},q_{4}\right)
. Then, the dynamics of the CMG system can be represented by a continuous-time nonlinear model of the form of (
1
), where
x(t)=\operatorname{col}\left(q_{1},q_{2},q_{4},\dot{q}_{1},\dot{q}_{2},\dot{q}_{4}\right)(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
,
u(t)=\operatorname{col}\left(i_{1},i_{2}\right)(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
y(t)=\operatorname{col}\left(q_{1},q_{2},q_{4}\right)(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Now, two LPV embeddings
\Omega_{\mathrm{LPV}}^{\mathrm{ana}}
and
\Omega_{\mathrm{LPV}}^{\mathrm{num}}
of the CMG model are obtained using
nlss2lpvss
with the respective options
'
analytical
'
and
'
numerical
'
, and both using
'
factor
'
. In this case, while

has
n_{\mathrm{p}}=15
scheduling variables,
\Omega_{\mathrm{LPV}}^{\mathrm{num}}
contains only
n_{\mathrm{p}}=13
. Note that this difference in scheduling dimensions is caused by the extraction process of the nonlinear elements from the matrix functions of the system, but both LPV models should be equivalent. Moreover, if required for any downstream tasks (such as controller synthesis), the scheduling dimension can be reduced using the LPV-SS scheduling dimension reduction methods present in
LPVcore
. To verify that the produced LPV models are equivalent to the original CMG model, we compare the simulation of the CMG model with the self-scheduled simulation of

and
\Omega_{\mathrm{LPV}}^{\mathrm{num}}

6
Conclusion

This paper has introduced an automated LPV model conversion approach for nonlinear dynamical systems
together with discussing its implementation in the
LPVcore
toolbox and demonstrating its capabilities via simulation and controller design examples. Compared to other LPV model conversion approaches, the key difference is that the proposed method achieves an automated LPV embedding without any approximation or need for exploring combinatorial options in the substitution or conversion of nonlinear terms, providing a readily usable solution for practical LPV modeling problems. The
LPVcore
toolbox is freely available on
www.lpvcore.net
, and the examples used in Section
5
can be found in
https://gitlab.com/Javi-Olucha/lpvs25-code-repo
.
