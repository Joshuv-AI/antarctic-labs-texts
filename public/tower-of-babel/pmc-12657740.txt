Global need, regional action: opportunities to improve cancer survivorship care in the Western Pacific
Authors: Michael Jefford, Fiona Crawford-Williams, Carolyn Taylor, Miyako Takahashi, Wendy WT Lam, Alexandre Chan, Bogda Koczwara, Yan Lou, Raymond J Chan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe Western Pacific region faces a significant cancer burden with over 12.8 million cancer survivors. Cancer survivorship care remains inconsistent across the region but is important regardless of diversity in healthcare systems and resources within countries. Cultural factors deeply influence survivorship experiences; thus, recognition of the importance of traditional medicine and the role of community-based support in survivorship care is needed. In this viewpoint, we highlight opportunities for regional collaboration to improve cancer survivorship outcomes, and advocate for incorporation of survivorship priorities into national cancer control plans. We call for coordinated action involving international organizations, local governments, and cancer survivor voices to deliver responsive, equitable survivorship care. Education, policy reform, and multinational partnerships are essential to address disparities and improve outcomes across the region.Keywords: Cancer survivorship, Health equity, Multinational partnership, Cancer care

Introduction

The Western Pacific region is one of six World Health Organization (WHO) regions and spans a vast geographic area from China in the north to New Zealand in the south.1 The region represents 1.9 billion people (approximately a quarter of the world's population) across 37 countries with extremely diverse sizes, geography, and population, with differing wealth, development, political and cultural characteristics.1,2 Despite these differences, many of these countries are connected through regional cooperation and organizations such as the Asia-Pacific Economic Partnership and the Pacific Islands Forum, working on trade, economic, environmental, and healthcare issues. Further details are explored in a recent review of cancer survivorship care in the region.2

In 2022, the region saw an estimated 6.8 million new cancer cases, accounting for 36% of the world's cancer burden.3 However, cancer prevalence varies significantly across the region. Cancer prevalence is impacted by factors such as the level of economic development within the country, healthcare resourcing, lifestyle and environmental factors.3 High income countries (HICs) may report higher cancer incidence and prevalence due to ageing populations, the availability of cancer screening programs, and a range of effective anti-cancer treatments.3 While not all countries in the region are able to accurately report cancer prevalence data, the number of cancer survivors is substantial across the region, with over 12.8 million people having a personal history of cancer.3 Regional collaboration, such as initiatives of the World Health Organization (WHO) and partnerships with international cancer organizations, support improved cancer control in the Western Pacific, including through vaccination and screening programs, capacity-building in oncology care, and support of cancer registries.

Cancer survivorship in the Western Pacific

Cancer survivorship, and particularly care after completion of cancer treatments, varies widely across the region. A review of cancer survivors’ experiences and models of cancer survivorship care in the Western Pacific has explored current practices and challenges in the diverse countries of the region.2 Survivors face disruptions in employment, social relationships, and daily functioning, regardless of their economic context.3,4 Additionally, lack of awareness about survivorship needs prevents access to appropriate care and reintegration into society.5,6 However, certain survivorship issues may be more evident in HIC settings. For example, while HICs generally offer more advanced treatments and greater access to psychosocial care, survivors encounter complex healthcare systems that are fragmented and difficult to navigate.5 Inadequate insurance coverage, high out-of-pocket costs, and disparities in access to specialized survivorship programs can create barriers even in resource-rich settings, especially for individuals with lower socioeconomic position.7,8 For cancer survivors in LMICs, cancer survivorship care is often lacking, as the focus of health systems is on diagnostic and treatment services and there are higher rates of cancer mortality.6 Accordingly, public health efforts have tended to focus on screening and palliative care services. While cancer survivors in the Western Pacific experience similar unmet needs, the evidence supporting survivorship care practices is limited to a few select countries—predominantly Australia, China and Japan.9,10

Despite the varying focuses between HICs and LMICs, cancer survivorship should be recognized as an important public health issue. For example, the ability to return to work and community life after cancer treatment, and to contribute to a productive society, is critical in any country. Further, a focus on quality survivorship care which includes appropriate surveillance and prevention of recurrence or secondary cancers, and effective management of long-term symptoms and comorbid illness can result in savings to individuals, communities, and the country's healthcare system.11

Cultural influences on cancer survivorship

Across the region, growing evidence underscores the significant role of culture, both in shaping cancer survivorship experiences, but also designing appropriate services. Survivors' understanding of illness, willingness to disclose a cancer diagnosis, use of traditional medicine, and access to support are all influenced by cultural norms, language, and beliefs. One example is the high use (>80%) of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), including Chinese herbs, acupuncture-moxibustion and Taiji-Qigong, among some Chinese cancer survivors.12,13 The role of culture in shaping cancer survivorship experiences was also reported in the ACTION Study, conducted across eight Southeast Asian countries, which found high levels of psychological distress among survivors were often unaddressed due to stigma, limited psychosocial services, and cultural norms discouraging emotional disclosure.14 Similarly, a systematic review of Indigenous survivorship in Australia, New Zealand, and the Pacific highlighted the need for models of care that respect traditional healing, community roles, and the intergenerational nature of care.15 As survivorship care and research often addresses the broader lived realities of individuals within society, this evidence suggests that policy makers and healthcare providers should move beyond a purely medical model and adopt a social ecological perspective. This shift requires careful attention to the voices of survivors and a commitment to effective health programs that include the perspectives of survivors, service providers and policy makers in government within each country's unique context.

Programs that integrate cultural context, through community engagement, language adaptation, and recognition of local values, are more likely to be effective. In Australia, for example, culturally tailored survivorship resources co-developed with Vietnamese, Arabic, and Italian communities were shown to improve understanding and engagement.16 The Stronger Together program in Vietnam is another example where peer support was adapted using a cultural framework to reflect regional dialects, karmic beliefs, and environmental realities.17 Nursing leaders in the region have called for greater investment in cultural competence training to address survivorship disparities.18 Together, this evidence supports the need for culturally responsive, survivor-informed models that strengthen health system responsiveness and equity across diverse contexts. Creating opportunities to exchange survivorship practices across countries with similar cultures and health care system contexts in the Western Pacific would foster more culturally grounded and responsive models of care.

Regional collaboration and innovation

Much of the innovation in survivorship care in the Western Pacific has stemmed from partnerships between academic institutions in HICs and local governments or health ministries in LMICs.19 These collaborations have been essential in providing the expertise needed to implement effective cancer control programs. For example, the WHO's Packages of Interventions for Rehabilitation provides evidence-based resources for 20 health conditions, including cancer,20 which has been endorsed by the Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer (MASCC) to encourage widespread adoption and integration into routine cancer care.21 As WHO and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) expand their global work in areas such as rehabilitation, there are opportunities for significant regional collaborations that advance cancer survivorship care and address local needs. Raising awareness of the WHO rehabilitation packages is an initial step before measuring the proportion of facilities implementing these interventions across the region to provide a basis for monitoring and continuous improvement.

It is also important that international oncology organizations, such as MASCC, UICC, the American Society of Clinical Oncology, European Society of Medical Oncology, International Psycho-Oncology Society, and International Society of Nurses in Cancer Care are engaged to support comprehensive survivorship care that aligns with the Institute of Medicine (IoM) report22 and Quality of Cancer Survivorship Care Framework.11 MASCC recently launched its Supportive Care 2030 Movement, which outlined a set of shared global ambition statements (13 statements and 2 sub-statements).23 Although these statements focus on supportive care as a broader concept than cancer survivorship, the ambitions (if realized) will assist countries to establish a clear strategic direction that will result in improved cancer survivorship care systems. These statements are available in 15 languages and outline a shared aspiration in areas including but not limited to: comprehensive survivorship care; self-management support; routine psychosocial screening; and financial impact minimization.23 There is an opportunity to leverage such frameworks to improve cancer survivorship efforts across the Western Pacific region, while responding to local challenges which include limited resources, diverse funding systems and limited workforce capacity.

Acknowledging the small workforce is particularly crucial in isolated locations such as the Pacific Islands. For this reason, survivorship care and education could be embedded in cancer care programs through education of all primary care providers, all nurses, or even all healthcare providers. Given the diversity of languages, cultures, and healthcare systems across the Western Pacific, it is crucial that organizations pursuing regional collaboration work closely with local hospitals, clinics, and health services to adapt and translate evidence-based survivorship recommendations and guidelines to ensure cultural fit.17

Policy and system-level opportunities

There are already examples of excellence in survivorship reform such as effective peer support programs in Southeast Asia and established survivorship programs in Australia, China, and Japan. However, there is a lack of greater regional collaboration, development of local leadership, and partnership with political agencies. There are many opportunities for collaborative work, such as defining professional roles, shaping policy and advocacy efforts, improving payment systems, and enhancing social care infrastructure. Recent calls have advocated for the formation of regional partnerships to enhance survivorship and palliative care in LMICs through collaboration among professional societies, governmental bodies, and non-government organizations.24 One example of effective collaboration in cancer survivorship is the AC2 project, supported by funding from the National Foundation for Australia-China Relations, which established a collaborative team representing the Chinese diaspora in the region to improve cancer survivorship outcomes for the Chinese population in the Asia-Pacific region.25 In October 2024, the inaugural AC2 leadership forum was convened in Hong Kong SAR, China. This forum facilitated the establishment of shared priorities for cancer survivorship tailored to Chinese communities. Key deliverables included: development and sharing of patient educational materials in relevant languages (English, Traditional and Simplified Chinese); webinars for health professionals on key survivorship issues provided free-of-charge and on-demand—shared on platforms that are conducive for Chinese populations across Greater China, and in Australia; and a Roadmap that outlines short-term and longer-term goals for practice development, research and education. This initiative serves as a model for global collaboration in addressing critical gaps in cancer care.

Recent reviews have found that many countries in the region do not have comprehensive national cancer control plans (NCCP), and that few countries globally have an NCCP that focuses on survivorship, with fewer still that consider psychosocial aspects in the post-treatment phase.26,27 However, several countries (e.g. Japan, South Korea, The Philippines, and China) have considered how to provide more effective survivorship care at a national level by focusing on survivorship within their NCCPs, which includes efforts to support return to work, rehabilitation, financial needs, long-term care, nutrition, psycho-oncology and peer support.28

Survivorship content within NCCPs could be strengthened by being more specific with stated targets and supported with adequate resources (including staff and money) for effective implementation. Different countries will have different priorities but may choose to focus on key aspects of survivorship care such as support to return to work, health promotion (e.g. tobacco cessation), and more systematic assessment and management of common physical and psychosocial consequences of cancer and treatment. We recommend that countries should consider how their NCCP can systematically and contextually develop goals, key performance indicators, and timelines against recommendations from the IoM report, MASCC Supportive Care 2030 Ambitions, and the WHO Package of Interventions for Rehabilitation.20, 21, 22, 23 There are significant opportunities for regional collaboration to support enhanced survivorship care by supporting the inclusion of realistic, achievable goals within NCCPs. A major reason that all countries should focus more on cancer survivorship is an economic one, particularly focusing on supporting survivors to return to work. Employment supports financial independence, reduces reliance on welfare, and contributes income tax to governments. Returning to work not only allows cancer survivors to find value as an individual in society but also fosters a sense of social belonging essential for achieving full recovery from cancer. A recent systematic review identified factors that influence the likelihood of returning to work: intrapersonal, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy-level factors.29 Among interpersonal influences, attitudes toward work can either facilitate or hinder this process and may be shaped by cultural context. Programs that address this wide spectrum of factors tend to be more effective. An example from within the region comes from Japan where ‘supporting working survivors’ has been included in the NCCP since 2012, advocating that treating physicians ask patients about their working status and encourage them to continue working.26 Pharmacists and nurses interview patients about the impact of treatment side effects on their capacity to work. Medical social workers can inform them of various support systems, and rehabilitation specialists can provide support that is tailored to the person's workplace activities. It is important that providing survivorship care is considered as part of healthcare providers' main duties and is reflected in the evaluation of their work. It is also important that survivorship care be reimbursed within the healthcare system of each country. For example, in Japan, physicians' sharing treatment information with a survivor's employer, and discussing a return-to-work plan is reimbursed under the universal health insurance system.

Community-based survivorship programs are examples of care models which are potentially feasible, especially in resource-limited settings. One promising model for expanding psychosocial and practical support for cancer survivors is Stronger Together, a peer mentoring program developed through a partnership between Global Focus on Cancer, the Vietnam National Cancer Institute and Vietnamese cancer survivors themselves.30 The program trains people with lived experience of cancer to become peer mentors, who are matched to newly diagnosed individuals based on age, cancer type, and treatment experience. This program addresses critical gaps in psychosocial care in low-resource settings where professional support services are limited or unavailable. A randomized controlled trial conducted across four oncology hospitals in Vietnam found that participants who received peer mentoring reported significantly reduced symptoms of depression and anxiety, higher self-efficacy, and improved emotional and functional well-being, compared to those receiving usual care.30 The model is now being adapted for use in Australia among Chinese and Vietnamese immigrant populations, and will soon be introduced in Malaysia, demonstrating its flexibility and relevance across different health systems, cultures and contexts. By centering on lived experience, Stronger Together offers a scalable complement to formal survivorship care, that is culturally grounded and can address local or Indigenous health beliefs. Peer support programs such as this are cost-effective, rapidly deployable, and can help bridge system-level gaps in both LMICs and HICs.31,32 There is an opportunity to continue to expand peer support programs like Stronger Together to other countries across the region.

Conclusion

To drive meaningful change in survivorship care across the Western Pacific region, a coordinated call to action is urgently needed. First, awareness of survivorship issues must be amplified through platforms led by WHO, UICC, MASCC, and national agencies so that policy and public discourse reflects the lived realities of survivors. This includes incorporation of survivorship care and survivorship priorities in NCCPs detailing benefits for individuals and society at large. Developing a clear regional blueprint that engages international peak bodies, and national champions, will reduce disparities in quality survivorship care. Second, education and training in survivorship care should be embedded across the health and social care workforce to ensure a holistic and coordinated response. While standard didactic educational efforts may maximize program accessibility, clinical/research fellowships and exchange programs offered by centers of excellence in the region can prepare emerging leaders who can then be mobilized to function as local champions for survivorship care improvements in a sustainable manner. Finally, effective multinational collaboration must be strengthened, leveraging academic, civil society, and governmental partnerships to develop, adapt, and scale culturally relevant models of care. These actions must not only reflect regional diversity but actively include people with lived experience to ensure they are contextually appropriate, feasible, and sustainable. Through shared commitment, practical action, and regional learning, survivorship care in the Western Pacific can be strengthened to meet growing needs and reduce inequities.
