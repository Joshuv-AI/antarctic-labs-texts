Tau‐associated neuronal loss in the intermediate nucleus of the human hypothalamus, a putative VLPO analog, in progressive supranuclear palsy and Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Shima Rastegar‐Pouyani, Caroline Lew, Felipe L. Pereira, Abhijit Satpati, Ana Moreno Arnas, Vitor Paes, Renata Elaine Paraízo Leite, Claudia Kimie Suemoto, Salvatore Spina, Gowoon Son, Helmut Heinsen, William W. Seeley, Christine M. Walsh, Thomas C. Neylan, Lea T. Grinberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDSleep phenotypes differ in progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) and Alzheimer's disease (AD). The human intermediate nucleus (IntN), a putative ventrolateral preoptic analog, promotes non‐rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, but its disease‐specific vulnerability is unclear.METHODS Post mortem hypothalami (n = 30; baseline [Braak stage I−II] n = 6; PSP n = 9; Braak III–IV n = 4; Braak V‐VI n = 11) underwent marker‐guided IntN delineation, galanin/phospho‐tau (T231) immunohistochemistry, and stereology.RESULTSAdvanced PSP showed profound IntN degeneration (84% neuron reduction vs baseline). In AD neuropathologic change, IntN neuronal loss and phospho‐tau burden were greater in higher Braak stages and preferentially affected galanin‐positive neurons (≈77% reduction in late AD); phospho‐tau burden was higher in galanin‐positive neurons.CONCLUSIONSThe IntN is a disease‐sensitive node, with near‐ablation in PSP and progressive, preferential loss of detectable galaninergic neurons in AD. Although longitudinal observations and premortem sleep/wake measurements were not available for this sample, these findings are consistent with disease‐level differences in NREM/ slow‐wave sleep disturbance reported in PSP and AD.

BACKGROUND

Sleep disturbances are common across neurodegenerative diseases and often exhibit disease‐specific profiles. 1 Although polysomnography/EEG studies are limited by laboratory adaptation effects and recording feasibility in dementia, they suggest divergent sleep phenotypes in progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) and Alzheimer's disease (AD). PSP features severe insomnia‐like symptoms with difficulty initiating and maintaining sleep, hyperarousal, and reduced sleep drive, typically without compensatory daytime sleepiness. 2 , 3 In contrast, patients with typical (amnestic) AD, in which memory impairment is the initial and dominant feature, frequently exhibit fragmented sleep, reduced sleep efficiency, increased wake after sleep onset, and marked slow‐wave sleep (SWS/N3) loss, a component of non‐rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, with relatively preserved REM sleep. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 A smaller subset of AD cases with non‐amnestic clinical presentations, often referred to as atypical AD, may show relatively preserved SWS, although REM sleep can be reduced. 6 , 8

The intermediate nucleus (IntN) of the preoptic/anterior hypothalamus is widely regarded as the human analog of the rodent ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO). 9 , 10 In rodents, VLPO neurons – particularly galanin‐expressing populations 11 , 12 – are critical for the initiation and maintenance of NREM sleep, including slow‐wave sleep (SWS), through inhibitory control of wake‐promoting systems. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18

Human studies provide the rationale for focusing on IntN galanin neurons. Garcia‐Falgueras et al. used galanin immunoreactivity to delineate the human IntN and reported variation in galanin‐neuron number with age and sex. 19 Lim et al. studied an older autopsy cohort with premortem actigraphy and found that fewer surviving galanin‐immunoreactive IntN/VLPO neurons were associated with greater sleep fragmentation and reduced sustained rest; they also reported marked depletion of these neurons in AD compared with non‐AD subjects. 20 However, prior studies did not determine whether IntN neuronal loss in AD was associated with phospho‐tau accumulation, did not quantify tau burden separately in galanin‐positive and galanin‐negative neurons, and did not examine PSP. Thus, the pathologic mechanism underlying IntN neuronal loss in AD and the status of the IntN in PSP remain unresolved.

In this study, we used unbiased stereology and double‐labeled immunohistochemistry on thick (30‐µm) sections from prospectively sampled hypothalami to examine phospho‐tau‐associated neuronal changes in the IntN across baseline, AD neuropathologic change (ADNC), and PSP. Primary analyses compared total neuronal number, galanin‐defined neuronal subpopulations, and neuronal phospho‐tau burden across disease groups. Within ADNC, Braak‐stage analyses were used to assess whether IntN involvement varied with AD neuropathologic severity. Although cases at Braak stage V–VI presented either with amnestic or non‐amnestic syndromes (atypical AD), because no Braak III–IV case met the criteria for an atypical clinical presentation and the Braak V–VI phenotype subgroups were small, phenotype‐stratified analyses were considered exploratory and are presented descriptively. IntN volume was analyzed as a secondary descriptive measure.

We pre‐specified three testable predictions: (1) between‐disease differences: PSP would show greater reductions in galanin‐positive neuron counts than AD and baseline, consistent with its profound SWS failure; (2) within‐AD selectivity: in AD, galanin‐positive neurons would be more vulnerable than galanin‐negative neurons; and (3) tau pathology: phospho‐tau would accumulate in both galanin‐positive and galanin‐negative neurons in both diseases. Together, these analyses address whether the IntN shows phospho‐tau‐associated neuronal vulnerability in PSP and ADNC and provide a neuropathologic framework for future studies that integrate post mortem IntN pathology with premortem sleep/wake measurements.

All specimens were de‐identified; the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) Institutional Review Board determined that this work did not constitute human subject research. Post mortem hypothalami were obtained from the Neurodegenerative Disease Brain Bank (UCSF) and the Brazilian Biobank for Aging Studies (University of São Paulo). Neuropathological evaluations followed established international protocols and diagnostic criteria. 25 , 26

Inclusion criteria required participants to be over 50 years of age, with no history of neurological or primary psychiatric disorders unrelated to the main diagnosis. The following neuropathological diagnostic categories were included: (1) ADNC 27 : any level of pathology, provided no other primary or contributing neuropathological diagnosis was present. AD cases were categorized as mid‐stage Braak (Braak stage III or IV) or late‐stage Braak (Braak stage V or VI). 28 Clinical phenotypes (typical vs atypical, e.g., posterior cortical atrophy [PCA], logopenic variant primary progressive aphasia [lvPPA], and corticobasal syndrome [CBS]) were adjudicated by experienced raters who were blinded to the histological measurements to minimize bias. 29 , 30 No Braak III–IV case met the criteria for atypical presentations; (2) PSP: confirmed PSP pathology 31 with Braak stage for AD pathology ≤ IV and absence of other primary neurodegenerative diagnoses. Clinically, PSP cases presented as Richardson's syndrome or non‐fluent/agrammatic variant primary progressive aphasia (nfvPPA) and were obtained at an advanced disease stage. 2 , 29 We use “advanced PSP at death” to indicate late clinical disease after longitudinal follow‐up. ADNC co‐pathology in PSP was staged separately and reported at the individual case level in Table S1; and (3) baseline: defined as Braak stage 0–II, Thal phase 0–1, lack of major brain co‐pathology, and Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) ≤ 0.5. 32

The presence of Lewy body pathology was an exclusion criterion, except for cases where Lewy bodies were confined to the amygdala (amygdala‐predominant), 33 as we examined the whole extent of the hypothalamus using immunohistochemistry against alpha synuclein and found no evidence of positivity in the hypothalamic axis (data not shown). Any kind of transactive response DNA‐binding protein 43 proteinopathy was also an exclusion criterion.

Clinical diagnoses were available for all cases, and functional cognitive levels were assessed using the CDR scale, reflecting cognitive status within 3 months prior to death. 32 Group‐wise demographic, clinical, and neuropathologic characteristics are summarized in Table 1, and individual case‐level data, including age at death, clinical diagnosis, bank/site of origin for each case, Braak stage, Thal phase, CDR/CDR‐SoB, and stereological measures, are provided in Table S1.

Study‐specific tissue processing and immunohistochemistry

Previous studies highlighted the difficulty in consistently delineating the IntN in Nissl‐stained human hypothalami. 11 , 37 To address this, we combined cytoarchitectonic and immunohistochemical approaches. First, we prospectively collected hypothalamic blocks in total (left and right sides separated), dissected from the brain before any slabbing to ensure the whole extent of the IntN and landmarks were present. Ten percent neutral buffered formalin‐fixed hypothalamic blocks encompassing the entire IntN were embedded in celloidin, serially sectioned at 30‐µm thickness, and collected in coronal or horizontal planes. 38 , 39

Before quantitative stereology, we first confirmed that the IntN could be reproducibly identified in the available material using pre‐specified cytoarchitectonic and anatomical criteria. The IntN was accepted as identifiable when a compact but three‐dimensionally irregular cluster of medium‐sized, round neurons was present in the preoptic/anterior hypothalamus, medial to the supraoptic nucleus, lateral to the paraventricular/periventricular region, and dorsal to the optic chiasm. Criteria included preserved regional landmarks, gallocyanin/Nissl‐defined neuronal morphology, continuity across serial sections and optical depth, and concordant galanin enrichment when the neuronal population was not severely depleted. Representative examples of these cytoarchitectonic criteria and the resulting Microbrightfield/Stereo Investigator ROI contour are shown in Figure S1.

Using these pre‐specified criteria, the IntN was delineated section by section in Stereo Investigator using a marker‐guided approach that combined galanin immunoreactivity, gallocyanin/Nissl cytoarchitecture, and established anatomical landmarks, including the third ventricle, optic chiasm, supraoptic nucleus, and paraventricular/periventricular region. Following established definitions, 11 , 20 , 40 the IntN was localized as a cluster of medium‐sized, round neurons within the preoptic/anterior hypothalamus (Figure S1). The ROI was defined as an anatomically guided, case‐ and section‐specific irregular contour rather than a predefined geometric sampling box. Contiguous marginal neurons were included when cytoarchitecture, marker profile, and spatial continuity supported assignment to the IntN, whereas isolated galanin‐immunoreactive neurons in the broader preoptic region were not assigned to the IntN solely on the basis of galanin positivity.

In PSP cases, zero counts were assigned only when no IntN neuronal cluster could be identified in serial gallocyanin‐counterstained sections at the expected anatomical location despite preserved regional landmarks, including the supraoptic nucleus, and exhaustive screening across the full section series. The zero‐count rule was not based solely on absence of galanin immunoreactivity. Because other galanin‐positive neuronal clusters were present in the same preoptic area sections, each slide also provided an internal positive control for galanin staining. This approach was intended to distinguish profound regional degeneration from generalized staining failure or sampling error.

For immunohistochemical staining, free‐floating hypothalamic sections were treated with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol to quench endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by a 50‐min incubation at 97°C in 0.01 M citrate buffer containing 0.1% Triton X‐100 in 1X PBS (pH 6.0) for epitope retrieval. To minimize non‐specific staining, sections were blocked for 40 min in buffer containing 5% milk in PBST (0.1% Triton X). Serial sections were double stained overnight at room temperature with monoclonal anti‐galanin antibody (1:300, mouse, MAB5854, Biotechne R&D System, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and monoclonal anti‐tau phosphorylated at threonine 231 (1:3000, rabbit, ab151559, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). T231 tau was detected using a goat anti‐rabbit IgG secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP; 1:400; Advansta, San Jose, CA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by visualization with ImmPACT DAB Peroxidase Substrate Kit (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA). Galanin was detected using a conjugated goat anti‐mouse IgG polymer‐alkaline phosphatase secondary antibody (MACH 2 Mouse AP‐Polymer; Biocare Medical, Pacheco, CA, USA), followed by visualization with Vector Red Alkaline Phosphatase Substrate Kit (Vector Laboratories). Sections were counterstained with gallocyanin (Alfa Aesar, Ward Hill, MA, USA) at an optimized pH of 2.1. Gallocyanin was used as a progressive Nissl stain that binds stoichiometrically to nucleic acids; excess stain can be washed out, producing stable visualization of Nissl substance, neuronal morphology, and nucleoli. This counterstain was intentionally retained because the study quantified total neurons, galanin‐positive neurons, and galanin‐negative neurons within the IntN, requiring visualization of the full neuronal population rather than galanin‐immunoreactive neurons alone. 12 , 19

Single‐primary, no‐primary, and chromogen‐order controls were performed for each immunohistochemical run. These controls confirmed absence of non‐specific secondary/chromogen signal and absence of detectable cross‐reactivity between the anti‐rabbit HRP/DAB and anti‐mouse AP/Vector Red detection systems. 41 All primary antibodies were lot‐consistent across cases. The anti‐galanin antibody was a commercial mouse monoclonal antibody raised against recombinant human galanin and validated by the manufacturer for human immunohistochemistry. Antibody performance for galanin was further assessed by the expected neuroanatomic distribution of staining in each section series. Internal positive controls included robust galanin‐immunoreactive neurons in the supraoptic nucleus consistent with published maps of galanin immunoreactivity in the human hypothalamus. We selected a phospho‐tau antibody directed at Thr231 because phosphorylation at T231 is a well‐described early event in the tau pre‐tangle cascade and disrupts tau–microtubule binding and shows good discrimination with controls. 42 Given the small, variably processed anterior hypothalamic region we studied, T231 provided more consistent detection while preserving sensitivity to early tau pathology. 43 Although PSP neuropathology includes both neuronal and glial tau lesions at the whole‐brain level, 31 within the IntN region of interest (ROI) the predominant T231‐positive profiles are mainly neuronal, with only rare tau‐positive glial elements. Because T231 is not disease‐specific, IntN phospho‐tau inclusions in PSP were not assigned individually to PSP‐type versus AD‐type tau pathology. In addition, we performed amyloid beta immunohistochemistry on formalin‐fixed, paraffin embedded sections containing the preoptic area from a different cohort of Braak stage 0, 2, and 6 cases, using the same protocol employed for routine diagnostic assessment in the brain bank, to assess whether plaques occur in the IntN and whether they are already present at early disease stages.

Unbiased stereology for estimating neuronal populations

We employed the optical fractionator probe in Stereo Investigator version 10 (MBF Bioscience, Williston, VT, USA) with live images captured via a high‐resolution camera attached to an Axio Imager.A2 microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, NY, USA) – a method not influenced by cutting plane and well established for human neuropathological assessments. 39

The ROI was traced under low magnification (5×) in each sampled section, yielding an anatomically defined irregular contour for the IntN. ROI delineation, neuronal classification/counting, and Cavalieri volume estimation were performed with clinical diagnosis and neuropathologic group masked to the investigators. The cytoarchitectonic basis for ROI placement and a representative Microbrightfield/Stereo Investigator contour is shown in Figure S1. Neuronal counts were then performed at 63× (oil immersion) within this traced ROI using the optical fractionator probe. Stereological parameters were determined using the resample–oversample method, 44 with a counting frame of 60 × 60 µm, a grid size of 120 × 120 µm, dissector height of 14 µm, and a guard zone of 6 µm. We calculated coefficients of error (CE) to ensure sampling precision, and values of ∼0.1 and under were considered acceptable (value for each participant on Table S1). Each neuron, identified by neuronal morphology on the gallocyanin counterstain within the traced IntN ROI, was classified by the presence of galanin expression and/or tau pathology in four groups (Figure 1). Throughout the manuscript, “galanin‐positive” and “galanin‐negative” refer to detectable galanin immunoreactivity at the time of death. Thus, “galanin‐negative” neurons include neurons lacking detectable galanin staining within the IntN ROI and may include neurons that previously expressed galanin but downregulated the peptide below immunohistochemical detection.

Statistical analyses

Demographic differences were analyzed using chi‐squared, one‐way ANOVA, Welch's ANOVA, or Kruskal‐Wallis tests, based on the data type, normality, and homogeneity of variance. Variations in neuronal counts and the proportions of neuronal subcategories were evaluated using the pairwise Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, with p values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini‐Hochberg method.

We fit mixed‐effects linear regression models to account for potential confounding from sex, age at death (AaD), and post mortem interval (PMI) (e.g., 𝑚0 = measure ∼ group + Sex + AaD + PMI + [1 | Subject]). Age and sex were included a priori because prior human IntN studies reported variation related to these factors. 20 , 45

Because eligible cases were rare, all available subjects were included in group comparisons. To assess the robustness of the findings, a post hoc bootstrap‐based sensitivity analysis (1000× resamples) was performed. For each measure, resampling was informed by the observed mean and standard deviation. The significance level was set at α = 0.05, and results were considered robust when the corresponding observed (post hoc) power in the bootstrap simulations was ≥80%.

Spearman correlation coefficients and corresponding p values were obtained using the “cor” function in base R and the “cor_pmat” function from the ggcorrplot package. Z‐scores were computed by subtracting the mean of the baseline group from each value and dividing by the baseline group standard deviation. To compare data exclusively from mid‐ and late‐AD cases as a function of Braak stage, we used a second mixed‐effects model that replaced the group term with Braak stage (e.g., m1=measure∼Braakstage+Sex+AaD+PMI+[1∣Subject]). All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Selective vulnerability of galaninergic‐positive versus negative neurons in IntN

Despite its small size, the IntN has a mixed neuronal population, of which about 40% are galanin‐positive. Galanin‐positive neurons have been implicated in SWS control in rodents. 13 , 17 Thus, we investigated whether the pattern of neuronal vulnerability of IntN to AD and PSP differed depending on whether the neuron synthesizes galanin. Reflecting the overall neuronal reduction, PSP cases exhibited statistically significant and powered decreases in both subneuronal categories: galanin‐positive neurons (−94.1%; 95% CI = −129.8% to −58.4%; p = 0.008; power = 97.7%) and galanin‐negative neurons (−78%; 95% CI = −111.5% to −44.5%; p = 0.009; power = 94.5%) compared to baseline (Figure 4A,B). Similarly, reductions in galanin‐positive and galanin‐negative neurons in AD cases paralleled the total neuronal count relative to baseline. Galanin‐positive neurons exhibited a more pronounced reduction, with a nominally significant but underpowered decrease of 51.3% in Braak III–IV stages (95% CI = −89.4% to −13.6%, p = 0.038; power = 46.3%) and a significant decrease of 76.8% in late‐Braak stages (95% CI = −112.4% to −41.1%, p = 0.001; power = 95.6%) (Figure 4A). For galanin‐negative neurons, reductions of 33.4% in Braak III–IV (95% CI = −73.7% to 7%, p = 0.133; power = 13%) and 47.2% in Braak V–VI stages (95% CI = −80.6% to −13.7%, p = 0.007; power = 62.3%) were observed, with neither achieving sufficient power; however, the latter reached statistical significance (Figure 4B). In the covariate‐adjusted mixed linear models, Braak III–IV reductions were not sufficiently powered, whereas Braak V–VI reductions remained statistically significant and powered for both galanin‐positive and galanin‐negative neurons; PSP reductions also remained significant and powered (Table 3). Despite the limited sample size, these data indicate a marked reduction in neurons with detectable galanin immunoreactivity in AD compared with the baseline group. Because peptide expression can be reduced in stressed or degenerating neurons, this reduction may reflect death of galanin‐expressing neurons, disease‐related downregulation of galanin in surviving neurons, or both. Phospho‐tau burden in galanin‐positive versus negative neurons of IntN

In Braak stages I−II (i.e., baseline), p‐tau inclusions were sparse, with 9.5% of galanin‐positive neurons and 4.8% of galanin‐negative neurons showing tau inclusions (Table 2). By Braak stages III–IV (i.e., mid‐Braak), the p‐tau burden increased in galanin‐positive neurons to 23.5%, while galanin‐negative neurons exhibited a burden of 7.3%. In late‐Braak cases, these numbers reach 75.7% and 38.7% for galanin‐positive and ‐negative neurons, respectively.

In PSP, 43.4% of galanin‐positive neurons and 20.5% of galanin‐negative neurons showed p‐tau inclusions when zero‐count PSP cases were retained in the group summary. Within the IntN ROI, T231‐positive profiles in PSP were predominantly neuronal, with only rare glial tau‐positive elements. Because IntN neuronal loss was profound in PSP, and because three PSP cases had no identifiable IntN neuronal cluster, these proportional estimates should be interpreted cautiously (Table 2, Figure 4A,B).

Exploratory phenotype‐stratified analyses in late‐Braak ADNC

Exploratory phenotype‐stratified analyses were restricted to late‐Braak AD cases because no Braak III–IV case met criteria for atypical presentation. In this small cohort, direct comparisons between typical and atypical late‐Braak AD were not significant for IntN measures (Tables S2 and S3 and Figure S3). Relative to baseline, however, both subgroups showed marked reductions in galanin‐positive neurons, and mean galanin‐positive counts were correspondingly lower, but not statistically significant, in typical than atypical cases (853.7 vs 1157.2 neurons) (Table S3). Because subgroup sizes were small, particularly for atypical late‐Braak AD (n = 4), these analyses are presented descriptively and should be interpreted as exploratory only.

DISCUSSION

We show that the human IntN, the putative analog of the rodent VLPO, is profoundly but differentially affected in AD and PSP, the two tauopathies included in this study. In advanced PSP, IntN total neuron number is reduced to a small fraction of baseline comparison values. In AD, IntN involvement increases with Braak stage and preferentially affects galanin‐positive neurons, which show greater phospho‐tau burden than galanin‐negative neurons. Together, these findings identify the IntN as a disease‐sensitive hypothalamic node vulnerable to tau pathology. These findings align with the well‐described divergence in sleep phenotypes between PSP and AD 3 and support a role for IntN pathology in human NREM/sleep‐maintenance biology. Because premortem sleep/wake measurements were not available in this cohort, these neuropathologic patterns are best interpreted as compatible with disease‐level sleep phenotypes reported previously in PSP and AD, rather than as direct evidence of sleep impairment in the present cases.

Although the rodent VLPO literature is extensive, human studies of its putative analog have been complicated by historical nomenclature. Brockhaus described the intermediate nucleus, Allen, Gorski, and colleagues mapped related interstitial nuclei of the anterior hypothalamus (INAH), and Swaab and colleagues described the sexually dimorphic nucleus of the preoptic area (SDN‐POA). 9 , 19 , 37 , 40 , 46 , 47 , 48 These descriptions are now understood to refer to the same or closely overlapping preoptic/anterior hypothalamic cell group centered between the supraoptic and paraventricular/periventricular regions, later shown to be enriched in galanin‐immunoreactive neurons. 19 Lim et al. further showed that the number of galanin‐immunoreactive IntN/VLPO neurons correlates with actigraphic sleep fragmentation and sustained rest, supporting the interpretation that this cell group is the human functional homolog of the rodent VLPO. 20 We extend this literature by mapping neuronal phospho‐tau burden within the same ROI and by examining both ADNC and PSP.

The IntN/VLPO homology supports the relevance of these findings for sleep regulation. Rodent studies show that galanin‐positive VLPO neurons stabilize NREM sleep, particularly SWS, through inhibitory projections to arousal systems, including the locus coeruleus, tuberomammillary nucleus, and raphe. 13 , 17 , 49 , 50 Contemporary work also emphasizes VLPO heterogeneity and local microcircuit organization. 50 Our data extend this framework to human tau pathology by showing disease‐ and cell‐type‐associated loss and phospho‐tau accumulation in the putative human VLPO analog.

In this cohort, the PSP profile was more severe than the AD profile, but this contrast should be interpreted cautiously because all PSP cases represented advanced disease, whereas the AD cohort spanned Braak stages. Even so, neuronal numbers remained markedly lower in PSP than in Braak V–VI ADNC. The PSP pattern – near elimination of IntN neurons – is compatible with the severe insomnia‐like phenotype, hyperarousal, and reduced slow‐wave sleep reported in PSP, 3 , 51 whereas the ADNC profile of progressive tau accumulation and preferential galanin‐positive neuronal loss is compatible with the gradual sleep fragmentation and slow‐wave sleep reduction described in AD. 5

Across Braak stage progression, our IntN data show a stepwise increase in neuronal p‐tau burden accompanied by a preferential loss of galanin‐positive neurons. Notably, we observed beta‐amyloid deposition only in late‐Braak cases (Braak stages V–VI), and even then, the pathology was mild, consisting exclusively of diffuse plaques (Figure S4). This pattern reflects a broader principle in sleep, wake, and circadian systems: Across multiple subcortical nodes that regulate arousal and sleep continuity, tau pathology tends to emerge earlier and more consistently than amyloid, 52 , 53 linking sleep–wake disruption more closely to tau than to plaque burden. In AD, wake‐promoting populations are particularly vulnerable to tau; our IntN results add a complementary, sleep‐promoting locus of involvement. Together, these observations suggest that disease‐specific sleep phenotypes track the spatial pattern and timing of tau across the arousal–sleep network.

Lim et al. 20 provide an important comparator for the present findings, although their cohort and analytic grouping differed from ours. They studied a substantially older community‐based autopsy cohort with premortem actigraphy and reported Braak stages for the included cases, but their primary comparison grouped participants by clinical‐pathologic AD plus dementia status rather than by the Braak‐stage categories used here. In that cohort, subjects with AD had markedly fewer galanin‐immunoreactive IntN/VLPO neurons than non‐AD subjects, and higher Braak stage showed a trend‐level association with fewer galanin‐immunoreactive IntN neurons. This trend is directionally consistent with our finding that IntN neuronal loss, particularly loss of detectable galanin‐immunoreactive neurons, increases with advancing Braak stage. Differences in absolute counts across studies likely reflect differences in cohort age, clinical‐pathologic grouping, case selection, tissue processing, and ROI/counting strategy, underscoring the need for standardized approaches when quantifying small hypothalamic nuclei.

The strengths and limitations of this study warrant balanced consideration. A key strength lies in the deliberate design choices that address methodological challenges unique to this brain region. The IntN is a small structure within the anterior hypothalamus and is rarely sampled consistently in aging‐focused brain banks. To ensure anatomical accuracy, we prospectively sampled the hypothalamus to preserve landmarks, used thick serial sections to improve boundary recognition, and delineated IntN using galanin immunoreactivity in combination with cytoarchitectonic cues. This marker‐guided approach enhances reproducibility across specimens and directly addresses known human variability in the preoptic area. For quantitative precision, we applied unbiased stereology to estimate total neurons and subpopulations, reported coefficients of error, and used Cavalieri‐based volume estimates. By distinguishing galanin‐positive from galanin‐negative neurons and quantifying p‐tau within each group, we reduced the interpretive ambiguity inherent in total Nissl counts and achieved convergent evidence across multiple outcome measures. We also minimized clinical heterogeneity by including well‐characterized cases without other primary neurodegenerative diseases.

By including AD variants across stages, we tested whether IntN degeneration tracks progression and whether subgroup patterns align with reported sleep phenotypes. Although underpowered, the relative preservation of galanin‐positive neurons in atypical AD is directionally consistent with reports of comparatively preserved NREM sleep in these variants. 8

Still, the study has limitations. Neighboring nuclei were not quantified in the present cohort, limiting our ability to conclude definitively that the neuronal loss observed here is specific to the IntN rather than part of a broader pattern of neuronal loss. This was not feasible in the present study because the preoptic area is largely composed of loosely organized neuronal groups with poorly defined borders, and sections selected for IntN analysis did not reliably contain the full profile of neighboring nuclei suitable for parallel stereological quantification. However, this question has been partly addressed in parallel studies using overlapping cases and a similar methodological pipeline. In those studies, neuronal numbers were preserved in neighboring supraoptic nuclei in AD, 54 and no neuronal loss was detected in the lateral hypothalamic area or tuberomammillary nucleus in PSP. 51 These observations support the interpretation that the pattern identified in the IntN is more compatible with disease‐related regional vulnerability than with a non‐specific effect of agonal factors or post mortem tissue degradation. Because galanin immunohistochemistry reflects peptide expression at death, some galanin‐negative IntN neurons may represent stressed former galaninergic neurons with reduced peptide expression. Total‐neuron estimates remain based on gallocyanin/Nissl morphology, but galanin‐positive loss should be interpreted as loss of detectable galaninergic phenotype/output, whether due to cell loss, peptide downregulation, or both. Sample sizes were modest, especially for mid‐Braak and atypical AD, and all PSP cases represented advanced disease, reflecting the rarity of optimally preserved anterior hypothalamus and the stringency of our inclusion criteria. Even so, most key comparisons showed large effect sizes and high post hoc power, whereas subgroup analyses remain underpowered and hypothesis‐generating. Absolute histologic volume should also be interpreted cautiously. Unlike neuronal counts obtained with the optical fractionator, volume estimates can be influenced by fixation‐ and processing‐related tissue changes, and shrinkage correction was not possible because pre‐embedding dimensions were unavailable. All specimens underwent identical celloidin processing, so the volume measures may still provide comparative information within this cohort; however, absolute values should not be interpreted as direct estimates of fresh‐tissue IntN size. Accordingly, neuronal counts are emphasized as the primary endpoint, whereas volume estimates are treated as secondary descriptive measures. In several PSP cases, no IntN neuronal cluster could be identified despite exhaustive screening; we therefore applied a pre‐specified zero‐count rule and report this transparently. Finally, no premortem sleep assessment was available, precluding direct correlations.

In sum, our findings identify the human IntN as a disease‐sensitive hypothalamic node in ADNC and PSP, with near‐ablation in advanced PSP and progressive, preferential loss of detectable galanin‐immunoreactive neurons with advancing Braak stage in ADNC. Considered alongside prior human data linking IntN/VLPO neuronal integrity to sleep continuity, these results position the IntN as one plausible circuit substrate underlying divergent sleep phenotypes reported in PSP and AD. The study provides a quantitative human neuropathology framework for future clinicopathologic and circuit‐based studies of sleep dysfunction in AD and PSP.
