Abstract
Autonomous agents are increasingly deployed in dynamic environments where their ability to perform a given task depends on both individual and team-level proficiency. While PSA ( PSA ) has been studied for single agents, its extension to a team of agents remains underexplored. This letter addresses this gap by presenting a framework for team PSA in centralized settings. We investigate three metrics for centralized team PSA: the MPB ( MPB ), the KS ( KS ) statistic, and the KL ( KL ) divergence. These metrics quantify the discrepancy between predicted and actual measurements. We use the KL divergence as a reference metric since it compares the true and predictive distributions, whereas the MPB and KS provide efficient indicators for in situ assessment. Simulation results in a target tracking scenario demonstrate that both MPB and KS metrics accurately capture model mismatches, align with the KL divergence reference, and enable real-time proficiency assessment.

Proficiency Analysis

To address the previous question, we start by introducing three different metrics for proficiency assessment. We first discuss the

Finally, we consider the

Measurement Prediction Bound (MPB)

Following the definition of

[

\displaystyle\!\!\!\mathcal{P}_{t}\left(\mathcal{M}|y_{1:t-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\,\ln f_{\mathsf{y}_{t}|\mathsf{y}_{1:t-1}}\left(y_{t}|y_{1:t-1};\,\mathcal{M}\right)}{\partial\,y_{t}^{2}}\right],

(2)

where the expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\right]
is taken with respect to the predictive density
f_{\mathsf{y}_{t}\,\mid\mathsf{y}_{1:t-1}}\left(y_{t}\mid y_{1:t-1};\mathcal{M}\right)

Under standard regularity conditions (differentiability, integrability, vanishing boundary terms) and for any predictor
\hat{\mathsf{y}}_{t}
with a finite second moment, the Bayesian information inequality (Van Trees) implies

[
22

23

\displaystyle\bigg(\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\hat{\mathsf{y}}_{t}-\mathsf{y}_{t})^{2}\right]\,\bigg)^{-1}\;\leq\;\mathcal{P}_{t}\!\left(\mathcal{M}\mid y_{1:t-1}\right),

(3)

i.e., the

upper-bounds the inverse MSE of the measurement prediction.

Extending the
in situ

MPB
of (
2

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{t}\left(\mathcal{M}\lvert\boldsymbol{y}_{1:t-1}\right)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{t}}\ln f_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{t}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{1:t-1}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:t-1};\mathcal{M}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{t}}\ln f_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{t}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{1:t-1}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:t-1};\mathcal{M}\right)^{T}\right],

(4)

with
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{t}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{y}_{t}}=\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{1,t}},\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{2,t}},\ldots,\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{N_{\text{D}},t}}\right]^{T}

Then, for any predictor with a finite second moment,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\big[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{t})(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{t})^{\!\top}\big]~\succeq~\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{t}\!\left(\mathcal{M}\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{1:t-1}\right)^{-1},

(5)

i.e., the vector

is an information matrix whose inverse lower-bounds the prediction-error covariance.

To represent the

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{T},t}\left(\mathcal{M}\lvert\boldsymbol{y}_{1:t-1}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{\text{A}}}\mathsf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{t}\left(\mathcal{M}\lvert\boldsymbol{y}_{1:t-1}\right)\right)\,,

(6)

where the normalization in (

The (two-sample)

The Kullback-Leibler Divergence

The

f_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}}(\cdot)
, to a candidate

, namely
f_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}}}(\cdot)

[
25

. Since

depends on the true state
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{t}
, which is not available in practice, the

, is to consider the value of

since very low values indicate good agreement between the two models.

IV
Case Study: Proficiency Evaluation for Target Tracking

We now consider a non-linear Gaussian vector
SSM
where the team of
AA
, placed in known positions
\boldsymbol{p}_{i,t}
, with
i=1,2,\ldots,N_{\text{A}}
, at time instant
t
, cooperates to track the state of a moving target
[
26
]
. We define the following
SSM
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{t}=\boldsymbol{A}\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{t},\\
\mathsf{y}_{i,t}=h_{i,t}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{t})+\mathsf{v}_{i,t},\quad\mathsf{y}_{i,t}\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{t},\end{cases}

(12)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{t}=[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}_{t},\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}}_{t}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{S}}\times 1}
is the state of the target (i.e., its position and velocity),
N_{\text{S}}
is the size of the state (e.g., for 2D target tracking, it is
N_{\text{S}}=4
),
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{S}}\times N_{\text{S}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{Q})
are the state transition matrix and the state noise process with covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{Q}
, respectively, set as in
[
27
]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{I}_{2}&T_{\text{s}}\mathbf{I}_{2}\\
\mathbf{0}_{2}&\mathbf{I}_{2}\end{array}\right],\quad\boldsymbol{Q}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{T_{\text{s}}^{3}}{3}\,\bar{\boldsymbol{Q}}&\frac{T_{\text{s}}^{2}}{2}\,\bar{\boldsymbol{Q}}\\
\frac{T_{\text{s}}^{2}}{2}\,\bar{\boldsymbol{Q}}&T_{\text{s}}\bar{\boldsymbol{Q}}\end{array}\right],

with
T_{\text{s}}
being the sampling interval and
\bar{\boldsymbol{Q}}=\operatorname{diag}([q_{x},\,q_{y}])
containing the variances of the changes in accelerations. In the observation equation,
h_{i,t}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{t})
is the observation function, and
\mathsf{v}_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{v,i})
is the measurement noise process.

By applying the traditional
EKF
(
EKF
) for tracking the state, the predictive distribution is given by

f_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{t}\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{1:t-1}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\lvert\boldsymbol{y}_{1:t-1};\mathcal{M})\!=\!\mathcal{N}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{t};\,\boldsymbol{h}_{t}(\boldsymbol{m}_{t\lvert t-1}),\boldsymbol{S}_{t\lvert t-1}\right),
where
\boldsymbol{h}_{t}=[{h}_{1,t},\ldots,{h}_{i,t},\ldots,{h}_{N_{\text{A}},t}]^{T}
,
\boldsymbol{m}_{t\lvert t-1}
is the predicted state obtained using the state transition equation. The innovation covariance matrix is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}_{t\lvert t-1}=\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{m}_{t\lvert t-1})\boldsymbol{P}_{t\lvert t-1}\boldsymbol{J}^{T}(\boldsymbol{m}_{t\lvert t-1})+\boldsymbol{R},

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{m}_{t\lvert t-1})
is the Jacobian matrix evaluated at the predicted state (i.e., at
\boldsymbol{m}_{t\lvert t-1}
),
\boldsymbol{P}_{t\lvert t-1}
is the predicted state covariance matrix, and
\boldsymbol{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^{2}_{v,1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{v,i},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{v,N_{\text{A}}})
is a diagonal matrix containing the noise observation variances of each agent.
For this
SSM
, the
MPB
is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{t}\left(\mathcal{M}\lvert\boldsymbol{y}_{1:t-1}\right)\approx\boldsymbol{S}_{t\lvert t-1}^{-1}.

(18)

Note that in linear/Gaussian
SSM
, the covariance

\boldsymbol{S}_{t\lvert t-1}
coincides with the true innovations covariance; thus,

\boldsymbol{S}_{t\lvert t-1}^{-1}
represents the exact
MPB
.
Instead, in the nonlinear case,
\boldsymbol{S}_{t\lvert t-1}
results from a local
linearization and only approximates the true predictive covariance,
so
\boldsymbol{S}_{t\lvert t-1}^{-1}
should be regarded as an approximation to
MPB
.
Considering (
17
) and (
18
), we can observe that the team proficiency depends on two key components. The first term accounts for the system geometry through the Jacobian matrix
\boldsymbol{J}(\cdot)

VI
Conclusions

This letter investigated the problem of proficiency assessment for a centralized team of
AA
.
We proposed a comparative analysis based on three metrics: the
MPB
, the
KS
statistic, and the
KL
divergence.
While the
KL
divergence was adopted as a ground-truth reference, the
MPB
and
KS
metrics provide practical and computationally efficient alternatives for
in situ
assessment.
Results in a target tracking scenario demonstrated that both
MPB
and
KS
-based indicators successfully capture model mismatch and align well with the
KL
-divergence benchmark, even without access to the ground truth state.
Future work will extend this framework to distributed teams.
