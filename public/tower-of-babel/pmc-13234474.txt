Global Epidemiological Insights: Temporal Trends and Disparities in Oral Cavity Cancer Mortality in the United States, 1999–2024
Authors: Xian Wang, Yuetong Wang, Shixi He, Cheng Ma, Zhengzhong He, Xuanping Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral cancer constitutes a significant public health burden globally and has long been associated with substantial socioeconomic, racial, and regional disparities worldwide. Population-level mortality surveillance in high-income countries such as the United States can provide crucial epidemiological evidence and methodological references for global cancer control and prevention strategies. However, national-level updated evidence on long-term trends, subgroup heterogeneity, and pre- and post-COVID-19 pandemic trends in oral cancer mortality in the United States remains limited. This study assesses the temporal trends and differences in oral cancer mortality in the United States from 1999 to 2024.MethodsMortality data were extracted from the CDC WONDER Underlying Cause of Death database. Overall oral cavity cancer was defined using ICD-10 codes C00–C06, while subsite analyses focused on C02–C06. Adults aged 25 years and older were included. Joinpoint regression was used to analyse temporal trends in age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs, per 100,000 population) and to estimate annual percent change (APC) and average annual percent change (AAPC), with stratification by sex, region, and subsite. In addition, custom time intervals of 1999–2019 and 2020–2024 were specified to assess trend changes before and during the pandemic period. A supplementary age-stratified analysis comparing adults aged 25 to 44 years and those aged ≥45 years was also performed.ResultsOverall oral cavity cancer mortality exhibited one joinpoint in 2009. Mortality declined significantly from 1999 to 2009 (APC = -1.81%, 95% CI: -2.48% to -1.28%, P < .001), but increased significantly from 2009 to 2024 (APC=1.29%, 95% CI: 0.99% to 1.66%, P < .001), whereas the AAPC for the full study period was not statistically significant (0.04%, 95% CI:-0.11% to 0.19%, P = .624). Mortality rates were consistently higher in males than in females, and pairwise comparison showed that sex-specific trends were neither parallel nor coincident (parallelism P = .012; coincidence P = .001). All 4 U.S. Census regions demonstrated an initial decline followed by a later rebound, although only the Midwest showed a significant net increase over the full study period. Marked heterogeneity was observed across subsites: tongue cancer (C02) was the only subsite with a significantly increased AAPC over the full study period, whereas floor-of-mouth cancer (C04) and palate cancer (C05) showed sustained net declines. Custom interval analyses further demonstrated a declining trend before the pandemic (1999–2019; AAPC=-0.2709%, 95% CI:-0.4281 to -0.1087, P < .001) and an increasing trend during the pandemic period (2020–2024; AAPC=1.2879%, 95% CI: 0.9922 to 1.6558, P < .001). Significant sex-based differences were observed, with additional heterogeneity across regions and subsites. In supplementary age-stratified analyses, no significant joinpoint was identified among adults aged 25–44 years, whereas adults aged ≥45 years showed a significant reversal in mortality trend, suggesting that the overall mortality rebound was driven predominantly by the older age group.ConclusionsIn the United States, oral cavity cancer mortality shifted from a declining trend to a sustained increase around 2009 and accelerated further during 2020–2024, indicating a biphasic rather than a simple linear pattern. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across sex, region, and subsite. Tongue cancer may have been a major contributor to the recent rebound in mortality, while the post-2020 trend reversal underscores the need to further examine the potential effects of diagnostic delay, disparities in healthcare access, and post-pandemic health system recovery on oral cavity cancer outcomes. Supplementary analyses further suggest that this mortality reversal was concentrated mainly in adults aged ≥45 years rather than in younger adults.Keywords: Oral cavity cancer, Mortality, CDC WONDER, Temporal trends, Joinpoint regression, COVID-19

Introduction

Oral cavity cancer constitutes a major component of head and neck malignancies. Its disease burden is reflected not only in its substantial incidence and mortality, but also in its long-term adverse effects on swallowing, speech, facial appearance, and overall quality of life.1,2 Globally, oral cavity cancer represents a significant public health challenge and is closely associated with several modifiable risk exposures. Evidence from Global Burden of Disease analyses and prevention-oriented reviews consistently indicates that tobacco use, including both combustible and non-combustible products, and excessive alcohol consumption are the principal risk factors for oral cavity cancer, while betel quid exposure remains an important carcinogenic factor in certain regions.3,4 Notably, unlike oropharyngeal cancer, in which human papillomavirus (HPV) plays a dominant etiologic role, the proportion of HPV-related cases in oral cavity cancer is relatively low, highlighting substantial etiologic heterogeneity across different anatomical subsites within head and neck cancers.5 Because early detection and timely treatment are critical determinants of prognosis, changes in oral cavity cancer mortality may, to some extent, reflect the combined influence of population-level risk factor exposure, the effectiveness of early diagnosis and treatment, healthcare accessibility, and treatment efficiency.

In the United States, recent population-level trends in oral cavity and related head and neck subsites have shown increasing evidence of site-specific divergence. Surveillance reports indicate that the incidence of cancers arising in the anterior tongue and gingiva has continued to increase, whereas other subsites, such as the floor of mouth and hard palate, have shown declining trends, suggesting that aggregated analyses across multiple anatomical sites may obscure epidemiologic patterns moving in opposite directions.6 At the same time, a growing body of research has reported a persistent rise in tongue cancer incidence, accompanied by a younger age distribution and shifting sociodemographic characteristics, raising concerns regarding emerging risk factors, pathways to care and referral, and disparities in access to preventive oral healthcare.7,8 International evidence further suggests that oral cavity cancer incidence and mortality differ by sex, stage, and anatomic subsite, and that temporal trends in high-burden subsites such as the tongue and gingiva differ substantially from those observed in other locations, including the oropharynx and buccal mucosa.9, 10, 11 These findings underscore the importance of refined anatomic stratification for etiologic investigation, optimization of screening strategies, and evidence-based resource allocation.

The COVID-19 pandemic further complicated the interpretation of temporal trends. During the early phase of the pandemic, both new cancer diagnoses and oral healthcare visits declined substantially, and both may have influenced the clinical stage at presentation and subsequent mortality burden of oral malignancies through a “delayed detection–delayed treatment” pathway.12, 13, 14 Oral cavity cancer is, to some extent, dependent on detection and referral through dental services and primary care systems; therefore, when access to dental care or primary healthcare is disrupted, diagnostic intervals may be prolonged, leading to more advanced-stage presentation.15 For a malignancy such as oral cavity cancer, in which timely recognition and treatment are critical, it is important to determine whether mortality trends changed after 2020. However, systematic trend analyses based on nationwide U.S. death certificate data remain limited for oral malignancies defined by ICD-10 codes C00–C06. In particular, high-quality evidence remains scarce for the pandemic-relevant period (2020–2024) and for refined anatomic subsite stratification, including C02 tongue, C03 gum, C04 floor of mouth, C05 palate, and C06 other/unspecified parts of the mouth.

Given the strengths of the CDC WONDER mortality database, including long-term continuity, nationwide coverage, and standardized comparability, we used national mortality data from CDC WONDER and applied Joinpoint regression to: (1) quantify annual percent change (APC) and average annual percent change (AAPC) from 1999 to 2024 and identify significant inflection points (joinpoints); (2) evaluate heterogeneous trends stratified by sex, geographic region (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West), and anatomic subsite (C02–C06); and (3) compare 2 predefined periods, the pre-pandemic period (1999-2019) and the pandemic/post-2020 period (2020–2024), to assess whether mortality trends were significantly altered in the context of a major public health disruption. In response to the recognized importance of age-related heterogeneity in oral cancer epidemiology, we also performed a supplementary age-stratified analysis comparing adults aged 25 to 44 years and those aged ≥45 years. This study aimed to provide up-to-date, anatomically refined national epidemiological evidence, identify subgroups experiencing worsening mortality burden, and generate hypotheses for future comparative and mechanistic investigations.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This study was a retrospective epidemiologic analysis based on publicly available national mortality registry data. Mortality data were obtained from the Underlying Cause of Death database available through the CDC WONDER platform.16 This database is derived from nationwide death certificate records compiled through the U.S. vital statistics system and processed by the National Center for Health Statistics using standardized cause-of-death coding procedures. Because CDC WONDER currently provides these data through 2 separate query portals based on different race-classification schemes, we extracted bridged-race data for 1999 to 2020 and single-race data for 2018–2024 separately, and then merged them after item-by-item cross-validation in the overlapping years (2018-2020). All CDC WONDER queries were restricted to individuals aged 25 years and older at the data extraction stage.

Case definition

For the overall analysis, oral cavity cancer was defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) as C00–C06. Subsite analyses focused on C02–C06, including C02 tongue, C03 gum, C04 floor of mouth, C05 palate, and C06 other/unspecified parts of the mouth.

Study population and outcome measures

The study population was restricted to individuals aged 25 years and older. The primary outcome was the age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR), expressed per 100,000 population. Analyses were based on the age-adjusted rates, corresponding 95% confidence intervals, and standard errors provided directly by CDC WONDER. Accordingly, both mortality counts and age-adjusted mortality rates corresponded to the age-restricted study population rather than to the total U.S. population. Further stratified analyses were conducted by sex, the 4 U.S. Census regions (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West), and anatomical subsite.

Statistical analysis

Temporal trends were assessed using the Joinpoint Regression Program developed by the U.S. National Cancer Institute.17 The AAMR provided by CDC WONDER was used as the dependent variable, and calendar year was used as the independent variable, with the corresponding standard error incorporated as the weighting factor, under an annual interval and a log-linear modelling framework. APC was estimated for each segment, and AAPC was calculated for specified time intervals. The minimum number of joinpoints was set at 0 and the maximum at 4. Model selection was based on the permutation test, and a 2-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. In the sex-stratified analyses, pairwise parallelism tests and coincidence tests were additionally performed. Within the Joinpoint framework, the parallelism test was used to assess whether the segmented trends in males and females were statistically parallel, whereas the coincidence test was used to assess whether the fitted trend functions were statistically identical across the 2 groups. These comparisons were conducted using the built-in pairwise comparison procedures of the Joinpoint Regression Program.

Supplementary analysis before and after the COVID-19 pandemic

To evaluate whether mortality trends changed before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, we conducted supplementary analyses based on the existing Joinpoint models by specifying 2 custom time intervals, 1999–2019 and 2020–2024, and calculating the corresponding AAPCs. These supplementary analyses were performed for the overall, sex-stratified, and region-stratified models. These period-specific analyses were intended to examine whether mortality trajectories differed before and after 2020, rather than to attribute observed changes solely to the pandemic itself.

In addition, we conducted a supplementary age-stratified analysis comparing adults aged 25–44 years and those aged ≥45 years. To avoid overlapping age categories, the age comparison was defined as 25–44 years versus ≥45 years. For each age group, temporal trends were assessed using the same Joinpoint regression framework applied in the primary analysis, and custom-range AAPCs were further calculated for 1999–2019 and 2020–2024.

Ethics statement

This study used de-identified, publicly accessible aggregated mortality data and did not involve personally identifiable information. Therefore, additional ethics approval was not required.

Results

Overall trends

From 1999 to 2024, a total of 100,051 deaths from oral cavity cancer were recorded in the United States, and the age-adjusted mortality trend demonstrated a clear biphasic pattern. Joinpoint regression identified one inflection point in 2009 (95% CI: 2007–2011) (Figure 1). Mortality declined significantly from 1999 to 2009 (APC=-1.81%, 95% CI: -2.48% to -1.28%, P < .001), followed by a significant increase from 2009 to 2024 (APC=1.29%, 95% CI: 0.99% to 1.66%, P < .001). However, the AAPC for the entire study period was 0.04% (95% CI: -0.11% to 0.19%, P = .624), indicating no statistically significant net change over the full period. In absolute terms, the age-adjusted mortality rate decreased from 1.80 per 100,000 in 1999 to 1.52 per 100,000 in 2009, and then increased to 1.87 per 100,000 in 2024 (Supplementary table 1).

From 1999 to 2024, a total of 63,835 deaths occurred among males and 36,216 deaths among females, indicating a consistently greater absolute mortality burden in males. In 1999, the numbers of deaths were 1,941 in males and 1,229 in females; by 2024, these numbers had increased to 3,527 and 1,892, respectively. Both sexes exhibited a biphasic pattern characterized by an initial decline followed by a subsequent increase, although the timing of the inflection point and the magnitude of change differed between males and females (Figure 2; Table 1).

Region-stratified results

From 1999 to 2024, a total of 100,051 deaths from oral cavity cancer were recorded across the 4 U.S. Census regions. The South had the highest mortality burden (37,998 deaths), followed by the Midwest (22,935 deaths), West (21,677 deaths), and Northeast (17,441 deaths). In 1999, the numbers of deaths were 630 in the Northeast, 679 in the Midwest, 1206 in the South, and 655 in the West; by 2024, these had increased to 881, 1,223, 2,135, and 1,180, respectively. One joinpoint was identified in each of the 4 regions, although the temporal patterns were not identical across regions (Figure 3; Table 3). In the Midwest, the decline before 2010 was not statistically significant (APC=-0.72%, P = .146), whereas mortality increased significantly from 2010 to 2024 (APC=1.86%, 95% CI: 1.24% to 3.56%, P < .001). The Midwest was also the only region with a significantly positive AAPC over the entire study period (0.72%, 95% CI: 0.38% to 1.08%, P < .001). In the Northeast, mortality declined significantly from 1999 to 2013 and then increased significantly from 2013 to 2024. In the South, mortality declined significantly from 1999 to 2009 and increased significantly from 2009 to 2024. In the West, mortality declined significantly from 1999 to 2013 and increased significantly from 2013 to 2024. However, for all 3 of these regions, the AAPC over the full study period was not statistically significant.

Subsite-stratified results

Subsite analyses were restricted to C02–C06. From 1999 to 2024, a total of 93,271 deaths were recorded across these subsites, of which C02 tongue cancer accounted for the greatest number (55,530 deaths), followed by C06 other/unspecified parts of the mouth (28,502 deaths), C05 palate cancer (4,680 deaths), C04 floor-of-mouth cancer (2,855 deaths), and C03 gum cancer (1,704 deaths). Marked heterogeneity was observed across subsites. For C02 tongue cancer, one joinpoint was identified in 2009. The initial decline was not statistically significant, whereas mortality increased significantly from 2009 to 2024 (APC=1.14%, P = .005). Notably, tongue cancer was the only subsite with a significantly positive AAPC over the full study period (0.34%, 95% CI: 0.03% to 0.70%, P = .034) (Figure 4; Table 4).

Custom interval analyses showed that overall oral cavity cancer mortality declined significantly during 1999–2019 (AAPC = -0.27%, 95% CI: -0.43% to -0.11%, P < .001), but increased significantly during 2020–2024 (AAPC = 1.29%, 95% CI: 0.99% to 1.66%, P < .001), indicating a clear reversal in mortality trends after 2020 (Figure 5; Table 5).

Sex-stratified analyses showed that mortality among males declined during the prepandemic period, although this decrease did not reach statistical significance, whereas a significant increase was observed after 2020. Supplementary age-stratified analysis

In the supplementary age-stratified analysis, adults aged 25–44 years and those aged ≥45 years showed distinct mortality patterns (Table 6). Among adults aged 25 to 44 years, no significant joinpoint was identified during 1999 to 2024, and the mortality trend remained statistically stable over the study period (APC = -0.54%, 95% CI: -1.29% to 0.19%, P = .156). Because the final selected model contained no joinpoint, the AAPCs for the custom ranges 1999–2019 and 2020–2024 were identical, indicating no evidence of a pandemic-related shift in mortality trend in this younger age group. In contrast, among adults aged ≥45 years, one significant joinpoint was identified in 2009 (95% CI: 2007-2011). Mortality declined significantly from 1999 to 2009 (APC = -1.76%, 95% CI: -2.55% to -1.16%, P < .001) and increased significantly from 2009 to 2024 (APC = 1.33%, 95% CI: 1.03%-1.69%, P < .001). Custom interval analyses further showed a significant decline during 1999–2019 (AAPC = -0.23%, 95% CI: -0.40% to -0.03%, P = .020) and a significant increase during 2020–2024 (AAPC = 1.33%, 95% CI: 1.03% to 1.69%, P < .001), suggesting that the overall reversal in mortality trend was primarily driven by the older age group.

Discussion

Based on nationwide U.S. mortality data for oral malignancies (ICD-10: C00–C06), this study demonstrated a clear reversal in mortality trends around 2009, with a significant overall decline during 1999–2009 followed by a significant increase during 2009–2024; the inflection point identified by Joinpoint regression was 2009. Although the overall AAPC for 1999–2024 did not reach statistical significance, the post-2009 APC and the custom AAPC for the pandemic period (2020–2024) indicate a sustained upward trajectory with clear public health relevance. These findings are consistent with previous national trend analyses of broader head and neck cancer categories (ICD-10: C00–C14), further supporting the stage-specific evolution of disease burden over time. At the same time, our results underscore that even within the oral cavity, different anatomical subsites may follow markedly divergent, or even opposite, temporal trajectories.6,18 This interpretation is strengthened by comparison with prior epidemiologic studies, but such comparisons also require caution. In the United States, Farhadi et al. reported temporal changes in mortality for broader lip, oral cavity, and pharyngeal cancer groupings from 1999 to 2019,18 while in the Netherlands, van Dijk et al. showed that oral cavity cancer incidence and mortality may evolve differently over time despite changes in survival and treatment patterns.9 At the global level, recent GBD-based analyses have suggested that mortality from lip and oral cavity cancer has increased in many regions, although these estimates are model-based and therefore not directly equivalent to observed death certificate data.11 Accordingly, the value of the present study lies not only in extending the observation window to 2024, but also in demonstrating that a non-significant full-period AAPC may still mask a clinically meaningful reversal in segmented mortality trends.

Sex-stratified analyses suggested that oral malignancy mortality trends shared both common and distinct features between males and females. Pairwise Joinpoint comparison demonstrated statistically significant differences between the 2 sexes (parallelism P = .012; coincidence P<.001), and the timing of the inflection point also differed (2009 in males vs 2011 in females). These differences may be attributable to sex-specific patterns of risk factor exposure, including smoking and alcohol consumption, differences in utilization of oral healthcare services, and variation in subsite composition, which is consistent with prior studies describing evolving epidemiologic disparities in oral cavity and oropharyngeal cancers.19,20 In addition, mortality rates remained consistently higher in males than in females throughout the study period. Previous studies have likewise reported substantially higher oral cancer mortality in males, with some estimates suggesting rates several-fold greater than those in females.21 Potential explanations include a higher prevalence of high-risk behaviors among males, a greater risk of HPV exposure or infection, and the possibility of protective effects associated with female hormonal factors, all of which may contribute to lower incidence and mortality in females.22,23 The observed male predominance is therefore broadly consistent with prior epidemiologic literature from both U.S. and international settings, in which males generally bear a higher burden of oral cancer incidence and mortality than females.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 However, published explanations for this disparity remain heterogeneous and are often based on indirect inference rather than direct causal evidence. Some studies emphasize differences in tobacco, alcohol, and sexual behavior exposures, whereas others suggest contributions from social, diagnostic, or hormonal factors.21, 22, 23 Our findings support the persistence of sex-based inequality in mortality burden, but they should not be interpreted as isolating a single explanatory mechanism. Moreover, the sex-specific joinpoints were model-derived estimates rather than discrete biologic thresholds, and their confidence intervals overlapped. Therefore, the difference between 2009 and 2011 should be interpreted cautiously rather than as evidence of a sharply distinct temporal shift between men and women. Notably, the AAPC during 2020–2024 was positive in both sexes, suggesting that pandemic-related disruptions in healthcare delivery may have amplified pre-existing vulnerabilities and further exacerbated unequal growth in mortality burden.

Regional heterogeneity was likewise pronounced. Joinpoint regression demonstrated that all 4 major U.S. Census regions showed upward trends after their respective inflection points, although the magnitude and timing of these increases varied. Notably, the Midwest was the only region with a significantly positive AAPC over the entire study period, whereas the Northeast exhibited a later inflection point, not until 2013, followed by the steepest rebound in APC. These differences may reflect regional variation in socioeconomic context, distribution of risk factors, accessibility of preventive oral health services, and capacity for cancer diagnosis and treatment. Similar geographic divergence has also been reported in other mortality trend studies based on CDC WONDER using Joinpoint analysis, suggesting that reliance on national aggregate trends alone will inevitably obscure regionally meaningful health inequalities of public health importance, thereby weakening the precision and effectiveness of policy interventions.13 Importantly, regional comparisons across studies remain methodologically challenging. Some published reports use registry-based incidence data, whereas others rely on death certificate-based mortality databases or modelled burden estimates; moreover, geographic units, observation periods, and disease definitions often differ substantially.11,18,20 For this reason, our findings should be interpreted less as a direct replication of previous regional analyses and more as an updated mortality-focused demonstration that meaningful geographic inequality persists within a single national health environment.

The subsite-specific findings further clarify the underlying drivers of the overall reversal in mortality trends. Tongue cancer (C02) shifted from a nonsignificant decline during 1999 to 2009 (APC=−0.8613%) to a significant increase during 2009 to 2024 (APC=1.14%; P = .005), with a full-period AAPC of 0.34%, which was statistically significant (P = .034). Other/unspecified parts of the mouth (C06) also increased after 2009 (2009-2024: APC = 1.62%). In contrast, floor-of-mouth cancer (C04) and palate cancer (C05) showed significant overall declines during 1999-2024 (C04 AAPC = −3.64%; C05 AAPC = −1.68%; both P < .001), indicating that the overall upward trend was not driven by a generalized increase across the entire oral cavity, but rather by marked increases in selected high-burden subsites, particularly the tongue. This finding closely aligns with the repeatedly reported phenomenon of “subsite divergence” in prior surveillance studies, in which increasing incidence in the gingiva and anterior tongue coexisted with declining trends in the floor of mouth. It is also consistent with epidemiologic evidence demonstrating a sustained rise in tongue cancer incidence.6, 7, 8 This subsite-specific heterogeneity is one of the most important findings of the present study. Prior surveillance studies in the United States have repeatedly suggested that oral cavity cancer should not be treated as an epidemiologically uniform entity, particularly because anterior tongue and gingival sites may move in a different direction from floor-of-mouth and palate sites.6,8 At the same time, the published literature is not fully concordant, partly because some studies are incidence-based, some combine oral cavity with pharyngeal sites, and many do not extend into the pandemic period.9, 10, 11 Our mortality-based findings therefore provide complementary evidence that the recent worsening burden is likely being driven disproportionately by selected high-burden subsites, especially the tongue, rather than by a generalized increase across the entire oral cavity.

Etiologic interpretation should be approached with caution. Although the principal established risk factors for oral cavity cancer remain tobacco and alcohol use, smoking prevalence in the United States has declined steadily since the mid-20th century and, in theory, would be expected to drive a long-term decrease in oral cavity cancer mortality.7,24 Accordingly, the post-2009 increase observed in this study is likely attributable to the combined effects of multiple factors, including cohort effects associated with differing exposure histories across birth cohorts, changes in alcohol consumption patterns, shifts in comorbidity burden and competing risks, as well as delays in diagnosis and treatment. Importantly, although high-risk HPV infection has been recognized as a major driver of the rising incidence of oropharyngeal cancer, its detection rate in oral cavity cancer is only approximately 3%, and it is found predominantly in the base of tongue and tonsillar crypts rather than in the typical anatomic sites of the oral cavity. Therefore, HPV is unlikely to represent the principal explanation for the overall increase in C00–C06 mortality.5,25 In addition, rising tongue cancer incidence has been observed in multiple countries and regions, yet its underlying causes remain incompletely understood and may involve behavioural, microbial ecological, or metabolic pathways. Further, mechanistic investigations integrating epidemiology, microbiome research, and molecular pathology are therefore warranted.9, 10, 11,26 In this regard, the current literature remains suggestive rather than definitive. Many available studies are ecological, registry-based, or burden-modelling analyses and therefore cannot disentangle the relative contributions of evolving behavioural exposures, oral microbiome alterations, socioeconomic transitions, and changes in diagnostic ascertainment. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as proof of a single dominant etiologic pathway.

The acceleration in mortality trends during the pandemic period is also biologically and health-system plausible. Surveillance data have shown a marked decline in cancer diagnoses during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, accompanied by a substantial reduction in dental visits, and accumulating evidence suggests that patients with oral cancer experienced delays in both diagnosis and treatment.12,13,15 Because mortality is an integrated downstream outcome reflecting both the natural history of disease and the effectiveness of healthcare intervention, its temporal pattern depends heavily on timely clinical evaluation, stage distribution at diagnosis, and the accessibility and timeliness of standard treatment.27 Consequently, pandemic-related reductions in routine dental attendance, disruptions in diagnostic and referral pathways, and delays in treatment were not merely transient disturbances; rather, through a cascade of delayed detection, more advanced stage at presentation, narrowing of the therapeutic window, and reduced survival, these disruptions may have gradually translated into an observable increase in mortality burden in subsequent years. Against a background of already rising trends, this process may have generated a public health “compounding effect” of substantial significance. Nevertheless, the present study cannot establish that the pandemic itself was the sole or direct cause of the post-2020 increase in mortality. The available supporting literature primarily documents reductions in cancer diagnoses, dental visits, and care continuity rather than long-term oral cancer mortality itself.12, 13, 14, 15,27 Our results are therefore best interpreted as being consistent with a pandemic-related contribution to worsening mortality trends, while also allowing for the influence of pre-existing secular changes in exposure profiles, healthcare access, and referral pathways.

The supplementary age-stratified analysis adds an important layer of interpretation to the present study. Adults aged 25 to 44 years showed no significant joinpoint and no clear evidence of a pandemic-related shift in mortality, whereas adults aged ≥45 years exhibited a distinct biphasic pattern, with a significant decline before 2009 and a significant increase thereafter. This suggests that the recent reversal in oral cavity cancer mortality in the United States was driven predominantly by the older population rather than by younger adults. This result is noteworthy because the existing literature has often emphasized a rise in oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma, particularly oral tongue carcinoma, among younger patients, yet most of that evidence comes from incidence-based registries, single- or multi-institutional retrospective cohorts, or reviews focused on young adults with oral tongue disease rather than nationwide mortality analyses.28, 29, 30, 31, 32 Several studies and reviews have described increasing incidence of oral tongue squamous cell carcinoma in younger patients, including young non-smoking women, and have suggested that younger oral cancer cohorts may differ in site distribution, risk-factor profile, and recurrence pattern from older patients.29,32, 33, 34 However, these studies do not consistently show worse overall survival or higher mortality in younger patients, and their conclusions are complicated by heterogeneous age cutoffs, referral bias, subsite restriction, and differences in treatment setting.30, 31, 32,35 In this context, our results are not necessarily contradictory to the prior literature; rather, they suggest that a reported rise in younger-adult incidence—especially for oral tongue cancer—has not yet translated into a detectable increase in nationwide mortality at the population level. A more cautious interpretation is therefore warranted: younger-onset oral cavity cancer may represent an epidemiologically important and possibly distinct subgroup, but the current evidence remains insufficient to conclude that it is the principal driver of contemporary mortality worsening in the United States. Instead, the burden of mortality reversal in our data appears to be concentrated in older adults, who may be more strongly affected by cumulative carcinogenic exposures, multimorbidity, delayed presentation, and vulnerability to healthcare disruption. This distinction also highlights an important methodological point: incidence-based alarm signals in younger adults and mortality-based burden signals in older adults may coexist, and both are informative, but they should not be conflated without direct age-specific linkage among incidence, stage at diagnosis, treatment access, and survival.

A major strength of this study lies in its national coverage, extended study period, inclusion of both the prepandemic and pandemic eras, and the use of a standardized Joinpoint approach to transparently identify inflection points and estimate temporal trends. By quantifying APCs and AAPCs across different time intervals, the study ensured a high degree of analytic transparency and methodological rigor. Nevertheless, several inherent limitations should be acknowledged. First, mortality data relied on the underlying cause-of-death coding recorded on death certificates. Although these data undergo standardized quality control by the CDC, misclassification may still occur because of anatomic site miscoding, misattribution in the presence of multiple primary cancers, or coder subjectivity. Previous studies have shown that such systematic misclassification can introduce directional bias in subsite-specific trend estimates.36 This limitation is particularly relevant for anatomically adjacent oral and pharyngeal sites, for which coding ambiguity may differentially affect subsite-level trend estimation. Second, updates and transitions in CDC population denominator estimation methods and in the handling of race/ethnicity population data may have introduced artificial discontinuities in trend estimates for certain years, and these issues should be addressed cautiously in sensitivity analyses. Finally, mortality data cannot directly capture incidence, stage distribution, or treatment patterns, and the follow-up period for the pandemic era remains relatively short, which may mean that the full long-term impact of the pandemic on prognosis has not yet been completely observed. In addition, comparisons with previously published studies should be interpreted cautiously because registry-based incidence studies, death certificate-based mortality studies, and GBD-modelled burden analyses differ in case ascertainment, outcome definition, and analytical assumptions.9, 10, 11,18,36 These methodological differences may partly account for apparent discrepancies across studies. Because CDC WONDER and SEER were developed for different surveillance purposes, they should be regarded as complementary rather than interchangeable sources; comparisons across them require caution in light of differences in case ascertainment, coding emphasis, and analytic structure.

In conclusion, the age-adjusted mortality rate of oral malignancies in the United States underwent a significant trend reversal in 2009, after which it entered a phase of sustained increase and accelerated further during the 2020–2024 pandemic period. This evolving pattern highlights the urgent need for a multilevel, differentiated, and evidence-driven prevention and control framework, including primary prevention cantered on tobacco and alcohol control, earlier detection and timely treatment supported by dental and primary care systems, and targeted interventions tailored to sex- and region-specific disparities, in order to reduce the future mortality burden. Future work should also incorporate age-stratified mortality analyses, real-world evaluation of diagnostic and treatment delays, and closer integration of mortality surveillance with incidence, staging, and treatment data in order to more precisely identify the mechanisms underlying recent adverse trends.

Conclusions

From 1999 to 2024, the age-adjusted mortality rate of oral cavity cancer in the United States exhibited a clear biphasic pattern, characterized by an initial decline followed by a subsequent increase, with the inflection point estimated to occur in 2009. Pronounced heterogeneity was observed across sex, geographic region, and anatomical subsite. Importantly, tongue cancer appears to have emerged as the principal subsite driving the recent worsening of oral cavity cancer mortality burden. Of particular concern, mortality increased more rapidly during the 2020–2024 pandemic period, suggesting that structural factors such as reduced diagnostic accessibility, disruption of standardized treatment pathways, and insufficient health system resilience may have materially affected terminal disease outcomes. Supplementary age-stratified analyses further suggest that this mortality reversal was driven predominantly by adults aged ≥45 years, whereas mortality remained comparatively stable among adults aged 25–44 years. Future studies should prioritize real-world investigations into diagnostic and treatment delays and incorporate health system recovery capacity into long-term surveillance frameworks for oral cavity cancer prognosis.
