Background

Common hemorrhagic neurocritical diseases include subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), traumatic brain injury (TBI), and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). Impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation in these patients can lead to secondary injury and poor outcomes [1, 2]. The brain is an extremely oxygen-consuming organ and is very sensitive to hypoxia [3]. Red blood cells are an important medium for oxygen delivery. Anemia occurs in up to 50% of patients with TBI and SAH [4, 5]. Anemia can lead to secondary brain injury by reducing arterial oxygen content and oxygen supply to the brain [6]. This is more harmful to neurocritical patients with impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation function [6].

Early red blood cell (RBC) transfusion may be beneficial to the prognosis of patients [5, 7]. However, previous studies have shown that RBC transfusion may increase the risk of thromboembolic events and severe progressive hemorrhagic injury events in neurocritical patients [8, 9]. A large randomized controlled study published recently found that in critically ill patients with traumatic brain injury, the free transfusion strategy did not reduce the risk of adverse neurological outcomes at 6 months [10]. Evidence supporting transfusion strategies for patients in neurocritical settings remains scarce. At present, the appropriate transfusion threshold for patients with severe neurological diseases has not been clearly defined. Therefore, we will conduct a randomized controlled trial to explore RBC transfusion strategies for neurocritical patients. The primary objective of our study is to evaluate whether a liberal RBC transfusion strategy (90 g/L) versus a restrictive RBC transfusion strategy (70 g/L) improves neurological outcomes in neurocritical patients. The secondary objectives will be to evaluate the effects of different transfusion strategies on in-hospital mortality, transfusion-related adverse reactions, and perioperative complications (Supplemental Appendix 2). We report the trial protocol following the SPIRIT statement. This trial is registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400083713).

Methods

Trial setting and eligibility criteria

This trial is a prospective, randomized, open-label, blinded endpoint assessment study conducted at Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University. A study coordinator screens all critically ill adult patients with acute brain injury daily to determine eligibility. The inclusion criteria are as follows: (i) 18 years ≤ age ≤ 65 years, (ii) proposed craniotomy hematoma removal for acute brain injury (TBI/SAH/ICH), (iii) hemoglobin concentrations below 90 g/L, and (iv) signed informed consent (Supplemental Appendix 1). We will exclude life-threatening patients in need of emergency treatment and pregnant or lactating women. We will also exclude patients who have already received a red blood cell infusion before surgery, patients who refuse transfusion, patients with Glasgow Outcome Scale (GCS) = 3, patients with dilated and fixed pupils, and patients with clinically diagnosed brain death. If severe electrolyte, acid-base balance disturbances or perioperative serious adverse cardiovascular events (such as myocardial ischemia, malignant arrhythmias, and cardiac arrest) occur during surgery, the study will be terminated. Assignment of interventions

Patients identified as eligible by the study investigator will be randomly assigned to a liberal RBC transfusion strategy (90 g/L) or a restrictive RBC transfusion strategy (70 g/L) in a 1:1 ratio. Randomization will be performed on the date of surgery. The randomized digital tables are generated by independent researchers using computer software (SAS 9.1). Random numbers are placed in light-tight envelopes. The researchers open the envelopes to see how patients are randomized according to the order in which they will be included. The attending anesthesiologist opens the randomization envelope for assignment information. Patients, outcome evaluators, data statisticians, and postoperative treatment team members will be blinded to the assignment of treatment groups. The anesthesiologist needs to be involved in the perioperative management of the patients concerned, so the anesthesiologist is aware of the study grouping. Researchers will be able to unblind only if patients experience a serious adverse event (such as death, or a life-threatening need for emergency rescue) that requires additional visits.

Interventions

For patients in the liberal transfusion strategy group, blood salvage techniques (BSI) will be used to perform intraoperative autotransfusion (IAT). Patients in the liberal transfusion strategy group will not receive an allogeneic RBC transfusion when hemoglobin is over 90 g/L after IAT. Patients will receive an allogeneic RBC transfusion if hemoglobin is less than 90 g/L after IAT. The infusion volume is one unit of RBC. If the hemoglobin is still below 90 g/L, the allogeneic RBC will be transfused again by one unit until the hemoglobin is over 90 g/L (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Study flow diagram. For patients in the restrictive transfusion strategy group, blood salvage techniques (BSI) will be used to perform intraoperative autotransfusion (IAT). Patients in the restrictive transfusion strategy group will not receive an allogeneic RBC transfusion when hemoglobin is over 70 g/L after IAT. Patients will receive an allogeneic RBC transfusion if hemoglobin is less than 70 g/L after IAT. The infusion volume is one unit of RBC. If the hemoglobin is still below 70 g/L, the allogeneic RBC will be transfused again by one unit until the hemoglobin is over 70 g/L (Fig. 2). The allocated transfusion strategy will be applied throughout the operating room. All patients will be admitted to the ICU or general ward after surgery, depending on their condition, and receive standard care.

Patient management after surgery

All patients will be admitted to the intensive care unit and treated according to our standard treatment protocol. Electrocardiogram, blood pressure, blood oxygen saturation, body temperature, urine volume, and drainage volume will be routinely monitored in all patients, and invasive arterial blood pressure and arterial blood gas analysis will be monitored if necessary. The neurosurgeon performs a daily physical examination of the neurological system, including the state of consciousness, speech, muscle strength, and physiological and pathological reflexes. Brain CT examination will be performed within 24 h after surgery. Thereafter, the frequency of brain CT examination is determined by the neurosurgeon according to the condition. Chest CT and lower extremity vein Doppler ultrasound will be performed once a week after surgery, and cardiac ultrasonography will be performed if necessary. Keep the airway open and use oxygen if necessary. For patients with obvious respiratory dysfunction, tracheal intubation connected to a ventilator can be used to assist ventilation, and tracheotomy can be selected if necessary. For patients on ventilators, arterial blood gas analysis is reviewed daily to adjust ventilator parameters. The nurse turned the patient over every 2 h and periodically aspirated sputum according to the situation. After surgery, all patients will be transfused with red blood cells in accordance with blood transfusion guidelines, that is, when hemoglobin is below 70 g/L, one unit of red blood cells will be transfused until hemoglobin > 70 g/L. Red blood cell transfusions need to be strictly recorded. The patient’s systolic blood pressure will be maintained at 120–150 mmHg and adjusted appropriately according to the condition. In patients with hypotension, liquid therapy is first selected, and norepinephrine is used to maintain blood pressure in patients who do not respond to liquid therapy.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the neurological outcomes at discharge. We are using the Glasgow Outcome Scale extended (GOSe) to assess patients’ neurological outcomes [11]. The GOSe is rated on an eight-point scale ranging from 1 (the most unfavorable prognosis, death) to 8 (the best prognosis, upper good recovery) based on the patient’s recovery. A score of 1–4 is defined as a poor outcome and a score of 5–8 is defined as a good outcome.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes include GOSe score at 6 months after surgery, in-hospital mortality, the incidence of transfusion reactions (Supplemental Appendix 3) [12], the incidence of systemic complications (Supplemental Appendix 2), length of ICU stay, length of stay, and hospitalization costs [13, 14]. For patients with SAH, we will evaluate the presence of cerebral vasospasm using transcranial Doppler ultrasound (TCD) on the fifth and seventh postoperative days. Cerebral vasospasm is defined as the mean blood flow velocity (MFV) of the middle cerebral artery ≥ 120 cm/s or Lindegaard index (ratio of MFV of the middle cerebral artery to the internal carotid artery) ≥ 3 [15].

Data collection

At enrollment, the investigators collect demographic and baseline characteristics of the patients and the current episode. Intraoperative hemoglobin, red blood cell transfusion volume, transfusion reactions, vital signs, anesthetic administration, fluid volume, blood loss, and urine volume will be recorded during surgery. Postoperative complications and adverse events will be collected after follow-up of the patients. At discharge, the GOSe score, the duration of mechanical ventilation during hospitalization, the length of ICU stay, whether the patient underwent secondary surgery, and the reasons will be recorded. Six months after the operation, the GOSe score will be obtained by telephone follow-up. Follow-up still needs to be completed for patients who drop out or change treatment regimens.

Data management

Raw data for all subjects will be collected via paper case report forms specially designed by the researchers and placed in dedicated lockers with locks. All researchers will strictly abide by the rules of professional confidentiality, keeping all personal information of patients confidential. The paper clinical report form will be destroyed 3 years after the end of the study. The electronic data will be stored encrypted after the patient’s personal information is hidden. Access to the electronic database is limited and only the principal investigators have access to the electronic database.

Sample size

According to a recently published small sample clinical study, the incidence of GOSe adverse prognosis in traumatic brain injury patients was 38% in the liberal transfusion strategy group and 57% in the restrictive transfusion strategy group [16]. Using PASS11.0, the total required sample size is 234 patients with 80% power and a two-sided alpha of 0.05, allowing for a 10% loss to follow-up.

Statistical methods

All analyses will be conducted by investigators who are blind to the intervention according to the principle of intention-to-treat, with per-protocol analysis required for primary outcomes. Primary outcomes will be expressed as percentages and analyzed by the χ2 test. The hemoglobin at perioperative time points and hemodynamic indexes at intraoperative time points will be compared by repeated-measures analysis of variance. For other outcome indicators, categorical data will be expressed as percentages and the χ2 test will be used for statistical analysis. Continuous data will be expressed using mean ± standard deviation, or median and interquartile intervals. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test will be used to determine whether continuous variables were normally distributed. The independent T-test will be used for data with normal distribution, and the Mann-Whitney U test will be used for data with non-normal distribution. When 50% of the participants are recruited, the researcher will conduct an interim analysis.

Interim analysis and stopping rules

The primary outcome will be assessed with the use of a group sequential testing procedure based on an O’Brien–Fleming–type alpha-spending function with one preplanned interim analysis [17]. When approximately 50% of the primary endpoint data has been accumulated, the Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) will conduct a review and perform an interim analysis. P values of 0.05 or less (two-tailed letter level) in the interim analysis will be considered to indicate statistical significance. If the interim analysis of the primary outcome exceeds the significance limit, it will be reported as the final analysis and no subsequent analysis will be performed. P values of 0.048 or less (two-tailed letter level) in the final analysis will be considered to indicate statistical significance.

Trial monitoring and data safety

Project Management Group: Composed of the principal investigator, study coordinator, and statistician. This group holds weekly meetings to review the daily progress of the trial, including recruitment, data collection, and protocol adherence. Trial Steering Committee (TSC): Composed of an independent chair, clinical experts, and methodologies. This committee meets every 6 months to provide strategic oversight of the trial’s overall conduct, scientific validity, and ethical compliance. Independent Data Monitoring Committee (DMC): The confidentiality of all data is managed by a designated Data Monitoring Committee established by the principal investigators. The Data Monitoring Committee is independent of the investigators The Data Monitoring Committee includes an anesthesiologist, a transfusion medicine specialist, a biostatistician, and a neurologist with expertise in neurocritical care. They review the data every 3 months, with a primary focus on data quality, patient safety, and efficacy endpoints. The principal investigator can obtain the interim analysis results.

Serious adverse events

An adverse event is defined as an adverse event or worsening of a pre-existing medical condition that occurs during treatment, whether or not it is considered to be causally related to the different transfusion strategies [15]. Researchers need to closely monitor and document adverse events. The principal investigator will report the adverse event to the Ethics Committee within 24 h of its occurrence.

Ethics and dissemination

Research ethics approval and consent process

This study conforms to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Before the study started, it was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University (Ethics No. KY2023-116-03). Since a patient with a disturbance of consciousness is temporarily unable to provide informed consent, informed consent from his or her legal representative should be sought. For these patients, the investigator will inform the patients of the study within 24 h after they regain consciousness, and the patients have the right to opt out of the study or refuse follow-up. This protocol is prepared following the SPIRIT 2013 guidelines.

Protocol amendments

Any amendments to the trial protocol must first obtain written approval from the sponsor, the funding agency, and the relevant ethics committee. Upon approval, the principal investigator will be responsible for notifying all investigators in writing. All deviations from the approved protocol, regardless of their scale, will be promptly identified and documented. We will use a standardized “Protocol Deviation Report Form” to record detailed information for each deviation, including the nature of the event, the cause of occurrence, impact assessment, and corrective measures taken. These records will be archived and regularly submitted to the ethics committee, the sponsor, and the Trial Steering Committee for review. Furthermore, any protocol amendments with a substantial impact on the trial will be promptly updated on the clinical trial registry platform after approval to ensure transparency and public access to information.

Confidentiality and access to data

The researchers will maintain the confidentiality of the study with coded identification, light-tight envelopes, locked filing cabinets, and offices. The principal investigators have access to the final trial dataset.

Dissemination policy

The study is designed, recruited, or clinically conducted without any patient involvement. The result of the research will spread to all study participants at the end of the study.

Declaration interests

All directors and investigators of this study undertake to be free from financial or other conflicts of interest.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not directly consulted during the formulation of the research questions or outcome measurements. None of the patients participated in this study’s design, recruitment, or clinical implementation. Summaries of our research findings are communicated to the broader patient community in a clear and accessible manner.

Discussion

This is a prospective randomized controlled study to investigate the effect of a liberal RBC transfusion strategy (90 g/L) versus a restrictive RBC transfusion strategy (70 g/L) on the outcome of neurocritical patients. The 2023 AABB International guidelines set a transfusion threshold of 70 g/L for adults, but neurocritical patients remain underrepresented in transfusion strategy studies [18, 19]. The transfusion threshold is individualized, and considering comorbidities and clinical conditions, the threshold of 70 g/L may not be appropriate for neurocritical patients [20]. Transfusion strategies for neurocritical patients with impaired autoregulation of cerebral blood flow have been controversial, so it is necessary to explore transfusion strategies that are more suitable for these patients. The results of this study will provide a theoretical basis for the use of blood products in neurocritical patients. This study will be carried out on the basis of blood protection, that is, intraoperative autotransfusion (IAT) will be used during the surgery, which was not involved in previous studies. Intraoperative autotransfusion can not only maintain the effective blood volume, but also reduce the risk of infection and immune reaction of allogeneic transfusion, which greatly reduces the transfusion cost and saves blood resources.

The human brain is a highly oxygen-consuming organ, sensitive to hypoxia, and 20%–25% of cardiac output is used to supply brain tissue [21]. Cerebral perfusion pressure is equal to mean arterial pressure to reduce intracranial pressure. Oxygen delivery to the brain is proportional to cerebral blood flow and arterial oxygen content [22]. Red blood cells are the main medium of oxygen delivery, and the concentration of hemoglobin affects the content of oxygen in arteries. In the brain of healthy anemia patients, adequate cerebral oxygen supply can be maintained by vasodilating and increasing cerebral blood flow [22]. When the hemoglobin concentration is reduced to 50–60 g/L, the cerebral vascular expansion reaches its limit and cannot be further expanded [22]. In patients with severe neurological disease, the central nervous system is damaged, cerebrovascular autoregulation function is not perfect, and cerebrovascular expansion is limited. In this case, the limit of cerebrovascular dilation may occur when the hemoglobin concentration is below 80–90 g/L [22]. Maintaining brain oxygenation and transfusing red blood cells are important to improve the prognosis of patients with severe traumatic brain injury [23]. Although red blood cell infusion can enhance cerebral oxygen and stabilize hemodynamics, it also carries significant risks, including transfusion-related lung injury and transfusion-related circulatory overload, highlighting the importance of careful transfusion management [23]. Baucom et al. found that the red blood cell infusion was associated with in-hospital mortality in patients with TBI, but this study could not prove causation [24]. Neurocritical patients have a high incidence of anemia. At least 50% of patients with TBI and 47% of patients with SAH suffer from anemia [21]. Multiple studies have confirmed that anemia is an independent predictor of cerebral vasospasm, poor neurological prognosis, and increased mortality [25, 26]. A randomized controlled study by Naidech et al. found that it was safe to use higher target hemoglobin concentrations in patients with SAH [27]. Oddo et al. conducted multiple neurological monitoring (including intracranial pressure, brain tissue oxygen partial pressure, and brain microdialysis) on 20 patients with SAH and found that hemoglobin lower than 90 g/L was associated with brain hypoxia and cell dysfunction [28]. However, due to the small sample size, the results of this study can only be regarded as preliminary results, and further verification is still needed. In a study that collected a decade of admissions of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and Massachusetts Institute of Technology from 2008 to 2019, liberal transfusion strategies may benefit survival in patients with early hemodynamics [29]. However, higher-quality prospective studies are needed to validate the results. In patients with TBI, a transfusion threshold of 100 g/L compared with 70 g/L increased the risk of thromboembolism and progressive bleeding without improving neurological outcomes [8, 9]. Some retrospective cohort studies have found that patients with brain injury who received red blood cell transfusion had a worse prognosis [30, 31]. A retrospective study demonstrated that RBC transfusion increased the risk of venous thrombotic events in patients with aneurysmal SAH [32]. But these studies do not rule out the possibility that complications from the primary disease may have influenced transfusion decisions. This is a common limitation of retrospective studies. Prospective observational studies have found that blood transfusion is associated with an increase in neurological complications in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage and therefore has a poor short-term prognosis, but no significant effect on long-term neurological prognosis [33]. Patients with SAH have a special condition, and the balance of advantages and disadvantages of RBC transfusion may change due to vasospasm [33]. This study will observe this adverse event through postoperative TCD examination. Some researchers believe that although restrictive transfusion strategies are effective, cerebral perfusion and cerebral oxygen balance need to be closely monitored during use, especially in severely ill patients [23]. Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) monitoring and brain tissue oxygen (PbtO2) monitoring can be used to guide personalized transfusion strategies [23]. This study focused on the hemoglobin concentration of patients during surgery, that is, the ultra-early hemoglobin concentration. Most of these patients will undergo emergency surgery after admission, and surgical intervention is the starting point and focus of treatment for these patients. Previous studies have shown that a sharp drop in hemoglobin levels during hospitalization is associated with poor prognosis [34]. Therefore, the researchers recommend keeping red blood cell levels close to the baseline level at admission [34]. However, other studies have shown that patients’ hemoglobin levels within 24 h are not associated with neurological outcomes upon leaving the ICU [35]. Therefore, it is unclear whether intervention with ultra-early hemoglobin levels in neurocritical patient conditions can improve prognosis.

In conclusion, the successful completion of this trial and the validation of its basic hypotheses will provide evidence for the selection of transfusion strategies in neurocritical settings and provide new therapeutic options for improving the prognosis of neurocritical patients.
