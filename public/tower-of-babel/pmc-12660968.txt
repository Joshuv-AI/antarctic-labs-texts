Design and implementation of a smart earthquake rescue robot to enhance rescue operations
Authors: Omar Khattab, B. Saravana Balaji, M. Omar Al-Kadri, Amal Alsaeed, Asmaa Alofaisan, Joud Aboutamar, Hooreyah Alharbi, Abdullatif Shikfa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the wake of catastrophic earthquakes, rescue operations encounter significant obstacles in locating and reaching individuals trapped under debris. This study introduces the Smart Earthquake Rescue Robot (SERR) prototype, a cutting-edge solution designed to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of earthquake rescue missions. The SERR is a mobile robot with advanced features, including live video streaming through an integrated camera, Grid-Eye temperature detection, and provisions for communication via built-in speakers and microphones, although these audio communication capabilities are pending implementation in the current prototype. It can be remotely controlled using a smartphone application, offering a safer and more efficient method for conducting rescue operations. Unlike recent advances discussed in the literature, SERR uniquely combines visual, thermal, and audio data with a multi-modal Convolutional Neural Network with Long Short-Term Memory (CNN-LSTM) model (RescueNet), achieving high accuracy (0.94), precision (0.90), recall (0.96), and F1-score (0.92) in detecting survivors, as validated by MATLAB simulations using USGS-PAGER data. The SERR’s rapid runtime (35 ms) highlights its promise as a tool to improve earthquake rescue outcomes.

Introduction

On February 6, 2023, two massive earthquakes with magnitudes of 7.7 and 7.5 struck south-eastern Turkey and northwestern Syria. According to the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), the earthquake affected an estimated 15.73 million people in Turkey and Syria, killing approximately 55,000 and injuring nearly 130,000. Millions have been displaced from their homes1. Rescue operations after earthquakes generally involve many steps. The rescue team uses dogs to enter the area and look for casualties on the surface. Then, it examines the situation beneath the rubble with video cameras. The rescue crew then seeks to confirm whether there are any trapped individuals. The most critical responsibilities in rescue operations are to assess the situation, locate the victims, and establish contact with them. However, human rescuers face great difficulty and risk attempting in this respect2.

There are several techniques based on sensor technologies in rescue operations. Using specialist sound equipment can pinpoint the slightest disturbances within a few meters. The site should remain silent while a rescue team member hammers thrice to elicit a response3. Generally, it is possible to find people buried under rubble using audio signal analysis. However, microphone accuracy suffers when there is a lot of background noise4. Another drawback is the inability of auditory detection technologies to locate unconscious individuals. To address these gaps, this paper presents the Smart Earthquake Rescue Robot (SERR) prototype, designed to enhance post-earthquake rescue operations by improving the efficiency and safety of locating survivors. SERR overcomes key limitations of existing sensor-based techniques, such as the susceptibility of audio detection to background noise and the inability to locate unconscious individuals. It offers a versatile, reliable solution that reduces risks to rescue teams through wireless remote control and advanced detection capabilities. SERR integrates live video streaming via an attached camera, thermal detection using a Grid-Eye sensor to identify living humans or animals under rubble, and a user-friendly smartphone application for remote operation. Additionally, a light enhances video stream quality, controllable via the app, while provisions for two-way audio communication (pending implementation) are included for future development.

In order to assess the performance of the SERR, this research integrated data from the USGS—PAGER (Prompt Assessment of Global Earthquakes for Response) system58. The PAGER system offers crucial assessments of the impact on fatalities and economic losses resulting from large earthquakes around the world. This dataset contains comprehensive data on the probable magnitude of structural damage to buildings, the probability of casualties, and the estimated financial consequences. These factors are crucial for developing accurate simulation environments. Nevertheless, acknowledging the significance of audio data in identifying human presence amidst debris, a few alterations were made to the PAGER-CAT dataset by incorporating simulated audio samples. The audio files were created to simulate several sounds that could be encountered in a post-earthquake situation, including distress calls, tapping, and other sounds produced by humans. This modification was essential to ensure that the simulations precisely reflect the demanding conditions encountered during real earthquake rescue operations. This multifaceted approach ensures that SERR can effectively navigate complex environments while providing critical live updates and communication capabilities to rescue teams. This Smart Earthquake Rescue Robot (SERR), a prototype uniquely combining live video streaming, Grid-Eye thermal detection for unconscious victims, and provisions for audio communication (pending implementation), surpasses56,57 by fusing visual, thermal, and audio data via a CNN-LSTM model (RescueNet). Validated through MATLAB simulations with USGS-PAGER data, SERR achieves high accuracy (0.94) and rapid runtime (35 ms), offering a safer, more effective solution for real-time disaster response in telecommunications, smart cities, and autonomous vehicle industries. The rest of the paper is organised as follows: Section II reviews related works. Section III details the design of the proposed SERR. Section IV covers the implementation and testing of the SERR, while Section V presents the performance evaluation. Finally, Section VI provides the conclusion.

Audio-based detection

In5, the authors proposed a microphone array-based sound localisation method for mobile robots. The microphone array employed in the system comprises four microphones positioned in a particular order on the vertices of a regular tetrahedron, since sound has an omnidirectional quality. This study creates two distinct localisation methods based on the time delay of arrival. In a noisy environment, the sound source emits sound. Using a microphone array unit, the sound signal, which consists of the target’s sound and noise, is transformed into an electric signal. The localisation unit can process the target’s electric signal by amplifying and denoising it in the pretreatment unit. The system records the arrival times of four channels in the localisation unit. The data reached the PC using serial communication of a single-chip for position calculation. In6, the authors described human search equipment that resembled a robotic car and acted as a backup system to save lives in the event of a catastrophe. Generally, the temperature sensor measures the thermal image of the human body. The robot was equipped with sensors to track people; if an object were found, the readings would change, indicating the presence of a person underneath. The device consisted of motors and an Arduino microcontroller. The module displayed the emission, humidity, and temperature quantities for the individual and the surrounding area using the gas, temperature, and humidity sensors. The system used a Bluetooth module in the device, allowing it to connect to a mobile phone via a mobile application and monitor all movements, including forward, backward, left, and right, with pinpoint accuracy. The Adafruit website was used to collect data directly from the sensors in the module.

In7, the authors suggested a method for employing an autonomous robot to find human survivors in destroyed areas. The system used a passive infrared sensor (PIR) to identify living people and a camera to capture images of the scene. A feed-forward neural network trained to find a human body or a portion of one in an obstructed environment was given the images. A PC was used to control the vehicle. Three different types of sensors were included in the system: a PIR sensor that used body radiation to identify people in the vicinity, an Infrared (IR) range sensor to identify obstacles in the robot’s path, and an image sensor, which was a conventional web camera, to acquire still photos following instructions from the control program. The Global Positioning System (GPS) was also installed on it. A wireless communication unit provided a reliable communication link with the rescuers. In8, the authors introduced a portable microwave system to search and rescue people buried in the rubble of a building that had collapsed during an earthquake or other disaster. The system used a continuous-wave X-band radar that could detect heart rate and respiration changes. The data was obtained from the backscattered electromagnetic field using the Independent Component Analysis algorithm (ICA) to separate mixed data. The proposed rescue system also used a remote elaboration unit for data processing. The output signals were given as input to a remote elaboration system utilising a low-frequency wireless channel.

In9, the authors introduced a system that used an electromagnetic microwave signal for motion detection and could detect the motion of moving objects. This system determined whether or not the object was moving. The working premise of the motion-detecting system was the wave’s Doppler frequency change. If an object was moving and present below the earth, the motion detection system used a microwave Doppler radar sensor to detect these waves reflected from the object. Once the motion was identified, it was possible to determine whether or not the item was a human. Also, the device used a microwave test bench to pick up the subject’s cardiac and breathing signals. The system consisted of a microwave-frequency electromagnetic signal, a PIC microcontroller, a buzzer, and a display (LCD). The microwave, the LCD, and the buzzer were all connected to the PIC microcontroller. The LCD showed"alive,"and the buzzer turned on when the system detected the motion of moving items. Otherwise, the LCD showed"No Response,"and the buzzer remained off. In10, the authors designed a rescue robot whose primary job was to locate the robot using a Bluetooth GPS receiver placed on the robot and to find survivors using real-time video transmission. A video camera was connected wirelessly to a laptop for real-time video transmission. The GPS was used for determining the location data for the PC, which contained the address of the area where help was needed. The Microcontroller was used to drive the robot according to the real-time video feedback received on the PC. The Arduino processor was connected to the motor driver responsible for the robot’s movement.

In11, the authors constructed a robotic vehicle prototype controlled by a smartphone using Bluetooth communication. The controller of the robot was an Arduino Uno R3 board. A mobile application was developed to provide an interface between the robot and mobile devices, enabling robot control. The robotic arm also used four servos to facilitate movement. In12, the authors introduced a rescue robot that used a living human detecting sensor consisting of two components: a"live body sensor“and an”amplifier circuit."The sensor detected the passive Infrared Radiation (PIR) released by living things, and if the movement of the living thing brought on a change, a difference in output was produced. The signal was amplified by a differential amplifier and fed to a PIC microcontroller, which was used to operate the robot’s motor and connect it to the robot model.

In13, the authors introduced a rescue robot that used a PIC-16F877A microcontroller. It could independently navigate through debris and use a temperature sensor to verify the air’s temperature. The real-time locating system was used to automatically locate and identify objects or people who were buried in building rubble. The Global System for Mobile Communication Module (GSM) was used to send the measured temperature to the user via Short Message Services (SMS). The robot was also equipped with a camera, LCD, and LED. The wireless camera and PC-Tuner Card recorded the visual and audio signals, where the PC controlled the robot’s movement. In14, the authors proposed a live human detection robot based on Arduino as a controller. It used an ultrasonic sensor to detect barriers and divert its path. It used a PIR that emitted infrared radiation to identify people. When a human target was found, the system started a buzzer and emitted a light alert, which could aid in quickly localising the victim’s location. The Arduino received signals from PIR, and according to these signals, the Microcontroller controlled the robot’s motors.

The proposed SERR research work has many novel contributions that are different from the articles discussed above:

This paper differs from previous studies, concentrating on a single sensor by combining thermal imaging and camera systems. The purpose is to enhance the precision and dependability of identifying trapped individuals. Using many sensors improves the robot’s capacity to detect and recognise humans in different situations, including those with limited visibility or when debris is present.

Workflow of the SERR system

This study explains the signal-capturing and data-processing operations for the sensors used in the Search and Rescue Robot (SERR) model. The automatic adaptation mechanism in the SERR, based on real-time feedback from sensor data, involves analysing the sensor readings and dynamically adjusting the threshold distance for obstacle detection. We employ cross-correlation and time-of-flight estimation techniques for the ultrasonic sensor to capture and process the received signal, allowing us to calculate the distance to objects. The infrared sensor utilises calibration parameters and statistical methods to capture and process the intensity of infrared radiation, enabling temperature estimation. Finally, the camera sensor captures images or video frames, and it employs convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for object detection and recognition, employing convolution operations and non-maximum suppression techniques. The overview of the system workflow:

Continuous sensor data monitoring

The SERR continuously monitors the sensor data from the ultrasonic sensor in real-time. The sensor data includes the measured distances to objects or obstacles in the SERR’s vicinity.

Data analysis and statistical processing

The SERR’s software or algorithm processes the sensor data to analyse the distribution and characteristics of the measured distances. Statistical techniques such as mean, standard deviation, or clustering algorithms can be applied to identify patterns and trends in the data.

Dynamic threshold adjustment

Based on the sensor data analysis, the SERR dynamically adjusts the threshold distance for obstacle detection. The adjustment can be made based on factors such as the average distance to detected objects, the distance variation, or the presence of outliers. The aim is to set a threshold distance that optimises obstacle detection while minimising false positives or negatives.

Feedback loop

The SERR continually evaluates the performance of the adjusted threshold distance. It compares the detected obstacles based on the adjusted threshold with the actual environmental obstacles. The feedback loop allows the SERR to assess the effectiveness of the current threshold and make further adjustments if necessary.

Iterative refinement

The automatic adaptation mechanism operates iteratively, continuously updating the threshold distance based on real-time feedback. As the SERR encounters different obstacles and environments, it refines the threshold to improve the accuracy and reliability of obstacle detection.

Hardware modules and their operation in SERR

The SERR integrates key hardware components, including an ultrasonic sensor for obstacle detection (halting at 30 cm), a Grid-Eye thermal sensor for human presence detection via temperature mapping, and a camera for live video streaming, all of which are controllable via a smartphone app. An Arduino Mega microcontroller processes inputs and manages a 4-wheel chassis with shock-absorbing suspension, powered by a rechargeable Lead-Acid battery (3-h operation). An RF module enables wireless control through a PC application. The RescueNet architecture consists of three convolutional layers for both video and thermal image streams, each with 32, 64, and 128 filters, respectively (kernel size 3 × 3, ReLU activation), followed by max pooling (2 × 2). The output feature maps are flattened and concatenated with extracted audio features (e.g., Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients—MFCCs). The combined feature vector is input to a two-layer LSTM network with 128 units per layer. The output is fed into two fully connected layers (one each for survivor and obstacle detection), each followed by a softmax activation. The model is trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, categorical cross-entropy loss, and a batch size of 32 for 50 epochs. Early stopping is applied to prevent overfitting.

Features are concatenated post-pooling into a single vector, which is then fed into a two-layer LSTM (128 units each) for temporal modelling over 10 frames. The output is provided via two fully connected heads (survivor and obstacle detection) with softmax activation, accompanied by confidence scores (threshold 0.7). Data Preprocessing: The input data X, comprising video, thermal images, and audio signals, is preprocessed to normalise and enhance relevant features. This step ensures that the data fed into the model is consistent and highlights essential features for detection and classification.

Feature Extraction Using CNN: Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) extract spatial features from the video and thermal image data. The convolutional layers apply filters to detect features such as edges, textures, and patterns.

Pooling layers down-sample the feature maps, reducing their dimensionality while preserving important information. This step helps reduce the computational complexity and retain the most relevant features.

Temporal Feature Extraction Using LSTM: The pooled features from the CNN layers are flattened and concatenated with the audio features. This combined feature vector captures both spatial and auditory information. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) layers capture temporal dependencies and patterns in the data. LSTMs are particularly useful for analysing sequential data and identifying continuous signals, such as those from survivors.

Output Layer: Fully connected layers process the features extracted by the LSTM layers and output the probabilities of detected survivors and obstacles using softmax activation functions. The coordinates and confidence levels for the detected survivors and obstacles are calculated from the output probabilities, providing real-time detection results.

This algorithm is designed to be robust and efficient, enabling real-time analysis and detection of survivors and obstacles in disaster scenarios. The combination of CNN and LSTM layers allows the model to effectively process both spatial and temporal data, making it well-suited for applications in rescue missions.

During the development of the SERR prototype, several design simplifications were implemented to accelerate the testing and evaluation processes. The current version of the robot prioritises stability and safety during initial tests by stopping at obstacles; however, the eventual objective is to enhance its obstacle navigation capabilities. Future developments will prioritise equipping the robot with the ability to manoeuvre around obstacles independently, enhancing its performance in complex surroundings.

RescueNet, unlike conventional CNNs that primarily extract spatial features from static images for tasks like object classification, uniquely integrates multi-modal data (visual, thermal, and audio) and employs LSTM layers for temporal modeling, enabling real-time survivor detection by capturing both spatial patterns and sequential dynamics in dynamic disaster environments. The model was trained from scratch using the synthetic USGS-PAGER dataset augmented with simulated audio samples (2,000 earthquake scenarios, as detailed in Section"Computational simulation-based performance evaluation"), rather than fine-tuning on pre-existing models, to ensure tailored learning of features specific to earthquake rescue contexts. This approach allowed full control over architecture design (three CNN layers with 32, 64, 128 filters; two LSTM layers with 128 units each) and optimization (Adam optimizer, learning rate 0.001, 50 epochs with early stopping), though it required extensive computational resources and a custom dataset for training.

Computational simulation-based performance evaluation

The proposed SERR model was implemented in MATLAB R2022b to evaluate its performance in detecting disaster victims within earthquake debris environments. The simulation was conducted on a standard desktop computer with an Intel Core i7 processor and 16 GB of RAM. The experimental setup aimed to replicate the challenges encountered in real-world earthquake disaster sites, where debris of varying sizes, densities, and distributions can significantly impede search and rescue efforts. The earthquake debris simulation in MATLAB R2022b was designed to cover a land area of 100 square meters, representing a typical urban search and rescue operation area.

The purpose of these simulations was to evaluate the robot’s capacity to identify and locate humans who are trapped under debris. The simulation settings were derived from real-world scenarios and utilized data from the USGS—PAGER system58. This system, known as Prompt Assessment of Global Earthquakes for Response, offers estimates of fatalities and economic losses resulting from large earthquakes around the world. The dataset used for training and evaluation consisted of 2,000 simulated earthquake scenarios generated using MATLAB and the USGS-PAGER system, covering a 100 m2 area with variable debris density. Each scenario included both visual (RGB), thermal (Grid-Eye), and simulated audio streams (distress calls, tapping, ambient noise). The data was split 70:15:15 for training, validation, and testing. The dataset represents a range of real-world conditions (lighting, debris occlusion, background noise) and includes both trapped and non-trapped (negative) samples to avoid bias.

Since the PAGER-CAT dataset lacks audio data, which is essential for recognizing human presence in debris, adjustments were made to include simulated audio recordings in the dataset. The audio files were created to simulate several sounds that could be encountered in a post-earthquake situation, including distress calls, tapping, and other sounds produced by humans. This modification was essential to ensure that the simulations adequately mirrored the demanding settings encountered during real earthquake rescue operations. This integration facilitated a more accurate and thorough assessment of the SERR’s capabilities. The PAGER system data contains comprehensive details regarding the probable severity of building damage, the probability of casualties, and the estimated economic impact. By integrating this data with the simulated audio data, a comprehensive and authentic simulation environment for evaluating the search and detection algorithms of the SERR is created. By integrating this data, simulations can more accurately imitate the chaotic and demanding conditions experienced during real earthquake rescue missions. While the integration of simulated audio data with the USGS-PAGER dataset enhances the practicality of the simulation environment, it also recognizes the limitations of relying on synthetically generated audio signals. These signals, which include distress calls and tapping sounds, are created under controlled conditions and may not fully capture the complexity and variability of real-world audio in post-earthquake scenarios. Real-world environments often include unpredictable background noise from sources such as machinery, wind, or ongoing structural collapses, which could mask survivor sounds or lead to false positives in detection. Additionally, the acoustic properties of debris, such as sound attenuation or reflection, may differ significantly from simulated models. Future work will focus on collecting and integrating real-world audio data from disaster sites or controlled experiments to refine the SERR’s audio processing algorithms and improve robustness against such environmental variability.

To augment the USGS-PAGER dataset, which lacks audio data essential for human presence detection in debris, simulated audio samples were integrated into the 2,000 earthquake scenarios used for SERR evaluation in MATLAB. These audio files were sourced from publicly available sound libraries (e.g., Freesound.org, BBC Sound Effects) and custom recordings created in controlled environments to mimic post-earthquake conditions. Source files included distress calls (e.g., human shouts for help, 30% of samples), tapping sounds (e.g., repetitive knocks on debris, 25% of samples), and other human-induced noises (e.g., faint breathing or movement, 20% of samples), with ambient noise (e.g., rubble shifting, wind, 25% of samples) added as distractors. Augmentation techniques were applied using MATLAB’s audio processing toolbox to simulate real-world variability: (1) Volume scaling (50–100% of original) to represent distance attenuation under debris; (2) Noise overlay (signal-to-noise ratio of 0.5–2.0) using recorded disaster site background noise to mimic environmental interference; (3) Reverberation (echo delay 0.1–0.5 s) to emulate acoustic effects of rubble enclosures; and (4) Frequency filtering (low-pass at 1–3 kHz) to simulate muffled sounds through debris. Each audio sample (duration 2-5 s, 16-bit PCM, 44.1 kHz) was paired with visual/thermal data in scenarios, with 70% of victim samples assigned audio signals (conscious state) and 30% silent (unconscious state). This approach, although synthetic, aimed to replicate challenging audio conditions in rescue operations. The simulated audio signals integrated with USGS-PAGER data were not evaluated for realism by domain experts due to logistical constraints and the preliminary nature of this study. Dust and smoke degrade image and thermal sensor quality, leading to reduced detection accuracy, especially in heavier debris scenarios where visual occlusion is already severe. Vibration may introduce noise or misalignment in sensor readings, while electromagnetic interference can cause intermittent sensor dropout or communication failure, further hampering reliable detection. Failure case analysis

Problems with survivor detection generally happen as a result of unacceptable or insufficient sensor data due to severe natural conditions. The main cases of failure are: (a) heavy debris along with a complete blockage of both thermal and visual sensors, (b) so loud external noise that it conceals survivor sound, and (c) sensor malfunction (e.g., discharged battery). In such cases, recall was lower than 80%. In the case of debris being present that covers the visual and thermal sensors entirely, neither sensor will detect any individual with characteristic features, and detection becomes impossible. Also, if the incoming noise becomes extremely loud, for example, from external sources such as factory machinery, earthquakes, or people, the audio signals from survivors will be completely covered, and the audio-based method becomes less effective for the said purpose. A sensor failure, which is mostly due to battery exhaustion or electromagnetic interference, may then worsen matters even more by shutting down one or more sensors temporarily or permanently. The specified problems are mainly due to sensor-based approaches, and hence the need for more sensors that are capable of fault tolerance and complementing the functionality of other sensors, as well as for signal processing technology that is more advanced and efficient. More sensors (e.g., radar for in-ground detection or ultra-wideband for imaging through debris), as well as error correction and data fusion algorithms that are very strong and efficient, could contribute to reducing some problems when traditional forms of vision, thermal, or audio signals are not available or are compromised due to other reasons. Therefore, the continuous development of the integration of multiple sensor technologies and the means to combat the faults in the same is essential for the effective recovery of the victim during the search and rescue operations.

Experimental limitations and roadmap for real-world validation

The evaluation of the Smart Earthquake Rescue Robot (SERR) shows promising results via MATLAB simulations and synthetic datasets (e.g., USGS-PAGER with augmented audio), but it lacks real-world field testing due to logistical constraints and ongoing development. Simulations cannot fully mimic real disaster site challenges like dust, smoke, vibration, or electromagnetic interference, which may affect sensor performance. Also, the synthetic audio data, mimicking distress calls, has not been validated for realism by experts, which may underestimate real-world noise issues. To address this, a roadmap for real-world validation in controlled disaster training environments is planned. Phase 1 (6–12 months) involves testing SERR with disaster response teams in urban search and rescue (USAR) facilities, checking mechanical stability, sensor reliability under dust/smoke, and navigation over 20 cm debris. Phase 2 (12–18 months) will test multi-robot setups in larger 500 m2 zones, focusing on coordination, 3-h battery life, and app control latency up to 100 m. Phase 3 (18–24 months) targets limited real disaster site testing under supervision, pending ethical and regulatory approval, integrating with rescue protocols. Ethically, SERR will support, not replace, human rescuers, with protocols to hand over to teams upon detection, and survivor data privacy will be protected via encryption and restricted access, following GDPR standards. For safety, SERR will have fail-safes like auto-stop on sensor failure and a lightweight aluminum chassis to avoid debris risks. Operator training and regular safety audits per FEMA guidelines will ensure safe use, with design updates to fix identified issues. This plan aims to validate SERR’s real-world use while prioritizing ethics and safety in disaster response.

Conclusion

In this paper, a prototype of the Smart Earthquake Rescue Robot (SERR) has been proposed to address the challenges faced by human rescuers in locating and communicating with survivors trapped under earthquake debris. The SERR has advanced features such as live video streaming, thermal detection capabilities, and wireless control via a smartphone application. The SERR model is enhanced using a novel machine-learning model for real-time data analysis. This model, named RescueNet, is a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) layers tailored explicitly for analysing the data transmitted by the robot, including video feeds and thermal images. The performance tests were conducted using MATLAB simulations and real-world data from the USGS—PAGER system, which offers critical impact estimates for large earthquakes. By using PAGER system data, a more accurate evaluation of the SERR’s effectiveness in detecting and locating individuals trapped under debris was achieved. The SERR demonstrated high accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score in detecting trapped individuals and a fast runtime. These findings validate the SERR’s potential to enhance the effectiveness and safety of earthquake rescue operations. The SERR’s ability to wirelessly search for and detect survivors under debris while providing real-time information to rescue teams makes it a valuable tool for improving disaster response and saving lives in the aftermath of devastating earthquakes.

While the SERR demonstrates significant potential with high performance metrics (accuracy of 0.94, runtime of 35 ms) in simulations, it is acknowledged that there are key limitations that temper these findings. The reliance on synthetic data (USGS-PAGER with simulated audio) may not fully capture real-world complexities like unpredictable noise or debris acoustics, and the lack of physical field testing limits validation of the prototype under actual disaster conditions. Additionally, the two-way audio communication feature remains unimplemented, restricting direct survivor interaction. These constraints highlight the need for real-world testing and data integration to ensure practical applicability in earthquake rescue missions.

The SERR has the potential to integrate sophisticated communication capabilities to improve the real-time interaction between the robot and rescuers. These future improvements will encompass two-way audio communication, enabling the robot to relay audio messages between trapped individuals and rescue teams through microphones and speakers. Additionally, remote monitoring will enable rescuers to access live audio and video feeds from the SERR through the control application, improving situational awareness and facilitating decision-making. Future work will prioritize real-world field testing to bridge the gap between simulation results and practical deployment. Planned tests in controlled disaster simulation environments will provide critical insights into the SERR’s operational challenges and performance under authentic conditions, ensuring its readiness for actual earthquake rescue missions.
