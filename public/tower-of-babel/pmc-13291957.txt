Real-Time and In Situ Monitoring of Pesticide Uptake and Transportation in Plants Using Surface-Enhanced Raman Scattering Nanosensors
Authors: Ziyue Xie, Tao Cheng, Xiaoyu Guo, Ying Wen, Xinling Liu, Haifeng Yang, Yiping Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pesticides are essential for maintaining the global crop productivity and food security. Improving their efficiency and minimizing their environmental impact require a detailed understanding of their uptake and translocation in plants. However, existing techniques for tracking pesticides in living plants are largely destructive, ex situ, and incapable of real-time monitoring, which limits progress toward sustainable agriculture. To address this gap, we developed noninvasive, ratiometric surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) nanosensors that enable in situ, real-time mapping of pesticide dynamics in plants. The nanosensors feature gap-enhanced gold nanorods (AuNNRs) with 4-mercaptobenzonitrile (4-MBN) embedded in the nanogap as a stable internal standard for ratiometric SERS quantification. Once delivered into the leaf intercellular spaces, the nanosensors create functional “sensor-equipped” plants for real-time mapping of pesticide distribution. Using this platform, we compared pesticide translocation under different application methods. The results show that root-irrigated tricyclazole moves upward in a concentration- and time-dependent manner, whereas foliar-sprayed pesticides are poorly absorbed due to the barrier posed by the leaf cuticle. Furthermore, commercial tricyclazole formulations are taken up and transported more efficiently than pure reagent-grade compounds. This SERS-based sensing strategy offers a powerful tool for optimizing pesticide delivery and supports the development of precision agriculture practices that are aimed at sustainability.

Introduction

Enhancing food production is critical in meeting the demands of the global population burst, − and pesticides play an important role in this process. , In pesticide application to plants, both the application method (e.g., root absorption, foliar spraying) and the pesticide formulation play crucial roles. Suboptimal choices in either can lead to inefficient delivery and result in significant waste. According to statistics from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), global pesticide usage reached approximately four million metric tons annually by 2022, with over 99.9% of pesticides failing to reach the target organisms. , This is not only a waste of resources but also poses a persistent threat to the ecosystems and public health. Faced with such a severe situation, it is necessary to implement strategies for precise pesticide application. However, a prerequisite for executing this action is an in-depth understanding of the absorption, transport, and distribution of pesticides within the organisms.

Currently, methods for detecting pesticides in plants include ex vivo techniques such as chromatographic analysis , and radioactive isotope labeling. , However, these methods typically involve destructive sampling, offer only discrete time point data, and may disrupt the native physiological state of the plant. To overcome these limitations, recent research efforts have increasingly focused on the development of in vivo plant nanosensors that are engineered for integration into living plant tissues to enable the detection and quantification of specific biological and chemical targets. By combining such sensors with spectroscopic or electrochemical readouts, molecular recognition events are converted into detectable signals. − This strategy allows for the noninvasive, in situ, and real-time monitoring of dynamic processes, providing spatially resolved and longitudinal tracking of pesticide distribution and behavior within intact plants.

Against this background, surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS)-based plant nanosensing has emerged. By utilizing the enhancement effect of plasmonic nanomaterials, SERS offers the following significant advantages in vivo plant monitoring: (1) nondestructive detection: allows in situ analysis without damaging plant tissue; (2) high specificity: capable of obtaining characteristic fingerprint spectra of pesticide molecules; and (3) high sensitivity: suitable for studying trace pesticide transport. Earlier, He et al. developed a SERS platform to study the single-point penetration of pesticides on plant leaves, − while Li et al. achieved visual pesticide detection on fruit surfaces by integrating SERS imaging with machine learning and chemometrics. , Although these studies demonstrate the feasibility of SERS for in-plant analysis, they focus primarily on localized detection and have yet to systematically reveal the spatiotemporal transport processes of pesticides throughout the entire plant. Elucidating the latter is essential for understanding pesticide absorption and utilization mechanisms and is particularly important for evaluating the efficiency of application methods, such as root irrigation. Moreover, commercial pesticide formulations contain additional adjuvants. However, current SERS-based research predominantly focuses on laboratory-formulated pesticides, which are typically prepared by dissolving pesticide molecules in specific solvents, resulting in a significant discrepancy from real-world application scenarios.

Herein, we developed a novel SERS nanosensor for the real-time visualization of tricyclazole translocation and distribution in pea plants (Pisum sativum L.) (Scheme ). The sensor consists of gap-enhanced gold nanorods (AuNRs) functionalized with the internal standard molecule 4-mercaptobenzonitrile (4-MBN), whose characteristic Raman peak lies at 2227 cm–1 within the biological silent region. Upon needle-free injection into selected leaf regions, the nanosensor enables dynamic tracking of tricyclazole via its distinct Raman peak at 425 cm–1. Quantitative analysis is performed using the intensity ratio I 425/I 2227, and therefore, a spatiotemporally resolved kinetic model of pesticide translocation within plants was established. Furthermore, differences in the uptake and transport of tricyclazole were revealed when comparing different application methods (foliar spraying versus root irrigation) as well as between different formulation types (laboratory-prepared versus commercial products). This work provides a feasible sensing strategy for pesticide efficiency assessments in precision agriculture.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of AuNNRs-Based Nanosensors

Figure a illustrates the synthesis of AuNNRs as high-performance SERS nanosensors, adapted from a reported procedure with modifications. The process starts with the preparation of gold nanorods (AuNRs, 25 nm × 60 nm, aspect ratio 2.4, Figure S1), which are functionalized with 4-MBN Raman reporter molecules via Au–S bonds. , A dense silver shell is then grown to form Au@AgNRs, followed by a controlled etching-replacement step that converts the silver layer into an engineered nanogap, yielding the final gap-enhanced AuNNRs. This architecture offers two key advantages: the plasmonic coupling within the nanogap strongly enhances and stabilizes the Raman signal of the embedded probe, while the gold outer shell provides improved chemical stability and biocompatibility compared to silver-based structures, making these nanosensors well suited for biological studies.

(e) Representative histogram of the SERS intensities at 2227 cm–1 in (d). Data is depicted as mean ± 1.0 × standard deviation (S. D.) from three independent measurements. The number at the top of each histogram indicates the mean. (f) FDTD simulation of electromagnetic field distribution surrounding AuNNRs. Scale bars for all images are 10 nm.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed the morphologies of the as-synthesized nanomaterials (Figure b). The nanostructures display a uniform bilayer rodlike geometry, with a clearly defined interlayer gap of approximately 2 nm. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental analysis further confirms the composition: both the core and shell consist of gold, while the gap region is primarily composed of sulfur with trace amounts of silver. These signals correspond to the loaded internal standard probe 4-MBN and the sacrificial Ag layer, respectively.

The optical properties of the nanomaterials at different synthesis stages were characterized by ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectroscopy (Figure c). The original AuNRs exhibit two characteristic absorption peaks at 645 and 515 nm, corresponding to their longitudinal and transverse plasmon resonances, respectively. After coating with a 1 nm thick silver shell, the longitudinal resonance shifts to 615 nm and a new absorption peak emerges at 415 nm, characteristic of silver. The final gap-enhanced AuNNRs retain the longitudinal resonance near 615 nm but show a broadened absorption profile. The disappearance of the silver-related peak at 415 nm indicates the successful removal of the silver shell, consistent with the TEM observations.

The SERS response of 4-MBN was evaluated across different noble-metal substrates (Figure d–e). A significantly enhanced signal was observed from both AuNNRs and Au@AgNRs compared to conventional AuNRs, highlighting the essential contribution of the nanogap to field amplification. The distinct –CN peak at 2227 cm–1 served as a stable internal reference. , Although Au@AgNRs exhibited nearly 5-fold higher SERS intensity, AuNNRs were chosen for in-plant studies due to their superior biocompatibility. The gap width in AuNNRs was precisely tuned via an etching-replacement method (Figure S2), with a 2 nm gap delivering optimal enhancement and reproducibility (Figure S3b–c). FDTD simulations of AuNNRs with varying gaps revealed the most uniform electromagnetic field distribution at 2 nm (Figures f and S4), providing a physical basis for signal stability. Strong field localization at the rod tips further ensured detection sensitivity. Hence, all further experiments were conducted using AuNNRs with a fixed gap width of 2 nm.

The SERS Responsiveness of Nanosensors to Tricyclazole

Tricyclazole is commonly used in agriculture to control fungal pathogens via foliar spraying, soil irrigation, or seed treatment. Understanding its absorption, translocation, and distribution in planta is essential for optimizing application efficiency, enhancing disease control, and minimizing environmental release. To enable such a study, we first characterized the in vitro SERS response of tricyclazole using the AuNNRs nanosensors. SERS spectra were collected from standard solutions across a wide concentration range, from millimolar levels down to nanomolar dilutions (Figure a,c). To suppress interference from chlorophyll autofluorescence in plant tissue (Figure S5), all Raman measurements were carried out with a 785 nm laser at low power. Under these conditions, the internal reference peak at 2227 cm–1 remained highly stable. Tricyclazole’s characteristic SERS peaks at 425, 1310, and 1370 cm–1 grew consistently with increasing concentration and became prominent at 1 × 10–3 mol·L–1. These signals originate from C–N–C deformation and –CN stretching modes within the five-membered heterocycle of tricyclazole (Figure g). The peak at 425 cm–1 showed the strongest concentration dependence and was therefore used for quantification via the intensity ratio I 425/I 2227.

(b, d) Corresponding ratiometric intensity I 425/I 2227 and its relationship with concentration. Two distinct quantification ranges were examined: a trace-level range corresponding to the limit of detection and a low-concentration range relevant to practical application conditions. As shown in Figure b,d, the intensity ratio I 425/I 2227 exhibited a linear relationship with the logarithm of tricyclazole concentration from 1 × 10–4 to 1 × 10–8 mol·L–1, and a direct linear response with concentration from 1 × 10–3 to 2 × 10–4 mol·L–1. The detection limit is determined to be 1 × 10–9 mol·L–1. This wide dynamic range and high sensitivity confirm the suitability of the nanosensor for real-time tracking of fungicide translocation in living plants.

Based on the field EC50 of tricyclazole, three concentrations (40, 70, and 100 mg·L–1) were selected for SERS measurements to reflect practical application levels. , A commercially available formulation was included as a fourth point for comparison. SERS spectra of all four aqueous solutions were acquired (Figure e). The I 425/I 2227 ratio obtained from the commercial product was comparable to that of the laboratory-prepared 70 mg·L–1 solution (Figure f), indicating that its effective concentration falls within the typical agronomic range. The SERS spectrum of the formulated tricyclazole exhibited an oscillatory baseline, likely due to nutrient or inorganic additives present in the commercial product. Nevertheless, these baseline fluctuations did not significantly affect the characteristic peaks of tricyclazole or the internal standard, ensuring the reliability of subsequent quantitative analyses.

Localization of Nanosensors in Living Plants

Prior to in vivo pesticide monitoring, the nanosensors were delivered into pea leaves via stomatal infiltration using low-pressure needle-free injection. Given that the stomata apertures of plant leaves can reach up to 10 μm, , nanomaterials can easily enter the plant leaf. As shown in Figure a, only mild local infiltration was observed on the abaxial side immediately after injection; this subsided within 20 min and disappeared completely after 4 h, confirming the minimal invasiveness of the method. Confocal laser scanning microscopy was therefore performed 4 h post-infiltration (Figure b). Chlorophyll autofluorescence is shown in red, and rhodamine 6G-labeled nanosensors are shown in green. In epidermal sections (Figure b, i–iv), the nanosensors were localized predominantly along cell walls within the intercellular matrix, with notable accumulation around stomata, and did not enter epidermal cells. This is consistent with the size exclusion limit (SEL) of plant cell walls, which restricts internalization of nanoparticles larger than ∼20 nm. ,

To study the impact of vascular tissue on the in vivo transportation of nanosensors, the AuNNRs were deliberately introduced to one side of the leaf, followed by fluorescence imaging analysis at the veins (Figure b, v–viii). The images showed detectable fluorescence signals from the nanosensors on both sides of the leaf veins, with notably higher intensity on the introduced side. No fluorescence was observed within the xylem and phloem tissues, confirming that the nanosensors did not passively diffuse into the vascular system. This indicated that needleless injection confined the distribution of nanosensors to specific plant tissues, primarily in the extracellular regions around the leaf veins. This distribution characteristic was of significance for monitoring the uptake of exogenous substances like pesticides by plants through leaves or roots. It helped achieve the spatiotemporal consistency between the nanosensors and target pesticides in plant tissues, which is a prerequisite for establishing a real-time and long-term monitoring platform for pesticide translocation in living plants.

The fluorescence confocal 3D imaging further confirmed that nanosensors were primarily located within the internal leaf, rather than on the epidermal surface (Figures b, ix–xii). The spatiotemporal distribution of the nanosensors was tracked at 4 and 48 h post-infiltration (Figure S6). After 48 h, the nanosensors remained accumulated around stomata and adjacent to cell walls; however, the overall fluorescence intensity decreased, likely due to gradual degradation of the R6G dye in the plant environment. This result indicates that fluorescence-based imaging is not well suited for long-term in vivo tracking. Hence, the development of an in vivo detection system based on SERS, which offers superior photostability and does not rely on organic dyes, holds considerable value for both fundamental research and practical applications.

Real-Time and In Situ Monitoring of Pesticide Transport within Plants

Mature pea plants with three fully expanded leaves were used as the model system for real-time in vivo pesticide analysis. To address potential background interference from the plant matrix in SERS analysis, control measurements were performed on nanosensor-infiltrated leaves without pesticide (Figure S7). No SERS response was observed around 425 cm–1, confirming the absence of spectral interference and validating the accuracy of our in vivo pesticide detection. As outlined in Figure a, the first three leaves from the base upward were selected as monitoring sites, labeled as the first, second, and third leaf, and infiltrated with AuNNRs to generate sensor-enabled plants. Tricyclazole was then introduced via a hydroponic exposure system. Seedlings were transferred to an imaging chamber, where a laser was focused on selected leaf positions for in situ SERS spectral acquisition, enabling real-time, in vivo monitoring (Figure b). Time-dependent SERS spectra collected from the three leaf positions revealed the dynamic translocation of the pesticide throughout the plant (Figure c). Key observations include: (1) the internal standard peak at 2227 cm–1 remained stable across all treatments, regardless of pesticide concentration or exposure duration; (2) the characteristic peak of tricyclazole at 425 cm–1 appeared in all leaf positions (first-third) and intensified over time with extended root exposure; and (3) after 4 h of incubation, detectable signals were present in all plants treated with 40, 70, and 100 mg·L–1 tricyclazole (4 h data shown in Figure ), displaying a consistent intensity gradient of first > second > third leaf. This spatial pattern is consistent with the passive transport of tricyclazole via the transpiration-driven xylem flow.

In plants treated with the commercial tricyclazole formulation, the characteristic peak at 425 cm–1 was detected in all monitored leaves (first-third) as early as 2 h after application (Figure c, pesticide). By contrast, in plants treated with laboratory-prepared aqueous solutions of tricyclazole, the characteristic signal appeared only after 4–24 h (Figure c). Although the nominal tricyclazole concentration in the commercial product was similar to that in the 70 mg·L–1 laboratory solution (Figure f), the commercial formulation exhibited substantially faster uptake and translocation. This enhancement is likely attributable to surfactants or other additives in the formulated product, which can improve solute penetration and systemic movement. Together, these findings demonstrate that (1) tricyclazole rapidly enters the vascular system of pea plants upon root exposure, (2) its distribution within the plant is time-dependent, and (3) the formulation strongly influences pesticide absorption and translocation kinetics.

SERS spectral data were statistically analyzed and visualized as heatmaps to highlight temporal and spatial patterns of tricyclazole distribution (Figure ). In both aqueous-solution and formulation groups, tricyclazole accumulation exhibited clear time-dependence, with the most pronounced increase occurring within the first 12 h. Thereafter, distinct trends emerged: (1) in the 40 mg·L–1 aqueous group, tricyclazole levels declined in all three leaves; (2) in the 70 and 100 mg·L–1 aqueous groups, levels decreased in the upper leaves (second and third) but continued to rise in the basal leaf (first); and (3) in the formulation group, levels in all leaves peaked at 12 h before gradually decreasing. Notably, at every time point and leaf position, tricyclazole concentration was higher in the formulation group than in the aqueous groups. This pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity likely reflects differences in the uptake, translocation, and metabolic processing of tricyclazole, influenced by its physicochemical form and the presence of formulation additives.

Based on the plant cell water potential (ψw) model, , the driving force for water uptake by plant roots originates from the water potential gradient between the vascular system solution and the external environment. Water potential is primarily composed of pressure potential (ψp) and osmotic potential (ψπ): ψw=ψp+ψπ

Transpiration from upper leaves creates negative pressure (ψp < 0) in the xylem vessels, forming the main pulling force for water ascent. Simultaneously, pesticide molecules dissolved in the external water from the environment increased the concentration of solutions, leading to lower external solution osmotic potential (ψπ‑ex) compared to root vascular fluid osmotic potential (ψπ‑root), creating an osmotic potential difference (Δψπ = ψπ‑ex – ψπ‑root < 0). These water potential differences (Δψw = Δψp + Δψπ < 0) collectively drive continuous water uptake by the roots to prevent cellular dehydration. Tricyclazole was thus passively transported along the transpiration stream to leaves at different heights within the plant. As tricyclazole accumulated in the leaves, the osmotic potential (ψπ‑apo) in the intercellular spaces gradually decreased (increasing negative value), causing the extracellular water potential (ψw‑apo) to approach or even fall below the intracellular fluid water potential (ψw‑cyt). This resulted in water molecules diffusing outward, reducing cell turgor pressure. When turgor pressure drops to zero, if the plants continue to absorb pesticide-containing water, cells face the risk of plasmolysis. To avoid osmotic stress, plants may activate defense mechanisms: On the one hand, they may reduce transpiration rate through stomatal regulation, decreasing water (and accompanying pesticide) absorption, entering a “low absorption state”; On the other hand, they may activate metabolic pathways (such as efflux transporters) or produce reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated detoxification reactions to clear excess tricyclazole. Therefore, the observed phenomenon of tricyclazole concentration in leaves slowly declining after reaching a peak may be the result of these coordinated processes (Figure ).

To evaluate foliar uptake of tricyclazole, nanosensors were first introduced into the third leaf, followed by surface application of the pesticide. After 6 h, the distribution of tricyclazole within the leaf was analyzed using confocal SERS intensity imaging. Previously, the in vivo functionality of the nanosensors had been confirmed via root exposure experiments. Interestingly, no tricyclazole was detected in the third leaf at any of the sprayed concentrations (Figure a). To understand this low absorption efficiency, we next examined changes in leaf surface properties following spray application.

In each image, the left color bar indicates the intensity profile of the internal standard peak (2227 cm–1) with imaging depth, and the right bar corresponds to the tricyclazole characteristic peak (425 cm–1). Warmer (redder) colors denote higher tricyclazole levels, revealing its depth-dependent distribution in the third leaf after 6 h of treatment.

Figure shows pea leaves before and after spray application. On both adaxial and abaxial surfaces, the tricyclazole solution formed discrete spherical droplets, consistent with the relatively hydrophilic nature of the leaf surface. After approximately 6 h of drying, brown crystalline or powdery residues remained where droplets had evaporated (Figure c). These deposits were readily removed by rinsing with water, simulating rainfall. The observed droplet behavior and easy wash-off suggest that the surface properties of pea leaves limit wetting and adhesion of the spray solution, thereby reducing effective foliar uptake and increasing the risk of pesticide loss through runoff. These results indicate that foliar spraying is an inefficient delivery route for tricyclazole in pea plants.

Conclusions

This work presents a ratiometric SERS nanosensor based on gap-enhanced AuNNRs functionalized with 4-MBN for the real-time monitoring of tricyclazole translocation in pea plants. The results demonstrate that formulated tricyclazole exhibits faster uptake and more efficient systemic transport than its reagent-grade counterpart. Furthermore, root absorption proved to be significantly more effective than foliar spraying, as the leaf cuticle acts as a barrier to spray-applied pesticides. This SERS-based sensing platform offers a noninvasive tool for studying pesticide dynamics in living plants, supporting the development of precision application strategies that can enhance the pesticide efficiency.
