Can chin–nape circumference and the ratio of neck circumference to chin–nape circumference predict difficult mask ventilation or difficult intubation in obese patients?
Authors: Adem Selvi, Esra Ozayar, Erbil Turksal, Aysun Kurtay, Onur Kucuk
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
We aimed to evaluate the success of the ratio of chin–nape circumference (CNC) and neck circumference (NC) to CNC in predicting difficult mask ventilation (DMV) or difficult intubation (DI). Eighty-seven obese patients (body mass index ≥ 30) were evaluated between September 15, 2019 and September 15, 2020. Mallampati score, mouth opening, upper lip bite test, distance between incisors, thyromental distance (TMD), sternomental distance (SMD), and thyromental height were recorded. NC and CNC were measured in a neutral position. The ratio of NC to TMD and the ratio of NC to CNC were calculated. Mask ventilation was graded according to the method described by Han et al (Grade 3 or 4 equals DMV). DI assessment was performed using the intubation difficulty scale (IDS, IDS < 5 equals easy intubation, IDS ≥ 5 equals DI). Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the variables thought to be effective in DMV and DI. Receiver operating characteristic curves were generated for each parameter considered effective. DMV was found in 17.2% (n = 15) of the patients included in the study, and DI was found in 16.1% (n = 14). The relationship between DMV and age, male gender, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, NC, ratio of NC to TMD, ratio of NC to SMD, ratio of NC to CNC, and CNC was significant. Male gender and NC >45.5 cm were independent risk factors for DMV. The relationship between DI and distance between incisors, NC, ratio of NC to TMD, ratio of NC to SMD, and CNC was significant. A distance between the incisors < 4.85 cm and a NC > 41.5 cm were found to be independent risk factors for DI. CNC may predict DMV and DI in obese patients. The ratio of NC to CNC may predict DMV in obese patients.

1. Introduction

In the difficult airway algorithms published by the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) and the Difficult Airway Association, difficult intubation (DI) occurs when “multiple interventions are required for tracheal intubation in the presence or absence of tracheal pathology.”[1,2] DI, one of the situations most feared by anesthesiologists, is more common in obese patients.[3,4] Predicting a difficult airway and preparing the necessary equipment in advance can facilitate the management of the situation and reduce the mortality and morbidity associated with it. Various methods, such as Mallampati (MP) scoring, thyromental distance (TMD), mouth opening, sternomental distance (SMD), neck circumference (NC) measurement, and upper lip bite test, are used to evaluate an airway.[5–7] While multifactorial indices outperform single measurements, no single anatomical measurement has been reported to have high accuracy in predicting DI and difficult mask ventilation (DMV). In recent years, TMD, NC measurement, and NC to TMD ratio (NC/TMD) are methods that have been reported to be successful in estimating difficult airways.[5,6,8]

However, there is no method in the literature that predicts DMV or DI, especially in obese patients. We thought that the increased subcutaneous fat tissue in the nape region in obese patients may restrict head extension and cause a difficult airway, and the increase in subchin fat tissue may be higher than in the nape region. We assessed the chin–nape circumference (CNC) measurement, the NC to CNC ratio, and their effect on DMV or DI prediction in obese patients.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This prospective observational study was initiated after its approval by the Ethics Committee of the Ankara Keçiören Education and Research Hospital (its new name is Ankara Atatürk Sanatorium Training and Research Hospital) of the University of Health Sciences with the protocol number 2012-KAEK-15/1959 and dated September 11, 2019. The study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki and was included after obtaining informed consent from all participants.

Mask ventilation (MV) was graded according to the method described by Kheterpal et al[9] Normal effective MV is rated as Grade 1. If an oral airway or another assistive device is needed, that is Grade 2. Extremely difficult is Grade 3. It is also Grade 3 if ventilation of the lungs is insufficient to maintain oxygenation, MV is unstable, or 2 people are needed for ventilation. Finally, impossible MV is Grade 4. In this study, DMV was defined as MV Grade 3 or 4. After adequate muscle relaxation was achieved, tracheal intubation was performed using direct laryngoscopy with an appropriately sized Macintosh blade.

The intubation difficulty scale (IDS) was used to assess intubation difficulty as follows: number of intubation attempts (1 point for each attempt after the first attempt), additional operators (1 point for each person who tried intubation after the first person), alternative intubation techniques (each alternative technique 1 point), vocal cord position (abduction: 0 adduction: 1 point), Cormack and Lehane’s classification (CLC) for laryngoscopic view, need for external laryngeal pressure (no need: 0 needed: 1 point), and the laryngoscopy force (low force needed: 0 higher force needed: 1 point).[10] Any IDS value below 5 was considered an easy intubation, and IDS = 5 and above were considered a DI. Tracheal intubation was considered successfully completed when evidenced by an appropriate end-tidal CO2 waveform and both lung sounds were heard equally on auscultation.

2.2. Patients and methods

Patients undergoing general anesthesia, aged 18 years and over, with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 and ASA I to III were included in the study. The study excluded patients with beards, cervical spine anomalies, or limitations (cervical mobility < 80°, suspected cervical spine injury, definitive cervical spine injury, use of cervical collar), a history of DIs, emergency interventions, and scheduled awake intubations.

Patients were evaluated by the same anesthesiologist in the preoperative area. Medical history, including age, gender, ASA status, history of snoring, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS), and continuous positive airway pressure device use was obtained. Height, weight, BMI, and presence of beard were recorded during preoperative anesthesia evaluation. With shoes off, shoulders relaxed, facing forward with the head, and back towards the wall, height was measured using a Stadiometer. With only the bare minimum of clothing on, weight was determined using a Tanita T5896. For accuracy and dependability, each measurement was carried out twice and averaged. MP score, upper lip biting, NC, CNC, distance between incisors, TMD and SMD parameters were measured in the preoperative waiting room with a plastic tape calibrated up to 1 mm weekly. NC was measured at the level of the thyroid cartilage, and CNC was measured at the level of the mentum in a neutral position. In a supine position, with the lips closed and the head fully extended, the SMD was measured. The bony point of the mentum and the upper border of the manubrium sterni were measured along a straight line. With the head fully extended and the mouth closed, the straight distance between the bony point of the mentum and the top border of the thyroid cartilage was measured as TMD. Distance between incisors was measured when the patient opened his or her mouth, and the distance between incisors was obtained. NC/TMD, NC/SMD, and NC/CNC were calculated from these measurements. Wilson et al[11] recommended approach was used to assess the cervical mobility range. A pencil was positioned vertically on the forehead of the patients while they were requested to fully extend their necks. The neck was flexed and the angle was measured while the pencil was firmly in place. All these data were recorded in the preoperative evaluation form.

Before the induction of anesthesia, the patients were placed in a ramp position. Standard monitorization (ECG, pulse oxygenation, NIBP) and preoxygenation were performed. Anesthesia induction and maintenance were carried out using the standard protocol of our institution. An anesthesiologist who had at least 4 years of clinical experience in airway management and was unaware of preoperative measurements was involved in each case. MV was performed using a face mask of the appropriate size for each patient, as determined by the attending anesthesiologist.

2.3. Outcome criteria

The primary outcome is the measurement amount of CNC and the ratio of NC to CNC, which are predictors of DMV and DI in obese patients.

The secondary outcome is anatomical measurements or rates and demographic characteristics that may predict DMV and DI in obese patients.

2.4. Sample size

Power analysis was performed using the PASS11 program (Hintze J. NCSS, LLC, Keysville, UT). Since no study in the literature looked at NC/CNC, NC/SMD in the study by Khan et al[5] was taken as a reference while performing the power analysis of the study. Accordingly, at least 12 patients with easy intubation (IDS < 5) and at least 12 patients with DI (IDS ≥ 5) should be included in the study to obtain a significant difference in 90% power and 0.05 alpha error level. Since the DI rate was lower than the easy intubation rate, all obese patients were included in the study until there were at least 12 DI patients.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Coding and statistical analyses of the data were performed using SPSS for Windows, version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). In the analysis of categorical data, the chi-square test was used in 2 × 2 tables if the necessary conditions were met, and the Fisher exact test was used if not. The conformity of continuous variables to normal distribution was analyzed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. A t test was used if they fit a normal distribution, and a Mann–Whitney U test was used if they did not. Univariate logistic regression analysis was used to determine the variables thought to be effective in DI and DMV, and multivariate logistic regression analysis was established for the parameters that were significant in univariate calculations. For each parameter thought to be effective in the univariate logistic regression analysis for DI and DMV, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the predictive role of intubation or MV in terms of difficulty and the cutoff value was determined for each parameter. The algorithm described by DeLong et al was used to compare the areas under two or more ROC curves. The statistical significance level was accepted as 0.05 in all analyses.

3. Results

A total of 87 patients (16 male and 71 female) were included between September 15, 2019 and September 15, 2020 (Fig. 1).

3.1. Difficult mask ventilation data

DMV was encountered in 15 (17.2%) of 87 patients included in the study, and easy MV was encountered in 72 (82.8%) patients. The mean age of the patients with DMV was 52.93 ± 11.25 years, while the mean age of the patients with easy MV was 44.37 ± 14.13 years. Of the 16 male patients included in the study, 10 (66.7%) were DMV and 6 (8.3%) were easy MV. Of the 71 female patients included in the study, only 5 (33.3%) were DMV. Six patients in the DMV group and 10 in the easy MV group had OSAS. Of the 15 patients with DMV, 10 had a CLC score of 2, 4 had a CLC of 3, and 1 had a CLC score of 4.

A statistically significant relationship was found between DMV and age, gender, OSAS, NC, CNC, NC/CNC, NC/TMD, and NC/SMD. There was no significant relationship between DMV and BMI, ASA score, MP score, upper lip bite test, distance between incisors, TMD, SMD, or thyromental height (Table 1).

4. Discussion and conclusion

Prediction and management of difficult airways is crucial for the prevention of anesthesia-related complications and reduction of morbidity and mortality. Due to the insufficiency of bedside tests to determine intubation difficulty, clinicians continue to search for easy-to-apply, reliable, and high-accuracy predictive tests.[4,12] Predictability and effective management of DMV are vital for anesthesiologists. MV may be more difficult in obese patients due to increased adipose tissue in the pharynx. Therefore, obese patients should be evaluated more carefully in terms of DMV. The incidence of DMV varies between 0.08% and 15% in the literature, depending on differences in definitions.[9,13,14] In our study, the incidence of DMV was 17.2% in obese patients.

Langeron et al[15] found a correlation between 55 years of age and DMV in the general population. Cattano et al[13] showed that being over the age of 48 is an independent risk factor for DMV. In our study, there was a significant relationship between age and DMV, and we found the cutoff value to be 50.5 years. However, age was not an independent risk factor for DMV.

Some studies on morbidly obese patients found male gender to be an independent risk factor for DMV.[14,16] Our study supported these findings and identified male gender as an independent risk factor for DMV.

Riad et al[16] found that male gender with a BMI > 45 kg/m2 was an independent risk factor for DMV. Langeron et al[15] showed that a BMI above 26 kg/m2 is an independent risk factor for DMV. Kheterpal et al[9] revealed that a BMI >30 kg/m2 in 22,600 patients was an independent risk factor for DMV. Some studies could not show a relationship between obesity and DMV.[17] In our study, no significant relationship was demonstrated between BMI and DMV in obese patients.

Leoni et al[14] studied obese patients with a BMI > 30 kg/m2 and found that NC > 46 cm, male gender, MP scoring, and limitation of mandible movement were risk factors for DMV. Riad et al[16] reported a significant relationship between an NC >42 cm and DMV in their study. Shailaja et al[18] also compared obese and thin patients and found that NC > 35 cm, SMD < 12.5 cm, presence of OSAS, BMI > 35 kg/m2, body weight > 70 kg, and neck movement limitation were risk factors for DMV. Cattano et al[13] found that an NC of more than 43 cm, being short-necked, and being over 49 years old were predictors of DMV. In our study, male gender and NC >45 cm were found to be independent risk factors for DMV. Neck extension restriction occurs with the increase of adipose tissue in the nape region, which leads to MV difficulties.[19] We excluded patients with cervical mobility below 80° from the study and minimized the effect of cervical mobility on measurements. We examined the relationship between CNC and DMV, which has never been searched before, and we found a statistically significant relationship (P < .01). We also examined the relationship between the ratio of NC to CNC and DMV and found a significant relationship. However, both values were not independent risk factors for DMV.

Endotracheal intubation of obese patients is when anesthetists feel most insecure in the clinical management of obese patients. In different studies, the use of special laryngoscope blades or auxiliary tools and the CLC system have been used to define DI.[20,21] With these differences in definition, the incidence of DI in obese patients has been reported as 1.8% to 14.3% in the literature.[3,18,22] A study reported the incidence of DI as 10.3% to 20.2% in obese patients.[23] We found the incidence of DI to be 16.1% in obese patients. We used the CLC system as part of the IDS scoring system. Nine of the 13 patients with CLC scores of III to IV had DI, and there was a significant correlation between CLC score and DI. These different findings in the literature may be due to differences in the definition of DI and in the demographic characteristics of the patients.

A significant relationship between obesity and DI has been shown in many studies.[3,4,16] A study revealed that BMI > 50 kg/m2 and NC > 42 cm are independent risk factors for DI and increase DI by 5 times.[16] There are also studies suggesting that there is no relationship between obesity and DI.[12,22] Brodsky et al[22] study could not show that BMI is an independent risk factor for DI. In our study, we could not find a significant relationship between DI and BMI in obese patients.

Shiga et al[4] revealed that MP does not provide an advantage in detecting DI or difficult laryngoscopy. Lee et al[12] reported that MP was a poor predictor of DI. Kim et al[6] showed that MP, Wilson score, and NC/TMD are independent risk factors for DI. MP scores of 3 and 4 and a balance of 43 cm in NC were found to be associated with DI in obese patients.[24] In our study, we found a significant correlation between MP and DI. However, MP could not be shown to be an independent risk factor for DI in the multivariate regression analysis.

Studies have suggested that obesity alone is not a predictor of the development of complications related to DI.[25] However, complications may increase due to the presence of factors that predispose patients to DI, such as OSAS and increased NC, which are more common in obese patients. DI can be seen due to changes in the upper airways of patients with a history of OSAS.[22] Siyam and Benhamou[26] found a significant correlation between DI and OSAS, but found none between the presence of severe OSAS, which was differentiated by the apnea-hypopnea index score, and DI. Neligan et al[27] did not find a relationship between OSAS and DI. In our study, we found no significant relationship between the presence of OSAS and DI.

Brodsky et al[22] found that increasing NC from 40 to 60 cm increased DI by 7 times. Riad et al[16] determined that a BMI >50 kg/m2 and an NC >42 cm are independent risk factors for DI. Gonzalez et al[24] reported a significant relationship between NC and DI in obese patients and found that an NC >43 cm was an independent risk factor for DI. Neligan et al[27] did not show a significant relationship between NC and DI in their study. Shailaja et al and Moon et al[18,28] did not find a significant relationship between NC and IDS scores. In our study, we detected NC as an independent risk factor for DI in obese patients. The NC cutoff value was 42.5 cm to predict DI.

There is no study in the literature regarding the role of CNC in predicting DI. Obesity increases nuchal thickness and limits neck extension, which may lead to DI. Therefore, we thought that an increase in CNC would increase the risk of DI. We revealed a statistically significant relationship between CNC and DI (P < .01). However, CNC alone was not an independent risk factor for DI. In obese individuals, more adipose tissue accumulates under the chin than in the nape region. Therefore, we aimed to determine the predictive value of NC/CNC in predicting DI in obese patients. We could not detect a significant relationship between NC/CNC and DI (P > .05).

Kim et al[6] thought that the increase in neck thickness and the formation of a short neck in obese patients would increase the difficulty of intubation and that NC/TMD would predict DI more accurately than just NC measurement. They found that it is a factor and that the NC/TMD ratio is the strongest predictor. Similarly, in our study, there was a significant relationship between NC/TMD and DI (P < .05) and a significant relationship between NC/SMD and DI (P < .05).

Etezadi et al[8] suggested the thyromental elevation test as a new method for estimating DI. In our study of obese patients, no significant relationship could be shown between the thyromental height test and DI. This difference in results can only be explained by our study of obese patients.

Arné et al[20] found a significant relationship between a TMD < 6 cm and DI. Shiga et al[4] found a significant correlation between decreased SMD and high IDS scores. Our study does not support these findings. Seo et al[29], in their study evaluating the risk factors for DI defined by the IDS scale, found a significant relationship between the distance between the incisors and DI, but they did not show an independent risk factor. We found a significant relationship between the distance between incisors and DI, and we showed that a distance of <4.85 cm between incisors is an independent risk factor for DI. In our study, 5 of 15 patients with DMV also had DI. A significant relationship was shown between DMV and DI. Some studies in the literature have found results similar to ours.[17,30]

Our study was limited in several ways. Since we are not a large hospital, our number of participants is not so high. Patients with cervical mobility <80° were not included in the study. Although, we tried to minimize the effect of cervical mobility on the measurements, we did not exclude all patients with cervical mobility limitation. This may have had a minimal effect on our measurements. Since our patient population consists of obese patients, it should be noted that cervical mobility is not completely normal in this patient group. This study needs to be supported by further, larger studies.

We believe that CNC may predict DI and DMV in obese patients. We think that the ratio of NC to CNC may be successful in predicting DMV in obese patients.
