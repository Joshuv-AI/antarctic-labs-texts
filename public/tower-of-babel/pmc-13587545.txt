Shared oral microbiome signatures linking periodontitis and autoimmune thyroiditis: NHANES 2009–2012
Authors: Xiangkun Meng, Lei Li, Rui Hao, Zhifeng Lin, Ke Liu, Gaoman Chi, Jingyuan Ren, Ru Liu, Yuge Gao, Xiaotong Yu, Xiaolin Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the association between periodontitis severity and autoimmune thyroiditis (AIT) in a nationally representative US sample and to explore oral microbiome features shared between the two conditions.MethodsWe analyzed adults from NHANES 2009–2012 with complete periodontal examinations, thyroid autoantibodies, and oral microbiome data (n = 1,346). Periodontitis was classified using CDC/AAP case definitions. AIT was defined as positivity for thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPOAb ≥ 9 IU/mL) and/or thyroglobulin antibodies (TgAb ≥ 4 IU/mL). Survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression estimated adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Oral microbiome diversity and differentially abundant genera were compared between groups.ResultsIn the fully adjusted model, mild and severe periodontitis were associated with higher odds of AIT compared with periodontally healthy individuals (mild: aOR 2.15, 95% CI 1.20–3.84; severe: aOR 2.77, 95% CI 1.22–6.28), whereas moderate periodontitis was not significantly associated (aOR 1.47, 95% CI 0.63–3.43). Genera enriched in both AIT and periodontitis included Fusobacterium, Bergeyella, Johnsonella, and Prevotella_2. Fusobacterium abundance increased across periodontal severity categories and showed a weak inverse correlation with TPOAb, whereas TT3 and FT3 showed weak positive correlations with Fusobacterium. ConclusionPeriodontitis severity was associated with AIT in this cross-sectional NHANES analysis, and Fusobacterium emerged as a shared genus associated with both periodontal status and thyroid autoimmunity. Longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to assess causality.

Introduction

AIT, mainly represented by Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, is one of the most common organ-specific autoimmune diseases with incidence estimates in the range of 0.3–1.5 per 1,000 people and a clear female predominance [1]. AIT is characterised by lymphocytic infiltration of the thyroid and circulating thyroid autoantibodies [2]. Both genetic susceptibility and environmental factors have been implicated in its pathogenesis, and chronic inflammatory exposures are considered potential triggers of thyroid autoimmunity. Periodontitis is a chronic infectious and inflammatory disease triggered by dysbiosis of dental plaque microorganisms, affecting hundreds of millions of people worldwide. Its primary clinical manifestations include gingival inflammation, periodontal pocket formation, alveolar bone resorption, and ultimately tooth loss [3]. Traditional views considered periodontitis a disease confined to the oral cavity, but research over the past two decades has considerably revised this understanding. As a persistent, low-grade source of systemic inflammation, periodontitis may influence tissues and organ systems beyond the oral cavity. Periodontal pathogens and their virulence factors (such as lipopolysaccharides) can enter the bloodstream, directly or indirectly activating systemic inflammatory pathways. These processes can trigger the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) and have been associated with the onset and progression of various systemic diseases [4].

Periodontitis is also a modifiable chronic inflammatory condition. Recent randomised trials have shown that supportive periodontal care protocols can improve periodontal outcomes over long-term follow-up, supporting the concept that sustained periodontal management may reduce inflammatory burden [5]. In addition, clinical trials have identified host regulatory layers, such as periodontal microRNAs associated with periodontal tissue remodelling, suggesting that host–microbe interactions in periodontal disease extend beyond taxonomy and may involve molecular mediators [6].

We hypothesised that periodontitis is associated with autoimmune thyroiditis and that shared oral microbial signatures may contribute to this relationship. Using NHANES 2009–2012, our primary aim was to evaluate the association between periodontitis severity and the prevalence of AIT in a nationally representative US adult population. Our secondary aim was to identify oral microbial genera enriched in both periodontitis and AIT and to explore their correlations with thyroid-related biomarkers.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

We used data from NHANES 2009–2012, a multistage, stratified survey designed to represent the non-institutionalised civilian US population. Adults aged ≥ 18 years were eligible. We included participants with complete periodontal examination data, thyroid autoantibodies (TPOAb and TgAb), and oral microbiome profiles.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria and missing data

Participants were excluded if they had missing data on periodontal status, thyroid autoantibodies, oral microbiome profiles, or prespecified covariates (e.g. age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, smoking status, hyperlipidemia, urinary iodine, and thyroid function markers). We performed a complete-case analysis and retained 1,346 participants in the final analytic sample. Participant selection is shown in Figure 1.

Screening flowchart for this study.

Survey weights

We accounted for the complex NHANES sampling design by incorporating examination weights, strata, and primary sampling units. For analyses combining NHANES 2009–2010 and 2011–2012, 2-year MEC weights were rescaled according to NHANES analytic guidance to obtain 4-year weights.

Exposure: periodontitis severity

Periodontal status was defined using the NHANES ‘Oral Health—Periodontal Exam’ protocol (six sites per tooth, up to 28 teeth). Periodontitis severity (none, mild, moderate, severe) was classified according to the CDC/AAP case definitions based on probing depth (PD) and clinical attachment loss (AL).

Diagnostic criteria

Serum thyroid hormone levels were quantified using standardised immunoassay techniques. Concentrations of free triiodothyronine (FT3), total triiodothyronine (TT3), and total thyroxine (TT4) were determined via competitive binding immunoenzymatic assays. Free thyroxine (FT4) was measured using a two-step enzyme immunoassay, while thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was assessed using a third-generation two-site immunoenzymatic assay. Titre of TPOAb and TgAb were evaluated with the Beckman Access2 Immunoassay System. Reference ranges for TSH, FT4, and FT3 were defined as 0.39–4.6 mIU/L, 9–25 pmol/L, and 2.5–3.9 pg/mL, respectively, based on previously established NHANES criteria [7].

Outcome: AIT

AIT was defined as positivity for TPOAb ≥ 9 IU/mL and/or TgAb ≥ 4 IU/mL [8,9], measured as described under Diagnostic criteria.

Sample collection and microbiome data processing

All microbiome assessment procedures and bioinformatics analyses were conducted by the NHANES study team, with processed data subsequently released via the NCHS Research Data Centre. Our research team performed downstream integration and analysis of the relevant datasets. Oral rinse samples were collected by instructing participants to rinse with mouthwash for 5 seconds, followed by three consecutive 5-second gargles. Examiners then changed gloves and carefully transferred the specimens into sterile collection cups, avoiding contact with the rim to prevent contamination. Genomic DNA was extracted using the Puregene DNA Purification Kit (Qiagen). Purified DNA—including NHANES oral rinse samples, blank controls, and quality control materials (artificial oral and gut communities)—was aliquoted into 96-well plates and shipped on dry ice to the Knight Laboratory at the University of California, San Diego. A total of 72 plates (2009–2010) and 60 plates (2011–2012) were prepared. Although sample placement across plates was not randomised, a standardised protocol and inclusion of quality control samples on each plate were implemented to mitigate batch effects. Upon arrival, plates were stored at −20 °C until further processing. PCR amplification targeted the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene using 515F–806R primers, as previously described. Sequencing was performed across 19 independent HiSeq runs. Any run or lane that failed (i.e. yielded no reads or < 100 reads across all samples), along with associated failed participant samples, was re-sequenced. For participants with samples sequenced in multiple runs, only data from the final (re-sequenced) run were retained for analysis.

Demultiplexed raw sequence files were initially processed using QIIME1, yielding 10,442 forward and 10,440 reverse FASTQ files. Due to the limited read length (125 bp), which impeded efficient overlap for paired-end merging, only forward reads were retained for downstream analysis using the DADA2 pipeline (v1.2.1). Each sequencing run was processed independently, and the resulting datasets were subsequently merged. DADA2 generated amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), representing high-resolution microbial features after quality filtering. ASVs were compiled into a feature table that recorded per-sample read counts. A phylogenetic tree was constructed based on ASV sequences, and non-bacterial or unassigned taxa were excluded using DADA2’s ‘Remove unassigned bacteria’ function.

Microbial diversity was assessed using QIIME2. α-diversity, reflecting within-sample diversity, was quantified using multiple metrics, including Observed ASVs (richness), Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, Shannon–Wiener index, inverse Simpson index, and Chao1. β-diversity, indicating between-sample dissimilarity, was evaluated using unweighted UniFrac, weighted UniFrac, and Bray–Curtis metrics. These distance matrices were visualised using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS); these ordinations were descriptive and were not used as formal tests of group separation.

Normalisation, rarefaction depth, and potential batch effects

For α- and β-diversity analyses, samples were rarefied to 10,000 reads to mitigate bias from uneven sequencing depth. The 10,000-read threshold was selected to retain the majority of samples while reaching near-plateau diversity patterns in rarefaction curves. For taxonomic composition analyses, relative abundances were calculated by dividing ASV counts by the total reads per sample.

Because samples were processed across multiple plates and sequencing runs and plate allocation was not randomised, we inspected the distribution of study groups across runs/plates and evaluated potential batch effects using ordination patterns of quality-control samples and sensitivity analyses adjusting for sequencing run/plate where metadata were available. Residual batch effects cannot be fully excluded and are considered in interpretation.

Covariates

The following continuous variables were included in the analysis, each presented with their Mean and Standard Error (SE): Age (also categorised into four groups: < 45, 45-54, 55-64, 65-74); Total bilirubin; Uric acid; Urinary iodine; Body mass index (BMI); Waist circumference (WC); Thyroglobulin antibodies; Free triiodothyronine; Free thyroxine; Thyroid stimulating hormone; Thyroid peroxidase antibodies; Total triiodothyronine; Total thyroxine. In addition to the continuous variables, several categorical variables were included, presented as follows: Age: categorised into < 45, 45-54, 55-64, 65-74; Marital status: categorised into Married, Widowed, Divorced, Separated, Never married, and Living with partner; Perio class: categorised into None, Mild, Moderate, and Severe; Gender: categorised into Male and Female; Race: categorised into Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and Other Race (including Multi-Racial); Smoking status: categorised into Never, Former, and Current; Education level: categorised into < High school, High school, and College or above; Drinking status: categorised into No and Yes; Physical activity level: categorised into Low and High physical activity (Poverty Income Ratio): categorised into < 1 and ≥ 1; Energy intake: categorised into Low and High. Several health status variables were also included, each presented as No or Yes: Hypertension; Diabetes; Depression; Sleep disorders; Hyperlipidemia; Anxiety; Cardiovascular disease. These variables were considered as potential confounders. Covariates included in the multivariable models were selected a priori based on established or plausible associations with both periodontitis and thyroid autoimmunity, including demographic, metabolic, thyroid-related, and iodine-related factors.

Statistical analysis

We accounted for the complex NHANES sampling design by incorporating examination weights, strata (SDMVSTRA), and primary sampling units (SDMVPSU). Continuous variables are reported as survey-weighted means (standard error, SE), while categorical variables are presented as weighted percentages (unweighted sample size, n). Group differences were assessed using survey-weighted t tests for continuous variables and Rao–Scott χ2 tests for categorical variables.

We used survey-weighted logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for AIT across periodontal categories (none, mild, moderate, severe), with periodontal health as the reference. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, and education. Model 3 further adjusted for prespecified covariates selected a priori based on known relationships with both periodontitis and thyroid autoimmunity (e.g. hyperlipidemia, urinary iodine, TSH, FT3, and total bilirubin).

To test for a linear trend, periodontal status was entered as an ordinal variable (0–3) in the regression model. Given the observed non-monotonic pattern, we also report the global Wald p-value for periodontal status treated as a categorical term. Subgroup analyses were exploratory and interpreted cautiously due to limited numbers of AIT cases within strata. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.0), and two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For comparisons of α-diversity and genus-level relative abundances across multiple periodontal groups, Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by Dunn’s post-hoc tests were used. For these multiple group comparisons, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Differentially abundant taxa were additionally identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) with a logarithmic LDA score threshold of 2.0. Spearman’ s rank correlation was used to explore associations between selected genus-level relative abundances and host variables. These correlation analyses were exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and nominal two-sided P values are reported.

Results

Clinical characteristics

Characteristics of the participants

The analytic sample comprised 1,346 participants, of whom 188 had AIT and 1,158 did not. The weighted mean age was 47.21 years (SE 0.42), with a weighted mean BMI of 29.40 kg/m2 (SE 0.31) and waist circumference of 100.29 cm (SE 0.78). Within the AIT patient group, 63.87% were female, and 70.05% had attained at least a university-level education. AIT prevalence varied significantly with age, educational attainment, ethnicity, and hyperlipidemia status (p < 0.05). In addition, 79.55% of participants with AIT were Non-Hispanic White. Participants with AIT had higher bilirubin and TSH levels and lower urinary iodine and FT3 levels (p < 0.05). A comprehensive summary of the baseline characteristics is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Univariate logistic regression

Univariate logistic regression (Table1) identified the following factors associated with higher AIT prevalence: age 45–54 years (OR = 1.80, 95% CI 1.25–2.59, p < 0.01), female sex (OR = 1.95, 95% CI 1.19–3.18, p < 0.05), elevated educational attainment (high school: OR = 2.31,95% CI 1.16–4.60, p < 0.05; college or above: OR = 2.57, 95% CI 1.51–4.39, p < 0.01), and hyperlipidemia (OR = 2.01,95% CI 1.30–3.12, p < 0.01). In contrast, current smoking was inversely related to AIT (OR = 0.54,95% CI 0.30–0.97, p < 0.05). Higher total bilirubin was also associated with higher odds of AIT (OR = 2.38,95% CI 1.19–4.74, p < 0.01).

Relationship between periodontitis and AIT

Table 2 presents the results of the weighted multi-model logistic regression analysis examining the association between periodontal disease severity and AIT. A non-monotonic pattern of association was observed in the fully adjusted models. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), which controlled for demographic factors, lipid profile, thyroid function tests, and urinary iodine levels, both mild and severe periodontitis were independently associated with higher odds of AIT compared to the periodontally healthy reference group. Specifically, the adjusted odds ratios (aORs) were 2.15 (95% CI 1.20–3.84, p = 0.020) for mild periodontitis and 2.77 (95% CI 1.22–6.28, p = 0.027) for severe periodontitis. In contrast, the association for moderate periodontitis was not statistically significant (aOR = 1.47, 95% CI 0.63–3.43, p = 0.381). In a parallel analysis with TgAb positivity alone as the outcome, no periodontal category was associated with TgAb - defined thyroid autoimmunity in any model.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were exploratory. Given the limited number of AIT cases within several strata, detailed subgroup results are provided in Supplementary Table 2 and should be interpreted with caution.

Oral microbiome dysbiosis in periodontitis

Relative abundance of species

Microbial composition shifted with increasing periodontitis severity (Figure 2A–D). At the phylum level, the dominant bacteria across the none, mild, moderate, and severe groups were Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Fusobacteria (Figure 2A, B). At the genus level, Streptococcus, Rothia, Prevotella_7, Veillonella, and Haemophilus were identified as the predominant genera across different periodontal statuses (Figure 2C, D). As disease severity advanced, the proportions of Bacteroidetes and Fusobacteria increased, while that of Streptococcus decreased.

Figure 2E showed differences in the abundance of core genera across the four groups. The severe group showed an increased relative abundance of periodontitis-associated genera such as Porphyromonas, Treponema, and Peptostreptococcus, whereas Streptococcus was more predominant in the healthy group. Although each group harboured some unique taxa, the majority of microbial communities (218 OTUs/ASVs) were consistently present across all four groups (Figure 2F). These patterns suggest that periodontitis is characterised not by the emergence of new species but by a redistribution of commensal and opportunistic bacteria.

Diversity analysis

Alpha diversity, assessed using the Shannon and Chao1 indices, showed a gradual increase with increasing periodontitis severity (Figure 2G, H). For beta diversity, community dissimilarities were visualised using PCoA and NMDS (Figure 2I, J). The ordinations suggested partial separation among the four periodontal groups, particularly along the first ordination axis. These patterns are descriptive and, by themselves, do not establish statistically significant differences in overall community structure, but they are consistent with graded shifts in microbial composition across periodontal status.

Microbial differences among none, mild, moderate, and severe groups

After filtering with LDA ≥ 2.0, 17 genera showed significant differential abundance. Periodontitis-associated genera—Porphyromonas, Treponema, Prevotella, and Fusobacterium—were enriched in the severe group, whereas health-associated commensals such as Streptococcus and Haemophilus were predominant in the healthy group (Figure 2K). Differential abundance analysis further supported the over-representation of periodontal pathogens and depletion of Streptococcus in the severe group (Figure 2L). These taxa may reflect a shift from a commensal-dominated to a pathogen-enriched microbiome.

Diversity analysis

Alpha-diversity was evaluated using the Shannon index (Figure 3E) and the Chao1 index (Figure 3F). Both metrics tended to be lower in the AIT group than in the None_ AIT group, but these differences did not reach statistical significance; these observations are consistent with modestly reduced microbial richness and evenness in AIT and should be interpreted cautiously. Beta-diversity was assessed through PCoA (Figure 3G) and NMDS (Figure 3H). Visual inspection of the ordinations suggested a tendency toward separation between the AIT and None_ AIT groups along the first principal coordinate (PCo1, 17.7% explained variance) and the first NMDS axis; these descriptive patterns are consistent with, but do not establish, differences in overall microbial community structure (Figure 3G, H).

Microbial differences between AIT and None_ AIT groups

A total of seven genera exhibited differential abundance between groups. G_Johnsonella, g_Blastocatella, g_Prevotella_2, g_Capnocytophaga, g_Bergeyella, and g_Fusobacterium were significantly enriched in the AIT group, whereas g_Atopobium was more abundant in the None_ AIT group (Figure 3I, J). Correlation analyses identified associations between differentially abundant genera and host factors. The abundance of g_Fusobacterium was positively correlated with urinary iodine levels and negatively correlated with age, whereas g_Atopobium showed the opposite trend, being positively associated with age and negatively associated with race. Additionally, g_Capnocytophaga showed a negative correlation with Perio class, suggesting a potential link between this genus and periodontal health status.

Fusobacterium, Bergeyella, Prevotella_2, and Johnsonella showed distinct flow patterns in the severe periodontitis and AIT groups, suggesting differences in their relative abundance across these groups (Figure 4B).

Figure 4B shows that the relative abundance of the shared genera varied with periodontitis severity. The genus Fusobacterium increased progressively across severity categories, with the lowest levels observed in the periodontally healthy group and the highest levels in the severe periodontitis group. In contrast, Bergeyella displayed a different pattern, with its peak abundance occurring in the mild periodontitis group, followed by a decrease in the moderate and severe categories. The abundance of Prevotella_2 was elevated in all periodontitis groups compared to the healthy baseline, though a strictly progressive increase with disease severity was not evident. The relative abundance of Johnsonella remained relatively stable across all groups, with only a marginal increase noted in the moderate periodontitis group.

When participants with and without AIT were compared, more modest differences were observed (Figure 4C). A slight increase in the relative abundance of Fusobacterium and Prevotella_2 was noted in the AIT group compared to the non-AIT group. The abundances of Bergeyella and Johnsonella were largely comparable between the two groups.

Discussion

In this nationally representative NHANES sample, periodontitis severity was associated with autoimmune thyroiditis prevalence, and oral microbiome profiling highlighted Fusobacterium as a shared genus enriched in both conditions. Participants with AIT also had higher total bilirubin and lower urinary iodine levels. AIT was also more prevalent among non-Hispanic White participants (79.55% of AIT cases). We observed a non-monotonic pattern across periodontal categories, with significant associations for mild and severe periodontitis but not for moderate disease. This pattern may reflect heterogeneity within the moderate group and limited power for some contrasts, rather than a true biological discontinuity. Accordingly, categorical models may better capture potential non-linear associations than a simple linear trend test, and these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. TgAb showed no significant association with any grade of periodontitis across all models. These observations suggest that periodontitis may be more closely associated with TPOAb -defined than with TgAb -defined thyroid autoimmunity. Consistent with our findings, a recent cross-sectional study reported a higher prevalence and greater severity of periodontitis in patients with autoimmune hypothyroidism than in healthy controls, and TPOAb levels correlated with mean clinical attachment loss [10]. Together, these data indicate an association between periodontal disease and thyroid autoimmunity, although the direction of this relationship cannot be determined from cross-sectional evidence.

Further analysis of the oral microbiota associated with periodontitis and AIT identified shared genera, including Fusobacterium, Bergeyella, Johnsonella, and Prevotella_2. TPOAb was weakly inversely correlated with Fusobacterium abundance, with higher TPOAb levels corresponding to lower Fusobacterium abundance.TT3 and FT3 showed weak positive correlations with Fusobacterium, while Prevotella_2 also correlated positively with FT3, indicating that higher levels of these hormones were accompanied by higher abundances of these genera. BMI and WC correlated negatively with Fusobacterium, indicating lower Fusobacterium abundance with greater obesity. Age correlated negatively with Fusobacterium, showing lower abundance with increasing age. Smoking showed positive correlations with Bergeyella and Johnsonella, indicating that behavioural factors may influence these taxa. Although current smoking was inversely associated with AIT in univariable analyses, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given potential residual confounding and reverse causality. We did not design the study to infer a protective effect of smoking, and any microbiome-related interpretation remains speculative.

The inverse correlation between TPOAb and Fusobacterium was weak (ρ = −0.07) and was derived from an exploratory correlation analysis. Therefore, it should not be interpreted as evidence for either a pathogenic or protective effect of Fusobacterium. TPOAb reflects only one component of thyroid autoimmunity, while genus-level relative abundance is compositional and may vary with host characteristics and the abundance of other taxa. Given the cross-sectional design, the directionality and biological significance of this association cannot be determined. Accordingly, our findings support Fusobacterium as a shared microbial feature rather than as a causal common pathogen.

Excessive inflammatory responses lead to periodontal tissue destruction and alveolar bone loss, with oral microbial dysbiosis being a key contributing factor. Porphyromonas gingivalis is widely recognised as a key pathogen in periodontitis. Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) produced by Porphyromonas gingivalis act as potent immune stimulants, while proteases such as gingipains degrade host proteins, disrupt tissue structures, and interfere with immune signalling. LPS is a major component of the cell wall in Gram-negative bacteria, possessing potent immunostimulatory effects that activate the innate immune system and trigger inflammatory responses. LPS promotes inflammation by activating the Toll-like receptor (TLR) signalling pathway, particularly TLR4, thereby inducing the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [11]. In experimental mice, LPS together with thyroglobulin abrogated self-tolerance and induced thyroiditis, suggesting that LPS may participate in the progression of autoimmune thyroiditis by modulating autoimmune responses [12]. Additionally, LPS may further promote inflammatory responses by activating non-canonical inflammatory pathways mediated by inflammasomes such as caspase-11 [13]. Although these findings point to potential pathways through which periodontal inflammation might contribute to thyroid autoimmunity, they remain mechanistic hypotheses that require direct experimental validation.

Oxidative stress may represent a plausible mechanistic link between periodontal dysbiosis and thyroid autoimmunity. Periodontal inflammation driven by dysbiotic communities can increase local and systemic reactive oxygen species, amplify redox-sensitive inflammatory signalling, and promote cytokine release. In autoimmune thyroiditis, oxidative stress has been implicated in thyroid tissue injury and loss of immune tolerance, potentially facilitating autoantibody production. Chronic periodontal inflammation and dysbiosis may therefore contribute to a systemic pro-oxidative milieu that favours thyroid autoimmunity in susceptible individuals. Future studies integrating oxidative stress biomarkers and longitudinal designs are needed to test this hypothesis.

Oral microorganisms, particularly pathogenic bacteria, can metabolically produce various short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate and propionate. These molecules typically exhibit pro-inflammatory effects at high concentrations and can induce cytokine production [14]. Recent research indicates that the tryptophan-indole metabolic pathway in Porphyromonas gingivalis plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of periodontitis. Indole not only functions as a signalling molecule between bacteria, influencing biofilm formation and microbial community structure, but also directly modulates the host's inflammatory response and contributes to tissue destruction [15]. A Mendelian randomisation study suggests that microbial tryptophan metabolism into indole compounds (e.g. 3-indoleacetic acid) may exacerbate AIT by activating inflammasomes such as NLRP3 [16]. The modifiability of periodontal inflammation is clinically relevant. A randomised controlled trial evaluating supportive periodontal care protocols reported meaningful improvements in bleeding on probing and related periodontal outcomes over long-term follow-up, supporting the concept that sustained periodontal management can modify the inflammatory burden [5]. In addition, a randomised clinical trial reported that periodontal microRNAs associated with periodontal tissue remodelling change during clinical interventions, highlighting host regulatory layers that may interact with microbial dysbiosis [6]. Together, these data support further interventional and mechanistic studies to determine whether periodontal treatment can influence thyroid autoimmunity markers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this cross-sectional NHANES analysis, periodontitis severity was associated with a higher prevalence of autoimmune thyroiditis, and this association was more evident for TPOAb-defined thyroid autoimmunity. Oral microbiome profiling identified Fusobacterium as a shared genus enriched in both periodontitis and AIT, supporting the existence of a potential shared microbial signature between the two disorders. However, causal inference is not possible, and residual confounding and batch effects in microbiome data cannot be excluded. Prospective and interventional studies are needed to determine whether periodontal treatment and supportive periodontal care can modify thyroid autoimmunity markers and clinical outcomes.
