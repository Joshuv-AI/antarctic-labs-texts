Dual microvascular decompression for concurrent trigeminal neuralgia and hemifacial spasm
Authors: Eduardo López, Contanza Muñoz, Esteban Torche, Juan Varela, Sophie Scheel, Jorge Mura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Concurrent trigeminal neuralgia (TN) and hemifacial spasm (HFS) arising from two separate offending vessels, each compressing a distinct cranial nerve, is an uncommon and markedly disabling presentation of neurovascular compression syndrome. Unlike cases in which a single artery accounts for both syndromes, this dual-vessel configuration demands individual identification and decompression of each conflict through a shared operative corridor.Case Description: A 71-year-old man presented with a 13-year history of pharmacologically refractory left V1–V3 TN (Barrow Neurological Institute [BNI] grade IV) and left HFS only partially controlled with quarterly botulinum toxin injections; pre-operative facial motor function was intact (House-Brackmann grade I). Fast Imaging Employing Steady-state Acquisition (FIESTA)-sequence magnetic resonance imaging identified a dolichoectatic basilar artery compressing the left trigeminal nerve and a separate conflict between the left anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) and the facial nerve. Through a left retrosigmoid approach, both conflicts were addressed with independent Teflon pledget interposition secured with fibrin sealant. Lateral spread response (LSR) monitoring served as a continuous electrophysiological marker, with LSR abolition confirming adequate decompression following AICA mobilization.Conclusion: At 6-month follow-up, the patient maintained complete pain freedom (BNI I), sustained HFS resolution, and intact facial function (House-Brackmann I), having discontinued all anticonvulsant therapy and botulinum toxin injections. This case illustrates that dual microvascular decompression via the retrosigmoid approach can safely address concurrent TN and HFS caused by two anatomically distinct vessels, and reinforces intraoperative LSR monitoring as a reliable endpoint for decompression adequacy.

INTRODUCTION

Neurovascular compression syndrome (NVCS) encompasses disorders caused by pulsatile arterial or venous contact with a cranial nerve.[10] The most common entities are trigeminal neuralgia (TN), characterized by recurrent, brief, electric shock-like unilateral facial pain triggered by innocuous stimuli, affecting 4–5/100,000 persons per year, and hemifacial spasm (HFS), defined by involuntary contractions of facial muscles innervated by the facial nerve, with an incidence of 0.74– 11/100,000 per year.[1,2,4,11]

The simultaneous occurrence of TN and HFS in the same patient is uncommon.[6,8,12] Critically, the underlying neurovascular anatomy is not uniform: while some reported cases involve a single offending vessel compressing both cranial nerves, others, as in the present case, are caused by two entirely distinct arterial structures, each independently compressing a separate nerve. This dual-vessel configuration carries specific surgical implications, requiring identification and decompression of two independent neurovascular conflicts. In patients with dolichoectatic vertebrobasilar disease and concordant clinicoradiological findings, microvascular decompression (MVD) represents the definitive etiologic treatment for both disorders in a single procedure.[6-8] This video article illustrates the surgical technique and outcome of dual MVD for concurrent TN and HFS caused by a dolichoectatic basilar artery compressing the trigeminal nerve and a separate anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) compressing the facial nerve [Video 1].

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 71-year-old man with a history of arterial hypertension and ischemic stroke in 2023 with residual left hemiparesis (Medical Research Council grade 4/5) presented with a 13-year history of recurrent paroxysmal lancinating left facial pain. Pain initially involved the left maxillary division (V2) and subsequently progressed to affect all three trigeminal divisions (V1–V3). Episodes were brief but severe, occurring multiple times daily, with a pre-operative Barrow Neurological Institute (BNI) pain score of IV, indicating inadequate pain control despite medical therapy. He had required multiple emergency department visits and had been treated with analgesics, benzodiazepines, and anticonvulsants without sustained benefit, consistent with pharmacologically refractory TN.

Concurrently, he had been followed by a neurologist for left HFS and was receiving botulinum toxin injections every three months, achieving only partial and temporary symptomatic control. At pre-operative evaluation, facial motor function was intact (House-Brackmann [HB] grade I), with no evidence of facial paresis. Neurosurgical evaluation confirmed left TN involving V1–V3 and concomitant left HFS.

Magnetic resonance imaging with FIESTA sequences demonstrated a dolichoectatic vertebrobasilar system producing compression and marked displacement of the left trigeminal nerve, as well as a neurovascular conflict between the left AICA and cranial nerve VII. Given the concordant clinical and neuroimaging findings of double neurovascular conflict and the failure of conservative management, surgical MVD was indicated.

Illustrative surgical technique

Anesthesia, positioning, and monitoring

After induction of total intravenous anesthesia, the patient was placed in the park-bench (lateral decubitus) position with the left side up. The head was fixed in a three-pin Mayfield clamp and positioned to optimize exposure of the left cerebellopontine angle (CPA), with slight neck flexion and rotation. Continuous intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) was established, including free-running and triggered electromyography of facial muscles (orbicularis oculi and orbicularis oris). Pathognomonic lateral spread response (LSR), an electrophysiological hallmark of HFS resulting from ephaptic transmission within the facial nerve, was identified and continuously monitored as a surrogate marker for facial nerve decompression adequacy. The scalp was shaved, prepared with chlorhexidine, and draped with an antimicrobial incise film.

Craniotomy and dural opening

A left retroauricular curvilinear incision was made, and dissection was carried through subcutaneous tissue and musculature in a layered fashion, preserving the occipital neurovascular structures. A left retrosigmoid craniotomy was performed, centered over the junction of the transverse and sigmoid sinuses. The bony opening was sized to provide adequate working angles to both the trigeminal and facial nerves without excessive cerebellar retraction. The dura was opened in a Y-shaped fashion and reflected toward the sigmoid sinus. The cerebellomedullary cistern was opened microsurgically to allow cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, providing satisfactory cerebellar relaxation without the need for fixed retractors.

Microsurgical exploration and decompression

Under high-power microscopic magnification, careful stepwise arachnoid dissection of the CPA was performed. The trigeminal and facial nerves were systematically identified. Direct microsurgical inspection confirmed a dolichoectatic and tortuous basilar artery compressing the trigeminal nerve, producing visible nerve displacement, consistent with the long-standing history of TN. The left AICA was identified in close neurovascular conflict with the facial nerve, consistent with the electrophysiological finding of LSR.

Microsurgical mobilization of the offending vessels was performed under continuous IONM. A shaped Teflon pledget was carefully interposed between the basilar artery and the trigeminal nerve, ensuring adequate cushioning without excessive bulk. Subsequently, a second Teflon pledget was precisely positioned between the AICA and the facial nerve. Both implants were secured with fibrin sealant to prevent displacement. Satisfactory decompression was confirmed by intraoperative disappearance of the LSR following facial nerve decompression, a reliable electrophysiological endpoint associated with favorable HFS outcomes.

Closure

Meticulous hemostasis was achieved under microsurgical visualization. The dura was closed in watertight fashion using 4-0 nylon suture, reinforced with a fascial patch graft. Fibrin sealant was applied over the dural repair. The wound was copiously irrigated with normal saline. The craniotomy bone flap was replaced and fixed, and the wound was closed in anatomical layers. The patient tolerated the procedure without intraoperative complications and was transferred to the intensive care unit for post-operative monitoring.

Post-operative course and follow-up

In the immediate post-operative period, both HFS and TN resolved completely. The patient reported no facial pain from the 1st post-operative day, and no spontaneous facial muscle contractions were observed. Facial motor function remained intact (HB grade I), with no new neurological deficits attributable to the surgical intervention.

At 6-month follow-up, the patient maintained complete pain freedom (BNI score I) and had not experienced any recurrence of HFS. He discontinued all anticonvulsant and analgesic medications and no longer required botulinum toxin injections. Facial motor function remained preserved (HB grade I). No delayed surgical complications, including CSF leak, pseudomeningocele, infection, or hearing loss, were identified during the follow-up period. The postoperative outcome is consistent with successful cranial nerve decompression.

DISCUSSION

Dual NVCS involving concurrent TN and HFS is uncommon. A critical anatomical distinction separates these cases: some involve a single vessel compressing both nerves, while others, as here, present with two entirely separate offending arteries, each independently responsible for one syndrome. This dual-vessel configuration demands individual identification and decompression of each neurovascular conflict. The coexistence of both syndromes may initially be managed separately without recognizing their shared compressive substrate and the possibility of a single definitive surgical solution.[6,10] Pre-operative magnetic resonance imaging with FIESTA constitutes the current standard for characterizing these conflicts, providing precise delineation of neurovascular relationships at the brainstem.[5]

The retrosigmoid approach provides a single surgical corridor for simultaneous exposure of both the trigeminal and facial nerves in the CPA.[7,8] Park-bench positioning with meticulous head fixation, combined with CSF drainage through the cerebellopontine cistern, achieves adequate cerebellar relaxation without fixed retractors, minimizing retraction-related injury. Teflon interposition secured with fibrin sealant offers durable, biocompatible decompression with low recurrence rates for both TN and HFS.[8,9,11]

Intraoperative LSR monitoring is indispensable in HFS cases: Its persistence after apparent decompression should prompt further exploration, while its disappearance, as confirmed in this case after AICA decompression, provides real-time electrophysiological assurance of adequate facial nerve decompression, with abolition rates of 70–90% in favorable outcomes.[3,11] At 6-month follow-up, complete pain freedom (BNI I), HFS resolution, and preserved facial function (HB I) were maintained, consistent with published results for MVD in dual compression syndromes.[8,12] Long-term surveillance remains necessary given the possibility of late recurrence after initially successful MVD.[9,11]

CONCLUSION

This case illustrates a rare dual NVCS involving concurrent TN (BNI IV, refractory to pharmacotherapy) and HFS caused by a dolichoectatic basilar artery and AICA compression. In patients with concordant clinical and neuroimaging findings, dual MVD via the retrosigmoid approach allows definitive etiologic treatment of both disorders through a single operative corridor. IONM with continuous LSR surveillance is an indispensable tool for confirming adequate facial nerve decompression. At 6-month follow-up, the patient achieved complete pain freedom (BNI I), HFS resolution, and preserved facial motor function (HB I), without requirement for further medical therapy. These results support MVD as the gold-standard treatment for selected patients with dual compression syndrome and highlight the importance of systematic clinicoradiological correlation to identify a shared surgical target.
